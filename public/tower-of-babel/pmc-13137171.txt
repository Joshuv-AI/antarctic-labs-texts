Numerical analysis of three-dimensional middle ear and inner ear based on nonlinear and viscoelastic constitutive relations
Authors: Wenjuan Yao, Jiakun Wang, Junhua Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hearing is a critical sensory function for humans, and the dynamic behavior of the human ear is influenced by its constitutive relation. In this paper, a finite element model (FEM) of the middle ear with a nonlinear constitutive relation and the inner ear with a viscoelastic constitutive relation was developed based on CT scanning data of the clinical human ear. The frequency-response at the tympanic membrane (TM) and stapes footplate (SF), as well as the amplitude-frequency response and time-domain response curves at different positions of the basilar membrane (BM) were obtained by using the acoustic-solid and fluid-solid coupling dynamic analysis. The results indicated that the previous linear elastic model exhibits an error of 28% relative to experimental data, while the nonlinear-viscoelastic model proposed in this paper demonstrates an error of only 5% relative to experimental data. This model not only better align with the actual biomaterial properties of ear tissue, but also more accurately simulates the mechanical behavior of the human ear during sound perception. The realistic biomaterial model provides a reasonable and accurate numerical simulation platform for studying the biomechanical behavior of the real human ear, laying an applied foundation for related research into hearing damage.

Introduction

The ear is an important sensory organ of the human body, which mainly includes the outer ear, middle ear, and inner ear. The function of the middle ear is sound transmission, which converts the sound received by the outer ear canal into mechanical signals, and then transmits it to the inner ear through the tympanic membrane (TM) and the ossicular chain. The pressure difference between the scala vestibuli (SV) and the scala tympani (ST) causes the vibration of the basilar membrane (BM) in the cochlea, which contains the mutual coupling of multiple physical fields.1 However, the sound perception mechanism is so complex and is still not fully understood, which will restrict the clinical treatment of hearing loss and become one of the medical problems that need to be solved urgently.

Due to the fine structure of the middle ear and inner ear and the complex and diverse boundary conditions, researchers have been limited in their research on the mechanical of sound perception process to a certain extent. Early scholars mostly used anatomy and experimental methods to observe and study the mechanical behavior of the middle ear and inner ear,2, 3, 4, 5 so as to obtain a preliminary understanding of the mechanism of sound perception. Many scholars use Laser Doppler Vibrometer to measure the vibration parameters of the TM and stapes footplate (SF) of the animal's middle ear under external excitation.6,7 In the later period, some scholars represented by Gan systematically tested the conduction dynamic parameters of the middle ear, and obtained the displacement of the umbo and stapes of middle ear in the direction perpendicular to the SF through the pure tone excitation of the fresh temporal bone samples of the human body.8 The experimental exploration in the inner ear mainly focuses on the structure of the cochlea. In 1960, Békésy first proposed the traveling wave theory of BM through the experimental exploration of the cochlea in the inner ear, and thus he won the Nobel Prize in Medicine or Physiology.9

However, due to the requirements of moral ethics and the limitation of the accuracy of measurement equipment, the difficulty of experimental measurements is greatly increased. In addition to experimental measurement research, researchers have proposed many analytical model and numerical simulation model for the middle ear and cochlea to explore the sound transmission mechanism of the human ear and the relationship between ear structure and function. Eiber10 used the theory of multi body systems to study the mechanical problems of the middle ear structure, and found that the amplitude of the stapes decreased at high frequencies, while at low frequencies, the amplitude was relatively large. Pozrikidis used the boundary integral method to analyze the dynamics of the cochlea and the BM, and the results showed that the vibration of the BM is less affected by the position of the cochlear bone wall and the precise position of the oval window (OW) and round window (RW).11 Prendergast et al.12,13 studied the influence of the TM's ventilation tubes on TM vibration and stress and the influence of the size characteristics of TM's ventilation tubes on sound transmission by using a finite element model (FEM) without considering the structure of the middle ear cavity. Kolston established a three-dimensional (3D) linear cochlear mechanical model and solved the relationship between the amplitude and phase of BM under frequency changes in the steady-state.14 Yao et al.15 developed a mechanical model of the spiral cochlea and a numerical simulation model that conforms to the human ear, which not only reproduced the classical BM traveling wave motion, but also revealed the existence of a new standing wave vibration mode at low frequencies. Gan et al. completed a finite element analysis of the coupling of sound-solid-fluid multiple physical fields by establishing a complete 3D FEM of the human ear consisting of a linear cochlea, and predicted the internal pressure change of the cochlea caused by the sound pressure in the ear canal and the frequency selection characteristics of the BM motion.16,17 In most of the current model studies, the ear model (including middle and inner ears) based on linear materials is widely used, and there are still some differences between the numerical simulation results and the experimental results. This approach fails to capture the nonlinear mechanical properties of the middle ear and the viscoelastic characteristics of cochlear tissue.

With the rapid development of theoretical model and numerical simulation model, many scholars have continuously improved the material properties of human ears such as TM, stapes tendon and RW membrane through experiments,18, 19, 20, 21 and obtained the constitutive relationship of the corresponding ear biological tissue materials, which makes the calculation and analysis of the model more convenient and effective, and provides rich data for human ear research. Wang et al.22 developed a FEM of the middle ear that includes material nonlinearity by fitting experimental data, and analyzed the dynamic characteristics of the TM and the SF under static-pressure loading. Zhang and Gan23 investigated the viscoelastic characteristics of the human RW membrane based on experimental measurements and finite element modeling, providing important guidance for subsequent research into the mechanics of the middle ear and cochlea. Zhao et al.24 established a FEM of the human cochlea incorporating viscoelastic properties of the RW membrane, and explored hearing loss caused by anterior fissure sclerosis and base-turn and apex-turn of the cochlear window. It is worth noting that in the aforementioned modeling studies, researchers have typically considered only the individual effects of nonlinearity in the middle ear or viscoelasticity in the inner ear, with few studies integrating both of these material properties into a combined model of the middle and inner ears.

Apart from the cochlea's active feedback mechanism, a more significant reason for the discrepancy between numerical simulations and experimental results is that the established model neglects the nonlinear and viscoelastic material properties of the human ear. Therefore, based on the real CT scanning data, this study employs the nonlinear constitutive relations of the middle ear and viscoelastic constitutive relations of the inner ear to better reflect the true biomaterial properties of the ear structures. Simultaneously, the finite element method is used to investigate mechanical behavior, accounting for fluid-solid coupling effects between the BM, RW, OW, and perilymph fluid within the cochlea, thereby providing a more accurate portrayal of the passive sound transmission mechanism in the human ear.

The establishment of FEM

The geometric dimensions of the FEM are based on images obtained from CT scanning of the normal human ear in Zhongshan Hospital affiliated to Fudan University. The specific steps for creating geometric models of the middle and inner ears from CT scanning data were as follows:1.Image processing. Due to CT scanning images being 2D data, these were stored in DICOM format, imported into a self-compiling Matlab program, and used to extract geometric structures based on differences in image gray-scale thresholds. The data was then imported into Mimics software to create 3D solid models of the middle and inner ear structures, thereby generating preliminary rough geometric models.2.Geometric optimization. The preliminary geometric model obtained above exhibited structural defects such as distortion and rough surfaces. This rough preliminary geometric model was then imported into Patran software, where functions such as local refinement and surface fitting were utilized, and smoothing processing was applied to generate smooth 3D geometric models of the middle and inner ears.

Model computational details

The meshing details of the human ear model, including the overall structure and each section, are shown in Fig. 1. The middle and inner ear's model developed in this paper comprises a total of 13,326 nodes, 26,567 Tet4 elements, 4047 Hex8 elements, 778 Quad4 elements, and 30 Tria3 elements. After model optimization, the overall average mesh quality is approximately 0.81, which is close to 1. This meets the accuracy and convergence requirements for the fluid-solid coupling calculation process. In addition, using Comsol software, this model calculates the mechanical response of the middle and inner ear structures in the frequency range from 0.1 kHz to 10 kHz. The frequency-domain analysis was performed using the iterative solver MUMPS with a convergence factor of 0.0001. The computer used an Intel Pentium 2020M CPU with a max-turbo frequency of 2.4 GHz and 8 GB of RAM, with each computational run taking approximately 5 h.

To further verify mesh convergence, three sets of Coarse, Medium, and Refine meshes were established for mesh-independence verification. As shown in Fig. 2, under identical computational conditions, the BM displacement curves for the medium and refine meshes (at approximately 19 mm from the base of the cochlea) are essentially identical, with an error of less than 5%. This indicates that the computational results exhibit good mesh convergence and further meet the requirements for computational accuracy. Refine mesh were used in the subsequent calculations of this research.Fig. 2The verification of mesh independence (frequency-domain response of BM displacement at 19 mm from the cochlear base).Fig. 2

Especially in the high frequency range, the linear FEA results is significantly lower than the experimental data, with an average reduction of 3dB-5dB. It can be seen that compared with the linear FEA results, the nonlinear FEA results of the middle ear have improved and are more in line with the experimental data. Therefore, it is more reasonable and effective to use the nonlinear model to analyze the mechanical behavior of the middle ear.

Discussion

In order to better understand the dynamic behavior of the BM, time-domain analysis was carried out based on this model. The time step is defined as 0.000005s, and the total calculation time is 0.003s. From the time domain calculation, it can be seen from Fig. 11 that the pressure of the SV and ST near different positions of the BM changes over time, and the two basically change symmetrically with respect to the zero value of pressure. The farther away from the bottom of the BM, the later the peak pressure appears, and the shorter the time to maintain the peak pressure. As the distance from the BM increases, the maximum pressure value decreases and then increases, and the maximum pressure value is 0.23Pa. It can be seen that the pressure at a position of 30 mm near the top of the BM will be close to zero. This is due to the fact that the SV and ST are connected at the apex of the helicotrema, and the pressures offset each other. It can be concluded that the pure tone excitation acts on the OW, forming a pressure difference between the SV and ST, and the resulting pressure wave is transmitted along the longitudinal direction of the BM from the base to the apex, and finally dissipated at the helicotrema.

The displacement time relationship curves at different positions of the spiral BM calculated from Fig. 12 show that the farther away from the base of the BM, the later the peak displacement appears, and the shorter the time to maintain the peak displacement. The displacement-time relationship curve is consistent with the pressure-time relationship curve, with the maximum displacement at 3 mm, 12 mm, 24 mm, and 30 mm positions being 1.18 nm, 1.08 nm, 2.33 nm, and 3.46 nm, respectively. The reason why the displacement of 12 mm is smaller than that of 3 mm is that the elastic modulus of the BM is still relatively large, and the pressure difference between the SV and ST is relatively small.

Compared with the previous linear elastic models, the viscoelastic model reveals distinct frequency-dependent differences in BM mechanical response, which derive from the passive energy dissipation characteristics of living cochlear tissue. For the displacement at the same position of the BM, the viscoelastic model prediction results decreased amplitude in the lower-to-middle frequency range (0.1kHz-3.5 kHz), reflecting the damping effects of cochlear viscoelasticity that the linear elastic assumption cannot capture. However, compared with experimental observations, the linear elastic model overestimates the BM's amplitude in the 0.1-5 kHz range (by 28% relative to experimental data) and exhibits a premature peak frequency at 2 kHz (with an error of 20%), which leads to inconsistencies with the actual dynamic behavior of the human ear's BM. In contrast, the viscoelastic model achieved peak positions that closely matched the experimental data (with an error of approximately 5%) and the same peak-amplitude, demonstrating that the frequency-dependent viscoelastic damping is crucial for accurately capturing the real BM's dynamic characteristics.

Similarly, the effective application of the strain-related nonlinear elastic modulus E(ε) (for middle ear tissues) and the time-related viscoelastic modulus E(t) (for inner ear tissues) addresses a critical limitation of traditional linear elastic models, specifically: traditional linear elastic models cannot account for the strain response characteristics of the TM or the relaxation time-dependent damping of the cochlear structure. A key finding of the parameter sensitivity analysis is that, within physiologically reasonable ranges, variations in the parameters of nonlinear and viscoelastic materials result in only minor changes in the model predictions (with errors <5%), and the results remain relatively stable. Consequently, this validates the reliability of the nonlinear constitutive relationship of the middle ear and the viscoelastic constitutive relationship of the inner ear. Furthermore, the relevant material parameters, derived from previously published literature,18,22,23 enables a more accurate simulation of the passive sound transmission mechanism in the human ear.

Limitations and future prospects

Based on real CT scanning data, this research employs 3D inverse modeling to establish FEM of the middle ear (with a nonlinear constitutive relation) and inner ear (with a viscoelastic constitutive relation) consistent with human tissue properties. The aim is to investigate the differences between its passive biomechanical behavior and that of linear elastic constitutive models, thereby demonstrating that incorporating the nonlinear constitutive model for the middle ear and the viscoelastic constitutive model for the inner ear can effectively improve the accuracy of the model. This model currently has the following main limitations. First, the organ of Corti and its associated cochlear microstructure have not been effectively modeled. Second, the active cochlear mechanisms, such as active amplification by outer hair cells, are not included in the current model. Although passive models are sufficient for revealing the passive sound transmission mechanisms from the middle ear to the cochlea, future modeling studies will fully account for the multiscale, multi-structural interaction between the microstructure of the organ of Corti and the macrostructure of the cochlea, as well as the fluid-solid-electric-ionic multi-field coupling and energy transfer processes involved in active cochlear sound perception, thereby enabling more comprehensive numerical simulations to elucidate the active amplification mechanisms of the cochlea. Finally, this study focuses on the frequency range of 0.2 kHz to 10 kHz, consistent with most published experimental data on the dynamics of the human middle ear and cochlea (covering clinically relevant speech frequencies and validated measurement ranges). Future work will expand this model to higher frequency ranges (above 10 kHz) to investigate the effects of nonlinearity and viscoelastic material properties on high-frequency sound transmission.

Conclusion

In this study, a 3D FEM for the middle ear with nonlinear constitutive relations and the inner ear with viscoelastic constitutive relations is developed, enabling the representation of the actual biomaterial properties of ear tissue. The study showed that compared to previous linear elastic models, this realistic biomaterial model exhibits an error of 5% relative to experimental data, whereas the error in previous linear elastic models reaches as high as 28% relative to experimental data. Therefore, this realistic biomaterial model can more accurately reflects the biomechanical behavior during sound transmission and perception. This study provides a realistic and more accurate numerical computational model for investigating the mechanical behavior of the human ear structure, and also offers an effective application platform for the clinical treatment and hearing restoration of human hearing impairments.
