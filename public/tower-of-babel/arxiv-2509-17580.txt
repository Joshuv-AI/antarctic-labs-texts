Certifying localizable quantum properties with constant sample complexity
Zhenyu Du, Jinchang Liu, Elias X. Huber, Zi-Wen Liu, Xiongfeng Ma

Abstract
Characterizing increasingly complex quantum systems is a central task in quantum information science, yet experimental costs often scale prohibitively with system size. Certifying key properties—such as entanglement, circuit complexity, and quantum magic—using simple local measurements is highly desirable but challenging. In this work, we introduce a highly general certification framework based on a physical phenomenon that we call localizable quantumness: for generic many-body states, essential quantum properties are robustly preserved within the projected ensembles on small subsystems after performing local projective measurements on the rest of the system. Leveraging this insight, we develop certification protocols that certify global properties by witnessing them on a small, accessible subsystem. Our method dramatically reduces experimental cost by relying solely on local Pauli measurements, while achieving constant sample complexity, constant-level noise robustness, and soundness for mixed states—exponentially improving the sample complexity and overcoming major limitations of previous methods. We further present a random-basis variant to certify state fidelity, with numerical evidence strongly suggesting it maintains constant sample complexity and robustness for generic states, representing a substantial improvement over existing methods. Our results provide a practical, scalable toolkit for certifying large-scale quantum processors and offer a novel lens for understanding complex many-body quantum systems.

Taken together, our results establish localizable quantumness as a unifying principle for understanding many-body quantum systems and provide a practical, scalable toolkit for certifying quantum systems.

Overview of Methods and Results

This work presents a unified and practical framework for quantum certification based solely on local Pauli measurements.
In certification tasks, one typically has reliable prior knowledge of the quantum system. Specifically, a pure target state

Our certification framework relies on two key ingredients: (1) establishing that many quantum properties are localizable, meaning they concentrate into the small subsystem
A

The central concept of our approach is
localizable quantumness
. For a broad class of physically relevant many-body states, essential quantum properties of the global system remain robustly encoded in small subsystems even after local projective measurements on the complement. Concretely, if the global target state
\psi
possesses a localizable quantum property
\mathsf{P}
(see Table
1

\mathcal{E}_{\psi}
necessarily differs from
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
for any state
\rho

\mathsf{P}
, as shown in Fig.
1

\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
.

To harness localizable quantumness, we avoid the intractable task of directly evaluating projected-ensemble–averaged nonlinear quantities, such as localizable entanglement
Verstraete et al. 2004
;
Popp et al. 2005
. Instead, we compare the projected ensemble of the experimental state with that of the target. To this end, we introduce the notion of
conditional fidelity
, which evaluates the local fidelity between each projected state
\rho_{{\bm{z}}}
and its corresponding
\psi_{{\bm{z}}}
, conditioned on the measurement outcome
{\bm{z}}
on
B
.
A naive strategy—estimating each fidelity
\tr(\psi_{\bm z}\rho_{\bm z})
—is inefficient because we have only single-copy access to
\rho_{\bm{z}}
without post-selection. In contrast, we estimate the ensemble-averaged local fidelity
\sum_{{\bm{z}}}p_{\rho}({\bm{z}})\tr(\psi_{\bm z}\rho_{\bm z})
via randomized local Pauli measurements on the small subsystem
A

McGinley 2024
.
By estimating the conditional fidelity, we can certify whether the property
\mathsf{P}
is present or the state lies in the free set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{P}}
(states lacking
\mathsf{P}
), thereby turning the localizable quantumness into a robust witness of global quantum properties. In summary, we establish the following:

Complexity certification.

A highlight of our results is the first explicit construction of complexity witnesses.
We show that circuit complexity—defined as the minimal circuit depth required to prepare a state—can be certified to exceed a constant threshold with constant sample complexity and robustness.
The key idea is to certify reliable signatures of circuit complexity, such as high entanglement, on a carefully chosen small subsystem.
This approach also extends to certifying
measurement-assisted
circuit complexity, which can be exponentially smaller than standard state complexity and is therefore more challenging to certify

;
Lu et al. 2022

Our framework thus opens a new pathway for probing intricate structures in many-body quantum systems.
As two important examples, it enables efficient certification of long-range entanglement

Wen 2013

Entanglement certification.

Our method enables highly efficient certification of entanglement. For bipartite entanglement, we show that localizable entanglement on a small subsystem suffices to certify entanglement across exponentially many bipartitions with constant sample complexity.
More strikingly, we establish a highly efficient method for certifying
fully inseparable entanglement
—entanglement across all possible bipartitions
Eisert and Gross 2006
—using only logarithmic sample complexity. Fully inseparable entanglement is a crucial resource for quantum error correction
Rodriguez-Blanco et al. 2021
and for the activation of genuine multipartite entanglement
Palazuelos and Vicente 2022
, making our certification protocol especially valuable. We further demonstrate its efficiency on several Hamiltonian systems.

Corollary 2

(Certifying fully inseparable entanglement, informal)
.

If the target state exhibits localizable entanglement on certain small subsystems, then fully inseparable entanglement can be certified with
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
sample complexity while achieving constant noise robustness.

Magic certification.

We further apply our framework to quantum magic (or non-stabilizerness) certification, where the free set consists of convex mixtures of all stabilizer states. Since local Pauli measurements are free operations in the magic resource theory and preserve stabilizerness, the presence of localizable quantum magic after measuring a large complement in the computational basis directly certifies the magic of the target state.

Corollary 3

(Certifying quantum magic, informal)
.

If the target state exhibits localizable magic on a small subsystem, then quantum magic can be certified with
\mathcal{O}(1)
sample complexity while achieving constant noise robustness.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first protocol that certifies quantum magic using only single-copy local measurements, while achieving soundness for mixed states. Localizable magic is a key resource in quantum error correction, particularly in magic-state injection
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
. As such, our framework provides an efficient certification protocol for this magic concentration phenomenon, offering the potential for exponential improvements in sample complexity in practical experiments
Niroula et al. 2024
.

Fidelity certification.

Finally, we show that not only are quantum properties localizable in small subsystems, but global state information is also robustly encoded within them. Specifically, we propose a fidelity certification protocol that compares local fidelities on a small subsystem after measuring the large complement in local Pauli bases, and we prove that such local comparisons suffice to certify the state fidelity for a measure-one subset of all pure states. Numerical experiments provide compelling evidence that our method achieves constant sample complexity and constant noise robustness for generic states. This represents a substantial improvement over state-of-the-art fidelity-certification methods, whose costs grow with system size and whose noise tolerance diminishes
Huang et al. 2025
, as well as over adaptive local-measurement approaches that require challenging classical feedback and long-lived quantum memory to store states between measurement rounds
Gupta et al. 2025
;
Li and Zhu 2025
.

Theorem 2

(Certifying state fidelity, informal)
.

Conditional fidelity can be used to certify fidelity to typical pure target states. Numerical evidence suggests that our method achieves
\mathcal{O}(1)
sample complexity and constant noise robustness for generic target states.

III
Mathematical framework and protocol

Having outlined the key ideas and results of our work, we now present the certification framework and protocol in detail. We begin by formalizing the notion of localizable quantumness, which serves as the foundation of our approach. Building on this, we show how to construct property witnesses through conditional fidelity. Then, we present the complete protocol scheme, together with an analysis of its performance guarantees.

III.1
Localizable quantumness and conditional fidelity

We partition the
n
-qubit system into a small subsystem
A
(with
n_{A}
qubits) and its complement

n_{B}

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{{\bm{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{B}}}\sqrt{p_{\psi}(\bm{z})}\,\ket{\psi_{\bm z}}_{A}\otimes\ket{\bm z}_{B},

\rho_{{\bm{z}}}\coloneqq\frac{\tr_{B}[(I_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{\bmz}{\bmz}_{B})\rho]}{p_{\rho}({\bm{z}})}.

(2)

with probability
p_{\rho}({\bm{z}})\coloneqq\tr[(I_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{\bmz}{\bmz}_{B})\rho]

To quantify the strength of localizable quantumness for a property
\mathsf{P}
, we first specify its associated free set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{P}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{AB})

\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}\coloneqq\{\rho_{\bm{z}}:\rho\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{P}},\;p_{\rho}(\bm{z})\neq 0\}.

(3)

For the target state
\psi
, we then define the maximal fidelity at each outcome

by

\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi_{\bm{z}})\coloneqq\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}}\,\mathrm{tr}(\sigma\,\psi_{\bm{z}}).

(4)

Small values of

remains far from all property-free projected states on

\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi)\;=\;\sum_{\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{B}}}p_{\psi}(\bm{z})\,\bigl[1-\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi_{\bm{z}})\bigr].

(5)

This quantity plays a central role in our certification protocols, serving as the gap that separates the target state from all property-free states (Lemma
1
).
Unlike localizable entanglement

B

We also consider random-basis measurements on

\left\{\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{0}{0},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{1}{1},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{+}{+},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{-}{-},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{+i}{+i},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{-i}{-i}\right\}.

(6)

\bm{x}\in\{0,1,+,-,+i,-i\}^{n_{B}}

The random-basis metric of localizable quantumness is then

\widetilde{\mathrm{LQ}}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi)=\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathsf{X}^{n_{B}}}\tilde{p}_{\psi}(\bm{x})\,\bigl[1-\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi_{\bm{x}})\bigr].

\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi)
for the chosen partition and
\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi_{\bm{z}})
for the sampled projected states.
Rather than exhaustively enumerating the full projected ensemble

\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi)
by sampling only a small subset of projected states.
As discussed, it is not necessary to compute

This substitution reduces the gap from

to a smaller value

(Eq. (

)), and we may adjust the threshold to

accordingly.
In all applications considered here, subsystem

is of constant size, which makes the computation of
\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi_{\bm{z}})
or its upper bound efficient.
Throughout this section, we therefore treat the partition
A\cup B
together with the values of
\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi)
and
\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi_{\bm{z}})
as given, deferring their explicit construction to the discussion of specific applications.

In each experimental round

\{\ket{\bm z}\}_{\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{B}}}
, obtaining a bit string

.

In the post-processing step, we use the collected data to evaluate

.
The informationally complete POVM on subsystem

enables an unbiased estimator
\omega_{i}\coloneqq\tr\Bigl\{\psi_{{\bm{z}}_{i}}\Bigl[\bigotimes_{j\in A}(3\outerproduct{\bmx_{i,j}}{\bmx_{i,j}}-I_{j})\Bigr]\Bigr\}
of
\tr(\psi_{\bmz_i}\rho_{\bmz_i})
using local classical shadow

\{\omega_{i}-\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi_{{\bm{z}}_{i}})\}_{i=1}^{T}
.

Finally, we certify the property by comparing the estimator to
\eta^{\ast}=\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi)/3
. This decision threshold is placed between the maximal free-state value (
\leq 0
) and the ideal value
\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi)
, leaving margins of
\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi)/3
above

and
2\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi)/3
below

.
We have the following performance guarantee:

(Performance of Protocol
III.2
, formal version of Theorem
1
)
.

Given a property
\mathsf{P}
, a target state

A\cup B
. Protocol

1.

(Soundness): If the input state

An important benefit of our protocol is its robustness to constant-strength noise and its soundness for mixed input states. Specifically, our scheme accepts any input state

;

and magic

The decision boundary

While our protocol is experimentally efficient, its practical application also relies on the computational cost of classical processing. In our protocol, this cost is dominated by obtaining classical descriptions of the projected states
\psi_{\bm{z}}
. Although, in general, computing
\psi_{\bm{z}}
may require exponential time, for a broad class of physically relevant states, this computation is tractable.
Tensor-network representations enable efficient classical computation for a wide class of large quantum systems

;
Jordan et al. 2008
;
Vidal 2008
;

;

—the projected states are known exactly by construction. Hence, our certification scheme is both sample-efficient and computationally tractable for a broad range of states.

Crucially, our framework decouples the quantum experiment from the classical computation by using non-adaptive local measurements.
During the quantum stage, no prior knowledge of state preparation is required, so data collection is highly efficient.
Consequently, the same dataset can be analyzed offline for exponentially many different properties or even different target states

;
Huang et al. 2025

V

Given that quantum resources are currently far expensive than classical ones, this decoupling is a significant practical advantage.

IV
Certifying quantum circuit complexity

Having established our certification framework, we now illustrate its power and versatility by applying it to four central tasks in quantum information: certifying quantum circuit complexity, entanglement, magic, and state fidelity.

We begin with circuit complexity. It quantifies the minimal resources required to prepare a quantum state and plays a fundamental role connecting quantum computation
Aaronson 2016
;
Yi et al. 2024
, quantum learning
Huang et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Landau and Liu 2024
, many-body physics
Chen et al. 2010
;
Wen 2013
;
Brandão et al. 2021
;
Cotler et al. 2023
;
Du et al. 2025
;
Mark et al. 2024
, and black-hole physics
Brown and Susskind 2018
;
Susskind 2018
;
Oszmaniec et al. 2024
. Certifying that a prepared state has high complexity is therefore crucial both for probing novel structures of quantum matter and for benchmarking experimental platforms.

IV.1
Certifying unitary circuit complexity

We first consider the standard notion of circuit complexity for unitary circuit architectures. Formally, for a given circuit architecture specified by a graph
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
where vertices represent qubits and edges indicate the qubit pairs on which native two-qubit gates may act, we define the circuit complexity of a quantum state as the minimum circuit depth required to prepare it within
\mathsf{G}
. For a pure target state
\psi
, the circuit complexity is given by

C_{\mathsf{G}}(\psi):=\min\left\{d:\ket{\psi}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\bigotimes_{j}V_{i,j}\right)\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\right\}

(13)

where
V_{i,j}
is a two-qubit gate in the
i
-th layer with
\operatorname{supp}(V_{i,j})\in E
or a single-qubit gate, and the gates in the same layer act on disjoint qubits.
For a mixed state
\rho
, we adopt the convex-roof extension. Any
\rho
can be written as
\rho=\sum_{k}p_{k}\psi_{k}
with
p_{k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{k}p_{k}=1
.
One may prepare
\rho
by sampling
k
with probability
p_{k}
and then preparing
\psi_{k}
using a circuit of depth
C_{\mathsf{G}}(\psi_{k})
. The quantum circuit complexity of
\rho
can therefore be no larger than the largest depth needed among the
\psi_{k}
. Formally,

\begin{split}C_{\mathsf{G}}(\rho):=\min\Bigl\{d:\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\psi_{i},\text{ with }p_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i}p_{i}=1,\\
\text{ and }C_{\mathsf{G}}(\psi_{i})\leq d\text{ for all }i\Bigr\}.\end{split}

(14)

We apply our framework to certify that the complexity of a state exceeds a certain depth
d
on a
D
-dimensional lattice.
While learning-based protocols exist
Huang et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Landau and Liu 2024
, they are often restricted to pure states and require a sample complexity that grows with system size, limiting their usage in realistic noisy settings.
In contrast, our approach overcomes these limitations and exponentially improves sample complexity, achieving constant sample complexity for certifying constant-depth complexity (
d=\mathcal{O}(1)
) and remaining sound for mixed states.

Our approach is based on a key physical insight: in a low-depth circuit, information can propagate only a limited distance. This light-cone effect dictates that local measurements on the periphery of a system cannot substantially alter the entanglement structure deep within its center.
Consequently, low-complexity states yield projected states with limited entanglement.

Formally, we focus here on an
m\times m
two-dimensional case (
D=2
, with circuit complexity denoted
C_{2}
). Our method readily extends to higher dimensions.
Let
A
be the set of qubits in a
2w\times 2w
square and
B
its complement, as illustrated in Fig.
3
a, where the width
w
is chosen slightly larger than the threshold depth
d
.
The key observation is that, for a circuit of depth
d
, the entanglement between the left and right halves of region
A
(
L
and
R
in Fig.
3
a) is bounded.
This is because only gates near the boundary between
L
and
R
can generate entanglement across this cut, and a shallow circuit contains few such gates.
We formalize this as the following lemma. Detailed proofs are given in Appendix
B
.

Figure 3:
Projected ensembles for certifying circuit complexity
d
.
(a) Partitioning of a two-dimensional lattice: the full

and

.
(b) Entanglement in the projected ensemble certifies complexity. The free projected-state set

e
. If a state

has small unitary circuit complexity

, all of its projected states lie in
\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}
. If a state
\rho_{2}
has small measurement-assisted complexity

, a large fraction of its projected states lie in
\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}
. Our protocol efficiently certifies the complexity of a high-complexity target

whose projected states are far from

Lemma 2

(Entanglement in states of low unitary circuit complexity)
.

Let
\ket{\phi}
be an
(m\times m)
-qubit pure state with circuit depth

. Consider the partition
A\cup B
with

, as depicted in Fig.

. Then, for every measurement outcome
{\bm{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{B}}
,

E_{L\mid R}(\phi_{\bm{z}})\leq 8wd,

(15)

where
E_{L\mid R}(\xi_{LR})\coloneqq S(\xi_{L})=-\tr(\xi_L \log\xi_L)
is the entanglement entropy of the state
\xi_{LR}
across the bipartition
L\mid R
.

In essence, Lemma
2
characterizes the projected free set
\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}
for the property
\mathsf{P}
of “circuit complexity
C_{2}>d
”: it contains only projected states with low entanglement across
L\mid R
. Conversely, if the projected states of the target are highly entangled, they must lie far from
\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}
. Thus, the high entanglement entropy of the projected ensemble provides a clear signature of circuit complexity, as illustrated in Fig.

Theorem 4

(Certifying 2D quantum circuit complexity)
.

Let
\psi
be the target state and consider the bipartition

shown in Fig.
3
, with

for some

. Suppose projected ensemble of

on

for a constant

Thus, for constant circuit complexity

, our protocol achieves constant sample complexity and noise robustness.
This constant-depth regime is particularly important: in finite spatial dimensions, depths

are associated with long-range entanglement in many-body systems

;
Chen et al. 2010
;
Wen 2013
.
Consequently, our protocol provides a sample-efficient method for certifying long-range entanglement by certifying depths just beyond the constant regime using
\omega(1)

Moreover, the protocol requires only polynomially many samples up to depths

(or more generally

).

IV.2
Certifying measurement-assisted circuit complexity

We now turn to a stronger notion—measurement-assisted circuit complexity
Tantivasadakarn et al. 2023
;
Du et al. 2025
.
Measurement-assisted circuits permit mid-circuit single-qubit measurements with classical feedforward, and have attracted significant recent interest because they can provide exponential depth shortcuts for preparing important many-body states, including topologically ordered phases
Briegel and Raussendorf 2001
;
Piroli et al. 2021
;
Lu et al. 2022
;
Bravyi et al. 2022
;
Tantivasadakarn et al. 2024
.
The recently introduced spacetime quantum circuit complexity also unifies notions of circuit width, depth, and measurements, and reveals a novel complexity concentration phenomenon in measurement-assisted circuits
Du et al. 2025
.

Here, we focus on the definition of measurement-assisted circuit complexity as the minimum number of measurement layers required to prepare a target state
deterministically
. This definition underpins the hierarchy of topological order and the notion of nontrivial measurement-equivalent quantum phases
Tantivasadakarn et al. 2023
.
Formally, fix an architecture
\mathsf{G}=(V,E)
on
n=|V|
qubits, a depth-
d
measurement-assisted circuit consists of
d
rounds. In round
i\in[d]
, conditioned on the previous measurement record
m_{<i}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{i-1})
, one applies parallel two-qubit gates
\{V_{i,\ell}^{m_{<i}}\}_{\ell}
on pairwise disjoint edges of
E
(and arbitrary single-qubit gates if desired), followed by single-qubit projective measurements
\{P_{i,q}^{m_{<i}}(b)\}_{b\in\{0,1\}}
on a subset of qubits
q
, yielding outcomes
m_{i,q}\in\{0,1\}
. Gates and measurements within the same round act on disjoint qubits, and future operations may depend on the cumulative measurement record
m_{<i}
. Let
m=(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})
denote the full outcome record. The unnormalized post-measurement state is

\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_m}=\Bigl[\prod_{i=1}^{d}\Bigl(\prod_{q}P_{i,q}^{\,m_{<i}}(m_{i,q})\Bigr)\Bigl(\prod_{\ell}V_{i,\ell}^{\,m_{<i}}\Bigr)\Bigr]\ket{0}^{\otimes n},

(17)

with branch probability
p_{m}=\|\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_m}\|_{2}^{2}
and normalized state
\ket{\phi_m}=\ket{\widetilde{\phi}_m}/\sqrt{p_{m}}
. The measurement-assisted circuit complexity of a pure state
\psi
is

\begin{split}C_{\mathsf{G}}^{anc}(\psi)\coloneqq\min\Bigl\{d:\exists\ \text{depth-}d\ \text{measurement-assisted circuit}\\
\text{s.t.}\ \forall m\ \text{with}\ p_{m}>0,\ \ket{\phi_m}=\ket{\psi}\Bigr\}.\end{split}

(18)

For mixed states, we use the convex-roof extension same as in Eq. (
14
).

Low-depth measurement-assisted circuits are also expected to generate limited entanglement across appropriately chosen cuts. Nonetheless, incorporating the effects of measurements is subtle. For example, depending on the partition, measurements can induce significant entanglement in post-measurement states even when the underlying unitary depth is shallow
McGinley et al. 2025
. We address this by choosing the specific bipartition in Fig.
3
and leveraging the fact that entanglement entropy is a monotone that is non-increasing on average under local measurements
Vidal 2000
. The next lemma quantifies the average entanglement remaining on
A
after measuring its complement
B
.

Lemma 3

(Entanglement in states of measurement-assisted circuit complexity)
.

Let
\ket{\phi}
be an
(m\times m)
-qubit pure state with measurement-assisted circuit complexity
C_{2}^{anc}(\phi)\leq d
. Consider the partition
A\cup B
with
A=L\cup R
as depicted in Fig.
3
. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}\sim p_{\phi}({\bm{z}})}[E_{L\mid R}(\phi_{\bm{z}})]\leq 12wd.

(19)

Therefore, although certain projected states
\phi_{\bm{z}}
may exhibit high entanglement, the average entanglement remains small. As a result, most projected states lie in a low-entanglement set (Fig.
3
b). Nonetheless, this crucial distinction makes the conditional fidelity witness in Lemma
1
less effective. To address this, we adopt a linear witness of the form

\tilde{O}\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{z}}\bigl[t+(1-t)\Theta_{t}\bigl(\mathrm{Fid}(\psi_{\bm{z}})\bigr)\bigr]\,\outerproduct{\psi_{\bm z}}{\psi_{\bm z}}_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{\bm z}{\bm z}_{B},

(20)

where
t\in(0,1)
and
\Theta_{t}(x)=\mathbf{1}[x\leq t]
is a threshold function.
The precise construction of
\tilde{O}
, along with the set defining the maximal fidelity
\mathrm{Fid}(\psi_{\bm{z}})
, is provided in Appendix
B
, together with the full proofs.
Using this construction, we establish the following:

Theorem 5

(Certifying 2D measurement-assisted circuit complexity)
.

Let
\psi
be the target state and consider the bipartition
A\cup B
shown in Fig.
3
, with
w=\eta d
for some
\eta>6
. Suppose the projected ensemble of
\psi
on
A
satisfies

\Pr_{{\bm{z}}\sim p_{\psi}({\bm{z}})}\left[E_{L\mid R}(\psi_{{\bm{z}}})\geq c\absolutevalue{L}+1\right]\geq p=\Omega(1),

(21)

for a constant
c=(6+\Omega(1))p^{-1}\eta^{-1}
. Then, estimating

above a threshold
\eta^{*}
certifies that the measurement-assisted circuit complexity of
\psi
exceeds
d
with constant failure probability
\delta
using
\exp\bigl(\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}d^{2})\bigr)

copies of
\psi
and tolerating noise up to
\varepsilon=\Omega(1)
.

In the regime
d=\mathcal{O}(1)
, our protocol again uses only
\mathcal{O}(1)
sample complexity. This enables, in particular, the certification of nontrivial measurement-equivalent quantum phases (those with
C_{2}^{\mathrm{anc}}=\omega(1)
)
Tantivasadakarn et al. 2023
using only
\omega(1)

IV.3
Performance on generic quantum states

We now show that our approach applies to generic quantum states and a wide range of physically relevant states. The detailed proofs are given in Appendix
C
.

An important class of states is those drawn Haar-randomly, which could demonstrate the average-case performance of our protocol over the full Hilbert space. For Haar-random states, the projected states on subsystem
A
are highly entangled.
This can be established via the concept of deep thermalization
Cotler et al. 2023
;
Choi et al. 2023
: higher moments of the measurement-induced projected ensemble within a subsystem are close to their thermal values (i.e., to those of the Haar ensemble), so the ensemble forms an (approximate) state design.
The high entanglement inherent in second-order state designs
Liu et al. 2018
suffices to satisfy the requirement in Theorems
4
and
5
, enabling sample-efficient certification of complexity for these deep-thermalized states.

Proposition 1

(Certifying circuit complexity of deep-thermalized states)
.

Let
\psi
be an
n
-qubit state whose projected ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\psi}
on a specific
\Theta(d^{2})
-qubit system
A
(see Fig.
3
) forms an
\epsilon
-approximate 2-design with
\epsilon=\mathcal{O}(2^{-n_{A}/2})
. Then, our protocols can certify that
\psi
has complexity
C_{2}
(or
C_{2}^{anc}
) larger than
d
with a sample complexity of
\exp(\cO(d^2))
, while achieving constant noise robustness.

Using deep thermalization results for Haar-random states
Cotler et al. 2023
, we establish the average-case performance of our protocol for Haar-random states.
Moreover, deep thermalization has been observed in many chaotic physical quantum systems
Ippoliti and Ho 2023
;
Chang et al. 2024
;
Mark et al. 2024
, so our protocol also applies to these physically relevant states.

To further assess practical performance, we consider more experimentally relevant ensembles: states generated by brickwork quantum circuits, where each layer consists of two-qubit gates arranged in a staggered pattern. Such architectures serve as canonical ensembles for studying the generic behavior of physical quantum states
Brandão et al. 2016
;
Nahum et al. 2017
;
Haferkamp et al. 2022
;
McGinley et al. 2025
.
We prove in Appendix

that for finite

-dimensional circuits, as long as the circuit depth

is slightly larger than the complexity

we aim to certify—specifically
d^{\prime}\geq kd
for some global constant

—these depth-

circuits yield highly entangled projected states, which are therefore far from
\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}
. This provides a sample-efficient method for certifying the complexity of shallow-brickwork-circuit states. We summarize the result below, which generalizes to any finite
D
-dimensional circuit.

Proposition 2

(Certifying circuit complexity of random brickwork-circuit states)
.

There exists a constant
k
such that, for a state
\psi
drawn from random

brickwork circuits with depth

, with probability at least 0.99, our protocols can certify that
\psi
has complexity
C_{2}
(or
C_{2}^{anc}
) larger than
d
with a sample complexity of
\exp(\cO(d^2))
, while achieving constant noise robustness.

Taken together, these two propositions establish the sample efficiency of our protocol for both generic quantum states and experimentally realizable states on near-term quantum platforms.

V
Certifying quantum entanglement

Entanglement is a fundamental feature of quantum mechanics, and certifying it is a central while challenging task in quantum physics
Ekert 1991
;
Mayers and Yao 2004
;
Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
.
Here, we demonstrate how our certification framework provides a highly efficient solution for certifying both bipartite and fully inseparable multipartite entanglement. We begin by showing that entanglement across exponentially many bipartitions can be certified with constant sample complexity. We then tackle the more demanding task of certifying fully inseparable entanglement. Remarkably, we demonstrate an exponential improvement over prior methods, reducing the required sample complexity from linear to logarithmic in the system size for generic quantum states.

V.1
Bipartite entanglement

We first consider certifying entanglement across a bipartition
A\mid B
. In general, certifying bipartite entanglement is a daunting task
Gurvits 2003
;
Liu et al. 2022c
. Entanglement witnesses provide a powerful route: every entangled state admits a witness, which is very useful in experiments
Gühne and Tóth 2009
. Nevertheless, designing witnesses using simple local measurements is highly nontrivial
Dimić and Dakić 2018
;
Rodriguez-Blanco et al. 2021
.

Our framework circumvents this obstacle by reducing global certification to a local entanglement witnessing task on a small subsystem. Let
A_{1}\subset A
and
B_{1}\subset B
be two small, disjoint subsystems. The key insight is that, if a state
\rho
is separable across
A\mid B
, then any state on
A_{1}B_{1}
obtained by performing local measurements on the complement
R:=[n]\backslash(A_{1}\cup B_{1})
must also be separable. (Note the difference in notation adopted in this section: The system
R
corresponds to
B
in protocol
III.2
and
A_{1}B_{1}
corresponds to
A
.) Therefore, for the property
\mathsf{P}
of “entanglement across
A\mid B
,” the free projected-state set
\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}
on
A_{1}B_{1}
is precisely the set of separable states on
A_{1}B_{1}
.
We define the localizable entanglement between
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
as

\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}(A_{1},B_{1})\;\coloneqq\;\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{Ent}(A,B)}(\psi),

(22)

making explicit its dependence on the chosen subsystems. By Theorem
3
, if the target state
\ket{\psi}
robustly preserves this resource—namely, if
\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}(A_{1},B_{1})=\Omega(1)
—then our protocol for certifying the bipartite entanglement is efficient.

Proposition 3

(Certifying bipartite entanglement)
.

Consider two disjoint subsystems
A_{1},B_{1}\subseteq[n]
of constant size,
n_{A_{1}},n_{B_{1}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. If the target state
\psi
satisfies

\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}(A_{1},B_{1})=\Omega(1),

(23)

then Protocol
III.2
certifies that
\psi
is entangled across any bipartition
A\mid B
(with
A_{1}\subseteq A,B_{1}\subseteq B
) with
\mathcal{O}(\ln\delta^{-1})
sample complexity, while achieving constant noise robustness.

Notably, if the protocol outputs
\mathsf{accept}
, then we simultaneously certify entanglement of
\rho
across all bipartitions that separate
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
. Indeed, if
\rho
were separable across some bipartition
A\mid B
with
A_{1}\subseteq A
and
B_{1}\subseteq B
, then local measurements on
R
cannot create entanglement between
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
. Consequently, every projected state
\rho_{\bm{z}}
on
A_{1}\cup B_{1}
would be separable, and the certification would output
\mathsf{reject}
.

We remark that prior definitions of localizable entanglement
Verstraete et al. 2004
;
Popp et al. 2005
;
Rodriguez-Blanco et al. 2021
optimize over local measurement bases, whereas here we do not. Avoiding this optimization eliminates substantial classical optimizations and removes stringent device requirements such as adaptive local measurements, yielding a simple, nonadaptive protocol. The price to pay is that our fixed-basis approach may fail to certify certain families of states for which basis optimization is required—for example, the
n
-qubit GHZ state
(\ket{0^{n}}+\ket{1^{n}})/\sqrt{2}
.
Nevertheless, as discussed in Sec.
IV.3
, for generic classes such as deep-thermalized states and random brickwork-circuit states, the fixed-basis localizable entanglement remains constant, i.e.,
\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}(A_{1},B_{1})=\Omega(1)
.
Hence, our method provides a powerful and practical certification tool across a broad range of states.
We further validate its efficiency with numerical studies of ground states of local Hamiltonians in Sec.
V.3
.
Beyond its experimental simplicity, we now show that our framework also enables exponential reductions in sample complexity for certifying more complex entanglement structures.

V.2
Fully inseparable entanglement

We now address the more stringent task of certifying fully inseparable entanglement, where a state is entangled across every possible bipartition
Eisert and Gross 2006
.
This structure is a key resource for quantum networks: it is the necessary and sufficient form of entanglement for activating genuine multipartite entanglement
Palazuelos and Vicente 2022
;
Zwerger et al. 2019
. It also provides a meaningful benchmark for noisy devices aiming to implement quantum error correction
Rodriguez-Blanco et al. 2021
. Consequently, certifying fully inseparable entanglement offers a stringent test of entangling power and a stepping stone toward quantum networks and fault-tolerant quantum computing.

At first glance, certifying fully inseparable entanglement appears challenging, as one must rule out separability across exponentially many bipartitions.
Nonetheless, Proposition
3
provides a path forward.
It suffices to certify localizable entanglement between qubits
i
and
i+1
for each
i\in[n-1]
.
Indeed, if
\rho
were separable across some bipartition
A\mid B
, then there exists
i\in A
satisfying
i+1\in B
, therefore the test on the pair
\{i,i+1\}
would fail.
A straightforward implementation that runs our protocol separately for every
i
would require
n-1
distinct experiments, yielding total sample complexity at least linear in
n
, similar to existing methods
Rodriguez-Blanco et al. 2021
.

Here, we exponentially improve this scaling by decoupling the measurement scheme from the choice of pairs and reusing a single dataset across all tests, with the spirit underlying classical shadows
Huang et al. 2020
.
Concretely, rather than measuring the complement of a specific pair in the computational basis, we perform single-qubit random Pauli measurements on all qubits. Data collection is thus independent of which pair will be tested in postprocessing. To leverage this dataset, we use the random-basis analogue of (
22
):

\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(A_{1},B_{1})\;\coloneqq\;\widetilde{\mathrm{LQ}}_{\mathsf{Ent}(A_{1}\mid B_{1})}(\psi).

(24)

Protocol
III.2
extends directly to this random-basis setting: Measure the complement
R
of
A_{1}B_{1}
in a random Pauli basis instead of the
Z
basis, then estimate
\tr(\psi_{\bm x}\rho_{\bm x})
and
\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi_{\bm{x}})
on
A_{1}\cup B_{1}
.
Replacing the condition in Eq. (
23
) of Proposition
3
by
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(A_{1},B_{1})=\Omega(1)
yields the same performance guarantees by using the random-basis variant of Protocol
III.2
.

Now, we show our modified protocol reduces the sample complexity from linear to logarithmic in
n
(proofs in Appendix
D
). Concretely, we collect a single dataset from
T
rounds of random Pauli measurements on
\rho
and reuse the same dataset to certify localizable entanglement for the
(n-1)
pairs
A_{1}=\{i\},B_{1}=\{i+1\}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. If all tests output
\mathsf{accept}
, we certify fully inseparable entanglement.
This protocol also extends to any set of
n-1
pairs that connects the
n
-vertex graph.

Proposition 4

(Certifying fully inseparable entanglement with logarithmic sample complexity)
.

Let
\psi
be a quantum state satisfying

\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(i,i+1)=\Omega(1)

(25)

for all
2\leq i\leq n
.
Then the fully inseparable entanglement of
\psi
can be certified with failure probability
\delta
using
T=\mathcal{O}(\log n+\log\delta^{-1})
samples, while achieving constant noise robustness.

For generic states, such as Haar-random states, the condition
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(i,i+1)=\Omega(1)
holds simultaneously for all
i
with high probability (Appendix
D
).
Consequently, our protocol provides an exponentially more sample-efficient method for certifying this strong form of multipartite entanglement in generic quantum states.

V.3
Case study: ground states of Hamiltonian systems

Building on the general result that the random-basis localizable entanglement
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}
can remain constant for generic many-body states, we now investigate this metric for ground states of concrete Hamiltonian models, thereby demonstrating the practical efficiency of our protocol.

We begin with the spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
XXZ chain, a paradigmatic and exactly solvable model of quantum magnetism with a rich phase diagram
Takahashi 1999

Calabrese and Cardy 2004
;
Gu et al. 2005
;
Popp et al. 2005

Brydges et al. 2019
and optical cavities
Luo et al. 2025

H_{\mathrm{XXZ}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[\sigma_{x}^{i}\sigma_{x}^{i+1}+\sigma_{y}^{i}\sigma_{y}^{i+1}+\Delta\,\sigma_{z}^{i}\sigma_{z}^{i+1}\Bigr],

(26)

where

is the anisotropy, with periodic boundary condition
\sigma_{\alpha}^{n+1}\equiv\sigma_{\alpha}^{1}
.
For a translational-invariant non-degenerate Hamiltonian, its unique ground state is also translational-invariant, so
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(i,i+1)
is identical for all
i
.
We numerically evaluate
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(1,2)
of the ground state
\psi
for system sizes up to

using the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) algorithm
White 1992
.
Details of the numeric experiments are provided in Appendix
D
.

The results are shown in Fig.
4

We observe that
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(1,2)
remains nonvanishing throughout the critical regime
|\Delta|<1
, with only mild finite-size drift as
n
increases.
Notably,
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(1,2)
reaches maximal at the isotropic point

, at which the XXZ chain undergoes a Kosterlitz-Thouless transition from gapped Néel antiferromagnet (
\Delta<-1
) to critical Luttinger liquid (
\absolutevalue{\Delta}<1
)
Takahashi 1999
.
Moreover, it vanishes at another phase transition point
\Delta=1

\Delta>1
.
Therefore, the behavior of
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(1,2)
is able to indicate various phase transitions.

We next consider the spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}

Chowdhury et al. 2024

Concretely, with periodic boundary conditions, the Hamiltonian is

and
\sigma_{\alpha}^{n+k}\equiv\sigma_{\alpha}^{k}

Here, we set

\psi

At the Majumdar–Ghosh point

VI
Certifying quantum magic

Here, we further demonstrate the power of our framework by applying it to certify quantum magic (or non-stabilizerness), a critical resource for fault-tolerant quantum computing
Bravyi et al. 2016
;
Bravyi et al. 2019
;
Seddon et al. 2021
.
Its characterization has recently received significant attention
Leone et al. 2022
;
Haug and Kim 2023
;
Haug et al. 2024
;
Gross et al. 2021
;
Hinsche and Helsen 2025
;
Warmuz et al. 2025
.
Nonetheless, robust certification of this critical resource remains challenging.
Existing protocols often lack soundness for mixed-state inputs or require demanding measurements
Haug and Kim 2023
;
Gross et al. 2021
;
Hinsche and Helsen 2025
.
We overcome these limitations and present the first protocol that certifies quantum magic using very few local measurements and achieves soundness for mixed states. Furthermore, it only uses constant sample complexity and achieves constant noise robustness.

A pure
n
-qubit stabilizer state can be written as

\ket{\psi}=C\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
,
where
C
is a Clifford circuit acting on the initial all-zero state
Gottesman 1997
.
The set of free states—namely, the stabilizer set—is defined as the convex hull of stabilizer states:

\begin{split}\mathrm{STAB}_{n}\coloneq\{\rho:\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i},\text{ with }p_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i}p_{i}=1,\\
\text{ and }\ket{\psi_i}=C_{i}\ket{0}^{\otimes n},C_{i}\text{ Clifford}\}.\end{split}

(28)

A key feature of the stabilizer set is its closure under Pauli measurements
Gottesman 1997
.
Consequently, the free-projected set
\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}
is simply the set of stabilizer states on
A
, denoted
\mathrm{STAB}_{A}
.
The maximal fidelity
\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\phi)
then becomes the well-known stabilizer fidelity
Bravyi et al. 2019
;
Liu and Winter 2022
:

\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\psi_{\bm{z}})=\max_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathrm{STAB}_{A}}\bra{\phi}\psi_{\bm{z}}\ket{\phi}.

(29)

Let
\mathrm{LM}(\psi)\coloneqq\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{Magic}}(\psi)
denote the localizable magic of the target. By Theorem
3
, if
n_{A}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\mathrm{LM}(\psi)=\Omega(1)
, then our protocol has constant sample complexity and noise robustness. This condition is expected for generic states: their projected ensemble on
A
is typically random states
Cotler et al. 2023
, which possess abundant quantum magic and require many layers of non-Clifford gates to prepare
Turkeshi et al. 2025
;
Leone et al. 2025
.
Hence, our certification protocol is both efficient and robust for generic states.

However, in many practical scenarios, magic is a scarce resource, and the interesting case is when magic is injected into a Clifford circuit in only a single layer
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
;
Niroula et al. 2024
.
To illustrate the performance of our protocol in such a physically motivated scenario, we study a magic-injection-and-scrambling model (Fig.
5
a).
Starting from a product of single-qubit stabilizer states
\ket{+}^{\otimes n}
, we apply a layer of single-qubit rotations
R_{Z}(\alpha)=\exp(-i\alpha Z / 2)
to each qubit, where
\alpha\in[0,\pi/4]
controls the amount of injected magic (with
\alpha=\pi/4
yielding maximal magic).
We then apply a uniformly random
n
-qubit Clifford circuit
C
to scramble the injected magic across the system.
The resulting state is

\ket{\psi}=C\left[R_{Z}(\alpha)\ket{+}\right]^{\otimes n}.

(30)

After performing single-qubit computational-basis measurements on the
n_{B}
-qubit subsystem
B
, the resulting projected states
\psi_{\bm{z}}
are defined on the
A
subsystem, which contains
n_{A}=n-n_{B}
qubits.

It is also interesting that
\mathrm{LM}

\rho=(1-p)\psi+p\,I/d

VII
Certifying quantum state fidelity using random-basis-enhanced conditional fidelity

While certifying quantum properties is valuable, the strongest certification is certifying the state fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
. Recent protocols achieve this using only local measurements
Huang et al. 2020
;
Gupta et al. 2025
;
Li and Zhu 2025
, but they face trade-offs between experimental simplicity and certification power. Adaptive protocols can, in principle, certify arbitrary states, yet they require challenging classical feedback and long-lived quantum memory to store the state between measurement rounds
Gupta et al. 2025
;
Li and Zhu 2025
. By contrast, non-adaptive protocols that employ a state-independent measurement scheme are far more compatible with near-term hardware. However, state-of-the-art non-adaptive methods for generic states, such as the shadow-overlap protocol
Huang et al. 2025
, exhibit polynomially growing sample complexity and diminishing noise tolerance, limiting practicality.
This motivates our central question: can a non-adaptive, local, state-independent protocol certify generic quantum states with constant sample complexity and constant noise robustness?

We find that this question can be addressed by enhancing our conditional fidelity framework. Measuring subsystem
B
in a single, fixed basis (e.g., the computational basis) is insufficient: the product state
|\psi_{0^{n_{B}}}\rangle_{A}\otimes|0^{n_{B}}\rangle_{B}
achieves unit conditional fidelity even though its global fidelity with
|\psi\rangle
could be exponentially small in
n_{B}
.
The core issue is that a fixed measurement basis on
B
fails to capture the rich correlation across the
A\mid B
partition, which is revealed only in other bases (e.g., the relative phases among projected states).
Our solution is to measure subsystem
B
in randomly chosen single-qubit Pauli bases. The intuition is that random basis choices produce projected states on subsystem
A
that are closely correlated with one another, so the conditional fidelity captures the global fidelity structure and becomes a sensitive witness of the overall state fidelity. To formalize this idea, we define the following observable:

O_{\psi}=3^{-n_{B}}\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathsf{X}^{n_{B}}}\outerproduct{\psi_{\bmx}}{\psi_{\bmx}}_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{\bmx}{\bmx}_{B}.

(33)

By construction,
\tr(O_{\psi}\psi)=1
.
The ability to certify fidelity is determined by the spectral gap

\Delta(O_{\psi})\coloneqq 1-\max_{\phi\perp\psi}\tr(O_{\psi}\phi).

(34)

A strictly positive gap,
\Delta(O_{\psi})>0
, guarantees that any state orthogonal to
\psi
has an expectation value less than 1, making certification possible. The gap can vanish for certain target states (e.g., product states across
A\mid B
), but we establish that it is almost-surely non-zero for Haar-random
\ket{\psi}
, even for the minimal choice of a single-qubit subsystem
A
. Proofs are given in Appendix
E
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\ket{\psi}
be drawn Haar-randomly from the
n
-qubit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
with
d=2^{n}
and
n_{A}\geq 1
. Then, with probability
1
,
\Delta(O_{\psi})>0
.

While this theorem confirms the validity of our approach, the practical performance depends on the magnitude of this gap, as shown in the following:

Proposition 5

(Certifying state fidelity with local Pauli measurements)
.

Let
\psi
be the target state with
\Delta(O_{\psi})>0
, and fix parameters
0<c<1/2
,
0<F<1

(35)

1.

\mathsf{reject}
with probability at least

;

Output
\mathsf{accept}
with probability at least
1-\delta

\tr(\psi\rho)\geq 1-(1-2c)\Delta(1-F)
.

Therefore, a larger spectral gap
\Delta
directly improves both sample complexity and noise tolerance.
To show the practical performance of our protocol, we performed numerical simulations on physically relevant states.
We sampled
N_{s}=50

\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{s}}
generated by local quantum circuits of depth

.

VIII
Discussion and outlook

We introduced the notion of localizable quantumness and a conditional fidelity certification framework, based on which we devise a powerful general approach for certifying several central quantum properties.
We established rigorous guarantees for generic many-body target states as well as canonical brickwork-circuit states.
Practically, our protocol uses simple state-independent, non-adaptive local measurements, achieves mixed-input soundness, and significantly improves both noise tolerance and sample complexity.
A current limitation is that our complexity witnesses primarily target relatively shallow circuits. Nonetheless, the circuit complexity can grow exponentially, and measurements can concentrate exponentially large circuit complexity into small subsystems
Du et al. 2025
. This raises an interesting question: can the complexity concentration phenomenon be harnessed to design sample-efficient witnesses for higher circuit complexity?
For state-fidelity certification, our numerics support constant-sample-complexity scaling for generic states using only local Pauli measurements, revealing a novel feature of many-body states.
An important open question is to prove the constant lower bounds on
\Delta(O_{\psi})
with
n_{A}=\mathcal{O}(1)
for generic quantum states.

Localizable quantumness offers a unifying lens on several previously established effects. For example, complexity concentration
Du et al. 2025
, localizable entanglement
Verstraete et al. 2004
;
Popp et al. 2005
, magic concentration
Zhang and Gu 2024
;
Niroula et al. 2024
, and magic-state injection
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
can all be viewed as special cases in which a global resource localizes into a small subsystem after local projective measurements on the complement.
This highlights the broader significance of projected-ensemble properties such as
\mathrm{LQ}_{\mathsf{P}}
and their potential applications and impact on resource theories and many-body physics.
In Hamiltonian systems, it is especially compelling to identify which key parameters are localizable, i.e., can be signaled by projected-ensemble properties.
Another exciting direction is to develop a theory of localizable magic, paralleling localizable entanglement
Verstraete et al. 2004
, as a tool to probe quantum many-body magic.

Extending localizable quantumness to mixed states and developing protocols that certify mixed-state properties—such as mixed-state entanglement and magic—represent especially promising directions.
Recent work also shows that, with tolerable additional overhead, one can obtain good bounds on nonlinear quantities of the projected ensemble, enabling powerful certifications such as emergent quantum state designs
McGinley 2024
. It is therefore compelling to investigate whether nonlinear projected-ensemble quantities can certify stronger quantum properties or yield sharper, more powerful property-detection criteria.

More broadly, our approach complements and extends existing paradigms for certification and estimation—such as direct fidelity estimation
Flammia and Liu 2011
and randomized measurements
Huang et al. 2020
;
Elben et al. 2022
—by showing that simple local measurements can certify global properties with favorable scaling.
A particularly promising avenue for future work is to tailor our method to other physical platforms, leveraging native, low-overhead measurements to certify key properties—for example, nonclassicality in bosonic systems
Mari et al. 2011
and non-Gaussianity in fermionic systems
Hebenstreit et al. 2019
;
Gluza et al. 2018
.

Appendix A
Additional analysis on the performance of Protocol
III.2

In this appendix, we rigorously analyze the performance of Protocol
III.2
. We first collect the statistical tools used throughout our work, including local shadow tomography, standard concentration inequalities, and the median-of-means estimator. We then establish the performance guarantee based on those tools.

A.1
Local classical shadow

We briefly review the local classical shadow protocol
Huang et al. 2020
.
For a given
k
-qubit input state
\rho
, the protocol performs random single-qubit Pauli measurements in the
X
,
Y
, or
Z
basis independently on each qubit.
This is equivalent to applying a POVM composed of six elements:

\left\{\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{+}{+},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{-}{-},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{+i}{+i},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{-i}{-i},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{0}{0},\,\frac{1}{3}\outerproduct{1}{1}\right\}.

(A1)

Suppose the POVM yields measurement outcomes
{\bm{x}}\in\mathsf{X}^{k}
.
The classical shadow estimator for
\tr(O\rho)
is given by

\omega=\tr\Bigl\{O\Bigl[\bigotimes_{i\in[k]}(3\outerproduct{\bmx_i}{\bmx_i}-I_{i})\Bigr]\Bigr\}.

(A2)

This estimator is unbiased:

\mathbb{E}[\omega]=\tr(O\rho),

(A3)

and its variance is bounded by

\mathrm{Var}[\omega]\leq 4^{k}\norm{O}_{\infty}^{2}

(A4)

Crucially,
\omega
uses only a single copy of
\rho
, which is essential for estimating ensemble-averaged local fidelities in our protocol.

Lemma S1
.

Let
\bm{z}\sim p
and
\omega
be a random variable such that
\mathbb{E}[\omega|\bm{z}]=o_{\bm{z}}\geq 0
and
\operatorname{Var}[\omega|\bm{z}]\leq\sigma^{2}
for all
\bm{z}
.
Then,
\mathbb{E}[\omega]=\sum_{\bm{z}}p({\bm{z}})o_{\bm{z}}
and
\mathrm{Var}[\omega]\leq\sigma^{2}+\max_{{\bm{z}}}\absolutevalue{o_{\bmz}}^{2}
.

Proof.

The proof is from the laws of total expectation and variance,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\omega&=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}}\mathbb{E}[\omega|{\bm{z}}]\\
&=\sum_{{\bm{z}}}p({\bm{z}})o_{\bm{z}},\\
\mathrm{Var}[\omega]&=\mathbb{E}_{z}\mathrm{Var}[\omega|{\bm{z}}]+\mathrm{Var}_{{\bm{z}}}\mathbb{E}[\omega|{\bm{z}}]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}}\sigma^{2}+\mathrm{Var}_{{\bm{z}}}o_{{\bm{z}}}\\
&\leq\sigma^{2}+\max_{{\bm{z}}}\absolutevalue{o_{\bmz}}^{2}.\end{split}

(A5)

∎

This enables unbiased estimation of projected-ensemble–averaged linear quantities
McGinley 2024
.
Combining the lemma with the variance bound in (
A4
) yields the following guarantee for estimating conditional fidelities.

Corollary S1

(Variance of conditional fidelity)
.

Let an
n
-qubit state
\rho
be partitioned as
A\cup B
, and measure
B
in the computational basis to obtain
\bm{z}
with probability
p_{\rho}(\bm{z})
and post-measurement state
\rho_{\bm{z}}
on
A
. Construct
\omega
by applying local classical shadows on
\rho_{{\bm{z}}}
to estimate
\tr(\psi_{\bm z}\rho_{\bm z})
. Then,
\mathbb{E}[\omega]=\sum_{{\bm{z}}}p_{\rho}({\bm{z}})\tr(\psi_{\bmz}\rho_{\bmz})
and
\mathrm{Var}[\omega]\leq 4^{n_{A}}+1
.

A.2
Median-of-means estimator

The median-of-means estimator is a standard statistical method that uses medians to suppress the contribution of outlier estimators, which has the benefit of exponentially suppressing the failure probability.
We first introduce Hoeffding’s inequality, which is useful for bounded-value estimators:

Proposition S1

(Hoeffding’s inequality,
(
Kliesch and Roth 2021
, Theorem 8)
)
.

Let
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
be i.i.d. random variables with
a_{i}\leq\omega_{i}\leq b_{i}
, and define
S_{T}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{T}\omega_{i}
. For any
t>0
,

\Pr[S_{T}-\mathbb{E}[S_{T}]\geq t]\leq\exp\left[-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{T}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\right]

(A6)

Then, we introduce the median-of-means estimator:

Proposition S2

(Median-of-means estimator)
.

Let
T=BK
and
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
be i.i.d. random variables with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.
For each block
l\in\{1,\dots,K\}
define the empirical mean

o_{l}\coloneq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=(l-1)B+1}^{lB}\omega_{i},

(A7)

and let
\omega=\mathrm{median}\{o_{1},o_{2},\cdots,o_{K}\}
.
Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
. By choosing
B=\frac{6\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}
,
K=\frac{9}{2}\ln(\delta^{-1})
, we have that

\Pr[\absolutevalue{\omega-\mu}\geq\varepsilon]\leq\delta.

(A8)

Equivalently, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\absolutevalue{\omega- \mu}<\sigma\sqrt{\frac{27\ln(\delta^{-1})}{T}}

(A9)

Proof.

For each block mean
o_{l}
we have
\mathbb{E}[o_{l}]=\mu
and

\mathrm{Var}[o_{l}]=\sigma^{2}/B=\varepsilon^{2}/6
.
By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\Pr[\absolutevalue{o_l - \mu}\geq\varepsilon]\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}[o_{l}]}{\varepsilon^{2}}=\frac{1}{6}.

(A10)

Define the indicator
\tilde{o}_{l}\coloneqq\mathbf{1}\bigl[\lvert o_{l}-\mu\rvert\geq\varepsilon\bigr]
, then
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{o}_{l}]\leq 1/6
and
0\leq\tilde{o}_{l}\leq 1
.
Applying Proposition
S1
to
\sum_{l=1}^{K}\tilde{o}_{l}
, we have that:

\begin{split}\Pr[\absolutevalue{\omega-\mu}\geq\varepsilon]&=\Pr[\sum_{i=1}^{K}\tilde{o}_{l}\geq\frac{K}{2}]\\
&\leq\Pr[\sum_{i=1}^{K}\tilde{o}_{l}-\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{K}\tilde{o}_{l}]\geq\frac{K}{3}]\\
&\leq\exp\left[-\frac{2(\frac{K}{3})^{2}}{K}\right]=\delta,\end{split}

(A11)

completing the proof.
∎

B.1
Proof of Lemma
2
: entanglement of low-complexity states

Proof.

Because

, the state
\ket{\phi}
can be prepared from

by a two-dimensional depth-
d

U=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\bigotimes_{j}V_{i,j}\right)

(B16)

where the gates

in the

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{B,d+1}&=B,\\
\mathcal{L}_{B,i}&=\mathcal{L}_{B,i+1}\cup\left[\bigcup\left\{\mathrm{supp}(V_{i,j})\;\big|\;\mathrm{supp}(V_{i,j})\cap\mathcal{L}_{B,i+1}\neq\emptyset\right\}\right].\end{split}

(B17)

Thus

contains all qubits that can interact with

d

and

(see Fig.
3

(\mathcal{L}_{B,1}\cap L)\subseteq L_{1},\quad(\mathcal{L}_{B,1}\cap R)\subseteq R_{1}

(B18)

The unitary

is defined as the product of all gates whose supports lie outside the backward light cone of

V=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\bigotimes_{\mathrm{supp}(V_{i,j})\not\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{B,i}}V_{i,j}\right),

(B19)

The remaining two-qubit gates define the unitary

W=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\bigotimes_{\mathrm{supp}(V_{i,j})\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{B,i}}V_{i,j}\right).

(B20)

By construction,

acts only on subsystem

commutes with the operator
\bra{\bmz}_{B}\otimes I_{A}

{\bm{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{B}}

\begin{split}\ket{\widetilde{\phi_{\bmz}}}&=(\bra{\bmz}_{B}\otimes I_{A})\ket{\phi}\\

(B21)

Eq. (
B18

\begin{split}V(\bra{\bmz}_{B}\otimes I_{A})W\ket{0}_{AB}&=V[(\bra{\bmz}_{B}\ket{\phi_1}_{BL_{1}L_{2}})\ket{0}_{L_{2}R_{2}}]\\

(B22)

Normalizing this state, we obtain

\ket{\phi_{\bmz}}=V\left(\ket{\varphi}_{L_{1}R_{1}}\otimes\ket{0}_{L_{2}R_{2}}\right).

(B23)

The circuit

∎

\Pr_{{\bm{z}}\sim p_{\psi}({\bm{z}})}\left[\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}^{p^{\prime}}}(\psi_{{\bm{z}}})\leq 1-\tfrac{1}{4}[c-6\eta^{-1}/(1-p^{\prime})]^{2}\right]\geq p>0.

(B46)

Hence for
t=1-\frac{1}{4}[c-6\eta^{-1}/(1-p^{\prime})]^{2}
,

F_{\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}^{p^{\prime}}}(\psi,t)=\Pr_{{\bm{z}}\sim p_{\psi}({\bm{z}})}\left[\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}^{p^{\prime}}}(\psi_{{\bm{z}}})\leq t\right]\geq p>0.

(B47)

By Lemma
S3
, the gap is therefore

\Delta(\psi,t,p^{\prime})=(1-t)[F_{\mathcal{FP}_{\mathsf{P}}^{p^{\prime}}}(\psi,t)-(1-p^{\prime})]\geq\tfrac{1}{4}[c-6\eta^{-1}/(1-p^{\prime})]^{2}[p-(1-p^{\prime})].

(B48)

When
\eta cp>6
, choosing
p^{\prime}=1-(p+6\eta^{-1}c^{-1})/2
gives

\Delta(\psi,t,p^{\prime})\geq\frac{c(\eta cp-6)^{3}}{8\eta(\eta cp+6)^{2}}.

(B49)

∎

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Choose
t,p^{\prime}
as specified in Lemma
S4
, and define
O\coloneqq\tilde{O}(\psi,t,p^{\prime}),a\coloneqq t+(1-p^{\prime})(1-t)\geq\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{P}}}\tr(\tilde{O} \rho)
and
b\coloneqq\tr(\tilde{O} \psi)
according to Lemma
S3
.
From Lemma
S4
we know that the gap
\Delta\coloneqq\Delta(\psi,t,p^{\prime})=b-a=\Omega(1)
.
Set the decision threshold to
\eta^{\ast}\;\coloneqq\;a+\tfrac{\Delta}{3}
.

Soundness
-For any
\rho\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{P}}
, we have
\tr(\tilde{O}\rho)\leq a
. Estimating this quantity to an additive error
\varepsilon=\Delta/3
ensures

\omega<\tr(\tilde{O}\rho)+\varepsilon\leq\eta^{\ast}
, so all property-free states are rejected with high probability.

Completeness
-For any state
\rho
satisfying
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\psi)\leq\varepsilon
,

\tr(\tilde{O}\rho)-\varepsilon\geq\tr(\tilde{O} \psi)-\norm{\tilde{O}}_{\infty}\norm{(\rho-\psi)_{+}}_{1}-\varepsilon=\eta^{\ast}.

(B50)

Here, for a Hermitian operator
M=M_{+}-M_{-}
,
M_{+}
is its positive part. The inequality follows because
\tilde{O}
is positive semidefinite. Therefore, the estimator satisfies

\omega>\tr(\tilde{O}\rho)-\varepsilon\geq\eta^{\ast}

(B51)

So
\rho
is accepted with high probability.

By Proposition
S2
, the sample complexity required to achieve additive error
\varepsilon
is given by

T=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}\ln(\delta^{-1})}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}(4^{n_{A}})=\exp(\cO(\eta^2 d^2)),

(B52)

where
\sigma^{2}\leq 4^{n_{A}}+1
. For the two-dimensional setting with
n_{A}=\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}d^{2})
and constant failure probability, this gives
T=\exp(\cO(\eta^2d^2))
, which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Performance analysis of complexity certification for generic states

In this section, we prove the performance of our complexity certification schemes for generic quantum states, including Haar-random states and brickwork-circuit states.

C.1
Deep-thermalized and Haar-random states

We first establish the performance guarantees for deep-thermalized states. We begin by introducing the basic definitions of quantum state designs and their associated entanglement properties. We then use these tools to establish the desired performance guarantees.

C.1.1
Entanglement in quantum state designs

Quantum state designs are defined as approximations to the Haar random distribution
Brandão et al. 2016
. In this context, we denote by
\mathrm{Haar}(n)
the Haar random distribution over
n
-qubit pure states. Given a state ensemble
\mathcal{E}=\{p_{\psi},\psi\}
, we define its
k
-th moment as

\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{(k)}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathcal{E}}\left[(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})^{\otimes k}\right].

(C53)

Following
Cotler et al. 2023
, we define an
\epsilon
-approximate
k
-design as follows:

Definition S1

(
\epsilon
-approximate
k
-design)
.

An
n
-qubit state ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is an
\epsilon
-approximate
k
-design if

\norm{\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{(k)} - \rho_{\mathrm{Haar}(n)}^{(k)}}_{1}\leq\epsilon.

(C54)

where
\norm{\cdot}_{1}
denotes the trace norm.

Note that an
\epsilon
-approximate
k_{1}
-design is also an
\epsilon
-approximate
k_{2}
-design for any
k_{2}\leq k_{1}
. There is also an alternate definition of approximate state designs, using the name “frame operator” (FO).

Definition S2

(
\epsilon
-FO-approximate
k
-design,
(
Liu et al. 2018
, Definition 10)
)
.

An
n
-qubit state ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is an
\lambda
-FO-approximate
k
-design if

\norm{\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{(k)} - \rho_{\mathrm{Haar}(n)}^{(k)}}_{1}\leq\lambda D_{k}^{-1}.

(C55)

where
D_{k}=\binom{k+N-1}{k}
and
N=2^{n}
.

It follows that the ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is an
\epsilon
-approximate
k
-design if and only if it is a
\lambda
-FO-approximate
k
-design with
\lambda=\epsilon D_{k}
.

Intuitively, states in a state design exhibit high entanglement, a fact that is rigorously established in
Liu et al. 2018
. To show this, we consider the generalized Rényi entropy. In the following, all logarithms are taken to base 2.

Definition S3
.

The quantum Rényi entropy of a density matrix
\rho
is defined as

S^{(\alpha)}(\rho)\;=\;\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\tr\bigl[\rho^{\alpha}\bigr].

(C56)

Taking the limit as
\alpha\rightarrow 1
, the Rényi entropy converges to the von Neumann entropy:

S(\rho)\coloneqq\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 1}S^{(\alpha)}(\rho)=-\tr[\rho\log\rho].

(C57)

By Jensen’s inequality,

\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}\log p_{i}\leq\log\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}^{2},

(C58)

it follows that
S(\rho)\geq S^{(2)}(\rho)
.

Here, we fix the system to be
LR
, where both
L
and
R
consist of
m
qubits, with a total of
2m
qubits. We now present the following result.

Lemma S5

(Purity in a state design)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an
\epsilon
-approximate
2
-design on system
LR
. For sufficiently large
m
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathcal{E}}\tr(\psi_L^2)\leq\frac{d_{A}+d_{B}}{d_{A}d_{B}+1}+\epsilon\leq 2^{-m+1}+\epsilon.

(C59)

Proof.

The ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is also a
\lambda
-FO-approximate
2
-design with
\lambda=\epsilon D_{2}
. From Theorem 26 in
Liu et al. 2018
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathcal{E}}\tr(\psi_L^2)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathrm{Haar}(2m)}\tr(\psi_L^2)+\frac{\lambda}{D_{2}}=\frac{d_{A}+d_{B}}{d_{A}d_{B}+1}+\epsilon\leq 2^{-m+1}+\epsilon,

(C60)

where we use
\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathrm{Haar}(2m)}\tr(\psi_L^2)=\frac{d_{A}+d_{B}}{d_{A}d_{B}+1}

(
Liu et al. 2018
, Eq. (4.11))
.
∎

C.1.2
Proof of Proposition
1
: deep-thermalized states

Proof.

Since
E_{L\mid R}(\phi)=S(\phi_{L})\geq S^{(2)}(\phi_{L})
, we can bound
\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\big[E_{L\mid R}(\phi)\geq c|L|+1\big]
as follows:

\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[E_{LR}\left(\phi\right)\geq c|L|+1\Bigr]\geq\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[S^{(2)}(\phi_{L})\geq c|L|+1\Bigr]=\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[-\log\tr(\phi_L^2)\geq c|L|+1\Bigr]=\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[\tr(\phi_L^2)\leq 2^{-(c|L|+1)}\Bigr].

(C61)

By Markov’s inequality,

\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[\tr(\phi_L^2)>2^{-(c|L|+1)}\Bigr]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[\tr(\phi_L^2)\Bigr]}{2^{-(c|L|+1)}}=2^{c|L|+1}\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[\tr(\phi_L^2)\Bigr].

(C62)

Hence,

\begin{split}\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[E_{L\mid R}\left(\phi\right)\geq c|L|+1\Bigr]&\geq\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[\tr(\phi_L^2)\leq 2^{-(c|L|+1)}\Bigr]\\
&=1-\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[\tr(\phi_L^2)>2^{-(c|L|+1)}\Bigr]\\
&\geq 1-2^{c|L|+1}\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[\tr(\phi_L^2)\Bigr].\end{split}

(C63)

Lemma
S5
shows that

\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[\tr(\phi_L^2)\Bigr]\leq 2^{-|L|+1}+\epsilon=\mathcal{O}(2^{-|L|}).

(C64)

Choosing, for example,
\eta=5
and
c=\tfrac{9}{10}
, we have

\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[E_{L\mid R}\left(\phi\right)\geq c|L|+1\Bigr]\geq 1-2^{c|L|+1}\Bigl[2^{-(|L|+1)}+\epsilon\Bigr]=1-\mathcal{O}\Bigl(2^{-(1-c)|L|}\Bigr)=\Omega(1).

(C65)

Therefore, the sample complexity in Theorem
4
is
\exp(-\mathcal{O}(d^2))
.

Moreover, for example, when choosing
\eta=7
and
c=\frac{9}{10}
, we still have
\Pr_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\Bigl[E_{L\mid R}\left(\phi\right)\geq c|L|+1\Bigr]\geq 1-o(1)
. In this case, the gap given by (
B44
) is
\Omega(1)
and hence the observable
\tilde{O}(\psi,t,p^{\prime})
can be used to certify the measurement-assisted circuit complexity with
\mathcal{O}(1)
sample complexity.
∎

C.1.3
Haar-random states

For Haar-random states, we use the following result on deep thermalization.

Fact S1

(Haar-random states deeply thermalize,
(
Cotler et al. 2023
, Theorem 1)
)
.

Let
\ket{\psi}
be chosen Haar-randomly from the set of
n
-qubit pure states. Then, the ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\psi}
forms an
\epsilon
-approximate
k
-design with probability at least
1-\delta
, provided that

|B|=\Omega\left(k|A|+\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)+\log\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

(C66)

For complexity certification task, by choosing

C.2
Brickwork-circuit states

We now establish the performance guarantee for brickwork-circuit states.
In an
D
-dimensional brickwork circuit, qubits are labeled by
D
-dimensional coordinate vectors in
[L]^{D}
, where
L
is the edge length.
Within every
2D
consecutive layers, the
(2i-1)
-th and
2i
-th layers, for
1\leq i\leq D
, each act on adjacent qubits that differ only in their
i
-th coordinate.
Specifically, in the
i
-th coordinate, these two layers respectively couple qubits with coordinates
(2x_{i},\,2x_{i}+1)
and
(2x_{i}-1,\,2x_{i})
.

We first show that the projected states of shallow-depth brickwork-circuit states are highly entangled.
Building on this, we then derive the corresponding performance guarantee for such states.

C.2.1
Entanglement in projected states

Here, we establish the entanglement growth result for general finite dimension
D
.
We begin by using the result on entanglement growth for a single-qubit random unitary.

Fact S2

(Entanglement growth under random unitary,
(
Nahum et al. 2017
, Appendix B)
)
.

Let
\ket{\phi_t}_{1234}
be a pure state, and define
\ket{\phi_{t+1}}_{1234}=U_{23}\ket{\phi_t}_{1234}
, where
U_{23}
is a two-qubit unitary sampled from the Haar measure. Assume subsystems
2
and
3
each have dimension
2

\mathbb{E}_{U_{23}}\tr(\rho_{t+1,12}^2)=\frac{2}{5}\bigl(\tr(\rho_{t,1}^2)+\tr(\rho_{t,123}^2)\bigr).

(C67)

Then, we establish the entanglement growth of the projected ensemble for random brickwork-circuit states.
We denote
\mathcal{E}
as the random state ensemble
\{p(\psi),\psi\}
.
The projected ensemble is defined as
\mathcal{E}_{A}\coloneqq\{p(\psi)\,p_{\psi}(\bm{z}),\psi_{\bm{z}}\}

is a region of size

in the lattice (for the 2D case, see Fig.

).
We have the following result:

Lemma S6

(High entanglement in
D
-dimensional random brickwork-circuit states)

Let

be the ensemble of output states from a random

\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathcal{E}_{A}}[\tr(\psi_L^2)]\leq(4/5)^{(2w-4s)^{D-1}\min(s,\lfloor d^{\prime}/2D\rfloor)}.

Recall the definition of the backward light cone of the subsystem

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{B,d^{\prime}+1}&=B,\\

and

D.2
Localizable entanglement of Haar-random states

Here, we prove that the localizable entanglement of Haar-random states is bounded below by a constant with overwhelmingly high probability, thereby validating the performance of our protocol on generic states.

Lemma S7
.

Let
\ket{\psi}
be chosen Haar-randomly from the set of
n
-qubit pure states.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
for
\delta=\exp(-\exp(\Omega(n)))
, we have

\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}(1,2)=\Omega(1),\qquad\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(1,2)=\Omega(1).

(D82)

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon=\tfrac{1}{10}
. By Fact
S1
, the projected ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\psi}
on qubits
\{1,2\}
is an
\epsilon
-approximate 2-design with probability
1-\delta_{0}
, where
\delta_{0}=\exp\bigl(-\exp(\Omega(n))\bigr)
.
Then, by Lemma
S5
,

\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\bigl[\tr(\phi_1^2)\bigr]\leq\frac{4}{5}+\epsilon=\frac{9}{10}.

(D83)

Now we show for the constant
e_{0}=\frac{1}{20}
, it holds that
\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}(1,2)\geq e_{0}
.
Denote by
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
the Schmidt coefficients of the projected state
\phi
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}
. We have
\lambda_{2}=1-\lambda_{1}
,
\tr(\phi_1^2)=\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}
and
\mathrm{Fid}_{\mathsf{P}}(\phi)=\lambda_{1}
.
Therefore,

\begin{split}\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}(1,2)&=\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}[1-\lambda_{1}]\\
&\geq\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}[\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{1})]\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}\!\left[2\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{1})-1\right]+\tfrac{1}{2}\\
&=-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\mathcal{E}_{\psi}}[\tr(\phi_1^2)]+\tfrac{1}{2}\\
&\geq\tfrac{1}{20}.\end{split}

(D84)

Hence
\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}(1,2)\geq e_{0}
with probability at least
1-\delta_{0}
.

To lower bound
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(1,2)
, define
\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}^{{\bm{b}}}
for
{\bm{b}}\in\{X,Y,Z\}^{n_{B}}
as the localizable entanglement under measurement basis
{\bm{b}}
on
B
.
By local unitary invariance of the Haar measure,
\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}^{{\bm{b}}}(1,2)\geq e_{0}
for Haar-random
\psi
with probability at least
1-\delta_{0}
.
Let
f(\psi,{\bm{b}})\coloneqq\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}^{{\bm{b}}}(1,2)\geq e_{0}]
. Then for
{\bm{b}}
chosen uniformly from
\{X,Y,Z\}^{n_{B}}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\psi}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{b}}}f(\psi,{\bm{b}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{b}}}\mathbb{E}_{\psi}f(\psi,{\bm{b}})\geq 1-\delta_{0}.

(D85)

By Markov’s inequality,

\Pr_{\psi}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{b}}}f(\psi,{\bm{b}})\leq 1-\sqrt{\delta_{0}}\right]\leq\sqrt{\delta_{0}}.

(D86)

Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta_{1}
for
\delta_{1}\coloneqq\sqrt{\delta_{0}}=\exp\bigl(-\exp(\Omega(n))\bigr)
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{b}}}f(\psi,{\bm{b}})&>1-\delta_{1},\\
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(1,2)&=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{b}}}\,\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}^{{\bm{b}}}(1,2)\\
&>(1-\delta_{1})e_{0}\\
&=\Omega(1).\end{split}

(D87)

Combining the bounds, we conclude that with probability at least
1-\delta
for
\delta=\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}=\exp\bigl(-\exp(\Omega(n))\bigr)
,

\mathrm{LE}_{\psi}(1,2)=\Omega(1),\quad\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(1,2)=\Omega(1).

(D88)

∎

By a union bound, with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
for
\delta^{\prime}=n\delta=\exp\bigl(-\exp(\Omega(n))\bigr)
, we have
\widetilde{\mathrm{LE}}_{\psi}(i,i+1)=\Omega(1)
for all
i\in[n-1]
.
This establishes that our certification protocol for fully inseparable entanglement is sample-efficient for Haar-random states.

E.1
Proof of Theorem
6

Firstly, we show that a Haar-random pure state is almost surely entangled.

Lemma S8
.

Let
\ket{\psi}
be drawn Haar-randomly from the bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
with local dimensions
d_{A},d_{B}\geq 2
. Then, with probability
1
,
\ket{\psi}
is entangled across the cut
A\mid B
.

Proof.

The set of all pure states in
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
, modulo a global phase, forms the complex projective space
\mathrm{CP}^{d-1}
with real dimension
2d-2
, where
d=d_{A}d_{B}
.
In contrast, the set of product (separable) states forms the Cartesian product
\mathrm{CP}^{d_{A}-1}\times\mathrm{CP}^{d_{B}-1}
, whose real dimension is
(2d_{A}-2)+(2d_{B}-2)
.
Because
d_{A},d_{B}\geq 2
, we have

(2d_{A}-2)+(2d_{B}-2)<2d_{A}d_{B}-2,

(E89)

so the manifold of separable states has strictly lower dimension than the manifold of all pure states.
A lower-dimensional submanifold has Haar measure zero within the larger manifold, implying that the probability of sampling a separable state under the Haar measure is zero. Hence, a Haar-random pure state is almost surely entangled.
∎

The notation
O_{\psi}
suppresses the dependence on the size
n_{A}
of subsystem
A
. Here, we write
O_{\psi,n_{A}}
to make this dependence explicit. We now show that when
B
consists of a single qubit (i.e.,
n_{A}=n-1
), the observable
O_{\psi,n-1}
has a strictly positive spectral gap almost surely.

Lemma S9
.

Let
\ket{\psi}
be drawn Haar-randomly from the
n
-qubit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
with
n_{B}=1
. Then

\Delta\bigl(O_{\psi,n-1}\bigr)>0

(E90)

with probability 1.

Proof.

Lemma
S8
guarantees that
\ket{\psi}
is entangled across
A\mid B
almost surely. Observe that

O_{\psi,n-1}=\tfrac{1}{3}\bigl(O_{X}+O_{Y}+O_{Z}\bigr),

(E91)

where

O_{Z}=\psi_{0}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}+\psi_{1}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1},

(E92)

with

\psi_{0}=\frac{\tr_{B}[(I_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0})\,\psi]}{\tr[(I_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0})\,\psi]},\qquad\psi_{1}=\frac{\tr_{B}[(I_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1})\,\psi]}{\tr[(I_{A}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1})\,\psi]},

(E93)

and
O_{X},O_{Y}
are defined analogously in the
\{\ket{\pm}\}
and
\{\ket{\pm i}\}
bases. Entanglement implies
\psi_{0}\neq\psi_{1}
; otherwise
\ket{\psi}
would be separable.

To prove
\Delta(O_{\psi,n-1})>0
, we show the maximal eigenvalue of
O_{\psi,n-1}
is nondegenerate. Suppose a pure state
\ket{\phi}
attains this maximum so that
\tr(O_{\psi,n-1}\phi)=1
. Then

\tr(O_X\phi)=\tr(O_Y\phi)=\tr(O_Z\phi)=1.

(E94)

The condition
\tr(O_Z\phi)=1
implies that when subsystem
B
of
\ket{\phi}
is measured in the computational basis, the projected states on subsystem
A
are precisely
\psi_{0}
(for outcome
\ket{0}
) and
\psi_{1}
(for outcome
\ket{1}
). Hence,

\ket{\phi}=a_{0}\ket{0}_{B}\ket{\psi_{0}}_{A}+a_{1}\ket{1}_{B}\ket{\psi_{1}}_{A},\quad a_{0},a_{1}\in\mathbb{C}.

(E95)

The uncertainty principle dictates that
\phi_{B}
cannot simultaneously be an eigenstate of at least two of the operators
\sigma_{X},\sigma_{Y},\sigma_{Z}
. Assuming without loss of generality that
\phi_{B}
is not an eigenstate of
\sigma_{X}
and
\sigma_{Z}
, it follows that
a_{0},a_{1}\neq 0
.
Similarly, for the entangled state
\ket{\psi}

\ket{\psi}=b_{0}\ket{0}_{B}\ket{\psi_0}_{A}+b_{1}\ket{1}_{B}\ket{\psi_1}_{A},\quad b_{0},b_{1}\neq 0.

(E96)

Next,
\tr(O_X\phi)=1
forces the projected states of
\ket{\phi}
and
\ket{\psi}
after measuring
B
in
\{\ket{\pm}\}
to coincide. Thus

a_{0}\ket{\psi_0}+a_{1}\ket{\psi_1}\propto b_{0}\ket{\psi_0}+b_{1}\ket{\psi_1},

(E97)

so
a_{0}=eb_{0}
and
a_{1}=eb_{1}
for some
e\in\mathbb{C}
. Therefore
\ket{\phi}=e\ket{\psi}
, and normalization implies
|e|=1
. Hence
\ket{\phi}
and
\ket{\psi}
differ only by a global phase, proving the maximum eigenvalue is nondegenerate and
\Delta(O_{\psi,n-1})>0
almost surely.
∎
