Vaccinating against the ‘undruggable’—a new paradigm for fibrolamellar carcinoma
Authors: Meghana Singh, Anwaar Saeed
Published: 2026

Fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinoma (FLC) is a rare liver tumor that predominantly affects adolescents and young adults, with a median age at diagnosis of approximately 25 years (1,2). Outcomes remain poor, particularly in unresectable disease, where median survival is approximately 12 months (3). The rarity of FLC, combined with its distinct biology, has historically limited the development of effective systemic therapies and contributed to the absence of a clearly defined standard of care in advanced disease.

The discovery of the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion transcript a decade ago established a unifying oncogenic driver in the vast majority of FLC tumors (4,5). Prior to this, FLC was frequently misclassified as conventional hepatocellular carcinoma, likely contributing to the apparent rise in reported incidence. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative therapy; however, systemic treatment options for advanced disease are limited, and no standard regimen has been established. In practice, enrollment in clinical trials is strongly recommended. When this is not feasible, regimens such as GEMOX (gemcitabine with oxaliplatin) (with or without lenvatinib), FOLFOX (5-Fluorouracil/leucovorin with Oxaliplatin), or dual immune checkpoint blockade with ipilimumab and nivolumab are used, although supporting evidence is largely retrospective, and outcomes remain suboptimal (5).

Although the DNAJB1::PRKACA transcript has been identified, direct inhibition of this fusion protein is not feasible because of the risk of on-target toxicity. However, the immunogenic neoantigens generated by the DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion may serve as effective tumor targets. These neoantigens are uniformly expressed, exhibit higher immunogenicity than most point mutations, and are less susceptible to immune escape, making them compelling targets for immunotherapeutic strategies (6). Within this framework, the phase I study by Baretti and colleagues represents a notable conceptual advance—reframing an “undruggable” alteration as an actionable immunologic vulnerability(7).

In this first-in-human study, the investigators evaluated FLC-Vac, a therapeutic peptide vaccine targeting the DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein, combined with ipilimumab and nivolumab in children and adults with advanced FLC who were immune checkpoint inhibitor naïve (NCT04248569). The study assessed safety and immunogenicity by measuring T cell responses after a 10-week priming phase, defined as a ≥2.5-fold increase in IFN-γ-producing, neoantigen-specific T cells in peripheral blood. Sixteen patients were enrolled; twelve completed the priming phase and were evaluable for both clinical and immune responses. The median age was 24 years (range, 12–47 years), consistent with the current epidemiologic profile.

The treatment schema incorporated a structured priming phase, during which the vaccine was administered at weeks 0, 1, 2, 3, 6, and 9, alongside nivolumab (3 mg/kg) followed by ipilimumab (1 mg/kg) every three weeks for four doses. Maintenance therapy with FLC-Vac and nivolumab was subsequently continued. A safety lead-in was included, with the first three patients receiving vaccine monotherapy prior to the addition of checkpoint blockade, providing initial reassurance regarding tolerability.

From a clinical standpoint, the observed activity is notable. In the intention-to-treat population, the overall response rate was 15%. Among evaluable patients, the disease control rate reached 75%, including three partial responses (25%). Importantly, all patients who achieved partial responses subsequently underwent surgical debulking of residual disease and were reported to have no evidence of active malignancy at the time of publication. While these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size, the ability to convert patients to a surgically manageable state represents a potentially meaningful clinical outcome in this disease context.

The immunologic findings further strengthen the biological rationale for this approach. Seventy-five percent of evaluable patients developed DNAJ-PKAc-specific T-cell responses, meeting the study’s primary immunologic endpoint. Notably, all patients who achieved partial responses demonstrated neoantigen-specific T-cell expansion, suggesting a clear concordance between immunogenicity and clinical benefit. This alignment between mechanistic engagement and clinical activity is particularly compelling and supports the validity of targeting fusion-derived neoantigens in FLC.

The safety profile of the combination regimen was consistent with expectations. Grade 3 adverse events were observed in 37.5% of patients and were largely attributable to known toxicities of immune checkpoint inhibitors. No grade 4 or 5 treatment-related adverse events were reported. Patients who received vaccine monotherapy during the lead-in phase experienced no adverse effects, although the duration of exposure was limited. Overall, these findings suggest that the addition of the vaccine does not substantially exacerbate the toxicity profile of checkpoint inhibition.

An important and insightful aspect of the study lies in its exploration of mechanisms of acquired resistance. While loss of antigen expression is a common pathway of immune escape, the DNAJ-PKAc fusion remained detectable in patients who experienced disease progression. Instead, the data suggest that resistance is driven predominantly by T-cell exhaustion. The observation that rechallenge with ipilimumab and nivolumab resulted in renewed clinical response in at least one patient highlights the dynamic nature of this process and suggests that resistance may, in part, be reversible. These findings provide a rationale for future strategies aimed at sustaining T-cell function, including alternative checkpoint combinations or sequencing approaches.

Several strengths of this study merit emphasis. Targeting a tumor-specific neoantigen derived from a driver alteration that is nearly universally present in FLC represents a significant conceptual advantage. Unlike the heterogeneous and often subclonal neoantigen landscapes observed in other solid tumors, this approach leverages a consistent and biologically essential target. The persistent expression of the fusion transcript, even in the setting of disease progression, further underscores its central role in tumor biology and reduces the likelihood of immune escape through antigen loss. Additionally, the absence of this neoantigen in normal tissues minimizes the risk of off-target autoimmunity.

Beyond FLC, these findings may have broader implications for other fusion-driven malignancies, including EML4-ALK-positive lung cancer, BCR-ABL-positive leukemia, and Ewing sarcoma. The concept of targeting oncogenic fusions through immunologic strategies represents an increasingly relevant paradigm, particularly for alterations that have historically been considered pharmacologically intractable. The use of an off-the-shelf peptide vaccine also offers practical advantages, including improved scalability, reduced manufacturing complexity, and the potential for more rapid treatment initiation compared with individualized vaccine approaches.

The study is further strengthened by its detailed immunologic characterization. The use of a predefined and quantitative threshold for neoantigen-specific T-cell expansion provides a robust framework for assessing immune engagement. The identification of fusion-specific T-cell receptors as potential biomarkers of response introduces an additional layer of translational relevance, with implications for patient selection and the development of next-generation immunotherapeutic strategies.

Notwithstanding these strengths, several limitations should be considered. The small sample size, while expected in a rare disease, and possibly unavoidable as the annual incidence of 0.02 per 100,000 limits the statistical power of the study and precludes definitive conclusions regarding efficacy. This challenge is not unique to FLC-Vac, and we will need to take into consideration that the Food and Drug Administration recognizes that standard randomized controlled trials may not be feasible in ultra-rare cancers and single-arm studies with dual response rates may be accepted for regulatory decision-making in such settings. Early attrition during the priming phase, with 25% of patients not completing this period due to disease progression, raises the possibility of selection bias and may preferentially enrich for patients with less aggressive disease biology. This is a particularly relevant concern given that FLC exhibits a heterogeneous natural history.

Critically, the study design does not allow for clear attribution of clinical benefit to the vaccine versus checkpoint inhibition. A multicenter retrospective analysis has suggested modest activity of checkpoint inhibitors in FLC, with reported response rates of approximately 15.8% and a disease control rate of 36.9%, with a median progression-free survival of 5.5 months (8). A separate retrospective series of 20 FLC patients treated with nivolumab and lenvatinib reported an objective response rate of 40%, a disease control rate of 87%, with 3 complete responses (9). While the outcomes observed in this study appear more favorable compared to the ICI alone benchmark, they fall within the range of outcomes for immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based combination regimens. Cross-study comparisons should be interpreted with caution given the differences in patient selection, prior treatment exposure, and response assessment. Hence the potential for synergistic activity between the vaccine and checkpoint inhibitors remains biologically plausible, but remains to be formally established.

Furthermore, the durability of response and long-term survival outcomes remain unknown. While the observed responses and the potential for conversion to surgical resection are encouraging, FLC has a high recurrence rate even after complete surgical resection. The distinction between delaying recurrence and preventing it altogether is currently an open question, which can only be resolved with extended observation. Longer follow-up will be essential to determine whether these translate into meaningful improvements in overall survival. Future studies should also consider incorporating endpoints such as conversion to resectability and durability of immune response to better capture the full clinical impact of this strategy.

Overall, the preliminary results of the FLC-Vac trial are highly encouraging and represent an important advance in a disease with limited therapeutic options. A disease control rate of 75%, coupled with consistent induction of neoantigen-specific T-cell responses, supports the premise that the DNAJB1::PRKACA fusion transcript is not only a defining molecular feature of FLC but also a viable and actionable immunologic target. Importantly, the mechanistic insights derived from this study—particularly the identification of T-cell exhaustion as a key driver of resistance—provide a roadmap for future therapeutic refinement. Strategies aimed at preventing or reversing T-cell dysfunction, including checkpoint rechallenge or incorporation of additional immune-modulating agents, may further improve outcomes. Ultimately, this study reinforces a broader and increasingly relevant principle in oncology: that oncogenic drivers deemed “undruggable” may nonetheless be highly actionable when approached through the lens of immunotherapy.
