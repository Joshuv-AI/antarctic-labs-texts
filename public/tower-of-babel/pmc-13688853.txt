Whistleblowing and retractions concerning Iran-affiliated medical research: motives and editorial perspectives
Authors: Seyedeh Kimia Zamani, Amin Talebi Bezmin Abadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research integrity underpins credible scientific research. Whistleblowing on research fraud, including data falsification, fabrication, and plagiarism, is critical for identifying flawed publications. Here, we investigated whistleblowers’ roles in the retraction of Iran-affiliated, medically related publications between 2014 and 2024 and documented whistleblowers’ motives and journal editors’ perspectives. We used a structured questionnaire to interview 18 whistleblowers, 10 members of editorial boards, and four editors-in-chief. We identified three dominant whistleblower motives: commitment to academic integrity and ethics, frustration with institutional inaction, and, in some cases, retaliation or grievances. Whistleblowers express their vulnerability in high-risk environments, consistently reporting fear of legal, professional, or personal retaliation. Editors reported that they relied on whistleblowers to identify misconduct and acknowledged that assessing motives and navigating the Iranian institutional bureaucracy were difficult. Both groups highlighted that participants perceived ineffective ethics committees and insufficient protection measures as contributing to an environment conducive to misconduct. The findings suggest that whistleblowers are perceived as indispensable gatekeepers of integrity, and that a complex interplay of moral obligations, personal grievances, and institutional inadequacies may drive them to report transgressions. Based on these findings, we suggest that transparent editorial protocols, secure reporting mechanisms, whistleblower protections, enhanced institutional oversight, and culturally aware international interactions may represent useful considerations for safeguarding research integrity in complex academic environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12910-026-01560-x.

Introduction

Upholding scientific integrity underpins credibility and reproducibility. Erroneous, biased, or intentional research misconduct undermines the fundamental purposes of scientific studies, particularly concerning life sciences, which primarily aims to improve human wellbeing [1, 2]. During 2014–2024, the number of retraction notices rose globally [3, 4]. Such increases jeopardize the foundational role of integrity during scientific progress, erodes the public trust in science [1, 5], and implicates underlying systemic issues, including pressure to publish, questionable research practices, editorial bias, and deliberate fraud. Altogether, undermining credibility and loss of integrity squander substantial public or private funding. Whistleblowers [6] are pivotal in identifying problematic publications and academic misconduct, including data manipulation, data or image fabrication, plagiarism, and authorship disputes [7, 8]. While whistleblowing an ethical transgression initially is an allegation, subsequent investigations determine whether specific types of scientific misconduct are involved [9]. Whistleblowers’ reports often reach editorial board members and editors-in-chief who are responsible for scrutinizing allegations and finally recommending retractions. Cases reported on Retraction Watch or PubPeer show that whistleblowers importantly prompt corrective actions and help maintain the integrity of the scientific record. However, whistleblowing is controversial and coincides with risks, including retaliation, legal proceedings, job or career losses, professional intimidation, defamation, false accusations, ostracization, family and friendship problems, psychological/psychosocial outcomes, and health issues [10, 11].

Whistleblowers’ motives are largely varied. Though many genuinely intend to uphold scientific integrity, whistleblowing may be misused. For example, retaliatory whistleblowing may be motivated by personal disputes, academic competition, or institutional conflicts instead of ethical considerations. Though this underlying motivational dichotomy is challenging, it may foster legitimate, integrity-driven cases while discouraging malicious reporting that may unjustly damage careers and impede scientific progress.

Iran-affiliated research output, during 2014–2024, was characterized by substantial academic productivity in medicine and health sciences alongside an unfortunate high number of retraction notices [12, 13]. Throughout this decade, Iran-affiliated medical publications experienced a notable number of retractions, underscoring the need to examine the underlying processes driving these events, particularly the role of whistleblowers and editorial responses. Previous research has documented that Iranian medical faculty members face substantial publication pressure, with nearly 70% agreeing that their research output would be of higher quality in the absence of such pressures, and over 80% identifying tenure pressure as a strong behavioral influence on engaging in research misconduct [14]. Many retraction-related studies focus on superficial causes, such as plagiarism or image/text duplication [13, 15–18] without examining the underlying processes and human interactions that precipitate them. Thus, systematic studies exploring the underlying factors that drive retractions are lacking. Particularly, little is known about how whistleblowers are motivated in Iran and how journal editors assess, contextualize, and respond to their allegations. This gap warrants investigation. Accordingly, we aimed (1) to identify and qualify whistleblowers’ motivations and (2) to examine the decision-making processes, rationales, and contextual considerations by the editorial board members involved in the retraction of Iran-affiliated articles related to medical fields.

Materials and methods

Study design

We investigated the contribution by whistleblowers regarding retractions of Iran-affiliated articles related to medicine or medical sciences between 2014 and 2024. To encompass the ethical, confidentiality, and privacy challenges inherent in studying whistleblowing, we used a new methodological framework, comprising five domains: methods and procedures, governance and ethics, bias mitigation, security/privacy protocols, and compliance and objectives. This framework ensured a rigorous, ethical, secure, and private approach throughout the study. The questionnaire was constructed by the authors following consultation with ethics experts, specifically for use in this study (refer to the Supplementary Material). All protocols, questionnaires and methodological frameworks were critically reviewed by the ethics committee at the National Institute for Medical Research Development (Ethics code: IR.NIMAD.REC.1404.169). The study adhered to the ethical principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki [19]. Although extraterritorial, data-handling practices were designed to be consistent with GDPR-level protections, particularly regarding disclosures.

Participants

A purposive sampling strategy, complemented by snowball sampling, was employed to recruit participants with direct experience and substantial involvement in the article retraction process. Participants were recruited from three key stakeholder groups: whistleblowers, editorial board members, and editors-in-chief. Initial potential whistleblowers were identified through three complementary avenues: (i) the authors’ direct professional awareness of specific whistleblower cases, gained through confidential notifications received from journals during the retraction process; (ii) the corresponding author’s extensive professional network, built over more than a decade of service as a research ethics consultant at Iranian universities; and (iii) direct referrals from journal editors who had handled retraction cases and were willing to forward our invitation to the whistleblowers. To ensure the credibility of our sample, we applied strict inclusion criteria: each participant had to (a) confirm their direct involvement in a whistleblowing case that resulted in a retraction of an Iran-affiliated medical paper between 2014 and 2024, and (b) provide documentary evidence of their role, such as correspondence with the editorial office or a retraction notice mentioning their report. The exclusion criteria included inability to verify involvement, involvement only in non-retracted cases, and withdrawal of consent before and after the interview. For editorial board members and editors-in-chief, eligibility required a minimum of 10 years of experience in editorial or research integrity roles, and involvement in at least three retraction cases involving Iran-affiliated authors. These editorial stakeholders were identified through the corresponding author’s professional network and direct contact with journals known to have retracted Iran-affiliated papers during the study period.

Recruitment and data collection proceeded iteratively until data saturation was achieved. Saturation was assessed independently for each participant group (whistleblowers, editorial board members, and editors-in-chief). Following each interview within a participant group, the two researchers (SKZ and ATBA) reviewed the interview data, compared the emerging codes with those identified in previous interviews from the same group, and assessed whether additional interviews were likely to generate new concepts or perspectives. Recruitment within each participant group was discontinued when consecutive interviews yielded no substantially new codes or themes and the existing categories were considered sufficiently developed to address the study objectives. Questionnaire and semi-structured interviews

We requested each participant to complete a structured, 20-item English questionnaire aimed at contextualizing motivations, decision-making processes, and experiences. Interviews were conducted in English via secure digital platforms (Google Meet or WhatsApp) and followed standardized guides focusing on decision-making, institutional responses, and preventive strategies. Questionnaires and interview guides were reviewed by three ethics experts (with at least 15 years’ experience) from different academic journals to ensure clarity, sensitivity, and methodological fitness. Because whistleblowers may be stigmatized or ostracized, strict confidentiality and privacy protocols were maintained. All interviews were conducted by the principal investigator; detailed written notes were taken instead of audio-recording to minimize risking the participants. Qualitative data derived from interview notes were grouped by themes. To eliminate bias, we used a triple-layer anonymization to minimize any risk in the study protocol. We used pseudonymization as we encoded identifiers during data entry. We omitted indirect identifiers and used fully anonymized datasets for questionnaires and interviews. We anonymized reporting such that no identifiers could be traced back during data analysis. To ensure data security, identifiable data were accessible only to the principal investigator. Raw data were backed up on an air-gapped system to preserve data and prevent unauthorized access. The complete questionnaires and semi-structured interview guide are provided as Supplementary Material (File S1-3).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using conventional qualitative content analysis as described by Graneheim and Lundman [20]. Although the research team initially intended to audio-record all interviews, during the initial phase of data collection several participants expressed discomfort with being audio-recorded. The research team considered that insisting on audio recording might reduce participants’ willingness to openly share their experiences, introduce response bias, and undermine the trust required for discussing highly sensitive experiences of whistleblowing. Therefore, in accordance with participant-centered qualitative research principles, audio recording was discontinued. Instead, detailed written notes were taken throughout each interview and were expanded immediately after each interview into comprehensive interview text while the content of the discussion remained fresh in the researchers’ memory. The documented interview notes served as the primary data source for coding and thematic analysis.

The written interview notes were reviewed repeatedly to achieve immersion in the data and gain a comprehensive understanding of participants’ experiences. The notes were read line by line, and meaning units relevant to the study aim were identified. These meaning units were subsequently condensed while preserving their core meaning and were assigned initial codes. The codes were continuously compared based on similarities and differences and were grouped into subcategories. Related subcategories were then organized into broader categories that reflected the manifest content of the data. Through an iterative and interpretive process, an overarching theme was developed to capture the latent meanings embedded within participants’ accounts. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently, allowing emerging insights to inform subsequent interviews and facilitate theoretical sensitivity. The coding process was iterative, involving constant comparison across interviews to refine categories and ensure conceptual consistency. To further enhance analytical transparency, an illustrative coding framework demonstrating the progression from representative quotations to condensed meaning units, initial codes, subcategories, categories, and overarching themes is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Researcher reflexivity and positionality

Because recruitment partly relied on professional networks, some participants may have been acquainted with members of the research team. To minimize the potential influence of these relationships, participation was entirely voluntary, informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality was emphasized throughout the study. The interviewers remained aware of their potential influence on participants’ responses and sought to minimize this by using a semi-structured interview guide, asking open-ended questions, avoiding leading prompts, and encouraging participants to elaborate on their experiences in their own words.

The multidisciplinary research team engaged in ongoing reflexive discussions throughout data collection and analysis to critically examine how their academic backgrounds, professional experiences, and preconceptions regarding research integrity might shape data interpretation. Coding decisions were reviewed collaboratively, and differences in interpretation were resolved through repeated examination of the interview records until consensus was achieved. Alternative interpretations of participants’ accounts were actively considered to minimize interpretive bias and strengthen the credibility, confirmability, and dependability of the findings.

Results

Thirty-two participants—including whistleblowers (n = 18), editorial board members (n = 10), and editors-in-chief (n = 4)—participated in the study. Table 1 summarizes the aggregated demographic and professional characteristics of the participants.

More than 43 individuals were invited to participate. Ten whistleblowers withdrew after completing the questionnaire but expressed concerns about confidentiality, digital traceability, or reputational risk. One participant withdrew after completing the interview. Furthermore, at least 15 whistleblowers initially agreed to participate but later declined to complete all stages; thus, we excluded their data. Finally, 18 whistleblowers completed both questionnaires and interviews. The high withdrawal rate may reflect participants’ concerns regarding confidentiality, digital traceability, or potential reputational consequences associated with discussing whistleblowing. Because this is the first survey of its kind, we did not expect a considerable number of participants to withdraw.

Whistleblower effectiveness and institutional responses

Seventeen participants reported that whistleblowing contributed to the identification of research misconduct that subsequently resulted in article retractions. They also perceived that limitations in institutional responses reduced the likelihood that these cases would lead to broader policy changes. All whistleblowers stated that they perceived their reporting had not led to formal policy changes. Overall, participants reported that, in their experience, their whistleblowing efforts had rarely resulted in broader policy or organizational changes.

Perceived comparative advantage of whistleblowing

Participants were divided when asked whether Iran-affiliated whistleblowers have an advantage compared with overseas stakeholders when reporting misconduct. Ten respondents (55.6%) rejected, whereas eight (44.4%) affirmed this notion. However, qualitative data showed that Iran-affiliated whistleblowers operate under significant risk compared to whistleblowers in other countries. All Iranian participants expressed profound concern for their safety, particularly when communicating with international organizations such as Retraction Watch or PubPeer. This perception hindered cooperation. Interestingly, some whistleblowers recounted that they had sufficient data on different questionable publications but feared disclosing them.

Barriers to reporting misconduct

Participants collectively declared that retribution was the most significant deterrent. They feared prosecution under national security charges, travel bans, job loss, or permanent judicial proceedings as systemic retributions to follow. They identified dismissal, blacklisting, loss of academic privileges, and loss of future academic or funding opportunities could result in professional repercussions. These factors were compounded by institutional inaction, lack of transparency, and absence of unbiased investigational mechanisms.

Perceptions of international editorial boards

Respondents described editorial teams as professional, neutral, and evidence-driven, with no systematic bias against Iran-affiliated academic submissions. The majority of Iran-affiliated whistleblowers expressed perceived trust in the impartiality of the international editorial boards and maintained that the boards act urgently when considering reports about Iran-affiliated submissions.

Motivations for whistleblowing (interview findings)

Qualitative content analysis of interviews revealed mainly three motives:

Ethical commitment to scientific integrity

Most participants regarded whistleblowing as a moral obligation fundamental to safeguarding scientific integrity, ensuring patient safety, and preserving the global credibility of Iranian scholarship. Many reported experiencing an initial guilt when circumstances compelled them to abandon whistleblowing efforts, a feeling that ultimately reinforced their motivation to persist. Collectively, their statements reflected whistleblowing as an ethical imperative that transcends personal interests and institutional boundaries. As one whistleblower stated: “I knew reporting the paper would create problems for me, but I felt that remaining silent would be a greater violation of my responsibility to science.” (WB-02).

Personal and professional frustration

Many participants reported long-standing exposure to research misconduct and persistent institutional inaction. Whistleblowing became their viable means to challenge systemic failures. Many recounted approaching officials before reporting cases to public or editorial boards, but authorities dismissed or ignored their concerns. Research vice-chancellors were reportedly reluctant—or in some cases unable—to engage with whistleblowing processes or ethics committees of institutions where alleged offenders held executive roles. One participant explained: “The evidence was clear, but months passed without any meaningful response from the institution. I eventually went directly to the journal.” (WB-09). These accounts indicate potential gaps in institutional preparedness and variability in commitment to upholding research integrity principles.

Elements of retaliatory and strategic whistleblowing

A minority acknowledged that interpersonal conflicts or academic rivalries urged them to report. Some Iran-affiliated whistleblowers described receiving threats from implicated researchers or unidentified sources, which deterred them to continue their efforts. Four participants reported that they did not start any new whistleblowing after threats or retaliations. A whistleblower acknowledged: “I admit that part of my motivation was a long-standing rivalry with the senior author, but the evidence of data fabrication was real, and I could not close my eyes forever.” (WB-14). Another whistleblower stated that “those research team were already tried to communicate with a XX journal and retract our collougue s paper” and additionally noted that “we tried to retaliate.“(WB-10). These findings suggest the role of retaliatory pressures and institutional constraints in shaping whistleblowing behavior.

Perceived ineffectiveness of ethics committees

Nearly all participants (17 out of 18; 94.4%) expressed dissatisfaction with Iran’s institutional ethics committees, citing procedural delays, lack of transparency, and absence of meaningful protections for complainants. Participants generally perceived these committees as ineffective and potentially biased when addressing allegations of scientific misconduct. Across participant accounts, a common theme was that: whistleblowing operates as a mechanism to protect scientific integrity, yet it unfolds within a high-pressure academic environment characterized by professional risks, institutional limitations, and competing personal motives.

Perspectives of editorial boards and editors-in-chief

All participating editors had processed at least three retractions involving Iran-affiliated authors between 2014 and 2024, providing a substantial experiential basis for their observations. Interviews of ten editorial board members and four editors-in-chief revealed a coherent set of themes regarding whistleblowing practices, editorial responses, and the handling of Iran-affiliated misconduct cases.

Theme 1: High dependence on whistleblowers for detecting misconduct

Editors reported that, in their experience, no retraction had been initiated without a formal whistleblower report, suggesting a central role for whistleblowing in detecting image manipulation, peer-review fraud, authorship disputes, or other forms of misconduct that often elude automated tools. Despite the geopolitical sensitivities associated with Iran-affiliated submissions, all ten editors unanimously reported that investigating such cases did not demand disproportionate resources. One editor noted: “Without whistleblowers, we would miss 80% of the serious misconduct cases – especially image manipulation and data fabrication.” (EB-04). As an editor pointed out, “many cases of allegations from Iran are a serial type reports, like a flood”; (Eb-03): this observation may indicate recurring patterns in the reporting of scientific misconduct. This perception is consistent with previously reported serial retraction cases involving Iran-affiliated publications, although the present qualitative study was not designed to quantify such patterns. Editors described managing whistleblower reports from Iran as generally consistent with their standard investigative workflows, requiring neither additional staff time nor procedural modifications.

Theme 2: Difficulty assessing whistleblower motives and prevalence of retaliatory reporting

Editors highlighted that distinguishing legitimate whistleblowing from those driven by interpersonal conflict was persistently difficult. Nine out of 10 editors reported receiving allegations concerning Iran-affiliated researchers that they believed were retaliatory or driven by competition. This incidence suggests a recurring editorial challenge: receiving claims with mixed or ambiguous motivations versus maintaining rigorous due diligence. Editors-in-chief perceived that whistleblowing among Iran affiliated researchers was frequently motivated by personal rivalries and institutional tensions, although they acknowledged that distinguishing this from ethical concerns remained difficult. Several whistleblowers have reported experiencing various forms of threats from the ethics committee subsequent to the initial proffering of their allegations. Such findings apparently underscore the necessity for alternative educational and cultural capacity-building initiatives targeted at committee members, in order to equip them with the requisite competencies to address such matters in a more appropriate and professional manner. Importantly, all four editors indicated that university ethics committees play little role in managing or coordinating whistleblowers and the whistleblowers’ reports.

Theme 3: Cultural and institutional blind spots

Participants were consistently challenged by their limited knowledge of Iran’s academic and institutional structures. Editorial boards reported that unfamiliarity with local hierarchies, authorship customs, and institutional bureaucracy increase the risk of overreaction (e.g., unnecessary retractions) and underreaction (e.g., inadequate investigations). All ten editors emphasized that the absence of transparent institutional frameworks in Iran complicates the verification of allegations. Editors reported that universities frequently provided inconsistent documentation or nontransparent statements, which several editors perceived as reflecting limited institutional cooperation. The editors maintained that this opacity hinders fair and thorough assessment of misconduct cases.

Theme 4: Retractions trigger substantial institutional and political backlash

All editors mentioned that retractions involving Iran-affiliated authors provoked significant external pressure. All ten editors reported receiving formal complaints from affiliated institutions, allegations to the journal’s impartiality or reputation, and in some cases, political appeals contesting the legitimacy of their decisions. Therefore, retractions of Iran-affiliated articles are rarely treated as routine editorial corrections; rather, they frequently escalate into institutional or political disputes, complicating the enforcement of publication ethics.

Theme 5: Measures and gaps related to safeguarding whistleblowers

Despite acknowledging the risks that challenge whistleblowers in Iran, all ten editors confirmed that their journals do not implement any special protections for Iran-affiliated whistleblowers beyond standard confidentiality policies. These accounts suggest the possibility of a structural gap in existing publication ethics procedures for supporting whistleblowers working in high-risk environments.

Theme 7: Interaction dynamics among whistleblowers, editors, and authors

Participants described retractions as commonly following three stages. External whistleblower’s alert, often anonymous, is followed by editorial reviews of evidence using image-forensics software or plagiarism detectors. Finally, formal retraction notices are issued without authors’ voluntary involvement. Participants reported persistent gaps in communication and mistrust among whistleblowers, editors, and authors through the stages. Such dynamics contribute to procedural opacity, heightened tension, and delayed resolutions.

Discussion

This study was fundamentally designed to reinforce adherence to ethical principles in the drafting and publication of research articles among Iranian medical university-affiliated authors. We empirically examined whistleblowing and retractions within the Iranian politically sensitive academic environment. Participants’ accounts suggest that research integrity was perceived to be upheld largely through external mechanisms. Guided by the study design—to elucidate whistleblowers’ motivations and to analyze how editors evaluate and action allegations involving Iran-affiliated papers— our findings suggest that participants perceived important vulnerabilities affecting both the occurrence of research misconduct and the processes through which allegations were addressed.

Whistleblowers as perceived safeguards within a weak institutional oversight system

Participants across all groups perceived whistleblowers as playing an important role in identifying and correcting problems within Iran-affiliated scientific publications. Participants reported that whistleblower alerts frequently contributed to the identification of cases that subsequently resulted in article retractions. Fear of prosecution, professional retaliation, and personal harm hindered whistleblowers’ reporting of misconduct. Politically sensitive cases magnified the fear factor and contributed to high withdrawal during the study. Participants’ accounts suggest that limitations in domestic and international safeguards may expose whistleblowers to continued vulnerability [21]. This paradox may reflect, according to participants’ accounts, limitations in existing governance and institutional oversight mechanisms. Our findings align closely with those of Jackson et al. [22], who described whistleblowing as an ethical responsibility undertaken despite considerable personal and professional risks. Similar to the nurses in their study, our participants reported fear of retaliation, professional isolation, and organizational resistance after raising concerns. These parallels suggest that fear and institutional silence are persistent features of whistleblowing across both healthcare and academic settings. However, our findings also extend Jackson et al.‘s work by demonstrating how these challenges are intensified in environments with weak research integrity governance. Whereas the nurses in Jackson’s study operated within established reporting systems, our participants frequently encountered ineffective or absent institutional oversight and therefore sought assistance from international journal editors. Our findings suggest that international journals may increasingly assume responsibilities that participants perceived as insufficiently addressed by local oversight mechanisms. Consequently, whistleblowers in our context face not only the universal risks associated with speaking out but also additional legal, professional, and political vulnerabilities arising from limited institutional support reported by participants.

Ambiguous motivations and “retaliatory” whistleblowing

While participants’ motivations varied, many were ethically committed to scientific integrity and patient safety, whereas others reacted to institutional neglect or professional injustice. These insights echo global patterns, confirming that whistleblowing is rarely driven by a singular moral impulse but is shaped by personal and relational dynamics. However, the high prevalence of perceived retaliatory whistleblowing reported by editors (90%) may complicate editorial assessment of allegation credibility. Editors frequently struggled to distinguish bona fide evidence-based reports from those driven by personal rivalry, competitive tension, or interpersonal conflicts. The editors-in-chief reported that whistleblowers’ reports emerged largely where contested authorship practices, hierarchical research structures, and internal tensions existed. Our findings on ambiguous motivations gain additional insight when juxtaposed with the comparative study by Mansbach et al. [23], which examined willingness to blow the whistle among experienced nurses (n = 83) and nursing students (n = 82) in a healthcare context. Several convergent and divergent patterns emerge. First, both studies identify the protection of others—patients in clinical settings and scientific integrity in academic contexts—as a central driver of whistleblowing behaviour. In Mansbach et al., participants responded to vignettes involving misconduct that could endanger patient safety [23]. Similarly, participants in current study consistently framed reporting as a moral obligation to safeguard scientific integrity and, by extension, patient welfare. This parallel suggests that the ethical imperative to prevent harm extends across professional domains. Second, both studies underscore the moderating role of professional experience, albeit in different ways. Mansbach et al. reported that nursing students perceived misconduct as less severe than experienced nurses, yet were more willing to report both internally and externally. In contrast, our findings indicate a more nuanced pattern: while experienced whistleblowers demonstrated greater awareness of the severity and consequences of misconduct, this awareness was frequently accompanied by heightened fear of retaliation, which in some cases inhibited reporting. Rather than reflecting idealism, willingness among less experienced individuals may therefore reflect fewer perceived institutional risks, whereas experience may introduce caution in politically or institutionally sensitive environments. Third, organizational context appears to play a critical role in shaping whistleblowing behaviour. Mansbach et al. identified professional experience as a significant predictor of whistleblowing intentions in multivariate models. Extending this, our findings suggest that in the Iranian academic context, the effect of experience is mediated by fear of legal and professional repercussions, a dimension less salient in the healthcare setting examined by Mansbach et al. This highlights the importance of context-sensitive protective structures for potential whistleblowers operating under institutional constraint. Collectively, these studies suggest that while the moral impulse to report misconduct may be broadly shared, the translation of this impulse into action is strongly shaped by professional experience and organizational environment. Our findings extend those of Mansbach et al. [23] by illustrating how governance fragility and institutional opacity intensify perceived risks, transforming whistleblowing from an act of professional responsibility into a potentially high-cost decision. These findings suggest an ethical tension arising from the coexistence of legitimate concerns about research integrity and the possibility of mixed personal motivations. Whistleblowers’ reports may reveal misconduct while also introducing challenges for fair investigative processes. These findings suggest that journals should focus on evidence-based investigative procedures capable of evaluating allegations regardless of the whistleblower’s underlying motivations.

Participants’ perceptions of institutional limitations and externalized enforcement of research integrity

Our findings suggest that participants frequently perceived institutional responses as inadequate. In this study, whistleblowers characterized national ethics committees as ineffective, opaque, or nonfunctional, while editors reported that Iranian institutions hindered rather than facilitated investigations. Participants perceived these experiences as reducing local accountability. Whistleblowers often bypassed national unresponsive avenues and appealed directly to international editorial boards. Editors reported being required to adjudicate cases without contextual clarity, often encountering institutional non-cooperation and political pressures without local input. Our findings suggest that establishing a formal oversight channel under the Office of the Vice President for Research within the relevant ministries may help address this governance gap by providing a more structured mechanism for receiving and investigating allegations of research misconduct. This pattern, as perceived by participants, suggests that international editorial boards were often perceived as playing a more active role in responding to allegations of research misconduct than local institutional mechanisms. While these observations are limited to the experiences of the present sample, they may help inform future discussions on strengthening institutional oversight.

Implications for strengthening editorial and institutional integrity systems

Based on the perspectives of participants in this study, several participant-informed implications may help inform discussions on strengthening global and national research integrity frameworks. First, it may be prudent for international editorial boards to develop standardized, evidence-based evaluation protocols for whistleblowers’ reports. Such protocols could help mitigate the risks arising from allegations with mixed or ambiguous motives, while maintaining rigorous due diligence. Enhanced anonymity and protection measures might also be considered for whistleblowers who operate in high-risk geopolitical contexts, where legal or professional retaliation is perceived as a significant threat. Within the Iranian academic context, our findings suggest that participants perceived the absence of independent and transparent research integrity mechanisms as an important challenge. Establishing such mechanisms may therefore warrant further consideration as one possible approach to strengthening local accountability. Formal whistleblower safeguards may further reduce insecurity and the fear-based underreporting reported by participants. Additionally, reducing hierarchical and opaque authorship structures could help address some of the interpersonal and professional grievances that appear to contribute to conflict-driven whistleblowing. At the global level, context-sensitive editorial training might reduce misinterpretations of allegations that originate from culturally or politically distinct academic environments. Finally, it may be worth considering that high retraction counts, when viewed in isolation, could reflect underlying systemic weaknesses—such as inadequate institutional oversight—rather than solely indicating publishing or editorial failures, or individual authorial malpractices. These implications emerge from the qualitative accounts of our participants and are offered as interpretative suggestions, rather than as empirically validated policy recommendations. They are intended to inform ongoing discussions on research integrity, particularly in settings where institutional protections for whistleblowers remain limited and where governance structures face substantial challenges.

Toward a more equitable system of research integrity

Ultimately, our findings suggest that participants perceived research integrity depends heavily on external editorial oversight rather than consistently trusted local institutional mechanisms. In this context, whistleblowing was often viewed as a mechanism that depended more on individual moral commitment and external pressures than on consistently trusted institutional structures. Participants described an environment in which weak organizational support, concerns about retaliation, and inconsistent institutional responses discouraged reporting, while the visibility of journal policies, publication requirements, and international research integrity standards provided important external incentives for accountability. Although these observations reflect participants’ experiences within the Iranian medical research context and should not be generalized beyond similar settings, they highlight the importance of strengthening institutional mechanisms that protect whistleblowers, ensure fair and transparent investigations, and foster a culture in which research integrity is supported by both organizational commitment and individual ethical responsibility. These findings may also inform discussions in other settings where institutional protections for reporting research misconduct remain limited, while recognizing that the transferability of qualitative findings depends on contextual similarities rather than statistical generalization [24].

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations. The sample was limited to 18 whistleblowers and 14 editorial stakeholders, which may not capture the full diversity of experiences within Iran or internationally. The high withdrawal rate may have introduced selection bias, as individuals who withdrew could have differed systematically from those who completed the study. Although data saturation was achieved among the remaining participants, this attrition may nonetheless have limited the diversity of perspectives captured, particularly from individuals who perceived greater personal or professional risks associated with whistleblowing or who held more complex or divergent experiences. Participants may also have under- or over-reported certain motivations because of social desirability or concerns about potential repercussions, despite assurances of confidentiality and voluntary participation. Furthermore, because participants were recruited through purposive and professional network-based sampling within the specific context of Iranian medical research, the findings should be interpreted as context-specific and may have limited transferability to other institutional, cultural, or national research environments with different research governance structures, whistleblowing mechanisms, or sociopolitical contexts. In addition, because the qualitative analysis was based on detailed interview records expanded immediately after each interview rather than verbatim audio transcripts, some nuances of participants’ wording or emphasis may not have been fully preserved. Accordingly, the findings and implications should be interpreted as participant-informed, context-dependent insights rather than empirically validated policy recommendations.
