Synergistic phyto-formulated microemulsion for improved collagen deposition and healing of infected wounds
Authors: Rutuja Gumathannavar, Arti Londhe, Manoj Kadam, Nidhi Sapre, Medini Dingre, Atul Kulkarni, Santosh Koratkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic and infected wounds pose a major clinical challenge due to delayed healing, persistent inflammation, and impaired tissue regeneration. The present study aimed to develop and evaluate a phyto-based Microemulsion (Phy-ME) composed of potato peel powder, Himalayan Curcumin powder, neem oil, and Aloe Vera gel, focusing on its physicochemical stability, cell viability, antibacterial activity and wound-healing efficacy. The formulation was characterized using UV–visible spectroscopy, FTIR, zeta potential, and FE-SEM, confirming stable particle formation (− 33.8 mV) with uniform dispersion and compatible phyto-constituents. In vivo studies were conducted on BALB/c mice with full-thickness excision wounds infected with Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. The Phy-ME-treated groups, particularly at 250 µg/mL, exhibited accelerated wound closure (> 90% by day 14), a reduced bacterial load, and improved granulation tissue formation compared to the controls. Histopathological analysis revealed dense collagen deposition, fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis, and complete epithelialization in Phy-ME-treated tissues. The combined phyto-constituents exhibited synergistic antibacterial, antioxidant, and regenerative properties, promoting rapid wound recovery. This study demonstrates that Phy-ME serves as a biocompatible and sustainable formulation capable of improving collagen re-modeling and wound recovery, supporting its potential translation into therapies for chronic and infected wounds.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Wound healing is a complex, multi-stage biological process involving haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodelling phases that restore tissue structure and function following injury. Chronic and infected wounds, however, remain a significant global health challenge due to delayed healing, persistent inflammation, and recurrent bacterial infections. These wounds impose a substantial socioeconomic burden, especially among diabetic, burn, and trauma patients. Bacterial colonization and biofilm formation within the wound microenvironment led to chronic infection, tissue necrosis, and impaired epithelialization, severely hindering natural repair mechanisms.

A variety of bacteria, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative species such as Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumonia, are commonly implicated in wound infections. These pathogens form biofilms that create a physical barrier to antimicrobial agents and host immune cells, prolonging inflammation and impeding healing. Compounding the issue is the growing global threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), AMR is among the most critical public health concerns of the twenty-first century, responsible for nearly 5 million deaths annually and projected to reach 10 million deaths per year by 2050. The excessive and often inappropriate use of antibiotics in wound management has accelerated the emergence of resistant strains, making many conventional therapies ineffective. This pressing challenge highlights the urgent need for biocompatible, non-antibiotic, and multifunctional wound-healing agents that can simultaneously promote tissue regeneration and control bacterial infection.

In this context, microemulsions (MEs) have gained considerable attention as advanced nano-carrier systems for topical and transdermal drug delivery. MEs are thermodynamically stable, isotropic mixtures of oil, water, and surfactant often with a co-surfactant forming nanosized droplets (10–100 nm). Their unique physicochemical characteristics, such as low interfacial tension, high solubilisation capacity, optical transparency, and ease of preparation, make them ideal for delivering both hydrophilic and lipophilic agents [1]. The nanoscale droplet size enhances drug penetration through the stratum corneum, increases local bioavailability, and provides sustained release at the target site [2] Moreover, microemulsions maintain a moist wound environment, which is essential for cellular migration, granulation tissue formation, and epithelialization [3, 4].

Several studies have demonstrated the advantages of MEs in wound healing, which enhance the absorption of poorly soluble bioactive compounds, promote oxygen diffusion, and provide a protective barrier against secondary infections. Additionally, MEs can improve the stability, bioactivity, and controlled release of natural compounds, overcoming the limitations associated with conventional ointments and gels. Importantly, their biocompatibility and minimal irritation potential make them suitable for sensitive or damaged skin, a critical factor in wound care formulations [5, 6].

Recent advances in microemulsion (ME)-based wound healing systems demonstrate strong potential for accelerating tissue regeneration and managing bacterial infections, particularly when natural components such as essential oils and plant-derived bioactives are incorporated. Microemulsions, due to their nanometer-sized droplets, thermodynamic stability, and high solubilizing ability, enhance drug penetration through biological membranes and facilitate controlled delivery to wound sites. Over the last 5 years, numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of these systems. For example, researchers developed a tea tree oil-based ME that effectively inhibited bacterial growth and biofilm formation in burn wounds, showing the synergy between natural antimicrobial oils and ME nanostructures [7]. Likewise, researchers used a continuous microemulsion with Melaleuca essential oil, showing strong antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and aiding in wound healing in vivo. Further concept developed with a eugenol-based ME strengthened by silver nanoparticles, resulting in enhanced antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects highlighting how combining natural compounds with nanocomposites can improve therapeutic outcomes [8, 9]. Another innovative strategy by involved co-encapsulating fusidic acid (an antibiotic) and benzocaine (a local anesthetic) in a microemulsion, achieving better wound healing and higher antibacterial effectiveness than traditional creams, emphasizing the versatility of MEs in delivering multiple agents simultaneously [3, 10]. Supporting these practical studies, investigated incorporating glucocorticoid-loaded MEs into bacterial cellulose dressings, creating an anti-inflammatory wound dressing that offers controlled drug release and excellent skin compatibility [11].

Although several herbal microemulsion-based formulations have been investigated for wound healing, most studies have focused on the delivery of a single phytoconstituent or a limited combination of herbal extracts. In contrast, the present study reports the development of a surfactant-free phyto-based microemulsion incorporating Himalayan curcumin, neem seed oil, aloe vera, and potato peel extract, each contributing complementary antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-regenerative properties. The formulation was designed to enhance the stability and topical delivery of multiple bioactive phytoconstituents while avoiding conventional synthetic surfactants. Furthermore, its therapeutic potential was systematically evaluated through physicochemical characterization, cytocompatibility, antioxidant activity, antibacterial efficacy, and in vivo wound-healing studies using infected wound models, providing a comprehensive assessment of its potential as a natural wound-healing platform [12–15].

Combining herbal bioactive with MEs offers a promising strategy to harness the therapeutic properties of plant extracts while benefiting with nanoscale delivery. Curcumin (Curcuma longa), rich in Curcumin, possesses strong anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties that enhance collagen synthesis and tissue remodelling. Neem oil (Azadirachta indica) provides broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, while Aloe Vera accelerates epithelialization, angiogenesis, and fibroblast proliferation. Potato peel extract, an underutilized natural source, is rich in phenolic compounds and glycoalkaloids, which possess antioxidant and regenerative properties that support granulation tissue formation. Previous studies have shown that these phytochemicals, either individually or in combination, promote faster wound closure, reduce microbial burden, and modulate inflammatory responses.

Despite the proven bioactivity of these ingredients, their integration into a single microemulsion formulation capable of providing synergistic wound healing and antimicrobial effects has not been extensively explored. The present study aimed to develop and evaluate the phyto based microemulsion (ME) composed of potato peel powder, Himalayan Curcumin powder, neem oil, and Aloe Vera gel, to investigate its physicochemical properties and in vivo wound-healing efficacy. This work introduces a novel, biocompatible, and eco-friendly herbal microemulsion platform that offers dual therapeutic action, antimicrobial defence and tissue regeneration without relying on synthetic surfactants or antibiotics.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) of Symbiosis School of Biological Sciences with study approval no. SSBS/IAEC/07–2024. All experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the standard operating procedures and guidelines approved by the IAEC and the Committee for the Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA).

Plant materials and chemicals

Glycerin I.P. was procured from Paras Chemical Industries (Pune, India). Nutrient Broth and Aloe Vera powder (100% purity) were obtained from Hi-Media and Hi-Fi Company (Rajasthan, India), respectively. Neem oil was purchased from Cheminova Agro Tech (Nashik, India), and Himalayan Curcumin was sourced from the local market in Sikkim, India. Organically grown potatoes were obtained directly from a local organic farmer and used fresh for peel processing. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Milli-Q water was utilized throughout the study.

Nutrient Broth was purchased from Himedia, and standard Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 2079) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (NCIM 2200) was purchased from NCIM Pune. 96-well flat bottom plates, micro tips (10µL, 200 µL & 1000 µL), centrifuge tube (15 mL & 50 mL) procured form Tarson.

Synthesis of phyto based microemulsion Phy-ME

The microemulsion was synthesized using a spontaneous emulsification protocol. The ingredients included potato peel powder, Himalayan Curcumin powder, neem oil, and Aloe Vera gel. Various formulations were standardized by adjusting the proportions of these four constituents in different ratios, with the optimized combination being 1:1:3:2 (potato peel powder: Himalayan Curcumin powder: neem oil: Aloe Vera gel). For preparation, the powdered ingredients and neem oil were mixed with a glycerine–Milli-Q water solution (1:1 v/v) to serve as the aqueous phase. Glycerine was incorporated as a co-solvent and stabilizing agent to improve the dispersion of hydrophilic and lipophilic phytoconstituents. In addition, the naturally occurring amphiphilic phytochemicals present in the formulation facilitated self-assembly and stabilization of the oil–water interface, eliminating the requirement for conventional synthetic surfactants or co-surfactants. The resulting mixture was then homogenized at room temperature using a probe sonicator operating at 40% amplitude for 2 h. This process ensured the uniform dispersion of both hydrophilic and lipophilic constituents, resulting in a stable microemulsion system without the need for synthetic surfactants. The synthesized Phy-ME was transferred into airtight, sterile containers and stored at 4 °C until further use in physicochemical characterization and biological evaluation experiments.

Formulation optimization

Initially, six formulations with different ratios of Himalayan turmeric powder, neem seed oil, aloe vera powder, and potato peel powder were prepared and screened based on their physical appearance, homogeneity, stability, ease of dispersion, and cytocompatibility. The formulation containing Himalayan turmeric powder: neem seed oil: aloe vera powder: potato peel powder in the ratio 2:1:0.5:0.5 exhibited the most suitable characteristics for in vitro biological evaluation and was selected for subsequent in vitro studies. Following these preliminary investigations, the formulation was further optimized for topical wound application by increasing the proportion of aloe vera and potato peel powder to improve viscosity, spreadability, hydration, and retention at the wound site. Consequently, the formulation ratio 1:1:3:2 was selected for the in vivo wound-healing studies.

Construction of the pseudo-ternary phase diagram

The pseudo-ternary phase diagram was constructed using the aqueous titration method. Different combinations of Himalayan turmeric powder, neem seed oil, aloe vera powder, and potato peel powder were prepared at predetermined ratios, followed by the gradual addition of distilled water under continuous magnetic stirring at room temperature. The phase behavior of each formulation was assessed visually. Formulations exhibiting a clear, homogeneous, isotropic, and single-phase appearance without visible phase separation or precipitation were considered to fall within the microemulsion region, whereas turbid or phase-separated systems were excluded. A total of six formulations were initially screened, and the optimized formulation was selected based on physicochemical stability, homogeneity, ease of dispersion, and cytocompatibility. Physicochemical characterizations

The UV–visible spectra of the samples were recorded using a JASCO V-750 spectrophotometer, while FTIR analysis was performed on a Shimadzu IR Affinity instrument in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻1 to identify functional groups involved in Phy-ME stabilization. The zeta potential (ZP) was measured using an Anton Paar Litesizer 500 equipped with a 658 nm red diode laser, calibrated with a polystyrene standard. The morphology of the samples was examined using a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM; JSM-7600F). Cellular morphology and wound images were captured using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Nikon Eclipse TS100), and the percentage of wound closure was analysed using VImage 2016 software.

Antibacterial activity studies

Phy-ME was evaluated for its antibacterial activity against the Gram-positive bacterium Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 2079) and the Gram-negative bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa (NCIM 2200) using the broth microdilution method. The antibacterial activities of the individual components (potato powder, Aloe vera powder, curcumin powder, and neem oil), along with the Phy-ME formulation, were assessed to determine their minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), defined as the lowest concentration that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth. Two-fold serial dilutions of Phy-ME were prepared from an initial concentration of 1000 µg/mL to obtain concentrations of 1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.2, 15.6, and 8 µg/mL. The assay was performed in sterile 96-well microplates by mixing log-phase bacterial cultures (OD595 = 0.1) with the respective concentrations of Phy-ME or the individual control components in triplicate, along with a negative control containing bacterial culture alone. The plates were incubated at 37 °C under shaking conditions, and bacterial growth was monitored spectrophotometrically at 595 nm every hour for 9 h using a Bio-Rad microplate reader. The average optical density values obtained during the incubation period were used to determine the MIC of Phy-ME and the control formulations [16].To determine the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), aliquots from wells showing no visible bacterial growth at the MIC and higher concentrations were plated onto Mueller–Hinton agar and incubated for an additional 24 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration that completely prevented bacterial colony formation.

Cell viability studies

The cytotoxicity of the Phy-ME was evaluated using Vero cells. Vero cells were cultured at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) medium supplemented with 10% Foetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. A microemulsion stock solution (1 mg/mL) was prepared and subsequently diluted in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics to obtain a concentration range from 500 to 3.92 µg/mL.

The MTT assay was conducted as described by Huang et al., 2016, using 96-well flat-bottom plates. Cells (1 × 104 per well) were cultured with 100 µL of growth medium and incubated under optimal conditions until a confluent monolayer formed. After incubation, the growth medium was replaced with 100 µL of the test microemulsion solution in separate wells, and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well, followed by incubation in the dark for 4 h. Then, 100 µL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a Multiskan plate reader (Thermo Scientific) [17]. Wound healing analysis (scratch-assay)

Vero cells were plated in six-well plates and cultured to 100% confluence in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The cell surface of the confluent cells was scraped manually with the help of a 100 µl pipette tip. The cells were washed once with DMEM media containing 10% FBS and cultured with 62, 125 and 250 µg/mL concentrations of the Microemulsion and observed for the effectiveness on lesion healing effect for different time period of 0 h, 24 h and 48 h. The cell images for healing activity were captured using inverted phage-contrast microscope (Nikon) at the various time points mentioned. The total amount of wound closure was calculated using VImage 2016, software. All the experiments were done in triplicates and repeated thrice [2].

In vivo study of wound healing

In vivo experiments were conducted on 36 BALB/c mice (4–8 weeks old) obtained from the Tata Memorial Centre, Advanced Centre for Treatment, and Research & Education in Cancer (ACTREC). Prior to and during the experiments, animals were housed in a group of 6 mice in individually ventilated cages under controlled environmental conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, temperature 22 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity 50–60%). Mice had free access to standard rodent pelted feed and portable water ad-lib.

Wound creation and bacterial inoculation

Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (50 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg). The dorsal region of each mouse was shaved and disinfected using 70% ethanol. A full-thickness excisional wound of 6 mm diameter was created on the dorsal surface on both the flank region using a sterile surgical biopsy punch. The wounds were then inoculated with a 50 µL suspension of Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a specific bacterial cell count of 108 CFU/mL. The inoculation was allowed to establish infection before treatment administration [17].

Wound assessment

Each treatment group consisted of three BALB/c mice, with two independent full-thickness excisional wounds created on the dorsal region of each animal, resulting in six wound sites per treatment group. Wound healing was evaluated longitudinally by measuring the wound area on Days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 21, allowing repeated assessment of wound contraction and healing progression throughout the study while minimizing animal usage in accordance with the 3Rs principles.

The progression of wound healing was evaluated by measuring the wound area on the 1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, and 14th day post-wounding. Measurements were obtained using a digital Vernier calliper to ensure precision and accuracy. The wound margins were carefully traced, and the mean wound diameter was calculated in millimetres. From these values, the wound area (mm2) was determined.

The percentage of wound contraction was calculated by comparing the wound area at each time interval with the initial wound area on day 0. Bacteriological analysis

To assess the bacterial load present at the wound site, samples were collected using sterile cotton swabs under aseptic conditions on predetermined observation days. Each swab was carefully rolled over the wound surface to ensure a representative sampling of the wound exudate and surrounding tissue. The collected swab samples were then transferred into sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and thoroughly mixed using a vortex mixer to remove and suspend the adhered bacteria, forming a homogeneous bacterial suspension. Subsequently, serial dilutions of the suspension were prepared in sterile PBS. From each dilution, 20 µL was aseptically spotted onto nutrient agar plates. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions, allowing for colony development. After incubation, the number of viable bacterial colonies was counted. The results were expressed as colony-forming units per millilitre (CFU/mL) of sample, providing a quantitative measure of bacterial burden in each treatment group. This analysis enabled comparison of the antibacterial efficacy of the different treatments by evaluating the reduction in bacterial load over time relative to the infected control groups.

Histological analysis

The mice were euthanized at 14th and 21st days’ time interval and skin tissues were collected for histopathological analysis. The mice were euthanized using overdose of CO2 asphyxiation. Granulation tissues from the wound area were excised and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h. The tissues were then processed for paraffin embedding, and 5 µm-thick sections were obtained using a rotary microtome. The sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess general tissue morphology, inflammatory cell infiltration, and epithelial regeneration, and with Masson’s trichrome to evaluate collagen deposition and organization. Microscopic examination was performed under a light microscope, and representative images were captured for analysis. Histological parameters such as epithelialization, fibroblast proliferation, collagen formation, angiogenesis, were evaluated. A semi-quantitative scoring system was used to grade each parameter on a scale based on the extent and intensity of tissue response, allowing comparison among the different treatment and control groups.

Histopathological evaluation was performed using a semi-quantitative scoring system by an independent pathologist in a blinded manner. Fibroblast proliferation, collagen formation, angiogenesis, and epithelialization were assessed using a five-point scale: 0 (None), 0.5 (Few), 1 (Moderate), 2 (Many), and 3 (Considerable), corresponding to the qualitative grades of Negative (−), Mild (+), Mild to Moderate (++), Moderate (+++), and Intensive (++++) respectively. Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate biological replicates (n = 3), each performed with technical duplicates. Quantitative results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The p-value was calculated using a Student’s t-test and compared to the control and graphs were made using Origin 9 software and Grph-pad prism. Physicochemical characterization

The physicochemical characteristics of the prepared Phy-ME were analyzed using UV–Visible spectroscopy, zeta potential measurement, FTIR spectroscopy, and Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) as shown in Fig. 1. The UV–Visible absorption spectra exhibited a prominent peak at 420 nm, corresponding to the π–π* electronic transitions of Curcumin derived from Himalayan Curcumin, confirming its successful incorporation into the microemulsion system. Additional absorption bands at 277 nm and 271 nm were attributed to aromatic compounds present in potato peel and Aloe Vera, respectively, while the band near 255 nm indicated the presence of fatty acids characteristic of neem oil. The combination of these peaks confirms the coexistence and compatibility of all bioactive herbal components within the formulated microemulsion. The FTIR spectrum (Fig. 1b) confirmed the presence of multiple functional groups associated with phytoconstituents in the microemulsion. The absorption peak at 918 cm⁻1 corresponds to C=C stretching of a monosubstituted alkene, while the band at 1033 cm⁻1 represents C–O stretching associated with sulfoxide or alcohol groups. The peaks at 1396 cm⁻1 and 1628 cm⁻1 correspond to C=C bonds from conjugated alkenes and O–H bending vibrations of carboxylic acids, indicating contributions from plant-derived polyphenols [20, 21]. A sharp absorption at 2152 cm⁻1 is indicative of C≡N or N=N=N asymmetric stretching, while the band at 2885 cm⁻1 corresponds to C–H stretching in aliphatic chains. A broad peak observed at 3255 cm⁻1 reflects O–H and N–H stretching, typical of alcohols, phenols, and amines present in the herbal ingredients [22–24]. Overall, these findings confirm the successful formulation and structural integrity of the herbal microemulsion. The presence of characteristic peaks corresponding to phytochemicals from Curcumin, neem, potato peel, and Aloe Vera validates their synergistic incorporation, while the stable zeta potential and uniform morphology support the colloidal stability and compatibility of the system for biological applications.

The zeta potential analysis shown in Fig. 1c revealed a surface charge of − 33.8 mV, demonstrating strong electrostatic repulsion between particles, which contributes to the high colloidal stability of the microemulsion. The inset of this figure represents the particle size distribution, corresponding to a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 354 nm, indicating a moderately polydisperse and well-dispersed colloidal system in aqueous medium. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed a mean hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 354 nm with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.268, indicating a relatively narrow particle size distribution and acceptable homogeneity of the formulation. The measured zeta potential of − 33.8 mV further suggests good colloidal stability by providing sufficient electrostatic repulsion between dispersed droplets, thereby reducing the likelihood of aggregation. The combined PDI and zeta potential results indicate that the developed Phy-ME possesses a stable and homogeneous colloidal dispersion suitable for topical application. Morphological evaluation using FE-SEM (Fig. 1c inset) showed that the microemulsion consists of semi-spherical particles with moderate dispersion and minimal aggregation. The size distribution, as depicted in the inset, showed an average particle size ranging from 20 to 25 μm in the dried state, which may be attributed to particle agglomeration during the drying process for imaging purposes.

The presented pseudo-ternary phase diagram in Fig. 1e illustrates the compositional domain of a four-component microemulsion system comprising Aloe Vera extract, Neem oil, Curcumin powder, and Potato-peel powder. Discussion

The present study demonstrates that a phytoconstituent-based microemulsion (Phy-ME) formulated from Himalayan curcumin, neem oil, Aloe vera, and potato peel possesses combined antibacterial, antioxidant, and wound-healing activity. The convergence of the in vitro and in vivo evidence indicates that these effects are not incidental but reflect a genuinely synergistic formulation, in which infection control, oxidative balance, and tissue regeneration act as interlinked rather than independent processes.

Phy-ME displayed broad-spectrum activity against both the gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus and the gram-negative Pseudomonas aeruginosa, with an identical MIC of 31.2 µg/mL for both organisms and complete bactericidal activity at 100 µg/mL for P. aeruginosa and 125 µg/mL for S. aureus. This bactericidal potency compares favourably with previously reported plant-oil-based microemulsions, including tea tree oil and Melaleuca alternifolia systems that achieved biofilm inhibition and antibacterial activity in skin-wound models [9, 29], and appears more potent than a eugenol microemulsion–silver nanocomposite system, which reduced S. aureus and L. monocytogenes viability by only ~ 25–30% [30]. This enhanced activity likely reflects the concurrent contribution of several mechanisms rather than a single active principle: curcumin-mediated disruption of the bacterial membrane, the fatty-acid content of neem oil, and phenolic compounds derived from potato peel. Because this activity arises from multiple, physicochemically distinct mechanisms acting together rather than from a single molecular target, Phy-ME may be comparatively less vulnerable to the single-target resistance mechanisms that constrain conventional antibiotics and antibiotic-loaded microemulsions, such as fusidic-acid-based systems [9, 16] a consideration of particular relevance given the increasing prevalence of antimicrobial-resistant pathogens in chronic wound infections.

Phy-ME exhibited concentration-dependent DPPH radical-scavenging activity, rising from 39 to 60% and approaching, though not matching, that of ascorbic acid at the highest concentration tested. This activity is consistent with the known hydrogen-donating and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging capacity of the polyphenols, flavonoids, and terpenoids contributed by curcumin, Aloe vera, and potato-peel phenolics, and is in line with prior reports that plant-derived microemulsions rely on similar phytoconstituents for ROS neutralisation and cellular protection. This property is mechanistically relevant to wound repair: unresolved oxidative stress is known to impair fibroblast activity and delay collagen maturation, so the antioxidant capacity of Phy-ME may help sustain a favourable redox environment for cell proliferation and tissue remodelling, thereby complementing its direct antibacterial action.

In the scratch assay, Phy-ME-treated cells showed greater wound-gap closure than controls within 24 h, consistent with a pro-migratory effect on keratinocytes relevant to early-phase re-epithelialisation. The increased variability observed at 48 h may reflect a narrowing of the therapeutic window between antimicrobial potency and cytocompatibility at higher local concentrations, rather than a loss of regenerative activity as such; a comparable pattern, in which enhanced migration is accompanied by concentration-dependent cytotoxic thresholds, has been reported for fusidic-acid- and Melaleuca-based microemulsions [31]. Taken together, these observations suggest that Phy-ME supports the early proliferative phase of healing [18], while indicating that optimisation of the effective concentration window warrants further investigation.

The in vitro findings were substantiated in vivo: the 250 µg/mL Phy-ME formulation achieved more than 90% wound contraction by day 14 in both S. aureus- and P. aeruginosa-infected wounds, accompanied by a marked reduction in bacterial load and histological evidence of organised collagen deposition, fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis, and complete re-epithelialisation. The concordance between the antibacterial, antioxidant, and histological readouts supports a model in which the four constituents act through complementary, non-redundant mechanisms: curcumin contributing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity, neem oil providing broad-spectrum antibacterial action, Aloe vera promoting angiogenesis and collagen synthesis, and potato-peel phenolics supporting fibroblast proliferation and epithelial regeneration [24, 32–34]. This multi-target mode of action is consistent with the broader literature on microemulsion-based wound therapeutics, which attributes part of the intrinsic antimicrobial effect of such systems to their nanoscale droplet structure and surfactant interface, independent of any single active ingredient, and with earlier work showing that plant-oil microemulsions can limit sepsis and accelerate healing in animal models [34, 35].

Collectively, these results indicate that Phy-ME integrates antimicrobial defence with pro-regenerative activity within a single, biocompatible nanocarrier, distinguishing it from conventional single-mechanism antibiotic or antiseptic wound dressings. This positions herbal, surfactant-free microemulsions of this type as a plausible platform for managing chronic and polymicrobial wound infections, particularly where antimicrobial resistance limits the utility of standard antibiotic-based therapies. The present findings are nonetheless derived from a single small-animal model and two reference bacterial strains; extending the antimicrobial evaluation to a wider range of clinically relevant pathogens, elucidating the underlying cytokine and growth-factor pathways, and undertaking eventual clinical evaluation will be necessary before the translational potential of Phy-ME can be fully established.

Conclusion

In this study, the Phy-ME formulated with bioactive ingredients such as Curcumin, neem oil, Aloe Vera, and potato peel demonstrated significant therapeutic potential for enhancing wound healing and bacterial clearance in infected BALB/c mice. The microemulsion formulation exhibited strong antibacterial properties against Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as promoting rapid collagen formation, fibroblast proliferation, and epithelial regeneration, thereby accelerating wound closure. The 250 µg/mL ME formulation showed the most promising results in both macroscopic wound closure and histopathological scoring, confirming the synergy between the herbal components. Physicochemical characterization of the Phy-ME, including UV–Visible spectroscopy, zeta potential, and FTIR analysis, confirmed the stability and uniform dispersion of the formulation, further validating its potential as an effective therapeutic system. The long-term colloidal stability of the Phy-ME can be attributed to the synergistic effect of glycerol and naturally occurring amphiphilic phytochemicals, which promote self-assembly at the oil–water interface and provide both steric and electrostatic stabilization. The negative zeta potential (− 33.8 mV) indicates sufficient electrostatic repulsion between droplets, minimizing aggregation and contributing to the observed physical stability of the formulation. The findings suggest that this herbal ME could serve as an effective alternative to conventional antimicrobial and wound healing treatments, offering a natural solution to managing chronic, infected wounds. The present study has several limitations. The wound-healing efficacy of the phyto-based microemulsion was evaluated only in a BALB/c mouse model, which may not fully replicate the complexity of chronic or infected wounds in humans. Although the formulation demonstrated significant antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, its antimicrobial spectrum was not assessed against a broader range of clinically relevant pathogens or multidrug-resistant strains. In addition, the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced wound healing, including inflammatory cytokine modulation, angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and growth factor expression, were not investigated. Long-term stability, skin permeation, pharmacokinetic behavior, and dermal toxicity studies were also beyond the scope of this work. Although semi-quantitative histopathological scoring was performed to assess collagen formation, fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis, epithelial regeneration, and inflammatory cell infiltration, detailed quantitative histomorphometric analyses were beyond the scope of this study. Future investigations should incorporate these analyses to further validate the wound-healing efficacy of the formulation. Furthermore, clinical efficacy and safety in human subjects remain to be established. Future studies should address these limitations through mechanistic investigations, expanded antimicrobial evaluation, long-term safety assessments, and well-designed clinical trials to facilitate the translational development of this herbal microemulsion for wound management.
