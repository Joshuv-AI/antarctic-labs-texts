Pycnodysostosis With Papilledema and Isolated Low Parathyroid Hormone Levels in an Eight‐Year‐Old Girl: A Genetically Confirmed Case Report
Authors: Meghana Krishna Kesineni, Naveen Jagadish Kandregula, Sameer Kumar Majety, Yulia Alekseevna Pimenova, Bhargav Kadali, Vineeth Potluri, Prudhvi Lekkala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPycnodysostosis is a rare autosomal recessive skeletal dysplasia resulting from pathogenic variants in the CTSK gene, which encodes cathepsin K, a lysosomal cysteine protease expressed in osteoclasts. Deficiency of this enzyme leads to defective bone resorption and generalized osteosclerosis. We report an 8‐year‐old girl, the firstborn of a third‐degree consanguineous marriage, who presented with poor height and weight gain since birth and delayed developmental milestones, including independent walking at 2 years of age. Physical examination revealed proportionate short stature with microcephaly and brachycephaly. Craniofacial findings included frontal bossing, beaked nose, low‐set posteriorly rotated ears, micrognathia and a high‐arched fissured palate with dental crowding and supernumerary teeth. Thickened palmar skin, pectus carinatum, and saddle toe deformity were also seen. Fundoscopic examination demonstrated bilateral papilledema. Radiographic evaluation showed diffuse cranial sclerosis, wormian bones, hypoplastic clavicles, acro‐osteolysis, and generalized increased bone density. Endocrine assessment revealed isolated low parathyroid hormone levels, an exceptionally rare association. Molecular genetic testing by clinical exome sequencing (CES) identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in exon 6 of the CTSK gene, establishing the diagnosis of pycnodysostosis. The patient was initiated on recombinant growth hormone therapy and referred for multidisciplinary follow‐up, including endocrinology, dentistry, orthopedics, and ophthalmology. This case underscores both classical manifestations and unusual findings such as papilledema and low PTH levels. Early diagnosis through careful clinical evaluation and genetic confirmation enables timely initiation of appropriate interventions. Growth hormone therapy demonstrated consistent improvement in linear growth, emphasizing the potential for better long‐term outcomes. A coordinated multidisciplinary approach remains essential to enhance prognosis and overall quality of life in children with this rare skeletal dysplasia.Keywords: craniofacial abnormalities, CTSK gene, pectus carinatum, pycnodysostosis, short stature

Introduction

Pycnodysostosis was first described in 1962 by Maroteaux and Lamy as a disorder of osteoclast dysfunction leading to osteosclerosis [1]. It is also known as “Toulouse‐Lautrec Syndrome,” named after the French artist Henri de Toulouse‐Lautrec, who was believed to have the condition [1]. The disorder affects both sexes equally, with an incidence of 1.7 per million, and is associated with parental consanguinity in approximately 30% of cases [2]. Pycnodysostosis results from homozygosity or compound heterozygosity for biallelic pathogenic variants in the CTSK gene, which encodes cathepsin K, a lysosomal cysteine protease expressed in osteoclasts that degrades bone matrix and collagen [3]. Deficient cathepsin K activity leads to impaired bone resorption and generalized osteosclerosis.

This rare skeletal dysplasia, a subtype of osteopetrosis, is characterized by short stature and craniofacial abnormalities such as open sutures, wormian bones, frontal and occipital bossing, hydrocephalus, blue sclera, beaked nose, visual or hearing abnormalities, mandibular hypoplasia, and abnormal dentition. Other findings may include hypoplastic clavicles, brachydactyly, acro‐osteolysis, increased bone fragility, sleep apnea, and osteomyelitis [4]. Diagnosis is based on characteristic clinical and radiographic features, while CTSK gene sequencing by clinical exome sequencing (CES) confirms the diagnosis [5].

Treatment is mainly supportive, including growth hormone therapy, orthopedic care, and dental management. Prognosis is generally good with appropriate multidisciplinary follow‐up [5]. We report a rare case of pycnodysostosis in an 8‐year‐old girl born to third‐degree consanguineous parents, presenting with classic skeletal abnormalities, bilateral papilledema, and isolated low parathyroid hormone levels. Notably, early initiation of recombinant growth hormone therapy resulted in improved linear growth. This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of pycnodysostosis, highlighting its uncommon endocrine and ophthalmologic associations and emphasizing the importance of early, multidisciplinary management.

Case History/Examination

An 8‐year‐old girl, the firstborn of a third‐degree consanguineous marriage, was brought by her parents for evaluation of severe short stature and skeletal abnormalities noted since early childhood. There was a history of poor height and weight gain from birth, and she had consistently remained the shortest in her peer group. Developmental milestones were delayed, with independent walking achieved at 2 years of age. Birth history revealed a full‐term, forceps‐assisted vaginal delivery following prolonged labor, complicated by meconium aspiration requiring neonatal intensive care for 7 days. She was exclusively breastfed for 2 years, with weaning initiated at 11 months. On examination, the child was alert, with preserved cognitive and scholastic performance. Her weight was 12.5 kg, height 99.5 cm, BMI 12.6 kg/m2, and upper‐to‐lower segment ratio was 1.12. Arm span measured 91 cm, and mid‐parental height was 148.5 cm. Head circumference was 47 cm, consistent with microcephaly. She exhibited proportionate short stature without signs of chronic systemic illness. General physical examination revealed microcephaly, brachycephaly, frontal bossing, beaked nose, low‐set posteriorly rotated ears, pectus carinatum, short hands and feet and saddle toe deformity (Figure 1a). Intraoral examination showed a high arched fissured palate, dental crowding, and a double‐row/pseudo‐supernumerary dentition appearance, likely reflecting retained deciduous teeth with concurrent eruption of permanent teeth (Figure 1b). The extremities exhibited brachymetacarpia and brachymetatarsia, with thickened skin over the palms and soles (Figure 1c,d). No hearing or visual deficits were noted. Radiographic evaluation revealed classical features of pycnodysostosis. Skull X‐ray showed diffuse sclerosis of the cranial vault with wormian bones in the lambdoid region (Figure 2a). Chest X‐ray revealed hypoplastic clavicles, more pronounced laterally (Figure 2b). Radiographs of the hands and feet showed generalized increased bone density, acro‐osteolysis of the terminal phalanges, and shortening of the metacarpals and metatarsals (Figure 2c,d). Fundus evaluation demonstrated bilateral papilledema. A prior CT scan of the brain revealed sclerosis and thickening of the cranial bones and axial skeleton, with thickening of the parietal bones and no intracranial mass lesion, hydrocephalus, or other intracranial abnormality. Given the absence of structural abnormalities on imaging and parental preference to avoid invasive evaluation, lumbar puncture with opening pressure measurement and other invasive assessments for raised intracranial pressure were not performed. The patient was therefore monitored conservatively with planned serial ophthalmologic follow‐up and clinical surveillance for symptoms suggestive of intracranial hypertension. Endocrine evaluation showed a low parathyroid hormone level (PTH 7.7 pg/mL) with normal serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, magnesium, and 25‐hydroxyvitamin D. Given the absence of hypocalcemia or hyperphosphatemia, this biochemical profile was interpreted as an isolated low PTH rather than established hypoparathyroidism, and repeat biochemical monitoring was planned. Based on clinical, radiological, and familial findings, pycnodysostosis was suspected. Genetic analysis was performed using clinical exome sequencing (CES). The analysis identified a homozygous missense variant in the CTSK gene associated with Pyknodysostosis.

The detected variant was NM_000396.4:c.436G > C, which results in the amino‐acid substitution p.Gly146Arg at the protein level. This variant is located in exon 6 of the CTSK gene and affects the mature catalytic domain of cathepsin K, an enzyme essential for osteoclast‐mediated bone resorption.

The variant was detected in the homozygous state, consistent with the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern of pyknodystosis. The genetic testing methodology used was clinical exome sequencing.

This variant has been previously reported in individuals with pyknodysostosis and is documented in public genetic variant databases, including ClinVar, where it is classified as pathogenic according to ACMG criteria. The p.Gly146Arg substitution affects the catalytic domain of cathepsin K and has been associated with impaired osteoclast‐mediated bone resorption. Although multiple pathogenic variants in the CTSK gene have been described, this variant represents a recurrent pathogenic variant reported in affected individuals, supporting its clinical significance in pyknodysostosis.

Differential Diagnosis

Pycnodysostosis shares overlapping clinical and radiological features with several sclerosing bone dysplasias. A key differential is osteopetrosis, which also presents with generalized osteosclerosis. However, osteopetrosis typically includes hematological complications such as anemia and pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly due to extramedullary hematopoiesis, and cranial nerve compressions from narrowed neural foramina [6]. In our patient, hematological parameters were normal, and there were no hepatosplenomegaly or neurological deficits, making osteopetrosis unlikely. Cleidocranial dysplasia was also considered, given the hypoplastic clavicles and dental crowding with a pseudo‐supernumerary (double‐row) appearance; however, cleidocranial dysplasia lacks the diffuse skeletal sclerosis observed in our patient's radiographs, effectively ruling it out [6]. However, cleidocranial dysplasia lacks the diffuse skeletal sclerosis observed in our patient's radiographs, effectively ruling it out [6]. Idiopathic acro‐osteolysis can mimic the terminal phalangeal resorption seen in pycnodysostosis but lacks systemic osteosclerosis and the craniofacial dysmorphism seen in our case [7]. Although acro‐osteolysis was present in our patient, the additional findings of increased bone density, short stature, and craniofacial anomalies confirmed the diagnosis of pycnodysostosis. Other conditions such as skeletal fluorosis, Paget's disease, myelosclerosis, acromegaly, and certain skeletal malignancies may cause increased bone density or bone deformities [6]. However, these were excluded based on the absence of systemic signs and symptoms, normal biochemical markers, and the specific radiographic findings seen in our patient.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow up)

The patient was initiated on recombinant growth hormone therapy at a dose of 0.3 mg subcutaneously at night, alongside nutritional counseling focused on a high‐protein diet. The family was counseled on the nature of the disorder and the importance of long‐term multidisciplinary care, and genetic counseling was provided. Over follow‐up, linear growth was sustained, with height increasing from 99.5 cm at baseline to 108 cm at 19 months and weight increasing from 12.5 to 18 kg (Table 1). Ophthalmologic follow‐up at 6 months showed persistent bilateral papilledema without appreciable interval change, and continued observation was advised. PTH was reassessed at 6 months and 12 months and remained low despite otherwise normal mineral parameters; further serial PTH testing was deferred at family preference given clinical stability and satisfaction with growth response. In the absence of clinical features suggestive of raised intracranial pressure and with unremarkable intracranial imaging, conservative monitoring was continued, with ongoing multidisciplinary follow‐up.

Discussion

Pathogenic variants in the cathepsin K (CTSK) gene on chromosome 1q21, which codes for an enzyme necessary for the osteoclastic breakdown of bone matrix proteins, result in pycnodysostosis, a rare autosomal recessive skeletal dysplasia [2, 5]. There have been fewer than 200 cases recorded globally since Maroteaux and Lamy first characterized it in 1962 [2, 8]. It usually manifests in early life as skeletal anomalies, increased bone fragility, short stature, and craniofacial dysmorphism. In addition to the typical clinical and radiological characteristics, our patient, who was born to third‐degree consanguineous parents, also exhibits uncommon abnormalities such as papilledema and isolated low parathyroid hormone levels.

Papilledema in pycnodysostosis is uncommon but has been reported in association with intracranial hypertension secondary to craniosynostosis, despite pycnodysostosis classically featuring delayed suture closure. In published cases, raised intracranial pressure and papilledema have been linked to premature fusion of cranial sutures requiring neurosurgical or craniofacial intervention in some patients [9, 10]. Importantly, papilledema may occur even when vascular imaging does not demonstrate dural sinus obstruction; in one report, MR venography was unremarkable while lumbar puncture confirmed elevated opening pressure [11]. In our patient, CT imaging showed cranial osteosclerosis without hydrocephalus or mass lesion, and invasive intracranial pressure assessment was declined; given persistent papilledema on follow‐up, she will remain under serial ophthalmologic surveillance with a low threshold for further neuroimaging and reconsideration of intracranial pressure evaluation if symptoms or visual findings progress.

Isolated low PTH level in our patient occurred in the setting of normal calcium, phosphate, magnesium, 25‐hydroxyvitamin D, and alkaline phosphatase, and therefore does not fulfill the typical biochemical definition of chronic hypoparathyroidism, which is characterized by hypocalcemia with low or inappropriately normal PTH [12, 13]. In this context, potential explanations include transient or functional suppression of PTH secretion or assay variability; however, current evidence does not support a consistent association between pycnodysostosis and true hypoparathyroidism. Prior endocrine observations in pycnodysostosis have described altered calcium‐regulatory hormone dynamics (e.g., calcitonin responses), suggesting that mineral homeostasis may be variably perturbed in some individuals [14]. Given persistently normal mineral parameters on serial monitoring, management remains conservative with continued surveillance and escalation of evaluation if symptoms or biochemical derangements emerge.

Generalized osteosclerosis and brittle bones are the results of the pathogenic mechanism, which involves poor collagen breakdown brought on by deficient cathepsin K activity [2, 5]. There have been reports of isolated cases brought on by de novo mutations, even though the majority of cases are inherited in an autosomal recessive fashion. Although it is still uncommon and needs more research, our patient's low parathyroid hormone suggests that there may be a larger endocrine component to this illness [6, 8].

Short stature, brachycephaly, micrognathia, dental crowding with an apparent increased tooth number, hypoplastic clavicles, acro‐osteolysis, and chest abnormalities are some of the defining characteristics. Given the patient's age (8 years), the apparent increased tooth number may reflect mixed dentition with retained deciduous teeth and eruption of permanent teeth, producing a pseudo‐supernumerary/double‐row appearance rather than true supernumerary teeth [15]. To broaden the clinical range, our case additionally showed papilledema, thickened palmar skin, and saddle toe deformity [16]. In accordance with the conventional radiographic pattern, radiographs verified wormian bones, acro‐osteolysis, diffuse cranial sclerosis, and generalized increased bone density. These results aided in distinguishing the condition from idiopathic acro‐osteolysis, osteopetrosis, and cleidocranial dysplasia. Although patients are at risk for recurring fractures, dental issues, and comorbidities including obstructive sleep apnea and, less frequently, neurological manifestations like elevated intracranial pressure, the prognosis is generally favorable in terms of life expectancy [5, 16]. Multidisciplinary management is still crucial. The patient in our example saw continuous height gain, demonstrating the effectiveness of growth hormone therapy, which has been shown to promote linear growth in affected youngsters [17]. While dental, ophthalmologic, and sleep medication therapies assist address other issues, orthopedic follow‐up is necessary for skeletal monitoring and fracture prevention. It is essential that impacted families receive genetic counseling.

This instance emphasizes how crucial it is to identify pycnodysostosis early on using radiological and clinical signs, then confirm it genetically. The uncommon discoveries of papilledema and parathyroid hormone insufficiency contribute to our expanding understanding of disease diversity. The positive reaction to recombinant growth hormone emphasizes how important prompt action is. Long‐term, comprehensive multidisciplinary care can greatly enhance these patients' quality of life and results.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, invasive evaluation for raised intracranial pressure, including lumbar puncture opening pressure, was not performed because the family declined further invasive testing in the setting of unremarkable intracranial imaging and clinical stability. Therefore, elevated intracranial pressure could not be objectively confirmed or excluded. Second, although an isolated low PTH level was identified and rechecked at 6 and 12 months, further serial PTH monitoring was deferred at parental preference, which limited longitudinal biochemical characterization. Third, growth response to recombinant growth hormone was documented with serial anthropometric measurements; however, standardized deviation scores (SDS or Z‐scores) were not available at all follow‐up time points, limiting formal quantification of SDS change over time. Finally, as a single case report, the findings, particularly the coexistence of papilledema and isolated low PTH, cannot establish causality or generalizability.
