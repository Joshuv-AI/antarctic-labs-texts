Switching from Premixed Insulin to Insulin Degludec/Insulin Aspart for the Management of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: Implications of a Real-World Study on Insulin Degludec Dosing
Authors: Yiming Wu, Junqing Zhang, Ang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhen switching from premixed insulin to insulin degludec/aspart (IDegAsp), IDegAsp usually starts at the same dose as the premixed insulin according to limited clinical experience or at a dose according to clinician discretion. The dose of insulin degludec used in the real world after switching has been poorly investigated.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients with type 2 diabetes who switched from premixed insulin to IDegAsp from October 2016 to December 2023. Repeated measures analysis of variance was used to compare changes in insulin dose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and postprandial blood glucose (PBG) before and after switching.ResultsSixty-six patients with prior low-ratio premixed insulin and 22 with prior mid-ratio premixed insulin were included. Among the low-ratio insulin users, the total daily dose of insulin degludec (IDeg) decreased by 21.43% and 19.05% at 3 and 6 months, respectively, after switching, compared with prior basal insulin dose (both p < 0.001). Conversely, among mid-ratio insulin users, the IDeg daily dose increased by 10.71% and 32.14% at 3 and 6 months, respectively, after switching, compared with prior basal insulin dose (both p < 0.001). In all patients, HbA1c levels decreased by 0.70%, FBG decreased by 1.00 mmol/l, and PBG decreased by 1.61 mmol/l after 6 months of switching (all p < 0.05); the total daily insulin dose and injection frequency significantly decreased after switching (both p < 0.05); age and disease duration did not affect IDegAsp effects on HbA1c reduction.ConclusionsIn the setting of transition to IDegAsp from premixed insulin, the dose of basal insulin in the premixed formulation can be a valuable reference for adjusting insulin degludec dose. IDegAsp is superior to premixed insulin in blood glucose control with reduced total daily dose and injection frequency. IDegAsp could be the best choice for the management of diabetes in elderly patients.Keywords: Insulin degludec dosing, Insulin degludec/insulin aspart, Premixed insulin, Real-world study, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Global diabetes prevalence has been increasing. It was estimated that there were over 500 million people with diabetes worldwide in 2021; this number was projected to exceed 1 billion by 2050 [1]. Insulin is an important part of diabetes treatment, and optimal insulin regimens are crucial for improving blood glucose control and reducing complications [2].

Premixed insulins, consisting of three formulations, namely low-, mid-, and high-mix [3], are widely used in clinical practice due to their convenience [4, 5]. In China, only low- and mid-mixes containing rapid- and intermediate-acting insulins are available. Premixed insulin has certain limitations, e.g., the shoulder effect (the overlapping effect of rapid-acting and intermediate-acting components) and insufficient acting duration [4]. Insulin degludec/insulin aspart (IDegAsp; Novo Nordisk, Denmark) overcomes these shortcomings. This formulation contains 70% ultra-long-acting insulin degludec and 30% rapid-acting insulin aspart. Insulin aspart provides a blood glucose-lowering effect at mealtime, while insulin degludec provides a stable, long-acting blood glucose-lowering effect [4]. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials and real-world studies has shown that IDegAsp significantly reduces blood glucose levels, insulin doses, and hypoglycemic events compared with premixed insulins [6–13].

Currently, there are only expert opinions but no guidelines on the clinical use of IDegAsp; the expert opinions focus on IDegAsp use in special populations, dose timings relative to meals, and IDegAsp intensification [14]. Insulin degludec dose after switching from different formulations of premixed insulins has been poorly studied. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the real-world dose of insulin degludec after switching from premixed insulin in Chinese patients with type 2 diabetes with the hope that our findings could assist clinicians in optimizing the use of IDegAsp.

Methods

Patients

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking University First Hospital (approval number: 2024 Yan 240-001) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (identifier: ChiCTR2400085570). Informed consent of the patients was waived by the Ethics Committee due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients managed under the “in-person remote” diabetes self-management support model at the Peking University First Hospital were included in this single-center, retrospective, single-arm cohort study. The multidisciplinary team of the support model consists of diabetes specialists, diabetes education nurses, diabetes care practitioners, and dietitians who provide patients with offline as well as online disease self-management education and support as described elsewhere [15].

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was diagnosed according to the guidelines for the prevention and treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus in China [16]. Inclusion criteria included: (1) patients with T2DM registered at the Diabetes Clinic of the Peking University First Hospital from October 2016 to December 2023; (2) aged ≥ 18 years at the time of registration; (3) the treatment was switched from premixed insulin or its analogs to IDegAsp; and (4) both premixed insulin and IDegAsp had been used for ≥ 6 months over a period of two standard follow-ups with one at 3 months and the other at 6 months after switching. Exclusion criteria were: (1) lack of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) data before, at 3 months or at 6 months after switching; (2) patients with other metabolic diseases (e.g., hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism); and (3) patients with severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (e.g., myocardial infarction and stroke) or malignant tumors.

Data Collection

Patients’ data, including age, sex, weight, body mass index, disease duration, comorbidities (hypertension, hyperlipidemia), doses and frequencies of premixed insulin and IDegAsp, HbA1c, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and postprandial blood glucose (PBG) before and 3 and 6 months after switching were collected and analyzed. Low-ratio premixed insulins included recombinant human isophane insulin (30R), insulin aspart 30, and protamine zinc insulin lispro (25R), while the mid-ratio premixed included recombinant human isophane insulin (50R), insulin aspart injection 50, and protamine zinc insulin lispro (50R).

Statistical Analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). A normality test was conducted on the quantitative data. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while data not normally distributed were presented as median (first quartile, third quartile). Categorical data were expressed as number (percentage) and analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. HbA1c, FBG, and PBG levels at different time points were analyzed using one-way repeated measures analysis of variance. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the type of insulins (low- and mid-ratio premixed), age, and disease duration using two-way repeated measures analysis of variance. Age and disease duration were divided into subgroups using the automatic binning method in SPSS with equal intervals. HbA1c were categorized into four subgroups (≤ 7%, > 7% and ≤ 8%, > 8% and ≤ 9%, and > 9%). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and Baseline Characteristics

A flow chart for patient screening is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 99 patients who switched from premixed insulin to IDegAsp and met the study criteria were identified and included for analysis. The average age of all patients was 65.38 ± 8.13 years; 57 (57.58%) patients were male and 42 (42.42%) were female. The average weight and body mass index were 70.12 ± 11.03 kg and 25.71 ± 4.43 kg/m2, respectively. The average disease duration was 19.10 ± 8.20 years, with 64 (64.65%) and 75 (75.76%) of cases having hypertension and hyperlipidemia, respectively; 77 patients (77.78%) used a low-ratio premixed insulin before switching, while 22 (22.22%) used a mid-ratio premixed insulin. The median baseline HbA1c, FBG, and PBG before switching were 7.75% (7.30%, 8.93%), 8.20 (7.00, 9.40) mmol/l, and 10.50 (8.98, 12.03) mmol/l, respectively (Table 1).

Changes in HbA1c and Blood Glucose after Switching

Of the 99 patients, 78 had HbA1c results while 47 had FBG and PBG data at all time points, namely before switching and at 3 and 6 months after switching (Table 2). It was found that HbA1c, FBG, and PBG levels at 3 and 6 months after switching to IDegAsp significantly decreased (all p < 0.001, Table 2). We divided patients into subgroups according to age or disease duration, no significant differences in HbA1c levels were observed among subgroups (Table 2).

Changes in Insulin Doses and Injection Frequency

Of the 99 patients, 88 had insulin dose data at all time points (Table 3). The total daily doses of IDegAsp at 3 and 6 months after switching were 0.46 (0.35, 0.62) U/kg and 0.49 (0.39, 0.62) U/kg, respectively, both significantly lower than 0.57 (0.42, 0.75) U/kg of premixed insulins (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the doses of insulin degludec at 3 and 6 months after transition were significantly lower than those of intermediate-acting insulins (Table 3). Of interest, when patients were divided into subgroups according to the formulations of premixed insulins, we found that the daily dose of insulin degludec decreased by 21.43% and 19.05% at 3 and 6 months, respectively, compared with that of intermediate-acting insulins in patients treated with the low mix (Table 3, p < 0.001). On the contrary, the daily dose of insulin degludec increased by 10.71% and 32.14% at 3 and 6 months, respectively, compared with that of intermediate-acting insulins in the mid-mix group (Table 3, p < 0.001). When patients were divided into four subgroups according to HbA1c levels before switching, insulin degludec dose changes were not significant among the subgroups (Table 3). After switching, 13 patients transitioned from twice-daily premixed insulin to once-daily IDegAsp (p < 0.001), while the remaining patients maintained the same number of daily injections, i.e., twice daily.

Discussion

In this study, we report the following findings: (1) transition from premixed insulin to IDegAsp resulted in substantially decreased levels of HbA1c, FBG, and PBG in patients with T2DM; (2) the total daily dose of IDegAsp was significantly lower than that of premixed insulin; (3) age and disease duration did not affect IDegAsp effects on HbA1c reduction; and (4) patients with prior low-mix insulin took a significantly lower dose of insulin degludec while patients with prior mid-mix insulin took a markedly higher dose of insulin degludec than their respective intermediate-acting insulin doses.

IDegAsp is superior to premixed insulin in glycemic control [9–13]. We demonstrated that switching from premixed insulin (low-mix and mid-mix as well) to IDegAsp led to significantly lower HbA1c, FBG, and PBG levels, supporting previous findings [9–13]. In the present study, we were unable to evaluate the effect of IDegAsp on hypoglycemia due to incomplete data. Other studies have revealed that patients with type 2 diabetes have fewer hypoglycemia episodes after switching from premixed insulin to IDegAsp [9, 10, 12].

Several studies have looked into the change in total daily insulin dose after the transition to IDegAsp from premixed insulin in patients with T2DM with discordant reports [9–13, 17]. Kang et al. revealed no significant differences in total daily insulin dose before vs. after switching [11]. In contrast, in a real-world, prospective, non-interventional study, Fulcher et al. found that the total daily insulin dose was significantly reduced in adults with T2DM after switching to IDegAsp from premixed insulin [9]. A retrospective cohort study also showed decreased the total daily insulin dose in patients with T2DM after switching to IDegAsp from premixed insulin [10]. Such a finding has been documented by other groups [12, 13]. Taken together, the majority of literature data along with ours demonstrated that switching to IDegAsp from premixed insulin decreased total daily insulin dose. In the present study, we also observed a reduction in insulin injection frequency in a significant number of patients after switching, in agreement with the data reported in a recent study [13].

Blood glucose control in elderly patients with T2DM with long disease durations has always been a clinical challenge. In view of cognitive ability decline, comorbidities, polypharmacy, and high risk of hypoglycemia in elderly patients, the Chinese guidelines for the management of diabetes mellitus recommend the “simple treatment concept” and “de-intensified treatment strategy” for this specific group of patients [18]. In the present study, we discovered that age and disease duration did not affect IDegAsp effects on HbA1c reduction. Therefore, IDegAsp, which has a high safety profile and a low risk of hypoglycemia, appears to be more suitable for elderly patients with diabetes who are particularly prone to hypoglycemia [19].

Although IDegAsp shows advantages over other insulin formulations, there are no guidelines on IDegAsp dose to be used after transitioning from premixed insulin [14]. It is suggested that IDegAsp should usually be started at the same unit dose and injection frequency as the premixed insulin according to limited clinical experience or at a dose according to clinician discretion [20]. Data on the dose of insulin degludec used in the real world after switching are lacking. In the present study, we compared basal insulin doses in patients with T2DM after switching from premixed insulin to IDegAsp and revealed some novel findings: the total daily dose of insulin degludec at both 3 and 6 months after switching was significantly lower than that of intermediate-acting insulin taken by low-ratio premixed insulin users; conversely, the total daily dose of insulin degludec at both 3 and 6 months after switching was significantly higher than that of intermediate-acting insulin taken by mid-ratio premixed insulin users. To our knowledge, this is the first description of insulin degludec dose relevant to that of basal insulin in premixed formulations. This data provided a valuable reference for adjusting insulin degludec dose when switching from premixed insulin to IDegAsp.

This study had some limitations. First, the number of patients was small, limiting the analyses of other parameters, such as the use of other types of hypoglycemic medications. Second, as a single-arm, single-center study, there was a potential for selection bias. Last, changes in body weight and hypoglycemia risk were not analyzed due to incomplete data. Multicenter, prospective, real-world studies should be conducted in the future to validate our findings.

Conclusions

IDegAsp is superior to premixed insulin in blood glucose control with reduced total daily dose and injection frequency. IDegAsp could be the best choice for the management of diabetes in elderly patients due to its lower risk of hypoglycemia. When transitioning to IDegAsp, the dose of intermediate-acting insulin in the premixed formulation may serve as a useful reference for adjusting the insulin degludec dose. However, caution is advised due to the small sample size, particularly for the prior mid-ratio group.
