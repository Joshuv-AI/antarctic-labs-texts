Utilizing fNIRS to investigate the impact of calligraphy training on cognitive and upper limb motor function in stroke patients: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yang Liao, Yiyan Wang, Qingqing Dai, Yang Yang, Jingjing Yuan, Yingying Wang, Fengxia Wang, Daoqian Gao, Ye Chen, Lingyu Liu, Haiping Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke is a leading cause of long-term disability, often resulting in cognitive and upper limb motor impairments that significantly impact activities of daily living. While conventional rehabilitation approaches have shown effectiveness, challenges such as high cost, limited accessibility, and poor adherence hinder their widespread implementation. Calligraphy training has emerged as a promising rehabilitation strategy, offering a multimodal intervention that integrates motor and cognitive processes. However, the underlying neural mechanisms and the combined effects of calligraphy training on cognitive and motor recovery remain underexplored. This study aims to investigate the effects of calligraphy training on cognitive function and upper limb motor recovery in stroke patients.MethodsA single-center, open-label, three-arm design, randomized controlled trial will be conducted at Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital. A total of 93 subacute stroke patients will be randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three groups: the calligraphy training group, the reading control group, or the conventional rehabilitation control group. The intervention group will receive a four-week calligraphy training program alongside routine rehabilitation, while the control group will undergo standard rehabilitation alone. Assessments will be conducted at baseline (T0) and post-intervention (T1). The primary outcome measure is cortical activation changes as assessed by functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), with oxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHbO) levels serving as an indicator of brain activity. Secondary outcomes include cognitive function, upper limb motor function, hand dexterity, activities of daily living, grip strength, muscle strength, and muscle tone. Statistical analyses will be conducted using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to compare pre and post-intervention outcomes, adjusting for baseline values.DiscussionThis study provides empirical evidence regarding the efficacy of calligraphy training in improving cognitive function and upper limb motor skills in stroke patients. By utilizing fNIRS to monitor dynamic neural changes, this research will deepen our understanding of the neurophysiological mechanisms behind calligraphy-based rehabilitation. The findings may inform the development of cost-effective, accessible, and culturally relevant rehabilitation strategies, thereby bridging the gap between clinical and home-based recovery.Trial registrationchictr.org.cn ChiCTR2500095411. Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide [1], often resulting in significant impairments in both motor and cognitive functions [2, 3]. These impairments severely affect patients’ ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs) and substantially reduce their overall quality of life. While conventional rehabilitation therapies—such as constraint-induced movement therapy and computerized cognitive training—demonstrate efficacy in controlled environments, their real-world implementation is often faced with considerable challenges [4, 5]. These challenges include a high reliance on specialized equipment, limited ecological validity, and poor adherence in home-based rehabilitation settings, an issue highlighted by the WHO Rehabilitation 2030 initiative [6]. This disparity underscores the urgent need for cost-effective interventions that effectively bridge the gap between clinical and family-based rehabilitation paradigms.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in non-traditional therapeutic activities, such as handwriting training, within the field of neurorehabilitation [7]. Calligraphy training stands out as an intervention that engages both motor and cognitive processes, offering a unique approach to rehabilitation [8, 9]. Recognized for its potential to promote both motor and cognitive recovery, calligraphy training involves complex motor-cognitive demands, making it an effective method for enhancing functional outcomes in stroke rehabilitation [10].

Calligraphy training presents a distinctive, multimodal intervention, incorporating visual-spatial processing, fine motor control, and working memory engagement [11, 12]. These features align with the “cognitive-motor interference” theory, which posits that motor and cognitive functions are interdependent and should be rehabilitated in tandem after a stroke [13, 14]. Notably, while reading training—another language-related activity—also involves visual symbol processing and working memory, it lacks the fine motor component essential for writing. This distinction makes reading training an ideal active control, as it matches the cognitive load while excluding the motor component, thereby allowing for the specific assessment of motor-cognitive coupling in calligraphy training. Previous studies have demonstrated that Chinese character writing training activates a wide range of neural networks related to motor and cognitive functions [15, 16]. Specifically, the fine motor skills required for calligraphy stimulate upper limb motor functions, while the memorization and processing of character structures activate cognitive functions such as attention, memory, and executive control [11, 17]. However, several key limitations persist in the current literature: (1) previous research has primarily focused on the isolated effects of calligraphy training on either motor or cognitive function, lacking integrative studies on the combined motor-cognitive networks; and (2) investigations into the neural mechanisms underlying these effects have been predominantly confined to static measurements in controlled laboratory environments, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which fail to capture the dynamic changes in brain activity that occur during training.

To address these gaps in knowledge, the present study employs a single-center, open-label, randomized controlled trial to systematically assess the synergistic effects of Chinese character writing training on cognitive and upper limb motor functions in stroke patients. A three-arm design is implemented, comprising a calligraphy group, a reading group, and a conventional rehabilitation group. In addition, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is incorporated to construct brain network connectivity maps during task performance, providing a network neuroscience perspective to explore the neural mechanisms induced by the training. This study aims to evaluate the effects of Chinese calligraphy training on cognitive function and upper limb motor recovery in stroke patients, with a specific focus on neurophysiological mechanisms.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study will be a randomized, open-label, prospective, single-center clinical trial with three parallel arms, conducted at Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital. A total of 93 patients in the subacute phase of stroke will be recruited. Participants will be randomly assigned to either the calligraphy training, reading training, or control group in a 1:1:1 ratio. Calligraphy training intervention will be carried out for a total of 4 weeks. The trial procedure is outlined in Fig. 1. The protocol adheres to the SPIRIT 2013 statement [18].

Participants

To be eligible for inclusion in the trial, participants must meet the following criteria: they should be diagnosed with a first-ever stroke through neuroimaging assessment, be over 18 years of age, and be in the subacute stroke stage (7 days to 6 months post-onset). They should exhibit mild to moderate cognitive impairment, with a Montreal Cognitive Assessment score (MoCA) between 10 and 26 [19], and be capable of sitting without support. Additionally, participants must have no significant impairments in comprehension, hearing, or vision.

Exclusion criteria include a history of other neurological disorders or upper limb surgery, end-stage conditions involving severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal failure, malignancies with a life expectancy of 6 months or less, or obvious shoulder pain [20]. Participants meeting any of these exclusion criteria will not be enrolled.

The dropout criteria for this study include: participants who experience significant complications, such as severe shoulder pain or fatigue; those whose disease progression interferes with the study protocol; individuals who voluntarily withdraw for any reason; and participants who engage in another intervention trial or independently enroll in a different rehabilitation program during the study period.

Recruitment

Participants for this single-center clinical trial will be recruited from Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital. Researchers will inform patients about the trial during the hospital admission process, provide detailed information, and allow sufficient time for decision-making. Interested patients may contact a research assistant to undergo screening for trial eligibility.

Informed consent

If patients are willing and eligible to participate in the study, participants will be asked to sign a written informed consent form. The informed consent process will ensure that patients understand that participation is voluntary, with enrolled individuals retaining unrestricted termination rights throughout the investigation period. Consent acquisition will be executed under the supervision of the designated principal investigator.

Randomisation and blinding

Participants will be assigned a unique study number in sequence at registration. Randomization to the three groups, in a 1:1:1 ratio, will be determined using a computer-generated random number table. An investigator not involved in the trial will perform the randomization. The allocation sequence will be kept concealed in opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. Upon recruitment, participants will be assigned to either the intervention or control group by selecting one of these envelopes. The study is open-label, with blinding applied solely to the outcome assessor and data analyst.

Interventions

In the control group, participants will receive routine rehabilitation therapy and care. Therapeutic measures will be carried out by occupational therapists, physiotherapists, etc., and will include task-related dexterity exercises, such as strength training, finger stretching, and activities of daily living training components.

As the active control group, participants in the reading group will incorporate reading exercises into their regular rehabilitation regimen. The reading materials will be selected based on patient interests and will include health science books, short essays, and novel excerpts to stimulate cognitive function without engaging in writing-related motor training. The total daily reading duration will be 30 min. If a patient is unable to complete the session in one sitting, it will be divided into three 10-min sessions. Reading will take place in a quiet, comfortable room with adequate, soft lighting to minimize distractions and prevent eye strain. A researcher will be present throughout the session to ensure adherence to the program, maintain patient focus, and facilitate comprehension. When difficulties arise, the researcher will provide appropriate assistance and encouragement to enhance patient confidence and motivation. Reading progress and effectiveness will be regularly assessed, with records kept on session duration, comprehension levels, and patient feedback.

Participants in the calligraphy training group will incorporate additional calligraphy transcription training into their routine rehabilitation therapy. The content of the calligraphy training will be selected based on the participants’ interests, including short essays or daily sentences. Grid-style paper, known as Tian-zi-ge, will be provided for the training (Fig. 2). This specialized paper format is designed for learning and practicing Chinese characters, helping users understand the spatial structure and stroke order of each character. The training volume will be set at 300 words per day. Participants are asked to maintain proper posture while holding the pen and to follow the correct stroke order when writing Chinese characters. They are instructed to sit upright with their feet flat on the floor, and the table and chair height will be adjusted to ensure natural arm positioning during writing. Calligraphy transcription training is conducted individually in a quiet, comfortable room to minimize external distractions. The writing area is well-lit with soft lighting to reduce eye fatigue. The researcher supervises the entire transcription practice to ensure correct posture and writing quality. Patients receive appropriate encouragement and guidance to overcome challenges during transcription. Details such as the time spent and completion of daily training sessions will be recorded. Assessments

In this study, data will be assessed at two measurement points: pre-intervention (T0) and post-intervention (T1). Specifically, T0 refers to the day when patients will be selected for enrollment based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, while T1 refers to the period following the completion of the entire intervention cycle, after all 28 days of training have been completed. During the pre-intervention phase (T0), socio-demographic and clinical characteristics, cognitive function, and upper limb motor function will be collected. In the post-intervention phase (T1), cognitive function and upper limb motor function will be reassessed.

Additionally, changes in cerebral hemoglobin concentration will be measured using an fNIRS device at T0 and T1, indirectly reflecting brain activity levels [21]. A continuous-wave fNIRS system (Nirsmart, Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co, Ltd, China) will be used for assessment (Fig. 4). This system operates at two wavelengths (695 nm and 830 nm) with a sampling frequency of 11 Hz and will be localized according to the international EEG 10–20 system. The oxygenated hemoglobin concentration in the corresponding cortex will be calculated by the Beer-Lambert law. The original light intensity signal changes during motion collected by the fNIR instrumentation, will be exported to HomER2, a fNIR data processing software package developed by MATLAB 2014a (The MathWorks Inc., USA) based on MATLAB, for data preprocessing as follows:

Illustration of fNIRS collection. a Multichannel fNIRS configurations in the international 10–20 system; b corresponding brain regions of interest

a. The transformation of the light intensity signal will be converted to a change in optical density, and then the artifacts in the signal will be identified using the motion artifact detection function and filled in using cubic spline interpolation, followed by filtering to remove physiological noise signals using 0.01–0.2 Hz.

b. Based on the different absorption spectra and assumed scattering constants of oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin, the optical density changes will be converted to the relative changes in the concentration of oxyhemoglobin (ΔHbO) and deoxyhemoglobin (ΔHbR) in the corresponding brain regions using the modified Lambert-Beer law (ΔLambert-Beer law).

c. The present ΔHbO will be selected as an indicator of brain activation changes in this study because changes in oxygenated hemoglobin can more sensitively reflect cortical activation characteristics. According to the channels in the template setup above, the corresponding brain regions will be found, and the average ΔHbO between 5 and 25 s in different brain regions will be calculated separately for subsequent analysis and discussion.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome indicators will include fNIRS results, Montreal Cognitive Assessment Scale scores, and Fugl-Meyer Upper Extremity Motor Function Scale scores [22].

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes will include hand dexterity and coordination (evaluated with the Purdue Pegboard Test) [23], activities of daily living (as measured by the modified Barthel Index) [24], grip strength, muscle strength (assessed by the Manual Muscle Testing), and muscle tone (evaluated with the Modified Ashworth Scale).

Data management and quality control

This study will include research subjects selected strictly according to the inclusion criteria to ensure random group allocation and maintain consistency in baseline characteristics. Prior to the intervention, all research team members and staff involved in the study will undergo standardized training, ensuring uniformity in intervention methods and approaches. This training is designed to prevent quality discrepancies and minimize bias in the intervention. The intervention implementers will be proficient in the specific methods and precautions of the joint exercise program, establishing a trusting relationship with the participants, and adhering strictly to the program’s protocol during implementation.

The learning and instruction phase will be conducted uniformly by rehabilitation therapists and staff to prevent inconsistencies in movement and assessment criteria. During data analysis, to ensure the completeness and accuracy of the collected data, the data collectors will be blinded to group allocation and intervention details to prevent measurement bias. The completeness and accuracy of data collection forms will be checked on-site. Any missing data will be documented, and the time and reasons for missed visits will be analyzed using intention-to-treat methods. All data will be verified and coded, with Epidata Parallel Dual Entry used to ensure accurate data entry. Any abnormal values identified will be promptly verified by the data collector to ensure data accuracy for statistical analysis. These data will be securely managed throughout the entire trial and will be subject to review by quality control experts.

Sample size and statistical power estimation

G*Power 3.1 software (G * Power Software Inc., Kiel, Germany) was used to calculate the required sample size for this study, with the effect size set at 0.4 and alpha at 0.05 [25]. The calculations indicated that a total of 84 participants would be needed to achieve a statistical power of 0.9. To account for potential participant dropout during the intervention, a 10% attrition rate was considered, resulting in a required sample size of 93 participants. Therefore, 31 participants will be allocated to each group.

Missing data

Relevant researchers will contact participants by phone or review electronic medical records to obtain any missing baseline and follow-up data. If a participant does not respond or if the missing data are unavailable, the approach to handling the missing data will depend on the underlying mechanism of the missingness. For instance, missing data may be imputed using methods such as mean imputation or the last observation carried forward. Additionally, sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the trial results. When analyzing the primary outcome, comparative analyses will be performed between complete cases (excluding missing data) and incomplete cases (including missing data).

Safety consideration

The potential adverse effects may include shoulder pain, hand soreness and numbness, muscle strain, among others. After each treatment session, patients will be asked to rate the severity of pain and fatigue on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). All adverse events occurring during the intervention will trigger immediate notification protocols to the primary investigator. The research team will document the adverse events, including their duration, severity, and location. Should severe complications arise that jeopardize intervention continuity, a temporary suspension of trial activities will be enacted, accompanied by the implementation of supportive care measures. The primary investigator will report all adverse events to the institutional review board.

Patient and public involvement

The trial design process will not incorporate direct contributions from patient representatives or community stakeholders. Participants will have access to the study results upon request. Prior to obtaining written consent, comprehensive discussions will be conducted with prospective participants regarding both anticipated therapeutic advantages and possible adverse effects associated with the research.

Trial status

The study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital (approval no. 2024-102). It was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry on 7 January 2025. Recruitment of candidates will be from 2025-01-07 to 2026-02-01.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data, such as gender and literacy level, will be described by frequency and percentage. Continuous data, such as age and scale scores, will be tested for normality. Data conforming to a normal distribution will be expressed as mean ± standard deviation (X̄ ± s), while data not conforming to a normal distribution will be expressed as quartiles (M [P25 to P75]). The normality of the data for both groups will be tested initially. For normally distributed data, a two-sample t-test will be applied, while the Mann-Whitney U test will be used for non-normally distributed data. The effects of the intervention will be analyzed by comparing changes in cognitive function and upper extremity functionality between groups using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) of change scores, with baseline values as a covariate and adjustments for potential confounders. The significance level for all tests will be set at P < 0.05, with correction for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method.

Ethics statement

The study will be conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital (study no. 2024-102, approval date: November 8, 2024). The study was registered at chictr.org.cn on January 7, 2025 (ChiCTR2500095411). The authors will provide recruited patients with detailed information about the study, including its objectives, recruitment criteria, study protocol, potential risks, and expected outcomes. Patients and their guardians will make the final decision regarding enrollment or withdrawal from the study. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants or their legal representatives before the screening process and prior to randomization.

Patients’ identifiable information, along with their clinical information and research data, will be stored separately by one of the authors responsible for patient randomization. To protect patient confidentiality, only the study director, authors, and the ethics committee will have access to the patient’s personal information and medical records. At the conclusion of the study, these data will be discarded three years after the end of the study. The results of this trial will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journal publications and presentations at national or international academic conferences. Participants will not be individually informed of the results.

Conclusion

The key adaptive advantages of calligraphy training lie in its high user acceptability, affordability, flexibility, and practicality. Compared to professional rehabilitation therapies, calligraphy exercises do not require expensive equipment, specialized therapists, or large training spaces, making it a highly feasible and accessible rehabilitation method [26]. Calligraphy training can effectively bridge the gap between professional rehabilitation and daily self-training, enhancing the continuity between the two and allowing patients to engage in exercises independently without the presence of a therapist [27]. This not only promotes sustained participation but also supports long-term rehabilitation outcomes.

Previous studies have suggested that calligraphy training may have potential benefits in improving upper limb function and cognitive function in stroke patients [9, 28]. However, the comprehensive effects and the interactions between these benefits remain unclear. This study aims to establish calligraphy training as a viable rehabilitation method for stroke patients, addressing gaps in professional therapy accessibility. Its affordability, ease of implementation, and potential cognitive and motor benefits make it an appealing adjunct or alternative to conventional rehabilitation. By utilizing fNIRS technology, we also seek to uncover the neural mechanisms behind these potential benefits, thus contributing valuable insights into stroke recovery.
