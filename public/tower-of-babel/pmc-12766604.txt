Fully automated on-scanner aortic four-dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging processing and hemodynamic analysis
Authors: Justin Baraboo, Michael Scott, Haben Berhane, Michael Markl, Ning Jin, Kelvin Chow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFour-dimensional (4D) flow cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is a valuable technique for evaluating cardiovascular hemodynamics, but it requires cumbersome, offline preprocessing and regional segmentation prior to quantifications or visualizations.MethodsThe Framework for Image Reconstruction (FIRE) framework was used to integrate 4D flow processing tasks directly into the scanner reconstruction pipeline. The method builds containerized applications with standardized raw and image CMR data input/output in the open-source Magnetic Resonance Data format. In this study, deep learning models and algorithms from previous work (4D flow pre-processing, three-dimensional [3D] aorta segmentation, aorta velocity maps, quantification of aortic systolic peak velocities) were implemented in TensorFlow and executed within a containerized Python 3.6 environment on the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner, directly following the MRI data acquisition. All tasks were executed in-line using the MRI system's own computational resources. Analysis results were returned alongside the standard 4D flow CMR magnitude/phase images, available for review on-scanner console immediately after the CMR scan. In a study with 20 subjects (n = 10 patients with aortic disease, n = 10 healthy controls), FIRE performance was evaluated and compared to manual 4D flow analysis (reference standard).ResultsWe successfully implemented on-scanner automated 4D flow hemodynamic analysis on a 1.5T MRI system. Total on-scanner computation time for 4D flow analysis was 220 ± 35 s. Dice scores between manual vs deep learning processing (eddy current static tissue selection: 0.84 ± 0.14; noise voxel detection: 0.92 ± 0.04; aortic 3D segmentation 0.92 ± 0.06) demonstrated good to excellent pipeline performance. Bland-Altman analysis revealed a small but significant bias (0.04 m/s, p = 0.01) for peak systolic velocities between manual and deep learning processing with good limits of agreement (−0.10, 0.18 m/s) and a mean relative difference of 4% (0.8/20).ConclusionAn automated 4D flow processing workflow was successfully deployed for fully automated on-scanner hemodynamic analysis with good in-line vs human performance, indicating its potential for increased workflow efficiency.Keywords: 4D flow, Automated analysis, In-line, Deep learning, Aorta

Introduction

Four-dimensional (4D) flow cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) has been shown to be a valuable technique for evaluating altered cardiovascular hemodynamics in aortic disease, including aortic valve abnormalities, dissection, and coarctation [1], [2], [3]. Recent developments in highly accelerated acquisitions with advanced reconstruction techniques such as compressed sensing [4] have brought historically long acquisitions down to under 5 min for aortic imaging [5]. However, broader adoption in clinical practice is still limited by time intensive, manually driven offline post-processing. 4D flow analysis can be cumbersome for novice users and requires specialized training on how to process the data before regional quantification, further limiting the accessibility of this promising technique outside of major academic clinical settings.

The major components of 4D flow analysis include eddy current correction [6], [7], [8], denoising [9], velocity anti-aliasing [10], [11], [12], three-dimensional (3D) segmentation of the vessel of interest (e.g., thoracic aorta), and hemodynamic visualization. While there have been significant advancements in automating these individual tasks through deep learning techniques or algorithmic advances [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], an efficient implementation of the complete collection in a single automated package is lacking. The in-line integration of these tasks on the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner directly following image reconstruction could allow for efficient near real-time 4D flow processing and quality control. The resulting analysis would be in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format, seamlessly integrated into clinical workflows with standard post-processing software and archived with standard imaging data in the Picture Archiving and Communication System. However, modification of an MRI scanner’s reconstruction environment can be technically challenging and requires MRI vendor–specific modifications.

In this study, we utilize a recently developed prototype Framework for Image Reconstruction Environments (FIRE), which enables integration of custom algorithms and deep learning networks with standardized MRI raw and image data input/output in the open-source Magnetic Resonance Data (MRD) format, a standard developed by the International Society for Magnetic Resonance in Medicine (ISMRM) community [18], [19], [20]. These applications are run in a containerized environment on the vendor’s existing computational resources without the need for external hardware or setup. We integrated a series of 4D flow processing tasks previously developed and validated by our group (deep learning–based eddy current correction, noise masking, velocity anti-aliasing, aorta 3D segmentation, algorithms for visualization and quantification of aortic peak velocities) [15], [16], [21], [22], [23] into a complete analysis package that is automatically executed on the scanner with FIRE [24]. We hypothesize that these deep learning networks can be utilized for efficient on-scanner 4D flow analysis that achieves human-level performance.

Methods

4D flow pre-processing, aortic 3D segmentation and hemodynamic analysis

4D flow analysis of the aorta requires multiple processing steps, including pre-processing, anatomical 3D segmentation of the thoracic aorta, and subsequent velocity visualization and quantification. Over the past years, our group has developed a fully automated 4D flow analysis pipeline that is based on a series of deep learning models and traditional algorithms for 4D flow analysis without any manual user interaction. The analysis pipeline includes the following processing tasks: deep-learning–based eddy current correction, noise masking, velocity anti-aliasing, 3D segmentation of the aorta, calculation of an aortic peak velocity maximum intensity projection (MIP), and volumetric quantification of peak systolic velocity. FIRE framework

Fully automated 4D flow MRI processing was implemented on a 1.5T MRI system (Magnetom Sola, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The FIRE framework provides an open interface between the vendor reconstruction pipeline (Image Calculation Environment [ICE] Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) and third-party algorithms using the ISMRM raw and image data (MRD) formats [25]. It is an evolution of the Gadgetron [26] streaming framework, which pioneered the integration of custom C++ code in MRI vendor frameworks. FIRE generalized these concepts to the MRD format itself, enabling other languages, including MATLAB and Python code to be used, and simplifying the rapid integration of deep learning models or other analysis tasks. The MRI vendor imaging reconstruction pipeline consists of a series of processing steps, and FIRE interfaces to custom user algorithms through user-defined “emitter” and “injector” points (Fig. 1). In our implementation, magnitude/phase image data from standard vendor image reconstruction were “emitted” from the ICE pipeline for 4D flow analysis and results were “injected” back into the pipeline for DICOM creation. The emitter converts data to MRD format for custom processing and MRD data converted back into the vendor data format by the injector. Data are communicated between the vendor environment and the user code using standard socket-based Transmission Control Protocol network connections, enabling both local and remote processing.

The FIRE framework allows to create an interface where data can be sent out and back into the vendor image reconstruction pipeline (ICE) via FIRE emitter and injector. Reconstructed magnitude/phase 4D flow images are sent to a containerized Python environment. A 3D phase contrast MRA is calculated before input to turnkey execution of a series of deep learning neural networks (CNNs, outputs above). An aortic velocity MIP is calculated from the velocity data and aortic segmentation and sent back to the image reconstruction pipeline for rapid visualization on the scanner console. In this work, the emitter was inserted near the end of the pipeline, after 4D flow MRI images were reconstructed, including Maxwell term [6] and distortion correction by vendor product algorithms. Subsequently, deep learning 4D flow pre-processing, 3D segmentation, and hemodynamic visualization/quantification tasks (velocity MIP of the thoracic aorta) were performed. This information was overlaid on the magnitude images and used to generate DICOM images with proper header information. These were injected back into the vendor pipeline as color DICOM series and could be displayed on MRI scanner console alongside the reconstructed 4D flow MRI images (Fig. 2).

Processing task deployment through FIRE

Our previously developed deep learning models and algorithms [15], [27], [28], [29] for 4D flow image denoising, eddy current correction, segmentation, and hemodynamic quantification of the thoracic aorta were implemented in Python 3.6.8 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, Oregon) environment using the TensorFlow 1.12.0 (Google, Mountain View, California) package. Using the artificial intelligence (AI)-derived 3D aortic segmentation as a mask, the peak velocity magnitudes were found for each voxel in the aorta. A cardiac time–resolved velocity MIP was generated by calculating peak velocities of the 3D segmented aorta in the sagittal orientation. Peak velocity was calculated as the 95th percentile of aortic velocities, to mitigate noise, within the highest velocity time frame. For all processing tasks, the algorithm code, auxiliary scripts for intermediate data processing, deep learning network calls, and downstream hemodynamic parameter analyses were all packaged into a single Docker container (version 24.0.6, build ed223bc, Docker, Inc., Palo Alto, California). A self-contained file system was generated from the Docker container for use with the FIRE framework and installed on a 1.5T scanner (Magnetom Sola, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The emitter and injector points were defined based on the research 4D flow sequence utilized for the study.

On-scanner 4D flow data analysis—healthy subjects

To test the performance of the FIRE augmented 4D flow analysis pipeline, 10 healthy controls (age: 39 ± 12 years, sex: 9 males) were prospectively recruited and scanned on a 1.5T Sola system. Data acquisition included retrospectively electrocardiogram–gated 4D flow MRI with the following sequence parameters: sagittal-oblique 3D volume covering the entire aorta, generalized autocalibration partially parallel acquisition (GRAPPA) R = 2, echo time (TE) = 2.27–2.34 ms, reconstructed temporal resolution = 25–37ms, velocity encoding (venc) = 175 cm/s, reconstructed cardiac time frames = 30, spatial resolution (SR) = 2.4–2.5 × 2.4–2.5 × 2.4–2.8 mm3. Variation in temporal resolution was due to a fixed number of cardiac time frames reconstructed. FIRE–based 4D flow analysis was executed on-scanner following the end of the data acquisition. Volunteers were excluded if they had MRI contraindications, were pregnant, had severe claustrophobia, were non-English speakers, or were unwilling or unable to give informed consent. This study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board(IRB), and all participants gave informed consent in writing (IRB Number STU00064035).

Retrospective 4D flow data analysis—patients

Ten previously reported [30] patients with aortic dilation, valve repair, and/or aortic aneurysm (Table 2, six bicuspid aortic valve [BAV], three tricuspid aortic valve [TAV], one prosthetic valve) underwent 4D flow MRI on a 1.5T Magnetom Aera (Siemens Healthineers) with the following sequence parameters: GRAPPA R = 2, TE = 2.27–2.54 ms, venc = 150–350 cm/s, SR = 2.4–2.5 × 2.4–2.5 × 3.5–3.6 mm3. Raw k-space data were used to retrospectively reconstruct images in the vendor reconstruction environment (ICE, Siemens) on a computer with a NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080 (NVIDIA, Santa Clara, California) with an Intel i7-8700k processor and 16 GB of RAM. This environment allowed the iterative development and debugging of the pipeline before deployment on the scanner. This study was approved by the local IRB, and all participants gave informed consent in writing (IRB Number STU00064035).

Performance evaluation

To assess the performance of each processing task included in the FIRE 4D flow analysis pipeline (noise masking, static masking, aortic segmentation, peak velocity quantification), results were compared to manual processing by one, separate, observer in each cohort. Differences for eddy current correction (identified static tissue for offset correction), noise making (identified noisy voxels), and 3D segmentation were assessed with Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) scores reported as mean and standard deviations. For peak velocities, bias and limits of agreement between FIRE and manual processing were assessed by Bland-Altman analysis. In addition, interobserver analysis between two manual observers with 5 years of experience was performed for all processing tasks (noise masking, static tissue identification for eddy current correction, and aorta peak velocity quantification).

Results

FIRE–based 4D flow data analysis was successfully deployed and utilized on the scanner for all 10 healthy controls. Total analysis time (after image reconstruction) was 220 ± 35 s, measured in three controls. Loading the pre-trained network weights for each AI task and subsequent execution of the network took 26 ± 3 s. Additional time was consumed by image streaming and, primarily, other data handling algorithms such as converting image values into velocity data matrices, which may be further optimized. Fully processed 4D flow data and aortic velocity MIPs for all cardiac time frames were delivered to the scanner as color DICOM images, viewable on the console in movie mode directly as after the 4D flow scan session (Fig. 3).

Aortic systolic velocity MIPs are shown for all 10 patients in Fig. 3 with time–resolved velocity MIPs shown in the Supplementary Material Fig. S1. For patients, in-line reconstructed MIPs agreed with manual 4D flow processing, with the FIRE-derived framework being able to characterize the BAV–induced aortic systolic jets (Fig. 3a, d, f), aortic velocities in a patient with a prosthetic valve (Fig. 3c), and capture aortic regurgitation (Fig. S1a, f).

Dice scores between manual vs deep learning processing for the total cohorts (10 volunteers, 10 patients) were for eddy current static tissue selection 0.84 ± 0.14; noise voxel detection 0.92 ± 0.04; and aortic 3D segmentation 0.92 ± 0.06 (Fig. 4). For controls alone, the static tissue selection was 0.93 ± 0.02, noise masking was 0.96 ± 0.01, and aortic segmentation was 0.90 ± 0.02. For the patient group, the values were 0.74 ± 0.15, 0.89 ± 0.04, and 0.93 ± 0.08, respectively. Bland-Altman analysis (Fig. 6) revealed a small, significant bias (0.04 m/s, p = 0.01) for peak systolic velocities between manual and automated FIRE processing with good limits of agreement (−0.10, 0.18 m/s). Mean relative differences were 4% (0.8/20) ± 6% (1.2/20) (1% (0.1/10) ± 3% (0.3/10) for the controls and 7% (0.7/10) ± 8% (0.8/10) for the patient subgroups).

Discussion

The main finding of this study was the successful on-scanner deployment of a fully automated 4D flow MRI analysis pipeline using the FIRE framework in 10 patients with aortic pathology (retrospective analysis) and 10 healthy controls (prospective acquisition). The added time for on-scanner processing was approximately 4 min for the available hardware configuration of our 1.5T MRI system, which is sufficient for integration in the clinical workflow. We did not find meaningful differences in performance in any stage of 4D flow processing and quantifications between the deep learning derived pipeline results compared to manual observers, highlighting the effectiveness of the networks for the given processing tasks.

Direct integration of automated 4D flow analysis within the in-line scanner reconstruction process could help the clinical translation and utilization of 4D flow MRI innovations compared to offline 4D flow processing frameworks that may disrupt standard clinical workflows. Further development may also enable automated analysis of advanced 4D flow–derived quantities, such as wall shear stress [31], [32], pulse wave velocity [33], or regional 4D flow–derived quantities, such as forward or reverse flow at automatically placed analysis planes. Although considerable research has been dedicated to developing sophisticated and automated flow quantification methods, the integration of these tools into clinical workflows remains limited. The proposed pipeline offers a robust framework for incorporating site-specific hemodynamic quantifications. Specifically, in the context of aortic analysis, this pipeline establishes the necessary foundation for advanced quantifications through pre-processing and segmentation. Also, any intermediate calculations, such as 3D vessel segmentation, or results of pre-processing tasks (e.g., static tissue mask for eddy current correction), can be exported at a secondary imaging series, allowing for further offline development or validation studies. Alongside this, the turnkey processing structure of the proposed pipelines allows for the removal of current processing tasks, the integration of others, or customized processing of cloned 4D flow data through a secondary pipeline. As an example, this would allow the removal of our eddy current network, the inclusion of a different eddy current algorithm, or the exporting of processed 4D flow images with an eddy current algorithm and a separate imaging series without one. It should be noted that for this specific pipeline, the current networks have only been previously validated in the current order of execution and, thus, performance may vary.

Other automated 4D flow processing and analysis tools are performed offline [13], [14], [34], [35]. These could be integrated into or used to replace existing models within the framework to suit individualized needs. In-line integration of 4D flow analysis tools is lacking. Other initial in-line 4D flow analysis demonstrates the feasibility and robustness for in-line flow quantification for the aorta and utilizes an augmentation to the proposed pipeline structure presented here [36].

The FIRE framework may help simplify the translation of research techniques into clinical environments through the integration of deep learning models or algorithms directly to the scanner, requiring no user input. Execution in any native environment through a containerized environment (Python for this project) accelerates translation of algorithm improvements into the workflow without the need for reimplementation in the vendor’s proprietary framework. Further refinements to individual algorithms or models can also be easily deployed in this manner due to the independent nature of containerization. Software updates or upgrades may require reinstallation of FIRE. However, they are not expected to affect pipeline performance, as all processing tasks are executed within containerized applications that operate independently of the scanner software environment.

Other techniques have previously been reported for on-scanner deployment of research methods. Gadgetron [37] is an open-source framework that also allows integration of automated algorithms and tools within the scanner reconstruction pipeline through the use of modules termed “gadgets.” The framework is implemented in C++ and focused on high–performance reconstruction applications and has been used for the deployment of a variety of novel imaging applications on the scanner [38], [39], [40], [41]. Recent interfaces for MATLAB and Python have expanded its scope, enabling the deployment of deep learning models for reconstruction and image analysis. FIRE enables pure Python or MATLAB algorithms without the complexity of the Gadgetron framework and was thus better suited for this non-reconstruction image processing application. The FIRE framework has also been used for the deployment of a resolution-enhancement generative adversarial in-line network [42] to accelerate cine imaging, deep learning–based artifact reduction of highly accelerated free-breathing radial cine imaging during exercise [43], and combined with Pulseq [44] for an end-to-end open-source framework for pulse sequence design and reconstruction [19].

The open-source MRD format and containerization foundation of FIRE also enable offline processing of data in addition to in-line integration. DICOM images from previous studies can be converted into MRD format, enabling batch analysis of existing data with the same code as used in-line. New analysis modules for advanced metrics such as wall shear stress can be added to the pipeline and simultaneously evaluated in both retrospective and prospective studies. This provides a significant benefit over the conventional workflow of manually loading and processing individual subjects in most post-processing platforms. It should be emphasized that fully or partially processed 4D flow data can still be used in conjunction with third-party software support when deemed appropriate by the user. The flexibility of framework would allow the exporting of images from any stage of the processing pipeline as a separate imaging series. This allows users to selectively integrate components of the workflow with other software solutions. For example, a user may wish to apply the FIRE pipeline noise masking algorithm to the 4D flow data while utilizing a vendor–specific eddy current correction method. In such cases, the partially processed images can be exported as separate imaging series and subsequently imported into the vendor’s software platform.

In this work, the performance of the neural networks was comparable to previously reported values, with median DSC scores of 0.92 vs 0.95 for aortic segmentation, 0.92 vs 0.85 for noise mask detection, and 0.84 vs 0.93 for static tissue mask delineation. Differences in aortic segmentation were typically due to the deep learning inclusion of more of the left ventricular outflow tract and the branching aortic vessels (brachiocephalic trunk, left common carotid, and left subclavian artery). The healthy control subcohort had much closer agreement for static tissue and noise mask detection compared to the previous results than the patient subcohort. This is likely due to underestimation by the manual observer compared to the deep learning tools in that cohort, as the aortic myopathies do not affect static tissue or noise distribution of the images. The flow quantifications derived were robust to these differences, with fairly narrow limits of agreement, but a small, significant bias. Again, the control subcohort had better agreement with the deep learning network, likely due to the poorer static tissue mask agreement in the patient cohort and, thus, eddy current correction. Typically, the eddy current correction would not moderately impact a voxel-wise peak velocity detection, but we utilized a distribution percentile-based method in this study. Implementing a voxel-wise peak velocity detection, alongside regional segmentation of the aorta into ascending, arch, and descending, may better assess blood flow and capture peak velocities in the aortic valve jets. Implementation of this, or any update, to the pipeline would only require changes to the algorithms code, regeneration of the Docker image, and uploading to the scanner. There was also good visual agreement between the manual and deep learning MIPs generated. Together, these generate quantitative and qualitative ways to assess a patient’s aortic blood flow, directly on the scanner and exported with the imaging series. The 4D flow scan parameters chosen for this study were circumscribed to values used for training the models. Our previous studies have shown that deep learning–based aortic segmentation performance was not associated with variabilities in age, venc, heart rate, or body mass index. In addition, differences in field strength or study participant sex did not impact deep learning performance. However, deep learning–based aortic segmentation was sensitive to voxel size (0.0012 decrease in dice score per 1-mm3 increase) and the administration of gadolinium contrast [16]. Future studies are thus warranted to conduct a systematic review of the 4D flow analysis pipeline performance relative to changes in scan parameters such as spatial or temporal resolution.

Limitations

This study is limited in scope by the low number of subjects and controls tested but represents the potential of a fully automated processing pipeline. We only utilized 10 healthy controls and 10 patients with a narrow range of aortic pathologies. This study was designed to demonstrate the feasibility of an in-line 4D flow analysis and was limited to essential pre-processing steps for aortic flow quantification. While the current framework provides a foundation that can be adapted to meet site-specific needs, it may not be sufficient to address a full range of clinical questions. Incorporation of additional processing steps, such as flow and pressure drop quantifications (e.g., pressure gradients, forward and reverse flow) and evaluation of other hemodynamic phenomena, such as aortic regurgitation, will be needed to enable a more comprehensive evaluation of aortic hemodynamics across a wider spectrum of aortic pathologies. This pipeline has only been developed for one specific use case: aortic 4D flow analysis. Beyond the aorta, 4D flow has been utilized to quantify and assess other structures, including pulmonary flow, intracardiac flow, valvular heart disease, congenital heart disease, pre- and post-surgical interventions, and on-scanner analysis should be extended for these use cases in the future [45], [46], [47], [48]. This was also a single-site, vendor, and MRI system study for FIRE implementation. Further, all deep learning models were trained with 4D flow MRI data from a single vendor with a sagittal-oblique image orientation. Future should thus include the expansion of training datasets to include greater image orientation diversity to enhance model generalizability. More acquisition and scanner parameters should also be tested, including higher and lower field strengths, the effect of temporal resolution changes, and SR changes for the entire 4D flow MRI analysis pipeline. FIRE processing times may vary due to differences in MRI system computer hardware, and deep learning tools may differ in performance on cohorts outside of their training. Additional studies are warranted to explore how the container-based framework can be shared with other sites and implemented on other MRI vendors' systems. Future work also includes integration and validation of additional processing tasks that were absent for our current pipeline, such as velocity anti-aliasing or quantification of advanced hemodynamic metrics such as wall shear stress, pulse wave velocity, or flow energetics. This work was limited to the application to 4D flow MRI of the thoracic aorta, although segmentation of additional vessels can be easily added when developed. Finally, we did not conduct a workflow analysis of the clinical integration and use of the derived images and quantifications. As automated quantification tools evolve, it will be important to assess their usefulness and incorporate clinical feedback into their development.

Conclusion

4D flow deep learning models and algorithms were successfully integrated within the FIRE framework on the scanner and evaluated in 20 subjects. Deep learning results agreed with manual analysis, adding 220 ± 35 s of extra reconstruction time. We aim to expand the automated tools for more comprehensive analysis, including anti-aliasing, wall shear stress, and pulse wave velocity.
