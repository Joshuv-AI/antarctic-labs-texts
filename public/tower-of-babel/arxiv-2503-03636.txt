Expected number of jumps and the number of active particles in TASEP
Paweł Hitczenko and Jacek Wesołowski

Abstract
For a TASEP on ℤ \mathbb{Z} with the step initial condition we identify limits as t → ∞ t\to\infty of the expected total number of jumps until time t > 0 t>0 and the expected number of active particles at a time t t . We also connect the two quantities proving that non-asymptotically, that is as a function of t > 0 t>0 , the latter is the derivative of the former. Our approach builds on asymptotics derived by Rost and intensive use of the fact that the rightmost particle evolves according to the Poisson process.



1
Introduction

Totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP) is among the most investigated interacting particle systems. At any time there is at most one particle at each of the sites of a one dimensional lattice
\mathbb{Z}
and the dynamics of the model is as follows: each particle waits an
\rm{Exponential}(1)
time (which we will refer to as a ’clock’) and then jumps to the site to its right, provided that there is no particle at that site. If that site is occupied, the particle does not move and its clock resets. The
\rm{Exponential}(1)
clocks for the same particle and the clocks corresponding to different particles are all mutually independent. In this paper, we consider a TASEP with the step initial condition, i.e. initially particles are placed at the sites
0,-1,-2,\dots
and the sites
1,2,3,\dots
are unoccupied.

TASEP is the simplest of the interacting particle models, referred to as simple exclusion processes. They were introduced in mathematics in the late 1960s by Spitzer
[
17
]
and have been studied ever since. Aside from the early work by Spitzer, followed by Liggett’s
[
10
]
, later contributions by Rost
[
14
]
and Schütz
[
16
]
seem to have been quite influential. In particular, the latter paper gives a determinantal formula for the probability that there are particles at any finite set of sites in
\mathbb{Z}
at a time
t
, given their positions at a time
0
, while the former is concerned with the asymptotic properties of the number of particles in a given region. Rost’s results were further elaborated on e.g. in
[
15
]
.

In recent years, there has been substantial interest in TASEP as it has been identified as one of the simplest models in a universality class of stochastic growth models referred to as the KPZ universality class. In this context, the so-called height function and its fluctuations have been the main objects of the studies, see, for example,
[
3
,
5
,
6
,
9
,
13
]
where TASEP with the step initial condition (or its generalizations) is considered.
A more detailed discussion of TASEP and its role in the KPZ universality class may be found in
[
1
,
11
,
12
]
and references therein. In particular, in
[
11
, Theorem 3.13]
TASEP is used to construct the so–called KPZ fixed point (see also
[
7
, Section 16]
where this is further discussed and convergence of TASEP to the directed landscape is established).

As we mentioned earlier, most of the up–to–date work on TASEP concerns the height function and its fluctuations. For example, for the step initial condition it was shown in
[
9
]
that the scaling limit of the height function is the Tracy–Widom largest eignevalue distribution for the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble.

In this paper, we will focus on two aspects of TASEP that seem basic to describing its evolution, though we were not able to trace related results in the literature: they concern the total number of jumps of all particles up to a time
t
and the number of active particles at the time
t
(i.e., the particles that can move at a time
t
). Specifically, we will establish the asymptotics of the expected values of these quantities (after a suitable normalization). We refer to Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
4.1
below for the precise statements.

It is natural to expect that these two quantities are related: how fast the process is evolving at a given time should depend on how many particles can move at that time. Nonetheless, we have not seen any such formal statements in the literature.

For TASEP with the step initial configuration we will formalize this relationship. Namely, we will prove in Theorem
5.1
below that the number of active particles at a time
t
is the derivative of the number of jumps by the process up to that time, at least at the level of the expected values. It is conceivable that similar statement is true for more general classes of interacting particle processes.

Our methods are elementary. We rely heavily on the fact that the jumps of the rightmost particle are unconstrained and thus follow a Poisson process. We will also exploit some of the techniques and results established by Rost’s paper
[
14
]
that heavily influenced our work.

2
Notation and Preliminaries

Following Rost
[
14
]
we let
X(k,t)
be the indicator of the event that there is a particle in a position
k
at time
t
, and we let

S(k,t):=\sum_{i>k}X(i,t)

be the number of particles to the right of
k
at time
t
. For step initial conditions, limiting behavior of the distribution of
S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)
for fixed
u\in[0,1)
and
t\to\infty
was determined in
[
9
]
, see Corollary 1.7 there.
For our purpose, the following estimate for the expected value of
S(k,t)
will be sufficient.

When we refer to particles, we will number them from the right to the left, i.e. the rightmost particle will be referred to as the first, the second rightmost as the second, etc.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
t>0
we have

{\mathds{E}}\ S(k,t)\leq\frac{t^{k+1}}{k!}.

(1)

Proof.

We first write

{\mathds{E}}\ S(k,t)=\sum_{m\geq 1}{\mathds{P}}(S(k,t)\geq m).

Next, note that
S(k,t)\geq m
means that there are at least
m
particles to the right of
k
. This means, in particular, that the
m
th particle made at least
m-1+k+1=k+m
jumps by the time
t
. Hence, the first particle must have made at least as many moves by the time
t
. In other words

\mathds{P}(S(k,t)\geq m)\leq\mathds{P}(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq k+m),

(2)

where
N_{t}^{(1)}
is the number of jumps by the first particle up to time
t
. Since
N_{t}^{(1)}
is
{\rm Poisson}(t)
random variable, we further have

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 1}\,\mathds{P}\left(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq m+k\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 1}e^{-t}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{t^{m+k+j}}{(m+k+j)!}\leq\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\sum_{m\geq 1}e^{-t}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{t^{m+j}}{(m+j)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\sum_{m\geq 1}\,\mathds{P}(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq m)

where for the inequality we used
(m+k+j)!\geq(m+j)!k!
.
The assertion follows since

\sum_{m\geq 1}\,\mathds{P}(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq m)=\mathds{E}\,N_{t}^{(1)}=t.

∎

3
Total number of jumps

Let
P_{t}
denote the total number of jumps of (all) particles in a TASEP until
t>0
, which corresponds to the area below the height function representation of the TASEP. In this section we establish the following asymptotics for
{\mathds{E}}\,P_{t}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

As
t\to\infty

\frac{\mathds{E}\,P_{t}}{t^{2}}\to\frac{1}{6}.

Proof.

To facilitate the proof, we represent
P_{t}
as follows.
For
t\geq 0
we visualize the graph of the function
x\mapsto S(\lfloor x\rfloor,t)
as columns of squares of unit size on the upper half-plane whose heights are decreasing (by no more than
1
) with
x
(see Figure
1
). When a
j
th particle jumps a new square is added at the right end of the
j
th row. With this interpretation, the total number of jumps by the process
up to time
t
is the number of added squares, i. e., the area between the graphs of
S(\lfloor\cdot\rfloor,t)
and
S(\lfloor\cdot\rfloor,0)
.

That is,

P_{t}:=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(S(k,t)-S(k,0)).

(3)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\,P_{t}

\displaystyle={\mathds{E}}\,\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(S(k,t)-S(k,0))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\mathds{E}\,(S(\lfloor v\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor v\rfloor,0))dv

\displaystyle=t\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\mathds{E}\,(S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0))du=t^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\mathds{E}\,\left(\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0)}{t}\right)du.

Therefore, we need to show that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\mathds{E}\,\left(\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0)}{t}\right)du=\frac{1}{6}.

(4)

As was shown in
[
14
, Proposition 2]

for every
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)/t
converges almost surely and in
L_{1}
to
h(u)
, defined by

h(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u,&u<-1;\\
\frac{1}{4}(1-u)^{2},&-1\leq u\leq 1;\\
0,&u>1.\end{array}\right.

(5)

To complete the proof, we will justify passing with the limit under the integral sign in (
4
). In order to do it, we confine ourselves to the integration over the positive half–line. To this end, first note that by symmetry of the particles and empty sites (which we will refer to as ’holes’), for every
t\geq 0
, we have

(X(j,t))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(1-X(-j+1,t))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}},

(6)

where the symbol “
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
”means equality in distribution. Further, if we let

S^{\prime}(k,t):=\sum_{j<k}(1-X(j,t))

be the number of holes to the left of
k
then for all
t\geq 0
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}

S^{\prime}(k,t)=k-1+S(k-1,t).

(7)

This is because the equality holds at
t=0
and when a particle (at a position
k
, say) jumps to the right, the number of holes to the left of
k+1
increases by 1.

Moreover, in view of (
6
), for every
t\geq 0
we have

(S^{\prime}(-k,t))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(S(k+1,t))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}.

(8)

Therefore, for any
u_{0}\geq 0

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{-u_{0}}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0)}{t}du\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{SkS}}}{{=}}\int_{-\infty}^{-u_{0}}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S^{\prime}(\lfloor ut\rfloor+1,t)+\lfloor ut\rfloor-\lfloor ut\rfloor}{t}du

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{u_{0}}^{\infty}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S^{\prime}(\lfloor-ut\rfloor+1,t)}{t}du\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{SS}}}{{=}}\int_{u_{0}}^{\infty}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor+1,t)}{t}du

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{u_{0}}^{\infty}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)}{t}du-\int_{u_{0}}^{\infty}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{X(\lfloor ut\rfloor+1,t)}{t}du.

Note that in the second line above, when we referred to (
8
), we first used at the left–hand side the identity
\lfloor-ut\rfloor+1=-\lfloor ut\rfloor
for
ut\notin\mathbb{Z}
.

Similarly,

\int_{-u_{0}}^{0}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0)}{t}du=\int_{0}^{u_{0}}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)}{t}du-\int_{0}^{u_{0}}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{X(\lfloor ut\rfloor+1,t)}{t}du.

Now,
X(\lfloor ut\rfloor+1,t)=1
means that there is a particle at the position
\lfloor ut\rfloor+1
at the time
t
. This, in turn, means that the first particle is to the right of
\lfloor ut\rfloor
at the time
t
.
Since the first particle jumps according to the Poisson process, we have

\int_{u_{0}}^{\infty}\,\mathds{E}\,X(\lfloor ut\rfloor+1,t)du\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\mathds{P}(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq\lfloor ut\rfloor)du\leq 1.

Likewise

\int_{0}^{u_{0}}\,\mathds{E}\,X(\lfloor ut\rfloor+1,t)du\leq 1.

Hence, if for any
u_{0}>0
we write

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0)}{t}du=\left(\int_{|u|\leq u_{0}}+\int_{|u|>u_{0}}\right)\left(\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0)}{t}\right)du

(9)

then
(note that
S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0)=0
for
ut>0
)

\displaystyle\int_{|u|\leq u_{0}}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0)}{t}du

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{u_{0}}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)}{t}du+O(t^{-1}),

(10)

\displaystyle\int_{|u|>u_{0}}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)-S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,0)}{t}du

\displaystyle=2\int_{u_{0}}^{\infty}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)}{t}du+O(t^{-1}),\mbox{as}\quad t\to\infty.

(11)

We first handle the integral (
11
). Consider
u>u_{0}
with
u_{0}>0
to be specified later.

By Lemma
2.1
the integrand on the right–hand side of (
11
) is bounded by
t^{\lfloor ut\rfloor}/\lfloor ut\rfloor!
. From Stirling’s approximation

n!=\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\left(1+O(n^{-1})\right)

it is clear that there exist a
u_{0}>1
and
\alpha>0
and
C>0
such that this integrand is further bounded by

\frac{t^{\lfloor ut\rfloor}}{(\lfloor ut\rfloor)!}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\lfloor ut\rfloor}}\left(\frac{et}{\lfloor ut\rfloor}\right)^{\lfloor ut\rfloor}\left(1+O(\lfloor ut\rfloor^{-1}\right)\leq C\frac{e^{-\alpha tu}}{\sqrt{tu}},

uniformly over
u\geq u_{0}
whenever
t
is bounded away from zero. Consequently,

\int_{u_{0}}^{\infty}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)}{t}du\leq C\int_{u_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\alpha ut}}{\sqrt{tu}}du=o(1),\quad\mbox{as}\quad t\to\infty

(12)

which shows that the contribution of (
11
) is negligible.

We now turn our attention to (
10
).
The integrand on the right hand side of (
10
) is bounded by the integrable function
\mathbb{I}_{[0,u_{0}]}
since by the monotonicity of
S(k,t)
in
k
and Lemma
2.1

\mathds{E}\,S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)\leq\mathds{E}\,S(0,t)\leq\,t.

Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{u_{0}}\,\mathds{E}\,\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)}{t}du=\int_{0}^{u_{0}}\lim_{t\to\infty}\,\mathds{E}\frac{S(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)}{t}du=\int_{0}^{u_{0}}h(u)du=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{2}du=\frac{1}{12}.

Combining this with (
9
)–(
11
) and (
12
) proves (
4
).
∎

4
Number of active particles

Let us denote by
a_{t}
number of active particles at time
t\geq 0
, i.e.

a_{t}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\,X(k,t)(1-X(k+1,t)).

(13)

Theorem 4.1
.

We have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathds{E}\,a_{t}}{t}=\frac{1}{3}.

Proof.

We write,

\mathds{E}\,a_{t}=\,\mathds{E}\,\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}X(k,t)(1-X(k+1,t))=\,\mathds{E}\,\left(\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}+\sum_{|k|\geq k_{t}}\right)X(k,t)(1-X(k+1,t))

for
k_{t}
which will be chosen later.

Consider first

\mathds{E}\,\sum_{k\geq k_{t}}X(k,t)(1-X(k+1,t))\leq\,\mathds{E}\,\sum_{k\geq k_{t}}X(k,t)=\mathds{E}\,S(k_{t}-1,t)\leq\frac{t^{k_{t}}}{(k_{t}-1)!}

where the last inequality is by Lemma
2.1
.
Choosing
k_{t}\sim\beta t
for sufficiently large (but fixed)
\beta>1
and using Stirling approximation as in an earlier argument
we conclude that the quantity on the right vanishes as
t\to\infty
, that is

\,\mathds{E}\,\sum_{k\geq k_{t}}X(k,t)(1-X(k+1,t))=o(1),\quad t\to\infty.

Reversing the roles of
X(k,\,\cdot\,)
and
1-X(k+1,\,\cdot\,)
and using the migration of the first hole rather than the first particle we similarly get that

\mathds{E}\,\sum_{k\leq-k_{t}}X(k,t)(1-X(k+1,t))=o(1),\quad t\to\infty.

It remains to asymptotically evaluate

\frac{1}{t}{\mathds{E}}\,\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}X(k,t)(1-X(k+1,t))=\frac{1}{t}{\mathds{E}}\,\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}X(k,t)-\frac{1}{t}{\mathds{E}}\,\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}X(k,t)X(k+1,t).

By
[
14
, Theorem 1]
, as
t\to\infty

\frac{1}{t}\sum_{ut<k<vt}X(k,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{u}^{v}f(w)dw,

(14)

where

f(w)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}(1-w),&|w|\leq 1;\\
1,&w<-1;\\
0,&w>1.\end{array}\right.

(15)

By the bounded convergence theorem, the expected value of the left–hand side of (
14
) converges as well. Therefore,

\frac{1}{t}\,\mathds{E}\,\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}X(k,t)=\frac{1}{t}\,\mathds{E}\,\sum_{-\beta t<k<\beta t}X(k,t)\longrightarrow\int_{-\beta}^{\beta}f(w)dw

(16)

as
t\to\infty
.

Further, Rost proved that (see
[
14
, (1) and (3) of Section 4]
; note the ambiguity of display enumeration across the sections in
[
14
]
), in our notation

\lim_{t\to\infty}\,\mathds{E}\,X(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)X(\lfloor ut\rfloor+1,t)\to f^{2}(u),

(17)

where
f
is given in (
15
). Therefore, using
k_{t}\sim\beta t
again we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\,\mathds{E}\,\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}X(k,t)X(k+1,t)=\frac{1}{t}\,\mathds{E}\,\int_{-\beta t}^{\beta t}X(\lfloor v\rfloor,t)X(\lfloor v\rfloor+1,t)dv

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{-\beta}^{\beta}\,\mathds{E}\,X(\lfloor ut\rfloor,t)X(\lfloor ut\rfloor+1,t)du\longrightarrow\int_{-\beta}^{\beta}f^{2}(u)du

(18)

as
t\to\infty
.
We can exchange the order of the integrals by non–negativity and we can take the limit inside by the bounded convergence theorem since the integrands are bounded by
\mathbb{I}_{[-\beta,\beta]}(u)
.

Combining this with (
16
) and recalling that
\beta>1
we obtain

\frac{1}{t}\,\mathds{E}\,\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}X(k,t)(1-X(k+1,t))\longrightarrow\int_{-\beta}^{\beta}(f(u)-f^{2}(u))du=\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\frac{1-u}{2}-\frac{(1-u)^{2}}{4}\right)du=\frac{1}{3}.

∎

5
A connection

As we mentioned earlier, it is natural to expect a connection between the speed with which the process moves at a given time and the number of active particles at that time.
Since we have not seen any formal statement in the literature linking these two quantities we include a statement and its proof here.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
g(t)=\mathds{E}\,P_{t}
,
t\geq 0
. Then

g^{\prime}(t)={\mathds{E}}\,a_{t},\quad t>0.

(19)

Proof.

We first consider the case of the right derivative. That is, we take
h>0
.
Note that by (
3
)

g(t+h)-g(t)=\mathds{E}\,(P_{t+h}-P_{t})=\mathds{E}\,\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(S(k,t+h)-S(k,t)).

Set

\Delta_{k,t,h}:=S(k,t+h)-S(k,t).

Then,

g(t+h)-g(t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\mathds{E}\,\Delta_{k,t,h}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\,\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m).

(20)

To evaluate these probabilities, for a given
k
and
t
consider the following events (where we suppress the dependence on
t
in the notation):

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\bullet}}\circ\dots\right\}\quad\mbox{(there is a particle on the $k$th position but not on the $(k+1)$st at time $t$})

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\circ}}\bullet\dots\right\}\quad\mbox{(no particle on the $k$th position but there is one on the $(k+1)$st at time $t$})

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\bullet}}\bullet\dots\right\}\quad\mbox{(both the $k$th and the $(k+1)$st positions are occupied at time $t$})

\displaystyle D_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\circ}}\circ\dots\right\}\quad\mbox{(both the $k$th and the $(k+1)$st positions are empty at time $t$}).

Then, the probability on the right hand side of (
20
) is the sum

\,\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,A_{k})+\,\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,B_{k})+\,\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,C_{k})+\,\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,D_{k}).

(21)

We will show that the dominant contribution to the right hand side of (
20
) comes from the case

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq 1,A_{k}).

We have

\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq 1,A_{k})=\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq 1|A_{k})\,\mathds{P}(A_{k})=(1-\,\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}=0|A_{k}))\,\mathds{P}(A_{k}).

Given
A_{k}
,
\Delta_{k,t,h}=0
means that the particle that is on the
k
th position at a time
t
does not move in the time interval
[t,t+h]
which happens with probability
e^{-h}
.
Thus,

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\mathds{P}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq 1,A_{k})=(1-e^{-h})\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\mathds{P}(A_{k})=(1-e^{-h})\,\mathds{E}\,\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathbb{I}_{A_{k}}=(1-e^{-h}){\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}

(22)

which asymptotically as
h\to 0
is
h\,\mathds{E}\,a_{t}
. Thus, the theorem will be proved once we show that all other terms are of lower order as
h\to 0
.

For
m\geq 2
and
k\geq 0
the event
\{\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,A_{k}\}
means that at least
m
particles crossed position
k
between
t
and
t+h
and that the first particle made at least
k
jumps by the time
t
(at time
t
there is a particle on the position
k
, so it must have made at least
k
jumps, and hence the first one must have made at least that many jumps). For these things to happen, the first particle must have made at least
k
jumps by the time
t
, the particle at the position
k
at the time
t
must have jumped between
t
and
t+h
and the clocks of each of the
m-1
particles closest to
k
from the left must have rung at least once each
between
t
and
t+h
(some of their jumps may have been blocked, but the clocks must have rung). Since all these events are independent, we see that

{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,A_{k})\leq(1-e^{-h})^{m}{\mathds{P}}(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq k).

(23)

If
k<0
,
\{\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,A_{k}\}
means that at least
m
particles crossed position
k
between
t
and
t+h
and that the first hole made at least
k
moves by the time
t
. Throughout the remainder of the proof by the first hole we mean the leftmost hole, i.e. the hole positioned at
1
when
t=0
; the number of moves by that hole up to time
t
is denoted by
H_{t}^{(1)}
and, in view of the particle-hole duality,
(H_{t}^{(1)})
is a Poisson process.
Consequently, arguing as for (
23
), we see that

{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,A_{k})\leq(1-e^{-h})^{m}{\mathds{P}}(H_{t}^{(1)}\geq-k).

Clearly,
N_{t}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}H_{t}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{P}_{t}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{t}
is a
\rm{Poisson}(t)
random variable. Thus it follows from this and (
23
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 2}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,A_{k})

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-h})^{2}e^{h}\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}{\mathds{P}}(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq k)+\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathds{P}}(H_{t}^{(1)}\geq k)\right)

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-h})^{2}e^{h}(1+2{\mathds{E}}\,\mathcal{P}_{t})=(1-e^{-h})^{2}e^{h}(1+2t),

which is of order
h^{2}
as
h\to 0
.

The argument for
B_{k}
is virtually the same. Here, for at least
m
crosses of
k
between
t
and
t+h
, the clock of the particle in position
k+1
must ring in this interval and the clocks of the
m
preceding particles must ring between
t
and
t+h
. Also, since there is a particle in position
k+1
at time
t
(a hole in position
k
if
k<0
), the first particle must have made at least
k+1
jumps by the time
t
(or the first hole must have made at least
-k+1
moves if
k<0
). Using independence and summing over
m\geq 1
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
we get

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h},B_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\geq 1}(1-e^{-h})^{m+1}\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}{\mathds{P}}(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq k+1)+\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathds{P}}(H_{t}^{(1)}\geq k+1)\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1-e^{-h})^{2}e^{h}t.

The argument for
C_{k}
and
D_{k}
is a bit more delicate since we do not know where the first particle (in the case of
D_{k}
) or the first hole (in the case of
C_{k}
) at time
t
is.

Consider first
k<0
for
D_{k}
. For
m
crosses of
k
between
t
and
t+h
, each of the clocks of the
m
particles closest to
k
from the left must ring at least twice between
t
and
t+h
. Further, since the first hole is in position at most
k
at the time
t
,
H_{t}^{(1)}\geq-k+1
on
D_{k}
.
Therefore, for
k<0

{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,D_{k})\leq(1-e^{-h})^{2m}{\mathds{P}}(H_{t}^{(1)}\geq-k+1).

(26)

Consider now
k\geq 0
.
We split
D_{k}
according to whether the position of the first particle at the time
t
is to the left or to the right of
k
:

D_{k}=D_{k}^{>}\cup D_{k}^{<},\quad\mbox{where}\quad D_{k}^{>}=D_{k}\cap\{N_{t}^{(1)}\geq k+2\},\quad D_{k}^{<}=D_{k}\cap\{N_{t}^{(1)}<k\}.

Thus, by independence

{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,D_{k}^{>})\leq(1-e^{-h})^{2m}{\mathds{P}}(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq k+2).

(27)

It follows from (
26
) and (
27
) that

\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,D_{k})\leq\frac{(1-e^{-h})^{2}e^{h}}{2-e^{-h}}(2{\mathds{E}}\,\mathcal{P}_{t}-1)+\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k\geq 0}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,D_{k}^{<}).

The first term on the right–hand side is
O(h^{2})
so it remains to show that

\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k\geq 0}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,D_{k}^{<})=o(h).

(28)

First note that the inner sum is in fact over
k\geq 1
(as the leftmost position, the first particle can ever be at, is 0). At time
t
the first particle can be at any position
i
,
0\leq i<k
, and must be to the right of position
k+m-1
at time
t+h
(to make room to the right of position
k
for at least
m-1
particles). In other words, the first particle makes
i
jumps up to time
t
and at least
k+m-i
additional jumps between
t
and
t+h
. Probability of this event is

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{\mathds{P}}(N_{t}^{(1)}=i,N^{(1)}_{t+h}-N^{(1)}_{t}\geq k+m-i)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{\mathds{P}}(N_{t}^{(1)}=i){\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\geq k+m-i)

where we have used the independence of
N_{t}^{(1)}
and
N_{t+h}^{(1)}
and where
\mathcal{P}_{h}
is a
\mathrm{Poisson}(h)
random variable. We split the sum on the right as

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}+\sum_{i=\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}^{k-1}\right){\mathds{P}}(N^{(1)}_{t}=i){\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\geq k+m-i)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}{\mathds{P}}(N^{(1)}_{t}=i){\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\geq k+m-\lfloor k/2\rfloor)+\sum_{i=\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}^{k-1}{\mathds{P}}(N^{(1)}_{t}=i){\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\geq m+1)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\geq k+m-\lfloor k/2\rfloor)+{\mathds{P}}(N^{(1)}_{t}\geq\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1){\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\geq m+1).

(29)

Summing the above expressions over
k,m\geq 1
and using

{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\geq r)\leq\sum_{i\geq r}h^{i}=\frac{h^{r}}{1-h}

and
\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1\geq k/2
we see that the
double
sum of the second terms in (
29
) is bounded by

\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathds{P}}(N_{t}^{(1)}\geq k/2)\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{h^{m+1}}{1-h}=2{\mathds{E}}\,N_{t}^{(1)}\frac{h^{2}}{(1-h)^{2}}=2t\frac{h^{2}}{(1-h)^{2}}.

For the double sum of the first terms we get
(recall that necessarily
k\geq 1
)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\,\sum_{m\geq 1}

\displaystyle\,{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\geq k+m-\lfloor k/2\rfloor)\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\,\sum_{m\geq 1}\,\tfrac{h^{k+m-\lfloor k/2\rfloor}}{1-h}=\tfrac{1}{1-h}\left(\sum_{m\geq 1}h^{m}\right)\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}h^{k-\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{(1-h)^{2}}\left(\sum_{j\geq 0}h^{2j+1-j}+\sum_{j\geq 1}h^{2j-j}\right)=2\frac{h^{2}}{(1-h)^{3}}.

This shows (
28
) and combined with the earlier estimates gives

\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,D_{k})=O(h^{2}),\quad h\to 0.

(30)

It remains to consider the
C_{k}
. If
k\geq 0
then the same argument as for the
B_{k}
(the first particle must have made at least
k+1
jumps by the time
t
since there is a particle in position
k+1
; for at least
m
crosses of
k
between
t
and
t+h
the clock of the particle in position
k+1
at a time
t
and the clocks of
m
particles to the left of
k
must have rung between
t
and
t+h
) yields

\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k\geq 0}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,C_{k})\leq\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k\geq 0}(1-e^{-h})^{m+1}{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{t}\geq k+1)=O(h^{2}){\mathds{E}}\mathcal{P}_{t}=O(h^{2}).

(31)

When
k<0
we split
C_{k}=C_{k}^{<}\cup C_{k}^{>}
according to whether the position of the first hole at the time
t
is to the left of
k
or to the right of
k+1
. Note that on
C_{k}^{<}
we have
H_{t}^{(1)}\geq-k+2
(as the first hole have moved from
1
to the left of
k
by the time
t
) and the clocks of at least
m+1
specific particles rung between
t
and
t+h
. Therefore,

\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k<0}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,C_{k}^{<})\leq\sum_{m\geq 1}(1-e^{-h})^{m+1}\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{t}\geq k+2)=O(h^{2}).

(32)

Finally, consider
C_{k}^{>}
. Let the position of the first hole at the time
t
be
k+2+i
for some
0\leq i\leq-k-1
(the upper bound follows from
k+i+2\leq 1
as
1
is the rightmost position the first hole can ever be at). That means that up to time
t
, the first hole made
-k-i-1
moves. It needs to make additional moves between
t
and
t+h
. Namely, it needs to move to position
k+1
(which takes
i+1
moves) and then it needs to make at least
m
additional moves to facilitate that many crossings of
k
; a total of at least
m+i+1
moves between
t
and
t+h
.
Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,C_{k}^{>})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{-k-1}{\mathds{P}}(H_{t}^{(1)}=-k-i-1,H_{t+h}^{(1)}-H_{t}^{(1)}\geq m+i+1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}{\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{t}=j-i-1){\mathds{P}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\geq m+i+1).

Using arguments parallel to those used for (
29
) and (
30
) we infer that

\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k<0}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,C_{k}^{>})=O(h^{2}).

Combining the last estimate with (
32
) and (
31
) we conclude that

\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathds{P}}(\Delta_{k,t,h}\geq m,C_{k})=O(h^{2}),\quad h\to 0.

(33)

Now, (
), (
25
), (
30
) and (
33
) imply that (
22
) is asymptotically dominant among expressions (
21
). Thus, referring to (
20
), we obtain that

\lim_{h\to 0+}\frac{g(t+h)-g(t)}{h}={\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}.

The argument for
h\to 0^{-}
is basically the same with
t-h
playing the role of
t
and
t
playing the role of
t+h
. This completes the proof.
∎

6
Remarks

6.1
Jumps of the rightmost particles

For
r\geq 1
let
N^{(r)}_{t}
be the number of jumps of the
r
th particle up to time
t
. Then

N_{t}^{(r)}\leq N_{t}^{(r-1)}\leq\dots\leq N_{t}^{(1)}

and since the number of jumps of the first particle by the time
t
is governed by a Poisson process
we conclude that for every fixed
r\geq 1

{\mathds{E}}\ N_{t}^{(r)}\leq t.

Nonetheless, while the
r
th particle moves slower than the first one, the next statement asserts that asymptotically it still moves at a rate
t
as
t\to\infty
.

Proposition 6.1
.

For any
r\geq 1
we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{{\mathds{E}}\ N_{t}^{(r)}}{t}=1.

Proof.

By the earlier considerations

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{{\mathds{E}}\ N_{t}^{(r)}}{t}\leq 1

so it remains to prove that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{{\mathds{E}}\ N_{t}^{(r)}}{t}=1.

(34)

To this end, we first note that

N^{(r)}_{t}=r-1+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\inf\{k:S(k,t)<r\}

(in particular, for the first particle
N^{(1)}_{t}=\inf\{k:S(k,t)<1\}
).
Consequently,

\{N^{(r)}_{t}\geq k\}=\{S(k-r,t)\geq r)\}

and it follows that

{\mathds{E}}N_{t}^{(r)}=\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathds{P}}(N_{t}^{(r)}\geq k)=\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathds{P}}(S(k-r,t)\geq r)\geq\sum_{k\geq 0}{\mathds{P}}(S(k,t)\geq r).

Pick any
0<\epsilon<1
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathds{E}}\ N_{t}^{(r)}}{t}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k\geq 0}{\mathds{P}}(S(k,t)\geq r)=\frac{1}{t}{\mathds{E}}\,\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{I}_{S(k,t)\geq r}\geq\frac{1}{t}\sup_{y>0}y{\mathds{P}}(\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{I}_{S(k,t)\geq r}\geq y)

(35)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\epsilon)t+1}{t}\,{\mathds{P}}(\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{I}_{S(k,t)\geq r}\geq(1-\epsilon)t+1)\geq(1-\epsilon){\mathds{P}}(\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{I}_{S(k,t)\geq r}\geq\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor+1).

Since
S(k,t)
is decreasing in
k
, for
t>0
we have

\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{I}_{S(k,t)\geq r}\geq\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor+1)
if and only if
S(\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor,t)\geq r
.
Thus,

{\mathds{P}}(\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{I}_{S(k,t)\geq r}\geq\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor+1)={\mathds{P}}(S(\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor,t)\geq r)={\mathds{P}}\left(\frac{S(\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor,t)}{t}\geq\frac{r}{t}\right).

(36)

Let
t_{0}=8r/\epsilon^{2}
. For
t\geq t_{0}
, we have
r/t\leq\epsilon^{2}/8
and thus for such
t

{\mathds{P}}\left(\frac{S(\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor,t)}{t}\geq\frac{r}{t}\right)\geq{\mathds{P}}\left(\frac{S(\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor,t)}{t}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{8}\right).

(37)

As
t\to\infty
,

\frac{S(\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor,t)}{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}h(1-\epsilon)=\frac{1}{4}\epsilon^{2}

where we have used the expression for
h(u)
given in (
5
).
Therefore,

{\mathds{P}}\left(\frac{S(\lfloor(1-\epsilon)t\rfloor,t)}{t}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{8}\right)\longrightarrow 1,\quad t\to\infty.

Combining this with (
35
)–(
37
) yields

\liminf_{t>0}\frac{{\mathds{E}}\ N^{(r)}_{t}}{t}\geq 1-\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon
was arbitrary, this proves (
34
) and the Proposition.
∎

6.2
Slow decrease

Although we furnished independent proofs of Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
4.1
one might expect that Theorem
5.1
would provide a way to deduce one from another. In fact, were the statements of Theorems
3.1
and
4.1
true for all
t
rather then only in the limits, Theorem
4.1
would have been an obvious corollary to Theorems
3.1
and
5.1
. In general, this is not true and one would need additional information about
{\mathds{E}}\ a_{t}/t
to deduce Theorem
4.1
from Theorems
3.1
and
5.1
. One such condition is a slow decrease.
Recall (see e.g.
[
2
, Definition p. 41; it seems that a ’slowing’ in that definition should read ’slowly’]
or
[
8
, (6.2.2), (6.2.3), Section 6.2, p. 124]
) that a function
f
is slowly decreasing
if

\lim_{\lambda\searrow 1}\liminf_{x\to\infty}\inf_{\alpha\in[1,\lambda]}\left(f(\alpha x)-f(x)\right)\geq 0.

(38)

(Note that since the infimum in (
38
) is non–positive, (
38
) is equivalent to the quantity on the left being equal to zero.) For a slowly decreasing functions
f
one has (see
[
2
, Item 15 Section 1.11, p. 59]
or
[
8
, footnote to Theorem 68, p. 126]
)

\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}\,f(t)\,dt\longrightarrow c,\quad x\to\infty\quad\implies f(x)\to c,\quad x\to\infty.

Knowing that the function
f(t)={\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}/t
,
t\geq 0
, is slowly decreasing would allow one to derive Theorem
4.1
from Theorems
3.1
and
5.1
.
Since we provided a direct proof of Theorem
4.1
we only sketch a proof that
{\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}/t
is, indeed, slowly decreasing:

Proposition 6.2
.

The function

t\longrightarrow\frac{{\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}}{t},\quad t\geq 0

is slowly decreasing.

Proof.

(Sketch)
Since

\frac{{\mathds{E}}\,a_{\alpha t}-\alpha{\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}}{\alpha t}=\frac{{\mathds{E}}\,a_{\alpha t}-{\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}}{\alpha t}-\frac{(\alpha-1){\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}}{\alpha t},

by superadditivity of
\inf
and
\liminf
, the left–hand side of
(
38
) for our function is bounded below by

\lim_{\lambda\searrow 1}\liminf_{t\to\infty}\inf_{\alpha\in[1,\lambda]}\frac{{\mathds{E}}\,a_{\alpha t}-{\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}}{\alpha t}-\lim_{\lambda\searrow 1}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\sup_{\alpha\in[1,\lambda]}\frac{(\alpha-1){\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}}{\alpha t}.

Since
1\leq a_{t}\leq N_{t}^{(1)}
we have
1\leq{\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}\leq t
and hence, the second term vanishes as
\lambda\searrow 1
.
We need to show that

\lim_{\lambda\searrow 1}\liminf_{t\to\infty}\inf_{\alpha\in[1,\lambda]}\frac{{\mathds{E}}\,a_{\alpha t}-{\mathds{E}}\,a_{t}}{\alpha t}=0.

(39)

For
t>0
and
s=(\alpha-1)t

\displaystyle{\mathds{E}}\,(a_{t+s}-a_{t})

\displaystyle={\mathds{E}}\,\left(\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}+\sum_{|k|\geq k_{t}}\right)\Big(X(k,t+s)(1-X(k+1,t+s))-X(k,t)(1-X(k+1,t))\Big).

Similarly as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
we conclude that the sum over
|k|\geq k_{t}\sim\beta t
vanishes as
t\to\infty
.
The sum over
|k|<k_{t}
we split into

\displaystyle{\mathds{E}}\,\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}\Big(X(k,t+s)-X(k,t)\Big)+{\mathds{E}}\,\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}\Big(X(k,t)X(k+1,t)-X(k,t+s)X(k+1,t+s)\Big).

Again referring to the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we use (
16
) and (
18
) to derive the limits

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\alpha t}{\mathds{E}}\,\sum_{|k|<k_{t}}\Big(X(k,t+s)-X(k,t)\Big)\to\int_{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}f(w)dw-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\int_{-\beta}^{\beta}f(w)dw

and

\tfrac{1}{\alpha t}\,{\mathds{E}}\,\sum_{|k|\leq k_{t}}\Big(X(k,t)X(k+1,t)-X(k,t+s)X(k+1,t+s)\Big)\to\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\int_{-\beta}^{\beta}f^{2}(w)dw-\int_{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}f^{2}(w)dw

as
t\to\infty
. Since
1<\alpha<\lambda\searrow 1
the right–hand sides above converge to zero, and thus (
39
) follows.
∎
