PSVRP: a pig structural variant reference panel for complex trait genomics and precision breeding
Authors: Xiang Li, Yu Quan, Dong Li, Yulong Wang, Tiantian Yuan, Minghao Cao, Jin Wang, Rongrong Ding, Sicong Wang, Xiangjie Shi, Jiaming Zhang, Yu Jiang, Martijn F. L. Derks, Wansheng Liu, Quanzhong Liu, Taiyong Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPigs are not only a key source of animal protein worldwide, but also serve as important models in biological research. With the rapid development of short- and long-read sequencing technologies, genetic studies in pigs have advanced considerably. Although extensive research has been conducted on single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and small insertions/deletions (indels), which has provided important insights into pig domestication, evolution, and trait formation, structural variants (SVs) remain underexplored due to technical limitations in sequencing resolution, challenges in variant detection, and insufficient population-scale sampling.ResultsIn this study, we constructed the Pig Structural Variant Reference Panel (PSVRP) by integrating 21 long-read and 1,193 short-read whole-genome resequencing datasets from globally diverse pig populations. A total of 319,058 high-confidence SVs were identified, comprising 196,620 insertions and 122,438 deletions. Phylogenetic and ADMIXTURE analyses revealed clear divergence between Asian and European pigs, consistent with results derived from SNPs and indels data. Selection scans highlighted candidate genes associated with key traits, such as EPAS1 and NOVA1 for high-altitude adaptation, and PLAG1 and MIB1 for body size regulation.ConclusionsThe PSVRP provides a high-resolution, population-scale pig SVs genotyping resource. This comprehensive panel deepens our understanding of genetic variation, facilitates the discovery of functional variants underlying adaptive and economic traits, and offers new insights for precision pig breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12273-7.

Introduction

Pig (Sus scrofa) was independently domesticated in the Near East and Asia approximately 10,000 years ago and has since become one of the most widely farmed livestock species, with populations distributed across Asia and Europe [1]. Pigs are not only a primary source of animal protein for humans, providing high-quality meat to billions of people worldwide [2], but also serve as valuable model organisms in fields such as immunology, metabolic diseases, and environmental adaptation [3]. With the rapid advancement of genomic technologies, researchers have gradually uncovered the molecular mechanisms underlying genetic diversity, adaptive evolution, and the formation of economically important traits in pigs. These studies contribute to improving the productivity of pigs and provide vital insights into the genetic basis of complex traits [4].

With the continuous advancement of sequencing technologies and analytical algorithms, there has been increasing attention on the role of structural variants (SVs) in genetics. SVs are defined as genomic alterations of 50 bp or larger, including insertions, deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations, with insertions and deletions comprising the majority of observed SVs [5, 6]. SVs encompass larger genomic regions and can directly influence gene structure, expression regulation, and function [7]. For instance, they may alter the sequence, splicing, or copy number of a gene, or modify the position or composition of cis-regulatory sequences [8]. Moreover, SVs play a crucial role in species adaptation and the formation of complex traits. Studies have shown that many traits, such as reproduction, disease resistance, and environmental adaptability, are closely associated with SVs [9, 10].

In recent years, the rapid development of short-read sequencing technologies has enabled comprehensive studies of SNPs and indels in pigs. However, due to the limitations of second-generation sequencing technologies and the lack of publicly available SVs reference panels, the detection and genotyping of SVs have long been challenging, leaving their potential in genomic research largely untapped [11, 12]. Long-read sequencing technologies, such as Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), have advanced considerably in recent years, emerging as one of the primary technologies for studying SVs [13]. These long-read sequencing platforms offer significant advantages for SVs detection due to their ability to generate reads that span entire structural variants, even in repetitive or complex genomic regions [14]. This capability not only improves the accuracy of SVs identification but also enables the precise characterization of breakpoints and the resolution of complex SVs, such as nested insertions, inversions, and large-scale duplications [15]. In addition, previous studies were constrained by limited sample sizes and insufficient breed representation, which hindered a comprehensive assessment of SVs in global pig populations. One such study constructed an SVs catalog using 1,060 short-read whole-genome resequencing datasets, but the absence of long-read data limited the resolution and accuracy of SVs detection [16]. Therefore, expanding the scope of research to include broader and more representative datasets, especially those incorporating long-read data, is essential for gaining deeper insights into the role of SVs in shaping genetic diversity and adaptive evolution in pigs.

Here, we constructed the Pig Structural Variant Reference Panel (PSVRP) by integrating 21 long-read and 1,193 short-read whole-genome resequencing datasets representing 74 pig breeds worldwide. In this study we focused on insertions and deletions, which are the most common and reliably detected SVs classes. Through advanced variant calling pipelines, stringent quality control, and comparative validation with SNPs and indels datasets, we systematically examined the population structure, phylogenetic relationships, and adaptive traits associated with SVs by genotyping 317 individuals from 56 pig breeds using the PSVRP. Our work not only establishes a high-resolution SVs reference panel for pigs but also offers novel insights into the genetic basis of important adaptive and morphological traits.

Methods

Sample collection and data generation

A total of 1,214 whole-genome sequencing (WGS) datasets were used to construct the PSVRP, including 21 long-read and 1,193 short-read datasets. These comprised both newly generated data from our laboratory and publicly available datasets retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) and the CNCB Genome Sequence Archive (GSA). For downstream population analyses, we selected 317 short-read datasets from 56 pig breeds to maximize geographic and breed diversity, while ensuring average sequencing depth (> 17×) and data quality.

Read processing

Long-read sequencing datasets were aligned to the reference genome (Sus scrofa 11.1) using Minimap2 (v2.28) [17] with default parameters. Sequence Alignment Map (SAM) files were converted to Binary Alignment Map (BAM) format using SAMtools (v0.1.17) [18]. SVs were called using Sniffles (v2.2) [19] retaining only insertions and deletions ranging from 50 bp to 100 kb. SVs call sets were merged across samples using SURVIVOR (v1.0.7) [20] with a merge distance of 50 bp, a minimum distance threshold of 1 kb, and strict type matching.

Short-read sequencing datasets underwent quality control using fastp (v0.23.4) (v0.23.4) [21] with default parameters. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (Sus scrofa 11.1) using NVIDIA Parabricks (v4.2.1-1.1.1) with HaplotypeCaller [22]. BAM files were generated, and PCR duplicates were removed using SAMtools (v0.1.17) [18].

For the 1,193 short-read samples used in panel construction, SVs were detected using Manta (v1.6.0) [23] Only high-confidence insertions and deletions between 50 bp and 1 Mb with the “PASS” flag were retained. SVs call sets across these samples were merged with SURVIVOR (v1.0.7) [20] generating the short-read SVs dataset for panel integration.

For the 317 short-read samples used in downstream analyses, SNPs and indels were called with HaplotypeCaller (Parabricks v4.2.1-1.1.1), and GVCF files were merged with Sentieon (v202308) [24] to generate a unified SNPs/indels dataset. In parallel, SVs were detected from BAM files using Manta [23], filtered using the same criteria as above, and merged across samples with SURVIVOR (v1.0.7) [20], producing a Manta-based SVs dataset.

The PSVRP construction

The PSVRP was constructed using whole-genome resequencing data from 1,214 samples, encompassing Asian domestic pigs (AD), Asian wild boars (AW), European domestic pigs (ED), European wild boars (EW).

The final panel was obtained by integrating the short-read SVs set (1,193 samples) and the long-read SVs set (21 samples) using SURVIVOR (v1.0.7) [20] with optimized parameters (merge distance: 50 bp, minimum distance: 1 kb, strand and type matching).

SVs genotyping

To evaluate population-scale SVs, the 317 short-read samples representing 56 pig breeds were genotyped using the Paragraph (v2.4a) [25] pipeline, with the PSVRP serving as the reference panel. The resulting genotypes were first merged across samples using SURVIVOR (v1.0.7) [20] The merged call set was then filtered to retain only high-confidence insertions and deletions between 50 bp and 100 kb that were marked with the “PASS” flag, producing the final Paragraph-based SVs dataset. The potential functional effects of SVs identified by the Manta and Paragraph pipelines were assessed with the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEPtools v113) [26].

Population structure and phylogenetic analyses

To analyze the SVs results in our study compared with the SNPs and indels results, we used PLINK (v1.9) to calculate the average share allele distance matrix between individuals (--distance-matrix) [27]. Besides, the Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the PLINK (module: PCA), The Neighbor-Joining (NJ) tree was constructed by MEGA X (v11.0.11) [28] and visualized with iTOL (v7.0) [29]. The population genetic structure was examined using the ADMIXTURE (v1.23) [30] with K values (the putative number of populations) from 2 to 10.

Genome scanning for selective signals

The fixation index (Fst), Nucleotide diversity (π) were calculated by VCFtools (v0.1.16) [31] with 50 kb sliding windows and a step size of 25 kb. Regions in the top 1% of the distribution were designated as candidate selective regions, and genes overlapping these regions were identified as candidate genes.

Gene functions annotation

For gene function annotation, Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were performed using g: Profiler (v2025_update) [32]. Only pathways with adjusted P < 0.05 were retained as statistically significant results. Functional predictions of candidate genes were conducted using the PigBiobank database [33].

Statistical analyses

All differences between groups were analysed using unpaired or paired Student’s t-test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; significance is denoted as P < 0.05 and P < 0.01. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). * P < 0.05. ** P < 0.01. *** P < 0.001. **** P < 0.0001.

Result

Construction and annotation of PSVRP

Following systematic data collection and rigorous quality control, we established the PSVRP through curating 1,214 whole-genome resequencing datasets, including 21 long-read and 1,193 short-read sequencing datasets (Supplementary Table S1). To ensure compatibility across different sequencing platforms, SVs were uniformly processed using an integrated pipeline prior to downstream analysis. The resulting SVs dataset focused on insertions and deletions ranging from 50 bp to 100 kb (Fig. 1), which represent the most prevalent and technically reliable SVs classes. The final dataset comprised 319,058 SVs, including 196,620 insertions and 122,438 deletions (Supplementary Figure S1a). Functional annotation revealed a distinct genomic distribution, with 44.8% of variants located in intergenic regions and 42.0% in intronic regions (Supplementary Figure S1b). In addition, SVs density plots showed that SVs tend to be more concentrated at both ends of most chromosomes (Supplementary Figure S1c).

Structural variants analysis

The 317 samples were chosen to ensure broad breed representation and geographic coverage, with an average sequencing depth greater than 17× and consistently high data quality (Fig. 3a) (Supplementary Table S2). After genotyping with the PSVRP-based Paragraph method and subsequent merging using SURVIVOR, a total of 305,299 high-confidence SVs were retained, including 188,851 insertions and 116,448 deletions. These SVs exhibited average lengths of 1,836 bp for insertions and 1,209 bp for deletions, with corresponding median lengths of 303 bp and 288 bp (Fig. 2b). In contrast, the Manta method detected 194,061 SVs comprising 76,392 insertions and 117,669 deletions. The average SVs lengths in this set were 205 bp for insertions and 1,924 bp for deletions, with median values of 270 bp and 299 bp respectively (Fig. 2c) (Supplementary Table S3). Notably, the Paragraph method demonstrated enhanced sensitivity for detecting large insertions compared to Manta, identifying 57,431 insertion events exceeding 1 kb in length (Fig. 2a). Regarding genomic distribution patterns, both methods showed similar localization trends, with the majority of SVs mapping to intronic and intergenic regions—accounting for 81.9% and 86.7% of total SVs calls for Manta and Paragraph respectively (Fig. 2d, e). Within coding regions, Manta identified 27,025 SVs, of which 90.3% were annotated as coding sequence variants. The remaining 9.7% were classified into other functional categories, including frameshift variant, inframe insertion, and inframe deletion. Similarly, Paragraph identified 5,757 SVs in coding regions, with 60.1% annotated as coding sequence variants and the remainder falling into the same functional categories (Supplementary Figure S2a, S2b). SVs with missing rate > 10% were excluded to ensure only high-confidence SVs were used in allele frequency analysis. A total of 126,229 variants were retained, among which 49.2% had an allele frequency below 0.05, indicating that nearly half of the detected SVs are rare variants (Supplementary Figure S2c).

Population structure analyses

After quality filtering (genotyping rate > 0.99, MAF > 0.05, HWE p > 1 × 10⁻¹⁰), 35,449 SVs were retained for population analysis, 433 were insertions and 35,016 were deletions. The small number of insertions reflects the fact that large insertions tend to occur as rare variants with low allele frequencies, many of which are filtered out during quality control. Phylogenetic trees, PCA, and ADMIXTURE consistently separated Asian and European pigs into distinct clusters (Fig. 3b, c and e). The cross-validation (CV) error values for K = 2 to K = 10 are provided in Supplementary Table S4. In contrast, SVs detected by Manta exhibited reduced clustering resolution, reinforcing the methodological advantages of Paragraph. We also integrated multiple variant types: 1,687,843 SNPs (Supplementary Figure S3) and 1,381,423 indels (Supplementary Figure S4) were retained after quality filtering and used in population genetic analysis. Consistent with the clustering patterns observed for Paragraph-derived SVs, these variant classes collectively confirmed the genetic divergence between European and Asian pigs (Fig. 2f). We also calculated the Fst and the π of between different populations based on the SVs detected in the Paragraph. The results showed that the Fst between AD and AW populations was the lowest, while the π of AW was the highest (Fig. 3d). The analysis of SVs revealed distinct breed-specific patterns in Asian and European pigs, underscoring their potential for uncovering traits associated with specific breeds.

Selection signature analysis of pig body size

Body size traits in pigs hold significant value for both agricultural optimization and biomedical studies [34]. In our study, a total of 60 pig accessions were analyzed and categorized into Normal and Mini group (Supplementary Table S5).

The Fst analysis based on SVs revealed an average value of 0.20 and a maximum of 1.0, with the top 1% threshold established at 0.78. Under the top 1% Fst threshold, 701 candidate genomic regions were identified, containing 642 genes (including both annotated and LOC-prefixed genes) associated with body size differentiation. SVs with the highest Fst values were located near the LPP and NEDD4 genes, while π analysis revealed higher genetic diversity in mini group (1.1 × 10⁻⁵) than in normal group (9.5 × 10⁻⁶) (Fig. 4b, c). The Fst analysis of based on SNPs demonstrated an average value of 0.32 and a maximum of 0.95, with the top 1% threshold set at 0.70 (Supplementary Figure S5a). Consistent with the SVs-based results, π analysis based on SNPs also showed higher diversity in the mini group (3.4 × 10⁻³) relative to the normal group (2.3 × 10⁻³) (Supplementary Figure S5b). Across SVs and SNPs, three genes (BBS9, CEP128, and TOX) were consistently detected. The result highlights the potential regulatory roles of these genes in the molecular mechanisms underlying pig body size determination (Fig. 4a). According to SVs-based Fst analysis, MIB1 and PLAG1 showed Fst values of 0.789 and 0.697, respectively, both of which exceed the top 1% threshold and indicate strong genetic differentiation. Haplotype analysis revealed distinct patterns between groups at the MIB1 locus in both SVs and SNPs datasets, whereas PLAG1 haplotype differentiation was primarily evident in SNPs (Fig. 4e, g). Importantly, within the MIB1 gene, we identified a 85,663 bp deletion on chromosome 6 (position 106,973,448) that was present in 73.3% of miniature pigs but absent in the normal group. This SV provides compelling evidence for a functional role of MIB1 in body size regulation (Fig. 4f). PigBiobank analyses further validated MIB1 associations with feed intake and growth (Fig. 4d). Focusing on results based on SVs, Fst and π analyses identified 481 and 461 candidate genes, respectively (Supplementary Table S6). GO enrichment of these genes highlighted biological processes related to organ development and cell growth, consistent with the phenotypic divergence observed between miniature and normal-sized pigs. (Fig. 4h, i).

Selection signature analysis of pig high-altitude adaptability

Pig breeds in high-altitude regions, such as the Tibetan pig, typically exhibit distinctive resilience traits, including hypoxia tolerance, cold resistance, and enhanced disease resistance [35]. In our study, a total of 90 pig accessions were analyzed and categorized into high-altitude (HA) and low-altitude (LA) group (Supplementary Table S5).

The Fst analysis based on SVs revealed an average value of 0.05 and a maximum of 0.69, with the top 1% threshold established at 0.28. Under the top 1% Fst threshold, 652 candidate genomic regions were identified, containing 529 genes (including both annotated and LOC-prefixed genes) associated with pig high-altitude adaptability. Comparative nucleotide diversity (π) analysis using SVs demonstrated the π value for the HA group was (1.2 × 10⁻⁵), and the π value for the LA group was (1.1 × 10⁻⁵) (Fig. 5b, c). The Fst analysis of based on SNPs demonstrated an average value of 0.07 and a maximum of 0.44, with the top 1% threshold set at 0.21. The π analysis based on SNPs showed the π value for the HA group was (3.2 × 10⁻³), and the π value for the LA group was (3.3 × 10⁻³) (Supplementary Figure S6a, S6b). Four genes, CNTN5, DGKG, FNDC3B and PTPRD, were commonly identified by all four methods (Fig. 5a). EPAS1 was identified in the top 1% of regions based on π analysis using SVs, suggesting its potential role in the adaptation of pigs to high-altitude environments. Importantly, NOVA1 exhibited clear haplotype differentiation between HA and LA pigs in both SVs and SNPs datasets (Fig. 5e, g). Within the NOVA1 locus, we identified a 312 bp deletion (position 72,852,338), which occurred at a frequency of 91.1% in HA pigs but only 24.4% in LA pigs (Fig. 5f). This variant likely represents a functional allele associated with high-altitude adaptation. (Fig. 5f). PigBiobank analyses further validated NOVA1 associations with reproduction (Fig. 5d). Focusing on results based on SVs, Fst and π analyses identified 391 and 611 candidate genes, respectively (Supplementary Table S7). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these candidate genes were enriched for pathways related to response to environmental stimuli and organ development, providing functional evidence for their roles in environmental adaptation. (Fig. 5h, i).

Discussion

Despite recent advances in pig genomics, SVs remain a largely underutilized class of genetic variation in functional and evolutionary studies. This study is the first effort to integrate both long-read and short-read whole-genome sequencing data on a large scale to build a publicly available SVs panel for pigs. This integrative approach enables deeper exploration of SVs contributions to population structure and complex traits such as body size and high-altitude adaptability.

PSVRP construction and SVs detection

By integrating 21 long-read and 1,193 short-read sequencing datasets, this panel addresses the limitations of traditional short-read sequencing in detecting SVs in repetitive or complex genomic regions and enhances both the resolution and representativeness of SVs genotyping. Compared to conventional short-read-based tools like Manta, the use of Paragraph in conjunction with PSVRP demonstrated superior performance, particularly in detecting large insertions that are often missed by other methods. The higher proportion of coding-region SVs detected by Manta may be due to its greater sensitivity to small variants, which are more likely to occur in gene-dense regions, and its limited ability to detect large structural events that often span non-coding regions. In contrast, Paragraph uses a predefined variant set and stricter genotyping criteria, leading to more accurate calls. Although the Paragraph method offers advantages in genotyping complex SVs, it remains limited by its reliance on precise predefined variant sets. The PSVRP, as an accurate and comprehensive variant resource, effectively captures biologically significant SVs, particularly complex insertions and deletions that are often overlooked by short-read methods, and thus compensates for the limitations of Paragraph [36]. Our study revealed that nearly half of the detected SVs had an allele frequency below 0.05, highlighting that most SVs occur only a few times in the population and thus represent low-frequency or rare variants. Rare SVs, despite their low population frequencies, may exert large effect sizes and contribute disproportionately to phenotypic variation. Their identification within the PSVRP offers valuable opportunities to elucidate associations between rare alleles and economically important traits. Nevertheless, similar to published findings, a subset of SVs approached fixation, suggesting potential roles in long-term evolutionary processes [16]. SVs genotyping using the PSVRP captured biologically meaningful variants while reducing false positives, especially in complex or repetitive regions, laying a solid foundation for SVs-based research in livestock.

Analysis of pig body size and High-Altitude adaptability

Leveraging the PSVRP, we investigated SVs associated with two economically and biologically significant traits: body size and high-altitude adaptation. Population differentiation analyses using Fst and π revealed candidate genomic regions with distinct SVs between phenotypic groups.

For body size, genes such as PLAG1 and MIB1 emerged as strong candidates. The role of PLAG1 in growth regulation is conserved across species, with murine knockout models showing developmental defects [37] and human Genome-wide association study (GWAS) linking it to height [38]. MIB1 is a regulator of the Notch pathway [39], nd has previously been associated with rib number in pigs [40]. In this study, we identified a 85,663 bp deletion within MIB1 that was prevalent in miniature pigs but absent in normal-sized pigs. This SV suggests a novel sight for body size variation in pigs and provides population-level evidence supporting the functional importance of MIB1 in growth regulation. In MIB1 knockout mice, impaired Notch signaling leads to significant developmental defects, underscoring its essential role in embryogenesis and tissue homeostasis [41]. Additionally, we also identified candidate genes associated with biological development processes, such as NR6A1 (involved in mammalian trunk development) [42], FGF7, BBS9, AXIN1, LMX1A and MAST4 (associated with bone development) [43–47].

For high-altitude adaptation, EPAS1 and NOVA1 emerged as key candidate genes.

EPAS1 plays a central role in regulating oxygen homeostasis [48]. This gene has been functionally validated in humans, cattle, sheep, and pigs for its involvement in enhancing erythropoiesis and vascular adaptation under hypoxic conditions [49–52]. NOVA1 encodes a splicing regulator critical for neurodevelopment [53, 54], and is potentially linked to reproductive function under hypoxia, as oxygen deprivation can impair hormone synthesis and fertility [55]. Within NOVA1, we identified a 312 bp deletion that occurred at a frequency of 91.1% in HA pigs but only 24.4% in LA pigs. This population-level differentiation strongly suggests that the deletion may contribute to adaptation under hypoxic conditions. The results of PheWAS and previous study further supports a correlation between NOVA1 and reproductive traits [56], suggesting a role in environmental adaptation. Other candidate genes related to high-altitude adaptation include CNTN5 (associated with immunity) [57], CCNC and DGKG (associated with lipid metabolism and accumulation) [58, 59].

These findings provide SVs-level evidence for the genetic basis of body size and high-altitude resilience in pigs, providing deeper insights into the genetic factors underlying mammalian development. Beyond trait associations, our study underscores the functional breadth of SVs. While 44.8% of SVs localized to intergenic regions—hinting at widespread regulatory impacts—coding SVs were enriched for frameshifts and inframe indels, suggesting direct effects on protein function. The PSVRP can also be incorporated into genomic selection frameworks to improve prediction accuracy, particularly for traits influenced by SVs. In addition, it serves as a valuable reference resource for the identification of candidate SVs relevant to precision breeding.

Conclusion

In summary, we established the PSVRP as a valuable genomic resource by integrating 21 long-read and 1,193 short-read whole-genome sequencing datasets, effectively addressing previous limitations in detecting SVs across complex regions of the pig genome. Utilizing the PSVRP, we achieved high-resolution and accurate SVs genotyping, surpassing conventional tools such as Manta, particularly in identifying large-scale insertions. This approach enabled the discovery of SVs associated with key traits, including body size differentiation and high-altitude adaptation. Our study highlights the pivotal role of SVs in pig genome research and provides a foundation for advancing precision breeding and genetic improvement in livestock.
