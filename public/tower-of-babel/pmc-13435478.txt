The Arabic nasal obstruction symptom evaluation scale (Arabic NOSE): validation and severity categorization in Middle Eastern population
Authors: Turki Aldrees, Basil Hassouneh, Zaid Almubarak, Saad Alsaleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to adapt the NOSE instrument into the Arabic language using a cohort that reflects the linguistic and cultural diversity of Middle Eastern nationalities and dialects. Additionally, the study aimed to evaluate score distribution and propose a preliminary severity categorization using a Middle Eastern cohort.MethodsThe NOSE scale was translated into Arabic using a forward–backward translation process. Adult native Arabic speakers (≥ 16 years) were recruited from Otolaryngology and Facial Plastic Surgery clinics. Participants included patients with nasal obstruction and asymptomatic controls with normal endoscopic examinations. Construct validity was assessed by correlations with patient-reported visual analog scale (VAS) scores (0–10 scale, where 0 indicates no obstruction and 10 indicates maximal obstruction). Internal consistency, test–retest reliability, responsiveness to surgical intervention, and score discrimination between groups were evaluated. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine the optimal cutoff score.ResultsA total of 235 participants completed the Arabic NOSE scale, including 182 patients with nasal obstruction and 53 normal controls. The Arabic NOSE score was significantly higher in patients with nasal obstruction than in controls (62.9 ± 22.2 vs. 7.8 ± 7.6; p < 0.001). The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92) and strong test–retest reliability (r = 0.93). Arabic NOSE scores correlated strongly with patient-reported VAS severity (r = 0.90) and surgeon-assessed severity (r = 0.83). ROC analysis identified a cutoff score of < 25 as optimal for distinguishing normal from symptomatic participants (sensitivity 96.2%, specificity 94.3%). Among surgical patients, mean NOSE scores improved significantly from 66.9 preoperatively to 10.9 postoperatively (mean change 56.0; p < 0.001), demonstrating high responsiveness. The effect size for pre- to post-operative change was large (Cohen’s d = 2.5).ConclusionsThe Arabic NOSE scale demonstrated excellent reliability, validity, and responsiveness for assessing nasal obstruction in Arabic-speaking populations. A cutoff score of < 25 demonstrated excellent discrimination between asymptomatic individuals and patients with nasal obstruction within this cohort. The proposed severity classification and ROC-derived threshold are preliminary and require prospective external validation in independent Arabic-speaking populations before widespread clinical adoption.Keywords: Nasal obstruction, NOSE scale, Arabic validation, Quality of life, Septoplasty, Rhinoplasty, Patient-reported outcome measures

Introduction

Nasal obstruction is a common presentation in Otolaryngology and Facial Plastic Surgery. Deviated nasal septum (with or without middle vault involvement), hypertrophy of the nasal turbinates, and nasal valve insufficiency are the most frequent etiologies in adults. Nasal obstruction has a significant impact on sleep, exercise tolerance, and overall quality of life. Therefore, accurate evaluation of the severity of nasal obstruction and its responsiveness to both medical and surgical interventions is critical.

Several methods have been used to evaluate nasal obstruction [1, 2]. Early investigations focused on objective physical measurements of nasal airflow, such as acoustic rhinometry and rhinomanometry. However, these methods failed to demonstrate a strong correlation with the severity of clinical symptoms [3, 4].The development of the Nasal Obstruction Symptom Evaluation (NOSE) scale represented a substantial advancement [5]. The NOSE scale is a disease-specific quality-of-life instrument composed of five items designed to assess the severity of nasal obstruction symptoms and their impact on daily activities. The NOSE scale has demonstrated good reliability in assessing symptom severity and proven responsiveness to surgical treatments such as septoplasty and functional rhinoplasty [6]. Several studies have evaluated the clinical applicability of NOSE scores in identifying symptomatic patients, grading severity, and establishing the minimal clinically significant difference following surgical intervention [7].

The increasing clinical importance of the NOSE scale has generated wide international interest, resulting in adaptations into several languages, including French, Dutch, Spanish, Greek, Italian, and Chinese [8–13]. The objective of this study was to rigorously adapt the NOSE instrument into the Arabic language using a multinational cohort reflecting linguistic and dialectal diversity across the Middle East. Unlike prior single-center Arabic validations, this study also aimed to establish a preliminary clinically meaningful severity categorization based on score distribution and ROC-derived thresholds to enhance clinical applicability.

Methods

Design

This was a prospective observational validation study using both cross-sectional (validity and reliability) and longitudinal (responsiveness) components.

Translation

The translation committee (SA, TA, BH) consisted of clinicians with high proficiency in both English and Arabic and active practices in Rhinology and Facial Plastic Surgery. First, forward translation of the original English NOSE scale into Arabic was performed independently by two professional translators. The two translated versions were piloted and reviewed by the translation committee, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus to produce a single unified version. Second, a backward translation from Arabic to English was carried out by a separate translator and reviewed against the original English NOSE scale to ensure conceptual equivalence. Both translators used in this study were blinded to the study objectives. One translator had a medical background, while the other was from non-medical linguistic background. Prior to translation, cognitive debriefing and pilot testing were conducted among native Arabic speakers to ensure cultural appropriateness, clarity, and comprehension across dialects.

Participants

Ethical approval was obtained from the King Saud University Ethics Committee (E-15-1700). Participants with nasal obstruction were recruited as consecutive patients presenting to Otolaryngology or Facial Plastic Surgery clinics. Recruitment was carried out in both academic and private practices in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, and Dubai, United Arab Emirates, between September 2015 and May 2017.

All participants were required to be 16 years of age or older and native Arabic speakers. Additional criteria for each group were as follows:

Inclusion criteria for the nasal obstruction group

Primary complaint of nasal obstruction.

Primary complaint of nasal obstruction.

Endoscopic nasal examination consistent with septal deviation, turbinate hypertrophy, or nasal valve insufficiency as the primary cause of nasal obstruction.

The diagnostic criteria for nasal valve insufficiency have been previously described [14]. Diagnosis was based on clinical examination by the surgeon demonstrating:

Moderate-to-severe obstruction on one or more of the following assessments: lateral wall collapse, supratip or middle vault pinching, alar rim collapse, or a narrow internal nasal valve angle.

Moderate-to-severe obstruction on one or more of the following assessments: lateral wall collapse, supratip or middle vault pinching, alar rim collapse, or a narrow internal nasal valve angle.

Absence of significant septal deviation or turbinate hypertrophy contributing to obstruction.

Absence of significant septal deviation or turbinate hypertrophy contributing to obstruction.

Inclusion criteria for the normal group

No nasal or sinonasal complaints.

No nasal or sinonasal complaints.

Normal endoscopic nasal examination.

Normal endoscopic nasal examination.

Control participants were volunteers or clinic attendees without any nasal complaints and normal endoscopic findings. This step minimized potential selection bias.

NOSE scale performance

Participants rated their overall nasal obstruction severity using a visual analog scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (no obstruction) to 10 (maximum obstruction). Correlations between these assessments and NOSE scores were evaluated. The ability of the Arabic NOSE to differentiate between normal participants and patients with nasal obstruction was assessed. Score distribution and the optimal cutoff point for distinguishing normal from symptomatic participants were also evaluated.

Internal consistency of the Arabic NOSE scale was examined. To assess test-retest reliability, randomly selected participants completed the NOSE scale again after a two-week interval without any intervention. Responsiveness to change was evaluated in surgical candidates by administering the NOSE scale three months following surgical intervention.

Statistical analysis

A sample size of 200 participants was planned to allow adequate representation (minimum of 25 participants) across symptom severity categories (normal, mild, moderate, and severe) and etiologies (septal deviation, turbinate hypertrophy, and nasal valve insufficiency). These were grouped because they were the most common causes of nasal obstruction and it was appropriate to be evaluated using the NOSE scale. Moreover, the instrument is symptom-based rather than etiology-specific. Descriptive statistics were reported as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Independent-samples t-tests were used to evaluate the ability of the NOSE scale to differentiate between normal and nasal obstruction groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to identify the optimal cutoff point distinguishing the two groups. The sample size for test–retest reliability (n = 27) is consistent with recommended methodological standards for reliability studies.

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, and correlations were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Responsiveness to change was assessed using paired t-tests comparing preoperative and postoperative scores. Statistical analysis was carried out using Stata version 11 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Translation

Forward translation revealed only minor wording discrepancies between the two versions. These were resolved by consensus based on clarity and readability among a diverse group of native Arabic speakers from the Middle East. Backward translation demonstrated high fidelity to the original English NOSE scale, with only minor wording variations. The final Arabic NOSE scale is presented in Fig. 1.

Arabic nasal obstruction symptom evaluation scale (Arabic NOSE)

Participants

Participants were recruited from Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates through both academic and private practices. A total of 235 consecutive participants completed the Arabic NOSE scale. Participant nationalities included Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, Iraq, Egypt, Libya, Morocco, and Tunisia.

The mean participant age was 30.4 years (SD 9.4; range 16–75 years). There were 162 males (69%) and 73 females (31%). The cohort included 53 normal participants (22.5%) and 182 patients with nasal obstruction (77.5%). The distribution of primary etiologies of nasal obstruction is shown in Fig. 2.

Distribution of primary etiology for nasal obstruction

Sixty-eight patients underwent surgical intervention to correct the primary cause of nasal obstruction. Of these, 28 (41%) underwent septoplasty, 9 (13%) turbinoplasty, 20 (29%) septo-turbinoplasty, and 11 (16%) rhinoplasty. All participants completed all five items of the Arabic NOSE scale.

The mean Arabic NOSE score was 7.8 (SD 7.6; range 0–25) in the normal group and 62.9 (SD 22.2; range 5–100) in the nasal obstruction group. Score distribution is shown in Fig. 3.

Distribution of Arabic NOSE scores for normal and nasal obstruction groups

Arabic NOSE performance

The Arabic NOSE scale effectively differentiated the normal group (mean 7.8; 95% CI 5.8–9.9) from the nasal obstruction group (mean 62.9; 95% CI 59.6–66.1; p < 0.001). The ROC curve is presented in Fig. 4, demonstrating excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.97). A cutoff score of < 25 provided optimal discrimination between normal and symptomatic participants, with a sensitivity of 96.2% and specificity of 94.3%.

Receiver operating characteristic curve for NOSE. The graph shows the ability of the NOSE score to correctly identify nasal obstruction patients from normal participants. Sensitivity and specificity are provided for various cutoff points

There was a strong correlation between Arabic NOSE scores and participant-reported VAS severity (r = 0.90). Correlation with surgeon-assessed severity was slightly lower (r = 0.83). Score distributions according to VAS and surgeon severity assessment are shown in Fig. 5.

A Distribution of NOSE score according to the visual analog scale. B Distribution of NOSE score according to the surgeon’s assessment of severity

Internal consistency of the Arabic NOSE scale was excellent (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92). Test-retest reliability was assessed in 27 participants and demonstrated strong stability over time (Pearson’s r = 0.93, p < 0.001). Among the 68 surgical patients, mean preoperative NOSE score was 66.9 (95% CI 62.1–71.7), which improved to 10.9 (95% CI 7.9–13.9) postoperatively. The mean paired change was 56.0 points (95% CI 50.3–61.8; p < 0.001), corresponding to a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 2.5). This effect size should be interpreted with caution, as the responsiveness analysis was confined to surgically treated patients — a selected subgroup likely characterised by greater symptom severity and more pronounced anatomical indications for intervention. The observed magnitude of change may therefore overestimate the responsiveness of the instrument across the broader nasal obstruction population.

Discussion

In this study, the NOSE scale was translated into Arabic using a rigorous forward-backward translation process, followed by pilot testing to ensure clarity and comprehension. The backward translation demonstrated excellent consistency with the original English NOSE, indicating high fidelity of translation. Careful consideration was given to dialectical and linguistic differences across Middle Eastern countries. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates host large expatriate Arabic-speaking populations, which was reflected in the inclusion of participants from ten different nationalities. All participants were able to complete the scale, demonstrating excellent readability and comprehension. Pilot testing assessed readability and comprehension across participants from multiple Arabic speaking nationalities.

The Arabic NOSE scale demonstrated excellent ability to distinguish between normal participants and patients with nasal obstruction, as well as high responsiveness to surgical intervention. These findings are consistent with the performance of the original English NOSE reported in previous validation studies.

An important aspect of this study was the evaluation of symptom severity classification and determination of an optimal cutoff score. Rhee et al. conducted a systematic review evaluating NOSE score distributions across multiple studies and suggested that such data may help stratify nasal airway obstruction severity [15]. However, definitive cutoff points were not proposed due to heterogeneity among studies. Lipan et al. later suggested severity categories based on score distribution but acknowledged overlap between asymptomatic and mildly symptomatic individuals [7].

In the present study, normal participants were prospectively recruited and confirmed by normal endoscopic examination. This approach allowed for accurate differentiation between asymptomatic individuals and those with mild symptoms. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that a cutoff score of < 25 provided optimal separation between normal and symptomatic participants. Furthermore, prior work by Ziai et al. identified a minimum clinically significant difference of 25 points following septoplasty [16]. Therefore, individuals scoring near this threshold should not be considered asymptomatic.

Based on the findings, we propose a preliminary severity classification, as follows: normal (0–20), mild (21–45), moderate (46–75), and severe (76–100). The revised categories eliminate score gaps and provide continuous, non-overlapping ranges, that may improve clinical usability and interpretability. Importantly, both the severity categories and the ROC-derived cutoff of < 25 were derived from and evaluated within the same cohort. This derivation-only design may overestimate diagnostic performance, and these thresholds must be considered preliminary pending prospective external validation in independent Arabic-speaking cohorts before adoption in routine clinical practice.

A recently published Egyptian validation study offered important foundational insights into the adoption of the Arabic NOSE scale for Arabic-speaking patients [17]. The present study expanded the work of the Egyptian validation study by incorporating a broader multinational cohort and emphasizing cross-dialectal adaptation, which may improve applicability across diverse Arabic-speaking populations.

This study included patients across the full spectrum of nasal obstruction severity and etiologies, including nasal valve insufficiency—an important distinction from studies limited to septoplasty populations. Given the prevalence of functional and aesthetic rhinoplasty in the Middle East, the availability of a validated Arabic NOSE scale is essential.

This study has several limitations. First, although the cohort included participants from multiple nationalities, recruitment was limited to two countries, which may affect generalizability. Specifically, although participants represented ten Arabic-speaking nationalities, all recruitment occurred within Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates. The majority of non-Saudi and non-Emirati participants were expatriate residents seeking care in Gulf healthcare settings, rather than patients recruited within their countries of origin. Healthcare-seeking behaviour, surgical referral patterns, and patient profiles in Gulf subspecialty clinics may differ from those in North African, Levantine, or Iraqi healthcare systems, limiting generalizability beyond the Gulf region. Future multicentre validation spanning diverse Arabic-speaking healthcare systems is therefore recommended. Second, grouping different etiologies of nasal obstruction may introduce clinical heterogeneity. Third, the sample size for test–retest reliability was relatively small. Additionally, test-retest reliability was evaluated via Pearson’s correlation coefficient rather than intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC is the methodologically preferred measure for absolute agreement in validation studies of clinical instruments, as it accounts for both consistency and systematic bias between administrations, unlike Pearson’s r which reflects only linear association. In the present case, raw paired data were not available to calculate ICC retrospectively. Future validation studies should prospectively collect test–retest data suitable for ICC analysis to provide a more complete and rigorous assessment of agreement. Fourth, although professional translators and clinicians were involved, the absence of a formally trained linguistic academic may be considered a limitation. Furthermore, the responsiveness analysis was limited to surgical patients, who may represent a selected subgroup with more severe symptoms or anatomical indications for intervention, potentially contributing to the large observed effect size. Accordingly, the Cohen’s d of 2.5 should not be generalised as the expected effect size in medically treated or less severely affected patient populations; responsiveness in such groups warrants dedicated prospective evaluation. Finally, long-term responsiveness beyond three months was not assessed.

In conclusion, the Arabic NOSE scale represents an important contribution to the literature and will facilitate regional and international collaboration in assessing functional nasal outcomes. The revised severity categorization may further improve clinical decision-making and outcome assessment across diverse patient populations; however, widespread adoption of the proposed severity thresholds and ROC-derived cutoff should be contingent on prospective external validation in independent Arabic-speaking cohorts.
