Rice varietal intercropping mediates resistance to rice blast (Magnaporthe Oryzae) through core root exudates
Authors: Guang-Yu Han, Rong-Ping Li, Xiao-Qiao Zhu, Pieter-Willem Hendriks, Min Tang, Bin Li, Yong Xie, Qing-An Zan, Yun-Yue Wang, Ping He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRice blast (caused by Magnaporthe oryzae) severely limits rice production worldwide and poses an ongoing threat to food security. Intercropping of rice varieties shows effective rice blast control but the role of root interactions in mediating resistance remains unclear.ResultsHere we show that intercropping a susceptible and a resistant variety enhances disease resistance in both varieties under field and greenhouse conditions through root-secreted metabolites that activate defense pathways. Transcriptome analysis further showed that intercropping triggered a broad immune response. Metabolome profiling identified systemic changes in root exudate composition, including enrichment of flavonoid/phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways and antifungal metabolites. Correlation analysis linked these metabolites to resistance-regulating genes, leading to the identification of four core compounds: azelaic acid (AzA), sebacic acid (SA), betaine (Bet), and phenyl acetate (PhAc). Functional validation confirms AzA and SA significantly inhibited the mycelial growth of M. oryzae, and exogenous application of all four compounds before inoculated with M. oryzae significantly reduced the rice blast disease incidence under greenhouse conditions.ConclusionThese findings indicate that intercropping can induce resistance in susceptible varieties through the key root exudates, providing insights into the mechanisms by which rice root metabolites mediate resistance to rice blast in intercropping systems.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07724-y.Keywords: Rice, Rice blast resistance, Varietal intercropping, Transcriptome, Metabolome, Root exudates

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is the vital cereal crop and staple food for more than half of the global population [1]. Rice blast (M. oryzae), one of the most destructive diseases worldwide, induces abnormal growth and dysfunction in rice plants, resulting in yield loss up to 80% [2–4]. Varietal intercropping (or cultivar mixtures) is the practice of cultivating two or more crop cultivars simultaneously [5, 6], which has been demonstrated as an effective strategy to mitigate rice blast by avoiding large-scale monoculture and restricting pathogen spread [7–9].

This protective effect is partly explained by a mechanism called neighbor-modulated susceptibility (NMS), whereby neighboring cultivars influence each other’s resistance or susceptibility, ultimately reducing overall disease pressure in rice [10, 11] Efficacy of rice disease control through varietal intercropping rests on complex interactions between rice plants, microorganisms, root metabolites, and pathogens [12–15]. In particular, below-ground interactions play a key role in the sustainability and productivity benefits of intercropping [16–19].

Root metabolites have been identified as signaling molecules enhancing plant resistance and immunity [20]. They act as chemical signals or allelochemicals that directly or indirectly participate in subterranean biotic and abiotic interactions with plant-rhizosphere system, thereby effectively suppressing disease occurrence [21, 22]. In intercropping systems, these metabolites mediate interspecific nutrient absorption among plants, enhancing the systemic resistance of neighboring plants [23]. In faba bean-wheat intercropping, wheat metabolites promoted the growth of faba bean seedlings through elevating the activity of broad bean’s defense enzymes, inhibiting the growth of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. fabae (FOF), and alleviating the occurrence of broad bean wilt [24]. Furthermore, root metabolites also inhibit pathogenic fungi directly [25–28]. In Chinese chive-banana intercropping, the root metabolites of Chinese chive exhibited inhibitory effects on the mycelial growth and spore germination of the banana wilt pathogen [29]. Root metabolites also drive plant interactions to produce chemical signals that suppress disease occurrence [30]. For instance, terpenes and aromatic hydrocarbons from the rhizome and aerial parts of Atractylodes lancea alter the rhizosphere microbial community structure in the A. lancea-peanut intercropping system, promoting peanut roots to secrete phenolic acids and organic acids, which reduce peanut root rot and enhance disease resistance and nutrient absorption [31]. Additionally, root metabolites recruit different microorganisms, altering the soil microbial community structure and inhibiting disease occurrence [32, 33]. In shallots (Allium cepa var. aggregatum)-tomato intercropping, taxifolin in shallots root metabolites changes the chemical properties of tomato root metabolites, indirectly promoting the colonization of specific Bacillus sp. in tomato roots and reducing tomato verticillium wilt [34]. These studies have elucidated the role of root metabolites in belowground interactions among different crops in intercropping systems. However, research on the functions of root exudates in varietal intercropping (within the same crop) remains limited.

Plants’ responses to pathogen infections involve extensive modifications in gene expression and metabolites [35]. Integrated transcriptomics and metabolomics analysis provide an approach to understanding plant defense responses to pathogens at the molecular and cellular levels [36–39]. For instance, combined metabolome and transcriptome analysis has revealed that benzoxazinoids plays a dominant role in wheat resistance against Fusarium crown rot (FCR) [40]. Similarly, eriodictyol has been identified as an essential metabolite in enhancing brown planthopper and rice blast fungus resistance through integrated metabolome and transcriptome analysis [41]. Furthermore, bioformulated chitosan nanoparticles against M. oryzae as demonstrated combined metabolome and transcriptome analysis [42].

However, the mechanisms by which mixed planting of rice varieties enhances the disease resistance of neighboring susceptible rice through resistant varieties, and whether root metabolome mediate this process, remains unclear. Based on our previous results on rice blast control effects through rice varietal intercropping [43], this study aims to further identify the rice blast resistance in different rice combinations and screen intercropping combinations with high blast resistance. Metabolome and transcriptome sequencing will be used to assess how varietal interactions influence root metabolites, gene expression and associated pathways in intercropping systems. Integrated analysis of these datasets will reveal the relationships between changes in root metabolites and differentially expressed genes in rice. Furthermore, both in vivo and in vitro experiments will be conducted to verify the disease control effect of core metabolites on rice blast. From the multi-faceted interaction perspective of “plant-exudate-pathogen”, this study systematically elucidated the pathways and mechanisms by which root metabolome in intercropping systems drive rice resistance to rice blast (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Rice materials and strain sources

Five rice blast-resistant varieties, Shanyou 63, IIyou 838, Hexi 39, Liangyou 1259, and Yunguang 101, were selected to be intercropped with the susceptible variety Huangkenuo. The rice blast strain GUY11 (M. oryzae) was selected for pathogen inoculation [44]. The strains were cultured in potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium, consisting of 200 g of glucose, 10 g of sugar, and 15 g of agar per liter of nuclease-free water, at 28℃ for 7 days. Subsequently, the activated strain was then transferred to a Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB) medium, composed of 200 g of glucose and 10 g of sugar per liter of sterile water, and cultured at 28 °C for 3 days.

A volume of 500μL of the cultured pathogen suspension was then spread onto a tomato oat medium, prepared with 30 g of oatmeal, 150 g of tomato, and 16 g of agar per liter of sterile water, and cultured for 5 days. Following this, the cultured mycelium was scraped away to facilitate the production of spores at 28℃ for 5 days. Subsequently, the spores were washed out using sterilized water to prepare the final spore suspension.

Field trial of rice blast resistance identification of varietal intercropping combinations

The experiment to identify rice blast resistance in five intercropping combinations was conducted from March to October 2022 in Jianshui County, Yunnan, China (23°36'N, 102°46'E), which has a subtropical monsoon climate with an average annual rainfall of 805 mm and annual temperature of 19.8 °C. The soil at the experimental site is classified as red loam (Ultisol) with pH≈6.2 and moderate fertility. Three treatments were established: varietal intercropping (one row of susceptible varieties interplanted with four rows of resistant varieties), monocropping of resistant varieties (eight rows), monocropping of susceptible varieties (eight rows), with an intra-variety row spacing of 16.67 cm and an inter- variety row spacing of 26.67 cm. Each treatment included four groups, with a group spacing of 33.33 cm, and three plots’ replicates were set for each intercropping combination [43]. The occurrence of panicle blast was investigated during the yellow ripe stage, with twelve plants of resistant varieties and susceptible varieties were surveyed separately in the intercropping and monocropping plots. The investigation criteria for rice blast referred to the national standard of China (GB/T 15790–2009). Disease index, incidence rate and control effects were recorded and calculated to select dominant resistant combinations with high efficacy.

Indoor identification of rice blast resistance

Rice seeds of Huangkenuo and Shanyou 63 were sterilized by using 70% alcohol and 2% sodium hypochlorite solution. The rice seeds were then placed in a 28 °C incubator with controlled humidity for 2 days to germinate [45]. The germinated seeds were transplanted into plastic pots filled with complete nutrient solution. The transplanted rice was cultured in an incubator, with a light intensity of 120 μmol·m−2·s−1, 28 °C/25 °C (daytime/nighttime), 60%−70% humidity for 15 days. The rice plants with three leaves were inoculated with the prepared spore suspension (1 × 105/mL) and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h in dark before being transferred to a greenhouse [46]. The inoculated leaves were collected at 0 h, 24 h, 48 h to measure POD activity, ROS content, lignin content, and transcriptome sequencing. After inoculating pathogen for 7 days, the disease severity assessment was conducted according to the standard [47].

Sample collection of root metabolites

Root metabolites were collected from inoculated rice after 24 h inoculation with GUY11. In brief, the roots were rinsed with sterile deionized water for 10 min to fully remove the residual components of the nutrient solution, then the roots were placed in a box containing 100 mL of sterile deionized water (wrapped in tin foil and protected from light) and placed in an incubation environment for 4 h to allow continuous secretion and exudation from the roots. The samples were collected by filtration using a 0.22 μm membrane, labeled, and stored in a refrigerator at −80℃.

Determination of root metabolites by LC–MS/MS

The detection of root metabolites was conducted using an Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatograph (Agilent 7890, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a Mass Spectrometer (QTOF/MS-6545, LECO, St. Joseph, MI, USA), which was completed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The UHPLC separation was performed on an ExionLC System (Sciex Technologies, Redwood City, CA, United States). Detailed technical parameters for the LC–MS/MS setups are comprehensively listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Differential analysis of root metabolites

Principal components analysis (PCA) was performed at R (Version 3.5.1). Partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied univariate analysis (t-test) to calculate the statistical significance (P-value). The metabolites with VIP > 1 and P-value < 0.05 and fold change ≥ 2 or FC ≤ 0.5 were differential abundance metabolites. Volcano plots were used to filter metabolites of interest which based on log2(FoldChange) and -log10(p-value) of metabolites by ggplot2 in R (Version 2.8.0). For clustering heat maps, the data were normalized using z-scores of the intensity areas of differential abundance metabolites and were plotted by ComplexHeatmap in R (Version 2.8.0). Identified metabolites were annotated using KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), annotated metabolites were then mapped to KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Pathways with significantly regulated metabolites mapped were then fed into MSEA (metabolite sets enrichment analysis), their significance was determined by hypergeometric test’s p-values.

RNA extraction isolate and qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from inoculated rice leaves using Trelief® Hi-Pure Plant RNA Kit Hi-Pure Total Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Dual Column) (Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s manual.

RNA-seq analysis was performed to determine the gene expression profile of leaf samples from the varietal combination of Mono-HKN and Inter-HKN, Mono-SY63 and Inter-SY63 at 0 h, 24 h and 48 h after inoculation with pathogen. Each treatment was performed with three biological replicates. The cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform by the manufacturer’s instructions (Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), with an insert size of 250 bp. The Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for cDNA sequencing.

Raw reads with more than 10% uncalled bases (Ns) or more than 50% of low-quality bases (PHRED quality scores ≤ 5) were discarded, and adapter sequences were removed from the remaining reads. At the same time, Q20, Q30, GC (Guanine-Cytosine) content and the sequence duplication level of the clean data were calculated. The clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome sequence of rice (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/001/433/935/GCF_001433935.1_IRGSP-1.0/). Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million fragments mapped (FPKM) was used as a measure of gene expression level. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using DESeq2 v1.22.1, and the False Discovery Rate (FDR) was obtained by correcting the hypothesis test probability (p value), with the criteria for screening DEGs set at adjusted FDR < 0.05 and | log2 (fold change) |> 1. The DEGs were then subjected to enrichment analyses of gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathways using the DAVID tool (Fisher’s exact test, p values ≤ 0.05). qPCR was conducted to verify the transcriptome analysis using the QIAGEN kit. The rice OsActin gene was used as the internal reference. The primers required for this experiment are listed in Table S2. For the analysis of qPCR validation data, statistical significance between treatment groups was assessed by one-way ANOVA, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Screening core metabolites from correlation analysis of enriched metabolites and DEGs

Correlation coefficients between metabolites and gene expression profile were calculated by using the quantitative results from transcriptome and metabolome. Correlation clustering heatmaps were generated based on the Pearson correlation coefficients.

The control effect of core differential metabolites on rice blast disease

Plate confrontation assay of core differential metabolites (Azelaic acid, Betaine, Suberic acid, and Phenyl acetate) on the mycelial growth of M. oryzae was conducted to verify the inhibitory effect. The 8 mm M. oryzae mycelial discs obtained with a cork borer were inoculated in the center of the PDA media, which respectively contained Azelaic acid (concentrations of 1 mM, 2 mM, 5 mM, 10 mM), Betaine (concentrations of 10 mM, 20 mM, 50 mM, 100 mM, 200 mM), Suberic acid (concentrations of 1 mM, 2 mM, 5 mM, 10 mM, 20 mM), and Phenyl acetate (concentrations of 10 μM, 20 μM, 50 μM, 100 μM, 200 μM). The pathogen was inoculated onto blank PDA medium (without metabolites) as control treatment. When the growth of M. oryzae in the control treatment reached the entire petri dish at 28℃ in the dark, the colony diameters of metabolites-added treatments were measured using the cross-method, and the inhibition rates of mycelial growth were calculated, with triplicate repetitions for each treatment.

To further evaluate the control effect of these metabolites on rice blast, the HKN were sprayed with Azelaic acid and Suberic acid at concentrations of 1 mM, 2 mM, and 5 mM, as well as Betaine at concentrations of 10 mM, 20 mM and 50 mM, respectively, and Phenyl acetate at concentrations of 10 μM, 50 μM, and 100 μM, respectively, for 24 h prior to inoculation of GUY11. HKN was sprayed and treated with sterilized water as control. After inoculation of GUY11, the rice plants were moved to an incubator for 24 h under darkness with a humidity of 98%, and then incubated with constant humidity, 28℃ and 16 h light/8 h dark for 7 days. The disease survey was carried out after 7 days of inoculation, the leaf lesion was counted, and the incidence rate and disease index were calculated.

Statistics analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., USA), graphs were performed using Graphpad prism 9 (Graphpad Inc., USA) and Origin Pro (version 9.0; Origin Laboratory Inc., Northampton, MA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Duncan’s post hoc test (homogeneity of variances, Levene’s test, P > 0.05), t-tests and correlation analysis with the Pearson correlation coefficient were utilized for data analysis.

Results

Varietal intercropping exhibits significant control on rice blast disease

Five rice intercropping combinations were evaluated in field trails to investigate their impact on rice blast disease. The results showed that the five combinations significantly reduced the occurrence of rice blast in intercropping system, with blast control efficiency ranging from 46.70% to 73.27% (Fig.2A-C). However, intercropping resulted in a minor yield reduction (Table S3).

Among these, the Huangkenuo//Shanyou63 combination was identified as the most effective disease-resistant combination, based on both the current results and three years field data previously. A greenhouse experiment was subsequently conducted to confirm the blast resistance of Huangkenuo//Shanyou 63 combination. The results indicated that the disease index of Shanyou 63 (Inter-SY63) and Huangkenuo (Inter-HKN) were significantly reduced in the intercropping system compared to the monocropping system (Fig.2D). These findings demonstrate that the intercropping system could significantly reduce the incidence of rice blast disease.

Metabolites of rice root are altered by rice varietal intercropping

The metabolites secreted by plant root systems play a crucial role in plant-plant interactions. We analyzed the variations in root metabolites of rice cultivars after intercropping. The results showed that a total of 1,326 metabolites were detected. Among the comparisons between Mono-HKN/SY63 and Inter-HKN/SY63, the predominant metabolites included amino acids and their derivatives (24.64%), lipid and lipid molecules (12.58%), alkaloids (10.93%), phenolic acids (10.47%), organic acids and their derivatives (8.97%), and phenylpropanoids and polyketides (5.42%) (Fig. S1). PCA revealed significant differences in the metabolite profiles between the disease-susceptible variety Huangkenuo and disease-resistant variety Shanyou 63. OPLS-DA analysis further demonstrated clear segregation of metabolites between Huangkebuo and Shanyou 63 in both the monocropping and intercropping system (Fig.3A and Fig. S2). A total of 224 differential metabolites were identified in comparison with Mono-HKN vs. Inter-HKN//SY63, with 188 metabolites upregulated and 36 downregulated (Fig.3B). Similarly, in comparison with Mono-SY63 vs. Inter-HKN//SY63, 363 differential metabolites were detected, with 256 upregulated and 107 downregulated (Fig.3B).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted based on the differential metabolites. The pathways enriched in Inter-HKN vs. Mono-HKN included flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, arachidonic acid metabolism, lysine degradation, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig.3C). In comparison of Inter-SY63 vs. Mono-SY63, the enriched pathways included cyanoamino acid metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, monobactam biosynthesis, ABC transporters, phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis (Fig.3C). These results suggest that flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis are the significantly enriched following intercropping of HKN and SY63. Varietal intercropping altered the composition of metabolites in the disease resistance-related pathways, thereby enhancing rice resistance to rice blast.

A total of 21 differential metabolites were significantly upregulated in both Inter-HKN//SY63 vs. Mono-HKN and Inter-HKN//SY63 vs. Mono-SY63 comparisons. These metabolites included apigenin-6-C-arabinopyranoside-8-C-xyloside, Apigenin-6-C-(2"-glucosyl) arabinopyranoside, flavonoids such as isosafetaside, betaine and 2,4-dihydroxy-7-methoxy-1,4-benzoxazin-3-one glucoside alkaloids and substances with chemosensory activity such as nicotinamide, dulcitol, zeylanolactone (Fig.3D).

Disease resistance-related differential genes identified by transcriptome sequencing

Transcriptome sequencing underwent rigorous data quality control. The proportion of bases with a correct identification rate greater than 99% (Q20) ranged from 97.05% to 98.09%. The GC content was between 52.53% and 55.18% (Supplementary Table 4). The amount of clean bases for all tested samples ranged from 4.32 G and 6.28 G. When aligning the clean reads to the rice reference genome, the percentage of total sequences mapped to the reference genome exceeded 93.24% in total samples, and the percentage of uniquely mapped sequences was above 89.80%, indicating high sequencing quality (Supplementary Table 5). The log10(FPKM) values for all tested samples ranged from 0.3 to 2.25, suggesting that the gene expression levels were within the normal range (Fig. S3). Correlation analysis of transcriptome replicates revealed high intra-group replicate correlation (Fig. S4). PCA showed that PC1 and PC2 separated genes based on different treatments and time points, with a clear distinction between different rice varieties. The results indicated that Huangkenuo and Shanyou 63 exhibit distinct gene expression profiles under monoculture and intercropping patterns in response to of pathogen infestation (Fig. S5).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from the HKN and SY63 varieties in monoculture and intercropping patterns at 0 h, 24 h and 48 h after pathogen inoculation. The results indicate that the genes expression at 24 h post-inoculation (hpi) was significantly altered under intercropping conditions, compared to monocropping (Fig. S6). At 24 hpi, 470 genes were upregulated, and 909 genes were downregulated in HKN under intercropping (Mono-HKN_24h vs. Inter-HKN_24h) (Fig.4A). In contrast, 339 genes were upregulated, and 1116 genes were downregulated in the resistant varietySY63 (Mono-SY63_24h vs. Inter-SY63_24h) (Fig.4A).

KEGG enrichment analysis showed that in the comparison of Inter-HKN_24h vs Mono-HKN_24h, the most significantly enriched pathways included plant-pathogen interaction, biosynthesis of nucleotide sugars, plant hormone signal transduction, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, MAPK signaling pathway–plant, and ABC transporters (Fig.4B). In the comparison of Inter-SY63_24h vs. Mono-SY63-24h, the most significantly enriched pathways included plant-pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathway – plant, zeatin biosynthesis, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis, and carotenoid biosynthesis (Fig.4B). The overlapping pathways in both rice varieties included plant-pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK closely associated with rice disease resistance.

To further validate the gene expression, eight differentially expressed genes from the plant-pathogen interaction pathway (LOC4345951, LOC4332631, LOC4331506, LOC4331076, LOC4343929, LOC4351526, LOC4336520, LOC9270235) were quantified by qRT-PCR. The gene expression levels were then compared with those from RNA-seq. The results showed that the expression patterns of all genes were fully consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig.4C).

These findings suggest that these metabolites may act as potential immunologically active compounds that enhance disease resistance in the Huangkenuo//Shanyou 63 intercropping system.

Core differential metabolites of disease control in the rice varietal intercropping system

To assess the impact of core metabolites on rice blast disease, plate-confrontation experiments were conducted to evaluate the inhibitory effects of the metabolites on M. oryzae, and exogenous application of the metabolites was performed to test their potential in enhancing blast resistance. The results indicated that azelaic acid, at concentrations of 1 mM, 2 mM, 5 mM, and 10 mM, exhibited significant inhibitory effects against M. oryzae., with a 100% inhibition rate at the 10 mM concentration, and an IC50 value of 2.690 mM. Suberic acid, at concentrations of 2 mM, 5 mM, 10 mM, and 20 mM, also exhibited inhibitory effects on M. oryzae, achieving a 100% inhibition rate at the 20 mM concentration, and an IC50 value of 7.579 mM (Fig.6A and Fig S8).

B Leaf blast occurrence and disease index under Azelaic Acid, Suberic Acid, Betaine, and Phenyl Acetate treatments. The *, ** and *** symbol represents significant difference (P < 0.05, P < 0.01 and P < 0.001) according to analysis of variance. Bars represent standard deviation among the biological replicates

The pot experiment revealed that azelaic acid, suberic acid, betaine, and phenyl acetate significantly reduced the incidence and disease index of Huangkenuo rice blast. Specifically, the application of azelaic acid at concentrations of 1 mM, 2 mM, and 5 mM resulted in a decrease in the disease index, with the most notable effect Observed at 5 mM, showing a disease control efficacy of 42.82%. Similarly, suberic acid at concentrations of 1 mM, 2 mM, and 5 mM reduced the disease index, with the highest efficacy (55.74%) observed at 1 mM. Betaine, at the concentrations of 10 mM, 20 mM, and 50 mM, was also effective in lowering the disease index, with the optimal efficacy of 54.62% achieved at 20 mM. Phenyl acetate, applied at concentrations of 10 μM, 50 μM, and 100 μM, also reduced the disease index, with the best performance (66.27%) observed at 10 μM (Fig.6B).

In conclusion, the core differential metabolites-azelaic acid, suberic acid, betaine, and phenyl acetate- enhanced the resistance of disease susceptible Huangkenuo rice when grown in an intercropping system.

Discussion

Huangkenuo (HKN) is a tall, high-quality glutinous rice variety well adapted to the local climate and cultivation practices in Yunnan Province. Owing to its role in tradition traditional foods such as glutinous rice meals, rice cakes, and zongzi, HKN holds both economic and cultural importance among local famers [48]. However, it is highly susceptible to rice blast. While modern molecular breeding efficiently introduces resistant genes, traditional cultivars like HKN-with distinct quality traits but are limited disease resistance-require complementary conservation and management strategies [49]. In this study, intercropping HKN with conventional blast-resistant varieties, such as Shanyou 63 (SY63) effectively suppressed the occurrence of rice blast [50]. Thus, intercropping represents not a substitute for molecular breeding but an eco-intensification strategy that complements genetic improvement by maintaining the productivity of regionally valued, blast-susceptible varieties.

Intercropping systems have been demonstrated to enhance agricultural sustainability by improving land and nutrient resource utilization, thereby increasing crop yields [51, 52]. As an alternative to monoculture, intercropping diverse varieties within a single crop increases genetic diversity among rice plants, enabling large-scale and efficient ecological control of rice blast disease and supporting sustainable rice production [8, 53]. In this study, the susceptible variety Huangkenuo was intercropped with five resistant varieties for rice blast resistance identification. The results showed that the resistance to rice blast of the five intercropped combinations was significantly higher than that of the monoculture, with the disease control effect on the susceptible variety within the same combination being notably greater than that on the resistant varieties. This indicates that intercropping rice varieties with different resistance levels is particularly effective in controlling rice blast in susceptible varieties, which corroborates previous reports [11, 54].

Differences in crop genetic backgrounds and dominant traits in agronomic practices are important factors contributing to the functionality of diverse systems [55]. Reasonably combining diversity through intercropping of complementary traits can enhance the resistance of rice to rice blast, thereby optimizing the ecological and economic functions of rice intercropping systems [56]. Our field trials revealed that diversified intercropping significantly enhanced plant blast resistance, with variation observed among different combinations and between varieties within the same combination. Notably, the susceptible HKN demonstrated superior blast resistance when intercropped with five resistant varieties compared to the resistant cultivars themselves. In the superior HKN//SY63 combination, we observed significant alterations in root exudate profiles, including allelopathic compounds such as organic acids, terpenoids, and alkaloids. Core metabolites including azelaic acid and betaine showed positive correlations with immune-related gene expression, potentially inducing upregulation of resistance genes. Unlike previous studies focusing primarily on aboveground mechanisms in intercropping-based blast control, this works systematically screened optimal variety combinations and specifically investigated the role of belowground root exudates in mediating intercropping-induced disease resistance.

Due to variations in plant species, genotypes, photosynthetic activity, and soil conditions, plants secrete distinct metabolites in their rhizosphere and surrounding soil environments [57]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that plants can influence the immune responses of neighboring plants through underground chemical signaling, either by directly suppressing pathogens or by indirectly inducing plant resistance. For instance, soil-borne pathogens such as Fusarium and Verticillium dahliae can be mitigated by the root metabolites of intercropped plants, which protect adjacent crops by directly inhibiting spore germination and hyphal growth [58, 59]. Our study revealed that intercropping significantly altered the distribution patterns of root exudates in the superior HKN//SY63 combination. Both HKN and SY63 demonstrated distinct root exudate profiles compared to their monoculture counterparts, with particularly pronounced differences observed between Mono-SY63 and Inter-SY63. This suggests that SY63 may modify its root metabolism in response to neighboring HKN plants, potentially contributing to enhanced blast resistance in HKN. Rice roots are known to secrete various antimicrobial compounds, including diterpenoid phytoalexins (DPs) that confer nematode resistance [60], and oryzalides A and B which exhibit significant antimicrobial activity [61]. In our controlled hydroponic experiments with diversified intercropping systems, we identified multiple classes of potentially allelopathic compounds in root exudates: organic acids, terpenoids, free fatty acids, along with 145 alkaloids, 63 flavonoids, and 20 lignans/coumarins (totaling 228 related compounds). These findings indicate that specific components in intercropped rice root exudates may serve as key inducers of disease resistance. Future functional characterization of these compounds will provide both theoretical foundations and practical solutions for eco-friendly disease management in rice production systems.

Reducing plant susceptibility to pathogens is crucial for maintaining plant health and controlling the prevalence of crop diseases in fields [62]. Plant-plant interactions can modulate the basal immune responses of neighboring plants, thereby enhancing overall disease resistance. Previous studies have shown that applying roots metabolites from healthy chili pepper plants to the roots and shoots of maize can activate defense-related genes in maize roots, likely due to antimicrobial molecules in the chili pepper root exudates [63]. Similarly, p-coumaric acid from rice root exudates has been reported to inhibit fungal spore germination and systemically induce the expression of pathogenesis-related (PR) genes in watermelon. Furthermore, exogenous application of p-coumaric acid can effectively protect watermelon from Fusarium oxysporum infection [64].

In our study, we found that compared to monocropping, intercropping Huangkenuo with Shanyou 63 significantly altered the expression profiles of genes related to plant-pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction, secondary metabolite synthesis-related genes, and MAPK signal in Huangkenuo plants. Notably, genes involved in plant-pathogen interaction and plant hormone signal transduction pathways were significantly upregulated under intercropping conditions. Further correlation analysis between the core differential root metabolites and gene expression revealed that several compounds- including azelaic acid, adipic acid, suberic acid, ayapin, eucommiol, nicotinamide, betaine, D-panthenol, gluconic acid, and phenyl acetate significant positive correlations with the expression of disease resistant-related genes. These findings suggest that these metabolites may act as potential immunologically active compounds that enhance disease resistance in the Huangkenuo//Shanyou 63 intercropping system.

Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that betaine inhibits spore germination and reduces germ tube length in Penicillium expansum, demonstrating significant potential as a biocontrol agent for managing postharvest diseases [65]. Similarly, exogenous application of azelaic acid at different concentrations was shown to significantly upregulate the expression of antioxidant- and disease resistance-related genes in tobacco within 48 h, effectively suppressing the growth of Alternaria alternata and Phytophthora nicotianae [66], which aligns with our experimental observations.Our results indicate that the majority of core differential root exudates in the Huangkenuo//Shanyou 63 intercropping system exhibit significant positive correlations with upregulated immune-related genes in intercropped plants. This suggests that specific metabolites in the root exudates—particularly azelaic acid, suberic acid, betaine, and phenyl acetate—may induce transcriptional activation of immune-related genes in Huangkenuo plants, including those associated with plant-pathogen interactions and plant hormone signal transduction pathways. Importantly, our findings further demonstrate that this mechanism contributes to reduced leaf blast disease severity and enhanced resistance in susceptible varieties.

This study demonstrates that diversified rice intercropping systems can significantly enhance resistance to rice blast (M. oryzae) and elucidates the mechanism by which core differential root exudates in the Huangkenuo//Shanyou 63 intercropping combination upregulate immune-related gene expression, thereby improving disease resistance in susceptible varieties. The findings provide critical theoretical support and potential functional substance foundations for eco-friendly rice blast management, contributing to the sustainable development of ecological agriculture. Future research should focus on: (1) systematically deciphering the molecular mechanisms of key bioactive compounds, and (2) optimizing resistant-susceptible variety combinations to enhance field applicability. It should be noted that, due to the limitation of conducting this study at a single experimental site, the sample representativeness may be constrained. Subsequent studies should expand the geographical scope and incorporate multi-ecological zone trials to further validate the generalizability of the conclusions.

Conclusion

In this study, intercropping Huangkenuo with Shanyou 63 significantly enhanced disease resistance under both field and greenhouse conditions. Metabolome profiling identified systemic alterations in flavonoid/phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways and antifungal metabolites. Transcriptome analyses revealed that intercropping induced significant changes in disease resistance-related pathways, inducing increases in POD activity and lignin content. Correlation analysis linked these metabolites to resistant-regulation genes was screened core metabolites (Azelaic Acid (AzA), Sebacic Acid (SA), Betaine (Bet), and Phenyl Acetate (PhAc)), with functional validation confirming AzA and SA significantly inhibited the mycelial growth of M. oryzae, while exogenous application of AzA, SA, Bet, and PhAc before inoculated with M. oryzae significantly reduced the rice blast disease incidence in greenhouse conditions. These findings collectively demonstrate that rice intercropping systems mediate resistance through synergistic genetic and metabolic reprogramming, with specific exudate components serving as potential biocontrol agents requiring further mechanistic investigation.
