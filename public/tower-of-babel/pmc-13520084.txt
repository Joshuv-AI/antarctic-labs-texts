Valorization of Schizophyllum commune mycelia through green extraction and hydrolysate preparation: Proteomics-informed antioxidant potential and Caco-2 cell-based assessment
Authors: Chatchol Kongsinkaew, Suphawit Chrakankun, Sara Cutroneo, Francesca Accardo, Tullia Tedeschi, Awanwee Petchkongkaew, Chiara Dall'Asta, Florian Call, Francesco Crudo, Dino Grgic, Doris Marko, Theppanya Charoenrat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchizophyllum commune mycelia obtained from submerged fermentation were investigated as a sustainable source of bioactive ingredients using an integrated workflow involving green extraction, proteomics, in silico peptide-motif prediction, and Caco-2 cellular assays. Hot-water extraction (HWE) and ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) were compared at different biomass loads and extraction times. Protein solubilization increased strongly with biomass load regardless of extraction method, whereas polysaccharide recovery depended mainly on extraction time. In crude extracts, UAE gave higher phenolic content than HWE. Shotgun proteomics identified 312 high-confidence proteins, from which selected proteins were used as candidate precursor sequences for antioxidant-peptide motif prediction. The hydrolysate, which contained peptides together with polysaccharides and phenolic compounds, maintained >90% Caco-2 viability and significantly reduced H2O2-induced intracellular reactive oxygen species in a dose-dependent manner. These findings provide preliminary support for the further development of the hydrolysate as a multicomponent antioxidant ingredient for functional food applications.Keywords: Schizophyllum commune, Submerged fermentation, Proteomics, Antioxidant peptides, Caco-2 cells, Ultrasound-assisted extraction, Hot-water extraction, Hydrolysate

Introduction

The valorization of fungal biomass has emerged as a promising strategy in biorefinery research, supporting the demand for sustainable and multifunctional bioactive compounds. Edible and medicinal fungi such as Agaricus bisporus, Auricularia polytricha, Volvariella volvacea, Schizophyllum commune, Ganoderma lucidum, Pleurotus ostreatus, and Grifola frondosa are important sources of biologically active molecules, including proteins, polysaccharides, micronutrients, and secondary metabolites (Singh, Saini, Kumar, Chawla, & Kaushik, 2025). These compounds exhibit diverse biological activities; for instance, mushroom-derived polysaccharides possess antitumor (Meng, Liang, & Luo, 2016), antioxidant (Zhao et al., 2016), and anti-inflammatory (Yin et al., 2024) properties, while mushroom-derived peptides display anticancer (Khongdetch, Laohakunjit, & Kaprasob, 2022), antioxidant (Sangtitanu et al., 2020), and anti-inflammatory (Li, Lv, Xu, Ge, & Lin, 2021) effects. Owing to these multifunctional bioactivities, fungal metabolites are increasingly regarded as valuable resources for the development of functional foods, nutraceuticals, and therapeutic products, thereby positioning fungi as important contributors to the emerging circular bioeconomy.

Building on this diversity, S. commune, a globally distributed basidiomycete, has gained growing attention for its unique polysaccharide and protein fractions (Mišković, Rašeta, Krsmanović, & Karaman, 2023). This species produces several functional compounds, most notably the β-glucan schizophyllan, which exhibits well-documented immunostimulatory, anticancer, and antioxidant properties (Mišković et al., 2023). Its protein-rich biomass (∼15% on a dry weight basis; Singh et al., 2025) also contains essential amino acids (∼34% of total amino acids; Longvah & Deosthale, 1998), making it suitable for nutritional applications. While schizophyllan has been the primary focus of S. commune research, its protein fraction has received comparatively less attention. This component nevertheless represents an underexplored resource, as enzymatic hydrolysis can release short-chain bioactive peptides with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor activities, thereby broadening the valorization potential of S. commune. Previous work demonstrated that optimized hydrolysis of S. commune fruiting body proteins yields low-molecular-weight peptide fractions (<0.65 kDa) with strong antioxidant activity (Wongaem et al., 2021), and to the best of our knowledge, this remains the only study reporting the generation of bioactive peptides from this species. However, this study relied exclusively on fruiting body biomass, which is limited in scalability and consistency for industrial applications, since fruiting body cultivation typically requires more than three weeks (∼22 days) to complete a growth cycle (Bernabé-González, Cayetano-Catarino, & Martínez-Carrera, 2022). By contrast, mycelial biomass obtained through submerged fermentation (SmF) offers rapid growth, controlled cultivation, and high reproducibility (Bakratsas, Polydera, Katapodis, & Stamatis, 2021), making it a more promising platform for sustainable peptide production. S. commune has also been successfully cultivated under SmF conditions for polysaccharide production (Gou et al., 2023; Li et al., 2011b; Pilafidis et al., 2025), highlighting its adaptability to submerged systems. To realize the full potential of this approach, efficient and scalable extraction methods are essential.

In the valorization of fungal biomass, extraction methods that minimize or avoid the use of chemical solvents are particularly desirable, as they align with green processing principles and enhance the safety of the recovered products. Among such approaches, hot-water extraction (HWE) is widely employed for isolating fungal polysaccharides such as β-glucans, taking advantage of water as a safe and environmentally friendly solvent. For instance, polysaccharides from S. commune mycelia have been extracted at 100 °C for 3 h (Meng et al., 2021), while those from fruiting bodies were obtained by boiling for 2 h (Chen et al., 2020). However, despite its simplicity and eco-friendly nature, HWE is limited by the high thermal input, which can induce structural alterations in polysaccharides and reduce antioxidant performance (Chen et al., 2020). For instance, cold-water (4 °C, 1 h) extracts of S. commune fruiting bodies have shown significantly higher scavenging activity than their hot-water (100 °C, 1 h) counterparts (Abd Razak, Jamaluddin, Abd Rashid, Sani, & Abdul Manan, 2020). To address these challenges, ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) has gained attention as a greener, non-thermal, and energy-efficient alternative. A comparative study of UAE and HWE for extracting S. commune fruiting body polysaccharides showed that UAE yielded polysaccharides with lower molecular weight and higher antioxidant activity than HWE (Chen et al., 2020), suggesting that UAE may represent a more effective technique for recovering polysaccharides with enhanced bioactivities. Nevertheless, systematic comparisons of HWE and UAE for recovering key biomolecules, including polysaccharides, phenolics, and proteins, from SmF-derived S. commune mycelia remain scarce. Such studies are crucial to advancing the sustainable valorization of this versatile mushroom.

This study aimed to establish an integrated strategy for the sustainable valorization of S. commune mycelia produced by SmF. Specifically, (i) HWE and UAE were compared to evaluate their effects on the recovery of proteins, polysaccharides, and phenolic compounds; (ii) the soluble protein fraction obtained through aqueous protein extraction was subjected to proteomic analysis to identify major proteins; (iii) the trypsin-generated hydrolysate was analyzed in silico to predict potential antioxidant peptide motifs; and (iv) the hydrolysate was further evaluated for cytocompatibility and intracellular antioxidant activity in human colorectal adenocarcinoma (Caco-2) cells. The overall experimental workflow is summarized in Fig. 1, which highlights the two parallel routes used in this study: extraction comparison using HWE and UAE, and hydrolysate preparation from the simple aqueous protein extract. Overall, the findings provide practical insights into the green extraction and bioactivity evaluation of SmF-derived S. commune biomass and support its further development as a potential functional food ingredient.

Materials and methods

Preservation and identification

The pure fungal isolate strain TC001, provided by the Department of Biotechnology, Faculty of Science and Technology, Thammasat University (Rangsit Center), Pathum Thani, Thailand, was used throughout this study. The strain was routinely maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA). For the preparation of potato dextrose broth (PDB), 300 g of diced potatoes were boiled in 500 mL of distilled water for 1 h and then filtered through cheesecloth. Glucose monohydrate (22 g) was added to the filtrate, and the volume was adjusted to 1000 mL with distilled water. The mixture was heated to dissolve the glucose and sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. For PDA, 15 g of agar powder was added to the PDB, dissolved by heating, and sterilized under the same conditions.

Genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) was extracted from fresh fungal mycelia using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method described by Doyle and Doyle (1987). The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified with primers ITS5 and ITS4 under standard polymerase chain reaction (PCR) conditions (96 °C for 2 min; 35 cycles of 96 °C for 1 min, 53 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1.5 min; final extension at 72 °C for 10 min). PCR products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and sequenced bidirectionally (Macrogen Inc., Seoul, Korea). Consensus sequences were assembled using BioEdit and compared against GenBank reference sequences to determine taxonomic identity based on sequence similarity.

Submerged fermentation

Approximately 1 cm2 of the pure fungal isolate strain TC001 was transferred from a PDA plate into a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 100 mL of PDB. The culture was incubated at 30 °C on a rotary shaker at 200 rpm for 7 days under dark conditions. After incubation, the mycelial biomass was homogenized, and 1 mL of the homogenate was inoculated into a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of PDB. The flasks were incubated under the same conditions. The experiment was conducted in triplicate with a total of 21 flasks, and samples were collected on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 13 for subsequent analyses.

After sampling, the culture broth was centrifuged at 4347 ×g for 10 min at 25 °C to separate the mycelia from the supernatant. The pH of the supernatant was measured immediately after centrifugation, and the remaining portion was stored at −20 °C for glucose concentration analysis. The mycelial pellets were washed three times with distilled water, photographed with a scale bar placed alongside for size comparison, and subsequently freeze-dried until a constant weight was obtained.

The diameter of the mycelial pellets was measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The pellet photos were saved as JPEG files and imported into the software. The straight-line tool was first used to draw a line along the scale bar (in centimeters) to set the measurement scale. Then, the same tool was used to draw a line across each mycelial pellet, and the diameter was determined using the Analyze and Measure commands.

The glucose concentration was analyzed using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method (Miller, 1959). In brief, 250 μL of the sample was combined with 250 μL of DNS reagent containing 10 g/L DNS, 16 g/L NaOH, and 300 g/L sodium potassium tartrate. The reaction mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min to develop color, then rapidly cooled in cold water. After cooling, 5 mL of distilled water was added to dilute the solution, followed by thorough mixing. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a spectrophotometer (LIBRA S22, Biochrom, United Kingdom). The concentration of reducing sugars was calculated using a glucose standard curve.

Mycelial biomass production

Mycelial biomass was produced following the cultivation procedure described in Section 2.2. The culture was harvested at the optimum cultivation time. The freeze-dried mycelia were then ground into a fine powder using a blender.

HWE versus UAE

For extraction, biomass (0.1–0.5 g; Section 2.3) was suspended in 30 mL of distilled water in 50 mL tubes and subjected either to UAE using an ultrasonic bath (WIGGENS UA22MFDN, Germany) at 80% power and 25 °C for 0, 20, 40, 60, and 80 min, or to HWE using a bath circulator (JULABO CORIO CD-B33, Germany) at 100 °C for 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. After extraction, the mixtures were centrifuged at 4347 ×g and 4 °C for 10 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected and stored at −20 °C for analysis of protein, phenolic, and polysaccharide concentration.

Protein concentration was determined using the method of Lowry, Rosebrough, Farr, and Randall (1951). Absorbance was measured at 750 nm with a spectrophotometer (LIBRA S22, Biochrom, United Kingdom), and bovine serum albumin was used to construct the standard calibration curve. Polysaccharide extraction was carried out according to the protocol described by Meng et al. (2021). The clear supernatant was mixed with three volumes of absolute ethanol. The mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. The resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation (4347 ×g, 25 °C, 10 min), washed twice with 75% ethanol, and subsequently redissolved in distilled water. The concentration of polysaccharides was then determined using the phenol‑sulfuric acid assay (DuBois, Gilles, Hamilton, Rebers, & Smith, 1956). Briefly, 200 μL of the sample was transferred into a test tube. This was followed by the addition of 200 μL of 5% phenol solution, which was then thoroughly mixed. Subsequently, 1 mL of 98% sulfuric acid was added, and the mixture was vigorously stirred. The reaction mixture was allowed to stand for 30 min to ensure a complete reaction. The absorbance at 490 nm was measured, and the polysaccharide concentration was determined by comparing it against a glucose standard curve. The phenolic content was determined based on the reaction with the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent as previously described (Sari, Ikawati, Danarti, & Hertiani, 2023). In brief, 100 μL of the sample was mixed with 100 μL of the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, followed by thorough mixing. The mixture was left in the dark for 5 min. After that, 1 mL of a 7% sodium carbonate solution was added. Following this, 3.8 mL of distilled water was added to the mixture, followed by thorough mixing. After 120 min in the darkness, the absorbance of the samples at 750 nm was measured. The total phenolic concentration (TPC) in the extract was first calculated from the gallic acid standard curve and expressed as gallic acid equivalents per milliliter of extract (mg GAE/mL). Polysaccharides obtained from selected samples derived from HWE and UAE at extraction times of 120 and 80 min, respectively, were further characterized by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). After extraction, the mixtures were centrifuged to collect the supernatants, and the resulting precipitates were washed with distilled water and freeze-dried. The dried polysaccharide samples were subjected to FTIR analysis to identify functional groups and confirm the presence of characteristic polysaccharide bonds.

Simple aqueous extraction of mycelial proteins

Mycelial biomass powder (0.5 g; Section 2.3) was transferred into a beaker, and 30 mL of distilled water was added. The mixture was stirred on a magnetic stirrer for 20 min at 25 °C, followed by centrifugation at 4347 ×g and 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting supernatant, referred to as the simple aqueous protein extract, was aliquoted into 1 mL portions in separate tubes and subsequently freeze-dried. The remaining supernatant was used for the quantification of polysaccharide, protein, and phenolic concentration as described in Section 2.4.

Sodium dodecyl-sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was performed according to the method as previously described (Cutroneo et al., 2023). The simple aqueous protein extract obtained from Section 2.5 was used for SDS-PAGE analysis. Protein samples containing 40, 50, and 60 μg of protein were loaded onto a 10% Criterion XT Bis-Tris Gel (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Precision Plus Protein Standards (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) were used as molecular weight markers. The electrophoresis was run at 150 V for 45 min. The gel was stained with Coomassie blue at 20 rpm in the dark overnight. The gel was washed with double-distilled water, followed by the destaining solution (10% acetic acid and 40% methanol dissolved in distilled water) until clear. After that, the gel was washed with double-distilled water and scanned using a GS-800 calibrated imaging densitometer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Tryptic digestion, degree of hydrolysis, and shotgun proteomics

Preparation of tryptic hydrolysate

A 300-μL 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) in 100 mM NH4HCO3 was transferred into 6 mL of the simple aqueous protein extract (Section 2.5; initial protein concentration = 1.19 mg/mL). This was mixed and subsequently boiled for 10 min at 90 °C. After this, the sample was incubated at 25 °C for 1 h. Then, 240 μL of 0.5 M iodoacetamide in 100 mM NH4HCO3 was added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 1 h. A volume of 1.2 mL of 100 mM DTT in 100 mM NH4HCO3 was added, mixed, and incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 1 h. Trypsin solution (1 mg/mL in 100 mM NH4HCO3) was added to make a ratio of 1:20 of enzyme:substrate. This was incubated at 37 °C, 80 rpm overnight. A volume of 6 mL of 5% formic acid was added and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was centrifuged at 1932 ×g at 4 °C for 45 min. The supernatant was collected and dried under a nitrogen flow. After that, 6 mL of 0.1% formic acid was added to resolubilize. The sample was filtered through a 0.45 μm and collected for analysis of the degree of hydrolysis (DH) and peptide analysis. The rest of the sample was aliquoted and dried under a nitrogen flow and kept at 4 °C until used. The sample obtained from this step is referred to as the hydrolysate. Accordingly, throughout this study, the term “hydrolysate” refers specifically to the trypsin-digested product of the simple aqueous protein extract prepared by magnetic stirring.

Degree of hydrolysis

The DH was determined using the o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) method as previously described (Cutroneo et al., 2023). Briefly, the assay was performed by adding 20 μL of the sample - suitably diluted - to 2.4 mL of OPA/N-acetylcysteine (NAC) reagent, composed of: 5 mM OPA, 5 mM NAC, 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and 75 mM borate buffer, in 1:9 methanol: Milli-Q water, pH 9.5. Absorbance was measured at 340 nm using a JASCO B-530 UV–Vis-spectrophotometer (JASCO, Oklahoma City, OK, USA). For each sample, measurements were taken three times, and the mean was used for the calculation. The intrinsic absorbance of samples was also measured by adding 20 μL of the sample (diluted 1:20 with Milli-Q water) to 2.4 mL of Milli-Q water. To determine the DH, a calibration curve was performed using L-isoleucine. The calibration curve was analyzed in the same way as the samples. LC-MS/MS shotgun proteomics

Peptide analysis of the tryptic hydrolysate prepared in Section 2.7.1 was performed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using a Vion IMS QTof Mass Spectrometer (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), according to Calcinai, Prandi, Faccini, Puxeddu, and Tedeschi (2023). Raw LC-MS/MS data were processed using Proteome Discoverer™ software version 2.5.0.400 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Database searching was performed using Sequest HT against the UniProtKB Agaricomycetidae database downloaded on 16 September 2025. Trypsin with full specificity was selected as the digestion enzyme, with up to two missed cleavage sites allowed. The minimum peptide length was set to six amino acids. The precursor mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm, and the fragment mass tolerance was set to 0.8 Da. Dynamic modifications included oxidation of lysine, methionine, and proline, and phosphorylation of serine. Carboxymethylation of cysteine was set as a static modification. Peptide–spectrum matches (PSMs) and proteins were validated using Percolator based on q-values, with strict and relaxed false discovery rate (FDR) thresholds of 0.03 and 0.07, respectively. Protein grouping was performed using the strict parsimony principle. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository (Perez-Riverol et al., 2025) with the dataset identifier PXD080488.

Bioinformatic analysis of identified proteins and peptide motifs

Bioinformatic analyses were performed to characterize the identified proteins, evaluate the isoelectric point (pI)/molecular weight (MW) distribution of the protein dataset, map selected proteins to SDS-PAGE bands, and screen for potential antioxidant peptide motifs.

pI/MW distribution and functional enrichment analysis of identified proteins

Only proteins assigned to the high-confidence category based on the combined protein FDR confidence classification generated by Proteome Discoverer™ were included in this analysis. The theoretical pI and MW values of the high-confidence identified proteins were obtained directly from the software output file. These values were used to construct a scatter plot illustrating the pI/MW distribution of the identified protein dataset.

Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the UniProt accession numbers of the high-confidence identified proteins. The accession numbers were uploaded to the ShinyGO v0.85 web tool (https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/; Ge, Jung, & Yao, 2020; accessed in September 2025). The analysis was conducted using S. commune H4–8 (STRING v12.0; organism ID 578458) as the reference species. Gene Ontology (GO) biological process enrichment analysis was performed with the following parameters: FDR cutoff = 0.05, number of pathways to display = 20, pathway size range = 2–5000, and the options to remove redundancy and abbreviate pathway names were enabled. The resulting enrichment data were visualized as dot plots, hierarchical clustering trees, and network diagrams.

Protein selection and SDS-PAGE band mapping for antioxidant peptide motif screening

Proteins were selected for antioxidant peptide motif screening based on the following criteria: (i) the combined protein FDR confidence was classified as high, (ii) sequence coverage was ≥20%, and (iii) the theoretical MW was consistent with the corresponding predominant protein band observed in the SDS-PAGE gel. Only proteins meeting these criteria were retained as candidate protein precursors for downstream antioxidant peptide motif analysis.

For the proteins mapped to SDS-PAGE bands, the theoretical pI and MW values were also used to visualize their distribution relative to the overall identified protein dataset. The amino acid sequences of the selected proteins were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The BIOPEP-UWM database (https://biochemia.uwm.edu.pl/biopep-uwm/; accessed in September 2025) was then used to screen these protein sequences for known antioxidant peptide fragments. In silico antioxidant peptide motif analysis of LC-MS/MS-detected peptides using BIOPEP-UWM

Peptide sequences derived from the selected proteins that met the criteria for high-confidence identification, sequence coverage, and SDS-PAGE band consistency were extracted from the output file generated by Proteome Discoverer™ software. Only peptides with “PSM ambiguity” classified as “Unambiguous” were selected for further analysis. Because multiple PSM entries can correspond to the same annotated peptide sequence, duplicate peptide sequences were removed, and only unique peptide sequences were used for BIOPEP-UWM analysis.

These unique peptide sequences were then screened for known antioxidant peptide motifs using the Bioactive Peptides tool available in the BIOPEP-UWM database (https://biochemia.uwm.edu.pl/biopep-uwm/; accessed in September 2025). Thus, the BIOPEP-UWM analysis was performed on unique peptide sequences experimentally detected by LC-MS/MS and assigned as unambiguous PSMs. This analysis was used only to predict the presence of known antioxidant motifs and did not provide quantitative peptide abundance or direct experimental confirmation of the antioxidant activity of individual peptides.

Cytotoxicity and antioxidant assays

The Caco-2 clone C2BBe1 (ATCC-CRL-2102) cell line was cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) high glucose (4.5 g/L) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Paisley, UK), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% l-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and subcultured twice per week. Only cells within 25 passages were used for experiments.

The antioxidant activity of the hydrolysate (from Section 2.7.1) was tested using the dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA) assay as previously described (Lopes et al., 2025) with some modifications. Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 20,000 cells per well in a black 96-well tissue-culture-treated assay plate with a clear bottom. After 48 h of incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2), the medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 1×, pH 7.4). Subsequently, 100 μL of the test solutions containing increasing concentrations of hydrolysate (0.093–300 μg/mL) and 20 μM 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA), prepared in colourless high-glucose DMEM without supplements (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Paisley, UK), were added to the cells and incubated for 90 min (37 °C, 5% CO2). After incubation, the solutions were aspirated, and the cells were washed once with 100 μL PBS. A volume of 100 μL of colourless high-glucose DMEM without supplements was then added, and the background fluorescence was recorded at λex 485 nm and λem 528 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek/Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). Thereafter, the medium was aspirated, and 100 μL of 1 mM H2O2, prepared in colourless high-glucose DMEM without supplements, was added to the cells. Two control conditions were included in the assay: (i) cells without H2O2 to assess basal reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and (ii) cells treated with H2O2 alone, without the hydrolysate, to serve as the positive oxidative stress control. Between each measurement, the plate was returned to a humidified incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). The data are displayed as mean values with standard deviations. Results and discussion

Molecular identification and growth profile of strain TC001 in submerged fermentation

The pure culture of strain TC001 was identified using molecular techniques based on ITS region sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted and amplified using ITS-specific primers, yielding a 566 bp fragment. BLAST analysis of the obtained sequence (GenBank Accession No. PX446507.1) showed 99.65% sequence identity (query coverage: 99%) with S. commune (GenBank Accession No. MN490059.1), confirming that strain TC001 belongs to S. commune (Table S1).

Understanding the growth pattern of S. commune TC001 is important for efficient biomass production in large-scale cultivation. The growth behavior under SmF is shown in Fig. 2A. Biomass increased rapidly during the first 3 days, accompanied by a sharp decline in glucose concentration, indicating an active exponential phase. By day 5, glucose was fully depleted, and biomass reached a stationary level. The culture exhibited a maximum specific growth rate (μmax = 0.16 ± 0.04 day−1). After 13 days, the final biomass concentration, biomass yield on glucose (Yx/s), and biomass productivity (Qx) were 10.73 ± 0.42 g/L, 0.45 ± 0.06 g/g, and 0.69 ± 0.09 g/L/day, respectively (Table 1).

The pH of the culture medium increased from approximately 5 to 6.5 within 5 days and reached ∼7.3 by day 13. The initial acidity of PDB originates from organic acids naturally present in potatoes, primarily citric, malic, and oxalic acids (Bushway, Bureau, & McGann, 1984). The progressive alkalinization during cultivation is typical of fungal submerged cultures and is most likely driven by nitrogen metabolism. S. commune can deaminate amino acids and other nitrogenous compounds, releasing ammonia or related alkaline metabolites, which increases the pH. In addition, the utilization of organic acids in the medium may further contribute to the reduction of acidity. Although direct evidence of organic acid catabolism by S. commune is limited, Li et al. (2011a) showed that citric acid supplementation did not inhibit growth or metabolite formation, supporting the notion that organic acids may be metabolically consumed rather than accumulated.

Pellet morphology evolved markedly over time (Fig. 2B). Pellet diameter increased from day 1 to day 3, coinciding with rapid glucose consumption, and then stabilized after day 5. This stabilization may reflect substrate depletion and a limited capacity for further pellet enlargement under the applied inoculum conditions.

Overall, these results establish the molecular identity of strain TC001 and provide a clear growth profile under SmF, identifying day 5 as the transition to the stationary phase and a suitable point for biomass harvesting under the tested conditions. Accordingly, biomass harvested on day 5 was used in subsequent extraction studies to ensure consistent physiological conditions and allow reliable comparison between HWE and UAE (Section 3.2).

HWE versus UAE for the recovery of proteins, polysaccharides, and phenolic compounds

Protein recovery

The extraction behavior of bioactive compounds from S. commune mycelial biomass was examined at different sample weights (0.1–0.5 g; 0.33–1.67% w/v) and extraction times using UAE and HWE (Fig. 3). The amount of protein extracted by both methods increased with increasing biomass load but remained largely unaffected by extraction time. At 0 min, defined as immediate suspension of the dried mycelial powder in water, thorough mixing to obtain a homogeneous slurry, and centrifugation without additional sonication or heating, protein concentrations were approximately 0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.8 mg/mL for 0.1–0.5 g biomass, respectively (Fig. 3A, C). After prolonged UAE or HWE, the protein concentrations remained within a similar range. This pattern indicates that the recovered protein fraction was mainly composed of readily water-soluble proteins released during the initial hydration and mixing step, rather than proteins liberated primarily through ultrasound- or heat-induced cell-wall disruption. Pearson's correlation matrices confirmed this trend: protein concentration exhibited strong positive correlations with biomass load (r = 0.990 for UAE (Fig. 4A); r = 0.959 for HWE (Fig. 4B)), but negligible correlations with extraction time (r = 0.080 for UAE (Fig. 4A); r = 0.005 for HWE (Fig. 4B)). These results indicate that protein recovery was governed mainly by the amount of biomass suspended in water, rather than by prolonged sonication or heating. Therefore, under the tested conditions, UAE and HWE did not substantially enhance protein recovery beyond that achieved by simple aqueous extraction. A similar pattern was observed for protein extraction yield (mg/g), which declined with increasing biomass load. This decline was likely attributable to reduced solvent-to-solid ratios and mass-transfer limitations at higher solid loadings. Extraction time again showed no significant influence, with maximum yields obtained at 0 min (Fig. 3B, D).

Differences in extraction methods generally influence both the quality and the yield of extracted proteins (Ketemepi, Awang, Seelan, & Mohd Noor, 2024). The protein profiles from UAE and HWE were examined using SDS-PAGE (Fig. S1). At 0 min of extraction, both UAE (Fig. S1A) and HWE (Fig. S1B) showed more intense protein bands than those observed after 60 min, indicating that most electrophoretically detectable soluble proteins were released during the initial hydration and mixing step. The reduced band intensity after prolonged extraction may reflect decreased electrophoretic detectability caused by protein denaturation, aggregation, ultrasound-associated fragmentation, and/or partial degradation by endogenous proteases. Because protease activity was not measured, endogenous proteolysis cannot be excluded. In HWE, prolonged exposure to high temperature likely promoted protein denaturation and aggregation, consistent with irreversible unfolding under severe thermal conditions (Bischof & He, 2006). In UAE, sonication-induced cavitation, mechanical shear, and localized heating may have contributed to partial denaturation or fragmentation of heat-sensitive proteins; although the ultrasonic bath was initially set to 25 °C, the temperature rose to approximately 50 °C during operation (Antunez-Medina et al., 2025).

Polysaccharide recovery

Polysaccharide concentrations increased with both biomass load and extraction time for UAE (Fig. 3E) and HWE (Fig. 3G), showing a continuous upward trend throughout the extraction period. At 60 min, both methods yielded comparable polysaccharide concentrations (approximately 0.5–1.0 mg/mL), indicating similar final extraction performance under the tested conditions. This behavior was corroborated by the Pearson's correlation matrices, where polysaccharide concentration showed strong positive correlations with extraction time (r = 0.852 for UAE (Fig. 4A); r = 0.846 for HWE (Fig. 4B)) but only moderate correlations with biomass load (r = 0.393 for UAE (Fig. 4A); r = 0.306 for HWE (Fig. 4B)). These results suggest that polysaccharide recovery was primarily time-dependent, likely reflecting the gradual release of cell-wall-associated polysaccharides.

Literature reports have shown that extraction parameters, including ultrasonic power, temperature, extraction time, and biomass load, strongly influence UAE efficiency (Cheng et al., 2024). Similarly, in HWE, factors such as temperature (50–100 °C), extraction duration (1.5–5 h), and solid loading modulate polysaccharide yield from mushroom fruiting bodies (Cheng et al., 2024; Leong, Yang, & Chang, 2021). Therefore, longer extraction times would likely further increase polysaccharide yield but may also induce structural degradation due to prolonged exposure to elevated temperatures (Leong et al., 2021).

Polysaccharide extraction yield (mg GE/g) decreased consistently with increasing biomass load for both UAE (Fig. 3F) and HWE (Fig. 3H). This trend aligned with the corresponding negative correlations (r = −0.832 for UAE (Fig. 4A); r = −0.832 for HWE (Fig. 4B)), confirming the strong influence of solid loading on extraction efficiency, as reported by others (Chen et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2024).

Phenolic compound recovery

S. commune is known to contain phenolic compounds with documented bioactive and antioxidant properties (Basso et al., 2020; Mišković et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2016). The TPC (mg GAE/mL) increased markedly with increasing biomass load but remained independent of extraction time for both UAE (Fig. 3I) and HWE (Fig. 3K). This pattern was strongly supported by the Pearson's correlation matrices, where TPC showed strong positive correlations with biomass load (r = 0.977 for UAE (Fig. 4A); r = 0.874 for HWE (Fig. 4B)) but negligible correlations with extraction time (r = 0.151 for UAE (Fig. 4A); r = −0.283 for HWE (Fig. 4B)). When expressed on a biomass-normalized basis (mg GAE/g), PC remained nearly constant across all biomass loads (Fig. 3J, L), consistent with the minimal correlations between PC yield and biomass load (r = 0.018 for UAE (Fig. 4A); r = −0.215 for HWE (Fig. 4B)). These results indicate that, under the tested conditions, phenolic extraction efficiency was not strongly affected by solid concentration. The increase in TPC at higher biomass loads likely reflected the greater amount of soluble phenolic compounds released from the larger quantity of biomass, rather than an improvement in extraction efficiency. UAE consistently produced higher TPC values than HWE, likely due to reduced thermal degradation of heat-sensitive phenolic compounds during sonication-based extraction compared with hot-water extraction.

Supporting analyses of extract characteristics

The antioxidant activity of the extracts obtained from both methods was determined using the 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay (Fig. S1C and S1D). Both UAE and HWE extracts exhibited detectable DPPH radical-scavenging activity; however, the activity of UAE extracts increased with longer extraction time. This observation is consistent with previous reports on polysaccharide extraction from mushroom fruiting bodies, where UAE was found to be more effective for obtaining polysaccharides with strong antioxidant properties compared to HWE (Chen et al., 2020; Leong et al., 2021). Also, Mongkontanawat and Thumrongchote (2021) reported that the extraction method affects the antioxidants and chemical substances of the extract. The enhancement of DPPH radical-scavenging activity over time may be attributed to the release of intracellular antioxidant compounds or low-molecular-weight phenolics facilitated by cavitation-induced disruption of cell wall structures during ultrasonic treatment.

FTIR spectroscopy has been widely used to characterize mushroom polysaccharides, including β-glucans. The FTIR spectra of the polysaccharides extracted by HWE and UAE (Fig. 4D–E) showed similar characteristic absorption bands to those of the dried mycelial powder (Fig. 4C), indicating that the main polysaccharide structure was preserved after extraction. The broad absorption band around 3400 cm−1 corresponded to O—H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups commonly found in polysaccharides (Mancinelli et al., 2024; Nanta et al., 2024), whereas the peaks near 2920 cm−1 were attributed to C—H stretching vibrations (Jamshidian, Shojaosadati, Vilaplana, Mousavi, & Soudi, 2016; Mohammadi et al., 2018; Safaee-Ardakani et al., 2019). The band observed around 1651 cm−1 may be associated with vibrations of functional groups present in the extract, while the band near 1030 cm−1 corresponded to C—O linkages (Mohammadi et al., 2018). Additional peaks at approximately 1424, 1367, and 1261 cm−1 were also related to C—O stretching vibrations. Furthermore, the absorption bands at around 928 cm−1 and 883 cm−1 were assigned to C—H deformation vibrations characteristic of β-pyranoside structures (Mohammadi et al., 2018). Therefore, both HWE- and UAE-extracted polysaccharides exhibited characteristic β-glycosidic linkages and pyranose ring structures.

Overall comparison of HWE and UAE

Overall, the extraction behavior of S. commune showed clear differences among proteins, polysaccharides, and phenolic compounds. Protein solubilization occurred almost immediately upon contact with the solvent and depended mainly on biomass concentration, with limited influence from prolonged extraction under either UAE or HWE conditions. This indicates that simple mixing without heating was sufficient for efficient protein recovery and was preferable for downstream hydrolysate production because it avoids thermal denaturation while reducing processing time and energy demand. Polysaccharide extraction, by contrast, was strongly time-dependent, as reflected by the steady increase observed during both UAE and HWE and the strong positive correlations with extraction time. Phenolic extraction displayed a different pattern: TPC increased proportionally with biomass load but remained largely independent of extraction duration, and biomass-normalized yields stayed essentially constant. Between the two methods, UAE provided higher phenolic content and DPPH radical-scavenging activity than HWE, possibly due to reduced thermal degradation of heat-sensitive compounds and cavitation-assisted release of phenolic constituents. In contrast, both techniques produced comparable polysaccharide concentrations when extraction times were matched. FTIR spectra confirmed that neither method substantially altered the fundamental β-glycosidic linkages and pyranose-ring structures of the extracted polysaccharides. Together, these findings indicate that UAE is advantageous for phenolic-rich extracts with higher radical-scavenging capacity, whereas a simple, low-energy soaking-and-stirring step is suitable for protein extraction intended for hydrolysate preparation.

These results also explain the rationale for using the simple aqueous protein extract (Section 2.5; 20 min, 25 °C, magnetic stirring), rather than UAE- or HWE-derived extracts, for subsequent hydrolysate preparation. Because protein solubilization occurred rapidly during the initial hydration and mixing step, prolonged ultrasound treatment or heating did not substantially improve protein recovery. The simple aqueous extraction approach was therefore considered suitable for recovering the soluble protein fraction while minimizing potential protein alteration caused by heat or prolonged ultrasound exposure. Accordingly, the hydrolysate used for proteomic, in silico, and cell-based analyses was prepared from the simple aqueous protein extract.

SDS-PAGE profiling and LC-MS/MS shotgun proteomic identification of proteins

Proteomic analyses of S. commune have primarily focused on specific physiological responses (Desiderio et al., 2025; Murry et al., 2021; Singh, Kumar, & Thakur, 2017; Ujor et al., 2012), whereas information on the soluble protein composition of its mycelial biomass remains limited. In this study, we characterized the soluble protein fraction using an LC-MS/MS shotgun proteomics workflow to evaluate its suitability as a precursor source for antioxidant peptide motif prediction.

The simple aqueous protein extract prepared by magnetic stirring (Section 2.5) exhibited eleven prominent bands on SDS–PAGE (Fig. 5A), indicating the presence of electrophoretically detectable soluble proteins. The same extract was digested with trypsin and analyzed by LC-MS/MS, resulting in the identification of 312 high-confidence proteins based on the combined protein false discovery rate confidence classification generated by Proteome Discoverer™. Functional enrichment analysis using ShinyGO v0.85 (Fig. 5B–D) revealed strong enrichment of translation-associated pathways, including ribosomal structure, translation initiation and elongation, guanosine triphosphate (GTP)-dependent processes, and cytosolic protein biosynthesis. These terms formed a coherent functional cluster in the hierarchical tree (Fig. 5C) and displayed strong semantic connectivity in the Gene Ontology (GO) similarity network (Fig. 5D), reflecting the translationally active state of S. commune mycelia.

In silico analysis of antioxidant peptide motifs from selected proteins and LC-MS/MS-detected peptides

Wongaem et al. (2021) reported that S. commune fruiting bodies have the potential to generate antioxidant peptides; however, information on antioxidant peptide motifs derived from S. commune mycelial proteins remains limited. To evaluate whether the selected soluble proteins corresponding to major SDS–PAGE bands could serve as potential precursor sources of antioxidant peptide motifs, the BIOPEP-UWM database (Minkiewicz et al., 2019) was used for in silico screening. This tool has been widely applied for bioactive peptide prediction, including antioxidant peptide screening (Aursuwanna et al., 2022; Cui et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025).

A total of twenty-five selected proteins corresponding to the major SDS–PAGE bands (Table 2) were used as precursor sequences for protein-level BIOPEP-UWM analysis. These proteins were selected because they corresponded to the major visible protein bands in the extract and were supported by LC-MS/MS-based identification. As shown in Fig. 6A, several proteins contained a high number of predicted antioxidant fragments, particularly superoxide dismutase (D8PT51), ribosomal protein L15 (D8PPU3), and 60S ribosomal protein L20 (D8QER8). When the potential antioxidant activity index was evaluated (Fig. 6B), the small ribosomal subunit protein uS7 (D8QBZ9) showed the highest predicted antioxidative potential among the evaluated proteins. Importantly, several key metabolic enzymes identified in Table 2, such as glucose-6-phosphate isomerase, phosphoglucomutase, and 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase, also contained predicted antioxidant peptide motifs. These results suggest that both ribosomal proteins and central carbohydrate-metabolism enzymes in S. commune mycelia may serve as candidate precursor proteins for antioxidant peptide motif generation.

The LC-MS/MS-detected tryptic peptides derived from the selected parent proteins were further analyzed, and the results are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Only peptides assigned as unambiguous peptide–spectrum matches were included in this analysis. Therefore, the antioxidant motifs listed in Supplementary Table S2 were identified within experimentally detected peptide sequences, rather than being solely theoretical digestion products. However, peptide-level abundance was not quantified in this study; thus, these results should be interpreted as evidence for the presence of potential antioxidant peptide motifs, not as quantitative evidence of high peptide abundance or direct antioxidant activity of individual peptides.

The detected tryptic peptides had an average molecular weight of approximately 1.3 kDa, indicating the generation of low-molecular-weight peptide fragments. The degree of hydrolysis was 85.26 ± 0.93%, further supporting extensive hydrolysis of the soluble protein fraction. The average peptide MW observed in this study is consistent with previous reports indicating that peptides below 3 kDa often exhibit favorable antioxidant-related properties (de Carvalho Cavenaghi, de Barros, & de Castro, 2025; Li et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). The five most frequent antioxidant motifs detected within the tryptic peptides were LK, EL, FD, LT, and VE. More than 68% of the detected unique peptides contained predicted antioxidant motifs (Supplementary Table S2).

Amino acid-level evidence further supports this prediction. Amino acids such as tryptophan, methionine, histidine, lysine, cysteine, arginine, and tyrosine have been associated with antioxidant capacity due to their electron-rich side chains, including aromatic rings, sulfur-containing groups, and nitrogen-bearing functional groups, which can participate in electron or hydrogen donation to neutralize reactive species (Xu, Chen, & Liu, 2017). The presence of these residues within many identified motifs may therefore contribute to the predicted antioxidant potential of the peptide sequences detected in this study.

Cytocompatibility and intracellular ROS attenuation by the hydrolysate in Caco-2 cells

The S. commune hydrolysate showed good cytocompatibility, maintaining >90% Caco-2 cell viability across all tested concentrations (Fig. 7A). Upon H2O2 exposure, cells treated with H2O2 alone showed a sharp increase in intracellular ROS at 15–30 min, followed by a gradual decline (Fig. 7B). This decrease may reflect endogenous cellular antioxidant responses, including catalase- and glutathione-dependent detoxification systems that contribute to the restoration of intracellular redox balance in Caco-2 cells (Wijeratne, Cuppett, & Schlegel, 2005). Compared with the H2O2-treated control, hydrolysate-treated cells showed greater attenuation of intracellular ROS, indicating a cell-based antioxidant effect.

The ROS-lowering effect increased with hydrolysate concentration and was consistent with the compositional data in Table 3, where higher hydrolysate concentrations contained proportionally greater amounts of peptides, polysaccharides, and phenolic constituents. The peptide fraction may contribute to this response, as LC-MS/MS-detected tryptic peptides contained known antioxidant motifs and had an average molecular weight of approximately 1.3 kDa, which falls within the range often associated with antioxidant-related peptide properties (de Carvalho Cavenaghi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). However, the hydrolysate also contained substantial polysaccharides and detectable phenolic compounds, both of which may contribute to antioxidant responses (Kumar & Xu, 2025). Because component separation was not performed, the independent contributions of peptides, polysaccharides, and phenolic compounds could not be distinguished. Therefore, the observed intracellular ROS attenuation should be interpreted as the effect of the whole multicomponent hydrolysate rather than activity attributable exclusively to antioxidant peptides.

Although direct chemical antioxidant assays of the hydrolysate were not included, the DCF-DA assay provided cell-based evidence that the hydrolysate attenuated H2O2-induced intracellular ROS in Caco-2 cells. These findings provide preliminary support for the potential use of the hydrolysate as a multicomponent antioxidant ingredient. However, further studies are required before practical application, including complementary chemical antioxidant assays, molecular-weight-based fractionation, oxidative stress biomarker analysis, and simulated gastrointestinal digestion. Application-oriented properties such as bitterness, color, odor, and performance in food model systems should also be evaluated to assess formulation feasibility and consumer acceptability.

Conclusion

Mycelia of Schizophyllum commune produced by submerged fermentation were valorized through green extraction and hydrolysate preparation. Strain TC001 was confirmed as S. commune by ITS-based molecular identification, and day 5 was selected as the harvesting point based on the growth profile under submerged fermentation. Protein recovery was efficiently achieved by simple aqueous extraction, whereas ultrasound-assisted extraction enhanced phenolic recovery and DPPH radical-scavenging activity in the crude extract comparison. LC-MS/MS shotgun proteomics identified soluble mycelial proteins that may serve as precursor sources of predicted antioxidant peptide motifs. Tryptic digestion generated a multicomponent hydrolysate containing low-molecular-weight peptides with predicted antioxidant motifs, together with polysaccharides and phenolic compounds. The hydrolysate showed good cytocompatibility toward Caco-2 cells and attenuated H2O2-induced intracellular ROS. Because component separation was not performed, this cellular antioxidant effect should be interpreted as the activity of the whole multicomponent hydrolysate rather than the independent effect of peptides alone. Overall, these findings provide preliminary support for the use of S. commune mycelia as a source of multicomponent antioxidant ingredients for functional food development.

Future studies should include direct chemical antioxidant assays, oxidative stress biomarker analysis, molecular-weight-based fractionation, and validation of individual peptide fractions to clarify the antioxidant capacity and component-specific contributions of the hydrolysate. Further polysaccharide characterization, including molecular-weight distribution, monosaccharide composition, and β-glucan quantification, is also needed to confirm the identity of the extracted polysaccharides. In addition, sensory properties, gastrointestinal digestive stability, and performance in relevant food model systems should be evaluated to support practical application.
