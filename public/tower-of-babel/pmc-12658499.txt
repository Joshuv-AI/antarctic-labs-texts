Study protocol: use of virtual reality in a functional restoration programme for the spine—single-arm acceptance study
Authors: Romain Champagne, Pauline Ali, Yoann Ronzi, Mickael Dinomais
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic low back pain (CLBP) is a leading cause of disability worldwide, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life. Functional restoration programmes (FRPs) are recommended to improve functional abilities and alleviate kinesiophobia, but the integration of emerging technologies like immersive virtual reality (VR) remains underexplored. VR has shown potential in reducing pain and enhancing motor imagery (MI), but its application in group-based rehabilitation for CLBP is novel. This study protocol aims to assess the feasibility and acceptability of adding immersive VR to multicentre FRPs for CLBP.Methods and analysisThis multicentric, prospective, single-arm exploratory pilot study assesses the acceptance of incorporating an immersive VR headset into FRP for patients with CLBP. Participants will include healthcare providers and patients from centres in the Lombaction network. Patients will engage in standard FRP alongside three weekly VR sessions targeting trunk mobility and pain reduction. The primary objective is to evaluate the acceptance of the VR device among healthcare providers and patients (Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 2-based questionnaire). Secondary outcomes include acceptability, usability (System Usability Scale), adherence, safety and effects on kinesiophobia, MI, pain levels and functional capacities. Data collection includes baseline and post-intervention questionnaires, adherence monitoring and standardised scales (eg, Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia, Dallas Pain Questionnaire).Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was granted by Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud-Est VI on 10 January 2025; all participants will provide written informed consent. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and summaries to participating centres.Trial registration numberNCT06724679.Keywords: Virtual Reality, REHABILITATION MEDICINE, Back pain, Chronic Pain

Introduction

Chronic low back pain (CLBP) significantly impacts patients’ quality of life and leads to functional limitations. It represents a major public health issue due to its associated disability and societal costs, particularly through lost work productivity. cLBP is the leading cause of functional disability worldwide.1 2

The 2019 recommendations from the French National Authority for Health3 primarily advocate for exercise-based rehabilitation. In cases where outpatient rehabilitative management fails, a multidisciplinary rehabilitation programme is recommended. Currently, the gold standard for these programmes is the functional restoration programme (FRP),4 which aims to enhance patients’ functional capacities through gradual exposure to physical activity, particularly addressing kinesiophobia.

Kinesiophobia, or the fear of movement, is a significant concern for individuals with CLBP and is a contributing factor to the maintenance of chronic pain. It can be alleviated through gradual exposure or the use of motor imagery (MI) techniques.

MI involves the mental simulation of movement without physical execution, activating brain areas similar to those engaged during actual movement. This novel technique has demonstrated effectiveness in the rehabilitation of musculoskeletal disorders5 yet remains underutilised in the context of CLBP rehabilitation. MI is particularly beneficial for severely affected patients who may not be accessible to traditional gradual exposure methods, as it allows access to the brain’s motor functions.

A recent study published in The Lancet highlighted the efficacy of cognitive functional therapy (CFT), which aims to restore patients’ movement capabilities by redirecting their attention away from their fears and eliminating avoidance behaviours.6 Therefore, it is essential to recommend rehabilitation techniques that encourage movement in patients with CLBP while alleviating their apprehensions about physical activity.

Virtual reality (VR) has been used in rehabilitation for several years, in both neurological and musculoskeletal contexts. It has also shown promise in managing CLBP, primarily through exergames and non-immersive VR formats (projected on a screen away from the user).7,10 Immersive VR, delivered through a headset, has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing both acute and chronic pain. Moreover, it can be employed for gamification—a concept not currently applied in FRP but one that has proven effective in rehabilitating musculoskeletal disorders.11 12 Immersion and gamification align well with the principles of CFT, allowing patients to engage in movement while diverting their attention from pain, thereby limiting fears and avoidance behaviours. This approach also enhances patients’ MI abilities, as they must visualise their movements in immersive VR without real visual cues.

Despite the promising potential of immersive VR in rehabilitating CLBP, very few studies have incorporated such devices into rehabilitation practices. The impact on kinesiophobia has shown mixed results, necessitating further evaluation. We hypothesise VR adds value through immersive attentional shift reducing pain salience during movement, gamified graded exposure to trunk motions supporting CFT principles while minimising fear-avoidance, enhanced MI via congruent visuokinesthetic cues, and motivation and autonomy through feedback improving adherence in group-based FRPs. However, no studies have investigated the use of such devices within a group FRP context as an adjunct to multidisciplinary care. A primary challenge in using immersive VR is its inherently individual nature, highlighting the need to study the acceptability and feasibility of its use within a group rehabilitation programme.

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the acceptance of using an immersive VR device for healthcare providers managing patients with CLBP within a FRP. Additionally, the study aims to assess the preliminary and overall acceptance of the VR device from both healthcare providers and patients. We will explore the determinants influencing acceptance among both groups and evaluate the usability of the device from the patients’ perspective. Furthermore, we will monitor patient adherence to the VR sessions and examine the effects of the immersive VR programme on kinesiophobia, functional capabilities, pain levels and MI abilities. Through these objectives, this research seeks to enhance the integration of immersive VR into rehabilitation practices for CLBP.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study is a multicentric, prospective, single-arm exploratory interventional pilot trial aimed at assessing the feasibility of using an immersive VR device in a FRP for patients with CLBP.

Recruitment and inclusion process

Prior to patient recruitment, the coordinating investigator selects and recruits the caregivers who will take part in the study from the healthcare teams involved in the investigating centres’ FRP.

Patients receiving treatment for their condition in participating services and proposed for integration into an FRP will be invited to participate in the study. If a patient meets the selection criteria, the investigator will provide both oral and written information (in clear, understandable language) and address any questions. This information will be delivered during the consultation when the FRP is proposed or during preliminary assessments. In cases where a patient’s participation has been prearranged without a prior consultation, they will be contacted by phone 1 week before the programme for information dissemination and will receive the information letter by mail. In all cases, information will be provided several days before the programme begins to allow the patient time for reflection. If the patient agrees to participate, the informed consent form will be signed in duplicate by all parties involved. The date of information dissemination, the reflection period and the consent signing date will be documented in the patient’s source file.

Withdrawal criteria

Participants may withdraw from the study at any time, including during the programme, for any reason without needing to provide justification. The investigator has the authority to temporarily or permanently discontinue a participant’s involvement in the study or specific protocol-defined care if it is deemed in the participant’s best interest, particularly in cases of serious adverse effects or new developments concerning the research.

Study duration

The inclusion period will last 12 months, with a follow-up duration of 4–5 weeks (the length of the FRP), resulting in a total study duration of 13 months.

Intervention and treatment protocol

Patients participating in the study will engage in three 20 min VR sessions per week, in addition to the standard FRP, for a duration of 4–5 weeks. The VRFR intervention will use the H’ability system, a commercial VR rehabilitation solution providing a catalogue of configurable exergames (eg, supermarket, soccer, vegetable garden). The VR sessions will consist of a series of pre-programmed exercises designed to facilitate various trunk movements (flexion, extension, inclination, rotation) through exergames. Each exercise will last an average of 5–7 min, as recommended in the literature. These exercises have been developed in collaboration between the company providing the VR device and the rehabilitation teams at the participating centres.

The VR device used in this study is a headset featuring exercises and games designed to enhance motor skills during rehabilitation sessions. It is a wireless headset that does not require a controller, allowing for a wide range of movement without constraints. Once established, it provides a low-cost rehabilitation option with the possibility of monitoring and targeted activities tailored to individual needs.

Patients will be set up by a healthcare provider in a dedicated room for VR use. The provider will configure the device, assist the patient in donning it and initiate the programme, which consists of a predetermined series of exercises common to all patients in each centre. While the specific exercises may vary, the total duration of the session will be 20 min, consisting of various exercises interspersed with breaks. The principal investigator will propose a standardised series of exercises for all participating centres. The difficulty level and parameters of the exergames (eg, amplitude, speed, complexity of tasks) can be modified either between sessions or during the session itself, at the discretion of the supervising therapist, to ensure progressive adaptation to each patient’s abilities and tolerance. Depending on the centre’s resources, multiple devices may be available, allowing for simultaneous treatment of several patients. A healthcare provider will remain present throughout the session to monitor for any adverse effects. Following each session, a new patient will be prepared for the subsequent VR session.

We provide a detailed mapping of our intervention to the INVIRTUE reporting items in online supplemental table 3 (Slatman et al13, under review).

Patients will follow the standard FRP as typically offered at each centre. These multidisciplinary rehabilitation programmes may vary slightly among centres but generally consist of a four-week to five-week day hospital programme, including physiotherapy, hydrotherapy, occupational therapy, adapted physical activity and therapeutic education workshops. Some centres may also include sessions focused on psychomotricity, individual psychological support or dietary guidance. The scheduling of rehabilitation sessions will differ across centres, with activity durations ranging from 15 to 25 hours per week, and the number of patients per group may also vary.

Risks

The VR device is commonly used in individual rehabilitation programmes, with no significant side effects reported. However, some patients may experience adverse effects (nausea, vomiting, headaches, dizziness, sweating, pallor, loss of balance) during prolonged use of the device. Given the brief duration of the sessions in this study, these adverse effects are unlikely to occur; should they arise, they typically resolve quickly after discontinuing use. The healthcare provider present during the VR session will remind patients to report any discomfort or malaise so that the session can be stopped promptly and support can be provided until symptoms subside.

Follow-up procedures

Following inclusion in the study, caregivers complete the general questionnaire and the a priori acceptability questionnaire. Caregivers apply the FRP to patients taking part in the study, and in particular comply with the VR session procedures described. At the end of the study, after the last patient has been followed up at the centre, the caregivers complete the acceptance questionnaire.

On the first day of the programme, all patients enrolled in the FRP will complete a general questionnaire, an acceptability questionnaire (preliminary), the Dallas Pain Questionnaire (DPQ) and the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) to assess pain upon entering the centre. Patients included in the study will also complete the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK) and the Low Back Imagery Questionnaire (LBIQ) before the programme begins, with a total estimated completion time of 30 min for all five questionnaires.

Throughout the programme, participants will engage in three 20 min VR exercise sessions each week, in addition to their usual rehabilitation sessions and workshops. Pain levels will be evaluated using the VAS at the beginning and end of each VR session. Data to track adherence to the VR sessions will be collected weekly, noting whether sessions were completed and the reasons for any missed sessions.

On the last day of the programme, participants will again complete the VAS, the DPQ, the TSK and the LBIQ, along with the acceptance questionnaire and the System Usability Scale (SUS).

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome measure will be the overall score from the acceptance questionnaire designed for healthcare providers, completed at the end of the study by those involved in conducting the VR sessions. A higher score indicates that the provider views the use of the VR device as acceptable within the context of an FRP. This questionnaire was developed based on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 2 (UTAUT2) model,14 which is widely used in acceptability studies15 and has previously been employed in VR acceptability research.15,20 The questionnaire consists of several components (overall judgement, perceived usefulness, performance expectancy, ease of use, social influence, facilitating conditions, hedonic motivation, intention to use and adaptability), each with items rated on a Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Each component will be averaged to yield scores ranging from 1 to 7, with higher scores reflecting greater acceptability. This questionnaire will be administered to healthcare professionals (occupational therapists, physiotherapists, adapted physical activity instructors and psychomotricians) who are involved in the care of patients participating in FRPs and using the VR device. All participating providers will be trained in the use of the device and will be experienced in managing patients with CLBP within the FRP framework.

The scores from the individual components of the acceptance questionnaire will also be considered secondary outcome measures.

Secondary outcome measures

A general questionnaire will be completed by both healthcare providers and patients to collect data on various determinants (sociodemographic characteristics, profession, experience with VR, etc.). The subscores of the acceptance questionnaire for healthcare providers, based on the UTAUT2 model, will be analysed as secondary outcome measures. Additionally, a patient acceptance questionnaire using the same model will assess acceptance and its determinants among patients participating in the FRP and using the VR device. This questionnaire will be completed by patients on the final day of the programme.

Both acceptance questionnaires will also be administered to evaluate preliminary acceptability for healthcare providers (at the beginning of the study) and patients (on the first day of the programme), following explanations about the device’s functionality and intended use in the programme, as well as training for providers on device usage.

The SUS21 is a validated self-administered questionnaire validated in French22 (figure 1) designed to measure the usability of interactive systems. It comprises 10 items, each associated with a 5-point Likert scale (from 1=strongly disagree to 5=strongly agree). The overall score is calculated by subtracting 1 from the score for items 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9, and then calculating 5 minus the score for items 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 (which are negatively worded items). The 10 scores are summed and multiplied by 2.5, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 100, where a higher score indicates better usability. Bangor et al determined that a usability score above 52 is acceptable, above 73 is good and above 85 is excellent.23

French System Usability Scale.

Patient adherence to the VR sessions will be tracked, with the percentage of patients completing more than 80% of the sessions calculated. Reasons for any premature cessation of VR sessions during the programme will also be documented.

TSK

The TSK is a validated self-administered questionnaire, validated in French et al24 that evaluates an individual’s level of kinesiophobia. It consists of 17 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Total scores range from 17 to 68, with higher scores indicating greater levels of kinesiophobia. Vlaeyen et al25 consider a score of 40 or above to signify significant kinesiophobia.

DPQ

The DPQ is a self-administered questionnaire, validated in French et al,26 assessing patients’ perceived functional ability. It consists of four sections covering daily activities, work and leisure activities, anxiety and depression, and sociability. These sections are made up of three to seven Likert scale items. The result of each section is expressed as a percentage, with a higher percentage indicating greater activity limitation.

VAS

Pain will be assessed by patients at the beginning of the programme, before and after each VR session and at the end of the programme.

LBIQ

This innovative self-administered questionnaire, derived from the Motor Imagery Questionnaire, evaluates visual and kinesthetic MI abilities specific to CLBP. Patients will be asked to physically perform a specific movement and then imagine it, either by visualising (visual MI) or feeling (kinesthetic MI) it. They will then rate their ability to imagine the movement on a 7-point Likert scale (from 1=very difficult to imagine to 7=very easy to imagine). There are seven different movements, each assessed in both modalities. The total score is the sum of the 14 items, which can be divided into two subscores (visual and kinesthetic). A higher score indicates better MI capabilities. This questionnaire has been specifically designed for patients with CLBP to imagine movements typically limited in this population. The estimated completion time is 15 min, and to our knowledge, there is no specific questionnaire for this pathology.

Statistics

Given the organisational structure of the participating centres and the likelihood that not all healthcare providers will agree to participate in the study (particularly in completing the provider questionnaires), we estimate that approximately eight healthcare providers will be included. The target sample size for patient enrolment has been set at 20 patients.

Qualitative variables will be presented using standard graphical tools (bar plots, contingency tables) based on counts and frequencies, along with 95% CIs. Under acceptable conditions for applicability, χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests will be performed with a significance threshold set at 5%. Central tendency and dispersion indicators for quantitative variables will be described using means and SD for normally distributed variables, or medians, IQRs and minimum/maximum values for non-normally distributed variables. To graphically represent these variables, violin plots will be employed. Under acceptable conditions for applicability, Student’s t-tests or Mann-Whitney tests will be conducted with a significance threshold of 5%, depending on whether the data are paired or independent. The overall acceptability score will be calculated as the mean score from each healthcare provider and patient, as well as from the different subscale, with success defined as a mean score of at least 5 out of 7 and an intention-to-use score of at least 5 out of 7 at study end. The overall SUS score will be computed as the average of the self-administered questionnaires for each patient, with a SUS score greater than 73 considered acceptable and a score above 85 deemed excellent. Adherence will be judged acceptable if at least 70% of included patients complete at least 80% of the planned VR sessions, with reasons for missed sessions documented. Safety will be confirmed if no serious adverse events related to VR occur.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol has been submitted to the Comité de Protection des Personnes, which issued a favourable opinion for the implementation of this research on 10 January 2025, in accordance with the provisions of French legislation (Jardé Law (No. 2012-300) and the Public Health Code) and international ethical standards (such as the Declaration of Helsinki).

It is registered on clinicaltrial.gov NCT06724679 (submitted 2024-11-29). All participants will be fully informed about the purpose, procedures, potential risks and their rights and will provide their free and informed consent to participate in the study, in accordance with ethical guidelines. No identifiable data will be published. Any photographs including identifiable individuals will require written permission; otherwise, such images will be removed or anonymised. Findings will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations; a lay summary will be shared with participating centres.

Discussion

This study introduces an innovative rehabilitation approach through the integration of a VR headset into the standard care regimen for patients with CLBP. Previous research has demonstrated the beneficial effects of VR interventions in managing chronic pain, and it is anticipated that similar positive outcomes will be observed in the participants of this study. Central to this hypothesis is the assumption that the acceptance and usability of the VR device will be favourable within a group rehabilitation context.

The exploratory nature of this pilot study is essential, as it aims to evaluate the acceptance of the immersive VR device among both healthcare providers and patients participating in the FRP. If the findings indicate that healthcare providers and patients view the VR headset positively, along with satisfactory usability and adherence to the VR programme, it would confirm the feasibility of implementing this technology within the established FRP framework. Conversely, should the results reveal limited acceptance or usability, this will yield valuable insights into the specific barriers and facilitators impacting the effective use of VR in this context.

Given that this is the first time the VR device has been integrated into an FRP, the study is justified as it seeks to minimise organisational constraints while facilitating the incorporation of innovative care practices. The acceptance and usability questionnaires chosen for this study are specifically designed to evaluate the potential for recurrent use of the VR device within a structured and ongoing rehabilitation programme. By focusing on a well-defined population of patients with CLBP, the study aims to gather relevant data that can inform future clinical applications.

To comprehensively assess the acceptance of the VR intervention, evaluations will be conducted both prospectively and retrospectively. Prospective assessments will take place after participants receive explanations about the device’s functionality and intended use, allowing researchers to gauge expectations and perceptions surrounding this novel treatment modality. Retrospective evaluations will assess the actual acceptance of the intervention following its implementation, providing a balanced understanding of participants’ experiences.

Another significant aspect of this study is its exploration of the effects of VR sessions on MI capabilities—an area that has not been previously investigated in this population. To address this, an innovative CLBP-specific MI questionnaire will be used, facilitating exploratory assessments that could yield critical insights into the cognitive processes associated with pain management and rehabilitation.

The participating centres in this study are integral members of the Lombaction network, a well-established framework dedicated to the evaluation, management and research of CLBP. This affiliation not only provides access to a robust support system but also ensures that the study is grounded in best practices for managing this complex condition.

In conclusion, the potential implications of this study extend beyond confirming the feasibility of using VR technology in FRP. Should the initial results demonstrate promising effects on kinesiophobia, functional capabilities, pain levels and overall patient satisfaction, it would pave the way for larger-scale comparative trials. These future studies could more rigorously evaluate the contribution of VR techniques to multidisciplinary rehabilitation approaches for CLBP, ultimately enhancing therapeutic outcomes and patient engagement.

This single-arm pilot study is not designed or powered to test clinical efficacy. Results may be affected by selection and response biases. Heterogeneity across centres and therapist practices within FRPs may influence implementation outcomes despite a standardised VR dose. The short follow-up restricted to the programme limits inference on sustainability. Device-specific hardware/software and content may constrain generalisability to other VR solutions.

Publishing this protocol reduces research waste and ensures methodological transparency on a novel group-based VR integration within established FRPs. It enables peer scrutiny of our a priori feasibility thresholds, measurement strategy and workflow/safety procedures before outcomes are known. Given the organisational stakes of adding immersive VR to a day hospital multidisciplinary programme across Lombaction centres, protocol publication adds value for implementation science and informs the design of future comparative trials.
