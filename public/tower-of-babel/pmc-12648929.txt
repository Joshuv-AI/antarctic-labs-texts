Fine-scale assessment of ROH patterns and genomic inbreeding in diverse horse breeds using two SNP array densities
Authors: Moslem Moghbeli Damane, Ahmad Ayatollahi Mehrgardi, Ali Esmailizadeh, Mehdi Momen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInbreeding is caused by mating between related individuals and is associated with reduced fitness and performance. Generally, in the horse population, inbreeding is caused by geographically restricted areas and intensive natural or artificial selection. For this reason, assessing accurate inbreeding is essential for developing and implementing effective breeding strategies aimed at preserving genetic diversity and reducing the harmful consequences of inbreeding. One of the most accurate approaches for assessing genomic inbreeding and autozygosity is through the analysis of runs of homozygosity (ROH), which are long stretches of homozygous genotypes inherited from common ancestors and provide valuable insights into population diversity, demographic history, and selection.MethodsIn this study, we analyzed the distribution of ROH, estimated genomic inbreeding coefficients, and mapped ROH islands across 14 diverse horse breeds. We used 670 K and MNEc2M SNP array datasets, comprising 279,040 SNPs from 424 horses and 1,083,942 SNPs from 438 horses, respectively. A total of 35,396 and 17,382 ROHs were detected in all breeds for the 670 K and MNEc2M SNP array datasets, respectively.ResultsThe majority of the detected ROHs were < 16 Mb (only 2.23% and 1.38% were greater than 16 Mb in for the 670 K and MNEc2M SNP array datasets, respectively) and the average total number of ROHs per individual were 81.36 ± 33.38 and 38.71 ± 22.73 for the 670 K and MNEc2M SNP array datasets, respectively. The mean ROH length per individual was 248.57 Mb for the 670 K SNP array and 89.56 Mb for the MNEc2M SNP array. Genomic inbreeding based on ROH (FROH) was relatively higher than homozygosity-based inbreeding (FHOM) and ranged from 0.0364 ± 0.0049 to 0.2357 ± 0.0584 and from 0.004 ± 0.0044 to 0.1102 ± 0.0779 for 670 K and MNEc2M SNP array datasets, respectively.ConclusionsWe identified genomic regions with high ROH coverage so called ROH islands that may reflect recent selection events. Several chromosomes, including ECA7 and ECA11, contained large ROH islands that include genes associated with important traits such as pigmentation, fertility, and performance. These ROH islands represent genomic footprints of past selection events and serve as candidate regions for future functional and association studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05143-7.Keywords: Runs of homozygosity, High-density SNP arrays, Genomic inbreeding, ROH island, Horse

Background

Inbreeding arises when mating occurs between individuals that share one or more common ancestors, resulting in an increased probability that their offspring will inherit alleles that are identical by descent (IBD) [1]. While inbreeding is a key concept in evolutionary biology and forms the basis of many animal and plant breeding strategies, its interpretation can be confounded by failure to clearly differentiate between related and unrelated individuals. As highlighted by Templeton and Read [1], inbreeding should be defined as the mating of individuals who are more genetically similar than expected based on the average relatedness within the population. With the advent of genomic data, quantifying inbreeding requires distinguishing between alleles that are IBD and those that are identical by state (IBS), which can be difficult when analyses rely on single markers rather than haplotypes or genome-wide patterns, underscoring the need for high-resolution data and appropriate analytical methods [2].

Inbreeding is an inevitable process in horse populations, particularly in breeds subjected to strong artificial selection or with small effective population sizes. A key genetic consequence of inbreeding is a shift in genotype frequencies, characterized by increased homozygosity and reduced heterozygosity, while overall allele frequencies remain unchanged [3]. This alteration influences the distribution of genetic variation within and between populations, potentially reducing performance in fitness-related traits and increasing the likelihood of deleterious recessive alleles being expressed in homozygous form [3–5]. Because of the importance of inbreeding in animal breeding, conservation, and human health, various genomic tools have been developed to more precisely assess inbreeding levels and identify regions of concern across the genome.

Traditionally, inbreeding levels have been estimated using pedigree-based genomic inbreeding coefficients (FPED) [6]. While widely used, this approach has several limitations: it relies on the expected proportion of the genome that is identical by descent without accounting for recombination, assumes unrelated founders ignoring hidden relationships [7], presumes a uniform distribution of autozygosity across the genome, and is vulnerable to errors in pedigree records due to misidentification or incorrect assignment of parentage [8]. These limitations can result in biased estimates, emphasizing the need for genomic-based methods that provide more accurate and comprehensive assessments.

In response to these limitations, high-density, genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays have enabled the calculation of individual genomic inbreeding coefficients based on molecular data. One widely applied method is the detection of extended homozygous regions, known as runs of homozygosity (ROH) [9], defined as consecutive homozygous segments where the two haplotypes inherited from each parent are identical by descent [10]. ROH represent important genomic signatures and provide direct measures of inbreeding and individual autozygosity. They also serve as valuable tools to explore genome structure and diversity [11–15].

ROH patterns not only estimate inbreeding via FROH (the proportion of the genome in ROH segments) [13, 15], but also reflect demographic history and selection. Long ROH indicate recent inbreeding, whereas shorter ROH point to ancient relatedness. Population bottlenecks, genetic drift, and selection pressures further shape ROH distribution. ROH are not evenly spread across the genome but tend to cluster in “ROH islands,” often overlapping regions under selection, making them useful for identifying loci associated with economically important traits [16–19]. Compared to pedigree-based estimates, ROH-derived inbreeding coefficients offer more accurate predictions without reliance on pedigree data.

The resolution of ROH detection depends strongly on the density of SNP genotyping arrays. High-density panels improve detection of shorter homozygous segments [13, 20], while lower-density arrays may overestimate short ROHs (< 4 Mb) or underestimate very long ROHs (> 8 Mb) [21–23]. Therefore, marker coverage is a critical consideration in ROH-based analyses. These two platforms (670 K and 2 M) were selected to evaluate the impact of SNP density on ROH detection and to compare their resolution in capturing both short and long homozygous segments.

In horses, Khanshour et al. [24] applied ROH analysis to detect signatures of positive selection in the Arabian breed, revealing longer ROHs and elevated genomic inbreeding in Sorraia and Thoroughbred horses. Metzger et al. [25] analyzed ROH distribution across 10 populations, identifying 3,784 ROHs and noting population-specific variation for longer ROHs. Chen et al. [26] sequenced 97 horses from 16 breeds to characterize ROH patterns and estimate genomic inbreeding, highlighting breed-specific differences in contributions of ancient versus recent inbreeding and identifying 24 ROH islands in Thoroughbreds harboring 72 candidate genes under artificial selection. Dos Santos et al. [27] performed a genome-wide analysis of Mangalarga Marchador horses, delineating homozygous and heterozygous regions using the 670 K SNP array and assessing inbreeding through multiple coefficients, including ROH and ROHet.

Building on these previous studies, the present research further investigates ROH patterns across multiple breeds using high-density SNP arrays, aiming to provide a more detailed understanding of genomic inbreeding and selection signatures.

The primary question addressed in this study is how inbreeding patterns, particularly runs of homozygosity (ROH), vary across different horse breeds and how different SNP genotyping platforms (670 K and 2 M) influence the detection and characterization of these ROH regions. The present study fills a specific gap by providing a comprehensive analysis that examines ROH distribution across multiple breeds, evaluates the effect of SNP density on ROH detection, and identifies breed-specific as well as shared genomic regions under selection while elucidating their functional significance.

While previous studies have investigated ROH and inbreeding in horses using lower-density arrays [25, 27] this study provides a more comprehensive perspective by leveraging high-density SNP data, offering a detailed understanding of the relationships among genome structure, breed history, and selective pressures.

Considering the importance of preserving genetic diversity in indigenous horse breeds in Iran and worldwide, and the need for accurate estimation using appropriate methods, the aim of this study was to investigate ROH distribution across six Iranian native horse breeds and nine additional breeds using two high-density SNP genotyping arrays (670 K and 2 M SNPs). Genomic inbreeding coefficients based on ROH were calculated, and their correlation with homozygosity-based inbreeding (FHOM) was evaluated.

Materials and methods

Animals and samples

In this study, two previously published datasets were used. The first dataset included 101 horses, representing five native Iranian breeds: Persian Arabian (n = 71), Turkmene (n = 11), Caspian (n = 7), Kurdish (n = 7), and Dareshuri (n = 5). Genotyping was conducted using the Affymetrix Equine SNP 670 K BeadChip (Santa Clara, CA), which targets 670,796 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [28]. These samples were originally collected by Sadeghi et al. [29] and are publicly accessible via the Dryad Digital Repository [29].

The second dataset comprised 337 horses, from nine diverse breeds: Thoroughbred (n = 28), Quarter Horse (n = 72), Belgian (n = 22), Arabian (n = 36), Welsh Pony (n = 40), Standardbred (n = 39), Icelandic Horse (n = 18), Lusitano (n = 21), and Morgan (n = 61). These individuals were genotyped using the high-density MNEc2M SNP array and are available via the Dryad Digital Repository [30].

Dataset and quality control

Genotype quality control was carried out using PLINK v1.90 software [31], based on the following filtering criteria: (1) minor allele frequency (MAF < 0.05), (2) Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE P-value < 10− 6), and (3) Only biallelic SNPs on the 31 autosomes were retained, and both animals and SNPs with call rates < 80% were discarded. To generate the final datasets for evaluating the effect of marker density on the detection of genomic runs of homozygosity (ROH) and genomic inbreeding levels, two datasets were constructed as follows. The first combined dataset (hereafter referred to as the 670 K SNP array data) contained SNPs that were shared between the two original datasets. After merging and performing quality control, the final combined dataset included 279,040 SNPs from 424 horses, representing 14 different breeds. To construct the second combined dataset (hereafter referred to as the MNEc2M SNP array data), after merging, a reference population of the 337 horses with genotypes at ~ 2 M SNPs was used as a reference population for imputation. Imputation was performed using Beagle 5.5 [32], with default parameters, except for the effective population size, which was set to Ne = 1000. After quality control, the final dataset included 1,083,942 SNPs from 438 horses, representing 14 different breeds.

Effective population size (Ne) Estimation

Effective population size (Ne) can be inferred from molecular data, by analyzing linkage disequilibrium (LD), typically measured using the r² coefficient. In this study, Ne was estimated for each dataset, based on LD patterns, employing SNeP v1.1 software, with default settings [33].

Runs of homozygosity detection

Runs of homozygosity (ROH) were detected using PLINK v1.9, employing the --homozyg function, with a sliding window approach across all 31 autosomes. ROH were defined based on the following criteria: Given that no LD pruning was performed, a minimum ROH length of 1 Mb was set, to exclude the detection of false ROHs resulting from linkage disequilibrium (--homozyg-kb 1000) [34, 35]. at least 50 consecutive homozygous SNPs per ROH (--homozyg-snp 50), a minimum SNP density of one SNP per 50 kb (--homozyg-density 50), maximum gap of 1000 kb between adjacent SNPs (--homozyg-gap 1000), and the sliding window consisted of 50 SNPs (--homozyg-window-snp 50). The threshold for the maximum number of heterozygous SNPs, and the maximum number of missing SNPs was determined using the --homozyg-window-het and --homozyg-window-missing commands, respectively. A SNP was considered to be part of a ROH if it was found in at least 5% of the sliding windows meeting these criteria (--homozyg-window-threshold 0.05) [35]. Further filtering was applied based on ROH length: no heterozygous SNPs were allowed in ROHs shorter than 16 Mb, assuming a genotyping error rate of 0.2% [36]. The number of missing genotypes allowed within a ROH varied by length: ROH > 1 Mb — 0 missing, > 4 Mb — 1 missing, > 8 Mb — 2 missing, and ROH > 16 Mb — 3 missing. ROHs were categorized into four length classes: 1–4 Mb, 4–8 Mb, 8–16 Mb, and above 16 Mb [37, 38]. For each individual, the total ROH length was summed, and then averaged across all individuals within each breed to compute the mean sum of ROH per breed. Additionally, the total number, mean number, and average length of ROHs were calculated per breed and per length category.

Genomic inbreeding coefficients

Two genomic inbreeding coefficients were calculated: first, inbreeding coefficient based on runs of homozygosity (FROH), and the second by the excess of homozygosity-based inbreeding coefficient (FHOM). FROH was calculated as the proportion of the autosomal genome covered by ROHs, with the total length of ROHs in each predefined category divided by the total length of the autosomes [13]. ROHs were classified into four length categories: 1–4 Mb, 4–8 Mb, 8–16 Mb, and above 16 Mb, and FROH values were computed separately for each category and for all ROHs (including ROHs of all lengths). The FHOM coefficient was calculated in PLINK v1.9, based on the difference between the observed and expected number of homozygous genotypes across the genome. Detection of ROH islands and gene annotation within the breeds

ROH islands, representing genomic regions potentially subject to selection pressure, were identified across all 14 breeds. To identify ROH islands (also referred to as ROH hotspots), an approach based on the frequency of SNPs occurring within ROH segments was employed. ROH output, generated by PLINK v1.9, was used to calculate the number of certain SNP occurrences divided by the number of animals in each breed. For each breed, the top 1% of SNPs with the highest frequency in ROH were considered indicative of genomic regions with strong ROH association. Adjacent high-frequency SNPs located within 1 Mb of each other were then clustered together to define ROH islands [39]. A similar threshold has been considered in various studies on sheep and cattle breeds [40, 41].

To identify genes located within ROH islands, gene annotation data was extracted from the Equus caballus reference genome (EquCab3.0.110) using a GTF file. The gene features were imported using the “rtracklayer” package in R, and only entries annotated as “gene” were retained. For each breed, the coordinates of ROH islands were compared with the gene annotation data to detect overlaps. A gene was considered to be within an ROH island if its genomic coordinates overlapped with the start and end positions of the island on the same chromosome. For each ROH island, the number of overlapping genes was determined and recorded. This process was initially applied to all ROH islands per breed and then specifically to the top five ROH islands with the highest mean SNP frequency for each breed, to identify candidate genes potentially under selection [38].

Pathway and functional analysis

To investigate the biological relevance of genes within the most prominent ROH islands, functional annotation and enrichment analysis were performed. For each breed, genes from the top five ROH islands, identified based on the highest SNP frequencies, were extracted and queried against the Ensembl database via BioMart (biomaRt R package) for gene descriptions, biotypes, and associated Gene Ontology (GO) terms, classified into biological process, molecular function, and phenotype association. Enrichment analysis was performed using default parameters. This breed-specific analysis, based solely on ROH regions, highlights candidate genes under potential selection and their biological roles, building on previous studies linking ROH islands to selection signatures [27].

Results

Effective population size (Ne)

The results of the effective population size (Ne) estimated using the 670 K and MNEc2M SNP arrays are presented in Fig. 1. Analysis of the 670 K SNP array data, covering 15 to 999 generations ago in horse breeds, revealed a pronounced and consistent increase in Ne from recent to past generations. Around 15 generations ago, Ne was estimated at just 87 individuals, indicating a relatively small breeding population. From that point onward, Ne rose substantially, reaching 379 by 67 generations ago, 740 by 99 generations ago, and 1,260 by 150 generations ago. A notable decline is observed from older to more recent generations, during which Ne decreased markedly, likely reflecting the impact of intensive selection and controlled breeding practices rather than natural population growth. Data from the 670 K SNP array shows Ne declining from a historical peak of 4,132 at generation 983 to 87 at generation 15.

Comparison of Ne estimates across generations using two high-density SNP genotyping platforms

Similarly, the higher-density MNEc2M SNP array, representing the same populations but with greater marker resolution and starting at generation 40, shows a broadly similar downward pattern, albeit with consistently higher Ne values. For example, Ne decreased from 5,710 at generation 999 to 1,395 at generation 301 and 603 at generation 40. This may reflect the enhanced resolution and sensitivity of the higher-density array in capturing declines in effective population size. In summary, both datasets support the conclusion of a reduction in Ne in contemporary horse populations, with the most substantial declines occurring from mid to recent generations. The differences in Ne estimates between the arrays underscore the impact of marker density, and methodological considerations, on demographic inference.

ROH characteristics

One of the most important parameters characterizing the structure of ROH is the number and average sum of ROH lengths per animal. In the dataset with lower SNP array density (670 K SNP array), the highest mean number of ROH per individual was observed in the Thoroughbred breed (180 ROH/animal), followed by the Persian Arabian (101) and Lusitano (97). In contrast, the lowest mean values were found in the Turkmen (45), Kurdish (47), and Caspian (53) breeds (Fig. 2). When considering the average sum of ROH lengths per individual, the highest values were again observed in the Thoroughbred (534.45 Mb), followed by the Lusitano (326.37 Mb), Persian Arabian (296.80 Mb), and Belgian (291.31 Mb) breeds. The lowest ROH lengths per animal were seen in the Kurdish (82.51 Mb), Turkmen (119.96 Mb), and Dareshuri (145.71 Mb) breeds. This pattern indicates a higher level of autozygosity and possibly stronger inbreeding in some breeds (Fig. 3).

Although the overall number of detected ROHs per individual decreased due to the higher resolution of the MNEc2M SNP array, the trends between breeds remained generally consistent. The mean total length of ROH per individual was highest in the Persian Arabian (251.26 Mb), followed by Caspian (194.11 Mb) and Thoroughbred (144.55 Mb), whereas Welsh (10.94 Mb) and Icelandic (9.22 Mb) had the lowest values (Fig. 5).

Overall, breeds such as Thoroughbred, Arabian, and Persian Arabian consistently exhibited the highest ROH counts. In the 670 K SNP array, the mean number of ROH was 180, 81.97, and 101 in Thoroughbred, Arabian, and Persian Arabian, respectively, while in the MNEc2M SNP array, the corresponding values were 64, 52, and 90, respectively. This pattern may reflect historical population bottlenecks, small effective population sizes, or intensive selective breeding. Breeds such as Turkemen, Welsh, Icelandic, and Lusitano exhibited the lowest ROH counts. In the 670 K array, the mean ROH counts for Turkemen and Welsh were 45 and 69, respectively, whereas in the MNEc2M array, the lowest counts were observed in Icelandic and Lusitano, with mean values of 7 and 13, respectively. These differences likely reflect broader genetic diversity or distinct breeding strategies.

Regarding total ROH lengths, breeds such as Caspian, Persian Arabian, and Turkemen consistently had the highest total ROH per individual. In the MNEc2M SNP array, the mean total ROH lengths were 4.25, 2.78, and 2.60 Mb in Caspian, Persian Arabian, and Turkemen, respectively, while in the 670 K SNP array, the corresponding values were 4.50, 3.68, and 3.57 Mb in Caspian, Welsh, and Morgan, respectively. In contrast, breeds such as Welsh, Lusitano, and Icelandic had the lowest total ROH lengths in the MNEc2M array (1.23, 1.24, and 1.25 Mb, respectively), whereas the shortest ROH lengths in the 670 K array were observed in Kurdish, Dareshuri, and Turkemen, with mean values of 1.75, 2.23, and 2.68 Mb, respectively.

Finally, comparison between arrays highlights that SNP density affects ROH detection and length estimation, with the lower-density 670 K array generally reporting more ROH segments and longer lengths due to lower resolution for detecting shorter segments.

A summary of ROH segment statistics across horse breeds is presented in Tables 1 and 2. Regarding the classification of ROH by length, short ROHs (1–4 Mb) were the most common in nearly all breeds across both datasets. Long ROHs (> 16 Mb), often associated with recent inbreeding, were particularly frequent in breeds such as Persian Arabian (165 in low-density data, 112 in high-density data), Morgan (117 in low-density data), and Standardbred (90 and 23 in low- and high-density, respectively). In contrast, several breeds such as Icelandic, Lusitano, and Welsh, displayed no ROH segments longer than 8 Mb in the higher-density data, reflecting a history of more diverse ancestry or less recent inbreeding. These results indicate considerable variability in autozygosity among horse breeds, likely shaped by differences in breeding practices, effective population sizes, and historical bottlenecks.

Impact of SNP chip density on ROH identification

Investigation of the impact of SNP chip density on ROH detection using two genotyping platforms across 14 horse breeds, as shown in Tables 1 and 2, indicated that, overall, more ROH segments were identified using the 670 K panel compared to the MNEc2M array, with a total of 35,396 ROHs (87 ROHs per individual) detected with 670 K and 17,382 ROHs (42 ROHs per individual) detected with MNEc2M. The 670 K panel notably detected a higher number of ROHs in the 1–4 Mb category across nearly all breeds. For example, Persian Arabian horses had 6,146 ROHs with the 670 K chip, whereas only 5,481 were detected with the MNEc2M array. In Thoroughbred, 3,695 short ROHs were detected with 670 K compared to 1,651 with MNEc2M, and in Lusitano, 1,631 compared to 263, suggesting that the 670 K array may overestimate the number of short ROHs.

Breed-specific patterns were evident, but the ranking of breeds in terms of ROH number and length changed with SNP array density. Using the 670 K array, the highest ROH counts and lengths were observed in Thoroughbred (180 ROHs per individual, total length 534.45 Mb) and Persian Arabian (101 ROHs per individual, total length 296.8 Mb). In contrast, the lowest values were found in Turkmene (45 ROHs, total length 119.96 Mb), Kurdish (47 ROHs, total length 82.51 Mb), and Dareshuri (65 ROHs, total length 145.71 Mb). With the MNEc2M array, despite higher SNP density, breed rankings changed: the lowest values were observed in Welsh (9 ROHs, total length 10.94 Mb) and Lusitano (13 ROHs, total length 15.58 Mb), while the highest values remained in Persian Arabian (90 ROHs per individual, total length 251.26 Mb) and Thoroughbred (64 ROHs, total length 144.55 Mb). These ranking changes indicate that SNP array density not only affects ROH detection sensitivity but can also alter genetic diversity patterns and ROH length distributions across breeds. Differences are especially pronounced in short ROHs (1–4 Mb), which can lead to a reshuffling of breeds in terms of maximum and minimum ROH values, highlighting the importance of considering SNP array density and design in genetic analyses and breed comparisons.

Contrary to expectations, the number of short ROHs was higher in the lower-density 670 K array, which may be due to overestimation of short ROHs by this lower-density platform. As ROH length increased, the differences between arrays generally decreased, particularly for long ROHs (> 16 Mb), where the MNEc2M array often detected a similar or slightly lower number compared to 670 K. For example, in Caspian horses, 19 long ROHs were detected with 670 K and 17 with MNEc2M, indicating close agreement for long segment detection. However, in certain breeds such as Morgan and Welsh, long ROHs were more frequently detected in the lower-density 670 K array (117 versus 8 in Morgan, 95 versus 0 in Welsh). This can be explained by the reduced sensitivity of the lower-density array to heterozygosity and missing data; long regions that would be segmented into shorter ROHs in the higher-density array are recognized as single, longer ROHs in the lower-density array. These observations indicate that SNP array density not only affects the detection of short and medium ROHs but can also substantially influence the identification, number, and length distribution of long ROHs across different breeds, highlighting the complex interaction between array design and breed-specific genome structure.

We also show the coverage of runs of homozygosity per chromosome, categorized by ROH length, in both datasets (see Additional files 1 and 2). Overall, the comparison of genotyping arrays with MNEc2M and 670 K SNP densities demonstrates that each array exhibits distinct capabilities in detecting runs of homozygosity (ROH). The MNEc2M array, owing to its higher marker density, shows enhanced sensitivity for identifying shorter ROH segments, particularly within the 1–4 Mb and 4–8 Mb categories, providing more precise coverage than the 670 K array. In contrast, the 670 K array performs adequately in detecting longer ROH segments, such as those exceeding 16 Mb, and in certain breeds, it even exhibits higher coverage than MNEc2M. These differences highlight that array selection should be guided by the study objectives, the type of ROH analysis, and the expected trade-off between resolution and cost.

In general, the MNEc2M array is better suited for high-resolution studies focusing on inbreeding and genetic selection, whereas the 670 K array, with its lower cost and sufficient coverage of longer ROH segments, represents a suitable option for broader, population-scale analyses. Therefore, these distinctions should be carefully considered when designing genetic studies and selecting appropriate analytical platforms.

At the chromosomal level, the comparison of the two arrays further indicates that ROH coverage patterns are influenced by marker density. In the MNEc2M array, higher marker density results in more accurate and uniform coverage across larger chromosomes, particularly for shorter ROH categories such as 1–4 Mb, facilitating the precise detection of short homozygous segments.

Conversely, the 670 K array demonstrates substantial coverage for ROH segments > 16 Mb in certain chromosomes, particularly medium and small-sized chromosomes. For instance, in some breeds, the coverage of this category in the 670 K array exceeds 0.95, whereas in the MNEc2M array, this value is either lower or such long segments are virtually absent. Thus, the MNEc2M array is more appropriate for fine-scale analysis of short ROH in large chromosomes, while the 670 K array effectively identifies long ROH segments in specific chromosomes and can serve as a complementary tool for studies of inbreeding and artificial selection. These chromosomal differences should be carefully considered in the design of genetic analyses and the selection of suitable genotyping panels.

Genomic inbreeding coefficients based on FROH and FHOM

In this study, we estimated genomic inbreeding coefficients using two SNP genotyping platforms: the 670 K SNP array (Table 3) and the MNEc2M array (Table 4). The genomic inbreeding coefficients were computed based on the proportion of the genome in runs of homozygosity (FROH), stratified by ROH length classes (1–4 Mb, 4–8 Mb, 8–16 Mb, and > 16 Mb), as well as using the FHOM statistic, which reflects excess homozygosity relative to Hardy-Weinberg expectations.

Across the breeds analyzed from the 670 K SNP dataset, the average FROH_all ranged from 0.0364 ± 0.0049 in Kurdish horses to 0.2357 ± 0.0584 in Thoroughbreds. Thoroughbred horses exhibited the highest inbreeding levels across multiple ROH length categories, particularly in short (1–4 Mb, 0.122) and medium ROHs length (4–8 Mb, 0.0582), indicating contributions from both ancient and recent inbreeding. High inbreeding values were also observed in Lusitano (0.1439 ± 0.1009) and Persian Arabian horses (0.1309 ± 0.0876). By contrast, primitive or regionally conserved breeds such as Turkmene (0.0529 ± 0.0249) and Kurdish (0.0364 ± 0.0049) had lower genomic inbreeding levels.

Using the MNEc2M array, we observed lower average FROH_all values overall, likely due to differences in SNP density and genomic coverage. For instance, Icelandic horses had a much lower FROH_all (0.004 ± 0.0044), with ROHs observed primarily in the 1–4 Mb category. Notably, the Persian Arabian breed showed the highest mean FROH_all (0.1102 ± 0.0779), largely driven by short ROHs. Caspian horses also had a high FROH_all (0.0851 ± 0.113) due to substantial contributions from long ROHs.

The FHOM values varied more widely than FROH. For example, Standardbred horses had one of the highest FHOM values in the 670 K array (0.1557 ± 0.0723), while Kurdish horses exhibited very low FHOM values (0.0143 ± 0.0082). In the MNEc2M dataset, FHOM was highest in Thoroughbred (0.1627 ± 0.0984) and Standardbred (0.1360 ± 0.0696) horses.

To compare the two inbreeding estimators (FROH_all and FHOM), we performed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for each breed, as the values were paired per individual but not normally distributed. The test showed significant differences between FROH_all and FHOM in the majority of breeds. For example, significant differences (p < 0.01) were observed in Lusitano, Persian Arabian, Quarter Horse, and Thoroughbred populations in both SNP datasets, suggesting systematic discrepancies between the estimators.

ROH island characteristics across breeds and gene content

Genomic regions with a high proportion of homozygosity, which are subject to strong selection pressure and are likely to harbor targets of positive selection, are referred to as ROH hotspots [42]. In each breed, genomic regions with a strong association to ROH were identified by selecting the top 1% of SNPs with the highest frequency of occurrence within ROH segments (see Additional files 3 and 4). SNPs in close proximity (≤ 1 Mb apart) were subsequently clustered to define ROH islands.

ROH island patterns across 14 horse breeds

Analysis of the 670 K and MNEc2M SNP array datasets revealed ROH islands across all 14 horse breeds, with marked variability in their number, length, and genomic distribution (see Additional files 5 and 6). This analysis summarizes the distribution and characteristics of ROH islands, offering insights into breed-specific patterns of selection, inbreeding, and historical demography.

ROH islands, defined as genomic regions where extended homozygous segments are shared by multiple individuals, offer valuable insights into historical demographic processes, recombination patterns, and genomic regions shaped by positive selection. In terms of the number of ROH islands, some breeds consistently displayed high counts across both datasets, indicating strong signals of shared homozygosity. For example, Arabian horses exhibited the highest number of ROH islands in the 670 K array (48 islands) and maintained a substantial number in the MNEc2M dataset (25 islands). Conversely, the Quarter Horse showed relatively few islands in the 670 K data (14), but the highest number in the MNEc2M dataset (33), possibly reflecting differences in SNP density or sample structure. Morgan (32 islands), Persian Arabian (28), and Standardbred (25) also exhibited high ROH counts in the MNEc2M dataset, supporting widespread and potentially recent inbreeding or selection. In contrast, breeds such as Kurdish (19 in 670 K, 3 in MNEc2M), Icelandic (16 in 670 K, 3 in MNEc2M), Dareshuri (7 in 670 K, 8 in MNEc2M), and Turkmene (12 in 670 K, 10 in MNEc2M) consistently showed fewer ROH islands, which may reflect higher genetic diversity, larger effective population sizes, or less intensive selective breeding.

When considering the average length of ROH islands, several breeds stood out for having particularly long segments, suggesting recent or strong selection. The Quarter Horse displayed the longest average island length in the 670 K data (2.91 Mb), while the Caspian breed had the longest average length in the MNEc2M dataset (2.165 Mb). Dareshuri and Turkmene also exhibited long mean ROH islands in both datasets (Dareshuri: 1.91 Mb in 670 K, 1.64 Mb in MNEc2M; Turkmene: 1.72 Mb in 670 K, 1.738 Mb in MNEc2M), despite having relatively few islands. This suggests that while these breeds may not have widespread genome-wide homozygosity, the ROH that do occur are longer and may reflect selective sweeps at specific loci. In contrast, Belgian and Lusitano horses had shorter average island lengths across both datasets (Belgian: 1.64 Mb in 670 K, 0.763 Mb in MNEc2M; Lusitano: 1.41 Mb in 670 K, 0.858 Mb in MNEc2M), indicating that their homozygous regions may be older or more fragmented due to recombination. The Kurdish breed, which had one of the lowest island counts, also exhibited relatively short ROH segments (1.16 Mb in 670 K, 1.298 Mb in MNEc2M).

Looking at the extremes in ROH island size, several breeds had exceptionally large ROH blocks that may reflect regions under strong or recent selection. In the 670 K dataset, Arabian and Quarter Horse horses showed particularly long ROH islands on ECA7, measuring 15.15 Mb and 14.63 Mb, respectively. Morgan horses displayed a large island on ECA13 (12.37 Mb), and Thoroughbred had a notable region on ECA1 (9.77 Mb). These findings were echoed in the MNEc2M data, where large ROH islands were detected in Caspian (8.582 Mb on Chr5), Turkmene (4.902 Mb on ECA7), Persian Arabian (4.761 Mb on ECA11), and Dareshuri (4.370 Mb on ECA11). These long segments suggest recent fixation events or sustained selection at key functional loci. In contrast, very short ROH islands less than 0.01 Mb were detected in several breeds in the 670 K dataset, such as Belgian (0.01 Mb on ECA5), Welsh (0.01 Mb on ECA18), Morgan (0.08 Mb on ECA7), and Thoroughbred (0.24 Mb on ECA4), and in the MNEc2M dataset (e.g., 0.006 Mb in Thoroughbred on ECA16). These likely represent background homozygosity or ancient inbreeding rather than recent selection.

In terms of chromosomal distribution, ECA7 emerged as the most consistently involved across both datasets, appearing in 13 out of 14 breeds in the 670 K array and in 10 breeds in the MNEc2M array. This suggests that ECA7 may harbor loci under strong and widespread selection. Other frequently represented chromosomes included ECAs 5, 11, 3, 8, and 4, particularly in breeds with high ROH counts, indicating shared selective targets. Some chromosomes showed more restricted involvement; for example, ECAs 27 and 30 were involved in only a few breeds in the 670 K dataset, while Kurdish and Icelandic horses in the MNEc2M dataset had ROH islands on only two or three chromosomes, reflecting breed-specific selection histories or differences in demographic background. Notably, ECA31 did not show any ROH islands in either dataset, suggesting limited selection pressure or recent inbreeding signals on this chromosome across the breeds studied.

Overall, the integration of results from the 670 K and MNEc2M SNP arrays provides a comprehensive picture of ROH island patterns in horses. The concordance between datasets supports robust inferences about recent and historical selection, while discrepancies highlight the importance of SNP density, sample composition, and breed-specific demographic factors in shaping ROH landscapes. These findings reinforce the utility of ROH-based analyses for understanding inbreeding, selection, and genetic diversity in domestic horse populations.

ROH island distribution (counts and coverage) across chromosomes

This study also, investigated the distribution of runs of homozygosity islands across the equine genome using two high-density SNP arrays (670 K and MNEc2M platforms). As shown in Figs. 6 and 7, the number of ROH islands varied substantially across chromosomes in both datasets, reflecting diverse patterns of homozygosity accumulation. In the 670 K array, ECA4 showed the highest number of ROH islands (n = 37), followed by ECA8 (n = 34), ECA3 (n = 32), and ECA7 (n = 29). Similarly, in the MNEc2M array, ECA7 had the highest count (n = 23), followed by ECA3 (n = 18), ECA9 (n = 16), and ECAs 4 and 8 (n = 15 each). These recurrent hotspots across both platforms suggest regions of potential selection or low recombination that conserve homozygosity.

ROH islands that are frequent and consistent across individuals are considered indicators of past selective sweeps, where beneficial alleles rise in frequency, carrying surrounding genomic regions into homozygosity. In both SNP arrays, ECAs 7 and 11 consistently ranked among the top in ROH island count and coverage, suggesting these regions harbor genes under strong positive selection. The presence of extensive ROH coverage on these chromosomes aligns with expectations for genomic regions that either experience suppressed recombination or are under strong artificial selection in horse breeding programs. Other chromosomes such as ECA s 3, 4, 8, and 9 also showed moderate to high ROH density across datasets, reinforcing their relevance to adaptive or economically important traits. In contrast, chromosomes with minimal ROH accumulation, such as ECAs 12, 6, 27, and 2, likely evolve under neutral dynamics or reflect high recombination zones that prevent long ROH segment persistence.

Functional annotation of candidate genes in ROH Islands

To identify candidate genes under potential selection, we analyzed runs of homozygosity (ROH) islands across 14 horse breeds using SNP array data. For the 670 K SNP array data, a total of 927 genes were identified within the top ROH islands across all breeds. Among these, 69 genes were found in ROH islands shared by at least three breeds, suggesting possible signatures of common selection or conserved biological functions (Additional file 7).

For comparison, analysis using the MNEc2M SNP array (2 M SNPs) identified 538 genes within ROH islands, of which 48 were shared among three or more breeds (39 genes shared between the two datasets) (Additional file 8). The reduced gene count likely reflects the increased resolution and SNP density of the higher-density array. Functional annotation using Ensembl BioMart indicated that these genes are involved in diverse biological processes, including development, metabolism, reproduction, and immune response, highlighting them as promising candidates for further investigation of breed-specific and shared adaptive traits.

Functional enrichment analysis of genes located within ROH islands identified using the 670 K SNP array revealed several biologically relevant categories. Notably, a significant proportion of genes were involved in metal ion binding and zinc-related molecular functions, consistent with enrichment in zinc finger transcription factors such as ZNF276, ZNF469, and ZFPM1. These transcriptional regulators likely play important roles in gene expression modulation and hematopoiesis, as exemplified by ZFPM1’s involvement in erythroid development.

Additionally, the analysis highlighted genes associated with key biological processes including transcription regulation, immune response, and cellular membrane functions. Several genes linked to known mammalian phenotypes and diseases were identified, such as MC1R (coat color regulation), SPG7 (equine degenerative myeloencephalopathy), and FANCA (DNA repair). This suggests that ROH islands may harbor genomic regions under selective pressure related to physiological traits and health in horses.

Overall, these findings suggest that the genomic regions identified as ROH islands contain functionally important genes involved in diverse biological pathways, including hematopoiesis, immune regulation, and metabolic processes, which could contribute to breed-specific adaptations and phenotypic diversity in horses.

Functional enrichment analysis of genes identified within ROH islands using the high-density MNEc2M SNP array revealed enrichment for genes involved in diverse molecular functions and biological processes critical for development, immunity, metabolism, and cellular homeostasis. Several genes related to transcriptional regulation and chromatin remodeling (e.g., ANKRD11, CBFA2T3, ZFPM1) suggest roles in hematopoiesis and neural development.

Key genes associated with immune regulation and inflammation, such as IL17C and DEF8, were also detected, indicating potential involvement in mucosal immunity and autophagy. Metabolic pathways were highlighted through genes like ACSF3, ACAD8, and MVD, reflecting fatty acid oxidation and cholesterol biosynthesis.

Notably, genes with known associations to mammalian diseases were found, including MC1R (coat color variation), SPG7 (equine degenerative myeloencephalopathy), FANCA (DNA repair deficiency), and others linked to neurodevelopmental disorders, cancer, and lysosomal storage diseases. The presence of genes involved in RNA processing (U6, PABPN1L, SNORD68) and vesicle trafficking (VPS26B, VPS9D1) suggests fundamental cellular maintenance functions within these ROH islands.

Overall, these results indicate that ROH islands detected with the MNEc2M SNP array encompass functionally important genes linked to breed-specific traits, disease susceptibilities, and essential physiological processes in horses.

Gene enrichment and annotation

Functional annotation of genes located within ROH islands, identified using the 670 K SNP array and analyzed with the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) v6.8 [43], showed that these genes are present across all breeds, with some shared among multiple breeds. The analysis revealed that they are involved in diverse biological processes, including development, metabolism, immune response, and RNA processing, and several are associated with known equine phenotypes or mammalian diseases. Enrichment in Gene Ontology (GO) terms highlighted overrepresented pathways, providing insights into the potential phenotypic relevance of these candidate genes across breeds.

Analysis of candidate genes using the high-density MNEc2M SNP array revealed that these genes are located within top ROH islands, with some shared among multiple breeds. They participate in processes such as neural development, hematopoiesis, immune regulation, metabolism, and cellular homeostasis, and some are linked to known equine phenotypes or mammalian diseases.

Notably, several genes are directly associated with known equine phenotypes, such as MC1R (coat color) and SPG7 (equine degenerative myeloencephalopathy). This analysis indicates that ROH islands identified with the MNEc2M array encompass functionally important genes related to breed-specific traits, physiological adaptation, and disease susceptibility in horses.

Overall, these results suggest that ROH islands in horses harbor functionally important genes that play key roles in breed-specific adaptations, vital biological functions, and the maintenance of shared genetic features among breeds.

Discussion

Distribution and patterns of ROH

The analysis of runs of homozygosity (ROH) across a diverse panel of horse breeds revealed pronounced variability in both the number and cumulative length of ROH segments. We analyzed the distribution of ROH lengths across 14 distinct horse breeds. The majority of ROHs detected fell within the short category (1–4 Mb), making them the most common segment length. Medium-length ROHs (4–8 Mb) and long ROHs (8–16 Mb) were observed less frequently, while very long ROHs exceeding 16 Mb were relatively rare across the populations studied. Short ROHs are generally more abundant because they often result from historical recombination events and reflect ancestral genetic diversity accumulated over many generations, whereas longer ROHs typically indicate recent inbreeding or strong selection [11].

Our findings on the distribution of ROH lengths across horse breeds are consistent with those reported by Chen et al. [26], Grilz-Seger et al. [44], and Nosrati et al. [38], (in sheep), who also observed that short ROHs constitute the majority of detected segments, followed by medium and long ROHs, with very long ROHs being relatively rare in diverse populations. This similarity supports the robustness of ROH length patterns across different datasets and methodologies in equine genetics.

Our analysis of the number of ROHs per individual across two SNP array densities revealed consistent breed-specific patterns. These findings align well with those reported by Szmatoła et al. [45], who documented significant variation in ROH numbers among horse breeds and noted that primitive breeds tend to exhibit the lowest ROH counts. Welsh and Icelandic horses, often classified as primitive or ancient native breeds due to their long-standing genetic history and minimal intensive breeding, displayed low ROH counts consistent with this classification. Although the Lusitano is not traditionally considered a primitive breed [46] instead, it is a classical Iberian breed with a history of selection for riding and sport it similarly exhibited low ROH counts in our study. Therefore, for the purposes of interpreting ROH levels and genetic diversity, Lusitano was grouped alongside the primitive breeds as showing lower autozygosity compared to highly selected breeds like the Thoroughbred. Our observation that Iranian breeds such as Turkmen, Kurdish, Caspian and Dareshuri also possess comparatively fewer ROHs supports the idea of a richer genetic background and lower autozygosity than intensively bred or specialized breeds.

Data analysis indicates that Iranian horse breeds, with the exception of Persian Arabian, exhibit higher genetic diversity. For instance, native breeds such as Dareshuri and Kurdish show significantly lower total ROH lengths per individual (145.71 Mb for Dareshuri and 82.51 Mb for Kurdish), whereas well-known foreign breeds like Thoroughbred and Standardbred display much higher values (534.45 Mb and 180 ROHs for Thoroughbred, 280.52 Mb and 85 ROHs for Standardbred). The Persian Arabian is a distinct case, with a total ROH length of 296.80 Mb and 101 ROHs per individual, reflecting a high degree of genetic consolidation, particularly evident in the abundance of long ROHs (8–16 Mb and > 16 Mb), highlighting the need for careful management to maintain genetic diversity. In contrast, Iranian native breeds such as Kurdish, Dareshuri, and Turkmene predominantly display short ROHs (1–4 Mb) with very few or no long ROHs, indicating a broad genetic base and a demographic history with substantial diversity, which makes them valuable for conservation programs. Examination of foreign breeds such as Icelandic and Welsh shows that, despite being non-native, these populations have moderate total ROH lengths and average ROH numbers, suggesting higher gene flow and lower exposure to strong selective pressures compared to Iranian breeds. Comparison between the 670 K and MNEc2M SNP arrays shows that the lower-density 670 K array may overestimate short ROHs, whereas the higher-density MNEc2M array provides more accurate and conservative estimates.

Overall, these results indicate that most Iranian native horse breeds, except for Persian Arabian, maintain a more diverse and robust genetic reservoir, while many well-known foreign breeds, due to closed breeding and intensive selection, display distinct patterns of genetic consolidation. Importantly, long ROHs are not merely indicative of inbreeding; they also highlight genomic regions under strong historical or industrial selection and often encompass genes associated with reproduction, development, metabolism, and performance traits, suggesting that breed-specific selective pressures have significantly shaped ROH patterns across horse populations [25, 47, 48]. The Persian Arabian, given its high level of genetic fixation, requires careful management. These findings not only enhance our understanding of breed-specific genetic architecture but also provide valuable guidance for national conservation programs and selective breeding strategies aimed at maintaining genetic diversity and improving population health in Iran.

In our study, we investigated runs of homozygosity (ROH) across multiple Iranian and international horse breeds using high-density SNP arrays (670 K and MNEc2M). The results revealed clear differences in ROH patterns among breeds, reflecting the combined effects of historical demography, breeding practices, and selection pressures. For example, light horse breeds, such as Arabian and Persian Arabian, showed higher total ROH lengths and FROH values compared to draft breeds (e.g., Belgian) and primitive/native breeds (e.g., Icelandic and some Iranian native breeds), likely due to the relatively small, closed populations and strong directional selection applied in these breeds. Conversely, Iranian native breeds, such as Kurdish and Dareshuri, displayed predominantly short ROHs, indicative of a broader genetic base and limited recent inbreeding.

These observations are consistent with the findings of Szmatoła et al. [45], who assessed ROH patterns across horse breeds representing different utility types. Their study demonstrated that light horse breeds, such as Arabians, generally exhibit higher ROH lengths and FROH values than draft or primitive breeds, reflecting the combined effects of selective breeding and demographic history. Similarly, our data on gene-rich ROH islands highlight regions under potential selection for performance, musculoskeletal, and exercise-related traits, corroborating previous reports on functional associations of ROH with adaptive and breeding traits [44, 45].

Overall, these results provide robust evidence that ROH patterns in horses are shaped not only by population history and demographic events but also by breed-specific selection and management practices. The integration of our findings with previously published data strengthens the interpretation that ROH analyses can serve as a valuable tool for understanding the genetic consequences of breeding strategies and adaptive processes in equine populations.

The consistency across datasets and with previous studies underscores the role of breed history, population structure, and selection pressures in shaping ROH distributions, emphasizing the need to consider these factors when assessing genetic diversity and inbreeding in horse populations. In terms of cumulative ROH length per individual, our results obtained using the 670 K SNP array were comparable to those reported by Chen et al. [26], and Grilz-Seger et al. [44], who analyzed ROH patterns using lower-density SNP arrays specifically the 65 K Illumina array and the Affymetrix Axiom™ Equine genotyping array, respectively across diverse horse breeds. Their study also found variability in total ROH lengths among breeds, consistent with our observations. Furthermore, our findings are partially aligned with those of Druml et al. [49], who utilized a higher-density 700 K Affymetrix array to assess ROH across multiple horse populations. However, despite the similar trends observed, the total ROH lengths per individual identified in our study using the MNEc2M array were generally lower than those reported by Druml et al. [49], and Chen et al. [26], which may be attributed to methodological differences including SNP array platform, marker density, population structure, and the specific parameters used for ROH detection.

Genomic inbreeding coefficients

In this study, we estimated genomic inbreeding coefficients in a diverse panel of horse breeds using both the proportion of the genome in runs of homozygosity (FROH) and the excess homozygosity-based estimator (FHOM). These measures were derived from two SNP genotyping platforms the high-density 670 K SNP array and the ultra-high-density MNEc2M array allowing us to evaluate their relative performance and concordance across different marker resolutions. Across both datasets, our results consistently demonstrated that FROH was a more stable and informative estimator of inbreeding than FHOM, particularly for detecting historical and recent inbreeding patterns. This aligns with previous studies [50, 51] that demonstrated FHOM may underestimate inbreeding in admixed or highly heterozygous populations, compared to FROH. Overall, the FROH approach, particularly when based on longer ROHs, appears to be a more robust estimator of genomic inbreeding, especially in structured or composite horse populations.

In the 670 K SNP dataset, average FROH_all values ranged widely across breeds (e.g., 0.036 in Kurdish to 0.236 in Thoroughbred), with Thoroughbreds showing the highest genomic inbreeding coefficients due to contributions from both short and medium ROH length classes. These findings align with those reported by Chen et al. [26], who used whole-genome sequencing and also found high FROH in well-documented breeds like Thoroughbred due to limited founder populations. Similarly, Asti et al. [16], reported elevated FROH in structured breeds such as Bardigiano and Haflinger (local Italian horse breeds), underscoring the role of selective breeding and closed studbooks in shaping ROH patterns.

The inbreeding trends observed in our study are further supported by findings from Szmatoła et al. [45], who assessed ROH in Polish horse breeds. They reported that light horses, such as Arabian (0.110) and Malopolski (0.089), exhibited the highest FROH values, followed by intermediate values in primitive breeds like Polish Konik (0.088) and Hucul (0.084), and the lowest values in draft horses, including Sokolski (0.063) and Sztumski (0.058). Notably, when FROH was calculated using only ROHs > 4 Mb (more indicative of recent inbreeding), the trend reversed: the highest values were observed in primitive horses (Polish Konik 0.081; Hucul 0.071), with lower values in light (Arabian 0.067; Malopolski 0.051) and draft horses (Sokolski 0.039; Sztumski 0.033). This shift reflects differing demographic histories and selective pressures among utility types, consistent with our observations that longer ROHs can highlight recent inbreeding events particularly in structured or historically isolated populations.

Conversely, FHOM values varied more unpredictably, often producing lower or even negative values in some breeds, such as Dareshuri or Kurdish horses. This inconsistency aligns with previous studies; for example, Dos Santos et al. [27], found weak correlations between FHOM and FROH, especially in admixed populations, and highlighted the limitations of FHOM in capturing historical inbreeding. Moreover, the low correlation between FROH and FHOM, calculated in our study as 0.56 for the 670 K and 0.47 for the MNEc2M SNP arrays, supports this conclusion. In the 670 K SNP dataset, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests revealed significant differences between FROH_all and FHOM for several breeds, particularly Lusitano and Thoroughbred, further emphasizing the divergence in these estimators’ sensitivity and robustness.

The MNEc2M platform yielded lower average FROH values, likely due to its much higher SNP density and different genome coverage, which may affect the detection of ROH boundaries. Nevertheless, the breed-specific trends remained generally consistent. For instance, Thoroughbred horses again showed the highest genomic inbreeding coefficients, particularly with long ROHs (> 16 Mb), reflecting recent inbreeding events, as also observed in Chen et al. [26], and Colpitts et al. [37], where very long ROHs were linked to recent common ancestry. The elevated FROH >16 Mb values in Thoroughbred and Caspian horses in our MNEc2M data provide strong genomic evidence of intense recent inbreeding, aligning with expectations based on known breeding histories.

Overall, our findings reinforce the view that FROH is a more reliable genomic inbreeding estimator than FHOM, especially in structured or partially admixed populations where Hardy-Weinberg assumptions underlying FHOM are violated. FROH’s sensitivity to ROH length classes enables distinction of ancient inbreeding (short ROHs) from recent inbreeding (long ROHs), making it particularly suitable for breeds with incomplete or inaccurate pedigree data, a scenario commonly encountered in indigenous or regionally managed horse populations. Recent studies in horses have further demonstrated the utility of FROH as a robust estimator of genomic inbreeding and its relevance for conservation and breeding decisions. For example, Chen et al. [26] showed that FROH reliably captures differences in genomic homozygosity among 16 horse breeds, reflecting historical population structure and demographic events. Similarly, Santos et al. [52] reported that in Brazilian Quarter Horses, FROH identifies patterns of inbreeding resulting from targeted artificial selection. Asti et al. [16] further highlighted that FROH can distinguish ancient from recent inbreeding across five indigenous horse breeds, emphasizing its value for managing genetic diversity and informing sustainable breeding programs. These observations support the conclusions of Colpitts et al. [37], who emphasized that FROH provides insights into both population history and the genetic consequences of isolation, and recommend its use in conservation and breeding programs. The integration of FROH in genetic monitoring protocols can guide decisions aimed at maintaining genetic diversity, preventing excessive inbreeding, and promoting sustainable breed management.

Genomic distribution of ROH islands

Runs of homozygosity (ROH) islands regions where extended homozygosity is shared across individuals can arise through historical demographic events, inbreeding, and, importantly, selection. In this study, we analyzed ROH islands in horse breeds using both the 670 K and MNEc2M SNP arrays, and found considerable variability in the number, length, and chromosomal distribution of these regions, reflecting breed-specific histories and selection pressures.

Our results demonstrated that ROH islands were not uniformly distributed across the genome. In the 670 K array, chromosomes such as ECA7 (92.23% coverage), ECA11 (75.79%), ECA8 (49.12%) and ECA3 (48.13%) showed extensive ROH accumulation. Similarly, the MNEc2M array identified notable ROH coverage on ECA11 (41.26%), ECA7 (37.74%), ECA21 (26.55%), ECA18 (21.15%), ECA9 (20.31%), ECA3 (20.20%), and ECA8 (20.03%). These patterns highlight regions that may have been recurrent targets of selection or that reflect the influence of demographic processes, such as bottlenecks and founder effects. The differences in coverage between the two arrays also illustrate how marker density influences ROH island detection, with the higher-density MNEc2M array generally yielding lower ROH coverage estimates but still identifying similar hotspot chromosomes.

These findings are consistent with previous reports in breeds such as Thoroughbred and Noriker, where ROH islands have been associated with loci involved in performance, morphology, and pigmentation [26, 44]. For instance, ROH islands overlapping regions on ECA3 and ECA11 have been linked to genes such as MC1R (pigmentation), HOXB, and AURKC (development and fertility). In our dataset, these chromosomes also harbored large ROH islands, suggesting that similar genomic regions may have been under selection across diverse breeds.

Several ROH islands we identified were located in gene-poor regions or regions lacking annotated protein-coding genes. This observation, which aligns with findings in Thoroughbreds [26], may point to selection on regulatory or structural genomic elements, including enhancers, non-coding RNAs, or other features of genome architecture. Alternatively, these homozygous regions could reflect the effects of background selection or low recombination rates leading to reduced genetic diversity.

Potential functional relevance of ROH islands

Although direct phenotypic data were not available in the present study, we introduced the candidate genes identified within ROH islands based on previous reports and genomic databases, highlighting their potential relevance to reproductive, immune, and performance traits. Our findings indicate that ROH islands identified in native and international breeds may play a role in shaping adaptive phenotypes, particularly in traits related to reproduction and performance. Previous studies have demonstrated similar patterns; for example, in Pura Raza Española horses, increased genomic homozygosity was negatively correlated with fertility estimated breeding values, and several ROH islands were found to contain candidate genes associated with oocyte maturation and embryo development [53]. These results highlight that ROH regions are not merely markers of demographic history or breeding practices but may also harbor adaptive variants influencing fitness-related traits such as fertility. Incorporating such evidence strengthens the interpretation that ROH can serve as a powerful genomic tool for understanding the genetic basis of adaptation and for guiding sustainable breeding strategies in horses. However, we emphasize that further studies incorporating detailed phenotypic information and functional validation are required to confirm these associations.

ROH Islands as genomic signatures of selection

ROH islands are often interpreted as genomic footprints of selection when they are frequent and consistent across individuals. In the context of livestock genetics, regions with high ROH island density may contain genes linked to economically important traits and can reflect the cumulative effects of directional selection, inbreeding, and genetic drift. In this dataset, the extreme ROH accumulation on ECAs 7 and 11 suggests a strong selective history, possibly related to performance or reproductive traits. The consistency of ROH islands across multiple individuals and high-coverage chromosomes supports the hypothesis of selective sweeps, where favorable alleles and their surrounding genomic regions rise in frequency due to breeding practices. In contrast, chromosomes with minimal ROH accumulation (e.g., ECA12 and ECA6) may represent genomic regions evolving under neutrality or areas subjected to frequent recombination events that break down long tracts of homozygosity.

Limitations and future directions

Although our study identifies ROH islands and putative regions under selection, we did not have access to phenotypic data to directly validate the functional significance of these regions. Consequently, causality and the adaptive impact of these ROH islands have been inferred based on correlations and functional annotations rather than confirmed through experimental assays or phenotype-genotype associations. Future studies integrating phenotypic measurements, whole-genome sequencing, and functional analyses will be essential to directly assess the biological significance of these homozygous regions and their roles in breed-specific traits, adaptation, and performance.

In addition to the lack of phenotypic validation, another limitation of the present study is the absence of external control populations, such as wild or feral horses, which restricts the ability to fully distinguish breed-specific founder effects from genuine selection signals. Nevertheless, the inclusion of a diverse panel of both native Iranian and well-known international breeds partially compensates for this by allowing relative comparisons across populations with distinct demographic and breeding histories. Similar approaches have been adopted in previous livestock studies that primarily focused on within-breed ROH analysis [39, 40]. Future research incorporating wild or unmanaged horse populations as reference groups would further strengthen the interpretation of selection signatures and improve our understanding of breed-specific demographic histories.

A potential limitation of the present study is the geographic origin of the samples. For each breed, samples were collected from within a single country, and for native Iranian breeds, no significant regional substructure exists, as these breeds are generally restricted to specific localities. This limited geographic representation may affect the generalizability of our findings and the interpretation of runs of homozygosity (ROH) patterns. Nonetheless, the inclusion of multiple native and internationally recognized breeds allows for relative comparisons and meaningful assessment of breed-specific genomic characteristics. Future studies incorporating broader geographic sampling or reference populations, including wild or unmanaged horses, would further enhance the interpretation of selection signatures and population histories.

Conclusion

Our study provides a comprehensive assessment of ROH patterns across Iranian native and internationally recognized horse breeds, revealing substantial variation in the number, length, and chromosomal distribution of ROHs. As expected, Persian Arabian and Thoroughbred horses exhibited higher genomic inbreeding, reflecting intensive selection and closed breeding histories. Novel findings include the identification of large ROH islands in chromosomes such as ECA7 and ECA11 in several Iranian native breeds, likely representing regions under breed-specific selection pressures. These ROH islands encompass candidate genes related to reproduction, pigmentation, and performance traits, highlighting promising targets for future functional and association studies. Comparison of genomic inbreeding estimators confirmed that FROH provides more accurate and informative insights than FHOM, particularly in structured or partially admixed populations with incomplete or unreliable pedigree data. High-density SNP arrays (MNEc2M) improved ROH detection, especially for short and recent tracts, demonstrating the importance of marker density in genomic inbreeding assessment. We acknowledge several limitations. First, the lack of phenotypic data prevented direct validation of the functional significance of ROH islands, and adaptive inferences are based on correlations and functional annotations rather than experimental evidence. Second, the absence of external control populations, such as wild or unmanaged horses, limits the ability to fully distinguish founder effects from genuine selection signals. Third, the geographic coverage of samples was limited, as most breeds were sampled from a single country, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Nonetheless, the inclusion of multiple native and international breeds allowed meaningful relative comparisons across populations with distinct demographic histories and selection pressures. Despite these limitations, our results provide valuable insights into breed-specific genomic architecture and the impact of selection, supporting the integration of FROH and ROH island mapping into conservation and sustainable breeding programs aimed at maintaining genetic diversity and minimizing inbreeding depression. Future research incorporating broader geographic sampling, wild reference populations, detailed phenotypic measurements, and functional validation will be essential to fully elucidate the biological significance of these homozygous regions and their contributions to adaptation, performance, and breed-specific traits.
