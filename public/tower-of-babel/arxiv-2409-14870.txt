Testing Dependency of Weighted Random Graphs
Mor Oren-Loberman Vered Paslev Wasim Huleihel

Abstract
In this paper, we study the task of detecting the edge dependency between two weighted random graphs. We formulate this task as a simple hypothesis testing problem, where under the null hypothesis, the two observed graphs are statistically independent, whereas under the alternative, the edges of one graph are dependent on the edges of a uniformly and randomly vertex-permuted version of the other graph. For general edge-weight distributions, we establish thresholds at which optimal testing becomes information-theoretically possible or impossible, as a function of the total number of nodes in the observed graphs and the generative distributions of the weights. Finally, we identify a statistical-computational gap, and present evidence suggesting that this gap is inherent using the framework of low-degree polynomials.



1
Introduction

Consider the following decision problem. We observe two weighted random graphs that are either generated independently at random or are edge-dependent due to some latent vertex correspondence or permutation. For this basic problem, two natural questions arise: the detection problem, which concerns whether the graphs exhibit dependence, and the recovery problem, which concerns identifying the latent correspondence between vertices. Here, we address the former question, specifically, we aim to understand under what conditions, in terms of the number of vertices and the generative distributions, one can distinguish between the two hypotheses and detect whether these graphs are dependent or not, say, with high probability?

The fundamental question above was first introduced and analyzed in
[
1
]
, where for Gaussian-weighted and dense Erdős-Rényi random graphs on
n
vertices, sharp information-theoretic thresholds were developed, revealing the exact barrier at which the asymptotic optimal detection error probability undergoes a phase transition from zero to one as
n
approaches infinity. For sparse Erdős-Rényi random graphs this threshold was initially determined within a constant factor in the same paper. Later on, this result was refined in
[
2
]
, where a sharp information-theoretic threshold was established for a specific range of edge probabilities. Our work is closely related to these works, and much inspired by
[
1
]
; we study the detection limit under general weight distributions, beyond the Erdős-Rényi and Gaussian Wigner graphs models mentioned above, and thereby aim to answer
[
1
, Sec. 6, Problem 2]
.

In particular, for a given pair of generative weight distributions (under the null and alternative hypotheses) we derive information-theoretic lower and upper bounds on the phase transition at which optimal testing error probability jumps from zero to one, as
n\to\infty
, and show that under mild assumptions on the weight distributions, arising naturally from classical binary hypothesis testing (but include a wide range of distributions), these bounds are sharp. We also consider testing in polynomial time; we propose and analyze a statistically suboptimal but polynomial-time efficient algorithm. In a similar vein to
[
3
]
, we observe a statistical-computational gap in our problem, which we suspect is fundamental. We provide evidence that any polynomial-time algorithm will fail in the region where the information-theoretic exhaustive algorithm is successful and the above polynomial-time algorithm fails.

2
Problem Formulation

Probabilistic model.

We follow the formulation of testing problem proposed in
[
1
]
, but generalize to arbitrary weighted random graphs.
Specifically, let
G=([n],\mathsf{A})
and
G^{\prime}=([n],\mathsf{B})
denote weighted undirected graphs on the vertex set
[n]
, with weighted adjacency matrices
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, respectively. We assume that the edge weights of each graph, namely,
\{\mathsf{A}_{ij}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
and
\{\mathsf{B}_{ij}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
, to be i.i.d. Under the null hypothesis
{\cal H}_{0}
, the matrices
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
are statistically independent, with
\mathsf{A}_{i,j}\sim{\cal P}_{{A}}
and
\mathsf{B}_{i,j}\sim{\cal P}_{{B}}
, for all
i,j\in[n]
, respectively. It is assumed that
{\cal P}_{{A}}={\cal P}_{{B}}
, and we denote the product measure
{\cal Q}_{{A}{B}}={\cal P}_{{A}}\times{\cal P}_{{B}}
. Under the alternative hypothesis
{\cal H}_{1}
, given a latent random permutation
\pi\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}_{n})
, the random variables
\{(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}):1\leq i<j\leq n\}
are i.i.d., and each pair
(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}})\sim{\cal P}_{{A}{B}}
, with the same marginals as
{\cal Q}_{{A}{B}}
, i.e.,
{\cal P}_{{A}}={\cal P}_{{B}}
. Upon observing
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, the objective is to evaluate
{\cal H}_{0}
against
{\cal H}_{1}
.
For clarity and brevity, we will denote univariate random variables by
A
and
B
and adjacency matrices by
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
throughout this paper.
To summarize, the hypothesis testing problem we deal with is,

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle H}_{0}:(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{ij})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}{\cal Q}_{{AB}}

(1)

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle H}_{1}:(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}{\cal P}_{{AB}},\ \ \text{conditional on }\pi\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}_{n}).

Learning problem.

A test function for our problem is a map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\times\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\to\{0,1\}
, designed to determine which hypothesis
{\cal H}_{0}
or
{\cal H}_{1}
occurred. ”We characterize the risk associated with a test
\phi
the total of its Type-I and II error probabilities, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi)\triangleq{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\phi(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})=1]+{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}}[\phi(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})=0],

(2)

where
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
designate the probability distributions under the null and alternative hypotheses, respectively. Then, the optimal risk is,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}^{\star}\triangleq\inf_{\phi:\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\times\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\to\{0,1\}}\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi).

(3)

We remark that
\mathsf{R}
is a function of
n
, however, we omit them from our notation for the benefit of readability. We study the possibility and impossibility of our detection problem in multiple asymptotic regimes. A test

\phi
achieves
strong
detection if
\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi)
approaches
0
as
n
tends to infinity, and
weak
detection if
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi)<1
(i.e., strictly outperforming a random guess). Our main goal is to investigate when strong/weak detection is possible and impossible, as a function of
n
and the distributions
({\cal P},{\cal Q})
.

3
Main Results

In this section, we present our main results on the information-theoretic lower and upper bounds of weak and strong criteria of detection, under the setting presented in Section
2
. Finally, we present our computational lower bounds. All proofs of our main results appear in Section
4
. We begin with our impossibility lower-bounds.

Lower bounds.

Following
[
1
,
32
]
, we introduce a few important notations. For any
x\in{\cal X}
and
y\in{\cal Y}
, we let
{\cal L}(x,y)\triangleq\frac{{\cal P}(x,y)}{{\cal Q}(x,y)}
denote the single-letter likelihood function. It turns our beneficial to think of
{\cal L}
as a kernel, which induces an operator as follows. For any square-integrable function
f
under
{\cal Q}
, i.e.,
\int\int f^{2}(x,y){\cal Q}(\mathrm{d}x){\cal Q}(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
,

\displaystyle({\cal L}f)(x)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim{\cal Q}}\left[{\cal L}(x,Y)f(Y)\right].

(4)

Furthermore, the composition
{\cal L}^{2}={\cal L}\circ{\cal L}
is given by
{\cal L}^{2}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q}[{\cal L}(x,Z){\cal L}(Z,y)]
, and
{\cal L}^{k}
is defined similarly. We assume that
{\cal L}(x,y)={\cal L}(y,x)
, and hence
{\cal L}
is self-adjoint. Furthermore, if we assume that
\int\int{\cal L}^{2}(x,y){\cal Q}(\mathrm{d}x){\cal Q}(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
, then
{\cal L}
is Hilbert-Schmidt. Thus,
{\cal L}
is diagonazable, and we denote its eigenvalues by
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
. Accordingly, the trace of
{\cal L}
is given by
\mathsf{trace}({\cal L})=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim{\cal Q}}[{\cal L}(Y,Y)]=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{i}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the sequence of eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
is decreasing, namely,
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Finally, Lemma
2
in Section
4.1.2
shows that the largest eigenvalue of
{\cal L}
is unity, i.e.,
\lambda_{0}=1
.

The log-likelihood ratio (LLR) is denoted by
\mathscr{L}(x,y)\triangleq\log{\cal L}(x,y)=\log\frac{{\cal P}(x,y)}{{\cal Q}(x,y)}
, for
(x,y)\in{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
. To wit, the LLR is the logarithm of the Radon-Nikodym derivative between
{\cal P}
and
{\cal Q}
. Without loss of essential generality, we assume
{\cal P}
and
{\cal Q}
are mutually absolutely continuous; therefore
\mathscr{L}
is well-defined. We assume that
d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal P}||{\cal Q})
and
d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal Q}||{\cal P})
, which are the expectations of the LLR w.r.t
{\cal P}
and
{\cal Q}
, respectively, are finite. For
\theta\in(-d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal Q}||{\cal P}),d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal P}||{\cal Q}))
, the Chernoff’s exponents
E_{P},E_{{\cal Q}}:\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,\infty)
are,

\displaystyle E_{{\cal Q}}(\theta)\triangleq\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda\theta-\psi_{{\cal Q}}(\lambda),\quad,E_{{\cal P}}(\theta)\triangleq\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda\theta-\psi_{{\cal P}}(\lambda),

(5)

where
\psi_{{\cal Q}}(\lambda)\triangleq\log\mathbb{E}_{{\cal Q}}[\exp(\lambda\mathscr{L})]
and
\psi_{{\cal P}}(\lambda)\triangleq\log\mathbb{E}_{{\cal P}}[\exp(\lambda\mathscr{L})]
. In other words, the Chernoff’s exponents are the Legendre transforms of the log-moment generating functions. The following properties will be useful in our analysis. Note that
\psi_{{\cal P}}(\lambda)=\psi_{{\cal Q}}(\lambda+1)
, and thus
E_{{\cal P}}(\theta)=E_{{\cal Q}}(\theta)-\theta
. Furthermore,
E_{{\cal P}}
and
E_{{\cal Q}}
are convex non-negative functions. Since
\psi^{\prime}_{{\cal Q}}(0)=-d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal Q}||{\cal P})
and
\psi^{\prime}_{{\cal Q}}(1)=d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal P}||{\cal Q})
, we obtain that
E_{{\cal Q}}(-d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal Q}||{\cal P}))=E_{{\cal P}}(d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal P}||{\cal Q}))=0
, which in turn implies that
E_{{\cal Q}}(d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal P}||{\cal Q}))=d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal P}||{\cal Q})
and
E_{{\cal P}}(-d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal Q}||{\cal P}))=d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal Q}||{\cal P})
. Finally, it is well-known that
\lambda\theta-\psi_{{\cal Q}}(\lambda)
is concave, and its derivative at
\lambda=0
is
\theta+d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal Q}||{\cal P})
. Accordingly, the maximizer
\lambda^{\star}
of the concave optimization in the definition of
E_{{\cal Q}}(\theta)
is non-negative if
\theta\geq-d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal Q}||{\cal P})
. The same is true for
E_{{\cal P}}(\theta)
, provided that
\theta\leq d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal P}||{\cal Q})
. We are now in a position to state our main results.

Theorem 1

(Weak detection lower bound)
.

Consider the detection problem in (
1
), and fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Weak detection is statistically impossible, i.e.,
\mathsf{R}_{n}^{\star}=1-o(1)
, if

\displaystyle\chi^{2}\left({\cal P}||{\cal Q}\right)\leq\frac{(2-\epsilon)\log n}{\alpha n},\quad\mathsf{and}

(6a)

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left({\cal P}||{\cal Q}\right)+\delta_{n}\cdot\mathsf{Var}_{{\cal P}}\left(\mathscr{L}\right)\leq\frac{(2-\epsilon)\log n}{n},

(6b)

for any
\omega(1)=\delta_{n}=o(\log n)
, and any constant
\epsilon>0
.

Remark 1
.

It is well-known that
d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left({\cal P}||{\cal Q}\right)\leq\chi^{2}\left({\cal P}||{\cal Q}\right)
, for any pair

(see, e.g.,

cannot hold. Nonetheless, it can be shown that the class of distributions for which there is a constant

such that
\chi^{2}\left({\cal P}||{\cal Q}\right)\leq\mathsf{C}\cdot d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left({\cal P}||{\cal Q}\right)
is non-trivial and interesting. For example, it can be shown that (see, e.g.,
[
34
,
35
]
):

1.

Pairs
({\cal P},{\cal Q})
with sub-Gaussian LLR, i.e., satisfying
\mathscr{L}({A},{B})
, for

is sub-Gaussian.

2.

Pairs
({\cal P},{\cal Q})
with bounded LLR, i.e., satisfying

for

is bounded almost surely.

3.

A wide variety of other pairs
({\cal P},{\cal Q})
from a common exponential family.

Furthermore, for this class of distributions, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}_{{\cal P}}\left(\mathscr{L}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\cal P}\mathscr{L}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\cal P}\left[\frac{({\cal L}-1)^{2}}{{\cal L}}\right]

(7)

\displaystyle=\chi^{2}\left({\cal P}||{\cal Q}\right)\leq\mathsf{C}\cdot d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left({\cal P}||{\cal Q}\right),

(8)

where the second inequality follows from the fact that

, for

. Thus, in this case, the conditions in (

) boils down to

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left({\cal P}||{\cal Q}\right)\leq\frac{(2-\epsilon)\log n}{n},

(9)

by taking

and any sequence

. We let
{\cal D}_{1}
denote pairs

satisfying

, for some
\mathsf{C}>1
.

Example 1

(Gaussian case)
.

In the Gaussian case, we assume that
{\cal Q}_{{A}{B}}={\cal P}_{{A}}\times{\cal P}_{{B}}
, with

and

correspond to the densities of a standard normal random variable, whereas

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{{A}{B}}\equiv\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\

(10)

for some known correlation coefficient

. Thus, because

, and since

, Remark

implies that weak detection is impossible if

Example 2

(Bernoulli case)
.

In the Bernoulli case, we assume that
{\cal Q}_{{A}{B}}={\cal P}_{{A}}\times{\cal P}_{{B}}
, with

, for some

and
\tau\in[0,1]
, and
{\cal P}_{{A}{B}}
denotes the joint distribution of two correlated Bernoulli random variables. Specifically, under

, we have

, and

\displaystyle{B}|{A}

\displaystyle\sim\begin{cases}\mathsf{Bernoulli}(\tau),\ &\mathsf{if}\;{A}=1\\
\mathsf{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{\tau p(1-\tau)}{1-\tau p}\right),\ &\mathsf{if}\;{A}=0.\end{cases}

(11)

Here, Pearson correlation coefficient is given by,

\displaystyle\rho\triangleq\frac{\mathsf{cov}(A,B)}{\sqrt{\mathsf{var}(A)}\sqrt{\mathsf{var}(B)}}=\frac{\tau(1-p)}{1-\tau p}.

If

Upper bounds.

Let us present our detection algorithms and the corresponding upper bounds. Note that under
{\cal H}_{1}
, the latent random permutation
\pi
represents the hidden node correspondence under which
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
are correlated. For this reason, we refer to
{\cal H}_{1}
as the planted model and
{\cal H}_{0}
as the null model. As is well-known the optimal decision rule for (
1
) is the Neyman-Pearson test which correspond to thresholding the likelihood ratio,

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{n}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})\triangleq\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}|\pi)}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}_{n})}\left[\mathsf{L}_{n}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}|\pi)\right].

(13)

As is customary to other related testing problems with a latent combinatorial structure, analyzing the above optimal test is difficult due to the averaging over all
n!
possible permutations. Consider, instead, the generalized likelihood ratio test (GLRT), where the averaging is replaced with a maximum, namely,

\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\mathsf{L}_{n}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}|\pi)=\max_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}|\pi)}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}.

(14)

Next, we derive a closed-form expression for the conditional likelihood
\mathsf{L}_{n}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}|\pi)
. We have

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{n}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}|\pi)=\prod_{(i,j)\in\Lambda}{\cal L}(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}),

(15)

where
\Lambda\triangleq\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq n\}
.
For any
(i,j)\in\binom{[n]}{2}
,
{\cal Q}
denotes the joint density function of
\mathsf{A}_{ij}
and
\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}
under
{\cal H}_{0}
, and

{\cal P}
denotes the joint density function of
\mathsf{A}_{ij}
and
\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}
under
{\cal H}_{1}
given its latent permutation
\pi
. Accordingly, we define the test as,

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathsf{GLRT}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbbm{1}\left\{\max_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{n^{(2)}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\log{\cal L}(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}})\geq\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}\right\},

(16)

for some threshold
\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
n^{(2)}\triangleq\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
. We have the following result.

Theorem 2

(GLRT strong detection)
.

Consider the GLRT in (
16
), and suppose there is a
\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}\in(-d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal Q}||{\cal P}),d_{\mathsf{KL}}({\cal P}||{\cal Q}))
with

\displaystyle E_{{\cal Q}}(\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2\log(n/e)}{n-1}+\frac{2(1+\log n)}{n(n-1)}+\omega(n^{-2}),

(17a)

\displaystyle E_{{\cal P}}(\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}})

\displaystyle=\omega(n^{-2}).

(17b)

Then,
\mathsf{R}(\phi_{\mathsf{GLRT}})\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
.

Remark 2
.

The bounds in (
17
) can be simplified in many ways. For example, take
\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}=0
, and then,

\displaystyle E_{{\cal Q}}(0)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}-\psi_{{\cal Q}}(\lambda)=\mathsf{C}({\cal P}||{\cal Q})

(18)

\displaystyle\geq-\log\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{H}^{2}({\cal P}||{\cal Q})\right],

The GLRT involves a combinatorial optimization that is intractable. As so, it is of practical interest to devise and analyze efficient algorithms. Consider the following simple test. Define
\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})\triangleq\frac{\mathsf{cov}_{{\cal P}}({A},{B})}{\mathsf{Var}_{{\cal Q}}({A})}
as the Pearson correlation coefficient between
{A}
and
{B}
. For

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathsf{comp}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})\triangleq\mathbbm{1}\left\{\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\Lambda}(\mathsf{A}_{ij}-\mathsf{B}_{ij})\right|\leq\theta\right\},

(23)

if
\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})\in(0,1]

Theorem 3

(Comparison test weak detection)
.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\cal Q}}|{A}-{B}|^{3}}{\mathsf{Var}^{3/2}_{{\cal Q}}({A})},\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\cal P}}|{A}-{B}|^{3}}{\mathsf{Var}^{3/2}_{{\cal Q}}({A})(1-|\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})|)^{3/2}}=o(n),

(24)

The performance guarantee in Theorem

Computational lower bound.

The premise of the LDP framework is that all polynomial-time algorithms for solving detection problems are captured by polynomials of low-degree. By now, there is growing strong evidence in support of this conjecture. The ideas below were developed in a sequence of works in the sum-of-squares optimization literature
[

37

38
,
39

With regard to the problem of detecting graph correlations, recently in
[
25

[
25

that in the dense regime, if
\rho^{2}=o(1)
, then polynomial tests with degree at most
O(\rho^{-1})
fail for detection. Let us describe the basics of the LDP framework. We follow the notations and definition in
[

\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
induces an inner product of measurable functions

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{0}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[f(\mathsf{G})g(\mathsf{G})]

be the Hilbert space of functions

The idea in the LDP framework is to find the low-degree polynomial that best distinguishes

to

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{0}}

\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
from
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}

4.1.3

We start by describing the general program of conditional second moment method. Note that sometimes certain rare events under

{\cal E}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)\in{\cal E}\right\}}}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}({\cal E})}

(50)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)\in{\cal E}\right\}}.

(51)

Then, the likelihood ratio between the conditioned planted model
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
and the null model
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}

\displaystyle={\frac{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}}

(52)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}|\pi)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)\in{\cal E}\right\}}}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{Unif}(\pi)

(53)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}|\pi)}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)\in{\cal E}\right\}}\right].

(54)

Then, by the same reasoning that led to (
34
), the conditional second moment is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}\right)^{2}\right]=(1+o(1))\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi\perp\!\!\!\perp\pi^{\prime}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\prod_{O\in{\cal O}}\mathsf{X}_{O}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)\in{\cal E}\right\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi^{\prime})\in{\cal E}\right\}}\right]\right].

(55)

Compared to the unconditional second moment, the extra indicators will be useful for ruling out those rare events causing the second moment to blow up. By the data processing inequality of total variation, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}),\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}))

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi),\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi))

(56)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left((\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B},\pi)\not\in{\cal E}\right)=o(1).

(57)

Thus, we arrive at the following conditions for non-detection:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}\right)^{2}\right]=O(1)\quad\longrightarrow\quad d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}),\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}))\leq 1-\Omega(1),

(58)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})}\right)^{2}\right]=1+o(1)\quad\longrightarrow\quad d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}),\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}))\leq o(1).

(59)

Thus, one of our main goals is to choose
{\cal E}
wisely.

Let
{\cal F}
denotes the set of fixed points of
\sigma=\pi^{-1}\circ\pi^{\prime}
, so that
|{\cal F}|=n_{1}
. Let

\displaystyle{\cal O}_{1}\triangleq\binom{{\cal F}}{2},

(60)

\displaystyle\prod_{O\in{\cal O}_{1}}\mathsf{X}_{O}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i<j\in{\cal F}}\mathsf{X}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i<j\in{\cal F}}{\cal L}(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}){\cal L}(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{\pi^{\prime}_{i}\pi^{\prime}_{j}})

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left(\sum_{i<j\in{\cal S}}\log{\cal L}(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}})\geq m\tau\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left(-mE_{{\cal P}}(\tau)\right)

(70)

\displaystyle=n!\cdot e^{-n^{(2)}\cdot\lambda\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\cal Q}}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\log\frac{{\cal P}({A},{B})}{{\cal Q}({A},{B})}\right)\right]\right]^{n^{(2)}}

, and the second inequality is by Chernoff’s bound. For

Next, we bound the Type-II error probability as follows. Under
{\cal H}_{1}

\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, we may assume without loss of generality that the latent permutation is the identity one, i.e.,

. Then, for

,

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left[\phi_{\mathsf{GLRT}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})=0\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left[\max_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\log{\cal L}(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}})<n^{(2)}\cdot\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}\right]

(125)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left[\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\log{\cal L}(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{ij})<n^{(2)}\cdot\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}\right]

(126)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-n^{(2)}\cdot\lambda\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\log{\cal L}(\mathsf{A}_{ij},\mathsf{B}_{ij})\right)\right]

(127)

\displaystyle=e^{-n^{(2)}\cdot\lambda\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\cal P}}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\log\frac{{\cal P}({A},{B})}{{\cal Q}({A},{B})}\right)\right]\right]^{n^{(2)}}

(128)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[n^{(2)}\cdot\psi_{{\cal P}}(\lambda)-n^{(2)}\cdot\lambda\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}\right].

(129)

Again, because

(130)

and thus if,

\displaystyle E_{{\cal P}}(\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}})=\omega(n^{-2}),

(131)

then the Type-II error probability goes to zero.

5
Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we analyzed the problem of detecting the dependency weighted random graphs. To that end, we derived both lower and upper bounds on the phase transition boundaries at which detection is possible or impossible. We show that these bounds are tight in several special cases considered in the literature, and in other distributions as well. We observed a gap between the statistical limits we derive and the performance of the efficient algorithms we construct.
We conjecture that this gap is in fact inherent, and to provide an evidence for this conjecture we
follow show that the class of low-degree polynomials
fail the conjectureally hard regime. We hope our work has opened more doors than it closes. Let us mention briefly a few interesting questions for future research:

1.

It would be interesting to devise universal algorithms which are independent of the underlying generative distributions, which assumed known in our paper.

2.

In this paper, we investigated the average-case/noisy version of the famous worst-case graph isomorphism problem; accordingly it would be interesting and important to analyze the related subgraph isomorphism problem, where only a subgraph (say, chosen uniformly at random) in the original graph is correlated with a node-permuted version in the other graph.

3.

Similarly to
[
20
]
, it is quite important to devise efficient algorithms that achieve strong detection (rather than weak detection as we proposed in our paper).

A.1
Stable distributions

The set of stable distributions is broad, encompassing many well-known distributions such as the Normal, Cauchy, Lévy, among others. We recall the definition of a stable distribution.

Definition 1

(Stable distribution)
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent copies of a random variable
X
. Then,
X
is said to be stable if for any constants
\alpha,\beta>0
the combination
\alpha X_{1}+\beta X_{2}
has the same distribution as
\gamma X+\nu
, for some constants
\gamma>0
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
{A}
and
{W}
be two independent stable random variables with identical parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\mu
, and
\gamma
, where
\alpha
is the stability parameter,
\beta
is the skewness parameter,
\mu
is the location parameter, and
\gamma
is the scale parameter. For simplicity, we assume
\mu=0
, though the results can be easily extended to the general case where
\mu
is nonzero.
Define
{B}\triangleq c_{1}{A}+c_{2}{W}
. By the properties of stable distributions,
{B}
is also stable with the same stability and skewness parameters,
\alpha
and
\beta
. The scale parameter becomes
(\left|c_{1}\right|^{\alpha}+\left|c_{2}\right|^{\alpha})^{1/\alpha}\gamma
, whereas the location parameter remains
(c_{1}+c_{2})\mu=0
. To ensure that
{A}
and
{B}
have identical marginal distributions, we impose the condition
\left|c_{1}\right|^{\alpha}+\left|c_{2}\right|^{\alpha}=1
.
Next, we show that the correlation coefficient between
{A}
and
{B}
is
c_{1}
. Indeed, due to the independence of
{A}
and
{W}
, we have

\displaystyle\rho\triangleq\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{cov}({A},{B})}{\text{Var}({A})}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{A}{B}\right]}{\text{Var}({A})}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{A}(c_{1}{A}+c_{2}{W})\right]}{\text{Var}\left({A}\right)}=\frac{c_{1}A^{2}}{\text{Var}({A})}=c_{1}.

(173)

Thus, we set
c_{1}=\rho
, and
c_{2}=\pm(1-\left|\rho\right|^{\alpha})^{1/\alpha}
. We now compute the conditional expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\rho{A}+(1-\rho){W})^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]

(174)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}(\rho{A})^{\ell-k}\left((1-\rho){W}\right)^{k}|{A}=a\right]

(175)

\displaystyle=\rho^{\ell}a^{\ell}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}\rho^{\ell-k}(1-\rho)^{k}a^{\ell-k}\mathbb{E}\left[{W}^{k}\right].

(176)

Since
W
is independent of
A
and has parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\mu
and
\gamma
that are constant w.r.t.
a
, its moments
\mathbb{E}\left[W^{k}\right]
are also constant w.r.t
a
for all
k
. As a result,
\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]
is a polynomial of degree
\ell
in
a
with a leading coefficient of
\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})^{\ell}
. Therefore, the class
{\cal D}_{3}
properties are satisfied for the stable family of distributions considered.

A.2
Bernoulli distribution

Recall the definition of the Bernoulli case in Example
2
; in this case, we have
{A}\sim\mathsf{Bern}(ps)
, and,

\displaystyle{B}|A\triangleq\begin{cases}\mathsf{Bern}(s),\quad&{A}=1\\
\mathsf{Bern}\left(\frac{ps(1-s)}{1-ps}\right),\quad&{A}=0.\end{cases}

(177)

It is not difficult to check that the marginal distribution of
{B}
is identical to
{A}
.
Furthermore, a straightforward calculation reveals that,

\displaystyle\rho\triangleq\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{s(1-p)}{1-ps}.

(178)

Now, for
\ell=0
, we trivially have that
\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{0}|{A}\right]=1\cdot a^{0}
, and the leading coefficient is
1=\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})^{0}
. For
\ell\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Bern}(s)\right]^{\ell},&a=1\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Bern}\left(\frac{ps(1-s)}{1-ps}\right)\right]^{\ell},&a=0\end{cases}

(179)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}s,&a=1\\
\frac{ps(1-s)}{1-ps},&a=0.\end{cases}

(180)

Since
a
is either 0 or 1, we have that
a^{\ell}
is also zero or one, respectively. Therefore, the projection
\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]
is a polynomial of degree
\ell
in
a
for both cases
a=0
and
a=1
,
with leading coefficients,
\kappa_{\ell}=0
for all
\ell\geq 1
and
\kappa_{0}=1
. Therefore, the Bernoulli distribution above indeed falls inside
{\cal D}_{3}
, since for
\ell=0
we have
\kappa_{0}=1=\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})^{0}
, and for all
\ell\geq 1
we have
0\leq\left|\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})\right|^{\ell}
.

A.3
Poisson distribution

Let
{A}
be a Poisson random variable with parameter
\lambda
. We define,

\displaystyle{B}\triangleq{N}+{M},

(181)

where
{N}|A\sim\mathsf{Binomial}({A},\rho)
and
{M}\sim\mathsf{Poisson}((1-\rho)\lambda)
, with
{M}
independent of
(A,N)
.
To show that
B
follows a Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda
, we compute its moment generating function (MGF). For
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[s^{{B}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[s^{{N}+{M}}|{A}\right]\right]

(182)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[s^{{N}}|{A}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[s^{{M}}\right]\right]

(183)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\rho s+1-\rho)^{{A}}\right]e^{(1-\rho)\lambda(s-1)}

(184)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda(\rho s+1-\rho-1)}e^{(1-\rho)\lambda(s-1)}

(185)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda(s-1)},

(186)

where the second equality follows from the independence of
{M}
in
(A,N)
, and the third and fourth equalities follow by using the MGFs of the binomial and Poisson random variables. Therefore, (
186
)
matches the MGF of a Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda
, confirming that
{B}\sim\mathsf{Poisson}(\lambda)
.
Next, we show that
\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]
is a polynomial of degree
\ell
in
a
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[({N}+{M})^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]

(187)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{l}{k}\mathbb{E}\left[{N}^{k}|{A}=a\right]\mathbb{E}\left[{M}^{\ell-k}\right].

(188)

Since
M\sim\mathsf{Poisson}((1-\rho)\lambda)
, the term
\mathbb{E}\left[{M}^{\ell-k}\right]
is a constant w.r.t.
a
. Furthermore, the term
\mathbb{E}\left[{N}^{k}|{A}=a\right]
is the
k
th moment of the Binomial random variable
({N}|{A}=a)
, and can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{N}^{k}|{A}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}S(k,i)\rho^{i}a^{i},

(189)

where
\{S(k,i)\}
are the Stirling numbers of the second kind. Putting together, we conclude that
\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]
is a polynomial of degree
\ell
in
a
with a leading coefficient

\displaystyle\kappa_{\ell}=\binom{\ell}{\ell}S(\ell,\ell)\rho^{\ell}=\rho^{\ell}.

(190)

Finally, the correlation coefficient between
A
and
B
is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{cov}\left({A},{B}\right)}{\text{Var}(\mathsf{A})}

(191)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{cov}\left({A},{N}\right)}{\text{Var}({A})}

(192)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{A}\mathbb{E}\left[{N}|{A}\right]\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{A}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[{N}|{A}\right]\right]}{\text{Var}({A})}

(193)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho\mathbb{E}\left[{A}^{2}\right]-\rho\mathbb{E}^{2}\left[{A}\right]}{\text{Var}({A})}

(194)

\displaystyle=\rho,

(195)

where the second equality follows the independence of
A
and
M
.
Consequently,
\kappa_{\ell}=\rho^{\ell}=\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})^{\ell}
, confirming that the Poisson distribution described is indeed in the class
{\cal D}_{3}
.

A.4
Chi-square distribution

Let
A\sim\chi^{2}_{k}
be a Chi-square random variable with parameter
k
. Define,

\displaystyle{B}\triangleq{W}+{Z},

(196)

where
{Z}\sim\chi^{2}_{(1-\rho)k}
independent of
{A}
, and
{W}=\rho{A}
. It is well-known that the sum of two independent Chi-square distributed random variables
\chi^{2}_{k_{1}}
and
\chi^{2}_{k_{2}}
is distributed as
\chi^{2}_{k_{1}+k_{2}}
. Thus,
{B}={W}+{Z}\sim\chi^{2}_{k}
. Next, we show that the projection
\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]
is a polynomial of degree
\ell
in
a
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[({W}+{Z})^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]

(197)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{l}{k}\mathbb{E}\left[{W}^{k}|{A}=a\right]\mathbb{E}\left[{Z}^{\ell-k}\right]

(198)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{l}{k}\rho^{k}a^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[{Z}^{\ell-k}\right].

(199)

Note that since
{Z}\sim\chi^{2}_{(1-\rho)k}
, the term
\mathbb{E}\left[{Z}^{\ell-k}\right]
is a constant w.r.t.
a
. Hence,
\mathbb{E}\left[{B}^{\ell}|{A}=a\right]
is a polynomial of degree
\ell
in
a
, with a leading coefficient,

\displaystyle\kappa_{\ell}=\binom{\ell}{\ell}\rho^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[{Z}^{0}\right]=\rho^{\ell},

(200)

It can be verified that the correlation coefficient
\mathsf{corr}(A,B)=\rho
in a manner analogous to the calculations in (
191
)-(
195
).
Thus, we have
\kappa_{\ell}=\rho^{\ell}=\mathsf{corr}({\cal P},{\cal Q})^{\ell}
, which demonstrates that the
\chi^{2}
distribution described belongs to the class
{\cal D}_{3}
.
