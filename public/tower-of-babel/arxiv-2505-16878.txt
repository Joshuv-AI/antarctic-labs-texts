An MM-type algorithm for estimation of semiparametric finite mixture models with a copula-based dependence structure
Michael Levine

Abstract
In this manuscript, we consider a finite nonparametric mixture model with non-independent marginal density functions. Dependence between the marginal densities is modeled using a copula device. Until recently, no deterministic algorithms capable of estimating components of such a model have been available. A deterministic algorithm that is capable of this has been proposed by this author earlier. That algorithm seeks to maximize a smoothed nonparametric penalized log-likelihood; it seems to perform well in practice but does not possess the monotonicity property. In this manuscript, we introduce a deterministic MM (Minorization-Maximization) algorithm for estimation of components of this model that is also maximizing a smoothed penalized nonparametric log-likelihood but that is monotonic with respect to this objective functional. Besides the convergence of the objective functional, results on the convergence of sequences of density functions generated by this algorithm are also established. The behavior of this algorithm is illustrated using both simulated datasets and a real dataset. The results illustrate performance that is at least comparable to the earlier algorithm mentioned above. A discussion of the results and possible future research directions make up the last part of the manuscript.



Keywords—
Finite density mixture, MM algorithm, Nonparametric smoothed likelihood

Introduction

Consider a general finite density mixture model

g(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})

(1)

where the number of components
m
is known beforehand. We assume that
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})^{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As usual, it is assumed that the weights are positive and that they add up to
1
:
\lambda_{j}>0
,
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}=1
. We view this model as a nonparametric mixture model where individual components
f_{j}
do not belong to any parametric family. The number of components is fixed since model selection procedures for nonparametric finite density mixtures are not well developed yet; some of the (very limited) relevant research can be found in
[
16
]
and
[
19
]
.

The model (
1
) is known to be identifiable under certain conditions if each of the densities
f_{j}
is equal to the product of the marginal densities:
f_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{k=1}^{d}f_{jk}(x_{k})
for every
j=1,\ldots,m
. That latter assumption is known as the conditional independence assumption. The nonparametric finite mixture model with conditionally independent marginals has been originally introduced in
[
11
]
and its identifiability conditions worked out in
[
2
]
. In particular, identifiability conditions obtained in
[
2
]
require that the number of dimensions
d\geq 3
. This model has been studied in detail in e.g.
[
4
,
20
,
32
,
5
]
. Note that independence assumption is not always a realistic one, however: it is almost always violated in, e.g. RNA-seq data
[
27
]
. Thus, estimation of finite nonparametric mixtures with non-independent marginals seems desirable from the practical viewpoint.

In order to accommodate this lack of independence, we will use a copula device to model the dependence structure between marginals. As in
[
21
]
, we assume that every
d
-dimensional multivariate cumulative distribution function corresponding to a component of the mixture model (
1
), is represented as a copula of the corresponding marginal cumulative distribution functions. In other words, let
F_{j1}(x_{1}),\ldots,F_{jd}(x_{d})
be the marginal cumulative distribution functions of the cumulative distribution function
F_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
corresponding to the density
f_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
. According to the Sklar’s theorem
[
26
]
p
18
, we have
F_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=C_{j}(F_{j1}(x_{1}),\ldots,F_{jd}(x_{d}))
for a function
C_{j}:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow[0,1]
which is called a copula. The copula
C_{j}
is, effectively, a
d
-dimensional cumulative distribution function with uniform marginal distributions. Differentiating this expression
d
times, one obtains the representation of the
j
th multivariate density function that we need:

f_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=c_{j}(F_{j1}(x_{1}),\ldots,F_{jd}(x_{d}))\prod_{k=1}^{d}f_{jk}(x_{k})

(2)

where
c_{j}
is the density of the copula
C_{j}
. In this manuscript, we work under the parametric assumption concerning copulas
c_{j}
: each one of them is viewed as belonging to a
known
parametric family indexed by a parameter
\rho_{j}
. This assumption makes the use of the index
j
unnecessary for copula densities. The index will only be retained for the copula parameter.

Let
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})^{{}^{\prime}}
and
\bm{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m})^{{}^{\prime}}
. Then, we can define
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}},\bm{\rho}^{{}^{\prime}})^{{}^{\prime}}
to be the vector of finite-dimensional parameters to be estimated. We also denote
\mathbf{f}
the vector of multivariate densities
f_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
.
(
1
)-(
2
) together define a class of mixture models that we investigate. In general, this model is not identifiable.

Proving identifiability of non- and semiparametric finite density mixtures in general is known to be a difficult problem. To the best of our knowledge, no results at all exist for nonparametric finite mixtures of multivariate components where the dependence is modeled using copulas. Moreover, there are no known results even for purely parametric copula-based mixture models, such as those studied in
[
3
,
17
]
. If the main task is a parameter estimation, identifiability is an important problem. In this respect, the simulation section of this manuscript is reassuring in that the true parameters with which the data were simulated could be recovered. Thus, our proposed algorithm seems to belong to a group of seemingly well-behaved algorithms proposed for models where identifiability is not guaranteed see e.g.
[
21
,
33
,
24
]
. Note also that, from a clustering viewpoint, the identifiability problem is less important. In particular, a number of neural network models that are known to be unidentifiable have been used successfully for clustering purposes.

Note that, in general, estimation of nonparametric mixture models is a fairly new topic of research. Some of the modern overviews of the subject can be found in e.g.
[
31
]
and
[
14
]
. In particular, there has been very limited research on estimation of nonparametric mixture models with conditionally non-independent components which is exactly the topic considered in this manuscript. Some of the relevant publications are
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
, and also
[
21
]
. Also, some manuscripts proposing methods for models with conditionally independent marginals propose ideas that allow extension of relevant algorithms to the case of blocks of dependent marginals making up the entire set of available marginal distributions. The identifiability issue of one such model was considered already in
[
2
]
. Algorithms that consider these kinds of models have also been considered in e.g.
[
13
]
and
[
6
]
.
[
23
]
considers a special case of the general nonparametric mixture model that allows for a rather general dependence between marginals while assuming that the marginals themselves belong to a location family;
[
24
]
extends the previous setting to the case where marginals belong to a location-scale family. The algorithms proposed in both of those manuscripts are stochastic and do not optimize any particular objective function.
[
21
]
suggests an algorithm capable of estimating all of the elements of a nonparametric model, including copula parameters describing the dependence between marginal distribution, without the location-scale assumption on the marginal distributions. The proposed algorithm is also deterministic, unlike the algorithms of
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
. However, it is not monotonic with respect to any objective functional. Our main contribution in this manuscript is that we propose a deterministic algorithm that can estimate components of a general nonparametric mixture model with conditionally non-independent marginals, like the one considered in
[
21
]
, but that is also monotonic with respect to the appropriate nonparametric smoothed penalized likelihood. The proposed algorithm also preserves the known copula structure.

The rest of the manuscript is structured as follows. Section
2
introduces the algorithm we propose that can estimate a general multivariate nonparametric finite mixture model with the dependence between marginals modeled using copulas. We show that the proposed algorithm is monotonic with respect to a certain nonparametric smoothed likelihood. Section
3

2
Algorithm

2.1
General algorithm

In this subsection, we only consider the general model (
1
) without explicit specification of the marginal dependence structure. We begin with introducing the objective functional of our algorithm. First, we define a subset of a linear vector function space

{\cal F}=\{\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})^{{}^{\prime}}:0<f_{j}\in L_{1}(\Omega),\log f_{j}\in L_{1}(\Omega),j=1,\ldots,m\}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a subset of the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To proceed, we take
K(\mathbf{u})
to denote some kernel density function in the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is we assume that
K(\mathbf{u})\geq 0
and
\int K(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}=1
. Let
H
be a positive definite symmetric
d\times d
bandwidth matrix. In this manuscript, we use
|H|
to denote the determinant of a generic matrix
H
. Then, a rescaled version of the kernel density function is
K_{H}(\mathbf{u}):=|H|^{-1/2}K(H^{-1/2}\mathbf{u})
. Also, we define a linear smoothing operator
{\cal S}
for any function
f
as

{\cal S}f(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Omega}K_{H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})f(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}.

For our purposes, we consider a situation where a smoothing kernel used for smoothing
f_{i}
in the definition of the operator
{\cal S}
may be different from the one used to smooth
f_{j}
when
i\neq j
. In such a case, the kernel will acquire an additional subscript
j
as in
K_{j,H}
. To reflect this situation, we also define an extended linear operator
{\cal S}\mathbf{f}=({\cal S}_{1}f_{1},\ldots,{\cal S}_{m}f_{m})^{{}^{\prime}}
. We also define a nonlinear smoothing vector-valued operator
{\cal N}
as

{\cal N}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\left({\cal N}_{1}f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,{\cal N}_{m}f_{m}(\mathbf{x})\right)

where

{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\exp\{({\cal}S_{j}\log f_{j})(\mathbf{x})\}=\exp\left\{\int_{\Omega}K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\log f_{j}(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}\right\}

(3)

for
j=1,\ldots,m
and kernel functions potentially depending on
j
. We may note here that the idea of smoothing the logarithm of the density function goes back to
[
29
]
, where a penalty based on the second derivative of the log-density is discussed.
To simplify and shorten the notation, we also introduce the finite mixture operators

{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})

and

{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x}).

Note that, according to the above notation,
{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})
. Now, we are ready to introduce the appropriate objective functional

l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})=\int_{\Omega}g(\mathbf{x})\log\frac{g(\mathbf{x})}{({\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f})(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}.

(4)

In what follows, we would like to clarify why the functional (
4
) makes a sensible choice. We begin with some new notation. Let us use the notation

D(a|b)=\int\left\{a(\mathbf{x})\log\frac{a(\mathbf{x})}{b(\mathbf{x})}+b(\mathbf{x})-a(\mathbf{x})\right\}\,d\mathbf{x}

for the generalized Kullback-Leibler divergence between arbitrary non-negative functions
a(\mathbf{x})
and
b(\mathbf{x})
. As was shown earlier in
[
20
]
, this functional can be represented as

\mathnormal{l}(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})=D(g|{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f})+1-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\int{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}

(5)

To make the notation more concise, we will omit the subscript
\Omega
under the integral sign from now on.

By Jensen’s inequality, the smoothed “subdensity”
{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
has an integral that is less than or equal to
1
, although it is still non-negative.
Thus, the first additive term in (
5
) is analogous to a regular likelihood function for the “model” defined by the subdensity
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(x_{j})
. We place the word “model” in quotation marks since it does not define the true probabilistic model of the data generating process. The second term can be viewed as a “penalty” functional that is always non-negative, is equal to zero if no smoothing occurs and is positive for any non-zero bandwidth, again due to Jensen’s inequality. In other words, the second term becomes larger when the integral of the “non-smooth part” of the density function
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}f_{j}(x_{j})
becomes smaller. Thus, the functional
l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
can be viewed as a type of
smoothed nonparametric penalized log-likelihood
. We would like to note here that terms “maximum penalized likelihood estimation” and “maximum smoothed likelihood estimation” in the nonparametric contexts have been used before e.g.
[
10
]
pp. 7-8.

Our goal is to find a minimizer of
l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
subject to the constraint that
\lambda_{j}>0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}=1
. We achieve this goal by deriving an iterative algorithm that possesses a descent property with respect to the functional
l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
. In other words, we want to ensure that the value of the functional does not decrease from one iteration to the next.

Let us define an iteration operator
G_{\bm{\lambda}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\left\{G_{\bm{\lambda},1}f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,G_{\bm{\lambda},m}f_{m}(\mathbf{x})\right\}
where

G_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\alpha_{j}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\frac{g(\mathbf{u}){\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{u})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})}\,d\mathbf{u}

(6)

where
\alpha_{j}
is a proportionality constant that makes
G_{\bm{\lambda},j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})
integrate to
1
.

Let the starting point be
(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})
. When using this notation, the individual initial weights are denoted
\{\lambda_{j}^{0}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and the individual density functions are
\{f_{j}^{0}\}_{j=1}^{m}
. Our goal is to find
(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
such that the difference
l(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})-l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})\geq 0
; if this inequality is true, it would imply the negative smoothed nonparametric likelihood
l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
decreases after this iteration. In the same way as for
(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})
earlier, we introduce
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and
\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
.

To proceed, let us first denote
w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\lambda_{j}^{0}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{0}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}
. Note that
\{w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\}
is a sequence of positive weights that adds up to
1
, so
\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})=1
. We now prove the lemma establishing monotonicity of the proposed general algorithm. For simplicity, we establish it in the case where
\Omega
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{f}=\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}\in{\cal F}
where
f_{j}=G_{j}f_{j}^{0}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
. Let also
\bm{\lambda}=\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
where
\lambda_{j}=\int g(\mathbf{x})w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}
. Finally, we assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact. Then,

l(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})-l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})\geq 0.

Proof.

By definition,

\displaystyle l(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})-l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})=\int g(\mathbf{x})\log\frac{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{0}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int g(\mathbf{x})\log\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\lambda_{j}^{0}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{0}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\lambda_{j}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})}{\lambda_{j}^{0}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}.

Thus, using concavity of the logarithm function in conjunction with Jensen’s inequality, one finds out that

l(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})-l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})\geq\int g(\mathbf{x})\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\log\frac{\lambda_{j}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})}{\lambda_{j}^{0}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}.

By definition of the nonlinear smoothing operator
{\cal N}
, we have the ratio

\frac{{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{{\cal N}_{j}G_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}=\exp\left\{\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\log\frac{G_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})}{f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})}\,d\mathbf{u}\right\}.

Splitting the logarithmic function of the product in the above, and using the full expression for weights
w_{j}^{0}
we find that

\displaystyle l(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})-l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j}^{0}}\int g(\mathbf{x})\frac{\lambda_{j}^{0}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{0}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}

(7)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{0}\int g(\mathbf{x})\frac{{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{0}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\log\frac{f_{j}(\mathbf{u})}{f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})}\,d\mathbf{u}\right]\,d\mathbf{x}

The second term in the above can be rewritten, using Fubini theorem, as

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{0}\int\log\frac{f_{j}(\mathbf{u})}{f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})}\left[\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})g(\mathbf{x})\frac{{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{0}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}\right]\,d\mathbf{u}.

The quantity inside the square brackets is equal to, by (
6
),
\frac{f_{j}(\mathbf{u})}{\alpha_{j}}
. Therefore, the second term becomes

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\lambda_{j}^{0}}{\alpha_{j}}\int f_{j}(\mathbf{u})\log\frac{f_{j}(\mathbf{u})}{f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})}\,d\mathbf{u}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\alpha_{j}}D(f_{j}|f_{j}^{0})\geq 0.

Note that the first term on the right-hand side of (
7
) can be written as
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j}^{0}}\int g(\mathbf{x})w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}
. It can be maximized with respect to
\lambda_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
under the constraint
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}=1
using the standard Lagrange multipliers method. The resulting maximizer is
\lambda_{j}=\int g(\mathbf{x})w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}
. Since the choice of
\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{0}
makes this term equal to zero, it is clear that the value of this term when
\lambda_{j}=\int g(\mathbf{x})w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
is greater then or equal to zero. Therefore, indeed,
l(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})-l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})\geq 0
.
∎

Remark 1
.

Note also that, even if the weights
\lambda_{j}
are left unchanged between iterations, the difference between the values of smoothed regularized nonparametric likelihood
l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
remains non-negative. Indeed, assuming that
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}=\bm{\lambda}
identically, one will be left with only the second term on the right hand side of (
7
). Maximizing this term with respect to
\mathbf{f}
, one obtains the same result as above.

Remark 2
.

The algorithm proposed is somewhat similar to that proposed in
[
20
]
except that it does not operate at the level of marginal densities but rather at the level of multivariate density component functions. In
[
20
]
, we had to design the algorithm in such a way that the conditional independence of marginal densities is preserved from one iteration to the other since it was an outstanding feature of the model we investigated. No such concern is present when dealing with the model (
1
) and so we operate at the level of multivariate density components themselves.

Remark 3
.

The assumption of compactness of
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
has been made for convenience of illustration. If one wants to remove it, some additional assumptions have to be made to guarantee that the integral
\int g(\mathbf{x})\frac{{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{0}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{0}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\left|\log\frac{f_{j}(\mathbf{u})}{f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})}\right|\,d\mathbf{u}\right]\,d\mathbf{x}
is finite which, in turn, implies that the Fubini theorem can be used. Indeed, let us additionally assume that all kernels
K_{j}(\cdot)
are bounded from above uniformly by some positive constant
D
and that all of
\lambda_{j}^{0}>0
. Then, using the definition
f_{j}=G_{j}f_{j}^{0}
, the expression

\displaystyle\log f_{j}(\mathbf{u})\leq\log\left\{\alpha_{j}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{z})\frac{g(\mathbf{z}){\cal N}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{z})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}}{\cal N}{\mathbf{f}}^{0}(\mathbf{z})}\,dz\right\}\leq\log\left(\frac{D\alpha_{j}}{\min_{1\leq j\leq m}\lambda_{j}^{0}}\right)

and, therefore, we can guarantee that the integral
\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\log\frac{f_{j}(\mathbf{u})}{f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})}\,d\mathbf{u}
is finite as long as

\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq M}\int|\log f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})|\,du<\infty.

Later, we make these same assumptions on
\lambda_{j}
and on the maximum integral
\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq M}\int|\log f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})|\,du
to prove Theorem
1
.

Remark 4
.

It is also true that any algorithm based on the updating mechanism proposed in Lemma
2.1
is going to be an MM algorithm. Indeed, for this to be true, there must exist a majorizing functional
b^{0}(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
such that, when shifted by a constant, it majorizes the “original” functional
l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
:

b^{0}(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})+C^{0}\geq l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})

with equality when
(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})=(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})

[
15
,
30
]
. The exact form of the majorizing functional depends on the current parameter values and so does the constant
C^{0}
; this is why we are using the superscript for both
b^{0}(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
and
C^{0}
. The difference

\displaystyle l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})-l(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})\leq-\int g(\mathbf{x})\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\log\left(\lambda_{j}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})\right)\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle-\left(-\int g(\mathbf{x})\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\log\left(\lambda_{j}^{0}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\right)\,d\mathbf{x}\right)=b^{0}(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})-b^{0}(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})

where the functional
b^{0}(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})
is defined as

b^{0}(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})=-\int g(\mathbf{x})\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{x})\log\left(\lambda_{j}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})\right)\,d\mathbf{x}.

With this definition in mind, we have immediately that

l(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})\leq b^{0}(\mathbf{f},\bm{\lambda})+C^{0}

where
C^{0}=l(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})-b^{0}(\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0})
. Thus, we can confidently claim at this point that the algorithm that we proposed is, indeed, an MM algorithm

where

Remark 5
.

Note that the objective function of this algorithm converges. Indeed, we already showed that it is monotonically decreasing with respect to the negative smoothed log-likelihood defined in (
4
). It only remains to notice that this negative-smoothed log-likelihood function is bounded from below by zero. Indeed, due to the (
5
), it can be represented as a sum of the KL distance between the target density
g(\mathbf{x})
and the convex combination of the smoothed component densities, on one hand, and the difference

1-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\int{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}

on the other hand. The former is always non-negative. The latter is also non-negative since
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})\leq{\cal S}f_{j}(\mathbf{x})
due to Jensen’s inequality

2.2
Extended algorithm for estimation of copula parameters

In the previous section we obtained an MM algorithm capable of estimating both probability weights and the density functions of the general nonparametric density mixture model (
1
). In this version, the proposed algorithm does not attempt to characterize the dependence mechanism between marginals of each density function
f_{j}(\mathbf{x})
,
j=1,\ldots,m
. Let us now assume that every multivariate density function
f_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is represented as in (
2
) with the assumption that the parametric form of each copula
C_{j}
is known and only the value of the parameter
\rho_{j}
is unknown. In the current subsection, we show that, in this case, the algorithm we have just derived can be modified to estimate the parameter
\rho_{j}
in addition to the parameters discussed earlier.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an arbitrary vector with
x
being the value of its
k
th coordinate. Then, the
k
th marginal density function of the
j
th component at the
t
th step of iteration,
f_{jk}^{(t)}(x)
, can be obtained from the (current) estimate of the joint density function
f^{(t)}_{j}(\mathbf{x})
as

f_{jk}^{(t)}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})dx_{1}\ldots,dx_{k-1}dx_{k+1}\ldots dx_{d}.

(11)

Ordinarily, if the data are known, one can estimate marginal density functions directly from the data - no knowledge of the dependence structure between variables is needed. This is not the case here, however, since we don’t have the advanced knowledge of which observations are assigned to which component of the model and, therefore, we cannot estimate marginal densities
f_{jk}(x)
at first. As we proceed, obtaining more precise estimates of joint component density functions
f_{j}(\mathbf{x})
enables us to start estimating marginal density functions as well.

Next, the knowledge of marginal density functions implies that we can also obtain the marginal cumulative density function
F_{jk}^{(t)}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_{jk}^{(t)}(s)\,ds
. Let
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})^{{}^{\prime}}
where
z_{k}=F_{jk}^{(t)}(x_{k})
,
k=1,\ldots,d
. Then, using the representation (
2
), we can estimate the
j
th copula density function at
\mathbf{z}
at the
t
th step of iteration as

c_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{z}):=\frac{f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}f_{jk}^{(t)}(x_{k})}.

(12)

Finally, let
c(\mathbf{z};\rho_{j})
be the true
j
th copula density function, viewed as a function of
\rho_{j}
at the point
\mathbf{z}
. If there are
n
observations
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
from the model (
1
), we can define

\rho_{j}^{(t)}=\arg\min S_{n}^{(t)}(\rho_{j})

where

S_{n}^{(t)}(\rho_{j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[c(\mathbf{z}_{i};\rho_{j})-c_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{z}_{i})]^{2}.

This discussion suggests the following extended algorithm. We denote
\bm{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m})^{{}^{\prime}}
.

1.

Initialize the algorithm with a choice of
\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0},\bm{\rho}^{0}

2.

For
t=0,1,\ldots
define weights

w_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\lambda_{j}^{(t)}{\cal N}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})}

(13)

where
j=1,\ldots,m

3.

Find individual marginal densities
f_{jk}^{(t)}(x_{ik})
for all of
k=1,\ldots,d
using marginal integration as in (
11
)

4.

Obtain individual marginal cumulative densities
F_{jk}^{(t)}(x_{ik})=\int_{-\infty}^{x_{ik}}f_{jk}^{(t)}(s)\,ds

5.

Define a new set of probabilities

\lambda_{j}^{(t+1)}=\int g(\mathbf{x})w_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x},

(14)

j=1,\ldots,m

6.

Define a new set of
d
-dimensional density functions at the set of points

f_{j}^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{x})=\alpha_{j}^{(t+1)}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{u})\frac{g(\mathbf{u}){\cal N}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t+1)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})}\,d\mathbf{u}

(15)

for
j=1,\ldots,m
where
\alpha_{j}^{(t+1)}
is a normalizing constant that depends on the step of iteration
t
.

7.

Let
\mathbf{z}_{i}=(z_{i1},\ldots,z_{id})^{{}^{\prime}}
where
z_{ik}=F_{jk}^{(t)}(x_{ik})
. Estimate the copula density of the
j
th component at the point
\mathbf{z}_{i}
at the step of iteration
t
as

c_{j}^{(t+1)}\left(\mathbf{z}_{i}\right):=\frac{f_{j}^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}f_{jk}^{(t+1)}(x_{ik})}.

8.

Let
c(\mathbf{z}_{i};\rho_{j})
be the value of the true
j
th copula density function at the point
\mathbf{z}_{i}
. We now estimate the value of the copula parameter
\rho_{j}
as the solution of an optimization problem

\rho^{(t+1)}_{j}=\arg\min S_{n}^{(t+1)}(\rho_{j})

where
S_{n}^{(t+1)}(\rho_{j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[c(\mathbf{z}_{i};\rho_{j})-c_{j}^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{z}_{i})]^{2}
.

9.

Go back to step (
2
).

As opposed to the general algorithm setting where the kernel function could be an essentially arbitrary multivariate rescaled density function, here we use the value of the
j
th component density function from the first iteration
f_{j}^{(0)}
as a kernel. This kernel undergoes what amounts to a reweighting procedure at every step of iteration in (
15
) to become the next iteration
f_{j}^{(1)}
,
f_{j}^{(2)}
etc. In other words, we define

K_{j}(\mathbf{x})=c(F_{j1}^{(0)}(x_{1}),\ldots,F_{jd}^{(0)}(x_{d});\rho_{j}^{0})\prod_{k=1}^{d}f_{jk}^{(0)}(x_{k}).

(16)

We are choosing this specific kernel in order to guarantee that the next iteration preserves the specific parametric copula structure defined by the known copula density function
c(\cdot,\ldots,\cdot)
that is assumed to be known in advance. Note that, although available at the step of iteration
t
, the updated copula parameter value
\rho_{j}^{(t)}
and the updated values of marginal density functions are not used in the definition of the kernel function and the initialized values are used instead. The reason is the same reason that makes the algorithm of
[
20
]
and algorithms presented in
[
7
]
use a fixed bandwidth at each step of iteration - preservation of monotonicity. In principle, it does seem reasonable to update the bandwidth matrix
H
, the copula parameter
\rho_{j}
, all of the estimated marginal density functions and the estimated cumulative distribution functions in the proposed kernel function at each step of iteration since this amounts to using the new information available about estimated component densities. This, however, makes the objective functional (
4
) ill-defined and, therefore, invalidates the desirable monotonicity.

2.3
Practical implementation

The extended algorithm proposed above is a “conceptual” algorithm that assumes the knowledge of the target density function
g(\mathbf{x})
. Of course, this is not the case in reality and so we propose a discretized version of this algorithm. Since we have a sample of observations
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
that is assumed to have been generated from
g(\mathbf{x})
, we substitute the integral
\int g(\mathbf{x})w_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}
with
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
. To make our exposition easier to follow, we will give a detailed form of our algorithm in its discretized version. To prevent the label switching, we are working under the constraint
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{m}
.

1.

Initialize the algorithm with a choice of
\mathbf{f}^{0},\bm{\lambda}^{0}
,
\bm{\rho}^{0}

2.

For
t=0,1,\ldots
find individual marginal densities

for all of

using a discretized integral from (

3.

Obtain individual marginal cumulative density functions

4.

For

define weights

(17)

where
j=1,\ldots,m

5.

Define a new set of probabilities

(18)

j=1,\ldots,m

6.

Define a new set of

\mathbf{x}_{l}

(19)

for

Let
\mathbf{z}_{i}=(z_{i1},\ldots,z_{id})^{{}^{\prime}}

Let
c(\mathbf{y}_{l};\rho_{j})
be the value of the true
j
th copula density function at the point
\mathbf{y}_{l}
if
\rho^{(j)}
is the true value of the copula parameter. We now estimate the value of the copula parameter
\rho_{j}
at the
t+1
st step of iteration as the solution of an optimization problem

\rho^{(t+1)}_{j}=\arg\min S_{n}^{(t+1)}(\rho_{j})

where
S_{n}^{(t+1)}(\rho_{j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}[c(\mathbf{y}_{l};\rho_{j})-c^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{y}_{l})]^{2}
.

Go back to step (

).

At every step of iteration, it is also necessary to choose a numerical integration approach to obtain updated marginal densities
f_{jk}^{(t)}(x)
. Also, the algorithm requires performing a multivariate convolution every time an operator
{\cal N}
is applied. Note that there are few flexible copula families available for higher dimensions. Some possibilities include using e.g. vine copulas
[
1
]
or factor copulas
[
18
]
. However, attempting to use them results in a very difficult optimization problem at every iteration step whenever the new value of the copula parameter has to be obtained. Due to this, we only consider the low-dimensional case in this manuscript. In a low-dimensional case, one reasonable approach to numerical integration is to use e.g. adaptive Gaussian quadrature to integrate out all the unneeded coordinates in
f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})
. In particular, we use a modern R package
cubature

[
25
]
that uses an adaptive multidimensional Gaussian quadrature and works with vector-valued integrands over hypercubes. This approach is used both to obtain estimated marginal densities
f_{jk}^{(t)}(x)
and to compute numerical convolutions. Also, to simplify the exposition, we only consider copulas parameterized by a one-dimensional parameter. Using such copulas is typically enough whenever the dependence structure of the data can be adequately represented with a single measure of association such as, for example, Kendall’s tau or Spearman’s rho (see e.g.
[
26
]
Section
4
dedicated to Archimedean copulas). In the univariate copula parameter
\rho_{j}
case, the minimization of the criterion
S_{n}^{(t+1)}
can be performed using a basic method which is a combination of the golden section search and successive parabolic interpolation that is encoded in the R function
optimize

-\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t)}}{\cal N}f^{(t)})(x_{i})
become smaller than the prespecified tolerance value of
\varepsilon=10^{-8}
.

3
Convergence of functional sequences generated by the algorithm

In addition to the convergence of the objective function, we also investigate if a sequence
\{\lambda_{j}^{(t)},f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})\}_{j=1}^{m}
,
t=0,1,2,3,\ldots
, generated by the algorithm, converges as well. Such convergence results have been established earlier for related algorithms in e.g.
[

[

. For conciseness, we will also use the notation
\left\{\bm{\lambda}^{(t_{l})},\mathbf{f}^{(t_{l})}\right\}
for this sequence. The following result can be established.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let there exist
L>0
such that
|K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x})-K_{j,H}(\mathbf{y})|\leq L||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||
for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\Omega
and
j=1,\ldots,m
; in other words, we assume that the kernel density functions
K_{j}(\cdot)
are Lipschitz continuous. Moreover, we also assume that all of them are bounded away from zero. Finally, we assume that all of the initial
\lambda_{j}^{0}>0
and denote
\lambda=\min_{1\leq j\leq m}\lambda_{j}^{0}
. Then, there exists a subsequence
\left\{\bm{\lambda}^{(t_{l})},\mathbf{f}^{(t_{l})}\right\}
,
l=1,2,\ldots
that converges to a local minimum of the functional (
4
).

Remark 6
.

Since kernel function
K_{j}(\cdot)
are products of marginal density functions at initialization and the copula density function, boundedness away from zero requires that all of the initial marginal density functions and a copula density function must be bounded away from zero. An example of non-trivial family of copula density functions bounded away from zero can be found in e.g.
[
22
]
p.
4338-39
. The family introduced there is a perturbation of an independence copula which is a usual (trivial) example of a copula whose density is bounded away from zero. Note also that thus introduced family of bivariate copulas
C_{1,\lambda}(u,v)
(in the notation of
[
22
]
) is quite suitable for practical use since it is capable of modeling a pair of random variables with the full range of Pearson correlation from
-1
to
1
. The same copula family is also Lipschitz continuous on a compact set. Note that one can also choose initialized marginal density functions to be bounded away from zero. Hence, as long as the initialized marginal density functions are Lipschitz continuous on
\Omega
, the entire proposed kernel is Lipschitz continuous as well (because the product of Lipschitz continuous functions on a compact is Lipschitz continuous on that compact).

Proof.

The proof of this Theorem consists of the combination of proofs of Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
below.
∎

.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem (
3.1
) there exists a converging subsequence of the sequence
f_{j}^{(t)}
for any
j=1,\ldots,m
,
t=0,1,2,\ldots
generated by the proposed algorithm.

Proof.

This result can be proved using Arzelà-Ascoli theorem. To do so, we have to show, first, that the sequence
\{f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})\}
is uniformly bounded for all
t=0,1,\ldots
: there exists
F_{1}>0
such that
|f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})|\leq F_{1}
. Second, we have to show that the sequence
\{f_{j}^{(t)}\}
is also uniformly continuous: there exists
F_{2}>0
such that
|f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})-f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{y})|\leq F_{2}||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||
for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\Omega
and
t=0,1,2,\ldots
.

We begin with establishing a simple fact that
{\cal M}_{\lambda^{(t)}}{\cal N}f^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{u})
is bounded from above for any step of iteration
t
. This is the quantity that is found in the denominator of the integral that defines the next iteration of the function
f_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
according to (
10
). First, remember that all of the kernel functions are uniformly continuous on
\Omega
. Also, all of them are also strictly positive on a compact set. Therefore, there exist positive constants
a
and
A
such that
a\leq K_{j,H}(\cdot)\leq A
.
Indeed, using Jensen’s inequality, one can easily verify that
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{u})\leq\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}={\cal S}_{j}f^{(t-1)}_{j}(\mathbf{u})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\lambda^{(t)}}{\cal N}f^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{(t)}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{u})\leq\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{(t)}f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}

(20)

\displaystyle\leq A\int\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{(t)}f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}=A

because
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{(t)}f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})
is a density and so its integral is equal to
1
. Note also that the same argument produces boundedness of
{\cal M}_{\lambda^{(t)}}{\cal N}f^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})
, which is the quantity in the denominator of the weight
w_{j}^{(t)}
defined in (
8
) for any
t
as well.

Next,we show that
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})
is also bounded away from zero uniformly for every step of iteration
t
by mathematical induction. Due to boundedness away from zero of all initialized densities
f_{j}^{(0)}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
, all of these densities are also log integrable. With this mind, let
B=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\int|\log f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})|\,d\mathbf{u}<\infty
. First, for
t=0
we have
\int\left|\log f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})\right|\,d\mathbf{u}\leq B
for any
j=1,\ldots,m
by assumption. Due to this, we clearly have
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{0}(\mathbf{u})\geq e^{-AB}>0
. Next, we assume that
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{u})\geq a_{1}>0
for some
a_{1}
. Then, we conclude that, since
{\cal M}_{\lambda^{(t)}}{\cal N}f^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{u})\leq A
, we also have immediately that, due to (
10
),
f^{(t)}_{j}(\mathbf{u})
is also bounded away from zero uniformly for any
t
. But that, of course, immediately implies that
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})
is bounded away from zero. Thus, by induction,
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})
is bounded away from zero for an arbitrary step of iteration
t
.

Next, we are going to prove that
\lambda_{j}^{(t)}
is bounded away from zero at any step of iteration
t
using the mathematical induction. First, since
\lambda_{j}^{0}\geq\lambda
, we find immediately that
w_{j}^{0}\geq\frac{\lambda e^{-AB}}{A}>0

\min_{1\leq j\leq m}\lambda_{j}^{(t-1)}\geq\tilde{\lambda}>0

is bounded away from zero uniformly. Due to (

The uniform boundedness of all functions

\lambda^{(t+1)}_{j}
are bounded away from zero and the same is true about

{\cal M}_{\lambda^{(t+1)}}{\cal N}f^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle f_{j}^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{u})\leq\frac{1}{M}\alpha_{j}^{(t+1)}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})g(\mathbf{u}){\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}\alpha_{j}^{(t+1)}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})g(\mathbf{u})\left\{\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{u})f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z}\right\}\,d\mathbf{u}

are densities while

. Thus, we proved that there exists a constant

\left|f_{j}^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{u})\right|\leq F

To show the uniform equicontinuity, we consider the difference

\displaystyle|f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})-f^{(t)}_{j}(\mathbf{y})|\leq\alpha_{j}^{(t)}\int\frac{g(\mathbf{u}){\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{u})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{u})}\left|K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})-K_{j,H}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{u})\right|\,d\mathbf{u}

Proof.

First, note that by definition,
\lambda_{j}^{(t)}\leq 1

[

{\cal M}_{\mathbf{\lambda}^{(t_{l}+1)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t_{l})}\rightarrow{\cal M}_{\xi}{\cal N}\bm{\psi}

\rho(t)=t-\log t-1\geq 0

{\cal M}_{\xi}{\cal N}\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
, by Fatou lemma it is always true that

\int g(\mathbf{x}){\cal M}_{\bm{\xi}}{\cal N}\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{x})\leq\liminf\int g(\mathbf{x}){\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t_{l}+1)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t_{l})}(\mathbf{x}).

Due to the above, the inequality

\int g(\mathbf{x})\rho({\cal M}_{\bm{\xi}}{\cal N}\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{x}))\,d\mathbf{x}\leq\liminf\int g(\mathbf{x})\rho({\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t_{l}+1)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t_{l})}(\mathbf{x}))\,d\mathbf{x}.

is equivalent to

-\int g(\mathbf{x})\log\{{\cal M}_{\bm{\xi}}{\cal N}\psi(\mathbf{x})\}\,d\mathbf{x}\leq\liminf\int g(\mathbf{x})\log\{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t_{l}+1)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t_{l})}(\mathbf{x})\}\,d\mathbf{x}

which implies that the functional (
4
) is indeed a lower semicontinuous one.
∎

One can also prove the result of Theorem (
3.1
) in the case where the unknown density domain
\Omega
is not compact as long as the smoothing kernel is defined on a compact. To show that this is the case, we begin with establishing an alternative version of Lemma
1
. In order to prove this alternative version, we must use Fréchet-Kolmogorov theorem instead of the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem. The Fréchet-Kolmogorov theorem establishes necessary and sufficient conditions of relative compactness for a functional space
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
; in our case,
p=1
. Since it is a little less well known than Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, and also for convenience, we state this theorem in full.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
{\cal B}
be a set in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
p\in[1,\infty)
. The subset
{\cal B}
is relatively compact if and only if the following properties hold for any function
f\in{\cal B}
:

1.

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\int_{||x||>r}|f|^{p}=0
uniformly on
{\cal B}
,

2.

\lim_{a\rightarrow 0}||\tau_{a}f-f||_{p}=0
uniformly on
{\cal B}

where
||\cdot||_{p}
stands for the
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
norm and where
\tau_{a}f
denotes the translation of
f
by
a
, that is,
\tau_{a}f(x)=f(x-a)
.

A very nice proof of this result can be found in e.g. an expository paper
[
12
]
. Now we can start with establishing the new version of Lemma (
3.2
).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem (
3.1
) be true. We also assume that the kernel
K
has a closed rectangular support
\Delta
on which it is bounded away from zero. Then, there exists a converging subsequence of the sequence
f_{j}^{(t)}
for any
j=1,\ldots,m
,
t=0,1,2,\ldots
generated by the proposed algorithm.

Remark 7
.

Note that here we also have the freedom to define initialized density functions
f_{j}^{(0)}
on a (possibly large)
d
-dimensional compact set and to make them strictly positive and Lipschitz continuous. In that case, the kernel function (
16
) is going to have a compact support and be strictly positive as long as the copula density function is a strictly positive one such as e.g. the one corresponding to
C_{1,\lambda}
of
[
22
]
. Hence, the assumptions of Lemma (
3.5
) are going to be valid.

Proof.

Let us now check the first condition in the Theorem (
3.4
) for functions that belong to the set
B
. As a preliminary step, we show first that at any step of iteration
\lambda_{j}^{(t)}
is bounded away from zero. Indeed, from our algorithm we can see that
\lambda_{j}^{(t)}=\int g(\mathbf{x})w_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}
; thus, if we show that the weight
w_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})
is always bounded away from zero for any
t
, the probability
\lambda_{j}^{(t)}
is bounded away from zero as well. Since the kernel function
K
is bounded from below, we can easily claim that for any
f_{j}^{(t)}\in B

f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})=\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\phi_{j}(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}\geq\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\int\phi(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}=K_{*}>0
. Next, by definition of the smoothing operator
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})
, and since
f_{j}^{(t)}\in B
for any step of iteration
t
, we have
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})=\exp\left\{\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\log f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}\right\}\geq\exp\{\log K^{*}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}\}=K^{*}>0
since the kernel function is a proper density function. Now, recall that at each step
t
the weight is

w_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\lambda_{j}^{(t-1)}{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t-1)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})}.

(21)

Since the kernel
K
is continuous on a compact rectangle, it is also necessarily bounded from above by a positive constant
M
. Using Jensen’s inequality, we immediately find that
{\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t-1)}(\mathbf{x})
is always bounded from above by
M
as well.

Analyzing the constant
\alpha_{j}^{(t)}
, we see that

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{\int d\mathbf{x}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\frac{g(\mathbf{u}){\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t+1)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})}\,d\mathbf{u}}\leq\frac{M}{K^{*}}\frac{1}{\int d\mathbf{x}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})g(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}}.

The convolution
\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})g(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}
is always integrable as long as
K_{j,H}(\cdot)
and the density function
g(\mathbf{u})
are integrable themselves since

\int d\mathbf{x}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})g(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}\leq\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\int g(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}=1

due to Young’s convolution inequality. Thus, we can claim that
\alpha_{j}^{(t)}
is bounded by a finite constant that does not depend on
t
. Let us denote this constant
{\cal C}
.

This implies that

\displaystyle\int_{||\mathbf{x}||>r}|f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})\,dx\leq{\cal C}\int_{||\mathbf{x}||>r}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\frac{g(\mathbf{u}){\cal N}_{j}f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})}{{\cal M}_{\bm{\lambda}^{(t+1)}}{\cal N}\mathbf{f}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})}\,d\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\leq{\cal C}\frac{M}{K^{*}}\int_{||\mathbf{x}||>r}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})g(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}.

The last integral can be made smaller then an arbitrarily chosen
\varepsilon
with the appropriate choice of
r
independently of the index
t
since, due to Young’s convolution inequality,

\int d\mathbf{x}\int K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})g(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}

is finite.

To verify the second condition we note first that, due to Lipschitz continuity of the kernel function and the fact that it is defined on a finite closed rectangle
\Delta
, we have

\displaystyle\int\left|f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a})-f_{j}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})\right|\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq\int\,d\mathbf{x}\int\left|(K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{u})-K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\right|\phi(\mathbf{u}))\,d\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\int\phi(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u}\int\left|(K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{u})-K_{j,H}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\right|\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq{\cal D}||a||

for some positive constant
{\cal D}
. Hence, for any
\mathbf{a}
such that
||\mathbf{a}||<\rho
we have the integral bounded from above by an expression that does not depend on the function
f_{j}^{(t)}
.
∎

Note that the proof of Lemma (
3.5
) shows that all of
\lambda_{j}^{(t)}
are bounded
uniformly away from zero. Analyzing the proof of Lemma (
3.3
), we see that this is enough to guarantee its validity in the case of density functions defined on a non-compact interval. Thus, the combination of Lemmas (
3.5
) and (
3.3
) proves Theorem (
3.1
) once again.

We will illustrate the behavior of our algorithm using the famous iris dataset. As a reminder, it has
n=150
observations of
d=4

Iris: Iris setosa, Iris versicolor, Iris virginica
. For simplicity, and following the precedent of
[

, we will only use two of these characteristics as variables: the sepal length and the petal length. The algorithm proposed in Section

[
20

[
9

Selecting the number of clusters in nonparametric density mixtures is a difficult and mostly unsolved problem. Limited results available have only been obtained for models with conditionally independent marginals e.g.
[
19
]
and references therein. Due to this, we only consider the case with the number of clusters equal to the true number of
3
.

The obtained classification results are reported in Figure

6
Conclusion

The algorithm proposed in this manuscript is designed to estimate the parameters of an arbitrary copula-based semiparametric finite density mixture model. It has been developed to bypass an obvious limitation of the earlier algorithm proposed in
[
21
]
- its lack of monotonicity. The model considered is a general one - the marginal densities are not viewed as belonging to any specific family and the dependence between them is modeled using a copula. The algorithm is a deterministic algorithm of the MM type that shows good performance in illustrative numerical examples.

As is common in statistical research, the problem considered and the algorithm suggested to solve it raise a number of additional questions worthy of consideration. First, to the best of our knowledge, nothing is known about the identifiability of the model (
1
)-(
2
) considered in this manuscript. Moreover, even more circumscribed model considered in
[
24
]
where all of the marginal density functions are assumed to belong to a location-scale family is not known to be identifiable either. Investigating identifiability of these models represents an important avenue for future research.

Another important topic of future research would be to make this algorithm feasible for higher dimensional data. We believe that the starting point of this future research would be an attempt to model dependence of coordinates of multivariate distributions using Archimedean copulas
[
26
]
pp.
109-155
. There are two reasons for this, in our opinion. The first one is that Archimedean copulas can be written down in closed form which makes optimization considerably easier. Second, they can model a fairly wide variety of dependence structures using only one- or two-dimensional parameters.

As has been pointed out earlier in e.g.
[
20
]
, there is a certain sense in updating the bandwidth matrix at every step of iteration since this takes into account updated estimates of component densities. The same remark is also applicable to updating values of copula parameters
\rho_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
used to define the smoothing kernel. At the same time, such an approach would violate the monotonicity of the proposed algorithm. A possible research question would be to quantify the resulting loss of monotonicity with respect to the objective functional considered in this manuscript and, moreover, to investigate just how substantial such a violation of monotonicity is depending on, for example, the specific mechanism of the bandwidth matrix selection. The most pressing question would be, then, to show if the algorithm still converges despite the loss of monotonicity property.
