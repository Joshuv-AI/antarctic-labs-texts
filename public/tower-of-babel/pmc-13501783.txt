Status and influencing factors of obstetric violence in Shanghai, China: a multicenter cross-sectional survey
Authors: Nuannuan Pan, Qiaosong Wang, Jie Bai, Na Yin, Jiayun Zhou, Hui Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA substantial body of evidence indicates that obstetric violence can cause profound and lasting physical and psychological damage to pregnant women. However, there is notable lack of research examining the incidence of obstetric violence specifically within the Asia–Pacific region, highlighting an urgent need to elucidate its prevalence and delineate the multifactorial contributors to its occurrence. Therefore, the present study aimed to explore the prevalence and determinants of obstetric violence in secondary and tertiary healthcare facilities in Shanghai, China.MethodsThis hospital-based, cross-sectional study employed a convenience sample of 895 postpartum women recruited from four specialized maternal and child health hospitals for a questionnaire-based survey conducted between June and September 2025. Data analysis included univariate analyses, correlation assessments, and multivariate logistic regression modeling.ResultsThe overall self-reported prevalence of obstetric violence was 17.1% (153 of 895 participants). Verbal abuse emerged as the most prevalent form of obstetric violence (8.3%). Notably, both incidence and perceived severity of obstetric violence were significantly higher in secondary institutions compared to those in tertiary institutions (p < 0.05). Higher educational attainment, presence of pregnancy complications, and greater autonomy in maternal decision-making were associated with lower odds of experiencing obstetric violence (p < 0.05). Receiving care at a secondary-level institution was independently associated with obstetric violence (p < 0.05).ConclusionsObstetric violence was prevalent in both secondary and tertiary maternity specialty hospitals. This study advocates implementing multilevel interventions targeting individual mothers, healthcare providers, hospital systems, and broader societal structures for improving the quality of maternity care throughout the perinatal continuum and promoting the principles of respectful maternity care.Keywords: Obstetric violence, Respectful maternity care, Pregnancy, Birth; Woman, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Obstetric violence (OV) is a significant global public health concern with considerable conceptual and institutional transformation over the past two decades. Originally framed within the settings of low- and middle-income countries under the nomenclature “Disrespect and Abuse” (D&A), this phenomenon was characterized by clinical interactions or infrastructural conditions that were either perceived as, or intentionally conducive to, humiliating and degrading experiences, informed by local cultural norms [1, 2]. The institutional recognition of OV was initiated by Venezuela’s pioneering legislative enactment in Latin America (2007) [3], culminating in the formal declaration by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2014, which identified OV as a critical public health issue with direct implications for maternal and infant wellbeing. This pivotal event not only secured OV’s position within the international health governance framework but also promoted a transformative shift toward rights-based approaches for comprehending and addressing such experiences [1].

A prevailing scholarly consensus defines OV as a multifaceted phenomenon encompassing detrimental and non-evidence-based medical practices spanning the antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum continuum. The key dimensions of OV include disrespectful or abusive treatment, administration of medical interventions without obtaining prior informed consent or lacking clinical justification, and instances of neglect during obstetric emergencies. The taxonomic scope extends to incorporate delayed care provision; refusal of patient admission; denial of analgesia; incidence of physical/verbal/psychological abuse; violations of patient privacy and autonomy; coercive medical procedures; unjustified detention of parturients and neonates within healthcare facilities; and use of obsolete or superfluous interventions that contravene evidence-based guidelines, thereby causing iatrogenic and preventable harm [4, 5].

Disrespectful or abusive intrapartum care can precipitate traumatic childbirth experiences, resulting in profound long-term psychological sequelae such as increased incidence of birth-related trauma, postpartum depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [6]. Nonindicated medical procedures, such as routine episiotomies, nonmedically justified cesarean sections, and unnecessary pelvic examinations, directly compromise maternal physical health and elevate the risk of peripartum complications, including infection, hemorrhage, and severe perineal trauma [7–12]. Exposure to abuse, discrimination, or disrespect during labor can lead to immediate adverse emotional states, including fear, anxiety, and a profound sense of helplessness. These states not only manifest as acute psychological distress but also act as potent precursors to long-term mental health impairment, specifically increasing vulnerability to PTSD and postpartum depression [6, 13, 14]. The denial of autonomy during labor—entailing the right to choose medical interventions, delivery modes, and support persons, coupled with coercion into nonindicated procedures—constitutes a critical violation of the patient’s rights to self-determination, participatory decision-making, and informed consent [15, 16]. This violation directly undermines maternal satisfaction with care, erodes trust in healthcare providers and the overall medical system, and ultimately diminishes future healthcare engagement and cooperation [17–19].

Despite the lack of standardized reporting of OV within the global maternal health surveillance framework, extant epidemiological evidence indicates its widespread occurrence. In high-income countries, the documented incidence reaches 16.7% in the United States [20], whereas self-reported rates among primiparous women in Italy are approximately 20.1% [19]. In contrast, the prevalence of OV markedly increases in low- and middle-income countries, with reported incidence rates of 31% in Mexico [21], 25–33% across Latin American nations [22], 46.85% in East Africa [23], and as high as 75% in certain Ethiopian regions [24]. Collectively, these data firmly establish that OV is not merely an isolated phenomenon confined to specific economic strata or healthcare systems. Rather, it represents a ubiquitous global health challenge that transcends national income gradients and cultural contexts, highlighting the critical need for immediate and contextually tailored research initiatives.

During global demographic transitions, subreplacement fertility rates have become significant impediments to socioeconomic sustainability in various nations [25]. OV further complicates this demographic challenge by suppressing women’s reproductive intentions through traumatic childbirth experiences, thereby adversely affecting population growth trajectories and age-structure dynamics. This nexus has elicited decisive institutional responses. Key normative frameworks, such as the UN Secretary-General’s Global Strategy for Women’s, Children’s and Adolescents’ Health (2016–2030) [26] and the WHO Intrapartum Care Guidelines [27], explicitly mandate that high-quality maternal care must simultaneously prioritize positive childbirth experiences and clinical safety. Consequently, mitigating the issue of OV is essential to achieve these global objectives. This imperative is mirrored in adaptations at the national policy level, particularly in China’s recent promulgation of the “Several Measures on Accelerating the Improvement of the Fertility Support Policy System and Promoting the Development of a Birth-Friendly Society” [28]. This initiative represents a targeted policy instrument aimed to reverse declining fertility rates by establishing a robust support ecosystem.

In the context of comprehensive national policies for addressing declining fertility rates [28], preliminary investigations in East Asia, notably in China, document persistent OV manifestations. These include breaches of patient’s autonomy during gynecological examinations, nonindicated intrapartum interventions, insufficient information disclosure, and engagement of patients in clinical education without their explicit consent [29]. These experiences contribute substantially to the development of tokophobia (fear of childbirth) among gravid and postpartum women, frequently culminating in comorbid physical and psychological trauma [30]. Although a mixed-methods study conducted in Guangdong Province suggested geographical heterogeneity in the prevalence of OV within China [31], OV-based research across the Asia–Pacific region remains markedly underdeveloped. There is notable scarcity of large-scale quantitative analyses conducted in various metropolitan contexts and within stratified healthcare systems. Additionally, existing literature shows significant gaps pertaining to the structural determinants, contributory factors, and underlying mechanisms of OV. Moreover, there is marked absence of interventional frameworks that are conceptually aligned with and operationally integrated into broader reproductive support policy networks. Major East Asian metropolises, such as Shanghai, have unique regional configurations characterized by advanced healthcare infrastructure, intricate clinician‒parturient interaction dynamics, and specific demographic fertility profiles. Shanghai’s preeminent medical standards and sophisticated service architecture not only lead nationally but also represent the zenith of China’s healthcare capabilities. A cross-sectional investigation within this context can therefore provide insights into the contemporary landscape of OV within China’s most advanced healthcare jurisdictions. The findings could yield critical empirical benchmarks, informing the development of contextually adaptable intervention models for diverse global healthcare systems.

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across four major medical institutions in Shanghai to investigate OV among obstetric populations. It aims to (i) augment the global evidence base on OV, (ii) generate scientific evidence to support the standardization of obstetric care, (iii) facilitate advancements in perinatal healthcare quality, and (iv) contribute to the global progression of respectful maternity care (RMC) frameworks [27].

Methods

Study design

This study utilized a multicenter, cross-sectional design. OV incidence was assessed through a structured questionnaire, with subsequent data analysis to identify associated influencing factors.

Participants

The study was conducted in secondary and tertiary maternity and infant specialty hospitals located in Shanghai, China. A convenience sampling strategy was utilized for participant recruitment between June and September 2025. The participants included postpartum women attending four designated specialty hospitals in Shanghai for their routine postpartum check-ups. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) provided informed consent and were willing to participate in this study and (2) were alert, capable of normal communication, and could independently complete the survey. Participants with a history of mental illness, exhibiting communication barriers, or showing inability to comprehend the questionnaire were excluded.

Prior to data collection, all researchers underwent a standardized training program to ensure uniformity. The survey was administered only after written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A quick response (QR) code linked to the electronic questionnaire (generated through Questionnaire Star software) was prepared. The researchers then conducted face-to-face sessions to introduce the study, and the participants could subsequently access and complete the questionnaire by scanning the QR code. Data collection was scheduled to align with the routine 42-day post-partum follow-up visit. The participants submitted their responses anonymously through the electronic questionnaire accessed after scanning the QR code. They were instructed to complete the questionnaire independently. The researchers were present onsite to clarify any questions and supervise the submission process .

Measures

General information questionnaire

The questionnaire was developed by the research team and included key maternal sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, including age, educational attainment, employment status, parity, pregnancy complications, and monthly household income bracket.

Mothers on Respect (MOR) Index

The MOR Index, developed by Vedam et al. (2017) [32], evaluates women’s experiences of respect and discrimination throughout the perinatal period. The instrument comprises 14 items across three subscales: respect (7 items), discrimination (4 items), and healthcare collaboration (3 items). The items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale (e.g., from “never” to “always”), yielding a potential total score ranging from 14 to 84. Higher composite scores reflect a greater perceived level of respect. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α value for the MOR was 0.85.

Mothers’ Autonomy in Decision-Making (MADM) Scale

The MADM scale, developed by Vedam et al. (2019) [33], measures the extent of autonomy women experience in shared decision-making during clinical encounters. The scale comprises seven items, each rated on a 6-point Likert scale. The summed score ranges from 7 to 42. Higher scores correspond to a greater degree of decision-making autonomy. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α value for the MADM scale was 0.93.

Obstetric Violence Survey Scale (OVSS)

The Obstetric Violence Survey Scale (OVSS), an original instrument, was developed through a mixed-methods approach that integrated qualitative interviews and a systematic literature review, which was explicitly contextualized within China’s distinct sociocultural and healthcare landscape. The OVSS encompasses 23 items distributed across five domains: physical violence (4 items), verbal violence (6 items), psychological and emotional violence (4 items), neglect or discrimination (5 items), and inappropriate medical procedures (4 items). A 6-point Likert scale (Strongly disagree = 1 point, disagree = 2 points, somewhat disagree = 3 points, somewhat agree = 4 points, agree = 5 points, strongly agree = 6 points) is used for providing response to all items. The summed total score ranges from 23 to 138, with higher scores indicating a more severe overall experience of OV. In this study, to better identify potential victims, an OV event was defined as any item on the scale with a score exceeding 3.

A presurvey involving 212 pregnant women was conducted between May and June 2025 to evaluate the psychometric properties of the OVSS. The scale demonstrated robust psychometric properties. The item analysis confirmed good discrimination, with critical ratio values exceeding 3.50 for all the items (p < 0.001). The corrected item‒domain correlation coefficients ranged from 0.52 to 0.79, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.40. Exploratory factor analysis revealed a Kaiser‒Meyer‒Olkin measure of 0.891 and a significant result for Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ² = 2853.6, p < 0.001), supporting factorability. A five-factor solution was retained, accounting for 68.4% of the total variance. All factor loadings ranged from 0.53 to 0.86, indicating absence of salient cross-loadings (i.e., > 0.40). Content validity, assessed by expert review, was strong: the item-content validity index (I-CVI) score ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, and the scale-content validity index (Universal Agreement [UA] approach, S-CVI/UA) was 0.91. The internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.87), with subscale α coefficients ranging from 0.76 to 0.89. Test‒retest reliability over a 2-week interval, assessed through intraclass correlation coefficients, was good to excellent, with values ranging from 0.79 to 0.86. Sample size

The minimum required sample size was determined using the following formula for cross-sectional studies: N = Z0.5a²*p(1-p)/δ², where Z0.5a = 1.96 (corresponding to a 95% confidence level), p = 0.5 (to maximize sample size in the absence of a precise prior prevalence estimate), and δ = 0.05 (margin of error). This calculation yielded a target sample size of 384 participants.

During the study period, a total of 8,274 postpartum women attended the four participating hospitals for their routine 42-day postpartum checkups, including 3,608 and 2,861 women in the two tertiary hospitals (Grade III), and 929 and 876 women in the two secondary hospitals (Grade II). A total of 917 questionnaires were distributed. Following the exclusion of 41 invalid responses (due to incomplete or patterned answers), 895 valid questionnaires were retained for the analysis. The final analytic sample comprised 415 and 480 participants from tertiary and secondary hospitals, respectively, resulting in an effective response rate of 97.6%.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JASP (Version 1.6, JASP Team). Continuous variables showing normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Because the primary quantitative outcome measures (OVSS, MOR, and MADM scores) were nonnormally distributed, they are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Intergroup comparisons for continuous nonnormally distributed variables were performed by the Mann‒Whitney U test. The Kruskal‒Wallis H test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Associations between categorical variables were assessed by the chi-square (χ²) test. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to evaluate bivariate correlations between continuous variables. Variables showing significant associations (p < 0.05) in the univariate analyses were entered into a subsequent binary logistic regression model to identify independent factors associated with OV. Statistical significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

General information of the participants

The final analytical cohort comprised 895 postpartum women, with a mean (± SD) age of 31.5 ± 4.0 years. Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. The cohort showed a high level of education attainment, with over 90% of the participants possessing a postgraduate or bachelor’s/associate degree. A significant majority of the participants (80.8%) were employed during pregnancy, and over 80% reported a monthly household per capita income exceeding ¥10,000. The predominant household structure was identified as a nuclear family, specifically co-residence with spouses and parents (50.9%). Most participants were primiparous (63.7%) and reported no history of adverse pregnancy outcomes (79.8%) or pregnancy-related complications (85.8%).

In univariate analyses, categorical variables were compared using the χ² test. Significant between-group differences in OV scores were observed in relation to educational attainment, household income per capita, presence of pregnancy complications, and hospital tier (all p < 0.05; see Table 1).

Incidence of OV

Among the 895 participants, 153 (17.1%) reported experiencing at least one form of OV. The incidence varied markedly by hospital tier: 10.6% (44/415) in tertiary institutions versus 22.7% (109/480) in secondary institutions. Verbal violence was the most prevalent form (8.3%), followed by neglect/discrimination (6.6%), physical violence (5.5%), inappropriate medical procedures (4.7%), and psychological violence (4.6%). The overall OV incidence and the incidence of physical violence, neglect or discrimination, and inappropriate medical procedures were significantly greater in secondary hospitals than in tertiary hospitals (p < 0.05; detailed data shown in Table 2).

Discussion

Main findings

This study reveals that OV is a significant concern within the public health framework of Shanghai, China. OV showed an overall prevalence of 17.1% among postpartum women seeking care in the city’s secondary and tertiary healthcare facilities. This implies that approximately one in six women in our cohort experienced at least one form of OV, with verbal violence being identified as the most frequently reported subtype. These findings clarify the core principles of respectful, person-centered maternity care, which underpin the ethical foundation of contemporary healthcare systems. OV persistence is probably a multifactorial phenomenon, reflecting a complex interaction of individual (e.g., clinical and psychological), sociodemographic, and systemic factors inherent to the design and functioning of China’s tiered healthcare system. Consequently, these insights highlight the necessity to develop and implement multilevel, integrated intervention strategies.

Interpretation

In the present study, the incidence of OV (17.1%) was notably lower than that (51.6%) reported in a prior investigation conducted in Guangdong Province [31]. This notable variation may stem from several methodological and contextual factors. Specifically, differences exist in the methods, particularly in the assessment instruments, which likely capture varying constructs and thresholds of OV. Contextual and systemic differences are also pivotal in understanding this variation. Shanghai, a metropolitan hub equipped with top-tier healthcare resources and sophisticated management systems, probably provides more standardized care and greater resource accessibility, potentially representing a structural environment with a reduced risk of OV. Moreover, variations in sampling frames and settings may also contribute to this discrepancy. Our study included samples from the mainstream public hospital system (secondary and tertiary facilities) in Shanghai, catering to the needs of the majority of the childbearing population. In contrast, the Guangdong study sample was derived from a diverse hospital environment that included private institutions, without stratification according to hospital tier. Consequently, the aggregated prevalence rates reported in both studies may obscure critical variations within and between healthcare settings, resulting in a misrepresentation of the incidence in lower resource tiers of the healthcare system.

Contextualizing the findings of this study within the broader global context further highlights the complexity of this issue. Recent meta-analyses reveal that the global prevalence of OV co-infection is 50.8–61.7%, exhibiting considerable regional heterogeneity, for instance, a high prevalence in Africa (64.7%) and a markedly lower prevalence in the Americas (33.2%) [34–36]. In contrast, the prevalence rate of 17.1% observed in our cohort is substantially below these global estimates. However, direct comparisons must be approached with considerable caution, as all sources utilized for the meta-analyses exhibited substantial heterogeneity (I² > 99%) [34–36]. This heterogeneity likely reflects significant methodological and cultural variations across the primary studies, including differences in OV definitions, measurement instruments, and sociocultural contexts.

Notably, the most prevalent form of OV globally is non-consensual care (37%) [34]; in contrast, verbal abuse (8.3%) emerged as the most common OV type within our Shanghai-based sample. This discrepancy in the predominant forms of abuse may reflect fundamental differences in medical practices and underlying philosophies. Non-consensual procedures may be reported more frequently in an environment characterized by interventionist obstetric care. Conversely, in advanced metropolitan areas such as Shanghai, where standardized care protocols and respect for patient autonomy may be more consistently emphasized in clinical practice, overt physical or procedural violations are less commonly documented in self-reports. In this context, women tend to more prominently report interpersonal dimensions of care, such as the quality of communication. This discrepancy might also be linked with cross-cultural differences in the perception and reporting of OV or with variations in the classification of specific behaviors across the studies included in the meta-analyses.

Our Shanghai sample predominantly comprised highly educated, high-income urban women; this sample composition restricts the generalizability of our findings to rural, migrant, and low-income populations, among whom OV may be more common. Thus, the reported prevalence of 17.1% may underrepresent the prevalence of OV in populations and regions with limited resources, given the global evidence indicating a consistent association between low socioeconomic status and OV occurrence [35, 37]. In resource-constrained regions, variations in healthcare conditions, regulatory mechanisms, and patient awareness may influence the different patterns of OV and the observed relationships (e.g., the observed inverse associations between education/income and OV). Therefore, to more comprehensively understand the landscape of OV in China, future research should broaden sample coverage to include regions with differing levels of economic development and diverse sociodemographic populations.

Collectively, these findings highlight that OV remains a salient public health concern even in a highly developed metropolitan city equipped with a sophisticated healthcare infrastructure. Thus, it is plausible that the cases documented in this and similar studies constitute merely a fraction of a potentially larger phenomenon within the diverse healthcare landscape of China, warranting more comprehensive and stratified investigations.

Reduced maternal autonomy in decision-making has emerged as a significant correlate of OV. In the current context, maternal autonomy is defined as a woman’s capacity to access comprehensive information, express her preferences, actively participate in shared decision-making processes, and have her choices acknowledged during clinical interactions. This capacity may be influenced by both intrinsic factors (e.g., a woman’s self-advocacy competencies and health literacy) and extrinsic factors, particularly the commitment of the healthcare setting to foster respectful communication, ensure authentic informed consent, and facilitate shared decision-making [38].Consequently, OV should be conceptualized not solely as an isolated incidence of misconduct by individual providers, but rather as a systemic manifestation of power asymmetries within the clinician‒patient relationship. This paradigm shift suggests that future research may benefit from redirecting its focus from attributing blame to individual “bad apples” toward critically examining the structural and interactional dynamics inherent in the healthcare system. Empirical evidence indicates that in settings where respect for autonomy is emphasized as a core ethical principle in maternity care practice, the preconditions that favor OV may be less prevalent [39, 40]. Research on RMC has demonstrated its efficacy in enhancing provider communication, strengthening cultural competency, and implementing a rights-based framework for clinical practice. Therefore, the strategic incorporation of internationally recognized RMC principles into the maternity care policies and clinical guidelines of China may be a valuable consideration. This integration could signify a crucial step toward addressing OV and potentially mitigating its profound negative impact on maternal health and childbirth experiences.

The present analysis reveals a notable link between lower educational attainment and a higher likelihood of OV, a finding consistent with prior research [41]. This association may be explained by several factors: greater health literacy and more robust information-seeking capacity among highly educated women and enhanced awareness of patient rights, thereby resulting in more assertive and effective interactions with healthcare providers. Conversely, women with limited education may encounter challenges related to self-advocacy within clinical settings, a situation associated with reduced ability to negotiate care and greater vulnerability to OV. This finding further suggests that the elevated levels of urbanization and education in metropolitan regions such as Shanghai may be associated with lower population-level OV risk. Consequently, regions with lower aggregate educational attainment may exhibit higher population-level OV prevalence. In clinical practice, healthcare providers should recognize the impact of these social determinants of health and focus more on providing respectful, supportive care to women from vulnerable socioeconomic backgrounds. This study further advocates targeted epidemiological surveys in regions with lower socioeconomic development across China. Such research could help clarify the nationwide epidemiology of OV, particularly its distribution, correlates, and specific manifestations, thereby contributing to an evidence base for formulating context-sensitive yet cohesive national policies and interventions.

The analysis identified a counterintuitive finding: women with pregnancy-related complications showed a lower incidence of OV. This correlation requires careful interpretation, as it may stem from differential healthcare responses rather than any protective effect of the complications themselves. Women diagnosed with pregnancy complications are often classified as “high risk,” leading to referral to tertiary institutions or specialized teams where care entails close monitoring and use of more standardized, evidence-based protocols. This structured, risk-stratified management—exemplified by China’s Five-Color Alert System [42, 43]—may be associated with fewer opportunities for OV, potentially due to reduced subjective clinical decision-making. However, the implications of this finding do not advocate for the presence of complications; rather, this observation indicates a concerning pattern: the quality of respectful care may vary with a patient’s risk designation. In routine low-risk pregnancies, less systematic oversight may be associated with environments where OV is more frequently reported. Consequently, policy formulation and implementation may be strengthened by adhering to the principles of health equity. This necessitates developing evidence-based, equity-oriented protocols such that the quality of respectful care is not based on a patient’s risk profile but rather is consistently maintained across the entire spectrum of maternity services.

Verbal violence was the most frequently reported form of OV (8.3%), followed closely by neglect and discrimination (6.6%). This pattern may indicate a systemic deficiency in providing empathic communication and humanistic care during maternity services, a well-known challenge [31, 44]. The predominance of verbal and psychological violence is often associated with communication failures, which can profoundly affect the emotional and psychological well-being of parturients [45]. Therefore, healthcare institutions could gain significantly from prioritizing robust training programs to enhance staff communication competencies and cultivate a deeper, more responsive understanding of mothers’ lived experiences and needs.

Focusing solely on individual provider negligence may prove inadequate in thoroughly elucidating OV. As highlighted by a previous study [46], misconduct often reflects a deeper, structural source: the normalization of institutional authority and the hierarchical power dynamics prevalent in healthcare settings. Hierarchal structures in healthcare often exclusively facilitate one-way communication, primarily from the clinician to the patient, thereby preventing reciprocal discussion. This analysis suggests the potential value of interventions aimed at the institutional level. Healthcare organizations may need to critically examine and resolve their inherent power dynamics, actively fostering a culture of humanized care marked by unconditional respect for patient autonomy and the consistent implementation of shared decision-making practices.

The incidence of OV was notably greater in secondary hospitals (37.9%) than in tertiary hospitals (10.6%) in Shanghai (p < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression analysis further indicated that care received at a secondary institution was independently associated with OV. Moreover, the reported severity among women experiencing OV was significantly higher in secondary settings than in tertiary settings. The domains of physical violence and neglect or discrimination showed distinct differences in incidence and severity (p < 0.05). This strong association between healthcare facility tier and OV occurrence aligns with findings from other healthcare contexts, including a cross-sectional study in Ethiopia [47], indicating a potential universal dynamic related to resource stratification. Notably, the data also revealed a gradient in care experience: women delivering in tertiary institutions reported significantly higher scores for both decision-making autonomy (MADM) and perceived respect (MOR Index) than those in secondary hospitals (p < 0.001 for both). In the Chinese context, these disparities are probably closely linked with long-standing structural imbalances within the tiered healthcare system [48]. In China’s three-tiered system, tertiary hospitals have better human, material, and financial resources. This resource base is associated with more robust training programs and stringent, protocol-driven quality control measures [49]. This confluence of resources and systems in tertiary hospitals promotes their capacity to more consistently maintain clinical safety, systematically address humanistic service delivery, and support an environment characterized by respectful care and shared decision-making. In contrast, secondary institutions are often limited by constrained resources and less standardized quality assurance systems. Staff members in secondary institutions often undergo shorter, less comprehensive training programs and have fewer opportunities for continuous professional development. These conditions may be associated with lower operational proficiency, higher reported rates of occupational burnout [50], and a work environment characterized by elevated stress and diminished supportive capacity.

This observed disparity in care quality across hospital tiers possibly reflects structural violence—harm arising from social, economic, and political institutions and disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations [51]. In this framework, the institutional gradient in OV prevalence highlights critical deficiencies in the capacity of China’s tiered healthcare system to translate resources into equitable, high-quality outcomes. Addressing these systemic factors necessitates a comprehensive approach involving the following strategies: redirecting investments to rectify grassroots resource imbalance; implementing national, evidence-based standardized maternity care guidelines to establish universal benchmarks for RMC; exploring sustained training partnerships between tertiary and secondary institutions to bridge competency gaps; and developing cross-institutional quality collaborative networks that prioritize real-time data sharing and systematic dissemination of best practices.

In light of these findings, future initiatives may benefit from embracing evidence-based interventions across interconnected levels, explicitly aligned with the four-level theoretical framework for promoting respectful maternity care [52]: (1) maternal level: empowering women through targeted education on their rights and shared decision-making; (2) staff level: facilitating professional development through competency-based training in communication, implicit bias recognition, and adherence to medical ethics; (3) system level: refining performance evaluation to include metrics of care experience, autonomy, and rights while simultaneously addressing structural deficiencies; and (4) societal level: promoting public awareness campaigns to address childbirth-related stigma and reinforce norms upholding maternal health rights. This multilevel approach offers a coherent structure for translating principles into actionable policy and practice. The implementation of a comprehensive, multilevel strategy could effectively address the complex challenges identified in this study. This approach could protect women from physical and psychological harm, support their fundamental rights during childbirth, and improve the quality and equity of maternity care. Ultimately, this initiative may serve as a potential pathway for reducing and, ideally, eliminating OV within clinical practice.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. (1) Convenience sampling may lead to selection bias, as participants who agreed to participate might differ in characteristics from those who declined, potentially restricting the sample’s representativeness. (2) The reliance on maternal self-reports raises the possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias. Data were collected at 42 days postpartum, corresponding to the standard timeframe for routine postnatal examination in China, which facilitated participant access and data collection efficiency; moreover, within this timeframe, the participating women had experienced the full trajectory from antenatal care through the end of the puerperium, potentially yielding more representative reports of OV. However, this interval may have impacted the accuracy of women’s recollections of their childbirth experiences, thereby introducing potential recall bias. (3) Face-to-face interviews in hospital settings may have restricted the sharing of adverse experiences due to power dynamics and cultural norms of respect toward healthcare providers, potentially leading to underreporting of negative experiences. (4) This research was conducted in a healthcare system located in Shanghai. While this setting provides insights into a developed urban context, the findings may not be generalizable to regions with differing socioeconomic profiles, healthcare resources, or cultural norms, warranting caution in extrapolation.

It is important to acknowledge that our criterion of “any item > 3” is relatively lenient and may not precisely reflect the prevalence of OV. Currently, there is no gold standard for measuring OV, and the threshold-setting methods remain in the exploratory phase. Future research could consider incorporating behavioral frequency, severity ratings, or latent class analysis to enhance comparability and interpretability in clinical settings.

Conclusion

This study posits that OV could be conceptualized as a systemic and inequitably distributed challenge in the domains of public health and human rights, even within a leading metropolitan hub such as Shanghai. The findings highlight the potential value of a multipronged intervention strategy that simultaneously targets the individual (maternal), provider, health system, and societal levels. Efforts to improve care quality and provider communication may be strengthened by strictly adhering to the principles of health equity to extend benefits to all population segments. A part of these initiatives is protecting vulnerable groups’ rights and fostering collaborative decision-making, which are the core tenets of RMC. The evidence and multilevel framework presented here could provide valuable insights for developing context-adapted strategies to address OV, potentially contributing to the advancement of respectful care both within China and on the global health stage.
