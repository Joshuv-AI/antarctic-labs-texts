Ageing effects on chemical, physical, mechanical, and morphological properties of clear aligners - a systematic review
Authors: Nabeel Almotairy, Fahad Aleisa, Abdulrahman K Alshammari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClear aligner (CA) therapy has experienced rapid use over the past two decades to treat orthodontic malocclusions. However, evidence on CA material degradation in the oral environment remains limited and often focuses on single brands or isolated material properties.ObjectivesTo investigate CA ageing characteristics across different materials and brands and evaluate the chemical, physical, mechanical, and morphological changes following simulated or intraoral ageing.Search methodsFive databases (PubMed, Web of Science, MEDLINE [Ovid], ProQuest, and Scopus) were searched to 18 March 2026, with no restrictions.Eligibility criteriaStudies assessing CA properties after intraoral use or simulated ageing (thermocycling, cyclic loading, or liquid immersion) were included.Data collection and analysisStudy selection followed PRISMA 2020. RoB was assessed using QUIN for purely in vitro studies, JBI for cohort in vivo studies, and Cochrane RoB 2 for RCTs. Results were synthesised narratively and organised by property domain, as substantial methodological heterogeneity precluded formal meta-analysis. Where protocols were comparable, a simple pooled weighted mean was calculated and presented graphically.ResultsNinety-five studies were included. RoB was low in eight studies, moderate in sixty-two, and high in twenty-five. Chemical composition remained largely stable during ageing, though some brands showed trace elemental release. Physical, mechanical, and morphological properties showed material-dependent deterioration. Pooled discolouration was greatest with coffee (weighted mean ΔE = 70.9), versus tea (ΔE = 18.4) and red wine (ΔE = 11.5), with Invisalign® consistently exceeding the clinically perceptible threshold. Force decay of 40–90% typically occurred within 48 h. Thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU)-based and directly printed aligners (DPAs) generally showed greater susceptibility than polyethylene terephthalate glycol-modified (PETG)-based aligners, though findings on hardness, roughness, and stiffness were inconsistent.ConclusionsCA materials undergo clinically relevant degradation during use, particularly in TPU-based and DPAs aligners. Clinicians may need to prioritise material-specific protocols, reinforce dietary and cleaning instructions, and consider force decay when determining aligner replacement intervals. Introduction

The introduction of clear aligner (CA) therapy in 1999, pioneered by Align Technology’s Invisalign system, represented a significant advancement in orthodontic treatment [1]. This treatment modality offered patients an alternative to conventional fixed appliances, eliminating the need for metal brackets and wires. CA have since achieved widespread clinical adoption, with patient preference largely attributed to their superior aesthetics, enhanced patient comfort, and removability compared to conventional fixed appliances [2–7]. Once reserved for simple malocclusions, CA now encompass a broader range of orthodontic applications, including complex cases that were traditionally treated with fixed appliances [8].

CA are primarily manufactured from thermoplastic polymers, including polyethylene terephthalate glycol-modified (PETG), polyurethane (PU), polypropylene (PP), and other proprietary formulations [9]. Other types of aligners are made from directly printed resins [9]. CA materials exhibit viscoelastic behaviour, enabling them to apply controlled, continuous forces while maintaining adequate flexibility for comfortable wear [10, 11]. The mechanical properties of these aligners, including elastic modulus, stress relaxation resistance, and dimensional stability, are critical determinants of treatment efficacy and predicted tooth movement [12].

CA is continuously exposed to the complex intraoral environment during treatment. The oral cavity subjects these materials to various biological and mechanical factors, including saliva, oral microbiota, dietary substances, thermal fluctuations from cold to hot food and beverages, and functional forces associated with speech, deglutition, and parafunctional habits [9, 13–19]. Additionally, variations in manufacturing/post-production processes collectively contribute to material degradation over time [20, 21]. Such degradation may manifest as alterations in physical properties, chemical composition, mechanical integrity, and surface morphology. These changes may affect the consistency of force delivery and patient acceptability and may therefore influence the predictability of clear aligner therapy. However, the extent to which ageing-induced material changes translate into clinically meaningful differences in tooth movement remains uncertain. Therefore, understanding these changes is important for interpreting material performance, informing aligner material selection and replacement strategies, and identifying priorities for clinically relevant research.

Two previous systematic reviews have addressed aspects of CA ageing, although with specific limitations. Bakdach et al. focused exclusively on a single brand (LD30 Invisalign®) following intraoral wear, excluding studies on simulated ageing protocols and physical property evaluation [13]. Srinivasan et al. evaluated the physical and mechanical properties of CA. However, they did not assess chemical or morphological alterations and included studies on non-aged aligners in their as-received state [22]. These limitations necessitate a comprehensive investigation across multiple materials, brands, and ageing methodologies. This systematic review therefore aimed to: (1) compare ageing characteristics across different CA material types and commercial brands, and (2) systematically evaluate the physical, chemical, mechanical, and morphological changes following exposure to simulated or intraoral conditions.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol for the current review was a priori registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251110248), and the study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [23].

Eligibility criteria

This review followed the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study (PICOS) framework. The population consisted of plastics used to fabricate CA appliances (different brands and material types). The intervention involved intraoral/simulated ageing (in vitro, in vivo, or ex vivo) using thermocycling, stress, immersion, artificial saliva simulation, surface treatment, cyclic loading, etc. The comparisons included different CA brands and material types. The outcomes were categorised into four domains (chemical, physical, mechanical, and morphological). Chemical outcomes included ageing-related changes in material composition or chemical characteristics. Physical outcomes included changes in optical properties (e.g., transparency, transmittance, absorbance, and colour), water sorption, thermal properties, and thickness changes. Mechanical outcomes included changes in elastic modulus, tensile or flexural strength, yield strength, hardness, stiffness, stress relaxation, force decay, and force retention. Morphological outcomes included changes in surface topography and structural features, including surface roughness, cracks, scratches, porosities, irregularities, and peeling. When an outcome could reasonably be classified into more than one domain, it was assigned based on its primary measured characteristic. The study contexts were in vivo, in vitro, and ex vivo studies on CA property changes after the ageing procedure.

Information sources and search

Five electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, MEDLINE (Ovid), ProQuest, and Scopus) were systematically searched from inception to 18 March 2026. Search strategies were developed using a combination of free-text keywords and MeSH terms, linked with proximity operators (Table 1). No limits were applied to the database searches, including language, publication date, or publication type. Grey literature sources and clinical trial registries were not searched. However, we searched the backward and forward citations for all eligible studies to identify further relevant literature.

Database search strategy

Selection process

Search results were exported to Mendeley. Duplicate records were removed using the software’s built-in duplicate removal feature and manually confirmed for accuracy. The study screening process was performed in two stages: (1) Title and abstract screening to exclude irrelevant studies. (2) Full-text screening of remaining records to determine eligibility. Two reviewers (A.K.A. and F.A.) conducted both stages independently, and resolved disagreements through consensus and/or discussion with a third reviewer (N.A.).

Data extraction process and items

The two reviewers (A.K.A. and F.A.) carried out data extraction in duplicate using a customised extraction form developed specifically for this review’s objectives. Prior to data extraction, both reviewers were trained and piloted the form on five included studies to ensure consistency and clarity. The following information was extracted: (1) Study identification (first author, publication year, design, and country/-ies); (2) Ageing test type; (3) Used CA brands; (4) Polymer-based composition; (5) CA groups and subgroups; (6) Experimental tests; (7) Outcome variables, and (8) Main results. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through mutual consensus and/or discussion with the third author.

Synthesis method

The results of individual studies were organised in a comprehensive table. Given the considerable methodological heterogeneity across the included studies, formal meta-analysis across the full body of evidence was not feasible. Therefore, the results were synthesised descriptively and organised by property domain (chemical, physical, mechanical, and morphological). Where studies used comparable methodologies, we calculated a simple pooled estimate and presented it graphically. Consequently, seven studies with comparable methodologies that investigated CA discolouration (ΔE changes) after 7-day immersion in coffee, tea, or red wine at 37 °C were pooled [24–30]. Because some studies did not report the standard deviations of ΔE, a weighted mean was calculated as Σ(ni × ΔEi) / Σni, where ni is the sample size, and ΔEi is the colour change for each study arm. Because no meta-analysis was performed, quantitative assessment of publication bias was not feasible. Additionally, we did not formally assess selective outcome reporting within individual studies or the possibility of missing studies beyond the systematic database search described above.

Risk of bias assessment

We assessed the risk of bias (RoB) of the included studies according to study design. We evaluated studies with a pure in vitro design using the Quality Assessment Tool for In Vitro Studies (QUIN Tool) [31]. The QUIN tool consists of 12 key items. It offers scoring and grading options: two points were given for each adequately addressed question, one point for each inadequately addressed question, and zero points for questions that were not specified. We assessed the RoB of non-randomised cohort studies with intraoral ageing and subsequent ex vivo experimentation using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Cohort Studies (JBI). This JBI tool comprises 11 items, with each item rated as ‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘Unclear’, or ‘Not applicable’. For quantitative synthesis of the appraisal results, ‘Yes’ responses were scored as 1 and ‘No’ or ‘Unclear’ responses as 0, while ‘Not applicable’ responses were excluded from the denominator. The final cumulative score of each study for both the QUIN and JBI tools was then converted into a percentage of the maximum possible score. Before conducting the risk-of-bias assessment, the authors predefined percentage-score thresholds for categorising studies as low, moderate, or high risk of bias. Based on the final percentage scores, studies were classified as having low (79–100%), moderate (51–78%), or high (≤ 50%) RoB. However, the RoB of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with in vivo ageing and subsequent ex vivo laboratory assessment was evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. This tool assesses five domains, namely bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result. Using the tool’s algorithm, each domain was judged as having low, some concerns (moderate), or high RoB. For crossover randomised trials, we applied the crossover-trial variant of the RoB 2 tool. Two reviewers conducted the RoB assessment independently, and resolved disagreements through consensus or discussion with the third reviewer.

Results

Study selection

The database search returned 2471 studies (1075 after deduplication). The title/abstract screening phase excluded 940 studies, leaving 135 potentially eligible studies. The full-text screening phase excluded 40 studies for various reasons (Supplementary file 1), yielding a final list of 95 eligible studies (Fig. 1). Inter-reviewer agreement during study selection and data extraction was ≥ 0.81 (Cohen’s kappa coefficient), indicating good-to-excellent concordance.

Table 3 presents the ageing methodologies used in the included studies. Table 4 summarises the methodological subgroups of the ageing methods. Concerning the CA ageing protocol, fifty studies simulated chemical and hydrothermal exposure of CA through immersion in various solutions (artificial saliva, distilled water, cola, juice, red wine, coffee, tea, nicotine, staining food additives, and cleaning solutions) [10, 19–21, 24–30, 32, 33, 35, 37, 48, 49, 51, 53, 54, 56, 64, 65, 67, 69, 71, 73, 80, 81, 84–89, 91–95, 99, 100, 102, 103, 105–110]. In contrast to static immersion, several studies utilised thermocycling to introduce thermal fatigue by alternating between cold and hot temperatures [11, 14, 19, 36, 37, 42, 50, 57, 61, 75, 101], with cycles often calibrated to simulate specific clinical durations. To replicate intraoral mechanical forces, a third group of studies applied static or cyclic loads, including stress relaxation tests under constant deflection [14, 18, 34, 50, 55, 56, 62, 63, 70, 77, 80, 82, 99] and cyclic compression simulating masticatory forces [18, 40, 41, 46, 47, 50, 60, 76, 77, 83]. While three studies employed both thermocycling and in vitro immersion [19, 37, 42], one used both in vitro immersion and a cyclic loading protocol [94], three applied static or cyclic loads with thermocycling [11, 50, 61], and one applied in vitro static loads on intraorally aged CA [52]. Finally, 24 studies employed direct intraoral ageing, in which patients wore aligners for defined periods, typically 7 or 14 days [15, 17, 38, 39, 43–45, 51–53, 58, 59, 63, 66, 68, 72, 74, 78, 79, 90, 96–98, 104].

The included studies used different commercial brands and polymeric compositions (Table 2). The majority utilised PETG-based aligners, including brands such as Duran®, Erkodur®, Essix A+®, CA Clear Aligner®, Clear Cap®, CA-medium®, K line®, and Smartee® [10, 18–21, 24–29, 32–34, 36, 39–42, 44, 46–49, 52, 54–58, 60–62, 64, 67–70, 75–78, 80, 82, 83, 87, 88, 92, 96, 97, 101, 104–108, 110]. A second major material class comprised thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) aligners, including commercial systems like F22 Evoflex®, Zendura®, Spark®, and Invisalign® [10, 14, 15, 17, 20, 21, 24–28, 33–35, 37, 38, 40–42, 48, 53–55, 59, 61, 63, 65, 66, 69, 71–76, 79–82, 84–86, 89, 90, 95–97, 99–101, 103, 105, 106]. A smaller subset explored polycarbonate (PC) and PP based CA, such as Angel aligner® [28, 34, 56, 69, 75, 82]. Several studies investigated PU or copolyester blends, including multilayer configurations such as poly(ester-urethane) or polycarbonate-urethane hybrids [10, 17, 24, 29, 30, 34, 43, 47, 53, 54, 56, 62, 67, 68, 73, 82, 101, 103, 108]. In contrast, methacrylate-based photopolymer resins, primarily 3D-printed materials (DPAs) such as Dental LT Clear® or Tera Harz TC-85®, were investigated in twenty-six studies [20, 30, 40, 45, 49–51, 54, 55, 64, 74, 75, 77, 78, 89, 91–94, 96–99, 102, 103, 109].

Ageing effects on CA properties

Chemical properties

The chemical structure of most aligner materials, particularly PETG and PU, remains markedly stable during ageing, with Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy revealing no significant molecular degradation across eleven studies [28, 30, 32, 39, 58, 73, 87, 92, 98, 101, 105]. Only one study evaluated chemical degradation after 7 days of coffee or cola exposure, reporting greater chemical degradation in aligners with TPU outer layers (Invisalign®) compared to aligners with PETG outer layers (ClearCorrect®) [30]. Stable chemical composition post-ageing was otherwise consistently reported for PC-, PET-, and PU-based aligners [15, 17, 28, 38, 43, 63, 66, 73, 86, 101, 105]. Despite this chemical stability, trace element release has been reported, including mercury from Eon® aligners after two-week immersion in artificial saliva at 37 °C [35], aluminium and silicon from Invisalign® after two-week intraoral use [63], and multiple elements (Al, Si, Cu, Zn, Mn, Fe, Mg) from three-layered PETG-TPU-PETG (ClearCorrect®) aligners [43].

Physical properties

Water sorption

A critical ageing-related phenomenon is water sorption. Thermoformed aligners absorb significantly more water than DPAs but desorb faster [103]. Among the materials, TPU-based aligners (Zendura®) absorb more water than PETG [25, 26], and thermocycled PETG aligners absorb more than non-thermocycled ones [19]. Clinically, 14-day use of Invisalign® aligners increases water absorption [15], though one study found no changes in PETG-based aligners after 10-day intraoral use [39]. Material composition further influences sorption, as Invisalign® (SmartTrack™) aligners are more hydrophilic than Exceed30 Invisalign® aligners [53], while modified PETG with higher modulus of elasticity and abrasion resistance may reduce water adsorption [110]. Multilayered CA also absorbed more water than single-layered CA after immersion in hot coffee [32].

Translucency and absorbance

Immersion in water or artificial saliva, with or without food colouring, did not affect the translucency or transparency of TPU- or PETG-based CAs [25, 26, 49, 54, 71, 81, 85, 105]. Similarly, 14-day oral use did not affect absorbance or transmittance in PETG (Dentcare® and Smile aligners®) or PETG/TPU combination (Clearbite®) aligners [68], or in TPU (Zendura® Flx) aligners [59]. In contrast, 14-day intraoral use significantly reduced transparency in TPU-based aligners with inner or outer PETG layers (Invisalign® and ClearCorrect®, respectively) [15, 43], while PETG-based (Taglus®) and DPAs (Graphy®) showed reduced transmittance and increased absorbance, with a greater effect in DPAs [78].

Immersion in red wine or coffee reduced transparency in both TPU- and PETG-based CAs, with greater effects in TPU-based CAs, whereas nicotine did not produce a significant effect [25, 26]. Similarly, smoking and alcohol exposure significantly reduced translucency over 7 days, particularly in TPU-based CAs, with smoking producing the greater reduction [84]. Among DPAs, immersion in turmeric, coffee, or tea at 37 °C reduced surface transparency, with turmeric producing the greatest effect [49, 94]. After 4-week immersion in artificial saliva at 37 °C, DPAs (Nexdent®) also exhibited lower transparency than PETG-based aligners (Essix ACE®, CA Pro®, and Duran+®), although cleaning partially mitigated the reduction [64]. The effect of ageing on TPU-based CA transparency also appeared to be brand-specific, as F22® showed higher transparency than Invisalign® after two 14-day immersion cycles in artificial saliva containing food colouring at 37 °C [81].

Colour changes

TPU-, PET-, and PETG-based aligners and DPAs all exhibited discolouration following immersion in staining solutions, including coffee, black tea, red wine, and colouring spices [24–30, 33, 67, 69, 73, 86, 88, 92, 95, 108, 109]. Discolouration was generally more pronounced in DPAs than in thermoformed aligners [30, 49, 64, 92]. Among DPAs, colour stability was strongly influenced by printing parameters, with a 50 μm layer thickness combined with a 90° build orientation producing the greatest colour stability [109]. Thermoformed aligners made entirely of TPU or with an outer TPU layer also showed greater discolouration than those made of PET or PETG [24, 26, 28, 30, 67, 69, 73, 95]. While one study found no colour-change differences between PETG- and TPU-based aligners after staining immersion [33], another found greater colour changes in PET than in PETG aligners, particularly after 14-day immersion in coffee or red wine [27, 29]. Two-week intraoral use also produced significant colour changes in PETG-based aligners with an inner TPU layer (ClearCorrect®; ΔE = 4.04; 3.72 NBS units) [15], whereas another study found no significant difference in colour change between DPAs and PETG-based aligners (ΔE < 3.7) [78].

The magnitude of discolouration in TPU-based aligners appeared to be brand-specific, with Spark® showing significantly less colour change than Invisalign® following immersion in coffee at 37 °C for 12 h and 7 days [27]. Cleaning and disinfecting agents also influenced colour stability. Sodium hypochlorite, sodium bicarbonate, and chlorhexidine gluconate produced noticeable colour changes in Invisalign® aligners after 14 days of immersion [100], while Invisalign® cleaning crystals and Signal Whitening Toothpaste produced the greatest colour changes among the cleaning agents evaluated; Efferdent Denture Cleanser and colourant-free clear soap produced the least [72]. Conversely, Invisalign® cleaning crystals and Retainer Brite® tablets used with a Cordless Sonic Cleaner effectively removed tea stains, reducing ΔE by > 90%. However, coffee and red wine stains persisted in aligners with an inner PETG layer (Invisalign®). In contrast, aligners made entirely of PETG or with an outer PETG layer showed different residual colour changes (ΔE = 5–29.83) [24]. Similarly, Invisalign® Aligner Cleaning Foam and M3 Naturals™ Retainer Cleaner reduced staining from various solutions, although coffee and black tea stains persisted after cleaning [33].

Thickness changes

Thickness changes following intraoral use were inconsistent. One study reported divergent changes after 14 days of wear in two PETG-based aligners, with Erkodur® showing a decrease and Shinseki® an increase in thickness [58]. In contrast, two studies found no significant thickness changes in either DPAs or thermoformed TPU- or PETG-based aligners after 7–10 days of intraoral use [44, 97]. Similarly, immersion in artificial saliva at 37 °C for 7 or 14 days did not produce significant thickness changes in TPU- or PETG-based aligners [105].

Thermal properties and energy absorption

TPU-based aligners exhibited higher glass transition temperatures (Tg > 95 °C) than PETG-based aligners (80–89 °C) [25, 26, 43]. Intraoral use did not alter the Tg of TPU-based aligners with an outer PETG layer [43]. In contrast, PETG-based materials showed inconsistent changes in Tg following ageing, with a decrease observed in Erkodur® and an increase in Shinseki® [58]. Aligner thickness also influenced thermal behaviour, as 1-mm Duran® aligners demonstrated greater thermal stability than 0.8-mm Erkodur® aligners, evidenced by a higher post-ageing elastic modulus and lower viscous modulus and loss factor (tan δ) [57]. DPAs exhibited a significantly lower Tg (69.45 °C) than PETG-based aligners (101.8 °C), indicating an earlier transition to the viscoelastic state and potentially greater molecular mobility and lower thermal stability [77]. Additionally, Invisalign® (SmartTrack™) showed greater energy absorption after cyclic loading than PETG-based materials, suggesting greater susceptibility to localised failure due to energy concentration [40, 41].

Mechanical properties

Viscoelastic behaviour

Several studies reported that intraoral use or artificial ageing in aqueous environments at 37 °C reduced elastic modulus (E), ultimate tensile strength (UTS), and yield stress in PETG-, PU-, and DPA-based aligners [15, 19, 21, 91, 99, 104, 107]. In contrast, PET-based aligners (Biolon®) showed minimal changes [21]. However, other studies found no significant changes in E or tensile strength after 10–14 days of intraoral use [38, 39, 63], thermocycling [37, 75], or 2–3 weeks of immersion in distilled water at 37 °C [71, 85]. Elevated temperatures also reduced UTS and yield strength in both PETG- and DPA-based aligners [19, 50]. Similarly, 24-hour immersion in artificial saliva at 37 °C significantly decreased yield strength while increasing yield strain and elongation at break in TPU-, PETG-, and polycyclohexylenedimethylene terephthalate glycol (PCTG)-based aligners [80]. Immersion in coffee at 57 °C also significantly reduced E in Invisalign® aligners [37]. Comparatively, TPU-based aligners (Zendura® [25, 76] and Maxflex® [80]) exhibited higher tensile strength, E, and maximum stress than PETG-based (Duran® and Erkodur®) [25, 76, 80], PCTG-based [80], and DPA-based aligners [89]. In contrast, PETG-based CAs demonstrated higher E and yield strength than DPAs (Tera Harz TC-85®) [77]. Cleaning methods did not affect E [38], whereas greater material thickness was associated with higher strength in DPA-based aligners [94].

Stress relaxation was commonly characterised by a rapid initial force decline during the first few hours, followed by a plateau [34, 70, 82]. This relaxation was accelerated under warm and/or wet conditions [18, 55, 56, 62, 80] and varied substantially among materials. F22 Evoflex®, Essix ACE®, Erkodur®, Masel®, Noxi®, and Fusion Align® exhibited slower relaxation and greater force retention [34, 55, 56, 62, 80], whereas Duran®, Durasoft®, and DPAs, including Tera Harz TC-85®, Material X®, OD-Clear TF®, and Maxflex®, showed greater force decay [34, 55, 77, 80, 82, 99]. Creep, defined as progressive deformation under constant load, was significantly affected by thermocycling and mechanical loading, with ageing often shortening the clinically useful plateau phase of the creep curve [14]. However, one study reported no significant changes in stress relaxation or creep after 2 weeks of intraoral use [63]. In contrast to PETG-based CAs, which showed no shape recovery, DPA resins such as Tera Harz TC-85® exhibited accelerated shape recovery at 37 °C, consistent with their viscoelastic behaviour [50, 77].

Indentation-based properties

Studies assessing hardness using Vickers, Martens, or Shore D methods reported inconsistent findings. Several studies found no significant hardness changes after 10–14 days of immersion in distilled water or artificial saliva at 37 °C in PU-based aligners [35, 51, 71], after 10–14 days of intraoral use of PETG- and/or PU-based aligners [45, 51, 58, 59, 96, 104], or after thermocycling following 24-hour immersion in distilled water at 37 °C in PETG-based aligners [57]. Conversely, other studies reported significant hardness reductions following intraoral use or simulated ageing [15, 36, 37, 66, 85, 90, 93]. These reductions included an 8–34% decrease in hardness in Invisalign® aligners after 14 days of clinical use [15, 66, 90] or 3-week immersion in distilled water at 37 °C [85], as well as a significant reduction in Erkodur® but not Duran® after thermocycling [36]. Similarly, 14-day immersion in distilled water resulted in a greater hardness reduction in DLP-based DPAs (V Print Splint Comfort® and TC-85 DAC®) than in SLA-based DPAs (Dental LT Clear V2®) or PETG-based aligners (Biolon®) [93]. Invisalign® also showed a significant hardness reduction after thermocycling with tea and coffee exposure at 57 °C [37]. Conversely, one study reported increased hardness following intraoral ageing of Invisalign® aligners [17].

Findings for indentation modulus were similarly inconsistent. Two studies reported an 11–40% reduction after 14 days of clinical use of Invisalign® aligners [66, 90], whereas two others found no significant changes [45, 96]. Cleaning procedures also produced divergent effects, where brushing, with or without toothpaste, increased hardness, whereas cleaning tablets (Corega®) decreased it [102]. In addition, using CAs as bleaching trays for 2 weeks at 37 °C reduced hardness compared with immersion in distilled water [71].

Flexural and bending properties

Ageing generally reduced flexural strength and modulus across aligner materials. Three-week immersion in distilled water at 37 °C significantly reduced the flexural strength of Invisalign® aligners [85]. Similarly, thermocycling followed by 24-hour immersion in distilled water at 37 °C reduced flexural strength in both PETG- and PU-based aligners, although PETG-based aligners showed higher baseline values [42]. Zendura® (PU-based) consistently exhibited greater flexural strength and retention than Duran® (PETG-based) after cyclic loading [76]. Staining beverages also induced flexural strength decay in PET/PETG-based aligners; however, prolonged immersion led to water-absorption saturation, reducing differences between beverages, whereas PU-based aligners were unaffected [48]. In addition, 14-day immersion in water resulted in a significantly greater reduction in flexural strength in DLP-based DPAs (V Print Splint Comfort® and TC-85 DAC®) than in SLA-based DPAs (Dental LT Clear V2®) or PETG-based aligners (Biolon®) [93].

Material stiffness

Cyclic loading tests generally showed cyclic hardening, with stiffness increasing during the initial loading cycles before stabilising in PETG- [18, 46, 47] and PU-based aligners [47]. However, one study reported a 16–28% decrease in stiffness in PETG-based CAs (Duran®) following cyclic loading [60]. Material comparisons also demonstrated consistent differences in intrinsic stiffness, where PETG-based CAs (Accusmile®) exhibited higher stiffness than Invisalign® and Angel® [40, 75]. In contrast, single-layer materials (F22®, Duran®, and Duran+®) generally showed higher stiffness than multilayer materials (Erkoloc-Pro®, Durasoft®, CA-medium®, and Zendura® FLX) [34, 82, 101]. Thermocycling significantly increased stiffness in Duran+® (PETG-based) but not in PU-based aligners (Zendura® A) or multilayer aligners with outer PETG and inner PU layers (CA-medium® and Zendura® FLX) [101]. Similarly, thermocycling following 24-hour immersion in distilled water at 37 °C had minimal effect on the stiffness of a PETG-based aligner [57]. In contrast, 14-day immersion in distilled water significantly reduced stiffness in DPAs, whereas stiffness remained relatively stable in TPU-based aligners [89].

Force stability and decay

Ageing generally caused substantial force decay within the first 48 h, with reductions ranging from 40% to 90% depending on force type [10, 11, 60, 61], and exceeding 75% for some force/torque measurements [61]. Force decay was also influenced by aligner thickness, with thicker CAs (0.75 mm) retaining forces better than thinner aligners (0.4–0.625 mm) [60]. Although single-layer CAs generated higher forces before ageing than multilayer CAs, they also exhibited greater force decay after ageing [10, 11]. Following liquid immersion and cyclic loading, DPAs (Tera Harz TC-85®) showed minimal force decay after 50 cycles (0.4%), compared with substantially greater decay in Invisalign® (42.1%) [20]. Conversely, another study found that Invisalign® aligners generated lower initial forces but exhibited better force retention than other PU- and PETG-based CAs [11].

Fifteen-day immersion in artificial saliva at 37 °C increased disinsertion forces by approximately 56% and accelerated volumetric changes, particularly in PETG-based aligners (QuickSmile®) compared with Invisalign®, with severe crowding amplifying both effects and altering deformation timing [106]. Modified PETG aligners (Smartee®) also maintained force magnitude more effectively than conventional PETG aligners after 14 days of saliva immersion [110]. Beverage exposure, particularly to soy sauce and orange juice, resulted in significant force degradation during cyclic testing of Tera Harz TC-85®, CA Pro®, and Invisalign® aligners [20].

Morphological properties

Structural integrity

Modified PETG-based aligners exhibited less surface peeling than conventional PETG aligners after 2 weeks of immersion in artificial saliva at 37 °C [110]. At elevated temperatures (55–70 °C), PETG-based aligners underwent deformation [25, 32], with greater effects observed in single-layer than multilayer CAs [32], while TPU-based aligners developed bending and surface changes [25]. Surface morphology was also brand-specific, with Invisalign® showing the smoothest surface after 2-week immersion in artificial saliva at 37 °C compared to other brands, which exhibited irregularities and porosity [35]. After 12 h of in vitro ageing, SmartTrack™ Invisalign® aligners appeared more homogeneous than Exceed30 Invisalign® aligners, although both exhibited cracks; SmartTrack™ showed predominantly parallel cracks, whereas Exceed30 exhibited irregular porosity [53].

Fourteen-day thermocycling produced significant surface scratches, porosities, and cracks in PC- and PETG-based aligners, while DPAs (Tera Harz TC-85®) exhibited heterogeneous surfaces with cracks [75]. DPAs also showed pronounced surface irregularities [51], while immersion in colouring solutions, particularly curcumin, caused surface degradation and peeling, with minimal effects observed in PETG-based aligners (Erkodur®) [92]. Cyclic loading of PETG-based aligners resulted in surface shear bands, cracks, or voids [40, 41, 46, 70], as well as arch-form deformation [83], with lesser effects in thicker aligners (0.88 mm versus 0.75 mm) [46, 83]. The effects of cyclic loading were generally similar across polymer compositions [41, 47], although Ghost Aligner® (TPU-based) exhibited greater surface damage than PETG- and other TPU-based aligners [40, 41].

Intraoral use of thermoformed or 3D-printed PU-based aligners resulted in surface voids, delamination, microcracks, and distortion [15, 63], as well as localised surface wear [98] and changes in aligner adaptation [79]. Intraoral use also increased dimensional deformation in PETG- and TPU-based aligners but not in PET-based aligners [52]. Similarly, simulated ageing accelerated volumetric changes, with PETG showing 132% greater swelling than TPU [106].

Surface roughness

TPU-based aligners (Invisalign®) exhibited greater surface roughness than PC (Angelaalign®) and PETG (Smartee®) aligners after 7-day immersion in staining solutions [28]. Similarly, TPU-based aligners (F22 Aligner®) were rougher than PETG-based aligners (Erkodur®) after 7 or 14 days of immersion in artificial saliva at 37 °C [105]. Thermocycling significantly increased surface roughness in TPU-based aligners (Zendura®), but not in PETG-based aligners (Duran®) [42]; however, another study found no significant changes in roughness in either material following thermocycling [101]. Surface roughness also remained unchanged in Invisalign® after 2 weeks of carbamide peroxide immersion [71], 3 weeks of distilled-water immersion [85], or 4 weeks of artificial-saliva immersion [65], although cigarette-smoke exposure increased roughness [65]. Invisalign® also exhibited greater inner-surface roughness than PETG- or PET-based aligners after 12-hour immersion in coffee, tea, cola, and artificial saliva [73]. In contrast, 45-day immersion in artificial saliva increased surface roughness in both DPAs and thermoformed PETG-based aligners [49].

Following intraoral use, aligners with an inner PU layer (ClearCorrect®) showed increased surface roughness after 14 days [43]. Similarly, 7–14 days of intraoral use increased roughness in DPAs (Tera Harz TC-85®), whereas roughness remained relatively stable in Invisalign® [74]. Conversely, one study found no significant changes in roughness with Invisalign® after 14 days of use [15], whereas another found a significant, yet similar decrease at both 7 and 14 days [90].

Cleaning methods also affected CA surface roughness, although findings were inconsistent across studies [38, 72, 102]. Two studies reported that whitening toothpaste produced the lowest surface roughness in Invisalign®, whereas Invisalign® cleaning crystals produced the highest [38, 72]. Conversely, another study found that DPA (Clear A®) surfaces exhibited the greatest roughness after brushing with toothpaste, followed by brushing without toothpaste, while immersion in dissolved denture-cleaning tablets (Corega®) produced the lowest roughness [102].

Crystallinity

PETG-based aligners exhibited lower crystallinity than TPU-based aligners following immersion in staining agents or artificial saliva [25, 26]. However, another study reported no significant change in crystallinity in PETG-based aligners after 2 weeks of intraoral exposure [58] or after immersion in hot or cold beverages [32]. SmartTrack™ Invisalign® aligners also exhibited lower crystallinity than Exceed30 Invisalign® aligners after approximately 14 days of clinical use [17].

RoB assessment

Supplementary file 2 presents the detailed RoB assessment of the included studies. The results showed that eight studies had low RoB, twenty-five had high RoB, and the remaining sixty-two had moderate RoB. Among purely in vitro studies, the most frequent limitations were lack of blinding of operators, outcome assessors, or data analysts (65 studies); absence of reported randomisation of sample preparation, testing order, or allocation, including inadequate reporting of sequence generation and allocation concealment (60 studies); lack of information on the number, training, calibration, and intra- or inter-operator reliability of personnel involved in sample preparation and measurements (52 studies); inadequate reporting of the number, training, calibration, and intra- or inter-assessor reliability of outcome assessors (52 studies); and failure to report a sample size calculation (46 studies).

For cohort in vivo studies, the main limitations were concerns regarding the validity and reliability of exposure measurement (20 studies), inadequate identification of potential confounding factors (17 studies), inadequate handling of confounding factors (20 studies), and insufficient reporting of sample recruitment and participant characteristics (13 studies). Among the two RCTs, the main concerns involved inadequate sample allocation and randomisation, insufficient blinding of participants and intervention assessors, and potential bias in the selection of reported results. Overall, these methodological limitations suggest that confidence in the pooled findings should be considered low to moderate. Rather than indicating a consistent direction of bias, these limitations are more likely to contribute to the substantial inter-study variability observed across ageing outcomes. Discussion

This systematic review synthesises evidence of the ageing effect on CA’s chemical, physical, mechanical, and morphological properties. The findings suggest that while the core chemical structures of most CA polymers remain stable under simulated and intraoral ageing, the physical, mechanical, and morphological properties appear to undergo clinically relevant degradation. The extent of ageing-related deterioration appears to be largely material-dependent, with TPU-based aligners and DPAs generally showing greater susceptibility than PETG-based counterparts in several, although not all, property domains.

Several included studies indicated the overall chemical stability of CA materials following ageing in aqueous environments and staining solutions [15, 17, 28, 30, 32, 38, 39, 43, 58, 63, 66, 73, 86, 87, 92, 98, 101, 105]. These results suggest that the bulk molecular architecture of thermoplastic aligner materials is largely resistant to hydrolytic or oxidative degradation under clinically relevant conditions. Furthermore, several studies have also documented the release of trace amounts of metallic and non-metallic ions into oral fluids [35, 43, 63]. Although the reported concentrations were generally below established toxicological thresholds, cumulative exposure over a full orthodontic treatment may warrant ongoing biocompatibility evaluation.

Water absorption appeared to be a critical ageing-related phenomenon, with TPU-based materials exhibiting higher water uptake than PETG-based aligners [25, 26], and several studies further indicating that ageing tended to increase water absorption [15, 19, 53, 110]. Exposure to the oral environment allows moisture to penetrate the aligner’s structure, which may induce physicochemical alterations leading to mechanical deterioration and swelling [106]. This ageing-related increase in water uptake may be attributable to hydrolytic cleavage of ester and urethane linkages within the polymer backbone, generating additional polar hydroxyl and carboxyl end-groups that enhance the matrix’s hydrophilicity. Consequently, repeated thermal and mechanical cycling during ageing may disrupt polymer chain packing, increasing free volume and microvoid formation and thereby creating additional pathways for water diffusion into the bulk material. Dimensional changes carry direct clinical implications, as each aligner is designed to deliver approximately 0.25–0.30 mm of tooth movement per wear cycle [111]. Any reduction in CA dimension may effectively displace space intended for tooth movement, reducing the efficiency of force delivery and potentially compromising treatment outcomes. Consequently, as reported previously [112], low water absorption is considered a key property of an optimal orthodontic CA.

Among the physical properties evaluated, discolouration was commonly reported as an outcome following ageing in staining solutions [15, 24–30, 33, 67, 73, 86, 108]. Immersion in water or artificial saliva generally shows no effect on surface translucency [25, 26, 54, 71, 81, 85, 105], yet CA discolouration was more pronounced with coffee and tea, especially in TPU-based and DPAs aligners [24, 26–28, 30, 67, 73]. The urethane linkages (-NHCOO-) in TPU-based aligners such as Invisalign® [66, 113, 114] are thought to confer inherent colour instability, as their polarity promotes interaction with hydrophilic pigments in dietary liquids [114], which may explain the discolouration and reduced translucency observed in this review. These findings have direct clinical implications, as aesthetic acceptability is a principal motivation for CA therapy [115, 116]. The effectiveness of cleaning protocols in reversing staining was limited, particularly for coffee [24, 33]. This finding suggests that even compliant cleaning regimens may be insufficient to fully restore the optical clarity after exposure to common dietary colourants. Significant discolouration of CA may negatively impact a patient’s psychological well-being, particularly their self-confidence in social settings, which could ultimately result in poor adherence to the prescribed treatment protocol, potentially extending the overall duration of treatment and increasing associated costs. Therefore, it may be advisable to instruct patients using CA to remove aligners, especially Invisalign® and DPAs, when consuming strongly pigmented foods and beverages to prevent discolouration.

The ageing-related mechanical degradation described in the reviewed studies may have direct clinical implications for treatment planning and aligner wear protocols. The observation of stress relaxation with ageing [18, 34, 55, 56, 62, 70, 82], and particularly the rapid force decay within the first 48 h [10, 11, 60, 61], may suggest that the effective therapeutic window of a single aligner tray may be considerably shorter than typical replacement intervals of one to two weeks. However, as none of the included studies directly measured tooth movement outcomes, this interpretation should be regarded as a hypothesis derived from the reviewed mechanical force-decay data rather than a firm conclusion, and it warrants confirmation through studies correlating in vitro force decay with clinical tooth movement. If substantiated, this would suggest that meaningful orthodontic force delivery is concentrated in the early days of wear, with subsequent days contributing comparatively less to tooth movement. Clinicians should therefore consider whether current wear schedules align with the actual force-delivery kinetics of these materials. Another observation was the disparity between material types, with greater mechanical degradation in DPAs than PETG- or TPU-based alternatives [10, 11, 20, 34, 55, 77, 82, 99]. This suggests that material selection may carry meaningful clinical weight.

Hardness and tensile properties of CA showed mixed responses to ageing. Several studies documented significant reductions [15, 19, 21, 36, 37, 45, 51, 58, 59, 66, 85, 90, 91, 96, 99, 104], while others did not [35, 37, 38, 45, 57, 58, 63, 71, 75, 85, 96, 104]. The clinical significance of hardness changes relates to resistance to deformation and wear under occlusal loading, but inconsistent findings prevent definitive clinical guidance. This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in ageing protocols, specimen geometry, testing conditions, and the specific materials evaluated. However, this review suggested that TPU-based Zendura® outperformed PETG-based Duran® and Erkodur® in tensile and flexural strength before and after ageing [25, 76]. These comparative findings are insufficient to establish generalisable superiority of one material class over another. They nonetheless suggest that material selection may influence post-ageing biomechanical performance, a hypothesis that warrants confirmation through studies under standardised conditions.

This review identified CA structural changes following ageing, including microcracks, surface voids, delamination, shear bands, and porosity [15, 40, 41, 46, 63, 70, 75]. Microcrack propagation and shear band formation may result in material failure, and their presence following ageing suggests that cumulative structural degradation may compromise force delivery and increase pigment penetration. From a biological standpoint, increased CA surface porosity and surface voiding with ageing may increase surface roughness [28, 42, 43, 73, 74, 90, 105] and raise concerns regarding bacterial biofilm accumulation [117, 118]. These factors may also facilitate microplastic leaching, which is a major health concern [41, 119, 120]. These findings suggest that material durability under physiologically representative conditions should be a central consideration in both product development and material selection.

As DPA technology continues to gain traction due to its shape-memory advantage [50, 77], understanding and mitigating its accelerated force decay will be essential to ensuring therapeutic outcomes are equivalent to established thermoformed aligner materials. Across the included studies, twenty-six examined DPAs specifically, and only half were comparative studies [30, 49, 64, 74, 77, 78, 89, 92–94, 97, 102, 109]. Their findings indicated that DPAs generally lost more transmittance/transparency than PETG after ageing [64, 78] and generally discoloured more than thermoformed aligners [30, 49, 64, 92]. However, print layer thickness and orientation may influence [109]. While no thickness changes were observed in DPAs compared to thermoformed aligners after ageing [97], increased DPA thickness may increase their strength [94]. Simulated ageing and intraoral wear increased DPA roughness more than thermoformed CA [74], and cleaning methods most affected DPA roughness with toothpaste brushing [102]. DPAs were also more flexible but less resistant to permanent deformation than thermoformed aligners [77, 89]. However, 3D printing methods (DLP versus SLA) may affect DPAs’ hardness/flexural strength [93]. DPAs also showed greater force decay [55, 77, 99], surface degradation/peeling [92], and stiffness reduction [89] than thermoformed CA. Nevertheless, the evidence base remains heterogeneous, and findings cannot yet define a consistent ageing profile for DPAs. Future research direction could be directed to several evidence gaps: (1) how resin chemistry, printing technology, layer thickness, build orientation, and post-curing interact with intraoral ageing; (2) whether laboratory force decay and shape-recovery behaviour translate into clinically meaningful tooth movement; and (3) how long-term intraoral ageing affects force delivery, fit, dimensional stability, surface degradation, and patient-centred outcomes. Addressing these gaps through standardised, brand-specific, and longitudinal clinical evaluation should be a priority for future research.

Several limitations of the included studies should be acknowledged. Considerable methodological heterogeneity existed in ageing protocols (intraoral versus in vitro, duration, temperature, and immersion media), specimen preparation (including variable aligner thickness and commercial brand), and outcome measurement techniques, making direct comparisons across studies challenging. Most included studies showed moderate-to-high risk of bias, primarily due to inadequate blinding, randomisation, operator/assessor reporting, confounder control, and sample-size reporting. Collectively, this corresponds to an overall low-to-moderate certainty of evidence for the ageing effects reported in this review, as detailed in the RoB assessment section above. Furthermore, the clinical relevance of purely in vitro ageing models is inherently limited, as they cannot fully replicate the complex biomechanical, thermal, and chemical conditions of the oral environment. This review is also limited by potential publication bias, as studies reporting statistically significant ageing effects may be more likely to be published, and by incomplete retrieval of unpublished studies, which were not systematically searched. In addition, synthesising evidence across heterogeneous laboratory and clinical study designs that differ in ageing conditions, outcome measures, and follow-up duration poses inherent challenges to drawing unified conclusions and should be considered when interpreting this review’s findings. Moreover, funding sources and potential financial conflicts of interest were inconsistently reported across the included studies and were not captured as a formal domain of the appraisal tools; beyond the two studies noted above with author affiliations to CA manufacturers, the extent to which industry sponsorship may have influenced study conduct or reporting could not be systematically assessed and should be considered when interpreting the reported findings. Future research should prioritise standardised ageing protocols, longitudinal clinical studies with patient-centred outcomes, and evaluation of emerging DPA materials, which remain underrepresented in the current literature despite their growing clinical adoption.

Conclusions

Despite the relative chemical stability of their bulk polymer structures, CA appear to undergo clinically relevant ageing-related deterioration in physical, mechanical, and morphological properties. Discolouration, water sorption, stress relaxation, and surface degradation represent the principal concerns, with TPU-based and DPAs appearing to show greater susceptibility than PETG-based counterparts in several domains. The rapid force decay observed within the first 48 h of wear raises the hypothesis that current replacement intervals may not align with the biomechanical rationale for replacement. However, studies directly correlating force decay with clinical tooth movement are needed to confirm this. Indeed, the marked inter-material variability shown in this review suggests the need for material-specific clinical protocols rather than a uniform approach. However, most included studies exhibited a moderate-to-high risk of bias, and considerable methodological heterogeneity further limits generalizability; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Standardised ageing protocols, longitudinal clinical investigations, and dedicated evaluation of emerging DPAs are needed to advance evidence-based practice in CA therapy.
