Masticatory function and frailty: insights from NHANES and CHARLS
Authors: Ronghui Xiang, Jiaqi Wu, Yunan Zhang, Gongwei Wen, Haiyan Zhao, Xuan Zhang, Ziyang Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between oral health and frailty has been established. This study aimed to examine the association between masticatory function (assessed by functional tooth units (FTU) and edentulism), and frailty status with mortality risk among frail individuals.MethodsData from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were analyzed. Masticatory function was assessed using FTU and edentulism. Frailty was defined using a 49-item and 32-item index in NHANES and CHARLS respectively. Multivariate logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models, and restricted cubic spline analyses were used to examine associations between mastication, frailty, and mortality. Sensitivity analyses were conducted.ResultsThe prevalence of frailty was 29.97% and 10.66% in NHANES and CHARLS respectively. Individuals having FTU of 10-12 showed a 24% lower risk of frailty compared to those with FTU of 0-3 in NHANES, and edentulism had 88% higher risk in CHARLS. Among frail participants, higher FTU scores were linked to lower all-cause (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.60-0.89) and cancer-related mortality (HR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.33-0.80), but not cardiovascular mortality in NHANES, and edentulism had 56% higher mortality in CHARLS. The association between FTU and frailty was linear, subgroups and sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness.ConclusionsBetter masticatory function, as indicated by higher FTU, is associated with lower frailty prevalence and improved survival among frail individuals, while edentulism linked to higher prevalence and mortality. These findings emphasize the importance of maintaining functional dentition and oral rehabilitation in geriatric care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27510-1.Keywords: Masticatory function, NHANES, CHARLS, Frailty, Mortality

Introduction

Frailty, an emerging age-related global health burden with profound clinical and public health implications, represents a surrogate condition characterized by the decline of multiple physiological systems and increased vulnerability to stress [1]. The clinical manifestations of frailty are heterogeneous, and well-defined strategies for its screening, identification, and management remain limited [2]. The prevalence of frailty increases with age and predicts adverse health outcomes [3]. The pooled prevalence of physical frailty and frailty due to accumulated health deficits was 7% and 24%, respectively, among individuals aged 50 years [4]. The prevalence of frailty was 10.7% among individuals aged 60 years [5], 28% among those aged 90 years, and 39.5% among those aged 95 years [6].

Factors influencing frailty include age, depressive symptoms, comorbidities, cognitive decline, social isolation, and overall health status [7]. Female sex and disability in activities of daily living were also associated with frailty [8]. Notably, oral health is closely linked to general health and is also associated with frailty, including factors such as tooth number, oral function, accumulation of oral problems, and dry mouth [9]. Currently, oral frailty, defined as a combination of tooth number, chewing ability, articulatory motor skills, tongue pressure, and subjective difficulty in swallowing and eating, has emerged as a risk factor for physical frailty and mortality among older adults [10]. Poor oral health is particularly associated with frailty, especially in the domains of remaining teeth, masticatory function, and chewing difficulty [11].

Oral frailty is reported to be associated with age, sex, education level, physical frailty status, number of dentures, dry mouth, chewing difficulty, oral health score, and sleep quality [12]. Among these factors, masticatory function plays a pivotal role and has been linked to various general health outcomes and mortality. Masticatory function is influenced by multiple factors, including tooth number, functional tooth units (FTU), periodontal health, masticatory muscle condition, prosthesis use, and oral moisture [13].

Previous studies have reported that masticatory function is correlated with frailty. Both pre-frailty and frailty were associated with three masticatory parameters: maximum occlusal force, mixing ability, and self-reported chewing ability [14]. Furthermore, objective chewing ability was associated with frailty among community-dwelling older Thai adults; both the presence and severity of frailty were correlated with reduced chewing capacity [15]. Self-reported chewing difficulty was significantly associated with frailty-related adverse outcomes in older adults, including all-cause and pneumonia-related hospitalizations, as well as all-cause mortality [16]. Also, those with teeth had lower odds of cognitive impairment and being pre-frail compared to those edentulous [17] and edentulism had higher odds of sarcopenia [18]. Among individuals with frailty, baseline general health could predict changes in chewing ability [19]. Following the provision of new complete dentures, frail patients showed significant improvements in both masticatory function and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) [20].

Given that frailty is a multidimensional biopsychosocial syndrome for which validated diagnostic tools remain urgently needed [21], however, the association between masticatory function and both frailty and its prognosis has not been established in large, representative populations. This study aims to investigate the association between masticatory function, assessed by FTU and edentulism, and the presence and prognosis of frailty. It is hypothesized that better masticatory function is inversely associated with frailty prevalence and mortality among frail individuals, whereas edentulism is positively associated with frailty and mortality. FTU and edentulism measured mastication may thus serve as a practical, non-invasive tool for early identification of pre-frailty and frailty in routine clinical and preventive dental practice. The study will examine these associations in nationally representative cohorts from the U.S. and China, offering new insights into frailty management and prevention.

Methods

Data origin

This study used data from two major sources: National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), and China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). NHANES was used as the primary data source due to its relatively comprehensive data availability, designed to collect information on health and nutritional status through questionnaires, laboratory tests, and physical examinations across the U.S. CHARLS, used for complementary analysis, is a large-scale interdisciplinary project sponsored by the National Development Research Institute of Peking University in China and implemented by the China Social Science Investigation Center. The CHARLS study was approved by the Peking University Institutional Review Board, while NHANES received approval from the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). In both studies, all participants provided written informed consent. NHANES circles from 1999 to 2018 were utilized, and CHARLS waves 2011 to 2020 were used in the prospective analysis and wave 2018 was in the cross-sectional study.

Measurement of masticatory function

In NHANES, masticatory functions assessed by FTU was the independent variable, defined as pairs of opposing teeth (including both natural and artificial teeth) in the posterior region by the presence of tooth (third molars were excluded due to their impaction, occlusal abnormalities, and limited role in mastication). FTUs were counted as 1 for premolars and 2 for molars, with a total score ranging from 0 to 12 [22]. FTU were further categorized subgroups into 0-3, 4-6, 7-9, and 10-12. Masticatory dysfunction was defined as FTU less than 5 [23]. Premolar and molar FTUs were also included as exposure variables. Functional dentition and total tooth count were considered secondary exposure variables. Functional dentition is defined as having 20 natural permanent or prosthetic teeth, while nonfunctional dentition is defined as having fewer than 19 teeth [24]. Edentulous status was documented in NHANES. In CHARLS, self-reported edentulous status was also available, with the prospective study beginning in the 2011 wave. Both self-reported edentulism and masticatory dysfunction were recorded in the 2018 wave, which was used in the cross-sectional study.

Measurement of frailty

In NHANES, frailty served as the dependent variable in this study. Frailty status was assessed using a 49-item frailty index (FI), with a threshold of 0.21 [25]. Participants with scores ≥ 0.21 were classified as frail, while the remainder were considered non-frail. To ensure diagnostic reliability, only participants who completed at least 80% of the 49 items (approximately 40) were included in the analysis. Then, in CHARLS, 32 age related items were selected to construct the FI, with frailty was defined as 32-FI ≥ 0.25 [26]. Participants were excluded from scale analysis if they had missing values for more than 20% of the items.

Ascertainment of mortality

Mortality data were obtained from the National Death Index death certificate records updated through December 31, 2019. Causes of death were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). Mortality outcomes included all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality (deaths from heart and cerebrovascular diseases; ICD-10: I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, I60-I69), and cancer mortality (deaths from malignant neoplasms; ICD-10: C00-C97). In CHARLS, mortality status was ascertained through death certificates, medical records, or interviews in 2020, specific cause death was not available in CHARLS. The follow-up period was defined as the time from the interview date to the date of death.

Covariates

The inclusion of covariates was consistent with previous studies [27, 28]. In NHANES, this study included both categorical and continuous covariates. Categorical variables covered demographic factors such as sex (male or female), race (Mexican American, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, or other), education level (< high school, high school, or > high school), marital status (married or living with a partner, widowed, divorced/separated, or never married), and poverty income ratio (PIR: low income < 1.5, middle income 1.5-3.5, and high income > 3.5). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never, former, or current) and alcohol consumption (never, former, mild, moderate, or heavy). Clinical factors included diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, and CVD (coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, chronic heart failure, or stroke), as assessed through questionnaires (yes or no). Obesity status was determined by body mass index (BMI: normal < 25, overweight 25-30, and obese > 30). In CHARLS, the covariates were similar, with additional factors including residence (south/north, urban/rural) and alcohol consumption (yes/no), alongside the aforementioned factors.

Statistical analysis

This study applied Medical Examination Center (MEC) weights in the analyses to account for the complex survey design of NHANES, while no weight was used in CHARLS. Population-weighted descriptive characteristics were stratified into two groups according to the presence of frailty. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as proportions (%) and counts (n). Student’s t-tests and Chi-square tests were used to compare baseline characteristics. In the cross-sectional analysis, multiple logistic regression models were used to calculate odds ratios (OR) and confidence intervals (CI) to assess the effect of FTU on frailty across three models: Model I (unadjusted), Model II (adjusted for age, sex, race, poverty, marital status, and education level), and Model III (fully adjusted). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were performed to detect potential non-linear associations. Subgroup analyses were conducted to further explore the association between FTU and frailty in the fully adjusted model. In the prospective analysis, Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted to estimate the cumulative hazards of cause-specific mortality. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) for FTU and mortality in individuals with frailty, according to the three established models. Masticatory function parameters were included as exposure variables in sensitivity analyses. Participants under 45 years old were excluded in the re-analysis and those with less than one year of follow-up or those who died from accidental causes were excluded from the mortality analyses as part of the sensitivity tests. The CHARLS dataset was used for complementary analysis. Logistic and Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to investigate the effect of edentulism on frailty prevalence and mortality among frail individuals. Kaplan-Meier curves were then generated as well as subgroup analyses. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association between masticatory function and frailty

In NHANES, the correlations between FTU and frailty are detailed in Table 3. Continuous FTU was associated with a 4% lower risk of frailty (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.95-0.97). An FTU score of 10-12 was associated with a 24% lower prevalence of frailty compared with a score of 0-3 in the fully adjusted model (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.66-0.87). Trend analysis showed that higher FTU was associated with a lower risk of frailty (P for trend < 0.001). The associations between masticatory function parameters and the risk of frailty are shown in Table S5, premolar and molar FTU, tooth count and functional dentition were all linked to a decreased risk of frailty, while edentulism and masticatory dysfunction were positively associated with frailty.

Association between masticatory function and mortality in those with frailty

Kaplan-Meier survival curves showed that higher FTU scores were associated with lower all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality (log-rank P < 0.001) among individuals with frailty in NHANES (Fig. 3), while edentulism had higher mortality in those with frailty (Figs. 4 and 5). In NHANES, the correlations between FTU and mortality based on Cox regression are further detailed in Table 5. Compared with participants with FTU scores of 0-3, those with scores of 10-12 had a 27% (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.60-0.89) and 49% (HR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.33-0.80) lower risk of all-cause and cancer-related mortality, respectively, but not CVD-related mortality. Similarly, tooth count and masticatory dysfunction showed comparable results, while functional dentition was associated only with lower all-cause mortality and edentulism with higher all-cause, cancer-cause and CVD-cause mortality. In CHARLS, edentulism was associated with a 56% higher mortality risk among frail individuals (Table 6).

Restricted cubic spline, subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

In cross-sectional study, RCS analysis of FTU and frailty in NHANES is shown in Figure S1, indicating a linear association (P for nonlinear = 0.666). Subgroup analyses across various demographics (Figure S2) revealed consistent inverse associations between FTU and frailty with no interactions, suggesting the association is stable across subgroups. Edentulism was similarly associated with frailty in subgroup analyses in CHARLS, as shown in Figure S3.

In NHANES, excluding individuals under 45 years old yielded similar results, showing that FTU and edentulism were associated with frailty and mortality (Table S6). Excluding participants who died within the first year (Table S7), FTU was associated with a lower risk of all-cause and cancer-related mortality, but not CVD mortality. Excluding accidental deaths (Table S8), consistent results were found, revealing the robustness of the association between masticatory function and mortality in individuals with frailty.

Discussion

This population-based two nationally representative study unveiled an association between masticatory function (measured by FTU and edentulism) and frailty and its mortality, suggesting that a better mastication is associated with a reduced risk of frailty and better survival among frail individuals. Improved masticatory function is associated with lower all-cause and cancer-related mortality. These results highlight the potential of FTU and edentulism as an easily accessible dental index for frailty, offering promise for its use in the prevention, management, and treatment of frailty. However, the discrepancy in results that FTU was not associated with CVD mortality may be attributed to population characteristics and the disease burden related to frailty.

Masticatory performance, a fundamental aspect of oral health, plays a crucial role in the development and progression of frailty. Consistent with previous studies, objective chewing ability measured by chewing gum was lower in frail individuals [15]. Compared with individuals with pre-frailty, those in the robust group exhibited better oral function, including masticatory performance and oral diadochokinesis [29]. Frail elderly individuals, on the other hand, showed decreased maximum bite force and masticatory dysfunction [30]. Individuals with poor mastication had increased frailty scores, reduced cognitive performance, handgrip strength, skeletal muscle index, and masseter muscle thickness (MT), with low grip strength in females and low gastrocnemius MT in males serving as independent predictors of chewing ability [31]. In contrast, mixing ability measured by chewing gum score was an indicator of both physical and comprehensive frailty in Japanese individuals aged 65 years [32]. Furthermore, self-reported poor and moderate masticatory function were associated with incident functional disability, serving as a surrogate for frailty [33]. Individuals with chewing difficulty were more likely to exhibit stable and progressive frailty trajectories [34]. Additionally, masticatory parameters, including mixing ability and subjective chewing ability, were associated with frailty progression in a longitudinal study [35]. A meta-analysis also suggested that self-reported masticatory dysfunction is associated with frailty risk [36]. These findings indicate that masticatory function is associated with pre-frailty, frailty status, and its progression.

To be noted, oral frailty has become a global concern among the elderly, primarily characterized by abnormalities in oral structure and function, often accompanied by deterioration in physical, cognitive, and social capabilities [37]. Influencing factors include advanced age, poor oral health, few remaining teeth, denture use, a history of chronic disease, physical frailty, poor motor function, depression, and poor sleep quality [38]. These evidences revealed that there is a bi-directional relationship between oral frailty and general frailty.

The underlying mechanisms may involve inflammation, hemodynamics, and nutrition. Masticatory difficulty was associated with the incidence of malnutrition [39]. In individuals with poor chewing ability, intake of all food groups was significantly lower, except for cereals, confectionery, sugars, seasonings, and spices, this suggests that mastication is linked to nutrient intake and undernutrition [40]. In elderly Chinese individuals, those with malnutrition had lower bite force, tongue pressure, lip sealing pressure, and poor oral function, as well as reduced intake of plant fat, iron, vegetables, fruits, and seafood [41]. In older individuals with pre-frailty, meal care interventions could improve OHRQoL and meal satisfaction [42]. Notably, oral frailty has been associated with reduced appetite and dietary variety [43]. Oral health is also linked to frailty, malnutrition, and functional decline in older adults [44]. Occlusal support might also be associated with nutritional status in older population [45], and occlusal force was associated with vitamins intake and dietary fiber in 70-year-old Japanese [46]. In those with higher intake of selenium, the self-perception of health, chewing ability, or physical activity were better [47], also based on NHANES, those with higher dietary oxidative balance score, dietary total antioxidant capacity had improved periodontal health [48]. Conversely, those with periodontitis had lower intake of vitamin C, folic acid, magnesium, and fiber, which may be resulted from impaired mastication [49]. Regarding the role of oral rehabilitation in nutrients intake, in individuals with tooth loss, wearing dentures had a positive impact on nutritional intake and eating enjoyment [50]. When implant-supported overdenture was utilized, their dietary and plasma antioxidant capacity were improved compared with conventional denture [51]. Hence, masticatory function may be linked to antioxidant dietary intake, potentially leading to undernutrition or malnutrition and mortality in individuals with frailty.

Then, the underlying mechanisms may be attributed to obesity and periodontal inflammation, and disruption of the oral barrier. People with masticatory dysfunction may have a higher risk of increased body fat [52]. Salivary flow rate, a measure of masticatory ability, has been reported to correlate with a lower prevalence of overweight, abdominal obesity, and increasing skinfold thickness in the general Japanese population [53]. Dentate individuals had lower levels of CRP and fibrinogen than edentulous individuals, with the difference dependent on obesity [54]. Periodontitis is recognized as a source of systemic inflammation due to oral microbiome dysbiosis, with well-established associations with various diseases. Disruption of the oral microbiome contributes to elevated inflammatory biomarkers and alterations in the gut microbiome, which in turn have a broader impact on overall health. Longevity individuals with different numbers of teeth exhibited distinct oral functions and oral microbiome diversity [55]. Severe chewing difficulty also led to alterations in the salivary microbiome [56]. Poor mastication induced mechanical damage to the gingiva, causing T helper 17 (Th17) cell accumulation and interleukin 6 (IL-6) induction [57]. Then, several genera exhibited significantly different abundances with increasing frailty and age, suggesting that oral microbiome diversity could serve as a valuable indicator of frailty risk [58]. Based on NHANES data, decreased oral microbiome diversity was associated with increased frailty risk and reduced mortality, with tooth number acting as a mediator [59]. Additionally, the effect of diet quality on frailty was mediated by the oral microbiome [60]. These findings underscore the impact of oral cavity inflammation induced by the oral microbiome on frailty.

Although these studies suggest potential mechanisms linking masticatory function and frailty, a conclusive explanation remains lacking. The dynamic alteration of masticatory functions, general obesity status, systemic inflammation, and oral microbiome composition warrants further research. Meanwhile, rehabilitation of lost masticatory function in frailty trajectory is also needed. Meanwhile, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the employment of cross-sectional design in this study precludes establishing a causal association between FTU and frailty. Secondly, masticatory function is measured by FTU in NHANES and through a self-reported oral health questionnaire in CHARLS, which may not accurately reflect objective masticatory ability. Hence, both subjective and objective measures of mastication, along with standardized FTU measurements regarding tooth position and mobility, are required. Third, the presence of unidentified confounders may introduce bias. Fourth, the interaction between FTU and dental prostheses (fixed or removable dentures) on the prevalence of frailty and the prognosis of those with frailty was not assessed in this study, future studies could investigate this interaction. Lastly, different diagnostic criteria and components of frailty were used in NHANES and CHARLS, and no comparison was conducted between these two cohorts. While a universal diagnostic standard for frailty is essential and recommended, it is also necessary to establish cohort-specific standards for frailty to ensure proper validation in multi-population studies. Therefore, future prospective studies, along with investigations into the mechanisms underlying masticatory function and frailty, are needed to validate these results and confirm the role of mastication in frailty, particularly in the context of the current aging burden.

Conclusion

This study revealed an inverse association between masticatory function and the risk of frailty and mortality in frail individuals from two cohorts. The findings emphasize the importance of tooth preservation and the rehabilitation of oral function in the progression of frailty, reinforcing the established links between masticatory function and overall health. Given the potential impact of oral rehabilitation on improving frailty and its prognosis, its clinical implications in preventing and reversing frailty should be more prominently considered in geriatric care. However, further validation and replication of these findings are needed to establish the effectiveness of oral rehabilitation in clinical geriatric dentistry practice.
