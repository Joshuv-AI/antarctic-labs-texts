Development and validation of an amyloid PET dementia of the Alzheimer's type (DAT) score
Authors: Mitchell Edema, Karteek Popuri, Hyunwoo Lee, Lei Wang, Mirza Faisal Beg, Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlzheimer's disease (AD) can be debilitating if left untreated, but its progression may be altered through early detection.ObjectiveTo develop and evaluate a convolutional neural network (CNN) for detecting AD from amyloid PET brain images and to investigate the regions contributing to model predictions.MethodsA 3D CNN with residual connections was developed to classify amyloid PET brain volumes. Amyloid PET data were obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), with approximately 600 images from cognitively normal control (NC) and dementia of the Alzheimer's type (DAT) participants used for training, validation, and testing. Performance was assessed using repeated 5-fold cross-validation (10 total folds). The model was also evaluated across the AD continuum, including unstable normal control (uNC), progressive normal control (pNC), stable mild cognitive impairment (sMCI), progressive mild cognitive impairment (pMCI), and early DAT (eDAT). Saliency and class activation maps were generated to identify regions contributing to predictions.ResultsThe model achieved a mean testing accuracy of 92% across the 10 folds. Across the disease continuum, accuracies were 76% for uNC, 78% for sMCI, 24% for pNC, 65% for pMCI, and 78% for eDAT. Saliency and class activation maps highlighted the putamen, thalamus, hippocampus, corpus callosum, and posterior cingulate cortex, regions previously implicated in AD pathology.ConclusionsThe proposed 3D CNN accurately distinguished DAT from cognitively normal controls using amyloid PET imaging and showed promising performance across the AD continuum. Model interpretation identified biologically relevant brain regions, supporting the potential of deep learning for early AD detection and clinical decision support.Keywords: amyloid-PET, dementia of Alzheimer's type (DAT), machine learning, multi-scale ensemble classifier

Introduction

Dementia of the Alzheimer's type (DAT) is the leading cause of dementia, leading to life altering symptoms such as memory loss, inability to plan and problem solve and many others.1,2 Early detection of the disease can lead to positive outcomes through timely treatments that mitigate its risks and symptoms.

One of the imaging modalities in which Alzheimer's disease (AD) is identified is through amyloid PET imaging. 3 Amyloid PET images illuminate regions with concentrations of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques, commonly found in patients with AD. These plaques have been demonstrated to cause disruptions in neurological cell function. 4

In recent times, the massive advancements in the space of machine learning have been exploited to perform nuanced medical imaging tasks. Tasks such as image classification have been explored to distinguish between images from patients with AD and healthy controls. This analysis aimed to develop a network that can receive an amyloid PET volume and classify whether that volume is diseased or not. The output of the network is a continuous value between 0 and 1, providing a probability that the current image is from a patient with DAT. This prediction value is called the amyloid PET DAT score (APDS). Such a network can be used in the screening of AD or to further confirm a diagnosis, Consequently, its potential is vast as it can greatly benefit society and improve the well-being of those before they are affected by such a pervasive disease.

Related work

Current methods for classifying amyloid burden in AD usually rely on simple, interpretable biomarkers such as PET imaging measures like standardized uptake value ratio (SUVr) or the Centiloid scale, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) measures like Aβ42 levels or the t-tau/Aβ1−42 ratio. These approaches use fixed thresholds to decide whether amyloid levels are high enough to indicate AD, but they depend on predefined regions, summary averages, and cutoffs that may miss subtle or complex patterns. In contrast, deep learning–based methods learn directly from imaging data and can capture more detailed spatial and biological patterns of amyloid deposition. This can improve sensitivity, especially in early or borderline cases, and allows integration of multiple data types, but it typically requires large datasets and can be less interpretable than traditional threshold-based methods. Applying machine learning to medical image classification tasks has been frequently cited in literature due to its effectiveness and robustness. With respect to PET image classification, many studies have been conducted to compare and contrast various methods. Some notable methods employed in the literature are histograms of oriented 3D gradients as input to a support vector machine (SVM), 3D convolutional neural networks (CNN) and a sparse-response deep belief network (SR-DBN) along with an extreme learning machine (ELM).7–9 Great success has been demonstrated using these methods as classification accuracies in the range of 96% have been achieved. This is a fairly active field of research that encompasses a wide expanse of possibilities for innovation. The existing vacancy in the present literature is the use of simply a 3D PET volume and a deep learning network to provide accurate, yet efficient predictions. Using the entire 3D volume as input to the network facilitates the learning of complex interrelated spatial patterns that are present in 3D space. Also, deep learning methods have been shown to be quite robust in their application to unseen datasets. Combining the requirement for less input modalities, an entire 3D volume as input and a deep learning architecture can result in an accurate and robust network to classify amyloid PET volumes effectively.

Methods

Data

The data that was used in the training and validation of this network came from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset (adni.loni.usc.edu). The curators of ADNI have acquired a large amount of biomedical data relating to AD such as MRI images, PET scans, genetic information and blood biomarkers for the purpose of advancing research by making this data widely available. The amyloid PET scans, MRI images and clinical information available in this dataset were used in the development of this classification model. The data was also augmented using various translation, left-right flip and rotation transformations to increase the amount of available data for training. The translation transformations were limited to 5 voxels in any given dimension and the rotation transformations were −5 and +5 degrees in any given dimension.

Database stratification

We stratified the subject conditions into those proposed by Popuri et al. 10 in order to create the desired cohorts for this analysis. Initially in the ADNI dataset, there are 3 diagnostic groups: normal control (NC), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and DAT. Stratification was based on the longitudinal clinical diagnoses received by each subject across all available ADNI visits, using the clinical diagnosis assigned at each visit as the basis for classification. No minimum follow-up duration was required beyond the availability of at least one subsequent visit to determine diagnostic trajectory. Conversion between diagnostic categories was defined as a clinician-assigned change in diagnosis at any follow-up visit, consistent with standard ADNI clinical assessment protocols.

After stratification, we had 7 subgroups: stable NC (sNC), unstable NC (uNC), progressive NC (pNC), stable MCI (sMCI), progressive MCI (pMCI), early DAT (eDAT), and stable DAT (sDAT). The stable groups (sNC, sMCI, and sDAT) consist of subjects who maintained their respective diagnosis consistently across all ADNI visits. The unstable group (uNC) contains NC subjects who converted to MCI at a subsequent visit but did not progress to DAT within the observation window. The progressive groups (pNC, pMCI) are subjects who eventually received a DAT diagnosis at a later ADNI visit, regardless of the number of intermediate visits. The eDAT group contains subjects who received a DAT diagnosis at their imaging visit but had a documented prior ADNI clinical diagnosis of either NC or MCI, meaning their conversion to DAT is directly observable within the ADNI visit record. This distinguishes them from the sDAT group, whose DAT diagnosis was present at or before their ADNI baseline with no preceding NC or MCI ADNI visit. We aggregated groups that convert to or present with DAT as the DAT + group (pNC, pMCI, eDAT, sDAT), and those that do not as the DAT– group (sNC, uNC, sMCI). We used this stratification scheme to organize our data, producing the demographics shown in Table 2.

Sample size justification and power analysis

The resulting stratification revealed a notable imbalance across subgroups, with sNC comprising the largest proportion of subjects, followed by sDAT and pMCI. This reflects the relative prevalence of these conditions in the population, where cognitively normal individuals are more common than those with MCI or DAT. This imbalance was accounted for in our subsequent analyses. For the AD classification using the sNC and sDAT images, we trained and tested on 395 sNC images and 162 sDAT images, which is comparable to sample sizes reported in similar deep learning studies of amyloid PET imaging (Table 5). The remaining subgroups, while smaller, were only used for evaluating model generalization rather than training, reducing the impact of limited sample sizes on model performance.

Preprocessing

The data was preprocessed in a few steps. The first step was to coregister the ADNI preprocessed PET images (adni.loni.usc.edu/methods/pet-analysismethod/pet-analysis/) to the subject's personal MRI space. The FSL-FLIRT (fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/FLIRT) software was used to perform registration. After coregistration, the images were normalized using a reference region within the PET image. This region was taken to be a combination of the cerebellum and an eroded region of subcortical white matter as done in Landau et al. 13 These regions have been widely used for the normalization of amyloid PET images due to the absence of amyloid plaque accumulation in these regions. We acknowledge that some studies have reported low levels of amyloid deposition in the cerebellum in advanced stages of AD, which may introduce a slight underestimation of amyloid load in similarly progressed subjects.14,15 However, this is an accepted limitation of cerebellar reference region normalization and does not meaningfully affect comparisons across the cohorts examined in this study. The Freesurfer MRI segmentations for each subject were used to identify these reference regions and calculate the mean uptake in the region. Finally, the PET image was divided by this reference mean, producing a normalized image across each subject. Following normalization, the images were registered to a template space, in this case the MNI152 space with a voxel size of 1.5mm3. To generalize the intensity ranges, min-max scaling was applied such that the intensities were constrained between 0 and 1. Because these images were spatially normalized via the coregistration and registration steps, they were adequately preprocessed for the generation of saliency and class activation maps for model interpretation. As an extra step for data augmentation, we rotated the images to −5 and +5 degrees in all 3 dimensions before training.

Network architecture

The implemented network architecture was the ResNet model, incorporating the modifications proposed by Yee et al.16,17 The ResNet architecture is known for its effectiveness in imaging applications and its use of skip connections to train deep networks. This analysis aimed to develop a similar model to Yee et al., 16 where a 3-dimensional ResNet was used for volume classification. Key differences in this model include the use of instance normalization instead of batch normalization and global average pooling instead of traditional flattening and fully connected layers. Instance normalization normalizes feature maps of each channel of a single sample, unlike batch normalization which normalizes a mini-batch, potentially leading to overfitting. Global average pooling calculates the average of each feature map from the convolution layer and flattens it without having any trainable parameters. This helps reduce the risk of overfitting. The network produces the APDS as its classification output, representing the predicted probability that a given amyloid PET image belongs to the DAT group. The APDS is a continuous value in the range,[0,1] where values closer to 1 indicate a higher likelihood of DAT and values closer to 0 indicate a higher likelihood of NC. Early stopping was used if the best validation loss was the same for 8 consecutive epochs (Table 5).

Network evaluation and validation

To evaluate the model, 5-fold cross-validation was repeated twice, creating 10 total folds to incorporate more data in each fold. After training all folds, the model was tested on a separate dataset and its accuracy was compared to similar works. Saliency and class activation maps (GradCAM) were generated to visualize the regions contributing most to classification. Saliency maps were produced by back-propagating gradients from classification nodes, while GradCAM highlighted important regions using gradients from the last convolutional layers. A confusion matrix evaluated precision and recall, and uncertainty plots showed model confidence with a 0.5 threshold for class assignment. These metrics validated the model and indicated areas for further development.

Network generalization

As a final step, the network was evaluated on test images that were on the continuum of conditions between sNC and sDAT. Those conditions are uNC, sMCI, pNC, pMCI and eDAT. The subgroups that are DAT– (do not convert to AD) are the uNC and sMCI groups whereas the subgroups that are DAT+ (convert to AD) include pNC, pMCI and eDAT groups. The objective of this analysis was to observe how the model would generate predictions for images that are not yet diagnosed as AD. To further analyze this conversion, the time to conversion was also used as a grouping for subjects within a progressive subgroup (pNC and pMCI). These results identified the model's ability to predict an image as having the characteristics of AD at an earlier stage before diagnosis.

Discussion

The purpose of this analysis was to develop a CNN model to predict AD from amyloid PET images. The proposed APDS achieved performance comparable to or exceeding existing methods. Direct comparisons are complicated by the fact that most deep learning studies in this space have used FDG-PET, which captures glucose metabolism rather than amyloid burden directly. Relatively few studies have applied deep learning to amyloid PET specifically. Among the amyloid PET-based studies, the model matched Lee et al. 21 at 92% accuracy while achieving notably higher sensitivity (93.8% versus 84.5%). Compared to FDG-PET methods, the model performed similarly to Popuri et al. 10 and approached the performance of Yee et al., 16 though differences in modality should be considered when interpreting these comparisons. Beyond binary NC versus DAT classification, a notable strength of this work is the additional validation across conditions spanning the AD continuum, including uNC, sMCI, pNC, pMCI, and eDAT subgroups, which is not commonly reported in similar studies and provides a broader assessment of the model's generalizability. Using twice repeated 5-fold cross-validation and by carefully separating subjects, the results presented here are not a result of preferential or skewed testing.

The model generalized well on independent images from subjects across the AD continuum, distinguishing between healthy and diseased images in the uNC, sMCI, pMCI, and eDAT groups without being specifically trained on these conditions. The pNC group was more challenging, with an accuracy of only 24%, likely due to NC images containing healthy features until conversion to DAT. The pMCI and eDAT groups had sufficient spatial information for higher DAT classification, while the uNC and sMCI groups were more accurately classified as healthy. This demonstrates the model's ability to correctly classify images across the AD spectrum.

To further assess the model's generality, progressive subgroups were divided by time to conversion, evaluating early detection of DAT. For pMCI subjects, the model predicted DAT within 4 years to conversion with 65% accuracy (Table 7). Beyond 4 years, classification accuracy decreased due to the possible lack of necessary features and spatial information present at this time. The pNC group showed even lower performance, with no observable trend in prediction accuracy as time to conversion increased. Again, the pNC group in general is a more challenging group to classify, especially when the time to conversion is larger. The number of available pNC images were quite few, therefore not producing the most representative results of the model's performance on this subgroup.

By further separating each subgroup by age, we analyzed APDS trends relative to age. For the sMCI and uNC groups, the APDS score gradually increased from 0 to 0.35 with age (Figure 8), indicating a higher probability of DAT prediction in older patients. A possible reason for this may be a result of older patients exhibiting increased atrophy due to age, causing the model to misinterpret this as neurodegeneration due to AD. Accuracy heatmaps (Figure 9) showed increasing accuracy with decreasing age for these groups. No similar trends were observed in other subgroups, but there was a general increase in mean APDS from uNC to eDAT, as expected.

Based on the saliency maps, the model looked for brighter intensities in thalamus and putamen regions, indicating that there are higher concentrations of Aβ plaques. These findings are consistent with previous reports of amyloid deposits in the thalamus.23–25 The regions with darker intensities, or lower Aβ concentrations, were the hippocampus, corpus callosum and superior and inferior colliculus regions. Both the hippocampus and the corpus callosum have been observed to decrease in size due to the neurodegeneration caused by AD.26,27 This may then decrease the amount of Aβ plaques that are able to accumulate in these regions, thus causing the darker intensities.

In the activation maps for sNC classification (Figure 11), the posterior cingulate cortex was key in predicting the sNC class. Darker intensities in this region indicate fewer Aβ plaques, typical of a healthy population, demonstrating the model's ability to distinguish between healthy and diseased images. This use of the posterior cingulate cortex aligns with previous findings, which show higher Aβ accumulation in AD patients.16,28–31 The slightly offset highlighted region may be due to data augmentation transformations or interpolation inaccuracies during feature map resizing for visualization. For sDAT classification (Figure 12), the thalamus was highlighted, consistent with findings of amyloid deposits in AD patients.18,23 These deposits disrupt connections between connected hub regions (medial and lateral prefrontal cortices, medial and lateral parietal cortices, insula, and thalamus), leading to cognitive impairment characteristic of AD. 24 This supports the observation that AD selectively affects highly connected areas.24,25

We analyzed the interaction between CSF t-tau/Aβ1−42 values and APDS scores across the DAT continuum. A positive correlation was observed between CSF ttau/Aβ1−42 and APDS for conditions between sNC and sDAT, with significance in the sNC, sDAT, uNC, sMCI, pMCI and eDAT groups. Interestingly, no significant correlation was observed in the pNC group, which may be attributable to the limited sample size of this subgroup (n = 13). This is further supported by the power analysis, which confirmed that the pNC subgroup was underpowered (power = 0.23), falling below the conventional threshold of 0.80, suggesting that the absence of significance reflects insufficient sample size rather than a true lack of association. The pNC group is inherently small, as it requires subjects to have a diagnosis of NC at the time of imaging while still going on to develop DAT at some point within the ADNI window. Using a cutoff of 0.52 for CSF t-tau/Aβ1−42 showed high accuracy in both groups. For DAT– conditions, the mean APDS was lower in the τ/Aβ− group, while for DAT + conditions, the mean APDS was higher in the τ/Aβ+ group, reflecting the expected classification based on risk. The accuracy of using CSF t-tau/Aβ1−42 for predicting DAT trajectory was higher for τ/Aβ− subjects in the DAT– group and τ/Aβ+ subjects in the DAT + group. This aligns with previous findings on the effectiveness of CSF t-tau/Aβ1−42 in indicating AD. Since the analysis used amyloid PET images, higher t-tau/Aβ1−42 concentrations were more likely to be misclassified as DAT + in DAT– subgroups, and lower concentrations misclassified as DAT–. The uNC and sMCI plots in Figure 14 illustrate this, where higher τ/Aβ+ means led to lower accuracy compared to τ/Aβ− means in these DAT– subgroups.

To further support the use of deep learning in this analysis, we have reported the performance of more traditional CSF-based biomarker approaches (Table 1) for the diagnosis of AD. Conventional biomarker-based approaches rely on fixed thresholds applied to summary measures, such as CSF Aβ1−42 levels or the t-tau/Aβ1−42 ratio, which may miss subtle or spatially complex patterns of amyloid deposition. In contrast, the deep learning-based APDS learns directly from the full 3D amyloid PET volume, enabling it to capture intricate spatial and biological patterns that are not easily summarized by a single scalar biomarker. As shown in the literature values presented, the sensitivity, specificity, and overall discriminative performance (AUC) of the APDS exceed those of many conventional biomarker-based approaches, suggesting an improved ability to distinguish Alzheimer's disease from cognitively normal individuals. This supports the added value of deep learning–based modelling as a complementary or potentially superior tool to traditional biomarker methods for amyloid PET-based AD classification.

The data used in this study came from a single source, that being the ADNI dataset. Because ADNI consists of its own preprocessing steps and scanners used for data acquisition, it is difficult to determine whether this model will generalize well for images coming from different datasets. A future development of this analysis may be to test the model on independent datasets, or develop preprocessing steps to generalize the input images. However, this approach was shown to be feasible for identifying the presence of AD in amyloid PET images, contributing to the current collection of imaging modalities that are used to detect AD.

Future work and conclusion

The motivation for this analysis was to develop a method for detecting AD using amyloid PET images, as these images can contain valuable information relating to the presence of amyloid beta in the brain. This objective was achieved using a 3D ResNet structure, taking in a 3-dimensional PET image and classifying whether the image was from a healthy patient or a patient with AD. This architecture was shown to be proficient in completing this task and indicates its viability for further investigation. The model was developed to generalize well such that clinical applications may be possible, though further validation on independent datasets should be carried out first. This work can be extended by improving the network on images over the continuum of conditions between sNC and sDAT. This will give the ability to predict the onset of AD at a much earlier stage than when the patient begins to display initial symptoms. Also, combining related data, such as clinical data or other imaging modalities, with the amyloid PET images may provide the model with more information to better influence its prediction. Overall, there is overwhelming evidence to support the effectiveness of CNNs to complete complex tasks such as those presented in this analysis. The hope is that a model such as this can be implemented in a clinical setting to aid in the early detection of AD, enabling timely treatment and ultimately improving patient outcomes and well-being.
