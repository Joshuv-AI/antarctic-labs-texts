Triglyceride-glucose index and its additive interaction with ABCG2/SLC2A9 polygenic risk score on hyperuricemia in middle age and older adults: findings from the DLCC and BHMC study
Authors: Huijing He, Qiaolu Cheng, Shuo Chen, Qiang Li, Jingbo Zhang, Guangliang Shan, Minying Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aim to investigate the joint effect of triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and polygenic risk scores (PRS) of urate transporter genes ABCG2 and SLC2A9 on hyperuricemia.MethodsBaseline data from two prospective population-based cohort studies, including 30,453 individuals aged 50 years or older, were used to analyze the association between TyG index and hyperuricemia. A case-control study was then designed from the cohorts to investigate the interaction between genetic predisposition and TyG index on hyperuricemia among 595 matched pairs. PRS was constructed using 14 single nucleotide polymorphisms located in the ABCG2 and SLCA29 genes.ResultsIn both sexes, higher TyG index levels were correlated with elevated serum urate (SUA) levels (p values in both sexes < 0.001). In men, per unit increase of TyG was associated with a 1.44-fold (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.35–1.55) higher risk of hyperuricemia after adjusted for covariates. In women, this estimate was 1.69 (1.51–1.89). Demonstrated by the restrict cubic spline model, TyG index was both linearly and non-linearly associated with elevated SUA (both p values < 0.001). Association between TyG index and hyperuricemia was stronger among people with higher genetic risk, and vice versa. Compared to people with TyG < 9 and PRS < 2, the odds ratios (ORs) (95% CIs) for hyperuricemia in the TyG <9 but PRS ≥2, TyG ≥9 but PRS < 2, TyG ≥9 and PRS ≥2 groups were 3.30 (1.53–7.14), 3.16 (1.23–8.11) and 7.55 (2.76–20.65), respectively. Additive interaction was also significant, with 57.5% (30.5%–84.4%) of the excess risk attributable to the additive gene-TyG index interaction.ConclusionsThe impact of genetic predisposition on hyperuricemia was significantly greater among individuals with a higher TyG index. Over 50% of the increased risk can be attributed to the interaction, indicating a crucial synergy between genetic factors and TyG index when estimating hyperuricemia risk.

Introduction

Hyperuricemia is a disorder characterized by difficulties in the excretion of uric acid or abnormalities in purine metabolism. It presents in two phenotypes: urate hyperproducers and underexcretors, both related to abnormalities in either urate production or tubular re-absorption [1]. Hyperuricemia is not only a fundamental factor in the development of gout but is also believed to be associated with an increased risk of cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and renal diseases [2–5]. Data from the China National Health Survey showed that the prevalence of hyperuricemia exceeded 19.6%, with men being particularly affected at 25.1% [6]. This raises significant concerns for public health.

Previous studies have strongly suggested that insulin resistance (IR) may be associated with hyperuricemia and gout [7]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is considered a new index that reflects IR, similar to the high insulin-glucose clamp test, which is currently considered the standard diagnostic method for IR [8]. Therefore, the TyG index provides an additional option for clinically evaluating IR and preventing conditions associated with IR, as it is cost-effective and easily accessible compared to the high insulin-glucose clamp test. Recent studies have found that the TyG index is associated with inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and disorders in glucolipid metabolism [9,10]. It has also been proven to be useful in predicting hyperuricemia in various populations [11,12].

Metabolic profiles and genetic predisposition are important risk factors for hyperuricemia. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified various single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that significantly contribute to elevated serum urate concentrations and overall hyperuricemia risk [13,14]. SNPs in urate transporter genes, such as ABCG2 and SLC2A9, are closely associated with the onset of hyperuricemia and gout, particularly in Asian populations [15,16]. Previous studies have explored the relationship between variants of these genes and genetic risk for hyperuricemia, revealing significant associations [13,17]. As complex diseases, the occurrence of hyperuricemia and gout is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors, as well as their interactions. However, the comparative contributions of genetic risk and the TyG index to hyperuricemia, particularly their interaction effect, have not been well understood.

Therefore, our objective was to use baseline data from two large prospective cohort studies to investigate the relationship between the TyG index and hyperuricemia. Additionally, we sought to explore how the TyG index and genetic risk, as indicated by the polygenetic risk score (PRS) of the ABCG2 and SLC2A9 genes, combined to impact hyperuricemia in middle-aged and older adults in China.

Materials and methods

Study population and data source

The study population included individuals from two prospective population-based cohorts: the diverse life-course cohort (DLCC) and the Beijing Health Management Cohort (BHMC). Detailed information about the two cohorts was previously published [18–20]. In both cohorts, the study population was selected using a multi-stage cluster sampling method to ­consider disparities and representativeness in socio-economic status and geographic characteristics. Participants had to be aged 18 or older and have lived in the local area for more than one year to be eligible. Pregnant or lactating women, patients with severe mental disorders, and disabled individuals were excluded.

For the current study, individuals aged 50 years or older who were recruited from the health examination centers in DLCC and BHMC were included. Since a biobank was established in the DLCC study, a case-control study within the cohort population was designed to examine the genetic risk of hyperuricemia and the interaction effect between IR and genetic risk on hyperuricemia. Each case of hyperuricemia aged 50 or older at the Beijing Health Physical Examination Center from the DLCC cohort was matched with a control (defined as having normal serum urate levels and not taking uric acid lowering medication) based on age (within one year) and sex. A total of 595 case-control pairs were fully matched. The study design and flow chart are presented in Supplementary Fig.S1. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Boards of Nankai University (NKUIRB2016063) (for the DLCC cohort) and the Ethics Committee of Beijing Health Examination Center (2023-005) (for the BHMC cohort). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participating in the survey.

Measurements and definitions

In both the DLCC and BHMC studies, all participants underwent a standardized questionnaire, physical examinations, anthropometry measurements, and biochemical tests. Demographic information such as sex and age, health-related lifestyle factors such as cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption, anthropometric measurements including height, weight, and waist circumference (WC), blood pressure (specifically systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP)), biochemical tests for fasting serum lipids (total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and serum urates (SUA) were collected. The DLCC study additionally collected blood samples to establish a biobank. Details regarding the measurements and definitions of covariates can be found in our previous publications and the supplementary Table S1 [18–20]. Hyperuricemia was defined as a SUA level higher than 360 μmol/L (approximately 6 mg/dl) in women, and 420 μmol/L (approximately 7 mg/dl) in men, consistent with previous studies [21,22]. TyG index was calculated using the formula below [23,24]: TyG=ln⁡[FPG(mg/L)×TG(mg/L)/2]

Sample size estimation

We calculated the minimum sample size necessary for a matched case-control study using PASS 14 software (version 14.0.15). In this study, where the primary interest is the presence of a higher PRS level, we set the probability of exposure in the control group at 0.2 and the correlation coefficient for exposure between matched case and control at 0.2. We obtained a sample of 294 case participants, each matched with 1 control subject. This total sample of 588 patients (including cases and controls) has 90% power to detect an odds ratio of 2.00 versus equal odds, using a Chi-Square test with a statistical significance level of 0.05 (two-sided). Ultimately, we have matched a total of 595 cases with 595 controls based on age and sex to achieve enough statistical power.

Genotyping and quality control

DNA was extracted after natural thawing using TIANGEN whole blood DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH (BEIJING) CO., LTD). A total of 20 SNPs were selected as the target SNPs. The strategy for target SNPs selection is based on literature review, MAF in Chinese population (>0.05), and the linkage analysis. The genetic data of the case-control population were genotyped using MassARRAY SNP genotyping technology. Details about the technology is available in Supplemental materials and methods. Because genotype calls are made in real time during MALDI-TOF analysis, simultaneous visualization of all assays in a multiplex reaction are available immediately at the end of each run. Data is saved automatically in the MassARRAY database and can be retrieved at any time for future analysis. The details of the MassARRAY SNP genotyping technology can be found at http://www.biomiao.com/190318/1-1Z31Q42S1.pdf.

To produce large quantities of PCR amplicons for use with the MassARRAY Systems, the genomic DNA templates are highly pure, with the ratio of ultraviolet (UV) spectrophotometer readings at 260 nm and 280 nm wavelengths between 1.7 and 2.0 (i.e. A260/A280 = 1.7 ∼ 2.0).

Genetic risk score

A polygenetic risk score (PRS) was constructed using 14 out of a total 20 SNPs that were tested to be statistically significant in the univariate association analysis (p < 0.05, Supplementary Table S4). Each SNP was weighted based on its relative effect size for hyperuricemia, and then the weighted values were summed up to calculate the PRS using the formula provided below [25]: PRS=∑i=1Iβi1xi1+∑i=1Iβi2xi2

In the formula, xi1 represents the risk allele heterozygote of SNPi, and xi2 represents the risk allele homozygote of SNPi, and β represents the regression coefficient. Higher scores indicating a greater genetic predisposition for hyperuricemia.

Statistical analysis

After excluding individuals with missing values on major risk factors and outcome measures, the final analytical sample included 30,453 individuals in the cohort population and 1,190 in the case-control group. Continuous variables were presented as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range (if the data did not follow a normal distribution), while categorical variables were represented as frequency and percentage. We used the restrict cubic spline (RCS) models to examine the dose-response and non-linear relationships between TyG, PRS, and serum urate. General linear regression models (GLMs) were utilized, with SUA as the dependent variable and the TyG index as the independent variable, adjusting for potential confounders. Multivariable logistic regression models (conditional logistic regression for matched case-control design) were developed to analyze the association between independent risk factors and the outcome (hyperuricemia). To assess the additive interaction, we calculated the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), and the synergy index (SI) [26].

RERI is defined as: RERI=RR11−RR10−RR01+1 where RRab is the relative risk (RR) in the group with X1 exposure status a (1 = exposed, 0 = unexposed) as compared with the doubly unexposed group. An RERI of 0 implies no additive interaction, greater than 0 means positive interaction [27]. In this study, RR is replaced by OR assessed by the logistic regression model.

AP is defined as: AP=(RR11−RR10−RR01+1)/RR11=RERI/RR11

SI is defined as SI=(RR11−1)/((RR01−1)+(RR10−1))

If the 95% confidence interval (CI) for AP does not include 0 and the CI for SI does not include 1, then the study provides evidence of additive interaction. The RERI, AP, and SI were estimated using the regression coefficients and covariance matrix obtained from the logistic regression model [28,29]. The delta method was used to calculate the 95% CI for these indexes [28,30]. To ensure adequate statistical power and facilitate interpretation in the analysis of joint effects, PRS and TyG index levels were dichotomized based on the cut-off values obtained from the receiver operator characteristic curve (ROC). We calculated the 95% CIs for all coefficients and odds ratios (OR) estimates. A p-value (two-sided) less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R software (Version 4.2.2).

The association between TyG index and hyperuricemia in the cohort population

The scatter plots demonstrating the relationship between the TyG index and serum urate by sex are presented in Supplementary Figure S2. In both sexes, higher TyG index levels were correlated with elevated SUA (p-values in both sexes < 0.001). Results from the multivariable GLM showed that for every unit increase of the TyG index, there was a 14.08 μmol/L (11.30–16.87) increase in SUA in men and a 18.31 μmol/L (15.40–21.22) increase in women (Supplementary Table S3). Figure 1 illustrates a statistically significant relationship between the TyG index and the mean difference of serum urate, as indicated by p-values less than 0.001 for both linear and nonlinear components.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the combined impact of the TyG index and genetic predisposition on hyperuricemia in middle-age and older adults. We discovered that there exists both a linear and non-linear correlation between the TyG index and serum urate levels. Additionally, we found that a higher PRS and an elevated TyG index are associated with an increased likelihood of hyperuricemia. An additive interaction between TyG index and genetic predisposition on hyperuricemia was discovered, and the risk of hyperuricemia from the superimposed two exposures of TyG index and genetic predisposition was much greater than the individual effects of the two exposures and their sum.

Over the past decade, GWAS and several meta-analyses have identified more than 30 common sequence variants that can affect hyperuricemia/gout, most of which are found in urate transport proteins [31,32]. Three urate transporters, URAT1/SLC22A12, GLUT9/SLC2A9, and ABCG2/BCRP have been reported to play crucial roles in the regulation of serum urates, and their dysfunctions cause urate transport disorders (hypouricemia and/or hyperuricemia). The major exception urate transporter is ABCG2, while the GLUT9 protein (encoded by SLC2A9 gene) is important for reabsorption [33]. The heritable secretion component of urate homeostasis is principally mediated by the product of the ABCG2/BCRP gene [34,35]. The vast majority of identified variants are intronic, and allelic non-synonymous are only V12M and Q121K (rs2231142) in ABCG2, and R294H in SLC2A9 [36]. ABCG2 p.V12M has been identified as a protective variant with high probability (p < 0.001) in different ethnic groups [36]. However, functional characterization does not show the impact of this variant on GLUT9 function [37]. Non-synonymous allelic variants of ABCG2 have a significant impact on the earlier onset of hyperuricemia/gout and the presence of a familial history of gout. Dysfunction of ABCG2 has been reported as a strong independent risk factor for pediatric-onset hyperuricemia/gout [36]. A selection pressure analysis has revealed a significant enrichment of selection for the ABCG2 and ALDH2 loci in Japanese patients [38]. The SLC2A9 gene is involved in regulating serum urate concentrations. Previous studies have shown that the variant p.V282I (rs16890979) and c.1002 + 78A > G in the SLC2A9 gene decrease the risk of developing gout, while p.N82N in SLC22A12 gene increases the risk [39]. Moreover, SLC2A9: p.R294H has no reported relationship with function impact on protein expression, cytoplasmic localization, and urate uptake activity [40].

In addition to genetic factors, serum lipids and glucose metabolic systems also related with serum urate levels. A case-control study in Czech population found that gout and hyperuricemia were associated with alterations in plasma lipid profiles, with these associations being significantly stronger in early-onset or early disease detection [41]. Insulin is not only the most important hormone for regulating blood glucose levels, but it has also been shown to regulate urate in the kidneys by influencing the actions of certain urate transporters [35]. When IR occurs, compensatory hyperinsulinemia may decrease urate excretion by affecting the expression of urate transporters. This can contribute to the development and progression of hyperuricemia [42]. Several studies have investigated the associations between the TyG index and hyperuricemia in specific populations. For example, Dong et al. found that among older patients with hypertension, the TyG index was closely related to hyperuricemia, with an increase of one unit in the TyG index indicated a 2-fold higher risk of hyperuricemia [43]. Similarly, Qi et al. reported that the TyG index is an independent risk factor for hyperuricemia in patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, noting a linear relationship between the TyG index and hyperuricemia [44]. Additionally, several observational studies in general populations have also found a positive association between the TyG index and the risk of hyperuricemia [45,46]. Consistent with previous findings, our study revealed that a higher TyG index indicated a higher level of serum urate and an increased risk of hyperuricemia, identifying both the linear and nonlinear dose-response relationships between IR and SUA levels.

Previous GWAS studies have shown significant genetic correlations between serum urate levels and cardiometabolic traits, including insulin resistance [47]. For instance, McCormick et al. demonstrated a causal link between IR and hyperuricemia and gout using a Bidirectional Mendelian Randomization method [7]. They found that the majority of the variation in serum urate concentration is primarily due to two key genes: SLC2A9 and ABCG2, which accounted for 34% and 10% of the total variance, respectively. In a group of Czech control individuals, metabolic syndrome and the c.421C.A variant in the ABCG2 gene were independently associated with the SUA concentration, and SUA was positively correlated with the numbers of metabolic syndrome criteria, including IR [48]. In our study, we discovered that the PRS based on SNPs in these two genes is significantly associated with hyperuricemia. A higher PRS indicates a greater risk of hyperuricemia, especially in individuals with higher TyG index. When Comparing individuals with lower TyG and PRS levels to those with high levels of TyG index and PRS, we observed a significant increase in SUA levels, with an almost 80 μmol/L increase and a 7-fold higher risk of hyperuricemia.

Although several studies have examined the genetic-environmental interaction for hyperuricemia, most of them have focused on single SNP [49], or on other phenotypes [47,50]. However, there is currently no evidence on the impact of the interaction between high TyG index levels and PRS on hyperuricemia. Additionally, the epidemiological community acknowledges that measuring interaction on an additive scale is the most appropriate method for assessing the public health significance of interactions. The additive scale helps determine whether the effect of a risk factor would be greater in one subgroup compared to another, making it useful for targeting interventions to specific subgroups when resources are limited [26,29]. Both a higher TyG index and genetic predisposition contribute to the risk of hyperuricemia by increasing serum levels [13,43–45], but the specific mechanism by which the combined effect exceeds the sum of each individual effect remains unknown. The reasons for how genetic predisposition and IR interact to influence the risk of hyperuricemia still need to be determined.

Our study indicated that over half of the increased risk can be attributed to the interaction between the target genes and TyG index. If either of these factors were absent, a significant portion of the cases of excess hyperuricemia would not occur [51]. Since that TyG index acts as a surrogate for IR and is easier to modified and treated, from a public health perspective, individuals with a higher genetic risk should consider initiating IR screening and addressing its risk factors as early as possible.

The strengths of the current study include its large sample size in estimating the relationship between TyG and SUA, assessing the relationship in both linear and nonlinear scales. Additionally, we analyzed the interaction on an additive scale and determined the amount of relative excess risk attributable to the interaction, as well as the proportion of hyperuricemia exposed to both high TyG index levels and genetic predisposition due to the additive interaction. There is substantial ethnic-difference in frequencies of alleles in ABCG2 and SLC2A9 (see in the Supplementary Table S5), our study can contribute to the exploration of genetic predisposition to hyperuricemia among Asian populations. However, the current study also has several limitations. Firstly, the study was based on baseline data from two observational studies. Although we performed multivariable regression analysis, there may still be other biases caused by uncertain temporality or other confounding factors. Secondly, while the sample size in the case-control design was estimated to have sufficient statistical power, more samples may be needed for the interaction analysis to achieve a narrower confidence interval for the additive interaction indexes. Nonetheless, in the calculation of PRS, we utilized a weighted genotype score taking into account its relative risk to some extent to avoid model overfitting. Thirdly, as ABCG2 and SLC2A9 are only related to SUA transporters while SUA hyperproducers are mainly characterized by XO hyperactivity [1], genetic analysis on SUA transporters may not fully cover the hyperuricemia spectrum. Lastly, the study population is limited to certain areas in China and middle-aged and older individuals, so the study’s conclusions may not be generalizable to other populations.

Conclusions

In summary, our study used data from two large general population studies and found both linear and non-linear relationships between the TyG index and SUA. We also discovered that the impact of genetic predisposition on hyperuricemia was more pronounced in individuals with higher TyG index. Furthermore, we revealed an additive gene-TyG interaction effect on hyperuricemia, which explained over half of the excess risk and showed an important synergy between genetic factors and the TyG index in predicting the risk of hyperuricemia.
