Illusions induce contradictory perception of motion
Authors: Yuki Inoue Nakata, Yuki Kobayashi, Takuya Niikawa, Satoshi Nishida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCan we perceive motion and stillness simultaneously? Philosophical studies suggest that the perception of illusory motion can involve both of these contradictory contents. We examined this possibility by asking participants to rate both perceived motion and perceived stillness using stimuli with illusory or physical motion. The results showed that the negative correlations between the two ratings were much weaker in illusory motion, suggesting that motion and stillness can be disentangled in illusory motion perception. This is the first empirical examination of the hypothesis that perception can involve contradictory contents.Keywords: motion, illusion, aftereffect, perceptual bistability, consciousness

It has been argued that perceptual experiences cannot involve contradictory contents (Pautz, 2020). For instance, it seems that we cannot simultaneously perceive an object as being pure red and pure green, or being still and moving.

However, it is unclear whether this “non-contradiction” principle can be applied to illusory motion (Shapiro & Hedjar, 2019). Humans can perceive motion in a physically static object due to adaptation to preceding motion (motion aftereffect [MAE]) (e.g., Anstis et al., 1998) or a special configuration of opponent colors (rotating snakes [RS]) (Fraser & Wilcox, 1979; Kitaoka, 2017). The possibility that motion and stillness are simultaneously experienced in such illusory motions has been pointed out (Crane, 1988). If true, it would constitute a counterexample to the non-contradiction principle. However, the possibility has not yet been empirically investigated.

This study tests the hypothesis that motion and stillness can coexist in motion illusion experience, without relying on researchers’ potentially biased introspective reports. Participants were asked to rate the magnitudes of perceived motion and stillness under controlled conditions. To be clear, our aim is to identify the experiential contents of motion illusions, not their underlying mechanisms.

We conducted two online experiments that involved RS 1 and MAE, respectively (N = 150 × 2). In each trial of the RS experiment, three snake-like circles were presented for three seconds. They were either motion-illusion-inducing or not (Figure 1A and B). In the illusory motion condition, an illusion-inducing stimulus was shown statically, whereas in the physical motion condition, a non-illusory stimulus was physically rotated in random directions. In each trial of the MAE experiment, a 15-s adaptation was followed by a 3-s main stimulus presentation (Figure 2). During adaptation, two bandpass-filtered gratings and a white fixation dot were presented, and the gratings smoothly flowed to cause MAE in the illusory motion condition. However, in the physical motion condition, their flow was more stepwise; an appropriate setting of step size and velocity enables observers to perceive consistent motion but results in significantly weaker MAE (Nishida & Sato, 1992). In the main stimulus presentation, the fixation turned red, and the gratings were static (illusory motion condition) or slowly moving in the negative directions (physical motion condition). Casual pilot experiments confirmed that naïve observers had difficulty in distinguishing illusory and physical motion in both RS and MAE experiments, indicating that the two motion conditions created similar perceptual experiences. After stimulus presentation, a question about either the perceived motion or perceived stillness followed (Table 1). The two types of questions were asked in two separate blocks in each experiment. Each experiment and motion condition had ten levels of motion magnitudes, which were controlled by contrast of color (illusory motion of RS), smoothness of adapting movements (illusory motion of MAE), and physical velocity of non-illusory stimuli (physical motion of RS and MAE). After screening participants (excluding, in the physical-motion condition, those whose mean ratings in the maximum-magnitude condition were lower than those in the minimum-magnitude condition for either perceived stillness and perceived motion and those with zero variance in either of the ratings), we calculated Pearson's correlation coefficients (r) between perceived motion and perceived stillness for each experiment, motion type, and participant 2 . These correlations were based on ten data points, representing average ratings corresponding to ten motion magnitudes. Then, r values were converted according to the following equation (Fisher's Z-transformation), and z-scores that follow a normal distribution were obtained (Figure 3A).

These r-values were then Fisher-transformed to z-values. (B) Comparison of mean z-values between physical and illusory motion conditions. The error bars indicate standard errors.

T-tests on z-scores showed that the negative correlation was significantly weaker in illusory motion in both RS and MAE (Figure 3B, RS: mean z-score = −0.495 and −0.868, n = 128, t = 7.65, p < .001, d = 0.68, 95% CL = [0.28, 0.47]; MAE: mean z-score = −0.147 and −0.436, n = 118, t = 5.76, p < .001, d = 0.53, 95% CL = [0.19, 0.39]). This suggests that motion and stillness become more decorrelated in the experience of illusory motion. Although an inherent negative correlation is assumed to exist between them, they could potentially be more independent under the illusion condition. This suggests the possibility that their relationship is relatively closer to that of compatible perceptions, such as ‘large’ and ‘bright’. Although motion ratings for illusory-moving stimuli were generally lower than those for physically-moving stimuli, it is unlikely that this influenced the motion–stillness correlation in the experience of illusory motion, as the mean does not directly affect the correlation.

In addition to the pre-planned analysis described above, we further conducted similar tests using regression coefficients that were planned after data collection (for each participant, we regressed the perceived stillness ratings on the perceived motion ratings to obtain a regression coefficient, and then performed a paired t-test on these coefficients). Although correlation coefficients can be difficult to interpret because they vary depending on the ratio of measurement error to the total variance of the variable, regression coefficients are less susceptible to the biases caused by measurement error in dependent variables. Furthermore, they can be intuitively interpreted as an estimate of the change in perceived stillness for one unit change in perceived motion. These t-tests also supported the hypothesis that the effects of perceived motion on perceived stillness are weaker in illusory motion than in physical motion (RS: mean coefficients = −0.385 and −0.627, t = 5.76, p < .001, d = 0.51; MAE: mean coefficients = −0.158 and −0.479, t = 5.47, p < .001, d = 0.50). The same held true when the independent and dependent variables were interchanged. These results are at least consistent with the idea that motion and stillness perceptions could coexist in the experiential content of illusory motions.

Furthermore, we compared the motion–stillness correlations while taking into account differences in test–retest reliability between illusory motion and physical motion. Specifically, we calculated the correlation coefficient across repeated measurements for the motion ratings and that for the stillness ratings separately, transformed each coefficient to z, and then averaged the two z values as an index of test–retest reliability. We then added this average to the z-transformed correlation coefficient between motion and stillness. Because the correlations across repeated measurements were expected to be positive, whereas the correlation between motion and stillness was expected to be negative, this addition effectively served to offset the influence of differences in test–retest reliability. Even after accounting for such differences, the relationship between motion and stillness remained weaker for illusory motion than for physical motion in RS, although the same trend was also observed in MAE (RS: n = 119, p = .010; MAE: n = 100, p = .090).

The present result suggests the qualitative difference between the perceptions of illusory and physical motion, though the result does not indicate complete decorrelation (negative correlations in illusory motion were significant; p < .001). This may cast a question to psychophysical studies that have assumed that illusory motion should be cancellable with physical motion (e.g., Kuriki et al., 2008). More importantly, the present result suggests that motion and stillness can be more independent and potentially coexist in the experience of motion illusions. This serves as the first empirical challenge to the non-contradiction principle that perceptions cannot involve contradictory contents simultaneously. However, this study represents only an initial step toward quantitative examination of this principle, and we acknowledge that the present data may be open to alternative interpretations. For example, the experimental design does not rule out the possibility that motion illusions involve motion and stillness that alternate with attentional focus rather than perceived simultaneously. Regarding the methodology, the inclusion of double negatives in the response options (e.g., “I did not feel at all that the image was actually not moving”) may have caused confusion among participants, potentially introducing noise into the data. More controlled experiments are required to further examine whether the perception of motion illusions involves motion and stillness in a contradictory or coherent manner. Although the present results are not conclusive ones, we hope that this research will stimulate further discussion on the coexistence of contradictory perceptions.
