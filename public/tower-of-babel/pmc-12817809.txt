Prevalence of Anaemia and Related Micronutrient Deficiencies After Bariatric Surgery
Authors: Wei Wei Ng, Ijaz binti Hallaj Rahmatullah, Norasyikin binti A Wahab, Chee Xian Tan, Anilah Binti Abdul Rahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAnaemia and micronutrient deficiencies are common complications following bariatric surgery. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of anaemia after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) and sleeve gastrectomy (SG), and to identify anaemia associated risk factors.Materials and MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 100 adults who underwent RYGB or SG between 2016 and 2022 at Hospital Raja Permaisuri Bainun, Ipoh. Demographics, biochemical parameters, and multivitamin (MVT) adherence data were analysed according to the type of surgery.ResultsAmong the participants, 44 underwent RYGB and 56 underwent SG, with a mean follow-up of 3 years. Most were female and over half were still menstruating. The overall prevalence of anaemia increased from 14% pre-surgery to 47% post-surgery. Anaemia increased from 9.1% to 47.7% in the RYGB group and from 17.9% to 46.4% in the SG group, with no significant difference in severity of anaemia (P=0.897). Postoperatively iron, folate and vitamin B12 deficiencies were observed in 44%, 14%, and 9% of patients, respectively. Folate deficiency was higher after SG (23.2% vs. 2.3%, P=0.003), while vitamin B12 deficiency occurred only after RYGB (20.5% vs. 0%, P<0.001). Iron deficiency was slightly higher in the RYGB group but not statistically significant (P=0.547). Menstruating females had higher risk of iron deficiency (P=0.001). Only 46% showed good adherence to MVT intake.ConclusionAnaemia affected nearly half of post-bariatric surgery patients, with menstruating women at highest risk. Lifelong monitoring and prophylactic iron supplementation are essential to prevent anaemia and related micronutrient deficiencies.Keywords: Roux-en-Y gastric bypass; Gastrectomy, sleeve; Anaemia; Micronutrient deficiency

INTRODUCTION

Bariatric surgery is an effective treatment for obesity and its associated co-morbidities [1]. However, common post-surgical complications include micronutrient deficiencies and anaemia, primarily due to reduced dietary intake and malabsorption in the digestive systems. Restrictive procedures like sleeve gastrectomy (SG), typically cause milder micronutrient deficiencies. In contrast, malabsorptive procedures such as Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), often lead to more profound nutritional deficits. These deficits occur because malabsorptive procedures bypass key segments of the digestive tract that are responsible for nutrient absorption [1,2,3].

Both the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery and the British Obesity and Metabolic Surgery Society guidelines recommend a daily intake of 1 to 2 complete multivitamins (MVTs) and mineral supplements after both RYGB and SG [4,5]. Additionally, the guidelines advise menstruating women to take extra elemental iron to prevent iron deficiency [4,5]. Despite routine supplementation following bariatric surgery, micronutrient deficiencies can still occur. The prevalence of postoperative anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies varies across centres and is influenced by multifactorial factors such as MVT supplementation strategy, patient’s compliance to MVT, and dietary intake.

Despite the growing number of bariatric procedures in Asia, only a few studies evaluate micronutrient status and patient adherence to supplementation after surgery. Hospital Ipoh serves as a major referral centre primarily for the Northern region of Malaysia, where RYGB and SG are the most commonly performed procedures.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of pre- and postoperative anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies (specifically iron, vitamin B12, and folate) following RYGB or SG, and to compare these outcomes between the 2 groups at a single tertiary centre in Malaysia. Additionally, the study aimed to identify risk factors associated with post-surgery anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies. The secondary objective was to evaluate patients’ adherence to MVT supplementation post-surgery and to identify factors contributing to poor adherence.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a cross-sectional study involving patients who underwent RYGB or SG at Hospital Raja Permaisuri Bainun, Ipoh, from 2016 to August 2022. Out of the 150 patients, only 62 (42%) were still being followed up at our multidisciplinary bariatric clinic. However, 122 patients were successfully contacted by phone and 100 patients met the inclusion criteria. (Fig. 1). All patients provided informed consent prior to the study. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the National Medical Research Register (NMRR ID-22-02148-WUU IIR).

Flowchart showing the case selection process.

RYGB = Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, SG = sleeve gastrectomy.

We included all patients aged 18 years and above who underwent RYGB and SG between 2016 and August 2022 with a minimum of 6 months elapsed from surgery to the time of recruitment. Those who underwent other bariatric procedures besides RYGB or SG, pregnant or breastfeeding, underlying renal or liver impairment, malignancy, history of recent gastrointestinal or abnormal uterine bleeding was excluded.

Surgical techniques were performed by the same surgeons with uniform techniques throughout the study period. Post-surgery, all patients were advised to take 1 to 2 tablets of MVT supplements. The dose of the supplement was titrated according to routine laboratory measurements.

Demographic and biochemical data pre-surgery were derived from patient clinical notes. During the clinic visit, blood pressure, weight, height, body mass index (BMI) and percentage of total weight loss were assessed. Blood samples were drawn for full blood count, iron, total iron binding capacity (TIBC), ferritin, vitamin B12 and folate. The Malaysia Medication Adherence Assessment Tool was used to assess patient adherence to MVT supplements post-surgery (Appendix 1). A score of 54 or higher indicated good adherence, while a score below 54 indicated moderate to poor adherence [6]. Patients were also interviewed about factors affecting MVT adherence, with common factors chosen based on a review of relevant literature and clinicians’ entries in medical records. Other medications taken by patients were also recorded.

Biochemical assays

Anaemia was defined as haemoglobin levels <13 g/dL in men and <12 g/dL in women [7]. Microcytic anaemia was classified as anaemia with mean corpuscular volume (MCV) <80 fL, macrocytic anaemia as anaemia with MCV >100 fL, and normocytic anaemia as anaemia with MCV between 80 fL and 100 fL. Folate deficiency was defined as a serum folate level <4 ng/mL (10 nmol/L) [2,8] while vitamin B12 deficiency was classified as a serum vitamin B12 level <200 pg/mL (148 pmol/L) [2,9]. Iron deficiency was determined by a serum ferritin level <30 ng/mL or a calculated transferrin saturation (TSAT) <20% [10,11]. TSAT was calculated using the formula: TSAT = (Measured Serum Iron/Measured [TIBC]) × 100% [10,11].

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data were analysed using either χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and numerical data were analysed using Independent t-test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range) and assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to identify factors associated with anaemia and deficiencies in iron, folate, and vitamin B12 following bariatric surgery. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Of the 100 patients included, 44 underwent RYGB and 56 underwent SG. The mean age was 43.81±8.63, with no significant difference between the 2 groups (P=0.314). Approximately 73% of patients were female, of whom 57.5% were menstruating. Pre-operative BMI (49.28±8.89 vs. 48.31±9.29, P=0.599) and postoperative BMI (34.63±5.68 vs. 36.12±8.21, P=0.307) were comparable between RYGB and SG. The prevalence of anaemia increased from 14% pre-operatively to 47% postoperatively. Mean haemoglobin levels declined from 13.48 g/dL to 12.20 g/dL, with no significant differences between surgical groups for either anaemia prevalence or haemoglobin levels (Table 2). Iron deficiency was the most common micronutrient deficiency (44%), followed by folate deficiency (14%) and vitamin B12 deficiency (9%). Folate deficiency was significantly more frequent after SG (23.2% vs. 2.3%, P=0.003), whereas vitamin B12 deficiency occurred exclusively in the RYGB group (20.5% vs. 0%, P<0.001). The prevalence of iron deficiency did not differ between procedures.

Logistic regression analysis showed that female gender and longer duration since surgery were significant predictors of postoperative anaemia and iron deficiency (Tables 3 and 4). Pre-operative anaemia conferred 29-fold higher odds postoperative anaemia, while females had approximately 18-fold higher odds of postoperative iron deficiency compared with males. Non-menstruating individuals had 86% lower odds of iron deficiency. The type of surgery was not associated with postoperative anaemia or iron deficiency.

DISCUSSION

A substantial proportion of our patients developed anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies, including iron, vitamin B12, and folate after bariatric surgery. The rise in anaemia in our cohort aligns with longitudinal studies and meta-analyses reporting progressive declines in haemoglobin levels that rarely return to pre-operative levels [1,2,3,12]. Consistent with Kwon et al. [1], we observed no significant difference in postoperative anaemia between RYGB and SG, suggesting that both procedures carry comparable risk despite their anatomical differences. These findings underscore the need for vigilant, long-term haemoglobin monitoring in all post-bariatric patients.

Iron deficiency was the most frequent micronutrient deficiencies among our patients, a finding widely echoed in previous studies [1,2,3,13,14]. Numerous studies have described iron deficiency as a common late complication after both RYGB and SG, driven by reduced gastric acid secretion, decreased meat intake, and bypassing of the primary absorption segments in the case of RYGB [3,15]. Our findings of similarly high prevalence of iron deficiency in both procedures aligns with evidence from recent comparative studies showing that over time, the overall risk of iron deficiency converges between RYGB and SG [1,3,13,16,17]. This consistency across studies emphasises the importance of routine, long-term iron surveillance in all postoperative patients. Gender-related patterns in iron deficiency also paralleled findings from previous research. Premenopausal women in our cohort demonstrated markedly higher odds of anaemia and iron deficiency, similar to results from systemic review by Enani et al. [18] which identified female sex and ongoing menstruating as significant risk factors.

Vitamin B12 deficiency occurred exclusively after RYGB in our study, which in line with multiple meta-analyses demonstrating significantly higher risk following malabsorptive procedures compared to restrictive ones [1,2,3,17]. Notably, none of our patients who developed B12 deficiency were on metformin during the recruitment period. Following bariatric surgery, reduction in the secretion of hydrochloric acid and intrinsic factors from the parietal cells of the stomach leads to vitamin B12 deficiency [1]. Our prevalence was lower than some longer-term studies may be attributed to the longer postoperative duration in their study.

Conversely, our findings revealed a significantly higher prevalence of folate deficiency among SG patients. While many studies report similar folate deficiency between RYGB and SG [1,11], our findings differ slightly. Lower adherence to supplementation among SG patients may partially explain this variation, although MVT intake did not independently predict folate deficiency in our analysis. This discrepancy with previous studies could be due to small sample size, inconsistent reporting of MVT intake among patients, differences in follow-up duration, or variability in supplement formulations.

Long-term adherence to MVT supplementation remains a universal challenge and is well recognised in the literature [17,19]. Despite the generally high reported intake of MVT supplements in our cohort, only about half of the patients demonstrated consistent adherence. This aligns with the meta-analysis by Ha et al. [17], which reported long-term adherence rates below 20% across 82 studies. Forgetfulness and the perception that supplements were unnecessary were commonly cited reasons in our cohort, consistent with findings in other populations [19,20,21]. Additionally, the variable composition of commercially available MVTs further complicates the assessment of their effectiveness across studies.

To our knowledge, this is the first study conducted in Malaysia to examine the prevalence of anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies, with their associated risk factors following bariatric surgery. Furthermore, this research provides valuable insights that can help to improve our MVT supplementation protocol by investigating the degree of adherence to MVT supplements among our post-bariatric patients. The limitations of our study were the cross-sectional design and relatively small sample size. Selection bias may have occurred since only patients reachable by phone were included, and self-reported adherence to MVTs may introduce recall bias. A single assessment of micronutrient levels may not accurately reflect the overall nutritional status, as these levels can fluctuate over time. Furthermore, the information on pre-existing micronutrient deficiencies before bariatric surgery was lacking. Therefore, we need to conduct further research with larger sample sizes and multicentre cohort studies to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term nutritional consequences following bariatric surgery.

CONCLUSION

In this cohort of post-bariatric surgery patients, anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies particularly iron, vitamin B12 and folate were common, with menstruating women at higher risk of iron deficiency. These findings highlight the importance of ongoing monitoring of haemoglobin and micronutrient levels in this population. Future longitudinal and multicentre studies with detailed assessment of supplementation protocols and adherence are needed to strengthen evidence for population-specific nutritional management in Malaysia.
