New-Onset ANCA-Associated Vasculitis in Pregnancy Successfully Treated With Rituximab: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Zuzana Hajšelová, Vojtěch Krátký, Zdenka Hrušková, Martin Kment, Vladimír Tesař, Ondřej Slanař, Jan Miroslav Hartinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Development of new-onset antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis (AAV) in pregnant women is extremely rare. Thus, treatment options are not well established. We report the case of a woman diagnosed with rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis due to new-onset anti-myeloperoxidase AAV during the 11th week of pregnancy and discuss the case in the context of available literature. Rituximab and low-dose corticosteroids were used as an induction treatment, and the patient stayed in remission throughout the rest of pregnancy without any detectable side effects to the patient or the newborn. As rituximab placental transfer increases during the third trimester and may induce B-cell depletion in the newborn, a small dose of azathioprine was used as a maintenance treatment at the end of pregnancy when partial B-cell repletion appeared. To our knowledge, this case reports the first documented successful treatment of AAV with rituximab in pregnancy without any side effects and represents the fourth reported case of rituximab administration for de novo AAV in a pregnant woman. The administration of rituximab early during the pregnancy with subsequent azathioprine administration in the third trimester combines the high effectivity of rituximab induction treatment with azathioprine that does not comprise the risk of B-cell depletion and subsequent several months-long sustained rituximab-induced immunosuppression in the newborn.

Introduction

Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) comprises a heterogeneous group of severe systemic small- to medium-vessel vasculitis characterized by the development of autoantibodies mostly against neutrophil cytoplasmic antigens proteinase 3 or myeloperoxidase (MPO). AAV are rare, potentially life-threatening diseases that involve many organ systems, including the upper and lower respiratory tract, kidneys, skin, and nerves.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 AAV typically occur in the post-reproductive period, with a peak incidence in patients aged >40 years; therefore, the data on treatment, disease progression, and maternal and fetal outcomes in de novo disease that developed during pregnancy are scarce.

Ashok et al10 described pregnancy outcomes in 82 pregnancies in patients with stable AAV. All patients developed the disease before conception and underwent various induction treatments. During the pregnancy, they were mostly maintained on steroid and azathioprine (AZA) therapy. Regarding maternal outcomes, 10 patients developed preeclampsia, 1 had HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelets), and 7 had severe AAV flares during the pregnancy requiring treatment escalation. Three pregnancies were prematurely terminated, 1 due to AAV relapse, and there were 4 miscarriages and 1 stillbirth in the 25th week.10 According to the systematic review by Veltri et al,11 there have been 27 described cases of de novo presentation of AAV in pregnancy. Maternal age was between 18 and 40 years (median 28), and AAV occurred between the 5th and 39th weeks of gestation (median 20). Serious maternal complications were reported in 56% of cases (alveolar hemorrhage, respiratory and renal failure among others), 2 of which resulted in maternal death and 11 cases of premature delivery. Antenatal remission occurred in 10 patients (including 3 of 4 treated with AZA).11

Induction treatment of severe AAV flares consists of cyclophosphamide or rituximab (RTX) along with high-dose corticosteroids. Currently, RTX has become preferred modality, except in severe cases due to possible side effects of cyclophosphamide therapy including carcinogenicity or infertility. In rare reports, high doses of pulse AZA therapy were used as AAV induction treatment,12 but this did not become the standard therapy. RTX can be used as a maintenance therapy in AAV instead of AZA; however, RTX seems to be more effective.13 Methotrexate or mycophenolate mofetil can also be considered for AZA-intolerant patients.14 In pregnancy, mycophenolate mofetil and methotrexate are contraindicated, and cyclophosphamide is associated with a high risk of teratogenicity. AZA and corticosteroids are relatively safe, but there is still insufficient evidence to draw firm conclusions for RTX.15 However, the increasing body of evidence suggests that RTX is a safe treatment option during pregnancy,15, 16, 17 as opposed to cyclophosphamide, due to its known teratogenicity.18

RTX is a chimeric mouse/human monoclonal antibody that binds to the B-cell surface antigen CD20, causing B-cell depletion and immunosuppression. RTX as well as other molecules that contain Fc fragments are transported through placenta into fetal circulation during the third trimester of pregnancy.19 In some cases, this leads to B-cell depletion in newborns, with subsequent B-cell repopulation, and no clinically apparent adverse manifestations have been noted. In AAV, the reported RTX median half-life is 28 days but may be >30 days in some patients.20 This makes the time needed for complete RTX elimination up to 4 months; therefore, to prevent transplacental transfer during the third trimester, ideally, no RTX should be administered after the eighth week of gestation. When RTX is used for AAV treatment, peripheral B cells usually remain depleted for about 6 months after standard induction.21

Case Report

In July 2024, a 34-year-old White female nonsmoker (165 cm, 80 kg at presentation) was hospitalized during the 11th week of her pregnancy because of rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis due to new-onset anti-MPO AAV confirmed by kidney biopsy. The patient had also suffered from type 1 diabetes since the age of 2 years, had known and treated hypothyroidism, and was recently diagnosed with hypertension and anemia. Before her admission, she was treated with 100 μg levothyroxine every day, 10 mg folic acid every day, and insulin. Her pregnancy resulted from in vitro fertilization. Baseline creatinine at the time of the procedure was 0.89 mg/dL (79 μmol/L), estimated glomerular filtration rate 87 mL/min/1.73 m2, and albuminuria 6.5 mg/mmol. Upon admission, her serum creatinine level increased to 2.68 mg/dL (237 μmol/L) and C-reactive protein was 42 mg/L, and urine evaluation showed hematuria (186.0 × 106/L) and proteinuria with protein-creatinine ratio of 0.5 g/mmol. Extrarenal AAV manifestation was ruled out mainly by clinical evaluation due to restricted standard imaging options during pregnancy (eg, a chest x-ray). To confirm the AAV diagnosis, kidney biopsy was performed. Histopathology results showed necrotizing glomerulonephritis compatible with AAV diagnosis (Berden class – mixed)22 with epithelial crescents in 3 of 11 glomeruli and segmental sclerotization in 3 of 11 glomeruli with negative immunofluorescence and class IIa diabetic nephropathy23 with diffusely expanded mesangial areas and moderate, approximately 30%-35%, interstitial fibrosis (Fig 1).Figure 1This glomerulus demonstrates segmental necrosis of capillary tuft (arrow), along with epithelial crescent formation (arrowhead) and segmental sclerosis. After the initial assessment, the mode of treatment was considered. Cyclophosphamide was not considered due to the risk to the fetus and intravenous AZA is not available in the Czech Republic; therefore, RTX combined with corticosteroids was chosen as an induction therapy based on the positive benefit/risk ratio. The RTX was administered in two 1,000 mg doses 21 days apart (the second dose was postponed for a week due to national holiday and patient’s request); corticosteroid therapy was initiated with 3 daily pulses of 250 mg methylprednisolone and subsequent 40 mg prednisone every day with decreasing doses by 5 mg every 2 weeks until achieving a maintenance dose of 2.5 mg/d. The patient remained in clinical remission with B-cell depletion until the 30th week of pregnancy (November 2024, 126 days after the second dose of RTX) when 0.3% of CD19-positive cells were measured. Consequently, treatment with 50 mg AZA twice a day was started to ensure the patient’s remission was maintained. AZA was chosen as the preferred alternative instead of continuation in RTX due to the possible risk of B-cell depletion in the newborn caused by RTX treatment in the third trimester. AZA is readily transferred through the placenta but is a safe immunosuppressant in pregnancy according to European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations.26 It has low teratogenicity risk because the immature fetus probably cannot convert it to the active metabolite, mercaptopurine.27 Moreover, for safety reasons, the thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) genotype of both parents was determined, and the mother as well as the father had ∗1/∗1 genotype, ie, with both parents having 2 fully active alleles, possible TPMT deficiency in the fetus was excluded. Alongside AZA, she continued taking a maintenance dose of 2.5 mg prednisone daily. The patient remained under close obstetrics monitoring during pregnancy, considering multiple risk factors for developing preeclampsia (such as diabetes, kidney disease, and autoimmune disease). Her hypertension was adequately controlled with methyldopa 250 mg 3 times a day throughout her pregnancy and although her proteinuria mildly increased at the end of her pregnancy, serum preeclampsia biomarkers such as soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 and placental growth factor stayed within range.

Four days before the planned delivery, the AZA treatment was permanently discontinued. On the first day of her 40th week of pregnancy (January 20, 2025) she gave birth by planned caesarean section to a healthy female newborn with a birthweight of 3,270 g. The patient was given a maintenance RTX dose of 1,000 mg 3 weeks postpartum (February 2025) and a year after the diagnosis (July 2025), with no signs of relapse. The infant was healthy without any signs of immunosuppression or neonatal AAV due to anti-MPO transfer and successfully underwent all mandatory vaccinations. After the third dose of RTX (3 weeks postpartum), the patient’s lactation slowly ceased because of factors unrelated to diagnosis and/or treatment, but no other complications were reported. The infant remained bottle-fed and thriving during a follow-up 1 year after birth.

Discussion

A review of the few known cases of de novo presentation of AAV during pregnancy in pre-RTX period described high complication rates.11 Although alkylating cytotoxic agents are relatively contraindicated in pregnancy, high doses of intravenous AZA may be a relatively safe alternative for RTX. Nevertheless, AZA is not an established induction treatment for AAV28 and is inferior to RTX as a maintenance therapy.29 Moreover, AZA has previously been used during pregnancy as maintenance immunosuppression mainly in patients after organ transplantation, which requires lower dosage compared with induction therapy in the treatment of AAV. EULAR recommends keeping the AZA dose at maximum 2 mg/kg/d, which would be insufficient to induce active AAV remission. RTX may therefore represent the best option, and EULAR guidelines mention that RTX therapy may be used in pregnancy if needed to effectively control maternal disease,26 but the evidence for its use in pregnancy is still limited.

Only 3 reported cases describe the use of RTX in de novo presentation of AAV during pregnancy. The transient immunosuppression of the newborn after RTX administration to the mother was described in a case report of a 20-year-old woman who was diagnosed with MPO-AAV at 22 weeks of gestation. Corticosteroids and 4 weekly doses of RTX were used as induction therapy. After a preterm delivery at week 27 of gestation, the neonate presented with depleted CD19+ cell levels but no subsequent infectious complications.30 When assessing the risk of RTX administration during pregnancy, one must also recognize that AAV poses a significant risk to the fetus, as ANCAs may also be transported through the placenta during the third trimester. Schlieben et al31 described the case of a woman with established diagnosis of microscopic polyangiitis and increased MPO-ANCA levels who underwent an urgent cesarian section at the 33rd week of her pregnancy. Over 24 hours after delivery, the neonate developed pulmonary hemorrhage requiring mechanical ventilation, active renal impairment, and anti-MPO antibody positivity. The infant was successfully treated with a short course of corticosteroids and remained asymptomatic with no reoccurrence of anti-MPO antibodies.31 This shows the importance of disease control during pregnancy, not only to treat the mother but also to prevent the transplacental transfer of ANCAs with subsequent neonatal AAV symptoms. On the other hand, RTX can induce adverse effects such as late-onset neutropenia, as was reported in a second case of new onset anti-MPO AAV treated using RTX in 33-year-old woman presenting at her 12th week of gestation. She was treated with 4 weekly doses of RTX and corticosteroids and developed remission. However, she developed late-onset asymptomatic neutropenia (0.1 × 109/L) at week 26 of gestation (14 weeks post-RTX). At 36 weeks of gestation, she gave birth to a 2,800 g healthy male newborn with no evidence of transplacental transfer of anti-MPO antibodies, clinical manifestation of vasculitis, or neutropenia.32 This case of late-onset neutropenia suggests a need for thorough control of the patient during treatment with RTX. The third reported case of administration of RTX during pregnancy for new-onset AAV was unsuccessful treatment of ANCA-negative AAV in a woman presenting in her 11th week of gestation with active disease requiring dialysis. She developed accelerated hypertension in week 17, and the pregnancy was terminated. The patient was then transferred to treatment with cyclophosphamide due to non-responsiveness of the disease to RTX, revealed by repeated kidney biopsies showing active disease.33

Together, these case reports and other published literature provide evidence that the use of RTX together with corticosteroids as an induction treatment of AAV during the first trimester is a relatively safe and effective treatment, at least when commenced in an earlier stage of the disease with preserved kidney function; however in the later stages of the pregnancy, there might be a significant risk of B-cell depletion in both mother and neonate.2 Weighing the risk of B-cell depletion in the newborn and bearing in mind the risk of relapse and possible placental transfer of ANCAs, we decided to switch the patient transiently to AZA when CD19+ cells started to increase during the third trimester. Although RTX may be generally more effective than AZA for maintenance treatment in AAV, AZA remains a viable option during maintenance treatment of AAV14 and is also safe in pregnancy,34,35 so this was the best way to prevent RTX transfer to the newborn without compromising the overall efficacy of the treatment. If remission could not be achieved in our patient, or perhaps if the disease manifested with life-threatening extrarenal involvement, we would add pulse cyclophosphamide at the standard induction dose. In some cases, fetal toxicity of cyclophosphamide might be mild or even absent36; nevertheless, as it has well-described teratogenic potential,18 it should be reserved for RTX-unresponsive pregnant patients. Close monitoring of clinical status, laboratory parameters including antibody levels, and CD19 depletion is therefore necessary.

RTX therapy is not completely without risk. Risk factors for developing severe infections must be monitored, including in pregnant AAV patients, and appropriate treatment must be started immediately, as infections are the leading cause of death in AAV patients during the first year of treatment.37 Despite that, we did not prescribe sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, which is the recommended prevention of pneumocystis infection in AAV patients, considering its possible fetal toxicity.38

In conclusion, de novo development of AAV during pregnancy is a rare event with potentially severe consequences if left untreated, including irreversible kidney damage, proteinuria with preeclampsia, alveolar hemorrhage, and miscarriage, among others. Several of the common treatments are not suitable during pregnancy, including cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, or mycophenolate mofetil. Rituximab could therefore be considered for the first line of AAV induction therapy in the first trimester of pregnancy due to its efficacy and relative safety compared with alternative options. Nevertheless, more information is still needed to confirm the safety profile of RTX during pregnancy.
