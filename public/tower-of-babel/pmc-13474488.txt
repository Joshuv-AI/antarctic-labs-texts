Insecticide-treated net utilization and associated factors among infants in Southwestern Nigeria
Authors: Olumide Thomas Adeleke, Morenikeji Folasade Olawuwo, Timothy Kayode Samson, Akeem Opeyemi Akinbode, Oludamola Victoria Adeleke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsecticide-Treated Nets (ITNs) are essential tools to reduce malaria transmission, morbidity and mortality, especially among children in sub-Saharan Africa. Tĥere is need for context-specific evidence regarding the prevalence of ITN usage and its associated factors in the region. The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of ITN use and factors associated with ITN utilization among infants in Southwestern Nigeria in order to close the ownership-use gap.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 2,264 caregivers of infants (1–20 weeks) attending 18 immunization clinics in Ogbomoso community in South-west Nigeria. Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire administered to the parent/guardian of the study participants. Logistic regression was carried out to identify the determinants of insecticide-treated nets use at 95% confidence interval and p-value < 0.05.Results62.6% of the participants owned ITNs, while 55.2% of children slept under an ITN the previous night. Chi-square analysis showed significant associations between mothers' (p = 0.006) and fathers' educational levels (p = 0.002) and ITN usage (p < 0.05). However, no sociodemographic factors was significantly associated with ITN use after adjusting for confounders in multiple logistic regression. Background

Malaria is a major public health challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, with children under five years of age bearing the most burden [1]. Globally, the prevalence of malaria was estimated at 282 million cases and mortality was approximately 610,000 deaths in 2024, with sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) accounting for about 94% of cases and 95% of deaths [1]. More than three-quarters of malaria-related deaths occur among children under the age of five [1]. Analyses of Malaria Indicator Survey data show the pooled prevalence of malaria among under-five children as 27% across multiple sub-Saharan Africa countries [2]. Within Nigeria, recent nationally representative surveys estimate malaria prevalence among under-five children to be around 24–37% [3, 4]

This disproportionate burden of malaria among this vulnerable population necessitates targeted interventions [1]. Various interventions have been employed to prevent and reduce malaria transmission, morbidity and mortality in the past years. These include the use of rapid diagnostic tests for diagnosis, artemisinin-based combination therapies for treatment, intermittent preventive treatment for malaria control among children under five, indoor residual spraying and insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) for vector control [5–8]. Although these interventions are effective, their implementation, especially in low- and middle-income countries, is often constrained by their cost [9, 10]. ITNs have been widely recognized as the most cost-effective malaria prevention strategies in malaria-endemic regions [11, 12]. They provide protection by acting as a physical barrier to mosquito bites, exerting insecticidal effects that repel or kill mosquitoes, and reducing community-level vector populations when coverage is high [13].

Nigeria is the single largest contributor to global malaria burden, responsible for about a quarter of all cases and 30% of malaria-related deaths worldwide [14]. ITN distribution has been implemented in Nigeria, mainly through mass distribution campaigns, antenatal and immunization services [5]. These efforts have led to marked increases in household ITN ownership nationally. However, utilization among under-five children remains suboptimal. In 2024, the Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) reported that around 43% of under-five children slept under an ITN the night before the survey, with the South West zone recording the lowest proportion (20%) [15].

Multiple studies across Nigeria has highlighted the associations of socio-demographic, household, caregiver and contextual factors with the utilization of ITN among under-five children [16–18]. Factors such as state of residence, age of the child, wealth status, number of nets in the household, and caregiver education are associated with ITN use among under-five children [17, 19]. Common barriers included excessive heat under the net, perceived low mosquito activity, discomfort or irritation from the insecticide, difficulty hanging nets and insufficient knowledge on causes of malaria contribute to non-use even in households that own ITNs [18, 20].

Despite evidence of ITN effectiveness and expanded distribution, ITN utilization among under-five children in South-West Nigeria remains suboptimal and below national and global targets. There is a need for context-specific evidence on the prevalence of ITN use and the socio-demographic, household, and behavioural determinants that drive or hinder utilization in this zone. This study specifically sought to answer the following research questions: (1) what is the prevalence of ITN ownership among caregivers of infants in Ogbomoso community in Southwestern Nigeria? (2) What are the reasons for non-ownership and non-utilization of ITN? (3) What sociodemographic factors are associated with ITN usage? The goal is to refine targeting of limited resources, close the ownership–use gap, and inform behaviour-change strategies that move beyond net distribution to promote consistent and correct ITN use.

Method

Study setting

We conducted the study in Ogbomoso, a sprawling urban city in Oyo State, southwestern Nigeria, with a population of about 744,835 [21]. Ogbomoso was purposefully selected because it has both urban and peri-urban settlements, malaria is holoendemic due to its tropical climate with year-round rainfall supporting mosquito breeding, and it has multiple health facilities where ITNs are distributed through routine health services and periodic mass campaigns, making it ideal for assessing ITN use among infants attending immunization clinics.

Study design

A cross-sectional survey design was adopted to obtain data between 2nd December 2019 and 31st August 2021.

Study population and selection

The study population comprised parents/guardians of infants between 1 to 20 weeks old attending 18 immunization centres in Ogbomoso from the first to the fourth immunization visits.

Inclusion Criteria: parents/guardians of infants aged 1 to 20 weeks attending any of the 18 immunization centres whose parents/guardians provided written informed consent. Because the study focused on early infancy to reduce age-related variability in caregiver practices and sleeping arrangements and to enable systematic sampling through immunization clinics, where caregiver attendance is most consistent during early infancy, infants older than 20 weeks were excluded from the study.

Participants were selected from each immunization centre using systematic sampling method. An average daily attendance across the 18 immunization centres ranged from 20 to 65 caregiver-infant pairs per clinic visit. Based on the daily attendance records, every 3rd eligible caregiver-infant pair in each immunization centre was selected following parental informed consent. This sampling interval was applied consistently across the study centres until the sample size was obtained.

Sample size determination

The sample size was obtained using the Leslie Kish formulas: n = Zα2pq/d2,

Where: n = sample size; Zα = standard normal deviation corresponding to 99% confidence level (Zα = 2.576); P = prevalence of insecticide treated nets among children; d = level of precision (0.05); q = 1-p. A minimum sample size of 1327 was obtained using p = 0.50 (maximum variability to obtain maximum sample size), and a design effect of 2.0 was used to account for the cluster design effect across the 18 immunization centres. Accounting for the 10% non-response rate, a minimum sample size of 1460 was obtained but to enhance the precision of the estimates and reduce sampling error, the sample size was increased to 2264. Therefore, all analyses were conducted using a sample of 2,264 respondents, and all participant provided informed consent before that were allowed to participate in the study.

Data collection instrument and method

Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire administered to the parent/guardian of the study participants. The questionnaire was adapted from the Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) malaria module. The questionnaire had four sections to capture respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics (Section A), knowledge about malaria and ITN (Section B), ITN ownership (Section C) and ITN utilization (Section D). The questionnaire was pre-tested among 50 participants in Ibadan, a neighbouring community, with Cronbach's alpha of 0.8, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Three community health extension workers were trained over two days on the administration of the questionnaire.

Statistical analyses

Daily, completed questionnaires were reviewed for completeness and consistency before being entered into the database. Data cleaning included range checks, verification of inconsistent responses against original questionnaires, and the removal of duplicate data that were discovered. Missing data were minimal and limited to a few questionnaire questions rather than the entire dataset; thus, complete-case analysis was used for the pertinent variables. Following recruitment, no participant was excluded from the research due to missing data. The completed questionnaires were sorted, collated, and cleaned. The data were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS version 31.0). Descriptive statistics, such as frequencies, percentages were used for categorical variables, while means and standard deviations were used for quantitative variables. Chi square test and logistic regression were carried out to show relationship between sociodemographic factors and the use of ITN. The analysis was conducted at a 95% confidence level. The findings were summarized using tables and charts. The goodness of fit of the multiple logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, while multicollinearity was examined using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Bowen University Teaching Hospital Research Ethics on July 15th, 2019 with approval number: BUTH/REC-2640. Informed consent was obtained from the parent or legal guardian of the children under the study. Participation in the study was voluntary. The participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without any penalty.

The bivariate analysis of the demographics factors associated with ITN use were also investigated. Chi-square analysis showed that only mothers’ educational level (p = 0.006) and fathers’ educational level (p = 0.002) were found to be significantly associated with ITN use. Discussion

This facility-based cross-sectional study utilized a large sample size to determine the prevalence and factors associated with ITN use among infants using immunization centres as a strategic research platform. Remarkably, the study found a relatively low ITN utilization rate to be 55.2%. There was also ITN ownership—ITN Utilization gap of 7%. The study also revealed moderate level of awareness of ITN usage in preventing malaria. The finding that ITN was thought to be expensive despite its free distribution in many centres in Nigeria has important policy implications. Notably, the study also revealed some critical misconceptions regarding the use of ITNs and their implications for health education.

The ITN utilization prevalence in this study was 55.2%. This is lower than findings across other sub-Saharan African countries: Solanke reported 71.8% in Nigeria [22], Barrow reported 63.4% in Gambia [23], Tassembedo reported 58% in Burkina Faso [24], Aoki in Angola reported 62.7% [25] and Merga in Ethiopia reported 69.2% [12]. This could be due to different socio-demographics and geographies due to residence of all the study populations in different parts of Africa. However, a relatively lower ITN utilization rate of 39.7% was reported by Odoko et al. (2023) [26] in a study among under-5 children in South-south Nigeria may be due reflect the difference in the study population (infants being used in the present study compared to under-five children used in the study by Odoko). Our observation that 55.2% of the study infants slept under an ITN is disheartening given the burden of severe malaria and its complications in this vulnerable age group.

In this study, knowledge of ITN use for malaria prevention was noted as 66.2%, indicating moderate level of awareness. However, this implies that up to one-third of the responding caregivers/guardians lack this essential knowledge of malaria prevention. This gap in knowledge may contribute to the observed ITN ownership-utilization gap recorded in this study. A much lower knowledge level (45.7%) was reported in a recent study by Adeleke et al., (2025) [27] among caregivers of under-five children in Nigeria. The variations in knowledge across different studies: 77.3% by Odoko et al. (2023) [26] in Nigeria among pregnant women; 82.6% by Amoran et al. (2011) in Nigeria among the youth [28], 67.0% in Ghana by Dun-Dery et al. (2022) [29] and 61.9% in Ethiopia by Merga et al., (2024) [12] may reflect the different degree of awareness among the various study populations, their educational attainments and malaria health promotion activities in different regions.

Similarly, ITN ownership rate was found to be 62.6%. Despite the mass distribution campaigns of two nets per household in Nigeria over the past decades [30], the 62.6% ITN ownership rate recorded in this study falls below the universal coverage target of 80% recommended by the World Health Organization [31]. Similar findings have been reported as ITN ownership rates: 64.6% by Ezire et al. (2015) in a National survey in 2011 [32], 62.6% by Ogidan et al. (2025) in 2018 [33]; 57.0% by Eleazar et al. (2022) in South-East Nigeria [34], 77.0% by Kuetche et al. (2023) in Cameroun [35], 77.4% by Sangere et al. (2012) in Uganda [36], and 69.2% by Scott et al. (2021) in Mozambique [37]. Relatively much lower ownership rates of 44.8% was reported by Onyiah in Abuja, Nigeria [38]. Similarly rate of 48.2% and 28.8% were reported by Alawode and Ankomah [38–40]. This gap in malaria prevention suggests the need for additional ITN delivery strategies to reach all households.

Furthermore, this study finding of cost as a major reason for non-ownership of ITN among 34.4% of non-owners is a bit worrisome, given that ITNs are typically distributed free of charge in many government health facilities in Nigeria. This may suggest low knowledge of the free distribution, informal charges at some health facilities, or out-of-stock syndrome. The non-response rate above one-third (39.1%) indicates that many caregivers/guardians had no identifiable reason for non-ownership of ITN. Some respondents (5.2%) even reported not knowing where to get ITNs. The findings are similar to the reports of a study conducted in Mozambique by Scott et al. that the most common reasons for not having/owning an ITN are unavailability of ITNs, and not knowing where to obtain the ITNs [37]. There is a need to improve malaria health promotion literacy and engagement with malaria prevention messages among caregivers in Nigeria.

In this study, misconceptions about ITNs such as excessive heat, belief that mosquitoes still enter the net, and dislike for ITNs account for non-utilization of ITNs. A little above a third (38.1%) of the respondents had no specific reason for not using ITN. This is similar to the findings in Nigeria, Ghana and Uganda, where heat was identified as a major reason for non-utilization of ITN [20, 36, 41–43]. Other reasons for non-utilization of ITN among children under-5 include the perception that nets are not needed and the misconception that there is no mosquito [42]. Udoh et al. also found ‘low mosquito activity’ as another reason for non-utilization of ITN [20]. The different reasons identified from different studies reflect multi-dimensional factors influencing the utilization of ITN by people in different communities and countries. Health education messages should be tailored towards addressing these misconceptions to demonstrate proper ITN use and there is a need to develop more user-friendly ITN materials.

Finally, the study found that mothers’ educational level (p = 0.006) and fathers’ educational level (p = 0.002) were significantly associated with ITN use (p < 0.05) in bivariate analysis. However, neither maternal nor paternal education remained significant predictors of ITN usage after adjusting for potential confounders in multiple logistic regression. This contrasts with the findings by Barrow in Gambia, Kuetche in Cameroun, Merga in Ethiopia and Aoki in Angola where education was a significant factor influencing under-five ITN utilization. [12, 23, 25, 35]. The variations in the determinants found in different studies underscore the importance of context-specific studies to inform evidence-based targeted interventions.

Limitations

The study, however, has some drawbacks. The facility-based approach may exclude non-health-seeking households. This may result in overestimation of ITN usage. There is also the possibility of recall bias despite the short duration. The cross-sectional study design also limit the ability to establish causality and temporal relationships between studied variables and ITN utilization. The high non-response rate of 52.5% to the question on reasons for not sleeping under an ITN suggests the need for a mixed-methods research to provide deeper insights into behavioural determinants of ITN use. Similarly, some potentially important variables such as wealth index, housing characteristics and sleeping arrangements were not fully explored in the adjusted model, which may have influenced the observed associations. Lastly, ITN utilization in this study was assessed based on whether the infant slept under an ITN the night preceding the survey, which might not reflect consistent ITN utilization practice.

Conclusion

The study findings of substantial ITN ownership-utilization gap, moderate knowledge level of ITN use for malaria prevention and misconceptions regarding ITN ownership and utilization have several implications for malaria control policy and practice in Nigeria and other endemic countries. These findings suggest that addressing misconceptions regarding ITN ownership and utilization, as well as reducing the ownership–utilization gap, may contribute to improving ITN coverage and utilization among infants in Nigeria.
