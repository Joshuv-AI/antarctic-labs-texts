Successful hysteroscopic myomectomy of a submucosal leiomyoma in an adolescent female: a case report
Authors: Abdelrahman AlAshqar, Christina Lepore, Terri Huynh, Alla Vash-Margita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Uterine leiomyomas are rare in adolescents, making their diagnosis and management uniquely challenging. This case highlights a moderately sized, symptomatic submucosal leiomyoma in an adolescent female, successfully treated with hysteroscopic resection after the failure of medical management, and discusses key surgical considerations for effectively managing submucosal leiomyomas in this population.Case presentation:An 18-year-old nulliparous female presented several times with heavy menstrual bleeding despite an outpatient trial of oral contraceptives. Transabdominal ultrasound revealed a moderately sized submucosal leiomyoma measuring 3.8 × 4.1 × 3.6 cm. The patient was counseled on hysteroscopic myomectomy after the failure of multiple medical regimens to control her abnormal uterine bleeding. Preoperatively, she underwent a pelvic MRI to better delineate the leiomyoma characteristics. Upon performing a hysteroscopy, a FIGO type 2 leiomyoma was identified projecting from the left anterolateral wall. Diluted vasopressin was injected into the leiomyoma proper, and hysteroscopic resection was successfully performed using a MyoSure® device. The patient had an uncomplicated postoperative course, and her abnormal uterine bleeding has resolved.Clinical discussion:Uterine leiomyomas are rare in adolescents and are most often intramural, though submucosal lesions can cause severe bleeding. Refractory abnormal uterine bleeding should prompt early imaging. While ultrasound is first-line, MRI can aid in surgical planning. This case supports hysteroscopic myomectomy as a safe, fertility-sparing option when medical therapy fails.Conclusion:Uterine leiomyomas, though rare in adolescents, remain an important diagnostic consideration in patients with persistent heavy menstrual bleeding. Prompt imaging assessment and timely, fertility-sparing surgical intervention may reduce morbidity and support favorable long-term reproductive outcomes.

Introduction

Uterine leiomyomas, commonly known as fibroids, represent the most common benign neoplastic entity of the female reproductive tract and are found in approximately 80% of reproductive-age women[1]. While often clinically quiescent, uterine leiomyomas remain a considerable cause of morbidity and altered quality of life in the women harboring them, and to date, they are the most common indication for hysterectomy in the United States[1]. Uterine leiomyomas classically regress after menopause secondary to a state of hormonal deprivation limiting their growth and, likewise, are seldom seen in prepubertal and adolescent females, with only a handful of reports in the literature describing their occurrence and treatment approaches[2–5]. Despite their low prevalence, leiomyoma presentation in this young population can be dramatic and debilitating, ranging from hemodynamically significant bleeding to symptomatic pelvic masses requiring surgical intervention[2,3]. Awareness of leiomyoma occurrence in young females, although unusual, hastens their recognition, limits extensive diagnostic workup, and allows for prompt targeted intervention and expeditious recovery of impacted quality of life[3,4]. We herein present a case of a moderately sized, submucosal leiomyoma in an adolescent female successfully treated with hysteroscopic resection after failing medical management. Case report

An 18-year-old nulliparous African American female presented to the emergency department with heavy vaginal bleeding and chest pressure. Her gynecologic history was significant for heavy menstrual bleeding with passage of large clots, which required frequent tampon changes. She attained her menarche at age 11, and her cycles were otherwise regular, with bleeding lasting 7 days each cycle. She denied headaches, vision changes, and heat or cold intolerance. She was sexually active in a monogamous relationship with a male partner and intermittently used condoms. Her past medical history was notable for asthma, anxiety, and depression, but her surgical history was unremarkable. The patient reported a family history of fibroids but denied any history of bleeding disorders. On initial assessment, she was found to be markedly tachycardic with a heart rate in the 130s but was otherwise hemodynamically stable. Her abdominal exam was unremarkable without palpable masses, and she had minimal active bleeding on speculum exam. No genital trauma or abnormal lesions were seen.

Workup in the emergency department was notable for a hemoglobin level of 4.9 g/dL and a negative pregnancy test. She received two units of packed red blood cells for symptomatic anemia and was admitted for inpatient observation. Workup for bleeding disorders, including a coagulation profile and von Willebrand panel, was negative. A transabdominal ultrasound revealed a left anterolateral leiomyoma measuring 3.8 × 4.1 × 3.6 cm. She was started on an oral medroxyprogesterone taper (10 mg twice a day for 3 days, followed by once a day for 3 days, then 5 mg once a day until seen in the clinic) while inpatient and was discharged home after her bleeding improved. She returned to the emergency department 3 months later with worsening bleeding. She was hemodynamically stable but was again found to be acutely anemic, with a hemoglobin level of 5.5 g/dL, requiring transfusion with two units of packed red blood cells. Her medroxyprogesterone dose was increased to 10 mg twice a day, and a transvaginal ultrasound showed no interval growth in the leiomyoma compared to her prior ultrasound (Fig. 1). After discharge, the patient was seen in the outpatient pediatric and adolescent gynecology clinic, where she was counseled on treatment options. A shared decision was made to proceed with a hysteroscopic myomectomy, given the ineffectiveness of medical management, which had been attempted for 7 months after her initial presentation. To help inform surgical planning and further characterize the leiomyoma, a pelvic MRI was performed, which showed the same FIGO type 2 submucosal leiomyoma extending from the left anterior upper uterine segment into the endometrial cavity (Fig. 2). We proceeded with an exam under anesthesia and hysteroscopic resection of the fibroid. Prior to surgery, the patient reported persistent heavy menstrual bleeding, and her hemoglobin was 11 g/dL. Upon cavity entry during hysteroscopy, a 4-cm FIGO type 2 leiomyoma was noted projecting from the left anterolateral wall. Before initiating the resection, 10 cc of diluted vasopressin (20 U vasopressin:100 mL injectable saline) were injected into the leiomyoma proper to minimize fluid absorption and reduce bleeding via the SidekickTM Needle. Hysteroscopic resection was successfully performed using a MyoSure® XL device with a fluid deficit of 900cc of normal saline (Supplemental Digital Content Video, available at: http://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A63). The procedure was completed when the normal cavity shape was restored. Final pathology was consistent with leiomyoma. The patient was discharged home on the day of surgery. She recovered well postoperatively, and her bleeding pattern has significantly improved. Discussion

This case represents a rare occurrence of a moderately sized, symptomatic submucosal leiomyoma in an adolescent female who failed medical management and underwent a successful hysteroscopic resection. Although submucosal leiomyomas are exceedingly infrequent in adolescent patients, gynecologists providing care to adolescent patients should be alerted to their possible occurrence in atypical situations. Of note, providers may have limited understanding of the unique developmental aspects, prevalence, and treatment guidelines of leiomyomas in adolescents[5]. Pelvic ultrasonography, often forgone in young females with presumed non-anatomical etiologies of abnormal uterine bleeding, can easily detect the presence of leiomyomas, delineate their characteristics, and help guide the need for additional imaging and treatment options[3]. It, therefore, should be readily incorporated in the diagnostic workup, especially in patients with suggestive symptoms or those who fail empiric medical management. In our patient, for example, the presence of heavy menstrual bleeding with otherwise regular cycles, remote menarche (>2 years from the index event), dysmenorrhea, and a negative coagulopathy workup did not favor a non-structural cause of her bleeding (anovulation or coagulopathy), thus warranting pelvic imaging. MRI is not always necessary for surgical planning, especially prior to a hysteroscopic myomectomy, but was performed in our case to help assess the intramyometrial extension of the leiomyoma, choose the appropriate resection equipment, and determine the need for hemostatic agents based on size.

When extrapolating from the adult population, management of abnormal uterine bleeding secondary to leiomyoma in adolescents can be categorized into expectant, medical, and surgical approaches, with a primary goal to restore quality of life and correct anemia while preserving fertility. Although their efficacy for leiomyoma-related bleeding has not been established in an adolescent population, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and oral contraceptive pills are among the first-line agents, given their documented safety and widespread use for other indications in young females[3]. On the other hand, other lines of medical management tailored to the adult leiomyoma population, such as gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues and aromatase inhibitors, may either have more drastic side effects or are still being investigated for this indication and should, therefore, be used with caution[7].

Hysteroscopic resection of uterine leiomyomas in adolescents has been reported and is performed in a similar fashion to that in adults[3,5]. FIGO type 0 and 1 leiomyomas are classically treated hysteroscopically, given their predominant intracavitary component, rendering their resection more successful and decreasing the chances of recurrence and the need for repeat surgery[7]. In our patient, the decision was made to proceed with hysteroscopic resection of the FIGO type 2 leiomyoma, considering her heavy uterine bleeding, anemia requiring blood transfusion, as well as the accessible intracavitary component based on the MRI findings. The leiomyoma was not resected entirely, given the significant intramural component and higher risk of uterine perforation. The myomectomy was performed with the goal of restoring the endometrial cavity and improving her abnormal uterine bleeding. Alternative resection devices, including the bipolar or monopolar resectoscope, can be safely considered in this setting for more efficient and thorough resection. Interval reduction in cavity distention can also be used to allow the myometrium to contract, resulting in additional leiomyoma tissue projecting into the cavity for safe and complete resection, a technique that was employed in this patient to remove a maximal amount of fibroid tissue. Alternatively, resection of the leiomyoma within the myometrium can be considered in the correct clinical context and is dependent on surgeon comfort and experience. In addition, a paracervical block with dilute vasopressin or lidocaine with epinephrine can be used in place of vasopressin injected directly into the leiomyoma.

Nonetheless, patients with a similar lesion opting for hysteroscopic resection should be particularly counseled about the risk of recurrence, especially in young patients who have acquired such a lesion at an unusually early age[3]. In a recent large study evaluating the long-term risk of reintervention after surgical leiomyoma resection in women aged 18–50 years, hysteroscopic myomectomy was associated with a 37% risk of reintervention 7 years after the index procedure[8]. Importantly, this risk was noted to decrease with age and to be 35% higher in parous than nulliparous women[8]. Perioperative counseling can also include fertility-related considerations, such as the risk of Asherman syndrome, the risk of placenta accreta, and the ability to labor in the future. Asherman syndrome, or amenorrhea or infertility attributed to intrauterine adhesions, has been reported in ~ 20% of women undergoing hysteroscopic myomectomy in one study[9]. In addition, women with submucosal uterine leiomyomas who undergo hysteroscopic removal have similar birth outcomes to those who do not[10]. Lastly, a recently published Taiwanese study of 11 255 pregnant women with a history of myomectomy showed a higher risk of placenta accreta spectrum in women with such a history than in women without (adjusted odds ratio, 2.28; 95% confidence interval, 1.85–2.81)[11]. Of note, this risk was highest among patients undergoing a hysteroscopic myomectomy (adjusted odds ratio, 3.88; 95% confidence interval, 2.68–5.63)[11]. Such findings represent important data to incorporate while counseling adolescents to help set expectations regarding treatment effectiveness and, by extension, make well-informed decisions. In addition, awareness of recurrence risk facilitates a speedy recognition of symptoms on the patient’s part, allowing for targeted assessment, expeditious treatment, and adverse sequela prevention.

The strength of this case lies in its rarity and detailed documentation of a successful fertility-sparing, minimally invasive approach to a submucosal leiomyoma in an adolescent. It highlights the importance of early imaging, multidisciplinary evaluation, and individualized management in this unique population. However, long-term outcomes, including recurrence and reproductive implications, cannot be fully assessed.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of considering uterine leiomyomas in the differential diagnosis of abnormal uterine bleeding in adolescents, even though their occurrence is rare. Early imaging and timely fertility-sparing surgical management can effectively resolve symptoms, prevent morbidity, and restore quality of life.
