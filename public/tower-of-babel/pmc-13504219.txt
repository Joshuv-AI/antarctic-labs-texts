A Mimic of Metastatic Malignancy: Dietary Supplement‐Associated Drug‐Induced Lung Injury Presenting as Multiple Pulmonary Nodules With an Acute Fibrinous and Organizing Pneumonia Pattern
Authors: Yuto Goto, Yusuke Nakamura, Nana Yazawa, Kosuke Natsume, Nobuhiko Tsukada, Azusa Tsukada, Nobuaki Yoshida, Yuri Nomura, Akihiro Kawabe, Hiroaki Arakawa, Akihiro Takemasa, Kazuyuki Ishida, Yasuo Shimizu, Seiji Niho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDrug‐induced lung injury (DILI) may present with diverse radiological and histopathological manifestations. We report a case of dietary supplement‐associated DILI presenting as multiple randomly distributed pulmonary nodules mimicking metastatic malignancy and histopathologically exhibiting an acute fibrinous and organizing pneumonia (AFOP) pattern. A 62‐year‐old man who had taken a dietary supplement containing fermented grain extract, malt extract, and coenzyme Q10 for approximately 15 years developed multiple pulmonary nodules accompanied by marked peripheral eosinophilia. Computed tomography initially raised suspicion of metastatic malignancy. Bronchoalveolar lavage demonstrated eosinophilia, and transbronchial lung cryobiopsy revealed an AFOP pattern with prominent eosinophilic inflammation. The diagnosis of dietary supplement‐associated DILI was established based on the exposure history, exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and spontaneous regression of the pulmonary nodules and improvement in eosinophilia following discontinuation of the dietary supplement. This case highlights that dietary supplement‐associated DILI may mimic metastatic malignancy by presenting as multiple pulmonary nodules, while histopathological evaluation may reveal an AFOP pattern with marked eosinophilic inflammation.

Introduction

Drug‐induced lung injury (DILI) encompasses a broad spectrum of clinicopathological manifestations with diverse radiological and histopathological findings. Because no single feature is diagnostic, the diagnosis requires comprehensive assessment of the drug exposure history, imaging findings, histopathology, exclusion of alternative diagnoses and the clinical course after withdrawal of the suspected agent [1, 2].

Acute fibrinous and organizing pneumonia (AFOP), first described by Beasley et al., is a histopathological pattern characterized by prominent intra‐alveolar fibrin deposition (“fibrin balls”) and organizing pneumonia in the absence of hyaline membranes, a hallmark of diffuse alveolar damage [3]. AFOP has been reported in association with infections, autoimmune diseases, malignancies and DILI, suggesting that it may represent a nonspecific histopathological pattern of acute lung injury rather than a distinct clinicopathological entity [3, 4].

Dietary supplements have increasingly been recognized as potential causes of DILI. However, dietary supplement‐associated DILI presenting as multiple randomly distributed pulmonary nodules with an AFOP pattern has not, to our knowledge, been reported previously. We herein report a case of dietary supplement‐associated DILI presenting as multiple randomly distributed pulmonary nodules mimicking metastatic malignancy, with an AFOP pattern and marked eosinophilic inflammation on histopathology.

Case Report

A 62‐year‐old man with a history of remote pulmonary tuberculosis and chronic kidney disease was referred to our hospital after chest computed tomography (CT) performed for fever and chills revealed multiple randomly distributed pulmonary nodules. He had never smoked and had no history of occupational dust exposure, bird breeding or humidifier use. He had been taking a commercially available dietary supplement containing fermented grain extract, malt extract and coenzyme Q10 for approximately 15 years. There had been no recent change in the dosage or brand of the supplement, and no other new medications or dietary supplements had been introduced. Although there was no specific event that initially suggested an association between the supplement and the pulmonary abnormalities, DILI was included in the differential diagnosis, as is routinely considered when evaluating newly detected pulmonary abnormalities. Therefore, despite the low initial suspicion, the dietary supplement was discontinued as part of the diagnostic evaluation.

Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis (14,000 cells/μL), an elevated C‐reactive protein level (3.56 mg/dL), and marked peripheral eosinophilia (28.3%; 3960 cells/μL). Autoimmune serological tests were negative, and investigations for fungal infection, HIV infection and tuberculosis were unremarkable. 18F‐FDG PET/CT demonstrated mild uptake in the pulmonary nodules (SUV_max 2.04 in the early phase and 2.24 in the delayed phase), without evidence of an extrapulmonary primary malignancy.

Follow‐up CT obtained 5 days after discontinuation of the dietary supplement showed slight regression of the pulmonary nodules (Figure 1C,D). However, bronchoscopy was performed to exclude infection and malignancy and to obtain pathological confirmation.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid obtained from the right middle lobe revealed marked eosinophilia (39.2%). Microbiological cultures and cytological examination were negative. Histopathological examination of transbronchial lung cryobiopsy (TBLC) specimens obtained from the right upper lobe demonstrated prominent intra‐alveolar fibrin deposition (‘fibrin balls’) and organizing pneumonia without hyaline membranes, consistent with an AFOP pattern. Marked eosinophilic infiltration involving both the alveolar septa and airspaces was also observed (Figure 2).

(A) Haematoxylin and eosin staining (magnification, ×10) demonstrating patchy intra‐alveolar fibrin deposition and organizing pneumonia. (B) Higher‐magnification view of the boxed area showing prominent intra‐alveolar fibrin aggregates (‘fibrin balls’) accompanied by conspicuous eosinophilic infiltration involving both the alveolar septa and airspaces. No hyaline membranes are identified.

The drug‐induced lymphocyte stimulation test for the dietary supplement was negative. Infectious diseases, malignancy and autoimmune disorders were excluded. Chest CT obtained 5 months after discontinuation of the dietary supplement demonstrated further regression of the pulmonary nodules (Figure 1E,F). Peripheral eosinophilia transiently peaked at 7700 cells/μL before gradually decreasing to 1220 cells/μL at 1 month and 860 cells/μL at 3 months without corticosteroid therapy. Taken together, the exposure history, pathological findings, exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and favourable clinical course after withdrawal supported the diagnosis of dietary supplement‐associated DILI.

Discussion

We describe a case of dietary supplement‐associated DILI that presented as multiple randomly distributed pulmonary nodules mimicking metastatic malignancy and histopathologically exhibited an AFOP pattern with marked eosinophilic inflammation. Although the radiological findings initially raised suspicion of metastatic malignancy, the exposure history, eosinophilia, pathological findings, exclusion of alternative diagnoses and the spontaneous regression of the pulmonary nodules and the improvement in peripheral eosinophilia following discontinuation of the dietary supplement strongly supported the diagnosis.

The predominant histopathological finding was an AFOP pattern characterized by intra‐alveolar fibrin deposition and organizing pneumonia. In contrast to the typical pathological features of AFOP, however, this case also showed marked eosinophilia in both peripheral blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, together with prominent eosinophilic infiltration of the alveolar septa and airspaces. These findings suggest an AFOP pattern accompanied by eosinophilic inflammation, possibly reflecting an allergic immune response to the dietary supplement.

The radiological manifestations of AFOP are heterogeneous. A systematic review reported that the most common CT finding in AFOP was patchy or mass‐like air‐space consolidation (54.8%), followed by ground‐glass opacity (23.8%) and reticular and linear opacity (16.7%), whereas multiple pulmonary nodules accounted for only 4.8% of cases [5]. This case therefore represents an unusual radiological manifestation of AFOP and illustrates that dietary supplement‐associated DILI may present as multiple randomly distributed pulmonary nodules mimicking metastatic malignancy.

The diagnosis of DILI requires a comprehensive assessment because no single clinical, radiological, pathological, or laboratory finding is diagnostic [1, 2]. Although the drug‐induced lymphocyte stimulation test was negative, the exposure history, exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and spontaneous regression of the pulmonary nodules together with improvement in peripheral eosinophilia following discontinuation of the dietary supplement strongly supported the diagnosis.

The reason why lung injury developed after approximately 15 years of supplement use remains unclear. No recent change in the dosage or brand of the supplement was identified. Nevertheless, long‐term use alone does not exclude a causal association between the supplement and the lung injury. Notably, peripheral eosinophilia remained elevated (860 cells/μL) 3 months after discontinuation of the dietary supplement. This finding raises the possibility that a pre‐existing tendency toward eosinophilia may have increased the patient's susceptibility to lung injury. However, eosinophil counts before disease onset were unavailable, and this possibility could not be evaluated further.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of dietary supplement‐associated DILI presenting as multiple randomly distributed pulmonary nodules with an AFOP pattern. Although the radiological findings initially suggested metastatic malignancy, marked eosinophilia and transbronchial lung cryobiopsy demonstrating an AFOP pattern led to the correct diagnosis of dietary supplement‐associated DILI.

In conclusion, dietary supplement‐associated DILI should be considered in patients presenting with multiple pulmonary nodules accompanied by eosinophilia, even when metastatic malignancy is strongly suspected. Clinicians should perform comprehensive medication and supplement reconciliation, including long‐standing products, in any patient with newly identified pulmonary nodules that do not follow a typical pattern of primary lung malignancy. Histopathological evaluation, including transbronchial lung cryobiopsy when appropriate, may reveal an AFOP pattern and provide key evidence for establishing the diagnosis.
