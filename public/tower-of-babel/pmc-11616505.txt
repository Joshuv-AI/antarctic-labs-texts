Copper-Nickel/SiC composites for applications on contact electrodes
Authors: Isidro Cruz-Cruz, Roberto Hernández-Maya, José Emiliano Reséndiz-Hernández, Daniel Olvera-Trejo, Oscar Martínez-Romero, Nicolas A. Ulloa Castillo, Alex Elías-Zúñiga
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Novel copper-nickel matrix composites reinforced with silicon carbide (SiC) micro particles for metal contact applications were manufactured by powder metallurgy technology and were experimentally characterized. Cu and Cu alloys are commonly used as metal contact for either vacuum, oil, or SF6 in low-voltage circuit breaker devices, but their application in environments with the presence of oxygen is limited due to their tendency to form high-resistance copper oxides. Thus, the addition of Ni as an alloying element provides resistance to both humidity and several corrosive environments and increases the composites' hardness, mechanical strength, and wear resistance. Moreover, SiC was chosen due to its contribution to mechanical strength, mouldability, low production cost, and non-reactive nature at ordinary temperatures. Here, three SiC particle size distributions were considered, and it was found that the composites' physical properties are also dependent on the ceramic loading. After evaluating these properties, two specific SiC concentrations were potentially considered for electrode applications: 10 wt% and 20 wt%. The obtained hardness values are in the range of 55–65 HR30T which guarantees the necessary strength without detriment of the contact area whereas the welding is minimized. In addition to a homogeneous particle distribution in the solid material, specific wear resistance of ∼10−5 mm3N−1m−1 and electrical conductivities of ∼20 %IACS were obtained. Considering these parameters, Cu-Ni/SiC composites' performance is not compromised compared to commercial Ag/WC materials commonly used in metal contact applications.

Introduction

Efficient energy transport and storage are critical for modern society, as even the smallest devices require energy in mechanical, thermal, or electrical forms to operate effectively. The efficiency of electrical charge transport depends on the nature of the conducting material and the interface formed by the electrodes [1]. There are several alternatives for electrical conductors depending on their application: for high-power circuits or heavy-current contacts (∼106 A), metals such as copper, aluminum, and their alloys are preferred [2]. Conversely, low-current consumption devices (∼10−6 A or less) often use noble metal coatings, while polymer materials are gaining attention for their environmental friendliness, lightweight, and low cost [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

Circuit breakers, widely used since the early 20th century, require materials that ensure safe and efficient current transfer and control. In these devices, electrical metal contacts are crucial components because if they fail, the electrical current is interrupted or the charge transport can be significantly diminished due to inefficient charge injection into the circuit or device of interest [1]. Therefore, the electrical metal contacts must meet specific requirements according to their operating conditions, including thermodynamic stability, low friction coefficient (for sliding contacts), low weldability, high electric erosion resistance, and adequate values of wear resistance, electrical conductivity, density, and mechanical properties, among others.

Metal contacts can be classified as stationary and moving contacts, typically made from silver-based composites manufactured by powder metallurgy [1,7]. These materials meet stringent requirements, particularly high corrosion resistance which is essential for this type of application [[8], [9], [10], [11]].

Various alternatives for metal contact materials, such as Ni-C-Ag-N coatings [12] W/Cu composites manufactured by liquid infiltration route [13,14], hard carbon phase [15], and micro-layered composite materials synthesized by high-speed electron-beam evaporation-condensation [16], have been investigated. These alternatives underscore the ongoing research aimed at enhancing the performance of contact materials.

Copper is another common conducting material with properties well-suited for metal contact applications. It is malleable, ductile, has an electrical conductivity of 5.99 × 107 S/m (only surpassed by silver), and boasts exceptional thermal conductivity of 394 W m−1 K−1. In addition, it is easy to join, has better thermo-mechanical properties, and exhibits higher erosion resistance and arc resistance than silver [1,17]. In terms of sustainability, it is possible to incorporate recycled copper in the manufacturing process without compromising mechanical properties after an adequate recycling process [18]. Despite these advantages, copper exhibits poor mechanical properties and low resistance to aggressive environments composed of nitric acid, organic acids, aqueous ammonia, and ammonium salts, for example, and its oxides are thermodynamically stable, which adversely affects the contact resistance [19]. Consequently, copper-based metal contacts are typically used in controlled environments like SF6, oil, or vacuum, whereas silver-based contacts are preferred for use in air [1]. At this stage, copper-based metal matrix composites have emerged as a solution for improving the physicochemical properties of copper-based electrical contacts. Reinforcement agents (or dispersoids) in the copper matrix can be particulate fibers (whisker, short or continuous fibers), or sheets, either meso or nanostructured [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. Considering electrical properties, the reinforcement phase can be metallic [24,[28], [29], [30]] or ceramic [22,[31], [32], [33], [34]].

Ceramic reinforcement generally enhances high-temperature performance, improving mechanical and wear resistance without significantly deteriorating the metal matrix's thermal and electrical characteristics [35]. Silicon carbide (SiC) is a particularly promising ceramic for contact electrode application due to its high thermal and electrical conductivity, mechanical strength, mouldability, low production cost, and chemical inertness at ordinary temperatures [36]. However, Cu/SiC composites still require improved corrosion resistance; thus, incorporating metallic elements such as nickel into copper-based composites becomes a solution.

The common copper alloys use Al, Ni, Si, Sn, and Zn. These Cu alloys exhibit enhanced creep resistance with lower electrical and thermal conductivities when compared to pure Cu. There is evidence that by combining metals with ceramics such as nitrides and silicon carbides, mechanical properties, wear resistance, electrical and thermal properties are improved. A good overview of the processing methods, limitations, and critical characteristics of several copper alloys can be found in Ref. [37]. Therefore, the use of copper-nickel matrix composites with silicon carbide (SiC) alloys reinforced with ceramic micro-particles is becoming popular because these composite materials satisfy the electric industry technical specifications for producing welding electrodes, contactors, switches, electrical contacts, and circuit breakers to name a few [38].

Somani and Gupta investigated the mechanical and tribological behavior of copper composites reinforced with titanium carbide (TiC) nanoparticles, finding a significant enhancement in the hardness and tensile strength of 88.76 % and 37.26 %, respectively, with improved wear resistance [39]. Moreover, the surface roughness, material removal rate, and wear rate of an electrically discharged machining (EDM) process electrode made from Cu-SiC composite and pure copper were investigated in Ref. [40]. There, experimental results revealed that the Cu-SiC composite tool has better performance during machining hard materials with EDM [40]. Recently, Shao et al. found that adding Ni microparticles in the copper matrix forms an oxidation resistance barrier for the diffusion of Cu2+ ions during the oxidation process, which inhibits the further oxidation of Cu and Ni [41].

Hernández-Maya et al. used nickel in Cu/WC composites (from 0.5 to 1.5 wt%) because of its beneficial effects in producing contact electrode materials [42], providing resistance to humidity and several corrosive environments in both liquid and gas phases [43]. Additionally, M. V. Lungu et al. included 1.5 wt% Ni as a sintering additive in the tungsten-copper composites to improve their electrical and mechanical properties during electric arc breaking [13]. The addition of Ni increased the hardness, mechanical strength, and wear resistance of the composites.

To the best of our knowledge, Cu-Ni/SiC composites have not been studied for metal contact applications, highlighting the novelty of this research. This study aims to explore the potential of Cu-Ni/SiC composites for mass production, emphasizing their cost-effectiveness and sustainability. This study investigates the physical properties of Cu-Ni/SiC composites, focusing on surface hardness, electrical conductivity, density, and wear resistance.

In this study, metal composites were processed by powder metallurgy, a method with several advantages. It allows for efficient utilization of materials, good surface finish, facilitates the manufacture of complex shapes, is cost-effective, produces materials with uniform chemical composition, preserves the purity throughout the process, is energy-efficient, and environment-friendly, among many other benefits [44].

Experimental

Materials

Copper and Ni powders were acquired from Metalor Technologies Operations Mexico. Regarding SiC powder, it was provided by Tecno Abrasivos del Norte, and three size distributions were obtained from the raw material by sieving with 230 and 325 meshes (pore sizes 63 μm and 45 μm, respectively). SiC powder with particles sizing 45–63 μm (labeled as SiC-A) was obtained whereas the SiC powder labeled as SiC-B was obtained after the use of the 45 μm mesh (particles sizing <45 μm). The third size distribution was obtained after milling the coarser SiC particles (>63 μm) for 21 h to obtain a particle size lesser than 20 μm (SiC-C). The particle size of SiC-A and SiC-B was controlled through sieving, while the size of SiC-C micro particles was adjusted by varying the ball-milling time. Further purification of the powders was not performed.

Fabrication of Cu-Ni/SiC composites using powder metallurgy technology

Here, powder metallurgy is used to produce copper composites by following the same procedure described in Ref. [45]. The Ni content in the copper-nickel matrix was selected based on the findings reported in Ref. [42]. The Ni concentration was chosen to enhance corrosion and wear resistance. Therefore, in this phase of the composite material development, copper, silicon carbide, and nickel powders were homogeneous, and dispersed at several SiC concentrations (ranging from 0 to 40 wt%) with a constant Ni content (1.5 wt%) in a planetary ball-mill (Retsch, PM 100) without any process control agent. Here, the milling process was performed at 150 rpm for 1.5 h using 10 mm diameter zirconia balls at the ball-to-powder ratio 1:1. Then, the composite powders were uniaxially compressed in a Bench Top Laboratory Manual Press, model 4128 (Carver, Inc., Wabash, IN, USA) at 1330 MPa, with the assistance of a tungsten carbide mold. Lower pressure values were also considered, but poor cohesion of the green compact material was obtained (mainly at high SiC concentrations). After that, the green compacts were sintered in a GSL-1700X tube furnace at 900 °C for 4 h under an industrial Ar atmosphere. The heating ramp was fit at 10 °C/min and the cooling at 5 °C/min. During the heating, an Ar flow of 0.9 L/min was established to remove residual air in the system, from room temperature up to it reached 300 °C. After that, the system was isolated by valves. Finally, the samples were subjected to a re-pressing process at 830 MPa to improve the physical properties of the copper matrix composites, by using the same WC mold to produce cylindric samples with a diameter of 15 mm and ∼2.5 mm thick.

Characterization

Volume-based size distributions for the powders were analyzed by laser light scattering using a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 without any dispersant agent. Surface hardness was determined with a Wilson Rockwell 524T Hardness Tester, by considering both the 15T and 30T testing scales for the as-sintered and re-pressed samples, respectively. Measurements were performed with a 1/16” tungsten carbide ball indenter following the ASTM E18 standard. At least five measurements per sample were taken at room temperature. Surface hardness was considered here because, in a mass production scenario, suppliers of electrical contacts use hardness values as a quality measurement to ensure the properties of the metal contacts, along with the microstructural status of the compound.

The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the solid composite materials were characterized by using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS model EVO MA 25, Carl Zeiss Microscopy Ltd, Cambridge, UK). Both secondary and backscattered electron micrographs were considered for the study. In addition, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopic (EDS) analyses were performed to determine the chemical composition and elemental mapping of the copper-based materials. X-ray diffraction (XRD) data have been collected in the Bragg–Brentano geometry in reflection mode, in the 2θ range of 5°–150° at 18°/min, with a PanAnalytical X'Pert Pro PW1800 diffractometer by using Cu–Kα radiation. The equipment was operated at 40 mA and 45 kV.

Conductivity measurements were carried out in accordance with the ASTM E 1004 and DIN EN 2004-1 standards, with an eddy current conductivity meter Sigmascope model SPM10. An average of 5 measurements per experimental condition were considered. The density of the composite material was determined by Archimedes’ principle in a Mettler Toledo analytical balance model XPR205. Finally, wear resistance was evaluated with a universal mechanical tester (UMT, TriboLab-Bruker) using the linearly reciprocating configuration and based on the ASTM G133 standard (procedure A), but with an applied load of 5 N instead of 25.0 N, and a pin tip radius (carbon steel ball) of 2.38 mm instead of 4.76 mm as prescribed by the standard. All other provisions of the standard have been considered.

Considering the size distribution and according to the light scattering measurements, some particles are larger than the mesh used during the sieving process. This can be attributed to the measurement assuming spherical particles, which is not always the case, as observed in the SEM images in Fig. 2. Elongated particles may pass through the mesh, even though their volume could be comparable to larger spherical particles.

Cu and Ni particles (Figs. 2a and 2b) exhibit surface roughness and a porous nature. These features can be advantageous when adhesion between different phases is important, particularly in our case where the incorporation of Ni microparticles into the Cu solid solution is crucial for metal contact applications. Additionally, surface roughness could enhance the interaction with SiC particles. Fig. 2 matches well with the values in Table 1, particularly in terms of the d10 percentile. The difference between the sizes of Cu and Ni particles is easily observable, facilitating the filling of spaces between Cu and SiC grains in the green compact by the Ni particles. On the other hand, SiC particles exhibit a polyhedral nature with several edges. For SiC-C powder (Fig. 2c) a significant amount of fine SiC powder, resembling dust, is observed. In Fig. 2d, powders containing 40 wt% SiC-A and some Cu, Ni, and SiC-A particles are illustrated. However, their morphologies do not significatively change due to the milling process, indicating that the powders simply undergo a mixing process during milling under the specified experimental conditions. This implies that in a mass production scenario for this type of compound, the industrial processing of the powders using commercial powder mixers is feasible.

From the elemental analysis by EDX of the Cu powders (data not shown), an oxygen content of 1–3% (by mass) was found. Therefore, this oxygen is present in the green compact in addition to Ni and SiC micro particles.

The crystal structure of the as-received powders and copper-based composites was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. 3). The observed diffraction peaks agree with the standard XRD patterns of Cu (ICDD PDF 4–836), Ni (ICDD PDF 4–850), and SiC. Among the several SiC polytypes [46], the XRD pattern show contributions of α-SiC (ICDD PDF 49–1428) and cubic 3C-SiC (ICDD PDF 29–1129). Regarding copper oxides, the most intense peak at ∼36.5° associated with them was just visible in some samples of the green compact (data not shown), but it disappeared after the sintering process. This indicates that the inert atmosphere in the oven eliminates O2 of the pressed compounds. The green compact exhibits a difference in comparison to the as-sintered one. The diffractograms for the as-sintered solid samples shown in Fig. 3 display the convolution of the XRD patterns from Cu and SiC. However, in the green compact, the most intense peak related to Ni can also be observed (inset in Fig. 3). This indicates that the Ni microparticles act as an additional reinforcement agents in the green compact. After the sintering process, a Cu-Ni alloy is formed as expected because these two metals fulfill the Hume-Rothery rules [47]. The complete dissolution of Ni into the Cu matrix is a key factor in the proposed material, as it has been stablished that Cu-Ni alloys exhibit anticorrosive properties. During the sintering process, this dissolution occurs via solid state diffusion. The formation of Cu-Ni alloy was corroborated by SEM and XRD analysis of solid samples containing even higher quantities of Ni under similar manufacturing conditions, as will be shown in Fig. 7.

Several aspects affect the properties of electrical contacts, such as surface hardness, contact force, impurities in the material and/or the interfaces, contact resistance, and electrical conductivity, to name a few. Enhancing these properties directly affects the dimensions and distribution of the a-spots, which could significantly alter the operating temperature of the contact element [1]. In the context of metal contact applications, three main physical properties were considered here for evaluating the use of copper matrix composites as contact electrode material: hardness, electrical conductivity, and specific wear resistance.

The hardness of all the as-sintered materials follows the expected behavior as a function of the SiC concentration: it increases up to a maximum value, and then decreases. This behavior is the result of the interplay between the reinforcement effect due to the SiC phase (related to the SiC loading and its strength, interparticle spacing, and homogeneity in the distribution of the particles), the metal microstructure, and the interfacial bonding between the metal/ceramic microparticles [48]. Somani and coworkers found that adding a volume fraction of SiC particles to the copper matrix increases the composite material hardness. They noticed an increasing trend in the composite hardness values by adding volume fractions of 10 %, 15 %, and 20 % SiC to the copper matrix. The Cu-20%SiC composite material enhanced the composite material hardness value by about 48 %, mainly due to the high hardness of SiC particles [49]. While this behavior has been previously reported, the maximum value was obtained at different filler concentrations added into the matrix material [50]. The discrepancy arises from the manufacturing process we used to obtain the solid composite materials and the characteristics of the metal matrix. In the case of the as-sintered materials with rough Cu microparticles, the maximum value (∼40–45 HR15T) is reached at 10 wt% of SiC for both SiC-A and SiC-B, whereas for SiC-C metal composites the maximum value is reached at 5 wt%. For the as-sintered composites containing SiC-C, hardness measurements in the 15T scale for concentrations higher than 10 wt% were not obtained because the mechanical resistance of the coupons was insufficient.

The strengthening mechanism at low SiC concentrations is attributed to an increase in the effective grain boundaries in the composite material, promoted by SiC micro particles. These microparticles act as barriers to the movement of dislocations [19,51], thereby absorbing the energy of crack propagation through a large number of microcracks [52] and reducing the material's plasticity [53].

On the other hand, when considering SiC-C or higher SiC concentrations than the optimal one, the effect is the opposite due to the density of grain-boundaries being sufficient to promote the relative displacement of the grains with respect to each other [54]. In the scientific literature [55], it is reported that hardness is affected by particle size, which increases as the particle size increases when a fixed SiC content is considered. However, this trend was not observed in our case.

The maximum hardness value obtained for the as-sintered metal composites is not enough for the intended application [12,17]. As depicted in Fig. 5, the density of the materials remains relatively low, with porosity ranging from approximately 5 %–18 % and is largely unaffected by the SiC particle size but rather linked to the SiC concentration. Therefore, the re-pressing process was applied to enhance the physical properties of the copper-based composites.Fig. 5Density (a) and porosity (b) as a function of the SiC concentration in the copper-matrix composites. Several compaction pressures ranging from 250 to 1100 MPa were applied for the re-pressing process. It was observed that the material hardness increased with the compaction pressure, as expected. However, at high pressures, plastic deformation and some cracks in the solid samples were observed. Therefore, a compaction pressure close to 830 MPa was selected. Once the pressure value was determined, all composite materials were re-pressed after sintering. The corresponding hardness values attained are also shown in Fig. 4. It is noticeable that there is a negligible difference in the hardness values as a function of the SiC concentration, for both SiC-A and SiC-B micro particles, but a drastic decrease is observed for the composite reinforced with SiC-C micro particles.

After the re-pressing process, the as-sintered copper samples have considerably improved their hardness value and are now close to that obtained for oxygen-free high-conductivity copper processed from melted and refined electrolytic slabs (∼64.4 HR30T) [2], given that their hardness is also measured on the 30T scale. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the concentrations below 20 wt% exhibit adequate hardness values above 55 HR30T. This material hardness meets the requirements for producing metal contacts since it provides the necessary strength without detriment to the contact area or welding [12,17,56]. Additionally, after the re-pressing process, the density of the composite material rises in such a manner that the porosity is between 1.5 % and 9 %, which is also deemed suitable for industrial applications.

The surface morphology has been altered after the re-pressing process, as depicted in Fig. 6 for solid materials containing 20 wt% SiC-B. The as-sintered samples before re-pressed exhibit some cracks, voids, and spherical copper microparticles on the surface. These defects are linked to the wt% concentration of SiC; at high copper content (low SiC concentration) the presence of cracks decreases. After the re-pressing process, the cracks are almost absent, there is a lower density of voids and significantly fewer spherical copper particles are observed. Therefore, during the re-pressing, copper fills some of the available spaces in the composite material improving its physical properties. This is illustrated in Fig. 4 where the material hardness was measured in the 30T scale even at the highest SiC concentration (except for materials containing SiC-C particles). The material densification is also increased, as shown in Fig. 5. Elemental mapping of the cross-section of the solid samples was performed to determine the distribution of Ni in the bulk material. As shown in Fig. 7, nickel appears to be homogeneously distributed throughout the materials for all the cases. The formation of the Cu-Ni alloy was further confirmed using XRD, where materials with relatively low SiC content (10 wt%) were compared with Cu-Ni samples containing even higher Ni concentrations (30 wt% Ni), all sintered under the same experimental conditions. The lattice parameters were calculated based on the most intense peak in the diffractograms, and it was observed that they followed the expected behavior: an increase in nickel content shifted the main peak in the diffractograms from the copper peak position toward the nickel one. Considering the copper and nickel lattice parameters as 3.614 Å and 3.525 Å, respectively, as calculated from XRD measurements, the addition of Ni to the material modifies the lattice parameter from 3.613 Å at 1.5 wt% Ni to 3.583 Å at 30 wt% Ni. Although Ni and Cu have higher melting points than the sintering temperature, this temperature is also higher than the miscibility temperature (>300 °C for the bulk Cu-Ni alloy), which depends on the size, shape of the metal particles, and composition of the Cu-Ni alloy [47]. Nickel is present in the composite material, forming the Cu-Ni alloy, and it is expected that their physical properties such as tensile and yield strength, corrosion resistance, and elastic modulus, will enhance the physical properties of the metal composites compared to copper, while the density remains almost unaltered [57]. However, as reported by Tian et al. [51], the solid solution strengthening due to Ni could be relatively small, mainly because the Ni concentration used here was lower than that reported (3 wt%). Nonetheless, while copper promotes grain growth, nickel leads to shrinkage [58] which could contribute to the Hall-Petch effect.

Fig. 8 shows the electrical conductivity achieved in the composite material. The observed trend is a consequence of the addition of both Ni and SiC to the copper matrix. Additionally, it is noteworthy that the thermal and electrical conductivities are severely affected by the addition of nickel to the metal matrix [59,60], and there is only a slight difference in conductivity as a function of the SiC particle size. In fact, the electrical conductivity for coarser particles is higher than that for the smaller ones, except when considering lower SiC concentration (5 wt%). These observations align the findings of G. C. Efe et al. [55], where they observed that at a fixed SiC concentration, the conductivity increased with particle size. They attributed this behavior to fewer electrons being scattered in composites with large particle sizes (30 μm) and a uniform network in the metal composite. At low SiC concentrations, the effective charge transport is almost unaffected because scattering centers are located at relatively long distances, whereas in composites with high concentrations the situation is the opposite. When coarse SiC particles are considered as the second phase in the Cu matrix, the effect is similar to that observed at low concentrations, where the mean pathway for charge carriers is more significant than that related to fine SiC particles. In other words, the free-mean path for charge carriers is considerably reduced due to the homogeneous distribution of fine powder in the sample, which also contributes to the formation of a homogeneous network of potential barriers. Thus, electrical conductivity of Cu-based composites decreases as the weight percentage of SiC increases [55]. In contrast to what was reported by S. El-Khatib et al. [50], the enhancement in conductivity promoted by SiC due to the individual contributions coming from the partial decomposition of SiC at high temperatures during the sintering process was not observed, because the sintering temperature was set at 900 °C.Fig. 8Electrical conductivity as a function of the SiC concentration and the particle size distribution.Fig. 8

Electrical conductivity as a function of the SiC concentration and the particle size distribution.

The lower conductivity values for materials containing SiC-C, the smaller particles, can be attributed to the presence of fine SiC-C particles in the copper grain boundaries, which promotes strong charge dispersion when an electric field is applied through the sample. According to the Wiedemann-Franz law, it is expected that thermal conductivity must follow the same trend as observed in the electrical conductivity of the composite material.

Table 2 provides quantitative data regarding processing temperatures, hardness, and electric conductivity values of the developed Cu-Ni/SiC composite materials compare to those of Ag/WC listed in the literature. For metal contact applications, the required electrical conductivity will depend on the device and the environment hence, a conductivity close to 20 %IACS is generally sufficient in most cases [1]. For the intended application and in accordance with the interrupting capacity, the obtained electrical conductivity is good enough to accomplish the physical requirements of the selected device. Thus, the performance of the Cu-Ni/SiC composites is not compromised compared to commercial silver-tungsten-carbide alloys. Since hardness, density, and homogeneity have been achieved in the composite material, it is necessary to investigate its specific wear resistance because electrical contacts under operating conditions are subjected to continuous on/off cycles, which could promote wear and gradual failure of the contact surface. To ensure the expected device lifecycle, its wear resistance was evaluated as a function of pressure, SiC concentrations, particle size distribution, and subjected to a typical force of 5 N between contacts [64], as shown in Fig. 9. As illustrated in Fig. 9a, the specific wear resistance for all the particle size distributions decreases as a function of the compaction pressure in the re-pressing process. This is mainly due to pore volume reduction. However, for higher pressure values, some cracks appeared in the composites containing 20 wt% SiC because of plastic deformation. To avoid these defects, a maximum pressure value of 830 MPa was considered. The specific wear resistance tends to decrease as a function of the SiC concentration, as shown in Fig. 9b, mostly in the range of 10–30 wt%; the wear resistance value varies from 1.3 × 10−4 mm3 N−1 m−1 (for re-pressed copper) to 4 × 10−5 mm3 N−1 m−1 for metal composites containing 20 wt% SiC-B. This last value is adequate for the intended application [12]. Low specific wear rate can be attributed to the high hardness of the material and the reduction in the material's plasticity, and hence, to its toughness, which inhibits crack growth, leading to the material resist the sliding ball during the measurement [52,53]. The specific wear resistance for metal composites containing 5 wt% SiC does not follow the expected tendency: its value is higher even than that for copper. This situation can be explained after examining the wear track by SEM (data not shown). It was observed massive detachment of some SiC particles from the composite and easily dragged during the wear test causing the cut of the softer copper matrix and leading to an increased wear rate. At higher SiC compositions the situation is different because although some particles are still removed from the surface, there are also other ones remaining in the material which act as effective reinforcement agents.

Conclusions

In this work, composites based on copper-nickel alloys were processed by powder metallurgy. Samples of this new alloy were experimentally characterized as a function of the particle size distribution and the ceramic SiC concentration reinforcement content. For the first time, this allowed us to manufacture metal contacts that operate under air conditions, taking advantage of the nickel properties to avoid corrosion and increase mechanical properties at elevated temperatures. In fact, the formation of the copper-nickel alloy at the sintering temperature of 900 °C was confirmed by XRD measurements and EDS analysis. Furthermore, after a re-pressing process, the copper-nickel composite material exhibited physical properties similar to those used to produce electrical contacts, such as silver, rare-earth metals, or carbon.

We also found that our developed composite material exhibits enhanced mechanical properties with increasing SiC concentration compared to the Cu-sintered material. Based on the physical performance of the developed composite materials, two composite candidates were identified—those containing 10 and 20 wt% SiC-B—to be suitable for low-voltage molded-case circuit breaker applications operating up to 100 A. The structural and surface characterization confirms the incorporation of Ni as an alloying element in the Cu matrix, a critical factor for enhancing the anticorrosive properties (not studied here) of the developed material for use in metal contact applications. However, further evaluation of metal contacts for low-voltage circuit breakers, following the UL 489 standard, is needed to validate its application and performance. Currently, several tests on functional devices under real operational conditions are ongoing, and preliminary results indicate that the copper-based metal contacts exhibit similar performance to those based on silver metal.

From the results obtained during the development of copper-nickel matrix composites reinforced with silicon carbide (SiC) microparticles for metal contact applications, the following findings can be summarized.a)The impact of SiC powder with different SiC distributions influence the composite properties such as hardness, density, porosity, conductivity, and wear resistance as illustrated in Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, respectively. When the re-pressing process with pressure values ranging from 250 to 1100 MPa was applied, the composite material hardness increased.
