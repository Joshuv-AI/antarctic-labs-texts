Pharmacy students’ knowledge and attitudes regarding GLP-1 receptor agonist adverse effects in obesity management: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Alaa A. Khojah, Malaz J. Gazzaz, Mohammed M. Aldurdunji, Ahmad E. Safiraldeen, Ibrahim R. Al-Qurashi, Mohammad A. Alhazmi, Ashraf N. Abdalla, Roaya S. Alqurashi, Saad M. Wali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are important therapeutic options for obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Despite their increasing use, safety concerns remain, and pharmacy students’ knowledge of their adverse effects is unclear. This study evaluated pharmacy students’ knowledge and attitudes regarding GLP-1 RA adverse effects in obesity management. A cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted among undergraduate Doctor of Pharmacy (Pharm.D.) students (1st–7th year) at Umm Al-Qura University, Saudi Arabia, between November and December 2025. A newly developed questionnaire, reviewed by two experts and pilot-tested among 10 students, assessed 17 knowledge and five attitude items covering indications, mechanisms, adverse effects, and clinical scenarios. Of 1,118 eligible students, 296 completed the survey (response rate: 26.5%). Descriptive statistics and multivariable regression analyses were performed using RStudio (version 2024.9.1.394). Among participants (63.9% male), the mean knowledge score was 7.6 ± 4.0/17. Most agreed that adverse effects significantly impact clinical use (59.4%) and supported further education on less common side effects (61.1%). Nausea was the most frequently recognized adverse effect (59.1%), whereas thyroid C-cell tumors observed in rodent studies (25.0%) and diabetic retinopathy complications (36.5%) were less commonly identified. Only 37.2% correctly recognized that hypoglycemia is uncommon with GLP-1 RA monotherapy. Higher knowledge scores were significantly associated with greater confidence in knowledge (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06–1.17; p < 0.001) and stronger endorsement of further education on less common side effects (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06–1.18; p < 0.001), whereas gender and academic year were not significant predictors of any attitude item. Pharmacy students demonstrated limited knowledge but generally positive attitudes regarding GLP-1 RA safety. These findings highlight the need to strengthen pharmacovigilance and evidence-based medication safety education within pharmacy curricula to improve clinical preparedness and patient counseling.

Introduction

Obesity is a major global public health challenge worldwide, with more than one billion individuals projected to be affected by 2030, placing an enormous strain on healthcare systems globally [1]. The condition is associated with serious comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular disease, and certain malignancies, contributing substantially to morbidity, premature mortality, and economic burden [2,3]. The burden is particularly pronounced in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, where rapid urbanization, sedentary lifestyles, and dietary transitions have driven prevalence rates to among the highest globally [4]. In Saudi Arabia, the situation is especially alarming, with approximately 35% of the adult population classified as obese and over 75% either overweight or obese, alongside one of the highest T2DM prevalence rates in the world at approximately 18% [5,6]. The coexistence of these metabolic conditions has intensified the demand for effective pharmacological treatments. These converging epidemiological trends have prompted growing clinical and public interest in pharmacological interventions for weight management, particularly glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), which have demonstrated substantial efficacy in achieving clinically meaningful weight loss [7].

GLP-1 RAs mimic endogenous incretin hormones, promoting weight loss primarily through appetite suppression and delayed gastric emptying [8]. Agents such as liraglutide and semaglutide have received regulatory approval for chronic weight management in addition to T2DM, although their efficacy differs. In the pivotal trials, liraglutide 3.0 mg produced a mean weight reduction of approximately 8% [9], whereas semaglutide 2.4 mg produced approximately 15% [10]. However, their expanding use has raised pharmacovigilance concerns, as GLP-1 RAs are associated with gastrointestinal disturbances, pancreatitis, injection site reactions, diabetic retinopathy complications, and thyroid C-cell tumours, which have been observed in rodents but not established in humans [11,12]. Pharmacists play a critical role in counseling patients on these adverse effects, monitoring for contraindications, and ensuring safe medication use [13]. Despite this, studies suggest that obesity pharmacotherapy education remains underemphasized in pharmacy curricula worldwide, with few programs reporting that graduates are adequately prepared to counsel patients on anti-obesity medications [14]. In Saudi Arabia, while public awareness and utilization of GLP-1 RAs have been increasingly examined [15,16], to the authors’ knowledge, limited research has assessed pharmacy students’ knowledge of the adverse effects of GLP-1 RAs in the context of obesity treatment, representing a notable gap in the literature. Pharmacy students are a particularly relevant population in this context, as they represent the future pharmacy workforce and are at a stage of training where foundational knowledge of medication safety is developed and curricular gaps can be readily identified and addressed. Furthermore, although a few studies in Saudi Arabia have examined health-profession students’ knowledge and attitudes toward newer pharmacotherapies, data specifically related to the safety profile of GLP-1 receptor agonists remain scarce, further supporting the need for the present study.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the knowledge of pharmacy students at Umm Al-Qura University regarding the side effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists in obesity treatment, including their understanding of approved indications, mechanisms of action, common and serious adverse effects such as pancreatitis, diabetic retinopathy complications, and findings of thyroid C-cell tumors in animal studies, as well as their responses to clinical scenarios involving adverse effects. The secondary objectives were to explore students’ attitudes toward the safety, effectiveness, and clinical role of GLP-1 RAs in obesity management, including their level of confidence in their own knowledge, and to identify educational gaps, particularly in underrepresented areas such as rare adverse effects, contraindication recognition and counseling-related knowledge. These insights aim to support future curriculum development and targeted training strategies to better prepare pharmacy students for the evolving demands of clinical practice in obesity management.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was conducted to assess the knowledge and attitudes of pharmacy students regarding the side effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists used in obesity treatment. This study design was selected as an appropriate approach for evaluating the prevalence and distribution of knowledge and attitudes within the target population at a single point in time [17].

Study setting and participants

The target population comprised male and female undergraduate Doctor of Pharmacy (Pharm.D) students enrolled at the College of Pharmacy, Umm Al-Qura University, Makkah, Saudi Arabia. Data collection was conducted over a two-month period between November and December 2025. All Pharm.D students across all academic years (1 st to 7th year) were eligible for inclusion. Obesity pharmacotherapy and GLP-1 receptor agonists are primarily taught during 3rd- and 4th-year modules, followed by final-year clinical rotations. During the study period, the total number of enrolled Pharm.D students was 1,118. Students who did not provide informed consent or who submitted incomplete responses were excluded from the analysis.

A convenience sampling approach was used. The questionnaire was administered electronically using Google Forms, and the survey link was distributed through official student communication channels and class representatives. Participation was voluntary, and reminder messages were sent periodically throughout the data collection period to encourage participation.

Sample size calculation

A priori sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 for the primary analysis (multivariable linear regression of knowledge score). Based on a small-to-medium effect size (Cohen's f2 = 0.15), a significance level of 5%, a target statistical power of 95%, and 8 total predictors, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 160 participants.

Survey instrument

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was newly developed based on the study objectives and a comprehensive review of the relevant literature on GLP-1 receptor agonists and their adverse effect profiles [8,11,12]. The questionnaire comprised three sections. The first section collected demographic information, including gender and year of study. The second section consisted of 17 knowledge items assessing participants’ understanding of approved GLP-1 RA indications, known adverse effects (gastrointestinal, ocular, thyroid, and pancreatic), mechanisms of weight loss, appropriate clinical actions in adverse-event scenarios, and identification of specific GLP-1 RA agents. The knowledge domain was intentionally designed to span both awareness of approved indications and applied, agent-specific pharmacotherapeutic knowledge (e.g. distinguishing adverse-effect profiles across individual GLP-1 RAs), reflecting the medication-counseling and pharmacovigilance competencies expected of practicing pharmacists. Items were formatted as multiple-choice questions, including both single-response and multiple-response formats, with an ‘I don’t know’ option provided to minimize guessing. The third section included five attitude items, each measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), evaluating participants’ perceptions of GLP-1 RA safety, clinical utility, confidence in their own knowledge, and perceived need for additional education. Each attitude item was retained on its original 1–5 response scale and analyzed individually as a distinct ordinal variable; no total or composite attitude score was calculated.

To ensure content validity, the questionnaire was reviewed by two experts in clinical pharmacy and pharmacology prior to administration. In addition, a pilot study was conducted with 10 pharmacy students to assess clarity, comprehensibility, and completion time. Based on participant feedback, minor modifications were made before the final version of the questionnaire was distributed.

Knowledge score calculation

Each participant’s total knowledge score was computed as the sum of correct responses across 17 scored components (the 17 knowledge items referred to elsewhere in the manuscript). Although the knowledge domain comprised 12 questions, three of these permitted more than one correct answer and were therefore scored at the level of individual response options: the item on approved indications (2 correct options—type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity/weight management), the item on known adverse effects (4 correct options—nausea, pancreatitis, thyroid C-cell tumors, and injection-site reactions; the C-cell option referred to the tumours observed in rodent studies that underpin the product labelling, not to a risk established in humans), and the item on ocular side effects (2 correct options—diabetic retinopathy complications and blurred vision). The remaining nine questions were single-response items scored as one point each. For every component, a correct response was coded as 1 and an incorrect response—including selection of the ‘I don’t know’ option—was coded as 0. Summing all 17 binary indicators produced a total knowledge score ranging from 0 to 17, with higher scores reflecting greater knowledge.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Scientific Biomedical Research Ethics Committee, College of Medicine, Umm Al-Qura University (Approval No.: HAPO-02-K-012-2025-02-2538). All procedures complied with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants following a clear explanation of the study purpose and data confidentiality. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents had the right to withdraw at any stage.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and participants' knowledge scores. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD) and medians with interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The internal consistency of the knowledge domain was assessed using Cronbach's alpha, with values ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable [18]. The five attitude items were analyzed separately as distinct variables rather than summed into a composite score, given their heterogeneous construct validity (original Cronbach's alpha = 0.191); consequently, no aggregate attitude score or overall scoring range was derived.

Because the survey captured a limited set of variables, candidate covariates for both sets of models were pre-specified from all available demographic and questionnaire variables. The potential confounders considered were the two available demographic variables (gender and year of study); knowledge models additionally included the five attitude items as predictors, whereas the attitude models additionally included the knowledge score. All pre-specified covariates were entered simultaneously into each multivariable model. For knowledge, we constructed both univariable and multivariable linear regression models to assess the association between knowledge and participants’ gender, academic years and attitudes. Model assumptions were verified, including homoscedasticity (Breusch–Pagan test), independence of errors (Durbin–Watson test), normality of residuals (Shapiro–Wilk test), and multicollinearity (variance inflation factor, VIF < 5). Regarding the attitudes, multivariable ordinal logistic regression models were employed to examine each of the five attitude items as separate outcomes, with knowledge score as the primary predictor and participants’ gender and academic year as covariates. This approach is appropriate for ordinal Likert-scale outcomes. The proportional odds assumption was tested for each model using Brant tests (p > 0.05 indicating assumption satisfaction). Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (VIF < 5 for all models). All analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 2024.9.1.394, Boston, MA, USA, using R version 4.4.2). A two-sided significance level of p < 0.05 was applied for all statistical tests.

The records of 296 participants were analyzed in the current study. The majority were male (63.9%, n = 189), while females constituted 36.1% (n = 107). Participants’ responses to knowledge items

The majority of participants correctly identified that GLP-1 receptor agonists are approved for the treatment of type 2 diabetes (72.0%), while 45.3% indicated the approval for obesity and weight management. A considerable proportion of respondents (70.3%) correctly perceived that GLP-1 receptor agonists are not associated with weight gain as a side effect. For side effects, the most recognized adverse effect was nausea (59.1%), followed by pancreatitis (30.1%), injection site reactions (28.4%), and thyroid C-cell tumors (25.0%). Diabetic retinopathy complications were identified as a possible ocular side effect by 36.5% of students, while 31.8% selected blurred vision (Table 3).

Less than half of participants (48.6%) identified the restriction applying to patients with a family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC); the item was worded as use ‘with caution’, whereas these agents are in fact contraindicated in this situation, and a similar proportion indicated that GLP-1 receptor agonists promote weight loss primarily through suppressing appetite and delaying gastric emptying. When asked which GLP-1 receptor agonist is specifically approved for weight management in obese patients without diabetes, 37.2% correctly selected liraglutide (Table 3).

Participants’ responses to attitude items

Overall, 59.4% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that GLP-1 receptor agonist side effects significantly impact clinical use. A majority (51.0%) believed the benefits outweigh the risks, while 39.6% felt confident in their knowledge. Furthermore, 45.9% supported GLP-1 agonists as standard obesity treatment, and 61.1% agreed that more education is needed on less common side effects such as vision and thyroid issues (Figure 2).

Factors and predictors of participants’ knowledge

In univariable linear regression analysis, two attitude items were significantly associated with knowledge scores: confidence in knowledge (beta = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.46–1.21, p < 0.001) and recognition of need for further education on less common side effects (beta = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.42–1.23, p < 0.001). In the multivariable analysis, after adjusting for gender and year of study, the attitude items remained significantly associated with knowledge: confidence in knowledge (beta = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.40–1.17, p < 0.001) and perceived educational need (beta = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.37–1.16, p < 0.001, Table 4). Model assumptions were adequately met: homoscedasticity (Breusch–Pagan p = 0.197), independence of errors (Durbin–Watson p = 0.057), normality of residuals (Shapiro–Wilk p = 0.065), and no multicollinearity (VIF < 5 for all variables).

Discussion

This study evaluated pharmacy students’ knowledge and attitudes regarding the safety of GLP-1 receptor agonists in obesity management. Despite generally positive perceptions of their clinical value, students demonstrated limited safety-related knowledge, indicating a gap between familiarity with this increasingly prominent therapeutic class and understanding of its indications, adverse effects, and risk-management considerations.

Knowledge gaps in an era of expanding evidence

Awareness of the established role of GLP-1 receptor agonists in type 2 diabetes was greater than recognition of their approved use in obesity and weight management. This is notable given the increasing prominence of semaglutide and liraglutide in chronic weight management [9,10] and the high prevalence of obesity and T2DM in Saudi Arabia [5,6]. Limited awareness of anti-obesity indications may therefore affect future pharmacists’ preparedness for counseling and obesity pharmacotherapy.

Knowledge of safety considerations was similarly uneven. Common gastrointestinal adverse effects, particularly nausea, were better recognized than less common or clinically important safety concerns, including pancreatitis, thyroid C-cell tumors, and diabetic retinopathy complications. Limited recognition that hypoglycemia is uncommon with GLP-1 receptor agonist monotherapy may reflect conflation with insulin or sulfonylureas and insufficient understanding of the glucose-dependent mechanism of these agents [8]. Collectively, these findings suggest that general familiarity with the drug class is better established than agent-specific, mechanistic, and safety-related knowledge.

Risk contextualization and pharmacovigilance literacy

The safety profile of GLP-1 receptor agonists continues to evolve as real-world evidence accumulates, emphasizing the importance of interpreting emerging evidence rather than memorizing fixed lists of adverse effects. The risk mapping reported by Xie et al. [19], for example, places cardiometabolic benefits alongside a range of adverse-event signals whose absolute risks remain low. Interpreting such evidence requires students to distinguish relative from absolute risk, preclinical findings from demonstrated human outcomes, and pharmacovigilance signals from established causal relationships.

Recognition of the restriction concerning a family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) was limited. Importantly, the questionnaire item understated the regulatory position: GLP-1 receptor agonists carrying this warning are contraindicated, rather than merely used with caution, in patients with a personal or family history of MTC or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2. Responses to this item should therefore be interpreted cautiously. The distinction is clinically important because the contraindication relates specifically to MTC and MEN2 rather than thyroid disease generally. Although rodent studies identified thyroid C-cell tumors, an excess risk of thyroid cancer has not been established in humans [20]. Effective counseling therefore requires appropriate interpretation of both the regulatory warning and the limitations of the available human evidence.

Attitudes toward anti-obesity pharmacotherapy

Students generally regarded GLP-1 receptor agonist safety as clinically important and expressed a need for further education, while confidence in their own knowledge was lower. This pattern suggests awareness of limitations in their preparation. Similar educational gaps have been reported elsewhere; a survey of US pharmacy program leaders found that only 32% considered their graduates prepared to discuss obesity pharmacotherapy [14,21], while studies among Arab healthcare professionals and students have reported attitudes shaped by perceived benefits, barriers, and safety concerns [22]. In the present study, favorable perceptions of GLP-1 receptor agonists coexisted with limited recognition of several safety considerations, suggesting that positive attitudes do not necessarily correspond to comprehensive safety knowledge.

Because the attitude items represented distinct constructs and did not support a composite scale, they were examined individually. Greater confidence in knowledge about GLP-1 receptor agonist adverse effects and stronger endorsement of the need for additional education were associated with higher knowledge scores (Tables 4 and 5). These relationships suggest that knowledge is linked to specific educational attitudes rather than general perceptions of GLP-1 receptor agonists. However, given the cross-sectional design, the direction of these associations cannot be established [23,24].

Educational implications

GLP-1 receptor agonists are formally taught within pharmacotherapeutics modules in the third and fourth years. However, the present design cannot determine whether the observed knowledge gaps reflect uneven curricular exposure, retention of previously taught material, differences in participation across academic years, or learning from sources outside the formal curriculum. Accordingly, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of inadequate curricular reinforcement.

Nevertheless, the identified gaps provide areas that may warrant curricular review. Case-based teaching on adverse-event scenarios, interpretation of evolving safety evidence, risk–benefit communication, and obesity pharmacotherapy distinct from diabetes management could be considered. The effectiveness of such approaches should be evaluated prospectively, for example through pre- and post-intervention assessment.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding relationships between knowledge and attitudes. Second, this was a single-institution study, limiting generalizability to other pharmacy student populations. Third, the self-reported questionnaire is susceptible to social desirability and recall biases. Fourth, although the instrument underwent expert review and pilot testing, it has not undergone full psychometric validation. In addition, the item concerning family history of MTC described the restriction as use ‘with caution,’ whereas the relevant regulatory position is a contraindication; responses to this item should therefore be interpreted accordingly. The knowledge domain demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.802), whereas the attitude items showed low internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.191) and were consequently analyzed individually rather than as a composite scale.

The 26.5% response rate represents an important potential source of selection bias. Students with greater interest in pharmacotherapy, obesity management, or GLP-1 receptor agonists may have been more likely to participate, potentially resulting in higher knowledge scores and more favorable attitudes than among the wider student population. If so, the observed knowledge gaps may be conservative; however, because non-respondents were not characterized, the direction and magnitude of this bias cannot be determined. Uneven participation across academic years further limits between-year comparisons. Finally, external sources of knowledge, including social media, clinical rotations, and self-directed learning, were not assessed.

Conclusion

Pharmacy students demonstrated positive perceptions of GLP-1 receptor agonists but limited knowledge of several aspects of their safety profile, particularly less commonly recognized adverse effects, risk interpretation, and selected clinical precautions. Knowledge was associated with confidence in one’s own knowledge and perceived need for further education, although the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding directionality. Within the limitations of a single-institution study and a 26.5% response rate, these findings identify specific areas of safety knowledge that may warrant greater attention in pharmacy education. Future studies should evaluate whether targeted training in pharmacovigilance, evidence interpretation, and risk communication improves students’ preparedness for the expanding role of GLP-1 receptor agonists in obesity management.
