Online Density-Based Clustering for Real-Time Narrative Evolution Monitoring
Ostap Vykhopen, Viktoria Skorik, Maxim Tereschenko, Veronika Solopova

Abstract
Automated narrative intelligence systems for social media monitoring face significant scalability challenges when processing continuous data streams using traditional batch clustering algorithms. We investigate the replacement of HDBSCAN (offline clustering) with online (streaming/incremental) clustering methods in a production narrative report generation pipeline. The proposed system employs a three-stage architecture (data collection, modeling, dashboard generation) that processes thousands of multilingual social media documents daily. While HDBSCAN excels at discovering hierarchical density-based clusters and handling noise, its batch-only nature necessitates complete retraining for each time window, resulting in memory constraints, computational inefficiency, and inability to adapt to evolving narratives in real-time. This work evaluates a bunch of online clustering algorithms across dimensions of cluster quality preservation, computational efficiency, memory footprint, and integration compatibility with existing workflows. We propose evaluation criteria that balance traditional clustering metrics (Silhouette Coefficient, Davies-Bouldin Index) with narrative metrics (narrative distinctness, contingency and variance). Our methodology includes sliding-window simulations on historical datasets from Ukraine information space, enabling comparative analysis of algorithmic trade-offs in realistic operational contexts. This research addresses a critical gap between batch-oriented topic modeling frameworks and the streaming nature of social media monitoring, with implications for computational social science, crisis informatics, and narrative surveillance systems.

Introduction

Automated narrative intelligence systems have become essential tools for monitoring information ecosystems, particularly in contexts requiring rapid detection of emerging narratives, misinformation campaigns, and geopolitical discourse patterns. Organizations ranging from defense agencies to public health authorities rely on these systems to process high-volume social media streams and generate actionable intelligence reports. However, the computational architecture of such systems often reflects fundamental tensions between the batch-oriented nature of modern machine learning frameworks and the inherently streaming character of social media data.
This tension becomes particularly acute when examining HDBSCAN
12
, a clustering algorithm widely adopted for narrative discovery, especially popularised by BerTopic library
13
;
7
.

Despite its theoretical advantages, including the ability to handle varying density clusters, detect noise, and provide hierarchical structure, HDBSCAN’s strict batch processing requirement creates significant operational bottlenecks in production environments: (i) Memory constraints. Each clustering operation requires loading complete document embeddings (N × 100 dimensional UMAP reduced vectors) into memory, with O(NlogN) average case complexity for minimum spanning tree construction; (ii) computational inefficiency. For example, daily monitoring require complete re-clustering of new data each day, discarding previously learned cluster structures even as narrative topics evolve gradually; (iii) Parameter sensitivity. Production logs reveal frequent cluster size parameter tuning, indicating difficulty in maintaining stable cluster granularity across varying data volumes; (iv) inability to handle incremental updates. When new documents arrive, the entire clustering process must restart, preventing real-time cluster assignment or progressive refinement of narrative understanding; (v) scalability limitations. As historical data accumulates, the batch processing approach becomes increasingly untenable.

These limitations motivate our central research question:

Can online clustering algorithms effectively replace HDBSCAN in narrative monitoring pipelines while preserving cluster quality, maintaining system integration compatibility, and improving computational efficiency?

Specifically, we test clustering algorithms that not only support online mode but also automatically determine the number of clusters without requiring predefined cluster counts (as required by batch K-means) and demonstrate robustness to noise inherent in social media data streams. Furthermore, the ideal online clustering approach should achieve significant efficiency improvements, where the memory footprint of the online algorithm is substantially smaller than HDBSCAN’s batch requirements, and the time required for incremental updates is considerably faster than HDBSCAN’s complete re-clustering operations.

Our contributions are threefold:

1.

We introduce a realistic experimental framework for evaluating clustering under streaming conditions, including temporal pretraining, incremental daily updates, and sliding-window simulations on high-volume multilingual social media data.

2.

We conduct a systematic comparison of online density-based algorithms (DBSTREAM, DenStream) against the HDBSCAN baseline within a production-style pipeline, using a joint evaluation scheme that combines traditional clustering metrics (Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin) with narrative-specific metrics (distinctness, contingency, variance)

3.

We identify implementation-level scalability and correctness issues in widely used reference libraries and show how these affect real-world performance. Based on empirical and operational analysis, we position DenStream as the most promising online alternative and outline the architectural and downstream adaptations required for deployment in streaming narrative monitoring systems.

2
Theoretical Background and Related work

2.1
Density-Based Clustering

Density-based clustering algorithms, pioneered by Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) from
8
, have become foundational for discovering arbitrary-shaped clusters in spatial data while robustly handling noise. Unlike partition-based methods (k-means) or hierarchical agglomerative clustering, DBSCAN defines clusters as dense regions separated by sparse areas, requiring only two parameters:
\epsilon
(neighborhood radius) and min_samples (minimum points to form a dense region).

HDBSCAN extends DBSCAN by eliminating the need for
\epsilon
through hierarchical cluster extraction based on cluster stability. The algorithm constructs a minimum spanning tree over mutual reachability distances, then extracts a flat clustering by selecting stable branches of the hierarchical tree. This approach provides several advantages: (1) automatic determination of optimal cluster granularity, (2) varying density cluster detection, (3) hierarchical cluster structure, and (4) probabilistic soft cluster assignments via outlier scores.

Despite these strengths, traditional implementations of DBSCAN and HDBSCAN require access to the complete dataset to construct their distance-based structures and compute cluster hierarchies. This computational model assumes static data and the ability to hold all points in memory simultaneously, limiting applicability to streaming scenarios where data arrives continuously and retrospective reprocessing of the entire history is impractical. Furthermore, narrative monitoring applications, where topics emerge, evolve, and fade over time, demand algorithms that can discover clusters without predefining their number, adapting dynamically to the natural structure present in incoming document streams rather than imposing predetermined partitions.

2.2
Online and Streaming Clustering Algorithms

The streaming clustering literature addresses scenarios where data arrives continuously and memory constraints prevent storing the entire dataset.

CluStream
5
;
4
is one of the earliest and most influential data stream clustering frameworks, introducing a two-phase approach consisting of online micro-clustering and offline macro-clustering. In the online phase, it maintains temporal micro-clusters using summary statistics and timestamps, while the offline phase applies traditional clustering algorithms (e.g., k-means) over these summaries to generate final clusters at different time horizons. While CluStream supports temporal analysis and evolving streams, it requires the number of clusters to be specified in advance and is primarily suited for spherical cluster structures.

DBSTREAM
9
is a density-based data stream clustering algorithm that extends micro-cluster approaches by maintaining a shared density graph capturing the actual density between adjacent micro-clusters during the online summarization. At reclustering time, it uses this shared density information to connect micro-clusters into final clusters more accurately than methods relying only on distances or assumptions about internal distributions.

DenStream
3
is another density-based stream clustering maintaining micro-clusters (core-micro-clusters and outlier-micro-clusters) with temporal decay weights. Its crucial advantage is that it addresses concept drift by gradually forgetting old data and it supports arbitrary cluster shapes like DBSCAN. However, this introduces additional complexity in managing micro-cluster lifecycle and decay parameters.

Recent neural approaches, including deep clustering networks and variational autoencoders with clustering objectives, offer representation learning combined with clustering, yet they typically require large training datasets and provide limited interpretability, which is essential for narrative monitoring applications.

2.3
Streaming Topic Modeling

Traditional topic modeling methods such as LDA and NMF have been extended to streaming and online contexts using incremental variational inference and related techniques, and researchers have also developed enhanced versions that combine topic models with semantic representations
6
. However, the canonical implementations of LDA and NMF still operate on bag
-
of
-
words or weighted document
-
term matrices and do not inherently capture deep contextual similarity like transformer
-
based dense embeddings do, motivating hybrid models that merge topic modeling with embedding spaces for richer semantic coherence
15
.

Recent advances in neural embedding models have enabled a fundamentally different approach to streaming topic modeling that leverages the rich semantic representations of transformer-based architectures
1
;
2
. Rather than relying on term-frequency statistics, our approach builds on a pipeline that transforms documents into dense embeddings, applies dimensionality reduction to preserve local semantic structure in a lower-dimensional space, performs clustering to identify topically coherent groups, and finally employs large language models to generate interpretable topic labels and summaries. This embedding-first paradigm naturally captures contextual similarity and semantic nuance while remaining compatible with streaming scenarios through incremental clustering updates. By decoupling representation learning from cluster assignment, this framework addresses key limitations of traditional topic models, such as their inability to capture deep semantic relationships, while maintaining the flexibility needed for online, continuously arriving data streams.

Baseline System.

Our baseline employs HDBSCAN operating in strict global mode, where clustering is performed independently on each day’s data without reference to historical patterns. Specifically, for a target day d, HDBSCAN processes only the documents from day d, discovering clusters de novo without leveraging any structure learned from previous days. This approach represents the current production paradigm where each daily report requires complete re-clustering.

Online Algorithm Evaluation.

We evaluate three online clustering algorithms from the River library: DBSTREAM, DenStream, TextClust. Each online algorithm follows an incremental training protocol: (1) initialization on historical context (6 days of data preceding the target day), and (2) incremental updating and prediction on the day of interest. This approach simulates a realistic production deployment where the system has access to recent historical context before processing new daily data streams.

Evaluation Metrics.

We assess clustering quality using three standard metrics computed on the day of interest:
(i) Silhouette Score
[-1, 1], which measures cluster cohesion and separation, with higher values indicating better-defined clusters;
(ii) Davies-Bouldin Index
[0,
\infty
], measuring average similarity between clusters and their most similar cluster, with lower values indicating better separation;
(iii) Narrative Distinctness

10

(iv) Narrative Contingency and (v) narrative variance

14

Additionally, we measure computational efficiency through training time, prediction time, and the number of discovered clusters. These operational metrics directly address the scalability concerns motivating our investigation.

4.1
Cluster Quality Metrics

DenStream demonstrates superior cluster quality across conventional clustering metrics. It achieves the highest Silhouette Score (0.685), indicating well separated and cohesive clusters, representing improvement over the HDBSCAN baseline (0.592). Similarly, DenStream attains the lowest Davies-Bouldin Index (0.453) compared to the baseline (0.550), indicating better cluster separation with lower inter-cluster similarity.
DBSTREAM exhibits poor performance on standard clustering quality metrics. Its Silhouette Score (0.327) is notably lower than the baseline (0.592), while its Davies-Bouldin Index (1.220) is more than twice the baseline value (0.550), indicating substantial inter-cluster confusion. This suggests DBSTREAM’s density based micro-cluster approach may be insufficiently granular for the high dimensional semantic space of document embeddings.

4.2
Narrative-Specific Metrics

The narrative specific metrics reveal a more nuanced picture of algorithm behavior in the context of topic discovery for intelligence applications. Narrative Distinctness measures the uniqueness and separability of discovered narrative themes. While the baseline achieves the highest score (0.352), DenStream (0.319) outperforms DBSTREAM (0.266), indicating that DenStream is more effective than DBSTREAM at identifying distinct topical narratives, though it does not surpass the baseline. The baseline HDBSCAN achieves the lowest Contingency (0.299) and Variance (0.286), suggesting stable, predictable cluster structures within each daily batch. In contrast, both online algorithms exhibit substantially higher values (DenStream: 0.389, 0.319; DBSTREAM: 0.630, 0.459), indicating greater variability in narrative structure across time. This increased contingency likely reflects the incremental nature of online algorithms, which adapt cluster structures as new documents arrive rather than performing global optimization on complete daily batches.

HDBSCAN produces three times more clusters than DenStream, even though the Number of Narrative Candidates differs between them by only a factor of two. This may indicate excessive granularity in HDBSCAN, manifested in the fragmentation of a single narrative into multiple distinct clusters. Conversely, DenStream may tend to merge several distinct narratives into a single cluster.

5
Discussion

Overall, our results indicate that DenStream provides the most robust clustering performance, achieving clear improvements over the HDBSCAN baseline in both conventional and narrative-specific metrics. Its higher Silhouette Score and lower Davies–Bouldin Index suggest well-separated and cohesive clusters, which is further reflected in its superior Narrative Distinctness, highlighting its ability to recover meaningful and distinct thematic structures. In contrast, DBSTREAM performs poorly across standard clustering metrics, likely due to limitations of its micro-cluster representation in high-dimensional semantic embedding spaces. While the baseline exhibits greater temporal stability, as indicated by lower Narrative Contingency and Variance, the increased variability observed for the online algorithms appears to be an inherent trade-off of incremental clustering. This adaptability may be advantageous in dynamic intelligence settings, where evolving narratives are expected rather than static topic structures.

However, during the implementation and experimental evaluation of clustering algorithms, several technical limitations were identified in commonly used reference implementations, substantially affecting performance and scalability, which should be taken into consideration when choosing a method. For DenStream, the predict_one method in the River ML library recomputes final clusters by executing a DBSCAN-like procedure over all micro-clusters on every prediction call. While this behavior is consistent with fully online streaming assumptions, it leads to prohibitively slow prediction when the model state is static. This limitation is acknowledged by the River maintainers, who note that ‘DenStream.predict_one‘ performs excessive recomputation for certain use cases. In offline evaluation settings, this repeated reclustering is unnecessary. Additionally, inconsistencies were observed in River’s DenstreamMicroCluster implementation, particularly in the maintenance of running weighted statistics, which can result in incorrect micro-cluster centers, radii, and weights.

For CluStream, performance issues arise during training rather than prediction. When micro-cluster merging is required, the algorithm performs quadratic-time pairwise distance computations between all micro-cluster centers, resulting in (O(
M^{2}
)) complexity per merge event
11
. Combined with additional overhead from periodic k-means reclustering, this leads to rapidly increasing training time as the stream progresses. Due to these inherent scalability limitations, CluStream was fully rejected from further consideration in this study.

As a result of the identified performance and correctness issues, the River implementations were deemed unsuitable for large-scale experiments. Consequently, all DenStream-related results reported in this paper were obtained using pyDenStream, which more closely follows the original algorithmic specification and avoids repeated reclustering during prediction.

The experimental results reveal a fundamental tension between cluster quality and operational characteristics. While DenStream provides superior cluster quality metrics, the elevated Narrative Contingency and Variance suggest that incremental clustering introduces temporal instability in topic structure, a critical consideration for operational intelligence systems where analysts expect consistent topic definitions across reporting periods.
It is important to note that the differences observed in narrative specific metrics may be partially attributed to the fact that the narrative extraction step (LLM-based topic labeling and enrichment) was fine tuned on the output of HDBSCAN clustering. This means the narrative metrics may inherently favor HDBSCAN’s cluster structure, as the downstream processing pipeline was optimized for the characteristics of HDBSCAN outputs. The higher Narrative Contingency and Variance observed in online algorithms may therefore reflect not only their temporal adaptation behavior but also a mismatch with the expectations encoded in the narrative extraction layer.

The baseline HDBSCAN’s strength lies in its temporal stability (low contingency and variance), achieved through complete daily re-clustering that establishes consistent reference frames. However, this stability comes at the cost of computational scalability and inability to leverage historical patterns.

DenStream emerges as the most promising online alternative, offering substantial improvements in cluster quality that may justify the trade-off in temporal stability. The algorithm’s ability to discover more distinct narrative clusters (higher distinctness) while maintaining reasonable internal coherence positions it as a viable candidate for production deployment, particularly in scenarios where cluster quality takes precedence over strict temporal consistency.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

Overall, the results indicate that density-based online clustering can serve as a viable replacement for batch HDBSCAN in narrative monitoring pipelines, provided that system design accounts for the trade-off between cluster quality and temporal stability. Among the evaluated methods, DenStream offers the most favorable balance, achieving superior cluster separation and narrative distinctness while maintaining feasible computational performance for streaming deployment. We therefore recommend DenStream as the primary candidate for production integration in systems where scalability and adaptation to evolving narratives are critical.

Further investigation should examine whether DenStream’s temporal variability can be mitigated through hyperparameter tuning or post-processing stabilization techniques, potentially enabling the algorithm to achieve both superior cluster quality and operational stability required for narrative monitoring applications. Additionally, retuning the narrative extraction pipeline to accommodate the characteristics of online clustering outputs may help reduce the observed narrative metric discrepancies and provide a more fair comparison across algorithms.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study.

All experiments were conducted on data from the Ukrainian information space, characterized by high geopolitical intensity, multilingual content (Russian, Ukrainian, code-switched
surjik
), and elevated narrative volatility of wartime news stream. While this setting is representative of narrative monitoring applications, results may not directly generalize to domains with slower topic evolution, more formal language (e.g., scientific corpora), or lower noise levels. The relative advantages of online clustering are less pronounced in more static environments.

Clustering performance is inherently conditioned on upstream representation learning and dimensionality reduction. The experiments rely on transformer-based embeddings and UMAP projections; different encoder models, reduction techniques, or parameter settings could alter cluster geometry and affect the comparative performance of HDBSCAN and online methods. Thus, conclusions concern the clustering stage within this pipeline, not clustering in isolation.

Narrative-specific metrics are partly influenced by the LLM-based topic labeling and enrichment layer, which was originally tuned on HDBSCAN outputs. This may introduce structural bias favoring the baseline and partially penalizing online algorithms whose cluster shapes differ. Consequently, narrative metric comparisons may reflect both clustering behavior and downstream pipeline compatibility rather than pure cluster structure quality.

Some observed performance differences arise from practical limitations of available implementations (e.g., River), rather than intrinsic algorithmic properties. Although alternative implementations were used where necessary, system-level conclusions are influenced by software maturity and engineering trade-offs, which may change as libraries evolve.

The streaming simulation spans a limited historical window and a single evaluation day. While sufficient to study incremental behavior, longer-term dynamics such as gradual concept drift, seasonal topic cycles, or rare narrative shocks were not systematically evaluated. Extended longitudinal studies are required to fully assess stability and adaptation trade-offs.

Clusters are treated as proxies for narratives, yet narrative structure is inherently fluid and may not align perfectly with density-based cluster boundaries. Some differences in “narrative quality” metrics may therefore reflect modeling assumptions rather than true differences in narrative discovery capability.
