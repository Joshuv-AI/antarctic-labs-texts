Hydroxylated dendrimer nanogels co-deliver drugs and cytokines for chemoimmunotherapy of glioma
Authors: Jinxia Wang, Ziwen Zhang, Aiyu Li, Xianghao Xiao, Xiangyang Shi, Mingwu Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Developing simple blood-brain barrier (BBB)-crossing nanoplatforms capable of overcoming the restrictions of BBB and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) still remains challenging. Herein, we develop a dual pH/reactive oxygen species-responsive dendrimer nanogel (DNG) platform based on generation 3 poly(amidoamine) (G3 PAMAM) dendrimers for co-delivery of the hypoxia-activated prodrug tirapazamine (TPZ) and the immunomodulatory cytokine interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). First, DNGs were synthesized by crosslinking hydroxylated G3 PAMAM dendrimers partially modified with phenylboronic acid (PBA) with dual PBA-terminated poly(ethylene glycol) via a reverse microemulsion method, followed by physical loading of TPZ within the DNGs and surface complexation of IFN-γ. The prepared DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ with a size of 72.5 nm display a desired colloidal stability, and can release TPZ and IFN-γ under the TME condition to induce cancer cell death through TPZ-mediated cytotoxic radical generation and IFN-γ-induced upregulation of pro-apoptotic pathway. Moreover, the released IFN-γ is able to promote macrophage M1 polarization, dendritic cell maturation, T lymphocyte activation, and regulatory T cell suppression. Leveraging the rich hydroxyl groups and the enhanced permeability and retention effect of DNGs, the DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ can penetrate the BBB and demonstrate potent therapeutic efficacy in an orthotopic mouse glioma model. The developed DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ with a simple composition may be developed to co-deliver drugs and cytokines to tackle other cancer types.

Introduction

Glioma, one of the most aggressive primary tumor types, poses severe challenges in clinical management. The complex structure of brain hinders precise surgical resection, resulting in a high recurrence rate [1,2]. In addition, blood-brain barrier (BBB) composed of dense capillary endothelial cells, an intact basement membrane, pericytes, and astrocytes prevents approximately 98 % of small molecular drugs and 99.9 % of macromolecular therapeutics from entering the tumor site, and impedes the immune cell infiltration into the brain, thus resulting in suboptimal outcomes for immunotherapies (e.g., cytokine therapy, therapeutic vaccines, and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy) against glioma [3,4]. Recent advances in nanotechnology have enabled nanodelivery systems such as dendrimers, liposomes, or polymer nanogels (NGs) to cross the BBB for glioma targeting via passive enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect or through transferrin modification or cell membrane coating for active targeting [[5], [6], [7]]. However, the clinical translation of these nanoplatforms is impeded by the high costs and complicated steps of ligand conjugation and cell membrane extraction, as well as poor batch-to-batch stability. Therefore, it is imperative to develop innovative BBB-crossing nanodelivery platforms that can be prepared in a straightforward way with a scalable manufacturing process.

Chemotherapy remains one of the most common glioma treatment modalities. Among numerous chemotherapeutic agents, tirapazamine (TPZ) can be activated under hypoxic conditions to form highly reactive free radicals that can induce the break of DNA strands, thus selectively killing hypoxic tumor cells [8]. However, poor BBB penetration and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) limit its efficacy. Immunotherapy has emerged as an indispensable strategy due to its significant clinical potential [9]. The combination of chemotherapy with immunotherapy can improve therapeutic outcomes by modulating the immunosuppressive TME and activating the immune system to prevent tumor recurrence. Among various types of immunotherapy modes, cytokine therapy provides precise modulation of immune cells with desired biocompatibility, and can synergize with chemotherapy to enhance the antitumor efficacy [10]. Cytokines offer broader applicability and accessibility than the complex cell-based therapies such as CAR-T since they act as chemical messengers to regulate immune responses, cell proliferation, fibrosis, and angiogenesis [11]. For instance, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) potently suppresses tumors by activating signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) and caspase pathways, promoting macrophage M1 polarization, dendritic cell (DC) maturation, pro-inflammatory factor secretion, and T cell activation [12,13]. Nevertheless, the efficacy of natural free cytokines is hindered by a narrow therapeutic window and short circulatory half-life. It is vital to develop BBB-crossing nanocarriers to deliver both chemical drugs and cytokines to glioma for effective combination treatment.

Poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) dendrimers serve as promising nanocarriers for brain drug delivery due to their highly branched three-dimensional structure, easiness in surface functionalization, excellent biocompatibility after surface modification, and low immunogenicity [14,15]. Crucially, earlier studies have shown that dendrimers with hydroxyl termini are capable of crossing BBB for precise targeting of microglia to treat brain injury or glioma for effective treatment [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. For example, Sharma and coworkers covalently conjugated rapamycin to generation 4 (G4) PAMAM dendrimers with hydroxyl termini for systemic targeting of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in glioma [20]. Similarly, Liaw et al. engineered hydroxylated G4 PAMAM dendrimer-triptolide conjugates for TAM-specific drug delivery to glioma [21].

As one typical nano carrier system, polymer NGs exhibit tunable size, colloidal stability, desired biocompatibility, high drug-loading capacity, a magnified EPR effect, as well as easy incorporation of functional groups [22]. Further, polymer NGs can be designed to be stimuli-responsive to TME to improve the bioavailability of the delivered drugs [23]. Previously, we have demonstrated that generation 3 (G3) PAMAM dendrimers could serve as monomers to form dendrimer NGs (DNGs) for combined tumor therapy and imaging diagnosis, leveraging an amplified EPR effect [24,25]. Along this line, DNGs synthesized using dendrimers as monomers [26] that have combined advantages of both dendrimers and NGs may be developed for high-efficiency drug loading and BBB crossing to tackle brain glioma.

Herein, we developed a pH/ROS dual-responsive DNG platform based on G3 PAMAM dendrimers for the codelivery of the TPZ and IFN-γ (for short DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ), aiming to achieve efficient chemoimmunotherapy of glioma. This DNG platform was constructed by crosslinking G3 PAMAM dendrimers that were surface-modified with phenylboronic acid (PBA) and glycidol by dual PBA-terminated poly(ethylene glycol) (PBA-PEG-PBA) via a reverse microemulsion method. The formed DNGs were then loaded with TPZ through physical encapsulation and surface complexed with IFN-γ via dendrimer terminal PBA group-mediated ionic and cation-π interactions to form a co-delivery system of DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ (Scheme 1a) for chemoimmunotherapy of glioma based on the hypoxic and immunosuppressive microenvironment of glioma (Scheme 1b). The dual-responsive DNG system was characterized using different techniques and fully evaluated in vitro and in vivo using an orthotopic mouse glioma model in terms of the chemoimmunotherapy performances. In the designed DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ system, TPZ exploits glioma hypoxia as an “activation switch” to generate cytotoxic radicals for tumor-specific killing, while IFN-γ promotes M1-type macrophage polarization, DC maturation, pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, tumor CD4+/CD8+ T cell filtration and regulatory T cell (Treg) suppression to reverse immunosuppression. According to our literature investigation, we confirm that this is the first development of DNG systems that exploit the hypoxic TME to activate the chemotherapeutic agent TPZ for killing tumor cells while concurrently activating the immune response via IFN-γ for efficient chemoimmunotherapy of glioma. Experimental section

Synthesis of G3-PBA-OH dendrimers

G3.NH2 dendrimers (30 mg, in 5 mL DMSO) were first partially modified with 4-(bromomethyl)phenylboronic acid (Br-PBA, 7.5 mg, in 5 mL DMSO) under magnetic stirring at 70 °C in a water bath for 24 h. The reaction mixture was subject to dialysis against water and freeze dried to get the G3-PBA dendrimers. Then, the obtained G3-PBA dendrimers were reacted with excess glycidol to get the G3-PBA-OH dendrimers.

Synthesis of DNGs

The DNGs were prepared through boronic ester bond crosslinking between hydroxyl groups of the G3-PBA-OH dendrimers and PBA of the PBA-PEG-PBA crosslinker in an inverse microemulsion. Briefly, G3-PBA-OH dendrimer (10 mg) and PBA-PEG-PBA were co-dissolved in 1 mL of water under different dendrimer/crosslinker molar ratios (1:1, 1:3, 1:5, 1:10 and 1:15, respectively). The mixture solution was then slowly added into a Span 80/Tween 80 solution (5/1, w/w, 280 mg) in 10 mL of cyclohexane to form a water-in-oil emulsion. The obtained emulsion was ultrasonicated for 10 min and added with 500 μL triethylamine to initiate the crosslinking reaction under stirring at room temperature overnight. The resulting emulsion was centrifuged, and the precipitate was dispersed in 5 mL of ethanol, dialyzed against water to obtain the DNGs in aqueous solution. To prepare Cyanine5.5 (Cy5.5)-labeled DNGs, DNGs (10 mg, in 10 mL water) were reacted with Cy5.5 N-hydroxysuccinimide ester (Cy5.5-NHS, 0.1 mg, in 10 μL DMSO) under stirring for 24 h, dialyzed against water, and lyophilized to give a blue-colored powder.

Preparation of DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ

For TPZ loading, DNGs were mixed with the TPZ at different DNG/TPZ mass ratios in aqueous solution under stirring overnight at room temperature. Then, each mixture was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 15 min for 3 times using an Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filter (Darmstadt, Germany) with an MWCO of 10000 Da to discard non-loaded free TPZ and obtain DNGs-TPZ. We used bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a model protein to evaluate the protein-loading capacity of DNGs-TPZ. DNGs-TPZ with optimized TPZ loading were mixed with BSA at different DNG-TPZ/BSA mass ratios (4: 1, 8: 1, or 20: 1) for 30 min under stirring. The mixture solution was then centrifuged using an Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filter with an MWCO of 100000 Da to remove non-loaded BSA and obtain DNGs-TPZ/BSA. The method for loading IFN-γ onto the DNGs-TPZ was identical to that used for BSA loading.

Cell culture and in vitro assays

C6 cells (a rat glioma cell line), bEnd.3 cells (a murine brain microvascular endothelia cell line), murine DCs, and RAW264.7 cells (a murine macrophage cell line) were normally cultured, passaged, and employed for in vitro assays, including cytotoxicity, cellular uptake, BBB crossing, DC maturation, macrophage polarization, Western blot (WB) and cell apoptosis.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were carried out after approval by the Committee on Experimental Animal Care and Use of Donghua University (Approval No. DHUEC-NSFC-2023-22) and following the policy of the National Ministry of Health of China. The orthotopic glioma mouse model was built up for magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, biodistribution, and in vivo chemoimmunotherapy. Histological examination of the major organs, hemolysis assay of mouse blood, blood routine and blood biochemical analyses were performed to validate the biosafety of DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were represented as the mean ± SD through at least three experiments. One-way analysis of variance statistical method was adopted to analyze the experimental results through GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software (San Diego, CA). A p value of 0.05 was selected as a significance level, and the data were indicated with (∗) for p < 0.05, (∗∗) for p < 0.01, and (∗∗∗) for p < 0.001, respectively.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ

Based on the illustration in Fig. 1, G3 PAMAM dendrimers were first partially grafted with Br-PBA to render them with high protein binding affinity [27]. Subsequently, the G3 dendrimers with remaining terminal amines were reacted with an excess amount of glycidol, introducing hydroxyl functional groups onto the surface to obtain G3-PBA-OH dendrimers (Fig. S1). The synthesized dendrimer derivatives were characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 1a–b). The proton signals at 2.2–3.4 ppm are assigned to the characteristic methylene protons of the G3 PAMAM dendrimer, while the signals at 7.0–8.0 ppm are attributed to the aromatic protons of the PBA moieties, indicating the successful PBA modification onto the dendrimer surface. NMR integrations of the corresponding peaks revealed that approximately 5.9 PBA groups were modified onto each G3 PAMAM dendrimer. Subsequently, DNGs with dual responsiveness to acidic pH and ROS were synthesized via a reverse microemulsion method. The DNGs were formed through boronic ester bond crosslinking between the hydroxyl groups of G3-PBA-OH and the boric acid groups of the crosslinker PBA-PEG-PBA (see the reaction formula of G3-PBA-OH with PBA-PEG-PBA in Figure S2, and 1H NMR spectrum of PBA-PEG-PBA in Fig. S3). To optimize the particle size and dispersity, DNGs were prepared using varying molar ratios of G3-PBA-OH to PBA-PEG-PBA. Dynamic light scattering (DLS, Fig. 1c) and zeta potential measurements (Fig. 1d) were conducted to characterize the DNGs. At a G3-PBA-OH/PBA-PEG-PBA molar feed ratio of 1: 3, DNGs yielded display the smallest hydrodynamic diameter (107 nm) and the lowest polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.231, along with a positive zeta potential of 19 mV. Hence, the 1: 3 molar ratio was selected as an optimal one. The morphology and size of DNGs synthesized at the optimal molar ratio was further observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging. As shown in Fig. 1e, the DNGs display a spherical shape with a mean diameter of 66.5 nm and a uniform size distribution. Further, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to analyze the structures of G3, G3-OH, G3-OH-PBA, and the resulting DNGs. As shown in Fig. 1f, both G3-PBA and G3-PBA-OH display weak B-O stretching vibrations in the range of 1320–1380 cm−1. The DNGs also exhibit a significantly enhanced B-O stretching vibration peaks in the same region, confirming the successful DNG crosslinking via the formation of boronic ester bonds. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was further carried out to verify the existence of B-O bonds. The characteristic peak at 191.1 eV can be seen in the high-resolution B 1s spectrum (Fig. S4), suggesting the successful formation of boronic ester bonds in the DNGs. To quantify the composition of the DNGs, we firstly determined that the yield of the synthesized DNGs was approximately 87 %. Then, we synthesized DNGs using Cy5.5-labeled G3-PBA-OH dendrimers as monomers, and separated the unreacted precursors from the purified product via centrifugation. By employing ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectral analysis of the supernatant to determine the amount of free Cy5.5-labeled G3-PBA-OH dendrimers at 675 nm (absorption peak of Cy5.5), we confirmed that 62 % of the G3-PBA-OH dendrimers were present in the DNGs using the formula of [(initial weight of G3-PBA-OH – weight of G3-PBA-OH in the supernatant)/actual weight of DNGs] × 100 %. Hence, the content of PBA-PEG-PBA in the DNGs can be estimated to be 38 %.

Subsequently, TPZ was encapsulated within DNGs via physical encapsulation. The encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and loading capacity (LC%) were evaluated at various DNG/TPZ mass ratios (Table S1). The DNGs exhibit relatively high EE% (37.22 %) and LC% (15.69 %) at a DNG/TPZ mass ratio of 2: 1. Hence, the 2: 1 mass ratio was selected for the preparation of TPZ-loaded DNGs (for short DNGs-TPZ). As shown in Fig. 1g, the DNGs-TPZ exhibit a clear absorption peak at 460 nm, which is a characteristic feature of TPZ, suggesting the successful incorporation of TPZ within the DNGs. Next, IFN-γ was complexed with DNGs-TPZ through the dendrimer PBA terminal groups. To validate the protein loading, BSA was first used as a model protein. EE% and LC% were evaluated at different DNG-TPZ/BSA mass ratios. As shown in Table S2, at the DNG-TPZ/BSA mass ratio of 20: 1, BSA was completely loaded. Considering the low effective dose required for IFN-γ in biological applications, the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ mass ratio of 127.5: 1 was chosen to ensure complete protein encapsulation for subsequent cellular studies. As indicated in Table S3, the DNG-TPZ shows decent IFN-γ loading capacity with EE and LC of 100 % and 0.79 %, respectively at a DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ mass ratio of 127.5: 1. It should be noted that the DNGs formed through crosslinking of G3-PBA-OH dendrimers with PBA-PEG-PBA have surface PBA groups, which are available to complex IFN-γ, primarily through cationic-π and ionic interactions [27], although a portion of IFN-γ may be physically encapsulated within the DNG network. The resulting DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ were next examined by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE, Fig. 1h). Both the free IFN-γ and DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ samples display a protein band at approximately 15.5 kDa, corresponding to IFN-γ, thereby confirming the successful incorporation of the cytokine onto the DNG system. Our study also suggests that the complexation of IFN-γ onto the surface of DNG-TPZ does not seem to significantly alter the electrophoretic mobility of IFN-γ, in agreement with the literature [28]. The final DNGs-TPZ and DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ have hydrodynamic sizes of 123 and 165 nm, and zeta potentials of 22 and 21 mV, respectively (Table S4). It seems that the complexation of IFN-γ leads to increased hydrodynamic size of the DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ without affecting their surface potential. Further, the colloidal stability of the DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ exposed to water, phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) for one week was evaluated, and no obvious changes in their hydrodynamic size could be found (Fig. S5). TEM imaging indicates that the DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ exhibit a spherical shape with a mean diameter of 72.5 nm (Fig. S6a–b). Additionally, atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging shows that the height of DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ is approximately 6.7 nm, while the diameter measured is around 86.7 nm, which is close to the TEM result (Fig. S7a–b). The diameter/height ratio measured by AFM is around 12.9, which is a typical feature of NGs [29].

Furthermore, the release kinetics of TPZ from DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ was investigated under different conditions including PBS (pH 7.4) with or without 10 mM H2O2, and phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) with or without 10 mM H2O2 (Fig. 1i). The release of TPZ is significantly accelerated under acidic and oxidative conditions (pH 6.5 + 10 mM H2O2) with a cumulative release rate reaching 46.3 % after 48 h, markedly higher than under other conditions. Afterward, the release kinetics of the BSA (mimic of IFN-γ) from DNGs-TPZ/BSA was also verified under the above conditions (Fig. S8). In the presence of acidic pH and H2O2, BSA can be fast released to reach a level of 81.3 % after 48 h, likely due to the acidic pH/H2O2-mediated broken of boronic ester bonds. These results suggest that DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ enable tumor-specific drug/protein release through their TME responsiveness associated to acidic pH and ROS.

In vitro cytotoxicity, cellular uptake and BBB crossing evaluations

We first evaluated the cytotoxicity of drug-free DNGs on C6 cells and bEnd.3 cells. As shown in Fig. S9, even at a DNG concentration up to 200 μg/mL, both C6 and bEnd.3 cells maintain a relatively high viability, indicating that the prepared DNGs possess excellent cytocompatibility. Subsequently, the cytotoxicity of free TPZ, DNGs-TPZ, and DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ toward C6 cells was assessed under normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig. 2a). C6 cells treated with the free TPZ exhibit a concentration-dependent reduction of viability under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions. In particular, TPZ shows more significant cytotoxicity under hypoxic condition than under normoxic condition at a TPZ concentration of 2 μg/mL or greater (p < 0.05), implying that TPZ was effectively activated in a hypoxic microenvironment to generate cytotoxic free radicals, in agreement with the literature [30]. Furthermore, the DNG-TPZ treatment also results in decreased viability of C6 cells (Fig. 2b), which could be due to the improved intracellular uptake and delivery efficiency of TPZ when encapsulated within DNGs. When compared to the cytotoxic effects of both the TPZ and DNG-TPZ, the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ shows greater reduction in C6 cell viability at the higher TPZ concentrations (Fig. 2c). Further, the cytotoxicity of TPZ-free DNGs-IFN-γ toward C6 cells was also assessed under normoxic conditions (Fig. S10). Clearly, the viability of C6 cells reduces at an IFN-γ concentration of 2 μg/mL or greater. These results may be attributed to the presence of IFN-γ, which potentially induces apoptosis in C6 cells, thereby synergizing with the chemotherapeutic effect of TPZ. To investigate the cellular uptake behavior, Cy5.5-labeled DNGs co-loaded with TPZ and IFN-γ (designated as DNGs-Cy5.5-TPZ/IFN-γ) were incubated with C6 cells. The intracellular uptake was analyzed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry (Fig. 2d and Fig. S11a–b). C6 cells shows time-dependent red fluorescence intensity increase after incubated with the DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ, indicating the efficient cellular uptake and internalization of the materials. These observations are consistent with flow cytometry analysis, further confirming the effective cellular uptake of the DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ.

To evaluate whether the developed NG system is capable of crossing the BBB, an in-vitro BBB transwell model was established by culturing bEnd.3 cells on the upper chamber (Fig. 2e). When the transepithelial electric resistance value of the bEnd.3 monolayer exceeded 150 Ω cm2, the model was considered to be suitable for subsequent permeability studies. To assess the BBB permeability, either free BSA-Cy5.5 or DNG-TPZ/BSA-Cy5.5 was added to the upper chamber of the transwell system, respectively. After 6 h incubation, the fluorescence intensities of cells in both the upper and lower chambers were measured using an IVIS Lumina imaging system (Fig. 2f–g). The DNG-TPZ/BSA-Cy5.5 group demonstrated significantly enhanced translocation across the BBB model, with a penetration efficiency of 25.5 %, which is much higher than that of free BSA-Cy5.5 (14.0 %, Fig. 2h). Then, we further verified the BBB penetration ability of the final material DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ-Cy5.5. As shown in Fig. S12, the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ-Cy5.5 group also exhibits comparable BBB permeation efficiency of 22.5 %. These results suggest that the DNG delivery system display a desired BBB permeability.

We further checked the colocalization of DNG-TPZ/BSA-Cy5.5 with lysosomes (labeled with LysoTracker) in bEnd.3 cells (Fig. S13). CLSM images show that a large portion of DNGs-TPZ/BSA-Cy5.5 is retained in the lysosomes after incubation for 4 h, and the DNGs gradually escape from the lysosomes and are distributed in the cytoplasm with the extension of incubation time (Fig. S13a). The lysosome escape behavior of the DNGs can also be verified by Pearson's correlation coefficient, which peaks at 4 h and then decreases over time (Fig. S13b). These results demonstrate that the DNGs-TPZ/BSA-Cy5.5 are able to escape from lysosomes in bEnd.3 cells, likely conducive to the transcytosis of the particles across the endothelial cells for ultimate BBB crossing.

In vitro immune activation evaluation and anticancer effects

Given the potent immunomodulatory function of IFN-γ, we next investigated the ability of DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ to induce macrophage M1 polarization and DC maturation. The expressions of CD86 (an M1-type macrophage marker) and CD206 (an M2-type macrophage marker) in RAW264.7 cells were quantified by flow cytometry (Fig. 3a). Cells treated with both free IFN-γ and DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ show significantly upregulated CD86 expression. Notably, the M1/M2 ratios in the free IFN-γ and DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ groups reach 16.37 % and 10.44 %, respectively, markedly higher than those in other treatment groups (Fig. 3c), suggesting the effective IFN-γ-induced polarization of pro-inflammatory M1-type macrophages. Additionally, the DC maturation was assessed by quantifying the expressions of co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 using flow cytometry (Fig. 3b). Both free IFN-γ and DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ effectively promote DC maturation, and the proportion of mature DCs in the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ group reaches 11 %, close to the free IFN-γ group (11.6 %, Fig. 3d). In addition to activating immune responses, IFN-γ can also directly bind to IFN-γ receptors on the surface of cancer cells, leading to the activation of the STAT1 signaling pathway [31]. This activation subsequently leads to upregulated expressions of fatty acid synthase and TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligands, which in turn activate caspase-8 and caspase-3 through a series of biological cascades, ultimately inducing cell apoptosis [32]. To verify this mechanism, we performed WB analyses to evaluate the activation of the STAT1/caspase-8/caspase-3 apoptotic pathway in C6 cells after various treatments (Fig. 3e). As shown in Fig. 3f, both free IFN-γ and DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ groups display more significantly upregulated STAT1 expression than other groups (p < 0.001), while the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ group shows the most pronounced activations of caspase-8 and caspase-3 among all groups (p < 0.001). This is likely due to the enhanced cellular uptake and sustained intracellular release of IFN-γ facilitated by the DNG delivery system, as free IFN-γ is known to be difficult to be endocytosed by cells [33].

To further examine apoptosis and necrosis levels under normoxic and hypoxic conditions, C6 cells were differently treated and assayed by flow cytometry (Fig. 3g and Fig. S14). The PBS, DNG, and free IFN-γ groups display minimal apoptosis or necrosis. In contrast, cells treated with TPZ, DNG-TPZ, and DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ show substantially increased levels of cell death, and the percentages of apoptotic and necrotic cells under the same treatments are much higher under hypoxic conditions than under normoxic conditions. This should be attributed to the TPZ-mediated generation of toxic free radicals under hypoxic conditions. Notably, DNG-TPZ exhibits greater cytotoxicity than free TPZ, likely due to the enhanced intracellular delivery via DNGs. The DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ group displays the highest level of cell apoptosis and necrosis, which may be owing to the synergistic effects of hypoxia-activated TPZ cytotoxicity and IFN-γ-induced cell apoptosis.

Both BSA-Cy5.5 or DNG-TPZ/BSA-Cy5.5 groups show a time-dependent brain red fluorescence increase, and after the peak time point of 6 h, the red fluorescence intensities of brain in both groups decline. In any case, the DNG-TPZ/BSA-Cy5.5 group shows markedly stronger brain fluorescence intensity than the free BSA-Cy5.5 at various time points, indicating the greater BBB crossing ability of the DNG-TPZ/BSA-Cy5.5. The ability of DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ to penetrate the BBB was further verified by using Cy5.5 to mark IFN-γ. As shown in Fig. S15, DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ-Cy5.5 can be significantly accumulated in the brain, in consistence with the results of DNG-TPZ/BSA-Cy5.5 group. It can be safely concluded that the developed DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ can effectively cross the BBB. In vivo antitumor effects

We subsequently evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ in an orthotopic glioma mouse model using the given treatment schedule (Fig. 5a). Mice received intravenous injections of various formulations, and tumor progression was monitored using T1-weighted MR imaging with Magnevist® as a contrast agent (9.38 mg/kg). Representative pseudocolor T1-weighted MR images and the corresponding relative tumor volumes over time are given in Fig. 5b and c. Compared to the PBS, DNG, and free IFN-γ groups without significant tumor inhibition effect, mice treated with TPZ, DNG-TPZ, and DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ exhibit varying degrees of tumor growth inhibition. Notably, the DNG-TPZ group shows greater tumor suppression than the free TPZ group, indicating that DNGs effectively facilitate glioma TPZ delivery across the BBB with enhanced chemotherapeutic efficacy. Among all groups, the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ treatment results in the most pronounced tumor inhibition, which is presumably due to the synergistic effects of combined chemoimmunotherapy mediated by TPZ and IFN-γ, respectively. The best antitumor efficacy of the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ treatment can be also reflected by the mouse survival rate data (Fig. S16). The mouse survival rate of the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ treatment group reaches 80 % at 20 days post treatment, while the survival rates in the groups of DNG-TPZ and TPZ reach 40 % at 20 days post treatment and 20 % at 18 days post treatment, respectively. In contrast, all the other groups including IFN-γ, DNG and PBS have a 0 % survival rate at 17 days or shorter time period post treatment. Meanwhile, the proportion of Tregs in the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ group is the lowest (Fig. 6b and Fig. S18c). To assess immune cell infiltration within the TME, immunofluorescence staining of brain tumor sections was carried out (Fig. 6c and Fig. S19a–b). Significantly increased infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells can be found in the glioma regions of mice injected with DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ, suggesting potent activation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes by the combined chemoimmunotherapy. Furthermore, the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ group exhibits a marked reduction in CD206+TAMs and an increase in CD11c+DCs infiltration (Fig. 6d and Fig. S19c–d), suggesting the role of the material played for M1 macrophage polarization and DC maturation. In addition, serum cytokine levels were analyzed to evaluate systemic immune responses. As shown in Fig. 6e–g, the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ group exhibits the highest expression levels of antitumor proinflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α, further confirming the strong immune activation elicited by the treatment.

Biosafety evaluations

To assess the in vivo biocompatibility of DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ, in vitro hemolysis assay of mouse red blood cells (RBCs) was carried out (Fig. S20). The hemolysis percentages of RBCs incubated with the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ under varying TPZ concentrations are all lower than 5 %, implying the desirable hemocompatibility of the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ. Furthermore, the main organs of glioma-bearing mice after different treatments for 11 days were collected and sectioned before H&E staining examinations (Fig. S21). No significant pathological abnormalities are observed in all groups, demonstrating the good organ biocompatibility of the injected formulations. Lastly, blood samples of mice from different treatment groups were harvested for blood routine and biochemical analyses. RBCs, white blood cells (WBCs), and platelets (PLTs) in all groups remain within the respective normal physiological ranges (Fig. S22a–c). Blood biochemical analyses further confirm that the key indicators of hepatic and renal functions including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and uric acid (UA) for all groups are within the safe levels (Fig. S23a–c), showing no appreciable differences in comparison to the PBS group. These results collectively demonstrate the excellent biosafety profile of the DNG-TPZ/IFN-γ formulation.

Conclusion

We developed a dual pH/ROS-responsive DNG nanoplatform capable of crossing BBB for the codelivery of TPZ and IFN-γ to achieve synergistic chemoimmunotherapy of orthotopic glioma. We show that G3 PAMAM dendrimers partially modified with PBA and fully capped with glycidol hydroxyl groups can be crosslinked to form DNGs with dual pH/ROS responsiveness and a desired stability. The formed DNGs can be further physically loaded with TPZ and surface complexed with IFN-γ to generate a nanomedicine platform of DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ with a mean particle size of 72.5 nm. The DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ can cross BBB thanks to the rich hydroxyl functional groups and magnified EPR effect of DNGs, and release both TPZ that can be activated within hypoxic TME to generate cytotoxic radicals for tumor-specific killing and IFN-γ that can potentially induce tumor cell death by upregulating the STAT1/caspase-3/caspase-8 signaling pathway and trigger a powerful immune response by promoting M1-type macrophage polarization, DC maturation, and recruitment of tumor-infiltrating CD4+/CD8+ T cells, while reducing immunosuppressive Tregs and M2-type TAMs. The developed DNGs-TPZ/IFN-γ are proven to be biocompatible, and may represent a promising nanoplatform that integrates BBB penetration, chemotherapy, and immune activation for efficient glioma therapy.
