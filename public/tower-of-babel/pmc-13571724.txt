Mitochondrial reprogramming of alveolar macrophages by umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells restores immune homeostasis in Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide–induced aspiration pneumonia
Authors: Yun-Fei Lin, Han-Ying Jhuang, Meng-Hsun Tsai, Hsiu-Huan Wang, Ko-Jiunn Liu, B. Linju Yen, Yi-Wen Chen, Fei Liu, Li-Tzu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aspiration pneumonia represents a severe form of acute lung injury (ALI) in which oral pathogen–derived factors disrupt pulmonary immune homeostasis. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg), a key periodontal pathogen, acts as a potent immunogenic stimulus that drives macrophage (MΦ) dysregulation. Human umbilical cord–derived mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs) have emerged as living biomaterials with intrinsic immunomodulatory and regenerative properties; however, their mechanisms of action in Pg LPS–induced aspiration pneumonia remain incompletely defined. Here, we integrate in silico transcriptomic analysis, single-cell RNA sequencing, and in vitro and in vivo models to characterize MΦ subset–specific responses to Pg LPS and to evaluate UCMSC-mediated immunomodulation. Pg LPS induces robust M1 polarization programs associated with inflammatory signaling pathways, while single-cell analysis reveals baseline heterogeneity among lung MΦ subsets, including M1-skewed recruited MΦs, mixed interstitial MΦs, and predominantly M2 alveolar MΦs. In vitro, Pg LPS drives M1 polarization across all subsets, which is effectively reversed by UCMSCs. In vivo, Pg LPS exposure predominantly reprograms recruited and alveolar MΦs toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, whereas UCMSC treatment effectively restores alveolar MΦs to an anti-inflammatory M2 state. Mechanistically, UCMSCs function as bioactive carriers that transfer mitochondria to alveolar MΦs, thereby reprogramming their metabolic and immunological states. This is accompanied by suppression of IL-6 and TNF-α, increased IL-10 production, preservation of alveolar architecture, and improved physiological outcomes. Collectively, these findings identify alveolar macrophages as key immunological targets in Pg LPS–induced lung injury and establish UCMSCs as a next-generation immunomodulatory platform that restores immune homeostasis via mitochondrial transfer. This work highlights the potential of cell-based biomaterials as dynamic immunomodulatory adjuvants for treating aspiration pneumonia and other inflammation-driven diseases.

Introduction

Aspiration pneumonia is a serious pulmonary condition caused by the inhalation of oropharyngeal or gastric contents into the lower respiratory tract [1]. Among pathogens implicated, Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg), a Gram-negative anaerobe and major periodontal pathogen, has been increasingly recognized for its potential role in extraoral diseases [[2], [3], [4]]. However, Pg itself may not maintain stable virulence in the respiratory tract. Instead, the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) derived from Pg acts as a potent virulence factor that can initiate host immune responses [5,6]. When aspirated into the lungs, Pg LPS may provoke pro-inflammatory signaling cascades that can disrupt pulmonary homeostasis and may contribute to acute lung injury (ALI) [7], making it a clinically relevant link between oral dysbiosis and respiratory disease.

Lung macrophages (MΦs) are central regulators of immune defense and tissue maintenance in the respiratory system [8,9]. They consist of heterogeneous populations, primarily including resident alveolar MΦs (AMΦs), interstitial MΦs (IMΦs), and recruited monocyte-derived MΦs (RMΦs). These subsets differ in origin, localization, and functional specialization [10,11]. AMΦs reside on the alveolar surface and are the first immune cells to encounter inhaled particles, pathogens, and environmental insults [12]. Under steady-state conditions, AMΦs maintain an anti-inflammatory environment that prevents excessive immune activation in the alveolar space [13].

In response to infectious or inflammatory stimuli, lung MΦs can polarize toward distinct functional phenotypes. Classically activated M1 MΦs produce pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-6 and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), facilitating pathogen clearance but also promoting collateral tissue injury [[14], [15], [16]]. Alternatively activated M2 MΦs secrete anti-inflammatory mediators, promote tissue repair with strong mitochondrial metabolism [17,18], and contribute to the resolution of inflammation [19]. While transient M1 activation is essential for pathogen defense, prolonged or excessive M1 polarization can result in sustained inflammation and tissue damage [10,16]. In severe pneumonia, such an imbalance—favoring M1 over M2 phenotypes—has been associated with impaired resolution and worsened outcomes [16].

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have gained significant attention as potential therapies for inflammatory and immune-mediated diseases because of their strong immunomodulatory and regenerative properties [[20], [21], [22]]. Human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UCMSCs) are especially attractive for clinical application due to their non-invasive sourcing and high proliferative capacity [[23], [24], [25]]. Moreover, UCMSCs secrete a wide range of bioactive factors, including cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles that can modulate immune responses [26,27]. Through these mechanisms, UCMSCs have been shown to suppress pro-inflammatory activity, enhance anti-inflammatory responses, and facilitate tissue repair in various models of lung injury.

Preclinical studies increasingly demonstrate that UCMSCs often shift MΦs from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the regenerative M2 phenotype [28,29]. This immunomodulatory capacity suggests that UCMSCs could attenuate inflammatory lung injury while supporting resolution and regeneration. Although Pg LPS is known to be a strong inducer of M1 polarization [30], its differential impact across lung MΦ subsets remains poorly understood. Furthermore, previous MSC studies in ALI have largely used conventional Escherichia coli (E. coli) LPS-induced models and demonstrated broad anti-inflammatory effects, MΦ M2 polarization, mitochondrial transfer, or modulation of recruited mononuclear phagocytes [[31], [32], [33], [34], [35]]. However, whether UCMSCs modulate distinct lung MΦ subsets in the setting of oral pathogen-derived Pg LPS-induced pneumonia has not been elucidated.

In this study, we aim to characterize the polarization profiles of lung MΦ subsets following Pg LPS aspiration and to determine whether UCMSC administration can counteract Pg LPS-driven pro-inflammatory polarization and the underlying mechanism, thereby restoring immune balance in the pulmonary environment.

Methods

Cell culture

Human UCMSCs were purchased from commercial sources (Promocell, Heidelberg, Germany), and cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Invitrogen-Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% FBS (Hyclone-Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100U/ml of penicillin/streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine (all from Gibco-Thermo Fisher Scientific) [36,37]. UCMSCs at passages 8-10 were used for experiments.

Bioinformatic analyses

Transcriptomic data from human MΦs with or without Pg LPS exposure were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information–Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE24897) [38]. Principal component analysis (PCA) and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between Pg LPS and control groups were performed in Partek. Pathway enrichment of DEGs (fold change ≥ 2) in Gene Ontology Biological Processes (GOBP) was conducted using Metascape [39] and visualized with SRplot [40]. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was applied to assess positive enrichment of GOBP pathways, with criteria of normalized enrichment score (NES) > 1, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25, and p-value < 0.01. Molecule Activity Predictor (MAP) package in Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) was used to illustrate “Acute lung injury” network based on Pg LPS versus control groups. IPA highlighted regulatory relationships among up-regulatory (red) molecules, while the MAP tool predicted whether targeted processes or involved molecules were activated (orange) or inhibited (blue). Relationships were delineated with orange lines for positive regulation, yellow lines for inconsistent findings, and gray lines for cases where predictions were not made. Dashed lines represented indirect interactions derived from published data [41]. For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), data of CD45+ cells in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of healthy mice (GSE218884) were analyzed [42]. Cells with mitochondrial gene content less than 10% were retained, and downstream analyses, including clustering with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and gene expression with Violin plot, were conducted using the Seurat v5 package in R version (4.4.3).

In vitro pulmonary MΦ responses with Pg LPS and UCMSC treatment

Lung lobes from healthy mice were harvested in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI) after CO2 euthanasia, washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and placed in petri dishes containing 0.5 ml PBS. Tissues were minced into ∼1 mm fragments, then digested with 50 U/ml DNase and 1 mg/ml type IV collagenase at 37°C for 30 min. Digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of culture medium. Cell suspensions were filtered through a 100-μm strainer, red blood cells were lysed using hypotonic buffer (H2O with 0.15 M NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3and 0.1 mM Na2-EDTA), cells were washed with cold PBS. Lung-derived immune cells were cultured with Pg LPS at 1 μg/ml in the presence or absence of UCMSCs for 24 h. After culture, suspended cells were collected, washed, and stained with PerCP-LIVE/DEAD dye, APC/Fire750-anti-mouse CD45, eFluor506-anti-mouse F4/80, BV711-anti-mouse CD11b, APC-anti-mouse Ly6C, PE-anti-mouse CD86, and PE/Dazzle594-anti-mouse CD206 (all from BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 20 min, followed by washing and flow cytometry with UMAP-based algorithm or conventional analysis. CD45 were first gated for analysis of the frequency of CD11b−F4/80+ AMΦs and CD11b+ Ly6C−F4/80+ IMΦs or CD11b+Ly6C+F4/80+ RMΦs, and then CD86 and CD206 were analyzed for M1 as well as M2 frequency in the three MΦ subsets. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted by a different independent technician using a predefined gating strategy.

Mouse model of Pg LPS-related aspiration pneumonia and UCMSC injection

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of National Taiwan University (No. 20210084). Six-week-old C57BL/6J male mice procured from the National Laboratory Animal Center in Taiwan were used for the implantation study as previously reported [[43], [44], [45]]. Commercially pure grade IV titanium implants (0.5 mm diameter, 1.2 mm length) were placed in the maxillary edentulous region, with or without injection of Pg LPS (100 μg; InvivoGen, Hong Kong). UCMSCs (3 x 105 in 100 μl PBS) were administered intravenously on days 1 and 4. Mice were euthanized with CO2 on day 7, with lungs collected for evaluation of cytokine profiles, MΦ analyses, and histological evaluation.

Mouse model of Pg LPS-induced pneumonia and UCMSC injection

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Taipei Medical University (No. SHLAC2024-0085). Six-week-old C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of Zoletil (20 mg/kg)/Xylazine (5 mg/kg) mixture, and inoculated intratracheally with 50 μl of the Pg LPS suspension (100 μg). Control group mice were inoculated with 50 μl of PBS. UCMSCs with or without MitoTracker labeling (3 x 105 cells in 100 μl PBS) or PBS were intravenously injected 2 h after Pg LPS injection. At 24 h post-inoculation, mice were euthanized with CO2, and lungs were collected for MΦ analyses, cytokine profiling, and histological evaluation. For mitochondrial assessment, human UCMSCs were prelabeled with MitoTracker Green, washed four times with PBS [46], and then administered intravenously. Lung-derived cells were then harvested and stained with MitoTracker Red to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). For polymorphonuclear neutrophil (PMN) analysis, BV711-anti-mouse CD11b and FITC-anti-mouse Ly6G antibodies were used to identify PMNs.

ELISA

Culture medium, homogenate from the left lung lobe of each animal was harvested, and the levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and Il-10 in these samples, as well as serum surfactant protein D (SP-D) were measured by ELISA according to manufacturer's instructions (eBioscience Systems).

Histological analysis

One lobe of right lungs was excised and then fixed with 10% formalin overnight. Fixed lung tissue was sequentially dehydrated with 20% and 30% sucrose, embedded in optical cutting temperature compound, and sectioned at 10-μm thickness for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The scoring of lung tissue injury was performed in a blinded manner by an independent different technician, and done according to our previously report in which interstitial damage, vasculitis, peri-bronchitis, edema, thrombus formation, and pleuritis were all evaluated. Injury was graded on a scale of 0-4: 0 = normal/no injury, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe, and 4 = very severe [16].

Statistical analysis

For comparisons between two groups, Student's t-test was used, while for comparisons between multiple groups, ANOVA was used followed by Tukey test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (CA, USA) and data are shown as mean ± SD.

Results

Exposure to Pg LPS activates human MΦs and may contribute to ALI

To assess the effects of Pg LPS on lung injury, we first performed bulk RNA sequencing analysis of human MΦs with or without Pg LPS treatment in vitro. Principal component analysis (PCA) on these sequencing data revealed remarkable alterations in transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 1A) and the activation of inflammatory responses (Fig. 1B). Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that Pg LPS specifically activated MΦs through “Cytokine mediated signaling pathway,” “Macrophage activation,” and “Response to Interferon gamma,” accompanied by increased expression of key pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL6 and TNF, alongside suppression of IL10 (Fig. 1C–E). These changes were associated with pathways linked to “acute lung injury” (Fig. 1F). To explore lung MΦ heterogeneity, we analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq data of the BALF from healthy mice. We found that MΦs constitute the dominant immune cell population in BALF (Fig. 1G) and can be categorized into six clusters (0-5; Fig. 1H). Pseudotime and polarization analyses suggested a trajectory from an M2-like state (high Cd163, Mrc1/Cd206, il10, and Arg1) toward an M1-like state (increased Cd80, Cd86, Tnf, and Nos2 expression), even under baseline conditions (Fig. 1I–K). Phenotypically, AMΦs, identified by high Cd11c and Siglecf but low Cd11b, Cd14, Ccr2, Ly6c2, and Cx3cr1, were predominantly M2-like; RMΦs, high in Cd11b, Cd14, Ccr2, Ly6c2 but low in Cd11c and Siglecf, were mainly M1-like; and IMΦs, high in Cd11b and Cx3cr1, displayed a mixed M1/M2 phenotype (Fig. 1L–M). These findings further support the use of CD11b and Ly6C as key markers to distinguish RMΦs (CD11b+Ly6C+), IMΦs (CD11b+Ly6C−), and AMΦs (CD11b−Ly6C−) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Human UCMSC treatment reverses M1 polarization induced by Pg LPS in the three major lung MΦ subsets

To assess the effects of Pg LPS on lung MΦ subsets and the modulatory role of UCMSCs, we incubated lung-derived single cell suspensions with Pg LPS in the presence or absence of human UCMSCs for 24 h, followed by analysis of MΦ responses (Fig. 2A). In total lung MΦs, Pg LPS significantly increased M1 frequency, decreased M2 frequency, and elevated the M1/M2 ratio, all of which were significantly reversed by UCMSC treatment (Fig. 2B–D). Moreover, Pg LPS significantly increased CD86 expression (Fig. 2E), and decreased CD206 expression (Fig. 2F), resulting in a significant elevation of the CD86/CD206 expression ratio (Fig. 2G). UCMSC treatment significantly reversed these Pg LPS-induced changes by reducing CD86 expression, restoring CD206 expression, and lowering CD86/CD206 ratio, with minimal effect on lung MΦ responses under control conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). To delineate subset-specific effects, we categorized lung MΦs as CD11b+Ly6C+ RMΦs, CD11b+Ly6C− IMΦs, and CD11b−Ly6C− AMΦs [10,13], and found that M1 population was higher in RMΦs and M2 was higher in AMΦs (Fig. 2H). Pg LPS exposure significantly increased M1 frequencies in all three MΦ subsets, decreased M2 frequency in AMΦs (Fig. 2H–K), and elevated the M1/M2 ratios across all three subsets (Fig. 2L–N). UCMSC treatment reversed these Pg LPS-induced changes, most notably by reducing M1 frequencies in RMΦs and IMΦs, and by lowering M1/M2 ratios in all the three MΦ subsets. At the expression level, Pg LPS significantly increased CD86 expression in all three MΦ subsets. Conversely, it slightly decreased CD206 expression in RMΦs and IMΦs but significantly in AMΦs (Fig. 2O–T). As a result, significantly higher CD86/CD206 ratios were observed across all subsets (Fig. 2U–W). UCMSC treatment reversed these expression-level changes, particularly by significantly decreasing CD86 expression in RMΦs and AMΦs, restoring CD206 expression in AMΦs, and decreasing CD86/CD206 ratios across all subsets. Intravenous administration of UCMSCs reversed the M2-to-M1 polarization in lung MΦs caused by Pg LPS-mediated aspiration in mouse models

To assess the effects of Pg LPS-mediated aspiration on lung MΦs and evaluate the modulatory potential of UCMSCs, we developed a murine model in which dental implant placement was followed by peri-implant Pg LPS injection to induce aspiration during the procedure (Fig. 3A). UCMSCs were administered intravenously on days 1 and 4. After 7 days, mice were sacrificed, and lung-derived cells were collected for MΦ analysis with multi-color flow cytometry. UMAP analysis of flow cytometry data revealed distinct M1-like and M2-like clusters among lung MΦs (Fig. 3B). More M1-like cells were found in the lungs of Pg LPS-aspirated mice, whereas UCMSC administration shifted this distribution toward M2-like cells (Fig. 3C). Conventional flow cytometric analysis confirmed that Pg LPS-mediated aspiration significantly increased M1 frequencies, decreased M2 frequencies, and elevated the M1/M2 frequency ratio (Fig. 3D–F), while UCMSC administration partially reversed these changes. For marker expression, Pg LPS-mediated aspiration significantly increased CD86, decreased CD206, and elevated the CD86/CD206 ratio (Fig. 3G–I). UCMSC administration partially reversed CD86 and the CD86/CD206 ratio, and significantly restored CD206. UCMSC administration modulates resident alveolar MΦs from an M1 to an M2 phenotype in lungs with Pg LPS-mediated aspiration

To assess the effects of Pg LPS on lung MΦ subsets and the modulatory role of UCMSCs during Pg LPS-mediated aspiration, we analyzed lung MΦ subsets from Pg LPS-aspirated mice with or without UCMSC administration. UMAP analysis suggested enrichment of M1-like RMΦs and AMΦs after Pg LPS aspiration, with UCMSCs restoring M2-like AMΦs (Fig. 3B–C). Conventional flow cytometric analysis confirmed that Pg LPS-mediated aspiration significantly increased M1 frequencies in RMΦs, decreased M2 frequencies across all subsets, and elevated the M1/M2 frequency ratios in RMΦs and AMΦs (Fig. 3J–P). These changes were reversed by UCMSC administration, with the most pronounced effects on restoring M2 frequencies and lowering M1/M2 frequency ratios in AMΦs. At the expression level, Pg LPS-mediated aspiration significantly increased CD86 in RMΦs and AMΦs, decreased CD206 in IMΦs and AMΦs, and elevated CD86/CD206 ratios across all subsets (Fig. 3Q–Y). UCMSC administration significantly reduced CD86, restored CD206, and lowered CD86/CD206 ratios to baseline levels in AMΦs. These results indicate that AMΦs undergo a strong shift from an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype to a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype in Pg LPS-mediated aspiration, and that UCMSC administration specifically and effectively restores the M2 phenotype.

Intravenous administration of UCMSCs reversed the M2-to-M1 polarization in lung MΦs caused by intratracheal Pg LPS challenge in vivo

To directly assess the impact of Pg LPS on lung MΦ activation and the therapeutic potential of UCMSCs, we intratracheally delivered Pg LPS to target the lung microenvironment, followed 2 h later by intravenous UCMSC administration, and subsequently analyzed lung MΦ responses 24 h post-challenge (Fig. 4A). UMAP analysis revealed a clear separation between M1 and M2 populations in total lung MΦs, while a subset remained in an unpolarized state (Fig. 4B). Direct Pg LPS challenge markedly increased M1 density and decreased M2 density, whereas UCMSC administration shifted the distribution toward M2 dominance, closely resembling the pattern in control lungs (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometric quantification confirmed that Pg LPS exposure significantly increased M1 frequency, decreased M2 frequency, and elevated the M1/M2 ratio (Fig. 4D–F), all of which were significantly reversed by UCMSC administration. At the expression level, Pg LPS significantly upregulated CD86, downregulated CD206, and increased the CD86/CD206 ratio (Fig. 4G–I). UCMSCs mitigated these effects, significantly decreased CD86 and partially restored the CD86/CD206 ratio. These findings indicate that direct intrapulmonary Pg LPS exposure drives robust pro-inflammatory M1 polarization, which can be counteracted by UCMSC administration to promote an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in Pg LPS-induced pneumonia.

To determine the therapeutic efficacy of UCMSCs in Pg LPS-induced pneumonia, we performed inflammatory and histopathological assessments. Pg LPS exposure significantly increased PMN infiltration and levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6, whereas UCMSC treatment significantly reduced PMN infiltration and these pro-inflammatory cytokines and, notably, increased the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 5M–Q). Moreover, direct intratracheal delivery of Pg LPS resulted in pronounced immune cell infiltration and interstitial edema, leading to significantly elevated lung injury scores (Fig. 5R–S). UCMSC administration preserved normal lung architecture and significantly reduced inflammatory lesions. Importantly, Pg LPS-challenged mice exhibited a rapid increase in serum SP-D accompanied by body weight loss within 24 h (Fig. 5T–U), consistent with acute illness. UCMSC administration significantly reduced serum SP-D levels and mitigated Pg LPS-induced weight loss, while UCMSC treatment alone had minimal effects on serum SP-D levels in control mice (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that UCMSCs not only attenuate Pg LPS–induced lung tissue damage and inflammatory cytokine production—shifting the immune response from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory milieu—but also improve overall health, underscoring their therapeutic potential in Pg LPS–induced pneumonia.

Discussion

Our study provides new insight into the subset-specific responses of lung MΦs to Pg LPS and the immunomodulatory role of human UCMSCs in both in vitro and in vivo settings. By combining high-dimensional phenotyping and functional assays, we demonstrate that Pg LPS consistently drives M1 polarization in lung MΦs, but with markedly different patterns depending on the experimental context. In vitro, all three major MΦ subsets—RMΦs, IMΦs, and AMΦs— undergo robust M1 skewing. However, M1 polarization is largely confined to AMΦs in vivo whether Pg LPS is delivered via aspiration or direct intratracheal challenge. UCMSC treatment counteracts these pro-inflammatory shifts in both contexts via mitochondrial transfer, with broad effects in vitro and more AMΦ-focused effects in vivo (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Summary:Pg LPS-driven M1 polarization was reversed by human UCMSC administration, which promoted a transition to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, particularly in resident AMΦs. UCMSC treatment also markedly reduced inflammatory cytokine levels, mitigated lung injury, and restored lung homeostasis in Pg LPS-induced pneumonia.

Summary:Pg LPS-driven M1 polarization was reversed by human UCMSC administration, which promoted a transition to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, particularly in resident AMΦs. UCMSC treatment also markedly reduced inflammatory cytokine levels, mitigated lung injury, and restored lung homeostasis in Pg LPS-induced pneumonia.

In this study, we employed Pg LPS rather than live Pg to model aspiration pneumonia. Pg is a Gram-negative anaerobe with stringent growth requirements, making it technically challenging to sustain for reproducible pulmonary inoculations. In contrast, Pg LPS represents its major virulence determinant, capable of activating innate immune responses via Toll-like receptors (TLR2 and TLR4) [49]. The use of purified Pg LPS also avoids confounding variables associated with live bacterial infection, including colonization dynamics and interactions with the resident lung microbiome, thereby allowing precise control over dose, timing, and localization of the inflammatory challenge. This reductionist approach facilitates the dissection of host immune mechanisms—particularly MΦ subset responses. Moreover, the Pg LPS-mediated aspiration model during implantation was designed to mimic a clinically relevant setting in which oral/peri-implant inflammatory components may enter the lower respiratory tract, whereas the direct intratracheal Pg LPS model provided a more controlled system to validate the pulmonary effects of Pg LPS itself. The consistent UCMSC-mediated immunomodulatory effects observed across both models support the mechanistic robustness of Pg LPS-induced lung MΦ dysregulation and UCMSC-mediated immune restoration. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this model remains reductionist and does not fully recapitulate the complexity of human aspiration pneumonia, which may involve mixed oral/gastric contents, polymicrobial communities, digestive enzymes, and host-specific factors; therefore, future studies using fluorescent Pg LPS tracing, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid analysis, and live bacterial, polymicrobial, or gastric-content aspiration models are needed to further validate the translational relevance of UCMSC therapy.

The broader activation in vitro likely reflects uniform Pg LPS exposure in suspension culture, where ligand freely engages pattern-recognition receptors on all MΦs. The absence of epithelial and extracellular-matrix barriers, together with cytokine accumulation at higher cell densities, can amplify signaling, leading to broad M1 skewing across subsets. In contrast, in vivo responses reflect the compartmentalized lung architecture and spatially restricted Pg LPS deposition. In both aspiration and intratracheal models, the highest Pg LPS burden localizes to the alveolar lumen, where AMΦs act as first-responder sentinels [13]. RMΦs (monocyte-derived) [50] and IMΦs (interstitial/vascular gatekeepers) [10] likely encounter lower Pg LPS concentrations and are activated secondarily via cytokines/chemokines released by AMΦs. This localization explains predominant AMΦ M1 skewing in vivo, with RMΦs and IMΦs comparatively less affected during the early acute phase. These observations align with reports that AMΦs dominate immediate responses to inhaled bacterial products, whereas RMΦs and IMΦs contribute more to later exacerbation and regulation.

M2 MΦs are characterized by a metabolic program that strongly favors mitochondrial function, relying on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), fatty acid oxidation (FAO), and intact tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity, to support their anti-inflammatory and reparative phenotype [17,18,51,52]. Unlike proinflammatory M1 MΦs—which rapidly shift toward aerobic glycolysis and exhibit disrupted mitochondrial respiration—M2 MΦs maintain high mitochondrial membrane potential, enhanced electron transport chain activity, and efficient oxidative ATP production. This mitochondrial-centered metabolic state enables sustained production of anti-inflammatory mediators, tissue-remodeling factors, and pro-resolving cytokines. Consequently, interventions that improve mitochondrial quality or supply exogenous mitochondria—such as MSC-mediated mitochondrial transfer through direct cell contact or secreted vesicles [33,53]—can reinforce M2 polarization and amplify MΦ-driven resolution of inflammation.

UCMSCs reversed Pg LPS-induced M1 polarization in all MΦ subsets in vitro, consistent with direct cell-cell contact and unhindered diffusion of paracrine factors. UCMSC secretomes are enriched in anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10 [54], prostaglandin E2 [55], transforming growth factor-beta [56], and extracellular vesicles containing immunomodulatory miRNA or mitochondria [57], to modulate MΦ responses. In a closed in vitro system, these factors can achieve high local concentrations and act uniformly on all MΦ subsets, suppressing their activation and promoting M2 polarization. In vivo, UCMSCs were most pronounced in AMΦs, mirroring the subset most strongly polarized by Pg LPS. Intravenously infused UCMSCs preferentially lodge in the pulmonary microvasculature, enabling concentrated delivery of soluble factors to the alveolar space, where AMΦs reside. Moreover, UCMSCs may immunomodulate AMΦs more effectively due to a number of chemokines secreted by Pg LPS-induced AMΦs for their interaction, due to a process called priming [58]. Because RMΦs and IMΦs are less polarized in vivo under Pg LPS exposure, the scope for measurable “reversal” by UCMSCs is reduced, leading to apparent selectivity of effect. The ability of UCMSCs to selectively restore AMΦs toward an M2 phenotype in vivo is therapeutically relevant. AMΦs are not only central to initiating alveolar inflammation but also pivotal for orchestrating resolution and repair. By re-establishing their anti-inflammatory, tissue-protective functions, UCMSCs may dampen excessive lung inflammation and promote resolution of Pg LPS-induced injury.

While several previous studies of MSC therapy have used E. Coli LPS to model bacterial ALI, they have provided only limited insight into lung MΦ subset responses [33,35,59]. In contrast, little is known about MSC effects in the Pg LPS-driven inflammation. Pg LPS differs structurally and functionally from E. Coli LPS [60] and is clinically relevant to oral pathogen-associated aspiration events. Therefore, focusing on Pg LPS addresses an important knowledge gap in MSC-based therapy and enables deeper characterization of MΦ subset-specific mechanisms in oral pathogen-driven lung inflammation.

One limitation of this study is the flow-cytometry strategy used to define lung MΦ subsets and polarization status. Lung MΦ subsets were operationally classified using CD11b and Ly6C, without additional canonical AMΦ and MΦ markers such as CD11c, Siglec F, CD64, MerTK, or MHC II. Although this strategy was supported by our public scRNA-seq analysis and allowed consistent comparison across experimental groups, it may not fully distinguish AMΦs, IMΦs, and RMΦs under inflammatory conditions, where surface-marker expression can be dynamically altered. In addition, M1/M2 polarization was assessed primarily by CD86 and CD206 expression, which provides a simplified phenotypic readout and does not fully capture the functional diversity of MΦ activation states. Future studies using more comprehensive flow-cytometry panels, sorted MΦ subsets, functional assays, and single-cell transcriptomic validation will be needed to further confirm lung MΦ subset identity and polarization status. Another limitation is that, although MitoTracker-based analysis and mitochondrial ΔΨm assessment support UCMSC-associated mitochondrial modulation in AMΦs, we did not directly dissect the mitochondrial transfer mechanism or establish causality using functional assays such as Seahorse-based respiration analysis, ATP measurement, mtDNA PCR, transwell separation, or inhibition of tunneling nanotube formation; therefore, future studies are needed to determine whether contact-dependent mitochondrial transfer or paracrine/extracellular vesicle-mediated mechanisms drive UCMSC-induced AMΦ modulation.

Conclusions

This work demonstrates that Pg LPS elicits broad M1 polarization of lung MΦ subsets in vitro but predominantly targets AMΦs in vivo. UCMSCs reverse these inflammatory shifts in both contexts, acting broadly in vitro and selectively in vivo to restore AMΦs toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype via mitochondrial transfer, reduce cytokine release, and preserve lung structure. These findings deepen our understanding of MΦ subset biology in Pg LPS-mediated aspiration pneumonia and identity AMΦ-focused modulation as a promising therapeutic approach. Targeted delivery of UCMSCs or their bioactive products to the alveolar space could represent a next-generation strategy for managing Pg LPS-related lung injury and potentially other bacterial alveolar-centric inflammatory diseases.
