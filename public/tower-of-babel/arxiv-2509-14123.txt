HYCO: Hybrid-Cooperative Learning for Data-Driven PDE Modeling
Lorenzo Liverani † and Matthys Steynberg †, Enrique Zuazua † ∗ §

Abstract
. We present Hybrid-Cooperative Learning (HYCO), a hybrid modeling framework that iteratively integrates physics-based and data-driven models through a mutual regularization mechanism. Unlike traditional approaches that impose physical constraints directly on synthetic models, HYCO treats the physical and synthetic components as co-trained agents: the physical and synthetic models are nudged toward agreement, while the synthetic model is enhanced to better fit the available data. This cooperative learning scheme is naturally parallelizable and improves robustness to noise as well as to sparse or heterogeneous data. Extensive numerical experiments on both static and time-dependent problems demonstrate that HYCO outperforms classical physics-based and data-driven methods, recovering accurate solutions and model parameters even under ill-posed conditions. The method also admits a natural game-theoretic interpretation, enabling alternating optimization and paving the way for future theoretical developments.

1.
Introduction

We introduce Hybrid-Cooperative Learning, HYCO in short, a new hybrid learning paradigm designed to combine the strengths of physics-based and data-driven approaches while mitigating their respective limitations. In contrast to traditional methods such as PINNs
[
23
]
, which impose physical and data-driven constraints directly on a single synthetic model, HYCO adopts a fundamentally different approach. It trains two models in parallel: one grounded in physical principles – typically an ODE or PDE (the physical model) – and the other guided by data, using a neural network ansatz (the synthetic model).

Our motivation stems from a fundamental modeling dilemma: the trade-off between the flexibility of purely data-driven models and the interpretability of physics-based models. This challenge has become even more pressing with the widespread adoption of machine learning (ML) techniques. Given a dataset, one typically faces a choice: rely on a data-driven model – flexible and adaptive, but unable to capture the underlying physics – or use a physics-based model – rigid and interpretable, yet often difficult to specify and calibrate. Neither approach alone is sufficient to fully explain complex observations, especially when data is sparse or noisy. However, their strengths are naturally complementary: data-driven models excel in regions rich with observations, while physics-based models are better suited for extrapolation beyond the observed domain.

Rather than merging the two approaches into a single architecture, we propose allowing them to interact and inform each other, much like two experts exchanging views before reaching a consensus on a complex problem. HYCO formalizes this idea through the following procedure:

\diamond

We begin with a set of observations, whose availability may vary across models depending on the application. The data can be shared between the two models, partially overlapping, or entirely distinct. In some cases, observations may be available only for the synthetic model, while the physical model has none. Moreover, the observations themselves may be heterogeneous in nature: for example, one model might receive direct information about the solution, while the other relies on indirect measurements such as source terms, fluxes, or integral constraints. This flexibility in data allocation is a key feature of our approach.

\diamond

Training proceeds by alternating updates. At every step:

–

The physical model receives the current prediction
\bm{u}_{syn}
from the synthetic model and adjusts its parameters to fit the synthetic solution and the data it has access to (if any). Here, by “parameters” we mean the physical parameters appearing in the formulation of the PDE or ODE which is the physical model (e.g. Reynolds number, diffusivity, etc.).

–

The synthetic model, based on a neural network ansatz, in turn, receives the prediction
\bm{u}_{phy}
from the physical model and updates its parameters to match the physical prediction and the data it has access to. Here, the parameters are those appearing in the neural network formulation.

This back-and-forth learning dynamic helps each model align not just with the data it sees and with the physical ansatz, but also with the behavior of its counterpart, leading to improved generalization, especially in regions where data is sparse.

1.1.
Scope and Advantages of HYCO

The scope of HYCO is broad. At its simplest, it can be used to embed standard machine learning models with physical biases, much like in the spirit of PINNs. However, in HYCO the physical bias is not introduced through a residual loss, but rather computed via a high-fidelity solver in computational and applied mathematics. This bypasses common issues such as the requirement for differentiable activation functions and the instability often associated with physics-informed training.
At the same time, the synthetic model acts as a learnable regularizer for the physical one, enriching classical modeling tools with data-driven insights.

In general, HYCO excels at merging partial information from heterogeneous sources. For instance, it can be applied in settings where we aim to reconcile observations in one region with the known physical model in another. A typical case is that of an incomplete PDE problem, where the right-hand side is known only in part of the domain, while solution measurements are available elsewhere. On this note, we remark that HYCO naturally applies to parameter identification and inverse problems, where mutual regularization improves recovery in settings that are ill-posed or lack sufficient information. This is a well-known challenge in the inverse problems literature and has become a major focus in scientific machine learning
[
24
,
29
]
.
The numerical experiments presented in this work confirm that HYCO consistently outperforms both physical and data-driven approaches, especially when the training data is noisy or localized.

Compared to existing strategies, HYCO presents several advantages:

(1)

The
symmetric
formulation makes the method highly flexible. The physical and synthetic models are decoupled and do not need to be aware of each other’s internal structure. This means that any combination of models can be hybridized, depending on the application at hand.

(2)

The method is inherently
parallelizable
: since both components are optimized independently and at the same time, they can be assigned to separate computing units. This makes training more efficient. Besides, it opens the door to extensions with more than two models, which could have applications in domain decomposition or multiphysics contexts.

(3)

Along the same lines, HYCO adapts well to
distributed or privacy-aware
scenarios. Only the outputs of the models need to be exchanged, not their parameters or their structure. In fact, even the datasets could potentially differ, and different agents might collect data at different locations. As a result, each component can be trained locally with limited communication, mirroring the ideas behind federated learning
[
32
]
.

(4)

Finally, when models can be shared, inference can be performed using either the physical or synthetic component, depending on the desired trade-off between accuracy and online speed of computation.

An important strength of HYCO is that, despite training two models instead of one, it remains computationally competitive thanks to its parallel structure. The mutual regularization between the models also means that the physical model typically does not require a very fine resolution. In fact, this characteristic can make HYCO more efficient than PINNs in certain cases, as demonstrated by the experiment in Section
4
(see Table
4.1
), where PINNs required a dense set of collocation points to enforce physical residuals, while HYCO achieved similar or better accuracy using a coarse grid for the physical model.

1.2.
A Cooperative Modeling Game

Interestingly, HYCO can be easily framed in a game-theoretical perspective. This provides a theoretical justification to the approach, and helps in interpreting the inner workings of the methodology. In more technical terms, given two models with parameters
\Lambda
(physical) and
\Theta
(synthetic), we define three losses:

\diamond

The physical loss
\mathsf{L}_{phy}(\Lambda)
, which is the distance between the physical model output,
\bm{u}_{phy}
, and the available observations. This term may also be omitted entirely if no data is assigned to the physical model.

\diamond

The synthetic loss
\mathsf{L}_{syn}(\Theta)
, which is the distance between the synthetic model output,
\bm{u}_{syn}
, and the observations;

\diamond

The interaction loss
\mathsf{L}_{int}(\Theta,\Lambda)
, which is a suitable distance between the prediction of the physical model and the prediction of the synthetic one.

Then, we aim to solve the following minimization problem

(1.1)

\displaystyle\min_{\Theta,\Lambda}

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}:=\alpha\mathsf{L}_{syn}(\Theta)+\beta\mathsf{L}_{phy}(\Lambda)+\mathsf{L}_{int}(\Theta,\Lambda)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{phy}\text{ solves the physical model}

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{syn}\text{ solves the synthetic model},

where the weights
\alpha,\beta>0
serve as tunable hyperparameters to adjust the relative emphasis placed on each model.

In this context, by “solves” we mean that each model generates its output by adhering to its own internal structure: for the physical model, this typically involves solving an ODE or PDE; for the synthetic model, this may involve a static approximation, such as a neural network, a Gaussian mixture model, or even a least-squares fit, but also a learned dynamical system, such as a Neural ODE. A visual summary of the overall framework is provided in Figure
1.1
.

Figure 1.1
.

of the physical model to one of the parameters

of the synthetic one. The goal of the physical model is to minimize its loss
\mathsf{L}_{1}
, which is the sum of
\mathsf{L}_{phy}
and
\mathsf{L}_{int}
. Similarly, the synthetic model minimizes
\mathsf{L}_{2}
. The interaction loss
\mathsf{L}_{int}
receives the two predictions
\bm{u}_{syn},\bm{u}_{phy}
, while
\mathsf{L}_{phy}
and
\mathsf{L}_{syn}
are the discrepancies from the observations. The arrow leading from the observations to the physical loss is dotted because one might choose not to supply the physical model with data.

Problem (
1.1
) can be seen as a multiobjective optimization task and naturally framed in the language of game theory. Instead of viewing the physical and synthetic components as parts of a single optimization process, we can interpret them as two autonomous agents or players, each with their own objective but coupled through a shared interaction term. Player 1, the physical model, seeks to minimize

\mathsf{L}_{1}:=\beta\mathsf{L}_{phy}(\Lambda)+\mathsf{L}_{int}(\Theta,\Lambda),

reducing its discrepancy from the data while also remaining close to the synthetic model’s predictions. Conversely, Player 2, the synthetic model, aims to minimize

\mathsf{L}_{2}:=\alpha\mathsf{L}_{syn}(\Theta)+\mathsf{L}_{int}(\Theta,\Lambda).

The best possible outcome is a Nash equilibrium: a pair of strategies
(\bm{u}_{phy},\bm{u}_{syn})
such that neither player can reduce their loss by unilaterally changing their parameters. In practice, especially when the true dynamics governing the data are unknown or inaccessible, this equilibrium embodies a compromise: by casting the problem as a game between these two imperfect models, we acknowledge their limitations and allow them to inform one another. The Nash equilibrium does not guarantee a perfect reconstruction of the data, but under uncertainty, it represents a stable and justifiable synthesis and, arguably, the best one can achieve given the circumstances. For further discussions on the matter, we refer to Section
6
.

On the implementation front, the game-theoretic perspective justifies the alternating minimization scheme described in the introduction, which simply translates into minimizing
\mathsf{L}_{1}
over
\Lambda
, while keeping
\Theta
fixed, and
\mathsf{L}_{2}
over
\Theta
, keeping
\Lambda
fixed.

2.
An Introductory Example: the Gray-Scott Model

As a testbed, we consider the well-known Gray-Scott reaction-diffusion system
[
9
]
, which models autocatalytic and irreversible chemical reactions between two species,
u
and
v
. This system serves as a prototypical example of nonlinear pattern formation, known for its rich and complex spatio-temporal dynamics. However, its inherent sensitivity makes training particularly challenging. Purely data-driven models often struggle to capture the underlying dynamics, especially when asked to extrapolate beyond the observed data range. On the other hand, traditional Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), which encode the governing equations as soft constraints within the loss function, tend to suffer from instability in this context, largely due to the stiffness of the equations and the scarcity of available data.
For these reasons, the Gray-Scott model is a well-known benchmark for assessing the capabilities of PDE Learning models
[
21
]
.

In this experiment, we evaluate HYCO’s ability to learn the full solution, while identifying the model’s diffusivity parameters at the same time. Consistent with the general framework introduced earlier, observational data is provided only to the synthetic model, while the physical model is trained solely through the interaction term (corresponding to taking
\beta=0
in
\mathsf{L}_{1}
).

On the unit square
\Omega=[0,1]\times[0,1]
, the system is defined as

(2.1)

\begin{cases}u_{t}-D_{u}\Delta u-uv^{2}+F(1-u)=0,\\
v_{t}-D_{v}\Delta v+uv^{2}-(F+k)v=0,\\
u(0,x,y)=u_{0}(x,y),\quad v(0,x,y)=v_{0}(x,y),\\
\text{Periodic boundary conditions}.\end{cases}

Here,
D_{u}
and
D_{v}
are the diffusion coefficients for
u
and
v
, respectively;
F
is the feed rate of
u
; and
k
denotes the decay rate of
v
. The ground-truth parameters used to generate the data in this experiment are:

D_{u}=2\times 10^{-6},\quad D_{v}=0.8\times 10^{-6},\quad F=0.018,\quad k=0.051.

The initial values are

u_{0}(x,y)=\chi_{B_{0.1}(1/2)},\quad v_{0}(x,y)=\chi_{\Omega\setminus B_{0.1}(1/2)},

where
\chi_{A}
denotes the characteristic function of the set
A
and
B_{0.1}(1/2)
is the ball of radius 0.1 centered at
(x,y)=(1/2,1/2)
. In what follows, we assume the initial datum is known. This information will be supplied to all compared models.

The dataset is constructed by numerically solving the Gray-Scott equations on a 64
\times
64 grid, using a finite difference scheme with small temporal steps (
5000
times steps from
0
to
T=2000
) to generate a reference true solution
\bm{u}^{D}
. From this solution, we sample the values of both species at
M=5000
random points, obtaining the dataset

\mathcal{D}:=\{\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}=\{u^{D}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i}),v^{D}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M},

where the superscript
D
denotes “Data”. Note, in particular, that our data is not equispaced in space nor in time.

The two models cooperating together in the HYCO strategy are:

\diamond

The physical model, governed by (
2.1
), solved using a finite difference scheme with the same discretization used to generate the data. In this experiment, we supply the physical model of the initial datum
(u_{0},v_{0})
. However, the diffusivity coefficients
D_{u},D_{v}
are considered unknown. In the notation of the Introduction, these are the parameters that the physical model will try to identify

\Lambda=\{D_{u},D_{v}\}.

These parameters are initialized far from the true values, at

D_{u}=1\times 10^{-6},\quad\text{and}\quad D_{v}=0.5\times 10^{-6}.

\diamond

The synthetic model is implemented as a feedforward deep neural network. The input is the vector
(x,y,t)
, and the network predicts the value of the solution at that point

\bm{u}_{syn}(x,y,t)=(u_{syn}(x,y,t),v_{syn}(x,y,t)).

In more detail, the neural network has four hidden layers, each containing 128 neurons. The activation function is the ReLU, defined as
\sigma(x)=\max\{0,x\}
. In summary, we have

\bm{u}_{syn}(x,y,t)=h_{5},

where

h_{0}=(x,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad h_{k}=\sigma(A_{k}h_{k-1}+b_{k})\text{ for }k=1,\ldots,4,\quad h_{5}=W_{5}h_{4}

and

A_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times 3},\;A_{2},A_{3},A_{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times 128},\;W_{5}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 128},\;b_{1},\ldots,b_{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{128}.

We indicate the set of trainable parameters by
\Theta
. The synthetic loss is thus defined as

\mathsf{L}_{syn}(\Theta):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\bm{u}_{syn}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i})-\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i})\|^{2}

Note that we do not penalize the parameters
\Theta
of the synthetic model, as the current form of the functional, through its penalty on the distance to the physical approximation
\bm{u}_{phy}
, already ensures sufficient coercivity.

To complete the HYCO setting, we introduce an interaction loss that measures the discrepancy between the synthetic and physical models. To this end, we select
H=1000
random “ghost points”
(x_{i}^{G},y_{i}^{G},t_{i}^{G})
in the time-space domain (with the superscript “G” standing for “ghost”). At these locations, we evaluate the predictions of both the physical and the synthetic models.
The interaction loss is then defined as:

\mathsf{L}_{int}(\Theta):=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\|\bm{u}_{syn}(x_{i}^{G},y_{i}^{G},t_{i}^{G})-\bm{u}_{phy}(x_{i}^{G},y_{i}^{G},t_{i}^{G})\|^{2}.

This approach, very much in the spirit of Random Batch Methods (RBM), avoids the prohibitive cost of comparing the two predictions over the entire coarse grid at every iteration. In practice, as we shall see, evaluating the interaction loss at a small number of randomly selected ghost points is sufficient to enforce physical consistency with significantly lower computational needs.

In conclusion, the physical model will minimize the loss
\mathsf{L}_{1}=\mathsf{L}_{int}(\Theta,\Lambda)
over
\Lambda
for
\Theta
fixed, while the synthetic model will minimize
\mathsf{L}_{2}=\alpha\,\mathsf{L}_{syn}+\mathsf{L}_{int},
over
\Theta
, for
\Lambda
fixed, in an alternating fashion. Here
\alpha>0
is a tunable hyperparameter balancing data fidelity and physical consistency in the synthetic loss.

To evaluate HYCO, we compare its performance to:

\diamond

A purely data-driven feed-forward neural network, without the physical bias, implemented by setting the interaction loss in
\mathsf{L}_{2}
to zero, effectively training only the synthetic model.

\diamond

A classical PINN, namely, a neural network function

\bm{u}_{\text{PINN}}(x,y,t)=(\hat{u}(x,y,t),\hat{v}(x,y,t)).

with parameters
\Theta
, optimized to minimize:

\tilde{\mathsf{L}}_{\text{PINN}}(\Theta,\Lambda)=\mathsf{L}_{\text{PINN}}(\Theta)+\gamma\mathsf{R}(\Theta,\Lambda),

where
\mathsf{L}_{\text{PINN}}(\Theta)
is the discrepancy with respect to the data

\mathsf{L}_{\text{PINN}}(\Theta)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\bm{u}_{\text{PINN}}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i})-\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i})\|^{2}

and
\mathsf{R}(\Theta,\Lambda)
is the PDE residual

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}(\Theta,\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\|\hat{u}_{t}-D_{u}\Delta\hat{u}-\hat{u}\hat{v}^{2}+F(1-\hat{u})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\hat{v}_{t}-D_{v}\Delta\hat{v}-\hat{u}\hat{v}^{2}+(F+k)\hat{v})\|^{2}.

The hyperparameter
\gamma
quantifies the strength of each loss term.
Note that the PINN is also assumed not to know the diffusivity parameters
D_{u},D_{v}
, hence the dependence of the residual term also on
\Lambda
.

In this experiment, the PINN consists of the same architecture of the HYCO neural network (four hidden layers with 128 neurons each), but uses the
\tanh
activation. This is necessary because the PINN loss requires the activation function to be twice differentiable. The PINN uses a total of
50000
collocation points to compute the residual.

Both HYCO and the “pure” neural network where trained for 600 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-3}
. The PINN was trained for 3500 epochs. A different number of training steps was needed to ensure a fair comparison between the models, since different hyperparameters might be effective for different architectures. Furthermore, the strength parameter
\alpha
in the HYCO loss is here set at 1, while
\gamma
in the PINN loss is set at 100.

3.
State of the Art

Mathematical modeling traditionally relies on selecting a parametric structure, be it an algebraic equation, an ODE or PDE, or a neural network, and adjusting its parameters to best fit observed data. Two dominant paradigms have emerged: physics-based and data-driven modeling.

Physics-based models are grounded in physical laws and typically involve a small number of interpretable parameters. Their strength lies in theoretical guarantees, generalizability, and extrapolation. Classical examples include the Navier-Stokes equations or conservation laws in continuum mechanics, where the form of the equation is fixed and only a few parameters (e.g., Reynolds number, diffusion coefficients) need identification.

In contrast, data-driven models rely on flexible, high-capacity function approximators (typically neural networks) and large datasets. These models do not require prior knowledge of governing laws and have shown exceptional empirical performance, especially in unstructured tasks such as image and speech recognition
[
19
]
. However, their performance degrades in low-data regimes, and they tend to lack interpretability and extrapolation capacity.

3.1.
Hybrid Modeling

To combine the complementary strengths of both paradigms, hybrid models have been introduced. The idea is simple: whenever some physical knowledge is available, one should exploit it to guide the learning process. This approach has gained substantial attention in recent years, especially in scientific machine learning, where data is expensive and often sparse
[
4
,
22
]
.

A well-known example is Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
[
23
]
, where the loss function penalizes not only the distance from the data but also from satisfying a known differential equation. Other approaches include Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy), Neural Operators
[
11
]
, or Latent Dynamics Models
[
25
]
, which integrate physics either explicitly or in latent space representations. All these methods rely on one crucial assumption: an ansatz for the underlying physical law is available and can be enforced during training.

These strategies seek to find a compromise between model expressivity and structure, producing a single model that reflects both physical prior and data fit. However, enforcing hard constraints on the model may overly restrict its flexibility, especially when the physical knowledge is partial or noisy.

3.2.
The HYCO Perspective

Rather than enforcing a single hybrid model, HYCO seeks to optimize two separate models, a physical and a synthetic one, penalizing their distance from data and their mutual prediction discrepancies.

This architecture draws inspiration from ideas already present in the literature. A first connection is to Ensemble Learning
[
3
,
31
]
, where predictions from multiple models are aggregated together. However, the key difference is that our aggregation is implicit and done during training, not after. In a similar vein, one can view the interaction term as a regularizer, analogous to PDE-based regularization
[
1
,
20
]
, where the synthetic model is biased toward physically consistent behavior by the proximity to the physical model.
The HYCO method also closely relates to recent ideas used in ADMM-PINN hybrids
[
28
]
, where different models are coordinated through consistency terms. Yet, our method makes no assumption of model hierarchy or structure: the two models are independent in architecture and are trained solely through consistency with data and with each other. Unlike PINNs or ADMM-PINNs, however, HYCO does not modify the synthetic architecture to enforce the physics, nor makes assumptions on the model hierarchy. Indeed, both models influence each other iteratively and indirectly.
Finally, the game-theoretical framework behind HYCO shares conceptual similarities with the Federated Learning paradigm
[
32
]
, where multiple agents collaborate to train a model without exchanging data. Although HYCO does not follow the federated approach in a strict sense, since it involves the cooperative training of distinct models rather than a shared one, it retains a similar philosophy. Each agent operates independently and communicates only through model predictions, preserving the privacy. Connections between these distributed learning settings and game theory are increasingly explored in the literature; see for instance
[
18
,
14
]
.

4.
A Static Example: The Helmholtz Equation

While we began by illustrating our algorithm with a dynamic problem, the HYCO method is equally adaptable to static settings. To demonstrate this, we consider the classical Helmholtz equation, a widely used benchmark in the machine learning and PDE literature for both forward and inverse problems
[
5
,
6
,
17
,
27
,
30
]
. With minor modifications, our setup closely follows Example 3.3.2 from the seminal work by Yang, Meng, and Karniadakis on B-PINNs
[
30
]
, though similar experiments appear in other studies as well, such as
[
6
]
.

The ground-truth model is the 2D heterogeneous Helmholtz equation with null Dirichlet boundary conditions
[
8
]
. The governing equation is

(4.1)

\begin{cases}\-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(x,y)\nabla\bm{u})+\eta(x,y)^{2}\bm{u}=f(x,y),&\text{for }(x,y)\in\Omega,\\
\bm{u}(x,y)=0,&\text{for }(x,y)\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega=[-\pi,\pi]\times[-\pi,\pi]
.
The ground-truth coefficients
\kappa(x,y)
and
\eta(x,y)
, respectively the diffusion term and the wave number, are defined as

\kappa(x,y)=\varphi(x,y;\alpha_{1},c_{1})+1,\quad\eta(x,y)=\varphi(x,y;\alpha_{2},c_{2})+1,

with parameters

\alpha_{1}=4,\quad\alpha_{2}=1,\quad c_{1}=(-1,-1),\quad c_{2}=(2,1),

\varphi(\cdot,\cdot;\alpha,c)
being the Gaussian function with amplitude
\alpha
and center
c

\varphi(x,y;\alpha,c)=\alpha e^{-(x-c_{x})^{2}-(y-c_{y})^{2}}.

Given this setup, following
[
30
]
, we set the reference solution to

(4.2)

\bm{u}(x,y)=\sin(x)\sin(y),

the corresponding forcing term being (computable through elementary but tedious computations):

(4.3)

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=2(x+1),\varphi(x,y;4,(-1,-1))\cos(x)\sin(y)

\displaystyle+2(y+1),\varphi(x,y;4,(-1,-1))\sin(x)\cos(y)

\displaystyle+\left[(\varphi(x,y;1,(2,1))+1)^{2}+2(\varphi(x,y;4,(-1,-1))+1)\right]\sin(x)\sin(y).

We generate data for our experiment by sampling this solution. Specifically, we randomly place
M=25
sensors and evaluate the solution at these locations. The resulting dataset is

\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{u}(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}.

The goal is to solve the inverse problem of recovering the parameters defining
\kappa
and
\eta
out of this dataset
\mathcal{D}
. More precisely:

Goal:

Given the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, source
f
, and assuming the model form (
4.1
) is known, identify the parameters
(\alpha_{j},c_{j})
for
j=1,2
such that the corresponding solution best matches the observations.

We denote the parameter set by

\Lambda:=\{\alpha_{1},c_{1},\alpha_{2},c_{2}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}.

Being
c_{1},c_{2}
2D points, the total number of parameters to fit is 6.

We now describe the models we compare in this experiment. In contrast with the Gray-Scott experiment, here we supply the physical model with the dataset
\mathcal{D}
. Consequently, we will also perform a comparison between HYCO and the purely physics-based FEM parameter fitting. For clarity, all numerical parameters and computational results are presented in a dedicated subsection immediately following the description of the methods.

1. Finite Element Parameter Fitting.

In the classical setting of parameter identification, we discretize equation (
4.1
) using the Finite Element Method and search for a parameter set
\Lambda
that minimizes the functional

\mathsf{L}_{\text{FEM}}(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\bm{u}_{\text{FEM}}(x_{i},y_{i})-\bm{u}_{i}^{D}(x_{i},y_{i})\|^{2},

which measures the discrepancy between the FEM solution
\bm{u}_{\text{FEM}}
and the data. Optimization is performed via gradient descent on the parameters.

2. The PINN Approach.

PINNs aim to approximate the solution of a PDE by requiring the network to fit both the data and the governing equation. With respect to the experiment in Section
2
, here the residual
\mathsf{R}(\Theta,\Lambda)
reads

\mathsf{R}(\Theta,\Lambda)=\|\nabla\cdot(\kappa(x,y)\nabla\bm{u}_{\text{PINN}})+\eta(x,y)^{2}\bm{u}_{\text{PINN}}-f(x,y)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

3. The HYCO Strategy.

Our proposed method involves three key elements

\diamond

The
physical model
is the FEM discretization of equation (
4.1
). In this case, we train the physical model also on data. Therefore, we have the loss,

\mathsf{L}_{phy}(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\bm{u}_{phy}(x_{i},y_{i})-\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i},y_{i})\|^{2},

where
\bm{u}_{phy}
is the FEM solution of the physical model.

\diamond

The
synthetic model
is a neural network (whose architecture will be made precise below). Its parameters
\Theta
represent weights and biases. The associated loss is the mean squared error:

\mathsf{L}_{syn}(\Theta)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\bm{u}_{syn}(x_{i},y_{i})-\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i},y_{i})\|^{2}.

\diamond

The
interaction term
, measuring the discrepancy between the physical and synthetic models away from the data. In order to compute this term, we randomly place a certain number
H
of ghost sensors within the domain. The interaction loss thus looks as follows

\mathsf{L}_{int}(\Theta,\Lambda)=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\|\bm{u}_{syn}(x_{h}^{G},y_{h}^{G})-\bm{u}_{phy}(x_{h}^{G},y_{h}^{G})\|^{2}.

Here the suffix
G
stands for “ghost”.

We now detail the algorithm used with HYCO.

Step 1: Initialization.
The unknown parameters are initialized randomly. For this experiment, the initial values for the physical parameters were set to

\alpha_{1}=2.41,\,\alpha_{2}=1.12,\,c_{1}=(0.19,0.42),\,c_{2}=(0.50,0.49).

The neural network parameters
\Theta
are initialized using a normal distribution with variance scaling
[
10
]
.

Step 2: Training the models.
This is the core of the algorithm. Both models are trained via stochastic gradient descent, alternating updates between the physical and synthetic models. At each epoch, we perform the following steps:

(1)

Update
\Lambda
by performing a gradient descent step to minimize the combined loss
\beta\mathsf{L}_{phy}+\mathsf{L}_{int}
, keeping
\Theta
fixed.

(2)

Update
\Theta
by performing a gradient descent step to minimize the combined loss
\alpha\mathsf{L}_{syn}+\mathsf{L}_{int}
, keeping
\Lambda
fixed. Note that no further penalization on the parameters
\Theta
is needed in this step. The
\mathsf{L}_{int}
component of the loss plays the role of regularizer.

We train the models for a certain number
K
of epochs.

Stopping Criterion.
Parameter identification problems often require an a priori early stopping criterion to avoid unnecessary computation once training stabilizes. For this and the subsequent numerical experiments, we adopt a simple strategy based on the stabilization of the identified parameters. In particular, we stop if

(4.4)

\Big\|\Lambda^{(k)}-\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{j=k-Z}^{k-1}\Lambda^{(j)}\Big\|<\varepsilon,

where
\Lambda^{(k)}
denotes the identified parameters at epoch
k
,
Z=200
is the window size, and
\varepsilon=5\times 10^{-3}
is a fixed tolerance. For consistency, the same stopping rule is used in all our numerical experiments.

A note on the error.
Over the various numerical experiments in this paper, we will need different metrics to quantify the performances of each method.
In particular, in addition to the normalized
L^{2}
error with respect to the true solution described at (
2.2
), we introduce the normalized euclidean error with respect to the true parameters

(4.5)

\mathsf{e}^{m}_{p}:=\frac{\|\Lambda-\Lambda_{m}\|}{\|\Lambda\|},

where
\Lambda
are the parameters used to generate the observations,
p
denotes “parameters” and
m
denotes the method: FEM, PINN and HYCO Physical. It will also be useful to identify with a letter the discrepancy of the output of each trained model from the observations. We indicate this by

(4.6)

\mathsf{e}^{m}_{d}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\bm{u}_{m}(x_{i},y_{i})-\bm{u}_{i}^{D}(x_{i},y_{i})\|^{2},

the
d
standing for “data” and
m
for the method. Note that this error is nothing but the already defined
\mathsf{L}_{\text{FEM}},\mathsf{L}_{\text{PINN}},\mathsf{L}_{syn}
and
\mathsf{L}_{phy}
.

Numerical parameters.
All models are trained for 3000 epochs using the Adam optimizer. For both FEM and HYCO, we use a discretization grid of
18\times 18
. In HYCO, the physical and synthetic models are trained with learning rates
5\times 10^{-3}
and
1\times 10^{-3}
, respectively. The synthetic model uses a neural network taking as input the point
(x,y)
and outputing the value of the solution
\bm{u}(x,y)
, with 2 hidden layers of width 256, ReLU activation, and residual connections. With the same notation of Section
2
:

\bm{u}_{syn}(x,y)=h_{2},

with

h_{0}=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad h_{1}=W_{1}\sigma(A_{1}h_{0}+b_{1})+h_{0},\quad h_{2}=W_{2}\sigma(A_{2}h_{1}+b_{2})+h_{1}.

Here

W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 256},\;A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{256\times 2},\;b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{256}.
The number of ghost points used in the interaction term is
H=200
throughout training.
The PINN baseline adopts the same network architecture,
\tanh
activation, and uses 400 collocation points for both boundary and interior.

5.
The Abstract HYCO Strategy

In this section we introduce the abstract HYCO strategy, formulated in continuous terms to retain full generality and remain independent of any specific discretization scheme. This formulation ensures that the framework can be flexibly adapted to a range of numerical methods. For example, the discretizations adopted in Sections
2
and
4
are only one possible choice; others may lead to different results.

The HYCO methodology is fully agnostic to the specific model structure: given access to a physical model and a synthetic (data-driven) model, HYCO integrates them into a unified training framework. The core ingredients are:

\diamond

Observational data sampled from the ground-truth system;

\diamond

A physical model ansatz depending on unknown structural parameters
\Lambda
;

\diamond

A purely data-driven synthetic model depending on parameters
\Theta
.

Once these components are defined, the objective is to jointly optimize
\Lambda
and
\Theta
to fit the data. Since
\Lambda
encodes physical parameters, this automatically yields a solution to the corresponding inverse problem.

A word of warning.

We assume, for simplicity, that both models have access to the full dataset. As noted in the Introduction and the Gray-Scott experiment, this assumption is not required in practice, since the models only need to share their predictions.

We present the abstract strategy in the dynamic setting, as applied in Section
2
and
7
. The static setting discussed in Section
4
then emerges as a special case of this general framework.

The Data.
To maintain maximum generality, we assume access to time-dependent measurements collected at
M
spatial locations for each of
N
discrete time points
\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
within the time interval
[0,T]
. The dataset is thus given by

\mathcal{D}:=\{\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),t_{j})\}_{i=1,j=1}^{M,N},

where
x_{i}^{D}(t_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the spatial location of the
i
-th sample at time
t_{j}
, and
\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),t_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the corresponding measurement. We assume
T>0
and that the time steps are either equispaced or known in advance.

This setting encompasses both static sampling strategies (as in Section
2
) and mobile ones (as in Section
2
and
7
). In the static case,
x_{i}^{D}(t_{j})
is constant in
j
, a configuration commonly encountered in both theoretical and applied contexts. In this section, we consider the more general setting where sampling locations may vary with time.

The Physical Model.
The physical ansatz is given by a suitable numerical discretization of the system

(5.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{phy}(x,t)+\mathscr{F}(\kappa)\bm{u}_{phy}(x,t)=f,&x\in\Omega,\ t\in(0,T],\\
\mathscr{G}(\lambda)\bm{u}_{phy}(x,t)=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\ t\in(0,T],\\
\bm{u}_{phy}(0,x)=\bm{u}_{0}(x),&x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

which governs the evolution of the unknown field
\bm{u}_{phy}:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The operators
\mathscr{F}(\kappa)
and
\mathscr{G}(\lambda)
are differential operators depending on structural parameters
\kappa
and
\lambda
, which may be constants or functions. The source term
f
and the initial condition
\bm{u}_{0}
may be known or unknown. All unknown quantities are grouped into the set of physical parameters:

\Lambda:=\{\kappa(x),\lambda(x),\ldots\}.

If the physical model has access to data, it seeks to minimize the loss

(5.2)

\mathscr{L}_{phy}(\Lambda):=\frac{1}{MN}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ell_{phy}(\bm{u}_{phy}(x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),t_{j}),\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),t_{j}))+\mathscr{P}(\Lambda),

subject to
\bm{u}_{phy}
solving (
5.1
). The function
\ell_{phy}
is a positive bivariate function that quantifies the mismatch between the model and the data, typically the mean squared error in our experiments. The term
\mathscr{P}(\Lambda)
is a regularization on the physical parameters.

The Synthetic Model.
This component can take the form of any machine learning architecture. In this work, we consider the case of a feed-forward neural network
\bm{u}_{syn}
, as in Section
2
. The loss mirrors that of the physical model:

(5.3)

\mathscr{L}_{syn}(\Theta):=\frac{1}{MN}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{syn}(\bm{u}_{syn}(x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),t_{j}),\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),t_{j}))+\mathscr{P}(\Theta),

where
\mathscr{P}(\Theta)
is a regularization term, typically used to promote properties such as sparsity.

The Interaction Loss.

The interaction loss measures the discrepancy between the synthetic and physical predictions over space-time. Accordingly, we simply set

(5.4)

\mathscr{L}_{int}(\Theta,\Lambda):=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\ell_{int}(\bm{u}_{syn}(x,t),\bm{u}_{phy}(x,t))dxdt.

Approximating numerically this term precisely at every epoch can make the training expensive. Therefore, in concrete examples, we decided to adopt the idea of ghost points, already introduced in Section
2
. Drawing from the theory of Random Batch Methods (RBM), at every epoch we sample a certain number
H
of ghost points and compute the norm of the difference of the two models on this chosen set.

6.
Theoretical foundations

Proving the convergence of the HYCO strategy is challenging, primarily due to the non-convexity of the associated loss functionals, which stems from the nonlinear dependence of the synthetic model on its trainable parameters. To overcome this, in this section we focus on a linear PDE model combined with a mean-field relaxed neural network ansatz, which allows us to rigorously establish convergence.

As mentioned earlier, the HYCO strategy can be naturally framed within a game-theoretic perspective, which proves helpful for the analysis. Instead of optimizing a single joint loss, we view the physical and synthetic models as two interacting agents, each pursuing its own objective, but coupled through a shared consistency term that ensures alignment between both components.

Formally, we introduce two players. The first is the physical model, which minimizes the functional

\mathsf{L}_{1}(\Theta,\Lambda):=\beta\mathsf{L}_{phy}(\Lambda)+\mathsf{L}_{int}(\Theta,\Lambda)

with respect to its own parameters
\Lambda
, keeping the synthetic parameters
\Theta
fixed. The minimization is carried out under the constraint that the predicted solution
\bm{u}_{phy}
solves the physical model. The second player is the synthetic model, which minimizes

\mathsf{L}_{2}(\Theta,\Lambda):=\alpha\mathsf{L}_{syn}(\Theta)+\mathsf{L}_{int}(\Theta,\Lambda)

with respect to
\Theta
, treating
\Lambda
as fixed.

Definition 6.1
.

We say that a pair
(\Theta^{\star},\Lambda^{\star})
is a
Nash equilibrium
if

\mathsf{L}_{1}(\Theta^{\star},\Lambda^{\star})\leq\mathsf{L}_{1}(\Theta^{\star},\Lambda)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{L}_{2}(\Theta^{\star},\Lambda^{\star})\leq\mathsf{L}_{2}(\Theta,\Lambda^{\star})

for all admissible
\Theta
and
\Lambda
.

We now present an example where the existence of Nash equilibria can be rigorously established.

We begin with the physical player. The physical model ansatz will be the Poisson equation with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions,

(6.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta\bm{u}_{phy}(x)=f(x),&x\in\Omega,\\
\bm{u}_{phy}(x)=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\leq 3
, is a smooth, bounded domain. The source term
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is the unknown parameter to be optimized. We assume a constraint of the form
\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leq C
for some fixed constant
C>0
. The solution operator
(-\Delta)^{-1}
is compact and continuous from
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

As standard, we assume to have access to a finite number of observations
\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
. Thus, the physical loss can be written as

\mathsf{L}_{phy}(f):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|((-\Delta)^{-1}f)(x_{i})-\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i})\|^{2}.

This loss is convex in
f
and weakly continuous in
L^{2}(\Omega)
in dimensions
d\leq 3
.

For the synthetic model we introduce a convex relaxation of the standard shallow neural network with
P
neurons

(6.2)

\bm{u}_{syn}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{P}w_{j}\sigma(\langle a_{j},x\rangle+b_{j}),

where
\Theta=(w_{j},a_{j},b_{j})_{j=1}^{P}
collects the parameters and the activation function
\sigma
is chosen as ReLU. The empirical loss associated with this model, together with a sparsity-promoting regularization on the output weights, is given by

(6.3)

\min_{\Theta}\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{P}|w_{j}|+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{P}w_{j}\sigma(\langle a_{j},x_{i}\rangle+b_{j})-\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i})\right\|^{2}.

This optimization problem is generally nonconvex. To obtain a convex formulation relaxation, following
[
15
]
and relax the parameter space into a space of finite measures. Specifically, letting
K
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
containing all
(a,b)
parameters, we replace the finite sum by an integral representation:

(6.4)

\tilde{\bm{u}}_{\mu}(x):=\int_{K}\sigma(\langle a,x\rangle+b)\,d\mu(a,b),

where
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(K)
is a finite signed measure. The relaxed loss becomes

(6.5)

\mathsf{L}_{syn}(\mu):=\lambda\|\mu\|_{TV}+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_{\mu}(x_{i})-\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i})\right\|^{2},

which is convex in
\mu
.

The coupling between the two models is enforced through an interaction loss, defined as the
L^{2}
distance between the physical and synthetic predictions:

\mathsf{L}_{int}(f,\mu):=\|(-\Delta)^{-1}f-\tilde{\bm{u}}_{\mu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

This term is well defined because
\mu
is finite and
\sigma
is locally bounded.

In this setting, Player 1 (the physical model) minimizes

\mathsf{L}_{1}(f,\mu):=\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|((-\Delta)^{-1}f)(x_{i})-\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i})\|^{2}+\|(-\Delta)^{-1}f-\tilde{\bm{u}}_{\mu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

while Player 2 (the synthetic model) minimizes

\mathsf{L}_{2}(f,\mu):=\lambda\|\mu\|_{TV}+\frac{\alpha}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_{\mu}(x_{i})-\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i})\right\|^{2}+\|(-\Delta)^{-1}f-\tilde{\bm{u}}_{\mu}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

The admissible set for
f
is the ball
\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega):\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\}
, which is convex and weakly compact by Banach-Alaoglu. Similarly, the set of finite measures
\mathcal{M}(K)
is convex and weakly compact. Since both losses are convex in their respective controls and weakly lower semicontinuous, we can invoke Glicksberg’s generalization of Kakutani’s fixed-point theorem
[
7
]
, which guarantees the existence of a Nash equilibrium in this relaxed HYCO game, as defined in Definition
6.1
.

As demonstrated in this article, the computational efficiency of the HYCO method extends well beyond the convex relaxation setting, where rigorous convergence analysis is available.

7.
A Dynamic Example, the Heat Equation

We now turn to a classical time-dependent example: the heat equation with a spatially varying diffusion coefficient. Compared to the Helmholtz parameter identification experiment, this model introduces an additional layer of complexity, as the dynamics now evolve over time. On the other hand, compared to the Gray-Scott evolution model, here we also supply the physical model with data, so that we can draw a meaningful comparison also against a classical Finite Differences parameter fitting.

The governing equations are as follows

(7.1)

\begin{cases}\bm{u}_{t}-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(x,y)\nabla\bm{u})=0,&t\in[0,1],\ (x,y)\in\Omega\\
\bm{u}(x,y,t)=0,&(x,y)\in\partial\Omega\\
\bm{u}(x,y,0)=\bm{u}_{0}(x,y)=e^{-(x-1)^{2}-(y-1)^{2}}-e^{-(x+1)^{2}-(y+1)^{2}},&(x,y)\in\Omega.\end{cases}

The domain is
\Omega=[-\pi,\pi]\times[-\pi,\pi]
, and the diffusion coefficient
\kappa
is modeled as a sum of Gaussians:

\kappa(x,y)=\alpha_{1}e^{-\|(x,y)-c_{1}\|^{2}}+\alpha_{2}e^{-\|(x,y)-c_{2}\|^{2}}+0.1,

with ground-truth parameters

\alpha_{1}=3,\quad\alpha_{2}=2.5,\quad c_{1}=(-2,-2),\quad c_{2}=(1,1).

Unlike the Gray-Scott example, which used static spatial sampling, the dataset in this experiment is built using mobile sampling in the space-time domain. We divide the time interval into
N=100
discrete time steps
\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, and at each time step
t_{j}
we sample
M=60
random spatial locations. This yields the dataset

\mathcal{D}:=\{\bm{u}^{D}(x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),y_{i}^{D}(t_{j})),t_{j})\}_{i=1,j=1}^{M,N}

where
(x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),y_{i}^{D}(t_{j}))
denotes the location of the
i
-th sample at time
t_{j}
.
To compute the values of
\bm{u}^{D}
, we discretize equation
7.1
by finite differences on a 18
\times
18 spatial grid, using
1000
time steps, and then integrate the resulting scheme by Runge-Kutta.

The objective of this numerical experiment is the same as in Section
4
:

Goal:

Given the dataset
\mathcal{D}
and the initial datum
\bm{u}_{0}
, and assuming the model form is known, reconstruct the parameters
(\alpha_{i},c_{i})
that best explain the observed trajectories.

Accordingly, we compare the same three approaches as in the static Helmholtz setting.

1. Finite Differences Parameter Fitting.

We discretize the spatial domain using finite differences and integrate the resulting semi-discrete system in time using a Runge-Kutta scheme. The optimization is carried out over the parameters of the diffusion coefficient.

2. The PINN Approach.

We employ a Physics-Informed Neural Network as before. In this situation, the residual term reads

\mathsf{R}(\Theta,\Lambda)=\|(\bm{u}_{\text{PINN}})_{t}-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(x,y)\nabla\bm{u}_{\text{PINN}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

3. The HYCO Strategy.

We combine a physical solver and a synthetic model, as follows.

\diamond

Physical model:
This model is the same that we described at the first method, namely, finite differences integrated in time with Runge-Kutta.
The physical loss is thus given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{phy}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{MN}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\bm{u}_{phy}((x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),y_{i}^{D}(t_{j})),t_{j})-\bm{u}^{D}((x_{i}^{D}(t_{j}),y_{i}^{D}(t_{j})),t_{j})\|^{2}.

\diamond

Synthetic model:
The synthetic model uses a neural network (whose architecture will be made precise below), taking as input the point
(x,y,t)
and outputing the value of the solution
\bm{u}(x,y,t)
. Its parameters
\Theta
represent weights and biases.

Numerical Parameters.

The HYCO, FEM and PINN architectures are all trained for 3000 epochs, using the Adam optimizer. For the finite differences, we use a discretization grid of 18
\times
18 equispaced points. In the HYCO strategy, the learning rates are set to
5\times 10^{-3}
for the physical model and
1\times 10^{-3}
for the synthetic model. The synthetic model adopts an architecture with 2 hidden layers of width 256, ReLU activation, and residual connections. The mathematical description is the same as in Section
4
, the only difference being that the input is now 3-dimensional,
h_{0}=(x,y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The number of ghost initial points, and relative ghost trajectories, is set to 200 throughout training. The PINN network is composed by 2 layers, each with 256 neurons and
\tanh
activation.

The setup mimics the one of the Helmholtz experiment in Section

, with the sole exception that the data now also have a time component. More precisely, after fixing
N=100
equispaced time steps, we perform three experiments.

\diamond

\Omega

\diamond

In the second one on the subset

\mathsf{Q}_{1}:=[-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\pi]\times[-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\pi];

\diamond

Finally, in the third one, on the smallest region,

\mathsf{Q}_{2}:=[0,\pi]\times[0,\pi].

We initialize the parameters as follows:

\alpha_{1}=1,\quad\alpha_{2}=1,\quad c_{1}=(-2,-1.1),\quad c_{2}=(1.4,-1.3).

8.
Conclusions and perspectives

In this work, we introduced HYCO, a hybrid modeling strategy that integrates physical and synthetic models by optimizing them jointly while encouraging alignment between their predictions. HYCO treats both models as active participants in the learning process, allowing mutual influence that serves as a powerful form of regularization.

Through numerical experiments on both steady and time-dependent PDEs, we showed that HYCO performs well in a wide range of tasks, from learning the solution of an unknown physical system, to parameter identification. These gains are particularly evident when observations are noisy or spatially localized, as the interplay between the models enables the reconstruction of missing information.

Looking ahead, we outline different research directions.

(1)

We have validated the general empirical performances of HYCO on some classical examples. However, HYCO is a very general and flexible architecture. Future works will focus to applying the technique to several scenarios:

\diamond

In multiphysics scenarios (such as thermo-mechanical systems or electrochemical devices), each model may be associated with a different physical process and trained on distinct data. By properly designing the interaction term, the HYCO strategy can serve as a principled way to integrate these heterogeneous sources into a unified predictive framework.

\diamond

Similarly, HYCO can be applied to problems involving multiple spatial domains. By tailoring the interaction term to act only at the interfaces, the strategy naturally enforces continuity between the models’ predictions, making it suitable for domain decomposition methods or coupled simulations.

\diamond

More broadly, a key step for future work will be to validate HYCO on more complex and large datasets arising from real-world applications (climate modeling, robotics, etc.), where physical fidelity and data scarcity coexist. In this context, it will be interesting to investigate the performances of HYCO using other popular machine learning architectures for the synthetic model. Transformers and Neural Operators are an obvious first choice, in light of the important results recently obtained by these architecture in data-driven learning of physical systems
[
12
,
13
]
.

(2)

Furthermore, while we touched upon the potential for privacy-aware versions of HYCO, many aspects remain to be explored. One natural direction is to investigate how the method performs when each model is trained on its own private dataset without sharing inputs. Privacy could also be enhanced through pre-processing techniques, such as Gaussian smoothing of the training data. The effectiveness and convergence of HYCO in these settings is not yet known and requires investigation. Moreover, as prior work in Federated Learning has shown that privacy breaches are possible even without data sharing, it is critical to assess whether similar vulnerabilities might exist in our framework.

(3)

A last challenge is to provide a mathematical understanding of HYCO’s performance. Although neural networks complicate the theoretical analysis, it is important to note that the synthetic model is not restricted to being a machine learning architecture. This might allow, for instance, to obtain analytical results in simplified settings, providing some interpretability.

Besides, we also highlighted that a game-theoretic perspective offers a promising theoretical background. While this field is vast and complex, further exploration may uncover useful insights or training algorithms. For example, it would be interesting to investigate whether one might obtain results similar to those in
[
15
]
, where it is shown that there is no relaxation gap in the training of neural networks. In our context, it is natural to wonder whether the same is true for the Nash equilibrium described in Section
6
.

This equation appears as a description of the pressure of an incompressible fluid through a porous material
[
2

[
12

16

26
]

(A.1)

\bm{u}(x,y)=0,&\text{for }(x,y)\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

\Omega=[-\pi,\pi]\times[-\pi,\pi]
.

\kappa

\kappa(x,y)=C^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}A_{ij}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\times(i+1)x\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\times(j+1)y\right).
