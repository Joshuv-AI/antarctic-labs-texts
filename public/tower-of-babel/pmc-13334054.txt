Attaining sustainability by navigating challenges in the transition to sustainable buildings using Fermatean fuzzy AHP
Authors: Zulfiquar N Ansari, M Rashid Malik, Karishma M Qureshi, Muhammad Musa Al-Qahtani, Mohamed Rafik Noor Mohamed Qureshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSustainable buildings (SBs) have emerged as a potential solution to address the issue related to greenhouse gas emissions and provide quality living conditions for the occupants. However, their effective implementation is hindered by several barriers, especially in the developing economies. This study aims to analyze the barriers hindering the adoption of the SB concept in the construction sector. Based on literature analysis and consultation with an expert panel, 35 barriers were identified that were further categorized under 7 main barriers. These barriers were then ranked using the Fermatean fuzzy-analytic hierarchy process (FF-AHP) approach to identify the most influential barrier. The most significant barriers identified by the study include: “High cost of implementing sustainable building technologies”, “Lack of collaboration between designers, consultants and contractors”, “Fear of delaying the project resulting in additional cost” and “Missing stringent environmental laws, standard codes and regulations by governmental institutions”. This highlights the necessity to optimize costs through efficient building design and focus on research and development. A comprehensive understanding of the hurdles faced in the pathway of implementation of SBs concept will help practitioners discuss appropriate strategies. The study’s implications will act as guidance for the practitioners and policymakers in the effective adoption of SBs in developing economies.Keywords: Sustainable buildings (SBs), Sustainability, Barrier, Fermatean fuzzy (FF) set, Analytic hierarchy process (AHP)Subject terms: Engineering, Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies

Introduction

The construction industry plays an important role in changing the structural landscape by developing new infrastructure such as roads, buildings, airports, ports, railways, bridges, and utility systems. It is evident that the economic progress of any nation is linked to the rapid developments in construction activities. This is because it improves societal well-being through wealthy generations while creating employment opportunities for the community1. But construction activities pose serious threats to the environment and society in context of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, waste generation, energy consumption, landfills etc. For instance, this industry consumes 32% of the total world’s energy and is also a major contributor of emissions accounting for 34% of the GHG emissions2. The report also indicates that cement and steel, forming the main raw materials of construction industry contributes to 18% of total GHG emissions and generates a significant amount of construction waste. Further, it is estimated that the GHG emissions from construction activities would be around 15.6 billion tonnes of till 2030 thereby raising the global temperature3. As per Paris Agreement 2015, the aim is to keep the global temperature rising below 2 °C and the target is 1.5 °C4. The construction industry would be significant in addressing the concerns related to GHG emissions and controlling the global temperature rise when compared with other industrial sectors5. Around 84 gigatonnes of carbon emissions through the buildings can be reduced by 2050 through energy efficient design, switching fuels and shifting to renewable energy technologies. This points towards the adoption of sustainable technologies and innovations in building design to reduce energy consumption, emissions and waste. In this regard, the sustainable buildings (SBs) concept emerged to integrate sustainable solutions in the building environment to mitigate the environmental impact on eco-system6.

SBs are buildings designed and constructed considering the concepts of ecology. Olabi et al.7 define sustainable buildings as “the practice of creating structures and using processes that are environmentally responsible and resource-efficient throughout a building’s life cycle from siting to design, construction, operation, maintenance, renovation, and deconstruction”. The sole purpose of SBs is the conservation of resources. They are designed so that a better quality of indoor air is made available to the occupants, and the environmental impact is minimized by reducing water consumption, energy needs, and effective utilization of the resources. SBs have the potential to save almost 70% of the energy when compared with industry standards. It can save over 5000 over the entire lifecycle8.

Sustainable building is constrained less by a lack of technology and more by the organizational and procedural challenges of implementing new methods9. Reluctance towards adopting clean technologies in the residential building must be overcome to adopt the concept of SB10. Furthermore, the developing and emerging economies face severe issues in adoption compared as compared to the developed countries. The developed countries have efficient supply chains and advanced building technologies which ease the SBs adoption process11. In developing countries, the concept of SB is more significant as it focuses on energy efficiency, water conservation, and resilience towards infrastructure viability. Furthermore, global construction activity is expected to grow significantly in developing economies such as India, China, and Brazil, influenced by rapid population growth, accelerated urbanization, and an increasing demand for higher living standards12. While it is believed that the developing nations are the major contributors of emissions, extensive construction activities would further deteriorate the environmental conditions13. Hence, there is a need to accelerate the adoption of SBs in emerging economies to minimize the environmental impact. Unlike the developed nations that have well-documented regulations to promote new building designs for efficiency in energy and resources, the developing nations are still unfamiliar with the concept of SBs14. Several issues related to resource availability, logistical infrastructure, economic inequalities and diverse societal needs are faced by these nations15. This complicates the application and integration of the SBs concept in the developing countries. Hence, there is a need that the several barriers that are encountered in shifting to SBs concept is properly understood for its widespread acceptance. Several studies.

Literature on SBs has explored conceptual frameworks and assessment methodologies, emphasizing their importance3,5,16,17. However, the practical adoption of green building technologies and materials remains constrained by technical and economic challenges11,18–21, necessitating further context-specific studies15,22–24. In this regard, there are several country-specific studies that have been carried out, for instance, Oman25, Iran26, Qatar27, Brazil28, Kosovo29 and several developed economies such as England30 Sweden, the United States, and New Zealand31. Green building practices barriers may be eliminated using the available solution that may help in boosting the SBs9,32.

The existing studies in the Indian context have primarily focused on developing sustainability assessment frameworks for specific building types, such as high-rise residential projects16 and general building assessment models3. One of the review studies classified the green building barriers as Economic, Governmental, Organizational, and Social perception, Information, Technology, and material categories33. However, a comprehensive and systematic evaluation of the barriers hindering the adoption of SBs in the Indian context is unexplored to boost sustainable construction practices. Therefore, the present study proposes a structured framework to identify, evaluate, and prioritize the barriers to the adoption of SB practices, thereby supporting their effective control and implementation in India.

Sustainable buildings

SBs design is aimed at providing environmentally friendly and economically feasible building solutions that improve the quality of life for the occupants. In the context of application, the SBs concept is mainly concentrated on energy efficiency, water efficiency, indoor environmental quality (IEQ), integration of renewable energy systems, better living conditions and heathy environment and productivity34. The several sustainable approaches to achieve energy efficiency include increased use of renewable energy systems, using insulating materials and substituting windows to minimize heat gain, and providing for proper ventilation and daylight availability35. Water efficiency focuses on improving the water quality, treating the wastewater and enhancing the efficiency of water consumption through recycling approaches36. IEQ is significant to ensure healthy indoor environment for the well-being of occupants. Research gaps

The literature analysis indicates that the concept of SBs has attracted several researchers in the domain, with numerous studies examining the barriers to their adoption. Despite this growing body of literature, a comprehensive review reveals that studies have concentrated on specific dimensions of sustainability, such as barriers related to cultural heritage protection22 or the integration of circular economy principles24. While these contributions offer valuable insights, their limited scope restricts their applicability for understanding the broader and interconnected challenges associated with the implementation of SBs.

In the past, several studies have explored barriers to the adoption of SBs across specific countries25–28,31,39,41 however, methodological and analytical limitations persist. The comprehensive study on barriers to SBs and the interrelationships among them is yet to be studied in the Indian context. Thus, especially in the Indian context, existing literature lacks an integrated and systematic framework capable of comprehensively identifying, evaluating, and prioritizing barriers to SBs implementation. Addressing this gap is essential to facilitate more effective adoption of SBs practices, particularly in developing country contexts.

Previous studies have extensively examined the SBs barriers in adopting SB concepts across different countries. The findings and their applicability may be constrained considering the country’s cultural practices, traditions, climatic conditions, and priorities across social, economic, and environmental dimensions. Hence, the findings from existing studies regarding SB barriers may vary based on specific country conditions. In the Indian context, the adoption of SBs is still at a nascent stage42, with the construction industry facing considerable challenges in integrating sustainability principles into routine practices. As is common in many developing economies, greater emphasis is often placed on economic considerations, while social and environmental aspects receive comparatively less attention. This imbalance underscores the need for a structured and comprehensive framework capable of identifying and prioritizing SB barriers to support policymakers and industry stakeholders in formulating targeted strategies. Addressing this gap, the present study proposes a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM)-based framework to systematically evaluate and prioritize the barriers hindering the effective implementation of SB practices.

Preliminaries of Fermatean fuzzy sets

The input data for the conventional MCDM problem is usually in the form of real and crisp numbers. However, these numbers fail to address the uncertainty or vagueness inherent in complex decision-making problems. Fuzzy set theory was proposed by Zadeh48 to address this issue. Since then, several extensions to fuzzy set theory have been proposed such as intuitionistic fuzzy sets49; and Pythagorean fuzzy sets50. However, these sets also prove to be deficient for decision-making problems that have contradictions. The reason behind it is that in the case of IFSs the sum of the membership and non-membership degrees is less than 1, while for PFSs the sum of the squares does not exceed 1. This implies that for a point , intuitionistic membership grades (IMGs) are all points below the line , and the Pythagorean membership grades (PMGs) are all point with . The limitations associated with IMGs PMGs have been addressed by the introduction of Fermatean fuzzy set ( proposed by Senapati and Yager51. Hence, FFSs provides higher flexibility and broader space to the decision-makers in comparison to IFSs and PFSs. Figure 2 depicts the comparison of spaces between IFSs, PFSs and FFSs. FFSs could handle greater levels of uncertainties over IFSs and PFSs and hence demonstrate better performance52. Some basic functions of FFSs are presented in Appendix.

Fermatean fuzzy AHP

AHP allows for structured decision-making approaches by decomposing the problem in the form of a hierarchy. The hierarchy thus formed has the objective at the top while the criterion and sub-criterion at the subsequent levels. The other benefit includes its effectiveness in handling both types of criteria i.e. qualitative as well as quantitative. When compared with other potential techniques such as analytic network process (ANP), stepwise weight assessment ratio analysis (SWARA), and best-worst method (BWM); AHP is still the preferred choice. It is due to its simplicity and robustness in nature, ease for experts to apply, well-established, and flexibility53. However, traditional AHP is highly dependent on subjective inputs, increasing the chances of impreciseness and vagueness in the collected data. Hence, different fuzzy sets are integrated with the traditional AHP technique to address ambiguity and enable effective decision-making.

The FFSs are utilized to address the uncertainties and vagueness inherent in subjective opinions, enabling a robust decision-making framework44. It has the capability to effectively convey ambiguous linguistic information across different MCDM applications that includes human judgements in decision-making54. Also, the integration of membership, non-membership and hesitancy elements in the FFSs provides an inclusive approach while collecting human judgements and perceptions. This improves sensitivity and makes it adaptable for integration with other techniques, enhancing its versatility for real-case decision problems47. In this sense, this research work applied an integrated FF-AHP approach to evaluate the barriers in the adoption of the SB concept. By using the proposed FF-AHP approach, the idea is to utilize the individual strengths of both the approaches and hence provide a robust technique for SBs barriers assessment. FF-AHP has been explored by several research studies for decision-making in different application areas such as sustainable blue economy evaluation55; humanitarian supply chain management assessment56; solar power projects selection57; waste valorization process selection47; occupational hazards evaluation44.

Data collection

The finalized barriers are evaluated systematically using the MCDM technique known as FF-AHP approach. The barriers are categorized into main barriers and sub-barriers, that includes a total of 7 main barriers and 35 sub-barriers. To rank the barriers and recognize the most influential barrier, input data is collected from the identified experts. A pair-wise comparison matrix in the form of a questionnaire was provided to the five experts. The experts were asked to input their opinion in the pairwise comparison matrix of main barriers and sub-barriers using the linguistic term scale and corresponding FF numbers (Table 3). Before they arrived at a common consensus to input a value in the matrix several rounds of discussion were carried out.

Sensitivity analysis

The FFSs handle the uncertainty and the vagueness inherent in human judgements and hence derive results that are precise and reliable. Sensitivity analysis is conducted for (i) verifying how the results vary due to changes in the attitude of decision-makers and (ii) to ensure that global rankings of sub-barriers are stable and robust43. In this study there are seven main barriers hindering adoption of the SBs concept. Of these seven barriers, COMBs have the highest priority, followed by COLBs as the second most significant barrier. Hence, a small change in the weights of the COMBs and COLBs would result in variations of other barriers. In this context, this study carried out nine computations as shown in Table 22 by changing the weight of COMBs to a proportion of 0.9 to 0.1. This change is adjusted among the other six main barriers. Through sensitivity analysis, this study attempts to evaluate the change in the rankings of sub-barriers if the intensity weight of the main barriers is changed. Discussion

Ranking of barriers to SBs

Based on the reported findings, the ranking of the main barriers to the adoption of SBs in the construction sector is presented in the form of a diagram (Fig. 6). Additionally, the sub-barriers are illustrated in diagrammatic form in Fig. 7.

Weight scores (Percentage scores) of main barriers.

Ranking of sub-barriers to SBs using FF-AHP.

The commercial barriers are the most important barriers in the context of weight intensity. This indicates that to improve the adoption rate of the SBs concept there is an immediate need to formulate effective strategies focusing on mitigating the barriers categorized under the commercial barriers. Further, the barriers related to collaboration are the second most important barriers based on the weight scores. This finding contrasts with the most common belief that government related barriers are the other significant deterring factors in adoption of the SB. That is, it implies that although government related barriers such as “Missing stringent environmental laws, standard codes and regulations by governmental institutions” and “Lack of proper training and promotional platform at local level” are important, they are less pressing issue for the developer that hinders SBs adoption. Hence, this shows that the developers can be motivated to engage in the SBs concept through coordination with experienced designers and consultants to overcome the collaboration related barriers. This implies that it is not only the responsibility of the developers and government institutions, but designers and the consultants would largely influence the pathway to creating the environment to adoption of SBs.

The high cost of implementing sustainable building technologies is the top-ranked barrier followed by Lack of collaboration between designers, consultants and contractors. The third and the fourth-ranked barriers include Fear of delaying the project resulting in additional cost and missing stringent environmental laws, standard codes and regulations imposed by governmental institutions. Fear of exposing design principles among the stakeholders involved is the fifth-ranked barrier in the adoption of SBs concept.

Among the main barriers commercial related barriers are the most significant barriers. Further, within the commercial barriers “High cost of implementing sustainable building technologies” is the highest ranked barrier. The transition to SBs sets the need for adoption of energy and water efficient technologies. However, the upfront cost associated with the adoption of renewable energy technologies, sustainable building materials and green equipment is a significant barrier in attaining the SB20. From the developer’s point of view the cost of SBs is higher as compared to the conventional building. For instance, Thebuwena et al.59 implemented a double-glazed facade system over a concrete masonry wall with a window-to-wall ratio in an office building. This resulted in reduced energy consumption and maintenance, but the initial cost increased by 156% over the baseline façade due to the use of advanced materials and construction techniques. Further, double skin façades (DSF) show promise for energy efficiency, indoor air quality, and aesthetics. The second-ranked barrier in the commercial barrier is Fear of delaying the project resulting in additional cost59. While developers are familiar with conventional building construction activities, the sustainable building concept is relatively new for them. The unavailability of green materials at local level, the complexity associated with commissioning of innovative sustainable technologies, changes in the design even during the construction phase impact the timely completion of the project. This not only results in delaying the project but also increases the cost of the entire project, which discourages the developers from shifting towards the SB concept. Uncertainties and instability associated with the investment needed are the third ranked barrier under commercial barriers. Uncertainty associated with the cost of green technologies, sustainable/recyclable materials, energy-efficient equipment, logistics cost, and regulatory charges for documentation and certification is a major deterrent issue. Further, the developers are also unaware of the challenges and instabilities that may be faced during installation of sustainable technologies such as BIM software, LCA software and wastewater treatment plants.

Within the collaboration barriers, lack of collaboration between designers, consultants and contractors is the top-ranked barrier60. Different stakeholders have their own roles to play in the development of SBs. For instance, contractors are the ones who will make the building, designers are those who develop the plan of the structure and the layout of the building while the integration of energy-efficient sustainable technologies and the green materials is suggested by the consultants. A collaborative mechanism between these players would not only lead to understanding individual needs but also aid in utilizing the strengths of individuals. This would result in developing a cost-effective solution which is a major cause of hinderance in adoption of SBs. The second ranked barrier in the collaboration barriers is the Lack of government-contractors mechanism to create interest among consumers followed by Poor coordination between the developers and sustainable material suppliers61. Insufficient demand from consumers for SBs hinders wide-scale adoption of SBs. There is a need for the government authorities and contractors to coordinate and carry out collaborative campaigns and awareness programs to motivate consumers to adopt green buildings. The availability of materials on time and in the right form is crucial for the success of SBs. For this there is a need for effective coordination between the developers and sustainable material suppliers. This helps to avoid the delays in availability of construction material, streamline the supply chain, cost-effectiveness and adhering to the project timeline.

Within the government barriers, the ranking of sub-barriers is in the order GOV2–GOV3–GOV1–GOV5–GOV4. The hurdles related to government barriers indicate that although the governmental institutions have devised laws, standards and regulations for SBs, their rigorous implementation is missing. This is in line with previous research, which indicates that laws, codes and regulations are not implemented strictly, impacting the implementation process. There is a need that the government bodies to strengthen the sustainable building laws, policies and regulations, conduct proper inspections and audits during and after construction, and award the necessary certifications. Government institutions need to conduct training programs, especially at the local level, to educate developers about the concept of SBs. The developers need to be aware of the requirements of SBs, design considerations, sustainable materials, waste management, energy-efficient technologies, necessary processes to obtain certification and so on. This would create confidence among the developers in transition to SBs concept. The absence of guidance and support from government, lack of reward mechanism to developers and the complexities associated with certification and approval process are the other government related risks. Government needs to take lead in supporting and guiding the developers financially as well as technically. Incentives or rewards in the form of tax relief, discount on certification cost or registration amount, rewarding them publicly will boost their morale and enhance its adoption.

Among the corporate barriers, the ranking of sub-barriers is in the order CORB2–CORB1–CORB5–CORB4–CORB3. Contractors lack clear understanding of the execution process of SBs; mandatory compliance is needed for clearance and the documentation process for certification. These issues hold them back from developing SBs. Further, the buildings to be approved as SBs need to comply with government norms of energy and water efficiency, use of recyclable materials, and waste management through a proper certification process. The complexities associated with this deter the developers from implementing SBs concepts as they fear financial loss/increased cost in case the building does not clear the set norms. The perception of developers that construction of SBs is costly arises from the demand for the inclusion of well-experienced architects and consultants, use of eco-friendly materials and high-value technologies which is another hindering factor. The executives fear that improper execution of the project and faulty installation of the advanced technologies will not only increase the costs but also result in system inefficiencies and delay the project. This holds them back from endorsing innovative sustainable technologies, limiting their implementation process.

Among the technical barriers, the ranking of sub-barriers is in the order TECB1–TECCB5–TECB2–TECB6–TECB4–TECB3. Sufficient skills and expertise regarding sustainable building design (TECB1) are needed for successful implementation. It is essential to address issues related to indoor environmental quality, integration of energy-efficient technologies and effective waste management. Also, adequate research and development facilities (TECB5), are critical to developing innovative sustainable technologies for energy efficiency and water efficiency and cost-effective sustainable materials. Sustainable building technologies design demands rigid requirements (TECB2) and hence necessary technical support during the building design phase; procurement of sustainable materials and construction phase is needed for the developers to enhance motivation. A well-defined technical standard or system (TECB6) will act as a guiding tool for the designers, and contractors integrating SBs concept and minimizing the implementation hurdles. Also, skilled staff (TECB4) have key role to play in the implementation of the SBs concept. They not only ensure that the construction activities are in line with the proposed design but also ensure proper installation and maintenance of the sustainable technologies within the building.

Lastly, customer willingness to adopt green materials is an important barrier, as it significantly impacts market demands, thereby hindering developers from implementing sustainable concepts in the construction industry. In this context, high costs associated with the transition to the SBs concept and the uncertainty in returns inhibit its adoption. From a social perspective, limited awareness of green materials and lack of trust in construction developers restrict wide-ranging implementation. Environmentally, this resistance delays the shift toward low-impact construction practices. Hence, customer acceptance of green materials in the construction industry is a challenge to transitioning towards SBs, as its implementation demands the adoption of multi-dimensional sustainability aspects.

Comparative analysis of study with past studies

In this research work, a comparative analysis of the present research findings with the previous research contributions is also presented. The most important barrier based on the findings of this study that is ‘high cost of implementing sustainable buildings technologies,” which is in line with the outcomes of most of the studies62. Most of the extant literature reported high cost as the major deterring factor hindering the implementation of SBs.

However, this study also reports a unique viewpoint by suggesting the importance of barriers related to “collaboration” as a noteworthy barrier, especially in the context of adoption of the SBs concept. Based on the results “Lack of collaboration between designers, consultants and contractors”63 is the second most important barrier hindering the adoption of SBs concept. This barrier has been reported by an earlier study but has not been reported as the most influential barrier. Furthermore, the barrier namely “Fear of exposing design principles among the stakeholders (Rank 5)” and “Lack of government-contractors mechanism to create interest among consumers (Rank 9)” are also the barriers influencing the SBs adoption. These barriers have not been often reported in past research works, thereby indicating a significant gap in the literature. Furthermore, this research work also highlights the importance of “Lack of proper training and promotional platform at local level” under the government related barriers as a significant impeding factor.

Study implications

The SBs concept has gained importance due to its ability to address several sustainability issues, such as carbon emissions, energy crisis, and waste management13. While the construction sector boosts the economy of many countries, it simultaneously severely deteriorates the environment, causing unhealthy living conditions for society39. A proper understanding of the barriers to the adoption of SBs concept would help the decision-makers in mitigating their impact through appropriate strategies. This research presents the following implications (i) theoretical implications and (ii) practical implications to the practitioners, policymakers, and regulatory bodies associated with decision-making in SBs industry. This will not only result in economic gains but also improve the environmental conditions and human well-being.

Theoretical implications

This research presents significant contributions to the field of SBs by extending and enriching the understanding of prevailing theories. The identification of significant barriers offers deeper insights into the problem that enhances the understanding of SBs’ concept implementation hurdles. This study presents a systematic investigation of the barriers following a structured hierarchical framework using a multi-criteria decision-making approach. The findings report the relative significance of the barriers, with commercial barriers, collaboration barriers and government barriers having impeding effects9. Particularly, SBs are still in their infancy, especially in developing economies, and the adoption rate is very low. In this sense, the ranking of the barriers to the adoption of SBs within the Indian context becomes the basis64. This would act as a theoretical foundation for the decision-makers in overcoming the identified barriers and enabling successful adoption of SBs concepts in construction sector41. The research presents a complete analysis of how SBs concepts can be implemented in the construction sector, including optimizing costs, intensifying collaborative efforts, stricter implementation of environmental regulations, development of standard codes and guidelines and conducting workshops regularly for training and educating the workforce at the local level.

Practical implications

This research also offers some noteworthy practical implications for enhancing the adoption of SBs concept in construction sector. Strategies should be formulated to mitigate the effect of barriers hindering the adoption of SBs concept9. The findings of the study would help the practitioners, policymakers and government bodies to extricate the highly significant barriers and develop action plans directed to the shortening of the top ranked barriers in the short run. Firstly, efforts should be directed towards optimizing the cost of SBs technologies. This can be accomplished by improving efficiency in building design and construction activities, and research and development. Designing buildings for better provision of natural light, proper ventilation and energy efficiency would significantly reduce the upfront and functional cost37. Also, shift from outdated construction technologies to modern approaches is necessary for better efficiency in the built environment. Investment in research and development facilities would drive innovations in materials, technologies, and construction methodologies thereby resulting in reduced cost and better performance.

Furthermore, different stakeholders need to assemble on a single platform and exchange their intellectual expertise with each other. This means that designers, consultants, and developers rather than working for their own interests, should move towards collaboration. This would lead to discussion about the challenges faced by individuals who come up with some innovative solutions applicable to all. Conducting joint campaigns and programs to create awareness about sustainability in the built environment should also be on their agenda. It will help generate demand for SBs in the market. The integration of sustainability concepts within building codes is crucial to enhance the adoption of the SB concept. Government needs to develop effective policies and regulations and ensure that environmentally related laws are mandatorily implemented in the built environment. Establishing proper codes and standards would be critical in streamlining construction activities and compliance, thereby promoting the adoption of SBs. Additionally, the formulation of environmental laws and its stricter implementation will force the business models to integrate the concept that those who were otherwise eschewing it.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this research is focused on understanding the barriers hindering the adoption of SBs concept within the construction sector. Based on systematic literature analysis and expert panel opinion, the study identified and evaluated 35 sub-barriers categorized under 7 main barriers related to SBs. Then, the FF-AHP approach is utilized to develop structured hierarchical models for the barriers and hence analyze them. The evaluation led to the ranking of the barriers according to importance and the identification of the most influential barriers. Based on the results, the commercial barriers, collaboration barriers and government barriers are the most important barriers among the seven main barriers. Further, among the sub-barriers, the top five influential barriers include: High cost of implementing sustainable building technologies; Lack of collaboration between designers, consultants and contractors; Fear of delaying the project resulting in additional cost; Missing stringent environmental laws, standard codes and regulations by governmental institutions and fear of exposing design principles among the stakeholders involved. The findings reported in the study contribute to sustainability by presenting an in-depth analysis of the barriers hindering SBs initiatives. The results will aid the practitioners and policymakers in guiding them and developing appropriate strategies to mitigate their effect. For instance, if cost is the major deterring factor, efforts should be directed towards designing the building with efficiency and developing cost-effective sustainable solutions through investment in research and development activities.

This research presents two unique contributions. First, identification of an extensive set of barriers to the adoption of SBs is based on a thorough literature review and expert panel inputs. Secondly, the detailed analysis of the barriers and the ranking provide significant insights to practitioners in the context of understanding and hence promoting its widescale adoption.

This research, just like other studies, has certain limitations. These limitations rather than undermining the present research quality, open doors for future investigations and theoretical expansion. This study identified 35 sub-barriers categorized under seven main barriers. The identification and validation of the barriers are in the context of a specific region in Indian context. Future research should be conducted in (i) another region of Indian context and (ii) another developing economy; to check whether the results hold or not. The findings of the study are based on input from professionals at a well-established architectural firm. It is evident that government institutions and consumers are also important stakeholders. Hence, another extension of the research is to integrate the decisions of professionals from the government and consumers. In this study, FF-AHP is applied to analyze and rank the barriers. Further investigations may be carried out by applying other MCDM techniques such as the best-worst method, interpretive structural modeling, DEMATEL or structural equation modeling. The suggestions provided in this research would help in expanding the research, developing an in-depth understanding of the hurdles confronted, and developing appropriate action plans for social, economic and environmental sustainability.
