Microstructural Features Linking White Matter and Mathematics in Adolescence
Authors: Bryce L Geeraert, Kiara Kunimoto, R Marc Lebel, Catherine Lebel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMathematics is a complex skill requiring the coordination of distributed gray matter brain regions connected by white matter tracts. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies have revealed a network of white matter tracts that support math processing, but the specific microstructural features driving this relationship remain unclear. Other magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) methods—neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI), inhomogeneous magnetization transfer (ihMT), multicomponent driven-equilibrium single-pulse observation of T1 and T2 (mcDESPOT), and g-ratio imaging—can probe microstructural features like axon packing, fiber orientation, and myelin more specifically than DTI. We applied these methods alongside DTI to evaluate links between white matter microstructure and math in a longitudinal cohort of 33 6–16 year olds (66 datasets total). Partial correlations between metrics of white matter microstructure and math skill, controlling for age and gender, were carried out in the left superior longitudinal and inferior longitudinal fasciculi, corticospinal tract, and the splenium. Cross-sectionally, fiber coherence of the corticospinal tract and superior longitudinal fasciculus correlated to mathematics performance. Longitudinally, change in markers of axonal packing and myelin were linked to changes in both math skill and fluency in a regionally-specific manner, with links to myelin-sensitive metrics most prevalent. Notably, decreases in myelin were linked to improvements in mathematics over time, suggesting ongoing refinement of the math network. Findings presented here did not survive multiple comparisons corrections, but provide insight for future work elaborating upon these associations in larger samples.Keywords: Brain, development, diffusion MRI, mathematics, white matter microstructure

Introduction

Mathematics is one of the most important skills that a child must develop, and is associated with job success, problem solving, and decision making (Burks et al., 2008; Nieder, 2025; Rivera et al., 2005). Mathematical processing requires coordination of attention, sensory integration, working memory, planning, and fluency alongside math-specific functions like arithmetic calculations (Arsalidou & Taylor, 2011; Hawes et al., 2019; Matejko & Ansari, 2015; van Eimeren et al., 2008). As a result, a broad and typically left-lateralized network of gray matter including the fusiform, inferior frontal, and cingulate gyri, superior and inferior parietal regions, insula, cerebellum, and visual cortex may be involved in mathematical processing (Arsalidou & Taylor, 2011; Nieder, 2025). The white matter tracts connecting these distributed regions allow for rapid communication and coordination during mathematical processing (Collins et al., 2019; Grotheer et al., 2019; Matejko & Ansari, 2015). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables non-invasive investigations of how maturation of these tracts supports improvements in mathematical skill during development.

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies have shown that mathematical processing is supported by a network of white matter fibers including the posterior corpus callosum, superior and inferior longitudinal fasciculi (SLF, ILF), and corticospinal tract (CST), with structures in the left hemisphere often more strongly associated to math performance than right hemisphere homologues (Li et al., 2013; Matejko & Ansari, 2015; Navas-Sánchez et al., 2014; Nieder, 2025; Tsang et al., 2009; Van Beek et al., 2014; van Eimeren et al., 2010, 2008). These studies have established those who score higher on math processing tasks tend to have higher fractional anisotropy (FA) and lower mean diffusivity (MD) in the posterior corpus callosum, SLF, ILF, and CST. FA and MD are produced from the tensor model, with FA reflecting the degree of directional anisotropy in diffusion and MD the overall amount of diffusion occurring, per region. These metrics are influenced by presence of axonal membranes, myelin, fiber coherence, permeability and other microstructural features in white matter tissue, and are biased by extraneous factors including complex fiber architectures (crossing, fanning, etc), partial voluming, and processing considerations (Beaulieu, 2002; Hüppi & Dubois, 2006; Jeurissen et al., 2013; Jones et al., 2013; Lebel & Deoni, 2018; Yoshida et al., 2013). FA tends to increase during the developmental period while MD tends to decrease (Geeraert et al., 2019; Lebel et al., 2008; Lebel & Beaulieu, 2011). Therefore, we may conclude that better mathematical performance is supported by a more mature network of math-related white matter tracts. However, two important gaps remain in our understanding of links between white matter structure and mathematical processing.

First, because FA and MD are sensitive to many aspects of white matter anatomy, they cannot be used to determine specific microstructural features driving development or links to math skill. Inhomogeneous magnetization transfer (ihMT) and multicomponent driven equilibrium single-pulse observation of T1 and T2 (mcDESPOT) produce quantitative ihMT (qihMT) and myelin volume fraction (VFm), respectively, and are both more specific to myelin (Deoni et al., 2008; Geeraert et al., 2018; Varma et al., 2015). Neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) provides the orientation dispersion index (ODI) and neurite density index (NDI), sensitive to fiber coherence and axonal packing, respectively (Zhang et al., 2012). Finally, g-ratio imaging combines axonal and myelin volume fractions to describe the ratio between axonal caliber and myelin thickness, or g-ratio (Campbell et al., 2018; Mohammadi et al., 2015; Stikov et al., 2015). These measures have each been validated in vivo (Grussu et al., 2017; Hurley et al., 2010; Prevost et al., 2017; Schilling et al., 2018; Sepehrband et al., 2015; Wood et al., 2016), and been applied to describe white matter development (Geeraert et al., 2019). Similar studies linking cognitive measures to white matter metrics with greater microstructural specificity offer potential to elaborate our understanding of the mathematics network.

Second, studies linking white matter development over time to mathematics remain limited (Jolles et al., 2016). Broadly, white matter tract volume and structural refinement is rapid during early childhood, and gradual maturation of tracts continues in a regionally specific fashion throughout adolescence (Bethlehem et al., 2022; Geeraert et al., 2019; Lebel et al., 2008; Lebel & Beaulieu, 2011; López-Vicente et al., 2021; Nazeri et al., 2022). At the microstructural level, myelin content and axonal packing increase during adolescence, g-ratio gradually increases, and fiber coherence remains stable, although these trends are not consistent (Bartzokis et al., 2010; Benes, 1989; Bouhrara et al., 2021; Chang et al., 2015; Dean et al., 2015; DiPiero et al., 2023; Geeraert et al., 2019; Genc et al., 2023; Grydeland et al., 2013; Mah et al., 2017; Yakovlev et al., 1967; Zhao et al., 2021). Further, acquisition of cognitive functions is known to coincide with myelination of regions related to that function (Bells et al., 2019; Fields, 2008; Mabbott et al., 2006; Nagy et al., 2004). Acquisition of math skills occurs throughout the developmental period, but links between white matter development and math skill remain largely unexplored.

Here, we applied ihMT, mcDESPOT, NODDI, and g-ratio imaging alongside DTI in a longitudinal sample to identify the microstructural features driving previously-established relationships between white matter development and mathematics processing during late childhood and adolescence. We focused our analysis on tracts commonly linked to math in previous work: the corpus callosum splenium and left hemisphere SLF, ILF, and CST (Collins et al., 2021, 2019; Grotheer et al., 2019; Li et al., 2013; Matejko & Ansari, 2015; Navas-Sánchez et al., 2014; Tsang et al., 2009; Van Beek et al., 2014; van Eimeren et al., 2010, 2008). We hypothesized that all imaging metrics would be linked to mathematical skill in all investigated tracts, such that trends towards increased tract maturity (more myelin, larger axonal diameter, higher fiber coherence) would coincide with improved mathematical processing.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-three participants aged 6–16 years (16 females/17 males, gender determined by parent report) were recruited as part of a study of adolescent brain development and cognition. All subjects received an MRI scan and cognitive assessment at baseline (mean age: 10.6 ± 2.3 years) and again after two years (mean age: 12.7 ± 2.4 years, mean time between visits: 2.1 ± 0.4 years). Participant ages and time between visits for our longitudinal cohort is depicted in Figure 1. We report participant gender in this paper as parents were asked to report their child’s gender. Participants were recruited via word of mouth, posters, emails, and website advertisement. Inclusion criteria were: 1) uncomplicated birth between 37 and 42 weeks’ gestation, 2) no history of developmental disorder, psychiatric disease, or reading difficulty, 3) no history of neurosurgery, and 4) no contraindications to MRI. This study was approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (CHREB, ID: REB13-1346). All subjects provided informed assent and parents/guardians provided written informed consent.

Image Acquisition

Participants were scanned using a 32-channel head coil on a GE 3 T Discovery MR750w (GE, Waukesha, WI) system at the Alberta Children’s Hospital. Two diffusion-weighted datasets were sequentially acquired at b = 900 s/mm2 and 2000 s/mm2 using a spin-echo echo planar imaging sequence with TR/TE = 12s/88 ms, 2.2 mm isotropic resolution, with 5 b = 0 s/mm2 volumes and 30 gradient directions per dataset, scan time was 7:12 min:sec per diffusion dataset. IhMT images were acquired with a 3D spoiled gradient (SPGR) sequence: TR/TE = 10.46 ms/2.18 ms, 2.2 mm × 2.2 mm × 2.2 mm resolution, flip angle (α) 8°. The sequence included a 5 ms Fermi pulse with peak B1 of 45 mG and 5 kHz offset prior to each excitation. The MT condition cycled between positive offset (+5kHz), dual offset (±5 kHz), negative offset (−5kHz), and dual offset. A 32° α reference image with no MT pulse was acquired for quantification. Scan time for ihMT was 5:12 min:sec. For mcDESPOT, four images were acquired: multi-flip angle 3D SPGR images (α = 3°, 4°, 5°, 6°, 7°, 9°, 13°, and 18°) were collected with TR/TE = 9.1 ms/3.9 ms, 1.7 mm × 0.86 mm × 1.7 mm resolution. Inversion recovery SPGR (IR-SPGR) images were collected with 5° α, TR/TE = 9.1 ms/3.9 ms, 2.29 mm × 0.86 mm × 3.4 mm resolution. Finally, two multi-flip angle balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) images were collected at phase 0° and 180°, with α = 10°, 13°, 16°, 20°, 23°, 30°, 43°, and 60°, TR/TE = 6.6 ms/3.2 ms, 1.7 mm × 0.86 mm × 1.7 mm resolution. Total scan time for all mcDESPOT scan sequences was 16:35 min:sec. McDESPOT sequences were added after initial subject recruitment had started, enabling computation of VFm and g-ratio maps with a reduced sample. Finally, T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired, with TI = 600 ms, TR/TE = 8.2 ms/3.2 ms, 0.8 mm × 0.8 mm × 0.8 mm resolution, scan time 5:38 min:sec.

Image Processing

All images were visually inspected and rated for quality, then processed separately using modality-specific pipelines. T1-weighted images were processed in FreeSurfer 5.3 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) for intensity normalization and brain extraction. DTI datasets were preprocessed within ExploreDTI (Leemans et al., 2009), including signal drift correction (Vos et al., 2017), brain extraction, eddy current and motion corrections (Andersson et al., 2003; Andersson & Sotiropoulos, 2016), and registration to their corresponding post-FreeSurfer skull stripped anatomical image to correct geometric distortions induced by echo-planar imaging. The REKINDLE model was used to calculate FA and MD maps for each subject using the b = 900 s/mm2 shell only (Tax et al., 2015). Whole brain tractography was performed on b = 900 s/mm2 data following registration to the participant’s T1w anatomical space using constrained spherical deconvolution (Jeurissen et al., 2011) with L_max = 6, 2 mm isotropic seed voxels, 1 mm step size, FA threshold of 0.2, 30 maximum angle of deviation and an acceptable streamline range of 50 to 500 mm. Then, semi-automated methods (Lebel et al., 2008) were performed to segment the left hemisphere superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), corticospinal tract (CST), and the splenium. Example tracts in an 11 year-old female are included in Figure 2. An 11-year old female with high data quality was selected as the template participant for this process; all regions of interest (ROIs) were drawn on the template brain and then registered to other participants’ data for tracking in native space (Lebel et al., 2008). Processed multi-shell DTI datasets were also exported to the NODDI Toolbox (http://www.nitrc.org/projects/noddi_toolbox) for calculation of isotropic (fiso) and intracellular (ficvf, or NDI) volume fractions and ODI.

Mathematics Assessment

Mathematical skill was assessed using the Wechsler Individual Achievement Test – Third Edition: Canadian (WIAT-III CDN) (Wechsler, 2010). Participants completed the Math Problem Solving, Numerical Operations, and Math Fluency – Addition, Subtraction, and Multiplication subtests. From these subtests, Mathematics and Math Fluency Composite Scores were computed. Mathematics indicates a participant’s skill in recalling numbers and number facts, performing calculations, problem solving, and selecting appropriate strategies. Math Fluency describes the ability to quickly perform calculations, and facilitates more complex problem solving as fluency indicates having more efficient and accurate methods for computation.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using Jamovi (Love et al., 2021). Annual rate of change scores were calculated for all imaging metrics and mathematical composite scores by the following formula:

A filter was applied to identify and remove outlier data points from further analyses by transforming annual change scores to Z scores in each region and identifying data points with |Z| > 3. Normality of metrics was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Partial correlations were run on cross-sectional data between 7 imaging metrics and 2 mathematics scores across participants, then again for annual change scores in each tract. Gender and age at first visit were controlled for in all partial correlations, as age and sex effects are present for imaging metrics in this sample (Geeraert et al., 2019). Pearson correlations were computed unless a significant Shapiro-Wilk test was observed for either involved metric, in which case Spearman’s correlations were used. Multiple comparisons were accounted for using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995), correcting for 7 comparisons per math metric in each tract.

Discussion

Here, we describe links between white matter microstructural development and mathematical proficiency. Cross-sectional data from participants’ first visits revealed correlations between ODI, a marker of fiber coherence, in the SLF and CST and Mathematics, such that increased fiber coherence in these tracts was related to higher Mathematics scores. Over a two-year period, changes in measures sensitive to axonal packing were correlated to changes in Mathematics; NDI in the SLF and MD in the CST, such that increased axonal water content was linked to improved Mathematics scores. Decreases in MD were also linked to improvements in Math Fluency in the ILF. Change in VFm, a myelin-sensitive metric, was linked to change in Mathematics and Math Fluency in the splenium, and Math Fluency in the ILF. G-ratio findings were similar to VFm findings in the splenium and ILF, with an additional relationship between g-ratio and Math Fluency observed in the CST. Notably, decreases in VFm and increases in g-ratio were associated with increases in Mathematics and Math Fluency scores. Together our findings demonstrate that white matter tracts support math processing in regionally-specific ways, and that observed relationships shift depending on whether single or multiple time points are included in analysis. Given the findings presented here did not survive our multiple comparisons method, the results discussed here should be considered exploratory and replicated by future work in larger samples.

Cross-sectional analysis linked higher fiber coherence in the left SLF and CST to better Mathematics composite scores. This could reflect a unique role of these two major tracts in math processing, or could be driven by their anatomy as these tracts cross through superior temporoparietal white matter where several fiber tracts intersect. No relationships were observed between change in ODI and change in math ability in our longitudinal analysis, and no other structural metrics correlated to math scores cross-sectionally. Developmentally, ODI is known to decrease in early childhood but stabilizes during late childhood and adolescence (Chang et al., 2015; Geeraert et al., 2019; Mah et al., 2017). Absence of longitudinal associations may be due to low variance in ODI change scores, but it is also possible that change in ODI supports early acquisition of math ability and our sample is beyond the developmental stage where coherence change relates to changes in math skills. ODI may support stable inter-individual differences in math performance and serve as a trait marker of math ability in our sample, while other metrics track plasticity and ongoing change. However we cannot rule out that absence of effects is driven by our relatively small sample size, particularly for VFm and g-ratio, as brain-behaviour relationships are known to have small effect sizes (Marek et al., 2022). While large, consortium-driven databases are becoming increasingly leveraged to address this issue, they often lack specialized metrics like qihMT and VFm. Additional work is needed to further describe trait markers of math performance across development.

Longitudinal models revealed regionally-specific relationships between change in math composite scores and white matter microstructure. Decreases in MD in the CST and ILF were related to increases in Mathematics and Math Fluency, respectively. Significant associations for myelin markers in the left ILF with Math Fluency suggest the MD trend in this region may be driven by changes to myelination, although we cannot rule out other mechanisms. NDI exhibited a positive link with Mathematics in the left SLF, suggesting changes in axonal packing or diameter support ongoing math acquisition. Myelin appears to be a key developmental marker linked to ongoing change in Mathematics and Math Fluency, with VFm relationships observed in the ILF and splenium and g-ratio relationships observed in the CST, ILF, and splenium. No significant links between math scores and qihMT implies nuance in myelin physiology, as qihMT contrast is predominantly weighted towards presence of lipid bilayers while VFm probes myelin-bound water content (Hurley et al., 2010; Manning et al., 2017; Wood et al., 2016). This could indicate that changes in myelin water are more prevalent than changes in layer count during adolescence, but replication in a larger dataset is needed to verify this is not driven by sensitivity differences between the two metrics. These trends appear to be lateralized to the left hemisphere, as supplementary analysis in contralateral tracts revealed no significant correlations in the right CST or SLF, and correlations between MD and VFm with Math Fluency in the right ILF that did not survive multiple comparisons corrections (see Supplementary Table 1). Overall we identify several potential markers of plasticity and development in math skill complementary to ODI as a trait marker, but replication of these trends is needed.

Interestingly, VFm and g-ratio relationships linked decreased myelin and increased g-ratio with improved math performance. Developmentally, VFm increases in adolescence while g-ratio undergoes minor change (Berman et al., 2018; Bouhrara et al., 2021; Dean et al., 2016), including in a sample overlapping with the participants studied here (Geeraert et al., 2019). Two potential mechanisms may explain this trend. First, as g-ratio and myelin volume fraction are both ratios which factor in axonal water content, these metrics may be probing changes in axonal diameter. Larger axons tend to have thinner myelin sheaths, and have been linked to improved processing speed in previous work (Genc et al., 2023; Paus & Toro, 2009). Second, VFm and g-ratio trends may reflect decreased myelin content over short-range connections within math-related tracts. Changes in myelin co-occur as skills are learned or refined, and decreased myelination of short-range connections may support concurrent arrival of input signals on a target neuron (Fields, 2008; Filley & Fields, 2016). Perhaps decreases in myelin content—which were relatively small in magnitude across our two-year longitudinal period—reflect refinement within the math network for participants exhibiting increases in Mathematics and Math Fluency scores.

No significant correlations were observed with changes in FA and any math measure. Links between FA and math have been commonly reported in previous literature, particularly in the left hemisphere (Li et al., 2013; Matejko & Ansari, 2015; Tsang et al., 2009; van Eimeren et al., 2008), and FA increases during adolescence have been widely reported (Lebel & Beaulieu, 2011; Lebel & Deoni, 2018; Simmonds et al., 2014; Tamnes et al., 2010). Here, we observed reductions in diffusivity and myelin metrics (MD, VFm) linked to increases in math composite scores, and increases in axonal packing (NDI) and g-ratio linked to increases in math composite scores. Perhaps, as FA may be influenced by each of these factors, the broad sensitivity of FA has caused a ‘washing out’ of relationships. Absence of FA trends may also be a result of our choice to evaluate whole tracts including tract terminations, where partial voluming effects are most prominent. Supplementary analysis using core streamlines with terminations trimmed identified partial correlations between change in left SLF FA and change in Mathematics and Math Fluency, along with VFm and Math Fluency in the left ILF and splenium (see Supplementary Table 2), but findings did not survive multiple comparisons corrections. This may suggest DTI metrics are best suited to core tract analyses where partial voluming is minimized, or that our other metrics exhibit sensitivity to anatomy at the boundary between gray matter and white matter. Application of these novel metrics in larger samples and with a variety of regions of interest are needed to better understand these dynamics.

Across our sample, imaging metrics were within typical ranges for healthy subjects, and followed the expected developmental trends over time (Table 1), and math composite scores fell within population norms. Furthermore, no reading difficulties were identified within our sample (Geeraert et al., 2020). Effect sizes for significant relationships were moderate-to-high, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.41 to 0.65 for math composite scores. Likewise, our achieved power ranged from 0.59 to 0.87 (see Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Follow-up analysis in an unrelated dataset will help to replicate findings reported here and provide an opportunity to acquire microstructurally-specific metrics in a larger sample, further increasing power and helping to address corrections for multiple comparisons.

Findings presented here should be considered in light of the study’s limitations. First, available data for each relevant imaging and math metric varied, resulting in uneven assessments of normality and achieved power across correlations. While we have provided an important first evaluation of the microstructural underpinnings between white matter and math, future work should collect a larger dataset to confirm these findings. Additionally, collection of more than two time points would allow for clearer characterization of longitudinal trends. This would be especially helpful to determine the nature of the inverse trend observed here for VFm and g-ratio, and to elaborate upon the correlations reported here. Correlations assume linear relationships between variables and independence of observations. While these assumptions were appropriate for the presented dataset, longitudinal methods capable of modelling repeated measures per subject and non-linear trajectories may provide a more complete understanding of these relationships. Such methods may also reduce the influence of compounded measurement error intrinsic to change scores. Finally, while the measures applied here have been histologically validated (Grussu et al., 2017; Hurley et al., 2010; Prevost et al., 2017; Schilling et al., 2018; Sepehrband et al., 2015; Wood et al., 2016), each is confounded by additional sensitivities. However, through careful consideration of the sensitivities of each of these metrics, multiparametric analyses present a unique opportunity for convergent evidence towards microstructural trends.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we applied more specific measures of white matter microstructure alongside FA and MD to reveal markers of stable inter-individual differences and ongoing change in math performance during late childhood and adolescence. Fiber coherence was associated with cross-sectional differences in Mathematics, while changes in axonal packing and myelin were linked to ongoing change in Mathematics and Math Fluency. Relationships were observed in a regionally-specific fashion across math-related tracts, highlighting the heterogeneity of math processing. Correlations observed here were all moderate to high magnitude and achieved high power, although sample size should be considered in their interpretation. Additionally, no relationships between FA and math skill were observed, highlighting the strength of microstructurally-sensitive metrics to detect subtle relationships during adolescence that may be missed by more broadly-sensitive metrics.
