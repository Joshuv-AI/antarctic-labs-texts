Abstract
. We prove that for an open class of translation-invariant Hörmander-type phase functions, the corresponding curved Kakeya sets in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} have Hausdorff dimension at least ( 13 − 13 ) / 4 = 2.348 ​ … (13-\sqrt{13})/4=2.348\ldots . Previous methods, such as polynomial partitioning, have attained at best dimension 2 + 1 / 3 2+1/3 for curved Kakeya sets in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . The proof combines Wolff’s classical hairbrush argument with a new Kakeya estimate for 3-parameter families of curves satisfying stable geometric conditions that we call coniness and twistiness. The latter estimate extends ideas of Katz, Wu, and Zahl from the study of SL 2 \mathrm{SL}_{2} -Kakeya sets.

Introduction

1.1.
Curved Kakeya in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
beyond the
2+1/3
threshold

As first discovered by Bourgain
[
4
]
, curved Kakeya problems arise naturally from the wavepacket geometry of variable-coefficient oscillatory integral operators. This connection was further developed by Wisewell in
[
23
]
, and we refer the reader there for details.
Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth phase function satisfying Hörmander’s non-degeneracy conditions
[
12
]
, and write
\mathbf{x}=(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. For
\epsilon_{\phi}>0
sufficiently small and
(\xi,v)\in B_{\epsilon_{\phi}}^{2}\times B_{\epsilon_{\phi}}^{2}
, the associated
\phi
-curve is

(1.1)

\displaystyle\ell_{\xi,v}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in B_{\epsilon_{\phi}}^{3}:\nabla_{\xi}\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)=v\}.

We write
\mathcal{C}(\phi)
for this 4-parameter family and call
\xi
the direction parameter. In the
translation-invariant
case

(1.2)

\displaystyle\phi(x,t,\xi)=\langle x,\xi\rangle+\psi(t,\xi),

Hörmander’s non-degeneracy conditions are equivalent to
\partial_{t}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\psi(t,\xi)\neq 0
, and
the curves have the explicit parameterization

(1.3)

\displaystyle\ell_{\xi,v}(t)=(v-\nabla_{\xi}\psi(t,\xi),t).

By a change of variables, we may assume that
a translation-invariant phase function
\phi(\mathbf{x},\xi)=\langle x,\xi\rangle+\psi(t,\xi)
satisfies one of the two alternatives:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\psi(0,0)

\displaystyle=I,\

(positive-definite)

(1.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\psi(0,0)

\displaystyle=I_{-}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(non-positive-definite)

A compact set

is a

Kakeya set
if, for every
\xi\in B_{\epsilon_{\phi}}^{2}
,

contains a curve
\ell_{\xi,v}\in\mathcal{C}(\phi)
. Let
d_{\mathrm{set}}(\phi)
denote the infimum of the Hausdorff dimensions of such sets. A first step to understanding the
L^{p}
estimates for Hörmander-type oscillatory integral operators is to better understand the behavior of
\phi
-Kakeya sets.

The straight-line Kakeya problem and the restriction problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are obtained from the phase
\phi_{\mathrm{parab}}(\mathbf{x},\xi)=\langle x,\xi\rangle+t|\xi|^{2}
. Recently Wang and Zahl proved
d_{\mathrm{set}}(\phi_{\mathrm{parab}})=3
, resolving the Kakeya conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
22
]
.

Much less is known in the curved setting, though. There are certain
\phi
so that
\phi
-Kakeya sets can lie in a surface, giving the worst possible bound
d_{\mathrm{set}}(\phi)=2
, and for generic
\phi
, small portions of
\delta
-discretized
\phi
-Kakeya sets can compress near a surface
[
4
]
. There has been a long line of work in quantifying how poorly

-Kakeya sets can behave, in order to better understand the corresponding oscillatory integral operator, including but certainly not limited to
[

,
19
,
10
,
9
,
5
,
6

7

.

In all cases, excluding those
\phi

is simply diffeomorphic to a family of lines, the best unconditional estimates available were

.
For the very special class of phase functions satisfying

, the threshold

can be obtained by a polynomial partitioning argument
[

6
]
. See
[

for an example of a (non-translation-invariant) phase function satisfying Bourgain’s condition whose curves are not diffeomorphic to a family of lines. Earlier, Sogge attained Minkowski dimension

Theorem 1

(Main Theorem on
\phi
-curved Kakeya)
.

Let
\phi(x,t,\xi)=\langle x,\xi\rangle+\psi(t,\xi)
be a translation-invariant non-positive-definite phase function. Define

(1.6)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{t}^{2}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\psi(0,0),

(1.7)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{6}\partial_{t}^{3}\nabla_{\xi}^{2}\psi(0,0).

Suppose that the matrices
B
and
C
satisfy

(1.8)

\displaystyle|B_{12}(B_{22}-B_{11})+C_{12}|>\frac{1}{2}|(B_{22}+B_{11})(B_{22}-B_{11})+C_{11}+C_{22}|.

Then

(1.9)

\displaystyle d_{\rm{set}}(\phi)\geq\frac{13-\sqrt{13}}{4}\geq 2.348.

In fact the following stronger discretized statement holds. For all
\epsilon>0
there is an
M=M(\epsilon)
and
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\epsilon)
so that the following holds for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
. Let

be a
\delta
-direction separated collection of curves. Let

be a union of
\delta
-cubes and suppose that

(1.10)

\displaystyle|E|\geq\delta^{\epsilon}\lambda^{M}\delta^{\frac{a+1}{2}}(\delta^{2}\#L)^{3/4},

where

is the positive root of
a^{2}+3a-1
.

The hypothesis on
\phi
in Theorem

is stable under translation-invariant perturbations. A simple example of a phase function satisfying the hypothesis is

(1.11)

\displaystyle\phi(x,t,\xi)=\langle x,\xi\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\left(tI_{-}+t^{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\right)\xi,\xi\rangle.

The proof of Theorem

1.2.
The 3-parameter curved Kakeya estimate

The main new input is an incidence estimate for general 3-parameter families of curves. Let

(1.12)

\displaystyle X:B_{2}^{3}\times B_{2}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\ell_{\mathbf{p}}(t):=(X(\mathbf{p},t),t),

and write
\mathcal{C}(X)=\{\ell_{\mathbf{p}}:\mathbf{p}\in B_{2}^{3}\}
. We defer the formal definitions used in the theorem to Section
2
, but give their geometric idea here.

We say that
\mathcal{C}(X)
is
coney
(formally Condition
2
) if the directions of the 1-parameter family of curves in
\mathcal{C}(X)
through a point are not contained in a plane.

We now describe the
twistiness
condition (formally Condition
3
).
The curves intersecting a fixed curve in
\mathcal{C}(X)
(at a relatively small angle) fill out a certain twisting
grain
. The
grain direction
can be projected out to give a plane curve. Applying the same procedure for each fixed curve in
\mathcal{C}(X)
gives a
3
-parameter family of plane curves. We say that
\mathcal{C}(X)
is
twisty
if this family of curves forbids 2nd-order tangency in the sense of
[
25
]
.
Twistiness is motivated by the structure of
\mathrm{SL_{2}}
-Kakeya sets in
[
13
]
.

Theorem 2

(Coney-Twisty 3-parameter Kakeya)
.

Suppose that
X
is
\mathfrak{c}
-coney,
\tau
-twisty, and satisfies the basic conditions. For all
\epsilon>0
there is
M=M(\epsilon)
and
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\epsilon,C_{0},\ldots,C_{O_{\epsilon}(1)})
so that the following holds for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
.
Suppose that
L\subset\mathcal{C}(X)
satisfies the two-dimensional ball condition

(1.13)

\displaystyle\#(L\cap B)\leq(r/\delta)^{2}\text{ for all $r\in[\delta,1]$, and all balls $B$ of radius $r$.}

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a union of
\delta
-cubes, and suppose
|\ell\cap E|\geq\lambda
for each
\ell\in L
. Then

(1.14)

\displaystyle|E|\geq\delta^{\epsilon}\mathfrak{c}^{2}(\tau\lambda)^{M}\delta^{a}(\delta^{2}\#L),

where
a=(\sqrt{13}-3)/2\approx 0.303
is the positive root of
a^{2}+3a-1
.

Theorem
2
has implications to restricted projections, which we record in Section
2
. The new feature of Theorem
2
is that its hypotheses are stable under small perturbations of the family of curves.
The proof uses the techniques in
[
13
]
when the typical angle between curves are small, and the curved multilinear Kakeya theorem
[
2
, Theorem 6]
when the typical angle is large. Below we give a sketch of the proof of Theorem

by using Theorem
2
.

1.3.
Sketch of the proof of Theorem
1

We give a sketch of the proof of Theorem
1
by tracking the example
\phi_{A}(\mathbf{x},\xi)=\langle x,\xi\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\langle A(t)\xi,\xi\rangle
, where
\det A^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
.
There are two main ingredients.

(1)

Wolff’s classical hairbrush argument lets us extract a hairbrush configuration
H\subset L
to study.

(2)

Theorem
2
lets us estimate the union of curves in
H
.

For the sketch, we consider the special case
\lambda=1
and
\#L=\delta^{-2}
. Write
\mathbb{T}
for the collection of
\delta
-neighborhoods of curves in
L
. We can locate a multiplicity
\mu
between tubes in
\mathbb{T}
. By double counting,

(1.15)

\displaystyle\Big|\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}T\Big|\gtrsim 1/\mu.

We also need an estimate which gets better with large
\mu
. Since the average multiplicity is
\mu
, there is a large subset of tubes
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{T}
which are incident to
\mu\delta^{-1}
many other tubes. Take one of these tubes
T_{0}\subset\mathbb{T}
, and for the sketch assume that the tubes in the hairbrush
\mathbb{H}
around
T_{0}
make an angle
\sim 1
with
T_{0}
. If, for instance,
T_{0}
is the tubes with center curve
\ell_{0,0}
, then the center curves in
\mathbb{H}
are parameterized like

(1.16)

\displaystyle\ell_{\xi,\theta}(t)=((A(t)-A(\theta))\xi,t).

The parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
is the height at which the curve hits
\ell_{0,0}
, and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the curve’s direction (these are distinct from
\ell_{\xi,v}\in\mathcal{C}(\phi)
, where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
). Unfortunately Wolff’s original argument to bound
\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{H}}T
does not work. This was first observed by Wisewell in the special case of quadratic phase functions:

Observation 1

(Wisewell
[
23
, Proposition 14]
)
.

Even in the special case of
A(t)=tA+t^{2}B
, Wolff’s hairbrush argument works only when
B
is a multiple of
A
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{C}(\phi_{A})
consists of lines up to a change of variables.

The problem is that Wolff’s argument uses the rare property that the curves in a hairbrush can be organized into essentially disjoint surfaces. We instead can only rely on a property of curves which is stable under perturbations. These are Conditions
2
and
3
. When
\phi
satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem
1
, Theorem
2
says

(1.17)

\displaystyle\Big|\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{H}}T\Big|\gtrsim\delta^{a}(\delta^{2}\#\mathbb{H})\geq\delta^{a+1}\mu,

where
a\approx 0.303
.
By averaging the bounds (
1.15
) and (
1.17
), we find

(1.18)

\displaystyle\Big|\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}T\Big|\gtrsim\delta^{(a+1)/2},

corresponding to dimension
3-(a+1)/2\approx 2.348
.

We made simplifying assumptions at various points in this sketch. First, we cannot assume
\lambda=1
. To handle this, we will use the two-ends reduction which introduces an isotropic rescaling of the tubes. We also cannot assume the typical hairbrush angle is
\sim 1
. To handle this, we need a radial rescaling of a hairbrush and the bush argument to estimate the union of narrow hairbrushes. These technicalities require the conditions for Theorem
2
to be fairly flexible.

\displaystyle\text{ and for all $\ell\in L$, }X_{\ell}\geq\lambda/(2\delta))

(3.26)

(3.27)

\displaystyle\geq 1/2.

large enough. Fix such a realization

In this section we give the proof of Theorem
3
and sketch the proof of Theorem
4
. We leave them here in the appendix since they are not used to give the main result, but they extend the possible applications of the coniness and twistiness conditions.

A.1.
Proof of Theorem
3

Define the shadings
Y(\ell)=N_{\delta}(\ell)\cap E
.
By cutting the parameter space into
1\times 1\times\mathfrak{c}/|\log\delta|^{3}
-slabs, it is enough to show that

(1.1)

\displaystyle|E_{L}|\gtrsim_{\epsilon}\delta^{\epsilon}\mathfrak{c}^{1/2}\lambda^{2}\delta^{1/2}(\delta^{2}\#L),

where
\theta(L)
has diameter at most
\mathfrak{c}/|\log\delta|^{3}
. We now prove (
1.1
)

Finding a typical angle
r
and easy bound
:

We may assume that
Y(\ell)\subset\{\mathbf{x}:\#L(\mathbf{x})\geq\delta^{-\epsilon}\}
by cutting
Y(\ell)
into the low and high multiplicity parts as in Section
6
. As in Step 2 of Section
6
, we can pigeonhole a
r=|\log\delta|^{-j}
and a set of curves with their shadings
(L_{1},Y_{1})
with the following properties.

•

L_{1}\subset L
and
\#L_{1}\gtrapprox\#L
,

•

|Y_{1}(\ell)|\gtrapprox\lambda\delta^{2}
, and

•

(1.2)

\displaystyle Y_{1}(\ell)\subset\{\mathbf{x}\in Y(\ell):r_{L}(\mathbf{x})\sim r\text{ and }\theta(\ell)\in J_{L}(\mathbf{x})\}.

The set of curves through a point is one dimensional, so
\#L_{1}(\mathbf{x})\lesssim r/\delta
. This gives the easy bound

(1.3)

\displaystyle|E_{L}|\geq|E_{L_{1}}|

\displaystyle\gtrsim(\delta/r)\sum_{\ell\in L_{1}}|Y_{1}(\ell)|

(1.4)

\displaystyle\gtrapprox\lambda(\delta/r)(\delta^{2}\#L).

The curves are contained in disjoint
1\times r
-tubes and applying multilinear Kakeya inside
:

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a set of
O(r^{-3})
boundedly overlapping balls whose union covers
L_{1}
. For each
B\in\mathcal{B}
let
L_{1}^{B}=L_{1}\cap B
. Because of (
1.2
), there are
O(1)
balls
B
with
\mathbf{x}\in E_{L_{1}^{B}}
. Thus

(1.5)

\displaystyle|\bigcup_{\ell\in L_{1}}Y_{1}(\ell)|\gtrsim\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}|\bigcup_{\ell\in L_{1}^{B}}Y_{1}(\ell)|.

By the same application of the multilinear Kakeya theorem as in Step 8 of Section
6
, we find

(1.6)

\displaystyle|\bigcup_{\ell\in L_{1}^{B}}Y_{1}(\ell)|\gtrapprox_{\epsilon}\delta^{\epsilon}\mathfrak{c}\lambda^{3}r(\delta^{2}\#L_{1}^{B}).

Summing over
B\in\mathcal{B}
,

(1.7)

\displaystyle|E_{L}|\gtrapprox_{\epsilon}\delta^{\epsilon}\mathfrak{c}\lambda^{3}r(\delta^{2}\#L).

Upon taking the geometric mean of (
1.3
) and (
1.7
), we get the claim (
1.1
).

A.2.
Sketch of proof of Theorem
4

Steps 1 through 7 in Section
6
would yield Theorem
4
if one could take
\tilde{\rho}=(\rho r)^{1/2}
, instead of
\min((\rho r)^{1/2},\rho/r)
. That would precisely be the proof contained in
[
13
]
. The only obstruction is Lemma
5.1
, which forces the prisms to be too narrow. By assuming that
X
is planey, we have a better expansion of
\ell_{s,\theta^{\prime}}
as expected.

Lemma A.1

(Planey version of Lemma
5.1
)
.

Fix
s_{0}\in B_{1}^{1}
.
Suppose that
X
satisfies the basic conditions and is planey. Then the curve
\ell_{s,\theta^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{C}(X,\mathbf{p})
has the expansion

(1.8)

\displaystyle\ell_{s,\theta^{\prime}}(t)-\ell_{\mathbf{p}}(t)

\displaystyle=((t-s)(\theta^{\prime}-\theta+\eta_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta^{\prime}))\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}(s_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad+O(|\theta^{\prime}-\theta||t-s|^{2}+|\theta^{\prime}-\theta||t-s||s-s_{0}|),0),

where
\eta_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta^{\prime})=O(|\theta^{\prime}-\theta|^{2})
is a scalar function.

Proof.

With
Y
as in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, Taylor expansion in
t
gives

(1.9)

\displaystyle Y(\theta^{\prime},s,t)

\displaystyle=(t-s)(\omega_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta^{\prime})-\omega_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta))+O(|\theta^{\prime}-\theta||t-s|^{2}).

Planiness gives
\det(\dot{\omega}_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta^{\prime}),\ddot{\omega}_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta^{\prime}))=0
for every
\theta^{\prime}
. Consequently,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta^{\prime})-\omega_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta)=(\theta^{\prime}-\theta+\eta_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta^{\prime}))\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}(s),

where
\eta_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta^{\prime})=O(|\theta^{\prime}-\theta|^{2})
. Expanding
\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}(s)=\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}(s_{0})+O(|s-s_{0}|)
proves the lemma.
∎

We now describe the better grain structure as a consequence of Lemma
A.1
. Fix
r\geq\delta
,
\rho\in[\delta,(\delta r)^{1/2}]
, and assume
|\theta^{\prime}-\theta|\sim r
. Then

(1.11)

\displaystyle|\ell_{s,\theta^{\prime}}(t)-\ell_{\mathbf{p}}(t)|\sim r|t-s|

as long as
|\theta^{\prime}-\theta|,|s-s_{0}|,|t-s|\ll 1
. So
\ell_{s,\theta^{\prime}}
is inside
N_{\rho}(\ell_{\mathbf{p}})
for only
t
in the range

(1.12)

\displaystyle|t-s|\lesssim\rho/r.

Fix
s_{0}
and consider
|s-s_{0}|\leq(\delta/r)^{1/2}
. For this range of
s
and inside
N_{\rho}(\ell)
, we have

(1.13)

\displaystyle\ell_{s,\theta}(t)-\ell_{p}(t)=((t-s)(\theta^{\prime}-\theta+\eta_{\mathbf{p},s}(\theta^{\prime}))\gamma_{\mathbf{p}}(s_{0})+O(\delta),0).

Thus the image of the right-hand side of (
1.13
) inside
N_{\rho}(\ell)
and for
|t-s|\leq\rho/r
is contained in a
\delta\times\rho\times(\delta/r)^{1/2}
-slab. The analogues of Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
, and Proposition
5.8
continue to hold with
\rho\in[\delta,(\delta r)^{1/2}]
, so we may run Steps 1 through 7 without any
\rho^{a}
loss.
