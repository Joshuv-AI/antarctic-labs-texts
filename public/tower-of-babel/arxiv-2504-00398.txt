Robust Completion for Rank- 1 1 Tensors with Noises
Jiawang Nie, Xindong Tang, Jinling Zhou

Abstract
. This paper studies the rank- 1 1 tensor completion problem for cubic tensors when there are noises for observed tensor entries. First, we propose a robust biquadratic optimization model for obtaining rank- 1 1 completing tensors. When the observed tensor is sufficiently close to be rank- 1 1 , we show that this biquadratic optimization produces an accurate rank- 1 1 tensor completion. Second, we give an efficient convex relaxation for solving the biquadratic optimization. When the optimizer matrix is separable, we show how to get optimizers for the biquadratic optimization and how to compute the rank- 1 1 completing tensor. When that matrix is not separable, we apply its spectral decomposition to obtain an approximate rank- 1 1 completing tensor. The software SDPNAL+ is applied to solve the resulting large size semidefinite programs. Numerical experiments are given to explore the efficiency of this biquadratic optimization model and the proposed convex relaxation.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{R}
be the real field.
A real tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}

of order
m
can be viewed as the multi-array indexed such that

\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}\leq n_{1},\ldots,1\leq i_{m}\leq n_{m}.\end{subarray}}

When
m=3
, the
\mathcal{A}
is called a cubic tensor.
We focus on cubic tensors in this paper.
For vectors
a,b,c
, the notation
a\otimes b\otimes c

denotes the rank-
1
tensor such that

(a\otimes b\otimes c)_{ijk}\,=\,a_{i}b_{j}c_{k}

for all indices
i,j,k
in the range.
For the tensor
\mathcal{A}
, its
Candecomp-Parafac
(CP) rank

[
21
]

is the smallest integer
r
such that

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}+\cdots+\mathcal{A}_{r},

where each
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is a rank-
1
tensor.
The CP rank is sometimes just referenced as rank.
There also exist other notions of tensor ranks. We refer to

[
8
,
13
,
17
,
21
,
22
,
31
]
for related work.

For a partially given tensor, the
tensor completion problem
(TCP)
concerns how to fill its missing entries so that the whole tensor
has some attractive properties, e.g., its rank is low.
The whole tensor with all its missing entries filled
is called a tensor completion or completed tensor.
There are wide applications for tensor completions, such as
recommendation systems
[
15
,
25
]
,
imaging and signal processing
[
29
,
30
,
51
]
.
More applications can be found in
[
27
]
.

TCPs are natural generalizations of matrix completions
[
7
,
9
]
.
Solving TCPs is generally more challenging.
Indeed, it is shown in
[
12
]
that for rank-
1
matrix completions,
if the associated bipartite graph is connected,
then the second order Moment-SOS relaxation is tight when the observed matrix is noise-free.
However, there does not exist similar results for rank-
1
tensor completions,
to the best of the authors’s knowledge.
There are different methods for doing tensor completions,
based on different tensor decompositions, such as
CP-decompositions
[
4
,
38
,
51
]
,
Tucker decomposition
[
3
,
39
]
,
tensor singular value decomposition
[
26
,
50
]
,
tensor tubal rank
[
23
,
33
]

and multi-rank
[
26
]
.
There exist computational methods based on
tensor nuclear norm relaxations

[
35
,
45
,
49
]

and Riemannian-manifold optimization

[
6
,
14
,
16
,
28
,
41
,
44
]
.
Most tensor computation problems are NP-hard
[
20
]
.

In the earlier work, most tensor completion methods assume the observed tensor
entries are noise-free, i.e., the exact values of those entries are known.
However, in applications, there almost always exist noises.
It is rather desirable to predict tensor data from both noisy and incomplete observations.
In contrast to the abundant literature of noise-free TCPs,
there exist relatively less work for noisy TCPs.
For noisy rank-
1
matrix completion, stable completions can be computed
by the second order Moment-SOS relaxations
[
12
]
.
Tensor decomposition and total variation regularization based models
have been proposed to solve noisy TCPs
[
1
,
53
]
.
Tensor tubal rank based methods for solving third-order noisy TCPs
are introduced in
[
47
,
48
]
.
Besides that, a semidefinite relaxation method based on the sum-of-squares hierarchy
is given in
[
5
]
.

Most tensor optimization problems can be formulated
as polynomial optimization
[
36
]
.
Nuclear norm relaxations can be used to
get tensor completions
[
35
,
45
,
54
]
.
Low rank symmetric tensor approximations can be obtained
by solving equations about generating polynomials
[
34
]
.

Rank-
1
tensor completion.

A particularly interesting class of TCPs is the rank-
1
tensor completion, i.e.,
finding a rank-
1
tensor that matches all partially given entries.
The rank-
1
TCP has very special properties.
It is shown in
[
54
]
that this problem is equivalent to
a special rank-
1
matrix recovery problem.
The geometric properties of the rank-
1
tensor completion problem
are well studied by Kahle et al.
[
24
]
.

For a positive integer
n
, denote the index set
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
A cubic tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
is said to be
partially given

if there exists a set
\Omega\subseteq[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times[n_{3}]

such that the entry
\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
is given for each triple
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
,
while it is not given for
(i,j,k)\not\in\Omega
.
As shown in
[
54
]
,
\mathcal{A}=a\otimes b\otimes c

is a rank-
1
tensor completion if and only if the vectors
a,b
satisfy

(1.1)

\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}}\,=\,0

for all
(i_{s},\,j_{s},\,k),(i_{t},j_{t},k)\in\Omega
.
Based on this observation, a rank-
1
tensor completion can be obtained by
solving the quadratic system of equations like (
1.1
).
Nuclear norm and moment relaxation methods are given in
[
54
]

to solve (
1.1
).
Under some general conditions, the moment method can find a rank-
1
tensor completion
if there exists one, or it detects nonexistence of rank-
1
completions if it does not exist.

Contributions.

In the work
[
54
]
, the partially given tensor entries are supposed to have no noises.
However, observation of tensor entries often has noises.
In this paper, we consider the rank-
1
TCP such that the observed tensor entries

\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
for
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
have noises.
That is, there is a rank-
1
tensor
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}
such that

(1.2)

\mathcal{A}=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}+\delta\mathcal{A},

where
\delta\mathcal{A}
is the noise tensor.
Our purpose is to recover the rank-
1
tensor
\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}
.
Due to the existence of the noise
\delta\mathcal{A}
, which is unknown,
the tensor
\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}

cannot be uniquely determined. This is because for any small perturbation

(\delta\hat{a},\delta\hat{b},\delta\hat{c})
, it holds

\mathcal{A}=(\hat{a}+\delta\hat{a})\otimes(\hat{b}+\delta\hat{b})\otimes(\hat{c}+\delta\hat{c})+\delta\widehat{\mathcal{A}},

with
\delta\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
also small. For this reason,
it is more practical for us to look for a rank-
1
tensor

a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}
such that

(1.3)

(\mathcal{A}-a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*})_{ijk}\quad\mbox{is small for}\quad(i,j,k)\in\Omega.

Due to the noise, the vectors
a^{*},b^{*},c^{*}

for (
1.3
) typically do not satisfy equations in (
1.1
).
This is because
\mathcal{A}
itself typically does not have an exact rank-
1
completion.
Thus, the methods in
[
54
]
are not applicable
for finding a rank-
1
tensor completion required for (
1.3
).
Alternatively, one may consider to solve the nonlinear least squares (NLS) problem

(1.4)

\min_{a,b,c}\quad\sum_{(i,j,k)\in\Omega}(\mathcal{A}_{ijk}-a_{i}b_{j}c_{k})^{2}.

This is a nonconvex polynomial optimization problem of degree
6
.
Traditional nonlinear optimization methods are applicable to solve (
1.4
).
However, their performance is very heuristic,
since they usually can only guarantee to find a stationary point.
In view of polynomial optimization, the objective in (
1.4
)
has degree
6
, with three groups of variables
a,b,c
.
There are totally
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}
variables in the polynomial.
So, the classical Moment-SOS relaxations
[
36
]

are quite expensive for solving (
1.4
) directly.

To get a rank-
1
tensor completion for (
1.3
),
we propose to solve the following biquadratic optimization problem

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\min&\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{s},j_{s},k)\in\Omega\\
(i_{t},j_{t},k)\in\Omega\end{subarray}}(\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}})^{2}\\
\mathit{s.t.}&||a||=||b||=1.\end{array}\right.

The above is motivated as follows. If
a\otimes b\otimes c

is an approximated rank-
1
tensor completion for

\mathcal{A}=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}+\delta\mathcal{A}
, then for each
k
and for each

(i_{s},j_{s},k)\in\Omega
and
(i_{t},j_{t},k)\in\Omega
, it holds that

\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}}\approx 0.

Please see Section
3
for more details.
We remark that the problem (
1.5
)
only involves the vector variables
a,b
but not
c
.
Instead of applying the classical Moment-SOS relaxations,
we propose a specifically designed efficient convex relaxation,
which is given in (
4.4
), to solve (
1.5
).
After it is solved, we solve linear least square problems to
retrieve the vector
c
, which eventually produces a rank-
1
tensor completion for (
1.3
).

The main contributions of this paper are:

•

When
\mathcal{A}
is sufficiently close to
\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}
,
we show that the rank-
1
tensor
a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}
obtained by solving (
1.5
)
is also close to
\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}
.

•

The biquadratic optimization problem (
1.5
)
is more computationally attractive than (
1.4
),
since it only involves variables
(a,b)
and the degree is four.

•

Due to the biquadratic structure, we propose an efficient convex relaxation,
which is (
4.4
), to solve (
1.5
).
The relaxation (
4.4
) is more computationally appealing
than the general Moment-SOS relaxation for solving (
1.5
).

•

We show that the convex relaxation (
4.4
) is tight
if the matrix
G[y^{*}]
in (
2.4
) is separable,
for the optimizer
y^{*}
of (
4.4
).

•

The software
SDPNAL+
is applied for solving the convex relaxation (
4.4
),
which is typically a large size semidefinite program (SDP).
Numerical experiments are presented to show the computational efficiency.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, some notation and preliminaries are introduced.
Section
3
gives the biquadratic optimization model
and its robustness is shown.
In Section
4
, we propose a convex relaxation
and show how to get the rank-
1
completing tensor.
Numerical experiments are presented in Section
5
.
We make a conclusion in Section
6
.

2.
Preliminaries

Notation.

The symbol
\mathbb{N}
(resp.,
\mathbb{R}
)
denotes the set of nonnegative integers (resp., real numbers).
For an integer
n>0
,
[n]
denotes the set
\left\{1,\cdots,n\right\}
.
We denote by
e
the vector of all ones and by
e_{i}

the vector with the
i
th entry equaling
1
and all other equaling zero,
while their dimensions depend on operations with them.
The superscript
T
stands for the transpose of a matrix or vector.
For the vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
||a||=\sqrt{a^{T}a}
denotes the Euclidean norm.
For a symmetric matrix
M
,
M\succeq 0
(resp.,
M\succ 0
) means that
M
is positive semidefinite (resp., positive definite). The cone of all
N
-by-
N

real symmetric positive semidefinite matrices is denoted as
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{N}
.
For two matrices
A=(a_{ij})
and
B
, their Kronecker product is the block matrix

A\otimes B=(a_{ij}B).

In particular, for two vectors
a=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{n_{1}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
and
b
,

a\otimes b=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}b^{T}&a_{2}b^{T}&\ldots&a_{n_{1}}b^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

For two cubic tensors
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
,
their Hilbert-Schmidt product is

\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\rangle\,:=\sum_{(i,j,k)\in[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times[n_{3}]}\mathcal{A}_{ijk}{\mathcal{B}}_{ijk}.

This induces the Hilbert-Schmidt norm

\|\mathcal{A}\|=\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}\rangle}.

For an index set
\Omega\subseteq[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times[n_{3}]
,

\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
stands for the subtensor of
\mathcal{A}

whose entries are indexed by
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
.
Its norm is defined as

\|\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}\coloneqq\sqrt{\sum_{(i,j,k)\in\Omega}|\mathcal{A}_{ijk}|^{2}}.

2.1.
Separable matrices

We give brief introduction to separable matrices,
which are quite useful for solving the biquadratic optimization (
1.5
).
Denote the label set

(2.1)

\Gamma:=\{(i,j,k,l):\,1\leq i\leq j\leq n_{1},\,1\leq k\leq l\leq n_{2}\}.

Its cardinality is

|\Gamma|=\frac{1}{4}n_{1}(n_{1}+1)n_{2}(n_{2}+1).

Let
\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma}
be the space of vectors whose entries are labelled by

(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma
.
For
i,j\in[n_{1}]
and
k,l\in[n_{2}]
with
i\neq j
and
k\neq l
,
denote the basis matrices

(2.2)

\begin{array}[]{ll}E_{ii}=e_{i}e_{i}^{T}\in\mathcal{S}^{n_{1}},&E_{ij}=e_{i}e_{j}^{T}+e_{i}e_{j}^{T}\in\mathcal{S}^{n_{1}},\\
E_{kk}=e_{k}e_{k}^{T}\in\mathcal{S}^{n_{2}},&E_{kl}=e_{k}e_{l}^{T}+e_{l}e_{k}^{T}\in\mathcal{S}^{n_{2}}.\end{array}

Then, for all
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n_{1}})
and
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{2}})
, it holds that

\displaystyle(aa^{T})\otimes(bb^{T})

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i\in[n_{1}]}\sum_{j\in[n_{1}]}a_{i}a_{j}e_{i}e_{j}^{T}\Big)\otimes\Big(\sum_{k\in[n_{2}]}\sum_{l\in[n_{2}]}b_{k}b_{l}e_{k}e_{l}^{T}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n_{1}]}\sum_{k,l\in[n_{2}]}a_{i}a_{j}b_{k}b_{l}(e_{i}e_{j}^{T})\otimes(e_{k}e_{l}^{T})

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}a_{i}a_{j}b_{k}b_{l}E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl}.

This motivates us to define the linear matrix function in
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma}
:

(2.4)

G[y]\coloneqq\sum_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}y_{ijkl}E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl},

where
y=(y_{ijkl})_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}
.
For instance, when
n_{1}=n_{2}=3
,
G[y]
reads as

G[y]=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}y_{1111}&y_{1112}&y_{1113}&y_{1211}&y_{1212}&y_{1213}&y_{1311}&y_{1312}&y_{1313}\\
y_{1112}&y_{1122}&y_{1123}&y_{1212}&y_{1222}&y_{1223}&y_{1312}&y_{1322}&y_{1323}\\
y_{1113}&y_{1123}&y_{1133}&y_{1213}&y_{1223}&y_{1233}&y_{1313}&y_{1323}&y_{1333}\\
y_{1211}&y_{1212}&y_{1213}&y_{2211}&y_{2212}&y_{2213}&y_{2311}&y_{2312}&y_{2313}\\
y_{1212}&y_{1222}&y_{1223}&y_{2212}&y_{2222}&y_{2223}&y_{2312}&y_{2322}&y_{2323}\\
y_{1213}&y_{1223}&y_{1233}&y_{2213}&y_{2223}&y_{2233}&y_{2313}&y_{2323}&y_{2333}\\
y_{1311}&y_{1312}&y_{1313}&y_{2311}&y_{2312}&y_{2313}&y_{3311}&y_{3312}&y_{3313}\\
y_{1312}&y_{1322}&y_{1323}&y_{2312}&y_{2322}&y_{2323}&y_{3312}&y_{3322}&y_{3323}\\
y_{1313}&y_{1323}&y_{1333}&y_{2313}&y_{2323}&y_{2333}&y_{3313}&y_{3323}&y_{3333}\\
\end{array}\right].

The matrix
G[y]
is closely related to biquadratic optimization.
If there is a Borel measure
\mu
whose support is contained in a set

K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
such that

(2.5)

y_{ijkl}=\int_{K}a_{i}a_{j}b_{k}b_{l}{\tt d}\mu\quad\text{for all}\quad(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma,

then (
2.3
) implies

(2.6)

\displaystyle G[y]=\sum_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}\int_{K}a_{i}a_{j}b_{k}b_{l}\,{\tt d}\mu E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl}=\int_{K}(aa^{T})\otimes(bb^{T}){\tt d}\mu.

The matrix
G[y]
is said to be
separable
if there exist vectors

a^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
,
b^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
and
scalars
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
such that

(2.7)

\begin{gathered}G[y]=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(a^{(i)}{a^{(i)}}^{T})\otimes(b^{(i)}{b^{(i)}}^{T}),\\
\|a^{(i)}\|=\|b^{(i)}\|=1,\,\lambda_{i}>0,\,i=1,\ldots,r.\end{gathered}

It is interesting to note that
G[y]
is separable if and only if (
2.6
)
holds for some measure
\mu
whose support is contained in

K=\{(a,b):\|a\|=\|b\|=1\}
.
We refer to
[
37
]
for this fact.
Clearly, if
G[y]
is separable, then it must be positive semidefinite.

The case that
\mbox{rank}\,G[y]=1
is particularly interesting.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma}
be such that
G[y]\succeq 0
.
If
\mbox{rank}\,G[y]=1
and
\mathrm{Trace}\,G[y]=1
,
then there exist
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
such that

(2.8)

G[y]=(aa^{T})\otimes(bb^{T}),\quad\|a\|=\|b\|=1.

Proof.

We write
G[y]
in the block form

G\left[y\right]\,=\,\begin{bmatrix}B_{11}&B_{12}&\cdots&B_{1n_{1}}\\
B_{12}&B_{22}&\cdots&B_{2n_{1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
B_{1n_{1}}&B_{2n_{1}}&\cdots&B_{n_{1}n_{1}}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where each block
B_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}}
is also a symmetric matrix.
Since
\mbox{rank}\,G\left[y\right]=1
, we know
\mbox{rank}\,B_{ij}\leq 1
for all
i,j
.
Every diagonal block
B_{ii}\succeq 0
, since
G[y]\succeq 0
.
By (
2.4
), it holds that for all
i,j\in[n_{1}]

B_{ij}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq l\leq n_{2}}y_{ijkl}E_{kl}.

Note that each
B_{ij}
is also a symmetric matrix.
Therefore, for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq n_{1}
, there exist
\mu_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}

and
w_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
such that

(2.9)

B_{ij}=\mu_{ij}w_{ij}w_{ij}^{T},\quad\|w_{ij}\|=1,\quad e^{T}w_{ij}\geq 0,\quad\mu_{ii}\geq 0.

Since
G[y]\succeq 0
and
\mbox{Trace}\,G[y]=1
,
we can generally assume that
B_{11}
is a nonzero matrix
(if
B_{11}=0
, then
B_{1i}=0
for all
i
,
in which case we can consider the first nonzero block
B_{ii}\neq 0
).
Then
\mu_{11}>0
and
w_{11}
is a nonzero vector. Note that

\begin{bmatrix}B_{11}&B_{1i}\\
B_{1i}&B_{ii}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0,\quad\mbox{rank}\begin{bmatrix}B_{11}&B_{1i}\\
B_{1i}&B_{ii}\end{bmatrix}=1.

This forces that each
w_{1i}
is a multiple of
w_{11}
or
\mu_{1i}=0
.
Since
\|w_{1i}\|=1
and
e^{T}w_{1i}\geq 0
,
we have either
w_{1i}=w_{11}
or
\mu_{1i}=0
for each
i
.
Since
\mbox{rank}\,G[y]=1
, each block row must be a multiple of the first row block.
Therefore, we can further get that
either
w_{ij}=w_{11}
or
\mu_{ij}=0
, for all
i,j
.
For the case
\mu_{ij}=0
, we can also set
w_{ij}=w_{11}

and (
2.9
) still holds.

Let
U=(\mu_{ij})_{i,j\in[n_{1}]}
, then

G[y]\,=\,U\otimes(w_{11}w_{11}^{T}).

Since
G[y]\succeq 0
and
\mbox{rank}\,G[y]=1
,
we also have
U\succeq 0
and
\mbox{rank}\,U=1
.
There exists a vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
such that
U=aa^{T}
.
Let
b=w_{11}
. Since
\mathrm{Trace}\,G[y]=1
, we have

\|a\|=\|b\|=1
.
This completes the proof. ∎

When
\mbox{rank}\,\,G[y]>1
, the separability of
G[y]

can be detected by Algorithm 4.2 in
[
37
]
.
It requires to solve a hierarchy of moment relaxations.
If
G[y]
is separable, the algorithm can get a decomposition like (
2.7
).
If it is not separable, the algorithm can return a certificate for that,
i.e., it shows a moment relaxation is infeasible
and denies the existence of (
2.7
).
We refer to
[
37
]
for more details about separable matrices.

3.
Biquadratic optimization for completion

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

be a partially given tensor such that its entries

\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
are known for
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
,
for a given index set
\Omega\subseteq[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times[n_{3}]
.
We wish to look for a rank-
1
tensor
a\otimes b\otimes c

with
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
, such that

\mathcal{A}_{ijk}=a_{i}b_{j}c_{k}
for all
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
.
However, such vectors
a,b,c
usually do not exist
when there are noises in the observed tensor entries
\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
.
Typically,
\mathcal{A}
is close to a rank-
1
tensor, but they are not exactly equal.

Suppose there is a rank-
1
tensor

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}
,
for nonzero vectors
\hat{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
,

\hat{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
,
\hat{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
such that

\mathcal{A}_{ijk}=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{ijk}+\delta\mathcal{A}_{ijk}\quad\text{for all}\quad(i,\,j,\,k)\in\Omega,

where
\delta\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
stands for the noise at the
(i,j,k)
th tensor entry.
Typically, the noises
\delta\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
are small.
Throughout the context, we say the noise
\delta\mathcal{A}

is small if its norm
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}
is small.
Up to scaling, we can generally assume that

(3.1)

\|\hat{a}\|=\|\hat{b}\|=1.

This is because
\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}=(\alpha\hat{a})\otimes(\beta\hat{b})\otimes(\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\hat{c})

for all nonzero scalars
\alpha,\beta
.
Our goal is to recover the vectors
\hat{a}
,
\hat{b}
,
\hat{c}
from
the given tensor entries
\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
.

For each
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
, denote the index set of pairs

\Omega_{k}\,\coloneqq\,\left\{\ (i,j)\in[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]:(i,j,k)\in\Omega\ \right\}.

Let
m_{k}\coloneqq|\Omega_{k}|
, the cardinality of
\Omega_{k}
.
One can write that

(3.2)

\Omega_{k}=\{(i_{1},j_{1}),\,(i_{2},j_{2}),\ldots,(i_{m_{k}},j_{m_{k}})\}.

When all
\delta\mathcal{A}_{ijk}=0
,
for each
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
we have

(3.3)

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{m_{k}}j_{m_{k}}k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{a}_{i_{1}}\hat{b}_{j_{1}}\\
\hat{a}_{i_{2}}\hat{b}_{j_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{i_{m_{k}}}\hat{b}_{j_{m_{k}}}\end{bmatrix}\hat{c}_{k}.

This implies that

\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}}\,=\,0

for all
(i_{s},\,j_{s}),(i_{t},\,j_{t})\in\Omega_{k}
.
However, when there are noises, the above equations do not hold anymore.

When there are noises
\delta\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
, the equation (
3.3
) becomes

(3.4)

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{A}_{i_{m_{k}}j_{m_{k}}k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{a}_{i_{1}}\hat{b}_{j_{1}}\\
\hat{a}_{i_{2}}\hat{b}_{j_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\hat{a}_{i_{m_{k}}}\hat{b}_{j_{m_{k}}}\end{bmatrix}\hat{c}_{k}+\begin{bmatrix}\delta\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}\\
\delta\mathcal{A}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}\\
\vdots\\
\delta\mathcal{A}_{i_{m_{k}}j_{m_{k}}k}\end{bmatrix}.

When the noises
\delta\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
are small, one can see that

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}}\\
=&\hat{c}_{k}(\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}}\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}-\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}})+(\delta\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}-\delta\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}})\\
=&\delta\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}-\delta\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}}\\
\approx&0.\end{array}

This motivates us to find a rank-
1
tensor completion
a\otimes b\otimes c
such that

\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}}\approx 0.

So, we consider the set of quadratic homogeneous polynomials in
a,b
:

(3.5)

\Phi\coloneqq\left\{\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(i_{s},\,j_{s}),(i_{t},\,j_{t})\in\Omega_{k},\\
s<t,\,k=1,\ldots,n_{3}\end{array}\right.\right\}.

There are totally
\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\binom{m_{k}}{2}
polynomials in
\Phi
.
Each
\phi\in\Phi
is bilinear in
(a,b)
.
When
\mathcal{A}
is noise-free, i.e.,
\delta\mathcal{A}_{ijk}=0
for all
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
, we have

\phi(\hat{a},\hat{b})=0
for every
\phi\in\Phi
.
When the noises are small, we have

\phi(\hat{a},\hat{b})\approx 0
.

To get a rank-
1
completing tensor
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}

as in (
3.1
) and (
3.4
), we consider the
polynomial optimization problem

(3.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\displaystyle f(a,b)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\sum\limits_{1\leq s<t\leq m_{k}}(\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}})^{2}\\
\mathit{s.t.}&\|a\|^{2}=\|b\|^{2}=1.\end{array}\right.

The objective polynomial
f
is biquadratic in
(a,b)
, so it holds

(3.7)

f(a,b)=f(-a,b)=f(a,-b)=f(-a,-b).

This means that
(a^{*},b^{*})
is a minimizer for (
3.6
)
if and only if all four pairs
(\pm a^{*},\pm b^{*})
are minimizers.
Since (
3.6
) is a polynomial optimization problem,
one can apply the classical Moment-SOS hierarchy
[
18
,
36
]
to solve it.
However, this is not computationally appealing for solving (
3.6
).
In Section
4
, we propose an efficient convex relaxation,
which is based on the biquadratic structure.

3.1.
Stability analysis

Suppose
(a^{*},b^{*})
is the minimizer of (
3.6
).
We show that
a^{*}\otimes b^{*}
is close to
\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}

or
-\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}

when the noise
\delta\mathcal{A}
is sufficiently small.

Recall that
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}

with the normalization
\|\hat{a}\|=\|\hat{b}\|=1
.
For
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
and for
(i_{t},j_{t}),\,(i_{s},j_{s})\in\Omega_{k}
,
let
H_{s,t,k}
denote the Hessian of

(3.8)

\hat{\psi}_{s,t,k}(a,b)\,\coloneqq\,(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}})^{2}

at the point
(\hat{a},\hat{b})
, i.e.,

H_{s,t,k}\,=\,\nabla^{2}\hat{\psi}_{s,t,k}(\hat{a},\hat{b}).

Since
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{ijk}=\hat{a}_{i}\hat{b}_{j}\hat{c}_{k}
, one can verify that

H_{s,t,k}=\hat{z}_{s,t,k}\cdot(\hat{z}_{s,t,k})^{T},

where
\hat{z}_{s,t,k}
is the column vector

\hat{z}_{s,t,k}=\sqrt{2}\hat{c}_{k}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}e_{i_{t}}-\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}}e_{i_{s}}\\
\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}}\hat{a}_{i_{t}}e_{j_{t}}-\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}\hat{a}_{i_{s}}e_{j_{s}}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}.

When
\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}}\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}\neq 0
, we have the expression

\hat{z}_{s,t,k}\,=\,\sqrt{2}\hat{c}_{k}\hat{a}_{i_{s}}\hat{b}_{j_{s}}\hat{a}_{i_{t}}\hat{b}_{j_{t}}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{e_{i_{t}}}{\hat{a}_{i_{t}}}-\frac{e_{i_{s}}}{\hat{a}_{i_{s}}}\\
\frac{e_{j_{t}}}{\hat{b}_{j_{t}}}-\frac{e_{j_{s}}}{\hat{b}_{j_{s}}}\end{bmatrix}.

For the function

(3.9)

\hat{\psi}(a,b)\,\coloneqq\,\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq m_{k}}\hat{\psi}_{s,t,k}(a,b),

its Hessian at
(\hat{a},\hat{b})
is the matrix

(3.10)

H(\hat{a},\hat{b})\,\coloneqq\,\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq m_{k}}\hat{z}_{s,t,k}\cdot(\hat{z}_{s,t,k})^{T}.

Let
\hat{Z}
be the matrix whose rows are the vectors
(\hat{z}_{s,t,k})^{T}
, i.e.,

(3.11)

\hat{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}\vdots\\
(\hat{z}_{s,t,k})^{T}\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}.

Furthermore, we let

(3.12)

Z\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\hat{Z}\\
\hat{D}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{D}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{a}&0\\
0&\hat{b}\end{array}\right]^{T}.

For the polynomial
\hat{\psi}_{s,t,k}
defined as in (
3.8
), it holds

\hat{\psi}_{s,t,k}(\pm\hat{a},\pm\hat{b})=0.

This means that four pairs
(\pm\hat{a},\pm\hat{b})

are common zeros of polynomials in the set

(3.13)

\hat{\Psi}\coloneqq\left\{\hat{\psi}_{s,t,k}(\hat{a},\hat{b})\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(i_{s},\,j_{s}),(i_{t},\,j_{t})\in\Omega_{k},\\
s<t,\,k=1,\ldots,n_{3}\end{array}\right.\right\}.

The relationship between the biquadratic optimization (
3.6
)
and the rank-
1
tensor completion problem is given as follows.
Recall that
\delta\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
is the noise tensor given in (
1.2
).
Throughout the paper, by saying a property holds when

\delta\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
is sufficiently small, we mean that
there exists a small
\varepsilon>0
such that the property holds whenever
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}<\varepsilon
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}

with
\|\hat{a}\|=\|\hat{b}\|=1
. Assume
\mbox{rank}\,Z=n_{1}+n_{2}

and
(\pm\hat{a},\pm\hat{b})
are the only common zeros of polynomials in
\hat{\Psi}
.
Let
(a^{*},b^{*})
be a minimizer of (
3.6
).
Then, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
depending on

(\hat{a},\hat{b},\hat{c})
such that

(3.14)

\|a^{*}\otimes b^{*}-\theta\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\|\leq C_{1}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}\quad\text{for some}\quad\theta\in\{-1,1\},

when
\delta\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
is sufficiently small.

Proof.

To show the dependence of coefficients of
f(a,b)
on
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
, we define

F(a,b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\,\coloneqq\,\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\sum\limits_{1\leq s<t\leq m_{k}}(\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}})^{2}.

For a given
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
, the objective
f(a,b)
of (
3.6
)
is the same as
F(a,b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}).

The Lagrangian function of (
3.6
) is

L(a,b,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\coloneqq F(a,b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})-\lambda_{1}(||a||^{2}-1)-\lambda_{2}(||b||^{2}-1).

When
\|\mathcal{A}-\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\|_{\Omega}=0
,
we have
F(\hat{a},\hat{b},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=0
.
Since
F(a,b,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})\geq 0
for all
a,b
, we further have

\nabla_{(a,b)}F(\hat{a},\hat{b},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})\,=\,0.

Since both
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
are nonzero vectors,
the linear independence constraint qualification condition
(see
[
36
, Section 5.1]
) holds at
(\hat{a},\hat{b})
,
thus
\hat{\lambda}_{1}=\hat{\lambda}_{2}=0

is the unique solution to the following

Karush-Kuhn-Tucker
system about
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

(3.15)

\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{a}F(\hat{a},\hat{b},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})-2\lambda_{1}\hat{a}\\
\nabla_{b}F(\hat{a},\hat{b},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})-2\lambda_{2}\hat{b}\end{bmatrix}=0.

Let
\Lambda
be the function such that

\Lambda(a,b,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})\,\coloneqq\nabla L(a,b,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{a}F(a,b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})-2\lambda_{1}a\\
\nabla_{b}F(a,b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})-2\lambda_{2}b\\
1-a^{T}a\\
1-b^{T}b\end{bmatrix}.

Note that

\Lambda(\hat{a},\hat{b},0,0,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=0.

Its Jacobian matrix at
(\hat{a},\hat{b},0,0,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})
is

J=\begin{bmatrix}H(\hat{a},\hat{b})&\hat{D}^{T}\\
\hat{D}&0\end{bmatrix},

where
H
and
\hat{D}
are given as in (
3.10
) and (
3.12
), respectively.
We show that
J
is nonsingular. Suppose there exist

p=\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+n_{2}},\quad h\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad H(\hat{a},\hat{b})p+\hat{D}^{T}h=0,\quad\hat{D}p=0,

which implies

p^{T}H(\hat{a},\hat{b})p+p^{T}\hat{D}^{T}h=p^{T}H(\hat{a},\hat{b})p=0.

Moreover, by (
3.10
), we have

0=p^{T}H(\hat{a},\hat{b})p=\sum_{k}\sum_{s,t}\left|(\hat{z}_{s,t,k})^{T}p\right|^{2}\geq 0.

So
(\hat{z}_{s,t,k})^{T}p=0
for all indices
s,t,k
in the range.
Since
\hat{D}p=0
, we know
\hat{a}^{T}u=\hat{b}^{T}v=0
,
hence
Zp=0
. Since
Z
has full column rank, we get
p=0
.
This shows that
J
is nonsingular.

By the Implicit Function Theorem (see
[
40
, Theorem 9.28]
),
there exist an open neighbourhood
\mathcal{V}
of
(\hat{a},\hat{b},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})

and a continuously differentiable function

\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3},\varphi_{4}):\,\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V},

defined in an open neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega}
, such that

\varphi_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=\hat{a},\quad\varphi_{2}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=\hat{b},\quad\varphi_{3}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=0,\quad\varphi_{4}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=0,

\Lambda(\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\,\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\,\varphi_{3}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\,\varphi_{4}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})=0\quad\text{for all}\quad\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{U},

and
\varphi(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
is the unique solution to
\Lambda(a,b,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=0

such that

(\varphi(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\in\mathcal{V}\quad\text{for every}\quad\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{U}.

Since
\varphi
is continuously differentiable, there exist scalars
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
C_{0}>0
such that
\mathcal{U}_{0}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}:\|\mathcal{A}-\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\|_{\Omega}<\varepsilon_{0}\}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
,
and for all
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}
it holds that

(3.16)

\|\big(\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\big)-\big(\varphi_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega}),\varphi_{2}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})\big)\|\leq C_{0}\|\delta\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\|.

Note that

F(\pm\hat{a},\pm\hat{b},\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=0.

By the assumption,
(\pm\hat{a},\pm\hat{b})
are the only global minimizers of
the function
F(a,b,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})
on the set

S=\{(a,b):\|a\|=\|b\|=1\}.

For each
\delta>0
, we have

F(a,b,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})\,>\,0\quad\text{for all}\quad(a,b)\in S\setminus N_{\delta},

where

N_{\delta}\coloneqq\bigcup_{(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in\{(\pm 1,\pm 1)\}}\{(a,b):\big(a-\theta_{1}\hat{a}\big)^{2}+\big(b-\theta_{2}\hat{b}\big)^{2}<\delta\}.

Since
S\setminus N_{\delta}
is compact, there exists
\epsilon>0
depending on
\delta
, such that

F(a,b,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})\,\geq\,\epsilon\quad\text{for all}\quad(a,b)\in S\setminus N_{\delta}.

Note that
\delta\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}=\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}-\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega}
.
Since
F
is continuous, there exists
\varepsilon_{1}>0

such that for all
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}<\varepsilon_{1}
, it holds

(3.17)

F(a,b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\,\geq\,\epsilon/2\quad\text{for all}\quad(a,b)\in S\setminus N_{\delta}.

Note that for every
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{U}
,
\varphi(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
is the only solution to
\Lambda(a,b,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=0
such that
(\varphi(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\in\mathcal{V}
.
Thus, we fix
\delta>0
small enough so that for every
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{U}
, the pair

(\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))
is the only critical point
of (
3.6
) in the open ball given by

(a-\hat{a})^{2}+(b-\hat{b})^{2}<\delta.

Since for every
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in\{(\pm 1,\pm 1)\}
, it holds

\begin{gathered}\nabla_{a}F(\theta_{1}a,\theta_{2}b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})=\theta_{1}\nabla_{a}F(a,b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\\
\nabla_{b}F(\theta_{1}a,\theta_{2}b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})=\theta_{2}\nabla_{b}F(a,b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\end{gathered}

we have
\Lambda(a,b,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=0
if and only if

\Lambda(\pm a,\pm b,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=0
.
So, for each pair
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in\{(\pm 1,\pm 1)\}
,

(\theta_{1}\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\theta_{1}\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))

is the only critical point of (
3.6
) in the open ball

(a-\theta_{1}\hat{a})^{2}+(b-\theta_{2}\hat{b})^{2}<\delta.

By the continuity of
F
and
\varphi
, there exists
\varepsilon_{2}<\varepsilon_{1}
such that for all
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
satisfying
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}<\varepsilon_{2}
, it holds

\begin{gathered}F(\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\,\leq\,\epsilon/3,\\
\big(\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})-\hat{a}\big)^{2}+\big(\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})-\hat{b}\big)^{2}<\delta.\end{gathered}

This further implies the following holds for all
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in\{(\pm 1,\pm 1)\}
:

(3.18)

\begin{gathered}F(\theta_{1}\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\theta_{2}\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\,\leq\,\epsilon/3,\\
\big(\theta_{1}\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\theta_{2}\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\big)\in S\cap N_{\delta}.\end{gathered}

Since the feasible set of (
3.6
) is compact,
it must have global minimizers for all
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
.
Thus, by (
3.17
) and (
3.18
),
the global minimizers of (
3.6
) exist,
and they all belong to
N_{\delta}
, when
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}<\varepsilon_{2}<\varepsilon_{1}
.
Moreover, the only critical points of (
3.6
) in
N_{\delta}

are
(\pm\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\pm\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))
, and

F(a,b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})=F(\pm a,\pm b,\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}).

Therefore, when
\delta\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
is sufficiently small,

(\pm\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}),\pm\varphi_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}))

are the only global minimizers of (
3.6
),
and
(a^{*},b^{*})
must be one of these four pairs.
Since
\varphi_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=\hat{a}

and
\varphi_{2}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega})=\hat{b}
,
the tensor product
a^{*}\otimes b^{*}
must be sufficiently close to
either
\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}
or
-\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}
.
Therefore, for all
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
such that
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}<\min(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{2})
,
(
3.16
) implies that there is a constant
C_{1}>0

such that (
3.14
) holds for
\theta=1
or
\theta=-1
.
Moreover, the constant
C_{1}
only depends on
(\hat{a},\hat{b},\hat{c})
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The following remarks provide further expositions
for Theorem
3.1
and its assumptions.

(i)

Theorem
3.1
shows the existence of
\varepsilon

such that (
3.14
) holds. However, the theorem does not provide
an explicit estimation of such
\varepsilon
. This is interesting for future work.

(ii)

The assumption that
(\pm\hat{a},\pm\hat{b})

are the only common zeros of
\hat{\Psi}
does not necessarily imply that they are the only global minimizers of (
3.6
).
It concerns the uniqueness of common solutions (up to switching of signs)
for
\hat{\Psi}
, which is given by the noise-free tensor
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}

instead of the observed tensor
\mathcal{A}
that defines (
3.6
).
If there exists a pair of indices
(i,j)
such that
(i,j,k)\notin\Omega
for all
k
,
then this assumption typically does not hold.
For such a case, one can check that
(a,b)=(e_{i},e_{j})

is a global minimizer of (
3.6
)
since
f(e_{i},e_{j})=0
, and thus solving (
3.6
)
may yield a minimizer
e_{i}\otimes e_{j}
that is far away from
both
\pm\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}
.

(iii)

Our algorithm is applicable,
regardless of whether the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
hold or not.
Even for the case where there exists
(i,j)
such that
(i,j,k)\notin\Omega
for all
k
,
one may heuristically estimate some entry
\mathcal{A}_{ijk}
and add the triple
(i,j,k)
to
\Omega
before solving (
3.6
). This can prevent the algorithm from falling to the unwanted trivial solution
(e_{i},e_{j})
. We refer to Subsection
5.6
for this.

3.2.
Retrieval of rank-
1
tensor completion

Suppose that
(a^{*},b^{*})
is an optimizer pair of
(
3.6
)
.
We look for the vector
c^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
such that the distance

\|\mathcal{A}-a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}\|_{\Omega}
is minimum.
This requires to solve the linear least square problem:

(3.19)

\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}}\quad g(c)\coloneqq\sum_{(i,j,k)\in\Omega}(\mathcal{A}_{ijk}-a^{*}_{i}b^{*}_{j}c_{k})^{2}.

We remark that even if the perturbation
\delta\mathcal{A}
is small,
a minimizer
c^{*}
of (
3.19
) may not give a satisfying rank-
1

tensor completion
a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}
, i.e.,
the distance
\|\mathcal{A}-a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}\|_{\Omega}
may be large.
The following is such an exposition.

Example 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3\times 3}
be a partially given tensor with

\Omega=[2]\times[2]\times[3],\quad\mathcal{A}_{ijk}=\hat{a}_{i}\otimes\hat{b}_{j}\otimes\hat{c}_{k}+\delta\mathcal{A}_{ijk},\ (i,j,k)\in\Omega,

where each
\hat{a}_{i}=\hat{b}_{j}=\hat{c}_{k}=1
and the noise
\delta\mathcal{A}_{ijk}=10^{-6}
.
Since the variables
a_{3}
,
b_{3}
do not appear in (
3.6
), one may check that

a^{*}=b^{*}=(0,0,1)
minimizes (
3.6
) with the minimum value
f^{*}=0
.
Moreover, the objective value of (
3.19
)
is constantly equal to
12(1+10^{-6})^{2}
for all
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
,
since
a^{*}_{i}b^{*}_{j}c_{k}=0
for every
(i,j,k)\in\Omega
.
Thus, no matter how
c^{*}
is selected, we always have

\left\|\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}-a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}\right\|\geq 4\gg\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

The reason for the above case to happen is that the minimizer
(a^{*},b^{*})

makes the least square problem (
3.19
) singular.
This leads to the following definition.

Definition 3.4
.

A pair
(a,b)
of nonzero vectors is said to be singular if there exists
k\in[n_{3}]

such that
m_{k}=0
or

a_{i_{1}}b_{j_{1}}=\cdots=a_{i_{m_{k}}}b_{j_{m_{k}}}=0,

where
\Omega_{k}=\{(i_{1},j_{1}),\ldots,(i_{m_{k}},j_{m_{k}})\}
as in (
3.2
).
The pair
(a,b)
is said to be nonsingular if it is not singular.

We remark that if
m_{k}=0
for some
k
, then the variable
c_{k}

does not appear in (
3.19
) and we can choose arbitrary values for
c_{k}
.
Note that
(a,b)
is singular if and only if
all four pairs
(\pm a,\pm b)
are singular.
The objective
g(c)
in (
3.19
) can be equivalently written as

(3.20)

g(c)=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}g_{k}(c_{k})\quad\text{where}\quad g_{k}(c_{k})=\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega_{k}}(\mathcal{A}_{ijk}-a^{*}_{i}b^{*}_{j}c_{k})^{2}.

When
(a^{*},b^{*})
is nonsingular, the minimizer
c_{k}^{*}
of
g_{k}(c_{k})
is explicitly given as

(3.21)

c_{k}^{*}=\Big(\sum_{s=1}^{m_{k}}\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a^{*}_{i_{s}}b^{*}_{j_{s}}\Big)/\Big(\sum_{s=1}^{m_{k}}(a^{*}_{i_{s}}b^{*}_{j_{s}})^{2}\Big),

and the minimizer of (
3.19
) is

(3.22)

c^{*}\,=\,(c_{1}^{*},\ldots,c_{n_{3}}^{*}).

The distance between
\hat{c}
and
c^{*}
can be estimated as follows.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}

with
\|\hat{a}\|=\|\hat{b}\|=1
. Assume
\mbox{rank}\,Z=n_{1}+n_{2}

and
(\pm\hat{a},\pm\hat{b})
are the only common zeros of polynomials in
\hat{\Psi}
.
Suppose
(\hat{a},\hat{b})
is nonsingular
and
\hat{c}_{k}\neq 0
for all
k
.
Let
(a^{*},b^{*})
be a minimizer of (
3.6
)
and let
c^{*}
be given as in (
3.22
).
There exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
(\hat{a},\hat{b},\hat{c})
, such that

(3.23)

\|a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}-\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}\|\leq C\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega},

when the perturbation
\delta\mathcal{A}
is sufficiently small.

Proof.

Note that
(a^{*},b^{*})
is nonsingular if and only if all the four pairs

(\pm a^{*},\pm b^{*})
are nonsingular.
Since
(\hat{a},\hat{b})
is nonsingular for (
3.19
),

(a^{*},b^{*})
is also nonsingular when
\delta\mathcal{A}
is sufficiently small,
by Theorem
3.1
. So,
c^{*}
is the unique optimizer
for (
3.19
), given in (
3.22
).
For convenience of analysis, we can generally assume
(
3.14
) holds for
\theta=1
in Theorem
3.1
;
otherwise one can replace
a^{*}
by
-a^{*}
.

For a fixed
k\in[n_{3}]
, denote the vectors

\begin{array}[]{ccl}w^{*}&\coloneqq&\begin{bmatrix}a^{*}_{i_{1}}b^{*}_{j_{1}}&a^{*}_{i_{2}}b^{*}_{j_{2}}&\cdots&a^{*}_{i_{m_{k}}}b^{*}_{j_{m_{k}}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},\\
\hat{w}&\coloneqq&\begin{bmatrix}\hat{a}_{i_{1}}\hat{b}_{j_{1}}&\hat{a}_{i_{2}}\hat{b}_{j_{2}}&\cdots&\hat{a}_{i_{m_{k}}}\hat{b}_{j_{m_{k}}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},\\
v&\coloneqq&\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}&\mathcal{A}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}&\cdots&\mathcal{A}_{i_{m_{k}}j_{m_{k}}k}\end{bmatrix}^{T},\\
\hat{v}&\coloneqq&\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{i_{1}j_{1}k}&\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{i_{2}j_{2}k}&\cdots&\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{i_{m_{k}}j_{m_{k}}k}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.\end{array}

By the given assumption, we have

|\hat{w}^{T}\hat{v}|>0,\quad\|\hat{w}\|^{2}>0.

Let
\delta_{w}\coloneqq w^{*}-\hat{w}
and
\delta_{v}\coloneqq v-\hat{v}
, then

\|\delta_{v}\|\leq\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

By Theorem
3.1
, there exists a constant
D_{k}
such that

(3.24)

\|\delta_{w}\|\leq D_{k}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega},

when
\delta\mathcal{A}
is sufficiently small.
Note that
\hat{c}_{k}=\frac{\hat{w}^{T}\hat{v}}{\hat{w}^{T}\hat{w}}
.
Thus we have

\displaystyle c^{*}_{k}-\hat{c}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(w^{*})^{T}v}{\|w^{*}\|^{2}}-\frac{\hat{w}^{T}\hat{v}}{\|\hat{w}\|^{2}}=\frac{(\hat{w}+\delta_{w})^{T}(\hat{v}+\delta_{v})}{(\hat{w}+\delta_{w})^{T}(\hat{w}+\delta_{w})}-\frac{\hat{w}^{T}\hat{v}}{\hat{w}^{T}\hat{w}}

\displaystyle=\hat{c}_{k}\frac{\frac{\delta_{w}^{T}\hat{v}+\delta_{v}^{T}\hat{w}+\delta_{w}^{T}\delta_{v}}{\hat{w}^{T}\hat{v}}-\frac{2\hat{w}^{T}\delta_{w}+\delta_{w}^{T}\delta_{w}}{\|\hat{w}\|^{2}}}{1+\frac{2\hat{w}^{T}\delta_{w}+\delta_{w}^{T}\delta_{w}}{\|\hat{w}\|^{2}}}.

Since
\|\delta_{w}\|\leq D_{k}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}
,
for small
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}
, it holds

\frac{2\hat{w}^{T}\delta_{w}+\delta_{w}^{T}\delta_{w}}{\hat{w}^{T}\hat{w}}\geq-\frac{1}{2}.

So we can get

\frac{|c^{*}_{k}-\hat{c}_{k}|}{2|\hat{c}_{k}|}\leq\left|\frac{\delta_{w}^{T}\hat{v}+\delta_{v}^{T}\hat{w}+\delta_{w}^{T}\delta_{v}}{\hat{w}^{T}\hat{v}}-\frac{2\hat{w}^{T}\delta_{w}+\delta_{w}^{T}\delta_{w}}{\|\hat{w}\|^{2}}\right|.

By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\frac{|c^{*}_{k}-\hat{c}_{k}|}{2|\hat{c}_{k}|}\leq\frac{\|\delta_{w}\|\|\hat{v}\|+\|\delta_{v}\|\|\hat{w}\|+\|\delta_{w}\|\|\delta_{v}\|}{|\hat{w}^{T}\hat{v}|}+\frac{2\|\hat{w}\|\|\delta_{w}\|+\|\delta_{w}\|^{2}}{\|\hat{w}\|^{2}}.

By the given assumption, there exists a constant
K_{k}>0
such that

|c^{*}_{k}-\hat{c}_{k}|\leq K_{k}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

Let
C_{2}\coloneqq\sqrt{n_{3}}\max_{k}K_{k}
, then

\|c^{*}-\hat{c}\|=\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}|c^{*}_{k}-\hat{c}_{k}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq C_{2}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

Next, we note that

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\|a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}-\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}\|\\
\leq&\|a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}-\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes c^{*}\|+\|\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes c^{*}-\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\otimes\hat{c}\|\\
=&\|a^{*}\otimes b^{*}-\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\|\|c^{*}\|+\|\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b}\|\|c^{*}-\hat{c}\|\\
\leq&C\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}\end{array}

for some constant
C>0
. So (
3.23
) holds.
∎

Summarizing the above, we propose the following algorithm to
get rank-
1
tensor completions.

Algorithm 3.6
.

For a partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}
, let
S\coloneq\emptyset
and do:

Step 1

For each
k=1,\ldots,n_{3}
, obtain the index set
\Omega_{k}
.
Then, formulate the set
\Phi
of biquadratic polynomials as in (
3.5
).

Step 2

Solve the biquadratic optimization (
3.6
)
for a set
U
of some minimizers.

Step 3

For each
(a^{*},b^{*})\in U
, if it is nonsingular,
then apply the formula (
3.21
)-(
3.22
) to get
c^{*}
,
and update
S\coloneq S\cup\{a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}\}
.

Step 4

Output the set
S
of rank-
1
completions of
\mathcal{A}
, and stop.

The major computational cost of Algorithm
3.6
is to solve the biquadratic optimization (
3.6
).
In the worst case, finding global minimizers for biquadratic optimization problems is NP-hard; see
[
32
, Theorem 2.2]
.
In Section
4
, we propose a convex relaxation method for solving (
3.6
).
For the partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
,
this method solves the semidefinite program (
4.4
) with PSD matrix variable of size
n_{1}n_{2}
-by-
n_{1}n_{2}
.
In our computational experiments, this relaxation often finds global minimizers of (
3.6
).
This is demonstrated in Section
4
.
Semidefinite programs can be solved efficiently
by interior-point methods. We refer to
[
46
]

for the complexity of solving semidefinite programs.

4.
The convex relaxation

In this section, we propose a convex relaxation method for
solving the biquadratic optimization (
3.6
).

Recall the index set
\Gamma
given in (
2.1
).
We observe the expansion

(aa^{T})\otimes(bb^{T})=\sum_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}a_{i}a_{j}b_{k}b_{l}\,E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl},

where
E_{ij},E_{kl}
are the basis matrices as in (
2.2
).
Recall that
\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma}
denotes the space of vectors whose entries are labelled by

(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma
and the linear symmetric matrix function
G[y]
in

y\in\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma}
is defined in (
2.4
):

G[y]\,=\,\sum_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}y_{ijkl}E_{ij}\otimes E_{kl}.

It is worthy to note that there exist vectors
a=(a_{i})
and
b=(b_{k})
such that

(4.1)

y_{ijkl}=a_{i}a_{j}b_{k}b_{l}\quad\text{for all}\quad(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma

if and only if
G[y]=(aa^{T})\otimes(bb^{T})
.
By the expansion of
(aa^{T})\otimes(bb^{T})
as in (
2.3
),
we can write
G[y]
in the block form

(4.2)

\begin{array}[]{cl}G\left[y\right]&=\begin{bmatrix}B_{11}&B_{12}&\cdots&B_{1n_{1}}\\
B_{21}&B_{22}&\cdots&B_{2n_{1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
B_{n_{1}1}&B_{n_{1}2}&\cdots&B_{n_{1}n_{1}}\end{bmatrix},\end{array}

where each block
B_{ij}
is also a
n_{2}
-by-
n_{2}
symmetric matrix.
Note that
G[y]
itself is symmetric.

We expand the biquadratic objective
f(a,b)
of (
3.6
) as

f(a,b)=\sum\limits_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}f_{ijkl}a_{i}a_{j}b_{k}b_{l}.

For
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma}
, we define the bilinear function

\langle f,y\rangle\,\coloneqq\,\sum\limits_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}f_{ijkl}y_{ijkl}.

If (
4.1
) holds for
\|a\|=\|b\|=1
, then
f(a,b)=\langle f,y\rangle
and

\mathrm{Trace}\,G[y]=\mathrm{Trace}\,(aa^{T})\otimes(bb^{T})=(a^{T}a)(b^{T}b)=1.

Therefore, the biquadratic optimization (
3.6
) is equivalent to

(4.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\langle f,y\rangle\\
\mathit{s.t.}&\mathrm{Trace}\,G[y]=1,\\
&G[y]=(aa^{T})\otimes(bb^{T})\succeq 0.\\
\end{array}\right.

If
G[y]=(aa^{T})\otimes(bb^{T})
is ignored,
then we get the convex relaxation

(4.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma}}&\langle f,y\rangle\\
\mathit{s.t.}&\mathrm{Trace}\,G[y]=1,\\
&G\left[y\right]\succeq 0.\\
\end{array}\right.

Its dual optimization problem is

(4.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\mbox{max}&\gamma\\
\mathit{s.t.}&f(a,b)-\gamma\cdot\left\|a\otimes b\right\|^{2}\in\mathcal{Q},\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{Q}
is the cone of sums of squares of bilinear forms in
(a,b)
:

\mathcal{Q}\,\coloneqq\,\left\{(a\otimes b)^{T}Y(a\otimes b):\,Y\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n_{1}n_{2}}\right\}.

The following is a basic property for the relaxation pair
(
4.4
)-(
4.5
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Denote by
f^{*},p^{*},d^{*}
the optimal values for (
3.6
),
(
4.4
) and (
4.5
), respectively.
Then, both
d^{*}
and
p^{*}
are attainable, and

d^{*}=p^{*}\leq f^{*}.

Proof.

Let
(a,b)
be a feasible point for (
3.6
),
and let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma}
be such that (
4.1
) holds.
Then
y
is feasible for (
4.3
),
so
y
must also be feasible for (
4.4
).
The minimum value of (
4.3
) is
f^{*}
, so
p^{*}\leq f^{*}

since (
4.4
) is a relaxation of (
4.3
).

Let
\tilde{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma}
be such that
\tilde{y}_{ijkl}=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}
if
i=j
and
k=l
, and
\tilde{y}_{ijkl}=0
otherwise.
Then we have

\mathrm{Trace}\,G\left[\tilde{y}\right]=1

and
G\left[\tilde{y}\right]\succ 0
, because

G\left[\tilde{y}\right]\,=\,\Big(\int_{\|a\|=1}aa^{T}\mathtt{d}\mu_{1}\Big)\otimes\Big(\int_{\|b\|=1}bb^{T}\mathtt{d}\mu_{2}\Big),

where
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
denote the uniformly distributed probability measure
on the unit spheres of
\|a\|=1
and
\|b\|=1
respectively.
So
\tilde{y}
is a strictly feasible point, then we know

d^{*}=p^{*}
and
d^{*}
is attainable,
by the strong duality theorem (see
[
36
, Sec. 1.5]
).

Next, we see that

\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}a_{i_{t}}b_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}a_{i_{s}}b_{j_{s}}=(a\otimes b)^{T}(\mathcal{A}_{i_{s}j_{s}k}e_{i_{t}}\otimes e_{j_{t}}-\mathcal{A}_{i_{t}j_{t}k}e_{i_{s}}\otimes e_{j_{s}}),

so there exists
B\in\mathcal{S}^{n_{1}n_{2}}
such that

f(a,b)=(a\otimes b)^{T}B(a\otimes b).

If
\tilde{\gamma}
is negatively big enough, then
B-\tilde{\gamma}I_{n_{1}n_{2}}\succ 0
and

f-\tilde{\gamma}\cdot\left\|a\otimes b\right\|^{2}=(a\otimes b)^{T}(B-\tilde{\gamma}I_{n_{1}n_{2}})(a\otimes b)^{T}\in\mathcal{Q}.

This implies that (
4.5
) is also strictly feasible,
so the optimal value
p^{*}
is attainable, by the strong duality theory.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

To solve the biquadratic optimization (
3.6
), one may consider to
apply the classical Moment-SOS relaxations. However, this is not computationally efficient.
Even for the lowest order (i.e., two), the Moment-SOS relaxation
has a positive semidefinite (PSD) matrix variable with length equal to
\binom{n_{1}+n_{2}+2}{2}
.
In contrast, the relaxation (
4.4
) has one PSD matrix variable
whose length is only
n_{1}n_{2}
, which is much smaller.

Suppose
y^{*}
is a minimizer of (
4.4
).
A specially interesting case is
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]=1
.
By Proposition
2.1
, there must exist a pair
(a^{*},b^{*})
such that

(4.6)

G[y^{*}]=(a^{*}{a^{*}}^{T})\otimes(b^{*}{b^{*}}^{T}),\quad\|a^{*}\|=\|b^{*}\|=1.

For this case, the relaxation (
4.4
) is tight
and the pair
(a^{*},b^{*})
must be a minimizer of (
3.6
).
This can be implied as a special case of Theorem
4.3
.
The above
a^{*},b^{*}
can be obtained as follows.
Write
G[y^{*}]
in the block form (
4.2
).
Find the indices
i^{*},j^{*}
such that the block

B_{i^{*}i^{*}}\neq 0
and the
j^{*}
th column of
B_{i^{*}i^{*}}
is nonzero.
Then the vectors
a^{*},b^{*}
can be selected as

(4.7)

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\tilde{a}&=&\big((B_{11})_{j^{*}j^{*}},\ldots,(B_{n_{1}1})_{j^{*}j^{*}}\big),\\
a^{*}&=&\tilde{a}/\|\tilde{a}\|,\\
\tilde{b}&=&\big((B_{i^{*}i^{*}})_{1j^{*}},\ldots,(B_{i^{*}i^{*}})_{n_{2}j^{*}}\big),\\
b^{*}&=&\tilde{b}/\|\tilde{b}\|.\end{array}}

They must satisfy (
4.6
) if
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]=1
.

As shown in Proposition
4.1
, both (
4.4
) and its dual problem (
4.5
) are strictly feasible.
Therefore, the semidefinite program (
4.4
) can be solved in
polynomial time by interior point methods.
We refer to
[
46
]
for this.
At the minimizer
y^{*}
, if
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]=1
, then one can recover the global minimizer of (
3.6
) by (
4.7
).
In our computational experiments, we often get a rank-
1
minimizer matrix
G[y^{*}]
.
This is empirically demonstrated in Example
5.6
.
For these cases, the biquadratic optimization is globally solved
by the relaxation (
4.4
).

For the case
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]>1
, the relaxation (
4.4
)
is also tight and we can get several minimizers of (
3.6
)
if the matrix
G[y^{*}]
is separable.
This will be discussed in the next subsection.

4.1.
The case
G[y^{*}]
is separable

Recall that the matrix
G[y^{*}]
is separable if there exist vectors

a^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
,
b^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
and scalars

\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
such that

(4.8)

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{c}G[y^{*}]=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(a^{(i)}{a^{(i)}}^{T})\otimes(b^{(i)}{b^{(i)}}^{T}),\\
\|a^{(i)}\|=\|b^{(i)}\|=1,\,\lambda_{i}>0,\,i=1,\ldots,r.\end{array}}

We refer to the subsection
2.1
for separable matrices.
The following shows how to get minimizers for (
3.6
) from
G[y^{*}]

when it is separable.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
y^{*}
is a minimizer of the relaxation (
4.4
).
If the decomposition (
4.8
) holds, then
each pair
(a^{(i)},\,b^{(i)})
is a minimizer of (
3.6
).

Proof.

Denote by
f^{*}
and
p^{*}
the optimal values of (
3.6
),
(
4.4
) respectively.
By (
4.8
) and the equality constraint in (
4.4
), we have

\displaystyle 1=\mathrm{Trace}\,G[y^{*}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{r}\lambda^{(s)}\mathrm{Trace}\,(a^{(s)}{a^{(s)}}^{T})\otimes(b^{(s)}{b^{(s)}}^{T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{r}\lambda_{s}\|a^{(s)}\|^{2}\|b^{(s)}\|^{2}.

Since each
\|a^{(s)}\|=\|b^{(s)}\|=1
, it holds

\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}=1.

Furthermore, all
(a^{(i)},b^{(i)})
are feasible for (
3.6
), so

f(a^{(s)},b^{(s)})\geq f^{*},\quad\sum\limits_{s=1}^{r}\lambda_{s}f(a^{(s)},b^{(s)})\geq\sum\limits_{s=1}^{r}\lambda_{s}f^{*}=f^{*}.

On the other hand, we also have

\begin{gathered}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{r}\lambda_{s}f(a^{(s)},b^{(s)})=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{r}\sum\limits_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}f_{ijkl}\lambda_{s}a^{(s)}_{i}a^{(s)}_{j}b^{(s)}_{k}b^{(s)}_{l}\\
=\sum\limits_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}f_{ijkl}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{r}\lambda_{s}a^{(s)}_{i}a^{(s)}_{j}b^{(s)}_{k}b^{(s)}_{l}=\sum\limits_{(i,j,k,l)\in\Gamma}f_{ijkl}y^{*}_{ijkl}=p^{*}.\end{gathered}

Since
p^{*}\leq f^{*}
by Proposition
4.1
, we get

\sum\limits_{s=1}^{r}\lambda_{s}(f(a^{(s)},b^{(s)})-f^{*})\leq 0.

All scalars
\lambda_{s}>0
, so it holds

f(a^{(i)},b^{(i)})=f^{*},\quad i=1,\ldots,r,

which implies that every
(a^{(i)},b^{(i)})
is a minimizer of (
3.6
).
∎

The matrix
G[y^{*}]
is always separable when
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]=1
.
But it may or may not be separable if
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]>1
.
As mentioned in Section
2.1
,
we can check if
G[y^{*}]
is separable or not by
solving a hierarchy of semidefinite relaxations.
When
G[y^{*}]
is not separable, that method can detect non-separability.
When
G[y^{*}]
is separable, this method can get the vectors

(a^{(i)},b^{(i)})
satisfying (
4.8
).
We refer to
[
37
]
for how to check separable matrices.

4.2.
The case that
G[y^{*}]
is not separable

When
G[y^{*}]
is not separable, how to get minimizers of (
3.6
)
becomes very difficult. A typical reason is that
the relaxation (
4.4
) may not be tight.
For this case, we may need to solve (
3.6
)
by higher order moment relaxation methods (see
[
36
, Chap. 6]
).

In this section, we show how to get an approximate optimizer pair

(a^{*},b^{*})
for (
3.6
) when
G[y^{*}]
is not separable.
Let
r=\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]
. We compute the spectral decomposition of
G[y^{*}]
as

(4.9)

G[y^{*}]=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}d^{(i)}p^{(i)}(p^{(i)})^{T},

where each
d^{(i)}
is a positive eigenvalue and

p^{(i)}
is the associated eigenvector.
Since
G[y^{*}]\succeq 0
, all
d^{(i)}>0
.
The eigenvectors
p^{(i)}
are orthonormal to each other.
For each
i=1,\ldots,r
, we select the vectors

(4.10)

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\tilde{a}^{(i)}&\coloneqq&[p_{1}^{(i)},p^{(i)}_{1+n_{2}},\cdots,p^{(i)}_{1+(n_{1}-1)n_{2}}]^{T},\\
\tilde{b}^{(i)}&\coloneqq&[p^{(i)}_{1},p^{(i)}_{2},\cdots,p^{(i)}_{n_{2}}]^{T},\\
a^{(i)}&\coloneqq&\tilde{a}^{(i)}/\|\tilde{a}^{(i)}\|,\\
b^{(i)}&\coloneqq&\tilde{b}^{(i)}/\|\tilde{b}^{(i)}\|.\end{array}}

Each above pair
(a^{(i)},b^{(i)})
is a feasible point for (
3.6
).
If
(a^{(i)},b^{(i)})
is nonsingular for (
3.19
),
we can solve a similar least squares problem and get the best vector
c^{(i)}
.
If it is singular, we can just let
c^{(i)}=0
.
After this is done, we can select
(a^{*},b^{*},c^{*})
to be the best one among them, i.e.,
select the triple
(a^{(i)},b^{(i)},c^{(i)})
with the minimum distance

\|\mathcal{A}-a^{(i)}\otimes b^{(i)}\otimes c^{(i)}\|_{\Omega}.

4.3.
A convex relaxation algorithm

Summarizing the above, we propose the following algorithm for
solving the biquadratic optimization (
3.6
).

Algorithm 4.4
.

For the partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}
, do the following:

Step 1

Solve the convex relaxation (
4.4
) for a minimizer
y^{*}
.

Step 2

If
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]=1
, compute
a^{*},b^{*}
as in (
4.7
),
then output
U=\{(a^{*},b^{*})\}
and stop.
If
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]>1
, go to Step 3.

Step 3

Apply Algorithm 4.2 in
[
37
]
to check if
G[y^{*}]
is separable not.
If it is, compute the decomposition (
4.8
),
then output
U\coloneq\{(a^{(i)},b^{(i)}):i=1,\ldots,r\}
.
If
G[y^{*}]
is not separable, go to Step 4.

Step 4

Compute the spectral decomposition (
4.9
) for
G[y^{*}]
.
Then, get vectors
a^{(i)},b^{(i)}
from (
4.10
) and output the set

U\coloneq\{(a^{(i)},b^{(i)}):i=1,\ldots,r\}
.

If the set
U
is output in Step 2 or Step 3,
then each point in
U
is a minimizer for (
3.6
).
If the set
U
is output from Step 4,
then each point in
U
is an approximate minimizer for (
3.6
).
We would like to remark that for some cases, the convex relaxation (
4.4
)
may not be able to return a global minimizer for the quadratic optimization (
3.6
).
This can be seen in Example
5.5
.
For such a case, we refer to the tight relaxation method in
[
36
, Chap. 6]

for how to solve (
3.6
).

5.
Numerical experiments

We present numerical experiments for solving rank-
1

tensor completion with noises.
Note that for (
4.4
), the dimension of
y
is
significantly larger than the length of
G[y]
(see Table
2

for some typical cases).
The convex relaxation (
4.4
) is solved
by the software
SDPNAL+

[
43
,
52
]

with its default settings of parameters.
When
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]=1
, the relaxation is tight
and we apply (
4.7
) to get the global minimizer of (
3.6
).
When
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]>1
, we use
GloptiPoly 3

[
19
]

and
SeDuMi

[
42
]
to check if it is separable or not.
The computations are implemented in MATLAB R2022b on a Lenovo
Laptop with CPU@2.10GHz and RAM 16.0G.
For a matrix in our numerical experiments,
its rank means the numerical rank, which is counted as the number of
singular values bigger than
10^{-6}
.
For neatness, all computed vectors/matrices are displayed with four decimal digits,
while the time consumption, errors and values of
\mathcal{A}

are displayed with two decimal digits.

For a partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
,
the density of observed entries is

(5.1)

\texttt{den}=|\Omega|/(n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}).

We call

the observation density for
\mathcal{A}
.
For the rank-
1
TCP (
1.3
), since we aim at finding rank-
1
tensors close to the observed tensor
\mathcal{A}
on the index set

,
we measure the quality of a rank-
1
completing tensor

by the absolute and relative errors

\texttt{err-abs}=\|\mathcal{A}-(a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*})\|_{\Omega},\quad\texttt{err-rel}=\frac{\texttt{err-abs}}{\|\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}}.

Moreover, we measure the difference between the computed completion
a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*}
and the noise-free (

) rank-
1
tensor

(when it is available) as

\texttt{err-nf}=\frac{\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}-(a^{*}\otimes b^{*}\otimes c^{*})\|}{\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\|}.

Applications in image recovery and low rank tensor completion

Tensor completion has important applications in image recovery.
A color image can be represented as an
n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}
tensor
\mathcal{A}
,
where
n_{1}\times n_{2}
is the resolution and
n_{3}=3
represents three channels of colors (red, green, and blue). Typically, the tensor
\mathcal{A}
has noises and missing entries, due to physical sensor defects, errors in data transmission, or corruption during storage.
On the other hand, image tensors are usually low rank since the pixels exhibit strong local smoothness and the color channels are highly correlated.
Thus, for the partially given noisy tensor
\mathcal{A}
with the index set of observations
\Omega
, one is usually interested in finding a low rank tensor
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
such that

(\mathcal{A}-\widehat{\mathcal{A}})_{ijk}\quad\mbox{is small for}\quad(i,j,k)\in\Omega.

Algorithm
3.6
mainly deals with rank-
1
tensor completions with noises.
It can also be generalized to find low-rank tensor completions.
This is particularly useful in recovering real-world images.
As shown in the recent work
[
11
]
,
one can obtain low rank tensor completions
by repeatedly computing rank-
1
completions.
We describe how to do this in the following.

For a partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

with the index set
\Omega
and a priori given rank
r
,
let
\mathcal{A}^{*}=0
be the all-zero tensor of the same dimension.
Initialize
\ell\coloneqq 0
,
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}:=\mathcal{A}
.
For
\ell<r
, do the following:

1.

Apply Algorithm
3.6
to find a rank-
1
completion
a^{(\ell)}\otimes b^{(\ell)}\otimes c^{(\ell)}
for
\mathcal{A}^{(\ell)}
.

2.

If
\widetilde{A}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{*}+a^{(\ell)}\otimes b^{(\ell)}\otimes c^{(\ell)}
gives a better completion (i.e., the error of
\widetilde{A}
is smaller than that of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
), then update

\mathcal{A}^{*}\coloneqq\widetilde{A},\quad\mathcal{A}^{(\ell+1)}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}^{(\ell)}-a^{(\ell)}\otimes b^{(\ell)}\otimes c^{(\ell)},

and let
\ell\coloneqq\ell+1
.
Otherwise, output
\mathcal{A}^{*}
as the low rank completion for
\mathcal{A}
and stop.

We apply the above procedure to complete real-world image tensors

\mathcal{A}
with
n_{1}=128
,
n_{2}=96
.
We consider the cases where the density
den
of observed entries,
given as in (
5.1
), is
0.5
and
0.7
.
Since the PSD variable in the semidefinite program (
4.4
) is
n_{1}n_{2}
-by-
n_{1}n_{2}
,
the computer is out of memory to implement Algorithm
3.6
directly.
Instead, we partition this image tensor into
8\times 6
smaller tensors,
each of size
16\times 16\times 3
, and apply our method on each one of them.
Moreover, since the total number of pixels
n_{1}n_{2}
is relatively large,
there may exist a pair
(i,j)
such that
(i,j,k)\notin\Omega
for all
k
.
For such a pair
(i,j)
, we heuristically let

\begin{gathered}\Theta\coloneqq\{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}):i-1\leq i^{\prime}\leq i+1,j-1\leq j^{\prime}\leq j+1\},\\
\mathcal{A}_{ijk}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\Omega_{k}\cap\Theta|}\sum_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\Omega_{k}\cap\Theta}\mathcal{A}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k}.\end{gathered}

Then, we add the above triples
(i,j,k)
to
\Omega
before doing tensor completions.
The recovered images are shown in Figure
1
.
For
{\tt den}=0.5
and
0.7
,
the column “observation” shows the image corresponds to the partially given tensor
\mathcal{A}
,
where the missing entries are highlighted in distinct colors.
For
r=1,2,3
, the images under “rank
\leq r
” are produced
by the above procedure with the maximal rank equal to
r
.
Finally, we remark that in our computation,
the minimizer matrices of (
4.4
) are all rank-
1
when
\ell=1
,
and are mostly rank-
1
when
\ell>1
.
For instance, there are
2
exceptions for
\ell=2
and one exception for

\ell=3
among
48
blocks when
{\tt den}=0.5
.
All exceptions occur when the error of the last iterate is small (e.g.,
{\tt err-rel}<0.0015
),
which means the remainder
\mathcal{A}^{(\ell-1)}

is small and computed completion in the last iterate is close to
\mathcal{A}
.

Conclusions

This paper studies the rank-
1
tensor completion problem for cubic order tensors
when there are noises for observed tensor entries.
First, we propose a robust optimization model for getting a rank-
1
tensor completion.
It is a biquadratic optimization problem with sphere constraints.
When the observed tensor is sufficiently close to a rank-
1
tensor,
we show that the optimizer of this biquadratic optimization
will give a close rank-
1
completing tensor.
Second, we give an efficient convex relaxation for solving this biquadratic optimization.
When the optimizer matrix
G[y^{*}]
is separable, we show
how to get optimizers for the biquadratic optimization.
In particular, if
\mbox{rank}\,G[y^{*}]=1
, then the optimizer matrix
G[y^{*}]
is always separable.
When
G[y^{*}]
is not separable, we apply its spectral decomposition to
obtain approximate optimizers. We refer to the tight relaxation method in

[
36
, Chap. 6]
for such cases.
Numerical experiments are presented to show the efficiency of
this biquadratic optimization model and the proposed convex relaxation.

There are interesting applications of rank one tensor completions,
which can be found in Section
5.6
.
An interesting direction for future research is to explore the applications in broader ranges of fields.
Moreover, there are some interesting questions for future work:

•

When all minimizers of (
3.6
) are singular,
how can we find rank-
1
tensor completions efficiently?

•

How can we efficiently find rank-
1
tensor completions
when the tensor order
m\geq 4
?
