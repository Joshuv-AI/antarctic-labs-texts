Efficient uncertainty aware human activity recognition on microcontrollers using hyperdimensional computing and conformal prediction
Authors: Ismail Lamaakal, Chaymae Yahyati, Yassine Maleh, Khalid El Makkaoui, Ibrahim Ouahbi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this paper, we tackle a core challenge for wearable human activity recognition (HAR), namely the recognition of daily-living and locomotion activities from inertial sensor windows: delivering reliable, interpretable uncertainty on tiny microcontrollers where latency, RAM, and energy are tightly constrained. Existing embedded approaches either calibrate softmax confidences, which are cheap but brittle under sensor placement or tempo shifts, or rely on generative or posterior-sampling schemes that exceed TinyML budgets. We propose hyperdimensional distance- and uncertainty-aware human activity recognition (HDUQ-HAR), an on-device hyperdimensional computing (HDC) framework that encodes each IMU window into a bipolar hypervector, classifies via prototype similarity, and derives three complementary, lightweight uncertainty signals from the same representation: (1) distance to the class prototype, (2) similarity gap to the runner-up, and (3) vote-dispersion capturing n-gram consensus. A label-conditional conformal layer converts these scores into set-valued predictions with finite-sample coverage guarantees and exposes a human-readable reason code indicating why uncertainty increased. Across UCI HAR, WISDM, PAMAP2, and OPPORTUNITY with subject-out splits and realistic shifts (orientation, gain, time-warp, missing axis, cross-placement), HDUQ-HAR achieves near-target coverage at 1-δ =0.90 with near-singleton sets on i.i.d. data (average size 1.18–1.25) and robust shift/OOD detection (AUROC 0.92–0.96), while running in 3–5 ms/window on Cortex-M4 with ∼6–9 KB RAM and ∼5–7 KB Flash. By unifying HDC geometry with label-conditional conformal prediction, our method shows that efficiency and reliability can co-exist in wearables, yielding small, calibrated sets that expand gracefully under shift and actionable explanations practitioners can trust.

Introduction

Wearable HAR1 has moved from lab prototypes to always-on companions embedded in watches, bands, and patches, where inference must run continuously on microcontrollers with tens of kilobytes of RAM and strict energy budgets2–4. In this paper, HAR refers specifically to the recognition of short inertial-sensor windows into daily-living and locomotion activities, rather than to generic video-based action recognition or open-world behavior understanding. Depending on the benchmark, the target labels include classes such as walking, sitting, standing, lying, stair ascent/descent, jogging or running, cycling, and related postural or movement categories. Clarifying this scope is important because the usefulness, ambiguity, and difficulty of uncertainty-aware prediction depend strongly on which activities are being recognized and how similar they appear under wearable sensing. In these safety- and care-adjacent settings, fall-risk monitoring, post-operative mobilization, rehabilitation adherence, accuracy alone is not enough: stakeholders need systems that know when they are unsure and can say so in a way that downstream logic or clinicians can act upon5,6. A wrong single-label decision can suppress an alert or trigger nuisance alarms; in contrast, a set-valued output that expands under ambiguity (e.g., {walk, stairs}) supports deferral, secondary sensing, or human-in-the-loop confirmation7,8. Delivering such calibrated uncertainty on-device is challenging because many successful uncertainty quantification (UQ) techniques, ensembles, Bayesian sampling, Monte-Carlo (MC) dropout, depend on multiple forward passes, wide dense heads, and floating-point arithmetic that violate TinyML constraints9. What tiny MCUs can do efficiently are bitwise operations, integer additions, and table lookups. The core question we address is how to turn those primitives into reliable, interpretable, and calibrated uncertainty for streaming IMU windows10,11.

Prior embedded HAR work largely falls into two camps, each with limitations when the data distribution shifts12 due to sensor placement, orientation, or tempo. The first camp relies on confidence calibration and lightweight heuristics (temperature scaling, isotonic/Platt maps, entropy or energy thresholds)13,14. These methods are inexpensive and can improve probabilistic confidence in-distribution, but their signals are inherently logit-local: when cadence changes or a device moves from hip to wrist, the logits themselves drift and coverage degrades unless prediction sets grow aggressively, eroding utility15,16. The second camp uses generative or reconstruction-based UQ (VAEs, flows, predictive coding)17, which is more sensitive to covariate shift but requires convolutional backbones, iterative inference, or high-precision layers that are impractical for microcontrollers. Importantly, to the best of our knowledge, no prior on-device HAR UQ exploits hyperdimensional distance geometry to produce three complementary uncertainty signals and then wraps them with finite-sample calibrated prediction sets18,19. Existing TinyML HAR either returns a single label with a heuristic confidence, rejects using ad-hoc thresholds without coverage guarantees, or offloads UQ to a phone/cloud20–22.

Part of the proposed solution represents each window as a high-dimensional bipolar hypervector and classify by similarity to per-class prototypes while extracting three lightweight, complementary uncertainty signals from the same representation. Concretely, we symbolize the window into piecewise-aggregate segments, bind tokens with n-grams to inject short-range dynamics, bundle into a single hypervector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V\in \{-1,+1\}^D$$\end{document}, and compare V to class prototypes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document} via XOR+popcount. From that geometry, we obtain: (1) distance to prototype, a normalized Hamming dissimilarity that measures how well the window fits the class manifold; (2) similarity gap, the margin between the top-1 and runner-up similarities, which captures decision ambiguity when classes collide (e.g., walk vs. stairs); and (3) vote-dispersion, the average absolute accumulator margin before the final sign, which rises early when evidence is inconsistent (transitions, low SNR). We then apply a label-conditional conformal layer that converts these class-specific nonconformity scores into set-valued outputs with finite-sample coverage guarantees. All components are MCU-native: binding is XOR, bundling is integer addition, similarity is XOR+popcount, and calibration parameters fit in a few bytes per class.

Related work

TinyML for HAR2 has largely coalesced around compact convolutional and temporal convolutional (TCN) backbones23,24 operating on short IMU windows (typically 1.5–3 s) with 50–100 Hz sampling25. Early embedded pipelines hand-crafted statistics (energy, entropy, tilt)26, but modern TinyML HAR favors learned features with depthwise-separable 1D CNNs, lightweight TCNs, or small residual stacks to capture local periodicity and cross-axis correlations27,28. Latency and energy are dominated by the first convolutional stage and the final dense layer; both benefit substantially from integer quantization. Post-training quantization to int829 is common but can degrade accuracy when activation ranges drift; quantization-aware training (QAT)30 with symmetric per-tensor scales and ReLU6/HardSwish activations typically preserves accuracy while enabling use of microcontroller kernels (e.g., CMSIS-NN, TFLM)2 and POPCOUNT-friendly binary ops in custom heads. On-device benchmarks report end-to-end latencies of 1–10 ms per window on Cortex-M4/M33 (64–120 MHz) with RAM footprints under 50 KB and Flash under a few hundred KB3. Memory-aware designs therefore rely on (i) depthwise separable convolutions, (ii) small receptive fields with dilation or TCN repeats to reach 1–3 s context, (iii) aggressive reuse of buffers (in-place ops, ping-pong ring buffers), and (iv) minimal checkpointing to reduce peak RAM2. Although transformers have reached time-series HAR31, even “tiny”attention tends to exceed TinyML budgets once key/value caches and layer norms are accounted for, and their uncertainty estimates revert to the same softmax-based signals discussed below32.

Uncertainty quantification for time series9 spans a broad spectrum of techniques with very different cost profiles. Confidence calibration methods13 such as temperature scaling, isotonic regression, and Platt scaling are attractive in TinyML because they are single-pass21,22, model-agnostic, and cheap to store (one scalar or a small lookup). They improve the mapping between logits and probabilities in-distribution, but they are not inherently sensitive to covariate shift, if placement changes, cadence warps, or an axis fails, the logits themselves move, so calibrated probabilities can still be confidently wrong33. Monte-Carlo dropout34 activates stochastic masks at inference and estimates uncertainty from the variance or entropy across T forward passes; it is effective for OOD detection when T is moderate-to-large, but on microcontrollers multiple passes multiply latency and energy, and memory must hold all activations repeatedly33. Deep ensembles35 are stronger still, but the Flash/RAM cost of M models is generally prohibitive. Bayesian last-layer or Laplace approximations36 reduce cost by placing a Gaussian posterior on the classifier head; they add matrix operations and storage for covariances, which are non-trivial in int8 pipelines and can erode speed gains. Evidential (Dirichlet) approaches37 replace softmax with a head predicting evidence parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _k$$\end{document}; they keep single-pass inference and provide an interpretable notion of total evidence, but still require a dense head and calibration to avoid overconfidence under shift. Energy-based scores and margin-based conformal predictors (probability-only)11 are simple and often effective, yet they operate on the same learned logits and thus inherit their brittleness if features drift. Generative and reconstruction-style UQ (autoencoders, flows, predictive coding)17 can capture “input likelihood” better and often flag OOD windows even when logits look confident; the downside is compute and memory, they imply large decoders, iterative refinement, or floating-point convolutions that do not fit TinyML envelopes.

Hyperdimensional computing (HDC)38 offers a different trade-off that aligns naturally with embedded constraints. In HDC, signals are projected or encoded into high-dimensional bipolar (or binary) vectors, and classification proceeds by comparing to class prototypes formed through bundling (majority). Binding operations (Hadamard/XOR) inject structure such as position or n-grams; similarity is computed with XOR+popcount for binary or Hamming distance for bipolar encodings39. Prior on-device HDC work has demonstrated keyword spotting, EMG/EEG intent recognition, and classical HAR classification with compelling speed/memory profiles: packed-bit representations reduce RAM, and operations are integer-only and cache-friendly40. Crucially, however, most of this literature reports point predictions with a confidence surrogate (similarity or distance), often with dataset-specific thresholds, and lacks finite-sample calibration39. Some studies combine HDC with a shallow learned head41 or use prototype adaptation for personalization, but a general, uncertainty-calibrated HDC pipeline for HAR on microcontrollers, especially one that exposes multiple complementary signals and provable coverage, has not been established. This missing piece is precisely what we target: distilling three uncertainty signals (prototype distance, similarity gap, vote-dispersion) from the same hypervector computation and converting them into calibrated sets.

Set-valued prediction via conformal prediction42 provides a model-agnostic route from arbitrary nonconformity scores to finite-sample coverage guarantees under exchangeability. In classification, common nonconformities include the negative of the predicted probability, the softmax margin, or energy; class-conditional variants tighten efficiency by calibrating per label. Conformal prediction’s appeal for deployment is that the inference-time computation is trivial once calibration is done: compare a score to a stored quantile, optionally one per class and per target coverage11. Despite this operational simplicity, the embedded community has only sparsely adopted conformal methods. The main reasons are (i) reliance on probability-based nonconformities that degrade when logits drift under shift, leading either to under-coverage or to large prediction sets, and (ii) the absence of clear hooks for interpretability at the device edge. Distance-geometry-based conformal methods exist in vision and representation learning, typically using Mahalanobis or kNN scores in deep feature spaces43; they demand feature memories or matrix operations that are ill-suited to TinyML and still require a learned backbone that dominates cost.

Our approach bridges these strands in a TinyML-friendly way (see Table 1). From TinyML HAR, we inherit windowed streaming, efficient depthwise/TCN backbones when desired, and int8 quantization; from UQ, we adopt the insistence on calibrated uncertainty and per-class calibration; from HDC, we take the packed-bit representation, XOR binding, and prototype bundling that make similarity computations cheap and robust; and from set-valued prediction, we use conformal thresholds to convert scores into guarantees. The novelty is twofold: first, extracting three complementary uncertainty signals natively from HDC (prototype distance for “fit,” similarity gap for “rivalry,” and vote-dispersion for “consensus”) rather than relying solely on probabilities; second, integrating these signals with a label-conditional conformal wrapper that runs entirely on-device, storing only a handful of per-class quantiles and robust scalers. Compared to probability-only conformal baselines, the geometry-aware nonconformity maintains efficiency (smaller average set size at the same coverage) because prototype distances remain informative under placement and time-warp shifts even when logits drift. Compared to HDC-only classifiers with ad-hoc thresholds, the conformal layer supplies finite-sample coverage guarantees and a principled way to expand sets near transitions or low-SNR segments. Finally, relative to distance-based conformalizations in deep feature spaces, our bitwise geometry eliminates the need for floating-point matrix ops or large feature memories, enabling deployment on Cortex-M-class devices with single-digit kilobytes of RAM devoted to uncertainty. Windowing, symbolization, and feature lift

Hypervector encoding (binding and bundling)

We encode each window into a D-dimensional hypervector by binding (role–value association via elementwise multiplication/XOR) the level, position, and optional trend/axis tokens for every segment, and then bundling (superposition via addition/majority) across segments (and cheap 2-grams). This preserves order while denoising, producing a similarity-ready bipolar representation for prototype matching in the next stage.

Two encoder regimes are considered: (a) random/static item memories, where level/position (and optional axis/trend) vectors are one-shot i.i.d. bipolar draws and remain fixed (zero training cost, strong invariances); and (b) task-adapted item memories, where small corrections to the level (and optionally channel) vectors are learned only on the training split with a prototype-aware contrastive loss and orthogonality regularization, while keeping the same binding/bundling algebra. We report both; task-adapted encodings typically yield tighter clusters without increasing inference complexity.

The goal of this stage is to convert the discrete symbol stream produced in Section "Windowing, symbolization, and feature lift" into a single, robust bipolar hypervector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V\in \{-1,+1\}^{D}$$\end{document} that summarizes a window while preserving position and short-range dynamics. We first assign a random bipolar hypervector to every token type and position: level symbols \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b\in \Sigma =\{0,\ldots ,A{-}1\}$$\end{document}, positions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t\in \{1,\ldots ,M\}$$\end{document}, and, when used, axis identifiers \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c\in \mathscr {C}$$\end{document} (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {C}=\{\text {x},\text {y},\text {z}\}$$\end{document}) and trend symbols \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \in \{\textrm{U},\textrm{F},\textrm{D}\}$$\end{document}. Each token is represented by a vector drawn once from a symmetric Rademacher distribution and fixed thereafter:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S_b,\;P_t,\;A_c,\;T_\tau \;\sim \; \text {i.i.d. Rademacher}^{D} \;\in \; \{-1,+1\}^{D}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_b$$\end{document} is the hypervector for level symbol b, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_t$$\end{document} is the hypervector for position t, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_c$$\end{document} is the hypervector for axis c, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\tau$$\end{document} is the hypervector for trend token τ; “Rademacher” means each component is independently +1 or -1 with probability 1/2. In high dimensions (D\ge 2048), independently sampled token vectors are nearly orthogonal in expectation, which makes their superpositions and comparisons well-behaved.

We then bind the tokens present at segment t using componentwise multiplication (Hadamard product) and later bundle across segments with dimension-wise majority. Binding plays the role of a lossless key–value association (symbol ↔ position, optionally axis and trend), while bundling aggregates evidence over time. For a single-window, single-axis stream, the bound vector at segment t is8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} v_t \;=\; S_{b_t} \odot P_t \odot T_{\tau _t}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_t\in \Sigma$$\end{document} is the quantized level at segment t, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_t$$\end{document} is its position vector, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\tau _t}$$\end{document} is the (optional) trend token for that segment; ⊙ denotes elementwise multiplication, which is equivalent to XOR in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{0,1\}$$\end{document} bit representation. When multiple axes are retained, we form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_t = S^{(x)}_{b^{(x)}_t}\odot A_{\text {x}}\odot P_t$$\end{document} and analogously for y,z, and either (i) add the three bound vectors into the same accumulator before the sign, or (ii) sum them first within each segment and then add to the accumulator.

To capture short-range dynamics beyond a single segment, we include lightweight n-grams by binding adjacent segments before bundling. The default uses 2-grams, which are cheap yet effective for human motion. The window hypervector is then the sign of two additive votes: one from the unigrams \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_t$$\end{document} and one from the pairwise products \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_t\odot v_{t+1}$$\end{document}:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} V \;=\; \textrm{sign}\!\left( \ \sum _{t=1}^{M} v_t \;+\; \sum _{t=1}^{M-1} \big ( v_t \odot v_{t+1} \big ) \ \right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sign}(\cdot )$$\end{document} is applied componentwise, returning +1 when the summed vote in a dimension is nonnegative and -1 otherwise; the first sum aggregates segment-local evidence, and the second sum adds a vote for each adjacent pair (2-gram). Extending to 3-grams is straightforward by adding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum _{t=1}^{M-2} (v_t \odot v_{t+1} \odot v_{t+2})$$\end{document} if budget allows; empirically, 2-grams capture most of the cadence and micro-transition patterns that distinguish activities.

To make the effect of bundling explicit, it is convenient to write an integer accumulator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u\in \mathbb {Z}^{D}$$\end{document} that collects votes before the sign. In code this avoids allocating M temporary vectors:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} u \;=\; \sum _{t=1}^{M} v_t \;+\; \lambda _2 \sum _{t=1}^{M-1} (v_t \odot v_{t+1}) \;+\; \lambda _3 \sum _{t=1}^{M-2} (v_t \odot v_{t+1} \odot v_{t+2}), \qquad V=\textrm{sign}(u). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _2,\lambda _3\in \{0,1\}$$\end{document} toggle the inclusion of 2- and 3-grams (we use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _2{=}1,\lambda _3{=}0$$\end{document} by default), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_d$$\end{document} is the signed vote margin at dimension d. The magnitude \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|u_d|$$\end{document} measures consensus: large values mean many agreeing tokens contributed the same sign in that dimension, which we later reuse as a cheap dispersion proxy. This single pass over segments yields V without dynamic allocation.

For TinyML deployment, it is helpful to spell out the bit-level representation and its cost. We store bipolar vectors in packed bits by mapping \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{-1,+1\}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{0,1\}$$\end{document} via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi (+1){=}1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi (-1){=}0$$\end{document}. In this representation, Hadamard binding x⊙ y becomes XOR of their bit patterns, and similarity later reduces to XOR+POPCOUNT. Equation (7) does not require shipping a large table: we seed a simple PRNG (e.g., xorshift) with a fixed 32-bit key per token type and generate bits on the fly, or precompute and store a few kilobytes of packed vectors. In either case, memory scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(A+M+|\mathscr {C}|+3)$$\end{document} hypervectors of length D, which for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D{=}4096$$\end{document} corresponds to 512 bytes per vector.

The computational cost of encoding a window is dominated by the vote accumulation and one sign pass. With M segments and dimensionality D, Eq. (10) performs O(MD) integer additions and XORs; the final sign is O(D). Concretely, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M{=}16$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D{=}4096$$\end{document}, and 2-grams enabled, we perform roughly 16D + 15D ≈ 31D vector operations (each an XOR or an ± 1 add), which runs comfortably in a few milliseconds on Cortex-M4/M33 parts at 64–80 MHz. RAM use is a single D-length integer accumulator (e.g., int16 is sufficient when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|u_d|\le M + (M{-}1)$$\end{document}), plus a packed output buffer V.

Finally, we note two statistical properties that motivate this design. First, independently drawn token vectors satisfy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {E}[\cos (S_{b},P_t)]=0$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Var}[\cos (S_{b},P_t)] = 1/D$$\end{document}, so the dot-product (and hence cosine/Hamming) between unrelated bindings concentrates tightly around zero; this makes chance alignments exceedingly unlikely as D grows. Second, bundling by majority produces a stable prototype when many independent votes are added: for any fixed class and typical window, the accumulator margins \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|u_d|$$\end{document} are larger, yielding a V that sits close (in Hamming/cosine) to its class prototype, whereas anomalous, transitional, or mis-placed windows induce smaller margins and a drift toward chance similarity.

Putting the pieces together, Eqs. (7)–(10) define a single-pass21,22, allocation-free encoder that (i) binds per-segment information to preserve order and optional trend/axis context, (ii) bundles across time to reduce noise, and (iii) outputs a compact, similarity-ready hypervector V. In practice, we use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D\in \{2048,4096\}$$\end{document}, include 2-grams (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _2{=}1$$\end{document}) but not 3-grams, seed the PRNG once per deployment for reproducibility, and tie-break \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sign}(0)$$\end{document} to +1 for determinism. Complexity is linear in M and D, and all arithmetic is integer (XOR/add/sign), which keeps latency and energy well within microcontroller budgets while preserving the discriminative patterns needed by the downstream prototype comparison and conformal uncertainty layer.

Prototypes and class dispersion

Once each window has been mapped to a bipolar hypervector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V\in \{-1,+1\}^{D}$$\end{document} (Section "Hypervector encoding (binding and bundling)"), we form a compact, noise-robust representation of each activity class by bundling all training encodings belonging to that class and taking the componentwise sign. This yields a single prototype per class that acts as a high-dimensional “centroid” under Hamming/cosine geometry and is exceptionally cheap to store and compare on microcontrollers. Formally, for class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\in \{1,\dots ,K\}$$\end{document},11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H_k \;=\; \textrm{sign}\!\left( \sum _{i:\;y^{(i)}=k} V^{(i)}\right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V^{(i)}$$\end{document} is the encoded hypervector of the i-th training window in class k, the sum is taken componentwise in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}^D$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sign}$$\end{document} is applied componentwise with a deterministic tie-break to +1 when a component sum is zero. In packed-bit form, each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document} occupies D bits (e.g., 512 bytes for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D{=}4096$$\end{document}), so even a dozen classes fit comfortably in a few kilobytes of Flash. This majority vote is the hyperdimensional analogue of a class mean, but crucially it remains bipolar, which preserves XOR/POPCOUNT efficiency for similarity.

To turn prototypes into calibrated per-class typicality references for uncertainty, we summarize how far in-class training windows lie from their prototype using robust statistics computed on a held-out calibration split. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_k=\{\,D^{-1}\textrm{Ham}(V^{(i)},H_k):y^{(i)}=k\,\}$$\end{document} be the set of normalized Hamming distances (i.e., fractions of disagreeing bits) from class-k windows to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document}. We record a location and a scale summary:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mu _k \;=\; \mathbb {E}\!\left[ \frac{1}{D}\,\textrm{Ham}(V,H_k)\,\bigg |\,y{=}k\right] , \qquad \sigma _k \;=\; \textrm{MAD}\!\left[ \frac{1}{D}\,\textrm{Ham}(V,H_k)\,\bigg |\,y{=}k\right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _k$$\end{document} is the class-conditional expected normalized Hamming distance (estimated as the sample mean of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_k$$\end{document}), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{MAD}[\cdot ]$$\end{document} denotes the median absolute deviation around the sample median of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_k$$\end{document}, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{MAD}(X_k)=\textrm{median}\{|x-\textrm{median}(X_k)|:x\in X_k\}$$\end{document}. We prefer MAD to standard deviation because it remains stable under mild class heterogeneity (e.g., gait style/tempo ranges) and occasional mislabeled windows; both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _k$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _k$$\end{document} are stored as fp16/fp32 scalars per class (tens of bytes total). In practice, we also keep the raw sample median \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_k=\textrm{median}(X_k)$$\end{document} (useful for percentile reporting), and we recompute \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mu _k,\sigma _k)$$\end{document} per dataset or per deployment, optionally with per-user adaptation if a short warm-up session is available (Section "Ablations and sensitivity").

Two practical details make these summaries MCU-friendly. First, normalized Hamming distances are computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Ham}(V,H_k)/D = \textrm{POPCOUNT}(V\oplus H_k)/D$$\end{document} using a handful of 32-bit XORs and popcounts over the packed bit blocks, avoiding any floating multiplies until the final division. Second, the calibration pass is offline; only the tiny \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mu _k,\sigma _k)$$\end{document} arrays ship to the device, where they serve as per-class anchors to scale distance-based nonconformity (Section "Inference: similarities, gaps, and vote-dispersion"). Since hyperdimensional distances between unrelated vectors concentrate sharply around 1/2 with variance ≈ 1/(4D), we typically observe \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _k\ll 1/2$$\end{document} for in-class windows and a clean margin to the out-of-class regime, especially when 2-grams are enabled in the encoder.

Inference: similarities, gaps, and vote-dispersion

At test time, a window is encoded once to V (Section "Hypervector encoding (binding and bundling)") and compared to all class prototypes with operations that reduce to XOR and POPCOUNT. We convert Hamming distance to a similarity score so that larger values indicate stronger class affinity and so that cosine and Hamming agree numerically for bipolar vectors. Specifically,13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{sim}_k \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{1}{D}\,\textrm{Ham}\!\big (V,H_k\big ) \;=\; \cos \!\big (V,H_k\big ). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Ham}(V,H_k)$$\end{document} counts bit disagreements (computed as a popcount of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V\oplus H_k$$\end{document}), the leading 1-· rescales distance into [0,1] similarity (1 for equality, ≈ 0.5 for random), and the equality to cosine holds because \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V,H_k\in \{-1,+1\}^D$$\end{document} imply \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\cos (V,H_k)=D^{-1}\sum _d V_d H_{k,d}=1-2\cdot D^{-1}\textrm{Ham}(V,H_k)$$\end{document}. We then identify the top-1 class and a gap that measures ambiguity:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} k^* \;=\; \arg \max _{k}\ \textrm{sim}_k, \qquad \Delta \;=\; \textrm{sim}_{k^*}\;-\;\max _{j\ne k^*}\textrm{sim}_j. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k^*$$\end{document} is the nearest prototype in Hamming/cosine sense and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \in [0,1]$$\end{document} is the margin between the best and second-best similarities; small Δ indicates competing hypotheses (typical at activity boundaries) or an out-of-distribution window whose encoding sits near chance for all classes.

Beyond distances and margins, the internal consensus of the bundling step, available for free from the encoder, carries valuable uncertainty information. Recall the integer accumulator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u\in \mathbb {Z}^D$$\end{document} that sums unigram and n-gram votes before the final \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sign}(\cdot )$$\end{document} (Eq. (10)). Dimensions where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|u_d|$$\end{document} is large reflect strong agreement among bound tokens; dimensions with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|u_d|\approx 0$$\end{document} reflect weak or conflicting evidence. We summarize this into a scalar vote-dispersion in [0,1] by averaging the normalized absolute margins and flipping the sense so that larger dispersion means lower consensus:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} m \;=\; \frac{1}{D}\sum _{d=1}^{D} \frac{|u_d|}{\max _t w_t}, \qquad u_{\text {disp}} \;=\; 1 - m. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_t$$\end{document} is the per-segment vote weight (unity for unigrams; add 1 for each included n-gram that touches segment t), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max _t w_t$$\end{document} is a conservative per-dimension normalizer that upper-bounds how many votes can land in any dimension (for unigrams+2-grams, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max _t w_t\le 3$$\end{document}), m is the mean normalized consensus across dimensions, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{\text {disp}}$$\end{document} increases toward 1 as consensus collapses. Intuitively, even if V happens to land close to some prototype by chance, a low-consensus accumulator (many near-zero margins) reveals that the encoder constructed V from a fragile or contradictory set of token votes, a hallmark of transitions, unsteady cadence, or placement shifts.

These three ingredients, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sim}_k$$\end{document}, Δ, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{\text {disp}}$$\end{document}, are computed with negligible overhead. Similarities require K XOR+POPCOU NT passes over the packed bits of V and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{H_k\}$$\end{document}; the gap is a pair of scalar ops; and vote-dispersion reuses the accumulator already in memory before the sign pass, adding only a vector absolute-value and a single running mean. On embedded targets, we implement POPCOUNT via compiler intrinsics or CMSIS-DSP; the division by D is a fixed reciprocal multiply; and the normalizer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max _t w_t$$\end{document} is a small constant known at compile time.

From a statistical perspective, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sim}_{k^\{*}$$\end{document} concentrates near 1 (distance ≪ 0.5) for typical in-class windows because each segment contributes aligned votes to dimensions where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{k^\{*}$$\end{document} already has the same sign, whereas \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max _{j\ne k^\{*}\textrm{sim}_j$$\end{document} clusters around 0.5 (distance ≈ 0.5) thanks to near-orthogonality between unrelated bindings. As windows cross activity boundaries or encounter sensor perturbations, these effects weaken: the accumulator margins shrink (raising \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{\text {disp}}$$\end{document}), prototype distances increase (lowering \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sim}_{k^\{*}$$\end{document}), and rival classes close the gap (shrinking Δ). In Section "Conformal prediction (calibrated sets)", we convert class-specific combinations of these signals into nonconformity scores and calibrated prediction sets; on device, we also expose a human-readable reason code (“distance-driven,” “margin-driven,” or “low-consensus”) by reporting which term dominated for the selected class.

Nonconformity scores (class-conditional)

We convert the geometry of the hypervector space and the internal vote consensus into nonconformity scores that are small for typical in-class windows and large for atypical, transitional, or out-of-distribution ones. Each score is defined per class k, because classes have different natural dispersion (e.g., walking exhibits richer variability than lying). The three primary, complementary pieces are: (i) a distance term that measures how far the encoded window V sits from class prototype \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document}; (ii) a gap term that penalizes ambiguity between the best and second-best classes; and (iii) a dispersion term that detects weak internal consensus in the bundling accumulator. When a tiny discriminative model (e.g., a 1D-CNN/TCN) is also available, we optionally add a margin term that captures the classifier’s own uncertainty. All terms are designed to be monotone with respect to “being unusual,” and all are easy to compute on microcontrollers.

Distance nonconformity We normalize the Hamming distance to the class prototype using per-class robust statistics \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mu _k,\sigma _k)$$\end{document} computed on a calibration split (Section "Prototypes and class dispersion"):16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} u^{\textrm{dist}}_k \;=\; \frac{\frac{1}{D}\,\textrm{Ham}\!\big (V,H_k\big )-\mu _k}{\sigma _k+\epsilon }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Ham}(V,H_k)$$\end{document} is the Hamming distance (POPCOUNT of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V\oplus H_k$$\end{document}), D is the hypervector dimensionality, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _k$$\end{document} is the class-k mean (or median) normalized distance, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _k$$\end{document} is a robust spread (MAD or IQR/1.349), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon \!\approx \!10^{-6}$$\end{document} prevents division by zero. Positive values indicate the window is farther than typical for class k; negative values occur for very prototypical windows. Using per-class scaling avoids unfairly penalizing inherently variable activities.

Gap nonconformity We penalize ambiguous hypotheses via the inverse of the similarity margin between the best class and its nearest rival:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} u^{\textrm{gap}} \;=\; \frac{1}{\Delta +\epsilon }, \qquad \Delta \;=\; \textrm{sim}_{k^\star } - \max _{j\ne k^\star }\textrm{sim}_j. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sim}_k=1-D^{-1}\textrm{Ham}(V,H_k)$$\end{document} is the cosine-equivalent similarity, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k^\star =\arg \max _k \textrm{sim}_k$$\end{document} is the top-1 class, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon \!\in \![10^{-6},10^{-3}]$$\end{document} stabilizes the inverse when Δ is tiny (exact ties may set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {=}0$$\end{document}). Large gaps (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \!\uparrow$$\end{document}) yield small penalties (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u^{\textrm{gap}}\!\downarrow$$\end{document}), and vice versa. This term is global (not class-conditioned) and captures decision ambiguity even when the absolute distance to prototypes is moderate.

Dispersion nonconformity We reuse the vote-dispersion scalar \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{\text {disp}}$$\end{document} defined in Eq. (15) as a label-free nonconformity score:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} A_{\textrm{disp}}(x) \;=\; u_{\text {disp}}(x). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}High consensus across dimensions (m → 1) implies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{\textrm{disp}} \rightarrow 0$$\end{document}; weak, contradictory voting (m → 0) implies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{\textrm{disp}} \rightarrow 1$$\end{document}. This term is sensitive to transitions and sensor perturbations even before prototype distances shift markedly.

Optional TinyCNN margin If a compact discriminative model is available and quantized on-device, we add a per-class margin that penalizes being outscored by competitors:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s^{\textrm{prob}}_k \;=\; \max _{j\ne k}\ g_j - g_k. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_k$$\end{document} are the model logits (pre-softmax) for classes k=1,… ,K. Larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^{\textrm{prob}}_k$$\end{document} means class k is less favored relative to some rival. This score is cheap to compute (one subtraction and a max per class) and can be dropped entirely if no classifier is present; the HDC-only pipeline remains valid.

Robust scaling and clipping Because the raw scales of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u^{\textrm{gap}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u^{\textrm{disp}}$$\end{document} differ from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u^{\textrm{dist}}_k$$\end{document}, we map each to a comparable range on a calibration split via robust, monotone transforms. We denote these by tildes:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{u}^{\textrm{dist}}_k =\frac{u^{\textrm{dist}}_k - \textrm{median}(\mathscr {U}^{\textrm{dist}}_k)}{\textrm{MAD}(\mathscr {U}^{\textrm{dist}}_k)+\epsilon }, \quad \tilde{u}^{\textrm{gap}} =\frac{u^{\textrm{gap}} - \textrm{median}(\mathscr {U}^{\textrm{gap}})}{\textrm{MAD}(\mathscr {U}^{\textrm{gap}})+\epsilon }, \quad \tilde{u}^{\textrm{disp}} =\frac{u^{\textrm{disp}} - \textrm{median}(\mathscr {U}^{\textrm{disp}})}{\textrm{MAD}(\mathscr {U}^{\textrm{disp}})+\epsilon }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {U}^{\textrm{dist}}_k=\{u^{\textrm{dist}}_k(W^{(i)}):y^{(i)}=k\}$$\end{document} collects per-class distance scores on the calibration set, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {U}^{\textrm{gap}},\mathscr {U}^{\textrm{disp}}$$\end{document} collect global gap/dispersion scores. On device, we optionally clip each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{u}$$\end{document} to a small range (e.g., [-5,5]) to bound dynamic range and improve fixed-point stability.

Fused nonconformity (class-conditioned) We combine the scaled terms with nonnegative weights tuned on calibration (grid search or small convex fit) to obtain a single score per class:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s_k \;=\; \alpha \,\tilde{u}^{\textrm{dist}}_k \;+\; \beta \,\tilde{u}^{\textrm{gap}} \;+\; \gamma \,\tilde{u}^{\textrm{disp}} \;\;(+\;\zeta \,\tilde{s}^{\textrm{prob}}_k\ ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha ,\beta ,\gamma ,\zeta \ge 0$$\end{document} are scalar weights (shared across classes or made class-specific if desired), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{s}^{\textrm{prob}}_k$$\end{document} is the robustly scaled version of Eq. (19) if a classifier is present. The fused score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_k$$\end{document} is class-conditional through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{u}^{\textrm{dist}}_k$$\end{document}, which accounts for different prototype spreads; gap and dispersion act as universal ambiguity and consensus correctives. Larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_k$$\end{document} means less conformity with class k.

Prototype stability under deployment drift In the present formulation, the class prototypes are learned offline from the training set and remain fixed during standard deployment. This is appropriate for the evaluation protocol used in this paper, where training, calibration, and testing are performed under a controlled benchmark setting. However, if the data distribution changes substantially over time due to new users, altered sensor placement, hardware aging, long-term behavioral drift, or other deployment effects, these fixed prototypes may become less representative and their discriminative utility may decrease. In such cases, the resulting similarity scores and conformal sets may become less efficient, and prediction quality may deteriorate. We therefore emphasize that the current method should be interpreted as a calibrated on-device framework under a fixed-training-distribution assumption. At the same time, an important practical advantage of the proposed hyperdimensional formulation is that the prototypes can, in principle, be refreshed or adapted incrementally from newly collected labeled data without retraining a deep backbone. Lightweight prototype adaptation and recalibration under distribution drift therefore represent important directions for future work.

Conformal prediction (calibrated sets)

Integration with the conformal layer. Our conformal calibration is strictly post-hoc to the HDC stage: the encoder (random or task-adapted item memories) and class prototypes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document} are frozen after training on the train split, and no gradients or updates flow back from the conformal layer. We use three disjoint splits: train to build \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document} (and, if enabled, to learn small corrections to item memories), calibration to estimate per-class distance anchors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mu _k,\sigma _k)$$\end{document}, fit robust rescalers for the distance/gap/dispersion terms, optionally set nonnegative fusion weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\alpha ,\beta ,\gamma ,\zeta )$$\end{document}, and compute label-conditional thresholds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_k(1-\delta )$$\end{document}, and test only for final reporting. At inference, HDC emits the packed hypervector V, prototype similarities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sim}_k$$\end{document} (cosine \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$=1-\text {normalized Hamming}$$\end{document}), and the accumulator u; the conformal layer consumes these to form distance, gap, and vote-dispersion scores, applies the stored rescalers and weights to obtain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_k$$\end{document}, and returns the set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma (W)=\{k:\ s_k\le q_k(1-\delta )\}$$\end{document}. This one-way coupling (HDC → conformal) preserves split-conformal coverage guarantees, prevents calibration leakage, and keeps the MCU implementation simple (only small constants, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mu _k,\sigma _k)$$\end{document}, scaler coefficients, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\alpha ,\beta ,\gamma ,\zeta )$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_k$$\end{document}, ship to device). For retargeting to a new user/domain, only the calibration phase is re-run to refresh these constants; prototypes and encoder remain unchanged unless explicit retraining on the train split is desired.

Given the fused, class-conditional nonconformity scores \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_k(W)$$\end{document} defined in Section "Nonconformity scores (class-conditional)", we convert them into set-valued predictions with finite-sample coverage using a split conformal procedure that is label-conditional (often called “Mondrian”) so that each class calibrates to its own variability. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{cal}}$$\end{document} be a calibration split disjoint from training. For every class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\in \{1,\ldots ,K\}$$\end{document}, we evaluate the fused score on all calibration windows with label k and collect the resulting multiset22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {S}_k \;=\; \big \{\, s_k\!\big (W^{(i)}\big )\ :\ y^{(i)}=k,\ (W^{(i)},y^{(i)})\in \mathscr {D}_{\textrm{cal}} \,\big \}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_k(\cdot )$$\end{document} is the fused nonconformity score for class k (Eq. (21)), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {S}_k$$\end{document} contains \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\mathscr {S}_k|$$\end{document} real values, one per calibration window of class k. The label-conditional construction ensures classes with broader intrinsic dispersion (e.g., walking) get wider admissible ranges than compact classes (e.g., lying), which improves efficiency of the final sets.

The fused score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_k$$\end{document} in Eq. (21) should be interpreted as a practical deployment score rather than as a probabilistic quantity with a standalone theoretical meaning. Its role is to combine several complementary indicators of atypicality, prototype distance, similarity gap, vote-dispersion, and, when available, a classifier margin into a single scalar that is more useful for set construction than any individual term alone. We agree that the fusion weights are not derived from a probabilistic generative model and therefore should not be interpreted as having a universal optimality guarantee. For this reason, we tune them only on the calibration split, keep the search space deliberately small, and evaluate the final model on a disjoint test split. Importantly, the formal coverage guarantee of the conformal layer does not depend on the fused score being probabilistically grounded; it only requires that the same nonconformity function be used consistently on the calibration and test samples under the standard exchangeability assumption. In other words, the fused score is heuristic in construction, but the validity of the resulting conformal prediction sets remains formally supported by conformal prediction theory. We therefore view \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_k$$\end{document} as an engineering choice that trades a small amount of score-level interpretability for improved efficiency and robustness, while preserving finite-sample calibration of the final prediction sets. To retain interpretability, we also expose the dominant contributing term as a reason code, so that practitioners can distinguish whether uncertainty was driven mainly by prototype distance, class ambiguity, or weak internal consensus.

For a user-chosen miscoverage level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta \in (0,1)$$\end{document} (e.g., δ =0.1 for 90% coverage), we compute a classwise threshold as a finite-sample order statistic:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} q_k(1-\delta ) \;=\; \text {the } \Big \lceil \big (|\mathscr {S}_k|+1\big )\,(1-\delta )\Big \rceil \text {-th smallest element of } \mathscr {S}_k. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the ceiling \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lceil \cdot \rceil$$\end{document} implements the standard finite-sample correction that guarantees marginal coverage at least 1-δ under exchangeability of calibration and test examples (ties are broken conservatively in favor of inclusion). In practice, we precompute and store \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_k$$\end{document} for the few δ values of interest (e.g., 0.2, 0.1, 0.05) as fp16/fp32 scalars; this costs only K× (number of deltas) floats in Flash.

Because duty cycles are short and strictly sequential, energy per window is small (milliseconds of active current) and typically negligible compared to radio use; we report measured mJ/min on target hardware.

Experimental setup

We describe our evaluation protocol, datasets and subject-out splits, synthetic and real-world shifts, baseline comparators, coverage/size and OOD metrics, transition analyses, and MCU-level profiling.

Datasets and splits

We evaluate on four established HAR corpora and deliberately harmonize sensor rates and preprocessing to isolate the contribution of the proposed uncertainty machinery rather than dataset idiosyncrasies. Concretely, we use UCI HAR45 (smartphone IMU), WISDM46 (smartphone/wearable accelerometry), PAMAP247 (multi-sensor body-worn IMUs), and OPPORTUNITY48 (dense wearable/ambient sensors). We extract only the inertial streams needed for TinyML deployment (accelerometer magnitude or three accelerometer axes, optionally gyroscope if present), resample to a common \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${50.00}\,\hbox {Hz}$$\end{document}, and segment into windows of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta ={2.56}\,\hbox {s}$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L=\lfloor 50\times 2.56\rfloor =128$$\end{document} samples) with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${50.00}\,{\%}$$\end{document} overlap (S=L/2=64). Unless otherwise noted, we perform per-window z-score and unit-energy normalization (Section "Ablations and sensitivity"), PAA to M=16 segments, and level quantization with alphabet size A=7.

Division standard and leakage control. Across all corpora we adopt a subject-out protocol to measure generalization under distribution shift: subjects are partitioned into non-overlapping sets for training, calibration, and testing. All windows from a given subject appear in exactly one split, preventing train–test leakage via overlapping time windows or person-specific motion signatures. The calibration split is not used to learn the encoder or prototypes; it is held exclusively for computing conformal thresholds and for robust scaling of nonconformity components.

Why a 60/20/20 split? We use a 60/20/20 split by subject count (stratified when feasible) for three reasons. (i) Model capacity vs. subject diversity: 60% of subjects in training yields sufficient inter-subject variability to learn person-agnostic representations without overfitting to a few individuals. (ii) Conformal calibration needs: conformal methods require a dedicated set that reflects deployment conditions; allocating 20% to calibration provides enough samples per class to estimate label-conditional thresholds with low variance, while remaining entirely disjoint from training and test. (iii) Test power and reporting: 20% of subjects in the test split provides adequate per-class counts to report classwise metrics and confidence intervals without collapsing rare activities.

How splits are formed and repeated. Unless otherwise specified, splits are created at the subject level using a fixed random seed with stratification by class presence when feasible (to preserve label balance across splits). To mitigate partition sensitivity and increase reliability, we repeat the subject partitioning \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R{=}3$$\end{document} times with different seeds; we train and evaluate independently on each repetition and report the mean (and, where space permits, the 95% confidence interval). We release the exact subject-ID lists for each repetition and split for full reproducibility. This procedure reduces bias from any particular subject allocation while keeping the calibration/test disjointness strictly enforced.

Data access, ethics, and preprocessing protocol. All four datasets were obtained from their official public releases and used strictly under their original research-use terms. No new data were collected; no attempt at subject re-identification was made; all datasets are de-identified by their providers. We confirm that our use complies with the original licenses/terms of the respective maintainers.

Raw vs. preprocessed access. When both raw continuous streams and pre-windowed signals were available, we used the rawest available inertial time series (accelerometer ± gyroscope) and re-windowed them to our harmonized scheme (Section "Datasets and splits"). When a dataset only distributes segmented windows (e.g., author-provided fixed-length “Inertial Signals”), we start from those arrays but re-run our full normalization, featureization, and calibration pipeline so that downstream processing is identical across corpora.

Uniform scripts across datasets. To ensure fairness, we apply a single preprocessing pipeline to every corpus: resampling to 50.00 Hz , segmentation to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L{=}128$$\end{document} with stride \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S{=}64$$\end{document} , per-window z-score and unit-energy normalization, PAA to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M{=}16$$\end{document} , and level quantization with alphabet size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A{=}7$$\end{document} (details in Sections "Datasets and splits" and "Windowing, symbolization, and feature lift"). The same code path is executed for all datasets; the only dataset-specific configuration is a short YAML mapping that selects which channels to load (e.g., acc_xyz vs. acc_mag, optional gyro_xyz). No dataset receives bespoke feature engineering or special-case hyperparameters.

Data cleaning and filtering. Prior to harmonization we (i) inspect timestamps and, where a stream is nominally uniform but exhibits short gaps, perform linear interpolation for missing spans up to 200.00 ms; windows with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>\!10\%$$\end{document} missing samples in any channel are discarded; (ii) remove per-window means (already implied by z-scoring) to suppress sensor bias; (iii) apply a light Hampel outlier filter (window 7 samples) to attenuate isolated spikes without altering spectral content; and (iv) clip extreme values to the 0.1–99.9 percentile range computed on the training partition (the same clip limits are then used for calibration and test). We do not apply aggressive denoising, gravity compensation, or signal stabilization beyond these minimal steps, in order to keep deployment-time assumptions realistic for TinyML.

Axis handling and normalization choices. For phone and body-worn datasets, we retain the device-frame axes as recorded (no rotation to a world frame), reflecting deployment scenarios where attitude estimates are unavailable. To reduce orientation sensitivity when required by the experiment, we either (a) use the magnitude of acceleration as a single channel, or (b) use three-axis acceleration (and gyroscope when available) with per-window z-score + unit-energy normalization applied independently per channel. For multi-placement corpora (PAMAP2, OPPORTUNITY), we restrict each experiment to one IMU placement (wrist or hip) by default; when a dataset includes known sensor-axis sign conventions, we adopt the maintainers’ documented axis order without additional alignment. These choices are held constant across datasets so as not to inadvertently advantage any particular corpus, and we discuss their effect on calibration/uncertainty in Section "Shifts and OOD scenarios".

Reproducibility. We release the exact preprocessing scripts and dataset configuration files used to: (1) load channels; (2) resample and segment; (3) clean/interpolate/discard windows; and (4) normalize, PAA, and quantize. Alongside the subject-out splits, we provide per-repetition subject-ID lists and the activity label sets retained for each corpus.

Metrics

We report both calibration/coverage metrics and robustness/efficiency metrics.

Conformal coverage and average set size. For target coverage levels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-\delta \in \{0.8,0.9,0.95\}$$\end{document} we compute the empirical marginal coverage30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \widehat{\textrm{Cov}}(1-\delta ) \;=\; \frac{1}{N_{\text {test}}}\sum _{i=1}^{N_{\text {test}}} \textbf{1}\!\left\{ y^{(i)} \in \Gamma \!\big (W^{(i)}\big )\right\} , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where Γ (W) is the prediction set (Section "Conformal prediction (calibrated sets)") and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {test}}$$\end{document} is the number of test windows. We also report the average set size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{|\Gamma |}=\frac{1}{N_{\text {test}}}\sum _i |\Gamma (W^{(i)})|$$\end{document}, a key efficiency indicator.

OOD detection (AUROC). For each shift and overall (mixed shifts), we treat windows from held-out activities/perturbations as positives and compute AUROC using a scalar uncertainty score. For HDUQ-HAR the default score is the fused nonconformity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{k^\star }$$\end{document} of the predicted top class (or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max _k s_k$$\end{document} for a conservative signal). For probability baselines, we use predictive entropy or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-\max _k p_k$$\end{document}.

Transition robustness. To quantify responsiveness near true activity boundaries, we align uncertainty trajectories to the annotated transitions and compute the proportion of transitions that trigger an uncertainty spike within ± 2 windows (lead/lag). A spike is declared when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{k^\star }$$\end{document} (or entropy) exceeds the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90^{\text {th}}$$\end{document} percentile of in-class values for at least one of the adjacent activities; we report precision/recall/F1 of spike detection and the mean temporal offset (in windows).

On-device performance. We measure on-MCU latency as the median over 10k windows, with p5/p95 to capture jitter; Flash and RAM are taken from the ELF map after link-time optimization; energy is measured as mJ/min using a shunt-based power monitor (e.g., INA219) sampling at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge {1}\,\hbox {kHz}$$\end{document} and integrating over the active compute duty cycle. We report mean ± st.dev across three runs per configuration.

Results

We report results across four public HAR corpora with harmonized preprocessing (Section "Datasets and splits") and a strict subject-out protocol. Unless stated otherwise, the conformal coverage target is 1-δ =0.90, the HDC encoder uses (D,M,A)=(4096,16,7) with 2-grams, and the hybrid variant (when used) adds a łe 100k-parameter TinyCNN for the optional margin term. All values are averaged across three random subject partitions; we include 95% CIs where space permits. Qualitatively, the proposed HDUQ-HAR maintains near-target coverage with small set sizes on in-distribution (i.i.d.) data and inflates sets under realistic test-time shifts, while providing competitive OOD detection and clear uncertainty spikes at activity transitions. On-device, the entire pipeline runs in a few milliseconds per window with single-digit kilobyte RAM.

Transition behavior

We align uncertainty trajectories to annotated activity boundaries and look for “spikes” within ± 2 windows. HDUQ-HAR achieves a boundary F1 of 0.78 (precision 0.81, recall 0.76) on WISDM, with a mean lead of -0.4 windows (i.e., it anticipates the label change by ≈ 0.5 hop). Probability baselines either miss short transitions (recall <0.60) or flag too many (precision <0.65). On-device performance

Benchmarks were run on two fixed testbeds with identical firmware builds. Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense (Nordic nRF52840, Cortex— M4F @64 MHz) provides 256 KB RAM and 1 MB Flash; STM32L476 Nucleo (ST STM32L476, Cortex— M4F @80 MHz) provides 128 KB RAM and 1 MB Flash. Both devices ran bare-metal loops with interrupts/BLE disabled during timing, single-precision FPU enabled, and no RTOS. We compiled with arm-none-eabi-gcc (GCC 10/12), using-O3 -ffast-math -flto and CMSIS— DSP intrinsics for POPCOUNT; fixed-point paths used Q15/Q31 where noted. Latency was measured via cycle counters/timers over 10,000 windows (median and p5/p95 reported); power/thermal were at room conditions, USB-powered, and caches/warm-start effects amortized by a 1000-window warmup. Additional stronger baselines: feature-space, ensemble, and non-HDC conformal methods

Here, we added a broader set of stronger uncertainty baselines that are widely used in compact time-series classification. All methods are evaluated under the same preprocessing pipeline, the same subject-out protocol, and the same target coverage level 1-δ =0.90. In addition to the previously reported baselines, we now include three families of stronger competitors: feature-space distance methods, lightweight ensembles, and non-HDC conformal methods on learned embeddings. This expanded comparison is intended to isolate whether the gains of HDUQ-HAR come only from using conformal prediction, or more specifically from the combination of HDC geometry, lightweight uncertainty signals, and label-conditional conformal calibration.

Feature-space distance baselines We first consider uncertainty methods based on distances in a learned embedding space. The Mahalanobis baseline uses a lightweight temporal encoder to produce embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z\in \mathbb {R}^{d_e}$$\end{document}, fits a class-conditional Gaussian approximation on the training set, and uses the minimum classwise Mahalanobis distance as a confidence signal. We also consider a kNN-based UQ baseline in which uncertainty is derived from the distance to the nearest neighbors of the predicted class in the embedding space. To make these methods comparable to the proposed set-valued output, both are wrapped with a label-conditional conformal layer so that coverage is calibrated on the same calibration split.

Lightweight ensemble baseline We next consider a lightweight ensemble consisting of three independently initialized tiny temporal encoders with matched total capacity per member. The predictive distribution is obtained by averaging member posteriors, and uncertainty is quantified by predictive entropy and ensemble disagreement. A conformal wrapper is again applied on the calibration split to convert these scores into set-valued predictions. This baseline represents a stronger but more resource-intensive alternative to single-model calibration.

Non-HDC conformal embedding baseline Finally, we consider a non-HDC conformal embedding baseline in which a small temporal encoder is trained to produce embeddings and a standard linear classifier is used for class prediction. Uncertainty is then derived from the margin or distance in the learned embedding space, followed by class-conditional conformal calibration. Table 8 provides three main observations. First, the added baselines confirm that the original comparison was not merely against weak competitors: methods such as Mahalanobis conformal, kNN conformal, lightweight ensembles, and non-HDC embedding conformal are all substantially stronger than simple temperature scaling or standalone evidential confidence. Second, several of these stronger baselines achieve competitive coverage, and the lightweight ensemble baseline in particular slightly exceeds HDUQ-HAR in raw i.i.d. coverage. However, this comes at a much higher latency and memory cost, which is a major drawback in the intended TinyML deployment regime. Third, the proposed HDUQ-HAR framework provides the best overall trade-off: it achieves the smallest average prediction sets, the strongest shift coverage among single-model baselines, and the highest OOD AUROC, while remaining markedly more efficient than ensemble- and memory-based alternatives.

Partial-shift and mixed-shift ablation

To clarify the effect of distribution shift more precisely, we complement the main shift/OOD evaluation with two additional protocols. In the first, we apply each shift scenario to only a subset of test windows, while leaving the remaining windows unchanged. This setting is important because, in realistic wearable deployment, perturbations such as orientation changes, gain mismatch, missing axis, or cross-placement effects may affect only part of a stream rather than the entire test set. In the second protocol, we evaluate mixed-shift conditions, where multiple shift types are present simultaneously within the same test set. This better reflects practical operation, where different users or sessions may exhibit different perturbation modes at the same time.

Tables 9 and 10 show two important patterns. First, when only a subset of test samples is affected, the degradation is gradual rather than abrupt: as the perturbed fraction increases, prediction sets expand smoothly, top-1 accuracy decreases, and OOD separability improves because the detector sees a clearer mixture of clean and corrupted windows. This indicates that the proposed conformalized HDC framework does not fail catastrophically when shift is localized, but instead reacts by widening prediction sets in proportion to the contamination level. Second, mixed-shift conditions are consistently harder than single-shift conditions at the same affected fraction, since the detector must accommodate several perturbation modes simultaneously. Nevertheless, the coverage remains close to the target level and the OOD AUROC stays high, which suggests that the method remains effective even when different shift mechanisms coexist within the same deployment stream.

Overall, this ablation clarifies that the main shift results in the paper should be interpreted as a stress-test regime in which all test samples are perturbed by one scenario at a time, while more realistic partial- and mixed-shift settings lead to smoother but still well-controlled degradation. This strengthens the practical relevance of the evaluation and shows that the method can handle both localized and heterogeneous shift patterns.

Discussion

Why HDC helps Hyperdimensional computing endows the pipeline with a geometry that is both robust and frugal, exactly what TinyML HAR needs. The first pillar is distance concentration: in a bipolar space of dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D\!\gg \!1$$\end{document}, unrelated hypervectors are almost surely near-orthogonal, so their normalized Hamming distance concentrates tightly around 0.5. When we form class prototypes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document} by bundled majority vote, in-class windows map to hypervectors V that deviate systematically from that 0.5 baseline, yielding distances ≪ 0.5 and thus large cosine/bit-similarities. Unrelated classes, by contrast, remain clustered near the orthogonality floor. This simple fact produces robust margins without delicate feature engineering: the top-class similarity is stably high while runner-ups hover near chance, which directly sharpens the gap term and reduces spurious ties under benign perturbations (minor orientation changes, modest gain drift). The second pillar is n-gram binding for local dynamics. Unigram tokens represent coarse segment means; binding adjacent tokens (2-g, optionally 3-grams) injects short-range temporal context that disambiguates activities with similar static statistics but different cadence and phase, e.g., “walk” versus “stairs.” Crucially, binding is a Hadamard/XOR on bits, so the cost scales linearly and runs cache-friendly on microcontrollers while still imprinting enough order to stabilize decisions when tempo shifts within a window. The third pillar is dispersion from the accumulator u. Before the final sign, each dimension carries a signed vote margin; near boundaries or under sensor instability, votes cancel and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|u_d|$$\end{document} shrinks across many dimensions. Aggregating these margins into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{\text {disp}}$$\end{document} yields an early, model-internal warning that ambiguity is rising, often before distances fully degrade or competitors close the similarity gap. Together, concentrated distances, dynamic binding, and dispersion give three complementary signals, distance (fit to prototype), gap (decision ambiguity), and consensus (vote strength), that the conformal layer can calibrate into reliable prediction sets.

Interpretability: bit-vote margins, gaps, and reason codes Because the primitives are algebraic (XOR, POPCOUNT, sums), explanations are direct and device-friendly. First, bit-vote margins: plotting the histogram of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|u_d|$$\end{document} (or its normalized mean m) reveals whether the encoder saw a coherent pattern (high margins) or fragmented evidence (low margins). Practitioners quickly learn to associate margin dips with activity transitions, loose straps, or cross-placement shifts. Second, similarity gaps: visualizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{sim}_{k^*}-\max _{j\ne k^*}\textrm{sim}_j$$\end{document} cleanly distinguishes “rival-class” uncertainty (small gap, two plausible labels) from “mismatch” uncertainty (large gap but poor absolute similarity, often an OOD activity). On-device, we expose a single reason code, distance-driven, margin-driven, or consensus-driven, by reporting which component dominated the fused nonconformity of the selected class. These reason codes map to simple remediations: distance-driven alerts suggest posture/placement changes or unseen activities; margin-driven alerts suggest transient ambiguity (wait one hop); low-consensus alerts point to sensing quality (re-seat the band, check axis integrity). The key is that none of this requires storing raw signals or heavy saliency maps: one can explain a decision using a few integers and POPCOUNTs.

When it may fail Two regimes remain challenging. (1) Highly irregular motions with weak symbolization. If per-window z-score and energy normalization followed by PAA yield unstable segment means (free-form gestures, tremor-like micro-movements), the quantized alphabet may “chatter” and n-gram structure becomes noisy. Larger alphabets or 3-grams can recover information but also amplify volatility and cost. In such cases, augmenting the token set with a simple trend symbol (sign of first difference) or fusing a tiny CNN margin helps stabilize the gap without abandoning TinyML constraints. (2) Extremely low SNR or hard faults. Under heavy noise or persistent axis loss, dispersion skyrockets while all class similarities sag toward chance; conformal sets inflate, as they should, but utility drops. Robustness improves with median filtering across subwindows, axis-dropout training, and a fault-aware calibration protocol that includes synthetic missing-axis examples.

Limitations and ethics

Temporal dependence and exchangeability Conformal prediction guarantees rely on exchangeability between calibration and test samples. Windowed HAR streams, however, exhibit strong temporal dependence and distribution drift (e.g., changing cadence, mounting shift). We mitigate this mismatch by (1) using a subject-out protocol (calibration and test drawn from disjoint users), (2) reserving a contiguous calibration split drawn from the same acquisition protocol as test, and (3) reporting empirical coverage at several targets (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-\delta \in \{0.80,0.85,0.90,0.95\}$$\end{document}) in i.i.d. and shifted settings rather than relying on nominal guarantees alone. Nonetheless, coverage can dip below target during rapid regime changes (e.g., immediate post-transition windows) or persistent covariate shift (cross-placement). Practitioners should interpret the reported coverage estimates as data- and protocol-conditional, and consider recalibration after deployment if usage patterns differ.

Dataset biases, user variability, and outliers Public HAR corpora are skewed toward healthy adults and scripted activities in semi-controlled environments. This induces biases that limit external validity to populations with different gait, mobility impairments, or device-wearing habits. Our label-conditional calibration reduces but does not eliminate such effects: if a class’s variability is broader in the field than in the calibration set, prediction sets may under-cover. We partly address this by (2) testing under multiple perturbations (orientation, gain, time-warp, missing axis, cross-placement), (2) exposing reason codes to flag low-consensus or distance-driven anomalies, and (3) recommending optional per-user prototype adaptation under supervision. Still, extreme outliers, free-form movements, sensor slips, or environmental shocks, can produce large dispersion and inflated sets with limited semantic specificity. Field deployments should pair uncertainty spikes with simple user feedback (e.g., “adjust band,” “hold still,” or “activity unknown”) rather than forced labels.

Privacy and security Our design is on-device by default: symbolization, hypervector encoding, similarity computation, and conformal set construction occur locally; only compact model parameters and thresholds reside in Flash. No raw IMU streams or identifiable features need to leave the device for inference or calibration, and prototypes can be regenerated from seeded PRNGs to avoid storing sensitive vectors. If optional analytics are collected (e.g., aggregate singleton rate), they should be differentially private or anonymized, with opt-in consent and clear retention policies. Devices should implement secure boot and signed firmware updates to prevent model tampering.

Conclusion and future work

We presented HDUQ-HAR, a TinyML-ready framework that joins hyperdimensional computing with label-conditional conformal prediction to deliver calibrated, interpretable uncertainty for wearable HAR. The core idea is to encode each IMU window into a bipolar hypervector, classify via prototype similarity, and extract three lightweight uncertainty signals from the same computation, prototype distance, similarity gap, and vote-dispersion, so that a downstream conformal layer can produce set-valued outputs with finite-sample coverage guarantees. Across standard benchmarks (UCI HAR, WISDM, PAMAP2, OPPORTUNITY) and realistic shifts (orientation, gain, time-warp, missing axis, cross-placement), the method achieves near-target coverage at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-\delta {=}0.90$$\end{document} with near-singleton sets on i.i.d. data and strong shift detection (AUROC in the 0.92–0.96 band), while meeting strict TinyML budgets (3–5 ms/window on Cortex-M4, ∼6–9 KB RAM, ∼5–7 KB Flash). Equally important, the system exposes reason codes that map raw scores to human actions (distance-/margin-/consensus-driven), making uncertainty operational rather than a black-box number.

Future work naturally follows along four axes. Personalization and adaptation: we plan to support per-user prototype refinement and online, label-conditional recalibration using only high-confidence windows, keeping all computation on-device and refreshing stored quantiles \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_k(1-\delta )$$\end{document} via short, private warm-ups. Richer sensing and fusion: concatenating or binding hypervectors from accelerometer, gyroscope, and barometer can improve separability and make dispersion more sensitive to cross-sensor inconsistencies; we will explore late and early fusion strategies that preserve bitwise efficiency and memory frugality. Control and policies: beyond fixed δ, coverage can be adapted to context (e.g., higher caution at night or during rehabilitation sessions); we will study policies that translate set size and reason codes into actionable device behaviors (requesting another window, switching sampling rate, or alerting the user to adjust placement) with formal guarantees on false alarm rates. Learning and compression: a tiny auxiliary CNN or TCN can be trained to distill HDC-derived signals (distance, gap, dispersion) to further sharpen ambiguous boundaries; we aim to automate the trade-off between added latency and reduced set size, and to compile the margin term away when memory is at a premium.

Beyond HAR, the same blueprint, bitwise geometry for fast, robust scores plus conformal thresholds for guarantees, extends to other edge domains (gesture recognition, EMG/EEG intent decoding, acoustic event detection), where interpretable uncertainty is a prerequisite for safe autonomy. We believe this work demonstrates that efficiency and reliability need not be at odds on wearables: with the right representation (hyperdimensional), the right dynamics (n-grams), and the right wrapper (conformal sets), microcontrollers can both decide fast and know when not to decide.
