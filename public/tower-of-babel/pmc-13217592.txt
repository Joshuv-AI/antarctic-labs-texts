Community zoonoses prioritization (CZP): A One Health tool integrating participatory epidemiology and multicriteria decision analysis
Authors: Farah I. Mumin, Siobhan M. Mor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundZoonotic diseases impose a major burden on low- and middle-income countries, yet efforts to target limited resources effectively depend on prioritization tools that lack community input or are difficult to adapt to local contexts. The Community Zoonoses Prioritization (CZP) tool was developed to address this gap by combining the inclusivity of Participatory Epidemiology (PE) with the structured analytical logic of Multicriteria Decision Analysis (MCDA).MethodsThis paper introduces the conceptual foundations, procedural steps and analytical approach of the CZP tool, outlining a standardized six-step process for guiding community groups through: (1) health problem mapping; (2) zoonoses listing; (3) selection and weighting of prioritization criteria; (4) scoring via consensus; (5) action planning; and (6) contextual reflection. Sessions employ visual aids, proportional piling, and a scoring matrix to ensure both accessibility and analytical rigor. Implementation of the tool in one district is included to illustrate practical application, data capture, typical outputs, and considerations for aggregating across community groups.ResultsApplication of the tool demonstrated that the full CZP process can be delivered within a single session and implemented effectively with non-specialist audiences, including those with low literacy levels. The method generated structured outputs—including weighted criteria, disease scores, and ranked lists—that are suitable for analysis, reproducibility assessment, and, where appropriate, aggregation across groups. These observations indicate that the CZP functions as intended in field conditions and yields interpretable, policy-relevant prioritization information.ConclusionsThe CZP complements expert-driven frameworks by introducing a robust bottom-up mechanism that can integrate community perspectives into One Health decision making. The tool offers researchers, policymakers, and practitioners a replicable pathway for embedding meaningful community engagement in work plans, supporting more equitable and inclusive One Health interventions.

Introduction

Zoonotic diseases represent a substantial and growing global health burden, accounting for over 60% of known human infectious diseases and approximately 75% of emerging pathogens [1], [2]. Their impact falls most heavily on low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where livelihoods are closely tied to livestock and where public health and veterinary services are often limited [3]. In such contexts, prevention and control efforts must target scarce resources toward the most critical zoonotic diseases. Prioritization is therefore essential to rationalize investments and guide coordinated One Health interventions [4].

Several structured zoonoses prioritization frameworks have been developed to guide evidence-based decision-making. Most prominent among these are the One Health Zoonotic Disease Prioritization (OHZDP) tool developed by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [5] and the analytically oriented Phylum framework developed for the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH, formerly OIE) [6]. Both employ Multicriteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) to weigh factors such as human and animal health impact, economic burden, and feasibility of control. The OHZDP process emphasizes interdisciplinary participation and multisectoral consensus, bringing together representatives from public health, animal health, and environmental sectors, whereas the Phylum tool relies more heavily on quantitative data and expert-driven modelling.

While both approaches have notable strengths, they involve limited participation from communities and community-based actors. Consequently, the resulting priorities tend to mirror a top-down approach in which decisions are shaped by national-level perspectives that may not fully capture community realities [7]. Such centralization can weaken ownership at the operational level and reduce community buy-in during implementation. Further, while these frameworks have high policy utility, their dependence on specialized expertise and formalized procedures can hinder their applicability in contexts where access to formal education is constrained and health and scientific literacy is lower, underscoring the need for complementary, community-responsive methods.

Participatory Epidemiology (PE) has proven valuable for exploring disease patterns, perceptions, and priorities [8]. These semi-quantitative, dialogue-based techniques capture experiential knowledge and strengthen local ownership in surveillance and control programs [9]. However, PE outputs are often shaped by subjective perceptions and contextual variation, making them difficult to standardize or compare across groups and settings [9], [10]. The absence of explicit criteria weighting and structured scoring systems further limits their analytical transparency and policy utility. Balancing participatory inclusivity with methodological rigor remains a persistent challenge for translating community-derived insights into evidence-based prioritization frameworks suitable for decision-making beyond the local context.

This methodological gap between formal, top down, criteria-based frameworks (e.g., OHZDP) and context-rich, community-based approaches (e.g., PE) has limited the integration of local perspectives into One Health decision-making on zoonoses prevention and control. To bridge this gap, we developed the Community Zoonoses Prioritization (CZP) tool, a hybrid framework that combines the participatory strengths of PE with the analytical rigor and transparency of MCDA. The CZP tool enables communities to systematically identify and rank zoonotic threats using locally meaningful criteria. This paper outlines the conceptual foundation, methodology, and field application of the CZP tool, offering a replicable approach for researchers, policymakers, and practitioners seeking to embed community engagement within One Health interventions targeting zoonoses.

Conceptual framework and principles

The CZP tool integrates traditional knowledge systems with structured decision analysis to strengthen evidence-based decision-making in community settings. It draws on two complementary methodological traditions: PE and MCDA. PE provides the foundation for drawing local knowledge through inclusive, experience-based, participatory methods such as ranking, scoring, and mapping, which foster collective reflection and ownership of outcomes [8]. In contrast, MCDA contributes analytical structure by defining criteria, assigning weights, and applying consistent scoring rules to ensure transparent and reproducible prioritization [5], [11], [12]. The integration of these two methodological traditions forms a hybrid participatory–analytical framework that bridges local engagement with structured, evidence-based One Health decision-making. Within this framework, the CZP employs a value-based MCDA using a linear additive model (Weighted Sum Model) to integrate weighted criteria into overall scores [13], [14]. This conceptual model embeds decision logic within participatory processes, enabling communities to translate experiential knowledge into policy-relevant evidence while maintaining local ownership. The CZP tool is guided by five interrelated design principles that ensure both methodological rigor and contextual relevance. First, accessibility, rooted in PE methods, promotes usability in low-literacy settings through local languages, culturally appropriate facilitation, and visual tools. Second, transparency and consistency, drawing from both PE's emphasis on open dialogue and MCDA's structured scoring, ensure shared understanding and accountability through facilitated group decision-making. Third, systematic and reproducible processes, a hallmark of MCDA, provide a standardized, six-step structure that enables comparability across groups and contexts. Fourth, action orientation, grounded in PE's applied ethos and reinforced by MCDA's decision logic, links prioritization outcomes to feasible prevention and control actions with clearly defined responsibilities. Finally, inclusivity and gender responsiveness, central to PE, ensure representation across gender, age, and community roles so that resulting priorities reflect diverse perspectives.

CZP process and field application

The CZP process is implemented through six sequential, facilitated steps (Fig. 1). Each session engages approximately 10–15 participants and typically lasts three to four hours, led by trained facilitators and supported by dedicated data recorders. To illustrate the practical application of the CZP tool, we draw on a single session conducted in Ceelgeele village (Balcad District, Middle Shabelle Region, Somalia). The group consisted of 12 adult women (median age ≈ 42 years), including agro-pastoralists and community animal health workers (CAHWs). Subsequently, to demonstrate how CZP outputs can be combined across multiple groups, we include an illustrative example based on data from five villages in Middle Shabelle Region, each with separate male and female groups (10 groups in total). A more extensive multi-regional analysis (40 exercises across four regions of Somalia) is presented elsewhere (PREPRINT; [15]). Step 0: Preparation

The preparation phase establishes the foundation for effective implementation of the CZP process. During this step, the team leading the exercise prepares all the required material, identifies and trains facilitators and data recorders on the CZP methodology, and works with local stakeholders to obtain permissions as well as identify suitable participants. Involving local leaders in the latter helps validate the planned approach and build trust in the process.

Visual aids (Supplementary File 1) and standardized data-recording templates (e.g. Supplementary File 2) are prepared in advance, to ensure consistent information capture across sessions. A preliminary set of potential prioritization criteria is identified for later consideration by communities in Step 3. These criteria serve only as examples that participants may adopt, adapt, or replace. The initial criteria should be identified by a multidisciplinary team familiar with the local context. If zoonoses have already been prioritized nationally or regionally, the criteria used in that specific context should be reviewed for consistency and relevance. For example, “laboratory capacity or diagnostic testing capacity” is among the most frequently selected national-level criteria [12] but may not be applicable at the community level, whereas criteria such as “severity of disease in humans” may be more contextually relevant. The identified criteria are then translated into plain, locally meaningful language and illustrated with pictograms to enhance comprehension across literacy levels. Each criterion is then framed into a simple categorical question to guide group discussion. For example, the criterion “human mortality” is positively phrased as: “How likely is it that a person infected with this disease will recover?” with potential responses provided on a three-point scale (e.g., Not likely = 1, Likely = 2, Very likely = 3).

Facilitators and data recorders are typically drawn from organizations engaged in health, livestock, or development activities at sub-national level (e.g. government extension services, NGOs, academic institutions, or trained research teams). While facilitators are familiar with One Health concepts and local context, they do not introduce disease priorities or scoring judgments. Instead, their role is to guide the sequence of activities, support shared understanding of criteria and scoring rules, and ensure that group decisions are recorded accurately. Facilitation and data-recording responsibilities are assigned to separate individuals, and both receive training on the CZP protocol as well as participatory facilitation skills, including neutral questioning, reflexive practice, and strategies for managing group dynamics.

Participant selection follows a purposive rather than random approach, with the aim of ensuring representation across relevant community roles. Group composition is designed to capture diversity by gender, household decision-making roles (e.g. household heads, youth, grandparents), livelihood activities (e.g. pastoral, agropastoral, mixed farming), and social or functional roles such as traditional healers, community elders or chiefs, community health workers, and community animal health workers. In contexts where gender norms or social hierarchies may constrain open discussion, separate group sessions (e.g. by gender) are conducted to reduce power imbalances and to support prioritization that reflects a wider range of experiences.

Step 1: General health problem mapping

Objective: To establish rapport, contextual understanding, and a shared framing of community health before focusing on zoonoses.

Process: The facilitators begin by asking two open-ended questions: “What are the most important health problems affecting people in your community?” followed by “What are the most important health problems affecting your animals?” Participants brainstorm ideas that are recorded publicly on flip charts or written on cards. Through guided group discussion, participants first identify a broad list of health problems. This is followed by a consensus-based voting exercise in which the group selects and ranks the top five human and animal health issues using a simple ranking method.

Outputs: Separate lists of the top five human and top five animal health problems that are perceived as locally important by the community.

Example: In Ceelgeele village, the discussion revealed wide-ranging concerns, reflecting both household-level and livelihood-related risks. For human health, the top five ranked health problems were malaria, food-borne illness, Helicobacter pylori infection, visceral leishmaniasis, and water-borne diseases. For animals, the group ranked peste des petits ruminants (PPR), sheep and goat pox, intestinal parasites, contagious caprine pleuropneumonia (CCPP), and rabies as the top five challenges. These ranked problems provided a shared starting point for understanding health problems affecting the community and their animals.

Step 2: Zoonoses list generation

Objective: To develop a locally relevant list of zoonotic diseases for use in subsequent prioritization.

Process: The facilitators introduce the concept of zoonoses using an accessible definition adapted from the World Health Organization (WHO) [16]: “Zoonoses are diseases that can spread from animals to people.” Using this definition, participants brainstorm all diseases they believe fit this description. The initial list is typically broad and may include local disease names or syndromic descriptions, including diseases not classically considered zoonotic in the biomedical literature. All diseases proposed by participants are discussed at this step, with probing questions used to appreciate reasons why community members believe it fits the definition. Local terminology, symptom descriptions and perceived transmission pathways are discussed alongside facilitator biomedical knowledge to identify likely biomedical equivalents and clarify potential overlaps between human and animal syndromes. To refine the list, the facilitators lead a structured voting process in which participants raise their hands for each listed disease. Consistent with participatory epidemiology principles [9], facilitators support discussion and clarification but do not determine which diseases are retained. Diseases are carried forward into subsequent prioritization steps only if their inclusion is agreed by a simple majority (>50%) of participants. Diseases not reaching consensus, including those where facilitator biomedical interpretation differed from community perceptions, are excluded from next steps. Any uncertainties, discrepancies or alternative interpretations are recorded in session notes and reported alongside the final disease list, ensuring that the community-generated priorities are interpretable considering, but not constrained by, biomedical classifications.

Output: A refined list of approximately 10-15 zoonotic diseases perceived by the community as locally relevant, forming the basis for the prioritization exercise in subsequent steps.

Example: In Ceelgeele village, participants discussed which diseases in their area could spread between animals and people. Through open brainstorming, they identified twelve zoonotic diseases known or suspected to occur locally. The list included anthrax, brucellosis, bovine tuberculosis, dog rabies, rabies from white-tailed mongoose, visceral leishmaniasis, salmonellosis, toxoplasmosis, giardiasis, child bacterial diarrhea from raw milk, orf-related hand lesions, and sarcoptic mange (Sarcoptes scabiei). All twelve diseases were retained for prioritization in the following steps. The discussion reflected strong local understanding of zoonotic risks, particularly those associated with milk handling, carcass disposal, and dog bites, and underscored how community members linked animal health with household well-being.

Step 3: Criteria selection and weighting

Objective: To identify and assign relative importance to the criteria used for disease prioritization.

Process: The pre-identified set of potential criteria are presented using pictograms and lay terminology. Participants are also invited to propose additional locally relevant criteria. The group then selects the top five criteria they consider most important through consensus. Using proportional piling, participants distribute 50 counters (e.g., beans or stones) across the selected criteria to indicate their relative importance. The final allocation of counters is reached through group consensus.

Outputs: Five selected criteria, each assigned a numerical weight summing to 50, representing their relative importance for decision-making.

Example: In Ceelgeele village, after generating the zoonoses list, participants reviewed a set of possible prioritization criteria presented as pictograms by the facilitators. No new criteria were proposed. Using both lay-language explanations and Somali translations, the group discussed each option until a shared understanding was reached. The five most relevant criteria were then selected by the community group to guide subsequent disease scoring and ranking. Step 4: Zoonoses scoring and ranking

Objective: To systematically rank the shortlisted zoonoses by applying scoring across weighted criteria.

Process: A scoring matrix is prepared with up to 15 community-identified zoonotic diseases (from Step 2) in the rows and the five selected criteria (from Step 3) in the columns. To support participation of community members with low literacy, the facilitators take one disease at a time and discusses it against each of the five criteria in turn, so it is always clear which disease is being scored. Simple illustrations representing each criterion are displayed at the top of the matrix as visual reminders, and the possible response options for each criterion (e.g., High/Medium/Low, Yes/No) are shown using colored cards. For every disease-criterion pair, the group reaches consensus on the answer to the categorical question, and the corresponding colored card is placed in the matrix. The total weighted score for each zoonotic disease is then calculated as the sum of the criterion score multiplied by its weight (see below). Diseases are ranked from highest to lowest, with the highest-scoring disease ranked first. Step 5: Actions identification

Objective: To translate the prioritized zoonoses into actionable interventions.

Process: For the top five ranked zoonoses, the facilitators open the discussion by asking: “What can be done to prevent or control these diseases?” Participants identify feasible prevention and control measures, which are then discussed in terms of responsibility for implementation. Actions are categorized as those led by households, communities, government authorities, or non-governmental organizations (NGOs). This provides an opportunity to identify and address any misconceptions about zoonoses transmission and their prevention.

Output: A list of feasible and context-specific actions linked to prioritized zoonoses, with clear assignment of responsibility across stakeholder levels.

Example: In Ceelgeele village, participants identified practical prevention and control measures for the prioritized zoonoses. Through facilitated discussion, they identified household-level actions such as cooking meat thoroughly, boiling milk, deworming livestock, culling sick animals, and covering their hands with plastic bags when handling raw meat. The burial of dead animals was recognized as a communal responsibility requiring collective effort. This step emphasized feasible, locally appropriate practices and clarified the division of responsibility between households and the wider community, reinforcing community-led approaches to zoonotic disease prevention.

Step 6: Contextualization within broader health priorities (optional)

Objective: To situate zoonotic priorities within the community’s broader health concerns.

Process: The facilitators revisit the top five ranked lists of human and animal health problems from Step 1 and the top five zoonoses prioritized in Step 4. Using a simple ranking method, participants rank the lists and discuss how zoonoses fit relative to other pressing health issues. This reflective exercise fosters understanding of trade-offs and alignment with local health priorities.

Outputs: A nuanced understanding of how zoonoses are positioned within the community's overall health landscape, supporting interpretation and integration of results into local health planning.

Example: In Ceelgeele village, when the top-ranked human, animal, and zoonotic health problems were considered together, human health problems occupied several of the highest positions, including malaria (rank 1), water-borne diseases (rank 3), and food-borne illness (rank 4). Sheep and goat pox ranked highly among animal health problems (rank 2), while other animal health conditions such as intestinal parasites and peste des petits ruminants appeared lower in the combined ranking. Zoonotic diseases were distributed across the list. Anthrax ranked fifth overall, followed by brucellosis (rank 7) and child bacterial diarrhea associated with raw milk (rank 10). Rabies ranked 13th when considered explicitly as a zoonotic disease but was prioritized more highly when framed as an animal health problem (rank 9), suggesting that community concern is driven primarily by animal-related impacts rather than perceived importance as a human disease. Conversely, visceral leishmaniasis was ranked higher as a human health problem (rank 11) than as a zoonotic disease (rank 12), indicating stronger recognition of its impacts on humans over its zoonotic transmission dynamics. Data management

During the proportional piling exercise (Step 3), facilitators employ a fixed set of 50 counters to ensure consistent scaling of criteria weights across groups. Data are recorded using the predesigned CZP Excel template (e.g. Supplementary File 2), which standardizes entry for criteria, weighting, scoring, and ranking. The template automatically computes aggregate scores and generates ranked disease lists for immediate visual feedback. Where feasible, data are entered directly into laptops or tablets during the session to reduce transcription errors and enable instant review of results.

Reliability and Reproducibility

During the exercise, internal agreement is reached via consensus, with facilitators reviewing results with participants to verify accuracy and clarify any misunderstandings in real time. This reinforces transparency and creates a sense of shared ownership.

The exercise can be repeated across multiple groups to support planning across geographic scales. Overall agreement between groups is quantified using Kendall's coefficient of concordance (W), which measures the extent to which groups produce similar rankings; values range between 0 (no agreement) and 1.0 (perfect agreement), with values above 0.6 typically indicating strong consensus [17]. A sufficiently high W suggests that rankings can be justifiably aggregated (e.g. rankings from multiple villages aggregated to a district-level ranking). Aggregation is undertaken by summing disease scores obtained in Step 4 across groups and ranking diseases according to their total scores, with the highest total score indicating the highest priority. Conversely, low W values may signal meaningful differences in perceived importance that warrant further investigation. Differences between two subgroups—such as male and female groups—are evaluated using Kendall's rank correlation coefficient (τ), which ranges from −1.0 (perfectly reversed rankings) to 1.0 (identical rankings) [18]. For such comparisons, τ is calculated on the aggregated rankings for each subgroup, providing a measure of how closely the subgroup-specific priority lists align.

Discussion

The Community Zoonoses Prioritization (CZP) tool is, to our knowledge, the first framework to integrate the structured analytical rigor of multicriteria decision analysis (MCDA) with the inclusivity and contextual relevance of participatory epidemiology (PE) for zoonotic disease prioritization at the community level. Its six sequential steps are transparent and easy to facilitate, allowing community groups to produce priority lists that reflect their local realities while still being robust. By structuring participatory activities through explicit criteria selection, weighting, and consensus-based scoring, the CZP provides a practical, community-informed decision-support tool whose outputs can be interpreted and, where appropriate, aggregated to inform planning at local, subnational, and national levels. As a methods paper, this work focuses on the design, implementation, and reproducibility of the CZP process.

The CZP tool represents a significant advance over previous community-based attempts to identify or discuss zoonotic disease threats. Few documented examples exist of zoonotic disease prioritization conducted at community level. Participatory Epidemiology (PE) methods, including proportional piling, pairwise ranking, and problem-mapping, have been used to elicit community perceptions of livestock disease importance [8], but these approaches produce context-specific, non-standardized outputs that cannot be easily compared or scaled beyond the local setting. Quantitative prioritization approaches that incorporate public perspectives have also been described, such as the use of conjoint analysis to weight zoonotic disease criteria based on responses from the general public in North America [19]. While methodologically rigorous, this approach relies on technically intensive, survey-based data collection and was associated with low response rates and an over-representation of highly educated participants, making it less suited to facilitated, community-level implementation in many rural and resource-limited settings. More recently, the Community Zoonoses Priorities Project in Zambia appears to rely on open discussion and local knowledge sharing [20], with fewer details available about the use of explicit criteria or fully reproducible scoring systems.

In contrast, the CZP guides communities to articulate prioritization criteria that reflect their local context, assign relative importance to these criteria, and collectively evaluate zoonotic diseases using a consistent and interpretable scoring system. Through this process, qualitative judgments are translated into structured, semi-quantitative outputs that capture community-perceived disease impact, transmission risk, and livelihood consequences, grounded in lived experience, local observation, and community risk perceptions. While these outputs do not generate epidemiological measures, they provide contextual information that can complement surveillance data and expert assessments in broader health planning. In doing so, the CZP operationalizes principles articulated in the Generalizable One Health Framework, which emphasizes sub-national stakeholder inclusion as foundational for effective prioritization and planning [21]. Community engagement is central to the tool's design and ensures that prioritization reflects local realities and supports collective responsibility for priority setting, aligning with growing evidence that emphasizes these features as critical for effective One Health implementation [22], [23].

Beyond its methodological contributions, the CZP advances equity and inclusiveness by creating space for diverse community perspectives within the prioritization process. By engaging community leaders, community health workers, community animal health workers, and livestock keepers in identifying and prioritizing zoonotic threats, the tool brings together complementary forms of experiential, occupational, and locally embedded knowledge, reflecting growing calls to operationalize equity and inclusion as foundational principles of One Health implementation [24]. In addition, facilitating separate male and female discussion groups, where culturally appropriate, enables the systematic capture of gender-specific knowledge. This includes knowledge related to livestock care, household health management, and food preparation, as shown in recent CZP implementation work (PREPRINT; [15]). Together, these design features strengthen the fairness, relevance, and legitimacy of prioritization outcomes and support the identification of actions that are responsive to the needs of different segments of the community.

The CZP is designed to complement and extend national and expert-driven prioritization tools such as the OHZDP by introducing a structured, bottom-up perspective. National-level prioritization exercises provide strategic alignment and intersectoral coordination, while the CZP captures localized risk perceptions and livelihood impacts that are often less visible at higher administrative levels. Integrating and systematically comparing CZP outputs with national [12] and subnational prioritization exercises [25], [26], [27] can enhance vertical coherence within One Health governance by linking community knowledge and experience [22] with institutional priorities and by revealing areas of convergence and divergence. Convergence can reinforce shared priorities across community, subnational, and national governance levels. In contrast, divergences may highlight locally significant zoonoses that are under-recognized in national agendas. Together, these patterns underscore the value of community participation for more equitable and context-responsive One Health planning and provide a basis for targeted surveillance, locally tailored interventions, or adaptive subnational resource allocation.

The contextualization of zoonoses within broader health priorities (Step 6) ensures that zoonotic disease prioritization is situated within the wider landscape of community health concerns. This structured reflection step creates space for participants to compare their zoonoses rankings with other pressing human and animal health issues, supporting more informed interpretation and realistic action planning. In line with this, recent work highlights that local priorities are inherently dynamic and shaped by changes in livelihoods, environmental conditions, and social contexts [28], [29]. These insights underscore the need for iterative and flexible approaches that can adapt to changing local contexts, rather than relying solely on standalone technical rankings. The CZP's reflective approach aligns with these perspectives by supporting adaptive, context-sensitive planning that enhances the legitimacy and sustainability of both community- and policy-level decision-making.

While the CZP provides a transparent, reproducible, and adaptable framework, several limitations warrant consideration. As with all participatory approaches, prioritization outcomes may be influenced by facilitation quality, participant composition, and local power dynamics. These risks are mitigated through structured facilitators and data recorders training, standardized visual prompts and question framing, promotion of group consensus rather than individual judgments with transparent recording of decisions in full view of participants and purposive participant selection designed to promote gender balance and representation across community roles, as detailed in Step 0. Nevertheless, variability in group dynamics and facilitation effectiveness may still introduce heterogeneity across sessions. This underscores the importance of careful process documentation when interpreting and comparing results. Beyond these operational considerations, the CZP also faces inherent conceptual trade-offs common to participatory decision-making. The tool relies on community perceptions and experiential knowledge rather than measured epidemiological risk, meaning that highly relevant livelihood-related diseases may be prioritized over rare but high-consequence zoonoses. The participatory nature of the tool also requires substantial time, logistical planning, and community mobilization, which may limit its scalability in some settings. Accordingly, CZP outputs should be interpreted as structured representations of community-perceived risk and impact, and are best used in conjunction with surveillance data, expert assessments, and national or subnational prioritization exercises to support balanced and context-sensitive One Health decision-making.

The CZP has broader implications for how community knowledge is generated, interpreted, and used within One Health systems. As a methodological framework it offers researchers a structured approach to examining alignment between community-derived priorities, epidemiological evidence, and national agendas, while enabling cross-site comparison through standardized reliability measures. Future work could extend this approach through digital integration, multi-country application, and assessment of how CZP-informed priorities influence programmatic decisions and resource allocation. From a policy perspective, the CZP provides a practical mechanism for incorporating community perspectives into subnational and national One Health planning processes. When analysed alongside expert-driven tools such as the OHZDP, CZP outputs can support more vertically coherent planning by revealing both shared priorities and locally specific concerns that may warrant targeted response. For implementers and frontline practitioners, the CZP functions as a practical engagement tool that balances accessibility with analytical structure. Its use of visual facilitation, standardized materials, and a clearly defined protocol supports implementation in low-literacy and resource-constrained settings while maintaining transparency and comparability across contexts. Taken together, these features position the CZP as a link between participatory engagement and institutional decision-making, contributing to more inclusive and context-sensitive One Health governance.

Conclusion

The Community Zoonoses Prioritization (CZP) tool advances the evidence base for community-informed One Health operationalization by providing a standardized yet adaptable approach to community-level zoonotic disease prioritization. As a methods paper, this work demonstrates the feasibility of operationalizing analytical decision logic within participatory processes. The CZP is designed for use in resource-limited and low-literacy settings and generates structured, reproducible outputs that can be considered alongside existing prioritization tools to inform subnational and national planning processes. Future work should focus on broader application across contexts, digital data capture, and evaluation of how CZP-informed priorities influence policy, surveillance, and programmatic decision-making. Together, these future research and implementation priorities position the CZP as a practical tool for strengthening vertical coherence and advancing more inclusive and context-responsive One Health governance.
