Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis: A comparative review of pathophysiology, diagnosis and evolving management
Authors: Shriyeta Biswas, Juganta K. Kalita, Bedaparna Nath, N. Anirudh Singh, Md. Sarowar Hossain, Saikat Sen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are among the leading causes of chronic joint pain and disability, resulting in significant healthcare and economic burdens. OA is a degenerative disorder characterized by cartilage degradation, whereas RA is an autoimmune condition that leads to synovial inflammation. Despite their differences, both conditions share similar symptoms such as joint pain, stiffness, and functional decline, which can complicate early diagnosis and delay targeted treatment. Degradation of the extracellular matrix, chondrocyte senescence, and responses to biomechanical stress are considered key molecular events in OA. By contrast, RA is driven by abnormal immune activation, autoantibody production, and ongoing synovial inflammation. This review provides a comparative analysis of OA and RA, focusing on pathophysiology, diagnostic modalities, clinical assessment frameworks, and evolving management strategies. Diagnostic strategies that combine patient evaluation, advanced imaging techniques, and emerging molecular biomarkers to improve early detection are also discussed. Critical evaluations are provided of management strategies that encompass pharmacologic treatments, such as using nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, biologics, and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, as well as non-pharmacologic interventions, including physical therapy, lifestyle modifications, and surgical options. In addition, the limited disease-modifying options for OA and safety concerns with long-term immunosuppressive therapy in RA are noted, highlighting the need for biomarker guided personalized therapies.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are among the most prevalent forms of arthritis, affecting millions worldwide and contributing substantially to reduced quality of life and increased healthcare burdens.1 OA is primarily a degenerative joint disorder characterized by the progressive breakdown of articular cartilage and alterations in subchondral bone, predominantly affecting the aging population and leading to joint pain and impaired mobility.2,3 The disease involves structural changes throughout the joint, including cartilage degeneration, subchondral bone sclerosis, synovial membrane inflammation, joint capsule stiffness, ligament laxity, and peri-articular muscle weakness, collectively resulting in joint instability and functional limitation.4,5 Knee OA is particularly common and a leading cause of chronic pain and disability globally.6 Despite its high prevalence, no disease-modifying therapies are currently available for OA, and treatment mainly focuses on symptomatic relief and functional improvement. By contrast, RA is a complex and heterogeneous autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent systemic inflammation and progressive joint damage. RA encompasses diverse patient subsets with distinct clinical presentations, immunopathogenic mechanisms, and responses to therapy.7 The hallmark of RA is chronic synovial inflammation driven by dysregulated innate and adaptive immune responses, leading to synovial hyperplasia (pannus formation), immune cell infiltration, and progressive erosion of articular cartilage and subchondral bone.8 In addition to joint involvement, RA frequently exhibits extra-articular manifestations that affect multiple organ systems, including the lungs, kidneys, cardiovascular system, nervous system, gastrointestinal tract, skeletal system, and eyes, significantly contributing to morbidity and mortality.9 Although nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) remain central to symptomatic management, their long-term use is limited by serious adverse effects, highlighting the urgent need for safer therapies, predictive biomarkers, and improved disease-modifying strategies.

Current evidence regarding the management of OA and RA is critically summarized in this review. The aims of this review are to resolve diagnostic and therapeutic ambiguity by systematically comparing OA and RA across multiple levels, including pathophysiology, clinical manifestations, imaging findings, biomarker profiles, diagnostic, management, and current treatment strategies, thereby helping to clarify the key differences between degenerative and autoimmune joint pathology. Understanding these distinctions can improve the diagnostic accuracy by linking specific clinical patterns with appropriate imaging and serological markers. This integrated perspective can also guide clinicians in the selection of disease-specific management approaches, thereby reducing misclassification and enabling more targeted and timely therapeutic interventions. Thus, both the common features and fundamental distinctions between the two diseases are highlighted to improve the management of arthritis.

Epidemiology

Joint problems have been among the main global health burdens for decades. In 2020, OA affected ∼595 million people globally. By contrast, RA impacts ∼18 million individuals worldwide.10,11 According to epidemiological studies, the prevalence of OA is relatively higher in countries such as Germany, Italy, and Sweden, while the prevalence of RA is lower in East Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa but higher in Northern Europe and North America.10,12 However, this apparent disparity may partly reflect under-diagnosis or under-reporting in resource-limited settings and developing countries.11 The prevalence of OA is rising worldwide, particularly among individuals of working age, typically in the range from 15 to 64 years.13 In 2021, the global burden of OA among the working-age population was substantial according to key epidemiological indicators. The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was reported as 653.375 cases per 100,000 population (95% uncertainty interval [UI]: 513.361–808.530), while the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) reached 5989.794 cases per 100,000 population (95% UI: 5081.139–6933.053). In addition, the age-standardized disability-adjusted life years (ASDR) metric reflecting the overall disease burden was 208.001 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 99.073–416.366).13 Sex-specific analysis showed that females experienced significantly higher rates across all three metrics compared with males. The ASIR was 537.143/100,000 in males (95% UI: 536.866–537.419) and 768.641/100,000 in females (95% UI: 768.312–768.970). The ASPR was 4937.233/100,000 among males (95% UI: 4936.397–4938.069) and 7020.759/100,000 in females (95% UI: 7019.773–7021.745). In addition, females had a higher ASDR (244.172/100,000) than males (171.027/100,000). According to published findings, women have a higher incidence, prevalence, and disease burden than men, and thus there is clearly a sex-dependent difference in the OA profile.14

Global estimates indicate that the incidence of RA has increased significantly over recent years largely due to the diagnostic awareness that it often begins earlier at 30–50 years and is driven by autoimmune mechanisms. Approximately 739,962.95 RA cases were reported in this group in 2021, which is a very high increase of 90.52% compared with 1990. The ASIR has increased steadily in new RA diagnoses, rising from 12.75 to 14.09 per 100,000 population, and the number of working-age people with RA also increased to 11,878,843.81 cases in 2021, with a very high increase of 108.39% since 1990. Similarly, during the same time period, ASPR increased from 195.05 to 222.68 per 100,000 population. The increase in the ASDR life years (DALYs) rate was relatively modestly, reaching 34.54 per 100,000 population in 2021, despite the notable increases in incidence and prevalence.15 These age-specific differences in OA and RA cases are clinically important because older patients often experience higher disability and comorbidities, whereas earlier onset requires long-term disease-modifying therapy to prevent joint damage and functional decline. Understanding age-specific epidemiology can help to guide screening, early diagnosis, and targeted management strategies in different populations. These findings highlight the growing impact of RA on economically active populations and further emphasize the need for improved therapeutic interventions and early management strategies to mitigate long-term disability and societal costs.

Risk factors

In order to manage OA and RA, it is essential to understand the risk factors for early diagnosis, preventative measures, and the formulation of individualized treatment plans.16 The etiology of arthritis is type specific, differing across various forms of the disease where any genetic, environmental, or personal characteristic that raises a person chance of contracting a specific disease is referred to as a risk factor. In OA, the risk factors act mainly through biomechanical overload and metabolic inflammation. Aging, obesity, and joint trauma increase the mechanical stress on cartilage, while adipokines and metabolic imbalance promote inflammatory mediators that accelerate cartilage degradation and joint remodeling. In RA, risk factors trigger immune dysregulation. Pathophysiology: OA vs RA

OA and RA are musculoskeletal joint diseases that arise via fundamentally different etiologies but share several overlapping inflammatory and structural alterations within joint tissues. OA is viewed primarily as a disorder of cartilage wear and tear, but is now recognized as a complex, whole joint pathology influenced by mechanical overload and age-related cellular dysfunction.23 These factors disrupt the homeostatic balance of chondrocytes, driving them toward a catabolic and hypertrophic like phenotype. This phenotypic shift results in the robust upregulation of matrix degrading enzymes, particularly matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-13) and the aggrecanases A disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 4 and 5 (ADAMTS-4 and ADAMTS-5), which collectively accelerate the breakdown of type II collagen and aggrecan key structural components required for cartilage integrity and resilience, directly leading to cartilage thinning, joint space narrowing, and osteophyte formation detectable based on radiographs and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).24 As the extracellular matrix deteriorates, its fragmented components act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), activating pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors 2 and 4 (TLR2 and TLR4) on chondrocytes and synovial cells to initiate nuclear factor kappa light chain enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, thereby amplifying the production of proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and IL-6.25 These cytokines contribute to synovial hypertrophy and mild systemic inflammation, reflected in elevated biomarkers including C-reactive protein (CRP), synovial cytokines, and sometimes the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). NF-κB/MAPK-driven inflammation sensitizes nociceptive nerve endings in the joint, explaining the characteristic pain and stiffness. The combined effect of matrix breakdown, inflammatory signaling, and alterations in chondrocytes explains the functional impairment, joint instability, and progressive disability in OA. By mapping each molecular pathway to a corresponding clinical, biochemical, or imaging outcome, cellular pathology can be linked to observable patient symptoms.26

In parallel, mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced adenosine triphosphate production, and the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species promote oxidative stress-induced chondrocyte senescence and apoptosis, diminishing the tissue regenerative capacity. The pathological changes in OA extend beyond the articular cartilage, encompassing subchondral bone sclerosis, osteophyte formation, and bone marrow lesions, which are all correlated with pain severity and structural progression.27 Low grade, persistent synovitis driven by macrophage infiltration and synovial hypertrophy contributes to inflammatory sensitization and joint discomfort. Moreover, adipose-derived mediators such as leptin and resistin link obesity to metabolic forms of OA by promoting systemic and local inflammation, altering cartilage metabolism, and modulating pathways.28 Together, these interconnected mechanical, inflammatory, metabolic, and molecular processes underscore the multifactorial nature of OA and highlight why it is increasingly regarded as a whole-organ disease rather than a condition confined solely to cartilage. RA primarily affects synovial joints, with early symptoms typically beginning as pain and stiffness in the small joints of the hands and feet. Morning stiffness lasting longer than 30 min helps distinguish RA from other arthritic conditions such as OA. As inflammation persists, the synovial membrane proliferates, leading to swelling, tenderness, and progressive loss of joint mobility, and although RA initially targets small joints, larger joints become increasingly involved as the disease advances.29 Chronic synovitis drives cartilage degradation, bone erosion, and joint deformities, contributing to substantial functional impairment and reduced quality of life. Central to RA pathogenesis is a complex cytokine network involving chemokines, interleukins, colony stimulating factors, and interferons, and their overlapping receptor systems lead to pleiotropic and redundant signaling effects.30 Cytokine expression in synovial tissue shifts dynamically as RA progresses, with early T helper cell-derived cytokines giving way to increased TNF, IL-10, and interferon gamma levels. Key mediators such as IL-1 amplify inflammation and matrix degradation, whereas IL-1 receptor antagonists can alleviate disease severity.31 Other critical molecular targets include cluster of differentiation 44 (CD44), the upregulation of which is correlated with synovial inflammation, and the Janus kinase signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway, which transduces signals from numerous cytokines and represents a major therapeutic target, as seen with JAK inhibitors like tofacitinib.32 RA development progresses through triggering, maturation, and targeting stages driven by genetic susceptibility, exposure to environmental agents such as smoking and silica, mucosal immune activation, dysbiosis, and autoantibody formation. Diagnostic dilemma

Distinguishing early OA from seronegative RA remains a diagnostic challenge because both conditions may initially present with nonspecific joint pain and minimal structural changes on imaging. OA mainly involves low grade synovial inflammation and metabolic alterations may occur in early stages, leading to clinical overlap with RA. Seronegative RA may initially lack detectable autoantibodies such as rheumatoid factor (RF) or ACPA, which can delay definitive classification.34 The duration of morning stiffness provides an important clinical distinction, where OA typically causes short-lasting stiffness (<30 min) that improves with movement, whereas RA commonly presents with prolonged stiffness (>30–60 min) due to persistent synovial inflammation.38 Inflammatory markers, including CRP and ESR, are usually normal in OA but frequently elevated in RA, reflecting active synovitis, although normal values do not exclude early disease. Imaging is generally recommended when clinical findings are atypical, symptoms persist despite conservative management, or structural joint damage is suspected, with plain radiography serving as the first line modality, and ultrasound or MRI providing greater sensitivity for early inflammatory changes.35 Serological testing should be considered when persistent joint swelling, symmetrical small joint involvement, prolonged morning stiffness, or elevated inflammatory markers raise suspicion of autoimmune arthritis because RF and ACPA testing can support RA classification and guide early referral for disease-modifying therapy. Positive serology helps to confirm the diagnosis but also provides prognostic information because higher titers are associated with more aggressive disease and increased risk of joint damage.36 Even in seronegative patients, repeat testing or extended panels, including RF isotypes or ACPA fine specificities, may reveal autoantibodies that appear later in the disease course. Early identification of seropositive RA allows timely initiation of disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), which can slow radiographic progression and improve long-term functional outcomes. Integration of serological results with clinical assessment and imaging optimizes the diagnostic accuracy and ensures appropriate early management.

Diagnosis and classification

The evidence-based diagnostic guidelines for OA and RA from the American College of Rheumatology (ACR), European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR), and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) ensure alignment with current clinical practice. For OA, all major guidelines emphasize a primarily clinical diagnosis rather than formal scoring systems. The ACR criteria focus on joint pain accompanied by mechanical features such as brief morning stiffness, crepitus, bony enlargement, and the absence of inflammatory markers, with imaging findings considered supportive but not mandatory.37 EULAR similarly highlights activity-related joint pain, minimal morning stiffness, functional limitation, and typical joint involvement, recommending imaging only when the diagnosis is uncertain 38. The most recent NICE guidelines further simplify OA diagnosis, defining it based on age (≥45 years) and activity-related joint pain, without routine laboratory testing or imaging, supported by periodic clinical review. A key distinguishing feature is minimal or absent morning stiffness, usually lasting <30 min, which helps differentiate it from RA that presents with prolonged stiffness. Physical examination may reveal joint tenderness, crepitus, and reduced range of motion. OA is a chronic joint disorder, so it is recommended to monitor symptom progression, assess functional limitation, and adjust management strategies such as lifestyle modification, physiotherapy, or pharmacological treatment.39 The Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) guidelines for OA include core management (Level 1A, strong), topical NSAIDs, land-based or mind–body exercise, and optional weight management for all patients. Conditional therapies (Level 1B/2) include NSAIDs ± proton pump inhibitor, COX-2 inhibitors, intra-articular treatments, duloxetine, pain programs, and individualized non-pharmacologic strategies such as aquatic exercise or cognitive behavioral therapy.40

By contrast, RA diagnosis and monitoring rely on structured classification and disease activity assessment (Disease Activity Score, DAS). The ACR/EULAR 2010 criteria use a numerical scoring system incorporating joint involvement, serology, acute-phase reactants, and symptom duration to enable early and standardized classification.41 The NICE guidelines complement this framework by recommending regular monitoring using validated indices such as DAS28, which stratifies disease activity and guides treatment decisions within a treat to target strategy. In addition, ACR/EULAR 2022 indicates that Boolean remission rates are lower in established RA than early RA, with HAQ ≤0.5 observed in 57% of established cases versus 74% in early disease.42

The updated Boolean 2.0 criteria classify more patients as achieving remission while maintaining strong agreement with index-based measures and preserving predictive value for radiographic and functional outcomes in RA.44 EULAR/ACR 2024 includes patient reported joint swelling and difficulty making a fist, MRI detecting subclinical inflammation, and elevated CRP and RF positivity, helping stratify patients into low, moderate, or high progression risk in RA.43 Overall, the revision clearly differentiates the clinically driven diagnosis of OA from the score-based, dynamic assessment required in RA, reflecting contemporary guideline updates. Imaging techniques (OA vs RA)

Imaging plays a crucial role in diagnosing and monitoring OA and RA. MRI is highly sensitive and non-invasive, enabling early detection of synovitis, cartilage loss, bone marrow lesions, erosions, and soft tissue changes in both diseases.45 Despite its advantages, radiography remains the primary tool for OA diagnosis due to its affordability and accessibility, while it is used alongside computed tomography scanning to detect structural bone damage in RA, especially erosions.45 However, studies show that radiographic and clinical diagnostic agreement for knee OA and RA is often below 10%.47 Ultrasonography (US) has become an important complementary tool, outperforming radiography in identifying osteophytes, meniscal extrusion, and early cartilage changes in OA.46 The EULAR guidelines for RA recommend ultrasonography when synovitis is clinically unclear because it is more sensitive than physical examination for detecting early inflammatory changes and erosions in patients at risk.48 Table 3 shows the imaging techniques used for OA and RA. Biomarkers of OA and RA

Cartilage degradation and overall joint remodeling occur in OA, and several imaging and biochemical markers have been associated with pain progression, functional decline, and the likelihood of joint replacement, making them useful surrogate endpoints.53 Synovial fluid provides direct insights into OA pathology because it contains cartilage breakdown fragments such as fibronectin fragments, type II collagen Coll2-1, and other DAMPs, as well as inflammatory molecules that mirror intra-articular disease activity. Cartilage degradation biomarkers such as serum cartilage oligomeric matrix protein, urinary C-terminal telopeptide type II collagen, and hyaluronic acid (HA) can help to assess the whole body joint burden, as well as indicating cartilage turnover, whereas synovial biomarkers including calicitonin gene-related protein, MMPs, and elevated vascular endothelial growth factor promote angiogenesis and synovial inflammation or secondary responses.54, 55, 56, 57

In RA, traditional biomarkers remain essential for diagnosis and monitoring. For example, RF is present in up to 80% of RA cases but lacks specificity because it is also found in infections, malignancies, and other autoimmune diseases.58 ESR and CRP serve as indirect indicators of systemic inflammation and disease activity. Management of OA and RA

The comprehensive approaches required for the management of OA and RA involve surgical, non-pharmacological, and pharmaceutical interventions that are individualized for each patient severity and therapeutic response.69 In order to improve patient outcomes and reduce side effects, integrative strategies that combine contemporary medications with herbal remedies and lifestyle modifications are gaining popularity.70

Management strategies for OA

Core guideline recommendations

OA management primarily focuses on alleviating pain and improving joint function because universally accepted disease-modifying therapies are not currently available. Emerging agents that target cartilage degeneration show promise, but the current guidelines emphasize a combination of pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies, including structured exercise and weight reduction, with joint replacement reserved for severe or refractory cases.71

Among the pharmacological options, oral NSAIDs remain the most commonly used agents for OA pain. Recommendations from the ACR, American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), and European Society for Clinical and Economic Aspects of Osteoporosis, Osteoarthritis and Musculoskeletal Diseases (ESCEO) support their use in knee, hip, and hand OA, while the OARSI recommends their use primarily in patients without significant comorbidities.72,73 Given potential risks such as cardiovascular, renal, and gastrointestinal toxicity, clinicians must exercise caution, particularly in high-risk populations.

Topical NSAIDs are often preferred for hand and knee OA due to reduced systemic exposure and a more favorable safety profile.74 Non-pharmacological strategies are also core components of guideline-based management. Exercise interventions, including aerobic training, resistance exercises, aquatic therapy, and individualized physiotherapy, have consistently been shown to reduce pain and improve joint stability.75 Weight loss through balanced diets and physical activity further decreases the joint load and inflammatory burden, particularly in knee OA.76 Assistive devices such as orthotic insoles and canes can reduce mechanical stress and enhance mobility when tailored to individual patient needs.77 In addition, patient education and structured self-management programs, including mindfulness and cognitive behavioral therapy, improve coping strategies, treatment adherence, and overall quality of life.78

Conditional/second-line options

Several therapies are recommended conditionally when first line strategies provide insufficient relief. Acetaminophen offers modest analgesic benefit and may be considered when alternative analgesics are limited, although concerns regarding hepatotoxicity and relatively limited efficacy have led organizations such as OARSI to advise against routine use.79

Intra-articular corticosteroid injections provide short-term symptom relief and are conditionally recommended by OARSI and ACR, particularly during symptomatic flares. However, repeated administration requires caution due to potentially adverse effects on cartilage integrity.80

HA injections may improve joint lubrication and viscoelastic properties within the joint. OARSI and EULAR acknowledge possible longer-term benefits in selected patients, whereas AAOS and the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) do not recommend routine use because of inconsistent clinical outcomes across studies.72,81

Emerging/clinical trial therapies

Recent research has focused on disease-modifying therapies that target molecular pathways involved in cartilage degeneration and repair. Lorecivivint and sprifermin represent emerging therapeutic approaches that are currently under clinical investigation.

Lorecivivint is a Wnt/β-catenin pathway modulator designed to restore signaling balance in chondrocytes, thereby reducing cartilage breakdown and joint inflammation. Recent Phase III trials that evaluated lorecivivint in knee OA obtained mixed results. A randomized Phase III study showed that the drug was well tolerated but failed to meet the primary endpoint of pain reduction compared with placebo,82 where it provided symptomatic relief rather than definitive cartilage regeneration. Thus, lorecivivint is still considered a potential disease-modifying osteoarthritis drug but remains under investigation and is not yet approved.

Sprifermin, recombinant human fibroblast growth factor-18, promotes cartilage regeneration by activating fibroblast growth factor receptor-3 signaling, which stimulates chondrocyte proliferation and extracellular matrix synthesis. Phase II clinical trials have demonstrated increases in cartilage thickness, suggesting structural modification, although improvements in clinical symptoms remain variable. These agents represent a shift toward targeted and regenerative therapeutic strategies in OA management.83 Phase III trials were halted as sprifermin had the longest follow-up data period of 5 years and was shown to reduce loss of MRI measured cartilage thickness and pain scores. Thus, sprifermin is an investigational drug under trial.84

Exploratory herbal therapies

Exploratory herbal therapies have increasingly been explored due to their potential anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and chondroprotective effects. Boswellia serrata (boswellic acids) has been reported to inhibit COX-2 activity and reduce cartilage degradation, while curcumin from Curcuma longa modulates inflammatory pathways including TNF-α and NF-κB. Additional botanical agents such as Withania somnifera (ashwagandha), Zingiber officinale (ginger), and Eucommia ulmoides have been associated with improved joint flexibility, reduced inflammatory mediator production, and potential support for cartilage integrity.85 These phytotherapeutic agents are generally well tolerated but current evidence remains limited, and they are best considered adjunctive therapies that may complement standard pharmacological management and potentially reduce reliance on NSAIDs within an integrated OA treatment strategy. Herbal therapies exhibit anti-inflammatory properties in vitro and in animal models but clinical evidence is limited in humans, and often insufficient to support routine use in addition to evidence-based therapies.

Surgical interventions

Surgical management becomes necessary for patients with persistent pain and functional impairment despite optimal conservative treatment. Total joint replacement provides substantial improvements in pain and mobility, where modern prosthetic implants typically last 15–20 years (AAOS, 2023). Surgical choice depends on the severity of joint damage and patient characteristics. Arthroscopic debridement is generally not recommended.86 Younger patients with localized disease may be candidates for partial knee arthroplasty, whereas total knee arthroplasty is often preferred in older individuals with advanced joint degeneration. Advances in biomaterials and prosthetic design continue to improve outcomes, although long-term data are still evolving. Current EULAR guidelines emphasize shared decision making to optimize surgical outcomes by considering patient-specific factors such as body mass index and comorbidities, including diabetes. Management strategies for RA

Core guideline recommendations

Conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) remain the cornerstone of RA therapy by broadly suppressing immune and inflammatory pathways. Methotrexate (MTX) is the preferred first line agent due to its strong evidence base, affordability, and long-term effectiveness, where 30–50% of early-treated patients achieve remission through inhibition of dihydrofolate reductase, impaired lymphocyte proliferation, and anti-inflammatory adenosine release, although hepatotoxicity and gastrointestinal effects may occur.88 Sulfasalazine, often combined with MTX, decreases proinflammatory cytokines via its metabolites sulfapyridine and 5-aminosalicylic acid to improve joint symptoms.89 Hydroxychloroquine is recommended for mild RA because it reduces T-cell activation and may provide cardiovascular benefits, although retinal toxicity requires monitoring.90 Leflunomide, comparable to MTX, inhibits pyrimidine synthesis and provides effective inflammation control, but hepatotoxicity and hypertension necessitate careful supervision, including cholestyramine washout prior to pregnancy.91

Non-pharmacological approaches play vital roles in the holistic management of RA by relieving symptoms, preserving joint function, preventing disability, and improving quality of life while reducing dependence on medications. Maintaining musculoskeletal and bone health is essential because chronic inflammation and corticosteroid use can worsen osteoporosis.92 Adequate nutrition including calcium rich foods such as dairy products, fortified beverages, and leafy greens is important, while a Mediterranean diet abundant in fruits, vegetables, and omega-3 fatty acids may help reduce inflammation and slow disease progression.93 During active joint inflammation, brief periods of rest can reduce synovitis, swelling, and discomfort, and splinting helps stabilize joints and prevent deformities, although prolonged immobilization should be avoided. Exercise remains a cornerstone of RA care, where resistance and aerobic training improve strength, mobility, and overall physical function without harming joints. Weight management is also crucial for reducing cardiovascular risk and mechanical stress.94 Thermal therapies provide symptomatic relief, where heat increases circulation and flexibility, whereas cold reduces inflammation and muscle spasms, and paraffin wax baths are beneficial for both RA and OA by reducing stiffness. Mind–body practices such as yoga, tai chi, and mindfulness lower stress and pain perception, while massage can enhance mobility. Magnet therapy shows limited preliminary benefits and is not yet recommended for routine use.

Conditional/second-line options

For patients with inadequate responses to csDMARDs, biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) offer targeted immunomodulation, including agents that neutralize TNF-α, block IL-6 or its receptor, inhibit T-cell co-stimulation, or deplete B cells.95 TNF-α inhibitors such as infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, golimumab, and etanercept significantly reduce inflammation and joint damage, particularly when combined with MTX, which minimizes anti-drug antibody formation and improves long-term outcomes.96 IL-6 pathway inhibitors like tocilizumab and sarilumab effectively reduce inflammation and normalize ESR and CRP, where sarilumab has a stronger IL-6R binding affinity; however, infections, neutropenia, liver enzyme elevation, and lipid abnormalities require regular monitoring.97

Targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs), particularly JAK inhibitors, represent a major advance by selectively blocking the JAK-STAT pathway involved in cytokine signaling. Tofacitinib (JAK1/JAK3) and baricitinib (JAK1/JAK2) reduce the RA disease activity, joint destruction, and systemic inflammation, and may be used alone or with MTX. After inadequate responses to MTX and TNF inhibitors, JAK inhibitors (e.g., tofacitinib, baricitinib, and upadacitinib) are preferred in patients with persistent moderate to severe disease activity, particularly in ACPA positive or seronegative RA with inadequate TNF inhibitor response, where rapid symptom control and oral administration provide clinical benefit. However, according to ACR and EULAR safety recommendations, JAK inhibitors should be used cautiously or avoided in patients aged ≥65 years, with cardiovascular disease, malignancy risk, or thromboembolic history.98, 99, 100 According to the 2022 recommendations by EULAR, RA management follows a treat to target strategy, aiming to achieve sustained remission or low disease activity, with therapy adjustments if the target is not reached within 3–6 months, and disease activity monitored every 1–3 months during active disease.

Targeted therapies such as JAK inhibitors may be considered after inadequate response to conventional or biologic DMARDs, but careful evaluations of risk factors are required before their use. Sustained remission is typically defined using composite indices such as maintaining DAS28 < 2.6 for at least 6 months.44

Emerging/clinical trial options

Upadacitinib, olokizumab, and filgotinib are advanced targeted therapies for use in the management of RA. Upadacitinib is a tsDMARD that selectively inhibits JAK1, thereby reducing the cytokine-driven inflammatory signaling involved in RA pathogenesis. Upadacitinib has demonstrated significant efficacy in improving clinical symptoms and disease activity, and it is FDA approved and clinically available.101 Olokizumab is a bDMARD that targets IL-6 signaling, a key mediator of systemic and joint inflammation in RA. Trials demonstrated its efficacy but failed to differentiate from existing IL-6 inhibitors (tocilizumab and sarilumab), and Phase III trials indicate its potential to reduce disease activity and improve patient outcomes.102,103 Emerging developments such as stem cell therapies, biological modulation, and smart physiotherapy aim to promote joint regeneration and accelerate recovery. Filgotinib is a preferential JAK1 inhibitor that has been extensively evaluated for RA treatment in Phase IIb (DARWIN) and Phase III (FINCH) clinical trials. The DARWIN 1 trial (NCT01888874) assessed filgotinib in combination with MTX, whereas DARWIN 2 (NCT01894516) evaluated filgotinib as monotherapy. These studies were subsequently followed by DARWIN 3 (NCT02065700), an open-label long-term extension study of the Phase IIb parent trials.104

Exploratory herbal therapies

In addition to pharmaceutical therapies, many patients seek complementary herbal options due to concerns about the side effects of long-term DMARD use. Several herbal medicines exhibit anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory potential. Piper nigrum, rich in piperine, inhibits IL-1β and IL-6, suppresses the AP-1 pathway, and reduces joint damage in experimental models.105 Propolis, a flavonoid-rich resin, reduces paw swelling and cytokine levels in arthritis models.106 Fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum) alleviates the oxidative stress and inflammation associated with RA.107 Feverfew (Tanacetum parthenium) exhibits anti-inflammatory properties, although its clinical effectiveness is inconsistent.108 Ginger (Zingiber officinale) reduces prostaglandin and cytokine production, helping to relieve pain and stiffness.109 Justicia gendarussa exhibits anti-inflammatory and analgesic activities comparable to those of aspirin 110. Curcumin from Curcuma longa improves pain and stiffness in a similar manner to NSAIDs, although with fewer adverse effects, by inhibiting NF-κB and reducing the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6.111 Despite encouraging findings, issues such as inadequate standardization, variable bioactive compound contents, contamination, and quality-control challenges limit the widespread clinical integration of these herbal medicines. Thus, rigorous research and improved regulatory frameworks are required to establish the safety, efficacy, and therapeutic consistency of herbal medicines as complementary strategies in RA management.

Surgical intervention

When pharmacological treatments fail, surgical intervention becomes an important component of RA management. Synovectomy, which involves removing inflamed synovial tissue, is recommended for patients with chronic synovitis that is unresponsive to DMARDs or conservative therapy for six to 12 months, and it can be performed on joints such as the fingers, knees, or hips. Although it helps reduce symptoms and slow joint damage, it does not cure RA, and postoperative physiotherapy is essential for optimal recovery.112 Tendon weakness or rupture common in long-standing RA, especially in the hands and wrists, may require surgical tendon repair to restore stability, correct deformities, and preserve daily functional ability, with early intervention preventing permanent disability. Total joint arthroplasty is advised for patients with severe joint destruction and persistent pain, most commonly in the hips and knees. By replacing damaged surfaces with prosthetic components, arthroplasty significantly reduces pain, improves mobility, and enhances quality of life, where modern implants typically last >10 years.113 Advances in surgical techniques, personalized approaches, minimally invasive procedures, and improved postoperative care have further improved outcomes. Strength and limitations

This review provides a comparative overview of OA and RA, highlighting their distinct etiologies, overlapping clinical manifestations, and key molecular and immunological mechanisms. A major strength of the review is its integrative approach combining pathophysiological insights with current diagnostic strategies and therapeutic options. Evidence is synthesized regarding clinical assessment, imaging modalities, and emerging biomarkers, as well as discussing pharmacological treatments, including NSAIDs, corticosteroids, DMARDs, biologics, and JAK inhibitors. Non-pharmacological interventions such as physical therapy, lifestyle modification and surgical management are also discussed. This broad perspective supports a translational understanding of disease mechanisms and clinical practice.

However, the discussion is limited regarding disease heterogeneity and patient stratification, which may constrain the applicability of diagnostic and therapeutic approaches in the context of personalized care. Emerging biomarkers are highlighted but their accessibility, cost effectiveness, and feasibility in routine clinical practice are not explored because these aspects are beyond the scope of this review. In addition, as a narrative review, this review is subject to certain methodological limitations. A formal systematic search strategy, predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and quality assessment of the included studies were not applied, which may have introduced selection bias and limit reproducibility. In addition, the evidence base largely reflects studies conducted in North America, Europe, and East Asia, potentially restricting the applicability of findings to other regions with different healthcare systems and disease patterns. Furthermore, the underrepresentation of underserved or minority populations in the literature may limit the generalizability of the findings and overlook disparities in the disease burden, healthcare access, and treatment outcomes.

Despite these limitations, this review provides a valuable and integrative synthesis of current knowledge on OA and RA by bringing together knowledge of their pathophysiological mechanisms, diagnostic approaches, and evolving management strategies within a single framework. By highlighting both the similarities and differences between these two major arthritic conditions, this review contributes to improving conceptual understanding, and may support clinicians and researchers in identifying future directions for biomarker development, early diagnosis, and personalized therapeutic interventions.

Conclusion

At present, OA and RA are major musculoskeletal diseases worldwide. They impose a great socioeconomic burden on society due to long-term treatment needs and reduced work productivity. This review provides a clear and balanced comparison of OA and RA, integrating molecular mechanisms, clinical features, diagnostics, and management. The unified, evidence-based approach employed in this review provides translational insights and also highlights key similarities and differences between two diseases. OA treatment remains mainly symptomatic and disease-modifying therapies are still lacking. RA therapies are effective but limited by safety concerns, cost, and variable responses. In conclusion, future research into OA and RA should focus on biomarker-targeted, personalized treatments to achieve sustained disease control and improved quality of life.
