Adult Congenital Heart Disease Workforce Challenges in the United States: Current State and Future Needs
Authors: Ali N Zaidi, Ritu Sachdeva, Richard Kovacs, Wayne J Franklin, Michelle Gurvitz, Peter Ermis, Jordan D Awerbach, Gaby Weissman, Elisa Bradley, Daniel Cox, Curt J Daniels, David Gregg, Abigail Khan, Jeannette P Lin, Larry W Markham, Yuli Y Kim, Clayton Smith, Ayesha Salahuddin, Lowell H Frank, Eric V Krieger, Carole A Warnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe American College of Cardiology, through its Adult Congenital Pediatric Cardiology Council, convened an in-person Adult Congenital Heart Disease (ACHD) Workforce Summit on October 6th and 7th, 2024, in Washington, DC. This report reflects perspectives from ACHD cardiologists across the United States, representing academic and private practice settings, diverse training pathways and all career stages. The Summit aimed to examine drivers of ongoing workforce shortages and to identify actionable solutions. Key challenges were grouped into 4 domains: 1) ACHD fellowship training, exposure, and mentorship; 2) defining and sustaining clinical competencies; 3) professional and financial considerations; and 4) equitable access to quality ACHD care. These proceedings are not formal recommendations but highlight the need to address ACHD workforce challenges through earlier exposure, enhanced mentorship, optimized training pathways, and financial realignment to support a sustainable workforce.Key words: adult congenital heart disease, fellowship training, reimbursement inequities, workforce

For nearly 2 decades, adults have outnumbered children with congenital heart disease (CHD) yet the adult CHD (ACHD) workforce remains insufficient to meet growing demands.1, 2, 3 In 2010, an estimated 2.4 million individuals in the United States were living with CHD, including 1.4 million adults and 1 million children,4 reflecting a sustained demographic transition in which adults surpassed children by approximately a 2:1 ratio.5 This expanding ACHD population has major implications for clinicians and health systems, which must adapt to deliver specialized longitudinal care for a complex and heterogenous group of patients with varying disease severity and lifelong needs.6

Workforce gaps were recognized as early as 2001, when Warnes et al.7 highlighted that adults with CHD were underserved by existing care models. Almost 2 decades later this pattern persists, both due to inadequate supply of board-certified (BC) ACHD cardiologists entering the workforce, and the increasing survival of patients, particularly those with moderate and severely complex CHD.3 By 2023, there were only 509 American Board of Internal Medicine (ABIM) ACHD BC cardiologists, corresponding to an estimated ACHD patient to physician ratio of ∼1:4500.3,8 As of June 1, 2025, there were 500 BC ACHD cardiologists across the U.S. states and territories, of whom only 474 cardiologists held active ABIM ACHD board certification; while 9 ACHD BC cardiologists were practicing outside the United States.9 Most current board-certified ACHD cardiologists (87%) qualified via the now phased-out ABIM “practice pathway”; of these, 105 (21%) are ≥60 years of age, including 17 (3%) ≥70 years, while 11 (2%) have left the workforce.3 This data would suggest that at least 25% can be expected to depart from clinical ACHD roles within the next 10 years, underscoring the need to expand the ACHD pipeline with immediate effect. At the time of publication, the first ABIM ACHD recertification exam (October 2025) had been held, but results were not publicly available; however, the number of BC ACHD cardiologists is postulated to continue to decline.

Multiple factors contribute to the national shortage of ACHD cardiologists in the U.S. (Central Illustration). These include limited early exposure to ACHD during training, the relatively recent establishment of formal ABIM board certification in 2015, prolonged subspecialty training pathways, limited mentorship, misaligned compensation models, and challenges in accessing desirable and well-supported positions.3,10,11 Collectively, these factors may deter trainees from pursuing ACHD and contribute to recruitment and retention challenges, leading to a widening care gap.

To address these challenges, the American College of Cardiology (ACC), through its Adult Congenital and Pediatric Cardiology (ACPC) Council, convened an in-person ACHD Workforce Summit on October 6 to 7, 2024, at the Heart House in Washington, DC. This report summarizes insights from ACHD cardiologists representing diverse training backgrounds, practice environments, and different career stages. Discussions focused on 4 key domains: 1) fellowship training, exposure, and mentorship; 2) defining and sustaining clinical competencies; 3) professional and financial considerations; and 4) equitable access to high-quality ACHD care.

ACHD subspecialty fellowship training

Historical milestones to current status

Training in ACHD for adult cardiology (AC) fellows was first outlined by the ACC in the 1995 Core Cardiology Training Symposium (COCATS) Task Force 9,12 later updated in COCATS II (2002),13 and further expanded in the 32nd Bethesda Conference Task Force recommendations.14 By 2008, recognition of the growing clinical need and fragmented training pathways led the ACC’s ACPC Council to coordinate a multisociety petition to the ABIM and the American Board of Pediatrics (ABP) requesting the establishment of a formal ACHD board certification.15 This led to the approval of ACHD as an ABIM subspecialty in 2012,16 the introduction of a biennial ABIM ACHD board certification exam in 2015,16 and an Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME)-accredited 2-year ACHD fellowship in 2015.3,17 Before these milestones, ACHD fellowship training was inconsistent and often dependent on institutional or grant funding for non-ACGME training positions.18 In 2015, approximately 25 programs offered a non-ACGME ACHD fellowship, most of which (∼78%) had existed for <10 years and had trained fewer than 10 fellows. In total, this resulted in ∼100 to 250 individuals in the United States who had some form of ACHD training.18 Through 2019, ACHD board eligibility could be met by ABIM AC and ABP pediatric cardiology (PC) diplomates without formal ACHD training via a practice pathway that specified clinical competency requirements.3

The introduction of the standardized 2-year ACGME ACHD fellowship marked a significant turning point in the pathway to ACHD board certification, offering structured fellowships with defined competencies and the conceivable potential for more reliable workforce growth. On average, between 2017 and 2025, U.S. ACHD ACGME fellowship programs produced 14 ± 5 graduates each year (Figure 1). Despite the growth in ACGME-accredited ACHD fellowship programs in recent years, several ACGME fellowship positions remain unfilled, as evidenced by the following trends: 44% unfilled (4/9) in 2019, 26% unfilled (6/23) in 2020, and 15% unfilled (3/19) in 2021.19,20 In 2022, only 14 applicants applied nationally, resulting in 40% of positions (9/22) remaining unfilled.20 This trend continued into 2023, with 36% of ACHD fellowship positions remained unfilled (8/22). In 2024, there were 26 fellowship positions offered, with 46% positions unfilled (12/26).20 There was an inflection point in 2025, when 30 fellowship positions were offered in the United States, with 22 positions filled, with 27% unfilled positions (8/30).17 Since 2019, the number of applicants to the ACHD ACGME fellowships has varied annually from 6 in 2019 to 27 in 2025 (Figure 1).19,20 A very small number of ACHD fellowship positions (2-5) (not reflected in Figure 1) are filled outside the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP) Specialties Matching Service (SMS) without materially affecting the overall low ACHD fellowship applicant pool. At the time of publication, there are 31 ACGME-accredited ACHD fellowship programs in the United States, most training one fellow, whereas a select few programs training 2 or more fellows annually or every other year.21 Compared with other ABIM subspecialties, ACHD had few first-time examinees and modest pass rates over the past 5 years: 91% in 2021 (29/32), 81% in 2023 (25/31), and 84% in 2025 (21/25),22 raising concern, although underlying reasons were not discussed and were beyond the Summit’s scope.

Summit participants noted a recent increase in ACHD fellowship applications, representing encouraging progress. However, the longstanding concern remains the low absolute number of trainees and persistently unfilled positions. Although applications have nearly doubled in the most recent cycle, the durability of this trend is uncertain. From a workforce perspective, participants emphasized that the total number of graduates may be more meaningful than traditional fill rates alone. For example, a 50 to 75% fill rate producing 25 graduates may have greater impact than a fully filled match yielding only 15 graduates. Nevertheless, given projected shortages, sustained and deliberate efforts are needed to expand the ACHD training pipeline.

Lack of early exposure and mentorship

Multiple factors contribute to low recruitment into ACHD from AC and PC pathways. Summit participants identified limited early exposure as a key barrier, with many trainees encountering ACHD too late to pursue it intentionally. Earlier and consistent exposure to ACHD, ideally beginning in medical school, was viewed as essential. Despite 231 AC and 65 PC programs participating in Electronic Residency Application Service (ERAS) in 2024,23 yet only a small proportion of cardiology trainees receive meaningful exposure to ACHD during their training years. Early, consistent, and wide-spread exposure to ACHD in both AC and PC fellowship training is also not equal across training programs. There would be potential value in programs with low/no ACHD exposure to partner with established ACHD programs; however, funds, travel, and living away from home may be impractical and limit broad initiation of meaningful ACHD experience from such “away rotations”. Nevertheless, for some trainees, opportunity to more fully explore a high-volume ACHD program through such rotations or funded ACHD scholarships24 may pique interest and contribute, although likely in a small way, to strengthen the pipeline for future ACHD specialists.

Although limited early exposure to ACHD is important, inadequate mentorship is another major contributor to low enrollment. Strong mentorship has been cited as a key factor influencing career choice among ACHD physicians.10 Summit participants emphasized that early, sustained, and structured mentorship is a critical driver of career selection and professional satisfaction, helping guide trainees toward ACHD.25,26 Another consideration raised by Summit participants was the inherent challenge which maybe faced by trainees trained solely in pediatrics (ie, pediatric residency followed by PC) and then transitioning to providing care for adults with CHD. For many, the initial decision to pursue pediatrics and subsequently PC was rooted in a commitment to caring for children, making the shift to an adult-focused clinical environment uniquely challenging and often made later in their training pathway. This creates gaps in early engagement with adult congenital populations and limits consideration of ACHD careers. As mentorship is closely linked to exposure and often concentrated in major centers, participants highlighted the need for coordinated networks, cross-institutional partnerships, and virtual mentorship platforms to broaden access and strengthen the pipeline.24 Novel virtual peer-mentorship models have been described that may expand access to mentorship for trainees interested in cardiology and its subspecialties.27 Such efforts could be adapted for ACHD to strengthen trainee engagement and foster interest in pursuing subsequent ACHD subspecialty training.

ACHD fellowship training and emerging opportunities

The current 2-year ACGME-accredited ACHD fellowship was designed to support trainees from both adult and pediatric backgrounds. Eligible trainees must have completed residency in either internal medicine, pediatrics, or combined internal medicine-pediatrics, followed by either AC or PC fellowship. Although this flexibility is essential for attracting trainees from diverse backgrounds, it also adds to a lengthy postgraduate training burden, with a minimum of 8 to 9 years of training required for ACHD specialization (Table 1). Several potential solutions were discussed to address training duration, with consensus that any effort to shorten training length must maintain necessary exposure to achieve required competencies as outlined by the ACGME. The Cardiovascular Disease (CVD) to ACHD Advancing Innovation in Residency Education (AIRE) Pilot is currently in early rollout by the ACGME following ABIM approval only at a limited number of selected institutions, without broad generalizability. In the CVD–ACHD AIRE pathway, fellows are listed as “active full time” in the CVD program during years 1 to 2. In year 3, they transition to “active part time” in both CVD and ACHD programs as a “hybrid” model, enabling dual ACGME survey participation and milestones reporting. At year 4, fellows are designated as having “completed all accredited training” in CVD and continue as “active full time” in ACHD. On completion of the 4th year, their ACHD status is updated to completed all accredited training thereby allowing completion of total fellowship training within 4 years28 (Table 1). If the protracted pilot efforts demonstrate competency achievement, it is plausible this could be a viable option of shortening total training by 1 year, but currently only related to AC-trained fellowship applicants. A similar proposal was reviewed but not approved by the ABP, rendering PC-trained fellows (including Med-Peds and pediatric residency trained) ineligible for the shortened ACHD pathway outlined previously. Another option, although not broadly discussed at the Summit, was the “3 + 1” pathway, which was more common before the establishment of board certification. In this model, trainees complete 3 years of either AC or PC fellowship followed by 1 year of subspecialty ACHD training, provided that all required competencies are achieved (Table 1). Nevertheless, it remains unknown, whether a shorter pathway will permit ACHD fellows opportunities to develop expanded skills aligned with their interests, whether in research, electrophysiology, cardiac catheterization, advanced cardiac imaging, heart failure, or pulmonary hypertension, while fulfilling all core elements of ACHD training.29 Summit participants recognized that in any model widely adopted which may help to expand the ACHD workforce, core ACGME competencies need to be enforced to preserve training quality and ensure long-term success.30, 31, 32 ABIM has approved a pilot pathway for exceptionally qualified international medical graduates who complete internal medicine residency abroad and an ACGME-accredited fellowship in the United States to become eligible for ABIM certification, subject to the same requirements as traditional pathways;33 however, this was not addressed for ACHD at the Summit.

Clinical competencies

ACGME core competencies: from informal foundations to the formal core of ACHD fellowship

As highlighted in the previous sections, ACHD training has transitioned from informal mentorship to a fully standardized, competency-based subspecialty. Milestones including the 32nd Bethesda Conference, COCATS 4, and subsequent Society of Pediatric Cardiology Training Program Directors (SPCTPD)/ACC/American Heart Association (AHA) training statements have shaped the contemporary framework of ACHD fellowship education.14,34,35 Standardization advanced further with the launch of the 2-year ACGME-accredited ACHD fellowship in 2015,16 however, ongoing concerns about training length must be balanced with the need to maintain rigorous ACGME competency standards. Beyond the scope of the Summit, participants recognized that a major challenge in modifying ACHD training pathways is the need to satisfy the distinct requirements of the ACGME, ABIM, and ABP, each of which plays a different role in training, competency assessment, and board certification. It was stressed that any efforts to align training with workforce requirements or expanding fellowship capacity, needs to enforce ACGME competency-based curricula to ensure that trainees can adequately meet the rising clinical complexity and provide quality ACHD care.

Differences in competencies and training levels between adult cardiology and pediatric cardiology

ACHD is unique among the cardiology subspecialties as fellows may enter from either AC or PC, resulting in both shared yet disparate pathway-specific competency experience. This variability may complicate alignment of expectations during fellowship training, underscoring that “one size does not fit all” (Tables 1 and 2). Although both COCATS 434 and the SPCTPD/ACC/AHA PC statement35 are structured around the ACGME’s six core competencies, they differ in format: AC employs a three-tiered system (levels 1-3), whereas PC uses a dichotomous framework (core vs advanced)34,35 (Table 2). Since advanced (level 3) ACHD training per COCATS 434 requires two additional years of ACGME fellowship (Table 2), defining a level 2 training pathway was discussed as a pragmatic way to expand access to ACHD care, particularly in rural and underserved settings. Summit participants recognized that although other cardiovascular subspecialties have successfully implemented COCATS level 2 pathways, adopting a similar model for ACHD would require coordinated consideration by the ABIM, ABP, and ACGME and was outside the Summit’s purview. However, if pursued, such an approach could help narrow the gap between physician availability and patient needs especially in underserved regions. A dedicated ACC/AHA/SPCTPD ACHD fellowship training document will be published in 2026 and will outline competencies for ACHD fellowship; this was not discussed or was part of the Summit.

Time-based vs competency-based training

Although beyond the scope of the Summit, brief discussions were held on the concept of competency-based medical education,with less emphasis on fixed duration rotations, where advancement depends on validated skill attainment rather than fixed time.31,32 Participants noted that, analogous to the recently proposed CVD–(Clinical Cardiac Electrophysiology) AIRE pathway,36 flexibility in ACHD training structures should be explored if firmly grounded in rigorous, competency-based assessments. Similar to competency-based, integrated “2 + 2” training models as described in CCEP,36 the CVD–ACHD AIRE pilot28 incorporates a hybrid, double-counted third year of CVD toward ACHD, supporting earlier competency attainment and shorter training. Such integrated tracks offer adaptability and efficiency; however, they must be balanced against the value of broader general cardiology exposure and training, which remains critical for many ACHD cardiologists who also practice general cardiology asides from ACHD. Although competency-based medical education allows for achievement of milestones in an individualized manner, there may be logistical challenges, regulatory barriers, and hurdles related to changing the long-standing system set up specifically around time-based metrics∗.

Professional and financial landscape of ACHD

Compensation inequities in ACHD with divergent reimbursement models

A major focus of discussion at the Summit centered on the central role of financial considerations in driving career satisfaction and long-term retention for ACHD cardiologists across both academic and private practice settings. Summit participants recognized that compensation data for ACHD cardiologists remains difficult to define and likely vary by division (adult vs pediatric), institutional structures, and individual job descriptions with limited published information available. Although health care systems recognize the need for subspecialty ACHD care, they lack broadly available data sets to inform normative expectations for productivity and compensation in ACHD. Adherence to noninvasive AC and/or PC metrics for compensation are often significantly inaccurate, and do not reflect the type of workflow expectations of a true ACHD cardiologist. Summit participants emphasized that misaligned reimbursement models, unrealistic productivity expectations, and inadequate compensation may deter trainees and strain practicing ACHD physicians who are part of the current workforce, issues that become especially evident when comparing ACHD with other cardiology subspecialties. In addition, current work relative value units (wRVU) systems undervalue the importance of longitudinal complex congenital care, which may further contribute to recruitment and retention barriers.3,29 wRVUs underestimate ACHD productivity, and despite the 2020 CMS ACHD taxonomy code, inconsistent adoption limits impact; universal use is needed to track activity and define practice patterns. Alternative models (for example, time value units (TVUs) or adjusted salary with appropriately aligned incentives) may better support performance, teamwork, and equity.

Summit participants noted that procedure-based subspecialties offer higher lifetime earnings than ACHD, disadvantaging ACHD physicians despite similar clinical responsibilities.10,37 A 2019 survey of 30 ACHD cardiologists showed salaries in an intermediate range, lower than procedural subspecialties but slightly above general PC.11 Participants highlighted that primary departmental affiliation may further influence compensation, although data are limited.3,10,11,29 Many ACHD physicians also maintain AC or PC duties to meet wRVU expectations, creating misalignment with ACHD care complexity and reducing time for coordination and research, while increasing fatigue risk.10,11,38

Similar to ACHD, multimodality imaging requires additional training yet remains undercompensated, underscoring the need for wRVU-aligned models and stronger advocacy by national and professional societies.39 Financial misalignment emerged as a major Summit theme, with participants noting that inadequate compensation amid workforce shortages and a growing patient population may hinder ACHD recruitment and compromise care. Participants emphasized the need for ACHD-specific reimbursement benchmarks, tailored wRVUs, protected coordination time, and incentives for scholarship,40 while recognizing that building a fair, defensible ACHD compensation framework remains a Rubik’s cube: daunting but not impossible, and beyond the Summit’s scope.

Job satisfaction and readiness for practice

Despite nearly a decade of ACGME-accredited ACHD programs, questions remain about whether current training and employment models adequately support the workforce. Based on a small survey from 2019, the current ACHD ACGME fellowship prepares graduates for clinical care, but trainees have discussed enhanced mentorship, increasing procedural exposure, and integration of advanced skills (such as imaging, cardiac catheterization, advanced heart failure) or even leadership or program development as part of their skill set which may improve their readiness for clinical practice.11 Clinical competency data is primarily limited to recent ABIM ACHD board performance, with a board passing rate as follows: 91% in 2021 (29/32), 81% in 2023 (25/31), and 84% in 2025 (21/25).41 Although informative, these metrics provide an incomplete assessment of readiness for independent clinical practice and long-term professional success. Summit participants agreed that objective measures of job performance and career satisfaction are lacking, making it difficult to evaluate provider competency, job satisfaction, and support for long term professional development. Summit participants emphasized that systematic post-fellowship tracking of graduates could help identify gaps and guide strategies to improve satisfaction, retention, and long-term workforce stability.

A geographically dispersed job market and the absence of a professional home

Although the ACHD job market has grown over the past 2 decades, important challenges persist. Summit participants discussed that a major barrier to workforce sustainability is the lack of geographic diversity in position availability, with most jobs clustered in large urban academic centers.16 This limits opportunities for physicians seeking roles in community or regional settings and contributes to poor access to specialized ACHD care across the country. The lack of a centralized and transparent job network adds to the problem, making it difficult for early-career physicians to find openings and for programs to recruit qualified candidates. Currently, many faculty positions are shared informally by word of mouth, as no dedicated job listing or platform exists, leading to missed opportunities for both applicants and institutions and hindering regional program growth. The absence of a centralized, transparent job network further hinders recruitment, with many positions shared informally, leading to missed opportunities for both applicants and institutions. Participants noted this gap may reflect the lack of a dedicated professional “home” for ACHD. Although organizations such as the ACC ACPC Council, AHA, Adult Congenital Heart Association (ACHA), and International Society for Adult Congenital Heart Disease (ISACHD) have advanced the field, a unified entity could better support research, advocacy, workforce development, and national job infrastructure, improving recruitment and workforce stability.

Impact of workforce on delivery of quality ACHD care

Shortage of ACHD physicians in rural and urban settings

Although ACHD care centers are largely urban, ACHD patients live broadly, and often far from specialized care. Summit participants stressed that asides from the workforce shortage of ACHD physicians, equitable access to quality ACHD care is further limited by geographic and socioeconomic disparities and by patient-facing barriers such as financial strain, poor transition readiness, and low health literacy.42,43 Given the mostly urban-nature of ACHD care centers, clinicians are concentrated in metropolitan centers, leaving many patients, particularly those of lower socioeconomic status to travel long distances, endure extended wait times, or rely on nonspecialized providers in their local areas.16,44 Nearly half of ACHD patients, particularly those of lower socioeconomic status, live 1 to several hours from specialized centers. With the limited availability of ACHD BC cardiologists, many patients remain with pediatric providers lacking adult expertise, transition to adult cardiologists without congenital training, or are lost to follow-up.44 These challenges are amplified in rural hospitals, which often lack comprehensive cardiac services, let alone dedicated ACHD programs, resulting in worse care and outcomes.45,46 Keeping the current workforce numbers in context, Summit participants discussed possible solutions, including a “hub-and-spoke model”,44,47 in which ACHA-accredited ACHD comprehensive care centers (hubs) provide specialized support, guidance, and oversight to community physicians (spokes). This model allows hubs to offer shared care protocols, virtual consultations, comanagement of complex patients, and educational support, extending ACHD expertise while maintaining quality of care. In the presence of the current workforce shortages, telemedicine could extend specialized ACHD care to remote or underserved areas through virtual consultations, remote monitoring, and collaborative management with local providers, helping to mitigate geographic and workforce barriers.44,48 Summit participants also discussed that a COCATS level II designation in ACHD could help address workforce gaps, but would require thorough evaluation of its benefits and limitations, careful planning with input from multiple societies, and remains beyond the Summit’s scope.

Financial strain and health literacy in the context of the ACHD workforce challenges

Lifelong CHD care places significant financial and psychosocial burdens on patients and families,49 with complex cases averaging $2.2 million per patient and an estimated annual U.S. cost of $74 billion.50 Over 3 decades, the aging ACHD population has driven health care use higher, whereas nearly 90% of families face financial strain, worsened by limited insurance access in non-Medicaid expansion states.51, 52, 53 Rural communities in the United States, are known to face higher poverty and likely fewer ACHD providers, limiting access and worsening health care disparities.54 Financial barriers and geographic inequities compound ACHD workforce shortages, although not unique to the field. Participants noted that travel support to ACHD centers may improve access in underserved areas. Although beyond the Summit’s scope, expanding insurance coverage and improving ACHD reimbursement may enhance recruitment, retention, and equitable access to care in underserved areas.40

Participants emphasized that improving health literacy is critical to support adherence, follow-up, and outcomes amid workforce shortages. An estimated 36% of U.S. adults have limited health literacy, with disparities across demographic groups. Although ACHD-specific data are limited, poor health literacy likely contributes to adverse outcomes in this population.55 Summit participants noted that making the system easier to navigate, ensuring accessible records, supporting continuity of care, and expanding access through a hub-and-spoke model, especially in rural areas may help reduce strain on limited ACHD specialists while improving access to quality care.

Leveraging the advanced practice provider in the ACHD work force

Currently, the term "ACHD provider" typically refers to those with physicians with either practice pathway or dedicated fellowship training leading to ABIM board certification. While not discussed in depth at the Summit, participants emphasized the importance of diversifying the ACHD workforce and integrating advanced practice providers (APPs), including nurse practitioners and physician assistants, into ACHD care teams. These strategies are especially important given the ongoing shortage of ACHD-trained physicians in both urban and rural settings. APPs help expand access by managing routine and complex care, improving communication between patients and ACHD cardiologists, and providing appropriate independent care. APPs are an underutilized resource for expanding ACHD care and improving patient access. They can provide close outpatient follow-up, reduce readmissions, and support patients with moderate to complex CHD.44,56 Targeting APPs through close collaborative practice models with ACHD physicians, potentially leveraging the hub-and-spoke model, could rapidly expand the ACHD workforce and extend program reach. Structured training, clearly defined clinical roles, and ongoing oversight would ensure that APPs provide high-quality care while supporting ACHD specialists in managing complex patients. The continued integration of APP in the ACHD work force may help reduce physician workload and support more efficient care delivery, particularly in regions where access to ACHD providers remains limited.

Conclusions

Addressing the growing demand for ACHD providers requires a comprehensive multidimension assessment to better align educational pathways and employment opportunities with workforce needs, ensuring a sustainable and adaptable system for the future. Although beyond the Summit’s scope, potential solutions include shortening fellowship training while preserving core AC and PC competencies. Additional priorities include addressing limited exposure, misaligned reimbursement, productivity pressures, and mentorship gaps, alongside reducing patient financial barriers, improving health literacy, and expanding APP integration to enhance access and outcomes. Although many of these work force challenges are substantial, the AC, PC, and broader ACHD community remain committed to advancing and expanding high-quality, specialized care for adults with CHD.
