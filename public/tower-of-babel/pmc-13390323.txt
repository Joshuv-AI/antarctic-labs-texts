The prevalence of mental health difficulties and mental disorders among children with cerebral palsy
Authors: Manjula Manikandan, Claire Kerr, Jennifer Fortune, Reuel Jalal, Aisling Walsh, Aoife Twohig, Ian McClelland, Jessica Burke, Kimberley J. Smith, Mary Cannon, Michael Walsh, Ruth Kevlin, Jennifer M. Ryan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo investigate prevalence of mental health difficulties and mental disorders among children with cerebral palsy (CP) in Ireland, and identify associated risk factors.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with participants aged 6–17 years with CP. Data were collected via a survey that included demographics, CP characteristics, psychopathology (Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ)), mental disorders, physical well-being, and social factors. Linear and logistic regression were used to explore associations between risk factors and mental health outcomes.Results330 participants completed the survey. The mean (SD) SDQ total difficulties score was 13.4 (6.6), with 29% of participants being at risk for emotional and behavioural problems and 21% diagnosed with at least one mental disorder, of which anxiety was the most prevalent (14%). Higher SDQ total difficulties scores were associated with intellectual disability, poor sleep, challenges in forming friendships, bullying, and family dysfunction (p < 0.05). Female participants and those with low physical activity participation were more likely to experience mental disorders (p < 0.05).InterpretationThese findings highlight the multiple health and social factors associated with mental health difficulties emphasising the urgent need for targeted mental health interventions for young people with CP in Ireland to enhance overall mental well-being.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08167-x.

Introduction

Childhood and adolescence are critical periods for the emergence of mental disorders, with early symptoms often predicting adult mental health outcomes [1, 2]. Poor mental health in adulthood can adversely impact personal, social and economic aspects of life [3]. Children with cerebral palsy (CP) face unique challenges, such as discrimination and poor physical health issues [4] which may negatively affect their mental health [5, 6] and contribute to an increased risk of mental disorders in adulthood [7, 8]

Two reviews highlight a range of estimates for mental health difficulties in children with CP, with prevalence varying from 4% to 67% as measured using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) or Child Behaviour Checklist (CBCL) [9, 10]. More recent studies continue to demonstrate substantial burden, with prevalence estimates of 64% among adolescents with CP in Bangladesh and 30% among children with CP in Australia [11, 12]. Further, data from four studies suggest that young people with CP experience higher rates of mental disorders than their peers without CP including depression and anxiety [6, 12, 13, 14] However, prevalence estimates vary depending on the population studied, age groups included and diagnostic methods used.

However, there is limited evidence regarding risk factors for mental health difficulties and mental disorders in children with CP. In three studies that explored risk factors for mental disorders among children or adolescents with CP [12, 13, 14] girls were more likely to experience anxiety than boys [12] those in Gross Motor Functional Classification System (GMFCS) levels IV/V were more likely to experience any mental disorder than those in levels I-III [14], and children with communication impairment were more likely to have any disorder [13]. However, in one of these studies [14] ID, autism and ADHD as an outcome, which likely do not have the same risk factors as mental disorders that indicate mental illness, such as depression [15].

Only two studies examined risk factors for mental health difficulties, both published in 2008, found that having epilepsy, being in GMFCS levels IV-V, having ID, having pain, having disabled siblings, and living in a town compared to a big city may be associated with a poorer score on the SDQ [16, 17].

Despite growing awareness of mental health difficulties in children and adolescents, there is a lack of research describing mental health difficulties and mental disorders among children and adolescents with CP on the island of Ireland. While studies have examined prevalence of mental health difficulties and mental disorders among children with CP internationally, the majority were conducted several years ago and may not reflect the current context in which children with CP live. Prevalence estimates also vary widely because studies include small samples an often focus on a specific age-groups. Further, the evidence regarding risk factors for mental health difficulties and mental disorders comes from only three studies, two of which were published nearly 20 years ago and focused only on children aged 8–12 years [13, 16, 17]. Many potential risk factors, such as sleep, activity and adverse life events have not been investigated.

An understanding of current prevalence of and risk factors for mental health difficulties and disorders in children and adolescents with CP is needed to improve early intervention of mental health difficulties and prevent the long-term negative impact of experiencing mental health difficulties on the personal, social, and economic lives of people with CP [3].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the prevalence of mental health difficulties and mental disorders, and risk factors for both, among children and adolescents with CP.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was used with data collected using a survey. Data were collected across the island of Ireland. The island of Ireland consists of 32 counties and is divided into the Republic of Ireland (a sovereign state covering 26 counties) and Northern Ireland, which is part of the United Kingdom and covers the remaining 6 counties. The study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist [18].

Participants and recruitment

Eligible participants were children aged 6–17 years with CP and living on the island of Ireland. We shared information about the study through 12 organisations that provide health and social care services or provide advocacy or support services to people with CP. Gatekeepers from these organisations shared information about the study with potential participants by post or in person at clinics, or through newsletters or social media channels. Organisations posted information about the study to approximately 1,200 potential participants. The study was also promoted through social media platforms and a study website.

Data collection

Data were collected from September 2023 to June 2024. Children aged 11–17 years were encouraged to complete the survey independently or with assistance from a parent or guardian. However, parents or guardians could complete it on their child’s behalf if their children aged 11–17 years either preferred not to complete the survey or were unable to do so with support. For children aged 6–10 years, parents or guardians were asked to complete the survey with their child. Completing the survey was considered an indication of consent Participants could choose to complete the survey online (hosted via REDCap), over the phone, in person, or in paper format. All participants were informed that their responses would remain anonymous.

Outcomes

Mental health difficulties were assessed using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [19]. The SDQ is a validated, measure commonly used in research and clinical settings, including with children and adolescents with CP [9, 17]. This questionnaire is available in versions for parents of children aged 4–17 years and for adolescents aged 11–17 years. It contains 25 items across five subscales: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship problems, and prosocial behaviour. Each item is scored on a scale from 0 (“Not true”) to 2 (“Certainly true”), producing subscale scores and a total difficulties score. Total difficulties scores ≥ 17 for the parent-reported version and ≥ 18 for the self-reported version were classified as “high”, indicating elevated mental health difficulties (see Supplementary Material for full scoring details).

In addition to the SDQ, participants were asked if they had ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that they had one of 10 mental disorders, and if so, if they currently have the disorder. Finally, participants were asked whether they had thoughts of harming themselves in the past six months or any instances of self-harm in their life.

Potential risk factors

Potential risk factors assessed were: age, gender, GMFCS level, intellectual disability, pain, pain impact on activity, pain impact on sleep, physical activity participation, sleep quality, sleep hours, excessive adverse life events, bullying victimisation, difficulty making friends, family functioning, autism and ADHD.

The GMFCS, which classifies individuals with CP into five functional mobility levels, with level I representing the highest level of mobility and level V the lowest [20], was self-reported by participants [21]. Questions about pain and its impact on sleep and normal activities over the past 4 weeks were adapted from studies focused on children with CP [22]. Physical activity participation was assessed based on the number of days a child engaged in at least 60 min of exercise, sports, or other physical activities in the past week, as reported in studies on children with CP [6]. Sleep hours were evaluated using questions from the Growing Up Ireland [23]. Sleep quality was assessed through five items from the Groningen Sleep Quality Scale [24, 25], adapted for children with CP [26]. Questions about difficulty making friends and experiences of bullying were derived from the 2016 National Survey of Children’s Health (NSCH) and used in previous studies involving children with CP [5]. Excessive adverse life events were defined as experiencing at least three stressful life events [27]. Family functioning was evaluated using the General Functioning subscale of the McMaster 12-item Family Assessment Device (FAD-GF) [28, 29]. Further information about how each factor was assessed and scored is available in supplemental material 1.

Analysis

Distribution of data was explored using Q-Q plots and cross-tabulations. Data were summarised using mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range, minimum, maximum, counts and frequencies, as appropriate. Analyses were conducted with the data available and no data were imputed.

Linear regression was used to examine associations between potential risk factors and total difficulties scores on the SDQ. Logistic regression was used to explore associations between potential risk factors and the presence of at least one mental disorder. Potential risk factors of interest are described in supplemental material 1.

Initially, univariable models were fitted to examine unadjusted associations between each potential risk factor and outcomes. Multivariable models were then fitted including all potential risk factors. In multivariable analyses, pain presence only was included and impact of pain on sleep and activity were excluded. Assumptions of linear regression were checked by plotting Q-Q plots of residuals and scatterplots of residuals against fitted values.

A sensitivity analyses was conducted by repeating linear regression analyses on parent- and self-reported total difficulties scores separately. Linear and logistic regression analyses were repeated excluding people with self-reported diagnosis of intellectual disability, autism or ADHD.

Results

Surveys were completed by 330 participants (Table 1). 21% (n = 68) were completed by the young person alone, 10% (n = 34) were completed by the young person with support from a parent, and 69% (n = 228) were completed by a parent. The mean (SD) age of participants was 11.6 years (3.2) (minimum 6; maximum 17 years). The majority were male (64%) and from the Republic of Ireland (88%). Most were in GMFCS levels I (31%) and II (36%). 13% had autism and 7% had attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).

Physical health, family and social life

Over the past 12 months, 67.3% of participants experienced pain, with 31% reporting that it moderately to considerably affected their daily activities and 23.3% reporting that it moderately to considerably affected their sleep (supplemental Table 2). Most participants (74%) engaged in physical activity, with 44% doing so one to three days per week. The mean (SD) sleep duration was 8.5 (1.6) hours, with 22% reporting poor sleep quality. Two-thirds experienced difficulty making friends and 48% reported being bullied in the past 12 months. Mean (SD) family functioning as measured by the FAD-GF was 1.66 (0.52), below the 2.0 threshold that indicates ineffective family functioning.

Prevalence of mental health difficulties

The mean (SD) total difficulties score for the total sample was 13.4 (6.6) (available score range 0–40), with 29% having a high or very high total difficulties score (Table 2). Mean scores were highest for the hyperactivity and emotional problems subscales. Across domains, 12% of the total sample had a high or very high score for conduct problems, 20% for hyperactivity, 37% for peer problems and 38% for emotional problems. For the parent-reported SDQ (n = 217), 33% reported a high or very high total difficulties score. For the self-reported SDQ (n = 99), 20% reported a high or very high total difficulties score (supplemental Table 3).

Prevalence of mental disorders

21% of participants had a diagnosis of ever experiencing at least one mental disorder; 17% had a current diagnosis (Table 3). Anxiety disorder was the most common diagnosis (14%). For psychotic-experiences, 3.4% reported visual hallucinations, 3.4% reported spying and 4.4% reported auditory hallucinations (supplemental Table 4). Spying and auditory hallucinations were more prevalent in self-reported surveys than parent-reported surveys (p < 0.05). 14% reported thoughts of self-harming in past 6 months and 7% reported instances of self-harm ever.

Associations with mental health difficulties

In unadjusted analyses, intellectual disability (β = 5.60, 95% CI 4.10 to 7.09, p < 0.001), GMFCS level II vs. I (β = 3.32, 95% CI 1.59 to 5.04, p < 0.001), GMFCS level IV vs. I β = 3.77, 95% CI 1.02 to 6.51, p = 0.007), pain (β = 3.08, 95% CI 1.58 to 4.58, p < 0.001), pain that impacted activity (β = 2.85, 95% CI 1.18 to 4.52, p < 0.001), pain that impacted sleep (β = 3.81, 95% CI 2.05 to 5.58, p < 0.001), poor sleep quality (β = 3.21, 95% CI2.57 to 3.86, p < 0.001), bullying (β = 3.17, 95% CI 1.74 to 4.60, p < 0.001), difficulty making friends (β = 6.30, 95% CI 4.93 to 7.67, p < 0.001), poor family functioning (β = 2.77, 95% CI 1.39 to 4.15, p < 0.001), autism (β = 6.74, 95% CI 4.78 to 8.69, p < 0.001), and ADHD (β = 7.30, 95% CI 4.57 to 10.03, p < 0.001) were associated with higher total difficulties scores on the SDQ (Table 4). Associations with mental disorders

In unadjusted analyses, age (OR = 1.11, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.21, p = 0.016), GMFCS level V vs. I (OR = 2.75, 95% CI 1.21 to 6.24, p = 0.016), intellectual disability (OR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.89 to 3.75, p = 0.011), pain impacting sleep (OR = 2.02, 95% CI 1.07 to 3.82, p = 0.030), sleep quality (OR = 1.74, 95% CI 1.30 to 2.32, p < 0.001), difficulty making friends (OR = 3.62, 95% CI 2.06 to 6.37, p < 0.001), family functioning (OR = 1.72, 95% CI 1.02 to 2.88, p = 0.042), autism (OR = 4.38, 95% CI 2.23 to 8.62, p < 0.001) and ADHD (OR = 4.45, 95% CI 1.84 to 10.77, p = 0.001) were associated with a higher odds of having a mental disorder (Table 5). In unadjusted analyses, being physically active (OR = 0.22, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.40, p < 0.001) and longer sleep duration (OR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.90, p = 0.002) were associated with lower odds of a mental disorder (Table 5). In the adjusted analysis, girls had higher odds of developing a mental disorder (OR = 3.16, 95% CI 1.39 to 7.21, p = 0.006). In addition, children engaging in physical activity on 4–6 days per week (OR = 0.09, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.74, p = 0.025) or daily (OR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.01 to 1.07, p = 0.058) had lower odds of developing a mental disorder compared to people who did not participate in physical activity.

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analyses, similar results were observed when parent-reported and self-reported SDQ were included as the dependent variable in separate analyses (supplemental Table 5). However, some differences were evident in comparison to the primary analysis. In adjusted analysis, intellectual disability, poorer sleep quality, poorer family functioning were still associated with a higher parent-reported SDQ total difficulties score, but not, bullying and difficulties making friends. In adjusted analysis, bullying and difficulties making friends were associated with higher self-reported SDQ total difficulties score, while intellectual disability, poorer sleep quality, poorer family functioning were not. However, the sample included in this analysis was considerably smaller than the total sample (n = 77).

When children with Autism, ADHD, and ID were excluded (supplemental Tables 6 and 7), the adjusted analysis showed poor sleep quality and bullying were associated with higher SDQ total difficulties scores, while difficulty making friends and family functioning were not associated with SDQ total difficulties scores. In adjusted analysis, poorer family functioning was associated with higher odds of mental disorder, while gender and physical activity participation were not associated with presence of a mental disorder.

Discussion

This study provides insights into the mental health difficulties experienced by children and adolescents with CP on the island of Ireland, revealing 29% of this population are at risk of emotional or behavioural problems and 21% have a diagnosis of at least one mental disorder. The study identified factors associated with mental health difficulties, namely intellectual disability, poor sleep quality, bullying, difficulties making friends, and poor family functioning. Factors associated with mental disorders were female sex and participation in physical activity less than four times per week.

The prevalence of mental health difficulties, as indicated by the total difficulties score on the SDQ, slightly lower than the pooled prevalence of 35% reported in a 2018 systematic review [9] higher than the 21% reported in a European study in 2008 [30], but similar to the 30% reported in a sample of Australian children and adolescents with CP in 2020 [12]. Context, including time and geography, is important to consider when comparing prevalence with previous studies. There is limited data on the prevalence of mental health difficulties in children without CP in Ireland. A national survey of children and adolescents in Northern Ireland in 2019/20, found a lower prevalence of mental health difficulties, at 11%, and a substantially lower prevalence of high scores across all domains, from 3% for peer problems to 15% for hyperactivity [31]. However, a study from the Republic of Ireland found that 35% of adolescents had mental health difficulties in 2021 [32]. The higher prevalence in the latter study could be due to the older age group and data collection occurring during the COVID-19 pandemic.

with the prevalence of at least one mental disorder in this study was similar to the 15% reported by adolescents without CP in Ireland [33]. On comparison to studies of children with CP, this study found a higher prevalence of mental disorders than a Danish study of adolescents [14] and a Norwegian study of children [13], but lower than recent studies of the prevalence of clinically significant anxiety in Australian children [12], of depression and anxiety in children with CP in the US [6]. Access to and availability of child and adolescent mental health services, particularly for young people with CP, likely influences the identification and diagnosis of mental disorders and should therefore be considered when interpreting these prevalence findings.

Poor sleep quality, ID, experiencing bullying in the past 12 months, difficulty making friends and family functioning were associated with a higher total difficulties score in this study. Poor family functioning has been shown to be associated with depression, but not anxiety, in young adults with CP [34]. A previous study also demonstrated that bullying and difficulty with friendships may partly explain the increased prevalence of anxiety and behaviour/conduct problems in children with CP relative to children without CP [5].

Only gender and reduced participation in physical activity were associated with presence of any mental disorder after adjusting for other potential risk factors. Physical activity attenuates the association between CP and depression and anxiety, suggesting that inactivity partly explains the increased risk of these disorders in children with CP, even when controlling for other potential risk factors such as age, gender, autism, and ADHD [5]. Although we looked at associations with any mental disorder, anxiety was the most commonly reported disorder, which may explain why we found girls had a higher prevalence of any disorder, in keeping with other research showing girls with CP are more likely than boys to have anxiety [12]. Anxiety accounting for the majority of disorders, may also explain why pain was not a strong predictor of any mental disorder. Pain is associated with depression but not anxiety in young adults with CP [34]. Pain also appears to account for the increased prevalence of depression in CP compared to children without CP, but does not attenuate the association between CP and anxiety [6].

Our findings revealed that 20% of children with CP reported high or very high scores in the hyperactivity domain of the SDQ, yet only 7% had been formally diagnosed with ADHD, despite the well-documented comorbidity of ADHD in children with CP [35]. This gap in diagnosis highlights the importance of early screening for ADHD in children with CP to prevent significant impairments. In addition, these findings reinforce previous assertions that tailored physical activity promotion, consistent nutrition, and sleep together are important to promote health and well-being among individuals with CP [36]. Poor sleep can exacerbate behavioural problems, and overall mental distress [37, 38]. Adolescents with CP often face unique challenges related to sleep, such as discomfort due to physical impairments, which can disrupt sleep quality [26]. There is consistent evidence that children with CP often participate in little physical activity [39] and face multiple barriers to identifying inclusive and accessible activities [40].

Social challenges significantly impact on the mental health of children and adolescents with CP. More than physical impairments, bullying, difficulties making friends and poor family functioning contribute to mental health difficulties, that may progress without intervention and support. This builds on previous research which highlights that difficulties with social relationships and bullying are linked to mental health difficulties in young people with CP [5, 41]. These findings once again emphasise the need for holistic care approaches that take social and relational factors into account. One approach could be utilising systemic therapies [42]. Systemic psychological therapies consider the social system that a person lives in, the important people in that system (e.g., family, supporters, teachers) and the impact that interactions have on the psychological wellbeing of people in that system. Previous work has also suggested that taking a systemic approach to formulation in young people with CP is important to help to develop targeted intervention [41]. Our findings also highlight that family therapy could be a useful intervention for young people with CP and their families [43]. Therapeutic support that focuses on parental mental and physical health, parental confidence in their parenting capacity, sibling wellbeing, and family empowerment, could help prepare families for the future. Supporting the physical health and mental well-being of parents and all members of the family will in turn have a positive impact on the health and well-being of the child or adolescent with CP [44, 45].

In this study, adverse life events were not significantly associated with mental health outcomes. One possible explanation may be that individuals with CP already experience a higher baseline exposure to chronic stressors such as pain, functional limitations, and social discrimination. As a result, additional adverse life events may have a comparatively smaller incremental impact on mental health than is typically observed in the general population.

Limitations

While this study provides significant insights into the mental health of children and adolescents with CP in Ireland and developed by experts by experience and multi-disciplinary research team, several limitations must be acknowledged. Although the sample is largely representative of the CP population in terms of type of CP and GMFCS level, as described by data from the Northern Ireland CP Register, there was a smaller proportion of children with vision, hearing and communication impairment in the sample. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between risk factors and mental health outcomes. Reverse causality is plausible, with mental health difficulties or disorders resulting in changes in physical and social factors. Furthermore, the lack of direct comparisons with a group of children without CP in the same age range, assessed at the same time and using the same method, prevents us from determining if children with CP are at particular risk of mental health difficulties; although this has been established in previous studies [5]. Despite being the most widely used questionnaire of mental health difficulties in CP, the SDQ may conflate problems of neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism, with mental health difficulties [15]. To explore the impact of this on associations, we excluded children with ID, autism and ADHD in sensitivity analyse. We found that the conclusions largely remained the same with poor sleep and bullying risk factors for poor mental health existing in children with CP without ID, autism and ADHD. Future research should focus on understanding the developmental trajectory of mental health difficulties in adolescents with CP. Understanding this alongside the development of risk factors for mental health difficulties may identify key points for providing intervention and support and preventing long-term consequences of experiencing mental health difficulties.

In conclusion, this study reveals significant mental health challenges among children and adolescents with CP, with 29% at risk of emotional or behavioural problems and 21% diagnosed with a mental disorder. Key risk factors for mental health difficulties are bullying, difficulties making friends, poor sleep quality, poor family functioning and ID. Girls and those who participate in physical activity less than four times per week are more likely to have a diagnosis of a mental disorder. These findings highlight the need to take a holistic approach to the management of CP, which includes addressing the family’s needs, and promoting sleep and physical activity in order to benefit young people’s mental health.
