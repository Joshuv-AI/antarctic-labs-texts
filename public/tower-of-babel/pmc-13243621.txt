Simulation-driven experiments resolve throttle pedal vibration issues
Authors: Xinxing Liu, Fuwu Yan, Xiaorong Li, Pinbin Zhao, Yuhu Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the resolution of accelerator pedal vibration issues during horn activation in vehicle tuning through finite element analysis combined with real-world test data. For certain facelift models experiencing NVH challenges with pedal vibrations upon horn usage, simulation identified a pedal bracket modal frequency of 355.9 Hz, while experimental vibration transfer function (VTF) measurements from horn to pedal mounting points revealed a resonant peak in acceleration response—suggesting coupling between the horn excitation frequency and pedal bracket modal characteristics. Given the prohibitive cost of modifying production-specification horns or retooling existing pedal bracket molds, this research developed an innovative approach: comparative analysis between experimentally measured and simulated VTF curves was conducted to identify structural stiffness deficiencies along the transmission path from front longitudinal beam horn mounting points to firewall accelerator pedal attachments. Simulation-driven optimization pinpointed design improvements which were subsequently validated through physical testing. Most existing studies focus on the structural optimization of the pedal itself, with little discussion on the influence of accessory mounting points (e.g., horn) on pedal vibration. This study fills this gap and introduces how to select appropriate accessory mounting positions to break the vibration transmission path and reduce pedal-induced vibration at the target frequency. The implemented solution achieved an 88.4% vibration reduction (from 544.8 mm/s2 to 63.3 mm/s2), with significant subjective improvement in pedal vibration perception during horn operation. This simulation-testing integrated methodology demonstrates substantial cost savings and development cycle acceleration for in-production vehicle NVH refinement.Keywords: Experiment intertwined with simulation, Path of transmission, Frequency, Resonance, The accelerator pedalSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The automobile body, serving as a vessel, possesses a distinctive design that readily transmits external vibrations and noise into the cabin, thereby giving rise to various NVH issues1–3. The throttle pedal vibration is a quintessential NVH issue that compromises both driving comfort and safety, where insufficient stiffness at the pedal bracket mounting points or coupling with excitation frequencies from sources like the engine induces undesirable oscillations4–6, the prolonged tension in the driver’s calf and ankle muscles can lead to soreness or numbness, while the vibrations may be misinterpreted as signs of impending vehicle instability—particularly intensifying panic and anxiety during overtaking acceleration or in congested traffic. With technological advancements, a surge of innovative methods has emerged to analyze and optimize automotive NVH performance. For instance, the integration of AI and machine learning expedites analytical processes through tools, thereby shortening development cycles7. Through experimental and simulation methods, swiftly pinpoint the root cause of issues, then leverage simulations to propose effective solutions—validating these through testing to ultimately resolve real-world vehicle challenges8–10. Currently, during the development of new vehicle models’ NVH (Noise, Vibration, and Harshness) performance by many automakers, simulation engineers collaborate with product design engineers in the early design phase to ensure the designed product meets NVH standards. However, due to current limitations in simulation technology and the inability to provide actual operating condition parameters upfront, the models and computational scenarios established are based on idealized conditions. This makes it impossible to guarantee the avoidance of all structural NVH issues during the early design stage. Consequently, during the later prototype tuning phase, structural vibration and noise problems—such as insufficient stiffness at certain locations or improperly avoided structural frequencies—still emerge11,12.

In response to the aforementioned issue, this paper presents an experimental-simulation approach to address noticeable throttle pedal vibrations detected during NVH fine-tuning of a facelifted vehicle model when honking in stationary conditions. Through simulation to solve the characteristic equation13,14, obtain the vibration transfer function curve, and validate the reliability of the simulation data by combining experimental results. Through simulation optimization methods (ODS), structural issues in vehicle bodies can be swiftly identified and effective solutions proposed. The factory collaborates with simulation engineers to produce prototypes, which are then validated by development personnel through real-world vehicle testing.

Frequency recognition of issues

Testers measured the frequency response curve of the vehicle’s horn to the accelerator pedal (VTF) and observed a pronounced peak at 355Hz, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Finite element analysis revealed the bracket mode of the accelerator pedal to be 355.9Hz (Fig. 2), confirming that the horn’s excitation frequency closely matched the bracket’s natural frequency, resulting in resonance. However, to minimize costs without altering the bracket mold, this study employed simulation to construct a virtual physical model. Through simulation techniques, the research optimized structural stiffness in weaker design areas along the transmission path—from the front longitudinal beam of the vehicle body to the accelerator pedal mounting point on the front bulkhead lower panel—thereby reducing the amplification of excitation forces.

Finite element modeling

The current mesh modeling software is ANSA version 24.1.0. Since the speaker bracket is mounted on the front longitudinal beam thrust block, the excitation source is directly transmitted to the vehicle interior through the longitudinal beam. Considering the accuracy and computational efficiency between the calculation model and the actual vehicle model, this modeling adopts the TB model. The model is composed of the body-in-white, battery pack, four doors and two hoods, steering system, front subframe (the rear subframe is generally flexibly connected to the body and thus excluded), seats, interior and exterior trims, electronic components, and accessories.

To optimize computational resources while maintaining simulation accuracy, industry best practices suggest modeling easily extractable mid-surface sheet metal or plastic components as 8mm×8mm shell elements. For structurally complex or mission-critical areas, finer 5mm×5mm meshes should be employed. The surface mesh predominantly consists of quadrilateral elements, with triangular elements not exceeding 5% of the total. For complex structural components where mid-surface extraction proves challenging—such as door hinges, steering gears, and steering wheel skeletons—a hybrid approach employing 5mm×5mm hexahedral meshes and second-order tetrahedral elements is adopted. When performing mesh generation, add washers around holes larger than 5mm in diameter to prevent stress concentration. For holes smaller than 5mm, those at non-connection locations can be removed as they are insignificant. Additionally, eliminate fillets with radii smaller than 3mm.

The various components of the car body are connected through welds, bolts, adhesives, and spot welds. Welds, adhesives, and spot welds are simulated using RBE3-HEXA-RBE3 elements, while bolts are simulated with RBE2 elements. The hinges of the four doors and two lids are connected to the vehicle body by releasing degrees of freedom in the motion direction through RBE2 elements, while components such as latches, sealing strips, bumpers, and stoppers are assigned stiffness values in each direction via bush elements, with local coordinate systems established accordingly. The front subframe, seats, steering system, and battery pack were rigidly connected to the vehicle body using RBE2 elements for simulation17. For non-critical load paths or components with minimal dynamic impact—such as electronic devices, certain interior/exterior trims, and accessories—their mass properties are simulated by establishing concentrated mass points at their respective centers of gravity. The vehicle body structure was appropriately simplified according to the aforementioned method, with this model comprising 2,524,881 shell elements and 5,185,748 solid elements.

Model computation and post-processing

The simulation results obtained through the Optistruct 2020.1(Altair, USA. https://altair.com/hyperworks) solver were post-processed using META 2024.1(BETA CAE Systems, GR. https://www.beta-cae.com/meta) software, yielding comparative curves between simulation and experimental data as shown in Fig. 5. The graph reveals that the overall trends of the simulated and tested curves align closely, with both exhibiting pronounced peaks around 355Hz. However, due to inherent limitations where traditional simulation models cannot perfectly replicate actual vehicle prototypes—coupled with simplifications in certain modeling approaches and inevitable discrepancies in material parameters—some deviations in amplitude and frequency emerge between the simulated outcomes and physical test results.

Simulation experiment comparison curve.

According to the ISO 2631 standard for human vibration evaluation, the peak acceleration near 355 Hz in the simulation curve significantly exceeds the fatigue threshold of 0.15-0.3 g (approximately 150-300 mm/s2). This vibration intensity will cause obvious perception and long-term fatigue in the driver, severely affecting operational comfort and safety. Therefore, the simulation results can fully predict real-vehicle issues.

Root cause analysis and remedial measures

From the simulation curve, it is evident that the loudspeaker mounting position exhibits a significant peak under 355Hz excitation. Preliminary analysis suggests that the excitation force from the loudspeaker mounting location

transmits to the accelerator pedal through three primary paths: the Shutgun, front longitudinal beam, and front subframe, as illustrated in Fig. 6. During the force transmission process, modal coupling with certain structural components along the path may amplify the forces. To intuitively and clearly identify the specific locations where excitation forces get magnified, the simulation employs Operational Deflection Shape (ODS) analysis to examine and optimize vibration contour maps under specific frequencies and operating conditions19,20.

As shown in Fig. 7, through transfer path analysis of the excitation force at the speaker mounting points, it was found that Path 2 is the primary force transmission path, while Path 1 is the path with minimal force transmission. Figure 8 demonstrates the screening of candidate mounting points based on force transmission contribution ratio. Three speaker mounting points were selected from three paths in the simulation model as excitation sources, and the VTF response curves from each speaker mounting point to the throttle pedal response point were calculated respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 9, the curves indicate that Path 1 contributes the least to force transmission, with point1 showing the most significant optimization effect.

The mounting position is situated on the thrust block at the front end of the front longitudinal beam. The thrust block is installed on the vehicle body for crash safety considerations, designed to induce lateral displacement of the body structure during frontal collisions. As a result, the mounting surface is typically positioned far from the center of gravity. As shown in Fig. 11, the distance from the thrust block’s center of gravity to the mounting surface is 40 mm. The material is cast iron, weighing 3.5 kg, and is connected to the vehicle’s longitudinal beam with only three bolts. The thrust block’s center of gravity is positioned far from the plane formed by the three mounting points, creating a cantilevered structure. During rotation or movement, this configuration generates additional torque, causing the excitation from the horn to induce vibrations in the thrust block, which are then amplified into the body’s response. This results in noticeable shaking of the accelerator pedal when the horn is pressed21,22.

Center of gravity to mounting surface diagram.

Insufficient stiffness of sheet metal components, aging seals, or loose connections can lead to localized vibration amplification. In automotive construction, brackets serve as crucial structural elements that connect, support, or secure key components. As illustrated in Fig. 12, relocating the horn’s mounting point on the vehicle body to a stiffer position—where the installation site is welded to the Shutgun, longitudinal beams, and front strut towers—creates a tightly integrated, box-like high-stiffness structure through robust overlapping with surrounding components.

Multi-directional analysis and verification

During a frontal collision, vehicle body loads are primarily transmitted along the path of longitudinal beams \to front bulkhead \to A-pillar/B-pillar. The newly installed mounting point is precisely located at the overlapping region between the front bulkhead and longitudinal beams, forming an integral part of the main load transmission path rather than a vulnerable bypass point. Given the horn’s extremely low mass (0.4 kg), its inertial load during a collision is significantly lower than that of the vehicle body structure itself. Consequently, the horn imposes no additional burden on the main load path and does not alter the overall logic of collision energy management.

Under static operating conditions, the test engineer adjusted the mounting position of the horn on the vehicle body according to the simulation plan and measured the VTF curve from the horn mounting point to the accelerator pedal, as shown in Figure 13. Objective evaluation revealed a significant reduction in peak values, while subjective assessment indicated noticeable improvement in accelerator pedal vibration.

Under dynamic operating conditions, the excitation frequency range of the motor and road surface covers a wide spectrum, making frequency-based avoidance impractical. Therefore, experimental validation was conducted to evaluate the in-vehicle NVH performance during steady-state operation at 60 kph on rough cement pavement and under acceleration conditions.

A comparative analysis was conducted on the noise curves under two driving conditions: constant-speed cruising at 60 km/h and acceleration to 118 km/h on rough cement pavement. As illustrated in Figs. 14, 15, 16, and 17, after modifying the front and rear mounting points of the speaker, no abnormal peaks were observed in the noise curves of either the front or rear rows, and the overall sound pressure level (SPL) exhibited no significant fluctuations. Combined with subjective evaluation, the interior road noise remains at a normal level, which is equivalent to the performance of previously accepted vehicles.

Impact of errors

The mounting points for speakers around high-stiffness zones are predominantly made of cold-rolled mild steel (Q235), with a thickness tolerance of ±0.08 mm after stamping. By adjusting the thickness of surrounding components in the simulation model, the VTF (Vibration Transfer Function) curve from the speaker mounting points to the accelerator pedal was calculated. As illustrated in Figs. 22 and 23, the analysis indicates that within the allowable range of manufacturing tolerances, the peak at 355 Hz fluctuates by 0.4% around 149 mm/s2, which is below the human perception threshold specified in ISO 2631.

Cost savings

By relocating the speaker mounting position to a high-stiffness region with no surrounding interference, the machining cost for drilling an M8 bolt hole at the new mounting location is only 3-5 RMB per hole (including tooling adjustments). Even with subsequent minor adjustments to the robot bolt-tightening program, the total cost per vehicle remains extremely low.

In contrast, redesigning the speaker bracket and developing new molds would involve full structural development. Automotive sheet metal/plastic injection mold costs typically range from 20,000 to 80,000 RMB, with complex structures costing even more, and the development cycle would be extended by one month.

Conclusion

This paper investigates the throttle pedal vibration issue encountered during prototype vehicle calibration. The reliability of the simulation model is verified using experimental data, and the root cause is quickly identified through simulation optimization. A multi-directional validation is conducted by relocating the speaker to a high-stiffness region, which ultimately resolves the real-vehicle issue, with the workflow illustrated in Fig. 24. By systematically documenting such failure mechanisms, engineers can establish target values for the system in the early design stage to mitigate NVH risks, thereby laying a foundation for subsequent NVH development and improving overall development efficiency.
