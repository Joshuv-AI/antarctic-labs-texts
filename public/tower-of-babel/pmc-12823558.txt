Postwar wasting and associated factors among children aged 6–59 months at Adigrat Town, Eastern Tigray, Northern Ethiopia, 2025: a community-based survey
Authors: Abadi Hailay Atsbaha, Teshome Teklay Tareke, Hadera Hailu Gebreanenia, Medhin Mehari, Yonas Angaw, Embay Amare Alemseged, Hagos Degefa Hidru
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, wasting in children aged 6–59 months remains a critical public health challenge, experiencing significant regional and socioeconomic variations, and is most prevalent in low and middle-income countries where resources are limited. However, there is a scarcity of evidence related to wasting among 6–59 months aged children after the cessation of hostility in Northern Ethiopia, Tigray, particularly in Adigrat town. Hence, this study aimed to assess the postwar wasting and related factors among 6–59 months aged children in Adigrat town, Eastern Tigray, Northern Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 325, 6–59 months aged children from January 8 to February 20, 2025. Participants were selected using simple random sampling. A pretested interviewer-administered questionnaire and Mid-Upper Arm circumference direct measurements were used for data collection. Epi data version 4.4.2 was used for data entry and then exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were done for the frequency. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analysis were conducted to identify factors associated with wasting of 6–59 months aged children. A p-value of < 0.05 along with a 95% confidence interval was used to declare statistical significance.ResultsThe prevalence of wasting among 6–59 months aged children was found to be 17.5% (95% CI, 13.5–21.8), with 12.3% being moderately and 5.2% severely wasted. Squeezing out first milk [AOR = 3.2; 95% CI: 1.471–6.998], not still breastfeeding [AOR = 2.7; 95% CI: 1.295–7.729], and consuming only cereals [AOR = 3.5; 95% CI: 1.162–10.676] were found to be more likely of wasting among the children aged 6–59 months compared to their counterparts.ConclusionA significant proportion of 6–59 months aged children were wasted. Squeezing out initial milk, not still breastfeeding, and consuming cereals solely were all strongly linked to wasting among the children. Supporting and enhancing child feeding policies and practices that support healthy supplemental feeding practices as well as exclusive breastfeeding.Keywords: Wasting, Children, Postwar, Adigrat town, Postwar wastingSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Malnutrition is a significant global health issue characterized by inadequate or excess nutrient intake, leading to various health complications. It is classified into different types, including protein-energy malnutrition and micronutrient deficiencies, with severe implications for vulnerable populations, particularly children and expectant mothers1–3. Globally, there are approximately 15 million malnutrition cases, with an age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) of 2006.4 per 100,000 population4. The worldwide prevalence of malnutrition for children aged 6–59 months remains a significant public health concern, particularly in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) with limited resources. Of the 6–59 months old children in the LMICs, around 28.4% are stunted, 12.3% are underweight, and 5.4% are wasted5.

In children, wasting is the most lethal form and represents the most severe type of acute malnutrition, posing significant health risks, particularly in children under five. It is both preventable and treatable, primarily caused by insufficient nutrition and recurrent illnesses like diarrhea and malaria, which compromise the immune system and elevate mortality risk6,7. Approximately 45 million children globally are affected by wasting, with 13.6 (7.3%) million classified as severely wasted, contributing to one in five child deaths worldwide6. Survivors of severe wasting often face heightened vulnerability to illnesses and developmental delays6.

The prevalence of child wasting in India is estimated to be 17.3% with a significant regional discrepancy across the states, ranging from 5.8% to 29.1%8. The national waste for children in Indonesia is 7.7%9, and in Bangladesh is 8%10, Yemen is 16%, and Turkey is 1.7%11. In addition, wasting in Morocco is estimated to be 9.3% with the dominance of severe wastage (6.5%) over the moderate waste rate (2.8%)12. However, in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), waste is approximately 7.09% with the rate of severely wasted at 2.12% and moderately wasted at 4.97%13. The pooled prevalence of wasting in Ethiopia is 11% with a significant regional variation within the country, ranging from 6.2% in Debre Tabor town, Amhara region14 to 28% in the Habro district, Eastern Ethiopia15, 33% in Debre Berhan town, Ethiopia16, and 36.4% in Wonago district, Southern Ethiopia7. While in Kersa, Oromia region is 16.8%17, Wolkite town, Gurage zone, Southern Ethiopia is 14.7%18, Dabat district, Northwest Ethiopia, is 18.2%19, Gedio zone, Southern Ethiopia, is 10%20 and Wukro town, Tigray region, is 7.2%21.

The health and other dimension consequences of undernutrition, primarily wasting, are significant public health burdens. Undernutrition significantly contributes to morbidity and mortality across various demographics, particularly among children aged 6–59 months. It is a leading factor in child mortality, especially in developing countries, where it exacerbates the effects of infectious diseases. Globally, around 45% of childhood deaths are attributed to malnutrition, with a prominent prevalence in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Based on the study of 1,601 infant and child deaths, the identified causal factor in 39.5% of the cases was malnutrition, often in conjunction with infectious diseases22. Malnutrition and food insecurity account for more than 10% of the global disease burden23. Undernourished children are more susceptible to diseases like diarrhea and pneumonia, which are the leading causes of death in developing nations24.

According to various studies, some factors influence wasting among children aged 6–59 months. This includes socioeconomic factors like household income, wealth, maternal education, age of first birth, and occupation8,12,25,26, nutritional factors such as dietary diversity, breastfeeding practice involving initiation of breastfeeding, and complementary feeding15,25,27 environmental and sanitation factors, including access to improved sanitation and clean water sources15, and health-related factors such as health care access, vaccination, and infectious diseases like diarrheal disease, cough, and fever are the common risk factors associated with wasting in children13,15. Other related factors are low birth weight, birth interval, family size, knowledge of breastfeeding, anemia, feeding frequency, respiratory infection, community support, family and partner support, and media exposure13,15,21–23. Moreover, the effect of technology on malnutrition is considerable28,29.

Ethiopia made tremendous progress in the reduction of under-five morbidity and mortality by designing various policies and strategies, including the Nutritional aspects of management at the national level, involving some declarations30,31. In Ethiopia, efforts are being made to implement a multisectoral nutrition intervention plan (as prescribed in the Sekota Declaration and the National Nutrition Program) to end the high burden of undernutrition by 203032,33. Even though there is some encouraging progress, much remains to be addressed as the rate of undernutrition remains a high burden. The reasons identified for not achieving remarkable progress in the reduction of undernutrition are policy, strategy, objectives, and initiatives put into practice. Actions toward reducing malnutrition are primarily constrained by some emergency outbreaks, such as political instability, pandemics, institutions, environmental factors, and technology, by misleading information and overreliance on technology29,30.

The situation of malnutrition in Ethiopia, particularly in Tigray, has fluctuated significantly due to various factors, including conflict. Studies indicate a rise in malnutrition rates in Tigray34, exacerbated by the prolonged closure of health services during the recent war, which led the 83% of the health system being damaged, severely impacting healthcare infrastructure35. Despite some improvements in malnutrition rates across Ethiopia36, Tigray remains critically affected, with notably limited data available on specific conditions like wasting, particularly in Adigrat town. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of postwar and associated factors of wasting among the children aged 6–59 months in Adigrat town, Eastern Tigray, Northern Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from January 8 to February 20, 2025, among 325 randomly selected children aged 6–59 months in Adigrat town, Eastern Tigray, Ethiopia. Adigrat is about 883 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia, and 98 km from Mekelle, the capital of the Tigray region to the north. The town has a total population of 110,436, with 53,009 (48%) males and 57,427 (52%) females. Children aged 6–59 months make up approximately 12.95% of the total population. Postwar or current aid access is interrupted, and the market faces significantly higher costs, with ongoing inflation pushing prices up.

Population and eligibility criteria

All children aged 6 to 59 months in Adigrat town were considered source populations. This study recruited mothers who lived in Adigrat town for at least six months and had children between the ages of six and fifty-nine months. However, children who were physically or intellectually disabled and mothers in critical condition who couldn’t respond to a full interview at the time of data collection were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedures

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula by considering the assumptions: the prevalence of wasting in Habro district, Eastern Ethiopia, 28%15, level of confidence (5%), and margin of error of 5%. The determined sample was 309.78 ~ 310. After adding the 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was calculated to be 344. To keep our sample size representative, adjusting the sample size for the non-response rate is crucial. The final sample size was determined by adding a non-response rate to the calculated sample size by using the loss adjustment formula. The adjustment was made by dividing the calculated sample size (n) by (1-X), where “X” is the proportion of the expected to withdraw or non-response rate. N = n/(1-X), N = 310/ (1–0.1) = 310/0.9 = 344.4 ≈ 344.

The sample size was also calculated for factors associated with wasting using a double population formula, considering the assumption of power = 80%, CI = 95%, and ratio = 1:1. The maximum sample size using the double population formula was 188. Therefore, we used the largest sample size, which was 344. Three kebeles were selected using the lottery method. The list of 6–59-month-old children was taken from the health extension workers, and a simple random sampling using a lottery was applied to select the study participants.

Data collection tools and techniques

Data was collected using a pretested interviewer-administered questionnaire and Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) direct measurements. The questionnaire was adapted after reading different literature7,15,18,26,37,38, the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic Health Survey (EDHS)39, and national and international guidelines related to child nutrition32,33. First, an English version of the questionnaire was prepared and then translated into the local language, Tigrigna. The data collection tools included socio-demographic characteristics, child health status, anthropometric measurements, child feeding practices, maternal-related characteristics, and environmental conditions. A pretest was conducted with 5% of the sample in a different setting in Wukro town. The training was provided to the data collectors and supervisors on the questionnaire and MUAC measurement. A three-time measurement of the Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) was performed using a non-stretchable, color-coded tape on the left mid-upper arm. An average of the MUAC measurements was calculated. The data collectors checked for the presence of bilateral pitting edema to exclude other forms of acute malnutrition. Five Bachelor’s degree graduates and one Master’s degree holder in Public Health, all with previous relevant experience, participated in data collection and supervision. The data collection process was monitored by two supervisors to ensure data quality, completeness, and consistency on a daily basis.

Independent variables

Operational definitions

Wasting acute malnutrition represents the failure to receive adequate nutrition in the period immediately preceding the survey and may be the result of inadequate food intake or a recent episode of illness, causing loss of weight and the onset of malnutrition40. Describes current nutritional status; includes both Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM) and Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM).

Moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) > = 11.5 cm – <12.5 cm or weight for height (WFH) on Z-score <-2->= (−3) without bilateral pitting edema38.

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) MUAC < 11.5 cm or WFH <-3 on World Health Organization (WHO) z-score40.

Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) is an anthropometric measurement used to measure the circumference of the shoulder tip and the elbow tip along the vertical axis of the upper arm, with the arm positioned at a right angle40.

Diarrheal disease the passage of loose or watery stools at least three times in 24 h40.

Diarrhea it is defined as having three or more loose or watery stools in 24 h in the two weeks before the survey38.

Complementary food foods that are required by the child, after six months of age, in addition to sustained breastfeeding41.

Good hand-washing practice If the respondent washes their hands before and/ or after actions. (before cooking, before eating, after latrine visit, after child cleaning, before child feeding)42.

Continued breastfeeding at 1 year Children 12–15 months of age who continued breastfeeding after the age of 1 year42.

Continued breastfeeding at 2 years Children 20–23 months of age who continued breastfeeding after his/ her 23 months of age42.

Data quality management

Data collectors were trained for 2 days on the methods of data collection: interviews, anthropometric measurement, and data recording. The questionnaire was pre-tested on 5% of the sample size in another similar area outside our study area prior to the data collection period. Each day, group members met after data collection and discussed on day-to-day data collection process and ensured the completeness and consistency of the collected questionnaires on a daily basis. Three-time measurements of MUAC were taken to reduce measurement errors.

Data processing and analysis

Data was checked for completeness and consistency, cleaned, edited, coded, categorized, and stored to facilitate its analysis. Data was entered into EpiData version 4.4.2 and then exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were presented in mean (standard deviation) or median (inter-quartile range, IQR) for numerical variables, depending on data distribution and frequency percentage for the categorical variables. Bivariate analysis was done to check the existence of a crude association between each independent variable and with dependent variable. All explanatory variables associated with the outcome variable in binary analysis with a p-value of 0.25 or less are included in the initial logistic models of multivariable analysis. The final multivariable logistic regression was done to identify factors associated with wasting. The crude and adjusted odds ratios with 95% CI were presented, a p-value < 0.05 was declared statistically significant, and the goodness of fit of the model was assessed using Hosmer and Lem Show.

The majority of 286 (88%) and 301 (92.6%) respondents were Orthodox followers and Tigrayans. Nearly one-third, 106 (32.6%) of the respondents were housewives in occupational status. Maternal-related characteristics

The majority of the respondents, 295 (90.8%), had a history of Antenatal Care (ANC) visits during the recent pregnancies of their children under five. Of these, more than two-thirds, 206 (69.8%), had finished their ANC follow-up, while 30 (9.2%) had no ANC visits at all. More than half of the respondents, 198 (60.9%), had eaten four or more times during their pregnancies. Over one-fourth (29.5%) of the respondents reported experiencing illness when pregnant with their most recent child.

Environmental conditions-related characteristics

For 320 (98.5%) of the survey participants, pipe water was the primary source of drinking water, and fewer than one-fourth (22.2%) of the respondents had water treatment practices. Over half of the research participants’ households, 167 (54.1%), had VIP (Ventilated improved pit) latrines, while the majority, 303 (93.2%), had latrines. Over half (174, or 53.5%) of the homes surveyed have used a pit to dispose of solid trash (Fig. 2).

Prevalence of wasting in children aged 6–59 months

The prevalence of wasting in children aged 6–59 months was 17.5% (95% CI, 13.5–21.8). Moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) was identified in 40 (12.3%) of them, whereas severe acute malnutrition (SAM) was identified in 17 (5.2%).

Factors associated with 6–59 months of children’s wasting

Initially, binary logistic regression was used to examine the various factors, analyzing each independent variable in relation to the dependent variable. Among these eight variables, those with a p-value of 0.25 were selected for further analysis in the multivariable model to adjust for potential confounders. These included: maternal education level, squeezing out the first milk, mothers who are still breastfeeding, initiation of supplementary foods, food type, handwashing practices, frequency of ANC visits, and source of drinking water. Discarding the initial milk, mothers still breastfeeding, and meal type showed significant associations with wasting in the multivariate logistic regression analysis.

Children whose mothers discarded colostrum were 3.2 times more likely to be wasted compared to children whose mothers did not discard colostrum [AOR = 3.2; 95% CI: 1.471–6.998]. Children whose mothers weren’t still breastfeeding were 2.7 times more likely to be wasted compared to those children whose mothers continued to breastfeed [AOR = 2.7; 95% CI: 1.295, 7.729]. Children who consumed only cereals were 3.5 times associated with a higher likelihood of wasting compared to children who consumed cereals, legumes, fruits, and vegetables [AOR = 3.5; 95% CI: 1.162–10.676] (Table 4).

Discussion

According to the study, the prevalence of wasting among children aged 6 to 59 months was found to be 17.5% (95% CI: 13.5–21.8). Children whose mothers had discarded their first breast milk, children who had still not been breastfeeding, and children who had consumed only cereals were statistically significantly associated with wasting in the 6–59 months age group.

The current study shows that the prevalence of wasting was determined to be 17.5% (95% CI: 13.5–21.8), which aligns with findings from studies conducted in various regions, such as India8, Yemen11, the Dabat district in Northwest Ethiopia19, Wolkite town in the Gurage zone18, Kersa in Oromia17, and the Gursum district, Somali region37. This statistic is further supported by systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the nutritional status of children under five in Ethiopia43. The presence of acute malnutrition in these areas could be attributed to ongoing political unrest, chaos, and war conflicts, which create similar circumstances across these regions. These conditions lead to poverty, limited access to healthcare, collapse of the health system, drought, limited agricultural productivity that affects food security, and inadequate food supply contribute to the high prevalence of wasting among children. Overall, the interconnectedness of political, social, economic, and environmental factors plays a crucial role in shaping children’s nutritional status in these regions.

The other possible justification for the higher prevalence of wasting is highly related to the poor practice of decolonization of global health, which significantly affects acute malnutrition or wasting, especially in war or post-war areas, by addressing systematic inequalities, power dynamics that sustain health disparities, and improving local capacities. This corrects the imbalance between high-income and low-income countries. This approach highlights the importance of culturally relevant interventions and community engagement, which are vital in conflict-affected areas where malnutrition rates are alarmingly high44.

However, the current data reveals a higher incidence compared to research carried out in Indonesia9, Bangladesh10, Turkey11, Morocco12, Gedio in Southern Ethiopia20, Debre Tabor in the Amhara region14, Wukro town in Tigray21, and the mini Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey (EDHS) report of 201939. This disparity may be attributed to the blockade and siege of Tigray following the beginning of the conflict in Northern Ethiopia, leading to restricted access to essential resources like food, and the socio-demographics of the study participants. Conversely, the findings indicate a lower prevalence than the study conducted in the Harbo district of Eastern Ethiopia15, Debre Berhan town, Ethiopia16, and Wonago district, Southern Ethiopia7, and the broader Tigray region45. This variation could be due to the timing of the assessment post-discharge from outpatient treatment in the Harbo district, socio-demographic variation like age variation of the source population, as well as the comprehensive evaluation encompassing both rural and urban areas within Tigray.

Mothers who discarded their first breast milk (colostrum) were 3.2 times more likely to have wasted children compared to their counterparts [AOR = 3.2; 95% CI: 1.471–6.998]. This finding aligns with the study done in Nepal46, Gursum district, Somali region35, Shashemene, Oromia region47, and in the Afambo district, Northeast Ethiopia48, where discarding colostrum is related to wasting or acute malnutrition. Colostrum is rich in antioxidants, immunoglobulin, growth factors, and highly concentrated nutrients that are crucial for initiating immune defense, gut maturation, and optimal nutrient absorption in the newborn period. Discarding colostrum often reflects suboptimal breastfeeding, which is related in turn to low maternal education and cultural adherence, such as delayed initiation of breastfeeding or early introduction of prelacteal feeds, which can increase a child’s exposure to infection and reduce early nutrient intake, well-established drivers of acute malnutrition. Moreover, prelacteal feeds lack the protective and nutritional value of colostrum, potentially compromising the newborn’s immunity and increasing susceptibility to diarrhea and other illnesses that accelerate nutrient loss and hinder growth. Therefore, the observed association aligns with existing evidence that early breastfeeding practices strongly influence child nutrition and health outcomes.

In contrast to the case-control study conducted in Sodo town, Southern Ethiopia49, and a facility-based unmatched case-control study in Wonago district, South Ethiopia50 where children who didn’t receive colostrum in the first hour after birth did not waste, the current study’s findings stated that Mothers who discarded their first breast milk (colostrum) were 3.2 times more likely to have wasted children compared to their counterparts. The possible explanation for this variation is that the effect of the colostrum on malnutrition, particularly on acute malnutrition or wasting, does not directly affect children aged 6–59 months. Hence, such studies are highly prone to the recall bias for the self-report retrospective information provision.

Children whose mothers were not still breastfeeding were 2.7 times more likely to be wasted compared to those children whose mothers continued breastfeeding [AOR = 2.7; 95% CI: 1.295, 7.729]. This agrees with a study conducted in Sodo town, Southern Ethiopia49, and the Gaza Strip51 which found that cessation of breastfeeding before 24 months is highly associated with acute malnutrition. Breastfeeding provides numerous health benefits and plays a crucial role in providing essential nutrients for a child’s healthy growth, including protection against infections, improved cognitive development, and better overall health outcomes in which promote the healthy growth and development of children. Hence, early cessation of breastfeeding deprives children form the getting the vital nutrients which are crucial for growth and development that leading to acute malnutrition or wasting both directly and indirectly by compromising immunity and making the children prone to various infections.

Children who had consumed only cereals were 3.5 times more likely to be wasted compared to children who consumed cereals, legumes, fruits, and vegetables [AOR = 3.5; 95% CI: 1.162–10.676]. This finding is similar to studies conducted in the Harbo district, Eastern Ethiopia15, Sodo town, Southern Ethiopia49, Hawassa Zuria52, rural settings of Southern Ethiopia53, Jima Geneti district, western Oromia54, a facility-based unmatched case-control study in Wonago woreda, South Ethiopia50 and to the nutritional status of children under five systematic and meta-analysis was conducted in Ethiopia43 indicating that low-dietary diversified feeding practices from poor household or family socio-economic status lead to acute malnutrition or wasting. According to the mini Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey (EDHS) report of 201939, the majority of the wasted children were primarily from the lowest, and then the next from the second wealth quintile. However, a study conducted in the Dabat health and demographic surveillance system site55 is contrary to the current study, indicating that poor wealth status was not found to be a significant determinant of wasting. This variation might belong to the age variation of the children in which the children involved in the Dabat health and demographic surveillance system site were aged 6–24 months, whereas the children included in the current study were aged 6–59 months, when more dietary diversity more practiced.

Strengths and limitations

We measured the MUAC three times to ensure data quality. This study was subject to recall bias since mothers focus on major events. Although three MUAC measurements were taken, the risk of measurement errors still exists. The study did not include other measurement methods to improve data quality. Additionally, as long as there were displaced communities in the town and they lived or mixed with their family homes, this study was prone to selection bias. Moreover, this study is prone to social desirability bias. Furthermore, the confidence interval for cereal and others was somewhat wide due to the small sample size. This might indicate low precision in the effect size estimates and low reliability regarding specific risk factors.

Conclusions

A considerable proportion of children aged 6–59 months were wasted. Children’s mothers who had squeezed out their first breast milk, children who had still not been breastfeeding, and Children who had consumed cereals only were statistically significantly associated with 6–59 months of wasting. Supporting and enhancing child feeding policies and practices that support exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months and thereafter, suitable supplemental feeding techniques. Carrying out additional mixed studies to offer insightful information that guides improved procedures and treatments that may result in more focused approaches to enhancing child feeding practices.
