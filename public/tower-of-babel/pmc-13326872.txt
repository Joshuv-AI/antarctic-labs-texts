Deep-ZOMA: A Deep Learning-Based Approach for Automated Morphometric Analysis of Zebrafish Larvae Ocular Structures
Authors: Youyuan Zhuang, Chuang Xu, Wei Dai, Dandan Li, Xinyan Jiang, Huaiyuan Ding, Yuhe Yang, Ruowen Qiu, Zhen Ji Chen, Jiyuan Fang, Hui Yang, Jian Yuan, Jia Qu, Jianzhong Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeZebrafish (Danio rerio) ocular measurements are widely used in ocular disease research and drug discovery. Traditional methods rely on manual annotations, causing challenges in precision, efficiency, and workload. Here, we introduce a deep learning-based zebrafish ocular morphometric analysis (Deep-ZOMA) tool for quantitative measurement of zebrafish larvae ocular structures.MethodsA dual-center dataset of 1820 bright-field images (1010 internal; 810 external) was annotated for key ocular regions. A UNet++ segmentation network was trained with augmentation and hybrid loss. Sixteen morphometric parameters were computed using customized quantification algorithms. Performance was evaluated on internal and external datasets using Dice coefficient, IoU, Student's t-test, correlation, and agreement analyses. Utility was further tested on an slc4a7 knockdown/rescue dataset and compared with three ophthalmology graduate students.ResultsDeep-ZOMA achieved mean Dice coefficients of 0.96 on the internal set and 0.95 on the external set for primary ocular regions, with IoU values >0.90. Automated measurements showed strong correlations and excellent agreement with expert measurements. In the slc4a7 model, Deep-ZOMA accurately identified microphthalmia and rescue phenotypes consistent with expert annotations. Measurement and data-entry times were >20-fold faster than those of human observers, with comparable or better accuracy.ConclusionsDeep-ZOMA provides a reliable and efficient solution for high-throughput zebrafish ocular morphometry, supporting applications in ocular genetics, drug screening, and phenotypic studies.Translational RelevanceThis deep learning-based system enables accurate, reproducible zebrafish ocular morphometry, accelerating translational research by linking genetic or pharmacological perturbations with quantifiable ocular outcomes relevant to human eye diseases.

Introduction

Ophthalmic research increasingly relies on quantitative morphometry to connect genetic perturbations and therapeutic interventions with structural phenotypes. Among vertebrate model organisms, the zebrafish (Danio rerio) has emerged as a powerful system for ocular genetics and pharmacological research because it recapitulates many key aspects of human visual system development, ocular anatomy, and genetic architecture.1 Zebrafish larvae possess relatively large eyes in proportion to their body size, facilitating morphological observation, and their ocular tissues share similar developmental origins and layered organization with those of humans.2 Ocular development proceeds rapidly after fertilization, and the retina becomes functional within 72 hours post-fertilization, with cellular composition, laminar architecture, and phototransduction mechanisms broadly comparable to those in the human retina.3 In addition, zebrafish are highly amenable to a wide range of genome-editing approaches, including CRISPR/Cas9, TALENs, and zinc-finger nucleases, enabling efficient generation of disease-relevant models.4 These advantages have supported the establishment of diverse zebrafish models for inherited retinal degeneration, cataract, and metabolic eye disease,5–11 while also generating extensive bright-field and microscopy image resources for phenotype-based ocular studies.

Advances in genomic technologies and large-scale genetic studies have enabled the discovery of an increasing number of candidate genes associated with eye diseases,12–14 and the identification of novel pathogenic mechanisms has led to the emergence of new therapeutic drugs for ocular diseases.15–17 Consequently, the application scope of zebrafish as a model organism has been expanding, resulting in a surge of ophthalmic microscopic image data. Despite abundant image data, extracting zebrafish ocular biometrics accurately and efficiently remains challenging. First, traditional manual measurement methods require substantial human resources and time, and are prone to subjective influences, leading to poor mapping accuracy. Second, because of the vast amount of ophthalmic microscopic image data, the use of traditional manual measurement methods is often inefficient and cannot meet the demands of rapid large-scale data processing.18 Furthermore, the global shortage of ophthalmology specialists exacerbates the dilemma of human resource requirements.19–21

Artificial intelligence (AI) approaches have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in extracting and interpreting morphology-related patterns from biological and medical imaging data.20,22 Prior computational efforts in zebrafish phenotyping have primarily targeted broader developmental or behavioral endpoints rather than fine-scale ocular traits. For instance, deep learning approaches have been applied to whole-embryo classification and toxicity screening, including automated detection of embryonic developmental stages, morphological abnormalities such as pericardial edema, craniofacial malformations, or hemorrhage.23,24 Automated image analysis pipelines have also been developed for simple morphometric readouts like body length or yolk sac size,25 as well as fluorescence-based quantification of vasculature or immune cell distributions.26 In behavioral domains, machine learning has enabled seizure-like activity classification,27 and optokinetic response tracking.28 More recent work has extended to microinjection guidance and individual fish identification.29,30 Although these efforts demonstrate the versatility of machine learning in zebrafish research, no end-to-end solution exists that simultaneously segments the ocular surface, eyeball, anterior chamber and body axis and returns validated ocular biometrics at scale.

To address these gaps, we embarked on developing a deep learning-based zebrafish ocular morphometric analysis (Deep-ZOMA) to achieve automated quantitative analysis of zebrafish eye phenotypes. Deep-ZOMA can segment and quantitatively analyze various types of zebrafish eye microscopic images, converting spatial information extracted from the images into morphological parameter information, including a range of ocular structural parameters such as axial length, transverse diameter, eye area, major and minor axes of the ocular surface, eye-body ratio, and so on. By filling the technical gap for automated, multi-parameter zebrafish ocular morphometry, Deep-ZOMA aims to facilitate large-scale genetic screens, drug testing, and quantitative studies of ocular development and disease.

Methods

Ethics

Experimental protocols involving animals were carried out in strict accordance with standardized guidelines established by Association for Research on Vision and Ophthalmology for ophthalmic and vision research, with necessary approval granted by the Wenzhou Medical University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Zebrafish Husbandry and Embryo Culture

Adult zebrafish of the AB wild-type strain were obtained from the China Zebrafish Resource Center (Wuhan, China) and housed in a standard recirculating aquaculture system, which was maintained at a constant temperature of 28.5°C. Zebrafish embryos and larvae were cultured at a constant temperature of 28.5°C, as specified by the standard protocols.31

Image Acquisition and Dataset Construction

The study adopted a dual-center design. Bright-field microscopic images of zebrafish larvae (three to five days post-fertilization [dpf]) were collected from two independent sources: (1) Internal dataset (ZF-WMU-EH): A total of 1010 larvae were imaged using an Olympus SZX116 stereomicroscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at the zebrafish research platform of Eye Hospital, Wenzhou Medical University. (2) External validation dataset (ZF-WMU-SAH): A total of 810 larvae were imaged using a Nikon SMZ25 stereomicroscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) at the Second Affiliated Hospital, Wenzhou Medical University. For each larva, images were randomly captured from lateral or dorsal perspectives. The characteristics and partition ratios of the zebrafish eye image datasets can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Annotation and Quality Control

Morphometric parameters were manually measured using Olympus CellSens Standard software (version 1.14) for data acquired with an Olympus SZX116 stereomicroscope, while those from the Nikon SMZ25 stereomicroscope were manually measured with NIS-Elements (version 4.50), including the following: (1) Ocular parameters: ocular surface (area, minor/major axes, mean diameter, curvature), oculus dexter (OD) and oculus sinister (OS) axial length, OD and OS transverse diameter, OD and OS anterior chamber (depth, cross-sectional area, angle) and (2) Systemic parameters: axial length-to-body length ratio (Supplementary Fig. S1). Labels for the images were created using LabelMe (version 4.5.1) software package, an image annotation tool, to facilitate further image analysis. Annotations for each image included the zebrafish larval body region, the eyeball region and anterior chamber as viewed dorsally, as well as the ocular surface region as viewed laterally. All measurements and annotations were performed by two trained biologists, with inter-rater reliability assessed via intraclass correlation coefficient, which exceeded 0.95.

Data Partitioning and Augmentation

ZF-WMU-EH was randomly split into training (n = 807, 80%) and test sets (n = 203, 20%). To enhance model robustness, data augmentation was implemented during training. Specifically, each original image was augmented to produce five additional samples, resulting in an effective increase in the training dataset size. The augmentation techniques include the following: (1) Geometric transformations: Random horizontal and vertical flipping (P = 0.5). (2) Photometric enhancements: Random adjustments to brightness (±50%), saturation (±50%), and hue (±0.1). (3) Sharpening: Random sharpening with a sharpening factor ranging from 0.5 to 2.0.

Network Architecture Selection

During the experimental training process, we configured the following parameters: a learning rate of 0.01, a batch size of 2, and 100 epochs. Training was executed on the computational setup listed in the Experimental environment section. At our input resolution (480 × 480), we observed that batch sizes >2 resulted in frequent out-of-memory errors under our implementation; within the feasible range (batch = 2 and 4) batch = 2 achieved the best balance of GPU utilization, stability, and performance. For completeness, experiments at 224 × 224 with batch sizes 2, 4, 8 and 16 were also conducted and none outperformed the 480 × 480 / batch-2 configuration (Supplementary Table S2). The stochastic gradient descent algorithm was employed for optimization, and the mean Dice similarity coefficient was used as the evaluation metric. The models compared in the experiments were UNet, UNet ++ (Supplementary Fig. S2), and UNet 3+. Morphometric Analysis

To quantitatively assess ocular structural parameters in zebrafish larvae, we developed measurement methods for four kinds of parameters: area, curvature, angle, and length. Notable attributes of the implemented algorithms include the following: (1) During noise-affected segmentation, only the largest contour was retained; if no contour was found, it was recorded as unrecognizable. (2) For differentiating left and right eyes, centroid coordinates were calculated, with the first detected contour designated as the left eye and the second as the right eye. (3) When processing images of varying resolutions, the output size of each parameter in pixels and the scale-adjusted size (using a pre-provided scale) were provided. (4) Additionally, the algorithms supported batch measurement parameter visualization, enabling the visualization and saving of all parameters during batch measurements. This method automatically segmented different regions of the input zebrafish image and calculated all key parameters for each region. Results were stored in an XLSX file, with segmentation results and parameter visualization images saved in corresponding folders for subsequent analysis. The process is outlined below:

Where A was the area computed from the first formula, and P was the perimeter of the contour.

Angle Measurement

The contours were extracted as before. The upper and lower extreme points of the contour were identified, and the distance between these points was divided into five equal parts. The intersection points of the first and fourth dividing lines with the contour were selected, resulting in four intersection points. Let the upper extreme point be (x0, y0), and the intersection points of the first dividing line be (x1, y1) and (x2, y2). Similarly, the angle at the lower extreme point was calculated using the intersection points of the fourth dividing line with the contour. The anterior chamber angle was the average of these two angles.

Length measurement consisted of three parts: average diameter measurement, body length measurement, and the measurement of the longest and shortest axes.

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (NumPy, Pandas, SciPy, and statsmodels).

Error Compensation

Due to the inherent limitations in segmentation accuracy, discrepancies existed between the predicted and ground truth regions, leading to measurement deviations in the deep learning model's output. To mitigate measurement errors induced by segmentation, a scale based linear fitting approach (see equation below) was employed: (24)y=kx+bwhere x represented the parameter values obtained from computational measurements, and y denoted the corrected values after linear fitting. The coefficients k (slope) and b (intercept) were derived based on the linear relationship between the parameters obtained from the training set and the manually measured values.

Source of the slc4a7 Knockdown/Rescue Dataset for Zebrafish Ocular Genotype-Phenotype Analysis

To rigorously validate the reliability and practicality of Deep-ZOMA in zebrafish ocular phenotype measurement and assess its potential applications in ocular genetics research, we selected a published zebrafish ocular phenotype dataset encompassing slc4a7 knockdown and mRNA rescue experiments after obtaining appropriate consent.36 The dataset encompassed parameters such as axial length, transverse diameter, ocular surface area, and body length. We compared these data with the results obtained from Deep-ZOMA's automatic measurements to verify its accuracy and practicality.

Rules for Human-Machine Competition

The morphometric analysis of zebrafish larval ocular structures consisted of two key stages: image measurement and data entry. The practicality of the automated morphometric analysis tool therefore depended on the precision and speed of both image quantification and data entry. To objectively compare the performance of Deep-ZOMA and human observers in measuring zebrafish ocular parameters, a standardized human-AI comparison framework was established. The evaluation focused on accuracy, speed, and consistency, using three sets of 30 bright-field microscopic images corresponding to ocular phenotype parameters, each obtained through independent rounds of random sampling from an external dataset. We strictly controlled variables to ensure fairness in the measurement conditions between human and machine, thereby objectively quantifying the accuracy, efficiency, and reproducibility of Deep-ZOMA in measuring zebrafish ocular morphological parameters. The human observers consisted of three graduate students with systematic ophthalmology training, all using standardized image analysis software for manual measurements. The acceptable Standard answer was set as within the 95% to 105% range of the measurement results from one ophthalmology expert with ample experience in zebrafish ocular research. The software interface was uniform, and no additional measurement tools were permitted. Deep-ZOMA, employing an end-to-end deep learning algorithm, automatically performed segmentation and measurements without any human intervention. The measured parameters included axial length, transverse diameter, ocular surface area, major and minor axes of the ocular surface, and zebrafish body length. Three independent rounds of random sampling were conducted, with each round selecting 30 bright-field microscopic images corresponding to ocular phenotype parameters. During the measurement process, each participant was allowed only one measurement per image, without repetition or correction. All participants conducted the measurements independently without discussion or referencing others’ results. Similarly, Deep-ZOMA ran the measurement algorithm only once without any post-processing adjustments. Finally, the total measurement time for each participant and Deep-ZOMA, as well as all measured values, were recorded to assess and compare their accuracy, speed, and consistency.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses were executed using R software (version 4.1.1). Statistical significance between the outputs generated by Deep-ZOMA and the manually obtained measurements was conducted using the Student's t-test. The linear relationship between variables was quantified using Pearson's correlation coefficient. One-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's post hoc tests was employed to compare the means among multiple groups. Bar plots were shown as the mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was set at a P-value threshold of 0.05, with annotations: ns (not significant), * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), and *** (P < 0.001).

Experimental Environment

The network in this research was trained on a system running Windows, equipped with 128 GB of memory, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 Ti graphics card with 24 GB of VRAM, and an Intel Xeon Gold 5218R CPU @ 2.10 GHz.

Results

Deep-ZOMA Workflow for Automated Zebrafish Ocular Morphometry Analysis

Deep-ZOMA is an automated analysis pipeline that enables high-throughput and precise quantification of zebrafish ocular morphology through three core computational modules: segmentation, parametric measurement, and error refinement. For the segmentation module (Fig. 1A), this module leverages a UNet++-based deep learning network to process zebrafish eye images captured from three distinct perspectives. It precisely segments critical anatomical regions such as the eyeball, ocular surface, and body length. The network's robust architecture enables it to handle diverse image qualities and complexities, ensuring consistent segmentation accuracy across varying experimental conditions. For the parameter measurement module (Fig. 1B), building upon the segmented images, this module employs specialized algorithms to quantify parameters for four key regions: ocular surface, eyeball, anterior chamber, and body length. The process involves extracting a total of 16 specific ocular parameters, such as axial length, eye area, anterior chamber depth, eye-body ratio, and so forth. The module is designed with efficient batch processing capabilities, allowing researchers to analyze large datasets rapidly. Additionally, it offers comprehensive visualization tools, enabling intuitive interpretation of the measurement results and facilitating detailed comparisons between different samples. For the error fitting module (Fig. 1C), this module incorporates scale calibration and linear fitting techniques to ensure high-precision measurements. It automatically detects and corrects potential segmentation errors, such as minor deviations in contour delineation or discrepancies in size estimation. By refining the measurement outcomes, this module guarantees that each parameter is measured with optimal accuracy, minimizing the influence of technical artifacts and enhancing the reliability of the data. Furthermore, we rigorously validated Deep-ZOMA's performance using zebrafish eye image datasets from published studies (Fig. 1D).

Dataset Construction, Model Training, and Evaluation

A dual-center zebrafish eye image dataset comprising ZF-WMU-EH (internal dataset) and ZF-WMU-SAH (external validation dataset) was constructed to validate the universality and robustness of the Deep-ZOMA pipeline. The ZF-WMU-EH dataset comprised 1,010 meticulously annotated images for training (80%) and preliminary testing (20%), whereas the ZF-WMU-SAH dataset, consisting of 810 images, served as an independent validation set (Supplementary Table S1). Among the evaluated architectures (UNet, UNet++, and UNet 3+), UNet++ delivered superior semantic segmentation performance for the ocular regions, achieving Dice coefficients of 0.969 for the ocular surface and 0.963 for the eyeball (Fig. 2A). Its overall advantages in training speed, stability, and segmentation performance led us to adopt UNet++ as the principal architecture for subsequent experiments.

We next integrated data augmentation with strategic tuning of loss functions to further enhance model performance (Fig. 2B). By applying diverse data augmentation techniques to the internal training set, we evaluated pairwise combinations of cross-entropy loss with Dice loss, focal loss, and Tversky loss. Our analysis showed that Dice Cross-Entropy Loss achieved the optimal segmentation accuracy for three anatomical structures, including the eyeball (Dice = 0.978), ocular surface (0.976), and anterior chamber (0.925). Notably, for body region quantification, this loss function achieved nearly identical performance to Tversky cross-entropy loss, differing by only 0.001 from the highest observed value. Consequently, Dice cross-entropy loss was selected as the most effective loss function for this segmentation task (Fig. 2C). To assess the generalizability of our model, we further evaluated its performance on the external ZF-WMU-SAH dataset. The independently trained region-specific models continued to demonstrate excellent performance, achieving Dice coefficients of 0.984 for the ocular surface, 0.976 for the eyeball, 0.878 for the anterior chamber, and 0.965 for the body region (Fig. 2D; Supplementary Table S3).

To further evaluate whether separate training for each anatomical region was necessary, we additionally constructed a unified multi-class segmentation model with a shared encoder and compared its performance with that of the independent region-specific training strategy. The comparison showed that the unified model did not improve segmentation performance and instead exhibited reduced generalization, especially on the external validation dataset (Supplementary Table S4). On the internal test set, the mean Dice scores were comparable between the two strategies (0.959 for independent models vs. 0.956 for the unified model). However, on the external validation set, the mean Dice score of the unified model decreased to 0.908, compared with 0.951 for the independent models. This reduction was most pronounced for anterior chamber segmentation, where the Dice coefficient dropped from 0.878 to 0.769. In addition, the unified model produced several representative failure modes, including under-segmentation, over-segmentation, missed ocular structures, and false prediction of non-ocular regions as eye-related structures (Supplementary Fig. S3). These results indicate that, for our heterogeneous zebrafish imaging data, independent region-specific training provides more robust segmentation performance than a unified multi-class framework. Notably, both strategies were implemented based on the same UNet++ architecture, indicating that the observed performance difference was primarily attributable to the training strategy rather than the underlying backbone. Therefore all subsequent segmentation-based measurements and downstream analyses in this study were performed using the independently trained region-specific models.

Preliminary Quantitative Analysis of Zebrafish Ocular Biometric Parameters

On completing the image segmentation process, we developed a series of algorithms (described in the methods section under morphometric analysis) tailored to the anatomical characteristics of zebrafish eyes, enabling quantitative measurements across four eye-related regions and the extraction of multiple key parameters (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Table S5). To verify the accuracy of these measurements, we performed t-tests to evaluate the differences between computer and human-measured parameters in the training set. The t-test comparisons between the preliminary Deep-ZOMA measurements and manual measurements yielded P-values < 0.05 for nearly all parameters, except for body length (Fig. 3B), indicating significant differences.

Scale-Based Linear Fitting Reduced Ocular Parameter Measurement Errors

To investigate the low measurement accuracy of Deep-ZOMA, we first examined the distribution of its measurement data alongside various ocular parameters from the original datasets. With eye area as a case in point, we carried out a comparison of the distribution patterns between the measurement results yielded by Deep-ZOMA and the corresponding raw data from the internal test dataset (Supplementary Fig. S4A). The resulting scatter plots revealed linear distributions for eye area, suggesting an intrinsic correlation between the Deep-ZOMA measurements and the manual measurements (Supplementary Fig. S4B). This observation prompted us to perform a Pearson correlation coefficient analysis to rigorously confirm the linear relationship. The results demonstrated extremely strong correlations for most parameters, with the eye area achieving the highest correlation coefficient of 0.99, except for the anterior chamber and body, which showed strong or moderately strong correlations (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. S5).

Correlation and comparative analysis of Deep-ZOMA versus manual measurements in ocular surface region. (A) Scatter plots display the Pearson correlation analysis comparing ocular surface parameters measured by Deep-ZOMA with corresponding manual measurements, including results from both the internal (training and test) sets and the external dataset. (B) Bar charts illustrate the measurement outcomes obtained from Deep-ZOMA and human experts across the internal training and test sets, as well as the external dataset. The differences were statistically evaluated using Student's t-test, with the bar plots representing the mean ± s.e.m. Statistical significance was set at a P-value threshold of 0.05.

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the observed measurement discrepancies were associated with systematic bias in the segmentation-derived measurements. To address this bias, we introduced a scale-based linear fitting method applied to the training dataset. This approach retained the underlying correlation between the Deep-ZOMA measurements and the original data while significantly reducing the measurement discrepancies (Supplementary Figs. S4C, S4D). After correction, re-running the t-tests revealed that the P-values for all parameters exceeded 0.05 (e.g., eye area: P = 0.947; minor ocular axis: P = 0.901; major ocular axis: P = 0.893), indicating no statistically significant difference between computer-based and manual measurements at the group level (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. S5). Subsequently, we further assessed the reliability of the scale-based linear fitting method on both the internal and external test sets. This method markedly reduced the dispersion between automated and manual measurements. Student's t-test analysis confirmed that, for all 10 validation parameters, there were no significant differences between the fitted results and manual measurements (P > 0.05) across both datasets (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. S5; Supplementary Table S6).

To further evaluate agreement between automated and manual measurements, we additionally performed Bland-Altman analysis and ICC analysis as supplementary assessments. The Bland-Altman plots showed small mean biases after scale-based linear fitting, with most samples distributed within the 95% limits of agreement, indicating good consistency between the two measurement methods (Supplementary Fig. S6). Consistently, ICC analysis demonstrated high agreement between Deep-ZOMA and expert manual measurements across the validated parameters, with all ICC values exceeding 0.90 in the training, internal test, and external validation datasets (Supplementary Table S7). Together, these supplementary analyses further support the reliability and consistency of Deep-ZOMA measurements after scale-based linear fitting.

Deep-ZOMA Demonstrated Superior Performance in Detecting Genotype-Phenotype Correlations in Ophthalmic Disorders

Deep-ZOMA's practical effectiveness was validated by implementing the framework on a zebrafish ocular phenotype dataset derived from an slc4a7 knockdown model (a causative gene for inherited retinal degeneration). The correlation matrix between Deep-ZOMA and human expert measurements showed strong correlations across all parameters (Fig. 5A). The t-test analysis indicated that no statistically significant differences were observed between Deep-ZOMA's measurements of eye-related parameters across groups (control and slc4a7 morpholino oligonucleotide-treated groups receiving 0.50, 0.75, and 1.00 ng doses) and those obtained by human experts (Fig. 5B). Discussion

This study successfully developed a deep learning-based zebrafish ocular morphometric analysis system, achieving high-precision segmentation of four key anatomical regions (ocular surface, eyeball, anterior chamber, and body) and automated extraction of 16 morphological parameters. By using UNet++ segmentation network and cross-entropy loss with Dice loss, the system achieved average Dice coefficients of 0.96 and 0.95 on internal and external validation datasets, respectively, significantly outperforming traditional manual measurement methods. This loss combination balances stable pixel-level optimization with improved overlap-based supervision for small or imbalanced targets, whereas the nested skip connections in UNet++ enhance multi-scale feature fusion and boundary delineation. Additionally, to address parameter biases, we introduced a scale-based linear fitting compensation method, resulting in no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05) between the automated measurements and manual annotations. In practical validation of the knockout model for the causative gene of inherited retinal degeneration, Deep-ZOMA not only accurately reproduced the phenotype differences observed by manual measurements but also demonstrated a more than 20-fold improvement in processing efficiency, highlighting its potential application in high-throughput research.

Compared to traditional manual measurements (e.g., stereomicroscope system integrator software) and general image analysis tools (e.g., ImageJ), the core advantage of Deep-ZOMA lies in its end-to-end automation and simultaneous extraction of multiple parameters. Existing tools typically focus on individual parameters (such as body length or area) and rely on manual annotation of key points, leading to inefficiency and subjective bias. In contrast, Deep-ZOMA uses deep learning to directly analyze complex anatomical structures, enabling the first automated measurement of microscopic ocular parameters such as the ocular surface, axial length, and anterior chamber in zebrafish. This system fills a significant technological gap in zebrafish ocular phenotyping. Moreover, Deep-ZOMA's modular design offers flexibility for future functional expansions. Our next step includes the integration of multimodal data, combining optical coherence tomography with ocular fluorescence microscopy images for synchronous analysis of ocular structural and functional parameters.

Although most ocular parameters in this research have been successfully validated, strict cross-validation has not been achieved for anterior chamber area, angle, ocular surface mean curvature, and diameter. These parameters present challenges due to either their delicate anatomical structures (rendering manual measurements unreliable even by experts) or limitations of current microscope-integrated software lacking manual measurement capabilities. Anterior chamber area and angle are important indicators of anterior segment morphology and are closely associated with developmental abnormalities and glaucoma-related structural alterations.37,38 Subtle variations in these metrics play a significant role in revealing the openness of the anterior chamber angle and its relationship with genetic factors, which will help in rapidly screening and identifying genes associated with glaucoma. Meanwhile, ocular surface curvature and diameter comprehensively characterize global ocular geometry, with curvature correlating to refractive status and diameter evaluating ocular size and symmetry.39,40 Considering the substantial potential significance mentioned above, these parameters were integrated into the Deep-ZOMA system, even in the absence of cross-validation results. Future technical optimizations and supplementary validation methods aim to elucidate the biological significance in zebrafish ocular morphometrics and provide researchers with a more comprehensive and user-friendly analytical tool.

Deep-ZOMA demonstrated high accuracy and efficiency overall, but several limitations remain. Before applying the scale-based linear fitting compensation, the preliminary Deep-ZOMA measurements did not achieve statistical agreement with manual measurements for most ocular parameters, except for body length. To investigate the source of these discrepancies, we first performed measurements directly on manually annotated segmentation labels. On the external dataset, these measurements did not significantly differ from expert annotations (all parameters, P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S9), indicating that the measurement algorithm itself is reliable. In contrast, when measurements were derived from model-generated segmentation masks, all parameters except body length showed significant differences compared with expert annotations (P < 0.01; Supplementary Table S9), suggesting that the segmentation step is an important source of the observed bias. We further assessed whether boundary-level segmentation errors were associated with measurement deviations by analyzing boundary-sensitive metrics, including HD, HD95, and ASD. Correlation analysis showed that eye area and minor axis had weak-to-moderate correlations with some boundary metrics, whereas most other parameters showed weak or non-significant correlations (Supplementary Fig. S7; Supplementary Tables S10, S11). These findings suggest that boundary errors contribute to the bias of some contour-dependent parameters, but cannot fully account for the discrepancies observed across all measurements. This also indicates that high Dice scores do not necessarily ensure agreement in downstream morphometric analysis, because Dice mainly reflects regional overlap and may be insensitive to local boundary shifts, endpoint localization errors, and direction-specific deviations in small anatomical structures. In future work, we will explore hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures, boundary-aware refinement, and advanced augmentation strategies to further improve segmentation of fine ocular structures and enhance the robustness of parameter extraction.

The segmentation performance of the anterior chamber region (external Dice = 0.878) is notably lower than that of other regions, potentially due to the small size of the region, imaging differences across devices, and insufficient training samples. Future improvements could involve incorporating attention mechanisms or adaptive instance normalization (AdaIN) to address domain shift issues.41 Second, the current system only supports static single-fish image analysis and cannot track dynamic changes in the ocular features of live fish populations. Enhancing dynamic measurement capabilities by integrating object detection networks (e.g., YOLOv8) with video temporal analysis frameworks could address this limitation.42,43 Furthermore, although unified multi-class segmentation frameworks with shared encoders can improve parameter efficiency and feature sharing, prior studies have shown that the benefit of multi-task or shared-representation learning is highly dependent on task relatedness and may be limited by negative transfer when targets are heterogeneous or weakly correlated.44–46 Our comparative experiments further showed that this strategy was less effective than independent region-specific training in the present task, particularly on external validation data. A likely reason is the significant heterogeneity among the target structures in terms of scale, morphology, contrast, and imaging context, which may increase task competition within a single model. Since Deep-ZOMA is designed for accurate downstream morphometric quantification, we prioritized segmentation robustness and precision over architectural compactness.

Student's t-test is useful for evaluating whether there are statistically significant differences in group means between automated and manual measurements, but it does not by itself fully characterize agreement at the individual-sample level. Therefore we further supplemented our analysis with Bland-Altman and ICC analysis (Supplementary Table S7). These additional analyses showed that, after scale-based linear fitting, Deep-ZOMA exhibited small mean biases, acceptable limits of agreement, and high ICC values across validated parameters, further supporting good consistency between automated and expert manual measurements. Notably, our additional paired t-test analysis showed that even repeated manual measurements of the same zebrafish eyes could yield statistically significant differences because of subtle measurement variation. This suggests that significance testing alone may be overly sensitive for evaluating practical agreement between methods (Supplementary Table S12). At the same time, Student's t-test remains practically informative in the context of zebrafish phenotyping, where researchers are often interested in whether an automated method can reproduce group-level differences between biological conditions, such as gene knockout, rescue, or treatment groups. In this sense, the absence of significant group-level differences between Deep-ZOMA and expert measurements, together with the supplementary Bland-Altman and ICC results, indicates that Deep-ZOMA is suitable for routine morphometric analysis and experimental phenotype assessment.

From a practical application perspective, Deep-ZOMA is primarily intended for researchers in ophthalmology, vision science, and zebrafish-based disease modeling who require efficient, reproducible, and quantitative ocular phenotyping. Potential use scenarios include gene function studies related to ocular development and disease, evaluation of therapeutic efficacy or toxicity on ocular tissues, and assessment of environmental factors affecting the visual system. To improve generalizability, the training data in this study included bright-field ocular microscopy images acquired under varied experimental conditions, and extensive data augmentation was applied during training, including random horizontal and vertical flipping, brightness adjustment, saturation variation, hue shifting, and sharpness modification. Under routine imaging conditions broadly similar to those used in this study, Deep-ZOMA can generally be applied directly without additional fine-tuning. However, when images are acquired using substantially different devices or protocols, further fine-tuning or retraining on local datasets may help optimize performance. To facilitate such adaptation and future extension, we have made the full code and pretrained weights publicly available at https://github.com/WMU-SuLab/Deep-ZOMA.

Overall, Deep-ZOMA provides an efficient and accurate automated analysis tool for zebrafish ocular research, significantly reducing labor costs and subjective bias compared to traditional methods. Through its error compensation strategy and modular design, Deep-ZOMA maintains high precision while ensuring computational efficiency, laying the foundation for large-scale ocular-related genetic screening, drug toxicity assessments, and disease mechanism studies.
