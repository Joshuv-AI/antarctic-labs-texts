Geological Field Restoration through the Lens of Image Inpainting
Vladislav Trifonov, Ivan Oseledets, Ekaterina Muravleva

Abstract
We present a new viewpoint on a reconstructing multidimensional geological fields from sparse observations. Drawing inspiration from deterministic image inpainting techniques, we model a partially observed spatial field as a multidimensional tensor and recover missing values by enforcing a global low-rank structure. Our approach combines ideas from tensor completion and geostatistics, providing a robust optimization framework. Experiments on synthetic geological fields demonstrate that used tensor completion method significant improvements in reconstruction accuracy over ordinary kriging for various percent of observed data.



1
Introduction

Reconstructing the subsurface geological field from sparse measurements is a critical problem in geoscience. This problem has applications in resource exploration, groundwater modeling, environmental monitoring, resource extraction and more. Environmental engineers use geostatistics to make informed decisions about environmental processes, resource management and risk assessment. Robust decision-making leads to proper environmental management and an accurate assessment of environmental risks through the analysis of the spatial variability of hazards, exposure pathways and vulnerability factors.

Unlike 2D seismic slices or surface maps, 3D geological volumes pose a significant challenge. Only a small portion of the volume along wells is sampled, leaving vast regions unobserved. Conventional approaches model the field as a spatial random process and use kriging (Gaussian process regression, GPR) to interpolate missing values. Under certain assumptions about the spatial covariance, kriging provides the best linear unbiased predictor. However, GPR relies on estimating variogram/covariance models and requires specific engineering expertise. Moreover, real measurements can be much rare than the desired radius of locally correlated data for GPR interpolation.

In contrast, we frame geological restoration as an image inpainting problem. The 3D field is a three-way array
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J\times K}
, partially observed along well trajectories. This perspective can leverages recent machine learning advances in image reconstruction. We propose focusing on a classical algorithm from multidimensional linear algebra and using tensor completion to solve the problem. The task is formulated as an optimization problem of finding the best low-rank representation of a data tensor. This problem has a closed-form solution using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers algorithm.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We suggest a novel perspective for the important task of geological field reconstruction, reformulating it as the image inpainting task.

•

We apply tensor completion methods to reconstruct initial geological data tensors. We also demonstrate that this general approach can be adapted in various ways to suit a certain task.

•

We achieved superior reconstruction performance on complex 3D geological formation using the tensor completion method rather than kriging, having no prior knowledge of the problem.

Gaussian Process Regression

In geostatistics, kriging is the standard method of spatial interpolation of data volumes by observed data-points. Kriging treats the quantity of interest
Z(u)
at location
u
as a realization of a random field and constructs a linear predictor for unsampled locations. The Ordinary Kriging
[
Cressie 1990
]
estimator at a new point
u_{0}
is given by

\hat{Z}(u_{0})=\sum_{i}^{n}\lambda_{i}Z(u_{i}),

(1)

where weights
\lambda_{i}
minimize the prediction variance under the covariance model obtained from the observed data. The kriging method is widely used in various fields of geoscience
[
Delhomme 1978
]
. In addition to engineering applications, GPR has found many applications in machine learning
[
Seeger 2004
]
.

The Stanford Geostatistical Modeling Software (SGeMS) is used for baseline generation
[
Remy et al. 2009
]
. SGeMS is open-source software that implements all of the most common tools for spatial statistics. Additional information about kriging can be found in Appendix
B
.

3
Image Inpainting with Tensor Completion

Image inpainting an image processing task that involves filling in missing or damaged regions of an image using the surrounding information. This task comes from fields such as computer vision, machine learning, and signal processing
[
Elharrouss et al. 2020
]
. The reconstructed missing parts should be visually coherent with the rest of the image.

Tensors are multidimensional arrays that generalize the concept of scalars
\mathbb{R}^{1}
, vectors
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and matrices
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
to higher dimensions. The real-valued tensor of order
N
is denoted as
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{0}\times I_{2}\times\dots\times I_{N-1}}
, where
I_{n}
is the size of
n
-th dimension.

Treating spatial data as tensors allows for alternative modeling approaches. Tensor completion methods typically usually use the low-rank representation assumption to efficiently estimate the missing entries. Unlike matrices, tensors do not have a specific rank definition. Tensor rank is defined under certain tensor decompositions, leading to different modeling strategies.

A common convex relaxation of the Tucker rank is to minimize the sum of nuclear norms of the unfoldings
[
Cai et al. 2010
]

\min_{\mathcal{X}}:\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\|X_{(i)}\|_{*}
, where
\|X_{(n)}\|_{*}
is a nuclear norm, or sum of singular values, of a matrix. The mode-
n
unfolding of tensor
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{0}\times I_{2}\times\dots\times I_{N-1}}
is a matrix
{X_{(n)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{n}\times I_{0}\dots I_{n-1}I_{n+1}\dots I_{N-1}}
which first dimension is equal to
n
-th tensor dimension and the second dimension is a product of all the other dimensions.

4
Low Rank Tensor Completion

Primal problem we are solving is a reconstruction of a
\mathcal{X}
tensor from a partially observed tensor
\mathcal{Y}
. This can be formulated as follows:

\arg\min_{\mathcal{X}}\|\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{X})-\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{Y})\|^{2}_{F},

(2)

\Omega
is a binary tensor with the same dimensionality as
\mathcal{Y}
and corresponds to observed tensor elements,
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is a Frobenius norm of a tensor. A projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}
returns tensor element if it is present and zero otherwise:

\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{Y})=\begin{cases}y_{i_{0},\dots,i_{N-1}},&\text{for}\,\,(i_{0},\dots,i_{N-1})\in\Omega\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

(3)

The problem (
2
) is ill-posed and requires additional constraints. One needs to either estimate the tensor rank or add tensor rank minimization problem into objective (
2
). We formulate this as a convex optimization problem:

\begin{split}&\min_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\|X_{(n)}\|_{*}\\
&\text{subject to }\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{Y})\end{split}\penalty\ .

(4)

Intuitively, this objective encourages
\mathcal{X}
to have small rank in all three unfoldings, subject to matching the observed well data exactly. We solve this problem using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM).

Nuclear norm minimization with ADMM

Now we introduce the general ADMM method with tensor notations. Auxiliary variable
\mathcal{Z}
:

\min_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z}}\|\mathcal{Z}\|_{*}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Z},\quad\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{Y})

(5)

with an augmented Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{T})=\|\mathcal{Z}\|_{*}+\langle\mathcal{T},\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{Z}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{Z}\|_{F}^{2}\penalty\ .

(6)

Problem (
5
) has a closed form solution. Omitting derivation details, updates tensors
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{Z}
on the step
k
are:

\mathcal{Z}^{k+1}=\text{SVT}\Big(\mathcal{X}^{k}+\frac{1}{\rho}\mathcal{T}^{k},\beta\Big)\penalty\ ,

(7)

where
\beta
is a hyperparameter,
\text{SVT}_{\beta}
is a singular value soft-thresholding with hyperparameter
\beta
:
\text{SVT}(A,\beta)=USV^{T}
, where
A=U\Sigma V^{T}
is the SVD decomposition of matrix
A
. Matrix
S
is a diagonal matrix obtained by soft-thresholding:
\max{\{\sigma_{i}-\beta,0\}_{1\leq t\leq\text{rank}(\Sigma)}}
.

\mathcal{X}^{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{Y})+\mathcal{P}_{\Omega^{\perp}}\Big(\mathcal{Z}^{k+1}-\frac{1}{\rho}\mathcal{T}^{k}\Big)

(8)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}^{\perp}
is a complement of
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}
.

\mathcal{T}_{k+1}=\mathcal{T}_{k}+\rho_{k}\big(\mathcal{X}_{k+1}-\mathcal{Z}_{k+1}\big)\penalty\ ,

(9)

ADMM with spatial smoothing

Altough, the classical ADMM is powerful and converges under broad conditions, researchers have proposed many variants to improve convergence, adapt to special structures and handle large-scale problems more efficiently. In our experiments, the classical ADMM algorithm performed poorly so we employed the following variants based on the extensive literature
[
Liu et al. 2012
,
Wang et al. 2016
,
Chen et al. 2020
]
.

First, we process each mode as a separate tensor and the final update in each iteration is a mean of the primal variables of these tensors. This increases consistency across the low-rank representation of the different modes. Second, we used ADMM with graph Laplacian regularization for unfoldings corresponding to spatial dimensions, which encourages smoothness. We treat the XY-planes as the spatial dimensions and the Z-axis as the channel dimension. Thus, instead of the objective (
4
) we have:

\begin{split}&\min_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{n=0}^{2}\|X_{(n)}\|_{*}+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{1}\|D_{n}X_{(n)}\|_{F}^{2}\\
&\text{subject to}\quad\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{Y})\end{split}\penalty\ .

(10)

where
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
are discrete difference operators applied along spatial modes
0
and
1
. The resulting Algorithm
1
can be observed in Appendix
A
. The
\text{fold}_{n}
and
\text{unfold}_{n}
are folding and unfolding operators correspondingly; if
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{0}\times I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
, then
\mathcal{A}_{[...,n]}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{0}\times I_{1}\times I_{2}}
.

5.1
Setup

Overestimated results with kriging

In real scenarios, the first step is the variogram estimation. This is difficult task due to sparse and noisy data. Moreover, kriging requires the known data used for interpolation to be within a ”correlated” radius, which usually difficult to achieve since the wells can be kilometers apart.

Second, in our experiments, we used a true variogram, i.e.,
the one estimated from the entire dataset
. This dramatically imporves the kriging performance, since the available sparse data would have not allowed to fit the variogram model properly. Kriging performance can also be limited by noise and non-stationary structures.

Finally, synthetic datasets, like the one we used in this paper, are created as Gaussian random fields (GRFs). This means that with GPR, one can ”guess” the correct answer and take the parameters close to those used to generate the data.

Considering all these factors that overestimate kriging performance, our tensor completion approach achieves higher accuracy and better visual coherence with
no prior knowledge at all
.

Geological field benchmark

The performance of the proposed approach is demonstrated on the well-known benchmark SPE10 model 2
[

]

Reconstruction results

We illustrate the reconstruction results on a representative geological model and compare them to those of ordinary kriging. In all cases, i.e., number of wells, we treat the 3D field as a tensor and provide random well positions as input simulating exploration wells. One can observe reconstruction results in Figure

One of a key strengths of the tensor completion approach is its ability to capture global multilinear structure. By contrast, kriging can oversmooth or introduce block artifacts when the data is very sparse. Kriging can also produce noisy interpolation, while low-rank completion produces a smoother fields that match large-scale patterns.

The proposed approach yields higher-quality reconstructions of the upper formation (see Appendix
F
,
G
). When kriging smooths the entire field, the tensor completion approach distinguishes all major regions in the field. For the lower formation (see Appendix
H
,
I
), the tensor completion approach also better captures the general fluvial pattern. Tensor completion fills in the details of the ”riverbed” more coherently and creates smoother boundaries between different regions. This generation property can be beneficial for further mathematical modeling since rapid changes in properties (e.g., permeability) can degrade simulations.

Aside from the generally higher RSE, another advantage is qualitative: low-rank completion tends to respect layer continuity when it corresponds to the rank structure. In practical geological modeling, this can yield more coherent geological fields.

A more detailed comparison of the proposed formulation with the baseline method, a review of related work and limitations can be found in Appendix
D
.

6
Conclusion and Prospects

The tensor completion algorithm offers a deterministic method for recovering spatial variables. The proposed approach of nuclear norm minimization achieves higher generation quality than ordinary kiring with SGeMS. We believe the geology reconstruction task is closely related to the ideas of matrix completion, image inpainting and compressed sensing. By framing the problem in this manner, we can leverage robust convex optimization techniques. Our work demonstrates its applicability in the geoscience context.

In this paper, we focus on reconstruction from sparse well data (i.e., structured data) and formulate the problem as image inpainting. This formulation can naturally be extended to geological field reconstruction from unstructured observations.

By enforcing a low multilinear rank, we assume that the 3D geological field varies smoothly or similarly along different axes. This assumption is often reasonable for stratified subsurface media. Under this assumption, even a small number of wells can suffice to infer the full field when GPR would likely fail.

Appendix B
Kriging details

Kriging is a nonparametric technique that uses the observed data points to estimate the mean and covariance parameters of the distribution, and then uses this estimated distribution to make predictions for new input values
[
Cressie 1990
]
. System of equations for simple kriging built from a covariance matrix has following form
[
Remy et al. 2009
]
:

\begin{bmatrix}C_{11}&\cdots&C_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
C_{n1}&\cdots&C_{nn}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1u}\\
\vdots\\
C_{nu}\end{bmatrix},

(12)

where
C_{ij}
is a data-to-data covariance matrix,
C_{nu}
is a data-to-unknown covariance matrix. Both matrices are built from the prior stationary covariance model:

C_{ij}=\texttt{Cov}\big(Z(u_{i}),Z(u_{j})\big)=C(0)-\gamma(u_{i}-u_{j}),

(13)

where
C(0)=\texttt{Var}\big(Z(u)\big)
is a stationary variance,
\gamma(h)
is a corresponding stationary half-variogram model:

2\gamma(h)=\texttt{Var}\big(Z(u)-Z(u+h)\big).

(14)

There are several types of kriging that are commonly used in geostatistics, including: ordinary kriging (known constant local mean), simple kriging (unknown constant local mean), co-kriging (interpolation of correlated properties) and others. The ordinary kriging model is used as a baseline in this paper.

Appendix C
Experiment Environment and methods parameters

Environment

The kriging experiments were implemented with SGeMS software
[
Remy et al. 2009
]
; tested on Inter Core i5 with 4 cores. The tensor completion experiments algorithm was implemented in JAX
[
Bradbury et al. 2018
]
; tested on a single GPU Nvidia A40 48Gb.

While kriging is tested on way less efficient hardware, it does not lead to worse reconstruction quality. Moreover, the SGeMS is a highly efficient and robust software that produce results for large volumes with many obsereved data points just under a minute. We were not available to match SGeMS results with any other available Python library even with the more efficient hardware.

Methods parameters

Since the problem is reformulated as best low-rank approximation of a multidimensional tensor, one does not have many parameters to tune within the used tensor completion algorithm. On the other hand, engineers have to do a lot of expert work to find the best parameters for kriging, which are: (i) kriging type; (ii) spatial correlation: covariance model, variogram parameters, lags; (iii) search ellipsoid.

We used ordinary kriging with variogram calculated from the whole geological model. Search ellipsoid was obtained as follows. First we found the best radius and angle searching over various radiuses and angles with
500
sampled wells. Then we set the best radius and applied another angle search for each number of wells from
\{100,300,500,700\}
.

The tensor completion was iterated until convergence. The parameters for each set of wells was obtained searching in
\rho\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9,1.001,1.01,1.1\},\penalty\ \alpha\in\{10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1},1,1.1\}
and
\beta=0.1\rho
.

Appendix D
Methods comparison, limitations and related work

Kriging relies on choosing a covariance model (e.g., exponential variogram). In contrast, tensor completion implicitly learns correlations via the low-rank structure, without explicit covariance estimation. For very large grids with sparse data, kriging can become bottlenecked by covariance inversion. Tensor completion requires SVDs whose cost grows with grid dimensions.

The key assumption is that the true field is well-approximated by a low-rank tensor. If the geology is very irregular or high-frequency variations are present (e.g., complex crack networks), this assumption may fail. In such cases, kriging can capture small-scale detail better if sampled points are present in this area.

Main drawback of method used in this paper is the lack of native uncertainty quantification. Kriging explicitly models prediction uncertainty, which is critical in decision-making (e.g., assessing drilling risk). Our current formulation yields a single best estimate. A natural extension is to perform Bayesian tensor completion
Shi and Shen 2023
.

When multiple attributes (e.g. porosity and permeability) are available, one can use co-kriging to interpolate them simultaneously by modeling their spatial covariance together since the correlation is expected. The tensor completion method can be effortlessly used for data fields with multiple properties by extending to a joint tensor with more dimensions.

Related work

Low-rank completion has be effectively applied for seismic data interpolation
[
Andersen and Rayens 2004
,
Acar et al. 2005
,
Acar et al. 2006
,
Ely et al. 2013
]
and petrophysical properties estimation
[
Insuasty et al. 2017
]
. The most similar application for geology field restoration was performed in
[
Syed et al. 2022
]
. Our work is different from this paper in certain ways:

•

We reformulate the problem as image inpainting task providing another perspective on the problem.

•

We demonstrate the specific algorithms grounded into minimization problems with certain objectives. One can further use this formalism and introduce other domain-specific or method-specific knowledge. In
[
Syed et al. 2022
]
, authors used tensor-rank minimization methods as black-box methods.

•

While authors in
[
Syed et al. 2022
]
created artificial small gaps in the filed properties, we aim to imitate the real problems of rare data observations, i.e., well data.

•

We explicitly compare our method in reconstruction ability with the well-established method. It is important to note that while work
[
Syed et al. 2022
]
provides a good metric for comparison, i.e., fluid recovery simulation, its implementation demands significantly more engineering effort when one deals with really sparse observed data. We believe that first and foremost comparison metric should be a reconstruction with baseline interpolation techniques (GPR). We will consider fluid recovery simulation as a metric for further work.
