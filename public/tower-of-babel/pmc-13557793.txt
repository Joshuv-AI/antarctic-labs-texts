Exploring the potential targets and molecular mechanisms of cordycepin against gastric cancer based on bioinformatics analysis and network pharmacology
Authors: Zijing Huang, Shuiqing Li, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the molecular targets and anti-tumor mechanisms of cordycepin in gastric cancer (GC). Bioinformatics analysis was performed using the GSE65801 dataset to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in GC. Potential cordycepin targets were screened from the CTD database, and the key gene was identified by intersecting these targets with GC DEGs and constructing a PPI network. The expression of fibronectin 1 (FN1) in GC was validated using public databases (TIMER, GEPIA, and UALCAN) and in vitro experiments in GC cell lines (AGS and HGC-27). The functional effects of cordycepin, alone or in combination with FN1 overexpression, on cell proliferation (CCK8 and colony formation assay), apoptosis (flow cytometry), and the PI3K/AKT pathway (Western blot) were assessed. A total of 649 DEGs were identified in GC and 336 potential targets of cordycepin were screened. FN1 was screened as a key intersection target of cordycepin and GC, and was found to be significantly upregulated in GC tissues and cells. High FN1 expression was associated with poor patient survival. Cordycepin treatment significantly inhibited the viability of GC cells and downregulated FN1 protein expression. Functionally, cordycepin suppressed cell proliferation and induced apoptosis by the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway, and these effects were reversed by overexpression of FN1. In conclusion, cordycepin inhibited GC cell growth partially through the downregulation of FN1 and inactivation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, suggesting that FN1 was a novel potential target for cordycepin in the treatment of GC.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most common malignant tumors in the world, which seriously threatens human health.(1–3) Although the treatment methods have made significant progress, the prognosis of patients with advanced GC is still poor.(4,5) Therefore, it is urgent to explore the molecular mechanism of GC development and find new effective therapeutic targets and drugs.

Natural products have become an important source of anti-cancer drugs due to their rich biological activities and low toxic side effects.(6–8) Cordycepin, a natural nucleoside analogue extracted from Cordyceps militaris and other fungi, has been proved to have many pharmacological activities, including immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and anti-tumor effects.(9–12) Studies had shown that cordycepin inhibited cell proliferation and induced apoptosis of a variety of cancers.(13,14) Previous study suggested that cordycepin had antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects in H. pylori-infected mice, confirming that cordycepin might be used for the treatment of gastritis or GC caused by H. pylori.(15) However, the specific targets and molecular mechanisms of cordycepin in GC have not been fully elucidated, which limits its further clinical application.

With the development of network pharmacology and bioinformatics, using public databases to screen key genes and drug targets has become an efficient research strategy.(16–18) By integrating gene expression profiling data, researchers can systematically identify key genes aberrantly expressed in GC, and cross-analyze them with potential drug targets, so as to quickly locate the core molecules.(19,20) Fibronectin 1 (FN1), an important component of ECM, plays a key role in cell differentiation and proliferation.(21,22) It had been reported that and FN1 was overexpressed in GC, and its high expression was correlated with GC progression.(23,24) In our study, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of GC were screened from the GEO database by bioinformatics methods, and cross-analyzed with the potential targets of cordycepin, and FN1 was successfully locked as the core candidate gene. However, whether FN1 acts as a target of cordycepin in GC and how it regulates downstream pathways remain to be verified. This study takes FN1 as the object to explore whether cordycepin plays an anti-GC effect by regulating FN1-related pathways, hoping to provide new ideas for the targeted therapy of GC.

Materials and Methods

GEO database analysis

Gene data were obtained from GEO database (GSE65801, including 32 GC tumor tissues and 32 normal tissues). The Limma method was adopted to analyze the DEGs, with the screening criteria as |log2FC|>2 and adj. p<0.05. Identified DEGs were displayed as volcano map, and the top 10 up-regulated/down-regulated DEGs were shown as heat map.

GO and KEGG analysis

The obtained DEGs were input into the DAVID database (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/) for GO and KEGG analyses, with p<0.05 to filter. The bioinformatic platform was used for mapping (top 10).

Screening the key genes

CTD database was used to analyze the potential molecular targets of cordycepin. Venn diagram was performed to obtain the intersection of cordycepin targets and DEGs in GC. PPI network of intersected targets was constructed by STRING database and visualized by Cytoscape software, respectively. According to the ranking of degree value, FN1 was selected.

Database download

TIMER database analyzed FN1 expression in pan-cancer tissues and normal tissues. GEPIA and UALCAN databases were used to analyze FN1 expression in GC tissues and normal tissues. The association between FN1 expression and the survival rate of GC patients was analyzed by UALCAN database.

Cell culture, treatment and transfection

Human GC cells (AGS and HGC-27) and normal gastric epithelial cells (GES-1) were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China), and cultured in Ham’s F-12 medium, RPMI-1640 medium, and DMEM, respectively. Cells were treated with different concentrations (0, 20, 40, and 60 ‍μM) of cordycepin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for 24 ‍h, or treated with 40 ‍μM cordycepin for different times (0, 12, 24, and 48 ‍h). For transfection, cells were transfected with pcDNA FN1 overexpression vector or FN1 siRNA (si-FN1) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), followed by treated with 40 ‍μM cordycepin or 30 ‍μM PI3K/AKT inhibitor LY294002 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 ‍h.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated by TRIzol (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed by PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). PCR amplification was performed using SYBR Green (Takara Bio) with specific primers (Table 1). Relative FN1 mRNA expression was examined by 2−ΔΔCt methods.

Western blot (WB)

After isolated from cells, total protein was separated and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with antibodies, including anti-FN1 (1:2,000, PA5-29578; Invitrogen), anti-AKT (1:500, ab8805; Abcam, Cambridge, CA), anti-p-AKT (1:500, ab38449; Abcam), anti-PI3K (1:1,000, ab191606; Abcam), anti-p-PI3K (1:500, ab182651; Abcam), anti-β-actin (1:200, ab115777; Abcam), and secondary antibody (ab205718/ab205719; Abcam). Membrane was treated with ECL reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to visualize protein blots.

CCK8 assay

Cells were re-seeded into 96-well plates and then treated with cordycepin for different concentrations or times. Then, cells were incubated with CCK8 reagent (Beyotime), and cell viability was determined at 450 ‍nm.

Colony formation assay

Transfected and treated cells were re-seeded into 6-well plates (200 cells/well). After culturing for 14 days, the colonies were stained with crystal violet, and colony numbers were analyzed under a microscope.

Flow cytometry

After transfection and treatment, cells were collected and incubated with Annexin V-FITC and PI solution (Beyotime). Cell apoptosis rate was determined under a flow cytometer.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 software was used, and data were shown as mean ± SD. Differences were analyzed by Student’s t test or ANOVA. P<0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

DEGs screening and GO/KEGG analysis

Firstly, the DEGs in GC tumor tissues and normal tissues were screened from GSE65801 dataset. A total of 208 up-regulated and 441 down-regulated DEGs were screened and shown as volcano map (Fig. 1A). The top 10 most differentially up-regulated and down-regulated genes were exhibited as heat map (Fig. 1B). GO analysis exhibited that the mainly biological process of DEGs was digestion, regulation of wound healing and regulation of response to wounding (Fig. 2A); the main cellular component was collagen-containing extracellular matrix, apical part of cell and apical plasma membrane (Fig. 2B); the main molecular function was extracellular matrix structural constituent, glycosaminoglycan binding and metal ion transmembrane transporter activity (Fig. 2C). KEGG analysis showed that the main signaling pathways of DEGs included protein digestion and absorption, ECM-receptor interaction and gastric acid secretion (Fig. 2D).

Screening of intersected targets for cordycepin and GC

CTD database predicted the 336 potential molecular targets of cordycepin. Venn diagram showed that there were 15 intersection targets between 336 cordycepin targets and 649 DEGs from GC (Fig. 3A). The PPI network of 15 intersected genes were analyzed via the STRING website (Fig. 3B). Cytoscape software was used for visualization (Fig. 3C), and according to the ranking of degree values, FN1 was selected as a potential molecular target for cordycepin to regulate GC progression for exploration.

FN1 was overexpressed in GC tissues and cells

In GSE65801 dataset, FN1 expression was higher expressed in 32 GC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 4A). TIMER database showed that FN1 was differentially expressed in various cancers (Fig. 4B). Besides, according to the results of GEPIA database, FN1 also had increased expression in the tumor tissues of GC (stomach adenocarcinoma, STAD) (Fig. 4C). In addition, UALCAN database analysis showed that FN1 was upregulated in GC tissues (Fig. 4D), and its high level was associated with poor survival of GC patients (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, through qRT-PCR and WB, our study confirmed that FN1 mRNA and protein expression was significantly higher in both GC cell lines (AGS and HGC-27) than that in GES-1 cells (Fig. 5A and B).

Cordycepin decreased FN1 expression in GC cells

The structural formula of cordycepin is shown in Fig. 6A. To determine the optimal concentration and duration of cordycepin treatment of GC cells, this study examined cell viability in response to cordycepin treatment at different concentrations and durations. The viability of AGS and HGC-27 cells was gradually decreased with the increasing of cordycepin concentration and treatment time (Fig. 6B and C). Besides, cordycepin treatment at 60 ‍μM for 48 ‍h significantly inhibited the viability of GES-1 cells (Supplemental Fig. 1A and B*). After evaluation, the optimal concentration for cordycepin treatment of GC cells was 40 ‍μM and the optimal time was 24 ‍h, which did not cause cytotoxicity to normal GES-1 cells. By detecting FN1 expression, our study confirmed that cordycepin markedly reduced FN1 protein level in AGS and HGC-27 cells (Fig. 6D and E).

Cordycepin inhibited GC cell growth by inactivating FN1/PI3K/AKT pathway

Further analysis was used to investigate the role of FN1 in GC progression. After transfected with si-FN1 into AGS and HGC-27 cells, FN1 protein expression was markedly decreased (Supplemental Fig. 2A*). FN1 knockdown reduced colony numbers, promoted apoptosis rate, and decreased the protein levels of p-AKT/AKT and p-PI3K/PI3K in AGS and HGC-27 cells (Supplemental Fig. 2B–E*). These results suggested that FN1 might promote GC cell growth by activating PI3K/AKT pathway. To explore whether cordycepin regulated GC progression by FN1-related pathway, the rescue experiments were performed. The transfection of FN1 overexpression vector reversed the decreasing effect of cordycepin on FN1 protein expression, while the PI3K/AKT inhibitor LY294002 had no effect on FN1 expression (Fig. 7A). Through functional assay, FN1 overexpression reversed cordycepin-mediated colony number inhibition and apoptosis rate promotion in AGS and HGC-27 cells, while these effects could be eliminated by PI3K/AKT inhibitor LY294002 (Fig. 7B and C). Moreover, FN1 overexpression also rescued the decreasing effect of cordycepin on the protein levels of p-AKT/AKT and p-PI3K/PI3K in GC cells, and the above effects could be abolished by PI3K/AKT inhibitor LY294002 (Fig. 7D and E). All data suggested that cordycepin repressed GC cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis by inhibiting PI3K/AKT pathway via reducing FN1 expression.

Discussion

Bioinformatics analysis and network pharmacology play a vital function in drug development and disease treatment.(25) Although the anti-cancer effect of cordycepin has been demonstrated in a variety of cancers,(26) whether cordycepin inhibits GC progression and its potential targets remain unclear. In our study, through database crossover analysis, 15 intersection genes were screened from 649 DEGs of GC and 336 potential targets of cordycepin, and FN1 was selected as the follow-up research object according to the core degree in the PPI network. These results reconfirmed the important role of FN1 in GC development, which was consistent with several previous studies.(23,24) Furthermore, our finding was supported by multi-dimensional data: public databases (GSE65801, GEPIA, and UALCAN) consistently showed that FN1 was overexpressed in GC tissues and correlated with poor prognosis of patients. FN1 expression in GC cell lines was markedly higher at both mRNA and protein levels. Together, these results suggest that FN1 may be a potential breakthrough for the intervention of cordycepin in GC.

FN1 abnormal expression is associated with a variety of diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease,(27) diabetes,(28) and tumors.(29) In thyroid carcinoma, overexpression of FN1 could promote cancer cell proliferation and metastasis.(30) Also, FN1 expression was correlated with unfavorable prognosis of breast cancer.(31) Importantly, the oncogenic role of FN1 in GC has been confirmed in many studies.(23,24) Our in vitro experiments showed that under the determined optimal treatment condition (40 ‍μM for 24 ‍h), cordycepin reduced FN1 protein expression. To establish a causal link, our study performed functional reversion experiments. Results showed that cordycepin-induced cell proliferation inhibition (decreased colony formation ability) and apoptosis promotion could be reversed when forced overexpression of FN1. This key evidence directly demonstrates that the anticancer activity of cordycepin against GC cells, to a large extent, is achieved by targeted inhibition of FN1.

The PI3K/AKT pathway is the core signal axis that regulates cell survival and proliferation.(32) Previous researches have formally established that cordycepin has an inhibitory effect on the PI3K/AKT pathway,(33) and FN1 can promote PI3K/AKT pathway activity.(34) Our WB results showed that cordycepin reduced p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT levels, and this inhibitory effect was also successfully reversed by FN1 overexpression. This series of data clearly outlined a signal transduction chain from cordycepin to functional phenotype: cordycepin blocked the activation of PI3K/AKT pathway through down-regulating FN1, which eventually led to GC cell proliferation inhibition and apoptosis promotion.

It is also important to consider the potential implications of cordycepins structural properties. As a nucleoside analogue (3'-deoxyadenosine), cordycepin structurally resembles adenosine, which raises the question of whether its anti-tumor effects might partially stem from non-specific interference with nucleic acid metabolism or DNA synthesis. While our study focused on protein-level signaling, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that at higher concentrations or in different cellular contexts, cordycepin may inhibit DNA synthesis, contributing to its cytotoxicity. However, the optimal concentration used in this study (40 ‍μM) was carefully selected to minimize cytotoxic effects on normal gastric epithelial cells, suggesting that the observed anti-cancer activity is not solely due to broad-spectrum metabolic disruption. Furthermore, the fact that forced overexpression of FN1 significantly reversed the effects of cordycepin indicates that FN1 is a key specific mediator of its action. Nevertheless, to fully delineate the mechanism, future studies should incorporate direct measurements of DNA synthesis rates (e.g., EdU incorporation assays) and compare the effects of cordycepin with other inactive nucleoside analogues. Such investigations would help to further distinguish between target-specific signaling modulation and structure-related metabolic interference.

In conclusion, this study systematically revealed the molecular mechanism by which the natural product cordycepin inhibits GC cell growth through targeting the FN1/PI3K/AKT axis. These findings provide an important theoretical basis for the use of FN1 as a potential target for GC treatment and the development of cordycepin as a therapeutic or adjuvant drug for GC. Future studies will aim to validate this mechanism in an in vivo model and to deeply explore the upstream events of cordycepin regulation of FN1 expression.
