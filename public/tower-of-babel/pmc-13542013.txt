Polyneuropathy Assessment and Treatment Patterns in Patients with Hereditary Transthyretin Amyloidosis
Authors: Nicholas Streicher, Hermela Wubet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (hATTR) manifests as cardiomyopathy and/or polyneuropathy. Polyneuropathy may be suspected for a variety of reasons, but the clinical evidence supporting that impression varies. We used International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) coding to broadly capture patients thought to have polyneuropathy in a V142I-predominant cohort and examined what clinical documentation actually supported the diagnosis and whether documentation level influences treatment selection.MethodsRetrospective chart review of 54 patients identified by co-occurring ICD-10 codes for transthyretin amyloidosis and polyneuropathy at a major academic medical center, with pathogenic TTR variant confirmation. Polyneuropathy codes were then classified by the level of clinical evidence supporting the diagnosis. Treatment with TTR stabilizers and gene silencers was assessed.ResultsOf 54 patients (90.7% non-Hispanic Black, 87.0% V142I), 51 (94.4%) had confirmed cardiac involvement with 40/42 eligible (95.2%) receiving stabilizers. Sixteen patients (29.6%) received gene silencers, with 11 receiving both concurrently. Clinical evidence supporting the polyneuropathy code was identified in 43 patients (79.6%): ancillary testing confirmation in 18 (33.3%), provider documentation without ancillary testing in 14 (25.9%), and symptoms only in 11 (20.4%). The remaining 11 (20.4%) had insufficient documentation to support the code. Gene silencer use was highest among those with ancillary testing (50.0%) versus symptoms only (18.2%).ConclusionAmong patients with hATTR coded for polyneuropathy, only 33.3% had ancillary testing confirmation. Gene silencer use tracked with documentation quality rather than the presence of a code alone. These findings highlight the gap between administrative coding and clinical documentation and support standardized neurological assessment in hATTR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40119-026-00472-6.Keywords: Hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis, V142I, Polyneuropathy, Gene silencer, Tafamidis, HELIOS-B, Neuropathy assessment

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? The V142I variant of hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis is increasingly identified in cardiology settings, where it is recognized mainly as a cardiomyopathy and its peripheral polyneuropathy is easily overlooked.How that polyneuropathy is assessed, documented, and connected to treatment in routine care has not been well described, particularly in a predominantly non-Hispanic Black, V142I population. What was learned from the study? Nearly all patients had confirmed cardiac involvement, yet only about one-third had nerve conduction or electromyographic testing documenting the polyneuropathy for which they were coded.Gene silencer use tracked more closely with the quality of polyneuropathy documentation than with the presence of a diagnosis code alone, and gene silencers were added to rather than substituted for stabilizer therapy.Because the cohort predates the 2025 approval of vutrisiran for ATTR cardiomyopathy, which lets gene silencers be started without neurological input, it offers a baseline against which the expected decline in neurologic evaluation can be measured and supports peripheral nerve assessment independent of treatment access.

Introduction

Hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (hATTR) is an autosomal dominant systemic disease caused by mutations in the transthyretin (TTR) gene that lead to misfolding and tissue deposition of amyloid fibrils [1, 2]. Clinical manifestations primarily affect the heart and peripheral nervous system, with phenotypic expression varying by specific genetic variant, geographic ancestry, and individual factors [2, 3].

The V142I variant is the most common pathogenic TTR mutation in the USA, carried by approximately 3–4% of African Americans, representing an estimated 1.2 to 1.5 million individuals [3, 4]. This variant classically produces a cardiac-predominant phenotype with heart failure symptoms typically manifesting after age 60, leading to its historical characterization as a cardiomyopathy [3]. However, emerging evidence suggests neurological involvement is more common than previously recognized, and reported rates vary widely with how patients are ascertained. A multinational biobank analysis found V142I carriers had significantly more polyneuropathy than non-carriers, though absolute rates from diagnostic codes were low [5]. US series that systematically assessed symptomatic or referred patients found polyneuropathy in nearly all those evaluated [6, 7], whereas a UK national cohort of the same variant, reviewed as patients presented, found it in roughly 40% and generally mild [8]. These estimates differ less in the disease itself than in how patients are selected and how intensively they are examined.

Phenotypic expression varies considerably by genotype and geographic ancestry. V50M is the most prevalent pathogenic variant worldwide and the prototypical neuropathic genotype, whereas V142I predominates among individuals of African ancestry and is the most common variant among US patients in the Transthyretin Amyloidosis Outcomes Survey (THAOS) [9]. When neurological involvement occurs, non-V50M genotypes may follow a more rapidly progressive course [10]. Regional cohorts from Southeast Asia [11], Japan [12], and Brazil [13] describe distinct genotype distributions and ages of onset; in the Brazilian series, 44% of individuals with V142I presented with neurological rather than cardiac onset [13], and diagnosis is frequently delayed when polyneuropathy is the presenting feature [12].

The therapeutic landscape for hATTR has expanded substantially. TTR stabilizers (tafamidis, acoramidis, diflunisal) prevent tetramer dissociation, with tafamidis demonstrating mortality benefit in ATTR cardiomyopathy [14] and evidence supporting neurological benefit in patients with mixed phenotypes [15, 16]. Gene silencer therapies (patisiran, vutrisiran, eplontersen) reduce hepatic TTR synthesis through RNA interference or antisense mechanisms, with established efficacy for polyneuropathy [17–19] and emerging data supporting cardiac benefits [20]. Inotersen, an antisense oligonucleotide approved for hATTR polyneuropathy, was withdrawn from the US market in 2024.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with co-occurring International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes for transthyretin amyloidosis and polyneuropathy, together with genetic confirmation of a pathogenic TTR variant, at a major academic medical center. Our aim was to examine what clinical evidence supports polyneuropathy coding in this population, whether documentation quality varies across patients, and whether the level of polyneuropathy documentation influences treatment selection, particularly gene silencer utilization, in this predominantly V142I cohort.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We performed a retrospective cohort study of patients diagnosed with hATTR at MedStar Georgetown University Hospital, a tertiary care academic medical center within the MedStar Health system in Washington, DC. Patients were identified through institutional electronic medical record query of encounters over approximately 8 years (2017–2025) using co-occurring ICD-10 codes for transthyretin amyloidosis and polyneuropathy: E85.1 (neuropathic heredofamilial amyloidosis), E85.82 (wild-type transthyretin-related amyloidosis), and/or G63 (polyneuropathy in diseases classified elsewhere), with confirmation of a pathogenic TTR variant through genetic testing documentation. The wild-type code was included to cast a broad net, since hereditary cases may be coded as wild-type when the subtype is misclassified or not yet established by genetic testing. This code-based approach captured patients in whom polyneuropathy was clinically considered by the coding provider.

All identified cases underwent individual chart review to verify clinical status and confirm the genetic diagnosis. Because cohort entry required a polyneuropathy code, the cohort is not intended to estimate population-level polyneuropathy prevalence. We excluded patients with unknown genotype (n = 2), asymptomatic gene carriers (n = 2), and pre-symptomatic carriers identified through family screening without clinical documentation of symptoms (n = 5) (Fig. 1).

Data Collection

Electronic medical records were reviewed for demographic information, TTR variant, cardiac involvement documentation (echocardiography, cardiac MRI, technetium pyrophosphate scintigraphy), neurological assessments (neurology consultations, electromyography/nerve conduction studies), and treatments received. The diagnosis of hereditary ATTR amyloidosis rested on confirmation of a pathogenic TTR variant, present in all 54 patients (100%); cardiac and tissue studies served to document amyloid involvement rather than to establish the genotype-based diagnosis. Exclusion of light-chain (AL) amyloidosis through serum and urine monoclonal-protein studies is a routine part of the diagnostic evaluation at our center. Cardiac involvement was confirmed through available institutional records including echocardiography, technetium-99m (Tc-99m) pyrophosphate scintigraphy, cardiac MRI, and/or endomyocardial biopsy. Documentation of these modalities across the cohort is summarized in the Supplementary Material. Diagnostic evaluations performed at outside facilities may not be fully captured.

Polyneuropathy Classification

Patients were classified into four mutually exclusive categories based on highest level of polyneuropathy documentation:

Polyneuropathy with ancillary testing: Abnormal electromyography (EMG)/nerve conduction study consistent with polyneuropathy.

Polyneuropathy with ancillary testing: Abnormal electromyography (EMG)/nerve conduction study consistent with polyneuropathy.

Polyneuropathy without ancillary testing: Clinical documentation of polyneuropathy by a provider without confirmatory ancillary testing.

Polyneuropathy without ancillary testing: Clinical documentation of polyneuropathy by a provider without confirmatory ancillary testing.

Symptoms only: patient-reported neuropathic symptoms (numbness, tingling, pain) documented in medical records without clinical examination findings or formal neurological evaluation.

Symptoms only: patient-reported neuropathic symptoms (numbness, tingling, pain) documented in medical records without clinical examination findings or formal neurological evaluation.

Unclear: Insufficient documentation to assess neurological status.

Unclear: Insufficient documentation to assess neurological status.

“Confirmed or suspected polyneuropathy” was defined as the combined group of patients with polyneuropathy with ancillary testing, polyneuropathy without ancillary testing, or symptoms only, representing all patients with any clinical evidence suggesting neurological involvement.

In this cohort, peripheral nerve involvement was classified as polyneuropathy. Carpal tunnel syndrome and other mononeuropathies, although recognized as early peripheral manifestations of transthyretin amyloidosis, were assessed separately and were not counted toward the polyneuropathy classification or used as a basis for disease-modifying therapy.

Treatment Assessment

Current or prior treatment was assessed for TTR stabilizers (tafamidis, acoramidis, diflunisal) and gene silencers (patisiran, vutrisiran, eplontersen). Stabilizer eligibility for patients with cardiac disease was defined as confirmed ATTR cardiomyopathy without documented contraindication or patient refusal. Inotersen was not prescribed to any patient in this cohort.

Confounding Conditions

We documented conditions that may cause or contribute to polyneuropathy independent of amyloidosis: diabetes mellitus, lumbar spinal stenosis, and vitamin B12 deficiency. These confounders were assessed to contextualize polyneuropathy findings and treatment decisions.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages and continuous variables as median with interquartile range (IQR). Associations between polyneuropathy documentation status (confirmed or suspected versus unclear) and completion of neurology consultation and electromyography were assessed using Fisher’s exact test, with a two-sided p value below 0.05 considered significant. Analyses were descriptive and exploratory; given the sample size, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. The participant flow diagram (Fig. 1) was generated using Matplotlib (Python).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Fifty-four patients met inclusion criteria (Table 1). The cohort was older, with a median age at last follow-up of 77 years (IQR 69–81), and predominantly non-Hispanic Black (49/54, 90.7%) and male (35/54, 64.8%), with V142I as the most common variant (47/54, 87.0%). Other variants included V50M (3 patients), A140S (2 patients), T60A and P84L (1 patient each). The available records capture a single encounter per patient and do not permit estimation of longitudinal encounter volume.

Confirmed cardiac involvement was present in 51 patients (94.4%), consistent with the expected cardiac-predominant phenotype of V142I [3]. Among patients with confirmed cardiomyopathy who were eligible for stabilizer therapy, 40 of 42 (95.2%) were receiving treatment. Nine were ineligible because of contraindication or refusal, and two eligible patients were not receiving stabilizers for reasons not clearly ascertainable from records.

Eleven patients (20.4%) were receiving both a stabilizer and a gene silencer concurrently, with gene silencers added to stabilizer therapy rather than replacing it. No patients in our cohort were prescribed gene silencers under the ATTR cardiomyopathy indication; all gene silencer use reflected the polyneuropathy indication. Across the full cohort, 37 patients (68.5%) received the stabilizer tafamidis and 16 (29.6%) received a gene silencer, while 9 (16.7%) received no disease-modifying therapy and 3 (5.6%) underwent heart transplantation. Agent-level therapy detail and diagnostic evaluations, including cardiac imaging modalities documented in available cardiology notes, are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Discussion

V142I Phenotype and Neurological Involvement

This single-center analysis of 54 patients with hATTR, predominantly non-Hispanic Black and carrying the V142I variant, reflects the expected cardiac-predominant phenotype while documenting both frequent neuropathic involvement and wide variation in how that polyneuropathy was assessed and documented. Reported polyneuropathy prevalence among individuals with the V142I variant varies widely, from approximately 2–9% in studies based on administrative codes [5] to over 90% at specialized centers employing nerve conduction studies and skin biopsy [6]. This variation appears to reflect how intensively polyneuropathy is sought rather than true differences between populations: the more actively it is assessed, the more it is found.

Our cohort was assembled by selecting patients who already carried a polyneuropathy code, an enriched sample rather than one representative of all patients with hereditary ATTR amyloidosis. The proportion with documented clinical evidence is therefore not a population prevalence estimate, and the smaller proportion with electromyographic confirmation reflects documentation within this selected group rather than a true rate of disease. This documented burden is nonetheless substantial and consistent in direction with recent reports of greater neuropathic involvement in V142I [5] than its historical cardiac-only characterization [3] implied.

The majority of patients had at least one condition independently associated with polyneuropathy, including diabetes mellitus, lumbar spinal stenosis, and vitamin B12 deficiency. Polyneuropathy in a given patient may not be attributable to amyloid deposition alone. Both the method of assessment and the burden of confounders must be considered when interpreting polyneuropathy prevalence in hATTR.

Three patients lacked confirmed cardiac involvement despite carrying pathogenic TTR variants (one V142I, one V50M, one P84L). Patients such as these may represent early detection through cascade screening or genuinely neurologic-predominant phenotypes. As genetic testing expands, identifying gene-positive individuals before cardiac manifestations develop will become more common.

Although the present analysis concerns hereditary ATTR, wild-type ATTR is the more common cause of transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis overall, and peripheral nerve involvement, most commonly carpal tunnel syndrome as well as polyneuropathy, is increasingly recognized in that population as well. As screening for ATTR broadens, identifying the diverse neurological manifestations of hereditary and wild-type disease will become increasingly relevant.

Treatment Patterns

Stabilizer utilization among eligible patients with cardiac disease was high, reflecting established practice following pivotal trial data demonstrating mortality benefit [14]. Gene silencer use showed a clear gradient by polyneuropathy documentation: highest among patients with ancillary testing confirmation and among the lowest with symptoms alone. Most patients receiving gene silencers were also on concurrent stabilizer therapy. Three patients without confirmed cardiac disease received gene silencers, consistent with a neurologic-predominant phenotype.

Treatment selection was at the discretion of the prescribing physician, and this additive pattern, in which gene silencers were layered onto continued stabilizer therapy rather than substituted for it, reflects their use for neurological indications while stabilizers addressed cardiac disease. No patients in our cohort were prescribed gene silencers under the recently approved ATTR cardiomyopathy indication (FDA March 2025, based on HELIOS-B trial data [20]); all gene silencer access was predicated on neurological documentation.

A substantial proportion of patients received both a stabilizer and a gene silencer, although combination therapy is not proven superior. The cardiac and neurologic distinction is partly an artifact of referral, driven by the prescribing scope of cardiology and neurology. A recent network meta-analysis favored gene silencers over stabilizers, a finding its authors treat as hypothesis-generating in the absence of head-to-head trials [21].

Gene silencer use was concentrated among patients whose polyneuropathy was documented by electrodiagnostic testing or clinical evaluation. The few patients with unclear polyneuropathy status who received a gene silencer were those without confirmed cardiac involvement, in whom treatment was directed at a neurologic-predominant presentation. Carpal tunnel syndrome, a mononeuropathy that does not by itself establish polyneuropathy, was reviewed but was not used as a basis for disease-modifying therapy.

With vutrisiran now approved for cardiomyopathy, cardiologists can prescribe gene silencers without neurological documentation. Polyneuropathy may go uncharacterized, since documentation is no longer required for treatment access. Our finding that most patients in a cardiac-predominant cohort had documented polyneuropathy suggests that systematic neurological assessment adds clinical value beyond treatment access, including establishing baseline polyneuropathy severity, guiding monitoring, and identifying patients who may benefit from multidisciplinary management.

Limitations

As a retrospective chart review relying on administrative coding for cohort identification, ICD-10 codes may overcapture or undercapture patients. The single-center, V142I-predominant cohort limits generalizability to other variants, and the small number of patients without cardiac involvement (n = 3) limits inference for that subgroup. Ancillary testing was limited to EMG and nerve conduction studies; skin biopsy and autonomic testing were not performed, so small-fiber polyneuropathy may be undercounted [22]. The retrospective design also precluded determination of symptom-onset sequence and of the clinical reasoning behind individual treatment decisions.

Future Directions

Emerging tools including serum neurofilament light chain (NfL), nerve ultrasound, and structured patient-reported outcome measures may help standardize polyneuropathy detection in hATTR and provide perspectives on the diagnostic journey not captured in chart review.

Our own data illustrate the effect of ascertainment: the apparent polyneuropathy rate in our cohort varies widely depending on whether it is restricted to objective EMG confirmation or extended to clinically documented symptoms. In the UK cohort of the same variant, applying the broader case definition used in US clinical series raised the confirmed rate from roughly 40% to 60%, still well below the near-universal involvement those series reported, illustrating how strongly the case definition alone shapes these estimates [8].

Co-existing conditions capable of causing polyneuropathy were present in roughly three-quarters of our cohort, exceeding the roughly one-half reported by Desai and colleagues [7], and attribution of polyneuropathy to amyloid is hardest precisely where such conditions are most common. Standardizing polyneuropathy assessment criteria in hATTR, including which diagnostic modalities to employ and how to account for competing etiologies in this patient population, represents an important area for future investigation.

This study also points to a need for coding systems that more accurately reflect clinical documentation. As machine learning and other automated tools increasingly draw on coded data, the gap between a diagnostic code and the documentation behind it becomes more consequential [23].

Conclusions

In this cohort of patients with hereditary ATTR, primarily V142I, selected for a coded diagnosis of polyneuropathy, most had documented clinical support for that diagnosis, though the strength and quality of that support varied widely. Gene silencer use tracked with the strength of that documentation rather than the diagnosis alone. Concern for polyneuropathy marks where clinical reasoning should begin and calls for a standardized assessment protocol. Such evaluation distinguishes a recorded diagnosis from objective clinical and neurophysiologic findings and retains value even when cardiac disease dominates and treatment is in place.

As gene silencers become approved for cardiomyopathy and no longer require neurological documentation for access, the incentive to perform that assessment may fall, although its clinical relevance remains. Recognizing the full neurological burden of transthyretin amyloidosis, across hereditary variants and in wild-type disease as well, will require that assessment continue on clinical grounds, even where it is no longer required for treatment.
