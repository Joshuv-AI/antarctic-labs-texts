1.
Introduction

A real square matrix is called doubly stochastic if it has non-negative entries and all of its row and column sums are equal to one. The Symmetric Doubly Stochastic Inverse Eigenvalue Problem (SDIEP) asks for the necessary and sufficient conditions under which a given list of real numbers
\sigma=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n})
can be the spectrum of an
n\times n
symmetric doubly stochastic matrix
[
1
]
. By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, any such matrix must have a Perron eigenvalue of
1
, corresponding to the eigenvector of all ones. This fixes
\lambda_{1}=1
and, by Gershgorin’s circle theorem, bounds all other eigenvalues to the interval
[-1,1]
.

A significant body of research focuses on the realisability of a
Suleĭmanova spectrum
, a list of the form
(1,\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n})
where each
\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0
for
j\geqslant 2
and the trace is non-negative,
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}\geqslant 0
. A powerful constructive method for tackling this problem is the Schur template: given a diagonal matrix
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(1,\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n})
, one constructs a candidate matrix
P(\Lambda)=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
. If
Q
is an orthogonal matrix whose first column is the Perron eigenvector
q_{0}=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
, the resulting matrix
P(\Lambda)
is automatically symmetric, doubly stochastic, and has the desired spectrum. The entire problem is thus reduced to a single, challenging question: for a given spectrum
\Lambda
and a chosen basis
Q
, what conditions guarantee that
P(\Lambda)
is entrywise non-negative?

The papers
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
seem to be foundational for the problem. Recent work has approached this question from several angles. One approach provides general sufficient conditions that depend only on the sum of the non-Perron eigenvalues. A key result in this vein was provided by Gnacik and the author
[
1
]
, who used the eigenbasis of the
n
-cycle graph for
Q
to show that any Suleĭmanova spectrum is realisable if
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>1/2
. It was noted, however, that this bound appeared to be sharp for this basis, particularly in dimensions where
n
is a multiple of 8. A different approach, seen in the work of Sarkhoni
et al
.
[
9
]
, forgoes general sum-based conditions and instead derives necessary and sufficient conditions for highly structured spectra, such as
(1,0,\dots,0,\lambda_{n})
, by constructing a bespoke orthogonal matrix
Q
tailored to that specific list.

In this paper, we unify and advance these perspectives. We begin by revisiting the cycle-walk basis from
[
1
]
, replacing their baseline bound with a precise, dimension-dependent threshold that confirms the ‘
8\mid n
’ barrier is an artefact of the basis, not the problem itself. We then resolve this issue by introducing an optimally phase-aligned cycle basis that yields an improved bound, strictly better than
1/2
for all dimensions
n\geqslant 3
. Finally, we generalise the underlying principle to a criterion based on basis
coherence
, showing that bases derived from abelian 2-groups (Walsh–Hadamard matrices) achieve the ideal coherence, allowing for the realisation of
all
Suleĭmanova spectra with a strictly positive trace sum.

Let
\sigma=(1,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
with
\lambda_{j}\in[-1,0]
for
j\geqslant 2
. Following Paparella
[
5
]
, we call
\sigma
a
Suleĭmanova spectrum
and say it is
\delta
-Suleĭmanova
if

1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>\delta.

In
[
1
]
a Markov-chain-inspired template was introduced: take
Q=[w_{0},\ldots,w_{n-1}]
to be a real orthonormal eigenbasis of the simple random walk on the
n
-cycle, so
w_{0}=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
and, for
k\geqslant 1
,

w_{k}(j)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\;\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{2\pi kj}{n}+\tfrac{\pi}{4}\Big)\quad(j=0,\ldots,n-1).

Setting
P(\Lambda)=Q\,\mathrm{diag}(1,\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})\,Q^{\top}
gives a symmetric matrix with unit row sums. Using
S_{j}(k):=\sin\!\big(\tfrac{2\pi kj}{n}+\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)
one has

p_{kl}=\frac{1}{n}\!\left(1+2\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}\,S_{j}(k)\,S_{j}(l)\right).

The crude estimate
S_{j}(k)S_{j}(l)\leqslant 1
and
\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0
yields the sufficient condition
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>\tfrac{1}{2}
. It was also observed that when
8\mid n
there are
j,k
with
S_{j}(k)=1
, so this estimate cannot beat
\tfrac{1}{2}
for those sizes, and exhibited a
5\times 5
spectrum at sum
0.48
violating entrywise positivity. This suggests trying other orthogonal choices of
Q
to reduce the worst interaction
n\,|q_{j}(k)q_{j}(l)|
(all from
[
1
]
).

In the present paper, we keep the Schur template
P(\Lambda)=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
and:

•

compute
exactly
the worst coefficient for the canonical cycle basis, obtaining a closed-form
\delta_{n}
that improves
\tfrac{1}{2}
iff
8\nmid n
, with a matching sharpness example;

•

introduce an
optimally phase-aligned
cycle basis (still orthogonal, same Perron column) giving the
correct
piecewise bound
\delta_{n}^{\rm(ph)}<\tfrac{1}{2}
for every
n\geqslant 3
, which strictly improves the
8\mid n
case and is optimal within the cycle family;

•

prove a general ‘coherence
\Rightarrow
sufficient
\delta
’ lemma and apply it to Walsh–Hadamard bases (abelian
2
-groups) and Hadamard orders (via Paley), obtaining
\delta=0
in those orders only
. Here ‘
\delta=0
’ means the
strict
condition
1+\sum\lambda_{j}>0
suffices; this does not contradict obstructions on the boundary
1+\sum\lambda_{j}=0
(
e.g.
,
(1,0,\dots,0,-1)
in odd dimensions).

2.
The cycle-walk basis

From now on we reindex the list as
\sigma=(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})
with
\lambda_{0}=1
and
\lambda_{j}\in[-1,0]
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
, and use the cycle eigenbasis
Q=[w_{0},\dots,w_{n-1}]
described above.

Lemma 1
.

Let
Q
and
\Lambda
be as above. Then

(1)

p_{kl}=\frac{1}{n}\!\left(1+2\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}\,S_{j}(k)\,S_{j}(l)\right),\quad S_{j}(k):=\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{2\pi kj}{n}+\tfrac{\pi}{4}\Big).

If
\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0
, writing

c_{n}:=\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1}\ \max_{0\leqslant k,l\leqslant n-1}S_{j}(k)\,S_{j}(l),

we have the bound

(2)

p_{kl}\ \geqslant\ \frac{1}{n}\Bigl(1+2c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}\Bigr)\qquad(0\leqslant k,l\leqslant n-1).

Proof.

Orthogonality and
w_{0}=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
give
P(\Lambda)\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
. Expanding
Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
yields (
1
). Because
\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0
and
S_{j}(k)S_{j}(l)\leqslant c_{n}
for each
j
, multiplying the latter inequality by
\lambda_{j}

reverses
it, hence

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}S_{j}(k)S_{j}(l)\ \geqslant\ c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j},

and (
2
) follows.
∎

Lemma 2

(Exact value of
c_{n}
and the mod-
8
mechanism)
.

Define

\Delta_{n}\;:=\;\min_{m,t\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\frac{2\pi m}{n}-\frac{(2t+1)\pi}{4}\right|.

Then

c_{n}=\cos^{2}\Delta_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta_{n}=\frac{\pi}{4n}\,\rho(n),\qquad\rho(n)=\begin{cases}0,&n\equiv 0\pmod{8},\\
1,&n\equiv 1,3,5,7\pmod{8},\\
2,&n\equiv 2,6\pmod{8},\\
4,&n\equiv 4\pmod{8}.\end{cases}

Moreover, the maximum in
c_{n}
is attained with
k=l
.

Proof.

For fixed
j
with
\gcd(j,n)=1
, the multiset
\{2\pi kj/n+\pi/4:\ 0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1\}
is a permutation of the grid
\{2\pi m/n+\pi/4:\ 0\leqslant m\leqslant n-1\}
. Hence

\max_{k}|S_{j}(k)|=\max_{m}\Big|\sin\Big(\frac{2\pi m}{n}+\frac{\pi}{4}\Big)\Big|=\sin\!\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}-\Delta_{n}\Big)=\cos\Delta_{n},

where
\Delta_{n}
is the minimal angular distance from this grid to the odd half-angles. Therefore
c_{n}=(\max_{j,k}|S_{j}(k)|)^{2}=\cos^{2}\Delta_{n}
, and the maximum occurs already for
k=l
at a peak. As

\frac{2\pi m}{n}-\frac{(2t+1)\pi}{4}=\frac{\pi}{4n}\big(8m-(2t+1)n\big)

implies
\Delta_{n}=\tfrac{\pi}{4n}\min_{m,t}|\,8m-(2t+1)n\,|
. We now analyse residues of
n
modulo
8
.

•

n\equiv 0\pmod{8}
.
Then
(2t+1)n
is divisible by
8
for every
t
, so choosing
m=(2t+1)n/8
gives the minimum
0
. Thus
\rho(n)=0
.

•

n
odd.
Here
\gcd(n,8)=1
. By Bézout’s theorem, there exist integers
m,t
with
8m-(2t+1)n=1
. Hence, the minimum positive value is
1
and
\rho(n)=1
.

•

n\equiv 2,6\pmod{8}
.
Write
n=2k
with
k
odd. Then
8m-(2t+1)n\equiv 8m-2(2t+1)k\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)
but never
0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
. Hence, the minimum positive value is
2
and
\rho(n)=2
.

•

n\equiv 4\pmod{8}
.
Write
n=4k
with
k
odd. Then
8m-(2t+1)n\equiv 8m-4(2t+1)k\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
but never
0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)
. The minimum positive value is
4
and
\rho(n)=4
.

∎

Theorem 3

(Dimension-dependent bound for the cycle basis)
.

With
\Delta_{n}
as above, set

\delta_{n}:=1-\frac{1}{2\cos^{2}\Delta_{n}}\ =\ \frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\tan^{2}\Delta_{n}.

If
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>\delta_{n}
, then
P(\Lambda)=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
is symmetric and doubly stochastic. In particular
\delta_{n}<\tfrac{1}{2}
if and only if
8\nmid n
, whereas
\delta_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}
when
8\mid n
.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
, for every
k,l
,

p_{kl}\ \geqslant\ \frac{1}{n}\Bigl(1+2c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}\Bigr),\qquad c_{n}=\cos^{2}\Delta_{n}.

by Lemma
2
. Hence
p_{kl}\geqslant 0
whenever
\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}>-1/(2c_{n})
, i.e., when

1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}\ >\ 1-\frac{1}{2\cos^{2}\Delta_{n}}\ =\ \delta_{n},

which establishes entrywise nonnegativity under the stated hypothesis.

Since
Q
is orthogonal and
\Lambda
is (real and) diagonal (hence
\Lambda^{\top}=\Lambda
),

P(\Lambda)^{\top}=(Q\Lambda Q^{\top})^{\top}=(Q^{\top})^{\top}\Lambda^{\top}Q^{\top}=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}=P(\Lambda),

so
P(\Lambda)
is symmetric.

Let
q_{0},\dots,q_{n-1}
be the columns of
Q
and let
e_{0}=(1,0,\dots,0)^{\top}
. Since
q_{0}=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
and
q_{j}\perp q_{0}
for
j\geqslant 1
,

Q^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\big(\langle q_{0},\mathbf{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle q_{n-1},\mathbf{1}\rangle\big)^{\top}=(\sqrt{n},0,\dots,0)^{\top}=\sqrt{n}\,e_{0}.

Therefore

P(\Lambda)\,\mathbf{1}=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\mathbf{1}=Q\Lambda(\sqrt{n}\,e_{0})=\sqrt{n}\,Q(\Lambda e_{0})=\sqrt{n}\,Qe_{0}=\sqrt{n}\,q_{0}=\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\mathbf{1}}{\sqrt{n}}=\mathbf{1}.

Thus every row sum equals
1
. Finally,

\mathbf{1}^{\top}P(\Lambda)=\mathbf{1}^{\top}Q\Lambda Q^{\top}=(Q^{\top}\mathbf{1})^{\top}\Lambda Q^{\top}=(\sqrt{n}\,e_{0})^{\top}\Lambda Q^{\top}

=\sqrt{n}\,e_{0}^{\top}\Lambda Q^{\top}=\sqrt{n}\,e_{0}^{\top}Q^{\top}=(\sqrt{n}\,Qe_{0})^{\top}=\mathbf{1}^{\top},

where we used that
\Lambda e_{0}=e_{0}
because the first diagonal entry of
\Lambda
is
\lambda_{0}=1
. Hence, every column sum also equals
1
.

Combining the three parts shows that
P(\Lambda)
is symmetric and doubly stochastic, whenever
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>\delta_{n}
. The stated equivalence for
\delta_{n}<\tfrac{1}{2}
versus
8\nmid n
follows from Lemma
2
, which gives
c_{n}=\cos^{2}\Delta_{n}=1
iff
8\mid n
.
∎

Proposition 4

(Sharpness for the cycle basis)
.

Fix
n
and choose indices realising

c_{n}=\max_{j,k,l}S_{j}(k)S_{j}(l).

If
\lambda_{j^{\ast}}=t<0
and
\lambda_{j}=0
for
j\neq j^{\ast}
, then

p_{k^{\ast}k^{\ast}}=\frac{1}{n}\bigl(1+2t\,\cos^{2}\Delta_{n}\bigr),

which is non-negative if and only if
t\geqslant-1/(2\cos^{2}\Delta_{n})
, i.e.
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}\geqslant\delta_{n}
. Thus Theorem
3
is optimal for this
Q
among conditions depending only on
\sum_{j\geqslant 2}\lambda_{j}
.

Remark 5
.

We see that divisibility by
8
is the exact barrier. Indeed, by Lemma
2
, the grid
\{2\pi m/n+\pi/4\}
hits an odd half-angle iff
8\mid n
, and then
c_{n}=1
so the factor
\tfrac{1}{2}
is unavoidable for this
Q
. If
8\nmid n
, the nearest grid point sits at angular distance
\Delta_{n}=\pi\rho(n)/(4n)
from
\pi/2
, giving
c_{n}=\cos^{2}\Delta_{n}<1
and hence
\delta_{n}<1/2
.

3.
Phase-optimised cycle bases

The eigenbasis of the
n
-cycle graph is not unique. For each
j\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor\}
, the corresponding eigenspace
E_{j}
is two-dimensional, spanned by a sine and cosine vector. The canonical basis analysed in Section
2
represents just one choice of basis within these spaces, a choice that, for dimensions where
8\mid n
, leads to an unavoidable coherence peak and a bound of
\delta_{n}=1/2
. This section demonstrates that by rotating the basis in each eigenspace via an optimally chosen phase angle
\phi
, we can construct a new orthogonal matrix
Q^{\rm(ph)}
. This ‘phase-optimised‘ basis is designed to minimise the maximum magnitude of its entries, effectively smoothing the coherence peaks and removing the “
8\mid n
” artefact entirely. More specifically, for each
j\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor\}
the cycle eigenspace is two-dimensional,

E_{j}=\operatorname{span}\{u_{j},v_{j}\}
with

u_{j}(k)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\cos(\tfrac{2\pi jk}{n}),\ v_{j}(k)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\sin(\tfrac{2\pi jk}{n}).

Any orthonormal pair in
E_{j}
is of the form

w^{(\phi)}_{j}(k)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{2\pi jk}{n}+\phi\Big),\qquad\tilde{w}^{(\phi)}_{j}(k)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\cos\!\Big(\tfrac{2\pi jk}{n}+\phi\Big),

for some phase
\phi
. When
n
is even and
j=n/2
, the eigenspace is one-dimensional; choosing the phase so that
|\sin(\phi)|=1/\sqrt{2}
yields a unit vector with
|w_{n/2}(k)|\equiv 1/\sqrt{n}
, hence
n\,|q_{n/2}(k)q_{n/2}(l)|\equiv 1
.

We must control
both
columns in each
E_{j}
simultaneously. Write
n^{\prime}:=n/\gcd(j,n)
; then
\{\tfrac{2\pi jk}{n}\}
is a translate of the grid with step
2\pi/n^{\prime}
.

Lemma 6

(Two-phase covering radius on a grid)
.

Let
n^{\prime}:=n/\gcd(j,n)
and set
S:=2\pi/n^{\prime}
and
L:=\pi/2
. All angles are considered reduced modulo
L
. Define

\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})\ :=\ \begin{cases}\pi/n^{\prime},&n^{\prime}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4),\\[2.84526pt]
\pi/(2n^{\prime}),&n^{\prime}\equiv 2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4),\\[2.84526pt]
\pi/(4n^{\prime}),&n^{\prime}\ \mathrm{odd}.\end{cases}

For
w^{(\phi)}_{j}(k)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\sin(\tfrac{2\pi jk}{n}+\phi)
and

\tilde{w}^{(\phi)}_{j}(k)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\cos(\tfrac{2\pi jk}{n}+\phi)
one has

\min_{\phi}\ \max\!\Big\{\,\|w^{(\phi)}_{j}\|_{\infty},\ \|\tilde{w}^{(\phi)}_{j}\|_{\infty}\,\Big\}\ =\ \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\ \cos\big(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})\big),

and consequently

\min_{\phi}\ \sup_{k,l}\ n\,\max\{|w^{(\phi)}_{j}(k)w^{(\phi)}_{j}(l)|,\ |\tilde{w}^{(\phi)}_{j}(k)\tilde{w}^{(\phi)}_{j}(l)|\}\ =\ 2\cos^{2}\big(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})\big).

Proof.

Let
A:=\{mL:\ m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with
L=\pi/2
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(3)

\max\{|\sin x|,|\cos x|\}=\cos\big(\operatorname{dist}(x,A)\big).

Indeed, reduce
x
modulo
L
to
y\in[0,L]
. Then

|\cos x|=|\cos y|,\qquad|\sin x|=|\sin y|=|\cos(L-y)|.

Hence
\max\{|\sin x|,|\cos x|\}=\max\{|\cos y|,|\cos(L-y)|\}=\cos\big(\min\{y,L-y\}\big)
.
However
\min\{y,L-y\}=\operatorname{dist}(x,A)
by construction, giving (
3
).

Fix
j
and write
n^{\prime}:=n/\gcd(j,n)
. The set of phases

\Theta_{j}:=\Big\{\tfrac{2\pi jk}{n}\,:\ k=0,1,\dots,n-1\Big\}

is a union of
\gcd(j,n)
translates of the uniform grid
\{2\pi m/n^{\prime}:\ m=0,\dots,n^{\prime}-1\}
. Since a translation does not affect sup norms, it suffices to study the latter uniform grid. After adding a free phase
\phi
and reducing modulo
L
we obtain

X_{\phi}:=\Big\{\tfrac{2\pi m}{n^{\prime}}+\phi\ \bmod L:\ m=0,\dots,n^{\prime}-1\Big\}\ \subset\ \mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}.

This set is a coset (translate) of the cyclic subgroup
G:=\langle\overline{S}\rangle
of the circle
\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
, generated by the step
\overline{S}
which is the class of
S:=2\pi/n^{\prime}
modulo
L
.

The order
r:=|G|
is the least positive integer such that
r\overline{S}=\overline{0}
, i.e.

rS\in L\mathbb{Z}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{2\pi r}{n^{\prime}}=\frac{\pi}{2}\,q\ \text{ for some }q\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{4r}{n^{\prime}}=q\in\mathbb{Z}.

Thus
r
is the least positive integer with
n^{\prime}\mid 4r
, which is

r=\frac{n^{\prime}}{\gcd(n^{\prime},4)}.

Consequently, the
r
points of
G
are equally spaced on the circle
\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
with spacing

(4)

s_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{L}{r}=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{\gcd(n^{\prime},4)}{n^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}2\pi/n^{\prime},&n^{\prime}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4),\\[2.84526pt]
\pi/n^{\prime},&n^{\prime}\equiv 2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4),\\[2.84526pt]
\pi/(2n^{\prime}),&n^{\prime}\ \text{odd}.\end{cases}

For a fixed translate
X_{\phi}=\overline{\phi}+G
, the maximal distance from the origin
\overline{0}
to
X_{\phi}
is

\rho(\phi):=\max_{x\in X_{\phi}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\overline{0}).

Because
X_{\phi}
is equally spaced with gap
s_{\mathrm{eff}}
, the circle
\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
is partitioned into
r
closed arcs of length
s_{\mathrm{eff}}
whose endpoints are the points of
X_{\phi}
. The point
\overline{0}
lies in a unique such arc; its distance to the nearer endpoint is at most
s_{\mathrm{eff}}/2
, and equals
s_{\mathrm{eff}}/2
precisely when
\overline{0}
is at the midpoint of that arc. Therefore

(5)

\min_{\phi}\ \rho(\phi)\ =\ \frac{s_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}.

Moreover, such a minimising shift
\phi
exists: take any
x_{0}\in G
and choose
\phi
so that
\overline{\phi}+x_{0}=\overline{s_{\mathrm{eff}}/2}
.

For any
\phi
we have, by (
3
),

\max\Big\{\|w^{(\phi)}_{j}\|_{\infty},\ \|\tilde{w}^{(\phi)}_{j}\|_{\infty}\Big\}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\ \max_{x\in X_{\phi}}\max\{|\sin x|,|\cos x|\}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\ \max_{x\in X_{\phi}}\cos\big(\operatorname{dist}(x,\overline{0})\big).

Since
\cos
is decreasing on
[0,\pi/2]
, the inner maximum equals
\cos(d(\phi))
where

d(\phi):=\min_{x\in X_{\phi}}\operatorname{dist}(x,\overline{0}).

Thus

\min_{\phi}\ \max\Big\{\|w^{(\phi)}_{j}\|_{\infty},\ \|\tilde{w}^{(\phi)}_{j}\|_{\infty}\Big\}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\ \min_{\phi}\cos\big(d(\phi)\big)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\ \cos\!\Big(\max_{\phi}d(\phi)\Big).

Because
X_{\phi}
is a translate of a cyclic subgroup
G
of order
r=\frac{n^{\prime}}{\gcd(n^{\prime},4)}
consisting of
r
equally spaced points with gap
s_{\mathrm{eff}}=L/r
, the circle
\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
is partitioned into
r
arcs of length
s_{\mathrm{eff}}
with endpoints
X_{\phi}
. Placing
\overline{0}
at the midpoint of one such arc yields

\max_{\phi}d(\phi)=\frac{s_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}.

Combining with (
4
) gives the stated
\sqrt{2/n}\,\cos(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime}))
.

Finally, for either column in the pair, we obtain

\min_{\phi}\ \sup_{k,l}\ n\,|q(k)q(l)|\ \leqslant\ \min_{\phi}\ n\,\|q\|_{\infty}^{2}\ \leqslant\ n\cdot\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\cos(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime}))\Big)^{2}=2\cos^{2}(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})).

This upper bound is tight because, at the minimising shift, there are
k
attaining the distance
\rho(\phi)=s_{\mathrm{eff}}/2
, hence
|q(k)|=\sqrt{2/n}\,\cos(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime}))
for one of the two vectors. Taking the maximum over the two vectors then attains
2\cos^{2}(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime}))
on some pair
(k,l)
, proving the second claim.
∎

Because
\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})
decreases with
n^{\prime}
, the worst case is
\gcd(j,n)=1
,
i.e.
,
n^{\prime}=n
.

Theorem 7

(Phase-optimised coherence)
.

There exists an orthogonal matrix
Q^{\rm(ph)}
with first column
\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
such that

M\big(Q^{\rm(ph)}\big)\ =\ \begin{cases}2\cos^{2}\!\big(\dfrac{\pi}{n}\big),&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\[5.69054pt]
2\cos^{2}\!\big(\dfrac{\pi}{2n}\big),&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\[5.69054pt]
2\cos^{2}\!\big(\dfrac{\pi}{4n}\big),&n\ \mathrm{odd},\end{cases}

and this value is optimal within the cycle family.
Consequently,

\delta^{\mathrm{(ph)}}_{n}\;:=\;1-\frac{1}{M(Q^{\rm(ph)})}\ =\ \begin{cases}\displaystyle 1-\dfrac{1}{2\cos^{2}(\pi/n)},&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle 1-\dfrac{1}{2\cos^{2}(\pi/2n)},&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle 1-\dfrac{1}{2\cos^{2}(\pi/4n)},&n\ \text{odd},\end{cases}

and
0\leqslant\delta^{\mathrm{(ph)}}_{n}<\tfrac{1}{2}
for every
n\geqslant 3
. Moreover,
\delta^{\mathrm{(ph)}}_{n}=\delta_{n}
unless
8\mid n
, and in all cases
\delta^{\mathrm{(ph)}}_{n}\nearrow\tfrac{1}{2}
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

To show the upper bound, for each
j\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor\}
let us choose a phase
\phi_{j}
that attains the minimum in Lemma
6
for
n^{\prime}=n/\gcd(j,n)
, and replace the canonical pair
\{u_{j},v_{j}\}
by
\{w_{j}^{(\phi_{j})},\tilde{w}_{j}^{(\phi_{j})}\}
. This rotation within
E_{j}
preserves orthonormality and keeps the Perron column
w_{0}=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
unchanged; doing this independently for each
j
yields an orthogonal matrix
Q^{\rm(ph)}
.

By Lemma
6
, for each such
j
,

\max\big\{\|w_{j}^{(\phi_{j})}\|_{\infty},\ \|\tilde{w}_{j}^{(\phi_{j})}\|_{\infty}\big\}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\;\cos\!\big(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})\big),

hence for either column in this pair we have

n\,\|q\|_{\infty}^{2}\leqslant 2\cos^{2}\!\big(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})\big).

If
n
is even and
j=n/2
, the one-dimensional eigenspace yields a column with constant modulus
1/\sqrt{n}
, so
n\,\|q\|_{\infty}^{2}=1
.

Therefore

(6)

M\big(Q^{\rm(ph)}\big)\ \leqslant\ \max\Big\{\,\mathbf{1}_{\{2\mid n\}},\ \max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\ 2\cos^{2}\!\big(\Delta^{\star}(n/\gcd(j,n))\big)\Big\}.

Since
n^{\prime}\mapsto\cos(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime}))
is strictly increasing in
n^{\prime}
in each congruence class of
n^{\prime}\bmod 4
, the inner maximum is achieved when
n^{\prime}
is as large as possible, namely
n^{\prime}=\max\{\,n/\gcd(j,n)\,\}=n
(take any
j
coprime to
n
). Using the explicit values for
\Delta^{\star}(n)
from Lemma
6
gives

\max_{j}2\cos^{2}\!\big(\Delta^{\star}(n/\gcd(j,n))\big)=\begin{cases}2\cos^{2}(\pi/n),&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\[2.84526pt]
2\cos^{2}(\pi/2n),&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\[2.84526pt]
2\cos^{2}(\pi/4n),&n\ \text{odd}.\end{cases}

For
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
we have
2\cos^{2}(\pi/n)\geqslant 1
with equality only at
n=4
; for
n\equiv 2\pmod{4}
clearly
2\cos^{2}(\pi/2n)>1
; for odd
n
the
j=n/2
column is absent. Thus the outer maximum in (
6
) equals the displayed piecewise quantity, proving

M\big(Q^{\rm(ph)}\big)\ \leqslant\ \begin{cases}2\cos^{2}(\pi/n),&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
2\cos^{2}(\pi/2n),&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\
2\cos^{2}(\pi/4n),&n\ \text{odd}.\end{cases}

In order to demonstrate optimality (the lower bound), let us consider any orthogonal matrix
Q
obtainable from the cycle basis by independent rotations within each
E_{j}
(this is what we mean by “within the cycle family”). Fix
j
and write
n^{\prime}=n/\gcd(j,n)
. By Lemma
6
,
every
orthonormal pair drawn from
E_{j}
satisfies

\max\{\,n\|q\|_{\infty}^{2}:q\in E_{j},\ \|q\|_{2}=1\,\}\ \geqslant\ 2\cos^{2}\!\big(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})\big).

Indeed, fix
j
and recall that
E_{j}
is the two-dimensional eigenspace
spanned by
\{u_{j},v_{j}\}
. By Lemma
6
, for
any

orthonormal pair
(u,v)
spanning
E_{j}
one has

\max\{\|u\|_{\infty},\ \|v\|_{\infty}\}\ \geqslant\ \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}}\ \cos\!\bigl(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})\bigr),\qquad n^{\prime}:=\tfrac{n}{\gcd(j,n)}.

Squaring and multiplying by
n
yields

\max\{\,n\|u\|_{\infty}^{2},\ n\|v\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\}\ \geqslant\ 2\cos^{2}\!\bigl(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime})\bigr).

Since
u
and
v
are unit vectors in
E_{j}
, this shows the desired inequality.
In other words, whichever orthonormal basis of
E_{j}
we pick, at least
one vector in it has sup-norm squared of order
2\cos^{2}(\Delta^{\star}(n^{\prime}))/n
.
Thus the displayed inequality holds for
every
orthonormal pair
drawn from
E_{j}
. (In the special case
j=n/2
when
n
is even,
E_{n/2}

is one-dimensional, and one can choose
q
with
|q(k)|\equiv 1/\sqrt{n}
,
so
n\|q\|_{\infty}^{2}=1
; this agrees with

2\cos^{2}(\Delta^{\star}(2))=1
.)

Hence

M(Q)\ \geqslant\ \max\Big\{\,\mathbf{1}_{\{2\mid n\}},\ \max_{j}2\cos^{2}\!\big(\Delta^{\star}(n/\gcd(j,n))\big)\Big\}

=\begin{cases}2\cos^{2}(\pi/n),&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
2\cos^{2}(\pi/2n),&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\
2\cos^{2}(\pi/4n),&n\ \text{odd},\end{cases}

where the equality follows by the same monotonicity and the presence (when
2\mid n
) of the
j=n/2
column with value
1\leqslant
the right-hand side. Combining the upper and lower bounds yields the asserted equality for
M(Q^{\rm(ph)})
and proves optimality within the cycle family.

Finally, Lemma
9
with
M(Q^{\rm(ph)})
as above gives the sufficient condition

1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>1-\tfrac{1}{M(Q^{\rm(ph)})},

which is precisely the stated
\delta^{\rm(ph)}_{n}
.
∎

Corollary 8

(Uniform phase bound)
.

As
\delta_{\ast}:=\sup_{n\geqslant 3}\delta^{\mathrm{(ph)}}_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}
, for every
n\geqslant 3
and every Suleĭmanova list
(1,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
with
\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0
,

1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}\;>\;\delta_{\ast}\ (=\tfrac{1}{2})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q^{(\mathrm{ph})}\,\Lambda\,\big(Q^{(\mathrm{ph})}\big)^{\!\top}\ \text{is symmetric and doubly stochastic}.

4.
A coherence criterion

While the phase-optimised basis successfully removed the ‘
8\mid n
’ artefact, its coherence still approaches the maximum value of 2 as
n\to\infty
, preventing a uniform improvement on the
\delta<1/2
bound. To achieve a stronger result, we must look beyond the eigenbasis of the cycle graph. This section develops a general tool to guide this search. We first isolate the key property of the basis that governs the SDIEP bound, what we call its
coherence
, a measure of the peak magnitude of its entries, and prove that a lower coherence directly implies a better bound
\delta
. Armed with this criterion, we then exhibit two families of ‘flat’ bases, constructed from Hadamard matrices, which possess the ideal coherence of
M(Q)=1
. For dimensions where such matrices do exist, this allows us to prove the realisability of
all
Suleĭmanova spectra with a strictly positive trace sum.

Lemma 9

(Generic coherence criterion)
.

Let
Q
be any (real) orthogonal matrix with first column
q_{0}=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
. Define

M(Q):=\sup_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1}\ \sup_{0\leqslant k,l\leqslant n-1}\ n\,|q_{j}(k)q_{j}(l)|\ =\ n\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1}\|q_{j}\|_{\infty}^{2}.

If
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>1-\tfrac{1}{M(Q)}
and
\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0
, then
P(\Lambda)=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
is symmetric and doubly stochastic.

Proof.

As in Lemma
1
,
P(\Lambda)\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
and

p_{kl}=\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}\,n\,q_{j}(k)q_{j}(l)\Bigr)\ \geqslant\ \frac{1}{n}\Bigl(1+M(Q)\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}\Bigr)

since
\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0
and
n\,q_{j}(k)q_{j}(l)\leqslant M(Q)
.
∎

The cycle basis is the standard Fourier basis for the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. To achieve the minimal coherence of
M=1
, we turn from the cycle graph to a different algebraic structure: the
abelian 2-group
. An abelian 2-group is a group that is commutative and in which every element has an order that is a power of two; for a finite group, this is equivalent to its size being a power of two. The canonical example for any dimension
n=2^{d}
is the elementary abelian 2-group
G=(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{d}
, whose elements can be viewed as binary vectors
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
of length
d
, and whose operation is vector addition modulo
2
(bitwise XOR).

The natural analogue of the Fourier basis on this group is the basis of its
characters
. A character is a homomorphism
\chi\colon G\to\{\pm 1\}
. For each indexing vector
\mathbf{a}\in G
, there is a corresponding character
\chi_{\mathbf{a}}:G\to\{\pm 1\}
defined by the dot product:

\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})=(-1)^{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}x_{i}}.

The
Sylvester
(or
Walsh–Hadamard
) matrix
H_{n}
is constructed by indexing its rows and columns by the elements of
G
(typically in lexicographical order) and setting the entry at row
\mathbf{a}
and column
\mathbf{x}
to be
\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})
.

Remark 10

(Hadamard matrix orders)
.

It is a classical open problem, known as the
Hadamard conjecture
, that Hadamard matrices exist for every order
n
equal to
1
,
2
, or a multiple of
4

[
2
]
. Therefore, a Walsh–Hadamard matrix of order
3
does not exist.

A simple recursive construction, known as the Sylvester construction, generates these matrices for all orders
n=2^{d}
:

H_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1\end{pmatrix},\quad H_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad H_{2n}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{n}&H_{n}\\
H_{n}&-H_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

For example, the matrix for
n=4=2^{2}
is constructed as follows:

H_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{2}&H_{2}\\
H_{2}&-H_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|cc}1&1&1&1\\
1&-1&1&-1\\
\hline\cr 1&1&-1&-1\\
1&-1&-1&1\end{array}\right).

By construction, the entries of
H_{n}
are all
\pm 1
and its columns are orthogonal. When normalised to
Q=H_{n}/\sqrt{n}
, we obtain an orthogonal matrix whose entries are all
\pm 1/\sqrt{n}
. This “flat” structure gives a coherence of
M(Q)=1
, providing a powerful tool for the SDIEP as formalised in the following theorem.

Theorem 11

(bases stemming from abelian
2
-groups)
.

If
n=2^{d}
, let
H_{n}
be the Sylvester (Walsh–Hadamard) matrix of order
n
. After sign/permutation normalisation,
Q=H_{n}/\sqrt{n}
is orthogonal with first column
\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
and entries
\pm 1/\sqrt{n}
, hence
M(Q)=1
. Therefore, for
every
Suleĭmanova spectrum with
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>0
, the matrix
Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
is symmetric and doubly stochastic.

Theorem 12

(Hadamard orders via Paley-type constructions)
.

Whenever a (real) Hadamard matrix
H
of order
n
exists with first column
\mathbf{1}
, taking
Q=H/\sqrt{n}
yields
M(Q)=1
. Hence every Suleĭmanova spectrum with
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>0
is realised by a symmetric doubly stochastic matrix via
Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
.

Proof of Theorems
11
and
12
(combined).

Let
H
be a real Hadamard matrix of order
n
. By multiplying columns by
\pm 1
and permuting them (operations preserving the Hadamard property), normalise so that the first column is
\mathbf{1}
. Set
Q:=H/\sqrt{n}
. Then
Q
is real orthogonal, its first column is
q_{0}=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
, and all entries satisfy
q_{j}(k)\in\{\pm 1/\sqrt{n}\}
.

Hence
M(Q)=\sup_{j\geqslant 1,k,l}n|q_{j}(k)q_{j}(l)|=1
. By Lemma
9
, whenever
1+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j}>1-\tfrac{1}{M(Q)}=0
and
\lambda_{j}\leqslant 0
, the matrix
P(\Lambda)=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
is entrywise nonnegative. Symmetry is immediate, and

P(\Lambda)\,\mathbf{1}=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\mathbf{1}=Q\Lambda(\sqrt{n}\,e_{0})=\sqrt{n}\,Qe_{0}=\mathbf{1}

so
P(\Lambda)
is doubly stochastic. The Walsh–Hadamard case corresponds to
n=2^{d}
; Paley-type constructions and other Hadamard orders give further instances.
∎

Remark 13
.

(i) The result requires the
strict
condition
1+\sum\lambda_{j}>0
. Our lower bound on the matrix entries is
p_{kl}\geqslant\tfrac{1}{n}(1+\sum\lambda_{j})
, which guarantees positivity under the strict inequality. The boundary case
1+\sum\lambda_{j}=0
is not guaranteed to be realisable and is known to fail for certain spectra (e.g.,
(1,0,\dots,0,-1)
in odd dimensions).

(ii) Our use of Hadamard bases provides a constructive proof for
all
(not necessarily sorted) Suleĭmanova lists in these dimensions, provided the trace sum is positive. This complements results such as that of Johnson and Paparella
[
4
]
, which establish existence for
normalised
(sorted) Suleĭmanova lists.

5.
Further remarks and extensions

Asymptotics (large
n
)

For small angles,
\cos^{2}\theta=1-\theta^{2}+O(\theta^{4})
, hence

1-\frac{1}{2\cos^{2}\theta}\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\ -\ \frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\ +\ O(\theta^{4}).

For the canonical cycle basis,
\theta=\Delta_{n}=\frac{\pi}{4n}\rho(n)
(Lemma
2
), so

\delta_{n}\ =\ \begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},&n\equiv 0\pmod{8},\\[2.84526pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{32n^{2}}\rho(n)^{2}\ +\ O(n^{-4}),&n\not\equiv 0\pmod{8}.\end{cases}

Explicitly:
\delta_{n}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O(n^{-4})
for
n\equiv 4\pmod{8}
;
\delta_{n}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{8n^{2}}+O(n^{-4})
for
n\equiv 2,6\pmod{8}
; and
\delta_{n}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{32n^{2}}+O(n^{-4})
for odd
n
. For the phase-optimised basis (Theorem
7
),

\delta_{n}^{\rm(ph)}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O(n^{-4}),&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{8n^{2}}+O(n^{-4}),&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{32n^{2}}+O(n^{-4}),&n\ \text{odd}.\end{cases}

Tensor products and direct products of graphs

If
Q^{(1)}
and
Q^{(2)}
are orthogonal with first columns
\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n_{1}}
and
\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n_{2}}
, then
Q:=Q^{(1)}\otimes Q^{(2)}
is orthogonal with first column
\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n_{1}n_{2}}
and

M(Q)\ \leqslant\ M\!\big(Q^{(1)}\big)\,M\!\big(Q^{(2)}\big).

Thus Lemma
9
gives the sufficient sum-only condition

1+\sum_{j=2}^{n_{1}n_{2}}\lambda_{j}\ >\ 1-\frac{1}{M(Q^{(1)})M(Q^{(2)})}

for entrywise nonnegativity via
Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
. In particular, flat (
M=1
) factors propagate.

6.
Limitations and open problems

For the canonical cycle basis,
M(Q)=2
exactly when
8\mid n
(Lemma
2
), so no sum-only improvement below
\tfrac{1}{2}
is possible there. Even when
8\nmid n
,
M(Q)=2\cos^{2}\Delta_{n}=2\bigl(1-O(n^{-2})\bigr)
, so the gain is
O(n^{-2})
(Theorem
3
). The phase-optimised basis yields
\delta_{n}^{\rm(ph)}<\tfrac{1}{2}
for all
n
, but still not a single
uniform

\delta<\tfrac{1}{2}
independent of
n
.

Open problem.
Is it possible to construct, for each
n
, a real orthogonal
Q
with
q_{0}=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}
and
M(Q)\leqslant 2-\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
independent of
n
. By Lemma
9
, this would give a universal
\delta<\tfrac{1}{2}
for all (not necessarily normalised) Suleĭmanova lists.
