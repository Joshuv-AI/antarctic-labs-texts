The effect of amlodipin, a calcium channel blocker, on tendon healing: an experimental study in rat model of Achilles tendon injury
Authors: Sefa Erdem Karapinar, Umut Can Duvarci, Serdar Tunc, Unal Unsar Sarıısık, Omer Faruk Topcu, Recep Dincer, Serdar Kamil Cepni, Afra Gul Yonet, Ilkay Armagan, Orhan Berk Imeci, Halil Asci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeAchilles tendon injuries represent a major clinical challenge due to the tendon’s poor vascularity and limited regenerative capacity. Amlodipine, a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker, has demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and vasodilatory effects in various tissues. However, its potential influence on tendon healing remains unexplored.This study aimed to evaluate the histopathological and biomechanical effects of amlodipine on tendon regeneration in a rat model of Achilles tendon rupture.MethodsForty-eight male Wistar albino rats (300–350 g) were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 12 each): C14, A14, C28, and A28. A complete Achilles tenotomy was surgically created and repaired using the Kessler technique. Animals received either physiological saline (control) or amlodipine besylate (5 mg/kg/day, oral gavage) for 14 or 28 days. Tendons were harvested for either biomechanical testing (tensile strength and elongation) or histopathological analysis (fibroblastic activity, neovascularization, inflammation).ResultsAmlodipine significantly enhanced fibroblastic activity and neovascularization at both time points compared to controls. Inflammatory scores decreased markedly from day 14 to 28 in the treatment group, indicating a strong anti-inflammatory effect (p < 0.01 for all). Biomechanical testing revealed significantly increased tensile strength (22.23 ± 2.89 N vs. 15.07 ± 3.79 N; p < 0.05) and elongation (4.90 ± 1.62 mm vs. 2.70 ± 0.68 mm; p < 0.05) in the amlodipine group by day 28.ConclusionAmlodipine appears to accelerate and enhance Achilles tendon healing by promoting fibroblastic proliferation, angiogenesis, and biomechanical strength, while reducing inflammation. These findings suggest a potential role for amlodipine as a pharmacologic adjunct in tendon repair; however, further mechanistic and clinical studies are warranted.

Introduction

Tendons are highly specialized connective tissues responsible for transmitting mechanical forces between muscles and bones [1]. Among them, the Achilles tendon is the largest and strongest, yet remains particularly susceptible to rüptüre especially in physically active individuals aged 30–50 years [2]. Despite advances in surgical repair techniques, optimal healing remains a significant clinical challenge due to the inherently hypovascular and hypocellular nature of tendon tissue [3]. These biological limitations often lead to prolonged recovery periods, incomplete functional restoration, and high re-rupture rates [4].

Current therapeutic strategies focus primarily on mechanical stabilization and rehabilitation protocols. However, biological modulation of the healing cascade remains largely underutilized. Given that tendon regeneration involves a tightly regulated interplay of inflammation, fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, pharmacological agents with pleiotropic effects may offer novel therapeutic potential [4].

Amlodipine, a widely used dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker, is primarily indicated for the treatment of hypertension and ischemic cardiovascular diseases. Beyond its antihypertensive effect, amlodipine exhibits a range of vascular and cellular effects including nitric oxide (NO)-mediated vasodilation, upregulation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), inhibition of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. In addition, recent studies have suggested that amlodipine promotes tissue regeneration via enhanced microcirculation, reduced oxidative stress, and stimulation of collagen synthesis [5]. Despite these promising biological properties, the impact of amlodipine on tendon repair has not yet been elucidated. We hypothesized that oral administration of amlodipine following surgical repair of Achilles tendon rupture would accelerate healing by enhancing neovascularization, reducing inflammation, and improving biomechanical integrity of the repaired tissue. The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the histopathological and biomechanical effects of amlodipine in a rat model of Achilles tendon injury and repair.

Material and method

Animals and ethical approval

Prior to the commencement of the experiment, ethical approval was obtained from the local ethics committee, and the study was conducted at the Experimental Animal Center of the same institution (Approval date/meeting number/decision number: 2024/13/393). All rats were provided with a standard laboratory diet and free access to water. They were housed individually in standard cages in a temperature-controlled room maintained at 21–22 °C. Throughout the experiment, the rats were housed in four separate cages. To avoid stress and fear-related effects, the procedures were initiated following a 3-day acclimatization period.

Experimental design

A total of 48 adult male Wistar albino rats (weighing 300–350 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Research Center of Süleyman Demirel University. Following a standard acclimatization period, animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 21–22 °C; 12-hour light/dark cycle; ad libitum access to standard chow and water) and were randomly assigned into four experimental groups (n = 12 per group): C14, A14, C28, and A28. Within each group, six rats were randomly selected for biomechanical testing, while the remaining six were designated for histopathological evaluation. Treatment protocols were as follows:

Surgical technique

In this experiment, the right lower extremities of all animals were used. For surgical prophylaxis, 30 mg/kg of cefazolin sodium was administered intraperitoneally 30 min before the procedure. Following anesthesia with 8–10 mg/kg xylazine and 90 mg/kg ketamine, the area over the right Achilles tendon was shaved, cleaned, and disinfected with 10% povidone-iodine solution. A 1.5 cm longitudinal incision was made to expose the Achilles tendon. The Achilles and plantaris tendons were transected transversely 0.5 cm proximal to the calcaneal insertion. Only the Achilles tendon was repaired using a modified Kessler technique with 5 − 0 absorbable sutures. The skin was closed with 3 − 0 non-absorbable sutures (Fig. 3). No immobilization was applied, and the rats were allowed unrestricted movement in their cages postoperatively. Infection prophylaxis continued postoperatively for three days with 30 mg/kg of cefazolin sodium administered intraperitoneally twice daily. For postoperative analgesia, 2 mg/mL paracetamol was added to the drinking water (100 mL/day).Fig. 3Image showing the condition of the rats at the time of surgery. On days 14 and 28, rats scheduled for sacrifice were anesthetized using 8–10 mg/kg xylazine and 90 mg/kg ketamine, followed by cervical dislocation. The Achilles tendons were carefully dissected and excised without removing bone tissue from the femoral or calcaneal insertion sites [2].

Histopathological evaluation

Tissue isolation and preparation

Six rats were assigned to each group. The right Achilles tendons-previously operated and sutured-were meticulously dissected from the femoral and calcaneal attachment sites, ensuring that no bone tissue was removed. The harvested tissues were immediately placed into pre-labeled containers filled with 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathological evaluation. Samples were maintained at room temperature until complete fixation was achieved.

Tissue samples from each group were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 h. Following fixation, the specimens were rinsed under running water and subjected to dehydration through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and absolute ethanol). Subsequently, the tissues were cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin for histological processing. Longitudinal sections, each 4 μm thick, were obtained using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2155 RT, Nussloch, Germany). Two sections from each specimen were mounted on separate slides-one stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), and the other with Martius Scarlet Blue (MSB).

Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) staining

The paraffin-embedded tissue sections were first incubated in a 60 °C oven and subsequently deparaffinized in xylene. Rehydration was carried out through a graded alcohol series (96%, 90%, 80%, 70%, 50%), followed by rinsing in distilled water. Nuclear staining was performed using Hematoxylin, while Eosin was used to counterstain the cytoplasmic structures. The stained sections were then cleared in xylene and mounted using entellan. Tendon healing was histopathologically evaluated based on the modified Curtis method [6] (Table 1).This evaluation included three parameters: fibroblastic activity, neovascularization and inflammation. Fibroblastic activity was evaluated based on the presence of fibroblasts with oval nuclei. Inflammation was assessed by considering the density of inflammatory cells. Neovascularization was evaluated more objectively by counting the number of vascular structures in the area, regardless of vessel diameter. Five randomly selected fields per section were assessed under high magnification, and the mean score was calculated using the following scale: No change (0), Mild (1), Moderate (2), Prominent (3). H&E staining enabled the identification of inflammatory cells, tenocyte nuclei, and vascular structures. All histopathological evaluations and microphotographs were performed using a binocular light microscope equipped with a computer-assisted digital camera and imaging software. Martius Scarlet blue (MSB) staining

Tissue sections mounted on glass slides were first incubated at 60 °C, followed by deparaffinization in xylene. The samples were then rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (96%, 90%, 80%, 70%, and 50%) and rinsed in distilled water. Nuclear staining was performed using Weigert’s Iron Hematoxylin, prepared from stock solutions, followed by differentiation in 1% acid alcohol. The sections were then sequentially stained with Martius Yellow and Crystal Ponceau. Subsequent staining steps included treatment with Phosphotungstic Acid and Methyl Blue. After a final rinse in distilled water, the slides were dehydrated through ascending ethanol concentrations, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped using entellan. Histological evaluation of the stained sections focused on fibroblastic activity, neovascularization and inflammation. Scoring was performed according to the modified Curtis method, as previously described. All tissue assessments and microphotographs were conducted using a binocular light microscope (ECLIPSE Ni-U, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a computer-assisted digital camera and imaging software.

Biomechanical evaluation

The biomechanical strength of the healed tendons was evaluated using a universal testing machine (Shimadzu Autograph 50 kN; Shimadzu Corp., Japan), operated by a mechanical engineer who was blinded to the sample groups. To prevent slippage and tendon damage, 2-mm-thick non-slip rubber pads covered with sandpaper and bonded with cyanoacrylate adhesive were used for fixation. The tendons were clamped at both ends under a pressure of 6 bar. The tensile test was configured with a clamp separation rate of 0.5 cm and a speed of 0.1 mm per second. A preloading of 1 N was applied to ensure uniform tendon length and alignment of collagen fibers before the test. The test continued until tendon rupture, and the maximum force at the moment of rupture was recorded. Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27. Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed that the data followed a normal distribution. Therefore, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple group comparisons. Effect sizes (partial eta-squared) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated to assess the magnitude and precision of the results. The primary endpoint was tensile strength on postoperative day 28. Post hoc power analysis based on the observed effect size (Cohen’s d = 2.13) indicated > 95% power (α = 0.05, n = 6 per group). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The data were found not to follow a normal distribution. Therefore, to evaluate the statistical significance of the observed differences in mean scores between the four independent groups (C14, A14, C28, A28) for each histopathological and biomechanical parameter (Fibroblastic Activity, Neovascularization, Inflammation, tensile strength, amount of elongation), the one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was employed. When a significant difference was detected among the groups, post-hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

Group means and standard deviations (mean ± SD) for each parameter were visualized using bar charts. Statistical significance of the differences between groups was indicated by p-values, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Horizontal connector bars were added to the graphs for significantly different comparisons to visually denote the significant differences between groups.

Discussion

Achilles tendon injuries remain a formidable clinical issue due to the tissue’s avascularity, low metabolic activity, and limited regenerative capacity. These intrinsic limitations result in prolonged healing, suboptimal functional restoration, and high rates of re-rupture, even after surgical intervention. Therefore, exploring pharmacologic adjuncts that can biologically enhance the healing process is both timely and necessary. The current study is among the first to demonstrate the regenerative potential of amlodipine, a calcium channel blocker with known pleiotropic effects, on tendon healing in a controlled experimental model.

The Achilles tendon’s vascular supply is minimal, especially in the watershed zone, which severely limits nutrient delivery during healing. Amlodipine has been shown to enhance eNOS activity, leading to increased NO levels and vasodilation, thereby potentially augmenting microvascular perfusion in injured tissues [8]. This mechanism may partly explain the significant neovascularization observed in the amlodipine-treated groups in our study. Enhanced perfusion not only supplies oxygen and metabolic substrates but also supports angiogenic signaling and immune cell infiltration needed for the initiation of tissue regeneration.

Supporting this, prior studies in musculoskeletal injury models have demonstrated that amlodipine upregulates VEGF, a key driver of angiogenesis [9]. The presence of a more developed capillary network facilitates fibroblast migration and ECM deposition, processes that are essential for functional tendon restoration. In our study, histological analyses showed both enhanced neovascularization and elevated fibroblast activity in the amlodipine groups, particularly at day 28, indicating prolonged and effective matrix synthesis.

Inflammation is a necessary component of tissue repair, but its dysregulation can impair regeneration. Amlodipine has been shown to attenuate inflammatory cytokine production by modulating nuclear factor kappa B signaling and reducing expression of tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6 [10]. Our findings are in line with these data, showing that amlodipine reduced inflammatory scores significantly from day 14 to 28. This shift may represent an earlier resolution of inflammation, facilitating the transition to the proliferative and remodeling phases of healing.

Interestingly, the transient elevation of inflammation seen on day 14 in the amlodipine group may reflect a beneficial early immune response, characterized by the recruitment of M1 macrophages, which later polarize into an M2 phenotype. Such biphasic immune modulation is increasingly recognized as crucial for orchestrating regenerative outcomes [11]. The acceleration of this immune shift may underlie the histological and biomechanical improvements observed in our study.

Tendon fibroblasts play a central role in synthesizing type I collagen and remodeling the extracellular matrix. Their activity is influenced by growth factors such as transforming growth factor-beta and insulin-like growth factor-1, both of which are positively modulated by amlodipine and other calcium channel blockers [12]. The enhanced fibroblast proliferation observed histologically in the amlodipine groups likely reflects these upstream molecular events, contributing to the improved mechanical integrity of the tendon.

Indeed, biomechanical testing revealed that tendons from amlodipine-treated animals had significantly greater tensile strength and elongation compared to controls, particularly at day 28. These findings suggest not only increased collagen deposition but also improved matrix organization and crosslinking, which are hallmarks of mature tendon tissue. Although we did not measure collagen subtypes directly, it is plausible that type I collagen synthesis was enhanced, improving the structural and functional quality of the tendon.

Oxidative stress is known to disrupt tendon healing by damaging tenocytes and degrading the extracellular matrix. Amlodipine exerts antioxidant effects by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and enhancing antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase [13]. These effects may protect tenocytes from apoptosis and preserve the biomechanical properties of the healing tendon. The observed improvements in both structural integrity and mechanical function in our study support this notion.

Beyond improving tensile strength, amlodipine also enhanced tendon elasticity, as indicated by the increased elongation values. This may be a result of more organized collagen fiber alignment and increased glycosaminoglycan content, both of which contribute to the viscoelastic behavior of tendon tissue [14]. Enhanced elasticity has functional implications, as it supports shock absorption and dynamic loading during movement.

Our results align with studies in other tissues. For instance, Salah et al. (2024) demonstrated accelerated healing in burn wounds treated with topical amlodipine-loaded nanoparticles, which showed improved re-epithelialization, angiogenesis, and reduced inflammation. These findings underscore the systemic and local regenerative potential of amlodipine across various soft tissues [9]. Similarly, observed upregulation of bone morphogenetic protein-2 and VEGF in femoral fracture models treated with amlodipine, further supporting its role in enhancing connective tissue repair.

Amlodipine offers several practical advantages over other regenerative therapies. It is orally bioavailable, inexpensive, and has a well-established safety profile. Unlike growth factor injections or stem cell therapies, it does not require specialized handling or storage. This makes it particularly appealing for widespread clinical use, especially in settings where access to advanced regenerative interventions is limited.

Nevertheless, our study has limitations. We did not perform molecular analyses such as qRT-PCR, immunohistochemistry, or ELISA to confirm the involvement of VEGF, TGF-β, eNOS, or inflammatory cytokines. While the histological and biomechanical findings are strong, they remain correlative. Future studies should include such analyses to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying amlodipine’s effects on tendon healing.

Moreover, we employed a single dose (5 mg/kg/day) and administration route (oral gavage). Dose–response studies and alternative delivery systems, such as peritendinous injection or nanoparticle encapsulation, could provide additional insights into optimizing therapeutic efficacy. It also remains to be determined whether combining amlodipine with physical therapies (e.g., pulsed electromagnetic field, LIPUS) might yield synergistic effects.

Another limitation is the absence of functional evaluation. While biomechanical testing provides quantitative insights into structural integrity, assessments such as gait analysis or locomotor activity tracking could reveal the true clinical relevance of the observed tissue-level improvements.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be noted. Firstly, we did not conduct molecular assays (e.g., VEGF, eNOS, cytokine expression) to confirm the proposed mechanisms. Secondly, only a single dosing strategy and administration route were tested. Finally, functional assessments such as gait analysis were not performed, limiting the translational applicability.

In conclusion, the current study demonstrates that amlodipine enhances Achilles tendon healing by promoting angiogenesis, modulating inflammation, stimulating fibroblastic proliferation, and improving biomechanical resilience. These findings contribute to a growing body of literature supporting the repurposing of cardiovascular drugs in regenerative medicine. With further validation, amlodipine may emerge as a practical, safe, and effective adjunct in the clinical management of tendon injuries.

Conclusion

In this experimental study, we demonstrated for the first time that oral administration of amlodipine significantly improves the histopathological and biomechanical parameters of tendon healing following Achilles tendon rupture. Amlodipine-treated animals exhibited enhanced neovascularization, increased fibroblastic activity, reduced inflammatory infiltration, and superior tensile strength and elasticity, particularly at the late healing phase. These regenerative effects are likely mediated through a combination of vasodilatory, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and pro-angiogenic mechanisms, including upregulation of eNOS and VEGF pathways, and attenuation of proinflammatory cytokine signaling via NF-κB suppression.

Importantly, amlodipine offers the advantage of being a widely available, cost-effective, and systemically administrable agent with a well-established safety profile. Unlike experimental therapies involving biologics or gene modulation, its clinical translation is feasible and immediate. While our findings are promising, further studies incorporating molecular validation, alternative dosing strategies, and long-term functional evaluations are needed to fully elucidate its therapeutic potential and optimize its application in orthopedic and regenerative medicine.

In conclusion, amlodipine shows potential as a pharmacological adjunct that may enhance tendon regeneration. However, further studies including molecular validation, dose-response exploration, and functional outcome measures are needed before considering clinical application.
