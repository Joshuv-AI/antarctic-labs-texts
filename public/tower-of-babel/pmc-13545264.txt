Annotated datasets for waste electrical and electronic equipment identification and battery detection and classification
Authors: Olivier Rukundo, Rizwan Khan, Eoin Martino Grua, Ailbhe O’Connell, Gerard Keane, Mark Morgan, Colin Fitzpatrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper describes the initial release of annotated datasets from the advanced robotics and artificial intelligence for critical raw materials recycling in the circular economy (XBAT+) project. Relatively small battery waste electrical and electronic equipment (WEEE) devices were sorted from cages of general WEEE during visits to recycling facilities and collection events. From 50 initially defined battery-WEEE categories, applying a frequency criterion reduced them to 15 categories comprising 421 battery-WEEE devices. Optical and x-ray imaging of battery-WEEE devices produced multiple images per device in some cases, resulting in 3045 static images. These were organised into high-quality, varying-quality, and RGB datasets, semi-automatically annotated, and split into training and test sets. Specifically, within each of the 15 categories, 20% of the images were allocated to the test sets, the rest to the training sets. Evaluation using YOLO26 and RF-DETR demonstrated promising mAP@50 and recall for supervised learning-based battery-WEEE identification and battery/nobattery detection, highlighting the potential of XBAT+ datasets to enable WEEE filtering and mitigate battery-based fire hazards in recycling facilities.

Background & Summary

The European Union (EU) has launched an ambitious policy agenda, articulated in the EU green deal and circular economy action plan to develop a circular economy and increase self-sufficiency in a host of vital critical raw materials (CRMs), notably materials related to battery manufacture1. Meanwhile, the fire hazards associated with the growth of batteries within appliances has created a significant risk to employee welfare, property and the environment in the waste electrical and electronic equipment (WEEE) recycling sector1. In response to this, the advanced robotics for critical raw materials recycling in the circular economy (XBAT+) project was developed to advance current safety and rapidity practices in WEEE recycling workflows through many pathways, including the development of an automated battery detection, filtering and sorting system for deployment in WEEE recycling facilities. Specifically, the XBAT+ project focused on the use of non-destructive detection methods (e.g., optical and x-ray imaging supported by AI-based models) to identify battery-WEEE from general WEEE and to detect battery presence within battery-WEEE, as shown in Fig. 1. Also, to support greater recovery of strategic CRMs, the XBAT+ proposed sorting battery-WEEE according to battery chemistry. In addition to supporting the ambitious battery collection and recycling targets proposed by the EU, this sorting approach can assist workers during manual battery removal by providing advance information on battery chemistry, thereby enabling preparation for appropriate handling procedures. Recent studies have explored the use of non-destructive methods to create datasets or leverage imaging data have primarily focused on isolated cases or tasks such as battery detection in WEEE2,3, general WEEE classification4–6, battery type classification7, hierarchical and mixed waste classification8, component-level WEEE detection9, mixed waste including WEEE and batteries10 and general waste classification11. Although these studies demonstrated significant progress within individual tasks, the datasets created or used were generally not publicly available with annotations and lacked an acquisition strategy structured by WEEE device type or did not systematically support multiple linked tasks, as can be seen in Fig. 1. Consequently, to the best of our knowledge, there are still no public annotated small battery WEEE datasets that enable the integrated analysis of WEEE identification, battery detection, and battery chemistry classification for safe and rapid battery-based filtering of WEEE and deployment in recycling facilities. In addition, current studies on WEEE datasets did not consider the material diversity of battery WEEE devices during imaging data acquisition. Also, none of existing ones developed strategies for optimizing the image quality of x-rayed WEEE devices to optimize battery detection and chemistry classification. In this regard, the XBAT+ project addresses limitations of existing datasets which are restricted in scope defined by XBAT+ project. Methods

As of December 2025, only battery-WEEE from six drums were x-rayed and optically imaged. RGB and x-ray image acquisition

To acquire both HQ and VQ images of battery-WEEE, the NTB EZ 240 X-ray scanner, a digital x-ray imaging system (Fig. 2a, blue box), was used together with the iX-Pect software, which serves as its x-ray acquisition and imaging platform13. To acquire RGB images of battery-WEEE, the Ultrasharp DELL webcam, a high-resolution 4 K colour imaging webcam device, was used14. Figure 2b showed the procedure followed to position the battery-WEEE before optical and x-ray imaging. All image files produced were named as follows: (Ref. ID)_(Integration Time)_(Tube Voltage)_(View Side). Apart from Ref. ID and View Side, other information was obtained from the x-ray machine and software. Here, 421 distinct battery-WEEE devices, optically imaged and x-rayed, resulted in 421 RGB images, 2203 VQ images, and 421 HQ images that formed RGBD, VQD and HQD datasets, respectively. Initially, an RGB image of the battery-WEEE was captured followed by x-raying it according to the procedure to position battery WEEE devices in Fig. 2b.

In this context, all captured RGB images immediately formed the RGBD datasets. For x-ray images, after saving the image, initial image quality assessment was performed via iX-Pect software to decide the (progressive) increase or decrease of the tube voltage with reference to the arbitrarily selected reference voltage (see Table 2, TV4 column). This progressing process resulted in producing more images at different tube voltage settings and stopped until satisfaction or dissatisfaction in terms of the visible image quality (see Table 2). As result, multiple images (per battery-WEEE device) were produced and formed the VQD dataset. Here, the only time-consuming challenge, during the creation of the VQD, was the variation in material type and size of the WEEE device. Such variations had a direct effect on the x-ray scanning time. From VQD images, only one high-quality x-ray image was selected, per WEEE device, to form the HQD dataset. To speed up the high-quality image selection, the subjective image quality assessment was performed following each round of multi-voltage x-ray image acquisition. During image quality assessment, a positive and negative judgment had to be made based on the perception of both x-ray image edges and texture details and the voltage corresponding to the best choice was labelled a “Y” and highlighted (in green, see Table 2) while poor choices were all labelled as a “N” letter. Also, during the image quality assessment, a document containing all records and highlights was kept to later facilitate the automation of the process of creating HQD from the VQD. Datasets annotation

To annotate the total of 3045 images, we proceeded as follows: First, we post-processed all images to ensure they have a square shape according to the image post-processing pipeline shown in Fig. 3, whose script is also available on Zenodo15. This was done because acquired images had different sizes (more details are provided in the data record section), and we converted them into square images, by padding each image based on its maximum dimension, for consistency and to ease subsequent use. Next, annotations were performed using the Roboflow software platformi 16–19, which a web-based platform that provides many tools, including for manual and AI-assisted labelling. Initially, we manually annotated a few x-ray and RGB images. Next, we used them to train YOLO26 (a convolutional neural network-based object detector) to be able later leverage the AI label assistant provided by the Roboflow software up the annotation process per WEEE-defined category. Here, it is important to note that, initially, 50 categories were defined based on visual appearance and characteristics of the collected battery-WEEE. However, after inspecting these 50 categories-based battery-WEEE, we observed differences in the variability of devices within each of the 50 categories.1Fig. 3Image post-processing pipeline for resizing raw x-ray and RGB images.

Image post-processing pipeline for resizing raw x-ray and RGB images.

Specifically, some categories contained only a very small number of battery-WEEE and often lacked sufficient variability between models or manufacturers within the same battery-WEEE device category, as those battery-WEEE devices were sometimes all from the same manufacturer or corresponded to the same model, thereby limiting diversity within the category of interest. Based on these observations, categories containing five or more battery-WEEE devices provided better variability than categories with fewer battery-WEEE devices. As a result, 15 categories were selected from the 50 initially defined using this five-device-per-category frequency criterion. Subsequently, we divided our yet-to-be-annotated image data into 15 selected categories (Fig. 4a) and placed those belonging to the same category into separate folders to speed up the assignment of categorical labels. It is important to note that when image data were organised into folders containing a single category, label assignment was more straightforward than when folders contained multiple categories, where each image had to be visually verified before assigning the corresponding label. This distinction was particularly important for x-ray images, for which labels were assigned not only for battery presence or absence, but also for the battery-WEEE category in which they were found. Next, we uploaded each categorical folder via the Roboflow unassigned section to start annotation. Here, each defined category represented a label and was named accordingly. We then used the Roboflow’s AI label assistant (Fig. 5c) to semi-automatically generate annotations for each image during the annotation process. In rare cases, where annotations generated by the label assistant were not correct or were missing, we used the bounding box tool (Fig. 5a) to correct or redraw rectangles/squares with relevant labels around the areas of interest, delineating the objects of interest. Train/test set creation

Here, separate train and test sets, a train-test split type introduced in21 to mitigate or minimize data leakage, was applied to HQD, RGBD and VQD, as shown in Table 3. Since all images, in each of HQD, RGBD and VQD, were generated from 15 battery-WEEE categories, within each of the 15 categories, 20% of the images formed the test set, as previously explained. The remaining 80% formed the train set. This procedure resulted in 330 images for the train set and 91 images for the test set (Table 3). This procedure was equivalent to a device-level split, which removed any risk of data leakage during the subsequent training and model evaluation. In other words, images derived from the same battery-WEEE could not be present in both the training and test subsets, at the exception of the VQD. However, for the VQD, the same procedure was followed and resulted in 482 images for the test set and 1721 images for the train set. Given that VQD initially contained multiple images of the same battery-WEEE device acquired under different x-ray tube voltage settings, the risk of data leakage in VQD was real. Data Records

The XBAT+ 1.0 release of annotated train and test sets is publicly available and accessible via the Zenodo repository12: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18022530. Note that, after raw image post-processing, RGB images were selected for inclusion in the RGBD only after verifying that the corresponding WEEE devices had associated x-ray images in the HQD datasets. Also, note that RGB and x-ray images were referenced via a unique device identifier according to the naming format mentioned earlier. The RGBD consisted of images originally stored in JPG format with a 24-bit depth, while original image dimensions varied from 1920 × 1920 ~ 3840 × 3840 (pixel). In contrast, the HQD and VQD images were originally stored in BMP format with an 8-bit depth, and original image dimensions varied from 920 × 920 ~ 2972 × 2972 (pixel). Note that a fixed integration time of 20 milliseconds and a pixel binning factor of 1 were applied during x-raying. These parameters specific to x-ray acquisition were not applicable to the RGBD.

Technical Validation

The XBAT+ 1.0 release was verified through two complementary procedures: (a) expert review of annotations and (b) model-based validation. No performance optimisation or benchmarking was conducted. No data augmentation was applied during YOLO26 and RF-DETR training.

Expert review of annotations

As mentioned earlier, all annotations were systematically reviewed within the Roboflow platform, where they were approved or rejected prior to inclusion in the final dataset. Particular attention was given to x-ray images, where the annotation task was inherently challenging. In selected cases, battery-WEEE devices were physically inspected to confirm battery presence and ensure annotation correctness.

Object detection model-based validation

Here, object detection models used included the RoboFlow-DEtection TRansformer (RF-DETR)22,23, a transformer-based object detector, and You-Only-Look-Once 26 (YOLO26)24,25, a convolutional neural network-based object detector. The quantitative results, in Table 4, indicated that both YOLO26 and RF-DETR achieved strong detection performance, across key classes as well as mentioned datasets classes in general. In the context of XBAT+ project, these results support the XBAT+’s pathway related to the development of an automated battery detection, filtering and sorting system. In Fig. 1’s workflow, results support the inspection stages where battery-WEEE devices were routed either to mechanical treatment, battery removal stage or to x-ray imaging. In particular, these models achieved promising recall values for the battery class (85.4% for YOLO26 and 80.5% for RF-DETR) which indicated that the models did not significantly miss positive instances, which would otherwise allow battery-WEEE containing batteries to enter mechanical treatment undetected and increase the risk of fire during shredding.
