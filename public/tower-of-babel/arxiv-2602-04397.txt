Optimal Rates for Feasible Payoff Set Estimation in Games
Annalisa Barbara, Riccardo Poiani, Martino Bernasconi, Andrea Celli

Abstract
We study a setting in which two players play a (possibly approximate) Nash equilibrium of a bimatrix game, while a learner observes only their actions and has no knowledge of the equilibrium or the underlying game. A natural question is whether the learner can rationalize the observed behavior by inferring the players’ payoff functions. Rather than producing a single payoff estimate, inverse game theory aims to identify the entire set of payoffs consistent with observed behavior, enabling downstream use in, e.g. , counterfactual analysis and mechanism design across applications like auctions, pricing, and security games. We focus on the problem of estimating the set of feasible payoffs with high probability and up to precision ϵ \epsilon on the Hausdorff metric. We provide the first minimax-optimal rates for both exact and approximate equilibrium play, in zero-sum as well as general-sum games. Our results provide learning-theoretic foundations for set-valued payoff inference in multi-agent environments.



1
Introduction

The central question in
inverse game theory
is the following
(
Waugh et al. 2011
;
Kuleshov and Schrijvers 2015
)
: given observed equilibrium behavior, what utility functions could have generated it?
We study the problem of recovering the
entire
set of possible payoffs compatible with the players’ observed behavior, given action samples generated by their strategies. We are interested in the minimax sample complexity of approximately identifying such a set.
Even when equilibrium behavior is observed exactly (let alone through samples), the problem of inferring an agent’s utilities from behavior is inherently underdetermined, as multiple payoff functions can rationalize the same observed play
(
Ng and Russell 2000
;
Waugh et al. 2011
)
.
This issue is often referred to as
unidentifiability
. For example, the zero payoff matrix is compatible with any equilibrium. This ambiguity is usually addressed by imposing additional structure, such as restricting attention to parametrized low-dimensional payoff families
(
Waugh et al. 2011
;
Kuleshov and Schrijvers 2015
)
, or by choosing a priori a selection principle such as maximum entropy
(
Vorobeychik et al. 2007
;
Waugh et al. 2011
)
, or bounded-rationality models (such as quantal response)
(
Yu et al. 2022
)
.

Rather than imposing additional modeling assumptions or fixing an
a priori
selection principle, we focus on identifying the entire set of payoff functions consistent with the observed strategies,
i.e.
, the feasible payoff set. The idea of this approach is to defer the selection of a particular payoff to a later stage, thereby making the entire process modular and more adaptable to the requirements of the application at hand.
For example, given the entire set of possible equilibria, one may select equilibria according to a maximum-entropy criterion
(
Waugh et al. 2011
)
, add additional structural constraints to the set (
e.g.
, if we know that the game is symmetric or has a potential-game structure), or choose a robust representative such as the incenter solution
(
Cui et al. 2025
)
.
This set-valued perspective is standard in structural econometrics and inverse game theory (see, among others,
Brown and Matzkin 1996
;
Manski 2003
;
Tamer 2010
;
Waugh et al. 2011
).
However, the corresponding learning problem remains poorly understood from a theoretical standpoint, and even in the simplest multi-agent settings, we lack a sharp characterization of the
statistical
difficulty of such set-valued identification.

In this work, we address this gap by providing minimax-optimal sample complexity guarantees for the problem of recovering the feasible payoff set from the observed behavior of two players in an unknown bimatrix game playing a Nash equilibrium. In particular, we consider two players repeatedly interacting in an unknown
n
-dimensional normal-form bimatrix game with payoff matrices
(A,B)
.
The players play a (possibly approximate) Nash equilibrium
(x,y)
of the game, and the learner observes i.i.d. action pairs
(X_{t},Y_{t})
sampled from the product distribution
x\times y
.
The learner’s goal is to infer the
feasible payoff set
:
the set of all payoff matrices
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
for which the strategies
(x,y)
constitute a Nash equilibrium (or an
\alpha
-Nash equilibrium) of
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
. The goal of the paper is to answer the following question:
How many equilibrium samples are required to estimate the feasible payoff set up to Hausdorff error
\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
?

Our main contribution is the first minimax-optimal characterization of this question. Our results cover both
general-sum
and
zero-sum
games, and both approximate and exact Nash equilibria.

Technical challenges

An important technical ingredient in our analysis is the correspondence (a set-valued function) that maps equilibrium strategies to the feasible payoff sets that rationalize them. Regarding the lower bounds, we first show that, when observing exact equilibria (
i.e.
,
\alpha=0
), the strong discontinuity of such correspondence makes the problem unlearnable. Therefore, we relax the learning requirement and provide minimax-optimal rates when the strategy profiles have a minimum probability of playing each action that is bounded away from
0
.
Conversely, when
\alpha>0
, we can show that such discontinuities are somewhat mitigated. Interestingly, our results reveal that small values of
\alpha
can substantially degrade the rates through an unavoidable dependence on
\alpha^{-1}
. Informally, the cause is that when
(x,y)
play some actions with probability
\propto\alpha
, small changes in the strategy profiles lead to large changes in the payoff sets.
For the upper bounds, the major technical obstacle in obtaining tight rates is establishing this
\alpha^{-1}
dependence in the approximate-equilibrium case.
To this end, focusing on a term that depends only on the
x
-player, we first show that the Hausdorff error scales with
\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-1}(\hat{x}-x)^{\top}Ay)
, where
\hat{x}
is the empirical mean of
x
after
m
samples and
A
is any feasible payoff for
(x,y)
(an analogous argument holds for the
y
-player).
Hence, to get a tight rate, we need to show that the empirical value of the game
\hat{x}^{\top}Ay
converges to the true value
x^{\top}Ay
as
{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\alpha(n+\log(1/\delta))/m})
, where
m
is the number of samples (while standard concentration of
\hat{x}
to
x
would only yield
{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n+\log(1/\delta)/m})
). We argue that such rates are achievable by studying a fractional knapsack problem that arises in controlling the error.

1.1
Related Works

Early empirical work on payoff learning treats utilities as regression targets when realized utilities are observed for sampled strategy profiles, enabling supervised payoff approximation
(
Vorobeychik et al. 2007
)
.
Closer in spirit to our setting,
Kuleshov and Schrijvers 2015
introduced inverse game theory in the context of succinctly-representable games and studied the computational problem of finding utilities that rationalize an observed equilibrium (primarily via feasibility of linear/convex programs under structural restrictions).
Waugh et al. 2011
study an inverse equilibrium problem (for correlated equilibria) in a parametrized setting, and recover a polytope of compatible payoff functions used to compute the maximum-entropy equilibrium.
Recently,
Goktas et al. 2024
studied how to recover a
single
payoff-parameter/Nash-equilibrium pair consistent with observed behavior, relying on some form of oracle access to payoffs during the inverse optimization. This is fundamentally different from our setting, where we aim to recover the feasible set of payoff matrices from i.i.d. equilibrium action samples alone.
A related line of works focus on learning adversary models in Stackelberg games, with the main goal of enabling behavioral modeling of attackers in security games
(
Haghtalab et al. 2016
;
Sinha et al. 2016
;
Wu et al. 2022
)
. This problem differs from ours in the fact that the learner can probe the adversary (
i.e.
, the player with unknown payoffs) by observing their responses to ad hoc strategies.
In the context of sponsored search auctions,
Nekipelov et al. 2015
studies how to estimate players’ valuations from observed contextual data, by assuming each player places bids following a no-regret algorithm.
Finally,
Ling et al. 2018
attempts to fit parametric games end-to-end by replacing Nash equilibrium with differentiable fixed points, such as quantal response equilibrium, enabling gradient-based estimation of game parameters.

Related to our work is the single-agent version of our problem, usually studied in inverse reinforcement learning (IRL).
In IRL, the objective is to infer rewards for which a demonstrated policy is optimal
(
Abbeel and Ng 2004
)
. It is well known that even in single-agent settings, the problem is fundamentally
ill-posed
, as multiple reward functions may be compatible with the observed behavior
(
Ng and Russell 2000
)
. Analogously to the game theory setting, there have been several attempts to resolve this ambiguity by focusing on specific selection criteria such as maximum margin, maximum entropy, and minimum Hessian eigenvalue
(
Ratliff et al. 2006
;
Metelli et al. 2017
;
Zeng et al. 2022
)
. In this work, we take inspiration from an alternative recent line of research that attempts to overcome the ambiguity issue by estimating the
feasible reward set
,
i.e.
, the entire set of reward functions compatible with the observed data
(
Metelli et al. 2021
;
Lindner et al. 2022
;
Metelli et al. 2023
;
Poiani et al. 2024
)
. Remarkably, this approach captures the inherent ambiguity of the problem, supports robust prediction and counterfactual reasoning, and avoids committing to arbitrary equilibrium-selection or regularization choices.

There are many works that extend the single-agent IRL problem to the multi-agent IRL problem (MAIRL), including,
e.g.
,
(
Natarajan et al. 2010
;
Yu et al. 2019
;
Fu et al. 2021
;
Reddy et al. 2012
;
Zhang et al. 2019
)
. As in inverse game theory, most MAIRL approaches resolve the inherent ambiguity through a priori selection principles or additional modeling assumptions. There are recent exceptions that study the learning problem of returning the entire set of feasible rewards
(
Freihaut and Ramponi 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024
)
. However, unlike what happens in game-theoretic applications such as mechanism design, auctions, and pricing, the primary goal of (MA)IRL is to serve as a preprocessing step within a larger pipeline: the learned model and payoff are subsequently used to train multi-agent policies, which are then evaluated in the true environment.
As noted by
Freihaut and Ramponi 2024
, in strategic settings this pipeline lacks guarantees without further restrictions (such as uniqueness of equilibria or structural assumptions that enforce it,
e.g.
, entropy-regularized Markov games), since the new learned equilibria for the approximated payoffs might perform arbitrarily bad in the original setting.

Finally, a closely related area is
revealed preference
analysis, which asks whether observed price/purchase observations can be rationalized by utility maximization and constructs utility representations consistent with the available observations
(
Samuelson 1948
;
Afriat 1967
;
Varian 1982
)
. There is a related line of works studying the sample complexity of learning to predict optimal bundles of items for a buyer with an unknown demand function from some specific class (e.g., linear, separable piecewise-linear concave, and Leontief) from observed choices
(
Beigman and Vohra 2006
;
Zadimoghaddam and Roth 2012
;
Balcan et al. 2014
)
.

Preliminaries

Mathematical notation

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\Delta_{n}
the
n
-dimensional simplex. Furthermore,
[n]
denotes the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
. We also denote by
e_{i}
the
i
-th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any
x\in\Delta_{n}
, we denote by
\textup{supp}(x)=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}>0\}
. Let
\mathcal{X}_{1}
,
\mathcal{X}_{2}
be two non-empty subsets of a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
. Then, the Hausdorff distance
(
Rockafellar and Wets 2017
)

H_{d}(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})
is:

\displaystyle\max\left\{\sup_{x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}}\inf_{x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}_{2}}d(x_{1},x_{2}),\sup_{x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}_{2}}\inf_{x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}}d(x_{1},x_{2})\right\}.

We note that
H_{d}
is defined based on the metric
d
.
In this work, we will be primarily interested in the case where
d
is the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm between (subsets of) Euclidean spaces. From now on, we will drop the dependency on
d
.

General-Sum Games and Nash-Equilibrium

A general-sum game (GSG) is specified by two matrices
A,B\in[-1,1]^{n\times n}
. For mixed strategies
x,y\in\Delta_{n}
, the expected losses incurred by the two players are
x^{\top}Ay
and
x^{\top}By
, respectively. Each player seeks to choose a mixed strategy that minimizes their own expected loss. Given
\alpha\geq 0
, we say that a pair of strategies
x,y
is an
\alpha
-Nash equilibrium for
\mathcal{G}
if the following conditions are met:
x^{\top}Ay\leq e_{i}Ay+\alpha
for all
i\in[n]
, and
x^{\top}By\leq x^{\top}Be_{j}+\alpha
for all
j\in[n]
.
For any
x,y
we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)
the set of all matrices for which
x,y
is an
\alpha
-Nash equilibrium, that is, the set of matrices
A,B
that verify the following constraints for all indices
i,j\in[n]^{2}
:

\displaystyle x^{\top}Ay\leq e_{i}^{\top}Ay+\alpha\quad\textnormal{ and }\quad x^{\top}By\leq x^{\top}Be_{j}+\alpha.

Formally, we can define the following sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{x}_{\alpha}(x,y)=\{A\in[-1,1]^{n\times n}:x^{\top}Ay\leq e_{i}^{\top}Ay+\alpha~~\forall i\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{y}_{\alpha}(x,y)=\{B\in[-1,1]^{n\times n}:x^{\top}By\leq x^{\top}Be_{j}+\alpha~~\forall j\},

so that we can write
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)=\mathcal{G}^{x}_{\alpha}(x,y)\times\mathcal{G}^{y}_{\alpha}(x,y)
.

Zero-Sum Games and Nash-Equilibrium

A zero-sum game (ZSG), instead, is specified by a single matrix
A\in[-1,1]^{n\times n}
. In this case,
x,y\in\Delta_{n}\times\Delta_{n}
is an
\alpha
-Nash equilibrium if
x^{\top}Ae_{j}-\alpha\leq x^{\top}Ay\leq e_{i}^{\top}Ay+\alpha
holds for every
i,j\in[n]^{2}
.
For any pair of mixed strategies
x,y
, we denote by
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}(x,y)
the set of all matrices for which
x,y
is an
\alpha
-Nash equilibrium, that is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\left\{A\in[-1,1]^{n\times n}:x^{\top}Ae_{j}-\alpha\leq x^{\top}Ay\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69046pt\textnormal{and }\left.x^{\top}Ay\leq e_{i}^{\top}Ay+\alpha,\quad\forall i,j\in[n]^{2}\right\}.

Note that
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}(x,y)\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)
. Indeed, it is sufficient to slice
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)
on the hyperplane where
A=-B
.

Remark 2.1
.

When
\alpha
is not specified, we refer to the exact equilibrium case. There, we will simply write
e.g.
,
\mathcal{G}(x,y)
and
\mathcal{Z}(x,y)
instead of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}(x,y)
.

Learning Framework

We consider a setting in which two players follow a pair of mixed strategies
x,y\in\Delta_{n}\times\Delta_{n}
that constitute an
\alpha
-Nash equilibrium of an unknown general-sum game. The objective of the agent is to recover the feasible payoff set
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)
of matrices for which
(x,y)
is an
\alpha
-Nash equilibrium. In particular, the interaction framework is as follows: during each round
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the learner only observes
X_{t},Y_{t}\sim x,y
.
The learning algorithm takes in input a precision level
\epsilon>0
together with a maximum risk parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
and the parameter
\alpha\in[0,1)
and is composed of (i) a stopping rule
\tau_{\delta}
that controls the end of the data acquisition phase, and (ii) a recommendation rule, which we express as a function
f
that takes in input a pair of matrices
A,B
and returns
1
if the agent believes that
(A,B)
belongs to
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)
and
0
otherwise. We note that
f
uniquely describes a set
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}=\{(A,B):f((A,B))=1\}
, which denotes the guess of the agent for all the pair of matrices belonging to
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)
. Hence, in the following, we will refer to
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}
as the recommendation rule (similarly,
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{\alpha}
is the guess of the agent for the set
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}(x,y)
).

Among the possible learning algorithms, we are interested in those that are
(\epsilon,\delta)
-correct w.r.t. the Hausdorff distance. More precisely, the algorithm has to guarantee that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(H(\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y),\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha})\geq\epsilon)\leq\delta

in the general-sum case, with an analogous requirement for
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
in the zero-sum setting.

We make a remark that distinguishes the two learning objectives. At first, one might think that the problem of learning
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}(x,y)
could be easily solved once we have a set
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}
that approximates
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)
. Nonetheless, we did not find any trivial way to slice a generic set
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}
so that it approximates
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}(x,y)
. On one hand, one might be tempted to obtain
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{\alpha}
by slicing
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}
on
A=-B
. However, this only ensures one direction of the Hausdorff distance.
1

1

Note that, in principle, the resulting set could even be empty.
Similarly, one could think of slicing
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}
by taking all the pair of matrices
(A,B)
which are almost zero-sum,
e.g.
,
\|A-(-B)\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
. However, this set might be too large and could fail to satisfy the other direction of the Hausdorff distance. For these reasons, we study zero-sum and general-sum games as separate problems, providing minimax rates for both these settings.

3.2
On Approximate Equilibria

In this section we provide a lower bound for the case in which players are playing an
\alpha
-approximate Nash equilibrium. A natural question is whether the difficulty of learning the support of the equilibrium strategies again leads to an infinite sample-complexity bound. We argue that, in this setting, the answer is
no
. The presence of
\alpha
in the definitions of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y)
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}(x,y)
prevents these sets from having a representation analogous to
Equation

2
. Intuitively, this removes the strong discontinuity exhibited by
\mathcal{G}(x,y)
and
\mathcal{Z}(x,y)
when
(x,y)
vary across strategy profiles with different supports. Informally, the parameter
\alpha
acts as a tolerance that absorbs the error contributed by actions that are played with small probability.
To have an intuitive picture of this phenomenon, it is instructive to consider the simple instance that we analyzed for the
\alpha=0
setting.
We recall that
y=y^{0}=y^{1}=(1,0)
,
x^{0}=(0,1)
, and
x^{1}=(\pi,1-\pi)
for some
\pi
. Let
\Delta_{A}=a_{21}-a_{11}
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{G}^{x}_{\alpha}(x^{0},y^{0})\Leftrightarrow-2\leq\Delta_{A}\leq\alpha;

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{G}^{x}_{\alpha}(x^{1},y^{1})\Leftrightarrow\Delta_{A}\in[\max\{-2,-\tfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\},\min\{2,\tfrac{\alpha}{1-\pi}]\}.

As
\pi\to 0
(or, equivalently, as
\pi\ll\alpha
), the two sets collapse. Thus,
\alpha
is absorbing the error of actions that are played rarely. These sets are depicted in

Theorem 3.3

Let

\epsilon\leq 1/128

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\tau_{\delta}]\geq\Omega\!\left(\frac{n+\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{\epsilon^{2}\,\alpha}\right).

(4)

Theorem

4.1
shows the same rate of the lower bound of

Proof sketch of
Theorem

4.1
(General-Sum Games)

The proof works as follows. We fix a generic
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and we derive a high-probability upper bound on the Hausdorff distance between
\mathcal{G}(x,y)
and
\mathcal{G}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
. Then,
m
is chosen so that this upper bound is below
\epsilon
. The line that we follow to derive this upper bound is inspired by the lower bound of
Section

3
. In particular, we know that the support of
(x,y)
and
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
should match in order to avoid the degenerate behavior that we identified. Hence, we start with the assumption that
(x,y)
and
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
are such that
\textup{supp}(x)=\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
and
\textup{supp}(y)=\textup{supp}(\hat{y})
, and we later discuss how to guarantee that this holds in high probability once
m
is large enough. Under the assumption that
\textup{supp}(x)=\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
and
\textup{supp}(y)=\textup{supp}(\hat{y})
, we upper bound
H(\mathcal{G}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(\hat{x},\hat{y}))
by showing that for any
(A,B)\in\mathcal{G}(x,y)
there exists
(\hat{A},\hat{B})\in\mathcal{G}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
such that:
7
7

7

We also show that the symmetric claim is true, since we need both directions to upper bound the Hausdorff distance.

\displaystyle\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\infty}\leq 4\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1},\,\|B-\hat{B}\|_{\infty}\leq 4\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}.

(5)

Note that this directly gives us an upper bound on the Hausdorff distance of
4(\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}+\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1})
. In other words, the error scales linearly with the
\ell_{1}
-distance between the true strategy profile and the empirical one.

Now, we discuss how to obtain
Equation

5
(we do that for
A
, and the construction for
B
follows from a similar reasoning).
Starting from
A\in\mathcal{G}^{x}(x,y)
(and assuming equal support), we know that if
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{G}^{x}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
, then for all
i\in\textup{supp}(x)
,
\hat{x}^{\top}\hat{A}\hat{y}=e_{i}^{\top}\hat{A}\hat{y}
. Then, since we want to construct
\hat{A}
close to
A
, our goal is to "redistribute" the payoffs of
A
according to the shift of
\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}
so that, in all the rows in
\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
, the value obtained by the x-player is left unchanged. To this end, for any
i\in\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
, we first define the following quantity:

\displaystyle\textstyle\Delta_{i}=\max_{k\in\textup{supp}(\hat{x})}\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{kj}-\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{ij}.

(6)

Here,
\Delta_{i}\in[0,2\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}]
represents the maximum payoff difference (for the
x
-player) in playing the pure strategy
i
w.r.t. playing any other strategy
k\in\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
. Intuitively, this is the shift, for actions in
\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
, that
\hat{A}
should meet so that
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{G}^{x}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
.
Instead, for all the rows that do not belong to
\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
, we only need to ensure that their value remains greater than those of actions in
\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
.
Given these intuitions, we define
\hat{A}
as follows:

\hat{A}_{ij}:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{A_{ij}}{1+2\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}},&\text{if }i\in S^{\star}_{\hat{x}},\\[12.0pt]
\dfrac{A_{ij}+\Delta_{i}^{x}}{1+2\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}},&\text{if }i\in\textup{supp}(\hat{x})\setminus S^{\star}_{\hat{x}},\\[12.0pt]
\dfrac{A_{ij}+2\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}}{1+2\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}},&\text{if }i\notin\textup{supp}(\hat{x}),\end{cases}

(7)

where
S^{\star}_{\hat{x}}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{k\in\textup{supp}(\hat{x})}\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{kj}
. Ignoring the denominator, which is simply a rescaling to guarantee that
\hat{A}
remains in
[-1,1]^{n\times n}
, we observe that the construction does not modify values in
S^{\star}_{\hat{x}}
, but it increases the values of other actions played in
\hat{x}
so that any action in
\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
yields the same value. Moreover, this construction increases the value of all the actions which are not played by
\hat{x}
, so that they do not obtain values which are better than actions that are actually played by
\hat{x}
. It is then possible to verify that
\hat{A}
belongs to
\mathcal{G}^{x}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
, and that
\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\infty}\leq 4\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}
.

Finally, to conclude the proof, it is sufficient to upper bound with high-probability
\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}
and
\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}
, and to guarantee that the support of
(x,y)
aligns with that of
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
. Standard concentration tools yield
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{(n+\log(\delta^{-1})/m})
for the former and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\log(\delta^{-1})/\log((1-\pi_{\min})^{-1}))
for the latter. Putting everything together this gives
Theorem

4.1
.

What changes in Zero-Sum Games

The proof for the zero-sum game problem follows from a similar argument; however, there is an important difference in how to upper bound the Hausdorff distance once the support of
(x,y)
and
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
matches. Specifically, constructing
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
is more challenging as now
\hat{A}
should satisfy the constraints for both players simultaneously. In particular, we show in
Section

B.2
that a matrix
\hat{A}
constructed as in
Equation

7
fails to meet the constraints for the

i.e.

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{Z}(x,y),\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},y))+H(\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},y),\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},\hat{y})).

This error decomposition allows us to apply the technique for general-sum games even in the zero-sum setting.

Theorem 4.2

Proof sketch of
Theorem

4.2
(General-Sum Games)

As for
Theorem

4.1
, we fix a generic
m\in\mathbb{N}
and derive a high probability upper bound on the Hausdorff distance between the two sets as a function of
m
. Then, we choose
m
so that this upper bound is below
\epsilon
. We start by employing the same decomposition that we used for zero-sum games and
\alpha=0
in order to upper bound
H(\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y),\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\hat{x},\hat{y}))
with

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y),\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\hat{x},y))+H(\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\hat{x},y),\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\hat{x},\hat{y})).

This allows us to study only the error components that arise from one of the two strategy profiles,
x
or
y
. For clarity of exposition, we explain how to upper bound
H(\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(x,y),\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\hat{x},y))
, and in particular we focus on the component corresponding to the first player, namely the sets
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(\cdot,\cdot)
(as for
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{y}(\cdot,\cdot)
we have specular arguments).

Thus, we start by considering any
A\in\mathcal{G}^{x}_{\alpha}(x,y)
and construct
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{G}^{x}_{\alpha}(\hat{x},y)
which is close to
A
.
To this end, we follow a reasoning that is similar to the above,
i.e.
, we first quantify
A
’s constraint violation of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(\hat{x},y)
and we try to correct that error with an appropriate construction. Specifically, let us denote by
g_{A}
the following quantity

\displaystyle g_{A}\coloneqq\max_{i\in[n]}\left(\hat{x}^{\top}Ay-e_{i}^{\top}Ay\right).

If
g_{A}\leq\alpha
, then
A\in\mathcal{G}^{x}_{\alpha}(\hat{x},y)
as
\alpha
is absorbing the error that arises from
\hat{x}\neq x
. If
g_{A}\geq\alpha
, instead, we construct
\hat{A}
as
\hat{A}=\frac{\alpha}{g_{A}}A
. This multiplicative construction ensures that
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\hat{x},y)
since, for all
i\in[n]
, we have that:

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{\top}\hat{A}y-e_{i}^{\top}\hat{A}y=\frac{\alpha}{g_{A}}(\hat{x}^{\top}Ay-e_{i}^{\top}Ay)\leq\alpha.

The difference between
A
and
\hat{A}
is then given by:

\displaystyle\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{g_{A}-\alpha}{g_{A}}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(\hat{x}-x)^{\top}Ay}{\alpha}\right),

where the second step follows from
g_{A}\geq\alpha
and the fact that
A\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(x,y)
.
8
8

8

We add and subtract
x^{\top}Ay
. Then we use
x^{\top}Ay-e_{i}^{\top}Ay\leq\alpha
.

Hence, to control the Hausdorff distance related to
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(x,y)
and
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(\hat{x},y)
, we need to control:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\sup_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(x,y)}{(\hat{x}-x)^{\top}Ay}{}.

(8)

At first, one might be tempted to bound
(\hat{x}-x)^{\top}Ay
with
2\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}
. However, this naive argument yields sub-optimal rates. Since with high probability
\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{(n+\log(\delta^{-1})/m}
, the number of samples to ensure that
Equation

8
is below
\epsilon
would roughly be
(n+\log(\delta^{-1}))/{\epsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}}
, which does not match the lower bound of
Theorem

3.3
.
The difficulty is that the bound
(\hat{x}-x)^{\top}Ay\leq 2\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}
fails to exploit the fact that
A\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(x,y)
and does not leverage its geometric structure.

In the following, we show how to obtain better rates by exploiting the structure of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(x,y)
. Indeed, the properties of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(x,y)
will ensure that the variance of
(\hat{x}-x)^{\top}Ay
is small, thus leading to faster high-probability concentration. To this end, we need to manipulate appropriately
Equation

8
. First of all, we bound
Equation

8
as follows

\displaystyle\sup_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(x,y)}\frac{\sum_{i:x_{i}>0}(\hat{x}_{i}-x_{i})(Ay)_{i}}{\alpha}+\frac{2\sum_{i:x_{i}=0}\hat{x}_{i}}{\alpha},

(9)

to get rid of the components where
x_{i}=0
in the optimization problem. Let us ignore the second term for now, and focus on the remaining optimization over
A
(we return to the second term later in the proof sketch).
We will show that this remaining optimization problem presents a hidden fractional knapsack structure. First, let us introduce some notation. For any
A\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{x}(x,y)
, let
\star\in[n]
be any row that belongs to
\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{i\in[n]}e_{i}^{\top}Ay
. Then, let
c_{i}=x_{i}[(Ay)_{i}-(Ay)_{\star}]
. Then, we can rewrite that optimization problem as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\sup_{c:c_{i}\in[0,2x_{i}]}\sum_{i:x_{i}>0}\frac{\hat{x}_{i}-x_{i}}{x_{i}}c_{i}\quad\text{s.t.~}\sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}\leq\alpha.

(10)

This problem is well-known to be solved by a greedy algorithm that sorts the indexes
i
’s according to their value (
i.e.
,
\frac{\hat{x}_{i}-x_{i}}{x_{i}}
) and allocates the maximum value of
c_{i}
(
i.e.
,
c_{i}=2x_{i}
), according to this order until the budget is fully depleted,
i.e.
, is stops once
\sum_{i:x_{i}>0}c_{i}>\alpha
.

Section

C.1
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(x)}\sum_{i\in S}(\hat{x}_{i}-x_{i}),

where
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(x)
is given by

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(x)\coloneqq\{S\subseteq[n]:x_{i}>0~\forall i\in S\land\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\leq\alpha/2\}.

Now, the main point is that each set
S\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(x)
is such that
\sum_{i\in{S}}x_{i}\leq\alpha/2
. Hence, we can treat
\sum_{i\in S}(\hat{x}_{i}-x_{i})
as a Bernoulli random variable with mean bounded by
\alpha/2
(and variance bounded by
\alpha/2
). Using Bernstein’s inequality, we have that
\sum_{i\in S}(\hat{x}_{i}-x_{i})
concentrates as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\alpha\log(\delta^{-1})/m})
. Taking an union bound over all the possible subsets

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\alpha(n+\log(\delta^{-1})/m)}
. Plugging this result in
Equation

\mathcal{O}(\frac{n+\log(\delta^{-1})}{\epsilon^{2}\alpha})
samples are needed to have the first error term below
\epsilon
.

Now, we still need to control the second term of

9

\hat{x}_{i}
is the empirical mean of

.

9

9

There is another asymmetry in bounding that direction of the Hausdorff distance. Specifically, when constructing

However,

, it decays as

Furthermore, the expectation decays also as

In this way, we concluded the proof of

What changes in Zero-Sum Games

For the zero-sum game problem, we follow an analogous argument.
The main difference is that we define
g_{A}
as:

\displaystyle g_{A}\coloneqq\max\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}\hat{x}^{\top}Ay-e_{i}^{\top}Ay,\max_{j\in[n]}\hat{x}^{\top}Ae_{j}-\hat{x}^{\top}Ay\right\}.

This is due to the fact that the matrix
\hat{A}
now needs to satisfy a larger set of constraints. We show that the errors introduced by this modification are bounded as in the general-sum case.

The challenges for
\alpha>0

We conclude by highlighting the challenges introduced by the case
\alpha>0
. The main difficulty lies in the fact that when the players are playing an approximate equilibrium, we cannot rely on strong characterizations of their behavior,
i.e.
,
Equation

2
, as we did for the
\alpha=0

shows that the dependence in

is complex, as it multiplies all the parameters of the problem due to geometric arguments that impact how much the Hausdorff distance changes when the strategy profiles have values around
\alpha
.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we study the problem of learning from demonstrations the payoff sets that are compatible with an unknown, possibly approximate, equilibrium. We derived tight minimax rates by providing matching lower and upper bounds. As we highlighted, these results required careful considerations on the geometry of the set of feasible payoffs.
Our work paves the way for several promising directions for future research. For instance, it would be interesting to extend these results beyond the notion of approximate Nash equilibrium to broader solution concepts, such as correlated equilibria. Furthermore, in this work, we assumed that the strategy profiles
(x,y)
are fixed. Future research should focus on the case where strategy profiles are evolving, and players are learning the game through regret minimization. Finally, we note that our analysis is instance-independent. In the future, it would be exciting to develop instance-dependent lower and upper bounds that directly depend on the unknown strategy profiles
(x,y)
. Here, one could take inspiration from the optimal instance-dependent results available in the bandit literature, see,
e.g.
,
Garivier and Kaufmann 2016
.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work aimed at advancing the field of machine learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

\dfrac{B_{ij}+\Delta_{i}^{x}}{1+\Delta},&\text{if }j\in S_{y}\setminus S^{\star}_{y},\\
\dfrac{B_{ij}+\Delta}{1+\Delta},&\text{if }j\notin S_{y}.\end{cases}

where
\Delta=2\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}
. The proof of the results than follows from arguments that are analogous to the ones that we presented for
Equation

34
.
∎

We now leverage
Lemma

B.1
to provide an upper bound on the Hausdorfff distance.

Lemma B.2

(Matrix Construction
\to
Hausdorfff Distance)
.

Let
\alpha=0
,
x,y,\hat{x},\hat{y}\in\Delta_{n}
. Suppose that
\textup{supp}(x)=\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
and
\textup{supp}(y)=\textup{supp}(\hat{y})
. Then, it holds that:

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{G}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(\hat{x},\hat{y}))\leq 4(\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}+\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}).

Proof.

The proof is a direct application of
Lemma

B.1
. Precisely:

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{G}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(\hat{x},\hat{y}))

\displaystyle=\max\Bigg\{\max_{(A,B)\in\mathcal{G}(x,y)}\min_{(\hat{A},\hat{B})\in\mathcal{G}(\hat{x},\hat{y})}\max\{\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\infty},\|B-\hat{B}\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\max_{(\hat{A},\hat{B})\in\mathcal{G}(\hat{x},\hat{y})}\min_{(A,B)\in\mathcal{G}(x,y)}\max\{\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\infty},\|B-\hat{B}\|_{\infty}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq 4\max\left\{\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1},\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}\right\}

(
Lemma

B.1
)

\displaystyle\leq 4(\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}+\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}).

which concludes the proof. Here, we simply remark that, in order to apply
Lemma

B.1
in the first inequality, it is important to recall that
\mathcal{G}(x,y)=\mathcal{G}^{x}(x,y)\times\mathcal{G}^{y}(x,y)
.
∎

At this point, we can combine
Lemma

B.2
with probabilistic arguments to obtain a high probability error bound on the Hausdorff distance that is directly related to the number of samples observed by our algorithm.

Lemma B.3

(High-Probability Hausdorff Bound)
.

Let
\alpha=0
and
\hat{x},\hat{y}\in\Delta_{n}
be the maximum likelihood estimators of
x,y
after
m
samples. Suppose that
m
satisfies:

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{\log\left(\frac{4n}{\delta}\right)}{\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\pi_{\min}}\right)},

(37)

then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that:

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{G}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(\hat{x},\hat{y})\leq 8\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)+2n\log(6m)}{m}}.

Proof.

We first define the following good event
\mathcal{E}
and shows that it holds with probability at least
1-\delta
. We define
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}=\left\{\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)+2n\log(6m)}{m}}\right\}\cap\left\{\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)+2n\log(6m)}{m}}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}=\{\textup{supp}(x)=\textup{supp}(\hat{x})\}\cap\{\textup{supp}(y)=\textup{supp}(\hat{y})\}.

Then, we have that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})=1-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\complement})\geq 1-(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\complement})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\complement}_{2}))
.
Hence, we simply upper bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\complement})
and
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\complement}_{2})
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\complement})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}>\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)+2n\log(6m)}{m}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}>\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)+2n\log(6m)}{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

(
Lemma

D.3
)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\complement})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\textup{supp}(x)\neq\textup{supp}(\hat{x}))+\mathbb{P}(\textup{supp}(y)\neq\textup{supp}(\hat{y}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

(
Equation

37
and
Lemma

D.1
)

Thus, we obtained that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta
.

Therefore, due to the definition of
\mathcal{E}
and by using
Lemma

B.2
, we have that, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{G}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(\hat{x},\hat{y}))

\displaystyle\leq 4(\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}+\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1})

\displaystyle\leq 8\sqrt{\frac{2\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)+2n\log(6m)}{m}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

is an

\displaystyle m

Proof outline

The proof of
Theorem

B.7
is similar in spirit to the one that we presented for
Theorem

B.4
. There are, however, a couple of important differences that complicate the analysis. Importantly, these differences arise from the additional constraints that are present in the definition of
\mathcal{Z}(x,y)
, which require a single matrix to verify constraints both for the
x
and the
y
players. Consider, indeed, any pair of strategies
(x,y)
and
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
such that
\textup{supp}(x)=\textup{supp}(\hat{x})
and
\textup{supp}(y)=\textup{supp}(\hat{y})
. Then, for any
A\in\mathcal{Z}(x,y)
, the construction of
\hat{A}
that we provided in
Lemma

B.1
for general-sum games fails to satisfy the constraints of
\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
. To this end, consider the following simple numerical example. Let:

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}\quad x=y=(0.5,0.5),\quad\hat{x}=(0.6,0.4),\hat{y}=(0.4,0.6)

In this case,
Lemma

B.1
yields:

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-0.42857143\\
-0.71428571&0.71428571\end{pmatrix}.

\displaystyle\hat{A}\approx\begin{pmatrix}1&-0.43\\
-0.71&0.71\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\hat{A}\notin\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
, we cannot rely on the previous argument for this more complicated case. More generally, we found particularly challenging building analytically a matrix
\hat{A}
that takes into account variations both in
x
and
y
strategy profiles. To this end, however, we developed a trick to take into account the single variations of the
x
and the
y
vector. Specifically, we start our analysis with
Corollary

D.11
which states that

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{Z}(x,y),\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},\hat{y}))\leq H(\mathcal{Z}(x,y),\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},y))+H(\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},y),\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},\hat{y})).

This result allows us to construct matrices to upper bound the Hausdorff distance again by construction. Specifically, we can fix two matrices
A_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}(x,y)
and
A_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},y)
and find two matrices
A_{3}\in\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},y)
and
A_{4}\in\mathcal{Z}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
such that
A_{1}\approx A_{3}
and
A_{2}\approx A_{4}
(
Lemma

B.5
and
Lemma

B.6
). Importantly, note that
A_{1}
and
A_{3}
belong to sets that “vary only across the
x
player’s strategy” (
i.e.
,
y
is fixed), while
A_{2}
and
A_{4}
belong to sets that “vary only across the
y
player’s strategy” (
i.e.
,
\hat{x}
is fixed).
Importantly the approximation errors of both matrices scale linearly with
\|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}
and
\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}
.
Then, once this is done, we proceed identically to
Theorem

B.4
.

We are now ready to start the proof of
Theorem

B.7
.
As anticipated, given
Corollary

D.11
, we start by fixing the
x
-vector and, for any matrix
A\in\mathcal{Z}(x,y)
we build an alternative matrix
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{Z}(x,\hat{y})
. A careful reader might note that the matrix constructed in the following lemma is similar to the one presented in

B.1
for general-sum games. However, here both the statement and the proofs are different. Indeed, (i) to construct
\hat{A}
we require that the support of
y
and
\hat{y}
coincide and (ii) we need to ensure that our construction satisfies a larger number of constraints.

We remark that in the following lemma,
x,y,\hat{y}
are generic strategies on the simplex.

Lemma B.5

(Alternative Matrix Construction (fix x);
\alpha=0
; Zero-Sum Games)
.

Let
\alpha=0
, and

\displaystyle\textup{supp}(y)=\textup{supp}(\hat{y})\implies\exists\hat{A}\in\mathcal{Z}(x,\hat{y}):\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\infty}\leq 4\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}

\hat{A}_{ij}:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{A_{ij}}{1+2\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}},&\text{if }i\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{k\in\textup{supp}(x)}\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{kj},\\[12.0pt]
\dfrac{A_{ij}+\Delta_{i}}{1+2\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}},&\text{if }i\in\textup{supp}(x)\setminus\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{k\in\textup{supp}(x)}\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{kj},\\[12.0pt]

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}=\max_{k\in\textup{supp}(x)}\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{kj}-\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{ij}

Proof.

Given the definition of
\hat{A}

B.1

\displaystyle\text{(i)}~\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\infty}\leq 4\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\text{(iii)}~e_{i}^{\top}\hat{A}\hat{y}\leq e_{j}^{\top}\hat{A}\hat{y}\quad\quad\quad\quad\forall i\in\textup{supp}(x),\forall j\notin\textup{supp}(x).

D.8

\displaystyle x^{\top}\hat{A}e_{j}=x^{\top}\hat{A}\hat{y}~\quad\forall j\in\textup{supp}(\hat{y})

\displaystyle x^{\top}\hat{A}e_{j}\leq x^{\top}\hat{A}\hat{y}~\quad\forall j\notin\textup{supp}(\hat{y}).

(40b)

Before proving

40a
and
40b

D.8

\displaystyle x^{\top}Ay=x^{\top}Ae_{j}=e_{i}^{T}Ay,\quad\forall\,i\in\textup{supp}(x),j\in\textup{supp}(y),

\displaystyle x^{\top}Ae_{j}\leq x^{\top}Ay\leq e_{i}^{\top}Ay,\quad\forall\,i\notin\textup{supp}(x),\ \forall\,j\notin\textup{supp}(y).

Now, let
\Delta=2\|y-\hat{y}\|_{1}

j\in[n]

\displaystyle x^{\top}\hat{A}e_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}\hat{A}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\Delta}\left(x^{\top}Ae_{j}+\sum_{i:x_{i}>0}x_{i}\Delta_{i}\right).

(Definition of
\hat{A}
)

Now, let us focus in detail on the term

\displaystyle\sum_{i:x_{i}>0}x_{i}\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:x_{i}>0}x_{i}\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}(A_{kj}-A_{ij})

(Definition of
\Delta_{i}
and
k\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{\star\in\textup{supp}(x)}\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{\star,j}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}\left(A_{kj}-\sum_{i:x_{i}>0}x_{i}A_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}\left(A_{kj}-x^{\top}Ae_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{kj}-\sum_{j:y_{j}>0}\hat{y}_{j}(x^{\top}Ae_{j})

(
\textup{supp}(y)=\textup{supp}(\hat{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[n]}\hat{y}_{j}A_{kj}-x^{\top}Ay

(
Equation

41
)

Hence, we obtained that, for all

\displaystyle x^{\top}\hat{A}e_{j}=\frac{1}{1+\Delta}\left(x^{\top}Ae_{j}-x^{\top}Ay+\max_{k\in\textup{supp}(x)}e_{k}^{\top}A\hat{y}\right)

(43)

We are now ready to conclude the proof. Consider

Lemma C.3

(Matrix Construction
\to

.

\alpha>0

Lemma D.1

Lemma D.9
.

Lemma D.10

(General Decomposition Error)
.

Let
(\mathcal{Y},d)
be a metric space and let
\mathcal{X}
be any set.
Consider a compact-valued correspondence
F:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\vbox{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\varrightarrow$\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\varrightarrow$\cr}}}}}{\mathrel{\vbox{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\varrightarrow$\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\varrightarrow$\cr}}}}}{\mathrel{\vbox{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\varrightarrow$\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\varrightarrow$\cr}}}}}{\mathrel{\vbox{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\varrightarrow$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\varrightarrow$\cr}}}}}\mathcal{Y}
, and let
x_{1},x_{2},\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
. It holds that:

\displaystyle H_{d}(F(x_{1},x_{2}),F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2}))\leq H_{d}(F(x_{1},x_{2}),F(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2}))+H_{d}(F(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2}),F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})).

Proof.

For any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, let
c(y)\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{c\in F(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2})}d(y,c)
. Note that, by Heine-Cantor, this minimum is attained since
d(y,\cdot)
is a continuous function and
F
is compact-valued. Then, recall that:

\displaystyle H_{d}(F(x_{1},x_{2}),F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\sup_{y\in F(x_{1},x_{2})}\inf_{\hat{y}\in F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})}d(y,\hat{y}),\sup_{\hat{y}\in F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})}\inf_{y\in F(x_{1},x_{2})}d(y,\hat{y})\right\}

(70)

Now, focus on the first term. We have that:

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in F(x_{1},x_{2})}\inf_{\hat{y}\in F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})}d(y,\hat{y})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in F(x_{1},x_{2})}\inf_{\hat{y}\in F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})}\left(d(y,c(y))+d(c(y),\hat{y})\right)

(Triangular inequality)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in F(x_{1},x_{2})}\left(d(y,c(y))+\inf_{\hat{y}\in F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})}d(c(y),\hat{y})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in F(x_{1},x_{2})}d(y,c(y))+\sup_{y\in F(x_{1},x_{2})}\inf_{\hat{y}\in F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})}d(c(y),\hat{y})

\displaystyle\leq H_{d}(F(x_{1},x_{2}),F(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2}))+\sup_{y\in F(x_{1},x_{2})}\inf_{\hat{y}\in F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})}d(c(y),\hat{y})

(Def. of
c(y)
)

\displaystyle\leq H_{d}(F(x_{1},x_{2}),F(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2}))+H_{d}(F(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2}),F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2}))

(
c(y)\in F(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2})
)

Similarly, by using the triangular inequality with
c(\hat{y})
, one has that:

\displaystyle\sup_{\hat{y}\in F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})}\inf_{y\in F(x_{1},x_{2})}d(y,\hat{y})\leq H_{d}(F(x_{1},x_{2}),F(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2}))+H_{d}(F(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2}),F(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2})).

Plugging these results within
Equation

70
, concludes the proof.
