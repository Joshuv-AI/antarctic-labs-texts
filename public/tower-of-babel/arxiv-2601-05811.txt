Learning Reconstructive Embeddings in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces via the Representer Theorem Thanks: This work has been submitted to the IEEE for possible publication
Enrique Feito-Casares, Francisco M. Melgarejo-Meseguer, José-Luis Rojo-Álvarez

Abstract
Motivated by the growing interest in representation learning approaches that uncover the latent structure of high-dimensional data, this work proposes new algorithms for reconstruction-based manifold learning within Reproducing-Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS). Each observation is first reconstructed as a linear combination of the other samples in the RKHS, by optimizing a vector form of the Representer Theorem for their autorepresentation property. A separable operator-valued kernel extends the formulation to vector-valued data while retaining the simplicity of a single scalar similarity function. A subsequent kernel-alignment task projects the data into a lower-dimensional latent space whose Gram matrix aims to match the high-dimensional reconstruction kernel, thus transferring the auto-reconstruction geometry of the RKHS to the embedding. Therefore, the proposed algorithms represent an extended approach to the autorepresentation property, exhibited by many natural data, by using and adapting well-known results of Kernel Learning Theory. Numerical experiments on both simulated (concentric circles and swiss-roll) and real (cancer molecular activity and IoT network intrusions) datasets provide empirical evidence of the practical effectiveness of the proposed approach.

Introduction

As experienced by any practitioner, data representation is critical to the application of
machine learning (ML)
, whatever the targeted task, supervised or unsupervised. In this sense, recent efforts have focused their attention on
representation learning (RL)
as an effective way to structure knowledge, enabling models to learn latent representations that capture the underlying patterns in the data and facilitate generalization. This trend has gained momentum with the rise of deep learning and unsupervised feature learning, where representations learned directly from raw data have demonstrated superior performance across a wide range of tasks
(
2

. Among the various approaches for generating representations,
manifold learning (MnL)
has attracted increasing attention as a powerful approach to
RL
for producing compact, low-dimensional encodings of high-dimensional inputs.
MnL
relies on the assumption that data lie on a lower-dimensional manifold embedded within a higher-dimensional space, and aims to uncover this latent structure by preserving the intrinsic geometry of the data. This approach provides meaningful representations that can be used to prepare data for downstream machine learning tasks.

Recent studies have highlighted the impact of incorporating
MnL
techniques into both
ML
and
deep learning (DL)
pipelines to address a variety of domain-specific challenges. In biomedical applications,
MnL
has been used for stress detection
(
3
)
, electrocardiographic signal assessment
(
29
)
, and ambient health monitoring in smart homes
(
22
)
. In engineering domains, it supports structural damage detection
(
6
)
, aerodynamic design optimization
(
20
)
, and cybersecurity in critical systems
(
8
)
. Moreover, the widespread use of
MnL
for data visualization has been well documented, with techniques such as Student’s t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)
(
34
)
and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)
(
21
)
facilitating the exploration and interpretation of data structures in lower-dimensional spaces
(
1
;
19
)
.

Whether previous
MnL
techniques tend to preserve distances, neighbourhood probabilities, and local linearity, the present work instead centers dimensionality reduction on the reconstruction geometry within a
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
, so the resulting embedding is shaped by data samples reconstruction ability rather than distance alone. To make this idea work for vector-valued data, the study introduces a separable operator-valued kernel that applies a single scalar kernel function uniformly across every output dimension. Finally, a kernel-alignment objective ties the high-dimensional reconstruction relationships to the low-dimensional embedding, ensuring that the learned latent space mirrors the structure uncovered in the original feature space. The contributions of the present work can be summarized as follows: (i) a new autoreconstructive criterion for manifold learning in RKHS, and (ii) an embedding mechanism that aligns reconstruction geometry with latent similarity. Both contributions are supported by numerical experiments on various datasets, including kernelized input data.

Related Work

In the context of the present proposal, several related works share a similar conceptual approach, particularly concerning dimensionality reduction tools and, more specifically, the use of Kernel Theory.

Dimensionality reduction techniques for high-dimensional data can be broadly classified into three paradigms, namely, spectral methods, probabilistic methods, and neural network-based methods
(
11
)
. Each paradigm provides valuable insights into the latent structure of data, but also presents limitations that motivate our approach. Spectral methods, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
(
15
)
, Fisher Discriminant Analysis (FDA)
(
13
)
, and their kernelized variants (e.g., KPCA
(
31

and KFDA
(

(
28
)
, SNE
(
14
)
, and UMAP
(
21
)
provide uncertainty quantification and capture complex nonlinear relationships. However, these methods are limited by intractable inference problems and restrictive noise assumptions and rarely yield explicit mappings. Neural network-based techniques, particularly Autoencoders (AEs), have proven effective in learning rich, nonlinear representations. However, while they can achieve state-of-the-art performance, their latent representations often lack transparency, making them true black-box models. In line with the proposed approach, recent attempts have focused on kernelizing autoencoder criteria, initially in shallow autoencoders
(
12
)
, and then extending this theoretical analysis to learning low-dimensional latent spaces in vector-valued
RKHS
. This generalizes the kernelization criterion of autoencoders to an arbitrary number of layers, using a reconstruction criterion defined on the input data space, in which the dimensionality reduction occurs at the bottleneck defined by the neural network
(
16
;
17
)
.

The criterion for reconstructing data samples based on neighbors was originally proposed under the Local Linear Embedding (LLE) algorithm
(
27
)
. Subsequent efforts led to the development of a kernelized variant (KLLE)
(
37
)
, which performs reconstruction in
RKHS
. While this method shares similarities with our approach, here instead, is considered self-reconstruction for the embedded data points, thus also focusing on the input space reconstruction simultaneously.

Kernel Dimensionality Reduction (KDR)
(
10
)
is a family of methods within the Sufficient Dimensionality Reduction (SDR) paradigm
(
18
)
. Despite the similarity in name, KDR is primarily a supervised statistical technique designed to reduce the covariance of data in the
RKHS
and project it into a lower-dimensional space. An unsupervised variant has also been proposed
(
36
)
; however, our methodology differs fundamentally from all existing variants of KDR.

Background on Dimensionality Reduction and Kernel Methods

In this section, we first outline the theoretical foundations of dimensionality reduction, introducing the manifold hypothesis as the central motivation for many nonlinear approaches. We then present the basic concepts of kernel methods, including kernel functions,
Postitive Semidefinite (PSD)
kernels, and feature maps, before extending them to operator-valued kernels. Building on these notions, we formalize the framework of
RKHS
in both scalar and vector-valued settings, together with their associated norms and representer theorems. Furthermore, an experienced practitioner with kernel methods can choose to skip this section or complement it with general references on the subject
(
30
;
26
)
.

Within the dimensionality reduction methodology, it is assumed that each feature of data points does not carry the same amount of information, meaning that the
D
-dimensional data points do not cover the entire
D
-dimensional Euclidean space (
\mathbb{R}^{D}
)
(
11
)
. Whether this hypothesis is true must be proven; however, since data usually comes from physical measurements and underlying elementary explanations might be available, this statement is often formalized under the manifold hypothesis, which motivates learning algorithms that exploit this latent structure.

Definition 1

(Manifold Hypothesis
(
7
)
)
.

The manifold hypothesis asserts a set of high-dimensional data
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
can lie approximately on a smooth, low-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
of intrinsic dimension
d\ll D
.

A majority of the introduced
MnL
algorithms specifically aim to identify non-linear manifolds within the data. In this context, kernel methods
(
30
)
could provide a valuable complement, offering a principled mathematical framework for learning nonlinear relationships by embedding data into high-dimensional or even infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. This methodological framework relies on the concept of kernel function, which can be informally understood as a similarity measure between pairs of inputs. Formally, a kernel function is defined as follows:

Definition 2

(Kernel Function)
.

A kernel function is a function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
that quantifies similarity between elements
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. In the context of machine learning,
\mathcal{X}
is typically a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
expresses how closely related
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
are, according to some fixed notion of similarity.

Not all kernel functions are suitable for constructing inner product spaces. For this, we require additional structure in terms of symmetry and
PSD
. These properties ensure that a given kernel corresponds to an inner product in a Hilbert space.

Definition 3

(Positive Semi-Definite Kernel)
.

A kernel function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
PSD
if it is symmetric (i.e.,
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=k(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x})
for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
), and for every finite subset
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
, the corresponding kernel matrix
\boldsymbol{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, defined by

\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}=k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),

(1)

is
PSD
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}\geq 0
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The abstraction provided by kernel functions when employed on a given dataset is materialized in matrix form as a kernel matrix (or Gram matrix) encoding inner products between mapped data points.
An alternative approach to
PSD
kernels is through a feature map, which expresses each kernel as an inner product in an associated Hilbert space.

Definition 4

(Feature Map)
.

Given a kernel function
k
, a feature map is a function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
into a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that

\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\langle\phi(\boldsymbol{x}),\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.

(2)

In the multi-output regression cases, where the relationship between inputs and outputs is more complex and better modeled through operators acting on a Hilbert space
(
23
)
. The kernel methodology paradigm is equivalently defined for vector-valued inputs and outputs.

Definition 5

(Operator-Valued Kernel Function)
.

An operator-valued kernel function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is a function that quantifies the similarity between elements
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
in terms of operators. Here,
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and the kernel function returns an operator
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, typically a bounded linear operator in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, which represents how closely related the inputs
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
are, based on a fixed notion of similarity.

Definition 6

(Operator-Valued Kernel Matrix)
.

Given a dataset
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
and an operator-valued kernel function
k
, the kernel matrix
\boldsymbol{K}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})^{n\times n}
is defined by
\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}=k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
.

Given the provided definitions, an RKHS formalizes the connection between kernels, feature maps, and function spaces and provides a rich and rigorous setting for both analysis and algorithm design. First, the classical scalar-valued case is presented before generalizing to vector-valued outputs.

Definition 7

(Scalar-Valued RKHS)
.

Let
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
PSD
kernel. The
RKHS

\mathcal{H}_{k}
associated with
k
is a Hilbert space of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

1.

For each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, the function
k(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)
belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.

2.

The reproducing property holds:

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\langle f,k(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{H}_{k},\;\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}.

(3)

In many applications, the learning task involves predicting structured or vector-valued outputs (e.g.,
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued responses). To accommodate these settings, the scalar RKHS framework must be extended to handle vector-valued functions. This leads to the theory of vector-valued RKHSs
(
32
)
.

Definition 8

(Vector-Valued RKHS)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a Hilbert space, typically
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. A vector-valued RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
is a Hilbert space of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
associated with an operator-valued kernel
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y})
, such that:

1.

For each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, the function
K(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)\boldsymbol{y}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{K}
.

2.

The reproducing property holds:

\langle f(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}=\langle f,K(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{H}_{K},\;\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}.

(4)

Both scalar and vector-valued RKHSs admit a natural norm structure that plays a critical role in regularization and model complexity control. These norms are derived from the inner product structure and, in practice, appear in closed form when functions are expressed in terms of kernel evaluations.

Definition 9

(RKHS Norms)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
be a scalar-valued function represented as

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R},

(5)

Then the RKHS norm is given by

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}).

(6)

In the vector-valued case, if
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x})\alpha_{i}
with
\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
, the norm in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
is given by

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle\alpha_{i},K(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\alpha_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}.

(7)

The following theorem states that the solution to a wide class of optimization problems in a scalar-valued RKHS lies in the finite-dimensional span of kernel evaluations at the training points. This result is the foundation of kernel methods in supervised learning.

Theorem 1

(Representer Theorem - Scalar-Valued Case)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be a scalar-valued RKHS with kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Given training data
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, the solution to the regularized empirical risk minimization problem

\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}(y_{i},f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))+\lambda\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}

(8)

admits a representation of the form

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

In the vector-valued setting, a similar result holds, where the coefficients become vectors and the scalar kernel is replaced by an operator-valued kernel. This generalization is critical for problems involving multi-output prediction and structured outputs.

Theorem 2

(Representer Theorem - Vector-Valued Case)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{K}
be a vector-valued RKHS with kernel
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y})
. Given training data
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, the solution to the regularized problem

\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))+\lambda\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}

(10)

admits a representation of the form

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\alpha_{i}},\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha_{i}}\in\mathcal{Y}.

(11)

To conclude, we highlight two widely used kernels: the linear and Gaussian kernels. These serve as canonical examples at opposite ends of the kernel spectrum.

Definition 10

(Linear Kernel)
.

The linear kernel is defined as

K(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}.

(12)

Definition 11

(Gaussian Kernel)
.

The Gaussian kernel (also known as the RBF kernel) is given by

K(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

(13)

where
\sigma>0
is a bandwidth parameter. This kernel defines an infinite-dimensional RKHS and is universal on compact domains.

4
Autoreconstructive Kernel Embedding

This section presents the proposed algorithmic framework for RKHS-based dimensionality reduction, referred to as Autoreconstructive Kernel Embedding (abbreviated as
KE
). The method proceeds in two stages: First, reconstruction weights are optimized to encode local geometry in the RKHS (A); Second, a low-dimensional embedding is learned by aligning its kernel structure with the original reconstruction kernel (B). Each step is supported by theoretical results, including a closed-form solution for the reconstruction coefficients and an alignment-based loss for the embedding.

4.1
Reconstruction Weight Learning

This phase estimates a coefficient vector that reconstructs each mapped data point in the
RKHS
as a weighted combination of its neighboring points. The coefficients are chosen to minimize the overall reconstruction error, thereby encoding the local geometric structure of the data in feature space.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a dataset consisting of
n
samples. Each data point
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is mapped into a
RKHS

\mathcal{H}
through a feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{H}
, associated with a PSD kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. The goal is to reconstruct each point
\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
in feature space using a linear combination of other points
\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
, without explicitly accessing
\phi
itself. The objective is to minimize the total reconstruction error in RKHS, which can be expressed as

\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-f_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

(14)

where
f_{\phi}
is the reconstruction function in the
RKHS
.
To characterize the solution to the minimization problem in Eq. (
14
), recall that the Representer Theorem constrains the form of the optimal reconstruction function in an RKHS. The following proposition states this result explicitly.

Proposition 3

(Kernel Expansion of the Reconstruction Function)
.

The reconstruction function
f_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
can be expressed as a kernel expansion in a vector-valued
RKHS
. By the Representer Theorem, the optimal function lies in the span of
\{\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
and is of the form:

f_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),

(15)

where
G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
is a kernel function, and the coefficients
\beta_{i}
are the learned reconstruction weights.
The kernel used in the reconstruction framework is assumed to be separable. Specifically, it can be written as the product of a scalar kernel function
G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
that measures the similarity between input points and the identity operator
I_{\mathcal{H}}
that acts on the vector-valued RKHS space. This separability allows the kernel to define pairwise similarities between input points while acting uniformly across all directions of the feature space. Formally, the kernel takes the form

K(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\cdot I_{\mathcal{H}},

(16)

where the scalar part
G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
captures the pairwise similarity between data points
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
, and this similarity is used to express relationships in the input space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the vector part, represented by the identity operator
I_{\mathcal{H}}
, ensures that the kernel operates uniformly across all dimensions of the RKHS.

Proof.

By the Representer Theorem, any solution to the regularized empirical risk minimization problem

\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\lambda\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}

(17)

must be of the form

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{i},

(18)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha_{i}}\in\mathcal{H}
. In this case, we use the separable kernel
K(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})I_{\mathcal{H}}
and represent the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha_{i}}
as
\boldsymbol{\alpha_{i}}=\beta_{i}\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
, yielding the kernel expansion:

f_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}).

(19)

Thus, the reconstruction function
f_{\phi}
lies in the span of
\{\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\}
, as required by the Representer Theorem.
∎

Given the proposed minimization problem in the RKHS, a central insight from Kernel Learning Theory is that it is unnecessary to work directly with the mapping functions
\phi(\cdot)
. This is because such problems can be expressed entirely in terms of inner products, which are already computed via the kernel matrix. The following propositions reformulate the optimization problem to derive a loss function expressed in terms of the kernel matrix.

Proposition 4

(Optimization of the reconstruction weights in an RKHS)
.

Fix a PSD kernel
G
with Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{G}=G(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
and separate its diagonal,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}\;=\;\boldsymbol{G}-\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{G}),\qquad(\;\tilde{G}_{ii}=0\text{ for every }i\;).

(20)

For a coefficient vector

\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})^{\!\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
put

\boldsymbol{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
and define the linear
reconstruction operator of
f_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})

\boldsymbol{F}\;=\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}\,\boldsymbol{D}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\;=\;\bigl[\,\sum_{j\neq 1}\beta_{j}G_{1j}\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),\;\dots\;,\sum_{j\neq n}\beta_{j}G_{nj}\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\bigr],

(21)

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=[\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\>,\>\dots\>,\>\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})]\in\mathcal{H}^{\,n}
. Then the empirical reconstruction error

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\;=\;\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}-\boldsymbol{F}\bigr\|_{F}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl\|\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\!\!\beta_{j}\,G(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\Bigr\|^{2}

(22)

admits the quadratic representation

\;\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\;=\;\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{A}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}\;-\;2\,\boldsymbol{c}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\;+\;\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{G})\;

(23)

with

\boldsymbol{A}\;=\;\boldsymbol{G}\circ\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}^{2}\bigr),\qquad c_{i}\;=\;\sum_{j\neq i}G_{ij}^{2}\;=\;(G\tilde{G})_{ii},

(24)

where “
\circ
” denotes the Hadamard (element-wise).

Proof.

Because
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\boldsymbol{G}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}
is symmetric,

\boldsymbol{\Phi}-\boldsymbol{F}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\Phi-\tilde{G}D\Phi}\;=\;\boldsymbol{(I-\tilde{G}D)\,\Phi},

(25)

and the Frobenius norm becomes

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\;=\;\bigl\|\boldsymbol{(I-\tilde{G}D)\Phi}\bigr\|_{F}^{2}\;=\;\operatorname{tr}\!\bigl[\boldsymbol{(I-\tilde{G}D)\,G\,(I-D\tilde{G})}\bigr].

(26)

Expanding the trace and using the cyclic property together with the
diagonality of
\boldsymbol{D}
, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{G})-2\,\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{G}\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}})^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sum_{j,\ell}\beta_{j}\beta_{\ell}G_{j\ell}\!(\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}^{2})_{j\ell}

(27)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{G})-2\,\boldsymbol{c}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\!\top}\!\bigl(\boldsymbol{G}\circ\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}^{2}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\beta},

which is Eq.(
23
) with the definitions in Eq.(
24
).
∎

Having expressed the
RKHS
reconstruction loss in quadratic form, the next step is to determine the coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
that minimizes this loss. The following proposition establishes the conditions under which a closed-form solution exists and provides the explicit expression for the minimizer.

Proposition 5

(Closed-Form Minimizer of the Reconstruction Loss)
.

If the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{G}\circ(\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}^{2})
is nonsingular, the minimizer of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is unique and given by

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}.

(28)

If
\boldsymbol{A}
is PSD but singular, the minimum-norm solution is

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{c},

(29)

where
†
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse.

Proof.

From the quadratic form in Proposition
23
, the gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}=2\boldsymbol{A}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}-2\boldsymbol{c}.

(30)

Setting this to zero yields the normal equations
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{c}
.
If
\boldsymbol{A}
is invertible, the solution is
\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}
and strict convexity ensures uniqueness.
If
\boldsymbol{A}
is singular but PSD, the set of minimizers is
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}\,|\,\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{c}\}
; the pseudoinverse yields the minimum-norm element in this set.
∎

The above propositions complete the first stage of the method by providing an explicit kernel-based formulation for computing the optimal reconstruction weights
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. These weights encode the local geometric relationships between points in the
RKHS
and serve as the foundation for the second stage, where is learned a low-dimensional embedding whose kernel structure aligns with the reconstruction geometry defined in this step.

4.2
Embedding via Kernel Alignment

With the reconstruction weights
\boldsymbol{\beta}
computed in the first step, this second step aims to learn a low-dimensional embedding that preserves the autoreconstructive geometry of the
RKHS
learned previously. The central idea is to construct an embedding whose induced kernel matrix matches, as closely as possible, the reconstruction kernel
\boldsymbol{G}_{R}
obtained from the high-dimensional feature space. The following proposition formalizes this alignment objective.

Proposition 6

(Kernelized Reconstruction Objective for the Embedding Step)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{G}_{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the reconstruction kernel from the RKHS stage, and let
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the fixed reconstruction weights with
D=\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
. For a target dimension
d
, parameterize the embedding as

\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{G}_{R}\,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},

(31)

with learnable
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{G}_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the kernel matrix computed from the embedded points
\boldsymbol{Z}
, and define

\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}_{H}\;=\;\boldsymbol{G}_{H}-\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{G}_{H}),

(32)

A(\boldsymbol{G}_{H})\;=\;\boldsymbol{G}_{H}\circ(\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}_{H}^{2}),

(33)

c(\boldsymbol{G}_{H})\;=\;\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{G}_{H}\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}_{H}),

(34)

where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product.
Then the latent RKHS reconstruction error induced by the operator

is

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\beta})\;=\;\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}A(\boldsymbol{G}_{H})\,\boldsymbol{\beta}\;-\;2\,c(\boldsymbol{G}_{H})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\;+\;\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{G}_{H}).

(35)

Minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
over
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
aligns the embedding-space kernel

with the original reconstruction kernel
\boldsymbol{G}_{R}
.

Although

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{G}_{R}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\boldsymbol{G}_{H}

Both phases share the same kernelized quadratic template in

4.3
Learning Algorithm

With the previous propositions, the proposed algorithm can be summarized as a two–phase procedure that progressively transfers autoreconstructive geometry from the high–dimensional
RKHS
to a low-dimensional embedding.

1.

Reconstruction Weight Learning. In the first phase, we determine the reconstruction weights
\boldsymbol{\beta}
that best express each feature–space point
\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
as a linear combination of its neighbors. This is formulated as

\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\beta_{j}\,G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},

(36)

where
G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
is the reconstruction kernel, i.e., the Gram matrix of the original data in the RKHS with its diagonal removed. This objective reduces to a convex quadratic form in
\beta
with closed–form solution.

2.

Learning Low–Dimensional Embeddings.
With
\boldsymbol{\beta}
fixed, we seek a latent projection
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
such that the embedding

\boldsymbol{Z}\;=\;\boldsymbol{G}_{R}\,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

(37)

induces a latent kernel
\boldsymbol{G}_{H}
that replicates the same reconstruction pattern as in previous step. Since
\boldsymbol{G}_{H}
depends nonlinearly on
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
through both
\boldsymbol{Z}
and the kernel map, no closed–form solution exists, and gradient–based optimization is used to minimized iteratively.

Together, these two phases constitute the Autoreconstructive Kernel Embedding procedure: local reconstruction relations are first encoded in the RKHS and then consistently transferred to the latent space through a kernel alignment objective

4.4
Out–of–Sample Embedding

Once the low-dimensional mapping
\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{G}_{R}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is learned on the training data, we extend the embedding to unseen test points using Nystrom approximation. Let
\mathcal{X}^{\star}=\{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a test dataset consisting of
m
samples and
\boldsymbol{G}_{R}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
denote the reconstruction kernel between test and training points, computed as

\boldsymbol{G}_{R}^{\star}=\begin{bmatrix}k(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{1})&\cdots&k(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
k(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{m},\boldsymbol{x}_{1})&\cdots&k(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{m},\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\end{bmatrix}

(38)

Given the learned projection matrix
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, a batch of new points is embedded through the kernel similarity matrix

\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{G}_{R}^{\star}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}.

(39)

This formulation respects the same RKHS-induced geometry learned during training and provides a principled way to embed new samples without retraining, maintaining consistency with the autoreconstructive structure.

The Swiss roll dataset is a canonical three-dimensional manifold with intrinsic curvature, commonly used to evaluate the ability of dimensionality reduction algorithms to preserve global geometry and nonlinear structure. The usual MnL goal is to project the curled 3D surface into a two-dimensional space while maintaining the relative ordering of points along the manifold.

IoT Network Intrusions

This last set of experiments was conducted on the CICIoT2023 dataset, a comprehensive real-world collection of IoT network traffic data containing both benign and malicious activities across multiple attack categories. The dataset encompasses diverse cyber threats, including DDoS attacks, DoS attacks, spoofing techniques, reconnaissance activities, web-based attacks, Mirai botnet variants, and brute force attempts

Given the heterogeneous nature of network traffic features and the multi-class classification challenge in cybersecurity, a custom multi-domain kernel was designed to capture domain-specific similarities across statistical properties, protocol behaviors, traffic patterns, and geometric flow characteristics, providing a more nuanced representation for IoT network intrusion detection. A key strength of our RKHS-based approach lies in its flexibility to incorporate domain knowledge directly into the kernel desing. To assess performance, we trained both UMAP and our
KE
model using only benign traffic. The goal is to evaluate the generalization ability to unseen attacks without any exposure to malicious labels. As shown in Figure

These numerical experiments support the effectiveness of the proposed autoreconstructive kernel embedding method across synthetic and real-world datasets. On tasks like concentric circles and Swiss roll, it outperforms Kernel PCA and UMAP in both clustering quality and embedding fidelity. These results highlight its capacity to preserve nonlinear structures under strong compression. In the molecular classification task, the method achieves competitive accuracy and ROC AUC while producing compact, informative embeddings. Overall, the approach offers a principled solution for structure-preserving dimensionality reduction, suitable for both visualization and downstream analysis.

6
Discussion and Conclussion

The proposed Autoreconstructive Kernel Embedding framework introduces a principled reconstruction-driven perspective for learning low-dimensional embeddings that retain the self-reconstructive geometry of high-dimensional data. A key strength of the method lies in its modular two-step design, which decouples reconstruction from embedding in a principled approach based on the Representer Theorem and the autorepresentation property as novelties. This allows the preservation of nonlinear similarity structures while yielding embeddings that are robust and effective for downstream tasks such as clustering and classification. The method generalizes well across domains with structured, high-dimensional, and possibly sparse data provided that an appropriate similarity kernel is available.

Nevertheless, several limitations remain. First, the method lacks an explicit inverse mapping from the embedding space to the input domain, which complicates generative applications. Second, out-of-sample extension requires additional approximation schemes, as the embedding is defined only for training samples. Finally, although our formulation is unsupervised, incorporating supervision example, via label-informed reconstruction weights or alignment penalties-could enhance its applicability to discriminative tasks.

Future work will also address scalability to larger datasets through sparse kernel approximations and explore supervised extensions and online versions of the algorithm. We also envision applications in areas such as anomaly detection, drug discovery, and explainable AI.
