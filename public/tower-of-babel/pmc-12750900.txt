Overexpression of Hspa1b in the mouse hippocampus may be associated with major depressive disorder
Authors: Xiaofeng Zhao, Yanan Shang, Wenli Zhu, Nicha Wareesawetsuwan, Lili Zhu, Chunjing Li, Jiarui Chen, Yuxiao Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Heat Shock Protein Family A Member 1B (Hspa1b) is an RNA binding protein that regulates transcriptional and post transcriptional processes. Previous studies have suggested its protective role in stress adaptation and post injury depression, as well as its potential therapeutic effects following antidepressant treatment in major depressive disorder (MDD). However, its direct involvement in MDD remains unclear. In this study, a mouse model with hippocampal Hspa1b overexpression was established. Integrated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (RIP seq) and RNA sequencing (RNA seq) were performed to investigate Hspa1b mediated transcriptional regulation and alternative splicing. Overexpression of Hspa1b resulted in 401 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including downregulation of several neuroinflammatory genes such as Lcn2, Ccl5, and Cd52, upregulation of oxytocin/neurophysin I prepropeptide (Oxt), and downregulation of intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (Icam1), which are all associated with depression pathogenesis. In addition, 1,397 significantly altered Hspa1b regulated alternative splicing events were identified. RT qPCR confirmed splicing changes in six genes, including Spata13 and Ptpro, among others linked to depression and neuronal functions. These findings demonstrate that hippocampal Hspa1b overexpression is associated with transcriptional and splicing alterations in genes related to immune, neuronal, and HPA axis pathways, which are key mechanisms implicated in MDD. Based on these alterations, Hspa1b may act as a double-edged regulator in MDD, warranting further investigation of its causal role and therapeutic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12993-025-00309-7.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common mental illness and one of the leading causes of disability worldwide [1]. Both genetic predisposition and exposure to stressful life events play significant roles in its development. Chronic emotional stress has been linked to activation of innate immune responses, thereby increasing vulnerability to MDD [2]. Neuroimaging and neurobiological studies have shown that MDD involves functional disturbances in brain circuits such as the affective-salience and cognitive control networks, along with structural changes, most notably in the hippocampus [3, 4].

Accumulating evidence suggests that immune system activation is a key component of MDD pathophysiology. Findings include the elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and their receptors, acute-phase reactants, chemokines, and soluble adhesion molecules in both peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid [5]. Three meta-analyses consistently confirmed that immune dysregulation increases susceptibility to MDD [6–8]. Nonetheless, no single mechanism sufficiently explains the complex and heterogenous of the disease.

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are major stress inducible proteins that perform essential protective functions. They repair or remove defective proteins, prevent the aggregation of dysfunctional proteins, inhibit apoptosis, and protect the cytoskeleton. Notably, HSPs modulate immune responses and facilitate the recognition of antigenic peptides by the immune system [9]. HSPs function as fundamental regulatory elements that interact with a wide range of proteins, including kinases, transcription factors, and other protein types, thereby influencing key processes such as protein homeostasis, cell growth, cell division, apoptosis, development, and immune defense against pathogens [10].

The HSP subfamily Hsp70 plays an important role in protecting the central nervous system (CNS) against stress related damage, particularly during embryonic development [11]. Three major Hsp70 genes, Hspa1a, Hspa1b, and Hspa1l, are located on chromosome 6p21.3 of the human genome and have been linked to the development of psychiatric disorders such as bipolar disorder [12], schizophrenia [13, 14], and anxiety disorder [15]. In addition, genetic variants in Hsp70 genes may influence therapeutic response to antidepressant in MDD [16]. Transcriptome analyses have shown that Hspa1b expression is reduced in depression, supporting its potential role in the pathophysiology of MDD [17].

Our study aimed to investigate the biological function of Hspa1b in relation to MDD. We hypothesized that Hspa1b contributes to MDD through its RNA binding capacity, which regulates gene expression and alternative splicing of genes associated with the disorder. To explore this, we established a mouse model with hippocampal overexpression of Hspa1b and performed integrated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing and RNA sequencing to analyze differential gene expression and alternative splicing events in genes previously linked to MDD.

Materials and methods

Construction of an Hspa1b lentiviral expression vector

A mouse Hspa1b lentivirus was produced by GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The lentiviral expression vector pGLV3-H1-GFP-Puro-Hspa1b was constructed to stably express Hspa1b. After DNA sequencing, lentiviral vector DNAs and packaging vectors were transfected into 293T cells. Vectors particles were collected 48 h post-transfection, centrifuged for 10 min to remove debris, pooled, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and stored at 4 °C until further use.

Tissue collection

Inbred C57BL/6J male mice aged 8–12 weeks, weighing 25–30 g, were obtained from Shanghai Slaccas Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The mice were randomly divided into three groups, two of which were treatment groups, and one served as a control group. Group 1 mice received a Lenti-Hspa1b injection via the tail vein (5 × 106 IU/mouse) (n = 5). Group 2 mice were injected with an equal amount of Lenti-Control via the tail vein (n = 5). Group 3 received no injection and served as a blank control (n = 5). The mice were euthanized with carbon dioxide following the injection, and hippocampal tissues were collected and stored at -80 ℃.

RIP-sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Mouse hippocampal tissues were dissected and homogenized (within 20 min) in ice-cold lysis buffer (10 mM HEPES (pH 7.3), 100 mM KCl, 0.5% NP-40, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM dithiothreitol, protease inhibitors, recombinant RNase inhibitors, 1 mM PMSF) using TissueLyser LT (50 Hz, 2 min). Cell lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by the addition of RQ I (Promega) to a final concentration of 1 U/µl. Next, the lysates were incubated in a water bath for 30 min at 37 °C and then cooled down on ice for 5 min.

For immunoprecipitation, the supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 13 µg of an Hspa1b antibody (Proteintech: 10995-1-AP) and a control IgG-antibody (CST:2729s). Two immunoprecipitation replicates were generated and further incubated with protein A/G Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h at 4 °C. After applying the samples to a magnet and removing the supernatants, the beads were washed sequentially twice with lysis buffer, high-salt buffer (250 mM Tris 7.4, 750 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% NP-40, and 0.5 deoxycholate), and PNK buffer (50 mM Tris, 20 mM EGTA, and 0.5% NP-40).

The beads were eluted in elution buffer (50 nM Tris 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, and 1% SDS). The suspension was incubated for 20 min in a heat block at 70 °C to release the immunoprecipitated RBPs with crosslinked RNA, and vortexed. The magnetic beads were removed on a separator, and the supernatant was transferred to a clean 1.5 ml microfuge tube.

Proteinase K (Roche) was added into the 1% input (without immunoprecipitation), and RBP with crosslinked RNA was immunoprecipitated with a final concentration of 1.2 mg/ml, then incubated for 120 min at 55 °C. RNA was purified using Trizol reagent (Life Technologies) and used to generate a paired-end sequencing library, which was further applied to the Illumina NovaSeq system for 150 nt paired-end sequencing.

Read alignment

Read alignment to the mouse genome was performed with TopHat2 [18], and only uniquely mapped reads were used in the subsequent analysis. The “ABLIRC” strategy was used to identify the binding regions of Hspa1b in the genome as previously described [19]. Reads with at least 1 bp overlap were clustered as peaks.

For each gene, computational simulation was used to randomly generate reads with the same numbers and lengths as reads in the peaks. The output reads were further mapped to the same genes to generate random maximum peak heights from overlapping reads. The whole process was repeated 500 times. All observed peaks higher than the random maximum peaks (P < 0.05) were selected. The immunoprecipitated and input samples were independently analyzed by simulation, and the immunoprecipitated peaks that overlapped with the input peaks were removed. The target genes of the immune precipitates were finally determined by the peaks, and the binding motifs of the immunoprecipitated protein were called using the HOMER software [20].

To categorize functional significance of peak-associated genes (target genes), Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways were identified using the KOBAS 2.0 server [21]. The hypergeometric test and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR controlling procedure were used to define the enrichment of each term.

RIP-qPCR

TRIzol (Invitrogen) was used to extract total RNA from the Hspa1b immunoprecipitated, following the manufacturer’s instructions. A random primer was used for cDNA synthesis. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) data represent the mean values from at least three independent experiments. Candidate genes were selected for PCR amplification in both Hspa1b and IgG immunoprecipitates.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from mouse hippocampus using TRIZOL (Ambion) and further treated with RQ1 DNase (Promega) to remove DNA. The quality and quantity of the purified RNA were determined by measuring the absorbance at 260 nm/280 nm (A260/A280) using SmartSpec Plus (BioRad, US). RNA integrity was further verified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis (Figure S1).

For each sample, 10 µg of total RNA were used for RNA-seq library preparation with NEBNext® Ultra™ Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (#E7420). Polyadenylated mRNAs were purified and fragmented and then converted into double-stranded cDNA. Following end-repair and A-tailing, the DNAs were ligated to the diluted NEBNext® Adaptor. After purification, ligation products corresponding to 300–500 bps were digested with heat-labile UDG, and single-stranded cDNA was amplified, purified, quantified, and stored at -80 °C until sequencing.

For high-throughput sequencing, the libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions and applied to the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system for 150 nt paired-end sequencing.

RNA-Seq Raw data clean and alignment

First, raw reads containing more than two N-bases were discarded. Then, adaptors and low-quality bases were trimmed from raw sequencing reads using the FASTX-Toolkit (Version 0.0.13). Reads shorter than 16 nt were also removed. Clean reads were aligned to the GRch38 genome using TopHat2 [18], allowing up to four mismatches. Uniquely mapped reads were used for gene read counting and fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM) calculations [22].

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis

To identify DEGs between the groups, the R Bioconductor package edgeR [23] was used. Differential expression was determined based on fold change (≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5) and a false discovery rate (FDR < 0.05). GO and enriched KEGG pathway analyses were performed using the KOBAS 2.0 server [21]. The Hypergeometric test and the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR controlling procedure were applied to define the enrichment of each pathway (corrected P < 0.05).

Alternative splicing analysis

Alternative splicing events (ASEs) and regulated alternative splicing events (RASEs) between the samples were defined and quantified using the ABLas pipeline as described previously [19, 24]. Briefly, the ABLas detects 10 types of ASEs based on splice junction reads, including exon skipping (ES), alternative 5’ splice sites (A5SSs), alternative 3’ splice sites (A3SSs), intron retention (IR), mutually exclusive exons (MXEs), mutually exclusive 5’ UTRs (5pMXEs), mutually exclusive 3’ UTRs (3pMXEs), cassette exons, A3SS&ESs, and A5SS&ESs.

To assess RBP-regulated ASEs, Student’s t-test was performed to evaluate the significance of the ratio alteration of ASEs. Events that were significant at a P-value cut-off corresponding to an FDR cut-off of 5% were considered RBP-regulated ASEs.

Western blotting analysis

Protein samples were loaded onto 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels based on their molecular weights and subsequently transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes. The PVDF membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk in a buffer containing 10 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20 for 1 h [25].

Following this, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, and later incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were visualized through chemiluminescence. Western blot band quantification was performed using ImageJ software. Antibodies used for Hspa1b were sourced commercially from Affinity Biosciences Company (AF5466).

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry of overexpressed Hspa1b in hippocampal tissue

First, paraffin-embedded hippocampal sections were deparaffinized using a series of xylene and ethanol concentration gradients, followed by rinsing three times with PBS for 2 min each. Excess PBS was removed using filter paper. To block non-specific binding, the sections were incubated with serum in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 60 min.

For immunostaining [26], sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Hspa1b (1:50, Affinity, catalog number: AF5466), which had been previously titrated to determine the optimal concentration. After incubation, the sections were washed five times with PBS for 3 min each.

Next, sections were incubated with a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody, fluorescent rabbit anti-goat IgG (H + L) (1:5000, MDL, catalog number: MD912533), for 1 h in the dark, and then washed five times with PBS for 3 min each. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, catalog number: C1005) for 10 min in the dark, followed by three PBS washes for 5 min each. Finally, the sections were mounted with glycerol and examined under a fluorescence microscope.

For confocal microscopy, the stained sections were mounted with a fluorescence mounting medium (Vectashield with DAPI, Vector Laboratories, catalog number: H-1200). Confocal images were captured using a LEICA SP8 confocal microscope with appropriate filter settings for each fluorophore.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry [27], tissue Sect. (20 μm) were prepared as described above. After blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting the overexpressed Hspa1b (1:50, Affinity, catalog number: AF5466).

The following day, sections were washed with PBS and incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signal amplification was performed using the avidin-biotin complex (ABC) method. Peroxidase activity was visualized by incubating the sections with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogenic substrate. Tissue morphology was assessed by counterstaining the sections with hematoxylin.

Finally, stained sections were mounted with Permount (Thermo Fisher, catalog number: SP15-100) and imaged using a bright-field microscope (Olympus BX53).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, differences between groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test.

Results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All experiments were performed at least three times independently, and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Hspa1b-mRNA interaction map in the mouse hippocampus

To obtain the transcriptome-wide Hspa1b-mRNA interaction map in the mouse hippocampus, we examined the hippocampi of overexpressing Hspa1b and control mice using iRIP coupled with high-throughput sequencing. The overexpression of Hspa1b in the hippocampus was evaluated using Western blot analysis, immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry analysis (Figure S2-S4). Further confocal microscopy revealed that the subcellular localization of overexpressed Hspa1b is in the cytoplasm of hippocampal tissue (Figure S5).

After removing the low-quality reads and adaptor sequences, Hspa1b and IgG immunoprecipitates recovered a total of 55,303,866 and 33,066,108 reads for replicate 1, respectively, and 71,182,157 and 43,901,307 reads for replicate 2, respectively (Table S1). The heatmap of the four immunoprecipitates, based on their global transcription profiles, demonstrated the stability of the experimental protocol (Fig. 1A-B). Principal component analysis, using transcript expression values for input control and Hspa1b immunoprecipitated (IP) samples, was shown in Figure S6.

The clean reads were aligned to the mouse GRCm38 (version 6, Ensembl 81) genome, with 62.71%-65.84% of the reads uniquely aligned (Table S2). Scatter plots depicting Pearson correlation between immunoprecipitated and input samples (Fig. 1C) indicate that Hspa1b exhibited low binding specificity in the mouse hippocampus. However, RIP-seq reads were slightly more enriched in intronic and intergenic regions compared to the input samples, as illustrated in the bar plots mapping iRIP-seq reads onto the mouse genome (Fig. 1D-E, Table S3).

We applied ABLife software to identify binding peaks in each experiment and subsequently performed motif analysis based on overlapping peaks. A total of 225 overlapping peaks were identified. The top 10 overrepresented motifs from the two immunoprecipitated replicates according to the HOMER analysis pipeline are presented in Fig. 1F. The identified peak genes were mapped to pathways in the GO and KEGG databases, resulting in 9 significantly enriched GO biological processes and 11 KEGG biochemical pathways. The top 10 pathways are shown in Figs. 1G, H, while detailed lists were presented in Table S4 and Table S5.

Hspa1b regulated the transcriptional levels of multiple Depression-Related genes

The potential function of Hspa1b in the mouse hippocampus was further investigated using transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) with five biological replicates. According to the FPKM values in the two experimental groups, Hspa1b expression was significantly increased in the overexpression group (Fig. 2A).

A two-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) and a heat map of 10 samples based on global transcription profiles are shown in Fig. 2B and C, illustrating the similarities between the biological samples. Among all expressed genes, we identified 69 upregulated and 332 downregulated genes (fold change > 2 or < 0.5, FDR P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D), indicating that Hspa1b broadly regulates gene transcription in the mouse hippocampus.

A KEGG enrichment analysis of up- and down-regulated DEGs identified 33 significantly enriched pathways, with the antigen processing and presentation pathway ranking first (corrected P < 0.05, Fig. 2E, Table S6).

We selected several DEGs for RT-PCR validation in hippocampal tissue, based on read depth ranking and a literature review (primer lists are presented in Table S7). The differential expression of all nine genes, including the immunoinflammation-related genes such as chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 5 (Ccl5) [28], cluster of differentiation 52 (Cd52) [29], C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (Cxcl10) [30], lipocalin-2 (Lcn2) [31, 32], interferon gamma-induced GTPase (Igtp) [33], interferon gamma-inducible protein 47 (Ifi47) [33], and IFI30 lysosomal thiol reductase (Ifi30) [33], as well as the depression-related genes such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (Icam1) [34] and oxytocin/neurophysin I prepropeptide (Oxt) [35–37], was validated in mouse hippocampal tissue following Hspa1b overexpression (Fig. 2F).

Hspa1b-Regulated alternative splicing events

Considering alternative splicing (AS) regulation, the uniquely mapped reads of each sample in the RNA-seq data were compared to the reference genome for alternative splicing analysis (Table S8). TopHat2 was applied for splice junction analysis, identifying 230,682 novel junctions and 181,888 known junction events across all samples. Furthermore, 1,397 significantly altered Hspa1b-RASEs were detected between the groups (P < 0.05).

The frequency distributions of different types of Hspa1b-RASEs are presented in Fig. 3A and Table S9. Intron A3SS&ES, A5SS, IntronR, and cassette exons were the main splicing events regulated by Hspa1b. Next, we performed functional clustering of genes associated with RASEs. These genes were primarily clustered in 23 significantly enriched GO biological processes (corrected P < 0.05, Fig. 3B, Table S10).

We conducted an overlap analysis between the DEGs from the overexpression RNA-seq analysis and genes associated with RASEs, identifying five overlapping genes (Fig. 3C). Eleven genes were selected for validation, and RT-qPCR assays were performed to confirm the Hspa1b-regulated RASEs, based on their P-value ranking, RASE ratio, and literature review. PCR primer pairs were designed to amplify the two different splicing isoforms (Model and AS) in these RASEs (Table S7).

Among the 11 tested events, the RT-qPCR results for six RASEs were consistent with the RNA-seq results. These events were located in spermatogenesis-associated 13 (Spata13), which has been associated with anorexia nervosa and comorbid depression in genome-wide analysis [38, 39] (Fig. 4A); the neurocognition-related gene protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type O (Ptpro) [40, 41] (Fig. 4B); nuclear mitotic apparatus protein 1 (Numa1) [42]; ring finger protein 146 (Rnf146) [43]; RNA binding fox-1 homolog 3 (Rbfox3) [44, 45]; and gephyrin (Gphn) [46, 47] (Figure S7).

Discussion

The mechanisms underlying depression have been widely studied, and in recent decades biological mechanisms have been recognized as important contributors to the pathophysiology of major depressive disorder [48, 49]. In this study, we showed that hippocampal overexpression of Hspa1b leads to broad RNA binding, particularly in intronic and intergenic regions, and is associated with marked changes in gene expression and alternative splicing. Many of these changes involve genes previously linked to MDD, suggesting that Hspa1b may regulate transcriptional and splicing processes relevant to disease pathophysiology.

The Hspa1b-mRNA interaction map showed low binding specificity, indicating widespread binding to RNAs, with enrichment in intronic and intergenic regions compared to controls. This finding is in line with Leone et al. [50], who reported that Hsp70 family proteins broadly bind RNA, including non-coding regions. Binding within introns suggests a role for Hspa1b in regulating alternative splicing, a mechanism implicated in depression through its role in dendritic spine dynamics and synaptic plasticity, as demonstrated in postmortem transcriptomic studies of MDD [51, 52]. Furthermore, enrichment analysis of Hspa1b-bound genes highlighted neuronal pathways, including synaptic transmission and glutamatergic synapse, consistent with evidence supporting neuroplasticity as a key mechanism in MDD [53–56]. Thus, Hspa1b may contribute to depression by influencing alternative splicing and gene expression in neuronal pathways, ultimately altering synaptic transmission and plasticity.

In the present study, transcriptome sequencing of the Hspa1b overexpression model identified 69 upregulated and 332 downregulated genes, confirming the broad regulatory effects of Hspa1b in the hippocampus. Notably, differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in immune and neuronal pathways. Immunoinflammatory genes were consistently downregulated in the overexpression model, suggesting a protective role of Hspa1b in depression pathogenesis. This interpretation is supported by evidence that upregulation of these genes in the hippocampus contributes to depression [57–59], whereas antidepressant treatment is associated with their downregulation [60, 61].

To further support our interpretation, several of the suppressed neuroinflammation-related genes have been previously implicated in depression. In a poststroke depression mouse model, Lcn2 was shown to promote hippocampal microglial activation and contribute to disease development [62]. Elevated Lcn2 expression in the central nervous system has also been linked to reduced neuroplasticity, thereby connecting inflammatory and neuroplastic mechanisms of depression [63–65]. In contrast, Ferreira et al. reported that Lcn2-null mice displayed depressive-like behaviors together with altered hippocampal cytoarchitecture and impaired synaptic plasticity [66, 67], suggesting that the role of Lcn2 in depression pathophysiology may be context-dependent. Beyond Lcn2, Ccl5 mRNA levels were found to decrease following antidepressant treatment in MDD patients [68], and Cd52 expression was reported to be upregulated in association with greater depressive symptom severity during pregnancy [69]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the neuroinflammatory mediators suppressed in our Hspa1b overexpression model are consistently implicated in the pathogenesis of depression.

Like neuroinflammation-related genes, Icam1, a cell surface adhesion glycoprotein, was found to be downregulated in the Hspa1b overexpression model. Higher serum levels of Icam1 have been correlated with depressive symptoms [70–72], and studies of CNS tissue have similarly reported significant increases in Icam1 in depression [73, 74]. On the other hand, Oxt was upregulated in our model, consistent with its known antidepressant effects [75, 76]. Oxytocin is an endogenous peptide that modulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and central immune responses and has been identified as a promising pathway in the pathophysiology of depression [77–80]. Together, these findings suggest a protective effect of Hspa1b in MDD by downregulating Icam1 and upregulating Oxt.

Additionally, Hspa1b not only altered gene expression but also regulated alternative splicing of genes implicated in MDD. We identified 1,397 significantly altered splicing events, among which six genes, including Spata13 and Ptpro, were validated. In a genome-wide association study of depression and alcohol dependence, a SNP in Spata13 ranked among the top markers associated with comorbid depression, suggesting that splicing alterations in Spata13 may plausibly contribute to disease mechanisms [39, 81–83]. While Ptpro has not been directly associated with depression, functional studies have shown that its overexpression in hippocampal neurons increases synaptic activity, whereas Ptpro knockdown reduces synaptic activity [84]. These findings imply a critical role of Ptpro in synaptogenic processes, linking Hspa1b-mediated splicing regulation to neuronal activity and potentially to the neuropathology of depression.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of Hspa1b overexpression in the mouse hippocampus at both molecular and transcriptomic levels. Our findings suggest that Hspa1b may exert a context-dependent dual role in the pathophysiology of MDD, acting as both a protective and pathogenic factor by broadly altering gene expression and alternative splicing of genes involved in immune regulation, neuronal signaling, and HPA axis pathways. Previous studies have reported mixed conclusions. In animal experiments, early-life stress induced persistent hippocampal overexpression of Hspa1b, which disrupted synaptic plasticity and increased susceptibility to depression-like behaviors [85, 86]. In clinical studies, serum HSP70 levels were significantly elevated in patients with MDD compared with healthy controls [87], and higher baseline serum HSP70 predicted the later development of MDD in longitudinal studies [88]. Conversely, Pae et al. [16] reported that polymorphisms in the HSP70 gene family were associated with reduced antidepressant efficacy in MDD, while transcriptomic analysis of postmortem frontal cortex revealed downregulation of Hspa1b in MDD compared with controls [17]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the role of Hspa1b in depression-related mechanisms is multifaceted and may vary depending on brain region, stress history, and disease stage.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our findings were derived from a mouse model with hippocampal overexpression of Hspa1b, which does not fully replicate the complex and heterogeneous nature of human MDD and may limit the translational relevance of the results. Second, although transcriptomic analysis revealed broad alterations in gene expression and splicing, functional validation at the protein and cellular levels remains necessary to establish causal mechanisms. Third, our study focused exclusively on the hippocampus, while other brain regions implicated in depression, such as the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, and thalamus, were not examined. Future studies integrating postmortem human brain data and behavioral assessments of depression-like phenotypes in experimental mice are needed to validate our findings. In addition, knockdown experiments targeting Hspa1b in the hippocampus are warranted to clarify its mechanistic role in depression-related molecular pathways.

Conclusions

Our preliminary animal-based study demonstrated that hippocampal overexpression of Hspa1b altered both gene expression and RNA splicing programs relevant to MDD. We identified 401 DEGs, including decreased expression of several neuroinflammatory genes, increased Oxt, and reduced Icam1, as well as 1,397 alternative splicing events with validated changes in Spata13, Ptpro, and others. These transcriptomic alterations were enriched in pathways related to immune regulation, neuronal signaling, and the HPA axis, all of which are central to MDD pathogenesis. Overall, our findings suggest that Hspa1b may serve as a double-edged regulator in MDD, exerting both protective and pathogenic effects, underscoring the need for further studies to clarify its causal role and therapeutic potential.
