Fenestrated endovascular aortic repair of a juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm in a patient with a destination left ventricular assist device
Authors: Mario Matabele, Rujul Kumar, Zion Rouege, Doran Mix, Karina Newhall, Grayson Pitcher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The population of patients on destination left ventricular assist device support is growing, with concomitant increases in cardiovascular comorbidities requiring intervention. We report a case of fenestrated endovascular aortic repair performed in such a patient for a 7.7-cm juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm. The aneurysm was repaired using a custom three-vessel fenestrated endograft with bilateral renal artery stenting, and the patient was discharged home on postoperative day 7. The physiology of rupture risk under continuous-flow hemodynamics and the relevant literature are reviewed. This case demonstrates that fenestrated endovascular aortic repair is technically feasible in destination left ventricular assist device patients with careful multidisciplinary planning.

The population of patients with left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) is growing, with approximately 3200 implants performed annually—nearly double the 2010 rate—and over 90% being HeartMate 3 (Abbott Laboratories) devices.1,2 Most are destination therapy, and 1-year survival has improved from 52% in 2009 to 83% in 2018, with 4-year survival increasingly reported.3 As these patients survive longer, noncardiac surgery (NCS) becomes increasingly necessary, reported in 1 in 6 LVAD patients.3 Advanced heart failure and abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) share risk factors, creating growing epidemiologic overlap. Fenestrated endovascular aortic repair (FEVAR) is the preferred approach for juxtarenal AAA at prohibitive open-repair risk.4 Standard infrarenal endovascular repair has been reported in LVAD patients, whereas FEVAR—which additionally requires precise target-vessel cannulation and fenestration alignment under continuous-flow hemodynamics with altered contrast dynamics and reduced angiographic conspicuity—has not.5, 6, 7 We report FEVAR in a patient with a destination continuous-flow LVAD.

Case presentation

A 77-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia underwent destination HeartMate 3 LVAD implantation in December 2021 following ST-elevation myocardial infarction with cardiogenic shock, having required Impella CP (Abiomed) support complicated by device thrombosis on the day of placement. The HeartMate 3 is an intrapericardial continuous-flow centrifugal pump with an inflow cannula in the left ventricular apex and an outflow graft to the ascending aorta. Contrast computed tomography on November 30, 2021, immediately before implantation, showed a 6.2-cm AAA; implantation proceeded emergently for cardiogenic shock, and aneurysm repair was not undertaken at that time.7 His post-LVAD course was marked by recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding from angiodysplasia requiring multiple transfusions and iron supplementation.

Surveillance computed tomography angiography (CTA) confirmed juxtarenal anatomy with a 4-mm infrarenal neck of 2.9-cm diameter and minimal angulation (10° suprarenal, 10° infrarenal), precluding standard infrarenal repair, and a maximum aneurysm diameter of 7.7 cm, grown from 6.2 cm over roughly 3 years (Fig 1). The superior mesenteric artery (SMA) measured 8 mm and both renal arteries 5.5 mm; bilateral primary renal arteries were patent, and bilateral lower-pole accessory renal arteries arose from the aneurysm sac. Moderate nonocclusive thrombus was present in the LVAD outflow graft. Operative technique

Preoperative interrogation showed stable device function (pulsatility index 3). Warfarin was held to a subtherapeutic international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.0 with a low-dose heparin bridge; baseline creatinine was 1.03 mg/dL. Two units of packed red cells were given preoperatively for acute-on-chronic anemia.

FEVAR was performed in a hybrid suite under general anesthesia with continuous LVAD monitoring by a dedicated perfusionist and transesophageal echocardiography. Bilateral femoral access was obtained percutaneously. Systemic heparin was given as a 7000-unit bolus then 1000 units/h, maintaining an activated clotting time above 250 seconds; platelet dysfunction was presumed from continuous-flow physiology rather than confirmed by assay, and desmopressin was given empirically. The three-vessel Zenith endograft was deployed under computed tomography fusion overlay. Fenestration sizes matched target-vessel diameter: 6 mm renal fenestrations for the 5.5-mm renal arteries, each stented with 6 × 22-mm iCAST covered stents (Atrium Medical), and a 10 × 10 mm SMA fenestration for the 8-mm vessel, left unstented. The device used the platform's three-fenestration maximum; the celiac axis was neither fenestrated nor scalloped and remained perfused through the open proximal fixation stent (Fig 2). The bilateral lower-pole accessory renal arteries were covered by the endograft. Postoperative course

Transient hypotension resolved with continuous-flow–appropriate mean arterial pressure targets, without escalating mechanical support. Renal function was preserved without clinical or radiographic evidence of infarction despite accessory renal coverage, and no perioperative LVAD complications occurred. A heparin infusion was titrated to an anti-factor Xa level of 0.2 to 0.5 units/mL; on postoperative day 4, it was stopped after the patient reported blood per rectum with straining, and after stable repeat hemoglobin, anticoagulation was transitioned to warfarin with an individualized INR target of 2.0 to 2.4. The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 7. Surveillance comprised CTA at 1, 9, and 18 months.

Discussion

We describe FEVAR in a destination LVAD patient, highlighting LVAD-specific challenges across operative technique, intraoperative hemodynamic management, perioperative anticoagulation, and postoperative surveillance.

Aortic remodeling and hemodynamic implications

The aorta’s Windkessel function—storing stroke volume in systole and releasing it in diastole through elastic recoil—is lost when pulsatile flow gives way to continuous flow, disrupting ventricular-arterial coupling and altering the wall. Patel et al8 showed that without pulsatile distention, the wall becomes stiffer and less distensible, changes not seen with pulsatile devices despite the HeartMate 3’s artificial pulse. Baroreceptor offloading from reduced pulse pressure raises sympathetic activity,9 activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and promoting hypertension—itself a risk factor for endoleak and sac destabilization.10 Rupture-risk data in LVAD patients are scarce: the only reported aortic rupture is a thoracic mycotic aneurysm 4 months after implantation,11 and no size criteria exist for this population. Whether the observed growth from 6.2 to 7.7 cm reflects continuous-flow physiology or natural history cannot be determined from a single case; the mechanisms above are plausible contributors, but no comparative growth-rate data exist.

Candidacy and decision-making

Repair was pursued only after serial surveillance. Anticipating slow growth, repair was initially deferred with a 70-mm threshold, given the elevated perioperative risk of LVAD dependence. The aneurysm crossed this threshold while enlarging faster than the expected 2 to 3 mm/y, both predictors of rupture; by conventional criteria, rupture risk at this diameter approaches 40% to 50%, though no data define rupture risk under continuous-flow physiology and whether reduced pulse pressure raises or lowers it is unknown—an uncertainty made explicit to the patient and family. Candidacy therefore weighed life expectancy against procedural risk. The patient had tolerated destination support for over 2 years without pump thrombosis or device exchange, and modern continuous-flow survival now extends several years; against this, his life expectancy was judged limited chiefly by the aneurysm and his transfusion-dependent hematologic disease. An estimated 30% to 40% perioperative complication risk was balanced against the higher rupture risk. After multidisciplinary review with vascular, cardiac-surgical, and cardiology colleagues and disclosure of the risks of access injury, bleeding, thromboembolism, colonic and renal ischemia, target-vessel injury, and death, the patient elected to proceed.

Technical and intraoperative considerations

LVAD physiology amplifies FEVAR’s technical demands. Preoperative contrast imaging must be timed carefully and may incompletely demonstrate vessel patency, and sluggish washout from laminar flow impairs real-time angiographic endoleak assessment. Continuous-flow support increases aortic wall stiffness and reduces distensibility; whether this affects endograft apposition or fenestration seal is unstudied, and we raise it as a hypothesis generated by this case rather than an established effect. Intraoperatively, a pulsatility index of three confirmed near-complete device dependence, making hemodynamic stability entirely reliant on LVAD function.

Postoperative surveillance

Surveillance after FEVAR is more demanding than after standard repair, requiring cross-sectional imaging to detect stent migration, component separation, and fenestration-gap changes that duplex alone cannot reliably identify.12,13 LVAD hardware and driveline artifact can further obscure fenestrated junctions and target-vessel stents, whereas contrast nephrotoxicity risk is elevated in the chronic low-flow state. CTA remains the primary modality with judicious contrast use, duplex is a useful adjunct for target-vessel patency and endoleak surveillance, and magnetic resonance angiography is precluded by the device’s magnetic resonance-unsafe labeling. A target-vessel occlusion would be managed individually—from surveillance of an asymptomatic, well-perfused occlusion to endovascular reintervention where feasible—with anticoagulation weighed against the patient’s bleeding risk.

Perioperative anticoagulation

Anticoagulation in LVAD patients undergoing NCS balances pump-thrombosis risk against procedural hemorrhage. Acquired von Willebrand syndrome and mandatory device anticoagulation raise bleeding risk, whereas interruption risks thrombosis—heightened here by the outflow-graft thrombus and transfusion-dependent gastrointestinal bleeding. Warfarin was held, and a heparin bridge maintained for titratable control. Emerging evidence supports subtherapeutic INRs during NCS, mitigating bleeding without significantly increasing thrombosis.14,15 At discharge, the INR target was individualized to 2.0 to 2.4, below the conventional 2.0 to 3.0, prioritizing hemorrhagic-risk reduction. A single self-limited episode of blood per rectum prompted heparin discontinuation and earlier warfarin transition without thrombotic or device-related complication, supporting an individualized, bleeding-weighted strategy and adding to the limited literature in this population.

Conclusions

As the LVAD population grows, surgeons will increasingly encounter complex aortic pathology requiring high-risk intervention. This case shows FEVAR to be a feasible, less invasive alternative to open repair in patients with significant cardiopulmonary disease. Successful management requires a multidisciplinary approach encompassing dedicated intraoperative perfusionist-directed device monitoring and hemodynamic management under continuous-flow physiology, individualized anticoagulation, and rigorous postoperative surveillance. Future studies should establish standardized long-term outcomes and management protocols for LVAD patients undergoing endovascular aortic repair.
