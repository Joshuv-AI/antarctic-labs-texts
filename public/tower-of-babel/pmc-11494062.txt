Blown sand environment characteristics and bridge sand hazard mechanisms of expressways in the South Tibet Valley
Authors: Shengbo Xie, Xian Zhang, Yingjun Pang, Keying Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
South Tibet expressways are built in the Yalu Tsangpo valley along the route to high-altitude and cold–temperature valleys, which are the main environmental characteristics of the region. Given that the blown sand environment and its potential harm are unclear, targeted prevention and control are not conducive, but its absence would result in severe wind–sand hazards on these expressways. In this research, the blown sand environment of the South Tibet Valley and the mechanism of sand damage to expressway bridges were studied via field observations, wind tunnel experiments and numerical simulations. The sand–moving wind is mainly west wind. The sand–moving wind frequency, sand drift potential, and maximum possible sand transport quantity are high in winter and spring (November–April) and low in summer and autumn (May–October). The sand drift direction is in the east direction. Expressway bridges have a considerable impact on the near–surface blown sand environment, forming a zone for increasing the wind speed between the top of the slope shoulder of the windward side of the bridge to the center of the bridge abutment, forming a zone for weakening the wind speed between the − 3 H distances of the upwind direction to the bottom of the slope middle of the windward side of the bridge, further forming a zone for weakening the wind speed centered in the slope shoulder of the leeward side of the bridge. The wind speed within a distance of 40 H downwind direction of the bridge generally did not recover. The wind–sand flow is partially blocked when it passes through the bridge and accumulates near the bridge, which cause harm. The impact of the bridge on the blown sand environment in the downwind is greater than that in the upwind. The results of this study can provide a reference for preventing and controlling wind–sand hazards on South Tibet expressways.

Introduction

The South Tibet expressways are constructed in the Yalu Tsangpo Valley and its tributaries, the Lhasa River and the Nianchu River, in the southern part of the Tibetan Plateau (Fig. 1), with high–altitude and cold–temperature valleys as the main environmental features along the route. At present, the expressways that have been built and opened to traffic in this region are as follows: Lhasa to the Konggar Airport Expressway, Quxu to the Naidong Expressway (numbered G4219 in China’s national expressway network), Lhasa to the Naqu Expressway (numbered G6 in China’s national expressway network), and Yaan to the Yecheng Expressway (numbered G4218 in China’s national expressway network) (Fig. 1). Influenced by the natural environment, such as arid and windy climates and sparse vegetation1–3, sand dunes/lands are widely spread along expressways, with abundant sources of sandy materials and strong wind–sand activities4–6. Owing to the unique blown sand environment with high–altitudes and cold–temperature valleys7–9, even small disturbances can change the material and energy balance of the ground surface. To a certain extent, expressway construction has inevitably destroyed the originally sparse vegetation and fragile ecology along the route10 and further exacerbated wind–sand activities near the surface. The expressway roadbed width is 2600 cm, and the pavement thickness is nearly three times greater than that of an ordinary expressway. The disturbance caused by the original blown sand environment after its construction is even stronger11,12, hindering the original wind–sand transport process on the ground surface and significantly altering the near–surface wind speed, wind–sand flow field, and conditions of transportation and accumulation13,14. These situations can exacerbate wind–sand hazards. According to preliminary investigations and statistics, South Tibet expressways have accumulated 53.9 km of blown sand sections, which are mainly distributed in the Shannan–wide valley section of the Yalu Tsangpo River (Fig. 1). These lines mainly include bridges, and a variety of sand dunes, such as bare flat sandy land, gobi, climbing dunes, and sand shadow dunes/falling dunes, are distributed on the surface along the line15–17. Given the insufficient understanding of the characteristics and laws of wind–sand movement near the surface in high–altitude and cold–temperature valleys and the impact of bridges on the blown sand environment, targeted prevention and control are not conducive, but their absence might result in severe wind–sand hazards on expressways (Fig. 2). This situation affects the smooth flow of expressways and threatens the safety of driving. This study was conducted to provide a reference for the prevention and control of wind–sand hazards on expressways.

Research methods

Field observation

Two field observation sites were selected in the sandy area along the expressway of the South Tibet Valley, marked as observation site 1 (Zhangda Village, Azha Township, Zhanang County, Shannan City) and observation site 2 (Jieqiong Village, Gadong Township, Bailang County, Shigatse City) (Fig. 1). The observation site 1 has the geographic coordinates of 29° 18′ 18″ N, 91° 18′ 31″ E and an altitude of 3581 m (Fig. 3). The observation site 2 has the geographic coordinates of 29° 15′ 06″ N, 89° 12′ 14″ E and an altitude of 3980 m (Fig. 3). The meteorological sensors were installed at a height of 2 m from the ground surface to record the wind speed, wind direction, temperature, and humidity every five minutes from May 2022 to April 2023. On the basis of the observed data, wind condition indicators, such as average wind speed, frequency and direction of sand–moving wind, wind rose, and sand–moving wind rose, were statistically analyzed. Then, wind–sand transport indicators, such as the sand drift potential (DP), resultant sand drift potential (RDP), resultant sand drift direction (RDD), maximum possible sand transport quantity (Q), resultant maximum possible sand transport quantity (RQ), and resultant angle of the maximum possible sand transport (RA), were calculated.

The maximum possible amount of sand transport was synthesized according to the vector synthesis rule to obtain RQ (kg m−1 a−1) and RA (°).

Finally, the wind–dynamic environment was explored, and the sand transport law was determined.

Wind tunnel experiment

According to China’s Technical Standard for Highway Engineering (JTG B01–2014), the minimum width of expressway pavement is 2600 cm, and the side slope ratio is 1:1.75 (the slope is 30°). The width of the bridge model used in this research was reduced and produced in accordance with the ratio of 1:100, the width of the bridge abutment was set to 26 cm, and the slopes of the two sides of the bridge were set to 30° (the side slope ratio remains unchanged) in accordance with the original ratio. The wind tunnel for the experiment was a direct current closed–blowing wind tunnel with a cross–section of 63 cm × 63 cm, the experimental section was 600 cm in length, and the boundary layer thickness was 12–15 cm. Therefore, the height of the bridge model was set to 8 cm which exactly within the boundary layer and meets the requirements of the model blown sand environment experiments conducted within the wind tunnel, the lengths of the bridge model was set to 62 cm (Fig. 4).

Fig. 4Expressway bridge model and experimental layout of the wind tunnel.

Expressway bridge model and experimental layout of the wind tunnel.

The wind speed observation positions used in the wind tunnel experiment are shown in Fig. 4. Ten observation positions were set up in the upwind direction of the bridge, and 12 observation positions were set up in the downwind direction. H represents the model height, − indicates the upwind direction, + indicates the downwind direction, −0 H indicates the slope foot of the windward side of the bridge, and 0 H indicates the slope foot of the leeward side of the bridge. The following five observation positions were set up for the bridge: the bottom of the slope middle of the windward side, the bottom of the slope shoulder of the windward side, the bottom center of the bridge abutment, the bottom of the slope shoulder of the leeward side, and the bottom of the slope middle of the leeward side. The wind speed of each observation position was measured at 10 different heights with a pitot tube, which was set at the bottom center of the wind tunnel (Fig. 5). The 10 different heights were 0.6, 0.8, 1.3, 2.1, 8.3, 12.2, 16.4, 20.2, 24.2, and 28 cm. Once the experimental wind speed stabilized, the wind speed was measured every 2 s, and the average value was taken after 30 consecutive measurements. A field wind–tunnel experiment was conducted to observe sand–moving wind speed20,21. A portable wind tunnel was transported to field, installed on the ground surface in the sandy area of the South Tibet Valley, increase the wind speed slowly, at the same time, the start moving of sand particle was monitored by a high speed camera, found the critical starting wind speed of local sand particle was 5.0 m s−1. As the sand–moving wind speed in the sandy area of the South Tibet Valley is 5 m s−1, the experimental wind speed in the wind tunnel should be greater than the sand–moving wind speed in order for the sand particle to start. Limited by hardware conditions, the maximum wind speed of the wind tunnel was 18 m s−1, therefore, the experimental wind speeds (wind speeds at the center of the entrance of the experimental section of the wind tunnel) were set to 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 m s−1.

The wind tunnel experimental layout used to determine the sand transport rate of the bridge is shown in Fig. 6. A sand bed with a length of 380 cm, width of 63 cm, and thickness of 5 cm was laid at a distance of 10H upwind from the bridge. A sand collector was set at a distance of 10H downwind from the bridge. A sand trap was set up every 1 cm increase in height, with a sand trap of 2 cm (width) × 1 cm (height), totaling 50 heights. The experimental wind speeds were set to 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 m s−1. In accordance with the abovementioned set up, the sand transport rate of the bridge was measured, and the initial sand transport rate without the bridge model was measured.

Numerical simulation

Using ICEM CFD, a three–dimensional model was established and meshed, designed at a 1:1 scale corresponding to the wind tunnel and bridge model, with a mesh quality of 0.80. The left side of the model is defined as the velocity inlet boundary condition, while the right side serves as the pressure outlet. The top and side boundaries are set as symmetry boundaries, and the ground and embankment surfaces are defined as no–slip wall boundary conditions. The wind speed at the inlet is defined using a user–defined function (UDF) with a logarithmic wind speed profile equation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu=\frac{\mu_{*}}{k} \text{In}\frac{z}{z_0}$$\end{document}

Where: µ is the horizontal wind speed at height z; k is the Karman constant (0.4); µ* is the friction velocity; z0 is the bed roughness.

An Eulerian two–phase flow model is employed for the transient simulation of the wind–sand two–phase flow, solved using the Phase Coupled SIMPLE algorithm with a time step of 0.1 s. The collision viscosity is selected as Gidaspow, while the friction viscosity is chosen as Schaeffer, with a maximum packing density of 0.63. To account for the interaction forces between the two phases, the lift coefficient is modeled using Saffman–mei, and the drag coefficient is modeled using Syamlal–obrien.

Influence of the bridge on the blown sand environment

The results of the wind speed at each observation point obtained from the wind tunnel experimental layout are shown in Fig. 14. When the height are 0.6, 0.8, 1.3, and 2.1 cm, the wind speed starts to decrease from the upwind direction of the bridge at a distance of − 3 H to lowest value at the bottom of the slope middle of the windward side of the bridge in the upwind direction. Subsequently (moving into the bridge), the wind speed increases significantly, to reach the highest value between the bottom center of the bridge abutment and the bottom of the slope shoulder of the leeward side. When exiting the bridge, the wind speed decreases significantly again. Beyond the 10 H distance in the downwind direction (10 H to 40 H distance), the wind speed is basically stable but lower than the initial wind speed in the upwind direction. The wind speed did not recover to its initial state which it has not been affected by the bridge. When the height is 8.3 cm (i.e., this point is located on the surface of the bridge abutment), as the height of the bridge model is 8 cm, the wind speed changes drastically and fluctuates greatly. The change starts from the slope shoulder of the windward side of the bridge; then, the wind speed decreases drastically, reaching 0 m s−1 at the slope shoulder of the leeward side of the bridge. Subsequently, the wind speed increases drastically. Beyond the 0 H distance in the downwind direction (0 H to 40 H distance), the wind speed stabilizes but is still lower than the initial wind speed in the upwind direction. The wind speed did not recover to its initial state which it has not been affected by the bridge. When the height are 12.2, 16.4, 20.2, 24.2, and 28 cm, the wind speed increases significantly from − 3 H in the upwind direction to 5 H in the downwind direction of the bridge. Beyond the 5 H distance in the downwind direction (5 H to 40 H distance), the wind speed stabilizes and returns to its initial state. The magnitude of the wind speed variation at the aforementioned five heights is notably smaller than those at the other heights, especially at the 8.3 cm height.

According to the aforementioned wind speed experimental results, wind speed contour maps of the bridge and its upwind and downwind directions were determined via the Kriging interpolation method, because the Kriging interpolation method is suitable for solving situations where the attributes of spatial continuity change are very irregular. In this investigation, the expressway bridge has a significant impact on wind speed, causing irregular changes in airflow field. Using the Kriging interpolation method can precisely solve this problem. Figure 15 shows an increasing area for the wind speed between the top of the slope shoulder of the windward side of the bridge to the center of the bridge abutment, a weakening area for the wind speed between the − 3 H distances of the upwind direction to the bottom of the slope middle of the windward side of the bridge. In addition, a weakening area for the wind speed is centered on the slope shoulder of the leeward side of the bridge. The extent of this weakening wind speed is greater than that in the previous area where the wind speed initially weakened. Overall, the wind speed between 5 H and 40 H in the downwind direction of the bridge is generally lower than the corresponding upwind wind speed (i.e., the incoming wind speed). These scenarios indicate that the wind speed and the flow field in the downwind direction generally did not recover, further suggesting that the bridge has a greater influence on the blown sand environment in the downwind direction than in the upwind direction.

From the numerical simulation results shown in Fig. 17, the presence of the bridge significantly alters the surrounding wind speed distribution, creating speed anomaly zones. There are notable differences between the windward and leeward sides of the bridge, with dramatic variations in wind speed. Near the bridge, the airflow experiences separation, resulting in a deceleration zone on the windward side, an acceleration zone above the bridge and near the ground, and a low–speed zone on the leeward side. Once the airflow passes the bridge and the obstruction is removed, the cross–sectional area for flow increases, allowing the airflow speed to gradually recover.

Discussion

The temperature and humidity data from synchronized observations are shown in Fig. 18. Winter and spring (November–April) in the South Tibet Valley have low relative humidity and low temperatures and a plateau temperate semiarid climate, with an yearly precipitation of 361 to 419.7 mm, more than 80% of which is concentrated in the summer and fall22. Precipitation is scarce in the winter and spring. The air is dry, the ground surface has a low water content, the vegetation is dry and has low cover, rivers have declining water levels similar to those in the dry season, and sandy outcrops are apparent23. These environmental features are synchronized and superimposed with the wind conditions, wind–sand hazards are exacerbated in winter and spring. By contrast, summer and fall are characterized by high precipitation, high air humidity, increased surface water content, increased vegetation cover, rivers with abundant water, and rising water levels that can flood valley bottom sands24,25. These environmental features, combined with the wind–sand conditions, weaken wind–sand hazards in the summer and fall. Therefore, wind–sand hazards are likely to occur mainly in winter and spring. When the wind–sand flow passes over an expressway bridge, the near–surface wind speed, wind–sand flow field, and transportation and accumulation conditions are significantly changed by the bridge26,27. In the experiment conducted in this study, by considering the dimensions of the bridge model and the cross–section of the experimental section of the wind tunnel, the blockage ratio of the bridge in the wind tunnel is 7.56%, the airflow in the upwind direction below the height of 8 cm of the bridge model (four heights of 0.6, 0.8, 1.3, and 2.1 cm) is blocked by the bridge, and the wind speed is reduced before it enters the bridge. Owing to the specific geometrical form of the bridge cross–section, particularly with clearances under the bridge abutment, the airflow entering under the bridge abutment is affected by the narrow tube effect, and the wind speed increases. After the airflow exits the bridge, the wind speed decreases because of the space recover. At a height of 8.3 cm (i.e., a height located on the bridge abutment surface), the airflow is severely blocked due to high friction, resulting in the wind speed decreasing significantly on the bridge abutment surface, followed by a rapid increase after passing through the bridge abutment. Above a height of 8.3 cm, the wind speed variability decreases because of the weakening influence of the bridge on the airflow. As wind–sand flow movement is a process of sand transport close to the ground, the sand material is distributed mainly below 10 cm from the ground surface28–30. In the presence of a zone for weakening the wind speed in each upwind and downwind direction of the bridge, the wind–sand flow is partially blocked when it passes through the bridge, and the sand material accumulates on the two sides of the bridge, which cause harm. The bridge, which blocked part of the sand material transport, also consumed part of the wind energy, resulting in an overall unrecoverable wind speed and flow field within a distance of 40 H downwind direction of the bridge. Therefore, the bridge has a greater impact on the blown sand environment in the downwind direction than in the upwind direction.

In this investigation, the correlation between field observation and wind tunnel experiment is mainly reflected in the following aspects:

Through on–site wind tunnel testing, the critical starting wind speed of local sand particle was determined to be 5 m s−1. Based on this, the wind speeds of wind tunnel experiment were set to 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 m s−1.

Through field observations, the natural causes of local wind–sand disasters are understood. Along the southern Tibet expressway, the wind–sand disasters show spatial non–uniformity, the main hazards wind direction is different, and most of them are concentrated in winter and spring, which provides the dynamic conditions for the wind–sand disasters. In addition, the construction of the expressway has changed the original structure of the wind–sand flow field. In order to explore the influence of expressway bridge on local wind–sand environment, the man–made causes of wind–sand disasters are understood by making bridge models and conducting wind tunnel experiments. Finding out the natural and man–made causes of wind–sand disaster on South Tibet expressway can not only reveal the disaster–causing mechanism, but also provide a more comprehensive understanding of the movement law of wind–sand along the expressway, and provide a basis for the prevention and control of wind–sand disaster.

Conclusions

The sand–moving wind in the South Tibet Valley is dominated by west wind. During winter and spring (November–April), the frequency of sand–moving wind, the DP, the RDP, the Q, and the RQ are high, and the degree of wind–sand hazard is severe. By contrast, in summer and fall (May–October), the frequency of sand–moving wind, the DP, the RDP, the Q, and the RQ are low, and the degree of wind–sand hazard is weakened. In observation site 1, the yearly DP is 41.05 VU, the yearly RDP is 25.26 VU, the yearly Q is 101.63 kg m−1 a−1, the yearly RQ is 66.99 kg m−1 a−1, and the yearly resultant sand drift direction (RDD and RA) is in the east direction. In observation site 2, the yearly DP is 29.02 VU, the yearly RDP is 22.63 VU, the yearly Q is 69.40 kg m−1 a−1, the yearly RQ is 55.00 kg m−1 a−1, and the yearly resultant sand drift direction (RDD and RA) is in the northeast direction.

The bridge has a considerable impact on the near–surface blown sand environment. In this study, large variations in the wind speed were observed at heights of ≤ 8.3 cm. Three distinct wind speed anomaly zones formed. These zones included an area of increasing wind speed between the top of the slope shoulder of the windward side of the bridge to the center of the bridge abutment, a zone for weakening the wind speed between the − 3 H distances of the upwind direction to the bottom of the slope middle of the windward side of the bridge, and a zone for weakening the wind speed centered in the slope shoulder of the leeward side of the bridge. The wind speed and its flow field within a distance of 40 H downwind direction of the bridge generally did not recover. When the wind–sand flow passed over the bridge, 30.54%, 42.61%, 34.64%, 12.36%, and 36.37% of the sand materials were blocked under the five sets of experimental wind speeds; they accumulated on the two sides of the bridge, which cause hazards. The bridge had a greater impact on the blown sand environment in the downwind direction than in the upwind direction.
