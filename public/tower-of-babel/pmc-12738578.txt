Assessing knowledge, attitudes, and barriers toward HPV cervical cancer and vaccination among female university students in Bangladesh
Authors: Hasibul Hassain Emon, Rawfun Nahin Kowbi, Md Nayeem Chowdhury, Tanha Rasheed Teep, Md Ahashan Habib, Farzana Tayeeba, Md Moyazzem Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman papillomavirus (HPV) is a leading cause of genital warts and cervical cancer (CC), which was reported as the second most common malignancy among women in Bangladesh in 2020. While vaccination against HPV stands out as the most effective strategy for preventing CC, however, misconceptions about its safety, effectiveness, and benefits may impact its prevalence. This study aimed to evaluate the knowledge of HPV infection, CC, and vaccination, as well as the attitudes and willingness to receive the vaccine among female university students in Bangladesh. A cross-sectional survey was conducted to collect primary data from 800 university students through face-to-face interviews. A well-structured questionnaire was administered to collect data on socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge of HPV infection, CC, and vaccination, as well as attitudes and willingness regarding vaccination. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors influencing knowledge about HPV infection and CC. Moreover, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) explored direct and indirect pathways affecting knowledge, attitudes, and willingness, validated by goodness-of-fit indices. Students with sexual health education demonstrated higher awareness of HPV infection and cervical cancer, with 23.4% having good knowledge, compared to only 11.0% without such education. Difficulty accessing healthcare increased the odds of poor knowledge in the unadjusted model (COR: 1.72. 95% CI: 1.01–2.93), whereas students from Khulna had higher odds (AOR: 4.45, 95% CI: 1.24–15.88). Moreover, 44.8% of participants recognized that the HPV vaccine prevents cervical cancer, and 64.7% were unsure of its effectiveness. The SEM highlights that attitude is the strongest predictor of vaccination willingness (0.61), while HPV vaccination knowledge has a moderate impact (0.46). University female students have limited knowledge about HPV, CC, and vaccination, yet they are willing to receive the HPV vaccine. Introduction

Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most common sexually transmitted viruses worldwide1. It affects individuals of all sexes and is easily transmitted through intimate skin-to-skin contact2. HPV is not a disease in itself, yet it can cause infections that are grouped under sexually transmitted diseases (STDs)3. Reproductive and sexual factors, such as having multiple sexual partners, multiple births, and first intercourse at a young age, contribute to an increased risk of HPV infection4. HPV infections are frequently asymptomatic, yet high-risk types pose a significant threat as they can lead to serious and potentially life-threatening health conditions if not addressed promptly5,6. Persistent HPV infections are associated with diseases such as genital warts and cancers like penile cancer, anal cancer, and so on, including cervical cancer7.

Cervical cancer is a malignant disease of the cervix and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women, making it the fourth most common cancer around the world8. High-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) types, including HPV-16 and HPV-18, are the causative agents of cervical cancer in approximately 95% of cases9. It is a severe health burden because it is often diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to high mortality rates10. Cervical cancer overall kills more than 350,000 women each year globally, causing around 660,000 new cases11. In Bangladesh, cervical cancer is the second most common cancer among women, with approximately 12,000 new cases and 6,000 deaths reported every year12.

The disease is easily preventable if detected early and through immunization13. Despite being preventable, cervical cancer remains prevalent due to low awareness and limited access to preventive measures14. The HPV vaccine offers a groundbreaking solution by providing effective protection against the high-risk HPV types responsible for most cervical cancer cases15. Studies show the vaccine is highly effective when administered before exposure to HPV10. Although the vaccine is available in Bangladesh, its uptake is remarkably low16. Several factors contribute to this hesitancy, including limited awareness about HPV, Cervical Cancer, and the HPV vaccine, misconceptions about the HPV vaccine, high prices, and fears of potential side effects10. All these areas need to be addressed to promote HPV vaccination and decrease Cervical Cancer.

Female university students, as an educated and influential demographic, are particularly important for this initiative. Most of these students are unmarried, making them ideal candidates for vaccination before marriage, when the vaccine is most effective10. Their knowledge and attitudes towards HPV and cervical cancer can significantly influence their peers and families, highlighting the importance of targeted educational campaigns. Moreover, this group is possibly to shape the existing culture of society towards HPV as well as cervical cancer17. If awareness among this educated group is low, one can easily imagine what the rest of society looks like in terms of awareness about such diseases. Research on HPV, cervical cancer, and HPV vaccination has been conducted both in Bangladesh and abroad. These studies primarily focus on the general female population or women of different age groups10,14,16. Some address knowledge levels and willingness to vaccinate among women, but rarely explore university students specifically14,16. Very few studies examine the awareness, attitudes, and willingness of female university students from diverse faculties towards HPV vaccination4,16. The lack of exclusive research targeting this group, despite their critical role in influencing societal attitudes, presents a significant gap. This study seeks to fill this void by focusing on female university students’ knowledge of HPV infection, cervical cancer, and the HPV vaccine, alongside their attitudes and willingness to vaccinate. Therefore, this study assesses the knowledge, attitudes, and willingness toward HPV vaccination among female university students at Jahangirnagar University, focusing on their understanding of HPV, cervical cancer, the vaccine, and barriers to vaccination. Moreover, this study highlights misconceptions, including fears of side effects, and uncovers gaps in awareness that hinder vaccine acceptance. By identifying the willingness and attitudes of female university students towards vaccination, the study aims to influence policymakers and media to prioritize affordable and accessible vaccine programs. By highlighting knowledge gaps, attitudes, and barriers, this study contributes to sustainable health goals through evidence that can strengthen awareness initiatives and vaccination programs, which are essential for reducing cervical cancer burden and empowering young women to make informed health decisions.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

The primary data were collected from female students enrolled in 37 departments across six faculties and three institutes at Jahangirnagar University in Bangladesh. As one of the six faculties and all institutes offer only a single department program, resulting in very small group sizes, they were merged for analysis to prevent sparse category issues, yielding a total of five faculties. A well-structured questionnaire was used to collect data in a cross-sectional survey.

Participants

This study included only female students currently studying at the undergraduate and graduate levels at Jahangirnagar University. The primary outcome of the study was to assess the knowledge level of female university students regarding HPV infection and cervical cancer. The secondary outcomes were (i) understanding how knowledge and attitude influence willingness to receive the HPV vaccine, and (ii) identifying barriers regarding HPV vaccine uptake. Since no prior studies have been conducted on this specific topic among this population in Bangladesh, we consider a prevalence of 0.5 to get the maximum sample size18. This conservative approach is widely recommended as it ensures the maximum sample size and sufficient statistical power in the absence of prior data. The final sample size was increased to 800 to enhance the study’s robustness and address potential non-response bias.

Sampling procedure

A two-stage proportional stratified random sampling technique was employed to select the participants of this study. In the first stage, faculties were treated as strata to ensure proportional representation across all disciplines, enhancing precision and comparability; using clusters could have excluded some faculties and introduced bias. Subsequently, within each selected faculty, departments were considered as secondary strata. The sample size for each department was determined proportionally to its size within the respective faculty. This procedure is consistent with other study19. Finally, data were collected from students who met the inclusion criteria, defined as currently enrolled female students of Jahangirnagar University who voluntarily consented to participate. To provide further transparency, a supplementary excel file has been included reporting the achieved sample sizes stratified by faculty and department.

Questionnaire design and data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire administered by the authors. The questionnaire was initially designed in English and translated into Bengali to enhance participant comprehension. The questionnaire consisted of four sections, and in the first section, socio-demographic characteristics were assessed. The second section focused on knowledge related to HPV infection, Cervical Cancer (CC), and HPV vaccination, comprising 16 items and divided into two aspects: knowledge about HPV infection and CC20, and knowledge about HPV vaccination21. The third section assessed participants’ attitudes toward HPV vaccination20, and the final section evaluated willingness to receive the vaccine, source of information regarding CC, HPV infection, and vaccine, and perceived barriers to HPV vaccination22. The description of the factors affecting attitude toward the HPV vaccine and willingness to uptake the HPV vaccine is given in Supplementary Table 1. Prior to the final survey, a pilot survey was conducted with 100 participants to check the errors of the questionnaire design. Data collection was carried out from 15 April 2025 to 02 May 2025, by a well-trained team under the supervision of the authors, ensuring anonymity and confidentiality throughout the process.

Variables and measurement tools

Independent variables encompassed socio-demographic characteristics (age, marital status, faculty, division, residence, parental education, household income, and personal expenditure), access to healthcare facility, prior health experiences (history of uterine infection and cervical cancer), and exposure to sexual health education, providing descriptive data about the participants. In Bangladesh, a formal curriculum on sexual health is absent. The textbook ‘Physical Education and Health’ (grades six to ten) includes only general health topics and does not cover HPV infection or cervical cancer. Therefore, in this study, the sexual health education received was defined as students’ self-reported exposure to sexual or reproductive health information through informal sources, which were subsequently identified in the survey. To ensure reliable and consistent measurement, knowledge, attitudes, willingness, source of information, and perceived barriers were assessed using a validated scoring scale. Three categories of questions are based on answer choices made up the questionnaire’s scoring system: (1) two-choice questions (yes/no); (2) three-choice questions (yes/no/do not know or true/false/do not know); and (3) four-choice questions (strongly agree/agree/disagree/strongly disagree, or yes/maybe/unsure/no). For two and three-choice questions, correct responses scored 2, while incorrect responses scored 0. Similarly, for four-choice questions, inaccurate responses scored 0, accurate responses scored 1, and extremely accurate responses scored 2. Generally, the responses were categorized into two groups: good knowledge/ positive attitude/ favorable (with + 1 or + 2 points) or poor knowledge/ negative attitude/unfavorable (0 points) for knowledge, attitudes, and willingness. To calculate the final scores, the total points for each aspect were divided by the maximum possible score and multiplied by 100%. A score of ≥ 60 indicates good knowledge of HPV, CC, and HPV vaccination, as well as a positive attitude and willingness toward HPV vaccination20. Categorization of response and predictor variables is presented in the Supplementary Table 2. The values of Cronbach’s Alpha are 0.79 for knowledge of HPV infection and CC, 0.80 for knowledge of the HPV vaccine, 0.79 for attitudes toward the HPV vaccine, and 0.89 for willingness to uptake the HPV vaccine, suggesting acceptable reliability. The hypotheses of this study are given in the Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, Chi-square, or Fisher’s exact test were used to identify associations between socio-demographic factors and knowledge of HPV infection and CC. Variables with p < 0.10 were considered as a significant predictor and included in the multivariable logistic regression. Crude odds ratio (COR), adjusted odds ratios (AOR), and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. To assess potential multicollinearity among explanatory variables included in the regression models, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated. All VIF values were close to 1, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern in this analysis. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was employed to find the relationships between observed variables and their underlying latent factors: knowledge of HPV infection and CC, knowledge of HPV vaccination, and attitudes and willingness toward vaccination. Features with loadings ≤ 0.5, were excluded from the model. Furthermore, the Structural Equation Model (SEM) was employed to explore direct and indirect pathways affecting knowledge, attitudes, and willingness, which provided a robust framework to account for complex relationships and measurement errors, incorporating four latent constructs23. To validate the SEM model, goodness-of-fit indices, including CFI, IFI, TLI, PCFI, PNFI, and RMSEA, were used. Data were managed and analyzed using Excel, SPSS, and R Studio programming language version 4.4.1.

Access to healthcare facilities showed a significant association (p = 0.053) with knowledge of HPV among participants, and it is observed that female students who access healthcare facilities easily have a higher prevalence of good knowledge (19.9%) compared to those who found it “difficult” (12.6%). Mother’s education was also significantly associated with the knowledge of HPV (p = 0.061). Findings depict that participants whose mothers had a graduate or higher education exhibited better knowledge (23.9%) compared to those whose mothers had no formal education (13.9%) or primary education (12.5%). Monthly consumption levels also have significant relationship with the knowledge of HPV (p = 0.020) and participants spending 4000–5000 BDT showing better knowledge (25.8%). Sexual health education was a critical factor (p < 0.001), as 23.4% of those who received education demonstrated good knowledge, compared to only 11.0% among those without such education. Attitudes toward premarital sex were also significantly associated (p = 0.048) with the knowledge of HPV, with 31.2% of those supporting premarital sex demonstrating good knowledge, compared to 17.1% of those opposing it. These findings underscore the importance of faculty type, geographical division, healthcare facility, mother’s education, monthly expenditure, sexual health education, and attitudes toward premarital sex in shaping participants’ knowledge about HPV infection and cervical cancer (Table 1).

Table 2 provides an overview of female university students’ knowledge about the HPV vaccination for cervical cancer prevention. A significant portion of participants (53.1%) were unsure whether the HPV vaccine protects against cervical cancer, and 44.8% of the participants knew that it does. Regarding the vaccine’s ability to protect against all types of the virus causing cervical cancer, most participants (64.7%) were unsure, while 27.4% answered correctly. Knowledge about the vaccine’s protection against sexually transmitted infections was low, with 70.7% unaware, and 18.4% incorrectly believing it protects against all such infections. Moreover, more than half of the participants (68.6%) were unaware that the vaccine was available for both men and women, and a majority (75.9%) of the participants did not know that vaccinated women need less frequent pelvic exams. Most of the participants (88.1%) mistakenly believed that the HPV vaccine eliminates the need for Pap smears, while 62.4% were unaware that vaccinated women are not entirely free from cervical cancer in the future (Table 2).

Table 3 highlights the factors associated with having heard of HPV infection and cervical cancer among female university students. Significant associations were observed for faculty type, division, and sexual health education in both unadjusted and adjusted models. Faculty type remained a significant predictor, with students from the Biological Sciences faculty being less likely to report poor knowledge compared to those from Arts and Humanities (AOR = 0.45; 95% CI: 0.26–0.78, p = 0.004). Students from Khulna showed significantly higher odds of poor knowledge (AOR = 4.45; 95% CI: 1.24–15.88, p = 0.021) compared to those from Barisal. Healthcare accessibility was significant in the unadjusted model (COR = 1.72; 95% CI: 1.01–2.93, p = 0.042), indicating that students facing difficulty accessing healthcare were more likely to have poor knowledge, although this association lost significance in the adjusted model. Sexual health education showed the strongest association with knowledge levels, as students who received sexual health education were significantly less likely to have poor knowledge (AOR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.28–0.67, p < 0.001) compared to those who did not (Table 3). It is observed that a majority of the students (73%) had a negative attitude, while only a few exhibited a positive attitude toward the HPV vaccine. This research reveals a widespread hesitancy or mistrust about HPV vaccination.

Figure 1 illustrates the willingness to vaccinate against cervical cancer among female university students of Jahangirnagar University in Bangladesh. Students who received sexual health education demonstrated greater awareness and willingness to accept the vaccine. Findings depict that 75.4% of those who received sexual health education had heard of the vaccine compared to 61.8% of those who did not receive sexual health education. Similarly, a higher percentage supported measures to prevent cervical cancer (86.6% vs. 78.3%) and were willing to take the vaccine (73.5% vs. 63.1%). Notably, sexual health education positively influenced willingness to give the vaccine to daughters (75.5% vs. 64.0%) and recommend the vaccine to others (75.8% vs. 68.3%). Conversely, the proportion of students who responded "Don’t know" or “No” was consistently higher among those who did not receive sexual health education, highlighting the importance of such education in improving knowledge and acceptance of cervical cancer prevention strategies (Fig. 2).

The Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) of indicators was used to assess knowledge of HPV infection and cervical cancer, knowledge of the HPV vaccine, attitudes toward vaccination, and willingness to be vaccinated, and the findings are presented in Fig. 2 and the Supplementary Table 4. Items (CC2 to CC8) measure knowledge of HPV infection and cervical cancer. From the findings, it is observed that factor loadings ranged from 0.51 to 0.74, indicating moderate to high associations. Their importance to the construct is confirmed by the loadings between 0.50 and 0.72 for knowledge of HPV vaccinations, which have indicators VAC1 to VAC7. The opinions of items AT2 through AT4 on the HPV vaccine have high loadings (0.54 to 0.89), particularly those of AT3 and AT4, which show great reliability. Moreover, WV2 to WV5, with strong loadings (0.55 to 0.93), especially WV3 and WV4, are used to determine willingness to uptake the HPV vaccine. There are significant associations between attitude and willingness to vaccinate (0.63) and knowledge of the HPV vaccine and attitude (0.43) (Fig. 2). Thus, the CFA model validates the constructs and demonstrates strong relationships among knowledge, attitude, and willingness to vaccinate, highlighting their importance in vaccination behaviors.

The model fit indices for assessing the goodness of fit of the structural equation model used in this study are presented in Table 4. The relative Chi-square value of 4.651 is below the recommended threshold of 5.00, indicating an acceptable fit. The Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Incremental Fit Index (IFI) both have values of 0.908, which exceed the recommended value of 0.90, suggesting a good fit. However, the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) is slightly below the threshold at 0.895. The Parsimonious Comparative Fit Index (PCFI) and Parsimonious Normed Fit Index (PNFI) are both above 0.50, indicating acceptable parsimony. Moreover, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) value of 0.068 is below the recommended value of 0.08, confirming a good model fit (Table 4). Overall, the indices suggest that the model fits the data well.

Figure 3 shows the Structural Equation Model (SEM) used to assess the relationships among knowledge of HPV infection and cervical cancer, knowledge of the HPV vaccine, attitude toward vaccination, and willingness to be vaccinated. The most significant variable in predicting willingness to get vaccinated is attitude toward vaccination, which has the largest path coefficient (0.61). Understanding the HPV vaccine has a moderate (0.46) impact on the formation of attitudes, which in turn affects willingness. The direct effect of HPV_CC knowledge on willingness, however, is negligible (0.07). Every observed indication has a factor loading between 0.51 and 0.93, indicating high dependability. The model demonstrates that developing suitable attitudes is the main factor influencing vaccination willingness (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study explored the factors influencing knowledge about HPV infection and cervical cancer among female university students. It further examined the relationships between HPV knowledge, vaccination attitudes, and willingness to vaccinate. Additionally, the research investigated information sources and barriers to vaccine uptake. The faculty of female university students was identified as a significant factor associated with knowledge of HPV infection and cervical cancer. Students from the biological sciences faculty demonstrated the highest level of knowledge, with 30.6% exhibiting good awareness compared to students from other faculties. Similar findings were reported in a previous study conducted in Bangladesh24, India25, and Morocco26, suggesting that students from biological disciplines are generally more aware of HPV infection. The reason behind this is the in-depth curriculum content related to health and diseases, as well as the direct relevance of these topics to their field of study, which enhances their knowledge of HPV infection and cervical cancer. This is supported by findings from a previous study24. This study found geographical variation as a significant factor, with participants from the Sylhet division showing the highest proportion of good knowledge (88.2%), while those from the Khulna division had the lowest (5.7%). Previous studies also showed that awareness and prevalence of cervical cancer and HPV infection differ by geographic location27,28. However, based on indicators such as antenatal care, institutional deliveries, cesarean deliveries, underweight and stunting in children, and literacy rate, a study29 suggests that people in Khulna generally have better access to healthcare and education, contributing to better health knowledge and outcomes compared to Sylhet. A nationwide study may help reconcile this discrepancy.

Participants whose mothers had a higher level of education demonstrated greater knowledge about HPV infection (23.9%) compared to those whose mothers had a lower level of education. Similarly, a study showed that mother-daughter education sessions about cervical cancer and HPV can positively influence both their awareness and preventive behaviors regarding HPV, highlighting the importance of targeted education programs30. Educated mothers might be more likely to be aware of health risks and preventive measures and may discuss health topics, including HPV, with their children. A study also pointed out that mothers play an important role in sexual education and can be the best source of sexual health education for their daughters31. Initially, difficulty accessing healthcare was associated with lower levels of knowledge about health issues with Students facing difficulties accessing healthcare were 1.72 times more likely to have poor knowledge of HPV infection and cervical cancer compared to those without such difficulties. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in the USA32. However, this relationship attenuated in the adjusted model when other socio-economic factors were taken into account. This suggests that access to healthcare may be affected by factors such as family education, geographical location, or exposure to sexual health education. A study demonstrated that individuals with better access to healthcare often come from more supportive socio-economic and educational backgrounds33, which may diminish the independent effect of healthcare access on HPV knowledge. In contrast, limited healthcare access reduces exposure to health education and preventive services, thereby limiting knowledge of HPV infection and cervical cancer34.

Sexual health education demonstrated the strongest association with knowledge levels, as students who received sexual health education were 57% less likely to have poor knowledge compared to those who did not. This finding is supported by previous studies, which also highlight a positive association between health literacy and knowledge of cervical cancer and HPV35,36. Students who received sexual health education have good knowledge because sexual health education enhances knowledge by providing structured and accurate information, increasing awareness, improving health literacy, influencing behavior, and reinforcing learning through evidence-based teaching37.

The findings reveal that over three-quarters of the respondents possess limited knowledge about the HPV vaccine, highlighting a critical gap in awareness. Previous studies have also shown that Bangladeshi students demonstrated poor knowledge about the HPV vaccine and cervical cancer24,38. This knowledge gap may be attributed to the absence of cervical cancer and HPV-related content in the Bangladeshi academic curriculum, suggesting that incorporating such topics could significantly improve students’ awareness and understanding39. This study also demonstrated through Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) that attitude toward the HPV vaccine has a direct, positive influence on individuals’ willingness to receive the vaccine. However, knowledge about the HPV vaccine does not have a direct effect on willingness. Rather, it exerts its influence indirectly through attitude. Similarly, a previous study among Chinese female college students found, using SEM, that HPV vaccine knowledge indirectly affects willingness to receive the vaccine, aligning with our findings40. Another study conducted among HIV-infected women in Zambia also showed that attitude influences vaccine uptake, while knowledge enhances vaccine uptake indirectly through attitude41. In contrast, a separate study among female college students in China reported a direct effect of knowledge on willingness to receive the HPV vaccine42, with all findings suggesting the necessity of increasing HPV vaccine knowledge to improve vaccine uptake.

The study found that a significant portion of students had never heard of cervical cancer, HPV, or the HPV vaccine. Among those who had heard of these topics, the majority reported learning about them through the internet/social media, family/friends, or public health campaigns. This study identified concerns about side effects, doubts regarding the vaccine’s efficacy, and high cost as the primary barriers to HPV vaccine uptake. A study conducted by43 also found that concerns about side effects and doubts regarding the vaccine’s efficacy were reasons for hesitancy in HPV vaccine uptake. Another study44 identified high cost as a barrier to HPV vaccine adoption.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Though there are a few studies on this topic considering different age-grouped populations, so far, our knowledge; this study is the first in Bangladesh to investigate female university students across multiple faculties, highlighting gaps in knowledge and attitudes regarding HPV infection and cervical cancer and their influence on vaccine willingness. Using a rigorous two-stage proportional stratified random sampling method strengthened internal validity and reduced sampling bias, and the study also identified barriers to vaccine uptake that have not been previously explored in this population. However, the conclusions of this study are limited in their generalizability because it was only carried out at one public university, Jahangirnagar University. The results may be impacted by variations in the institutional setting, student populations, and available resources between public and private universities. Students from a variety of institutions, especially public and private ones, should be included in future research to increase the external validity of the findings and provide a more representative perspective.

Conclusion

This study found that female university students in Bangladesh had limited knowledge of HPV infection and cervical cancer, with significant disparities across academic disciplines, geographic divisions, and access to health education. Greater knowledge and positive attitudes were closely linked to higher willingness to accept vaccination, while cost, safety concerns, and doubts about efficacy remained key barriers. To overcome these challenges, sexual health education should be systematically incorporated into secondary-level curricula, particularly within physical and health education. The Ministry of Health, in collaboration with NGOs, should prioritize institute-based awareness campaigns and promote reliable information through healthcare providers and trusted media outlets. Expanding affordable vaccination programmes and addressing safety concerns will be essential to reducing barriers and enhancing vaccine uptake, thereby advancing national efforts in cervical cancer prevention.
