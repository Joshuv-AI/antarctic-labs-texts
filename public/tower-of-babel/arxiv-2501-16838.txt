Spread Codes from Abelian non-cyclic groups
Joan-Josep Climent, Verónica Requena, Xaro Soler-Escrivà

Abstract
Given the finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} , for a prime power q q , in this paper we present a way of constructing spreads of 𝔽 q n \mathbb{F}_{q}^{n} . They will arise as orbits under the action of an Abelian non-cyclic group. First, we construct a family of orbit codes of maximum distance using this group, and then we complete each of these codes to achieve a spread of the whole space having an orbital structure.

1
Introduction

Network coding
is a part of information theory that describes a method to maximize the rate of a network which is modelled by a directed acyclic multigraph, with one or multiple sources and multiple receivers.
First introduced in
[
1
]
, the key point of this method is allowing the intermediate nodes of the network to transmit linear combinations of the inputs they receive.
The algebraic approach given by Kötter and Kschischang in
[
16
]
provided a rigorous mathematical setup for error correction when coding in non-coherent networks and, as a result, this theory was able to advance vastly.
In this setting, the transmitted messages (
codewords
) are vector subspaces of a given vector space
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
, where
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the finite field of
q
elements and a
subspace code
is just a collection
\mathcal{C}
of vector subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
When all subspaces of
\mathcal{C}
have the same dimension, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is a
constant dimension code
.
The minimum distance
d(\mathcal{C})
of
\mathcal{C}
is computed in the usual way by using a metric called
subspace distance
in the set of all subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
We refer the reader to
[
4
,
15
]
and references therein for further information regarding network coding and subspace codes.

One of the most important and studied families of subspace codes are
spread codes
(or simply
spreads
).
A spread code is a constant dimension code such that all its elements intersect pairwise trivially and their union covers the whole vector space.
Spreads are clearly a relevant family of constant dimension codes
since they reach the maximum distance and, at the same time, the maximum size for that distance.
For this reason, many papers in the literature about subspace codes are devoted to the study and construction of this type of codes (see
[
9
,
11
,
14
,
19
]
for instance).

A relevant way of constructing constant dimension codes is by considering the natural action of the general linear group,
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, on the Grassmannian
\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
, which is the set of all
k
-dimensional vector subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
(for an integer
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
).
Using this technique, the codes arise as orbits under the action of some specific subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
Constant dimension codes constructed in this way are called
orbit codes
.
In the linear network coding setting, they were first introduced in
[
27
]
, where their main properties are given.
Due to the group action point of view, orbit codes have a nice mathematical structure, and they have been investigated by many different authors since then (see for instance
[
3
,
10
,
20
,
23
,
26
]
).
Using powerful tools from group theory, the distance of this kind of codes can be calculated in a simpler way, and we can compute the size of the code in terms of the order of the acting group and the order of the corresponding stabilizer subgroup
[
27
]
.
In addition, there exist different algorithms for decoding orbit codes
[
22
,
26
]
and several of the known algebraic constructions of constant dimension codes can be seen as orbit codes.
Most of the research on this topic focus on the use of cyclic subgroups of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, in which case we speak about
cyclic orbit codes
.
In particular, in
[
27
]
appears the first construction of spreads with an orbital structure.
From here, we can find several works on spreads with an orbital structure provided by a cyclic group (for instance,
[
6
,
28
]
).

While research on cyclic orbit codes abounds, the same is not true when we want to focus on other types of subgroups of the general linear group.
As a first step, in
[
8
]
we approach the study of orbit codes through the action of Abelian non-cyclic subgroups of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, giving a specific construction of maximum distance.
Pursuing this line of research, the papers
[
7
,
25
]
are also concerned with Abelian non-cyclic orbit codes.
Nevertheless, as far as we know, the only construction on spreads through the action of a non-cyclic Abelian group is given in
[
7
]
.
In this paper, the authors construct an Abelian non-cyclic orbit code of
\mathbb{F}^{2k}_{q}
of dimension
k
having maximum distance and then obtain a
k
-spread of
\mathbb{F}^{2k}_{q}
just by adding two
k
-subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{2k}_{q}
.

Our main objective in this paper is to pursue the research of orbit codes constructed by using non-cyclic Abelian groups.
Specifically, we generalize the results obtained in
[
7
]
in the following sense: For an even integer
n
and
k
a divisor of
n
, we firstly construct an Abelian non-cyclic orbit code of
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
of dimension
k
having maximum distance.
Then, we achieve to complete this orbit code with a nice family of
k
-subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
in such a way the resulting code is a
k
-spread of
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
with an orbital structure which is not cyclic.
This generalization is not immediate and has required the use of new techniques, not used in
[
7
]
.

The paper is structured as follows.
In Section
2
, we collect all the background on finite fields and subspace codes that will be needed in the subsequent sections.
Section
3
is devoted to our orbital constructions of maximum distance codes and is divided into two parts.
Firstly, we construct a non-cyclic Abelian group
\mathbf{H}
and from it a maximum distance family of orbit codes of dimension
k
in
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
.
Secondly, we tackle the construction of
k
-spreads in
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
by completing each orbit code previously obtained. To do so, we define two different families of codes, one of them of orbital type. The final
k
-spread is then presented as a disjoint union of these three types of codes.
Finally, Section
4
concludes the paper.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Finite fields

In this section, we recall some basic notions and results concerning finite fields that will be needed later.
The reader can find more details in any basic book on this subject, e.g.
[
18
]
.

Given a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and a positive integer
n
, we will denote by
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
the set of all
n\times n
matrices with entries in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and by
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
the
general linear group
of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Assume that
n=ks
, for some positive integers
k,s
and consider a
primitive element

\alpha
of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}
, that is, a generator of the cyclic group
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}^{*}
.
The
companion matrix
of the minimal polynomial
p(\mathrm{x})=a_{0}+a_{1}\mathrm{x}+\cdots+a_{k-1}\mathrm{x}^{k-1}+\mathrm{x}^{k}
of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the matrix

M_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
-a_{0}&-a_{1}&-a_{2}&\cdots&-a_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

It turns out that
p(\mathrm{x})
is the characteristic polynomial of
M_{k}
and, then,
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}=\mathbb{F}_{q}[\alpha]
can be realized as a set of matrices of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times k}
through the following field isomorphism

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\phi:&\mathbb{F}_{q}[\alpha]&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{F}_{q}\left[M_{k}\right]\\
&\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}b_{i}\alpha^{i}&\mapsto&\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}b_{i}M_{k}^{i}.\end{array}

(1)

Therefore,
M_{k}
can be seen as a primitive element of the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}\left[M_{k}\right]
.
Equivalently, the multiplicative order of
M_{k}
, denoted by
o(M_{k})
, is
q^{k}-1
and

\mathbb{F}_{q}\left[M_{k}\right]=\{0_{k\times k}\}\cup\langle M_{k}\rangle,

where
0_{k\times k}
is the null matrix of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times k}
and

\langle M_{k}\rangle=\big\{I_{k},M_{k},M_{k}^{2},\dots,M_{k}^{q^{k}-2}\big\}

is the multiplicative group generated by
M_{k}
.

On the one hand, the isomorphism
\phi
provided in expression (
1
) allows to map vector subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}^{s}
into vector subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
(this is the well-known
field reduction
technique, see
[
17
]
for instance).
Specifically, each line generated by a vector
(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}^{s}
, will produce a subspace of dimension
k
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with the following injective map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\varphi:&\mathcal{G}_{q^{k}}(1,s)&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)\\
&\mathrm{rowsp}\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}&\dots&u_{s}\end{pmatrix}&\mapsto&\mathrm{rowsp}\begin{pmatrix}\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\phi(u_{1})&\dots&\phi(u_{s})\end{array}\end{pmatrix}.\end{array}

(2)

On the other hand,
\phi
is also useful to embed
\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})
into
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
By using it, we obtain the following group monomorphism

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\psi:&\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})&\longrightarrow&\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\\
&\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&\cdots&a_{1s}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{s1}&\cdots&a_{ss}\end{pmatrix}&\mapsto&\begin{pmatrix}\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\phi(a_{11})&\cdots&\phi(a_{1s})\\
\hline\cr\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\hline\cr\phi(a_{s1})&\cdots&\phi(a_{ss})\end{array}\end{pmatrix}.\end{array}

(3)

2.2
Subspace codes

We present here the main results of subspace codes that we need to understand this work.

The Grassmannian
\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
can be seen as a metric space endowed with the following
subspace distance
(see
[
16
]
)

d_{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})=\dim(\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{V})-\dim(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{V})=2(k-\dim(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{V})),

for all
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a non-empty subset of
\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
, then we say that
\mathcal{C}
is a
constant dimension code
and its
minimum distance
is defined as

d_{S}(\mathcal{C})=\min\left\{d_{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})\ |\ \mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{C},\ \mathcal{U}\neq\mathcal{V}\right\}\leq\begin{cases}2k,&\text{if $2k\leq n$},\\
2(n-k),&\text{if $2k\geq n$}.\end{cases}

(4)

If
|\mathcal{C}|=1
, we put
d_{S}(\mathcal{C})=0
; in any other case,
d_{S}(\mathcal{C})>0
. When the upper bound in expression (
4
) is attained, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is a
constant dimension code of maximum distance
.
Note that
d_{S}(\mathcal{C})=2k
only if
2k\leq n
and all the codewords in
\mathcal{C}
intersect trivially.
This class of codes of maximum distance are known as
partial spread codes
, and they were introduced in
[
12
]
as a generalization of the class of
spread codes
, previously studied in
[
19
]
.
The code
\mathcal{C}
will be a spread code, if the subspaces in
\mathcal{C}
pairwise intersect trivially, and they cover the whole space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
(see
[
13
]
).
These codes only occur in the case where
k\mid n
and have cardinality
\frac{q^{n}-1}{q^{k}-1}
.
In this way, it can be said that spread codes are partial spreads of maximum size, since the size of a partial spread code of dimension
k
(or
k
-partial spread code) is always upper bounded by

\frac{q^{n}-q^{m}}{q^{k}-1},

(5)

where
m
is the reminder obtained dividing
n
by
k
(see
[
12
]
).
Notice that any code of lines
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{q}(1,n)
with
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 2
is in particular a partial spread code with size
|\mathcal{C}|\leq\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
, whereas
\mathcal{G}_{q}(1,n)
can be seen as the spread of lines of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
These two types of codes will be useful in the following sections.

In case that we have
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
with
2k\geq n
, then we can consider the
dual code
of
\mathcal{C}
, that is, the set
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}=\{\mathcal{V}^{\perp}\ |\ \mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{C}\}
, which is a constant dimension code of dimension
n-k
with the same cardinality and distance as
\mathcal{C}
(see
[
16
]
).
In particular, if
d_{S}(\mathcal{C})=2(n-k)
, then
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
is an
(n-k)
-partial spread code and the size of
\mathcal{C}
can be also upper bounded in terms of expression (
5
).
This is the reason why from now on we consider that
2k\leq n
.

An important class of constant dimension codes are those called

orbit codes
, introduced in
[
27
]
.
These codes are defined as orbits under the action of some subgroup of the general linear group.
Consider
V\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
a full-rank matrix generating a subspace
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{rowsp}(V)\in\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
.
The map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)\times\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)\\
(\mathcal{V},A)&\mapsto&\mathcal{V}\cdot A=\mathrm{rowsp}(VA),\end{array}

defines a group action from the right of the general linear group on
\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
(see
[
27
]
) since it is independent of the choice of the generating matrix
V
.
Given a subgroup
\mathbf{H}
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, the
orbit code

\mathrm{Orb}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})
is the orbit generated by the action of
\mathbf{H}
on
\mathcal{V}
, that is,

\mathrm{Orb}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})=\{\mathcal{V}\cdot A\ |\ A\in\mathbf{H}\}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n).

The size of this orbit code is
|\mathrm{Orb}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})|=\frac{|\mathbf{H}|}{|\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})|}
, where
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})=\{A\in\mathbf{H}\ |\ \mathcal{V}\cdot A=\mathcal{V}\}
is the stabilizer subgroup of the subspace
\mathcal{V}
under the action of
\mathbf{H}
.
If
\mathbf{H}=\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})
, then
\mathrm{Orb}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})=\{\mathcal{V}\}
and
d_{S}(\mathrm{Orb}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V}))=0
.
In any other case, the minimum distance of the orbit code
\mathrm{Orb}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})
can be calculated as (see
[
27
]
)

d_{S}(\mathrm{Orb}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V}))=\min\{d_{S}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}\cdot A)\ |\ A\in\mathbf{H}\setminus\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})\}.

Recall that we are assuming
n=ks
and consider the field reduction map
\varphi
defined in expression (
2
) which maps lines of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}^{s}
into vector subspaces of dimension
k
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
Since
\varphi
is injective, it preserves intersections and, therefore, it follows that
d_{S}(\varphi(\mathcal{C}))=kd_{S}(\mathcal{C})=2k
, for any
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{q^{k}}(1,s)
.
In other words,
\varphi(\mathcal{C})
is a
k
-partial spread of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, for any code of lines
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}^{s}
.
In particular, if we consider the spread of all lines of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}^{s}
, it turns out that

\varphi(\mathcal{G}_{q^{k}}(1,s))\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n),

(6)

is a
k
-spread of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, which is called the
Desarguesian

k
-spread of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
(see
[
17
]
).
Originally due to Segre (see
[
24
]
), in the network coding setting, this construction appears for the first time in
[
19
]
.

Notice that the field reduction map
\varphi
, together with the group monomorphism
\psi
defined in expression (
3
), allow us to establish a relation between the group action of
\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})
on
\mathcal{G}_{q^{k}}(1,s)
and the group action of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
on
\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n)
as follows (see
[
2
,
21
]
)

\varphi(\mathcal{V}\cdot A)=\varphi(\mathcal{V})\cdot\psi(A),

(7)

for all
\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{G}_{q^{k}}(1,s)
and
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})
.
In particular, when we consider a subgroup
\mathbf{H}\leq\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})
and an orbit code
\mathrm{Orb}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V})
, for some
\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{G}_{q^{k}}(1,s)
, then

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathrm{Orb}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathcal{V}))

\displaystyle=\{\varphi(\mathcal{V}\cdot A)\ |\ A\in\mathbf{H}\}

\displaystyle=\{\varphi(\mathcal{V})\cdot\psi(A)\ |\ \psi(A)\in\psi(\mathbf{H})\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Orb}_{\psi(\mathbf{H})}(\varphi(\mathcal{V}))\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{q}(k,n).

(8)

3
Our construction

In the rest of the paper, we consider
n,k,s,t
positive integers such that
n=ks
, with
s=2t
and
\gcd(t,q^{k}-1)=1
.

The main purpose of this section is to show how we can construct the Desarguesian
k
-spread code of
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
given in expression (
6
) starting from the action of a specific Abelian non-cyclic subgroup
\bar{\mathbf{H}}
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
To do so, in Subsection
3.1
we will work first with an appropriate subgroup
\mathbf{H}
of
\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})
and its action on lines of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}^{s}
and then we will use the field reduction technique explained in Subsection
2.2
to build our subgroup
\bar{\mathbf{H}}
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
Then we will construct a
k
-partial spread of
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
as an orbit generated by the action of
\bar{\mathbf{H}}
.
Following this, in Subsection
3.2
we will explain how to achieve the whole
k
-spread.

3.1
A
k
-partial spread with an orbital structure

In this subsection we provide an explicit description of a
k
-partial spread of
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
with an orbital structure.
For this purpose, firstly, we will construct an Abelian non-cyclic subgroup
\mathbf{H}
of
\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})
with a suitable action on certain lines of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}^{s}
.

Let
M_{t}\in\mathrm{GL}_{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})
be the companion matrix of a primitive polynomial of degree
t
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}
.
As we explain in Subsection
2.1
, we have that
\mathbb{F}_{q^{kt}}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}[M_{t}]
and, therefore, the order of
M_{t}
is
o(M_{t})=q^{kt}-1
.
Consider
C:=M_{t}^{q^{k}-1}
whose multiplicative order, clearly, is
r:=\frac{q^{kt}-1}{q^{k}-1}
; and, let
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}
be a primitive element, whose multiplicative order is
q^{k}-1
.

We construct the matrices
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})
given by

h_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}C&I_{t}\\
0_{t\times t}&\alpha I_{t}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad h_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha I_{t}&-I_{t}\\
0_{t\times t}&C\end{pmatrix}.

(9)

The following result will be useful in order to compute the multiplicative order of
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
.

Lemma 1
.

For any positive integer
\ell
, one has that

\gcd(\ell,q-1)=1\ \text{if and only if}\ \gcd\left(\frac{q^{\ell}-1}{q-1},q-1\right)=1.

Proof.

First we notice that if
p
is a prime dividing
q-1
, then
q\equiv 1\pmod{p}
and so
q^{i}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
for
i\geq 1
.
Therefore,

\frac{q^{\ell}-1}{q-1}=q^{\ell-1}+q^{\ell-2}+\cdots+q+1\equiv\ell\pmod{p}.

Then, it is clear that

p\mid\ell
if, and only if,
p\mid\frac{q^{\ell}-1}{q-1}

and the result follows.
∎

Note that, since we are assuming that
\gcd(t,q^{k}-1)=1
, Lemma
1
states that
\gcd(r,q^{k}-1)=1
.

The following result is a generalization of Lemmas 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3 of
[
7
]
.
The proof runs analogously, and thus we omitted it.

Lemma 2
.

Consider the matrices
h_{1},h_{2}
defined in expression (
9
).
The following statements are satisfied:

(a)

The multiplicative order of
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
is
q^{kt}-1
.

(b)

The matrices
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
commute.

(c)

\langle h_{1}\rangle\cap\langle h_{2}\rangle=\{I_{s}\}
.

From the previous lemma, it follows that the group generated by the matrices
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
is an Abelian non-cyclic subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}})
.
Let us denote it us
\mathbf{H}:=\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle=\langle h_{1}\rangle\langle h_{2}\rangle
.
Thus, the order of
\mathbf{H}
is
(q^{kt}-1)^{2}
and we can express its elements as,

\mathbf{H}=\langle h_{1}\rangle\langle h_{2}\rangle=\left\{h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{b}\ |\ 1\leq a,b\leq q^{kt}-1\right\}.

Moreover, any arbitrary element of
\mathbf{H}
has the following matrix expression

h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{b}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^{b}C^{a}&D_{a,b}\\
0_{t\times t}&\alpha^{a}C^{b}\end{pmatrix}

(10)

where

D_{a,b}:=\sum_{j=1}^{a}\alpha^{j-1}C^{a+b-j}-\sum_{j=1}^{b}\alpha^{j-1}C^{a+b-j}

(11)

and

a,b\in\{1,\dots,q^{kt}-1\}
.
We will denote by
(h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{b})_{i}
the
i
-th row of the matrix
h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{b}\in\mathbf{H}
, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,s\}
.

Let us denote
\text{$e$}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}^{s}
the
i
-th canonical vector, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,s\}
.
We are interested in the action of the group
\mathbf{H}
on the lines of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}
generated by
\text{$e$}_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
.
For this reason, we analyse the corresponding stabilizer subgroup.
First, we need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Consider the matrix
D_{a,b}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}[M_{t}]
defined in expression (
11
), for
a,b\in\{1,\dots,q^{kt}-1
}
, then

D_{a,b}=0_{t\times t}\ \text{if and only if}\ a=b.

Proof.

Clearly, if
a=b
then the matrix
D_{a,b}
is the null matrix.
Conversely, assume that
D_{a,b}=0_{t\times t}
and let us see that necessarily
a=b
.

Arguing by contradiction, if
a>b
, then we can simplify the matrix
D_{a,b}
in the following way

\displaystyle 0_{t\times t}

\displaystyle=D_{a,b}=\sum_{j=b+1}^{a}\alpha^{j-1}C^{a+b-j}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{b}C^{a-1}+\alpha^{b+1}C^{a-2}+\cdots+\alpha^{a-1}C^{b}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{b}C^{b}\left(C^{a-b-1}+\alpha C^{a-b-2}+\cdots+\alpha^{a-b-1}I_{t}\right).

Notice that
\alpha^{b}C^{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}[M_{t}]
is a regular matrix, since it is non-zero and
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}[M_{t}]
is a field.
Thus, we obtain that

\displaystyle 0_{t\times t}

\displaystyle=C^{a-b-1}+\alpha C^{a-b-2}+\cdots+\alpha^{a-b-1}I_{t}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{a-b-1}((\alpha^{-1}C)^{a-b-1}+(\alpha^{-1}C)^{a-b-2}+\cdots+(\alpha^{-1}C)+I_{t}).

As a consequence, the right factor of this last product must be zero.
But this is a geometric series of ratio
\alpha^{-1}C
.
Let us see that
\alpha^{-1}C\neq I_{t}
.
If
\alpha^{-1}C=I_{t}
, then
C=\alpha I_{t}
and
r=o(C)=o(\alpha)=q^{k}-1
.
But this is a contradiction with the fact that
\gcd(q^{k}-1,r)=1
.
Thus, we can obtain the sum of this geometric series and write

\displaystyle 0_{t\times t}

\displaystyle=(\alpha^{-1}C)^{a-b-1}+(\alpha^{-1}C)^{a-b-2}+\cdots+(\alpha^{-1}C)+I_{t}

\displaystyle=\left((\alpha^{-1}C)^{a-b}-I\right)\left(\alpha^{-1}C-I\right)^{-1}.

Therefore, we obtain that
C^{a-b}=\alpha^{a-b}I_{t}
.
It means that

\frac{o(C)}{\gcd\left(o(C),a-b\right)}=\frac{o(\alpha)}{\gcd\left(o(\alpha),a-b\right)},

that is,

\frac{r}{\gcd\left(r,a-b\right)}=\frac{q^{k}-1}{\gcd\left(q^{k}-1,a-b\right)}=1

since
\gcd(q^{k}-1,r)=1
.
Thus, both
r
and
q^{k}-1
must divide
a-b
.
And then
q^{kt}-1=r(q^{k}-1)
divides
a-b>0
.
But this is not possible, since
a,b\in\{1,\dots,q^{kt}-1\}
.

If
a<b
then we have an analogous case to the previous one.
∎

As a consequence of this result, we are able to obtain the stabilizer subgroup of
\mathbf{H}
corresponding to the lines generated by
\text{$e$}_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
.

Theorem 1
.

For all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
, one has that

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathrm{rowsp}(\text{$e$}_{i}))=\langle(h_{1}h_{2})^{r}\rangle=\langle\alpha I_{s}\rangle.

Proof.

Let
h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{b}\in\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathrm{rowsp}(\text{$e$}_{i}))
, for some
a,b\in\{1,\dots,q^{kt}-1\}
.
Then we have that
\mathrm{rowsp}(\text{$e$}_{i})=\mathrm{rowsp}((h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{b})_{i})
.
And this happens exactly when the
i
-th row of the matrix
h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{b}
has the form
\alpha^{m}\text{$e$}_{i}
, for some
m\in\{1,\dots,q^{k}-1\}
.
Therefore, since
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
, considering the general expression of any element of
\mathbf{H}
given in expression (
10
),
it follows that the
i
-th row of the submatrix
D_{a,b}
of
h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{b}
is zero.
Since
D_{a,b}
is a matrix in the field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}[M_{t}]
, it follows that it must be the null matrix.
As a consequence, from Lemma
3
, we can write

h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{b}=h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{a}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^{a}C^{a}&0_{t\times t}\\
0_{t\times t}&\alpha^{a}C^{a}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{for some $a\in\{1,\dots,q^{kt}-1\}$}.

Moreover, the
i
-th row of
h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{a}
has the form
\alpha^{m}\text{$e$}_{i}
for some
m\in\{1,\dots,q^{k}-1\}
.
In particular, the
i
-th row of
\alpha^{a}C^{a}-\alpha^{m}I_{t}
is zero and then this is a non-regular matrix of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}[M_{t}]
.
Thus,
\alpha^{a}C^{a}-\alpha^{m}I_{t}=0_{t\times t}
, that is,
\alpha^{a}C^{a}=\alpha^{m}I_{t}=(\alpha I_{t})^{m}
.
Then we have

h_{1}^{a}h_{2}^{a}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^{a}C^{a}&0_{t\times t}\\
0_{t\times t}&\alpha^{a}C^{a}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(\alpha I_{t})^{m}&0_{t\times t}\\
0_{t\times t}&(\alpha I_{t})^{m}\end{pmatrix}\in\langle\alpha I_{s}\rangle.

Therefore, we obtain the equality

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathrm{rowsp}(\text{$e$}_{i}))=\langle\alpha I_{s}\rangle,

since the other inclusion trivially holds.
Finally, since
o(C)=r
, one has that
(h_{1}h_{2})^{r}=\alpha^{r}I_{s}
and then
\langle(h_{1}h_{2})^{r}\rangle\subseteq\langle\alpha I_{s}\rangle
.
Thus, we obtain the equality, since both groups have order
q^{k}-1
.
∎

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we have dealt with the orbital construction of a
k
-dimensional spread in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, where
n
is an even number and
k
divides
n
, using a non-cyclic Abelian group.
Our results generalise those obtained by Chen and Liang
in
[
7
]
for
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2k}
.
However, the techniques we have used are new and not easily detached from this work.
We have proceeded in two stages.
First, we have obtained a family of orbit codes of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
having dimension
k
and maximum distance.
For this, we have constructed a non-cyclic Abelian group of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and selected appropriate
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
using the field reduction technique.
Afterwards, from each previously given orbit code, we have managed to achieve a
k
-spread of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
by adding two specific subsets of subspaces, one of them with an orbital structure.
