Apheresis platelets modulate myeloid dendritic cell immune responses
Authors: Mengyuan Ding, Yan Li, Qi Xiao, Mingyuan Wang, Xiao Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlatelet transfusion is important in clinical treatment, especially for patients with hematological diseases. In this study, an in vitro transfusion model was established to explore the effects of apheresis platelets on the immune responses of myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs) and a specialized subset of DCs with blood DC antigen (BDCA3+). The results demonstrated that during viral infection simulation, exposure to apheresis platelets reduced CD83 expression on mDCs. However, no significant effect on the expression of surface antigens on BDCA3+ DCs was observed when they were exposed to apheresis platelets. In addition, exposure did not affect the production of inflammatory mediators by either type of DC. Apheresis platelets can alter the overall leukocyte inflammatory response of recipients. The levels of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-8, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) were increased upon exposure to apheresis platelets alone. In the simulation of viral infection, the levels of IL-8, MCP-1, IFN-γ and IL-12p70 were increased, and the IP-10 level was inhibited. In the bacterial infection simulation, the IL-8 and MCP-1 levels were increased, whereas the IL-10 and IP-10 levels were decreased. In conclusion, apheresis platelet transfusion may inhibit the maturation and activation of mDCs during viral infection and enhance the overall leukocyte inflammatory response in recipients.Keywords: Apheresis platelets, Blood dendritic cell antigen 3, Immune modulation, Myeloid dendritic cells, TransfusionSubject terms: Cell biology, Immunology

Introduction

Platelets are widely used in blood transfusion therapy (primarily for patients with hematological diseases). Platelets are a key treatment modality for maintaining normal blood coagulation function in patients and preventing severe bleeding. Moreover, platelets are the primary cellular mediators of hemostasis and are also closely associated with various inflammatory processes. They act as circulating sentinels by expressing Toll-like receptors (TLRs) to bind pathogens. Activated platelets also secrete and express numerous proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory molecules, thereby directly influencing adaptive immune responses1. Transfusion-related immune modulation (TRIM) associated with platelets potentially increases the risk of infection in transfusion patients and reduces graft rejection and tumor recurrence2. This immune modulation can be beneficial or harmful3–5. The inflammatory effects of platelets themselves and the immune modulation that is elicited after blood transfusion have become increasingly recognized3,4,5.

Dendritic cells (DCs) are the most effective antigen-presenting cells for initiating and regulating immune responses; additionally, they play a multifaceted role in immune regulation6. DCs are important for orchestrating both innate and adaptive immunity through cognate and cytokine-mediated mechanisms7.

In human peripheral blood, DCs are broadly divided into two major categories based on their lineage, including myeloid (CD11c+) and plasmacytoid (CD123+) DCs; moreover, these cells are further divided into the following four subgroups: myeloid blood DC antigen BDCA1+ and BDCA3+ DCs, as well as plasmacytoid BDCA2+ and BDCA4+ DCs8,9. The specialized functions of these cells are derived from the differential expression of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which recognize pathogen/danger-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs/DAMPs)10. BDCA3+ DCs, which represent a rare mDC subgroup, are equipped with PRRs that are conducive to virus recognition11,12. Upon PAMP/DAMP recognition, DCs are activated, undergo phenotypic shifts to present antigens, initiate downstream PRR signaling, drive maturation, regulate costimulatory molecules, and secrete cytokines/chemokines,10,13,14.

Platelets and platelet-associated molecules (which function as key inducers of monocyte maturation into cross-presenting cells resembling DCs) are integral to platelet-dependent signaling cascades that are crucial for innate immune activation and adaptive immune functions15. Currently, few studies have explored how platelet transfusions affect mDCs, with even fewer studies focusing on the rare BDCA3+ DC subtype among mDCs. In this study, an in vitro model of apheresis platelet transfusion was used to simulate the impact of apheresis platelets on immune responses in mDCs and BDCA3+ DCs in different transfusion contexts. We investigated the effects of these platelets on DC maturation and activation; cytokine and chemokine secretion; and the overall inflammatory response in transfusion “recipients”, thereby providing new information on the immunomodulatory role of platelets.

Materials and methods

Ethics declarations

All of the experimental protocols were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Suzhou Central Blood Station (No. 6, 2023). All of the methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from the donors who participated in the study.

Apheresis platelet collection

For platelet donors undergoing apheresis, platelet collection strictly followed the standard operating procedures for apheresis platelets and the instructions of a blood cell separator (Hemonetics, USA). The following requirements for platelet quality were employed. First, apheresis platelets should appear as a yellow cloudy liquid upon visual inspection, without abnormal color, protein precipitation, air bubbles or severe chylous appearance being observed. Moreover, the blood bag should be intact, and at least 15 cm of the heat-sealed catheter filled with platelets should be retained. The volume of apheresis platelets (with a storage period of 5 days) should be between 250 and 300 ml. At the end of the storage period, the pH should be between 6.4 and 7.4. Furthermore, the platelet content should be ≥ 2.5 × 101 platelets per bag, and the degree of leukocyte contamination should be ≤ 5.0 × 10⁶ leukocytes per bag. Erythrocyte contamination should be ≤ 8.0 × 10⁶ erythrocytes per bag. Additionally, no bacterial growth should be observed. A blood cell separator was used to perform platelet apheresis.

Blood collection for the extracorporeal transfusion model

Fresh whole blood from healthy donors was used as the blood of the recipients in the extracorporeal blood transfusion model. Samples were collected in blood collection tubes (Becton Dickinson BD] Biosciences, USA). Sodium citrate was used as an anticoagulant.

Establishment of the extracorporeal blood transfusion model

This study was designed to model responses to standard clinical apheresis platelet transfusion. A schematic figure for our study design model is shown in Fig. 1. A whole-blood model with a 25% blood exchange volume (equivalent to 2–3 units) was adopted. The apheresis platelet transfusion was simulated by coculturing the “recipient” whole blood (6 ml) with ABO blood group-compatible apheresis platelets (3 ml) and RPMI 1640 medium (3 ml; Invitrogen Corporation, USA) for 5.5 h (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Moreover, polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (polyI:C; 50 μg/ml, high molecular weight; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) or lipopolysaccharide (1 μg/mL, Escherichia coli [055:B5]; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to simulate potential viral polyI:C or bacterial (lipopolysaccharide) infections, respectively. In the repeated cultures, matched “no transfusion (no apheresis platelets)” controls were established, with RPMI 1640 replacing the volume of apheresis platelets (including only polyI:C and lipopolysaccharide controls). For the experiments evaluating the levels of intracellular inflammatory mediators, a transporter inhibitor (1 μg/ml, Golgi blocker; BD Biosciences, USA) was added during the last 4.5 h of culture. The cells were collected, and the supernatants were also collected from the cultures without the transporter inhibitor (stored at − 80 °C for subsequent cytometric bead array [CBA] analysis). RBCs were lysed (10 min, 22 °C; FACS lyse; BD Biosciences, USA). The remaining leukocytes were washed three times in 3% fetal bovine serum in phosphate-buffered saline (3% FBS/PBS; both from Invitrogen, USA) and then subjected to flow cytometry.

Schematic figure describing the study design model.

mDC and BDCA3+ DC surface antigen expression and intracellular inflammatory mediator production following apheresis platelet exposure

Leukocytes from the whole-blood transfusion model were stained (15 min, 4 °C) with the following DC panel of mouse anti-human monoclonal antibodies: lineage (Lin) markers CD34-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), BDCA3-brilliant violet (BV)605 (mAb; all from Biolegend, USA), and CD45-BV510 and CD11c-brilliant blue (BB)700 (mAb; BD biosciences, USA). To assess surface antigen expression, leukocytes were also stained with CD80-allophycocyanin (APC) (mAb; Biolegend, USA), CD40-phycoerythrin (PE-CY7), CD83-PE and CD86-BV421 (mAb; all from BD Biosciences, USA). The cells were subsequently washed and resuspended in 3% FBS/PBS for flow cytometric analysis.

For assessment of intracellular mediators, leukocytes were permeabilized (10 min, 22 °C; FACS Perm; BD Biosciences, USA) following staining with the abovementioned DC panel and labeled with an intracellular PE-conjugated mAb targeting IL-6 (mAb; Thermo, USA), IL-8, IL-12 (mAb; both from BD Biosciences, USA), TNF-α-BV421 (mAb; BD biosciences, USA), IP-10-APC (polyI:C panel) (mAb; Biolegend, USA) or IL-10-BV786 (LPS panel) (mAb; BD biosciences, USA) for 30 min (22 °C in the dark). The cells were subsequently washed (3% FBS/PBS) and resuspended in cell stabilizing fixative (BD Bioscience, USA) for flow cytometry.

Side scatter light (SSC) and CD45 were used to exclude granulocytes and lymphocytes. The selected monocytes were displayed with Lin and CD11c, and Lin⁻CD11c⁺ cells were selected as mDCs (Lin⁻CD45⁺CD11c⁺). Lin⁻CD11c⁺ cells were subsequently used for gating to select the BDCA3⁺ dendritic cell subset (Lin⁻CD45⁺CD11c⁺BDCA3⁺).

The median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of each surface antigen and inflammatory mediator expressed by mDCs and BDCA3⁺ dendritic cells was measured. On average, 30,000 mDCs and 400 BDCA3⁺ dendritic cells were analyzed for each marker. The presented data were obtained by using a two-step method. First, the “transfusion” data of apheresis platelets (using the ratio of MFI) were normalized relative to the matched “no transfusion” controls. Specifically, the data obtained after exposure to apheresis platelets alone were normalized relative to the matched control without apheresis platelets (RPMI only); additionally, the data of apheresis platelets + polyI:C were normalized relative to the polyI:C-only control, and the data of apheresis platelets + LPS were normalized relative to the LPS-only control. The MFI ratio was subsequently calculated as the logarithm of base 2 (log₂). This method effectively “equalized” the data around zero to demonstrate the fold change. Consequently, a twofold increase was observed for + 1, and a twofold decrease was observed for—1 compared with the matched controls (RPMI only, polyI:C only, or LPS only). This method establishes a baseline for each donor to account for the differences between the donors. The number of experimental repetitions for evaluating the expression of surface antigens and intracellular mediators was established as follows: n = 10 for the apheresis platelet-only group, n = 5 for the apheresis platelet + polyI:C group, and n = 5 for the apheresis platelet + LPS group.

Assessment of the inflammatory response following exposure to apheresis platelets.

The concentrations of inflammatory mediators (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-8, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IL-12p70, IP-10, IFN-α, IFN-γ, MCP-1, MIP-1α, and MIP-1β) in the culture supernatant of the blood transfusion model were detected by using a flow cytometry bead array (CBA) (n = 10 in the apheresis platelet only group and n = 5 in the apheresis platelet + polyI:C or LPS group). The culture supernatant was incubated with capture beads for 1 h (22 °C in the dark). Afterward, PE-labeled detection reagents were added and incubated for an additional 2 h (22 °C in the dark). At the end of the incubation, the unbound detection reagents were removed via the addition of washing buffer, followed by centrifugation (200×g, 5 min). The supernatant was removed, and the bead pellet was resuspended in washing buffer for flow cytometry analysis. The concentrations were determined according to standard curves performed in parallel.

Flow cytometry and statistical analysis

Three laser flow cytometers (NovoCyte3130; Agilent Technologies, USA) were used, and the data were analyzed with NovoExpress software (Agilent Technologies, USA). All of the statistical analyses and representations of the graphs were generated by using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to assess the differences in surface antigen expression and inflammatory mediator production following apheresis platelet exposure (no transfusion [RPMI only] vs. D2 apheresis platelets vs. D5 apheresis platelets; polyI:C only vs. D2 apheresis platelets + polyI:C vs. D5 apheresis platelets + polyI:C; and LPS only vs. D2 apheresis platelets + LPS vs. D5 apheresis platelets + LPS). Moreover, p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Exposure to apheresis platelets downregulated mDC surface antigen expression in a viral infection model

The immunomodulatory effects of apheresis platelets on the maturation and activation of mDCs and BDCA3+ DCs were evaluated by establishing an in vitro whole-blood model of apheresis platelet transfusion.

With respect to mDCs, the coculturing of apheresis platelets with polyI:C to simulate potential viral infection caused a decrease in CD83 expression on mDCs (p = 0.0404; Fig. 2b). In the models of apheresis in which platelets were exposed alone or via apheresis during bacterial infection, the effects on mDC activation and maturation were not significant (Fig. 2a, c, d).

mDC and BDCA3+ DC surface antigen expression profiles following exposure to D2 and D5 apheresis platelets. Flow cytometric assessment of mDC (a–d) and BDCA3+ DC (e–h) expression of maturation and activation molecules after exposure to D2 or D5 APs with or without polyI:C or LPS for 5.5 h (n = 10 for APs only, n = 5 for APs + polyI:C, n = 5 for APs + LPS). Data were normalized (log2) to the matched “no transfusion” control (dotted line at zero). The bars indicate the means ± standard errors of the means (SEMs). The data were analyzed by using ANOVA (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01) with Tukey’s post hoc test (a, no transfusion vs. D2 or D5).

For BDCA3+ DCs, no significant immunomodulation was noted after exposure to apheresis platelets or in the models of apheresis platelet transfusion during viral and bacterial infections (Fig. 2e-h).

Exposure to apheresis platelets had no significant effect on mDC or BDCA3+ DC production of inflammatory mediators

We measured a set of inflammatory mediators produced by mDCs and BDCA3+ dendritic cells to further explore the potential impact of apheresis platelet transfusion on the recipient’s immune response. Based on the different responses of mDCs and BDCA3+ dendritic cells to viral or bacterial stimulation, two groups of inflammatory mediators were measured. Moreover, IP-10 was used in the viral model, and IL-10 was used in the bacterial model. The results revealed that, regardless of whether apheresis platelets were present alone or in the viral or bacterial models, there was no significant effect detected on the production of inflammatory mediators by mDCs and BDCA3+ dendritic cells (Fig. 3 a–j).

mDC and BDCA3+ DC inflammatory profiles following exposure to D2 and D5 apheresis platelets. Intracellular flow cytometric assessment of mDC (a–e) and BDCA3+ DC (f–j) production of cytokines and chemokines after exposure to D2 or D5 APs with or without polyI:C or LPS for 6 h (n = 10 for APs only, n = 5 for APs + polyI:C, n = 5 for APs + LPS). Two panels were assessed, wherein IP-10 in the viral (polyI:C) infection model was replaced with IL-10 when mimicking bacterial (LPS) infection. Data were normalized (log2) to the matched “no transfusion” control (dotted line at zero). The bars indicate the means ± SEMs. The data were analyzed by using ANOVA (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01) with Tukey’s post hoc test (a, no transfusion vs. D2 or D5; b, D2 vs. D5).

The impact of exposure to apheresis platelets on the overall leukocyte inflammatory response

The impact of apheresis platelets on the overall leukocyte inflammatory response was detected in the culture supernatant of the infusion model (Fig. 4a–m). Simple contact with apheresis platelets increased the production of TNF-α (P = 0.0426), IL-8 (P = 0.006) and MCP-1 (P = 0.0027) (Fig. 4a, c, f). When viral infection was simulated, exposure to apheresis platelets increased the production of IL-8 (P = 0.0133), MCP-1 (P = 0.0039), IL-12p70 (P = 0.0424) and IFN-γ (P = 0.0052) (Fig. 4c, f, l, m) and inhibited the production of IP-10 (Fig. 4d, P = 0.022). When simulating bacterial infection, after exposure to apheresis platelets, the IL-8 (P = 0.0162) and MCP-1 (P = 0.0087) levels were increased (Fig. 4c, f), whereas the IP-10 (P = 0.0197) and IL-10 (P = 0.0057) levels were decreased (Fig. 4d, e). After the platelets were exposed to apheresis for 5 days (D5), IL-8 production was further increased.

Overall leukocyte inflammatory profile following exposure to D2 and D5 apheresis platelets. Peripheral leukocytes in the whole-blood model were exposed to D2 or D5 apheresis platelets with or without polyI:C or LPS (a–m). Cytokines and chemokines released into the culture supernatant were measured (n = 10 for AP only, n = 5 for AP + polyI:C, n = 5 for AP + LPS). Data were normalized (log2) to the matched “no transfusion” control (dotted line at zero). The bars indicate the means ± SEMs. The data were analyzed by using ANOVA (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01) with Tukey’s post hoc test (a, no transfusion vs. D2 or D5; b, D2 vs. D5).

Discussion

The mechanisms underlying TRIM are difficult to investigate; however, the role that platelets themselves may play in this immune modulatory phenomenon after transfusion is increasingly being acknowledged16,17. By using confocal microscopy in conjunction with immunofluorescence, researchers have revealed platelet-monocyte interactions, the subcellular localization of specific molecules, and the nuclear translocation of key signaling factors. Activated platelets form “adhesion synapses” with monocytes, wherein P-selectin molecules likely bind to P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL1) on the monocyte surface, thus forming a “bridging” structure at the contact interface and culminating in the formation of a synaptic junction that directs the transformation of monocytes into cross-presenting APCs. Furthermore, platelets can significantly induce the nuclear translocation of NF-κB in monocytes, thereby enabling them to acquire DC-like antigen-presenting capabilities (i.e., differentiation into phDCs)18.

DC maturation and activation are the key mechanisms through which these cells perform immune functions. Activated platelets release various effective inflammatory compounds that may regulate DC maturation and function19–22. Activated platelets release substantial amounts of TGF-β1 and serve as a major source of TGF-β1, which induces dendritic cell (DC) tolerance23–26. Research has revealed that TGF-β1-modified DCs (TGF-β1-DCs) exhibit enhanced immature features with reduced MHC-II/CD80/CD86/CD40 expression. These cells potently induce **CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs** by upregulating Foxp3 expression in Th0 cells. LPS-stimulated TGF-β1-DCs exhibit diminished IL-12 production, thus suggesting their anti-inflammatory potential27,28. Moreover, TGF-β1 promotes T4reg and T8reg differentiation and is positively correlated with the formation of PTCA after platelet activation and interaction with T cells. PTCA and Tregs participate in immunomodulation; however, excessive activation may increase the risk of infection29.

Glaser and colleagues demonstrated that platelet contamination impairs the yield of DCs after in vitro culture and that allogeneic platelets can shift the production of DC cytokines to the immunotolerance type19. In a viral infection model, Ki KK et al. reported that contact with a platelet concentrate reduces CD40 and CD86 expression in mDCs, along with reducing CD40, CD80 and CD83 expression in BDCA3+ DCs. Stored platelets express significant levels of CD40L, which has been observed to trigger proinflammatory pathways via interactions with different types of leukocytes30,31. More extensive immunomodulatory situations for mDCs and BDCA3+ dendritic cells were observed in the other two models utilized in the previously mentioned studies, including sole contact with platelet concentrate and contact with platelet concentrate in the context of bacterial infection32. Our research indicates that apheresis platelets inhibit the maturation of DCs in a viral infection model. This inhibition reduces CD83 expression, thus possibly resulting in DCs being unable to effectively activate T lymphocytes and impairing the signal transduction that is necessary for immune responses. This scenario may lead to persistent viral infection. However, exposure to apheresis platelets does not significantly regulate the maturation and activation of BDCA3⁺ DCs. Both of the aforementioned studies demonstrated that dendritic cell (DC) activation leads to platelet inhibition, although the inhibition was more extensive in their study. This result may be due to differing platelet concentrations and the fact that platelet concentrates (unlike single-component apheresis platelets) contain more residual WBCs. Moreover, due to the fact that WBC counts are positively correlated with TGF-β1 levels, they have a greater effect on DCs33. In a model facilitating intercellular interactions, Hamzeh-Koghnissi et al. reported that platelet concentrates do not regulate the phenotype of monocyte-derived dendritic cells34. Kissel et al. reported that contact with freshly isolated resting platelets does not affect CD40, CD83 or CD86 expression in monocyte-derived dendritic cells19. These results differ from our data, which is possibly due to the use of different models. Our study specifically used a whole blood culture-based transfusion model (instead of the isolated cell model utilized in the previously mentioned studies19,34) to better reflect the changes that may occur in transfusion recipients.

We investigated the effects of apheresis platelets on the production of cytokines and chemokines by mDCs and BDCA3+ dendritic cells, and these effects ultimately affect the downstream immune response. We observed that, regardless of whether apheresis platelets were used alone or in the viral or bacterial models, they had no significant effect on the production of inflammatory mediators in mDCs or BDCA3+ DCs. Perros et al. used a similar whole-blood model (only with PC supernatant) and reported that the inflammatory profile of mDCs was minimally regulated 35, which is consistent with our conclusions.

We detected the impact of apheresis platelets on the overall inflammatory response of recipient peripheral leukocytes in a transfusion model, which provides an in vitro characterization of the inflammatory mediators expected in the plasma of patients after apheresis platelet transfusion. We observed that in response to contact with apheresis platelets, the production of TNF-α, IL-8 and MCP-1 increased, and the immunomodulatory ability of platelets also increased, thus promoting inflammatory responses through multiple mechanisms. In the context of simulating viral infection in vitro, contact with apheresis platelets increased the production of IL-8, MCP-1, IFN-γ and IL-12p70 and inhibited the production of IP-10. This mechanism may eliminate viruses by enhancing the recruitment of early inflammatory cells and Th1-type antiviral immune responses while avoiding excessive immune damage through IP-10 inhibition. In the context of bacterial infection, after contact with apheresis platelets, IL-8 and MCP-1 levels increase, whereas IL-10 and IP-10 levels decrease, thus suggesting that a proinflammatory response also occurs. Karin Kissel et al. revealed that transfusion may perturb the production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines in patients, especially those with underlying bacterial infections36. This difference may be caused by differences in the simulated potential inflammatory levels of recipients and platelet transfusion dosages. Some studies have demonstrated an interaction between patients’ underlying inflammatory levels and platelet transfusion dosages. This interaction may affect patient prognosis by influencing the functions of immune cells (including monocytes and dendritic cells), such as cytokine production. This scenario also represents a limitation of this study and requires further research. No correlation between platelet storage time and the immune response was observed in the other cases, except for the finding that the production of IL-8 was increased in the bacterial infection model when the platelets were exposed to apheresis for 5 days (D5) compared with those stored for 2 days (D2). Some studies examining the efficacy of the storage of platelets for up to 7 days are insufficient for analyzing associations between platelet storage time and recipient outcomes (including morbidity and mortality)37,38,39. In summary, platelet transfusion may induce unnecessary inflammatory responses in ordinary patients. However, for patients with infections, platelet transfusion can promote inflammatory responses in the body, thereby resulting in the quick clearance of pathogens.
