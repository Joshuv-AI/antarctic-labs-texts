Geospatial variation of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa: Geographically weighted regression analysis

Introduction

Bottle feeding is the feeding of any liquid, including breast milk or semi-solid food, from a bottle with a nipple/teat to a child aged 0-23 months.1,2 According to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) classification system, a prevalence of 50% or less of exclusive breastfeeding is undesirable. 3 Children aged between 0 and 23 months are still often bottle-fed, despite several efforts to promote breastfeeding as the optimal feeding method for children. 4 Based on WHO guidelines for appropriate breastfeeding for children under two years, children aged 0-5 months should be exclusively breastfed, while those aged 6-23 months should receive both breast milk and additional food. 5

Globally, undernutrition accounts for approximately 45% of child deaths, or 3.1 million annually, with the burden particularly severe in low-income countries. 6 Increasing appropriate complementary feeding could prevent up to 6% of under-five deaths, especially in developing regions. 3 Bottle feeding among children aged 0-23 months influences survival, growth, and development by shaping nutritional status. 7 Poor breastfeeding conditions are closely linked to bottle-feeding practices, 8 which in turn expose children to risks such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, and gastrointestinal infections. 9 Bottle feeding has also been associated with malnutrition, iron deficiency, reduced birth spacing, 10 and long-term oral health problems, particularly when sweetened beverages are used. 5 Extended bottle feeding may contribute to delayed speech and motor development 8 and has been linked to malocclusions in primary dentition. 11 More than half of bottle-fed children present myofunctional alterations, including tongue hypotonicity. 12 Previous studies have identified maternal age, plurality, child age and sex, marital status, education, residence, household head sex, place of delivery, ANC and PNC visits, working status, and wealth index as determinants of bottle-feeding practice.5,7,13,14

However, most of these studies relied on global regression models, limiting their ability to capture local variations in the bottle-feeding practice. Based on our literature review, no study has been conducted using GWR analysis of factors associated with bottle-feeding practice. To address this gap, our study applies GWR to examine spatial heterogeneity in bottle-feeding practices among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa. This methodology is particularly important because GWR accounts for spatial heterogeneity in bottle feeding practices, revealing local variations in determinants that global models overlook. Such spatially nuanced evidence is essential for policymakers to design interventions that are responsive to regional differences in socioeconomic and cultural contexts.

Methods

Study design, settings, and period

This study employed a cross-sectional analytical design using secondary data from nationally representative Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) surveys conducted in East African countries from 2019 to 2024. The analysis focused on children aged 0-23 months, examining geospatial variation in bottle feeding practices. A GWR model was applied to assess spatial heterogeneity in the relationship between bottle feeding and selected explanatory variables. East Africa is a sub-region of Africa that comprises twenty internationally recognized countries. 15 Among those countries, this study used standardized DHS data from seven East African countries, including Kenya, Madagascar, Malawi, Mozambique, Rwanda, Tanzania, and Zambia, as shown in Figure 1. Based on information from the East African DHS, this study employed a GWR analysis approach to predict bottle-feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months.

Data source, study population, and sampling procedures

This study used standardized data from the East African DHS dataset. The bottle-feeding (m38) variable was recoded from the kids’ record (KR) dataset to produce the outcome variable. Countries were included in the analysis if they met three eligibility criteria. First, a DHS survey had to be conducted from 2019 to 2024 to ensure recency and comparability across settings. Second, the dataset needed to contain the relevant outcome variable, namely bottle-feeding practice. Third, the dataset had to provide complete and consistently coded predictor variables as defined by DHS guidelines. Therefore, seven East African countries were selected for this study. Before employing the analysis, we weighted the dataset to restore the representativeness of the sample data. Inclusion criteria: all children aged 0-23 months who were bottle-fed during the day or night before the interview in the selected enumeration areas and whose data were recorded in the dataset were included. Exclusion criteria: all children aged 0-23 months with missing data, zero coordinates, and who died were excluded from the study. A total of 35,206 children aged 0-23 months were initially identified from DHS datasets across seven East African countries. Records with missing information on bottle-feeding practices (n = 6,242) were excluded, and “don’t know” responses were merged with “No,” resulting in an analytic sample of 28,964 children. The DHS employs a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design, and all analyses applied DHS weighting procedures to adjust for sampling probabilities and non-response. After weighting, the final sample size was 28,562 children, which is nationally representative of the population of children aged 0-23 months in the study countries, as shown in Figures 2 and 3. We did not independently calculate the sample size for this study, as the analysis is based on secondary data from the DHS. Therefore, the analytic sample size reflects the DHS Program’s standardized procedures rather than a calculation specific to this research question. However, the DHS sample design provides adequate statistical power for national and regional estimates. The large weighted sample size was considered sufficient to detect meaningful geospatial variation in bottle-feeding practices, given the extensive coverage and robust design of DHS surveys.

Study variables

The dependent variable of this study was bottle-feeding practice (yes/no) of children aged 0-23 months. During the survey, children’s mothers were asked questions about their children under 2 years old who drank from a bottle with a nipple yesterday during the day or night before the interview.5,7,8,13,14 Whereas the independent variables were women’s age, twins/plurality, age of child, sex of children, marital status, women’s education level, residence, sex of household head, place of delivery, ANC visit, PNC visit, working status, and wealth index.5,7,13,14

Data management and analysis

The data were weighted before any statistical analysis was performed to maintain the representativeness of the survey. Data processing and analysis were completed using Microsoft Excel 2019, STATA version 17, ArcGIS version 10.7.1, and SaTScan version 10.1. We used spatial autocorrelation analysis, cluster and outlier analysis, hotspot analysis, interpolation analysis, SaTScan analysis, ordinary least squares analysis, and geographically weighted regression analysis to manage the analysis of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months.

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

The spatial autocorrelation (Global Moran’s I) statistic for this study assessed whether the study areas of child bottle-feeding practice patterns were scattered, clustered, or randomly distributed. A spatial statistic called Moran’s I was used on the whole data set to generate a single output value between -1 and +1 to quantify spatial autocorrelation. 16 The presence of spatial autocorrelation is indicated by a statistically significant Moran’s I (p < 0.05), which leads to the rejection of the null hypothesis.

Hotspot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi* statistic)

Hotspot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi* statistics) was generated to measure the variation in spatial autocorrelation over the research location. The statistical significance of clustering is assessed by computing the Z-score and calculating the p-value. High GI* statistical output is denoted as a “hotspot,” indicating high practice of bottle feeding, whereas low GI* data is denoted as a “coldspot,” indicating low practice of bottle feeding.17,18 Hotspots (high-value clusters) were defined as clusters with Z-scores > 1.96 and p-values < 0.05, whereas coldspots (low-value clusters) were defined as clusters with Z-scores < −1.96 and p-values < 0.05. A p-value ≥ 0.05 would indicate that the cluster is not statistically significant and should not be classified as a hotspot or coldspot. 19

Spatial interpolation

The technique of using known values of bottle feeding practice to estimate values at other unknown points is known as spatial interpolation. Ordinary kriging interpolation was used to estimate the bottle-feeding practice status among children aged 0-23 months for unknown regions of the countries. Because kriging is more accurate than IDW, it helps to reduce bias in the predictions.20,21

Spatial scan analysis

Pure spatial SaTScan statistical analysis was performed to test for the availability of statistically significant spatial clusters of bottle feeding practice. Spatial scan analysis supports the findings detected by the Gettis-Ord analysis and allows the study to report confirming findings. 22 The Bernoulli model was fitted by considering children aged 0-23 months who were bottle-fed as a case and children who did not have the practice of bottle-feeding as a control. Potential clusters of bottle feeding practices were ranked based on their log likelihood ratio (LLR) test with corresponding p-values and relative risk. Clusters with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significant hotspot areas, with the first-ranked cluster as the primary cluster, the second-ranked cluster as the secondary cluster, and the third-ranked cluster as the tertiary cluster.

Ordinary least square method

Exploratory regression was used to identify models with a high Adjusted R2 value to find models that satisfy the assumptions of the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method.23,24 The OLS regression model calculated a single coefficient for each explanatory variable throughout the whole study.25,26 The fundamental assumptions of OLS were linearity, independence, normality, homoscedasticity, and no multicollinearity concepts. 27 The main purpose of OLS was to select the best variables for further analysis in bottle feeding practice.

Geographically weighted regression analysis

Geographically weighted regression (GWR) analysis was used to assess the determinant factors of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months. An adaptation of the OLS regression model that offers local parameter estimates to account for differences in the relationship between a bottle feeding practice and its factors over space was the GWR model. 28 The total percentage of children aged 0-23 months who were bottle-fed, together with all the factors were taken into account for each cluster in the GWR analysis. 29 Based on the adjusted R-squared values and corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) generated by the global OLS and local GWR models, spatial heterogeneity for each coefficient was investigated. The best-fit model for estimating local parameters is the one that has the highest adjusted R-squared and the lowest AICc. 19

Ethical consideration

In this study, secondary data from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) program were used. The Measure DHS program (https://www.measuredhs.com) makes the DHS datasets publicly accessible. The DHS Program obtains informed consent from all participants and ensures strict confidentiality in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national ethical guidelines. Since this research involved analysis of anonymized, de-identified data, no additional ethical approval was required, and Human Ethics and Consent to Participate declarations are not applicable. We respected confidentiality and used the data only for the study after DHS approved data access.

Results

Characteristics of the respondents

Among the 28,562 children aged 0-23 months analyzed, nearly half (48.9%) were between 12 and 23 months of age. Women were most frequently aged 20-24 years (27.3%). In terms of household wealth, 45.7% of children were from poor households. Educational attainment was highest at the primary level (49.6%), with women having higher education. Working status showed that 60.3% of women were working. The majority of households were located in rural areas (74.4%), and most women were married (62.3%). Household heads were predominantly male (74.8%). The sex distribution of children was nearly equal, with 50.4% male. Health service utilization indicators revealed that 72.8% of women had not received PNC. Almost all children were single births (97.3%). The place of delivery was largely at health facilities (78.8%). ANC coverage was moderate, with 56.7% of women reporting ANC visits, as summarized in Table 1. The magnitude of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa was 13%, as shown in Figure 4.

Spatial analysis

Spatial autocorrelation (Global Moran’s Index) analysis

The spatial autocorrelation for bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months had a Moran’s I value of 0.121 (p-value less than 0.001), and a Z-score of 10.581, which showed that bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa was clustered. There is less than a 1% chance that this clustered pattern could be the product of random choice, as illustrated in Figure 5.

Spatial autocorrelation of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa, 2019-2024.

Hotspot analysis

The results of the cluster-level spatial analysis indicated that Rwanda, central and eastern Tanzania, central and some coastal regions of Madagascar, southern Kenya, and both southern and eastern Mozambique were identified as 99% statistically significant hotspot areas of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months. Whereas the majority of Malawi, northern and central parts of Mozambique, northern Tanzania, and both northern and southern parts of Madagascar were found to have 99% statistically significant coldspot areas, indicating markedly lower prevalence of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in these regions, as shown in Figure 6.

Ordinary least squares analysis results

The OLS regression was applied to identify the main factors influencing the bottle-feeding practice. The model accounts for about 26.4% of the variation (Adjusted R2 = 0.264), which reflects a moderate level of explanatory power. The overall model fit was confirmed by the joint F-statistic (83.04, p < 0.001) and the Wald chi-squared test (295.27, p < 0.001), both indicating strong statistical significance. Multicollinearity was not a major concern, as the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values ranged between 1.013 and 1.837, well below the critical threshold of 7.5. The Koenker (BP) statistic (χ2 = 136.73, p < 0.001) suggested heteroskedasticity, while the Jarque-Bera test (χ2 = 3321.56, p < 0.001) indicated non-normality of residuals. Although deviations from normality are common in large datasets, robust probability values were used to ensure reliable inference. As shown in Table 3, five predictors emerged as significant, such as higher educational status (β = 0.177, p < 0.001), rich wealth index (β = 0.059, p < 0.001), urban residence (β = 0.069, p < 0.001), child age between 12-23 months (β = 0.045, p < 0.001), and woman age between 30-34 years (β = 0.047, p < 0.001) were all positively associated with the bottle-feeding practice. To address spatial heterogeneity, a GWR was estimated.

Comparison of OLS and GWR

For model comparison, the data were fitted using both GWR and OLS. The adjusted R2, R2, and corrected AICc were used for model comparison. To address spatial heterogeneity, a GWR was estimated. This localized model provided a stronger fit, with the adjusted R2 rising to 55.1% compared to 26.4% in the OLS model. The R2 increased from 29.7% in OLS to 59.2% in GWR. The AICc also decreased substantially (from 1219.57 in OLS to 858.38 in GWR), confirming that the spatial model better captured geographic variation in the data, as presented in Table 5. In conclusion, while the OLS regression highlighted key socioeconomic and demographic drivers, the GWR approach offered a more nuanced understanding by accounting for spatial differences, thereby improving both explanatory power and model fit.

Discussion

Bottle feeding practice is one of the major causes of malnutrition, from which many countries around the world are suffering from the double burden of both over- and undernutrition. 14 The purpose of this study was to assess geographically weighted regression analysis and determinants of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa. The magnitude of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa was 13%. This finding was less than studies conducted in SSA (13.74%), 14 in Indonesia (37.9%), 13 in Namibia (35.7%), 30 in Sudan (39.7%), 31 and in Ethiopia (13.5%), 8 but it is greater than studies conducted in East Africa (10.08%) 5 and in Ghana (12%). 32 This disparity may be explained by variations in the study area, sample size, socioeconomic status, women’s perceptions and knowledge of the best ways to feed their children and infants, and the data type that we used. 14 This study used pooled data from seven East African countries; some earlier studies were carried out in a single study area with a small sample size. While this study used nationally representative standardized secondary data from recent DHSs in seven East African countries from 2019 to 2024, some studies also used primary data to estimate the prevalence of bottle-feeding practice.

Spatial autocorrelation revealed the presence of a statistically significant clustering pattern of bottle-feeding practice in East Africa. This study finding is similar to those of studies conducted in Ethiopia.7,8 The 99% statistically significant hotspot areas of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months were observed in Rwanda, central and eastern Tanzania, central and some coastal regions of Madagascar, Southern Kenya, and both southern and eastern Mozambique. Earlier studies identified notable regional variation in bottle-feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in Ethiopia.7,8 The importance of hotspot detection is to create awareness about the negative impact of bottle-feeding practice, and this approach guides healthcare infrastructure and strategic policymaking to reduce bottle-feeding practice inequities through evidence-based targeted interventions. 19 On the GWR model, women’s higher educational status, rich wealth index, urban residence, age of child 12-23 months, and age of woman 30-34 years were statistically significant predictors of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa. These findings highlight that socioeconomic status, maternal demographics, and geographic context strongly influence infant feeding behaviors.

The GWR analysis revealed that women with higher educational status had a strong association with bottle-feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa, with a coefficient range from -0.217 to 0.512. As the women’s educational status increased, the bottle-feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months increased. The highest coefficients (0.338-0.512) of higher educational status on bottle-feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months are found in the above central parts of Madagascar, Rwanda, southern Zambia, central Malawi, southern and northeastern Tanzania, and southern Mozambique, highlighting substantial spatial variation in the influence of women’s higher education status. The studies conducted in Indonesia, 13 Namibia, 30 and Ethiopia 8 supported this finding. Women who are educated might work longer hours than housewives, who don’t have paid jobs, and they might not have enough time for breastfeeding. 13 The results of the study on women’s educational backgrounds and bottle-feeding habits among children aged 0-23 months indicate that a mother’s educational background does not always translate into better knowledge and comprehension of the advantages of breastfeeding. 30 Women who had higher education may have the openness to understand health-related issues, including the use of bottle-feeding.

Children from high-income households were more likely to practice bottle-feeding than those from low-income families, with their coefficients varying from -0.153 to 0.399. The highest coefficients (0.235-0.399) of the rich wealth index on bottle-feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months are found in eastern Kenya, southern Mozambique, and some parts of northern and eastern Zambia, highlighting substantial spatial variation in the influence of the wealth index. This finding was supported by research conducted in SSA, 14 Indonesia, 13 Namibia, 30 and Ethiopia 8 supports this finding. This might be because affluent households have access to alternative forms of feeding, like bottle or nipple feeding. 30 This finding may be explained by the possibility that respondents in the wealthy category had easier access to more costly feeding options, which could have an impact on their decision to bottle feed. 5 The richest families are more likely to afford infant formula, bottles, and sterilize equipment, while poorer families often rely on breastfeeding due to cost constraints.

According to GWR analysis, residence was one of the significant predictors of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months. Children who reside in urban areas had more likelihood of practicing bottle-feeding practice with a coefficient from -0.142 to 0.340 than those who being rural dwellers. The highest coefficients (0.162-0.340) are found in southern Kenya, central and southern Mozambique, and some parts of southeast Zambia, highlighting substantial spatial variation in the influence of urban residence. Researches conducted in Indonesia, 13 Namibia, 30 and Ethiopia 8 supported this finding. Due to work demands, exposure to infant formula marketing, and easier access to feeding supplies, urban women were more likely to use bottles than rural women, who tended to breastfeed more conventionally. 8 Urban moms are more impacted by contemporary feeding trends and frequently have time constraints because of their jobs, which results in a decrease in exclusive breastfeeding and an increase in bottle use. Women from urban areas may have had a higher socioeconomic status than women from rural areas, which may have made it simpler to obtain information about breast milk and access breast milk substitutes. 13

The bottle-feeding practice had positively significant association with the woman’s age. Woman aged 30-34 years practiced bottle feeding more frequently, with a coefficient ranging from -0.381 to 0.06, than woman aged 15-19 years. The highest coefficients (0.275-0.615) are found in the parts of northern Zambia, southern and northeast Mozambique, and some parts of northern Kenya, highlighting substantial spatial variation in the influence of maternal age. This finding was supported by studies conducted in Brazil 33 and Sub-Saharan African. 14 The possible explanation might be that as age increased, women were not producing enough breast milk, were overworked, their children’s slow growth, and had a lack of knowledge about the advantages of breastfeeding. 34 Moreover, when women are between 30 and 34, they are busy with a lot of internal and external responsibilities, like working in governmental or non-governmental offices, leading and participating in different social aspects, administering their own business, and other roles and responsibilities are higher at this stage of women’s age. However, studies conducted in Ethiopia 34 contradict this finding. The possible explanation could be that as the mother’s age increases, her experiences in childcare increase as well, and she understands the negative effect of bottle feeding, and more appropriate care and feeding patterns were adopted, 3 and also due to the increasing probability of being a housewife to care for their children.

The child’s age was significantly associated with bottle-feeding practices among children aged 0-23 months. Children aged 12-23 months had more bottle-feeding practice, with coefficients ranging from -0.345 to 0.698, than children aged 0-5 months. The highest coefficients (0.290-0.698) are found in the parts of northern Kenya, southern Mozambique, and some parts of southeast Zambia, highlighting substantial spatial variation in the influence of child age. This result is supported by evidence from East Africa, 5 Indonesia, 13 Namibia, 30 Ethiopia, 8 Woldia-Ethiopia, 3 and Holeta-Ethiopia. 35 This is because a child may have more feeding options as they grow older, such as drinking water, tea, and processed milk, which could result in a higher rate of bottle feeding. 13 However, bottle-feeding children between the ages of 0 and 5 months may be linked to issues with digestion and absorption, which can ultimately result in diarrhea, vomiting, and infections because these children’s gastrointestinal systems are still developing. 8 Women with such prior experience might not begin bottle-feeding their child at a young age.3,5

Implication of the study

The findings imply that bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa is shaped by socioeconomic, demographic, and geographic determinants rather than individual choice alone. The identification of hotspot regions highlights the need for region-specific interventions, while the associations with maternal higher education, rich wealth index, urban residence, woman age (30-34 years), and child age (12-23 months) emphasize that feeding behaviors are influenced by lifestyle, access, and responsibilities. These results suggest that reducing bottle feeding requires coordinated action across health systems, social policy, and community education to promote exclusive breastfeeding and address inequities contributing to the double burden of malnutrition.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study highlighted the use of sophisticated spatial analysis techniques to find clusters of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa, where action is required. The study offers a comprehensive analysis by taking into account individual-level factors and thorough knowledge of bottle feeding practices, allowing for specific policy recommendations. Moreover, the DHS datasets that we used for this analysis were collected recently from 2019 to 2024. However, some countries were not included in the analysis because their DHS data was out-of-date. The underrepresentation of bottle feeding practices in high-risk populations may result from the coverage’s limitations or the underprivileged groups, restricting the findings' applicability to those situations. We were unable to account for or adjust for these temporal differences because the data are cross-sectional. The study did not contain habitual use of bottle feeding, nor did it integrate child health outcomes with bottle feeding hotspots. A limitation of this study is that no independent sample size calculation was conducted for the specific research question. Although the DHS sampling framework ensures representativeness and adequate power for population-level analyses, the absence of a tailored calculation may restrict precision in certain subgroup or spatial analyses.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study revealed that the magnitude of bottle feeding practice among children aged 0-23 months in East Africa was 13%, with significant clustering and hotspot areas concentrated in Rwanda, central and eastern Tanzania, coastal Madagascar, southern Kenya, and southern and eastern Mozambique. The geographically weighted regression analysis identified women education status, wealth index, place of residence, age of women, and age of child as significant predictors, demonstrating substantial spatial variation in feeding behaviors. These findings confirm that infant feeding practices are strongly influenced by socioeconomic and demographic contexts and that geographic evidence is essential for designing effective interventions.

Recommendation

It is recommended that policymakers and health practitioners implement region-specific interventions in identified hotspot areas, strengthen breastfeeding promotion campaigns, particularly in urban and affluent communities, and regulate infant formula marketing to reduce its influence on maternal choices. In addition, workplace policies such as lactation rooms and flexible schedules should be introduced to support working mothers, while age-sensitive counseling and guidance on safe complementary feeding are essential to address maternal age-related challenges and ensure healthy feeding practices for older infants. Together, these measures can help reduce bottle feeding prevalence, promote exclusive breastfeeding, and mitigate the double burden of malnutrition in East Africa. Future research can build on this foundation by integrating child health outcomes with bottle feeding hotspots.
