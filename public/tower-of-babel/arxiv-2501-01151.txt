Characteristic oscillations in frequency-resolved heat dissipation of linear time-delayed Langevin systems: Approach from the violation of the fluctuation response relation
Xin Wang, Ruicheng Bao, Naruo Ohga

Abstract
Time-delayed effects are widely present in nature, often accompanied by distinctive nonequilibrium features, such as net heat flow from a single thermal bath to the system. To elucidate detailed structures of the dissipation, we study the frequency decomposition of the heat dissipation in linear time-delayed Langevin systems. We decompose the heat dissipation into frequency spectrum using the Harada-Sasa equality, which relates the heat dissipation to the violation of the fluctuation-response relation (FRR). We find a characteristic oscillatory behavior in the spectrum, and the oscillation asymptotically decays with an envelope inversely proportional to the frequency in the high-frequency region. Furthermore, the oscillation over the low-frequency region reflects the magnitude and sign of the heat dissipation. We confirm the generality of the results by extending our analysis to systems with multiple delay times. Since the violation of FRR is experimentally accessible, our results suggest an experimental direction for detecting and analyzing detailed characteristics of dissipation in time-delayed systems.

Setup and the Harada-Sasa equality

We start with a general class of time-delayed systems described by a stochastic delay differential equation (SDDE):

\left\{\begin{aligned} &m\ddot{x}(t)=-\gamma\dot{x}(t)+F(x(t),x(t-\tau_{1}),\cdots,x(t-\tau_{n}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\xi(t)+\epsilon f^{p}(t),&(t>t_{\text{init}}),\\
&x(t)=\phi(t),&\hskip-55.00008pt(t\in[t_{\text{init}}-\tau_{\mathrm{max}},t_{\text{init}}]),\\
&\dot{x}(t_{\text{init}})=v_{0}.&\hskip-25.00003pt\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

Here,
m
and
\gamma
denote the mass and friction coefficient, respectively. The term
F(x(t),x(t-\tau_{1}),\cdots,x(t-\tau_{n}))
includes both time-local and time-delayed forces in the dynamics. We assume delay times
\tau_{1},\cdots,\tau_{n}
are constants and
\tau_{\mathrm{max}}=\max\{\tau_{1},\cdots,\tau_{n}\}
. The term
\xi(t)
is the white Gaussian noise with mean
\left\langle\xi(t)\right\rangle=0
and correlation
\left\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=2\gamma T\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, where
T
is the temperature of the surrounding heat bath. Throughout this study, we assume that the delay time
\tau
is sufficiently longer than the physical timescale of the environmental noise correlation, thus the noise can be safely modeled as uncorrelated in time. We also add a perturbation force
\epsilon f^{p}(t)
with a small strength
\epsilon
to define the response function below. The dynamics starts at
t=t_{\text{init}}
with a specified historical trajectory
\phi(t)
over
t\in[t_{\text{init}}-\tau_{\mathrm{max}},t_{\text{init}}]
and an initial velocity
v_{0}
.
The SDDE dynamics apply to various fields including ecology
[
12
]
, biophysics
[
18
]
, and engineering
[
21
,
6
,
7
]
. In experiments, such time-delayed dynamics can also be implemented using an optically levitated particle under time-delayed feedback control in underdamped
[
22
,
23
]
and overdamped
[
24
,
29
]
cases.

The dynamics may converge to a stationary process or diverge indefinitely depending on the functions
F(x(t),x(t-\tau_{1}),\cdots,x(t-\tau_{n}))
and initial conditions. In the following, we assume the convergence and take the limit
t_{\mathrm{init}}\to-\infty
to focus on the steady state. We use
\left\langle\cdots\right\rangle_{\epsilon}
to express the steady-state average over all possible realizations of trajectories with the perturbation strength
\epsilon
.

The stochastic heat dissipation rate
J(t)
is defined through

J(t)dt\equiv[\gamma\dot{x}(t)-\xi(t)]\circ dx(t),

(2)

where
\circ
represents the Stratonovich product
[
30
]
. Here,
-\gamma\dot{x}(t)+\xi(t)
represents the friction force and the random thermal force applied to the system by the environment. Therefore,
J(t)dt
represents the work done by the system’s counterforce on the environment over the displacement
dx
within time
dt
at the level of a single trajectory. This energy transfer is interpreted as heat dissipation. When this heat dissipation rate is positive (
J(t)>0
), energy flows from the system to the environment. The Stratonovich product in Eq. (
2
) ensures the validity of the first law of thermodynamics (see Ref.
[
30
]
for details).
For Markovian systems that satisfy Einstein’s relation, the average heat dissipation rate is always non-negative, consistent with the second law of thermodynamics. However, time-delayed systems may have a negative heat dissipation rate on average. This is compensated by extra heat dissipation in the external degrees of freedom that exert the time-delayed force to the system, which
are not modeled explicitly in Eq. (
1
). For example, the extra heat dissipation may arise due to the thermodynamic cost associated with the recorded information in
the feedback-control agency
[
22
,
52
,
60
,
61
]
.

This heat dissipation rate is related to two dynamical quantities of the system, as originally reported in Ref.
[
59
]
. The first quantity is the response function
R_{v}(t)
in the steady state, defined as

R_{v}(t-t^{\prime})\equiv\dfrac{\delta\left\langle\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle_{\epsilon}}{\delta[\epsilon f^{p}(t^{\prime})]}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}

(3)

which yields

\left\langle\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle_{\epsilon}=v_{s}+\epsilon\int_{-\infty}^{t}R_{v}(t-s)f^{p}(s)ds+O(\epsilon^{2}),

(4)

where
\delta/\delta[\epsilon f^{p}(t)]
represents the functional derivative and
v_{s}\equiv\langle\dot{x}(t)\rangle_{0}
is the steady-state velocity independent of
t
.
Notice that
R_{v}(t)=0
when
t<0
because of causality. The other quantity is the time-correlation function of velocity in the absence of perturbation in the steady state,

C_{v}(t)\equiv\left\langle\left[\dot{x}(t)-v_{s}\right]\left[\dot{x}(0)-v_{s}\right]\right\rangle_{0},

(5)

which satisfies
C_{v}(-t)=C_{v}(t)
.

The Harada-Sasa equality relates these two quantities to the heat dissipation rate in the steady state by the following identity
[
59
]
:

\left\langle J\right\rangle_{0}=\gamma\left\{v_{s}^{2}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)\right]\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\right\}.

(6)

Here and throughout this paper, we define the Fourier transform of a function
f(t)
as
\tilde{f}(\omega)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\exp(i\omega t)dt
and
\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)
denotes the real part of
\tilde{R}_{v}(\omega)
. Notice that
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)
represents the power spectral density of the velocity and
\epsilon\tilde{R}_{v}(\omega)
corresponds to the velocity response to the specific perturbation force
\epsilon f^{p}(t)=\epsilon e^{i\omega t}
. The fluctuation-response relation (FRR)
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)=2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)
always holds for all frequencies in equilibrium systems
[
62
,
63
]
, and thus the response and the power spectral density are always balanced. In contrast, FRR is generally violated in nonequilibrium systems. The Harada-Sasa equality shows that the FRR violation corresponds to a positive (negative) contribution to the heat dissipation rate if the power spectral density is stronger (weaker) than the response.
The Harada-Sasa equality was first proved for overdamped Langevin systems
[
59
]
, and then generalized to underdamped Langevin systems
[
63
,
64
]
, systems with colored noise
[
63
,
65
]
, density-field variables
[
66
]
, quantum systems
[
67
]
, and active matter
[
68
,
69
]
. It has been used to experimentally measure heat dissipation in Brownian particles
[
70
,
71
]
and biological systems
[
72
,
73
,
74
]
.

The Harada-Sasa equality holds for general time-delayed systems in Eq. (
1
). The proof of Eq. (
6
) for time-delayed systems can be done in exactly the same way as the proof for Markovian Langevin systems in Ref.
[
63
]
. Notably, our setup is regarded as a special case of the setup in Ref.
[
75
]
, and thus Eq. (
6
) is a special case of the Harada-Sasa equality proved in Ref.
[
75
]
. For completeness, we provide a proof of Eq. (
6
) in Appendix
B
.

Linear systems with a single delay time

In this section, we focus on a simple yet insightful class of time-delayed systems described by the following overdamped Langevin equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\gamma\dot{x}(t)=ax(t)+bx(t-\tau)+\epsilon f^{p}(t)+\xi(t),&(t>t_{\text{init}}),\\
&x(t)=\phi(t),&\hskip-20.00003pt(t\in[t_{\text{init}}-\tau,t_{\text{init}}]),\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(7)

where
a
is the strength of a linear force without time delay,
b
is the strength of a linear time-delayed force, and
\tau
denotes a single constant delay time (
\tau>0
). We assume that the other parameters share the same meaning as in Eq. (
1
).
The analytical solution to Eq. (
7
) has been provided by Küchler and Mensch
[
76
]
, which reads

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(t-t_{\text{init}})\phi(t_{\text{init}})+\frac{b}{\gamma}\int_{t_{\text{init}}-\tau}^{t_{\text{init}}}x_{0}(t-s-\tau)\phi(s)ds

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{t_{\text{init}}}^{t}x_{0}(t-s)[\xi(s)+\epsilon f^{p}(s)]ds,

(8)

where
x_{0}
is the fundamental solution, generally defined as the solution to the deterministic version of Eq. (
7
)
satisfying the following conditions:

\epsilon=0,\hskip 5.0ptT=0,\hskip 5.0ptt_{\mathrm{init}}=0,\hskip 5.0pt\phi(t)=0\,\,(t<0),\hskip 5.0pt\phi(0)=1.

(9)

For the linear dynamics with single delay [Eq. (
7
)],
x_{0}(t)
reads
[
76
]

x_{0}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor t/\tau\rfloor}\frac{(b/\gamma)^{k}}{k!}(t-k\tau)^{k}e^{(a/\gamma)(t-k\tau)}.

(10)

Notice that the first two terms of Eq. (
8
) are history-dependent deterministic functions in general, while the last term corresponds to a history-independent function with a Gaussian distribution.

8
), we can readily calculate the quantities of interest in the steady state (see Appendix

\displaystyle R_{v}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\left[ax_{0}(t)+bx_{0}(t-\tau)\right]+\frac{1}{\gamma}\delta(t),

(14)

\displaystyle C_{v}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2T}{\gamma^{3}}\bigl\{(a^{2}+b^{2})K(t)+ab\left[K(t+\tau)+K(t-\tau)\right]\bigr\}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2T}{\gamma^{2}}[ax_{0}(|t|)+bx_{0}(|t|-\tau)]+\frac{2T}{\gamma}\delta(t),

(15)

where we define
K(t)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}x_{0}(s)x_{0}(s+t)ds
, whose explicit formula is provided in Appendix

We can also obtain the average heat dissipation rate for
\tau>0
:

\left\langle J\right\rangle_{0}=\frac{T}{\gamma}a+\frac{2T}{\gamma^{2}}[(a^{2}+b^{2})K(0)+2abK(\tau)].

(16)

Note that this expression of the heat dissipation rate has been derived in Refs.
[
49
,
50
]
from the time-delayed Fokker-Planck equation
[
77
]
. In the vanishing delay limit (
\tau\to 0
), the expression of
\langle J\rangle_{0}
in Eq. (
16
) approaches
-bT/\gamma
. However, when
\tau
is exactly 0, Eq. (
16
) is no longer valid, and the heat dissipation rate strictly becomes zero since the heat dissipation is purely due to the time-delay force in the present setup.
Thus,
\langle J\rangle_{0}
shows a discontinuity in the limit
\tau\to 0
in the current overdamped model
[
49

50
]
. Such unphysical picture of the discontinuity originates from the failure of
\langle\xi(t)\xi(t-\tau)\rangle=0
and
\langle x(t-\tau)\xi(t)\rangle=0
in deriving Eq. (

\tau
is comparable to the timescale of the environmental noise correlation in physical scenarios (see Appendix
C
and Chapter 9 of Ref.
[

for related discussion).

As the main result of the present paper, we analyze the heat dissipation rate in more detail by decomposing it into the frequency components using the Harada-Sasa equality. From Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
), we can analytically calculate the Fourier transforms
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)
,
\tilde{R}_{v}^{\prime}(\omega)
and the spectral decomposition of the heat dissipation
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)
(see Appendix
D
for the derivation):

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{v}^{\prime}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+2ab\cos(\omega\tau)+b\gamma\omega\sin(\omega\tau)}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma},

(17)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)=-\frac{2T}{\gamma}\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+2ab\cos(\omega\tau)+2b\gamma\omega\sin(\omega\tau)}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]^{2}}\!+\!\frac{2T}{\gamma},

(18)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad=-2T\frac{b\omega\sin(\omega\tau)}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]^{2}}.

(19)

As shown in Fig.
2(a)
, throughout the frequency domain, the spectral decomposition of heat dissipation shows a long-lasting oscillation centered at zero with the period
2\pi/\tau
. A longer delay leads to an oscillation of the spectrum with a shorter period [Fig.
2(c)
]. The amplitude of the decaying oscillation increases as the magnitude of the strength of the delay term
|b|
increases, and a negative strength of the delay force (
b<0
) changes the overall sign of the spectrum [Fig.
2(d)
]. In particular, in the high-frequency domain (
\omega\gg|a|/\gamma
and
\omega\gg|b|/\gamma
), the denominator of
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)
is dominated by
(\gamma\omega)^{2}
; hence,

\displaystyle\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)\sim\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{b}{\gamma^{2}\omega}\sin(\omega\tau)+O\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\right),

(20)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)\sim\frac{2T}{\gamma}-\frac{4T}{\gamma^{2}\omega}b\sin(\omega\tau)+O\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\right),

(21)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)\sim-\frac{2T}{\gamma^{2}\omega}b\sin(\omega\tau)+O\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\right).

(22)

The asymptotic behavior of these spectra in high frequencies reveals nontrivial information about the time-delayed force: The envelope of the spectral curve decays inversely with frequency
\omega
and is proportional to the strength
b
of the time-delayed force. In the vanishing limit of
b
, the entire spectrum tends to zero. This shows an essential nonequilibrium behavior due to the presence of the time-delayed force.

There are two timescales of the dynamics that are important in determining corresponding thermodynamic behavior:

\tau/\pi
and
\mathrm{min}\{\gamma/|a|,\gamma/|b|\}
. By sweeping
\omega
from
0
to
\infty
, the FRR violation
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}_{v}^{\prime}(\omega)
periodically vanishes when
\omega
is an integer multiple of
\pi/\tau
, which leads to the oscillatory spectrum. In other words, the time-delayed force breaks FRR and contributes to heat dissipation only at timescale
\omega^{-1}
off-resonant with
\tau/\pi
divided by an integer. Furthermore, the envelope of the violation spectrum starts to decay around
\omega^{-1}\simeq\mathrm{min}\{\gamma/|a|,\gamma/|b|\}
. This means that the violation of FRR, and hence the contribution to the heat dissipation, is most prominent in timescales longer than the intrinsic relaxation timescale,
\omega^{-1}\geq\mathrm{min}\{\gamma/|a|,\gamma/|b|\}
. For small timescales (
\omega^{-1}\ll\mathrm{min}\{\gamma/|a|,\gamma/|b|\}
), the asymptotic scaling of the spectrum (
\sim 1/\omega
) in the high-frequency region and its oscillation around zero together lead to a negligible contribution to the heat dissipation.

We can preliminarily infer the sign of the heat dissipation and its parameter dependence from the information of the spectrum decomposition in the low-frequency region. Here, we focus on cases with a restoring nondelayed force (
a<0
). Specifically, for a positive delayed force
b>0
, the major contribution to the integral comes from the first valley below zero and the subsequent peak above zero. A shorter delay time results in a longer period of oscillation in the frequency domain, which shifts the first peak towards higher frequency without changing the decay of the envelope [Fig.
2(c)
]. Thus, a shorter delay time leads to a lower heat dissipation for
b>0
. In contrast, for negative strength of the delayed force
b<0
, the contribution from the first positive peak of the spectrum dominates, which results in positive heat dissipation [Fig.
2(d)
and Fig.
1
]. Experimentally, these properties allow us to infer the heat dissipation rate of the linear time-delayed systems with relatively small sampling resources.

Linear systems with multidelay times

Next, we examine a broader class of time-delayed systems to test the generality of these results. We consider a Langevin equation with multiple delay times:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\gamma\dot{x}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}A_{i}x(t-\tau_{i})+\epsilon f^{p}(t)+\xi(t),&(t>t_{\text{init}}),\\
&x(t)=\phi(t),&\hskip-25.00003pt(t\in[t_{\text{init}}-\tau,t_{\text{init}}]),\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(23)

where
A_{0}
is the strength of a linear force without delay (
\tau_{0}=0
),
A_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,N
are the strength of a linear time-delayed force term with a constant delay time
\tau_{i}
(
\tau_{i}>0
), and
\tau=\max_{i}\{\tau_{i}\}
. Other parameters share the same meaning as in Eq. (
1
).
Instead of Eq. (
11
) or (
12
), we require

A_{0}<-\sum_{i=1}^{N}|A_{i}|
to ensure the existence of a unique stationary solution, which can be shown by the method of Lyapunov functionals
[
78
,
79
]
. The solution of Eq. (
23
) is given by

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(t-t_{\text{init}})\phi(t_{\text{init}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\int_{t_{\text{init}}-\tau_{i}}^{t_{\text{init}}}x_{0}(t-s-\tau_{i})\phi(s)ds

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{t_{\text{init}}}^{t}x_{0}(t-s)[\xi(s)+\epsilon f^{p}(s)]ds,

(24)

in which
x_{0}(t)
is defined as the solution to Eq. (
23
) with the conditions in Eq. (
9
).

Following the same routes as in Sec.
III
, we can obtain the linear response function and the time-correlation function in the steady state as

\displaystyle R_{v}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{N}A_{i}x_{0}(t-\tau_{i})+\frac{1}{\gamma}\delta(t),

(25)

\displaystyle C_{v}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2T}{\gamma^{3}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}A_{i}A_{j}K(t+\tau_{i}-\tau_{j})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2T}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{N}A_{i}x_{0}(|t|-\tau_{i})+\frac{2T}{\gamma}\delta(t).

(26)

The Fourier transforms
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)
,
\tilde{R}_{v}^{\prime}(\omega)
and the spectral decomposition of the heat dissipation
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)
read

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{v}^{\prime}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}A_{i}A_{j}\cos(\omega(\tau_i-\tau_j))+\gamma\omega\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)\right]}{\left[\sum_{i=0}^{N}A_{i}\cos(\omega\tau_i)\right]^{2}+\left[\gamma\omega+\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)\right]^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma}.

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)=-\frac{2T}{\gamma}\frac{\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}A_{i}A_{j}\cos(\omega(\tau_i-\tau_j))+2\gamma\omega\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)\right]}{\left[\sum_{i=0}^{N}A_{i}\cos(\omega\tau_i)\right]^{2}+\left[\gamma\omega+\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)\right]^{2}}+\frac{2T}{\gamma}.

(28)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)=-2T\frac{\omega\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)\right]}{\left[\sum_{i=0}^{N}A_{i}\cos(\omega\tau_i)\right]^{2}+\left[\gamma\omega+\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)\right]^{2}}.

(29)

In the high-frequency domain (
\omega\gg|A_{0}|/\gamma
), we have

\displaystyle\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)\sim\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}\omega}\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)+O\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\right),

(30)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)\sim\frac{2T}{\gamma}-\frac{4T}{\gamma^{2}\omega}\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)+O\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\right),

(31)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)\sim-\frac{2T}{\gamma^{2}\omega}\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)+O\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\right).

(32)

As an example, we consider systems with two delay times [
N=2
in Eq. (
23
)].
Similarly to the case of a single delay, the decomposition spectrum shows an oscillation over the whole frequency domain [Fig.
2(b)
]. The amplitude of the oscillation of the spectrum increases as the delay strengths
|A_{1}|
,
|A_{2}|
increase [Fig.
2(f)
]; larger delay times
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2}
lead to a shorter period of the oscillation [Fig.
2(e)
]. In the high-frequency limit, the spectrum becomes a superposition of sinusoidal waves corresponding to different delay times, with a decaying envelope of scaling
1/\omega
.

For systems with two delay times, the contribution to heat dissipation still originates predominantly from the low-frequency region [Fig.
2(b)
]. Therefore, we can examine the parameter dependence of the heat dissipation by analyzing the resolved spectrum based on Eq. (
29
). For positive strengths of time-delayed forces (
A_{1},A_{2}>0
), shorter delay times generally yield lower heat dissipation [Fig.
2(e)
]. In Fig.
2(f)
, positive force strengths (
A_{1},A_{2}>0
) can induce negative heat flow, whereas negative strengths (
A_{1},A_{2}<0
) produce positive heat flow; the absolute value of heat dissipation increases with the magnitude of delay strengths of the same sign. Time-delayed forces with opposing signs of the strengths (
A_{1}>0,A_{2}<0
or
A_{1}<0,A_{2}>0
) result in higher heat dissipation compared to the both-positive case with the same magnitudes. Notably, flipping the sign (from positive to negative) of the force associated with the shorter delay time generates more dissipation than flipping that of the longer delay time [Fig.
2(g)
].

Discussion

In this work, we have studied the spectral decomposition of the heat dissipation rate
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)
in linear time-delayed Langevin systems using the Harada-Sasa equality. Our main results are summarized as follows: (1) The frequency-resolved spectrum of heat dissipation of linear time-delayed overdamped Langevin systems shows a characteristic oscillation with asymptotic scaling
1/\omega
.
The magnitude of the oscillation reflects the strength of the delayed force, and its period reflects the delay time. Overall,
such asymptotic behavior reveals nontrivial information about the time-delayed force. (2) The frequency-resolved spectrum in the low-frequency region reflects the sign and the magnitude of the heat dissipation. In experiments, the power spectral density
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)
can be directly obtained from the recorded trajectories, while the response function
\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)
is measured by applying a small oscillatory perturbation and analyzing the system’s output (see Appendix
A
for a brief recipe).
Our study provides a perspective for identifying and analyzing time-delayed effects in diverse systems, such as active matter, and for effectively quantifying the energetic costs in systems like colloidal particles, electrical circuits, and mechanical oscillators under time-delayed feedback control.

The
1/\omega
scaling and asymptotic oscillation of frequency-resolved spectrum of heat dissipation are arguably a specific feature of time-delayed systems. This is suggested by a known result for Markovian systems
[
80
]
: For general discrete-state Markovian systems with a general perturbation, the real part of the linear response function, the time correlation function, and the spectrum of heat dissipation approach their respective limits as
\omega\to\infty
by
1/\omega^{2}
, as shown by the Taylor expansion of Eq. (6) in Ref.
[
80
]
. Furthermore, we suspect that the asymptotic oscillations of
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)
and
\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)
are not specific to linear systems but may also arise in some nonlinear time-delayed systems (e.g., see
[
81
]
). A systematic verification of this behavior in a broader class of nonlinear time-delayed stochastic systems is an interesting direction for future theoretical investigation.

Additionally, time-delayed systems are often analyzed using enlarged models that explicitly incorporate additional degrees of freedom (for instance, through Markovian embedding using the linear chain trick
[
82
,
53
,
58
]
, or by directly modeling the feedback mechanism as an explicit degree of freedom
[
52
]
). Understanding our results using such enlarged models is also an interesting direction for gaining further physical insights into these characteristics.

Appendix A
Numerical methods

We briefly sketch the method of numerical calculation. The procedure is in principle applicable in experimental setups as well.

For a single trajectory
x(t)
at resolution
\delta t
, we assume the steady state after a sufficiently long time
t=t_{0}
and take discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of the data in
[t_{0},t_{0}+\mathcal{T}]\ (\mathcal{T}=N_{\mathrm{sp}}\delta t)
, as suggested in the Supplemental Material of Ref.
[
80
]
:

\tilde{x}(\omega_{k})=\frac{\sqrt{\delta t}}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{sp}}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sp}}}x(t_{0}+j\delta t)\exp[-i\omega_{k}(t_{0}+j\delta t)],

(33)

where
\omega_{k}=2\pi k/N_{\mathrm{sp}}\delta t\ \ (k=0,\cdots,N_{\mathrm{\mathrm{sp}}}-1)
. The power spectral density of velocity
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)
is obtained by

\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)=\langle|\tilde{v}(\omega)|^{2}\rangle=\omega^{2}\langle|\tilde{x}(\omega)|^{2}\rangle,

(34)

where
\langle\cdots\rangle
represents the ensemble average over a sufficiently large number of trajectories.
For the response function
\tilde{R}_{v}^{\prime}(\omega_{0})
for a specific
\omega_{0}
, first, we plug
f^{p}(t)=e^{i\omega_{0}t}
into Eq. (
4
):

\displaystyle\langle\dot{x}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=v_{s}+\epsilon\int_{-\infty}^{t}R_{v}(t-s)e^{i\omega_{0}s}ds+O(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=v_{s}+\epsilon\tilde{R}_{v}(\omega_{0})e^{i\omega_{0}t}+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(35)

It is more practical to consider the response to a real perturbation
f^{p}(t)=\cos(\omega_0t)=\mathrm{Re}[e^{i\omega_{0}t}]
, which is given by taking the real part of Eq. (
35
) (
v_{s}=0
in the steady state and ignoring
O(\epsilon^{2})
):

\langle\dot{x}(t)\rangle=\epsilon\{\text{Re}[R_{v}(\omega_{0})]\cos(\omega_0 t)-\text{Im}[R_{v}(\omega_{0})]\sin(\omega_0 t)\},

(36)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle{x}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\omega_{0}}\{\text{Re}[R_{v}(\omega_{0})]\sin(\omega_0 t)+\text{Im}[R_{v}(\omega_{0})]\cos(\omega_0 t)\}

\displaystyle\ \ +\mathrm{constant}.

(37)

The real part of
\tilde{R}_{v}(\omega_{0})
is extracted by taking the inner product of averaged trajectories
\langle x(t)\rangle
in
[t_{0},t_{0}+\mathcal{T}]
with
\sin(\omega_0t)
:

\text{Re}[R_{v}(\omega_{0})]\approx\frac{\omega_{0}}{\epsilon}\cdot\frac{2}{N_{\mathrm{sp}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sp}}}\langle x(t_{0}+j\delta t)\rangle\sin[\omega_{0}(t_{0}+j\delta t)].

(38)

In numerical simulations, we compute
\omega^{2}|\tilde{x}(\omega)|^{2}
for each trajectory without any perturbation force and then take the average
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)
and the standard deviation
\sigma_{C}
over an ensemble of
N
trajectories. Similarly, for a specified frequency
\omega_{0}
, we add a perturbation
f^{p}(t)=\cos(\omega_0t)
and compute the expression
2\omega_{0}(\epsilon N_{\mathrm{sp}})^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sp}}}x(t_{0}+j\delta t)\sin[\omega_{0}(t_{0}+j\delta t)]
for each trajectory.
Then we take the average as
\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega_{0})
and the standard deviation
\sigma_{R^{\prime}}
over
N
trajectories.
Since
\sigma_{C}
and
\sigma_{R^{\prime}}
are computed from different dynamical processes and noise realizations, the error bars for the spectrum
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)-2T\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)
are given by
\sqrt{(\sigma_{C}^{2}+4T^{2}\sigma_{R^{\prime}}^{2})/N}
.

We use the programming language
Julia
and the package
DifferentialEquations.jl

[
83
]
and
StochasticDelayDiffEq.jl

[
84
]
for numerical simulations using the Euler-Maruyama method
[
85
]
. For
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)
or
\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega_{0})
(with a selected
\omega_{0}
), we set the steady-state time
t_{0}=200
and simulate for a duration of
\mathcal{T}=500

with a resolution of
\delta t=0.001
over
N=50,000
trajectories in both single and two-delay cases.

Appendix B
Proof of the Harada-Sasa equality for systems with time-delayed forces

We prove the Harada-Sasa equality in Eq. (
6
) based on Refs.
[
63
,
75
]
. For convenience, we write the external force at time
t
as

\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}(t)\equiv F(x(t),x(t-\tau_{1}),\cdots,x(t-\tau_{n}))+\epsilon f^{p}(t),

(39)

which is a stochastic quantity.

We first consider overdamped systems (
m=0
) and use the path-integral formalism
[
86
,
87
]
. We consider discrete time points
t_{i}\equiv t_{\mathrm{init}}+i\Delta t
for
i=0,1,\dots
with a small interval
\Delta t
. We take the following discrete time evolution, which converges to the original time evolution in Eq. (
1
) in the
\Delta t\to 0
limit:

\gamma\Delta x^{i}=\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\epsilon}\Delta t+\sqrt{2\gamma T}\Delta W^{i},

(40)

where
\Delta x^{i}\equiv x(t_{i+1})-x(t_{i})
,
\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\epsilon}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}(t_{i})
, and
\Delta W^{i}
is the Wiener increment obeying the Gaussian distribution with mean
\langle\Delta W^{i}\rangle=0
and correlation
\langle\Delta W^{i}\Delta W^{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}\Delta t
(
\sqrt{2\gamma T}\Delta W^{i}/\Delta t\simeq\xi(t_{i})
). Given an initial history
\{\phi(s)\,|\,t_{\mathrm{init}}-\tau\leq s\leq t_{\mathrm{init}}\}
, the external force
\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\epsilon}
is regarded as a function of
\{\Delta x^{0},\dots,\Delta x^{i-1}\}
.

We consider a path from time
t_{\mathrm{init}}\equiv t_{0}
to
t_{N}
. Given an initial condition
\{\phi(s)\,|\,t_{\mathrm{init}}-\tau\leq s\leq t_{\mathrm{init}}\}
, a realization
\{\Delta x^{0},\dots,\Delta x^{N-1}\}
is completely determined by the realization of the noise
\{\Delta W^{0},\dots,\Delta W^{N-1}\}
. The noise obeys a multivariate Gaussian distribution, and thus the probability density of a path
\{\Delta x^{0},\dots,\Delta x^{N-1}\}
is given by

\displaystyle P\bigl[\{\Delta x^{0},\dots,\Delta x^{N-1}\}\,|\,\{\phi(\cdot)\}\bigr]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{N}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4\gamma T\Delta t}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}[\gamma\Delta x^{i}-\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\epsilon}\Delta t]^{2}\right\},

(41)

where

\mathcal{J}=\left|\det\left[\frac{\partial(\Delta W^{0},\dots,\Delta W^{N-1})}{\partial(\Delta x^{0},\dots,\Delta x^{N-1})}\right]\right|

(42)

is the Jacobian and
\mathcal{N}=(\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t})^{N}
is the normalization constant. The Jacobian matrix consists of the elements
\partial(\Delta W^{i})/\partial(\Delta x^{j})
. Using Eq. (
40
), we can find
\partial(\Delta W^{i})/\partial(\Delta x^{j})=0
for
j>i
, and thus the Jacobian matrix is a lower triangular matrix. Thus, the determinant is the product of the diagonal elements,
\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}\partial(\Delta W^{i})/\partial(\Delta x^{i})=(\sqrt{\gamma/2T})^{N}
. Notably, this determinant does not depend on the perturbation force
\epsilon f^{p}(t)
.

Taking the continuous limit (
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
), the probability density of a path
\{x(s)\,|\,t_{\mathrm{init}}<s\leq t\}
is given by the path integral

\displaystyle P\bigl[\{x(\cdot)\}\,|\,\{\phi(\cdot)\}\bigr]=\frac{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{N}}\exp[-\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\{x\})],

(43)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\{x\})\equiv\int_{t_{\mathrm{init}}}^{t}\frac{1}{4\gamma T}[\gamma\dot{x}(s)-\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}(s)]^{2}ds,

(44)

with
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{J}
independent of
\epsilon f^{p}(t)
.

Now we use this path integral to prove the Harada-Sasa equality. For
s^{\prime}\neq s
, we can rewrite the response function as

\displaystyle R_{v}(s^{\prime}-s)\equiv\frac{\delta\left\langle\dot{x}(s^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\epsilon}}{\delta\epsilon f^{p}(s)}\Bigg|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\delta\epsilon f^{p}(s)}\left[\frac{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{N}}\int\mathcal{D}\{x\}\,e^{-\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\{x\})}\dot{x}(s^{\prime})\right]\Bigg|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{N}}\int\mathcal{D}\{x\}\,e^{-\mathcal{S}_{0}(\{x\})}\dot{x}(s^{\prime})\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\{x\})}{\delta\epsilon f^{p}(s)}\Biggl|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{N}}\int\mathcal{D}\{x\}\,e^{-\mathcal{S}_{0}(\{x\})}\dot{x}(s^{\prime})\frac{1}{2\gamma T}[\gamma\dot{x}(s)-\mathcal{F}_{0}(s)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\gamma T}\langle\dot{x}(s^{\prime})[\gamma\dot{x}(s)-\mathcal{F}_{0}(s)]\rangle_{0}.

(45)

Combining this with the definition of the correlation function in Eq. (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle C_{v}(s^{\prime}-s)-2TR_{v}(s^{\prime}-s)

\displaystyle=\langle[\dot{x}(s^{\prime})-v_{s}][\dot{x}(s)-v_{s}]\rangle_{0}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\langle\dot{x}(s^{\prime})[\gamma\dot{x}(s)-\mathcal{F}_{0}(s)]\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\langle\dot{x}(s^{\prime})\mathcal{F}_{0}(s)\rangle_{0}-v_{s}^{2}.

(46)

So far, we have performed calculations for
s^{\prime}\neq s
.
We use this relation to rewrite the heat dissipation rate as

\displaystyle\left\langle J\right\rangle_{0}=\left\langle\mathcal{F}_{0}(s)\circ\dot{x}(s)\right\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{F}_{0}(s+t)\dot{x}(s)+\mathcal{F}_{0}(s-t)\dot{x}(s)\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma v_{s}^{2}+\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\gamma}{2}\bigl\{[C_{v}(t)-2TR_{v}(t)]+[C_{v}(-t)-2TR_{v}(-t)]\bigr\}.

(47)

On the other hand, the singularity of
C_{v}(0)
is given by
\gamma^{-2}\langle\xi(s)\xi(s)\rangle=(2T/\gamma)\delta(0)
and the singularity of
R_{v}(0)
is
\gamma^{-1}\delta[\epsilon f^{p}(s)]/\delta[\epsilon f^{p}(s)]=\gamma^{-1}\delta(0)
. Thus, the combination
C_{v}(0)-2TR_{v}(0)
has no singularity. Therefore, we can apply the equality
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(\omega)\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left[f(t)+f(-t)\right]
to
f(t)=C_{v}(t)-2TR_{v}(t)
. This leads to the Harada-Sasa equality in Eq. (
6
).

For underdamped systems (
m\neq 0
), we similarly consider the path integral by taking the velocity
v(s)\equiv\dot{x}(s)
as the variable. Given an initial condition
\{\phi(s)\,|\,t_{\mathrm{init}}-\tau\leq s\leq t_{\mathrm{init}}\}
and an initial velocity
v_{0}
, the path of the velocity
\{v(s)\,|\,t_{\mathrm{init}}<s\leq t\}
completely determines the path of the position
\{x(s)\,|\,t_{\mathrm{init}}<s\leq t\}
and hence the time-delayed force. By a similar procedure as above, we find the path integral expression of the probability density,

\displaystyle P\bigl[\{v(\cdot)\}\,|\,\{\phi(\cdot)\},v_{0}\bigr]=\frac{\mathcal{J}}{\mathcal{N}}\exp[-\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\{v\})],

(48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}(\{v\})\equiv\int_{t_{\mathrm{init}}}^{t}\frac{1}{4\gamma T}[m\dot{v}(s)+\gamma v(s)-\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}(s)]^{2}ds,

(49)

with the Jacobian
\mathcal{J}
and the normalization
\mathcal{N}
independent of
\epsilon f^{p}(\cdot)
.

We can prove the Harada-Sasa equality for underdamped systems similarly to Eqs. (
45
)–(
47
). The response function is calculated from the path integral as

R_{v}(s^{\prime}-s)=\frac{1}{2\gamma T}\bigl\langle v(s^{\prime})[m\dot{v}(s)+\gamma v(s)-\mathcal{F}_{0}(s)]\bigr\rangle_{0}.

(50)

Combining this with the definition of the correlation function, we obtain

\displaystyle C_{v}(s^{\prime}-s)-2TR_{v}(s^{\prime}-s)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\gamma}\bigl\langle v(s^{\prime})[-m\dot{v}(s)+\mathcal{F}_{0}(s)]\bigr\rangle_{0}-v_{s}^{2}.

(51)

The heat dissipation rate is thus given by

\displaystyle\left\langle J\right\rangle_{0}=\bigl\langle[-m\dot{v}(s)+\mathcal{F}_{0}(s)]\circ v(s)\bigr\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{2}\bigl\langle[-m\dot{v}(s)+\mathcal{F}_{0}(s)][v(s+t)+v(s-t)]\bigr\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma v_{s}^{2}+\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\gamma}{2}\bigl\{[C_{v}(t)-2TR_{v}(t)]+[C_{v}(-t)-2TR_{v}(-t)]\bigr\}.

(52)

Since there is no singularity of delta functions for underdamped systems, we can readily use the equality
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{f}^{\prime}(\omega)\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left[f(t)+f(-t)\right]
to reproduce the Harada-Sasa equality.

Appendix C
Derivations of
C_{v}(t)
,
R_{v}(t)
, and
\langle J\rangle_{0}

We directly calculate the linear response function and the time correlation function of the linear time-delayed overdamped Langevin systems with a single time-delay term [Eq. (
7
)]. From the formal solution Eq. (
8
), the velocity is given by

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=(\text{historical terms})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\int_{t_{\text{init}}}^{t}[ax_{0}(t-s)+bx_{0}(t-\tau-s)][\xi(s)+\epsilon f^{p}(s)]ds

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\gamma}[\xi(t)+\epsilon f^{p}(t)].

(53)

Taking the limit
t_{\text{init}}\rightarrow-\infty
,
the historical terms vanish. By taking the average
\langle\cdots\rangle_{0}
, we find that the steady-state velocity vanishes:
v_{s}=\langle\dot{x}(t)\rangle_{0}=0
.

By taking the functional derivative of Eq. (
53
), we obtain the linear response function at steady state,

\displaystyle R_{v}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\delta\left\langle\dot{x}(s)\right\rangle_{\epsilon}}{\delta\epsilon f^{p}(s-t)}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}[ax_{0}(t)+bx_{0}(t-\tau)]+\frac{1}{\gamma}\delta(t)

(54)

for all
t
. In particular, we have
R_{v}(t)=0
for
t<0
.

Next, using Eq. (
53
) and the steady-state velocity
v_{s}=0
, the time correlation function in the steady state is expanded as

\displaystyle C_{v}(t)\equiv\bigl\langle[\dot{x}(s)-v_{s}][\dot{x}(s+t)-v_{s}]\bigr\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\dot{x}(s)\dot{x}(s+t)\right\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\Biggl\langle\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{s}[ax_{0}(s-s^{\prime})+bx_{0}(s-\tau-s^{\prime})]\xi(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}+\frac{\xi(s)}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\times\Biggl(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{s+t}[ax_{0}(s+t-s^{\prime})+bx_{0}(s+t-\tau-s^{\prime})]\xi(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\frac{\xi(s+t)}{\gamma}\Biggr)\Biggr\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2T}{\gamma^{3}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{s}[ax_{0}(s-s^{\prime})+bx_{0}(s-\tau-s^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\times[ax_{0}(s+t-s^{\prime})+bx_{0}(s+t-\tau-s^{\prime})]ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2T}{\gamma^{2}}[ax_{0}(|t|)+bx_{0}(|t|-\tau)]+\frac{2T}{\gamma}\delta(t),

(55)

where we neglect additional finite terms that are only present at
t=0
because they will not contribute to the Fourier transform of
C_{v}(t)
. Noting that
x_{0}(t)=0
for
t<0
, we can replace the integral
\int_{-\infty}^{s}ds^{\prime}
in the last expression with
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds^{\prime}
. Defining

K(t)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}x_{0}(s)x_{0}(s+t)ds=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x_{0}(s)x_{0}(s+t)ds,

(56)

which satisfies
K(t)=K(-t)
, we can rewrite Eq. (
55
) as

\displaystyle C_{v}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2T}{\gamma^{3}}\left\{(a^{2}+b^{2})K(t)+ab[K(t+\tau)+K(t-\tau)]\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2T}{\gamma^{2}}[ax_{0}(|t|)+bx_{0}(|t|-\tau)]+\frac{2T}{\gamma}\delta(t)

(57)

for all
t
. By similar calculations, we can obtain the expressions of
C_{v}(t)
and
R_{v}(t)
for the systems with multiple delay times in Eqs. (
25
) and (
26
).

The steady-state heat dissipation rate for the system with a single delay time
\tau>0
[Eq. (
7
)] can be calculated as

\displaystyle\left\langle J\right\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle[\gamma\dot{x}(t)-\xi(t)]\circ\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle[ax(t)+bx(t-\tau)]\circ\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle[ax(t)+bx(t-\tau)]\circ\frac{1}{\gamma}\{[ax(t)+bx(t-\tau)]+\xi(t)\}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\Biggl\langle\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\!\int_{-\infty}^{s}[ax_{0}(s-s^{\prime})+bx_{0}(s-\tau-s^{\prime})]\xi(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\circ\Biggl(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{s}[ax_{0}(s-s^{\prime})+bx_{0}(s-\tau-s^{\prime})]\xi(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\frac{\xi(s)}{\gamma}\Biggr)\Biggr\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{\gamma}a+\frac{2T}{\gamma^{2}}[(a^{2}+b^{2})K(0)+2abK(\tau)],

(58)

where we used
\bigl\langle\bigl[\int^{s}_{-\infty}f(s^{\prime})\xi(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}\bigr]\circ\xi(s)\bigr\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(2\gamma T)f(s)
due to the Stratonovich product.
We also used
\langle\xi(t-s)\xi(t)\rangle=0
for
s\geq\tau
, and accordingly
\langle x(t-\tau)\xi(t)\rangle=0
.
The quantities
K(0)
and
K(\tau)
have the following analytical expressions under the conditions Eq. (
11
) or Eq. (
12
)
[
76
]
:

\displaystyle K(0)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\gamma\cdot\dfrac{b\sinh(l\tau/\gamma)-l}{2l(a+b\cosh(l\tau/\gamma))},\quad\quad&&(|b|<-a)\\
&\dfrac{b\tau-\gamma}{4b},&&(b=a)\\
&\gamma\cdot\dfrac{b\sin(l\tau/\gamma)-l}{2l(a+b\cos(l\tau/\gamma))},&&(b<-|a|)\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(59)

\displaystyle K(\tau)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &K(0)\cdot\cosh(l\tau/\gamma)-\frac{\gamma\sinh(l\tau/\gamma)}{2l},\quad&&(|b|<-a)\\
&K(0)-\frac{\tau}{2},&&(b=a)\\
&K(0)\cdot\cos(l\tau/\gamma)-\frac{\gamma\sin(l\tau/\gamma)}{2l},&&(b<-|a|)\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(60)

where we define
l=\sqrt{|a^{2}-b^{2}|}
and note that
a
and
b
in Ref.
[
76
]
correspond to
a/\gamma
and
b/\gamma
in this paper. In the
\tau\to 0
limit,
K(0)
approaches
-\gamma/[2(a+b)]
and
\langle J\rangle_{0}
approaches
-bT/\gamma
.

Note that when
\tau=0
,
\langle bx(t-\tau)\circ\xi(t)/\gamma\rangle
in the third line of Eq. (
58
) will contribute an additional term
bT/\gamma
, leading to
\langle J\rangle_{0}=-bT/\gamma+bT/\gamma=0
. This abrupt emergence of the additional term is due to the mathematical assumption of white noise
\langle\xi(t-\tau)\xi(t)\rangle\propto\delta(\tau)
. Physically, the white-noise assumption becomes invalid when
\tau
is as short as the timescale of the environmental noise correlation, and this additional term should gradually emerge when
\tau
approaches zero.

Appendix D
Derivations of
\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)
and
\tilde{R}_{v}(\omega)

We calculate the Fourier transform of linear response function
R_{v}(t)
and the time correlation function
C_{v}(t)
for systems with a single delay time. The Fourier transform of
R_{v}(t)
is

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{v}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\{\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}[ax_{0}(t)+bx_{0}(t-\tau)]+\frac{1}{\gamma}\delta(t)\right\}e^{i\omega t}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}(a+be^{i\omega\tau})\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)+\frac{1}{\gamma}.

(61)

Here,
\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)
is the Fourier transform of the fundamental solution, which can be calculated as follows.
Recalling that the fundamental solution
x_{0}(t)
is the solution of Eq. (
7
) with the conditions in Eq. (
9
), we have

\dot{x}_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[ax_{0}(t)+bx_{0}(t-\tau)\right]+\delta(t),

(62)

where the delta function arises from the discontinuity of
x_{0}(t)
at
t=0
from
0
to
1
due to the choice of the historic trajectory
\phi(t)
in Eq. (
9
). Using this relation, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}[x_{0}(t)e^{i\omega t}]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[ax_{0}(t)+bx_{0}(t-\tau)\right]e^{i\omega t}+i\omega x_{0}(t)e^{i\omega t}+\delta(t)e^{i\omega t}.

(63)

Integrating Eq. (
63
) from
t=-\infty
to
t=\infty
and using the definition of the Fourier transform gives

\displaystyle\big[x_{0}(t)e^{i\omega t}\big]\bigg|_{t=-\infty}^{t=\infty}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[a\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)+b\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)e^{i\omega\tau}\right]+i\omega\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)+1.

(64)

The left-hand side vanishes because
x_{0}(t)=0
for
t<0
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}x_{0}(t)=0
. Rearranging gives

\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)=-\frac{\gamma}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]+i[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]}.

(65)

Inserting Eq. (
65
) into Eq. (
61
) gives

\displaystyle\tilde{R}^{\prime}_{v}(\omega)=\text{Re}\left[\tilde{R}_{v}(\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\text{Re}\left[(a+be^{i\omega\tau})\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)\right]+\frac{1}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+b\sin(\omega\tau)[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+2ab\cos(\omega\tau)+b\gamma\omega\sin(\omega\tau)}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma}.

(66)

Next, the Fourier transform of the time correlation function in Eq. (
57
) reads

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{2T}{\gamma^{3}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigl\{(a^{2}+b^{2})K(t)+ab\left[K(t+\tau)+K(t-\tau)\right]\Bigr\}e^{i\omega t}dt

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{4T}{\gamma^{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[ax_{0}(t)+bx_{0}(t-\tau)\big]e^{i\omega t}dt\right]+\frac{2T}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{2T}{\gamma^{3}}\bigl[(a^{2}+b^{2})+ab(e^{-i\omega\tau}+e^{i\omega\tau})\bigr]\tilde{K}(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{4T}{\gamma^{2}}\mathrm{Re}\bigl[(a+be^{i\omega\tau})\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)\bigr]+\frac{2T}{\gamma}.

(67)

We have already calculated
\mathrm{Re}[(a+be^{i\omega\tau})\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)]
in Eq. (
66
). We need to calculate the Fourier transform of
K(t)
. We rewrite the definition of
K(t)
in Eq. (
56
) using the inverse Fourier transform
x_{0}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}
as

\displaystyle K(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x_{0}(s)x_{0}(s+t)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega^{\prime})e^{-i\omega^{\prime}s}\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)e^{-i\omega(s+t)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)e^{-i(\omega^{\prime}+\omega)s}e^{-i\omega t}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega^{\prime})\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)\delta(\omega^{\prime}+\omega)e^{-i\omega t}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\tilde{x}_{0}(-\omega)\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}.

(68)

Comparing this expression with
K(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{K}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}
, we obtain the Fourier transform of
K(t)
,

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\tilde{x}_{0}(-\omega)\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]^{2}}.

(69)

Inserting into Eq. (
67
) gives

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{v}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{2T}{\gamma}\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+2ab\cos(\omega\tau)}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{4T}{\gamma}\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+2ab\cos(\omega\tau)+b\gamma\omega\sin(\omega\tau)}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]^{2}}+\frac{2T}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2T}{\gamma}\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+2ab\cos(\omega\tau)+2b\gamma\omega\sin(\omega\tau)}{[a+b\cos(\omega\tau)]^{2}+[\gamma\omega+b\sin(\omega\tau)]^{2}}+\frac{2T}{\gamma}.

(70)

For the dynamics with multiple delay times,
we assume the condition
A_{0}<-\sum_{i=1}^{N}|A_{i}|

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}x_{0}(t)=0

\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)=-\dfrac{\gamma}{i\gamma\omega+\sum_{i=0}^{N}A_{i}e^{i\omega\tau_{i}}}.

(71)

Then,

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\tilde{x}_{0}(\omega)\tilde{x}_{0}(-\omega)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\gamma^{2}}{\bigl[\sum_{i=0}^{N}A_{i}\cos(\omega\tau_i)\bigr]^{2}+\bigl[\gamma\omega+\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\sin(\omega\tau_i)\bigr]^{2}},

(72)

where we use
\sin(\omega\tau_0)=0

By a similar calculation to the case of a single delay time, we conclude Eqs. (
