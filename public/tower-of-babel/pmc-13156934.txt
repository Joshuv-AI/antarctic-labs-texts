Investigating the impact of ALI on erectile dysfunction in American men: a cross-sectional study using NHANES data
Authors: Shengyin He, Simin Wang, Yi Dai, Luo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is commonly defined as a persistent inability to achieve or maintain a penile erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual activity.1 Although it is rarely life-threatening, the condition is far from benign: substantial decrements in physical well-being, psychological health, and relational quality have been repeatedly documented, with partners often experiencing a measurable spillover of distress.2 Estimates from population-based surveys suggest that roughly 18% of men in the United States—on the order of 18 million individuals—are affected.3 On a global scale, the burden is expected to expand sharply, with projections approaching 322 million cases by 2025, a trajectory that appears to track the accelerating prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities.4 Prevalence is also strongly age-dependent, climbing from approximately 20% among men younger than 30 years to nearly 90% among those older than 70 years, a gradient that underscores the magnitude of ED as a public-health concern.5

The pathogenesis of ED is heterogeneous and seldom attributable to a single pathway; rather, vasculogenic, neurogenic, hormonal, iatrogenic, and psychogenic mechanisms frequently intersect within the same individual.6 Organic forms of ED often coexist with diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and metabolic syndrome—conditions that converge on endothelial dysfunction and progressive cavernosal fibrosis.7 Meanwhile, psychogenic factors, particularly depression, anxiety, and sustained stress exposure, may compound vulnerability through disruption of central neurochemical regulation, thereby amplifying risk even in the presence of modest organic impairment.8

Over the past several years, chronic inflammation has been increasingly discussed not merely as an epiphenomenon but as a plausible contributor to ED. Elevated circulating concentrations of C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and related mediators have been reported among patients with ED, consistent with a model in which persistent low-grade inflammation compromises penile endothelial function, attenuates the nitric oxide (NO)–cGMP signaling cascade, and ultimately restricts cavernosal perfusion, thereby facilitating erectile failure.9,10

Despite this accumulating evidence, there remains no widely adopted, clinically convenient inflammatory composite that simultaneously captures nutritional status and immune-inflammatory balance for ED risk assessment. The advanced lung-cancer inflammation index (ALI), originally proposed by Jafri and colleagues in 2013 as a prognostic metric for advanced non-small-cell lung cancer, integrates body mass index (BMI), serum albumin, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR).11 Subsequent studies have extended its utility beyond oncology, suggesting that ALI may function as an independent correlate of several chronic inflammatory disorders, including CVD, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease.12-15 Relative to single biomarkers, ALI is attractive in pragmatic terms: it is inexpensive, straightforward to compute, and—importantly—reflects both nutritional reserve and systemic inflammatory tone, features that suit large-scale epidemiological analyses as well as routine clinical settings. Yet, while early exploratory work has hinted at an association between ALI and ED, evidence from a systematic assessment in a general population remains limited. Unlike previously studied indices such as NLR, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), or inflammatory burden index (IBI), which primarily reflect inflammatory burden, ALI integrates BMI, albumin, and NLR and may therefore better capture a composite nutrition-inflammation status rather than inflammation alone.

Given the well-established link between ED and chronic inflammation, we hypothesized that ALI might serve as a novel inflammatory surrogate capable of reflecting the systemic inflammatory burden underlying ED. We therefore leveraged the nationally representative 2001–2004 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to clarify the cross-sectional association between ALI and ED and to provide epidemiological evidence for future mechanistic and longitudinal studies. In this context, ALI should be interpreted as a pragmatic composite indicator of nutritional, metabolic, and inflammatory status, rather than as evidence of a distinct inflammatory pathway.

Materials and methods

Data source and ethics

We performed a cross-sectional analysis using data from the 2001–2004 cycles of the NHANES, a nationally representative program of the US civilian, noninstitutionalized population. Detailed survey protocols, questionnaires, and laboratory manuals are publicly available online. All participants provided written informed consent, and the original survey protocols received ethics approval. Because this study used publicly available de-identified data, additional institutional review was not required.

Study population

ED assessment was restricted to men during the 2001–2002 and 2003–2004 examination cycles. Starting with 21 161 participants, we sequentially excluded those aged <18 y (n = 9548), lacked ALI or ED data (n = 4072), or excluded due to missing covariate information (n = 105). Ultimately, the final analysis cohort consisted of 3735 men (Figure 1).

Flow chart of the study.

Exposure: ALI

ALI = BMI (kg/m2) × serum albumin (g/dL) ÷ NLR. All laboratory tests were performed during the same mobile examination center (MEC) visit: serum albumin by the bromocresol-purple colorimetric assay (Beckman Coulter LX-20) and differential leukocyte counts by the Beckman Coulter MAXM analyser. Owing to right-skewness, ALI was log-transformed (ln-ALI) for regression analyses; participants were additionally grouped into population-based quartiles (Q1–Q4) to examine dose–response relationships.

Outcome: ED

ED was assessed using the validated NHANES prostate conditions module, which was conducted via an audio computer-assisted self-interview (ACASI):

“How would you describe your ability to get and keep an erection adequate for satisfactory intercourse?”

Response options: (1) always/almost always able, (2) usually able, (3) sometimes able, (4) never able. Men answering (3) or (4) were classified as having ED; those answering (1) or (2) served as the reference group.

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, prior literature on ED, and the availability of standardized NHANES variables with adequate completeness in the 2001–2004 cycles. However, several potentially important factors, including hormonal status, depression, medication use, prostate-related disease, and other physical conditions affecting sexual function, were not consistently available or sufficiently complete for inclusion in the present analysis. All variables were collected during the same survey cycle as exposure and outcome to maintain temporal consistency.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1 using the “survey” package to account for the complex sampling design, including strata, primary sampling units, and 4-year MEC weights. Baseline characteristics were summarized as survey-weighted means with standard errors (SEs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables, and weighted percentages for categorical variables. Group differences were assessed using design-based t tests or Rao-Scott χ2 tests.

To evaluate the association between ALI and the outcome, multivariable logistic regression was applied. ALI was modelled in 2 forms:

(i) as a continuous log-transformed variable (per 1-unit increase in ln-ALI)

(i) as a continuous log-transformed variable (per 1-unit increase in ln-ALI)

(ii) as quartiles (Q1 as the reference).

(ii) as quartiles (Q1 as the reference).

Three nested models were constructed:

Model 1:no covariates were adjusted.

Model 2: adjust: race, educational level, smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, poverty income ratio, age, marital status.

Model 3: adjust: Model 2 + hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, CVD.

Because ALI is a composite index derived from BMI, albumin, and NLR, and some adjusted covariates may reflect related biological domains, the possibility of overadjustment was considered when interpreting the fully adjusted model. Covariates were introduced in a nested manner to distinguish sociodemographic/lifestyle confounding from downstream cardiometabolic comorbidity burden and to assess the robustness of the ALI–ED association under increasing levels of adjustment.

A penalized-spline GAM (4 degrees of freedom) was fitted to visualize non-linearity; if the smoothing term was statistically significant (P < .05), two-piecewise linear regression with a likelihood ratio test was applied to determine the inflection point and the corresponding P value.

Subgroup analyses were performed as exploratory analyses to assess potential effect heterogeneity in the association between ALI and ED. Interaction between ln-ALI and each stratum was evaluated using Wald tests; however, given the number of subgroup comparisons, these analyses were considered hypothesis-generating and interpreted cautiously; stratified ORs and 95% CIs are reported. Survey-weighted ROC curves and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CIs were used to descriptively assess the within-sample discriminatory ability of ALI for ED status. To reduce optimism, the analytic sample was randomly divided into training and validation subsets at a 7:3 ratio; however, both subsets were derived from the same cross-sectional dataset and therefore do not constitute external validation. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

With respect to sociodemographic factors, race/ethnicity did not differ significantly between groups (P = .173). In contrast, educational attainment, poverty income ratio (PIR), age category, and marital status differed significantly (all P < .001). Men with ED were more likely to have less than a high school education (29.40% vs 13.62%), and to be ≥50 years of age (79.61% vs 26.30%). They were also more likely to be married/living with a partner (77.38% vs 69.04%).

Regarding lifestyle factors, the ED group had a higher prevalence of smoking (70.13% vs 53.94%; P < .001) and lower physical activity levels (low physical activity: 45.54% vs 33.98%; P < .001). Alcohol use also differed between groups, with a lower prevalence of drinking among men with ED (80.11% vs 84.63%; P = .017).

Comorbidity profiles indicated a greater cardiometabolic burden among men with ED, including higher prevalences of hypertension (57.68% vs 27.68%), diabetes (27.76% vs 6.03%), hyperlipidemia (79.87% vs 70.82%), and CVD (24.67% vs 5.09%) (all P < .001). Obesity was also more common in the ED group (35.52% vs 27.46%; P < .001).

Table 2 summarizes the survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression results for the association between ln-ALI and ED. In the crude model (Model 1), higher ln-ALI was associated with lower odds of ED. After adjusting for demographic and lifestyle factors (Model 2), the inverse association remained statistically significant. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the inverse association persisted, with each 1-unit increase in ln-ALI associated with lower odds of ED (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.25–0.84; P = .033). As shown in Figure 2, smoothed curve fitting suggested a nonlinear (U-shaped) association between ln-ALI and ED. A 2-piece linear regression model identified an inflection point at ln-ALI = 1.912 (likelihood-ratio test, P = .001; Table 3). Below 1.912, each 1-unit increase in ln-ALI was associated with lower odds of ED (OR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.11–0.39; P < .001). Above 1.912, the association was not statistically significant (OR = 2.51, 95% CI: 0.72–8.80; P = .150), indicating attenuation of the inverse association beyond this threshold.

Although the direction and magnitude of the association appeared to vary across some strata, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously. Several subgroup-specific estimates were imprecise, with wide confidence intervals, suggesting limited statistical power in certain strata. For example, among men aged <50 years, the association was not statistically significant (OR = 2.59, 95% CI: 0.55–12.27, P = .240), and the apparent reversal in direction should not be overinterpreted. Given the number of subgroup comparisons, these analyses are best viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of effect modification.

Discussion

ED is a multifactorial condition. Obesity is widely recognized as an important correlate of ED, and previous studies have reported a high prevalence of ED among men with elevated BMI.16,17 Collectively, these findings support BMI as an established factor related to ED.

Serum albumin may also be relevant to erectile function. Moderate increases in albumin could facilitate nitric oxide bioavailability, while its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties may help preserve endothelial function and vascular relaxation.18-20 Albumin may additionally influence androgen transport and regulation, and a recent NHANES-based analysis reported an inverse association between albumin levels and ED.21,22

The role of inflammation in ED has received increasing attention. Studies have shown that circulating inflammatory mediators—including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, CRP, and IL-10—tend to be elevated in men with ED, supporting an association between systemic inflammation and impaired erectile function.9,10 Moreover, the NLR, a readily available inflammatory marker, has also been reported to be higher in individuals with ED.19,23

Most prior epidemiologic studies have focused on single inflammatory markers or indices emphasizing immune components alone.24,25 In contrast, ALI integrates nutritional status (albumin and BMI) with inflammation (NLR), and may therefore provide a broader assessment of the joint contribution of nutrition and systemic inflammation to ED. Accordingly, the potential value of ALI in ED may lie not in replacing established inflammatory indices, but in complementing them from a broader nutrition-inflammation perspective.

In this nationally representative sample of US men, higher ALI was associated with lower odds of ED. In the multivariable-adjusted model, ALI remained inversely associated with ED (OR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.21–0.52; P < .001), and the association persisted after additional adjustment (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.19–0.74; P = .014; OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.25–0.84; P = .033). Overall, these findings suggest that an index reflecting a more favorable balance of nutritional reserve and lower inflammatory burden is associated with lower odds of ED in this cross-sectional dataset.

Subgroup analyses suggested possible heterogeneity in the association between ALI and ED across certain strata; however, these results should be interpreted with caution. Because multiple subgroup comparisons were performed, false-positive findings cannot be excluded. Moreover, several estimates were imprecise, as reflected by wide confidence intervals, indicating limited power in some subgroups. In particular, the non-significant estimate in men aged <50 years should not be interpreted as evidence of a true reverse association. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as exploratory and require confirmation in larger studies specifically designed to assess effect modification.

ED is more common with advancing age, and hypertension is considered a key correlate of ED.26,27 ED and CVD also frequently co-occur and may share pathophysiological pathways, particularly endothelial dysfunction.28-31 In our analyses, although effect estimates differed across strata, the inverse association between ALI and ED tended to be more evident among older men and among those with hypertension or CVD. One potential explanation is that endothelial dysfunction and impaired vascular responsiveness are more prevalent in these groups, making erectile function more sensitive to systemic inflammation and nutritional status. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design, mechanistic interpretations remain speculative and require confirmation in longitudinal and experimental studies.

We also observed evidence of nonlinearity, with a U-shaped association and an inflection point at ln-ALI = 1.912. Below this threshold, higher ln-ALI was strongly associated with lower odds of ED, whereas above the threshold the association was not statistically significant. This exploratory pattern suggests that the association between a combined nutrition–inflammation index and ED may not be strictly monotonic; however, the apparent threshold should not be interpreted as definitive and requires confirmation in independent datasets.

Finally, the ROC analysis suggested that ALI had measurable internal discrimination for ED status in this dataset. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. First, both the training and validation subsets were derived from the same cross-sectional NHANES sample, so the observed AUCs do not represent external validation. Second, the ROC model included ALI alone, whereas ED is influenced by multiple clinical factors, such as age, diabetes, hypertension, and CVD. Therefore, these AUC values should not be interpreted as evidence of real-world predictive performance or clinical utility. Future studies should evaluate multivariable models, calibration, and external validation in longitudinal cohorts before considering any predictive application. In addition, we did not formally evaluate whether ALI improves discrimination beyond established ED-related risk factors such as age, diabetes, hypertension, and CVD; therefore, the current ROC findings should not be interpreted as evidence of incremental predictive utility.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it is based on the NHANES dataset, which is representative of the national population and includes a diverse multi-ethnic population, thereby enhancing the generalizability of our findings. We also conducted comprehensive statistical adjustments, accounting for various potential confounders, and we performed exploratory subgroup analyses to examine whether the association between ALI and ED varied across selected strata, although these findings require cautious interpretation. Additionally, the innovative use of the ALI index allowed us to simultaneously consider both inflammation and nutritional status, providing a broader framework for understanding the relationship between nutritional-inflammatory status and ED.

Although this study provides valuable findings, it has several limitations. Firstly, due to its observational design, we cannot infer causality. While we found a significant inverse association between ALI and ED, it remains unclear whether an increase directly leads to the occurrence of ED, or if the presence of ED triggers changes in ALI. Therefore, future prospective studies will help better clarify the causal relationship. Secondly, ED was assessed using a single self-reported NHANES item rather than a validated multi-item instrument such as the IIEF-5 or SHIM. Although this measure has been used in prior population-based studies, it may be more vulnerable to misclassification, may underestimate the true prevalence or severity of ED, and may limit direct comparability with studies using standardized clinical questionnaires. Additionally, despite adjustment for multiple sociodemographic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic factors, residual confounding remains possible. Several established correlates of ED, including testosterone status, depression, medication use (eg, antidepressants and antihypertensive agents), prostate-related disease, and other physical comorbidities affecting sexual function, were unavailable, incompletely measured, or not uniformly comparable in the NHANES cycles analyzed here. Therefore, the observed association between ALI and ED should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, because ALI incorporates BMI, albumin, and NLR, and some adjusted comorbidities may lie on related biological pathways, overadjustment cannot be fully excluded, particularly in the fully adjusted model. Thirdly, our analysis is based on the NHANES 2001–2004 dataset, and while the dataset contains an ED assessment module, its findings must be considered in light of evolving demographic and disease patterns, which may not fully represent current realities. Therefore, future studies should examine the applicability of our findings in the current US population and explore similar relationships in other countries and regions. Future studies with more comprehensive phenotyping should evaluate whether the ALI–ED association persists after accounting for hormonal, psychological, medication-related, and urological factors. Importantly, because BMI is directly embedded in ALI and is itself closely related to ED and cardiometabolic health, the observed inverse association may partly reflect broader metabolic and nutritional status rather than a specific inflammatory mechanism alone.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of US men, higher ALI was associated with lower odds of ED. These findings suggest that a composite indicator reflecting nutritional status and systemic inflammation may be related to ED status; however, no causal or predictive inference can be made. Further longitudinal studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify their temporal relationship. The present findings therefore support ALI as a composite correlate of ED status in this cross-sectional setting, rather than as evidence of an independent inflammatory mechanism or incremental predictive utility.
