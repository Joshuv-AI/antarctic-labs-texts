A 71-year-old man presenting with cough and bilateral pulmonary opacities
Authors: Sreosi Samajdar, Mayur Goel, Suranjan Mukherjee, Kanika Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

A 71-year-old man, with a background history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, benign prostatic hyperplasia and hypothyroidism, presented with a gradual-onset, persistent cough with minimal sputum production for 1 month. This was associated with exertional shortness of breath that was progressive and present for the same duration. He also complained of anorexia for the previous 5 months and significant weight loss of 20 kg over the past 1 year. There was no associated chest pain, wheezing, haemoptysis, nasal symptoms, recurrent chest infections or history of any allergy. He denied any significant exposure to inhaled agents, any recent travel or keeping any exotic pets. He was a former smoker, having quit smoking 30 years previously with 0.5 pack-years of smoking. He denied any excess alcohol intake. He is a retired bank officer. His daily medications included glimepiride, linagliptin, silodosin, dutasteride, levothyroxine, cilnidipine, metoprolol and finerenone.

On examination, he weighed 69 kg with a height of 168 cm. There was no pallor, clubbing, lymphadenopathy, pedal oedema or icterus. He was afebrile, normotensive, not tachypnoeic or tachycardic, and maintaining oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. On admission, his random blood glucose level was 87 mg·dL−1. He had no episodes of dysglycaemia during his course of illness. His jugular venous pressure was 8 cmH2O and neck veins were not engorged. On respiratory system examination, his breathing was unlaboured; auscultation revealed decreased vesicular breath sounds over the infra-axillary and infra-scapular regions of both sides without any added sounds. Heart sounds were normal, and no additional sounds were heard. Neurological and abdominal examinations did not reveal any abnormalities.

Routine haematological and biochemical investigations were normal (table 1) and two-dimensional echocardiography showed 56% left ventricular ejection fraction, with normal study findings. A chest radiograph was performed (figure 1).

Task 1

What does the chest radiograph reveal?

Answer 1

The chest radiograph shows patchy inhomogeneous opacity in bilateral mid and lower zone suggestive of consolidation, giving a reverse bat wing appearance (figure 1).

Task 2

What is the differential diagnosis of this chest radiograph abnormality?

Answer 2

Differentials of reverse bat wing pulmonary opacities include:

1) Organising pneumonia

2) Chronic eosinophilic pneumonia

3) Pulmonary vasculitis

4) Pneumonitis (e.g. radiation)

5) Pulmonary contusion (in the setting of trauma)

6) Diffuse alveolar haemorrhage

7) Sarcoidosis

8) Pulmonary adenocarcinoma

A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the thorax (figure 2) was performed in view of the abnormal chest radiograph.

Task 3

What does contrast-enhanced CT scan of the thorax reveal?

Answer 3

The contrast-enhanced CT scan of the thorax shows peripheral subpleural consolidation and ground-glass opacities in both lungs with few bronchocentric airspace opacities. There are cystic spaces in bilateral upper and left lower lobes (figure 2).

A fibreoptic bronchoscopy and a transbronchial lung biopsy were performed. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid showed a cell count of 88 cells·mm−3, with a differential of 20% neutrophils, 3% lymphocytes, 35% macrophages and 42% epithelial cells; negative for malignant cells. A cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test (GeneXpert) was performed to test for Mycobacterium tuberculosis; the result was negative. Gram stain and culture revealed no growth of microorganisms. Histopathology findings from the transbronchial lung biopsy are shown in figure 3 and immunohistochemistry in figure 4.

Task 4

What does the histopathology of the biopsied sample show?

Answer 4

Histopathology examination of the biopsied sample (figure 3a and b) shows part of the specimen with alveolar spaces lined by mucinous epithelium with basally located small nuclei and apical mucin. Intra-alveolar mucin is also seen. Focal suspicion of microinvasion with desmoplastic stroma can also be identified (figure 3c). These findings are consistent with mucinous adenocarcinoma with lepidic pattern and focal suspicion of microinvasion. Immunohistochemistry of the biopsied specimen shows that the neoplastic mucinous epithelial cells express CK7 (strong and diffuse) (figure 4a) and CDX-2 (weak and moderate; patchy staining) (figure 4b), and were immune-negative for TTF-1, napsin-A and CK20 (figure 4c). TTF-1 and napsin-A are expressed by pneumocytes lining the normal alveoli. The immunohistochemistry profile is consistent with mucinous adenocarcinoma with lepidic pattern.

Task 5

What is the diagnosis?

Answer 5

Mucinous adenocarcinoma of the lung with lepidic pattern with focal microinvasion.

After the immunohistochemistry mutation report, the patient was referred to a medical oncologist for further assessment and a decision regarding chemotherapy.

Discussion

In our case, the patient presented with cough, exertional shortness of breath and a reverse bat wing pattern on imaging, which carries a wide differential diagnosis (as discussed in the answer to Task 2). Differentiating these diseases is not always easy and often requires bronchoscopy with BAL and biopsy. Our patient had a bronchoscopy and transbronchial lung biopsy, which revealed mucinous adenocarcinoma with lepidic pattern.

Lung carcinoma represents the foremost neoplasm in terms of mortality and morbidity on a global scale, with adenocarcinomas being prevalent histological variants of this malignancy [1, 2]. In 2011, a collaborative effort by the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer, the American Thoracic Society and the European Respiratory Society culminated in the publication of a histological classification system for lung adenocarcinomas [3]. The primary focus of this classification lies in its prognostic implications, given that histological subtype is intimately linked to clinical, pathological and molecular characteristics [4, 5].

The new classification, where the former bronchoalveolar carcinoma (BAC) concept was used, includes 1) adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), which can be nonmucinous and rarely mucinous, 2) minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), which can be nonmucinous and rarely mucinous, 3) lepidic-predominant adenocarcinoma (nonmucinous), 4) adenocarcinoma, predominantly invasive with some nonmucinous lepidic component (includes some resected tumours, formerly classified as mixed subtype, and some clinically advanced adenocarcinomas formerly classified as nonmucinous BAC), and 5) invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA; formerly mucinous BAC) [3].

From a histopathological point of view, the terms AIS (small, ≤30 mm, localised adenocarcinoma with growth restricted to neoplastic cells along with pre-existing alveolar structures, and pure lepidic growth with no invasive features) [6] and MIA (≤30 mm, solitary adenocarcinoma with a predominant lepidic pattern and ≤5 mm invasion) [7] should not be used for diagnosis of small biopsies or cytology specimens. If a noninvasive pattern is present in a small biopsy, it should be referred to as a lepidic growth pattern. Similarly, if a cytology specimen has attributes of AIS, the tumour should be diagnosed as adenocarcinoma, with a possibility of AIS, at least in a part [8]. As in our case the biopsied sample was small and showed alveolar spaces lined by mucinous epithelium with basally located nuclei and intracytoplasmic mucin, as well as focal suspicion of invasion, possibilities included 1) IMA, 2) MIA and 3) AIS mucinous type. In our case, distinguishing between these possibilities was based on a small biopsy and only a focal area showed suspicion of invasion. However, the diagnosis of MIA and AIS cannot be made on small biopsy specimens. Thus, because of the suspicion of invasion, the first differential was IMA. Since the biopsy was small and the diagnosis of invasion was on the basis of suspicion only, the other two differentials were also given, to keep these possibilities in mind when examining the excised specimen.

Besides reverse bat wing pattern, adenocarcinoma with lepidic pattern (formerly called BAC) can present with different radiological patterns, most commonly consolidations (40%), mass lesions (32%), nodules ≥1 cm (20%), nodules <1 cm (7%) and pleural effusion (1%) [9].

When a lung adenocarcinoma contains both invasive and noninvasive areas, the invasive part usually appears solid on CT, while the noninvasive part shows a hazy ground-glass appearance [10]. In lepidic-predominant adenocarcinoma, the CT appearance can vary, but the most characteristic finding is a part-solid nodule or mass [10].

Indicators such as low-density consolidation and the presence of the CT angiogram sign (where contrast-enhanced pulmonary vessels are visible within hypoattenuating lung tissue) may suggest mucinous adenocarcinoma [11]. Cavitation within these consolidations occurs in 40–78% of cases [12].

With a CT pattern of consolidation, adenocarcinoma with lepidic pattern characteristically presents with peripheral and lower-lobe-predominant consolidation, findings that significantly distinguish it from eosinophilic pneumonias and tuberculosis [13]. Air bronchograms and surrounding ground-glass opacities are frequently observed, while heterogeneous consolidation, bronchial narrowing or dilatation, and bulging fissures are additional supportive features [13]. Accessory nodules, particularly centrilobular and bronchocentric distributions, are more commonly identified in lung adenocarcinoma compared with eosinophilic pneumonia, in which such findings are typically absent. In contrast to tuberculosis, adenocarcinoma with lepidic pattern demonstrates fewer cavitations and less centrilobular nodularity but a higher prevalence of ground-glass opacities. These imaging characteristics assist in differentiating lung adenocarcinoma from infectious and inflammatory mimics such as eosinophilic pneumonias and tuberculosis [13]. Adenocarcinoma with lepidic pattern can also mimic bacterial pneumonia when presenting as a consolidation with nodules, but peripheral distribution is more common in adenocarcinoma with lepidic pattern and atelectasis is more common in infectious pneumonia [14], as well as in Pneumocystis infection or pulmonary alveolar proteinosis when exhibiting a crazy-paving pattern.

A variety of distinct characteristics of adenocarcinomas observed through CT have been identified as significant prognostic indicators, both favourable and unfavourable [10]. Favourable prognostic indicators encompass a ground-glass opacity, bubble-like lucencies, air bronchograms and diminutive size. Conversely, unfavourable indicators consist of prominent spiculations, thickened bronchovascular structures, pleural retraction, concave margins and increased size. The presence of ground-glass attenuation within a nodule has been correlated with a lepidic growth pattern in the neoplasm, indicating a more favourable prognosis [15]. Bubble-like lucencies and air bronchograms indicate a well-differentiated tumour, suggesting a favourable outcome [16, 17]. In contrast, marked spiculations, thickened bronchovascular bundles and a reduced proportion of ground-glass attenuation in the tumour are linked to a heightened likelihood of lymph node metastases and vascular invasion [18]. A smooth tumour margin along with a “solid appearance devoid of air bronchograms” implies a poorly differentiated tumour, associated with a poorer prognosis [17]. Furthermore, the presence of a concave margin or notch on the tumour's edge is deemed a negative prognostic factor [19].

In summary, lung adenocarcinoma can present with various CT signs, often mimicking other conditions. A high index of suspicion together with appropriate investigations, including bronchoscopy with BAL and biopsy, can establish the diagnosis.
