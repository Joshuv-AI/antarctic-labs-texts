Determinants of pesticide safety behaviors among Iranian farmers: applying the health action model
Authors: Fatemeh Sedghi, Roya Khorsandi, Ghazaleh Yosofnejhad, Najmeh Khodadadi, Seyedeh Belin Tavakoly Sany
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesGiven the widespread use of pesticides in agriculture and associated health risks, this study examined the psychosocial determinants of safety behaviors among Iranian farmers using the Health Action Model (HAM), a critical step for informing effective public health interventions.MethodsThis cross-sectional study applied the Health Action Model (HAM) to assess pesticide safety behaviors among 384 farmers in Northeast Iran, selected via multistage random sampling. Validated questionnaires measured knowledge, attitudes, and environmental factors affecting safety practices. Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics (SPSS v26).ResultsThe sample was predominantly male (72.4%) with a mean age of 46.2 ± 15.3 years and limited formal education (59.6% with a diploma). Key findings revealed that: (1) Knowledge systems were strong predictors of safety intention (β = 0.39) and practice (β = 0.28), while environmental factors had a similar impact (β ≈ 0.32); (2) A strong relationship was found between behavioral intention and actual action (β = 0.47, p < 0.001); however, the moderate strength of this pathway indicates a significant intention-action gap; (3) The effectiveness of training followed a dose-response relationship, with multiple sessions having a significantly greater impact than a single session and (4) Gender and education differences were evident, and females showed significantly lower risky intention (β = -0.18) and action (β = -0.17) scores compared to males.ConclusionsThe findings underscore that environmental factors and knowledge are the most robust predictors of safety behavior, suggesting that interventions must address both cognitive and structural barriers. This framework offers valuable guidance for implementing effective multi-component interventions, combining targeted education with infrastructural support, particularly in developing nations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25643-3.Keywords: Occupational health, Safety behavior, Pesticide, Public health

Introduction

Pesticides are chemical or biological substances used to control pests, including insects, weeds, pathogens, and other organisms that threaten agricultural and horticultural productivity [1].While pesticides play a crucial role in enhancing crop yields, many are highly hazardous due to their bio accumulative, persistent, and toxic properties, posing significant risks to human health and the environment [2]. Exposure to these chemicals has been linked to acute poisonings, chronic illnesses, and even fatalities, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where safety regulations and protective measures are often inadequate [3].

Globally, approximately 3 billion kilograms of pesticides—including insecticides, herbicides, fungicides, and rodenticides—are used annually [4]. Intentional self-poisoning using pesticides constitutes a significant public health challenge worldwide, resulting in an estimated 110,000 to 168,000 annual fatalities, with the highest burden observed in Asian countries [5]. In these regions, limited knowledge of proper pesticide handling, storage, and disposal exacerbates occupational and environmental hazards [6, 7]. Despite their agricultural benefits, the excessive and unregulated application of pesticides has led to severe ecological damage, including soil and water contamination, biodiversity loss, and long-term health consequences for farming communities [8].

Farmers face the highest risk of pesticide exposure during critical activities such as mixing, application, storage, and disposal, with primary exposure routes being dermal contact, inhalation, and oral ingestion [9, 10]. Such direct exposure frequently leads to acute health symptoms, including skin irritation, respiratory distress, nausea, and headaches [11].

In addition to these immediate effects, chronic exposure to pesticides significantly elevates the long-term risks of severe health conditions, including various cancers, neurological disorders, reproductive issues, and cardiovascular diseases [12, 13].

In Iran, despite advancements in sustainable agricultural practices (e.g., integrated pest management and biological controls), unsafe pesticide use remains widespread. Reports indicate that 13,000 Iranian farmers suffer from pesticide poisoning annually [14]. Furthermore, the country’s reliance on chemical inputs has grown, with over 2 million tons of pesticides and fertilizers distributed in 2018—a 1.5–1.78% increase from previous years [14, 15].

To address these challenges, behavioral models provide a valuable framework for understanding the socio-psychological factors influencing farmers’ pesticide-use practices. Among these, the Health Action Model (HAM) offers a comprehensive approach by examining how beliefs, motivations, and social norms shape behavioral intentions, as well as the environmental and cognitive barriers that prevent intentions from translating into safe actions [16]. Developed by Tones (1990), HAM integrates psychological, social, and environmental determinants to explain health-related decision-making, making it particularly useful for designing targeted interventions [17]. While models like the Health Belief Model (HBM) or the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) primarily focus on individual-level cognitive factors, the Health Action Model (HAM) was selected for its unique integration of motivational, normative, and belief systems with an explicit environmental systems component. This makes it particularly suited for studying agricultural safety behaviors, which are not only shaped by personal knowledge and attitudes but are also critically constrained or enabled by external, structural factors such as access to protective equipment and workplace infrastructure.

Since the Green Revolution, pesticides have become indispensable in global agriculture, yet many farmers remain unaware of their health and environmental impacts [18, 19]. Previous studies in Iran have primarily concentrated on assessing farmers’ knowledge and safety practices regarding pesticide use [20–23], yet there remains a need to explore the cognitive and psychosocial factors that influence and motivate their safety behaviors. This study extends prior work by being the first to integratively apply the Health Action Model (HAM) in this context, simultaneously examining cognitive, motivational, and environmental determinants of both intention and actual safety behavior regarding pesticide use among Iranian farmers, thereby providing a comprehensive evidence base for targeted interventions.

Methods

Study design

This descriptive cross-sectional study employed the Health Action Model (HAM) framework to investigate health and safety practices related to agricultural pesticide use among summer crop farmers in Mashhad, Khorasan Razavi Province, northeastern Iran, during January-March 2024. The research utilized questionnaires distributed immediately following pesticide application events. Although described as self-administered, a trained research assistant was present throughout the process to clarify any questions participants had, ensuring comprehension despite the ‘basic literacy’ requirement.

Sampling strategy

The sampling strategy was designed to ensure representativeness of the target population of approximately 100,000 summer crop farmers documented by Agricultural Jihad records. The sample size was calculated using Krejcie and Morgan’s formula for known finite populations:

n = required sample size/N = total population size (100,000)/χ2 = chi-square value for 1 degree of freedom at the desired confidence level (3.841 for 95% confidence)/p = population proportion (assumed to be 0.5 to maximize sample size)/d = margin of error (0.05)”/*: Multiple.

This calculation yielded a minimum required sample size of 384 participants. Implementation involved a multistage random sampling approach beginning with a random selection of 100 farms from 1000 eligible agricultural units in Mashhad’s suburban areas. This was followed by the random recruitment of four participants per farm from a list of all eligible individuals (one farm manager and three workers directly involved in pesticide handling) present on the day of data collection. The response rate was approximately 96%, with 400 questionnaires distributed and 384 fully completed ones returned.

Eligibility criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old with minimum one year of farming experience, daily farm work exposure of two hours or more, basic literacy skills, and direct involvement in pesticide mixing/application processes. Individuals with cognitive or physical impairments were excluded, as were those unwilling to participate or who submitted incomplete questionnaires. The study protocol emphasized ethical considerations through written informed consent procedures that guaranteed confidentiality and voluntary participation. To minimize response bias, participants were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses. The questionnaires were checked for completeness on-site by the research team. Only fully completed questionnaires were included in the analysis, resulting in no missing data.

Ethical considerations

The study received ethical approval from Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUMS.REC.1403.4304032065) and all procedures adhered to Declaration of Helsinki guidelines for human subject’s research, with particular attention to informed consent protocols and data confidentiality measures throughout data collection and analysis phases.

The research instrument was a structured questionnaire based on the Health Action Model (HAM) constructs. It consisted of two main sections:

Demographic Information: Collecting data on age, gender, marital status, education, income, smoking status, farming experience, farm size, daily work hours, household size, and safety training history.

The questionnaire was developed through a sequential mixed-methods process to ensure its contextual and theoretical relevance. First, a comprehensive review of the scientific literature on pesticide safety behaviors and the Health Action Model was conducted to generate an initial item pool. Subsequently, this item pool was refined and contextualized through two focus group discussions with 15 farmers, which ensured the language, scenarios, and concepts were relevant and clearly understood by the target population. This qualitative phase was crucial for establishing face and content validity within the specific cultural and occupational context of Iranian agriculture. The final instrument was then subjected to quantitative validation by an expert panel, as described below.

Validity and reliability

Instrument validation followed rigorous protocols with evaluation by a 10-member expert panel spanning education, health and environmental science disciplines. Quantitative measures confirmed content validity with Content Validity Ratio of 0.94 and Content Validity Index of 0.98, while all items exceeded the 0.5 threshold for Item-Objective Congruence. Reliability assessment using data from the main study (N = 384) demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients as follows: Knowledge Systems (0.81), Belief Systems (0.78), Normative Systems (0.79), Motivation Systems (0.84), Environmental Systems (0.80), Behavioral Intention (0.83), and Action (0.85).

The final 62-item questionnaire generated total scores ranging from 0 to 62, which were categorized into three knowledge levels: inadequate (scores below 30), moderate (30–50), and adequate (above 50). Primary assessments included a 25-item pesticide safety knowledge evaluation spanning four domains - safe storage/usage with five dichotomous items, application safety with ten Likert-type items, post-application safety with six Likert-type items, and adverse effects knowledge with three Likert-type items. All other HAM constructs were measured using comprehensive 5-point Likert scales as detailed in Supplementary File.

The pilot test confirmed the clarity, relevance, and face validity of the items. No items were dropped based on feedback, and no participants dropped out, indicating good acceptability of the instrument.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26 and Stata/MP 18.0. Descriptive statistics, including means, frequencies, and standard deviations, were used to characterize the sample. The choice between parametric and non-parametric tests for bivariate analyses was guided by the assessment of normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Inferential analyses employed chi-square tests, ANOVA, Mann-Whitney U tests, and independent t-tests to examine bivariate relationships.

For multivariate analysis, linear regression models were constructed. Given the multistage sampling design, which induces clustering at the farm level, these models employed cluster-robust standard errors to account for intra-cluster correlation, ensuring valid statistical inference. The assumptions of linear regression were verified: data were screened for multicollinearity using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) statistics, with all values below 2.5 indicating no concerning multicollinearity, and the normality of residuals was confirmed via visual inspection of Q-Q plots. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. Test-retest reliability was not assessed due to time and logistical constraints in re-accessing the geographically dispersed farmer population.

Missing data

No missing data were present in the final analytical dataset, as only fully completed questionnaires were included in the analysis.

Result

The study population comprised 384 pesticide applicators from agricultural communities in northeastern Iran. Participants had a mean age of 46.2 years (SD = 15.3), with an age range spanning from 21 to 67 years. Gender distribution revealed a predominance of male workers (72.4%, n = 278) compared to female participants (28.0%, n = 107). The majority of respondents were married (61.0%, n = 234), while 39.0% (n = 149) reported being single, widowed, or divorced. Integrated multivariate linear regression analyses, using cluster-robust standard errors to account for the sampling design, were conducted to identify predictors of behavioral intention and action.

Application safety knowledge among farmers showed varied results. While 88% of participants recognized the importance of suitable weather conditions for pesticide application, only 26% were aware of the proper protocols regarding wind direction. It is concerning that 32% of farmers still believed that mixing pesticides in open environments was acceptable. Knowledge about post-application safety was particularly lacking, as only 23% understood the protective benefits of bathing and changing clothes after working with pesticides, even though 93% acknowledged the necessity of having farm shower facilities. Misconceptions about poisoning management were also prevalent, with 82% of farmers incorrectly believing that drinking milk could alleviate the effects of poisoning. Additionally, awareness regarding the environmental impacts of pesticide use was alarmingly low; only 8% understood how pesticide overuse could affect food quality, and merely 25% recognized the risks of environmental contamination from pesticides. These findings highlight significant gaps in awareness between personal protective equipment (PPE) usage and essential safety knowledge, especially concerning exposure prevention and environmental protection measures. In the cognitive factors section, knowledge systems emerge as the most robust positive predictors for both intention (β = 0.391, p < 0.001) and action (β = 0.281, p < 0.001). This indicates that a stronger understanding of safety practices consistently leads to improved outcomes. In contrast, belief systems significantly influence intention formation (β = 0.187, p = 0.004) but do not directly impact the implementation of safe practices, suggesting beliefs shape planning but may not translate into action.

Motivational and environmental components exhibit distinct patterns. Motivation remains a significant factor in both models, though its effect size diminishes for actual practices (β = 0.146, p = 0.030). Workplace environmental conditions, however, show a consistent and significant influence on both outcomes (β = 0.32 for both). Additionally, the relationship between intention and action is particularly strong (β = 0.469, p < 0.001), reinforcing the theoretical connection between these constructs within the health action model.

Higher education levels consistently correlate with a reduction in risky behaviors, with postgraduate degrees showing the strongest protective effect (β = −0.215 to −0.223). Female gender demonstrates modest but significant protective effects (β ≈ −0.18). Educational experience, especially when involving multiple sessions, emerges as the most influential demographic factor (β = 0.342–0.365). These findings underscore the importance of modifiable factors, such as education, over inherent characteristics in shaping outcomes and guiding interventions.

Model fit statistics highlight strong explanatory power, with the intention model accounting for 48.1% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.481) and the practice model explaining 42.7% (adjusted R² = 0.427.

In summary, the results highlight several key patterns: (1) Knowledge and environmental factors emerged as the strongest predictors of both safety intention and action, (2) A significant positive relationship was found between behavioral intention and actual practice, and (3) Demographic characteristics such as education, gender, and training history demonstrated significant associations with the outcomes. These findings set the stage for a detailed discussion of their theoretical and practical implications.

Discussion

Using the Health Action Model (HAM) to integrate psychosocial and environmental determinants, this study provided valuable insights into the factors influencing pesticide safety behaviors among Iranian farmers. Findings suggest that while farmers’ knowledge and awareness of safety protocols significantly shape their behavioral intentions, the actual implementation of these intentions is severely hampered by environmental and structural barriers, with the influence of environmental factors outweighing the influence of knowledge. This finding aligns with observations in Pakistan [24], Indonesia [25], and Uganda [26], but quantifies the relative strength of environmental predictors over cognitive ones in this specific context. This likely reflects Iran’s unique combination of relatively advanced safety knowledge with persistent infrastructural deficiencies, a challenge shared with other LMICs undergoing rapid agricultural transition.This plateau is attributed to factors suggested in prior literature, For instance, our finding that environmental barriers outweigh knowledge aligns with studies from Kenya [27], where limited access to PPE was a primary deterrent to safe practices, and India, where infrastructural deficits hindered the adoption of learned safety measures despite training [28]. Similarly, the intention-action gap we observed is a common theme in agricultural safety research across the world, as noted by Sapbamrer & Thammachai (2020) [29].

Key determinants of safety behavior

The strong intention-action relationship confirms key theoretical predictions while also revealing implementation gaps specific to agricultural contexts, where economic pressures and cultural norms may overshadow safety intentions more than in other occupational settings [30]. Our empirical testing of the Health Action Model revealed several key insights. First, the strong direct pathways from both knowledge and environmental systems to action underscore the necessity of addressing both cognitive and structural barriers in interventions. Second, the substantial coefficient (β = 0.47) between intention and action, while significant, also indicates the presence of a considerable gap, suggesting that factors beyond intention are at play. This nuanced view extends the application of HAM in agricultural settings by quantifying these critical relationships.

This gap raises important assumptions about the effectiveness of interventions that rely solely on improving knowledge and suggests that cognitive approaches alone may not be sufficient to overcome the structural barriers that farmers face despite strong intentions in resource-limited settings [29]. This underscores the importance of improving physical working conditions, including access to adequate protective equipment and facilities, as a direct and effective means of increasing safety behaviors [31]. As such, the findings support a more comprehensive approach that considers both cognitive and structural factors to effectively promote pesticide safety behaviors among farmers.

The influence of education and training

The study revealed that farmers’ education levels significantly influence their safety practices when using pesticides. Farmers with higher education (diploma or above) are more likely to utilize personal protective equipment and adhere to safety guidelines.

Illiterate farmers, farm workers, and individuals with low education levels face a heightened risk when handling pesticides, as they may struggle to comprehend instructions regarding safe usage and the potential harmful effects. Research consistently highlights that education and literacy are critical factors influencing the adoption of personal protective equipment (PPE) and the safe application of pesticides. Therefore, implementing educational programs to enhance farmers’ knowledge of pesticide safety should be treated as a top priority [29]. Our post-hoc analysis suggested that the positive effect of training on safety intention was more pronounced among farmers with lower education levels, hinting at a potential compensatory effect that warrants further investigation.

Interestingly, this positive correlation diminishes beyond a certain educational threshold, typically at the bachelor’s degree level. This plateau is attributed to factors suggested in prior literature, such as the potential delegation of hazardous tasks, overconfidence among educated farmers, persistent environmental challenges like insufficient safety equipment, and economic pressures that compel farmers—regardless of education—to prioritize immediate financial returns over strict adherence to safety protocols [25, 32]. Also, the counterintuitive finding that higher education was associated with lower safety compliance may be explained by confounding factors not fully captured in our model. These could include the delegation of hazardous tasks to laborers by more educated farm owners, overconfidence leading to riskier behaviors, or different work roles that inherently involve greater exposure, despite higher knowledge levels.

The training effect followed a clear dose-response pattern, with multiple sessions demonstrating a significantly greater impact than single sessions. This finding supports the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines on the importance of repeated education [33], Furthermore, research indicates that farmers who undergo training exhibit heightened environmental awareness. For instance, Ying et al. found that short-term training not only reduces fertilizer and pesticide use but also fosters technology diffusion within villages, influencing farmers’ behaviors in neighboring communities [34]. Notably, our study has empirically established a previously undocumented minimum effective threshold. Specifically, our cross-sectional data suggest a dose-response pattern, where farmers who received multiple training sessions reported significantly better safety practices. This points to the potential value of sustained educational efforts and provides a testable hypothesis for future intervention studies.

Gender dynamics in safety practices

Demographic analysis from the study indicates that female farmers generally demonstrate safer pesticide use practices. However, a review of other studies highlights that this difference is primarily influenced by the gender division of labor in Iranian agriculture, where women are less directly involved in pesticide application tasks [35, 36]. This distinction, though notable, is less pronounced compared to other countries [34, 37, 38]. In Iran, women often engage in less hazardous activities such as packing or transporting produce, while men predominantly handle pesticide application [39]. Furthermore, unequal access to safety training exacerbates this disparity, as most training programs are tailored for male participants. Cultural factors, including traditional beliefs surrounding masculinity and risk tolerance, also discourage male farmers from adopting safer practices [22, 40, 41]. These findings suggest that safety interventions should be gender-sensitive, ensuring training and resources are accessible to women who may be indirectly exposed, while simultaneously addressing the cultural norms that discourage risk-averse behavior among men.

Theoretical and practical implications

Our findings contribute to the theoretical development of HAM in agricultural contexts. First, they underscore that the ‘environmental systems’ construct should be considered a primary, direct determinant of behavior, not merely a peripheral factor moderating intention. Second, they reveal a context-specific attenuation of the belief-behavior pathway, suggesting that in resource-constrained settings, deeply held beliefs about safety may be overridden by immediate practical constraints, a nuance that could refine the model’s application in future studies.

The theoretical implications of these findings are substantial, necessitating modifications to conventional behavioral models when applied to agricultural settings. First, the study highlights the critical importance of incorporating environmental dimensions, such as climate variability and resource availability, into health behavior frameworks. These factors significantly influence decision-making processes in farming communities and cannot be overlooked in intervention designs. Second, it reveals context-specific modifications to the Health Action Model, particularly regarding the attenuated belief-behavior relationship within agricultural populations. For example, while farmers may express strong beliefs in the benefits of certain practices, external constraints such as financial limitations or seasonal demands often weaken the translation of beliefs into actions. Third, the study provides empirical evidence for non-linear training effects, demonstrating that the relationship between the number of training sessions and behavioral outcomes is not simply linear. Instead, diminishing returns may occur after a certain point, challenging traditional assumptions about intervention dosing in behavioral change programs and emphasizing the need for strategic optimization of training schedules.

From a practical perspective, these results suggest that agricultural safety programs should move beyond awareness-raising campaigns to integrated interventions that simultaneously address knowledge gaps, motivational barriers, and physical constraints. Policymakers should consider reallocating resources to ensure balanced investments in both education and infrastructure improvements, with particular attention to making safety equipment consistently available at worksites. Training programs should be redesigned to emphasize practical problem-solving skills and delivered through brief, frequent sessions rather than comprehensive but infrequent workshops.

Policy and practical recommendations

Based on the empirical findings of this study, we propose a multi-faceted approach for policymakers and agricultural extension services to effectively translate safety knowledge into consistent practice. To bridge the critical intention-action gap, interventions must move beyond awareness campaigns and concurrently address the structural and economic barriers identified. Key actionable strategies include the implementation of government or cooperative-led subsidy programs to enhance the financial accessibility of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) for smallholder farmers. Furthermore, the deployment of mobile training units can deliver the essential, repeated, and hands-on instruction directly to farming communities, thereby overcoming logistical barriers and fulfilling the documented dose-response training effect. It is also imperative to formally integrate pesticide safety and HAM-based behavioral modules into the core curriculum of existing agricultural extension programs. Finally, concurrent investment in rural infrastructure, such as designated pesticide storage facilities and on-farm washing stations, is vital to address the foundational environmental constraints that currently hinder the adoption of safe practices, even among knowledgeable and motivated farmers.

Limitations & future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The cross-sectional design prevents definitive causal conclusions, and self-reported measures may be subject to social desirability bias and may overestimate actual safety compliance. The regional focus on one province of Iran may limit generalizability to other agricultural contexts with different cultural norms, economic conditions, or levels of farm mechanization. Future research should employ longitudinal designs with objective outcome measures, mixed methods to explore contextual moderators, and intervention trials to test the effectiveness of integrated approaches.

Conclusion

This study makes key contributions to agricultural safety research by highlighting the critical influence of environmental factors in developing country contexts, such as limited access to protective equipment and inadequate infrastructure. The findings underscore the need to move beyond individual-focused interventions, advocating for comprehensive programs that simultaneously address knowledge gaps through targeted education campaigns, motivational barriers by fostering community-driven safety initiatives (e.g., peer-led training, farmer cooperatives), and physical constraints by improving access to resources and technology. By leveraging the Health Action Model, the analysis provides a robust framework for designing more effective safety interventions, offering actionable insights for policymakers, researchers, and practitioners. These interventions apply to similar agricultural settings globally, with particular relevance for Middle Eastern and developing economies that face challenges in translating safety knowledge into consistent practices due to cultural, economic, and logistical hurdles.
