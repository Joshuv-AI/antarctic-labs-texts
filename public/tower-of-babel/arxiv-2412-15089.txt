Four-manifolds, two-complexes and the quadratic bias invariant
Ian Hambleton, John Nicholson

Abstract
. Kreck and Schafer produced the first examples of stably diffeomorphic closed smooth 4 4 -manifolds which are not homotopy equivalent. They were constructed by applying the doubling construction to 2 2 -complexes over certain finite abelian groups of odd order. By extending their methods, we formulate a new homotopy invariant on the class of 4 4 -manifolds arising as doubles of 2 2 -complexes with finite fundamental group. As an application we show that, for any k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , there exist a family of k k closed smooth 4 4 -manifolds which are all stably diffeomorphic but are pairwise not homotopy equivalent.

1.
Introduction

Two closed smooth 4-manifolds
M
,
N
are said to be
stably diffeomorphic
if there exists
r\geq 0
and a diffeomorphism
M\#r(S^{2}\times S^{2})\cong N\#r(S^{2}\times S^{2})
. Kreck’s modified surgery
[
41
]
gives techniques to classify
4
-manifolds up to stable diffeomorphism, and these methods have been applied to study manifolds over a range of fundamental groups
[
26
,
29
,
28
,
24
,
25
,
23
,
34
,
38
]
.

The existence of exotic smooth structures shows that simply-connected oriented
4
-manifolds which are stably diffeomorphic need not be diffeomorphic,
but it follows from results of Donaldson
[
16
]
and Wall
[
64
]
that
such
4
-manifolds are
h
-cobordant and hence homotopy equivalent.

In contrast, Kreck-Schafer
[
42
]
produced the first examples of closed smooth
4
-manifolds which are stably diffeomorphic, but not even homotopy equivalent.
Their examples arose from the following
doubling construction
: for a finite
2
-complex
X
, let
M(X)
be the boundary of a smooth regular neighbourhood of an embedding
X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{5}
(see
Section

5
for more details).
The construction has the following properties (see
[
42
, Section 2]
):

(i)

If
X
and
Y
are finite 2-complexes such that
X\simeq Y
are homotopy equivalent, then
M(X)
and
M(Y)
are
h
-cobordant, hence homotopy equivalent.

(ii)

If
\chi(X)=\chi(Y)
and
\pi_{1}(X)\cong\pi_{1}(Y)
, then
M(X)
and
M(Y)
are stably diffeomorphic.

Their main result was that there exist pairs of finite
2
-complexes
X
,
Y
with
\chi(X)=\chi(Y)
and
M(X)\not\simeq M(Y)
such that
\pi_{1}(X)\cong\pi_{1}(Y)
is elementary abelian of odd order.
To achieve this, they defined a homotopy invariant (in
\mathbb{Z}/{2}
) for doubles
M(X)
with
\pi_{1}(X)
finite of odd order.

In this paper we will define and study the
quadratic bias invariant
, which generalises the invariant of Kreck-Schafer.
Let
G
be a finite group and
X
a finite 2-complex with
\pi_{1}(X)\cong G
which is
minimal
in the sense that
\chi(X)
is minimal over such complexes.
The
bias invariant
was defined by Metzler
[
47
]
to be a class
\beta(X)
in an abelian group
B(G):=(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times}/\langle\pm D(G)\rangle
where
m=m_{G}
(
Definition

3.4
) and
D(G)
is the image of a certain map
\varphi\colon\Aut(G)\to(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}
(
Definition

3.17
).
The quadratic bias invariant will be a class
\beta_{Q}(M(X))
in a quotient group
B_{Q}(G)
of
B(G)

(
Definition

6.10
).
Let
\mathscr{M}_{4}(G)
denote the set of homotopy types of
4
-manifolds
M(X)
where
X
is a minimal finite 2-complex with
\pi_{1}(X)\cong G
. We will show:

Theorem A
.

The quadratic bias invariant is a homotopy invariant. In particular, for a finite group
G
, the quadratic bias invariant defines a map

\beta_{Q}\colon\mathscr{M}_{4}(G)\to B_{Q}(G).

Furthermore,
\beta_{Q}(M(X))=q(\beta(X))
where
q\colon B(G)\twoheadrightarrow B_{Q}(G)
is the natural surjection.

The extent to which the quadratic bias can be used to distinguish manifolds depends on choosing the quotient
B_{Q}(G)
of
B(G)
so that
\beta_{Q}(M(X))
is both a homotopy invariant and can also be computed in non-trivial examples.

Sections

6
and
7
are directed towards this goal.
A description of the subgroup
N(G)=\ker(q\colon B(G)\to B_{Q}(G))
can be found in
Remark

6.11
(ii).

If
H_{2}(G;\mathbb{Z})
has a certain special form we can explicitly compute the quadratic bias obstruction group
B_{Q}(G)
.
Recall that a finite group
G
is
efficient
if
\chi_{\min}(G)=1+d(H_{2}(G;\mathbb{Z}))
, where
\chi_{\min}(G)
denotes the minimal Euler characteristic of a finite
2
-complex
X
with
\pi_{1}(X)\cong G
, and

d(\cdot)
denotes the minimal number of generators of a group
[
32
]
.

Theorem B
.

Let
G
be a finite group such that
H_{2}(G;\mathbb{Z})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{d}

for some
m\geq 1
,
d\geq 3
. If
G
is efficient, then there is an isomorphism

B_{Q}(G)\cong\frac{(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times}}{\pm(\mathbb{Z}/{m})^{\times 2}\cdot D(G)}

where
D(G)=\IM(\varphi_{G}\colon\Aut(G)\to(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times}/\{\pm 1\})
. If
G
is not efficient, then
B_{Q}(G)=0
.

It is known that finite abelian groups are efficient
[
57
, Proposition 5]
, but Swan constructed a group of the form
G\cong(\mathbb{Z}/{7})^{3}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/{3}
which is not efficient
[
60
, p196]
.

Remark 1.1
.

We would expect the structure of
B_{Q}(G)
to be much more complicated in general. Its definition involves new ideas related to the unitary isometries of multi-scaled hyperbolic forms arising from the decomposition of
H_{2}(G;\mathbb{Z})
into cyclic factors (see
Proposition

4.15
).

Using Theorems
A
and
B
, we are now able to effectively compare closed smooth 4-manifolds of the form
M(X)
up to homotopy equivalence. This allows us to establish the following, which answers a recent question of Kasprowski-Powell-Ray in the affirmative
[
36
, Question 1.5]
.

Theorem C
.

For each
k\geq 2
, there exist closed smooth
4
-manifolds
M_{1},M_{2},\cdots,M_{k}
which are all stably diffeomorphic but not pairwise homotopy equivalent.

These examples can be taken to be stably parallelisable and have finite abelian fundamental groups of the form
G=(\mathbb{Z}/{m})^{d}
, for any
d\geq 3
odd, and any
m\geq 3
with sufficiently many distinct prime factors. This generalises the case
k=2
which was established by Kreck-Schafer
[
42
]
over fundamental groups of the form
(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{d}
for a prime

p\equiv 1\,\,\text{mod}\,4
. We also construct examples over non-elementary abelian groups
(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}/t
where
d\geq 4
is even and certain values of
m
and
t
with
m\neq t
.

Remark 1.2
.

The result of
Theorem

C
is optimal for manifolds with finite fundamental group, since by
[
26
, Corollary 1.5]
there are only finitely many homeomorphism types of closed
4
-manifolds with a given finite fundamental group and Euler characteristic.
It remains open whether there exists an infinite collection of such manifolds with arbitrary fundamental group.

To obtain examples over other fundamental groups, we require a pair of finite
2
-complexes
X
,
Y
with finite fundamental group
G
and
\chi(X)=\chi(Y)
but which are not homotopy equivalent. Such examples have previously only been known to exist when
G
is either a finite abelian group
[
47
,
57
]
or a group with periodic cohomology
[
18
,
52
,
50
]
.
However, if
G
has periodic cohomology, then
H_{2}(G;\mathbb{Z})=0

[
61
, Corollary 2]
and so the bias invariant contains no information.

In spite of this, we will establish the following (see
Theorem

9.5
). This serves to demonstrate that the quadratic bias invariant is computable for non-abelian fundamental groups.

Theorem D
.

Let
G=Q_{8}\times(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{3}
where
p
is a prime such that
p\equiv 1\,\,\text{mod}\,8
. Then:

(i)

There exist minimal finite
2
-complexes
X
,
Y
with fundamental group
G
which are homotopically distinct.

(ii)

There exist closed smooth
4
-manifolds
M
,
N
with fundamental group
G
which are stably diffeomorphic but not homotopy equivalent.

This also gives the first example of a non-abelian finite group
G
which does not have periodic cohomology such that there exists homotopically distinct finite
2
-complexes
X
,
Y
with fundamental group
G
and
\chi(X)=\chi(Y)
.
Part (ii) follows from (i) by taking
M=M(X)
,
N=M(Y)
and applying the conditions of
Theorem

B
to show that
\beta_{Q}(M)\neq\beta_{Q}(N)
.

A number of interesting questions remain concerning the doubles
M(X)
.
If
X\simeq Y
, then
M(X)
and
M(Y)
are
h
-cobordant.
More generally, we ask:

Question 1.3
.

If
X\simeq Y
, then are
M(X)
and
M(Y)
diffeomorphic?

An important special case is when
X
is a point and
Y
is the presentation
2
-complex of a potential counterexample to the Andrews-Curtis conjecture
[
3
]
. In this case we have
X\simeq Y
,
M(X)=S^{4}
,
M(Y)\simeq S^{4}
and so
Question

1.3
is equivalent to the question of whether
M(Y)
is an exotic
4
-sphere.
Such examples were considered by Akbulut-Kirby in
[
2
]
and, for one such example,
Question

1.3
was shown by Gompf to have an affirmative answer
[
21
]
.

It was recently shown by Freedman-Krushkal-Lidman that all Seiberg-Witten invariants vanish for doubles
M(X)

[
20
, Proposition 1.3]
.
We could also ask
Question

1.3
for simple homotopy equivalent
2
-complexes, since in that case
M(X)
and
M(Y)
are
s
-cobordant
[
42
, p15]
.

1a
.
Comparison with quadratic
2
-type

In
[
26
,
6
]
, it was shown that a closed topological 4-manifold
M
with
\pi_{1}(M)
finite of odd order is determined up to homotopy equivalence by its quadratic 2-type

Q(M)=[\pi_{1}(M),\pi_{2}(M),k_{M},S_{M}]

where
k_{M}\in H^{3}(\pi_{1}(M);\pi_{2}(M))
denotes the
k
-invariant and
S_{M}\colon\pi_{2}(M)\times\pi_{2}(M)\to\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{1}(M)]
denotes the equivariant intersection form.
An isometry
\cong
of two such quadruples is an isomorphism of pairs
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}
respecting the
k
-invariant and inducing an isometry on
S
.
For arbitrary finite fundamental groups, there is a possibly non-zero additional invariant which lies in
\Tors(\Gamma(\pi_{2}(M))\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}G}\mathbb{Z})
.
However, we will show (see
Theorem

6.16
):

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathscr{M}_{4}(G)
.
If
Q(M_{1})\cong Q(M_{2})
are isometric, then
\beta_{Q}(M_{1})=\beta_{Q}(M_{2})
.

By
Theorem

C
and
[
37
,
35
,
26
,
6
]
, there are examples of stably diffeomorphic homotopy distinct 4-manifolds
M_{1},\cdots,M_{k}
such that
\Tors(\Gamma(\pi_{2}(M_{i}))\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}G}\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
. It is open whether this additional invariant is needed in order to determine such manifolds up to homotopy equivalence.

Although
\beta_{Q}(M)
is determined by
Q(M)
, it is not immediately clear how to compute
\beta_{Q}(M)
from
Q(M)
. It is also not clear how to use
Q(M)
directly in order to establish quantitative results such as the ones given in Theorems
C
and
D
(ii). The following is currently open:

Question 1.5
.

Do there exist closed smooth
4
-manifolds
M
,
N
with fundamental group
G
which are stably diffeomorphic but such that
\pi_{2}(M)
and
\pi_{2}(N)
are not
\Aut(G)
-isomorphic?

Similarly it is not known whether such manifolds exist such that
S_{M}
and
S_{N}
are not isometric modulo the action of
\Aut(G)
.
See
Section

2
for the definition of
\Aut(G)
-isomorphic.

Results in higher dimensions

In

, the invariant

is generalised to the doubles of finite

-complexes (see

We define the quadratic bias invariant

We also obtain explicit examples in higher dimensions, complementing the results of Conway-Crowley-Powell-Sixt

.

Theorem 1.6
.

For all

, there exist closed smooth

Remark 1.7
.

This result also addresses two gaps in the paper of Kreck-Schafer
[

]
, where this result was given in the case

. Firstly, the proof that examples exist in the case

is incomplete since it relies on a formula from

Secondly, the examples constructed by

In the case

2.
Preliminaries on CW-complexes

We begin by establishing some conventions and definitions.
Let
G
be a group. A
(G,n)
-complex
is an
n
-dimensional CW-complex
X
such that
\pi_{1}(X)\cong G
and
\widetilde{X}
is
(n-1)
-connected. Equivalently, it is the
n
-skeleton of a
K(G,1)
-space.
Note that a
(G,2)
-complex is equivalently a
2
-complex
X
with
\pi_{1}(X)\cong G
.
We say a group
G
has
type
F_{n}
if there exists a finite
(G,n)
-complex. In particular, a group has type
F_{2}
if and only if it is finitely presented. If
G
is a finite group, then
G
has type
F_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.

For a group
G
, a
G
-polarised space is a pair
(X,\rho_{X})
where
X
is a space and
\rho_{X}\colon\pi_{1}(X)\xrightarrow{\cong}G
is a group isomorphism which we often refer to as a
polarisation
.
If
h\colon X\to Y
is a map, then we can view
\pi_{1}(h)\in\Aut(G)
using the
G
-polarisations
\rho_{X}
and
\rho_{Y}
; formally, we use
\pi_{1}(h)
to denote
\rho_{Y}\circ\pi_{1}(h)\circ\rho_{X}^{-1}
.
Two
G
-polarised spaces
X
and
Y
are said to be
polarised homotopy equivalent
if there exists a homotopy equivalence
h\colon X\to Y
such that
\pi_{1}(h)=\id_{G}\in\Aut(G)
.
We will assume all
(G,n)
-complexes are
G
-polarised.

Let
\HT(G,n)
denote the set of homotopy types of finite
(G,n)
-complexes and let
\PHT(G,n)
the set of polarised homotopy types of finite
(G,n)
-complexes.

There is an action of
\Aut(G)
on
\PHT(G,n)
where
\theta\in\Aut(G)
maps
(X,\rho)\in\PHT(G,n)
to
(X,\theta\circ\rho)
. It follows easily that

\HT(G,n)\cong\PHT(G,n)/\Aut(G).

The following will be our algebraic model for finite
(G,n)
-complexes. We will view
\mathbb{Z}
as a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module with a trivial
G
-action.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
G
be a group. An
algebraic
n
-complex
over
\mathbb{Z}G
is a chain complex
C=(C_{*},\partial_{*})
of (finitely generated) free
\mathbb{Z}G
-modules
C_{*}
equipped with a choice of
\mathbb{Z}G
-module isomorphism
H_{0}(C_{*})\cong\mathbb{Z}
such that

(i)

C_{i}=0
for
i<0
or
i>n
.

(ii)

H_{i}(C_{*})=0
for
0<i<n
.

Let
\Alg(G,n)
denote the set of algebraic
n
-complexes over
\mathbb{Z}G
considered up to the equivalence relation where
C\simeq C^{\prime}
if there exists a chain map
f\colon C\to C^{\prime}
such that
H_{0}(f)=\id_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
H_{n}(f)
is a
\mathbb{Z}G
-isomorphism. We refer to this equivalence relation as
chain homotopy equivalence
.

If
C=(C_{*},\partial_{*})\in\Alg(G,n)
, then define
\chi(C):=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\rank_{\mathbb{Z}G}(C_{i})

where
\rank_{\mathbb{Z}G}(C_{i})
denotes the rank of
C_{i}
as a free
\mathbb{Z}G
-module.
This is a chain homotopy invariant and so does not depend on the choice of representative in
\Alg(G,n)
.

Let
\theta\in\Aut(G)
. If
M
is a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module, let
M_{\theta}
denote the
\mathbb{Z}G
-module with the same underlying abelian group but with
G
-action given by
g\cdot m:=\theta(g)\cdot m
for
g\in G
and
m\in M
.
We say two
\mathbb{Z}G
-modules
M
and
N
are
\Aut(G)
-isomorphic
, written
M\cong_{\Aut(G)}N
, if there is an isomorphism
M\cong N_{\theta}
for some
\theta\in\Aut(G)
.

The class of algebraic
n
-complexes over
\mathbb{Z}G
admit an action by
\Aut(G)
(see
[
53
, Section 6]
). If
C=(C_{*},\partial_{*})\in\Alg(G,n)
, then define

C_{\theta}=\big((C_{n})_{\theta}\xrightarrow{\partial_{n}}(C_{n-1})_{\theta}\xrightarrow{\partial_{n-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{2}}(C_{1})_{\theta}\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}(C_{0})_{\theta}\big).

Since each
C_{i}
is a free
\mathbb{Z}G
-module, we have that
(C_{i})_{\theta}\cong C_{i}
(see
[
53
, Lemma 6.1 (i)]
) and so
C_{\theta}\in\Alg(G,n)
. This action is well-defined on chain homotopy types and so induces an action of
\Aut(G)
on
\Alg(G,n)
(see
[
53
, Section 6]
).

If
X
is a finite CW-complex, then
C_{*}(\widetilde{X})
is a chain complex over
\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{1}(X)]
. Given a
G
-polarisation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(X)\to G
, we can then convert this to a chain complex over
\mathbb{Z}G
. We will denote this by
C_{*}(\widetilde{X},\rho)
when we want to emphasise the choice of polarisation.

The following two propositions are standard and show that, in order to study finite
(G,n)
-complexes up to homotopy equivalence, it suffices to study algebraic
n
-complexes over
\mathbb{Z}G
up to chain homotopy equivalence, and the action of
\Aut(G)
on this class. For a convenient reference, see
[
53
, Proposition 5.1 & Lemma 6.2]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
G
be a group of type
F_{n}
. Then:

(i)

If
X
is a finite
(G,n)
-complex, and
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(X)\to G
is a polarisation, then
C_{*}(\widetilde{X},\rho)
is an algebraic
n
-complex over
\mathbb{Z}G
. Furthermore,
\chi(C_{*}(\widetilde{X},\rho))=\chi(X)
.

(ii)

If
X
,
Y
are finite
(G,n)
-complexes and
\theta\in\Aut(G)
, then there exists a homotopy equivalence
f\colon X\to Y
such that
\pi_{1}(f)=\theta
if and only if there exists a
\mathbb{Z}G

3a
.
The strong minimality hypothesis

Since
G
is finite,
H_{n}(G)
is a finite abelian group and so
d(H_{n}(G))<\infty
. The following is the geometric analogue of the minimality hypothesis
[
57
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
G
be a finite group. We say that the pair
(G,n)
satisfies the
strong minimality hypothesis
if
\chi_{\min}(G,n)=(-1)^{n}+d(H_{n}(G))
.

This can be viewed as a higher dimensional generalisation of efficiency, restricted to the case of finite groups.
Recall that a finitely presented group
G
is
efficient
if

\chi_{\min}(G)=1-r(H_{1}(G))+d(H_{2}(G)),

where
r(A)
denotes the torsion free rank of
A

[
32
, p166]
. If
G
is finite, then
r(H_{1}(G))=0
and so
(G,2)
satisfies the strong minimality hypothesis if and only if
G
is efficient.

Remark 3.2
.

For
n\geq 2
and a finite group
G
, the pair
(G,n)
satisfies the
minimality hypothesis
if
\chi_{\min}^{\alg}(G,n)=(-1)^{n}+d(H_{n}(G))
, where
\chi_{\min}^{\alg}(G,n)=\min\{(-1)^{n}\chi(C):C\in\Alg(G,n)\}
(see
[
57
, Section 3]
). If
n>2
, then Wall
[
65
, Theorem E]
shows that
\chi_{\min}(G,n)=\chi_{\min}^{\alg}(G,n)
and so the minimality hypothesis and strong minimality hypothesis coincide in this case. If
n=2
, then
\chi_{\min}^{\alg}(G,2)\leq\chi_{\min}(G,2)
and equality holds if
G
has the D2 property
[
65
, Section 3]
,
[
22
]
,
[
33
]
.
It is currently not known whether or not
\chi_{\min}^{\alg}(G,2)=\chi_{\min}(G,2)
for all finitely presented groups
G
.

Note that
\chi_{\min}(G,2)\geq\chi_{\min}^{\alg}(G,2)\geq 1+d(H_{2}(G))
. Thus, if
(G,2)
satisfies the strong minimality hypothesis, then
(G,2)
satisfies the minimality hypothesis.

It is known that finite abelian groups and finite
p
-groups satisfy the minimality hypothesis in all dimensions
[
57
, Proposition 5]
, but finite
p
-groups are not known to be efficient in general. Moreover, Swan
[
60
, p196]
constructed a group of the form
G\cong(\mathbb{Z}/{7})^{3}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/{3}
such that
(G,2)
does not satisfy the minimality hypothesis, and so
G
is not efficient.

We will now give an alternate formulation of the strong minimality hypothesis, which will be useful in defining the bias invariant in
Section

3
. Define the
invariant rank
to be
r_{(G,n)}:=\rank_{\mathbb{Z}}(L^{G})
where
L=\pi_{n}(X)
for
X
any minimal
(G,n)
-complex. This does not depend on the choice of
X
.
The following can be proven similarly to
[
57
, Proposition 4]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
G
be a finite group. Then
\chi_{\min}(G,n)=(-1)^{n}+r_{(G,n)}
. In particular,
(G,n)
satisfies the strong minimality hypothesis if and only if
r_{(G,n)}=d(H_{n}(G))
.

If
X
is a minimal
(G,n)
-complex and
L=\pi_{n}(X)
, then it is a consequence of dimension shifting that
H_{n}(G)\cong\widehat{L}
, and so the natural surjection
L^{G}\twoheadrightarrow\widehat{L}
implies that
r_{(G,n)}\geq d(H_{n}(G))
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
, let
G
be a finite group and let
H_{n}(G)\cong\mathbb{Z}/m_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/m_{r}
where
m_{i}\mid m_{i+1}
for all

and

. Such a decomposition exists since

, but we need not have

for all

Define the

to be

By the classification of finite abelian groups,

does not depend on the choice of identification of

. As usual, when

, we will write

and

.

Remark 3.5

It follows by comparing Definitions

and

that

if and only if
(G,n)
satisfies the strong minimality hypothesis.

-complex, then the bias defines a map

Thus,

-complexes up to homotopy equivalence.

It follows from work of Metzler

that, for any finite abelian group

-complexes up to homotopy equivalence (

More details on the computation of the bias invariant, particularly for finite abelian groups, can be found in

Remark 3.19

4.
Preliminaries on hermitian forms

4a
.
Hermitian forms on
R
-modules

A convenient reference is Scharlau
[
56
]
(see also
[
63
, Section 6.2]
).
Let
R
be a ring with involution and let
L
be an
R
-module. We will mostly be concerned with commutative rings
R=\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/{m}
, with trivial involution, or
R=\mathbb{Z}G
, with involution
g\mapsto g^{-1}
, for
g\in G
a finite group.
A
sesquilinear form
on
L
is a bilinear form

h\colon L\times L\to R

such that
h(a\cdot m,b\cdot n)=a\cdot h(m,n)\cdot\overline{b}\in R
for all
a,b\in R
and
m,n\in L
. Define
\Sesq(L)
to be the set of sesquilinear forms on
L
.
This is an abelian group under pointwise addition of functions. The term
quadratic form
and notation
(L,q)
for
q\in\Sesq(L)
is standard in the algebraic theory.

Let
T_{\varepsilon}\colon\Sesq(L)\to\Sesq(L)
, where
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}\subseteq R
, denote the transpose operator, defined by
(Th)(m,n)=\varepsilon\overline{h(n,m)}
, for all
m,n\in L
.
An
\varepsilon
-hermitian form
on
L
is a sesquilinear form
h
such that
T_{\varepsilon}h=h
, or
h(m,n)=\varepsilon\overline{h(n,m)}
for all
m,n\in L
. Define
\Herm_{\varepsilon}(L)=\ker(1-T_{\varepsilon})
to be the set of
\varepsilon
-hermitian forms on
L
, which is a subgroup of
\Sesq(L)
. The standard terminology is
symmetric
for
\varepsilon=+1
and
skew-symmetric
for
\varepsilon=-1
.
When
\varepsilon=+1
, we will just write
\Herm(L)
to simplify the notation.

Let
\ad\colon\Sesq(L)\to\Hom_{R}(L,L^{*})
,
h\mapsto(m\mapsto h(-,m))
,
denote the
adjoint
map, where we consider
L^{*}
as a left
R
-module via the involution on
R
.
We say that an
\varepsilon
-hermitian form
h\in\Herm_{\varepsilon}(L)
is
non-singular
if
\ad(h)
is an
R
-isomorphism.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
L
be an
R
-module and let
h\in\Herm_{\varepsilon}(L)
. Then the associated
metabolic form
is

\Met_{\varepsilon}(L,h)\colon(L^{*}\oplus L)\times(L^{*}\oplus L)\to R,\quad(\varphi_{1},m_{1}),(\varphi_{2},m_{2})\mapsto\varepsilon\overline{\varphi_{1}(m_{2})}+\varphi_{2}(m_{1})+h(m_{1},m_{2}).

We have
\Met_{\varepsilon}(L,h)\in\Herm_{\varepsilon}(L^{*}\oplus L)
.
In matrix notation, we can write this as
\Met_{\varepsilon}(L,h)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
\varepsilon&h\end{smallmatrix}\right)
where the off-diagonal maps are understood to denote the maps induced by evaluation
(\varphi,m)\mapsto\varphi(m)
.
The
hyperbolic form
on
L
is defined as
H_{\varepsilon}(L):=\Met_{\varepsilon}(L,0)
.

A form
h\in\Herm_{\varepsilon}(L)
is called
even
if
h=q+T_{\varepsilon}q\in\IM(1+T_{\varepsilon})
for some
q\in\Sesq(L)
. In this case, we say the
h
admits a quadratic refinement
q
. A form
h\in\Herm_{\varepsilon}(L)
is called
weakly even
if
h(m,m)=a+\varepsilon\bar{a}
, for some
a\in R
and all
m\in L
. These notions are equivalent if
L
is a projective
R
-module, but not in general.

In the other direction, a form
q\in\Sesq(L)
is called a
non-singular
\varepsilon
-quadratic form
if its associated hermitian form

h=q+T_{\varepsilon}q
is non-singular.

4b
.
Hermitian forms on
\mathbb{Z}G
-modules

Let
G
be a finite group and let
L
be a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module.
We will now define an alternative notion of dual module.
For a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module
L
, define
L^{*}:=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(L,\mathbb{Z})
to be the
\mathbb{Z}G
-module where, for
r\in\mathbb{Z}G
and
\varphi\in\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(L,\mathbb{Z})
, we let
(r\cdot\varphi)(m):=\varphi(\overline{r}\cdot m)
for all
m\in L
.
This coincides with the notion of dual module defined previously via the
\mathbb{Z}G
-isomorphism:

\psi\colon\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(L,\mathbb{Z})\to\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}G}(L,\mathbb{Z}G),\quad\varphi\mapsto\widehat{\varphi},\text{\ with \ }\widehat{\varphi}(m)=\sum_{g\in G}\varphi(g^{-1}\cdot m)g)

(see, for example,
[
8
, VI.3.4]
). The inverse is given by
\widehat{\varphi}\mapsto\varepsilon_{1}\circ\widehat{\varphi}
, where for
h\in G
, we let
\varepsilon_{h}\colon\mathbb{Z}G\to\mathbb{Z}
denote the map
\sum_{g\in G}n_{g}g\mapsto n_{h}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
denote the set of homomorphisms

\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)=\{\varphi\in\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}L,\mathbb{Z})^{G}:\varphi(m\otimes n)=\varepsilon\varphi(n\otimes m),\text{\ for all\ }m,n\in L\}

where the action of
G
on
\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}L,\mathbb{Z})
is defined by
(g\cdot\varphi)(m,n)=\varphi(gm,gn)
.

In other words,
\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
is the set of
\varepsilon
-symmetric bilinear forms
h\colon L\times L\to\mathbb{Z}
such that
h(g\cdot m,g\cdot n)=h(m,n)
for all
g\in G
and
m,n\in L
.

There is a canonical isomorphism of abelian groups (with inverse induced by
\psi
):

\Xi\colon\Sesq(L)\xrightarrow{\cong}\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}L,\mathbb{Z})^{G},\quad h\mapsto(m\otimes n\mapsto\varepsilon_{1}(h(m,n))),

given by composing with
\varepsilon_{1}\colon\mathbb{Z}G\to\mathbb{Z}
.
The following is a consequence of the definition of
\Xi
on restriction to
\Herm_{\varepsilon}(L)\subseteq\Sesq(L)
.

Proposition 4.3
.

The map
\Xi
induces a natural correspondence
\Herm_{\varepsilon}(L)\cong\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
.

Convention 4.4
.

From now on, unless stated otherwise, we will use the natural correspondence
\Xi\colon\Herm_{\varepsilon}(L)\cong\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
to work with
\mathbb{Z}
-valued forms rather than
\mathbb{Z}G
-valued forms. In particular,

\Met_{\varepsilon}(L,h)
and
H_{\varepsilon}(L)
will mean the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued equivalent metabolic or hyperbolic forms.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and let
RG
denote the group ring with involution
g\mapsto g^{-1}
. Let
\SymCat_{\varepsilon}(G,R)
denote the category whose objects are forms
h\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
, for some
RG
-module
L
, with morphisms from
(L,h)
to
(L^{\prime},h^{\prime})
given by
RG
-module homomorphisms
f\colon L\to L^{\prime}
such that
h^{\prime}(f(m),f(n))=h(m,n)
, for all
m,n\in L
. If
G=1
is the trivial group, we will write
\SymCat_{\varepsilon}(R):=\SymCat_{\varepsilon}(1,R)
.
We will later be interested in
\SymCat_{\varepsilon}(R)
in the case where
R=\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/m
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}
(see
Definition

4.7
).

We will now restrict to the case where
R=\mathbb{Z}
.
There is an action of
\Aut(G)
on
\SymCat_{\varepsilon}(G,\mathbb{Z})
defined as follows. Let
\theta\in\Aut(G)
and
h\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
. Let
L_{\theta}
be the
\mathbb{Z}G
-module whose underlying abelian group
L
and with
G
-action defined by
g\cdot m:=\theta(g)\cdot m
, for all
g\in G
and
m\in L
. Then
h_{\theta}\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L_{\theta})
is the induced form on
L_{\theta}
.

Example 4.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group. If
M
is a closed oriented
4
-dimensional Poincaré complex with
\pi_{1}(M)\cong G
, then the equivariant intersection form
S_{M}
is a hermitian form on
\pi_{2}(M)
. That is,
S_{M}\in\Herm(\pi_{2}(M))
. Using the convention above, we will view this as a bilinear form

S_{M}\colon\pi_{2}(M)\times\pi_{2}(M)\to\mathbb{Z}

which is
G
-invariant and symmetric, so that
S_{M}\in\Sym(\pi_{2}(M))
.
The hermitian form
S_{M}
satisfies the Bredon condition
[
7
, Theorem 7.4]
that
S_{M}(\tau\cdot m,m)\equiv 0\,\,\text{mod}\,2
for all
m\in\pi_{2}(M)
and
\tau\in G
of order two. This leads to the observation that the
\mathbb{Z}G
-valued hermitian form is weakly even
if the universal covering of
M
is a spin manifold. Note that an even metabolic form is isometric to a hyperbolic form, but this is not true for weakly even metabolic forms.

The following result is well-known. Recall that a
\mathbb{Z}G
-lattice is a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module which is finitely generated and torsion free as an abelian group.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
L
be an
\mathbb{Z}G
-lattice, where
G
is a finite group of odd order. If
(L,h)
is a weakly even form, then
(L,h)
is even and the metabolic form
\Met_{\varepsilon}(L,h)
is hyperbolic.

Proof.

A metabolic form
\Met_{\varepsilon}(L,h)
is weakly even if the form
(L,h)
is a weakly even
\varepsilon
-symmetric form on the
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}G
module
L
, where
L
is a
\mathbb{Z}G
-lattice.
Since
h
is
\varepsilon
-symmetric, it defines a class

[h]\in\widehat{H}^{0}(\mathbb{Z}/{2};\Sesq(L))

which vanishes if and only if
h=q+T_{\varepsilon}q
for some
q\in\Sesq(L)
.
Since the obstruction group is an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
vector space, and the reduction mod 2 map

\widehat{H}^{0}(\mathbb{Z}/{2};\Sesq(L))\to\widehat{H}^{0}(\mathbb{Z}/{2};\Sesq(L/2))

is an injection (since
\Sesq(L)
is torsion free as an abelian group), it follows that
(L,h)
is an even form if and only if its reduction

(L/2,\bar{h})
is an even form with values in
\Lambda/2=\mathbb{F}_{2}[G]
.

If
G
has odd order, the ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}[G]
is semisimple
[
13
, Theorem 3.14]
, so the
\Lambda/2
-module
L/2
is projective. Hence
(L/2,\bar{h})
is an even form by
[
5
, Proposition 3.4]
, and so is
h
.
∎

Definition 4.7
.

The
fixed-point functor

\SymCat_{\varepsilon}(G,\mathbb{Z})\to\SymCat_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{Z})
is defined on objects by
h\mapsto h^{G}
, where
h^{G}\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L^{G})
is the restriction of the form
h
to the fixed set
L^{G}\subset L
. The
Tate functor

\SymCat_{\varepsilon}(G,\mathbb{Z})\to\SymCat_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{Z}/{|G|})
is defined on objects by
h\mapsto\widehat{h}
where

\widehat{h}(\overline{m},\overline{n})=[h^{G}(m,n)]\in\mathbb{Z}/{|G|}
for all
m,n\in L^{G}
, and where
\overline{\cdot}
denotes the quotient map
L^{G}\to L^{G}/(N\cdot L)=\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L)
and
[\,\cdot\,]
denotes the quotient map
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/{|G|}
.
The functors can be defined on the morphisms in the natural way.

Note that
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}=\widehat{H}^{0}(G;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}/|G|
is a ring and, if
L
is a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module, then
\widehat{L}
is a
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
module in a natural way. For example, we can take the action induced by the cup product

\widehat{H}^{0}(G;\mathbb{Z})\times\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L)\xrightarrow{-\cup-}\widehat{H}^{0}(G;\mathbb{Z}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}L)\xrightarrow{\cong}\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L).

The following is
[
8
, Exercise VI.7.3]
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
L
be a
\mathbb{Z}G
-lattice. Then the cup product and the evaluation map
L^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}L\to\mathbb{Z}
,
\varphi\otimes m\mapsto\varphi(m)
induce a non-singular duality pairing

\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L^{*})\times\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L)\xrightarrow{-\cup-}\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}L)\to\widehat{H}^{0}(G;\mathbb{Z}).

In particular, there is an isomorphism of abelian groups
\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L^{*})\cong\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L),\mathbb{Z}/|G|)
.

If
A
is a
\mathbb{Z}/|G|
-module, then we will write
A^{*}:=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(A,\mathbb{Z}/|G|)
. The above shows that, if
L
is a
\mathbb{Z}G
-lattice, then there is a canonical identification

\theta\colon(L^{*})^\cong(\widehat{L})^{*}
. If
f\colon L_{1}\to L_{2}
is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{Z}G
-lattices, then the duality pairing implies that
(f^{*})^{-1}\oplus f\colon L_{1}^{*}\oplus L_{1}\to L_{2}^{*}\oplus L_{2}
induces an isomorphism commuting with the identifications
(L_{i}^{*})^\cong(\widehat{L}_{i})^{*}
, for
i=1,2
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
h\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued hermitian form on a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module
L
. Then the form
\widehat{h}\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L))
defined in
Definition

4.7
coincides with the form induced by the cup product on Tate cohomology:

\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L)\times\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L)\xrightarrow{\ \cup\ }\widehat{H}^{0}(G;L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}L)\xrightarrow{\ h\ }\widehat{H}^{0}(G;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}/{|G|}.

If
L
is a
\mathbb{Z}G
-lattice and
h
is non-singular, then
h^{G}
is non-degenerate and
\widehat{h}
is non-singular.

Proof.

The cup product is induced by the composite

L^{G}\otimes L^{G}\subseteq(L\otimes L)^{G}\xrightarrow{h}\mathbb{Z}
,
and the form
\widehat{h}
is induced by the restriction

h^{G}\colon L^{G}\otimes L^{G}\to\mathbb{Z}
of
h
to the fixed subspaces. Hence the form
\widehat{h}
coincides with the form induced by the cup product.

If
L
is a
\mathbb{Z}G
-lattice and
h
is non-singular, then for any non-zero
x\in L^{G}
, there exists
y\in L
such that
h(x,y)\neq 0
, implying that
h(x,Ny)=h(Nx,y)=|G|\cdot h(x,y)\neq 0
, Hence
h^{G}
is
non-degenerate. To show that
\widehat{h}\colon\widehat{L}\times\widehat{L}\to\mathbb{Z}/{|G|}
is non-singular, we consider its adjoint
\ad\widehat{h}\colon\widehat{L}\to(\widehat{L})^{*}
. By
Proposition

4.8
, there is an isomorphism
\theta\colon(L^{*})^\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }(\widehat{L})^{*}
, such that

\ad\widehat{h}=\theta\circ(\ad h)^
. To check this formula, let
i\colon L^{G}\to L
denote the inclusion, and note that
\theta(\phi)(\bar{y})=\overline{(i^{*}\phi)(y)}\in\mathbb{Z}/{|G|}
, for any
\phi\in(L^{*})^{G}
and
y\in L^{G}
, where
i^{*}\colon(L^{*})^{G}\to(L^{G})^{*}
is the induced restriction map. Then for
x\in L^{G}
, we set
\phi:=\ad h(x)\in(L^{*})^{G}
and see that
(i^{*}\ad h)(x)(y)=h(x,y)
, implying the formula above.

Since
h
is non-singular,
\ad h
is an isomorphism, and hence

(\ad h)^
is also an isomorphism. Therefore
\ad\widehat{h}=\theta\circ(\ad h)^
is an isomorphism and
\widehat{h}
is non-singular.
∎

4c
.
Evaluation forms

Let
G
be a finite group and let
L
be a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module. The
evaluation form
on
L
is the bilinear form

e_{L}\colon(L^{*}\oplus L)\times(L^{*}\oplus L)\to\mathbb{Z},\quad(\varphi_{1},m_{1}),(\varphi_{2},m_{2})\mapsto\varepsilon\varphi_{1}(m_{2})+\varphi_{2}(m_{1}),

where
L^{*}=\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(L,\mathbb{Z})
and
e_{L}\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L^{*}\oplus L)
, for
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
is an
\varepsilon
-symmetric form.
When the module
L
is understood from the context, we will often write
e=e_{L}
. This coincides with the hyperbolic form previously defined, so that
e_{L}
is isometric to
H_{\varepsilon}(L)
and
\Met_{\varepsilon}(L,\phi)\cong\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
\varepsilon&\phi\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
\phi\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
. We will now define two related evaluation forms.

Definition 4.10
.

The restriction of the evaluation form
e_{L}
to the fixed set

(L^{*}\oplus L)^{G}=H^{0}(G;L^{*}\oplus L)
induces a bilinear form:

e_{L}^{G}\colon((L^{*})^{G}\oplus L^{G})\times((L^{*})^{G}\oplus L^{G})\to\mathbb{Z}.

This is an
\varepsilon
-symmetric form over the ring
\mathbb{Z}
with trivial involution, and coincides with
h^{G}
for
h=e_{L}
from
Definition

4.7
. We write
e_{L}^{G}\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}((L^{*})^{G}\oplus L^{G})
.

Definition 4.11
.

By functoriality of
\widehat{H}^{0}(G;-)
, and the isomorphism
(L^{*})^\cong(\widehat{L})^{*}
, there is an induced bilinear form:

\widehat{e}_{L}\colon((L^{*})^\oplus\widehat{L})\times((L^{*})^\oplus\widehat{L})\to\mathbb{Z}/|G|.

This is a non-singular
\varepsilon
-symmetric form of
\mathbb{Z}/|G|
-modules. We write
\widehat{e}_{L}\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{L}^{*}\oplus\widehat{L})
.

The form
\widehat{e}_{L}
in the definition above coincides with
\widehat{h}
for
h=e_{L}
from
Definition

4.7
. This is a consequence of
Proposition

4.9
.

The following will be useful later (see
Definition

4.7
).

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
\phi\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
be an
\varepsilon
-symmetric form such that
\phi^{G}=0
. If
h=\Met_{\varepsilon}(L,\phi)
is the corresponding metabolic form, then
h^{G}\cong e_{L}^{G}
and
\widehat{h}\cong\widehat{e}_{L}
.

Proof.

The first statement is immediate from the assumption that
\phi^{G}=0
. It follows from

Definition

4.7
that
\widehat{h}
is the reduction of
h^{G}
, and so we also have
\widehat{h}\cong\widehat{e}_{L}
.
∎

The following summary is the basis for the explicit models for
e_{L}^{G}
and
\widehat{e}_{L}
we give in
Proposition

4.15
.

Lemma 4.13
.

Under the inclusion map
(L^{*})^{G}\subseteq(L^{G})^{*}
induced by restriction,
e_{L}^{G}
is the restriction of the evaluation form

e_{L^{G}}:((L^{G})^{*}\oplus L^{G})\times((L^{G})^{*}\oplus L^{G})\to\mathbb{Z},\quad(\varphi_{1},m_{1}),(\varphi_{2},m_{2})\mapsto\varphi_{1}(m_{2})+\varphi_{2}(m_{1}).

Under the canonical identification
(L^{*})^\oplus\widehat{L}\cong(\widehat{L})^{*}\oplus\widehat{L}
,
the form
\widehat{e}_{L}
corresponds to the evaluation form

e_{\widehat{L}}\colon((\widehat{L})^{*}\oplus\widehat{L})\times((\widehat{L})^{*}\oplus\widehat{L})\to\mathbb{Z},\quad(\varphi_{1},m_{1}),(\varphi_{2},m_{2})\mapsto\varphi_{1}(m_{2})+\varphi_{2}(m_{1}).

Proof.

This follows directly from the definitions above and
Proposition

4.9
.
∎

4d
.
Matrix representations for the evaluation forms

Let
G
be a finite group, let
L
be a
\mathbb{Z}G
-lattice and let
e=e_{L}
be the non-singular evaluation form.
The following is a consequence of
[
42
, Propositions III.1 & III.2]
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Suppose
L^{G}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for some
d\geq 0
and that
\widehat{L}
has invariant factors
n_{d}\mid\cdots\mid n_{1}
(possibly with some
n_{i}=1
), i.e.
\widehat{L}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}/n_{d}
. Then there is an exact sequence of abelian groups:

0\to(L^{*})^{G}\xrightarrow{\rest}(L^{G})^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}/\beta_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}/\beta_{d}\to 0

where
\rest\colon\varphi\mapsto\varphi\mid_{L^{G}}
is the restriction map and
\beta_{i}\colon=|G|/n_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq d
so that
\beta_{1}\mid\cdots\mid\beta_{d}
.

By combining this with
Lemma

4.13
, we now have the following (which can be found in the discussion on
[
42
, p24]
).

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
n_{1},\cdots,n_{d}
and
\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d}
be as in
Lemma

4.14
. Then, by choosing bases for
(L^{*})^{G}
and
(L^{G})^{*}
such that
(L^{*})^{G}=\beta_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\cdots\beta_{d}\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}=(L^{G})^{*}
, the bilinear form

5.
The doubling construction

We will begin by defining the doubling construction for finite
(G,n)
-complexes. Details can be found in
[
42
, Section II]
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
G
be a group of type
F_{n}
. If
X
is a finite
(G,n)
-complex, then there exists an embedding
i\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. Let
N(X)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
be a smooth regular neighbourhood of this embedding (unique up to concordance, by
[
66
, p76]
) and define
M(X):=\partial N(X)
. This is a closed oriented smooth stably parallelisable
2n
-manifold (see, for example,
[
42
, p15]
).
If
X
is well defined up to homotopy equivalence, then
M(X)
is well-defined up to
h
-cobordism and, in particular, does not depend on the choice of embedding or smooth regular neighbourhood. A polarisation
\pi_{1}(X)\cong G
induces a polarisation
\pi_{1}(M(X))\cong G
and, from now on, we will assume that the manifolds
M(X)
come equipped with a polarisation of this form.

We will refer to
M(X)
as the
double
of
X
and the
doubling construction
as the function

\mathscr{D}\colon\HT(G,n)\to\{\text{closed oriented smooth $2n$-manifolds}\}/\simeq\,\,,\quad X\mapsto M(X).

We refer to the manifolds arising via this construction as
doubled
(G,n)
-complexes
and denote the set of all such manifolds up to homotopy equivalence by

d\text{HT}(G,n):=\IM(\mathscr{D})
.
The doubles of minimal finite
(G,n)
-complexes could then be denoted by
d\text{HT}_{\min}(G,n)
, but instead we will write
\mathscr{M}_{2n}(G):=\mathscr{D}(\HT_{\min}(G,n))
to simplify the notation, as in the Introduction.

This map is referred to as the doubling construction since it has the following equivalent form. By
[
42
, Proposition II.2]
, there exists a
2n
-thickening
L(X)
of
X
(which need not embed in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
) such that

M(X)\cong_{\hCob}\partial(L(X)\times[0,1])\cong L(X)\cup-L(X)

where
\cong_{\hCob}
denotes
h
-cobordism and
L(X)\cup-L(X)
is the double of
L(X)
along its boundary.
Note that more general notions of double exist (see, for example,
[
29
,
49
]
); our notion is sometimes referred to as a trivial double elsewhere in the literature.

The following is
[
42
, Proposition I.1]
. Since it is illuminating, we include a short proof below.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
X
and
Y
are finite
(G,n)
-complexes such that
\chi(X)=\chi(Y)
, then
M(X)
and
M(Y)
are stably diffeomorphic.

Proof.

Since
\chi(X)=\chi(Y)
, there exists
r\geq 0
such that
X^{\prime}=X\vee rS^{n}\simeq Y^{\prime}=Y\vee rS^{n}
are simple homotopy equivalent (see
[
70
]
). Then by
[
66
, Corollary 2.1]
,
N(Y^{\prime})
embeds in
N(X^{\prime})
, and the region
W=N(X^{\prime})-N(Y^{\prime})
is an
s
-cobordism between
M(X^{\prime})
and
M(Y^{\prime})
, which implies that
M(X^{\prime})
and
M(Y^{\prime})
are stably diffeomorphic (or diffeomorphic if
n>2
).
But
M(X^{\prime})\cong M(X)\#r(S^{n}\times S^{n})
and
M(Y^{\prime})\cong M(Y)\#r(S^{n}\times S^{n})
by construction, and so
M(X)
and
M(Y)
are stably diffeomorphic.
∎

We will now restrict to the case where
G
is a finite group.
In this case, we can identify the equivariant intersection form
S_{M(X)}
as follows.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
\varepsilon=(-1)^{n}
. If
X
is a finite
(G,n)
-complex, then

\pi_{n}(M(X))\cong\pi_{n}(X)^{*}\oplus\pi_{n}(X)

and
S_{M(X)}\cong\Met_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{n}(X),\phi)
are isometric for some
\phi\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{n}(X))
with
\phi^{G}=0
.

This is a consequence of
[
42
, Proposition II.2]
. The property that
\phi^{G}=0
is not stated explicitly and so we include further details on this below. Recall that
\Sym_{\varepsilon}(L)
denotes the
\varepsilon
-symmetric forms on a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module
L
, which admit elements
g\in G
as isometries (see
Definition

4.2
).

Proof.

By
[
42
, Proposition II.4]
, the equivariant intersection form

S_{M(X)}\cong\Met(\pi_{n}(X),\phi),

restricted to the summand
0\oplus\pi_{n}(X)
, defines a form
\phi\in\Sym_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{n}(X))
. It remains to show that
\phi^{G}=0
.
The
2n
-thickening
L(X)
can be constructed explicitly as follows (see the proof of
[
42
, Proposition II.2 (i)]
). Let
N(X^{(n-1)})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a
2n
-thickening of the
(n-1)
-skeleton of
X
. For each
n
-cell of
X
, the attaching map
f_{i}\colon S^{n-1}\to X^{(n-1)}
can be shown to induce an embedding
g_{i}\colon S^{n-1}\times D^{n}\hookrightarrow\partial N(X^{(n-1)})
. Then form
L(X)
by attaching
n
-handles to
N(X^{(n-1)})
along the embeddings
g_{i}
for each
n
-cell of
X
. It follows that
N(X)=L(X)\times I
and
M(X)=L(X)\cup-L(X)
(compare
[
29
, Lemma 3.3]
for uniqueness of the thickenings).

By an appropriate choice of these embeddings, one can ensure that the ordinary intersection form

S_{X}\colon H_{n}(L(X);\mathbb{Z})\otimes H_{n}(L(X);\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{Z}

is zero. This part of the proof is carefully explained in

[
42
, Proposition II.4(ii), pp17-18]
. By the transfer map on rational homology, we have

H_{n}(M(X);\mathbb{Z}G)^{G}\otimes\mathbb{Q}=H_{n}(\widetilde{M(X)};\mathbb{Q})^{G}=H_{n}(M(X);\mathbb{Q})=H_{n}(L(X);\mathbb{Q})\oplus H_{n}(-L(X);\mathbb{Q}).

Since the intersection forms respect this splitting, the restriction
\phi
of
S_{M(X)}
has the property that
\phi^{G}\otimes\mathbb{Q}=S_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q}=0
. It follows that
\phi^{G}=0
, as required.
∎

r\geq 1
, let
M_{r}(\Lambda)
denote the ring of
r\times r
matrices over
\Lambda
. If
a\mapsto\overline{a}
denotes the involution on
\Lambda
, then
\alpha^{*}=(\overline{\alpha}_{ji})
denotes the conjugate transpose matrix, for any
\alpha=(\alpha_{ij})\in M_{r}(\Lambda)
. If

\sigma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right)

is a
2r\times 2r
matrix in
GL_{2r}(\Lambda)
expressed in
r\times r
blocks, then

U^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)\subseteq GL_{2r}(\Lambda)
is the subgroup consisting of the matrices
\sigma
with the properties

(i)

\alpha\delta^{*}+(-1)^{n}\beta\gamma^{*}=I

(ii)

\alpha\beta^{*}
and
\gamma\delta^{*}
each have the form

\theta-(-1)^{n}\theta^{*}
for some
\theta\in M_{r}(\Lambda)
.

Remark A1
.

The unitary group
U^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)

is a subgroup of the
hermitian
unitary group

\Isom_{\varepsilon}(H(\Lambda^{r}))
, which consists of the matrices
\sigma\in GL_{2r}(\Lambda)
which preserve the
\varepsilon=(-1)^{n}
-hyperbolic form
H(\Lambda^{r})
but not necessarily the quadratic refinement. In other words, the relation

\sigma\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I\\
\varepsilon I&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\sigma^{*}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I\\
\varepsilon I&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)

holds in
GL_{2r}(\Lambda)
, and we have
U^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)\subseteq\Isom_{\varepsilon}(H(\Lambda^{r}))
. If
\sigma
is given as above, the weaker assumption is equivalent to conditions (i) and

(ii)
′

(\alpha\beta^{*})^{*}+(-1)^{n}\alpha\beta^{*}=0
and
(\gamma\delta^{*})^{*}+(-1)^{n}\gamma\delta^{*}=0
.

Condition (ii)
′
is implied by (ii) but is a strictly weaker condition over an arbitrary ring with involution
\Lambda
.

Lemma A2
.

Let
\Lambda
be a ring with involution , let
\varepsilon=(-1)^{n}
for some
n
and let
r\geq 1
. Then
U^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)=\Isom(H_{\varepsilon}(\Lambda^{r}))
provided
\widehat{H}^{n+1}(\mathbb{Z}/2;(\Lambda,+))=0
, where the additive group
(\Lambda,+)
is viewed as a
\mathbb{Z}/2
-module under the involution.
Furthermore:

(a)

For
n
even, this holds for
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}
or
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}/m
for
m
odd.

(b)

For
n
odd, this holds when
2\in\Lambda^{\times}
, hence for
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}/m
for
m
odd (but not for
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}
).

Proof.

Let
\Lambda
be a ring with involution such that
\widehat{H}^{n+1}(\mathbb{Z}/2;(\Lambda,+))=0
.
We note that
any matrix
X\in M_{r}(\Lambda)
such that
X+\varepsilon X^{*}=0
can be expressed as
X=D+V-\varepsilon V^{*}
, where
D
is diagonal with
D+\varepsilon D^{*}=0
and
V
is strictly upper triangular. The condition

\widehat{H}^{n+1}(\mathbb{Z}/2;(\Lambda,+))=0
applied to each diagonal entry of
D
shows that
D=E-\varepsilon E^{*}
.
It follows that condition (ii)
′
implies condition (ii) for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in M_{r}(\Lambda)
, and hence
U^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)=\Isom(H_{\varepsilon}(\Lambda^{r}))
.
For the examples (a) and (b), we must show that every
x\in\Lambda
such that
x+(-1)^{n}\bar{x}=0
is of the form
x=y-(-1)^{n}\bar{y}
for some
y\in\Lambda
. This is straightforward to check in each case. For example, if
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}
and
n
is even, then the statement is simply that
2x=0
implies
x=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

A(i).
The surgery obstruction groups
L^{h}_{2n+1}(\Lambda)

We will now define the group
L^{h}_{2n+1}(\Lambda)
as the quotient of the stabilised unitary group by a certain subgroup generated by elementary unitary matrices.

Definition A3
.

A
2r\times 2r
matrix in
U^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)
is called
elementary unitary
if it is a product of matrices
of the following forms (see the list in
[
44
, §1]
for the case
n\equiv 1\,\,\text{mod}\,2
):

(i)

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}Q&0\\
0&(Q^{*})^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
where
Q\in GL_{r}(\Lambda)

(ii)

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I&P\\
0&I\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I&0\\
P&I\end{smallmatrix}\right)
where
P=A-(-1)^{n}A^{*}
for some
A\in M_{r}(\Lambda)

(iii)

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)
where

A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&I\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,

B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,

C=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(-1)^{n}&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and

D=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&I\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

The subgroup in
U^{\varepsilon}_{2d}(\Lambda)
of elementary unitary matrices is denoted
EU^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)
.

After orthogonal stabilisation, one defines the inclusions

U^{\varepsilon}_{2}(\Lambda)\subset U^{\varepsilon}_{4}(\Lambda)\subset\dots\subset U^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)\subset U^{\varepsilon}_{2r+2}(\Lambda)\subset\cdots

whose union is the
stable unitary group

U^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)
.
More precisely, the inclusion map is given by

i\colon U_{2r}(\Lambda)\to U_{2r+2}(\Lambda),\quad\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha^{\prime}&\beta^{\prime}\\
\gamma^{\prime}&\delta^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in M_{r}(\Lambda)
, and where
\alpha^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
\beta^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\beta&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
\gamma^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\gamma&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
\delta^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\delta&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in M_{r+1}(\Lambda)
.

The union of the corresponding stabilisations

EU^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)\subset EU^{\varepsilon}_{2r+2}(\Lambda)
is the subgroup
EU^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)\subset U^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)
generated by all elementary unitary matrices. A key result is that
EU^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)
contains the commutator subgroup of
U^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)
(see Wall
[
67
, Chapter 6]
,
[
54
, Theorem 4.2]
). The abelian quotient group

L^{h}_{2n+1}(\Lambda)=U^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)/EU^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)

provides an algebraic description of the (unbased) surgery obstruction group.

A(ii).
The surgery obstruction groups
L^{s}_{2n+1}(\Lambda)

To define the
simple
obstruction groups
L^{s}_{2n+1}(\Lambda)
, one must take Whitehead torsion into account and work with based free modules (see
[
48
]
). For a general ring
\Lambda
with involution,
we have a natural group homomorphism by composition

\tau\colon U^{\varepsilon}_{2d}(\Lambda)\hookrightarrow GL_{2d}(\Lambda)\to K_{1}(\Lambda),

and we define the
special unitary group

SU^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)=\ker\tau
.
This is the group of simple
automorphisms of the
(-1)^{n}
-hyperbolic form

H(\Lambda^{r})=\Bigl(\Lambda^{r}\oplus\Lambda^{r},\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I\\
(-1)^{n}I&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\Bigr)

on a based free module, which preserve its quadratic refinement and the preferred class of bases.

The simple surgery obstruction groups are defined as above by a certain quotient group after stabilisation. In the notation of
Wall
[
67
, Chap. 6]
, the group
SU^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)
is the limit of the
automorphism groups
SU^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)
, where
\varepsilon=(-1)^{n}
,
under the natural inclusions

SU^{\varepsilon}_{2}(\Lambda)\subset SU^{\varepsilon}_{4}(\Lambda)\subset\dots\subset SU^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)\subset SU^{\varepsilon}_{2r+2}(\Lambda)\subset\cdots

Definition A4
.

We say that a
2r\times 2r
matrix in
SU^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)
is an
elementary special unitary
matrix if it is the product of the elementary unitary matrices in
Definition

A3
, provided that the elements of the form

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}Q&0\\
0&(Q^{*})^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\text{\ where $Q\in GL_{r}(\Lambda)$ \ }

satisfy the additional condition
\tau(Q)=0\in K_{1}(\Lambda)
, so that

Q\in SL_{r}(\Lambda)=\ker(GL_{r}(\Lambda)\to K_{1}(\Lambda)).

The subgroup in
SU^{\varepsilon}_{2d}(\Lambda)
of elementary special unitary matrices is denoted
RU^{\varepsilon}_{2r}(\Lambda)
.

The corresponding union
RU^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)\subset SU^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)
again contains the commutator subgroup (see
[
67
, Chapter 6]
,
[
54
, Theorem 4.2]
), and the abelian quotient group

L^{s}_{2n+1}(\Lambda)=SU^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)/RU^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)

provides an algebraic description of the (based) surgery obstruction groups.

There are exact sequences relating based and unbased obstruction groups (usually called the Ranicki-Rothenberg sequences
[
54
, Theorem 5.7]
):

(A.5)

\dots\to\widehat{H}^{0}(\mathbb{Z}/{2};\widetilde{K}_{1}(\Lambda))\xrightarrow{\partial}L^{s}_{2n+1}(\Lambda)\xrightarrow{i_{*}}L^{h}_{2n+1}(\Lambda)\xrightarrow{\tau_{*}}\widehat{H}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/{2};\widetilde{K}_{1}(\Lambda))\to\dots

where the relative terms are given by the Tate cohomology groups:

\widehat{H}^{n}(\mathbb{Z}/{2};\widetilde{K}_{1}(\Lambda))=\frac{\{A\in\widetilde{K}_{1}(\Lambda)\,|\,A^{*}=(-1)^{n}A\}}{\{A+(-1)^{n}A^{*}\,|\,A\in\widetilde{K}_{1}(\Lambda)\}}.

The map
\partial([A])=\left[\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&0\\
0&(A^{*})^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right]
for

is often referred to as the
hyperbolic map
and the map

is induced by the inclusion
i\colon SU^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)\hookrightarrow U^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)
which satisfies

. The map
\tau_{*}\colon L^{h}_{2n+1}(\Lambda)\to\widehat{H}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/{2};\widetilde{K}_{1}(\Lambda))
is induced by
\tau\colon U^{\varepsilon}(\Lambda)\to K_{1}(\Lambda)
.
For more details see Wall
[
67
, Chapter 6]
,
Ranicki
[
54
, Theorem 4.2, Theorem 5.6]
,
Lee
[
44
, §1]
, or Lees
[
45
, §6]
.

Remark A8
.

For integral group rings, the round

Appendix C
The numerical functions
e(d,n)
and
s(d,n)

We first establish some useful facts about binomial coefficients mod
2
.
We will adopt the convention that
\binom{a}{b}=0
if
a<b
and we will use
\equiv
to denote equivalence mod
2
.
The first property is well-known but is recalled here for convenience.

Lemma C1
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then
\binom{2a}{2b+1}\equiv 0

and
\binom{2a}{2b}\equiv\binom{2a+1}{2b}\equiv\binom{2a+1}{2b+1}\equiv\binom{a}{b}
.

Proof.

Firstly,
\binom{2a}{2b+1}=\frac{2a}{2b+1}\binom{2a-1}{2b}
implies that
\binom{2a}{2b+1}\equiv(2b+1)\binom{2a}{2b+1}\equiv(2a)\binom{2a-1}{2b}\equiv 0
.

Next note that

\binom{2a+1}{2b+1}=\frac{2a+1}{2b+1}\binom{2a}{2b}
. Clearing denominators similarly implies that
\binom{2a+1}{2b+1}\equiv\binom{2a}{2b}
. Similarly,
\binom{2a+1}{2b}=\binom{2a+1}{2(a-b)+1}\equiv\binom{2a}{2(a-b)}=\binom{2a}{2b}
by applying the result just proven.

Finally, note that
\binom{2(a+1)}{2(b+1)}=\frac{(2a+2)(2a+1)}{(2b+2)(2b+1)}\binom{2a}{2b}
and so
\binom{2(a+1)}{2(b+1)}\equiv\frac{a+1}{b+1}\binom{2a}{2b}
by clearing denominators. By induction, this implies that
\binom{2a}{2b}\equiv\frac{a(a-1)\cdots(a-b)}{b(b-1)\cdots 1}\binom{2(a-b)}{0}=\binom{a}{b}
.
∎

The following is often known as the hockey-stick identity.

Lemma C2
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then
\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{i+k}{k}=\binom{n+k+1}{k}
.

Proof.

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{i+k}{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left[\binom{i+k+1}{k+1}-\binom{i+k}{k+1}\right]=\binom{n+k+1}{k+1}
.
∎

We will now establish our main results concerning the parity of
e(d,n)
and
s(d,n)
respectively.

Proposition C3
.

Let
d,n\geq 2
. Then:

(i)

If
n=4k
, then

e(d,n)\equiv\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $d\equiv 0,1,3\,\,\text{mod}\,4$}\\
\binom{t+k}{t+1},&\text{if $d=4t+2$.}\end{cases}

(ii)

If
n=4k+1
, then

e(d,n)\equiv\begin{cases}\binom{t+k}{t},&\text{if $d=4t$ or $4t+1$}\\
\binom{t+k+1}{t+1},&\text{if $d=4t+2$ or $4t+3$.}\\
\end{cases}

(iii)

If
n=4k+2
, then

e(d,n)\equiv\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $d\equiv 1,3\,\,\text{mod}\,4$}\\
\binom{t+k}{t},&\text{if $d=4t$}\\
\binom{t+k+1}{t+1},&\text{if $d=4t+2$.}\\
\end{cases}

(iv)

If
n=4k+3
, then

e(d,n)\equiv\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $d=4t$ or $4t+1$}\\
\binom{t+k+1}{t+1},&\text{if $d=4t+2$ or $4t+3$.}\\
\end{cases}

Note that, if
n
is even and
d
is odd, then
e(d,n)
is even.
Throughout the proof, we will make repeated use of
Lemmas

C1
and

.

Proof.

(i)

. If
d
is odd, then
\binom{d+4i+1}{d-2}\equiv 0
for all

C1
and so

. If

. If

, then

by

and

.

(ii)

.

by

C1
and
C2
. If
r=2t+1
, i.e.
d=4t+2
, then
e(d,4k+1)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{2t+2i+1}{2t+1}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{t+i}{t}=\binom{t+k+1}{t+1}
by

C1
and
C2
.
If

, then
e(d,4k+1)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{2r+4i-1}{2r-1}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{r+2i-1}{r-1}
by

.
This coincides with the

(iii)

If
d=4t
, then
e(d,4k+2)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{4t+4i-1}{4t-2}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{t+i-1}{t-1}=\binom{t+k}{t}
by
Lemmas

C1
and
C2
.
If
d=4t+2
, then
e(d,4k+2)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{4t+4i+1}{4t}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{t+i}{t}\equiv\binom{t+k+1}{t+1}
by
Lemmas

C1
and
C2
.

(iv)
e(d,4k+3)=\sum_{i=0}^{2k+1}(2k-i+2)\binom{d+2i-2}{d-2}\equiv\sum_{0\leq i\leq 2k,\,\text{$i$ odd}}\binom{d+2i-2}{d-2}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{d+4i}{d-2}
.
If
d=2r
, then
e(d,4k+3)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{2r+4i}{2r-2}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{r+2i}{r-1}
by
Lemma

C1
. If
r=2t
, i.e.
d=4t
, then
e(d,4k+3)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{2t+2i}{2t-1}\equiv 0
by
Lemma

C1
.
If
r=2t+1
, i.e.
d=4t+2
, then
e(d,4k+3)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{2t+2i+1}{2t}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{t+i}{t}\equiv\binom{t+k+1}{t+1}
by
Lemmas

C1
and
C2
.
If
d=2r+1
, then
e(d,4k+3)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{2r+4i+1}{2r-1}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{r+2i}{r-1}
by
Lemma

C1
.
This coincides with the
d=2r
case, and so
e(d,4k+1)\equiv 0
for
d=4t+1
and
e(d,4k+1)\equiv\binom{t+k+1}{t+1}
for
d=4t+3
.
∎

The parity of
s(d,n)
can be evaluated in terms of a single binomial coefficient without splitting into cases for
n
and
d
mod
4
. However, in order to make the results more easily comparable to Proposition
C3
, we will also state the result in these terms.

Proposition C4
.

Let
d,n\geq 2
. Then
s(d,n)\equiv 1+\binom{d+n}{d}
. In particular, we have that:

(i)

If
n=4k
, then

s(d,n)\equiv\textstyle 1+\binom{t+k}{t},\quad\text{if $d=4t$, $4t+1$, $4t+2$ or $4t+3$.}

(ii)

If
n=4k+1
, then

s(d,n)\equiv\begin{cases}1+\binom{t+k}{t},&\text{if $d=4t$ or $4t+2$}\\
1,&\text{if $d=4t+1$ or $4t+3$.}\\
\end{cases}

(iii)

If
n=4k+2
, then

s(d,n)\equiv\begin{cases}1+\binom{t+k}{t},&\text{if $d=4t$ or $4t+1$}\\
1,&\text{if $d=4t+2$ or $4t+3$.}\\
\end{cases}

(iv)

If
n=4k+3
, then

s(d,n)\equiv\begin{cases}1+\binom{t+k}{t},&\text{if $d=4t$ or $4t+2$}\\
1,&\text{if $d=4t+1$ or $4t+3$.}\\
\end{cases}

It follows from
Lemma

C1
that, if
n
is odd and
d
is odd, then
s(d,n)
is odd.

Proof.

s(d,n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n+i+1}\binom{d+i}{d-1}\equiv 1+\sum_{i=-1}^{n-1}\binom{d+i}{d-1}\equiv 1+\binom{n+d}{d}
, by
Lemma

C2
. To evaluate
s(d,n)
in the various values of
n,d\,\,\text{mod}\,4
, we repeatedly apply
Lemma

C1
.
∎
