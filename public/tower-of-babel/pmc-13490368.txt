Tectonic controls governing reservoir heterogeneity within a stepped carbonate platform of the Sinian Dengying Formation in the Sichuan Basin, China
Authors: Yang Li, Zhaolei Xu, Wei Wang, Xiugen Fu, Xingzhi Wang, Rong Yang, Mingyang Wei, Ming Zhang, Yibei Zhao, Zikun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSinian microbialites represent crucial archives for investigating the co-evolution of life and the environment after the Marinoan glaciation, but their porous facies also form significant hydrocarbon reservoirs worldwide. The giant gas accumulation in the fourth member of the Upper Sinian Dengying Formation (Deng-4 Member, Z2dn4) in the Penglai area, Sichuan Basin, is hosted within such a reservoir. Reservoir development, however, is constrained by a complex interplay of tectono-sedimentary factors, generating notable heterogeneity. To elucidate the depositional model and the corresponding reservoir control mechanisms, this study presents a systematic analysis integrating core descriptions, petrographic thin sections, petrophysical measurements, and geophysical surveys. This investigation establishes a ‘step-like carbonate platform’ depositional model for the Deng-4 Member. The model reveals a multi-level, step-like palaeogeomorphic framework. This framework was shaped by the interplay of near north–south trending relict escarpments formed during earlier tectonic movements, and slope breaks created by near east–west trending, syn-sedimentary strike-slip faults. This complex palaeogeomorphology governed the type, evolution, and spatial arrangement of sedimentary facies, which in turn controlled the reservoir development and heterogeneity. This control operated through three principal mechanisms: Firstly, the sedimentary facies determined the origin and assemblages of pore spaces. Microbial mounds developed framework and vuggy porosity, enhanced by later bedding-parallel and honeycomb-like dissolution vugs. In contrast, grain shoals generated a ‘pin-point’ pore system comprising intergranular and intragranular dissolution pores. The inter-mound sea facies, composed of dense micritic dolomite, lacks effective reservoir space. Secondly, the facies framework directed the spatio-temporal differentiation of reservoir properties. The Deng-4 Member constitutes a single shallowing-upward depositional sequence, transitioning from relatively deeper-water grain shoals in the lower sub-member to widespread shallower-water microbial mounds in the upper sub-member. The vertical facies succession forced the locus of premium reservoir quality to migrate from the lower grain shoals to the upper microbial mounds. Thirdly, the sedimentary facies controlled the macroscopic reservoir distribution. The thickest effective reservoir zones correspond spatially to the dominant facies in each interval, matching the grain shoal distribution in the lower sub-member and the extensive microbial mound belts in the upper sub-member. The proposed depositional model provides a robust geological framework for predicting high-quality sweet spots in complex, deep to ultra-deep carbonate reservoirs.Keywords: Sichuan basin, Sinian, Dengying formation, Depositional model, Microbial dolomite, Reservoir characterizationSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The Sinian (Ediacaran) Period (635–539 Ma) represents a critical transitional stage in Earth’s history, marked by global environmental shifts following the Marinoan glaciation and the biological transition from microbial-dominated ecosystems to early metazoans radiations1–6. The termination of the Marinoan glacial episode triggered notable alterations in global ocean chemistry, resulting in the widespread deposition of thick cap carbonates7–12. This specific environmental backdrop facilitated the proliferation of microbial ecosystems, establishing microbialites as the predominant depositional constructs within shallow marine settings of this period13–15. These microbial buildups constitute essential geological records for deciphering Precambrian life-environment interactions16,17. Furthermore, the complex pore networks generated through subsequent diagenesis render these carbonates significant hydrocarbon reservoirs on a global scale18–27. Recent studies have increasingly emphasized the critical role of sedimentological and petrophysical heterogeneity in governing reservoir quality within shallow marine carbonate systems (e.g.28,29).

The Yangtze Platform in South China serves as a key region for Sinian stratigraphy research. The Upper Sinian Dengying Formation, developed upon this platform, represents a direct product of the major marine transgression subsequent to the Marinoan glaciation, forming a vast microbial carbonate platform system30,31. The fourth member of this formation (hereinafter referred to as Deng-4 Member), located in the central Sichuan Basin, has been confirmed as a crucial exploration target for deep to ultra-deep natural gas in China. Commercial-scale gas production within this member is primarily associated with microbial mound-shoal complex reservoirs32. A complex tectono-sedimentary background jointly controls the formation and distribution of these reservoirs. Regionally, the Nanhua rifting event, initiated by the breakup of the Rodinia supercontinent, established the foundational tectonic framework. A prominent feature of this framework is the near north–south trending Deyang-Anyue Aulacogen. This structural depression separated the platform from the basin, exerting long-term controls on the hydrodynamic conditions and sediment supply at the platform margin33,34. Superimposed upon this framework, the multi-episodic Tongwan tectonic movement during the terminal Sinian further modified the platform topography through differential uplift and erosion, creating a structured palaeogeomorphology35,36.

The multi-phase tectonic activities, combined with high-frequency sea-level fluctuations, induced significant spatio-temporal heterogeneity within the sedimentary facies of the Deng-4 Member. This facies architecture subsequently governed reservoir development and distribution. Although previous studies have documented the petrology, depositional environments, and reservoir characteristics of microbial mounds in the central Sichuan area (e.g.32,37–40), a comprehensive depositional model elucidating how multi-diresctional tectonic elements co-regulated the sedimentary architecture and high-quality reservoir distribution, remains incompletely established. In particular, systematic research is required to address how the intersection of north–south trending relict escarpments and east–west trending syn-sedimentary faults jointly sculpted a ‘step-like’ platform. Furthermore, detailing how this architecture directed the spatio-temporal succession of microbial mounds and grain shoals, alongside the differentiation of their reservoir properties, demands deeper investigation. The linkage between fault systems and associated fracture networks is also essential for deciphering fluid flow pathways in complex carbonate reservoirs41, while detailed facies architecture analysis provides the foundation for reconstructing tectonically mediated depositional models42.

To address these issues, this study investigates the Deng-4 Member in the Penglai area of the central Sichuan Basin. The primary objective is to systematically define the rock types, sedimentary facies, and their spatio-temporal distribution patterns. This is achieved through an integrated approach combining petrological and sedimentological analyses of cored wells with comprehensive interpretation of well logs, core-plug petrophysical tests, and seismic data. Building upon these results, a ‘step-like platform’ depositional model that couples tectonic palaeogeomorphology with sedimentary processes is constructed. This research further elucidates the systematic controlling mechanisms of this depositional framework on reservoir pore types, petrophysical properties, and macroscopic distribution. The findings aim to provide robust geological and theoretical support for the exploration and development of deep to ultra-deep carbonate gas reservoirs in analogous settings.

Geological setting

The Penglai area is located within the central Sichuan Basin, on the northwestern margin of the Yangtze Craton (Fig. 1a). Geologically, the study area constitutes a gentle monocline on the northern flank of the Central Sichuan Palaeo-uplift, immediately adjacent to the eastern platform margin of the Deyang-Anyue Aulacogen. The geographical extent of the area covers approximately 1.5 × 104 km2, stretching from Mianyang in the west to Nanchong in the east (Fig. 1b).

Regional tectonic evolution commenced during the late Neoproterozoic with the breakup of the Rodinia supercontinent33. This rifting event initiated sustained extension across the Yangtze Plate, culminating in the formation of the Nanhua Rift System34. This rift system subsequently dictated the development of the Deyang-Anyue Aulacogen within the Sichuan Basin. Governed by basement faults, the aulacogen exhibits a near north–south trending and a half-graben geometry characterised by a steep margin in the eastern Penglai-Gaoshiti Platform and a gentler one in the western Weiyuan-Ziyang Palaeo-uplift, establishing the basin’s fundamental “two-uplift-one-depression” framework46. The three-episode uplift of the Tongwan Movement further accentuated this configuration at the end of the Sinian Period. Episode I eroded the Deng-2 Member (Z2dn2), Episode II truncated the top of the Deng-4 Member, and Episode III modified the Lower Cambrian stratigraphy. These tectonic events finalised the structural outline of the Central Sichuan Palaeo-uplift47 and enhanced depositional differentiation across the aulacogen margins. Deep-water basinal facies accumulated within the aulacogen, whilst the eastern platform, encompassing the Penglai area, persisted as a high-energy environment. This elevated setting accommodated the deposition of mound-shoal complexes, prominently represented by the Deng-4 Member. The combined influence of differential subsidence and Tongwan erosional episodes significantly enhanced the reservoir quality of these carbonate complexes, establishing them as primary hosts for contemporary natural gas accumulations.

Following the Marinoan glaciation, the Upper Yangtze Platform underwent a major marine transgression. This event resulted in the widespread deposition of the Sinian Doushantuo and Dengying formations30,48,49. The Dengying Formation is divided into four lithostratigraphic members based on variations in microbial abundance and sedimentary structures. The first member (Deng-1, Z2dn1) consists of microbial-poor micritic to very fine-crystalline dolomite. The second member (Deng-2, Z2dn2) is distinguished by thick-bedded, microbial-rich dolomite. The third member (Deng-3, Z2dn3) exhibits argillaceous characteristics, comprising interbedded sandy dolomite and mudstone. The Deng-4 member (Z2dn4), characterized by high microbial abundance, is predominantly composed of microbial dolomite, very fine-crystalline dolomite, micritic dolomite, and siliceous dolomite.

The Deng-4 Member, the focus of this study, ranges in thickness from 100 to 300 m. The lower boundary shows a conformable and continuous contact with the underlying Deng-3 Member. Conversely, the upper boundary is defined by an unconformable contact with the overlying Lower Cambrian Maidiping or Qiongzhusi formations (Fig. 1c;Ref.50). This unconformity resulted from regional uplift and erosion associated with Episode II of the Tongwan Movement at the end of the Sinian Period. Based on lithological assemblages, well-log responses, and depositional cyclicity, the Deng-4 Member is subdivided into two sub-members. The lower Deng-4 sub-member is dominated by granular dolomite and micritic dolomite, whereas the upper Deng-4 sub-member primarily consists of microbial dolomite and chert. Collectively, the stratigraphic record of the Deng-4 Member indicates deposition within a platform environment conducive to microbial proliferation (Refs.51,52; Fig. 1c).

Samples and methods

This investigation was designed to systematically elucidate the depositional model of the Deng-4 Member in the Penglai area and to determine the controls of sedimentation on reservoir development. Depositional characteristics were primarily analyzed utilizing 966 samples obtained from 12 cored wells across the Penglai area. Comprehensive macroscopic descriptions of the cores were conducted to document lithology, bed thickness, sedimentary structures, and observable reservoir spaces. For microscopic fabric identification, 398 petrographic thin sections were prepared and stained with Alizarin Red-S using a 1.5% concentration solution to distinguish dolomite from calcite. The detailed classification of sedimentary facies was achieved through synthesising rock colour, lithology, sedimentary structures, microbial types, and interpretations of log and seismic facies. The classification scheme references established frameworks (e.g.53–55), which subsequently informed the interpretation of the depositional environment and the construction of the depositional model.

The petrophysical properties of the reservoir were quantified by measuring the porosity and permeability of 281 full-diameter core samples (length > 15 cm, diameter ≥ 10 cm) and 450 core plugs (2.5 cm diameter, 3–5 cm length). To ensure representativeness and mitigate the influence of large-scale structural fractures on matrix property evaluation, sampling was visually targeted at intervals with a relatively homogeneous matrix. The porosity measurements were conducted using the Boyle’s Law double-cell method with helium gas, while the permeability measurements were performed using nitrogen gas under a steady confining pressure of 5.0 MPa. All samples were performed at the Research Institute of Natural Gas Technology, PetroChina Southwest Oil and Gas Field Company, following standard protocols (GB/T 29,172–2012; SY/T 6385–2016). For the full-diameter core samples, 220 valid porosity and 97 permeability measurements were obtained, of which 88 were paired data sets. For the core plugs, 440 valid porosity and 57 permeability data points were obtained, all of which were paired sets. Additionally, 85 blue-epoxy-impregnated thin sections were prepared to characterize micro-pore fabrics and reservoir spaces.

Results

Petrology

Integrating previous lithological frameworks (e.g.15,56,57) and applying the classification scheme of Dunham58, three principal rock types have been identified through petrographic analysis: microbial dolomite, granular dolomite, and crystalline dolomite.

Microbial dolomite

The classification of microbial dolomites synthesizes criteria from Riding15, Mei59, and Wen et al.57. Accordingly, three distinct types are recognized based on mesoscopic internal structures: stromatolite dolomite, thrombolite dolomite, and foam dolomite.

Stromatolite dolomite

Stromatolite Dolomite exhibits distinct, sub-parallel laminae macroscopically. Differential weathering and erosion generated elongate pores, oriented parallel to these alternating light and dark laminae (Fig. 2a). The dark laminae, composed primarily of micritic dolomite, demonstrate continuous lateral extension for over a centimeter in length with a vertical thickness ranging from 10 to 300 μm. Bedding-parallel framework dissolution pores frequently excavate the boundaries between these dark laminae (Fig. 2b).

Thrombolite dolomite

Thrombolite dolomite possesses a greyish-white colour and displays irregular morphologies, typically appearing as clotted structures. Dissolution processes generated honeycomb-like vugs within these frameworks; these cavities generally range in size from 0.2 to 2 cm, with some reaching up to 3 cm (Fig. 2c). The thrombolites are composed of dark, organic-rich micritic clots. These clots bind together, forming inter-clot framework porosity that is subsequently occluded by microspar to sparry dolomite cement. The individual micro-clots range in size from 50 to 500 μm (Fig. 2d).

Foam dolomite

The presence of uniformly distributed, ellipsoidal vuggy pores distinguishes foam dolomite at the mesoscopic scale (Fig. 2e). The walls of these cavities are composed of dark, micritic to fine-crystalline dolomite. The interiors of the foam dolomite are either void or occluded by multiple generations of sparry dolomite cements, while the surrounding matrix consists of fine- to medium-crystalline dolomite. These cavities may interconnect to form an irregular, foam-like network or exist as isolated features within the matrix. Individual cavities measure between 50 and 250 μm (Fig. 2f).

Granular dolomite

Granular dolomite in the study area is predominantly represented by dolarenite. Unrecrystallised dolarenite appears grey to greyish-brown, exhibiting a dense fabric accompanied by limited porosity development (Fig. 2g). The grains, which range in diameter from 50 to 300 μm, are composed of micritic dolomite and display sub-rounded to rounded morphologies. The contact between the grains and the surrounding cement is predominantly gradational, although specific grains exhibit clear boundaries (Fig. 2h). In contrast, recrystallised dolarenite develops uniformly distributed ‘pin-point’ porosity (Fig. 2i). The micritic dolomite within the grains has been replaced by larger, subhedral, fine- to medium-crystalline dolomite, yet a relict granular fabric is preserved. The intergranular space is filled with sparry dolomite cement. Subsequent dissolution excavated intergranular and intercrystalline dissolution pores, which are typically less than 1 mm in diameter and frequently filled with solid bitumen (Fig. 2j).

Micritic dolomite

Micritic dolomite across the Penglai area is predominantly greyish-white or dark grey. This lithology typically manifests in thick, massive beds, although horizontal laminations are locally visible. The rock matrix is generally dense, hosting microfractures within specific stratigraphic intervals (Fig. 2k). Dolomite crystals of this rock type are generally smaller than 0.005 mm in diameter (Fig. 2l). Exposure to tectonic and dissolution processes later induces the localized development of a limited amount of dissolution porosities.

Sedimentary facies

During the deposition of the Deng-4 Member, the Penglai area was situated within a carbonate platform-margin setting. Integrating the available data with established facies classification schemes39,57,60–62 (e.g., Wilson, 1970; Lei et al.,2022), this study classifies the carbonate platform succession of the Deng-4 Member into three principal sedimentary facies: microbial mound, grain shoal, and inter-mound sea (Table 1).

Microbial mound

Microbial mounds typically developed upon a substrate of massive micritic dolomite. Thrombolite, stromatolite, and foam dolomites predominantly constitute the main bioconstruction. The vertical succession within the mounds often shows stromatolite dolomite overlying thrombolite dolomite, with the laminae exhibiting a systematic evolution from planar to wavy forms, and stromatolitic structures being common (Fig. 2a). The microbial communities responsible for these bioconstructions, which formed by the binding of carbonate mud and grains, were dominated by the filamentous cyanobacteria Girvanella (Fig. 3a–c) and Epiphyton (Fig. 3d–f), with occasional occurrences of Renalcis (Fig. 3g–i). The dominance of calcified cyanobacteria alongside the development of stromatolitic and thrombolitic structures indicates that these microbial buildups accreted in a shallow, well-illuminated marine environment. The vertical transition from planar to wavy laminae, coupled with the framework porosity, reflects an upward-shallowing sequence accompanied by progressively increasing hydrodynamic energy. This sedimentological assemblage is characteristic of a platform-margin setting located near or above the fair-weather wave base. Well-log response of microbial mound is typically characterised by low gamma-ray values, moderate resistivity, and moderate compensated neutron values (Table 1). On seismic profiles, these mounds manifest as mound-shaped external geometries containing weak internal seismic amplitudes and discontinuous progradational reflectors (Fig. 4).

Grain shoal

Grain shoals are depositional units genetically and spatially associated with the microbial mounds, and typically occur as belts fringing the mounds. Shoals are composed predominantly of well-rounded and sorted intraclasts (Fig. 2h). These grains were sourced from the intense scouring and fragmentation of pre-existing microbial or micritic dolomites under high-energy hydrodynamic conditions. The abundance of well-rounded and well-sorted dolomitic intraclasts reflects a depositional environment characterized by persistent wave agitation and storm-current reworking. This lithological assemblage, combined with the presence of graded bedding, suggests that these shoals formed in high-energy zones near the fair-weather wave base. Grain shoals are indicated by moderate gamma-ray values, low resistivity, and low compensated neutron values (Table 1) on well logs, and are difficult to distinguish from microbial mounds on seismic profiles as they share a similar lenticular geometry with weak, discontinuous, and progradational internal reflectors (Fig. 4).

Inter-mound sea

The topographically depressed regions separating microbial mounds and grain shoals define the inter-mound sea depositional environment. This facies is identified by thick successions of micritic dolomite (often exceeding 5 m) that preserve horizontal lamination and pressure-solution stylolites (Fig. 2l). The deposition of thick, massive micritic dolomite and the general absence of benthic microbial frameworks or coarse-grained sediments indicate a low-energy, subtidal environment. The prevalence of horizontal laminations further suggests that sedimentation occurred primarily through the suspension settling of carbonate mud below the storm wave base. In this deeper-water setting, restricted light penetration and weak water circulation limited microbial proliferation and high-energy hydrodynamic reworking. The corresponding well-log signature includes high gamma-ray, high resistivity, and high neutron values (Table 1). Seismic reflection is distinguished by multiple, parallel, high-amplitude, low-frequency, and continuous features (Fig. 4).

Sedimentary facies distribution

The single-factor analysis and composite mapping method proposed by Feng63 was used to delineate the planar distributions of these sedimentary facies. This approach integrates petrological and stratigraphic thickness data obtained from core analyses and well logs. Contour maps depicting the mound-to-stratum ratio and the shoal-to-stratum ratio were produced for both the upper and lower Deng-4 sub-members. By superimposing these maps, a comprehensive planar facies distribution was achieved (Fig. 5).

During the deposition of the lower Deng-4 sub-member, grain shoals developed extensively across the platform margin, concentrating in a belt-like trend along the stratigraphic pinch-out line, especially over Boreholes PS6 and PS15 and across the PS7-PS9 block, whereas microbial mounds remained localised. Shoal-to-stratum ratios ranged from 0.1 to 0.5 along this trend, peaking at 0.2–0.5 in the PS7-PS9 area, underscoring the dominance of this facies (Fig. 5b). In contrast, microbial mounds were confined to specific sites such as Boreholes JT1 and DB1. Their mound-to-stratum ratios generally fell between 0.1 and 0.3, though an anomalous 0.7 value indicates a localised buildup between JT1 and PS10 (Fig. 5a). The resulting facies distribution (Fig. 5c) delineates a platform margin bounded by an erosional zone to the west and a zone of stratigraphic thinning to the east.

During the upper Deng‑4 sub‑member deposition, the depositional system underwent a pronounced change: microbial mounds became the prevailing facies along the platform margin, while the formation of grain shoals declined markedly. These mounds expanded widely, creating a broad zone adjacent to the erosional limit, with the most extensive growth observed near Boreholes PS6, PS1, DB1, and across the JT1-PS10 block. Quantitatively, the dominance of the mounds is evident from the elevated mound‑to‑stratum ratio, which typically ranges between 0.5 and 0.9 along the erosional front (Fig. 5d). At the same time, grain shoals were confined to small, isolated patches, mainly around Boreholes PS6, PS11, and PS10, as reflected in the low shoal‑to‑stratum ratios of only 0.1 to 0.2 in those areas (Fig. 5e). Consequently, the facies distribution for this interval (Fig. 5f) confirms the continued presence of a platform‑margin environment, yet clearly indicates a shift toward a mound‑dominated system.

In general, the depositional architecture of the Deng‑4 Member mound‑shoal complex shows a distribution pattern that is clearly linked to the orientation and proximity of the Deyang‑Anyue Aulacogen.

Discussion

Depositional architectural evolution and model

Through a systematic examination of vertical facies sequences, planar facies distributions, and the regional tectonic framework of the Deng-4 Member, this study proposes an integrative depositional model for the Penglai area. Central to this model is the concept that the depositional environment and facies architecture were jointly influenced by the inherited palaeotopography resulting from the erosional surface of the post-Deng-2 Member, syn-sedimentary fault dynamics, and episodic tectonic uplifts associated with the Tongwan Movement. Specifically, the erosion of the Deng-2 Member established a foundational template characterized by multi-tiered, north–south trending escarpments. Simultaneously, the activity of pre-existing and contemporaneous strike-slip faults produced a series of east–west trending slope breaks. The combined impact of differential uplift during the first and second episodes of the Tongwan Movement acted upon these two orthogonal geomorphic features, collectively shaping a complex, multi-level, stepwise structural framework. Within this framework, each step-like structural block developed a relatively autonomous platform margin, which served as a preferential locus for the formation of characteristic depositional assemblages, including microbial mounds and grain shoals.

Controlling factors of the step-like platform margin

The primary controlling factor in this model is the inherited constraint imposed by the post-Deng-2 relict escarpments on the north–south trending of subsequent platform-margin mounds and shoals. These escarpments, characterized by a step-like morphology aligned north–south, represent the outcome of differential tectonic uplift followed by erosion during Episode I of the Tongwan Movement. Pronounced residual thickness variations within the Deng-1 and Deng-2 stratigraphic members delineate a series of north–south trending escarpments, which spatially correspond with the pinch-out boundary of the Deng-4 Member55 (Fig. 6a). The antecedent nature of this topographic relief is substantiated by the onlapping relationship of the overlying Deng-3 Member strata against the escarpment slopes (Fig. 7a). During the deposition of the Deng-4 Member, these inherited topographic highs functioned as shallow-water, high-energy substrates, enhancing local hydrodynamic conditions and photic zone availability, thereby facilitating benthic microbial proliferation. As a result, microbial mounds and grain shoals display a pronounced north–south alignment along the eastern margin of the Deyang-Anyue Aulacogen64. This spatial association is further supported by elevated mound-to-stratum ratios (0.7–0.9) concentrated along the escarpment margins (Figs. 5a, d and 8).

The second significant controlling factor pertains to the impact of syn-sedimentary strike-slip faulting on the east–west distribution of platform-margin facies. Regional en-echelon strike-slip fault systems, initiated during the Nanhua Period and reactivated throughout the Dengying depositional interval, produced an orthogonal set of near east–west trending slope breaks54,65,66 (Figs. 6c and 7b). Structural lineaments, including the Penglai and Penglai North low-amplitude slope breaks, truncate the pre-existing north–south escarpments55 (Fig. 6a). The syn-sedimentary character of these fault-controlled slope breaks is evidenced by localized progradational reflection patterns adjacent to fault zones46,67. The superimposition of these active east–west fault blocks intensified local vertical relief, generating discrete structural highs that governed the east–west zonation of platform-margin facies. Boundaries of the east–west trending mound-shoal belts, marked by elevated facies ratios, coincide with underlying strike-slip fault zones (Figs. 6b, c and 7c–e), indicating that syn-sedimentary tectonic compartmentalization directly influenced the step-like geomorphology and consequent facies distribution.

In conclusion, the near north–south trending escarpments, derived from the relict topography of the Deng-2 Member, combined with the near east–west trending slope breaks formed by syn-sedimentary strike-slip faulting, collectively established a complex, step-like palaeogeomorphic framework preceding the deposition of the Deng-4 Member. This framework controlled the positioning of the platform margin and the spatial distribution of associated microbial mounds and grain shoals (Fig. 8).

Sedimentary evolution model

Building on the established understanding of the controlling framework, the depositional evolution of the Deng-4 Member is elucidated through two representative cross-sections, which demonstrate the development of the step-like carbonate platform along both east–west (Fig. 9) and north–south (Fig. 10) transects.

(1) East–West Evolution of the Step-Like Carbonate Platform (Section A-A’)

The deposition of the Dengying Formation initiated upon a relatively subdued palaeotopography inherited from the underlying Doushantuo Formation, which had previously infilled pre-existing rift basins. A significant regression triggered the onset of carbonate sedimentation, with the Deng-1 and Deng-2 members accumulating within a broad, gently sloping carbonate ramp environment. Extensive, belt-like microbial mounds, potentially extending over hundreds of kilometers, thrived in the mid-ramp zone situated between the fair-weather and storm wave bases68 (Fig. 9a).

This ramp system underwent substantial modification during Episode I of the Tongwan Movement, which induced differential uplift and erosion of the Deng-2 Member. This tectonic event sculpted the landscape, resulting in the formation of Escarpment I and Escarpment II, thereby subdividing the strata into three distinct terraces (Fig. 9b). This transformation signified a fundamental shift from a ramp to a step-like platform depositional system. Subsequently, a major transgression ensued. The tectonic uplift of the Hannan Palaeoland to the north supplied a considerable influx of clastic material, which inhibited carbonate production and facilitated the deposition of the mixed siliciclastic-carbonate Deng-3 Member (Fig. 9c).

During the accumulation of the lower Deng-4 sub-member, a gradual regression commenced. Elevated relative sea levels and localised high-energy conditions along the seaward margins of the second and third terraces favored the development of grain shoals, accompanied by subordinate microbial mounds (Fig. 9d). As relative sea level continued to decline during the upper Deng-4 sub-member stage, shallower water depths became conducive to the widespread proliferation of microbial mounds, which also colonized the seaward edges of the terraces (Fig. 9e). The subsequent Episode II of the Tongwan Movement induced renewed regional uplift and erosion, variably truncating the Deng-4 Member across all three terraces. Preservation was most complete in the most distal settings, which subsequently formed a fourth terrace. This final tectonic phase ultimately shaped the present-day tectono-stratigraphic architecture (Fig. 9f).

(2) North–South Evolution of the Step-Like Carbonate Platform (Section B-B’)

In the north–south trending, the nascent form of the step-like platform predated the deposition of the Dengying Formation, influenced by pre-existing east–west trending strike-slip faults. These faults had already segmented the basement into multiple terraces (Fig. 10a). Within the Penglai area, the Penglai North Low-Amplitude Slope Break demarcated the boundary between the second and third terraces, with extensive, contiguous microbial mounds developing along the seaward margins of each terrace.

Following the deposition of the Deng-2 Member, the Tongwan I Movement induced regional uplift and erosion, predominantly affecting the third terrace. This tectonic event reactivated and intensified the strike-slip fault system, thereby increasing the vertical relief between terraces (Fig. 10b). The subsequent transgression resulted in the deposition of the mixed-lithology Deng-3 Member across this pre-existing topography (Fig. 10c).

During the lower Deng-4 sub-member phase, a gradual regression facilitated the formation of grain shoals and minor microbial mounds along the seaward edges of the second and third terraces (see Fig. 10d). As regression progressed into the upper Deng-4 sub-member, the increasingly shallow marine conditions favored the widespread development of microbial mounds along the margins of each terrace (Fig. 10e). Subsequently, Episode II of the Tongwan Movement induced substantial tectonic uplift, resulting in the complete erosion of the Deng-4 Member north of the PS6 area and minor truncation to the south, thereby generating a pronounced weathering unconformity. This tectonic event essentially established the depositional and structural framework observed in the present day (Fig. 10f).

Controls of sedimentary facies on reservoir development

The formation and spatial variability of the Deng-4 Member reservoirs within the Penglai area are primarily controlled by the distribution of the original sedimentary facies. These depositional facies not only determined the initial petrological fabric and physical characteristics of the reservoir rocks but also influenced the nature and extent of subsequent diagenetic processes. This dual influence ultimately governed both the macroscopic distribution and the microscopic pore architecture of the resulting reservoirs.

Control of sedimentary facies on reservoir pore system

Effective reservoir rocks in the study area are predominantly microbial and granular dolomites, with crystalline dolomites contributing only sporadically. The reservoir pore system can be categorized into three types based on pore size and genesis. The first category includes pores smaller than 2 mm in diameter, comprising primary intergranular, framework, and vuggy pores, as well as secondary intercrystalline and intragranular dissolution pores. The second category consists of secondary dissolution vugs exceeding 2 mm in diameter, which are morphologically classified as honeycomb, bedding-parallel, or isolated vugs. The third category encompasses a fracture network composed of tectonic fractures, dissolution-enlarged fractures, and stylolites. Each sedimentary facies, through its distinctive combination of lithologies and diagenetic history, developed a characteristic assemblage of these pore types (Fig. 11).

Principal rock types and associated reservoir pore systems for each sedimentary facies in the Penglai area.

Microbial mound

Within microbial mounds, reservoir properties display a pronounced vertical zonation. The upper sections of the mounds, composed of stromatolite dolomite, are characterized by bedding-parallel dissolution pores and vugs aligned along laminae. These pores are elliptical to elongate, measuring macroscopically between 2 and 5 cm in length and microscopically ranging from 0.5 to 2 mm, preferentially developing within the lighter-colored laminae (Fig. 11a). Foam dolomite contributes additional, albeit minor, reservoir capacity through pin-point vuggy porosity formed by the dissolution of Renalcis chambers; these vugs range from 0.1 to 0.5 cm in diameter and are evenly distributed (Fig. 11b). Thrombolite dolomite constitutes the core reservoir unit of the mounds. Its primary biogenic framework has been extensively modified and enlarged by subsequent dissolution, producing vugs ranging from 0.1 to 2 cm in diameter. Depending on the degree of dissolution, these vugs occur either as isolated features or as interconnected, honeycomb-like networks (Fig. 11c). In contrast, the micritic dolomite forming the mound substrate exhibits limited reservoir potential, developing only minor intercrystalline or intracrystalline dissolution porosity and fractures when subjected to later structural or diagenetic modification.

Inter-mound sea

The inter-mound sea facies exhibits the lowest reservoir potential among the studied facies. It predominantly comprises micritic and fine-crystalline dolomite, which only locally develops effective reservoir space following partial recrystallization. The porosity within this facies is primarily characterized by microscopic, often isolated and discontinuous intercrystalline pores or dissolution pores measuring between 0.1 and 1 mm in diameter with embayed margins, as well as fractures (see Fig. 11d).

Grain shoal

The grain shoal facies constitutes another significant reservoir type. These shoals are composed of dolarenites with thicknesses ranging from 2 to 15 m and are distinguished by a characteristic ‘pin-point’ pore system. This porosity comprises two principal types: irregular intragranular dissolution pores (0.1–0.5 mm) developed within the grains, and intergranular dissolution pores (0.2–0.8 mm) with embayed margins formed within the interparticle cement (refer to Fig. 11e). Although minor intraclastic components may be present within the mound facies, the extensive accumulation of grains within the shoals results in a substantially higher density, scale, and connectivity of this ‘pin-point’ porosity, thereby establishing a high-quality reservoir.

The petrophysical characteristics and spatiotemporal distribution of reservoirs were strongly governed by sedimentary facies, a relationship elucidated through integrated analyses of well-log data and core-plug measurements (Table 2).

In the lower Deng-4 sub-member, reservoir development was predominantly controlled by the grain shoal facies. Dolarenites within these shoals exhibited the highest average porosity (3.29%) and permeability (0.28 mD) among all facies in this interval. The microbial mound facies ranked second in reservoir quality, albeit with notable internal heterogeneity; thrombolite dolomites (porosity 3.10%, permeability 0.18 mD) demonstrated superior properties compared to the stromatolite dolomites (porosity 2.61%, permeability 0.05 mD) that typically overlie them. The inter-mound sea facies, composed mainly of micritic dolomite, displayed the poorest reservoir quality, with an average porosity of 2.93% and permeability of 0.07 mD.

In the upper Deng-4 sub-member, the locus of optimal reservoir development shifted to the microbial mound facies. Thrombolite dolomite emerged as the principal reservoir unit, with full-diameter core samples indicating an average porosity of 3.35% and a notably high average permeability of 0.96 mD. Stromatolite dolomites (porosity 2.84%, permeability 0.16 mD) were of secondary reservoir quality. In contrast, the previously dominant dolarenites exhibited comparatively lower petrophysical properties in this interval, with average core-plug porosity and permeability values of 2.57% and 0.80 mD, respectively.

Throughout the depositional evolution of the Deng-4 Member, a distinct migration of the reservoir sweet spot is evident, transitioning from the grain shoals in the lower sub-member to the microbial mounds in the upper sub-member. Within the mounds, thrombolite dolomite consistently represents the facies with the most favorable reservoir properties. This facies-dependent variation in petrophysical quality is fundamentally linked to systematic differences in pore structure. In the lower sub-member, the well-connected intergranular dissolution pore network within the grain shoals conferred superior permeability. Conversely, in the upper sub-member, thrombolites attained a marked permeability advantage due to the preferential development of extensive dissolution vug systems in the upper stratigraphic levels during palaeokarstic events.

Control of sedimentary facies on reservoir distribution

Sedimentary facies exerted a controlling influence not only on micro-scale pore systems and macro-scale physical properties but also on the overall spatial distribution patterns of reservoirs. In this study, effective reservoirs are defined, in accordance with exploration practices in the Sichuan Basin, as those exhibiting a log-derived porosity of at least 2% and permeability equal to or exceeding 0.01 mD. Within this classification, premium reservoirs (Class I) are characterized by a porosity of 6% or greater and permeability of 10 mD or higher, whereas reservoirs of moderate to poor quality fall into Class II and Class III categories, respectively.

Vertically, the distribution of reservoirs within the lower Deng-4 sub-member was predominantly controlled by grain shoals. This facies contributes 5.38 m of premium reservoir thickness, accounting for 78.18% of the total premium reservoir thickness in this sub-member. Although grain shoals contain the highest quality rock, the majority of the reservoir volume consists of moderate-to-poor quality rock, with a cumulative thickness of 278.5 m. Within this volume, grain shoals (99.25 m) and inter-mound sea facies (88.5 m) are the principal contributors (see Table 2). In contrast, the upper Deng-4 sub-member is primarily characterized by microbial mounds as the main hosts of premium reservoirs. The thrombolite dolomite facies in this interval contains 13.88 m of premium reservoir thickness, representing 73.01% of the total. Similarly, moderate-to-poor quality reservoirs dominate the overall volume, with a cumulative thickness of 810.8 m. The microbial mound facies (comprising both micritic and thrombolite dolomites) contribute 72.68% of this thickness, highlighting their critical role in the total reservoir volume (refer to Table 2).

From a planar perspective, the total effective reservoir thickness in the lower Deng-4 sub-member varies between 0 and 65 m (Fig. 12a). The thickest accumulations, exceeding 45 m, are concentrated in the PS8 (up to 65 m), DB1 (up to 59 m), and PS6 (up to 48 m) areas. These zones of maximum thickness exhibit a strong spatial correlation with the distribution of principal grain shoals (cf. Figs. 5c and 12a), confirming that grain shoals were the dominant control on reservoir distribution during this interval. This direct relationship between facies distribution and reservoir thickness patterns underscores that the depositional microfacies architecture governs the spatial heterogeneity of reservoir properties (e.g.69). In the upper Deng-4 sub-member, effective reservoir thickness increases markedly, ranging from 30 to 130 m (Fig. 12b). The thickest accumulations occur in the DB1 (up to 119 m), JT1 (up to 129 m), and PS8 (up to 74 m) areas. These thickness trends closely correspond with regions of extensive microbial mound development (cf. Figs. 5f and 12b), indicating that microbial mounds became the principal control on the macroscopic reservoir architecture in the upper sub-member.

The marked variations in reservoir quality among different facies fundamentally arise from a dual influence exerted by the depositional environment: a primary control on the initial rock fabric and a secondary control on the subsequent diagenetic evolution. Initially, the depositional facies directly dictated the primary lithology and fabric, thereby establishing the original pore framework. Microbial mounds, composed of stromatolites and thrombolites, inherently possessed a high potential for developing substantial primary porosity, a feature corroborated by modern analogues such as Shark Bay and the Bahamas70,71. This porosity originated through multiple mechanisms, including the formation of fenestral pores resulting from organic matter decay, framework porosity within the microbial buildups, and voids generated by metabolic gas bubbles72. Early rapid cementation likely contributed to the preservation of this primary porosity, which can reach values as high as 23.6–29.7%70, thereby establishing a superior precursor fabric and facilitating selective pathways for subsequent diagenetic fluids. In a similar manner, grain shoals formed in high-energy settings were well-sorted and exhibited significant primary interparticle porosity. Conversely, the low-energy micritic dolomites of the inter-mound sea were characterized by very fine pores and pore throats and were highly susceptible to compaction, resulting in considerable porosity reduction during early burial.

Secondly, the palaeogeomorphic positioning of each facies governed the diagenetic pathways, which in turn influenced the ultimate reservoir quality (see Table 2). The Deng-4 Member was deposited on an extensive, shallow-water platform where topographic highs—namely microbial mounds and grain shoals—were frequently exposed subaerially due to high-frequency sea-level fluctuations.

The initial diagenetic phase in these elevated facies involved early marine cementation. The precipitation of early cements within the primary framework pores of microbial mounds and the intergranular spaces of shoals imparted early mechanical rigidity, thereby mitigating the porosity-reducing effects of subsequent mechanical compaction during burial. Subsequently, subaerial exposure subjected these facies to meteoric freshwater dissolution. The undersaturated meteoric waters selectively dissolved chemically unstable carbonate components along pre-existing pore networks. In microbial mounds, this process generated bedding-parallel vugs observed in stromatolite dolomites (Fig. 2a, b) and interconnected, honeycomb-like vugs in thrombolite dolomites (Figs. 2c and 11c). This diagenetic pathway accounts for the elevated petrophysical properties measured in thrombolite dolomites. Similarly, in grain shoals, meteoric dissolution produced characteristic ‘pin-point’ intergranular and intragranular pore systems (Figs. 2i and 11e), resulting in high average porosity and permeability values (Table 2).

In contrast, the inter-mound sea facies occupied topographic depressions and largely avoided eogenetic meteoric dissolution. These sediments, predominantly composed of fine-grained carbonate mud, lacked an early rigid framework. As a result, they experienced substantial mechanical compaction during progressive burial, as indicated by the dense rock fabric and the widespread occurrence of pressure-solution stylolites (Fig. 2g, l). The destruction of primary porosity through early compaction impeded the infiltration of later diagenetic fluids, thereby limiting the development of secondary dissolution porosity within the facies. This is reflected in their restricted reservoir quality, with micritic dolomites exhibiting low average core-plug porosity and permeability values (Table 2). Consequently, the observed variability in reservoir quality across the step-like platform fundamentally arises from the primary depositional architecture, which governs exposure to porosity-preserving early cementation and porosity-enhancing meteoric dissolution.

Overall, sedimentary facies establish the foundational heterogeneity of reservoirs by determining the primary rock fabric and initial porosity, while the depositional setting influences exposure to critical diagenetic processes, particularly meteoric dissolution. Microbial mounds and grain shoals, which possess both favorable primary fabrics and have undergone the effective diagenetic enhancement, represent the most promising targets for hydrocarbon exploration in this region.

Accordingly, the intersection zones between the north–south trending relict escarpments and the east–west syn-sedimentary strike-slip faults constitute the most prospective exploration fairways. These structural intersections not only host the thickest microbial mounds and grain shoals but also exhibit the most intense fault-related fracturing and meteoric dissolution. Future exploration efforts should prioritize these structurally superimposed highs along the platform margin. Employing high-resolution three-dimensional seismic attribute analysis to identify mound-shoal complexes stacked against these slope breaks will be critical for delineating high-quality, deep-tier reservoirs (e.g.73).

Conclusion

A comprehensive study of the Deng-4 Member within the Penglai area has delineated three primary lithologies: microbial dolomite (encompassing stromatolite, thrombolite, and foam variants), granular dolomite, and crystalline dolomite. These rock types are systematically arranged into three discrete sedimentary facies—namely microbial mound, grain shoal, and inter-mound sea—which demonstrate a distinct vertical succession. Specifically, the lower Deng-4 sub-member is predominantly composed of grain shoals, whereas the upper sub-member is characterized by the widespread development of microbial mounds.

This research has proposed a ‘step-like carbonate platform’ depositional model that effectively accounts for the architectural evolution of the Deng-4 Member. The formation of this model was influenced by the interaction of two principal tectonic controls. The first control comprises a series of near north–south trending relict escarpments, inherited from differential erosion of the Deng-2 Member during Episode I of the Tongwan Movement. The second involves near east–west oriented, syn-sedimentary slope breaks, which are governed by underlying basement faults. The intersection of these two palaeogeomorphic elements generated a complex, multi-tiered submarine topography that critically influenced the lateral distribution of the microbial mound, grain shoal, and inter-mound sea facies.

The nature, temporal evolution, and spatial configuration of these sedimentary facies exerted a fundamental influence on the development and heterogeneity of the Deng-4 Member reservoirs, observable at three key scales. First, the depositional facies controlled the reservoir pore architecture: microbial mounds exhibit a complex pore network comprising framework, vuggy, and large-scale dissolution pores; grain shoals possess a distinctive ‘pin-point’ porosity system characterized by inter- and intragranular dissolution pores; and the dense fabric of the inter-mound sea facies significantly restricted reservoir quality. Second, the facies framework dictated the spatiotemporal variation of reservoir properties, resulting in a vertical shift of the reservoir sweet spot from the grain shoals in the lower sub-member to the microbial mounds (notably thrombolites) in the upper sub-member. Third, the sedimentary facies controlled the macroscopic distribution of reservoirs, with zones of maximum reservoir thickness closely corresponding to the dominant reservoir facies within each stratigraphic interval: grain shoals in the lower sub-member and microbial mounds in the upper sub-member.
