The friendship paradox for trees
Rajat Subhra Hazra

Abstract
We analyse the friendship paradox on finite and infinite trees. In particular, we monitor the vertices for which the friendship-bias is positive, neutral and negative, respectively. For an arbitrary finite tree, we show that the number of positive vertices is at least as large as the number of negative vertices, a property we refer to as significance, and derive a lower bound in terms of the branching points in the tree. For an infinite Galton-Watson tree, we compute the densities of the positive and the negative vertices and show that either may dominate the other, depending on the offspring distribution. We also compute the densities of the edges having two given types of vertices at their ends, and give conditions in terms of the offspring distribution under which these types are positively or negatively correlated. 1.1
Background

The friendship paradox says that for any finite graph the
average friendship-bias
is non-negative
[
7
,
8
]
. More precisely, let
G_{n}
be a
finite undirected graph
with
n
vertices, labelled by
u\in[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Define the friendship-bias of vertex
u
as

\Delta_{u,n}=\left(\frac{1}{d_{u}}\sum_{v\in[n]}A_{uv}d_{v}-d_{u}\right)\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{u}\neq 0\}},

where
A=(A_{uv})_{u,v\in[n]}
is the adjacency matrix of
G_{n}
, i.e.,
A_{uv}
is the number of edges between
u\neq v
while
A_{uu}
is twice the number of self-loops at
u
, and
d_{u}=\sum_{v\in[n]}A_{uv}
is the degree of
u
. Then the friendship paradox says that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{u\in[n]}\Delta_{u,n}\geq 0,

with equality if and only if all connected components of
G_{n}
are regular. Typically, there are
u
for which
\Delta_{u,n}
is
>0
,
=0
,
<0
. Even though the average is non-negative, there are graphs for which the number of vertices
u
with
\Delta_{u,n}<0
is larger than the number of vertices
u
with
\Delta_{u,n}>0
. One of the goals of the present paper is to analyse the number and the arrangement of such vertices in
finite trees
and
infinite trees
.

The friendship paradox was introduced in 1991 by the American sociologist Scott Feld
[
7
]
. Since then, numerous studies have explored and applied this paradox across various domains, including social sciences, information theory and epidemiology. For instance, researchers have used the friendship paradox to design efficient sampling methods for early detection of contagious outbreaks
[
4
]
, and to improve strategies for delivering information and monitoring its propagation
[
15
]
, as well as for reducing the spread of contagion
[
18
]
. The friendship paradox has applications in exploring the network, for example, quickly finding vertices of high degrees
[
1
]
and developing immunisation strategies
[
5
,
2
]
.

Apart from being interesting in itself, the friendship paradox has useful generalisations and implications. For instance, the friendship-bias can be viewed as a centrality measure, akin to PageRank centrality
[
17
]
and degree centrality. Co-authorship networks of Physical Review journals and Google Scholar profiles reveal that on average the co-authors of a person have more collaborations, publications and citations than that person has themselves
[
6
]
. On Twitter, most users, on average, follow others who are more popular and more active, i.e., tend to have more followers and share more viral content than they do themselves
[
10
]
. The friendship paradox has implications for individual biases in perception and thought contagion as well, because our social norms are influenced by our perceptions of others, and are strongly shaped by the people around us
[
13
,
16
]
.

Despite many practical examples,
mathematically
the friendship paradox has received only modest attention, and a proper
quantification
of friendship-biases in large-size networks is still lacking. In
[
3
]
, it is shown that a randomly chosen friend of a randomly chosen individual has stochastically more friends than that individual. In
[
8
]
, a quantification of the friendship paradox for sparse locally tree-like random graphs is carried out, and the notion of
significance
of the friendship paradox is introduced. Specifically, the friendship paradox is said to be asymptotically significant for a sequence of finite random graphs
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
labelled by the number of vertices
n
if the proportion of vertices with non-negative friendship-bias converges in probability as
n\to\infty
to a number
\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
. Interestingly, it is shown that the friendship paradox is significant for the homogeneous Erdős-Rényi random graph, the inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi random graph, as well as for a well-known case of the configuration model and the preferential attachment model.

In
[
9
]
, the
multi-level
friendship paradox is analysed, which addresses the friendship paradox at higher levels of friendship. More specifically, by extending the notion of friendship-bias to a multi-level setting, using random walks on graphs and imposing additional constraints on the graph, it is shown that the
k
-level average friendship-bias is non-negative for each fixed
k
, regardless of whether
k
-level friends are explored via backtracking or non-backtracking random walks.

In the present paper we develop a probabilistic framework for understanding and analysing the friendship paradox for trees. We isolate and quantify the local structure underlying the friendship paradox on trees, which has both deterministic and random features. By formulating the paradox in terms of vertex-level bias and type, we derive sharp results on the distribution and correlation of these types in tree environments. Our results yield a systematic framework for studying when and how the friendship paradox manifests itself in tree-like structures that arise naturally in real-world networks.

1.2
Notations

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n},\phi)
be a
rooted tree
with
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
vertices, vertex set
V(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, and root vertex
\phi
. To label the vertices, use the Ulam-Harris method of labelling a tree: each vertex
u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{n})\setminus\{\phi\}
is labelled by a finite word
\phi\,u^{1}\ldots u^{k}
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
and, for
u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, the
j
th offspring of
u
is

u_{j}=uj=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\phi\,j,&\text{if $u=\phi$},\\[5.69046pt]
\phi\,u^{1}\ldots u^{k}j,&\text{if $u=\phi\,u^{1}\ldots u^{k}$}.\end{array}\right.

For
u,v\in V(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, write
u\sim v
to indicate that vertex
u
is adjacent to vertex
v
. The edge set of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is
E(\mathcal{T}_{n})=\{\{u,v\}\colon\,u,v\in V(\mathcal{T}_{n}),u\sim v\}
. Let
d_{u}
be the degree of a vertex
u
. Define the
friendship-bias
of
u
as

\Delta_{u,n}=\frac{1}{d_{u}}\sum_{{v\in V(\mathcal{T}_{n})}\atop{v\sim u}}d_{v}-d_{u},

which represents the difference between the average degree of the neighbours of
u
and the degree of
u
itself. Throughout the paper we follow the natural convention of defining the empty sum as
0
, in particular, the friendship-bias of the root in a trivial tree is
0
. A vertex is said to be
positive
when its friendship-bias is strictly positive,
negative
when its friendship-bias is strictly negative, and
neutral
otherwise. Define, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle N_{n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\big|\big\{u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{n})\colon\,\Delta_{u,n}>0\big\}\big|,

\displaystyle N_{n}^{-}

\displaystyle=\big|\big\{u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{n})\colon\,\Delta_{u,n}<0\big\}\big|,

\displaystyle N_{n}^{0}

\displaystyle=n-N_{n}^{+}-N_{n}^{-}.

1.3
Friendship paradox for finite trees

The friendship paradox says that for an arbitrary finite graph
G_{n}
the
average friendship-bias
is non-negative
[
7
,
8
]
, i.e.,

\frac{1}{|V(G_{n})|}\sum_{u\in V(G_{n})}\Delta_{u,n}\geq 0,

where
V(G_{n})
denotes the vertex set of
G_{n}
. Equality holds if and only if all connected components of
G_{n}
are regular. Still, there are graphs for which the number of negative vertices is larger than the number of positive vertices. This leads us to the following notion.

Definition 1.1
.

[Significance for finite trees]

For a finite tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, the friendship paradox is said to be
significant
when
N_{n}^{+}\geq N_{n}^{-}
, and
insignificant
otherwise. It is said to be
strictly significant
when
N_{n}^{+}>N_{n}^{-}
.

\spadesuit

Our first theorem says that all finite trees are strictly significant, unless they consist of a single path of length
\neq 3
, and provides a lower bound for the gap between the numbers of positive and negative vertices.

Theorem 1.2
.

[Finite trees are significant]

For all finite trees
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, the following statements hold:

(a)

If there is an
u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{n})
such that
d_{u}\geq 3
, then

N_{n}^{+}-N_{n}^{-}\geq 1+\sum_{u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{n})}(d_{u}-2)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{d_{u}\geq 3\right\}}>0.

(1.1)

(b)

If
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is a path of size
n
, then

N_{n}^{+}-N_{n}^{-}=\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{n=3\right\}}.

1.4
Friendship paradox for infinite trees

Next, consider an infinite Galton-Watson tree
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
with offspring distribution
X
, and let
p_{k}=\mathbb{P}(X=k)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This model arises naturally as the local weak limit of large sparse random graphs, including the Erdős–Rényi and configuration models. Our goal is to characterise the limiting distribution of vertex types, i.e.,, positive, neutral, or negative, esspecially when the tree is explored from the root. Unlike the deterministic case, the randomness of the degrees introduces fluctuations that affect the sign of the friendship paradox at different generations. Simulations of such trees for various Poisson offspring distributions are shown in Figure
1
, illustrating how the relative frequencies of vertex types change with the mean degree.

Figure 1:
Four realisations of generations
0,\ldots,6
of an infinite rooted Galton-Watson tree with an offspring distribution that is Poisson
(\lambda)
with
\lambda=0.1,0.5,1,2
, respectively. Indicated are the locations of the positive (= red), neutral (= circle) and negative (= blue) vertices
in the infinite tree
. Negative vertices have a tendency to be adjacent to positive vertices. Smaller values of
\lambda
tend to produce a higher proportion of neutral vertices, while larger values of
\lambda
tend to produce a higher proportion of positive vertices. In addition, for small values of
\lambda
the root is almost always positive or neutral, while for large values of
\lambda
the probability of the root being negative increases, although it remains smaller than the probability of the root being positive or neutral. This is consistent with the findings on the friendship paradox in sparse Erdős-Rényi random graphs reported in
[
8
]
. Indeed, the local limit of the Erdős-Rényi random graph with edge density
\lambda/n
is the rooted Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution Poisson
(\lambda)
(see
[
12
, Theorem 2.18]
).

1.4.1
Density of vertex types in infinite Galton-Watson trees

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{m}=(V(\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{m}),E(\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{m}))
be the induced subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
with vertex set

V(\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{m})=\big\{u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{\infty})\colon\,\mathrm{dist}(\phi,u)\leq m\big\},

where
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the graph distance. Let
S=\{-,0,+\}
denote the set of types of the vertices. Put
N_{m}=|V(\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{m})|
and

N_{m}^{\chi}=\big|\big\{u\in V(\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{m})\colon\,\mbox{$u$ has type $\chi$ in $\mathcal{T}_{\infty}$}\big\}\big|,\qquad\chi\in S.

The latter counts the number of vertices of type
\chi\in S
in the infinite tree
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
that are at distance
\leq m
from the root. To investigate the significance of the friendship paradox for infinite trees, we present the analogue of Definition
1.1
for infinite trees.

Definition 1.3
.

[Significance for infinite trees]

For an infinite tree
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
, the friendship paradox is said to be
significant
when
\liminf_{m\to\infty}(N_{m}^{+}-N_{m}^{-})/N_{m}\geq 0
, and
insignificant
otherwise. It is said to be
strictly significant
when
\liminf_{m\to\infty}(N_{m}^{+}-N_{m}^{-})/N_{m}>0
.

\spadesuit

Our second theorem identifies the densities of positive, neutral and negative vertices in an infinite Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
p=(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
satisfying

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(k\log k)\,p_{k}<\infty,\qquad p_{0}=0,\quad p_{1}<1.

(1.2)

Write
\tilde{p}=(\tilde{p}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
to denote the
size-biased
offspring distribution given by
\tilde{p}_{k}=kp_{k}/\mu
with
\mu=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}kp_{k}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

[Densities of vertex types for infinite trees]

(a)

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
be the infinite Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
p
satisfying (
1.2
). Then, for each
\chi\in S
, there exists
f^{\chi}
such that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{N_{m}^{\chi}}{N_{m}}=f^{\chi}\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

(b)

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
S_{k}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}X_{l}
, where
(X_{l})_{l=1}^{k}
are drawn independently from
p
, and let
\tilde{X}
be drawn independently from
\tilde{p}
. Then

f^{\chi}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\Big({\rm sign}\big[\tilde{X}+S_{k}-k(k+1)\big]=\chi\Big).

The intuition behind Theorem
1.4
is as follows. The neighbours of
u
are its parent and its children. The offspring of the parent is drawn from the size-biased distribution, and is distributed as
\tilde{X}
. The offspring of the children are independent copies of
X
. Conditioned on
X_{u}=k
, with
X_{u}
the number of offspring of vertex
u
, the degree of
u
is
k+1
, and the total degree of the children of
u
is distributed as
S_{k}+k
, where
S_{k}
is independent of everything else. The type of
u
is thus determined in distribution by the sign of

\frac{1}{k+1}\big(\tilde{X}+1+S_{k}+k\big)-(k+1)=\frac{1}{k+1}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{k}\big)-k,

i.e., by

{\rm sign}\big[\tilde{X}+S_{k}-k(k+1)\big].

In contrast to finite trees, not all infinite trees are significant.

.

[Not all infinite trees are significant]

1.4.2
Correlations of vertex types in infinite Galton-Watson trees

It is interesting to investigate how the positive, neutral and negative vertices are arranged on the tree. A natural question is whether negative vertices tend to be adjacent to positive vertices or not. Figure
1
suggests yes.

Our third theorem quantifies the correlations between the vertex types. For convenience, suppose that the edges in the tree are directed from parents to children. Define

N_{m}^{\tilde{\chi}\chi}=\big|\big\{(u,ui)\in E(\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{m})\colon\,\mbox{$u$ has type ${\tilde{\chi}}$ and $ui$ has type $\chi$ in $\mathcal{T}_{\infty}$}\big\}\big|,

where
(u,ui)
denotes a directed edge from
u
to
ui
, and
E(\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{m})
now denotes the set of all directed edges. In what follows we use tilde-notations like
{\tilde{\chi}},\tilde{k}
for the parent vertex.

Theorem 1.6
.

[Correlations of vertex types for infinite trees]

(a)

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
be the infinite Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
p
satisfying (
1.2
). Then, for each
{\tilde{\chi}},\chi\in S
, there exists
f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}
such that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{N_{m}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}}{N_{m}}=f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

(b)

For

f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}=\sum_{\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\tilde{p}_{\tilde{k}}\,p_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\Big({\rm sign}\big[\tilde{X}+{S}_{\tilde{k}-1}+k-\tilde{k}(\tilde{k}+1)\big]={\tilde{\chi}}\Big)\,\mathbb{P}\Big({\rm sign}\big[\tilde{k}+S_{k}-k(k+1)\big]=\chi\Big),

where
S_{k}
and
\tilde{X}
are as defined in Theorem
1.4
.

Remark 1.7
.

[Original expression for correlation]

[Correlation in Galton-Watson trees]

1.5
Discussion and open problems

1.

Theorem
1.2
gives a lower bound of the difference of the numbers of positive and negative vertices in an
arbitrary finite tree
. It shows that this difference is always non-negative (significance) and is bounded below by the total surplus of edges at branching points in the tree.

2.

Theorem
1.4
identifies the densities of positive, neutral and negative vertices in an
infinite Galton-Watson tree
under mild conditions on the offspring distribution. Interestingly, the difference of the densities of positive and negative vertices is not always non-negative, and can have both signs depending on the choice of the offspring distribution. Theorem
1.6
identifies the densities of the edges having two given types of vertices at their ends. Theorem
1.8
gives a sufficient condition under which the positive vertices are negatively correlated along edges, while Example
1.9
shows that this condition is not necessary, and provides a case where the positive vertices are positively correlated along edges.

3.

The formulas for the densities of the vertex-types and the edge-types are explicit and numerically easy to implement. They provide a
quantification
of the friendship-bias in infinite Galton-Watson trees. Analytically they are not easy to manipulate, which is why a
full classification
of significance remains an open problem. It would be interesting to see whether similar limiting results could be obtained for other random trees, e.g. those arising as local limits of configuration model random graphs and preferential attachment random graphs
[
11
, Chapters 7–8]
.

4.

In
[
9
]
we looked at deeper levels of the friendship-bias, i.e., not only between vertices at distance 1 but at distance
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We found that different scenarios are possible when
n\to\infty
and
k\to\infty
jointly. It would be interesting to see what would happen on finite and infinite trees.

5.

A tantalising question is the following. In the infinite Galton-Watson tree, for those cases where the positive vertices are negatively correlated, can we prove that they do not percolate? Or conversely, for those cases where the positive vertices are positively correlated, can we prove that they do percolate? (Obviously, the answer must also depend on the offspring distribution of the tree.) Underlying these questions is the following: Can we establish a comparison with i.i.d. percolation on the same tree via coupling?

(

3.2
Not all infinite Galton-Watson trees are significant

In this section we show that the infinite Galton-Watson tree may be

\mathbb{P}\text{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

Proof of the statements in Example

.

Consider a Galton-Watson tree with an offspring distribution

. Let

denote the size of the offspring of vertex

(a) Let

, and

for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is positive}\mid X_{v}=1)

\displaystyle=1-q\,\frac{q}{\mu},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is neutral}\mid X_{v}=1)

\displaystyle=q\,\frac{q}{\mu},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is negative}\mid X_{v}=1)

\displaystyle=0,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is positive}\mid X_{v}=a)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is neutral}\mid X_{v}=a)

\displaystyle=(1-q)^{a}\,\frac{a(1-q)}{\mu},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is negative}\mid X_{v}=a)

\displaystyle=1-(1-q)^{a}\,\frac{a(1-q)}{\mu}.

It follows (via the full probability formula) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is positive})

\displaystyle=q-\frac{1}{\mu}\,q^{3},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is neutral})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}[q^{3}+a(1-q)^{a+2}],

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is negative})

\displaystyle=(1-q)-\frac{1}{\mu}\,a\,(1-q)^{a+2}.

\displaystyle f^{+}=\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is positive})

\displaystyle\to q,

\displaystyle f^{0}=\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is neutral})

\displaystyle\to 0,

\displaystyle f^{-}=\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is negative})

\displaystyle\to 1-q.

Hence, from Definition
1.3
and Theorem

and

and

large enough the friendship paradox for the tree is

\mathbb{P}\text{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

and

(b) Let

for some

, and
p_{k}=0
for all

. Set
\mu=q+2\hat{q}+a(1-q-\hat{q})
for the mean offspring. Note that a vertex of degree
3
can be negative if and only if the number of offspring of its three neighbours are either all

, or two are

and one is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is positive}\mid X_{v}=1)

\displaystyle=1-q\,\frac{q}{\mu},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is neutral}\mid X_{v}=1)

\displaystyle=q\,\frac{q}{\mu},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is negative}\mid X_{v}=1)

\displaystyle=0,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is positive}\mid X_{v}=2)

\displaystyle=1-\hat{q}^{2}\,\frac{2\hat{q}}{\mu}-3q^{2}\,\frac{q}{\mu}-6q\hat{q}\,\frac{q}{\mu}-\hat{q}^{2}\,\frac{q}{\mu},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is neutral}\mid X_{v}=2)

\displaystyle=\hat{q}^{2}\,\frac{2\hat{q}}{\mu},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is negative}\mid X_{v}=2)

\displaystyle=q^{2}\,\frac{q}{\mu}+2q\hat{q}\,\frac{q}{\mu}+\hat{q}^{2}\,\frac{q}{\mu}+q^{2}\,\frac{2q}{\mu}+2q\hat{q}\,\frac{2q}{\mu}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is positive}\mid X_{v}=a)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is neutral}\mid X_{v}=a)

\displaystyle=(1-q-\hat{q})^{a}\,\frac{a(1-q-\hat{q})}{\mu},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is negative}\mid X_{v}=a)

\displaystyle=1-(1-q-\hat{q})^{a}\,\frac{a(1-q-\hat{q})}{\mu}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is positive})

\displaystyle=q+\hat{q}-\frac{1}{\mu}\big[q^{3}+2\hat{q}^{4}+3q^{3}\hat{q}+6q^{2}\hat{q}^{2}+q\hat{q}^{3}\big],

\displaystyle f^{+}=\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is positive})

\displaystyle\to q+\hat{q},

\displaystyle f^{0}=\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is neutral})

\displaystyle\to 0,

\displaystyle f^{-}=\mathbb{P}(v\text{ is negative})

q+\hat{q}<\tfrac{1}{2}
and
a
large enough the friendship paradox for the tree is
\mathbb{P}\text{-}\mathrm{a.s.}
insignificant. It is
\mathbb{P}\text{-}\mathrm{a.s.}
strictly significant for
q+\hat{q}>\tfrac{1}{2}
and
a
large enough.
∎

3.3
Correlations of vertex types in infinite Galton-Watson trees

In this section we prove Theorems
1.6
–
1.8
. We use the same construction as in Section
3.1
., and the proof goes along the same lines.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

We show that, for
{\tilde{\chi}},\chi\in S
,

\frac{N^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}_{m}}{N_{m}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}-\mathrm{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi},\qquad m\to\infty,

(3.5)

with
f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}
in Theorem
1.6
(b). The proof follows the same line of thought as in the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Fix
{\tilde{\chi}},\chi\in S
. For
v\in V(\mathcal{T}_{\infty})
, let
Y_{v}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}
be the number of
{\tilde{\chi}}\chi
-edges
(vi,vij)\in E(\mathcal{T}_{\infty})
such that
vi
has type
{\tilde{\chi}}
and its offspring has type
\chi
. We next derive an expression for
\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{v}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}\big]
. Note that now the calculations span four generations instead of three, because the type of
vi
depends on the degree of its parent
v
and of its offspring
vij
. In turn, the type of vertex
vij
depends on the degree of
vi
and the degrees of the children of
vij
. We show that

\mathbb{E}[Y_{v}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}]=\mu^{2}f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}.

(3.6)

By definition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{v}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{X_{v}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[X_{v}+X_{vi1}+\cdots+X_{viX_{vi}}-X_{vi}(X_{vi}+1)\right]={\tilde{\chi}}\right\}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{j=1}^{X_{vi}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[X_{vi}+X_{vij1}+\cdots+X_{vijX_{vij}}-X_{vij}(X_{vij}+1)\right]=\chi\right\}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tilde{l}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{\tilde{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{l}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{l}+X_{vi1}+\cdots+X_{viX_{vi}}-X_{vi}(X_{vi}+1)\right]={\tilde{\chi}}\right\}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{j=1}^{X_{vi}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[X_{vi}+X_{vij1}+\cdots+X_{vijX_{vij}}-X_{vij}(X_{vij}+1)\right]=\chi\right\}}\Big],

where the first expression selects those children
i
of
v
for which a certain statistic has sign
{\tilde{\chi}}
, and counts how many of their children
j
satisfy a similar sign condition with respect to
\chi
, and in the second expression we condition on the root degree
X_{v}=\tilde{l}
. Since the
X_{vi}
are i.i.d., we can choose a representative child, say
X_{v1}
, and multiply by
\tilde{l}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{v}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tilde{l}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{\tilde{l}}\,\tilde{l}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{l}+X_{v11}+\cdots+X_{v1X_{v1}}-X_{v1}(X_{v1}+1)\right]={\tilde{\chi}}\right\}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{j=1}^{X_{v1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[X_{v1}+X_{v1j1}+\cdots+X_{v1jX_{v1j}}-X_{v1j}(X_{v1j}+1)\right]=\chi\right\}}\Big].

We next recall the size-biased distribution
\tilde{p}_{\tilde{l}}=\tilde{l}p_{\tilde{l}}/\mu
, and condition on the degree
X_{v1}=\tilde{k}
, to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{v}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}]

\displaystyle=\mu\sum_{\tilde{l}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\tilde{p}_{\tilde{l}}\sum_{\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{\tilde{k}}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{l}+X_{v11}+\cdots+X_{v1\tilde{k}}-\tilde{k}(\tilde{k}+1)\right]={\tilde{\chi}}\right\}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{k}+X_{v1j1}+\cdots+X_{v1jX_{v1j}}-X_{v1j}(X_{v1j}+1)\right]=\chi\right\}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mu\sum_{\tilde{l}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\tilde{p}_{\tilde{l}}\sum_{\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{\tilde{k}}\,\tilde{k}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{l}+X_{v11}+\cdots+X_{v1\tilde{k}}-\tilde{k}(\tilde{k}+1)\right]={\tilde{\chi}}\right\}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{k}+X_{v111}+\cdots+X_{v11X_{v11}}-X_{v11}(X_{v11}+1)\right]=\chi\right\}}\Big].

In the last line we exploit exchangeability and fix a representative grandchild. We condition further on the value of
X_{v11}=k
to separate the expectations and get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{v}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}]

\displaystyle=\mu^{2}\sum_{\tilde{l}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\tilde{p}_{\tilde{l}}\sum_{\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\tilde{p}_{\tilde{k}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{k}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{l}+k+X_{v12}+\cdots+X_{v1\tilde{k}}-\tilde{k}(\tilde{k}+1)\right]={\tilde{\chi}}\right\}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{k}+X_{v111}+\cdots+X_{v11k}-k(k+1)\right]=\chi\right\}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mu^{2}\sum_{\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\tilde{p}_{\tilde{k}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{X}+k+X_{v12}+\cdots+X_{v1\tilde{k}}-\tilde{k}(\tilde{k}+1)\right]={\tilde{\chi}}\right\}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\rm sign}\left[\tilde{k}+X_{v111}+\cdots+X_{v11k}-k(k+1)\right]=\chi\right\}}\Big]

Above we identified the sum over
\tilde{l}
as a random variable
\tilde{X}
with law
\tilde{p}_{\tilde{l}}
, and used the law of total probability. Finally, using independence, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{v}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}]

\displaystyle=\mu^{2}\sum_{\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\tilde{p}_{\tilde{k}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{k}\,\mathbb{P}\Big({\rm sign}\big[\tilde{X}+k+S_{\tilde{k}-1}-\tilde{k}(\tilde{k}+1)\big]={\tilde{\chi}}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathbb{P}\Big({\rm sign}\big[\tilde{k}+S_{k}-k(k+1)\big]=\chi\Big)=\mu^{2}f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi},

which proves (
3.6
).

The rest of the proof carries over almost verbatim as in the proof of Theorem
1.4
, except that we need to consider two generations. Put

Z_{m}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}=\sum_{v\in\partial\mathcal{T}^{m-2}_{\infty}}Y_{v}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi},

which counts the number of
{\tilde{\chi}}\chi
-edges between generations
m-1
and
m
. Since
Z_{m-2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}-\mathrm{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
as
m\to\infty
, the strong law of large numbers gives

\frac{Z_{m}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}}{Z_{m-2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}-\mathrm{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu^{2}f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi},\qquad m\to\infty.

Since, by (
3.2
),

\frac{Z_{m}}{Z_{m-2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}-\mathrm{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu^{2},\qquad m\to\infty,

we get

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{m}^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}}{Z_{m}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}-\mathrm{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi},\qquad m\to\infty.

(3.7)

The result in (
3.7
) says that the fraction of
{\tilde{\chi}}\chi
-edges between generations
m-1
and
m
converges almost surely to
f^{{\tilde{\chi}}\chi}
as
m\to\infty
.
The same holds for the fraction of
{\tilde{\chi}}\chi
-edges in the entire tree, by the same argument as we used for the density of vertices of type
\chi
.
∎

To prove Theorem
1.8
, we need two auxiliary facts stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S_{k}
and
\tilde{X}
be as defined in Theorem
1.4
, and let
X
be a random variable drawn independently from
p
. Subject to (
1.4
) the following hold:

(a)

For each
\tilde{k}
in the support of
\tilde{p}
, the function

k\mapsto\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{k}+S_{X}-X(X+1)>0\mid X>k\big)\text{ is non-increasing on the support of }p.

(3.8)

In particular, the function

k\mapsto\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{X}-X(X+1)>0\mid X>k\big)\text{ is non-increasing on the support of }p.

(3.9)

(a)

For any non-negative discrete random variable
Y
that is independent of everything else,

\mathbb{P}(Y>k)\geq\mathbb{P}\big(Y>k\mid\tilde{X}+S_{Y}-Y(Y+1)>0\big),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3.10)

Proof.

(a) Abbreviate

\rho(k)=\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{k}+S_{k}-k(k+1)>0\big).

The condition in (
1.4
) says that, for each
\tilde{k}
in the support of
\tilde{p}
,
k\mapsto\rho(k)
is non-increasing on the support of
p
. Write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{k}+S_{X}-X(X+1)>0\mid X>k\big)=\frac{1}{\sum_{l>k}p_{l}}\sum_{s>k}p_{s}\,\rho(s)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[\frac{p_{k+1}}{\sum_{l>k}p_{l}}\right]\,\rho(k+1)+\left[\frac{\sum_{l>k+1}p_{l}}{\sum_{l>k}p_{l}}\right]\,\frac{1}{\sum_{l>k+1}p_{l}}\,\sum_{s>k+1}p_{s}\,\rho(s)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[\frac{p_{k+1}}{\sum_{l>k}p_{l}}\right]\,\rho(k+1)+\left[1-\frac{p_{k+1}}{\sum_{l>k}p_{l}}\right]\,\frac{1}{\sum_{l>k+1}p_{l}}\,\sum_{s>k+1}p_{s}\,\rho(s)

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\frac{1}{\sum_{l>k+1}p_{l}}\,\sum_{s>k+1}p_{s}\,\rho(s)

(
by (
1.4
))

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{k}+S_{X}-X(X+1)>0\mid X>k+1\big).

(b) Abbreviate

\rho(k)=\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{k}-k(k+1)>0\big).

The condition in (
1.4
) says that
k\mapsto\rho(k)
is non-increasing on the support of
p
. Write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(Y>k\mid\tilde{X}+S_{Y}-Y(Y+1)>0\big)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{\sum_{s>k}\mathbb{P}(Y=s)\,\rho(s)}{\sum_{l\leq k}\mathbb{P}(Y=l)\,\rho(l)+\sum_{l>k}\mathbb{P}(Y=l)\,\rho(l)}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{\sum_{s>k}\mathbb{P}(Y=s)\,\rho(s)}{\rho(k)\,\mathbb{P}(Y\leq k)+\sum_{l>k}\mathbb{P}(Y=l)\,\rho(l)}\quad

(
by (
1.4
))

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{\rho(k+1)\,\mathbb{P}(Y>k)}{\rho(k)\,\mathbb{P}(Y\leq k)+\rho(k+1)\,\mathbb{P}(Y>k)}\quad

(
x\mapsto\frac{x}{a+x}
non-decreasing)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{\rho(k+1)\,\mathbb{P}(Y>k)}{\rho(k+1)\,\mathbb{P}(Y\leq k)+\rho(k+1)\,\mathbb{P}(Y>k)}\quad

(
by (
1.4

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{P}(Y>k).

∎

The intuition behind Lemma
3.1
is as follows. Similarly as in (
1.4

to be positive. The statement in (

Y
stochastically smaller. Note that (

Y
. The offspring distribution
p
participates through
S_{Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{Y}X_{i}
, just as in (
1.4
).

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

From (
1.7
), using the notations introduced therein, we can write

\displaystyle f^{++}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}^{*}+S_{\tilde{X}-1}+X-\tilde{X}(\tilde{X}+1)>0,\,\tilde{X}+S_{X}^{*}-X(X+1)>0\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}^{*}+S_{\tilde{X}-1}+X-\tilde{X}(\tilde{X}+1)>0\big)\quad

(
parent type +)

\displaystyle\quad\times\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{X}^{*}-X(X+1)>0\mid\tilde{X}^{*}+S_{\tilde{X}-1}+X-\tilde{X}(\tilde{X}+1)>0\big)\quad

(
child type + given parent type +)

\displaystyle={\tilde{f}}^{+}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{X}^{*}-X(X+1)>0\mid\tilde{X}^{*}+S_{\tilde{X}-1}+X-\tilde{X}(\tilde{X}+1)>0\big).

It remains to show that the last conditional probability is
\leq f^{+}
. This comes in two steps.

1.

The intuition is that, given that a parent is positive, the offspring
X
of a child tends to be large, which makes it harder for the child to be positive. The formal derivation is as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{X}^{*}-X(X+1)>0\mid\tilde{X}^{*}+S_{\tilde{X}-1}+X-\tilde{X}(\tilde{X}+1)>0\big)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\tilde{k}^{*}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}^{*}=\tilde{k}^{*},\tilde{X}=\tilde{k},S_{\tilde{k}-1}=s\mid\tilde{X}^{*}+S_{\tilde{X}}-\tilde{X}(\tilde{X}+1)>0\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{k}+S_{X}^{*}-X(X+1)>0\mid X>\tilde{k}(\tilde{k}+1)-s-\tilde{k}^{*}\big)\quad

(
S_{\tilde{X}-1}+X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}S_{\tilde{X}}
)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\tilde{k}^{*}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}^{*}=\tilde{k}^{*},\tilde{X}=\tilde{k},S_{\tilde{k}-1}=s\mid\tilde{X}^{*}+S_{\tilde{X}}-\tilde{X}(\tilde{X}+1)>0\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{k}+S_{X}^{*}-X(X+1)>0)\quad

(
by (
3.8
))

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}=\tilde{k}\mid\tilde{X}^{*}+S_{\tilde{X}}-\tilde{X}(\tilde{X}+1)>0\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{k}+S_{X}-X(X+1)>0)\quad

(
sum over
\tilde{k}^{*}
and
s
)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}^{+}+S_{X}-X(X+1)>0),

where
\tilde{X}^{+}
is the random variable with distribution
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}^{+}=\tilde{k})=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}=\tilde{k}\mid\tilde{X}^{*}+S_{\tilde{X}}-\tilde{X}(\tilde{X}+1)>0)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

2.

By (
3.10
) with
Y=\tilde{X}
, we have that
\tilde{X}
is stochastically larger than
\tilde{X}^{+}
. Hence, from (
1.4
),

\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}^{+}+S_{X}-X(X+1)>0)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{X}-X(X+1)>0)=f^{+}.

∎

Intuitively, the negative correlation
f^{++}\leq{\tilde{f}}^{+}f^{+}
holds for two reasons: (1) a child
u
of a positive parent tends to have a large number of offspring, so that it is less likely to be positive; (2) a positive parent has a degree that tends to be small, so that its children are less likely to be positive. Each of these reasons should be enough to settle the negative correlation. However, our proof relies on both.

3.4
Negative and positive correlation in Galton-Watson trees

In this section we consider two classes of infinite Galton-Watson trees in which the correlation between the positivity of a parent and the positivity of its child is negative. We also provide an example for which the correlation is positive.

Proof.

Let
S_{k}
and
\tilde{X}
be as defined in Theorem
1.4
.

(a) Consider a Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
p_{1}\in(0,1)
,
p_{a}=1-p_{1}
for some
a\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
, and
p_{k}=0
for all
k\notin\{1,a\}
. Then

\displaystyle f^{++}

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}>1\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big(S_{1}>1\big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{1}\,\tilde{p}_{a}\,p_{a}<\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{1}\,(\tilde{p}_{a}+p_{a})^{2}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\tilde{f}^{+}

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>2\big)+\tilde{p}_{a}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{a}>a(a+1)\big)=\tilde{p}_{1}(\tilde{p}_{a}+p_{a}),

\displaystyle f^{+}

\displaystyle=p_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>2\big)+p_{a}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{a}>a(a+1)\big)=p_{1}(\tilde{p}_{a}+p_{a}).

Hence
f^{++}<\tilde{f}^{+}f^{+}
.

(b) Consider a Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
p_{1}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,
p_{2}\in(0,1-p_{1})
,
p_{a}=1-p_{1}-p_{2}\in(0,1)
for some
a\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,2,3,4\}
, and
p_{k}=0
for all
k\notin\{1,2,a\}
. Then

\displaystyle f^{++}

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}>1\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big(S_{1}>1\big)+\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{2}\,\mathbb{P}\big(S_{2}>5\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>5\big)+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{2}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>4\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big(S_{2}>4\big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{1}(1-\tilde{p}_{1})(1-p_{1})+2\,\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{2}\,p_{a}+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{1}(\tilde{p}_{a}+p_{a})+2\,\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{2}\,p_{a}(\tilde{p}_{a}+p_{a}).

Also

\displaystyle\tilde{f}^{+}

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>2\big)+\tilde{p}_{2}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{2}>6\big)=\tilde{p}_{1}(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})+\tilde{p}_{2}(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a}),

\displaystyle f^{+}

\displaystyle=p_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>2\big)+p_{2}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{2}>6\big)=p_{1}(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})+p_{2}(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a}).

Hence

\displaystyle\tilde{f}^{+}f^{+}

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{1}(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})^{2}+\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{2}(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a})

\displaystyle\quad+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{1}(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a})+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{2}(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a})^{2}

\displaystyle>\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{1}(1-\tilde{p}_{1})(1-p_{1})+\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{2}(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a})

\displaystyle\quad+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{1}(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a})+2\,\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{2}\,p_{a}(\tilde{p}_{a}+p_{a}).

Since
\tilde{p}_{1}\leq p_{1}<\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a})>(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a})>2p_{a},

\displaystyle(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a})>(\tilde{p}_{a}+2p_{a})>(\tilde{p}_{a}+p_{a}).

Therefore
f^{++}<\tilde{f}^{+}f^{+}
.

(c) Consider a Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
p_{1}=\frac{1}{100}
,
p_{2}=\frac{5}{100}
,
p_{3}=\frac{94}{100}
, and
p_{k}=0
for all
k\notin\{1,2,3\}
. In this case

\tilde{p}_{1}=\tfrac{1}{293},\quad\tilde{p}_{2}=\tfrac{10}{293},\quad\tilde{p}_{3}=\tfrac{282}{293}.

Hence

\displaystyle f^{++}

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}>1\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big(S_{1}>1\big)+\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{2}\,\mathbb{P}\big(S_{2}>5\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>5\big)+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{2}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>4\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big(S_{2}>4\big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{1}(1-\tilde{p}_{1})(1-p_{1})+\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{2}\,p_{3}^{2}+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{2}\,\tilde{p}_{3}\,p_{3}+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{2}\big(\tilde{p}_{3}\,p_{2}+\tilde{p}_{2}\,p_{3}+\tilde{p}_{3}\,p_{3}\big)(2\,p_{3}\,p_{2}+p_{3}^{2})

\displaystyle\approx 0.0034.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\tilde{f}^{+}

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>2\big)+\tilde{p}_{2}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{2}>6\big)

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{1}(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})+\tilde{p}_{2}\Big(\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{3}^{2}+\tilde{p}_{2}(2\,p_{2}\,p_{3}+p_{3}^{2})+\tilde{p}_{3}(2\,p_{1}\,p_{3}+p_{2}^{2}+2\,p_{2}\,p_{3}+p_{3}^{2})\Big),

\displaystyle f^{+}

\displaystyle=p_{1}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{1}>2\big)+p_{2}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{X}+S_{2}>6\big)

\displaystyle=p_{1}(2-\tilde{p}_{1}-p_{1})+p_{2}\Big(\tilde{p}_{1}\,p_{3}^{2}+\tilde{p}_{2}(2\,p_{2}\,p_{3}+p_{3}^{2})+\tilde{p}_{3}(2\,p_{1}\,p_{3}+p_{2}^{2}+2\,p_{2}\,p_{3}+p_{3}^{2})\Big).

Hence
\tilde{f}^{+}f^{+}\approx 0.0028
, which is less than
f^{++}
.
∎
