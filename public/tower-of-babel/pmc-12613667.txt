BIGH3 as a potential biomarker for fibrosis in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: insights from mouse and human studies
Authors: Zhenjie Zhuang, Fen Zhuge, Yinlan Liu, Yu Song, Yan Luo, Jing Liu, Wenjun Yang, Jianing Kong, Huiqin Zhang, Jin Yang, Junping Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver fibrosis is the strongest predictor of long-term clinical outcomes in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease MASLD. Non-invasive methods for identifying these patients are urgently needed. We aimed to investigate the link between the transforming growth factor-β induced protein Ig-H3 (BIGH3) and fibrosis in both diet-induced mouse model and patients with MASLD, and explore its potential use as a biomarker for MASLD related fibrosis.MethodsHepatic gene expression and serum BIGH3 levels were studied in two diet -induced MASLD models. Forty patients with biopsy-proven MASLD were recruited. Serum BIGH3 levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. The difference between groups with or without fibrosis was analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. BIGH3 levels across different fibrosis stages were analyzed using Spearman correlation, Kruskal–Wallis test, and Dunn’s post-hoc test. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to explore its value in the diagnosis of MASLD related fibrosis.ResultsBigh3 was identified as a fibrosis-related gene in the liver. Serum BIGH3 levels were significantly higher in the mouse model of fibrosis than in controls (P < 0.01). Additionally, serum BIGH3 levels were significantly elevated in MASLD patients with fibrosis compared to those without fibrosis (P < 0.01), and showed a stepwise increase corresponding to the degree of fibrosis (ρ = 0.55, P < 0.01). The areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve to discriminate significant fibrosis were 0.790 (P < 0.01).ConclusionBIGH3 is a potential non-invasive biomarker of MASLD related fibrosis. It may be beneficial for diagnosing significant fibrosis in MASLD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04275-2.Keywords: BIGH3, TGFBI, Biomarker, MASLD, NASH, Liver fibrosis

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent chronic liver condition affecting over 30% of the global population [1, 2]. One-fourth to two-thirds of patients progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), and 9–25% develop fibrosis, which can ultimately lead to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [3]. Fibrosis is the most important liver-related morbidity and mortality risk factor in patients with MASLD [4–6]. Unfortunately, it often develops asymptomatically, and patients generally do not seek medical attention until cirrhosis complications develop. Both the Food and Drug Administration in US and European Medicines Agency recommend that patients with MASH and significant fibrosis (≥ F2) should be considered for treatment with pharmacological therapy [7]. Early diagnosis enables the initiation of interventions and improves survival.

Liver biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosing and staging liver fibrosis [8]. However, its invasive nature and associated risks render it suboptimal for routine practice.Moreover, the accuracy is questionable because of sampling errors and both intro- and interobserver variability. Imaging techniques such as ultrasound-based vibration-controlled transient elastography and magnetic resonance elastography are employed to measure liver stiffness as an indicator of fibrosis in MASLD patients [9–11], their performance is operator-dependent and can be affected by obesity, while their cost also limits their application in primary care. Considering the high prevalence of MASLD, simple and non-invasive blood biomarkers represent an attractive strategy for fibrosis screening, diagnosis, and staging. Proprietary blood tests are designed with specific markers directly related to various components of the extracellular matrix such as collagen III deposition and hyaluronic acid [12, 13]. However, their patented nature and high cost limit their availability in the clinical setting. Among non-patented blood tests, fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) is one of most popular and validated non-invasive parameters [14]. It utilizes clinical variables such as age, AST, and platelets, which are easily accessible in routine clinical practice. The disadvantages of FIB-4 is a large undetermined range [15]. Additionally, it is affected by factors such as age and platelet level [16]. Multiple non-invasive parameters may improve diagnostic performance [17]; therefore, more non-invasive methods for detecting fibrosis are urgently needed.

The aim of this study was to examine the associations between serum levels of transforming growth factor-β induced protein Ig-H3 (BIGH3), a potential non-invasive biomarker for fibrosis in MASLD identified in animal models, and fibrosis in both MASLD mice and MASLD patients, and further explore its performance as a biomarker for MASLD-related hepatic fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (22-24 g) were purchased from Shanghai Lingchang Biotechnology Co. Ltd. They were housed in the Animal Center of Hangzhou Normal University, maintained at 25 °C ± 3 °C with 53% ± 3% relative humidity with a 12:12 h dark–light cycle. Food and distilled water were freely accessible. After one week of acclimation, they were randomly divided into different groups.

Two animal models of MASLD were used in this study. The intervention diet used to induce MASLD fibrosis in first one closely mimics human dietary patterns, but the mice develop very mild fibrosis with low ratio. We take advantage of this heterogenicity to screen fibrosis related genes by comparing RNA sequencing results from mice with/without fibrosis. In the second model, the development of fibrosis was accelerated by both increasing energy intake and inhibiting liver triglyceride output using special diet detailed below, resulting severe MASH fibrosis model in short time. This model was used to measure serum BIGH3 levels. The sample size for animal experiments was determined based on our previous experience, the experimental objectives, as well as considerations of animal welfare and costs.

In the first set of animal experiments, mice were fed a normal diet (CON1 group) (n = 5) or high-fat high-sucrose diet (HFHS group) (n = 7) for 32 weeks. Diets were purchased from the Trophic Animal Feed High-Tech Company. For the HFHS diet, 58% energy was from fat and 25% was from carbohydrates (12.6% from sucrose).

In the second set of animal experiment, the mice were fed a normal diet (CON2 group) (n = 8) or l-amino acid defined diet (60% kcal% fat) with low methionine and no added choline (CDAHF group) diet for 12 weeks (n = 8). The CDAHF diet was purchased from Dyets, Inc. China.

By the end of experiments, the mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 60 mg/kg. Once confirmed to be complete unconscious, the mice’s livers were harvested for subsequent experiments. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Hangzhou Normal University. All animals’ data were included in the analysis.

RNA sequencing

Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp; PE150) on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 was performed by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China. Briefly, the clean reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome. After the final transcriptome was generated, String Tie was used to estimate the expression levels of all transcripts by calculating fragments per kilobase of exon model per million. Differentially expressed mRNAs were selected with a fold change > 2 or a fold change < 0.5 and with the parametric F-test comparing nested linear models (p value < 0.05) by R package edgeR (https://bioconductor.org/packages/reease/bioc/html/edgeR.html). The gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed by applying the online tool from DAVID Bioinformatics Resources (http://david.ncifcrf.gov).

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen liver tissue using the TRIzol reagent. Equal amounts of isolated RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using first-strand cDNA synthesis (Vazyme, China). PCR was performed on a Real-time PCR Detection System (Roche, USA) using the SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, China). The primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method and expressed as the increase in fold change compared to the corresponding control. GAPDH was used as the reference gene.

Hydroxyproline content assay

Liver tissue (0.1 mg) was minced and hydrolyzed with 6 mol/L HCl at 95 °C for 5 h. Hydrolysates were used for hydroxyproline quantification using the spectrophotometric method according to the instruction manual of the Hydroxyproline assay (Institute of Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering, China) with a BioTek Microplate reader at a wavelength of 550 nm. Data were expressed as hydroxyproline (μg) per wet liver weight (mg).

Sirius staining

Paraffin Sects. (5 µm) were dewaxed and hydrated according to standard protocols. Sections were incubated with sirius red reagent for 1 h, after briefly washing, the nuclear was stained with hematoxylin solution. The sections were dehydrated and cleared in xylene following standard protocols.

Serum BIGH3 analysis by Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assays (ELISA)

ELISA kits for both human and mouse BIGH3 were purchased from Invitrogen (EHBIGH3, EMBIGH3). Serum was diluted 300 times for the mouse sample and 2000 for the human samples. BIGH3 concentration was quantified according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density was measured using a BioTek Microplate reader at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Patients

Patients who attended the outpatient unit of the Hepatology Department of the Affiliated Hospital of Hangzhou Normal University between 2020–2022 were included in this study. Blood was obtained for laboratory test. For patients with abnormal ALT, AST, GGT or suspicious imaging results, biopsy was scheduled within one week and blood samples were saved at −80 °C for BIGH3 examination. Liver biopsies were performed under the ultrasound guidance by a single expert. The diagnosis of MASLD was based on the histological criteria and medical history. Patients with evidence of other liver diseases (autoimmune hepatitis, viral hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, hemochromatosis, cholestatic liver disease, or Wilson’s disease) and those who consumed excessive amounts of alcohol (alcohol intake > 20 g/day for women; > 30 g/day for men) were excluded. The sample size was estimated based on preliminary experiments using five samples from each group (with or without fibrosis). Using the ‘pwr’ package in R, with an effect size (d) of 1.14, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired power of 0.8, a minimum of 14 samples per group was determined to be sufficient. A total of 40 patients were recruited for the study.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Hangzhou Normal University (2020 (Lun 02) KS-027), and the study protocol was in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki Principles of 1975.

Liver fibrosis stage

Liver biopsy specimens were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin and Masson’s trichrome. The severity of fibrosis was independently graded according to the NASH-Clinical Research Network criteria (CRN) [18] by two pathologists specialized in the field independently: F0, no fibrosis; F1, perisinusoidal or periportal fibrosis; F2, perisinusoidal and portal/periportal fibrosis; F3, bridging fibrosis; and F4, cirrhosis.

Clinical and biochemical characterization

Anthropomtric and clinical parameters were measured using standardized methods. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), γ-glutamyl transferase, (GGT), low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), total bilirubin, albumin, total cholesterol, triglyceride, fasting glucose (7180 Biochemical analyser, Hitachi, Japan), platelet (XE2100 Automated hematology analyser, Sysmex, Japan), fasting insulin (Architect i2000SR immunoassay analyser, Abbott, USA) were measured following manufacturer’s protocol. Body Mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. The calculation formula for FIB-4 is as follows: age (year) × AST (U/L)/platelets (109/L) × √ALT (U/L).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and SPSS. Categorical clinical data between groups were compared using the chi-square test (Fisher exact test). Continuous variables were analyzed using the t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for highly skewed data for both human and animal data. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to compare the difference in serum BIGH3 levels between MASLD patients with and without fibrosis, adjusting for confounding factors including age, gender, presence of diabetes mellitus, and obesity. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to estimate the association between serum BIGH3 level and the severity of fibrosis in human subjects. The differences in BIGH3 levels among groups with varying fibrosis grade were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the performance of BIGH3 and FIB-4, alone or in combination. For combined ROC analysis, a logistic regression model was used and the predicted probability of the two indicators in combination was calculated. The ROC curve was plotted using the predicted probabilities. ROCs were compared with DeLong test, sensitivity and specificity was compared by McNemar’s test. For all analysis, two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Bigh3 gene expression in a mouse model of MASH induced by HFHS

Microvesicular steatosis, macrovesicular steatosis, and inflammation foci were evident in the livers of mice in HFHS group but not in CON1 group, as indicated by HE staining (Fig. 1A). Due to heterogeneity commonly seen in animal model induced by intervention diet mimicking human dietary pattern [19], three out of seven mice developed very mild fibrosis, as determined by Sirius Red staining (Fig. 1B). Based on this staining results, HFHS group was further divided into HFHS with fibrosis (HFHS-F) and HFHS without fibrosis (HFHS-NF). We further compared gene expression profiles of these groups, the differentially expressed genes are shown in Fig. S1A. Among 380 genes that specifically changed at fibrosis stage (Fig. S1B, blue area), Bigh3 was selected for further study for the following reasons. First, its expression product is a secretory protein that can be detected in serum, making it a good candidate for a non-invasive biomarker. Second, basic research has indicated that BIGH3 plays an important role in the fibrosis development in other tissues [20, 21], and a few clinical studies reported that its expression in leukocytes or turnover rate in blood is associated with liver fibrosis [22, 23]. However, serum BIGH3 as a biomarker has not yet been explored in the context of fibrosis in MASLD patients (see details in discussion). Third, by analysing RNA sequencing data, we found it was significantly enriched in GO terms related to extracellular matrix remodelling, cell adhesion, and angiogenesis (Table 1), all of which are closely associated with fibrosis [24–26].

Diagnostic performance of serum BIGH3 levels in the prediction of significant fibrosis in patients with MASLD

The area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) of BIGH3 for the detection of significant fibrosis (≥ F2) in patients with MASLD was 0.790, which is not statistically different from that of FIB-4 (0.805). Youden index is maximized to explore optimal cut-off for BIGH3 or FIB-4. For BIGH3, at the cut-off of 1203.2 ng/ml, the sensitivity and specificity were 92.9% and 65.4%, respectively. For FIB-4, at a Youden cut-off of 1.0712, the sensitivity and specificity were 78.6% and 69.2%, respectively. When these two parameters were combined, the AUROC increased to 0.860 with a sensitivity of 78.6% and an improved specificity of 80.8% (Fig. 4, Table 3), however, the difference in the ROC curves, sensitivity and specificity didn’t reach statistical significance.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationship between BIGH3 and MASH fibrosis in both mice and humans. We observed a significant increase in serum BIGH3 levels in CADHF model mice compared to CON2 control mice, as well as in MASLD patients with fibrosis compared to those without fibrosis. In addition, serum BIGH3 level was moderately correlated with the severity of fibrosis and demonstrated good diagnostic value according to the ROC analysis. These data suggest that serum BIGH3 is a potential biomarker of fibrosis in MASLD.

BIGH3 is an extracellular matrix protein induced by transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ) [27], the latter of which is the major molecule that induces the differentiation of hepatic stellate cells from fibroblasts to myofibroblasts and triggers fibrosis [28]. Several studies have suggested an association between BIGH3 expression and liver fibrosis. Lu et al. reported that BIGH3 was one of the genes upregulated in blood leukocytes from HBV patients with cirrhosis compared to those with chronic hepatitis B [22]. In a mouse model, hepatic BIGH3 correlated with fibrosis, especially when the scavenger stab2 was deficient [29]. In a study involving 11 subjects with fibrosis of various etiologies, Decaris et al. reported that the turnover rate of plasma BIGH3 correlates with that of collagen, a marker of fibrosis [23]. Two systematic studies identified an association between BIGH3 and MASLD in terms of liver damage using a bioinformatic approach, but no experimental validation was performed [30, 31]. In our study, we focused on serum BIGH3, particularly in relation to fibrosis in MASLD, and validated its usefulness in diagnosis of fibrosis in MASLD patients. In our study, we demonstrated upregulation of hepatic Bigh3 at fibrosis stage and validated that serum BIGH3 levels were elevated in both mice and MASLD patients with fibrosis. Our findings suggest that BIGH3 could be a potential biomarker of MASLD related fibrosis. To the best of our knowledge, no other study has validated the use of serum BIGH3 levels as a biomarker of fibrosis in patients with MASLD.

Live biopsy is the gold standard for the diagnosis of MASLD related fibrosis. However, its invasiveness and inconvenience have limited its application in routine clinical practice. FIB-4 as one of non-invasive parameters have been intensively studied in high-prevalence populations at secondary centers with the best performance in the diagnosis of F3-4 fibrosis (advanced fibrosis) [32, 33]. Identifying patients with “at-risk” MASH (biopsy-proven MASH with F2 or higher fibrosis) is a more recent area of interest [7]. In current study, we studied the performance of BIGH3 in detecting F2 fibrosis. ROC analysis suggests serum BIGH3 is able to detect F2 in our cohort, its performance is comparable to that of FIB-4. Even though not reaching statistical significance, the sensitivity appears slightly higher than FIB-4, and combined ROC seems improved. Whether this marginal advantage will persist in larger cohorts remains to be explored, or BIGH3 could be considered as a complementary biomarker in combination with other parameters. In a study with 126 biopsies proven MASLD subjects [34], the performance of five serum biomarkers hyaluronic acid, collagen type IV, procollagen type III, laminin and cholylglycine were assessed, collagen type IV outperformed other four serum markers in detecting significant fibrosis (≥ F2). The AUROC for collagen IV from previous literature was 0.718, whereas ROC for BIGH3 is 0.790 in our cohort, suggesting that BIGH3 is also a biomarker with comparable discriminative ability. Furthermore, correlation analysis showed that serum BIGH3 levels moderately correlated with fibrosis severity, this indicates its potential use as a complementary marker for monitoring fibrosis progression during treatment and drug development, however, this need to be further validated in longitudinal study in the future.

Our study revealed a close relationship between the serum BIGH3 levels and MASLD related fibrosis. However, no studies have examined its active role in liver fibrosis, and only a few reports have examined its effect on fibrosis in other tissues. For instance, an in vitro study demonstrated that silencing of BIGH3 inhibited the fibrotic effect induced by TGFβ in the human primary lung fibroblast cell line IMR-90 [20]. Another study reported that administering recombinant BIGH3 in mice delayed the resolution of venous thrombi, which is the reverse process of fibrosis [21]. Our RNA sequencing data indicated a significant increase in adhesion-related molecules during fibrosis, with Bigh3 being enriched in this GO term (Table 1). Cell adhesion represents initial event in the progression of fibrosis [25, 26]. TGFβ-induced myofibroblast differentiation relies on cell adhesion and integrin signalling mediated by focal adhesion kinase. BIGH3 contains an Arg-Gly-Asp sequence that can serve as a ligand recognition site for several integrins [27]. Depletion of BIGH3 resulted in cells losing adherence to the extracellular matrix and a decrease in focal adhesion kinase phosphorylation [35]. Our unpublished data also demonstrated that BIGH3 enhances the adhesion of the human stellate cell line to culture plates. We postulate that BIGH3 may regulate the focal adhesion kinase/integrin pathway to regulate the myofibroblast differentiation and the development of fibrosis. We plan to investigate the potential mechanism and test whether BIGH3 could be a target for fibrosis prevention.

Several limitations existed in our study. First, the relatively small sample size and lack of external validation render the results of the ROC analysis preliminary. Second, the use of liver biopsy as the gold standard for fibrosis staging introduces potential sampling bias because only patients with clinical indications for liver biopsy, such as abnormal blood parameters or suspicious liver imaging findings, were included in this study. Third, the enrolled patients in our center predominantly exhibited mild fibrosis, which may further bias the results. These selection biases limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader MASLD population. In addition, BIGH3 only showed moderate correlation with fibrosis, we must be cautious in considering it as a standalone biomarker. Future research should involve cross-sectional studies with larger and more diverse populations, multi-center cohort studies, and longitudinal investigations. These studies should aim to further validate the reproducibility, reliability across different populations, and the utility of BIGH3 in combination with other markers in diagnosing or monitoring fibrosis progression.

Conclusion

In the present study, we found that serum BIGH3 levels increased in MASLD patients with fibrosis and were moderately correlated with the severity of the condition. We conclude that serum BIGH3 is a promising biomarker of MASLD related fibrosis. Serum BIGH3 could serve as a complementary tool for diagnosing significant fibrosis in patients with MASLD, monitoring fibrosis progression in clinical settings, however, further validations are required.
