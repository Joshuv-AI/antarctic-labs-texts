Detection of DNA methylation from buccal swabs using nanopore sequencing to study stunting
Authors: Alim El-Hakim, Inswasti Cahyani, Muhammad Zulfikar Arief, Gilang Akbariani, Asep Muhamad Ridwanuloh, Syam Budi Iryanto, Ratih Rahayu, Daeng Deni Mardaeni, Vincentius Budhyanto, Yusnita, Wening Sari, Anggi Pn Hidayati, Intan Razari, Silviatun Nihayah, Kinasih Prayuni, Chandra Utomo, Ratih Asmana Ningrum, Susanti Susanti, Ahmad Utomo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStunting is the result of chronic malnutrition due to the lack of micronutrient-based methyl donors required for epigenetic programming during the first 1000 days of life. Methylation studies using bisulfite conversion from blood DNA are invasive and may not be practical for large-scale epidemiological investigation or nutrition intervention programs. Buccal epithelial methylation may reflect early germline methylation. Therefore, buccal cells can serve as convenient sample sources to collect biomarkers associated with the risk of stunting. This study aims to describe the feasibility of nanopore adaptive sampling in detecting DNA methylation from children’s buccal DNA. We used adaptive sampling of Oxford Nanopore Technology on barcoded samples to describe differential methylation associated with malnutrition. Overall, the level of 5-methylcytosine (5mC) was lower in stunted children than in normal children. We also found differentially methylated regions at the MIR6724 and RNA45SN1 gene loci on chromosome 21, which was higher in stunted children than in normal children. We described and detected differential DNA methylation in the locus previously not known to be associated with stunting. Interestingly, this locus on chromosome 21 has been implicated in the stunted phenotype of Down syndrome.KEYWORDS: Adaptive sampling, buccal swabs, methylation detection, nanopore sequencing, stunting

Introduction

Stunting is a phenomenon where a child’s height does not match his age. This means that the child is shorter than he should be. The risk factors for the development of stunting are chronic malnutrition in pregnant women or in the first 1,000 days after gestation, an unhygienic environment (chronic microbial infections) [1], and also socioeconomic factors. The report of the 2021 Indonesian Nutrition Status Study (SSGI) survey shows a stunting prevalence of 24% [2]. In 2022, the Ministry of Health stated that stunting had decreased to 21% [3]. However, this was still higher than the national achievement target for 2024, namely 14% [3]. If it is not immediately resolved, stunted children will be at risk of experiencing chronic diseases such as obesity and diabetes, as well as cognitive challenges in adulthood, resulting in health costs and a less competitive workforce [4].

Chronic malnutrition during the first 1,000 days after gestation can lead to epigenetic changes [5]. Studies have shown that differential methylation at the H19/IGF2 locus occurred in descendants of the Dutch Famine [6] and Chinese hunger studies [7]. This differential methylation due to chronic malnutrition has been linked to an increased risk of diabetes and hypertension in adulthood [8]. These findings highlight how maternal nutrition can influence a child’s disease risk through DNA methylation during pregnancy. However, the specific genes linked to stunting remain unclear. It is also uncertain whether nutritional interventions during the first 1,000 days can trigger epigenetic changes that allow stunted children to catch up in growth [9].

Conventional methylation studies using bisulfite conversion from blood DNA, which is invasive, may not be practical for large-scale epidemiological investigation or nutritional intervention programs [10]. As an alternative to studying DNA methylation in blood, buccal epithelial methylation may reflect early germline methylation and nutrition exposure [11]. Therefore, buccal cells can serve as convenient sample sources to collect DNA methylation biomarkers associated with stunting. Adaptive sampling is a next-generation sequencing workflow developed by Oxford Nanopore Technology. It is versatile because it can detect natural DNA methylation without the need for chemical bisulfite conversion that leads to DNA degradations [12]. We wished to report the feasibility of adaptive sampling to detect differential methylation between normal and stunted children using buccal DNA as a source of noninvasive sampling.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

This cross-sectional investigation included children aged 0–5 years (n = 231) who attended local community health facilities in three villages (Kadumanueh, Kadubelang and Medong) in Pandeglang district, Banten province, Indonesia. According to previous studies, stunting rate in Pandeglang, especially in Kadumaneuh, has been estimated to be around 30% [13]. We conducted a consecutive sampling of children who attended community health facilities on 10 September 2022, from 9 a.m. to 12 p.m.

Buccal cheek swabs were collected from all participants using commercial buccal DNA isolation kits [14]. After a detailed explanation of the study to participants, we obtained informed consent from parents or guardians. This study was carried out following the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the YARSI Ethics Committee No. 238/KEP-LPUY/VIII/2022.

Anthropometric assessments

The height or length of the children was measured directly by physical examination. We used a stadiometer to measure the height in the standing position of children over 2 years of age. For children less than 2 years of age, we used an infantometer to measure the length in the sleeping position. The height/length unit is in centimetres per the standard of the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Indonesia [10]. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm. The measuring instruments were an RGZ-20A microtoise Gea baby scale and a baby GEA WBC stature gauge. The children’s height-for-age z score (HAZ) was calculated using the WHO growth standard [15]. Children with HAZ < −2 were considered stunted and those with HAZ > −1 were considered non-stunted control [15].

DNA extraction and quantification

Total DNA was extracted using the Maxwell DNA Blood Kit (Promega, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with some modifications. In short, the modifications were as follows: a 2 ml suspension containing buccal swab sample was concentrated by gradual centrifugation prior to DNA extraction. First, 1 ml suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 2 minutes and ca. 700 μl supernatant was discarded. The remaining 1 ml of swab suspension was added and re-centrifuged. Supernatant was discarded until ca. 300 μl suspension remained. Concentrated buccal swab was then subjected to DNA extraction per Maxwell® RSC Blood DNA Kit (Promega, USA) instruction. The DNA was eluted in a 30 μl elution volume. Extracted DNA samples were quantified by fluorometric analysis (Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit, Invitrogen, USA) and visualised on 1% agarose gels. The A260/A280 ratio was measured with an Infinite 200 Pro Spectrophotometer (Tecan) to confirm DNA purity. Results were in the range of 1.8–2.1.

Library preparation

We used 50–250 ng of input DNA for library preparation according to the barcoding protocol (SQK-NBD114.24, Oxford Nanopore Technologies). We pooled 4 to 12 samples per library, typically grouping them by demographic or phenotypic characteristics. The barcoding protocol involved repairing and dA-tailing the DNA using the NEBNext Ultra II End repair/dA-tailing module (NEB cat. No. E7546). A unique dT-tailed barcode adapter was then ligated to the dA-tailed DNA. The barcoded samples were pooled and ligated to the sequencing adapter. The resulting library yields ranged from 147 to 806 ng.

We modified the barcoding protocol to address the typically low DNA yields and concentrations after extraction. The key changes included: a) Doubling the reaction volume during the DNA repair and end-prep step from 15 µl to 30 µl when needed, with buffer volumes adjusted accordingly. b) Increasing incubation times for enzyme reactions and for binding and elution from SPRI beads. c) Using 1X volume of SPRI beads for clean-up after ligation, instead of 0.4X. d) Using the Short Fragment Buffer (SFB) in wash steps after ligation.

Adaptive sampling sequencing and analysis

Adaptive sampling protocol from Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) was used to identify differential DNA methylation in buccal DNA. Libraries were run on PromethION flowcells R.10.4.1 with adaptive sampling mode and fast basecalling on. Barcoded libraries were generated using 32 samples, grouped into two based on phenotype: 12 normal/non-stunted DNA and 20 stunted DNA (Table S1). The target regions were set using the ONT’s RRMS (Reduced-representation methylation sequencing) BED file covering 310 Mb of the human genome including regions which were highly enriched for CpGs including ~28,000 CpG islands, ~50,600 shores and ~42,700 shelves as well as ~21,600 promoter regions [RRMS Protocol] and Supplementary Figure S1. https://nanoporetech.com/document/ligation-sequencing-gdna-lsk114-rrms. Human reference genome used the hg38 (GRCh38.p14).

The sequencing output of each group was processed according to the reduced representation methylation sequencing (RRMS) ONT workflow. In summary, Dorado (0.7.2) super accuracy mode (v.5.0.0) was used to basecall POD5 raw sequencing data and the 5mC (v.1), 5hmC (v.1), and 6 mA (v.1) DNA modifications. The outputs with aggregated methylation frequencies were analysed for differential methylation regions (DMR) using the ‘modkit dmr pair’ tool https://nanoporetech.github.io/modkit/. The modkit output files were then used for visualisation using the ‘methylartist’ tool [16].

Statistical analysis for demographic data

Chi-square analysis was used to test the proportions of sex and modified/methylated bases in two different groups of height according to age status (i.e normal and stunted) children. Normality of the age distribution was analysed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. The Mann–Whitney test was then performed to compare the median age difference between normal and stunted children. All p-values were set at <0.05 to be statistically significant.

Results

Effects of demographic characteristics (metadata) on sampling

Out of 231 children, we selected 32 children whose DNA met the minimal requirement of DNA amount (i.e., 87 ng). These 32 samples were grouped based on phenotypes into 12 normal and 20 stunted children. The distributions of age and sex are shown in Table 1. The different proportions of sex and age groups between normal and stunted children were not significantly different (Table 1). The median ages of normal and stunted children were 16 and 35 months, respectively, but these were not significantly different (Mann-Whitney, p-value = 0.274). Since the demographic profiles between normal and stunted groups were similar, we could reasonably compare the two without concern for demographic confounding factors. This enhances the validity of any comparative analysis performed between these groups.

Reduced-representation methylation sequencing (RRMS) using nanopore adaptive sampling

The RRMS method is based on nanopore adaptive sampling where sequencing is targeted to specific regions of the human genome (i.e., targets), including known CpG sites, islands, shores, shelves, promoters, and genes. The RRMS target regions cover about 310 Mb, or roughly 10% of the total genome. This provides about five times more coverage than the short-read-based reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) method [17], Table S1. Additionally, ONT demonstrated that RRMS data has lower bias, higher mapping rates, and greater reproducibility compared to RRBS. RRMS also allows for additional analyses, such as detecting copy number and single nucleotide variations, using information from rejected reads, provided there is sufficient coverage.

Barcoded adaptive sampling and optimised DNA input can be used to enhance depth of coverage of RRMS targets

The sequencing depth of the target regions in each of the 32 samples was analysed using mosdepth [18] and visualized with MultiQC [19]. We calculated and plotted the mean coverage for each target region per sample, comparing all reads (Figure S1a) to filtered reads with a minimum Q-score of 8 (Figure S1b). Sequencing depths varied among samples, where stunted data, such as S04, S18, and S15, generally showed lower coverage (Figure S1). This variation is directly correlated with the initial amount of DNA input used for library preparations (Methods).

Overall, the sequencing depths across samples were below the recommended minimum of 20X for confidently resolving DMRs. However, the coverage plots in Figure S1 only considered reads mapped to assembled chromosomes. When we examined global coverage distributions, many reads aligned to non-assembled chromosomes (e.g., alternative, random, or unknown chromosomes) showed much higher sequencing depths, even up to two orders of magnitude higher (Figure S2). Due to the lower-than-expected coverage, we grouped the samples into two based on their phenotypes (Table S1) to increase the power of detecting differentially methylated regions.

Methylation profiles between normal and stunted samples

Nanopore sequencing can detect DNA base modifications, such as cytosine methylation, because these bases generate unique electrical currents compared to unmodified ones. We used the basecaller tool Dorado [https://github.com/nanoporetech/dorado] in ‘super accuracy mode’ to identify modified bases (5mC, 5hmC, and 6 mA) and to maximise the accuracy and reliability of detecting DMRs.

Methylation levels are represented as a fraction, indicating the proportion of modified bases relative to the total bases at each nucleotide sequence. These values can be plotted to visualise the distribution of methylation levels across individual samples or groups. We created heatmaps with dendrograms to display the methylation values (5mC and 5hmC) and assess whether samples clustered according to specific patterns (Figure 1). Although the 32 samples do not cluster exclusively by their phenotypic groups, there is a tendency for sub-clustering between normal (N) and stunted (S) samples (Figure 1). Samples with distinct methylation patterns (e.g., the first five rows of the heatmap) are associated with lower sequencing depths (Figure S1).

Cytosine methylation levels across target regions show clustering tendency of normal (N) and stunted (S) samples.

Methylation levels for 5hmC and 5mC were calculated for all reads. The top five rows of the heatmap, showing distinct methylation patterns, correspond to samples with the lowest sequencing coverage (S04 to S02).

Additionally, methylation distribution across the 32 samples varied as expected, but most showed similar spreads (Figure S3a). Some samples, such as S04, S18, and S15, displayed more skewed distributions, which are linked to their lower coverage (Figure S1). The methylation distributions between normal and stunted groups did not show significant differences across all target regions (Figure S3b) or when aggregated based on specific genomic annotations (Figure S3c-e). Principal component analysis (PCA) further supported the relative similarity of these samples, except for those with low coverage values showing up as outliers in the plot (Figure S4).

Table 2 shows the methylation profiles of different base modifications and their proportions relative to unmodified bases. Stunted samples generally have significantly higher proportions of 6 mA (N6-methyldeoxyadenosine) (6.18%) than normal samples (4.68%, Chi-square p-values < 0.00001), though its significance is unclear due to limited research on 6 mA in mammalian genomes [20]. On the other hand, proportions of 5mC (5-methylcytosine) levels in normal samples (13.71%) were significantly higher than stunted ones (9.94%, Chi-square p-value <0.00001). This aligns with recent findings that 5mC levels were lower in severely stunted children [21].

Analyses of differential methylation regions (DMR) uncover novel differentially methylated loci between normal and stunted samples

We used the ‘dmr pair’ option in the ‘modkit’ package to analyse differentially methylated regions (DMRs) between normal and stunted groups by calculating a difference score. This score is based on a log marginal likelihood ratio, which compares how well the data fits two different models. It indicates the magnitude and direction of methylation differences between the two groups. While this ‘pairing’ mode does not directly provide p-values or other standard measures of statistical significance, it offers an intuitive sense of where differences might be meaningful. A higher score (positive or negative) suggests a more pronounced difference, likely reflecting true biological variation, while smaller scores indicate that the observed differences may not be statistically significant. Ten regions with the highest 5mC difference scores are listed in Table 3 and for 5mC and 5hmC in Table 4. Complete lists of the other regions and their difference scores can be found in Supplementary Figures S2 and S3.

To visualise DMRs, we focused on the MIR6724 and RNA45SN1 gene loci on chromosome 21, as these showed the largest differences in 5mC levels (Figure 2a). The 5mC level in an extended region around these genes was higher in stunted samples compared to normal ones. However, when both 5mC and 5hmC levels were analysed together, the difference disappeared, except for a short stretch between the two genes (Figure 2b). This highlights the importance of distinguishing between the two types of cytosine methylation, which can be done in a single assay using nanopore adaptive sampling. Methylation plays a key role in the epigenetic regulation of gene expression at the ribosomal DNA loci. Therefore, the discovery of these loci is significant as CpG methylation at the promoter of ribosomal RNA genes abolished their expression in age-related manner [22], whilst MIR6724 has been implicated in iron metabolism [23] as well as aging and caloric restriction [24].

We also examined the VTRNA2 (Figure 3) and H19-IGF2 loci (Figure S5) because these genes are linked to malnutrition [25,26]. The 5mC levels downstream of the VTRNA2 locus are higher in normal children than in stunted ones (Figure 3a), and this pattern remains the same when both 5mC and 5hmC levels are considered (Figure 3b). However, no differences were observed at the H19-IGF2 locus between normal and stunted samples across all cytosine methylation types (Figure S5a-b). Previous studies have shown that the expression of this paternally imprinted locus is regulated by CpG methylation at its promoter [27]. The lack of DMRs at the H19-IGF2 locus in our data may be due to low coverage in the intergenic region or the region being absent from the RRMS targets.

Discussion

Using adaptive sampling of Oxford Nanopore Technology, we identified differentially methylated loci at MIR6724 and RNA45SN1 on chromosome 21. Our findings are novel and significant for several reasons. First, we use non-invasive buccal DNA samples instead of invasively collected blood samples. Second, adaptive sampling method can sequence and enrich specific genomic regions of interest (target regions) instead of the whole genome. This reduces the sequencing time as target enrichment means sufficient coverage for analysis is reached faster. Adaptive sampling also allows more samples to be pooled in a single library, minimising sequencing costs.

ONT sequencing protocols also allow the sequencing of modified nucleotides (i.e., 5mC, 5hmC, 6 mA), without requiring damaging DNA pretreatment with sodium bisulfite to distinguish between unmodified and modified cytosines. Furthermore, modified 6 mA could also be detected, although its significance is unknown [28]. Our data suggest that less DNA could be used for library preparations instead of the typical 5000 ng needed for classical DNA methylation sequencing on the Illumina platform [29]. However, as our depths of coverage were still suboptimal, we suggest using at least 400 ng input DNA per sample or maximise the recovery of at least 100 ng barcoded DNA before pooling and ligating to the sequencing adapter.

Our data might suggest a new mechanism for stunting caused by the methylation of micro-RNA and ribosomal RNA genes involved in iron metabolism and protein translation [23,30,31]. Specifically, miR-6724 is linked to iron metabolism [14], which is crucial for children’s healthy development [23]. Additionally, the ribosomal RNA45SN1 gene on chromosome 21 is associated with Down syndrome due to promoter hypermethylation [23]. It is still unclear whether hypermethylation of this ribosomal gene also causes stunting in children without Down syndrome [31].

The RNA45SN1 gene, a repeated unit of the nucleolar organising regions (NORs) in the human genome, is a convenient and practical biomarker for diagnosing stunting because targeted sequencing coverage can be achieved faster than with other single genomic loci. Other NOR-related loci and mitochondrial sequences, important for regulating respiratory metabolism, can also be included as RRMS target regions. For better sequence alignment, focusing on assembled chromosomes could help concentrate reads and improve coverage depth.

Many developmental-related genes are imprinted and often directly linked to methylation status of the loci, e.g., H19-IGF2 [27]. This means haplotyping may be necessary to uncouple active from inactive allele and better resolve any DMRs. However, haplotyping requires high coverage depth in the range of 20-30X [32].

There are several immediate follow-up activities. First, we will continue to sequence the remaining children in our cohorts. Larger sample sizes are required to analyse the correlation between methylation levels and factors such as sex and nutritional intake (history of breastfeeding and food compositions). Second, as part of a planned seven-year follow-up, we will track these 230 children and take their oral swabs annually. We will sequence the extracted DNA and monitor methylation changes to see how they relate to outcomes like height, weight, cognitive development, and conditions such as childhood obesity and hypertension. These prospective data may also shed light on catch-up growth or height, where epigenetic programming was primarily fixed prenatally. Additionally, ageing studies can inform dynamic epigenetics that may change from infancy to school age.

Our studies have several limitations. First, we did not directly compare blood DNA and buccal DNA from the same individuals. We focused on buccal swab collection to increase participation since it is non-invasive and painless. Blood draws also pose environmental hazards, especially in remote areas without proper disposal facilities. Second, we did not test the two sequencing methods (ONT and Illumina Infinium) in parallel using the same samples. Using Illumina Infinium on the samples run on ONT platform would be ideal, but this requires specific Infinium equipment and reagents, which we can only do when funding allows.

Our DNA methylation study using ONT adaptive sampling can be expanded for molecular epidemiology research, especially in nutrition intervention programs for women before and during pregnancy. Ensuring compliance with healthy eating or supplement intake has been challenging due to the lack of objective measurements. Buccal swab collection for methylation studies offers many advantages: it is easy to collect, has high compliance, and provides sufficient DNA for ONT sequencing. The DNA methylation levels can indicate compliance with nutritional interventions [33]. Additionally, during early growth, DNA methylation in cortisol metabolism pathways in children’s buccal DNA may reflect the mother’s mental health during pregnancy [34]. Studies show that stressed mothers can affect the methylation of glucocorticoid receptors in their children, impacting cognitive development and susceptibility to stress and depression [34].
