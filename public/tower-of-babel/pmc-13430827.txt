Epidemic dynamics and age-specific distribution of respiratory pathogens: a direct-to-consumer surveillance study in four regions of China, October 2023–February 2025
Authors: Huili Jiang, Yuping Duan, Mingyue Jiang, Zeni Wu, Yanxia Sun, Luzhao Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study characterizes the epidemiological features of key respiratory pathogens across four regions of China following the COVID-19 pandemic, aiming to enhance understanding of post-pandemic circulation patterns and supporting the development of targeted prevention strategies.MethodsSurveillance data on seven respiratory pathogens were collected (October 2023-February 2025) using a direct-to-consumer (DTC) testing approach. Demographic distributions, temporal trends, pairwise correlation analyses and symptom profiles were assessed.ResultsRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and human rhinovirus (HRV) exhibited a child pattern. Influenza B virus (IFV-B), human adenovirus (HAdV), and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (M. pneumoniae) demonstrated a school-age child pattern. In contrast, influenza A virus (IFV-A) and SARS-CoV-2 showed an elderly pattern. The epidemic patterns of pathogens were categorized into seasonal and year-round circulation. Correlation analyses revealed positive pairwise correlations between IFV-A and RSV, and between HRV and M. pneumoniae, while a negative correlation was observed between IFV-A and SARS-CoV-2. Clustering analysis based on symptom profiles identified three distinct categories: an upper respiratory tract symptom cluster (RSV, HRV), a systemic and respiratory symptom cluster (influenza viruses, SARS-CoV-2), and a systemic symptom cluster (HAdV, M. pneumoniae). Analysis of typical symptom (fever, cough, throat pain) co-occurrence for the seven pathogens showed that only IFV-A (50.14%) met the current Chinese respiratory pathogen surveillance case definition (fever with cough or throat pain); for the other six pathogens, the proportion was below 50%.ConclusionsPathogen distribution exhibited significant age-specific variation. The seasonality of different pathogens exhibited regional variations, providing valuable references for future preventive interventions. Notably, half of the individuals with single-pathogen detection reported symptoms that did not meet the current influenza-like illness (ILI) surveillance criteria. Therefore, case definitions should be optimized according to pathogen type in future surveillance efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13668-x.Keywords: Respiratory pathogens, Epidemic dynamics, Direct-to-consumer testing, Symptom profiles, Surveillance

Introduction

Respiratory infectious diseases, cause by a diverse array of viral and bacterial pathogens, are transmitted through respiratory droplets, direct contact, and aerosols, contributing substantially to the global burden of illness and posing persistent challenges to public health systems [1]. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, well-defined seasonal patterns characterized the circulation of many respiratory pathogens, with some exhibiting latitudinal gradients in epidemic timing [2]. However, the widespread implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) profoundly disrupted the transmission dynamics of these pathogens [3]. For instance, during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, the detection of influenza viruses (IFV), RSV and M. pneumoniae declined in China. Following the relaxation of the NPIs, the typical seasonal characteristics of those pathogens significantly changed in various countries [4–6]. Notably, shifts in the age distribution of infections have been increasingly reported. Hospital-based surveillance data from Canada and China indicate that RSV cases shifted toward older individuals during and after the pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic seasons [7, 8]. A recent analysis also revealed a significant age shift in HRV-associated hospitalizations, with a growing proportion of cases among children and adolescents [9]. These observations underscore the need for sustained, multi- pathogen surveillance to monitor evolving epidemiological patterns, identify shifting risk groups, and inform timely public health responses.

Currently, most respiratory pathogen surveillance systems in China rely on healthcare-based sampling, primarily capturing individuals who seek medical care, and employ a uniform, pathogen-agnostic case definition—specifically, the ILI definition (fever with cough or throat pain) [10]. DTC testing —defined as in vitro diagnostic testing marketed directly to individuals without mandatory involvement of healthcare providers—offers a complementary approach. DTC respiratory pathogen testing facilitates large-scale, community-level surveillance across broader demographic groups. In this model, participants self-collect respiratory samples (e.g., throat swabs) and report basic demographic and symptom data, allowing for real-time monitoring of pathogen circulation prior to healthcare engagement. As such, DTC-based surveillance represents a novel form of early signal detection that complements traditional healthcare-based surveillance systems.

In this study, we leveraged DTC testing data from four regions of China between October 2023 and February 2025 to characterize the age-specific distribution and regional epidemic dynamics of seven respiratory pathogens. We further examined population-level pathogen correlations and symptom profiles associated with different pathogens. These findings aim to enhance understanding of post-pandemic respiratory pathogen epidemiology and support the development of targeted prevention strategies, including optimized vaccination strategies and personal protective guidance.

Methods

DTC surveillance network

A pioneering initiative was launched on August 22, 2023, to provide multi-respiratory pathogen (MRP) testing services through a nationwide pharmaceutical e-commerce platform, Meituan Healthcare (Beijing, China). Community residents of all ages who wished to undergo self-testing could access the paid MRP testing service via a mobile application. The MRP testing kit (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China) was delivered to the participant’s home by trained couriers within approximately 30–60 min. The kit included a combined oropharyngeal and nasal swab, a virus transport tube, and a return packaging bag. Participants were guided by an online instructional video on how to self-collect the specimens; for children, parents or guardians performed the sampling. The cost of the service was RMB 99–159 (U.S. $14-$22), covering kit delivery, sample pickup, laboratory testing, and result reporting. After sample collection, participants sealed the specimen in the provided packaging and the same courier transported it on the same day to a laboratory accredited by the China National Accreditation Service for Conformity Assessment (CNAS). Participants received their test results and automated medical advice via the online platform within approximately 3 h. To mitigate concerns about self-collection adequacy, we implemented: (1) an instructional video; (2) written instructions; (3) parent/guardian sampling for children; and (4) standardized courier training on sample handling and transport [11].

Participant enrollment and specimen collection

Enrolled participants self-collected combined oropharyngeal and nasal swabs and completed a questionnaire on demographics (age, sex) and symptoms. Self-reported symptoms encompassed the following categories: fever (body temperature ≥ 37.5 °C); respiratory symptoms (cough, throat pain, nasal obstruction, expectoration, sneezing, or hoarseness); pain symptoms (muscle soreness or headache); digestive symptoms (emesis or diarrhea). Additional self-reported information—including drug allergies, pregnancy or lactating, and exposure history—was collected but not analyzed in the present study.

All samples were tested for seven respiratory pathogens using multiplex real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China), targeting six viruses: Influenza A virus (IFV-A), IFV-B, RSV, HRV, HAdV and SARS-CoV-2; and M. pneumoniae.

Data management

Participants were categorized into six age groups: 0–1, 2–5, 6–17, 18–59, 60–74, and 75–100 years old. Seven participating cities were grouped into four geographic regions: North China (initiated on August 22, 2023), East China (initiated on December 11, 2023), South China (initiated on August 22, 2023), and Southwest China (initiated on January 26, 2024). The study period (October 2023 to February 2025) was divided into three seasonal intervals (the autumn and winter of 2023–2024: October 2023 to February 2024; the spring and summer of 2024: March 2024 to August 2024; the autumn and winter of 2024–2025: September 2024 to February 2025).

Joinpoint regression

The Joinpoint regression (JPR) model was used to describe trends that were not constant over ages (V.4.7.0.0, Statistical Research and Applications Branch, National Cancer Institute, USA). The Annual Percent Change (APC) value represents the year-to-year change in detection rate within each segmented age interval. Age-specific patterns were defined as follows: child pattern – detection rates peak in the 0–5 years age group; school-age child pattern – detection rates peak in the 6–17 years age group; adult pattern – detection rates peak in the 18–59 years age group; and elderly pattern – detection rates peak in the ≥ 60 years age group [15].

Definition of epidemic period and correlation analysis

The average weekly pathogen detection rate was calculated as the number of positive detections divided by the total number of samples tested in that week. The epidemic threshold was defined as one-fifth of the highest weekly detection rate observed for each pathogen during the study period, rounded up to the nearest integer [12]. Based on this threshold, the onset of an epidemic period was identified as the first week in which the detection rate exceeded the threshold and remained above it for at least two consecutive weeks. The end of the epidemic period was defined as the week immediately preceding the first week in which the detection rate fell below the threshold, allowing for a maximum time interval of two weeks [13]. The duration of the epidemic period was calculated as the number of weeks between the onset and end. In the three seasonal intervals, the Spearman nonparametric analysis of 7 pathogens activity was conducted sequentially to identify whether the epidemiological patterns were correlated.

Co-occurrence patterns of typical symptoms

Based on the existing case definitions for respiratory pathogen surveillance in China (ILI case definition: fever with cough or throat pain), fever, cough, and throat pain were defined as the three typical symptoms. To visually illustrate the co-occurrence patterns of these three symptoms among individuals with different detected pathogens, a proportional Venn diagram was generated for each pathogen.

Cluster analysis

For symptom profile analysis, only single-pathogen detections were included to ensure unambiguous attribution of symptoms [14]. Co-detections (≥ 2 pathogens) were excluded from this analysis. A hierarchical clustering approach was applied to group pathogens based on similarity in symptom reporting patterns. Pairwise distances between observation points were computed using Euclidean distance metrics. Hierarchical clustering was then performed using the complete linkage method.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included frequencies (percentages)for categorical variables, and medians, with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Pairwise comparisons of pathogen detection rates across age groups and regions, were conducted using χ² partitioning with Bonferroni correction to adjust the significance level. Effect sizes for categorical variables were assessed using Cramér’s V. All statistical analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.3.2). A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study participants

As of February 28, 2025, 757,064 participants were recruited and tested through the DTC surveillance network. After excluding 3,143 individuals due to incomplete or inconsistent data and removing early-phase data (August-September 2023) from analysis due to insufficient and unstable sampling, 753,921 participants from four geographic regions were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Their median age was 30 years (IQR: 8‒38 years), with 39.25% of participants aged < 18 years. By region, 44.99% were from North China, 37.11% from East China, 16.45% from South China, and 1.45% from Southwest China (Table 1).

Among participants who self-reported symptoms, 341,720 (45.33%) tested negative for all seven pathogens. Significant differences were observed between the group with symptoms but no pathogen detection and the pathogen-positive group in terms of sex, age group, region, and season (P < 0.001). However, effect sizes were small (Cramér’s V < 0.10 for all comparisons), indicating that the actual differences were minimal and that the statistical significance was largely driven by the large sample size. With the exception of expectoration (P = 0.935), symptom reporting rates for all other symptoms differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.001), but again the effect sizes were very small (Cramér’s V < 0.10 for all comparisons) (Supplementary Table 1).

Demographic and seasonal distributions

IFV-A more frequently detected in elderly populations (60–74 and 75–100 years). In contrast, IFV-B showed higher detection rates among school-aged children (6–17 years) and adults (18–59 years). The detection rate of RSV had the highest detection rates in children (0–1 and 2–5 years). HRV was more prevalent in minors (0–1, 2–5 and 6–17 years) than in adults (18–59 years) or elderly populations (60–74 and 75–100 years). HAdV and M. pneumoniae were most commonly detected in children aged 2–5 and 6–17 years. SARS-CoV-2 was frequently identified in infants (0–1 years), adults (18–59 years), and the elderly (60–74 and 75–100 years), but less so in children aged 2–5 and 6–17 years. (Table 1).

Seasonally, IFV-A, IFV-B, and RSV exhibited higher detection rates in winter compared to other seasons. HRV and M. pneumoniae showed higher detection rates in autumn. HAdV was most frequently detected in summer, while SARS-CoV-2 peaked in spring.(Table 1).

Pattern of age-specific positivity rates

JPR revealed age-specific patterns. RSV exhibited a child-elderly pattern, with detection rates increasing from 0 to 2 years, peaking at age 2, declining through childhood and adulthood, and rising again at age 37. HRV showed a child pattern, peaking at age 2. IFV-B, HAdV and M. pneumoniae demonstrated a school-age child pattern, achieving their highest detection rates at 6–17 years, with peak detection at 14, 6 and 11 years, respectively. SARS-CoV-2 showed an elderly pattern, with sustained high detection from adulthood onward. IFV-A exhibited a complex, elderly-predominant trend with two minor peaks at 5 and 23 years, and a significant upward turn at 31 (Fig. 1).

Temporal and regional epidemic dynamics

Based on their epidemic patterns, the pathogens can be broadly categorized into two types: seasonal and year-round circulation. Seasonally prevalent pathogens include IFV, RSV, SARS-CoV-2, and M. pneumoniae. Among these, IFV-A exhibited distinct epidemic periods primarily during cold months in North China (mid-November–mid December 2023; early December 2024–late January 2025), East China (mid–late December 2023; early December 2024–mid-February 2025) and Southwest China (late November 2024–mid-February 2025). In contrast, South China experienced both a warm-season outbreak (mid- April–mid-May 2024) and a cold-season wave (late-November–December 2023; late December 2024–February 2025). An epidemic period of IFV-B was observed during the autumn–winter season of 2023–2024 (North and East China: mid-December 2023–January 2024; South China: October 2023–early January 2024; Southwest China: late January 2024), with no resurgence observed during the 2024–2025 autumn-winter season. Additionally, a short epidemic period occurred in South China and East China during March 2024. RSV epidemic periods occurred primarily during autumn–winter in North China (late October 2023–January 2024) and Southwest China (mid-November 2024–January 2025). In East China, activity was mainly observed in spring (mid-to-late March 2024), while South China experienced distinct epidemic periods in both autumn (early-to-mid October 2023) and spring (March–April 2024). SARS-CoV-2 exhibited distinct epidemic periods during winter–spring (North China: late January–late April 2024; East China: mid- January–early May 2024; South China: mid-February–early May 2024; Southwest China: late January–May 2024) and summer–autumn (North China: mid-July–early September 2024; East China: mid-June–mid-September 2024; South China: July–early September 2024; Southwest China: June–early September 2024). Both North and South China exhibited M. pneumoniae epidemics during both cold (North China: October 2023; September–early December 2024. South China: October–mid-December 2023) and warm seasons (North China: July 2024. South China: mid-June–mid-July 2024). But no epidemic period was observed in South China during the cold season of 2024–2025 (Fig. 2). The two viruses—HRV and HAdV—were detected throughout the year, with periodically elevated detection rates during specific intervals (Fig. 2).

Symptomatology of pathogen detections

The reported proportions of fever, cough, and throat pain, as well as their overlap, varied by respiratory pathogen. Among individuals with single HAdV detection, isolated fever (33.45%) was most prevalent. A fever-cough combination was observed in 33.81% of single M. pneumoniae detections. A fever-throat pain combination was observed in 10.92% of single SARS-CoV-2 detections. Concurrent fever, cough, and throat pain were more frequent in single IFV-A and IFV-B detections (IFV-A: 14.62%; IFV-B: 15.90%). Isolated cough was reported in 22.85% of single RSV detections and 23.73% of HRV. Among the seven pathogens with single detection, the highest proportion of fever accompanied by cough and/or throat pain was observed for IFV-A (50.14%), while the proportions for all other pathogens were below 50%. (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Based on clustering analysis, the seven pathogens were grouped into three distinct clusters according to symptom profiles: upper respiratory tract symptom cluster (RSV and HRV) exhibited the lowest prevalence of fever, headache, and myalgia, but the highest rates of nasal obstruction and sneeze/rhinorrhea. Systemic and respiratory symptom cluster (IFV-A, IFV-B and SARS-CoV-2) showed relatively higher reported frequencies of fever and upper respiratory tract symptoms. Systemic symptom cluster (M. pneumoniae and HAdV) was characterized by high fever incidence but lower occurrence of respiratory symptoms compared to other clusters, showing that different pathogens present with distinct symptom profiles. Discussion

Utilizing a DTC surveillance approach, this study analyzed detection data for seven respiratory pathogens collected over 17-month (October 2023 to February 2025) to characterize the spectrum of pathogens and examine demographic variations, epidemic period, pairwise correlations and symptom profiles.

The pathogen spectrum varied across age groups, basically consistent with previous studies [15]. IFV-A exhibited elevated detection rates among individuals aged 60–74 and 75–100 years, presenting an “elderly pattern”, in contrast to the previously described “school-aged child pattern” where IFV-A peaked in those aged 5–17 years [15]. This divergence may stem from differences in the study population or circulating subtypes. While Li ZJ et al. analyzed data from patients with acute respiratory infections (ARIs), our data derive from the general community, suggesting a higher detection rate of IFV-A among the elderly in community settings. Since the elderly exhibit lower rates of fever following influenza infection [16]—often failing to meet the current ILI case definition—they are less likely to undergo influenza testing [17]. This may lead to an underestimation of the true influenza disease burden in this population [18], and also indicates that DTC surveillance based on the general community population can serve as a valuable complement to traditional healthcare-based surveillance systems. Moreover, findings from France indicated that although influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 was most detected in children aged 5–14 years, influenza A(H3N2) predominated in those ≥ 65 years [19]. Given that influenza A(H3N2) was the dominant strain in China from week 40, 2023 to week 10, 2024 [20], this may explain the observed shift in IFV-A age distribution. These findings highlight the need to strengthen influenza surveillance and vaccination efforts targeting the elderly population.

The age-related patterns for the four viruses—IFV-B, RSV, HRV, HAdV—and M. pneumoniae align broadly with pre-pandemic findings [15]. Differences were most evident in the children and school-aged groups. Specifically, we observed increased detection rates of RSV, HRV and HAdV within 0–5 years. Similarly, IFV-B and M. pneumoniae showed rising detection up to ages 14 and 11, respectively, extending beyond the previously reported peaks at ages 6 and 7. A similar backward shift in age distribution has been documented in studies from China and Canada [7–9]. These shifts may be attributed to the NPIs implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic, which reduced interpersonal contact and limited pathogen circulation. This, combined with waning maternal antibodies, likely contributed to the observed “age shift” phenomenon [21]. Future surveillance should continuously monitor the prevalence of respiratory pathogens across different age groups, and more direct analyses should be conducted to investigate the changes in the age distribution of pathogen-positive populations. SARS-CoV-2 followed an elderly pattern, with the highest detection rates notably among the elderly (60–74 and 75–100 years), consistent with existing research findings [22] and likely attributable to the decline and dysregulation of immune function in older adults [23], underscoring the persistent risk in this group. Furthermore, among individuals < 18 years, RSV and SARS-CoV-2 were most frequently detected in the 0-1-year age group [24], HRV and HAdV in the 2-5-year group [9, 25], and IFV-B and M. pneumoniae in the 6-17-year group [26, 27], indicating that prevention strategies, such as vaccination, should be tailored to the specific age groups with the high detection rates for each pathogen.

During the observation period, the epidemic periods of IFV and RSV were largely consistent with surveillance data reported by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC) [10, 20, 28]. Specifically, IFV-A activity occurred primarily during the cold season in North China. In contrast, South China experienced both warm and cold season peaks, mirroring the pre-pandemic seasonal pattern of IFV-A in China, suggesting a gradual return to pre-pandemic circulation patterns [2, 4]. HRV and HAdV maintained relatively stable, year-round detection rates [9, 15, 25, 29, 30]. Two SARS-CoV-2 waves occurred across all regions in 2024, peaking in February-April and July-September 2024, consistent with national trends reported by China CDC [28]. M. pneumoniae outbreaks occurred in both cold and warm seasons in North and South China. Understanding these epidemic patterns within the general community therefore provides a critical evidence base for the timely implementation of targeted prevention and control measures across different regions.

Correlation analyses revealed positive pairwise correlations between IFV-A and RSV, between HRV and M. pneumoniae, while a negative correlation was observed between IFV-A and SARS-CoV-2. These may reflect overlapping seasonality and shared susceptible populations [15]. However, correlations observed at the population level are influenced by multiple factors—including population demographics, surveillance duration, the sequence of pathogen circulation, and circulating pathogen strains—leading to substantial variations across studies [31–33]. Although such population-level findings cannot directly reflect interactions within individual hosts or pathogen transmission dynamics [34–36], they nevertheless provide a valuable foundational reference for informing future mechanistic and host-level studies.

In this study, single detections of RSV and HRV were more frequently associated with nasal obstruction and sneeze/rhinorrhea, whereas HAdV and M. pneumoniae were predominantly presented with systemic symptoms. IFV-A, IFV-B and SARS-CoV-2 were characterized by a combination of systemic and respiratory symptoms. These findings are consistent with previous studies [14, 37]. The classification of pathogens into three distinct clusters based on symptom profiles provides a basis for understanding the full spectrum of early clinical manifestations of respiratory pathogen infections in community populations. The results of the co-occurrence patterns of typical symptoms following single-pathogen detection for the seven pathogens showed that IFV-A (50.14%) met the case definition of current Chinese respiratory pathogen surveillance; for the other six pathogens, this proportion was below 50%. This finding has direct policy implications for optimizing pathogen-specific case definitions to improve surveillance sensitivity and supports the refinement of case definitions for community-level surveillance.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the voluntary DTC sampling model introduces potential selection bias, as individuals who purchased and completed the tests may not be fully representative of the general population due to socioeconomic factors, technology access, and health-seeking behaviors. Nevertheless, this study represents a real-world analysis of respiratory pathogen prevalence at the population level, with over 750,000 samples collected over a 17-month period, providing robust descriptive insights into community-based pathogen circulation patterns. Second, the detection rates may be influenced by the quality of self-collected samples. To mitigate this, participants were guided by professional instructional videos during swab collection, and all samples were transported to laboratories accredited by the CNAS for testing, ensuring standardized quality control throughout the process. Third, some participants underwent repeated testing during the study period. The total sample count reflects the number of testing episodes rather than the number of unique individuals. Because the DTC service is open to all community residents who wish to undergo respiratory pathogen testing, and because respiratory pathogens can cause repeat infections, our study aims to describe real-world positive dynamics in the community population. Consequently, multiple tests from the same participant were included in the analysis. This may introduce clustering of observations from the same individual; therefore, our results should be interpreted as episode-based rather than person-based estimates. Fourth, the DTC testing service was primarily utilized by individuals with mild symptoms who chose home-based testing prior to seeking formal medical care. Young children and older adults are more likely to develop severe clinical symptoms and thus tend to seek direct medical consultation rather than rely on home-based self-testing. This resulted in lower representation of these two age extremes in our cohort. Consequently, our findings primarily reflect the epidemiology of milder, community-managed infections and may not be fully generalizable to severe cases requiring hospitalization.

Despite these limitations, DTC multi-pathogen testing represents an innovative surveillance model. It facilitates large-scale community-based testing, provides timely feedback on results, and helps reduce healthcare-associated transmission risks during peak seasons. This approach complements traditional surveillance systems. Future efforts should focus on expanding DTC-based strategies to broader and more diverse populations, integrating demographic weighting techniques to mitigate selection bias, and combining DTC data with healthcare-based surveillance to enable comprehensive, multi-dimensional assessment of respiratory pathogen epidemic dynamics.

Conclusions

This study describes the epidemiological characteristics of respiratory pathogens across four regions of China from October 2023 to February 2025. Age-specific detection patterns highlight the need for targeted prevention in high-risk groups. Identified pathogen correlations provide preliminary insights into their epidemiological interactions. Differences in symptom profiles associated with different pathogens may inform the development of case definitions for community-level surveillance. Establishing a sustained and comprehensive multi-pathogen surveillance system is essential for understanding the dynamics of respiratory disease transmission and guiding effective vaccination strategies, clinical management protocols, and public health interventions.
