Abstract
I study how firms adjust to temporary disruptions in international trade relationships organized through relational contracts. I exploit an extreme, plausibly exogenous weather shock during the 2010–11 La Niña season that restricted Colombian flower exporters’ access to cargo terminals. Using transaction-level data from the Colombian–U.S. flower trade, I show that importers with less-exposed supplier portfolios are less likely to terminate disrupted relationships, instead tolerating shipment delays. In contrast, firms facing greater exposure experience higher partner turnover and are more likely to exit the market, with exit accounting for a substantial share of relationship separations. These findings demonstrate that idiosyncratic shocks to buyer–seller relationships can propagate into persistent changes in firms’ trading portfolios. Keywords : La Niña, Supply Chains, International Contracts, Weather Shocks JEL classification : F18, L14, O19, Q54

I study Colombian flower exporters and their U.S. importers during the extreme 2010–11 La Niña episode, which disrupted road access to cargo terminals used by exporters. The Colombian flower industry exemplifies relational trade under limited contract enforceability: exporters access international markets almost exclusively through established direct relationships, and forming new connections is costly. The setting therefore provides a sharp environment in which to study relationship portfolio decisions when firms have few outside options. Importantly, flower production itself was largely unaffected by flooding at production sites, and the industry relies minimally on upstream inputs, allowing me to isolate disruptions operating through transport access rather than input shortages. This enables a clean comparison between buyer–seller relationships exposed to disrupted routes and those that were not.

I construct a novel dataset linking road disruptions to exporter routes to cargo terminals for all buyer--seller pairs, using firm-to-firm Colombian customs data from 2007 to 2019. Exploiting variation in which roads were affected by the La Niña floods, I define relationship-level exposure based on whether a given buyer--seller link relied on disrupted transport routes. For importers, exposure varies within the firm because buyers source from suppliers located in different regions, which use different road networks. This design allows me to identify shocks at the level of individual buyer--seller relationships and to trace how disruptions to specific links transmit within firms to affect their broader relationship portfolios.

I estimate the effects of the La Niña disruption using an event-study design. To study relationship termination, I estimate linear probability models of relationship ending. Because terminations are infrequent at the monthly level, outcomes are aggregated to six-month periods when analyzing portfolio adjustments. I additionally examine short-run adjustment along other margins using monthly data at the 6-digit HS product level, including transaction incidence and traded quantities.

I document distinct effects at the relationship and firm levels. At the relationship level, pooled specifications show no differential termination between exposed and non-exposed links. Within importers, however, exposure reduces relationship termination during the disruption: the probability of termination falls by 7 percentage points, a 53% decline relative to a baseline hazard of 0.13. Transactions along exposed relationships decline during the shock but recover once transport access is restored.

At the firm level, exposure induces adjustment through exit and portfolio contraction, concentrated among highly connected buyers. Among importers with high pre-shock indegree, greater exposure increases relationship termination during the disruption, with effects that persist over time. These effects operate through firm exit: a one-standard-deviation increase in exposure raises the probability of importer exit by 10 percentage points, mechanically terminating all relationships, although exit occurs only during the shock period. High-indegree firms also experience a persistent decline in new relationship formation, leading to a contraction of supplier portfolios. In contrast, exposure has no effect on exit among low-indegree firms and only a small, marginally significant increase in relationship termination.

In the final section, I present a simple framework to rationalize how shocks to individual buyer–seller relationships translate into firm-level portfolio adjustment. The key assumption is that the surplus from any relationship depends on firm profits across the entire portfolio. Road disruptions are modelled as shocks that simultaneously reduce deliveries across multiple relationships, consistent with the empirical setting. The framework delivers an ambiguous effect on relationship surplus. When multiple links are disrupted, firms’ outside options deteriorate, increasing the value of maintaining existing relationships. At the same time, reduced deliveries lower relationship-specific surplus, particularly when forming new relationships is costly.

Which force dominates depends on the extent of exposure across the portfolio and the costs of reorganization. When replacing multiple relationships simultaneously is difficult, firms optimally retain disrupted links despite temporary delivery shortfalls. When relationship-specific surplus losses are sufficiently large, firms terminate relationships or exit. The framework highlights the interdependence of relationship-level decisions within firms and provides a rationale for persistent firm-level adjustment following relationship-level shocks.

Context: 2010-11 La Niña and Colombian flower exporters

2.1
The 2010-11 La Niña event

La Niña, part of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), is a major climate phenomenon that has been observed 24 times since 1903. During La Niña, specific weather patterns are more likely but not guaranteed: equatorial East Africa typically experiences drier-than-normal conditions from December to February, while the central Andes usually receive above-average rainfall.

The 2010--11 La Niña was among the strongest on record. Australian temperatures reached their second- and third-highest levels since 1900, and the Western United States and Midwest recorded snowfall 180% above average, with the exception of the Southern Rockies and Western Mountains.

2.2
The Colombian flower sector

Colombia is the second-largest exporter of cut flowers worldwide, after the Netherlands. As of 2010, Colombia accounted for approximately 68% of U.S. flower imports and held a 16.8% share of the global export market. The United States is the largest importer of flowers, with a market share of about 20% both in 2010 and today.
12
12

12

Export data are from the Economic Complexity Observatory. Import shares are averages over 2007–2020.

The Colombian–U.S. flower trade is particularly well suited for studying buyer–seller relationships. Trade in this sector relies on long-standing direct relationships between exporters and importers, many of which date back to the 1970s. Geographic proximity facilitates repeated interaction and relationship formation between Colombian producers and U.S. buyers.

Most Colombian flower exporters are concentrated in two regions: the Sabana de Bogotá (Savannah region), near the capital, and the Antioquia region near Medellín. In 2009, roughly 70% of export-oriented production was in the Savannah region, and 18% in Antioquia. High-altitude areas are preferred, as flowers grow optimally at temperatures between 15 and 25 degrees Celsius. Colombia produces approximately 1,600 flower varieties for export. The main species are roses (31%), hydrangeas (15%), carnations (13%), and chrysanthemums (11%).

Data and variables

3.1
Data

Firm-to-firm relationships.
Using Colombian customs data from 2007 to 2019, I track all monthly transactions between Colombian flower exporters (tax IDs) and U.S. importers (names).
16
16

16

For more details on the data refer to

The data include trade values, quantities, and customs offices used for each shipment. I construct an importer identifier and generate a monthly buyer–seller panel. Using this panel, I can follow relationships from January 2007 until their last transaction or December 2019, whichever comes first.

The dataset contains roughly 23,347 buyer–seller relationships between U.S. importers and Colombian exporters, classified under HS 2-digit code 06 (“trees and other plants, live; bulbs, roots and the like; cut flowers and ornamental foliage”). Ninety-three percent of monthly transactions correspond to cut flowers (HS 0603), with the remainder involving plants and roots. The final dataset includes 1,113 exporters and 1,833 importers.

Road disruptions and routes to ports.
Data on road disruptions come from multiple governmental sources. The Emergency Office produced a report in May 2011 documenting road closures, their locations, duration, and type (e.g., scheduled repair, landslide, or accident). To fill gaps in reopening dates, I reviewed Ministry of Transport reports, official news releases, and media coverage.

I focus on 37 road disruptions that affected exporters’ routes to cargo terminals (Figure
4
). Disruptions lasting only a single day or allowing one-way traffic are excluded. Duration matters because flowers have a limited shelf life and must reach international markets promptly.

Transportation routes are constructed using the Ministry of Transport’s SICE-TAC system and customs data on cargo terminal usage. Location, firm activity, and flooded areas.
Using National Statistical Agency reports on La Niña-related flooding, I identify producers in flooded municipalities. Flooding only affected the Bogotá-Savannah region. Municipalities are classified as flooded if at least 0.7% of their area was inundated, including both sudden and slow inundations. Overall, four municipalities with flower producers were flooded, affecting 57 producers (roughly 5% of pre-shock relationships).

Firm activity is collected from the Chamber of Commerce, covering 96% of exporters selling to the U.S.

17

Exporters active only in 2007 or after 2016, representing 12% of exporters, are not found. For remaining firms, activity is extracted from tax IDs in the Chamber of Commerce database.
Of all exporters, 22% are intermediaries (non-producers), 74% are producers, and 4% have no registered activity. Producer farm locations are obtained from the Colombian Agricultural Institute (ICA) database. Missing information is supplemented using firm websites, Google Maps, and online directories. In total, I identify farm municipalities for 669 exporters, including 71 producers with multiple locations; only 68 producers remain with unknown location.

How do firms respond to temporary shocks that disrupt their suppliers’ ability to deliver goods? In this section, I show that buyer–supplier relationships adjust in response to disruptions, distinguishing between relationship termination and efforts to preserve existing contracts through shipment reorganization. Setting and assumptions

Relationship surplus
. Importer and exporter decisions cannot be mutually exclusive since they decide on the same relationship. This reduces the choices firms can make. For instance, if the importer keeps a relationship, the exporter must also decide to keep it for it to continue. From predictions to empirical results
. The empirical results can be rationalized within this framework by distinguishing between the

At the same time, Figures

Prediction 2

When disruptions affect a large fraction of a firm’s portfolio, the indirect effect weakens, and the profitability of maintaining individual relationships falls below the cost of replacement or reallocation. In this case, firms optimally reduce their number of relationships or exit the market.

Taken together, the results indicate that temporary disruptions have heterogeneous effects depending on how concentrated exposure is within a firm’s portfolio. When exposure is limited to a subset of relationships, the indirect effect dominates and firms preserve existing matches. When exposure is widespread, the direct effect dominates, leading to relationship termination and portfolio contraction.

The framework clarifies why relationship-level estimates show increased relationship survival, while firm-level exposure predicts higher overall relationship churn and exit. However, the model is deliberately stylized and abstracts from several margins observed in the data, most notably delayed shipments and shipment reorganization. These short-run adjustments operate within existing relationships and are not explicitly modelled here. As a result, the framework captures firms’ extensive-margin decisions over relationships, but not the full set of intensive-margin responses used to absorb temporary supply disruptions.

7
Conclusion

This paper studies how temporary, relationship-specific supply disruptions affect the organization of international trade. In environments where relational contracts underpin market access, shocks that impair individual buyer–seller matches can generate effects that extend well beyond the directly affected transactions. Understanding these dynamics is increasingly important as climate-related, political, and trade-policy disruptions become more frequent and severe.

Using plausibly exogenous road disruptions caused by a La Niña episode in Colombia, I show that temporary shocks reduce relationship survival on impact and induce substantial reallocation within firms’ supplier portfolios. The effects are highly heterogeneous. Firms with limited exposure largely preserve existing relationships, whereas firms whose portfolios are heavily exposed experience widespread relationship termination and a higher probability of exiting the market. These adjustments persist beyond the disruption itself, shaping portfolio dynamics and firms’ market presence over time.

The results highlight the central role of portfolio composition in mediating the effects of temporary shocks. When exposure is concentrated, the value of maintaining relational contracts declines, leading firms to unwind multiple relationships simultaneously. When exposure is limited, surviving relationships become relatively more valuable and are preserved. This distinction helps reconcile opposing relationship- and firm-level responses to the same shock and underscores that resilience in international trade depends not only on the severity of disruptions, but also on the structure of firms’ trading networks.

Appendix C
Online Appendix

C.1
Classification of road disruptions

This section describes the methodology used to classify road disruptions and construct routes to cargo terminals. Routes are estimated using the municipality centroid or main city of each farm as the origin. I rely on the SICE-TAC software provided by the Colombian Ministry of Transport, which reports routes supplied by transportation service companies operating inter-municipal freight services. As a baseline, I assume a two-axle truck with a container trailer and include one hour of waiting time for both loading and unloading.

The software provides route information between specific origin–destination pairs, primarily connecting farms to distribution centers and cargo terminals in major cities such as Bogotá and Medellín, as well as secondary cities. For each route, SICE-TAC reports travel time, distance, cost per kilometer, and the precise path taken, including tolls. When multiple feasible routes are available for a given origin–destination pair, all alternatives are retained.

For expositional simplicity, road disruptions are grouped into two waves: October 2010 to April 2011, and May 2011 to June 2011. For each disruption episode, I first estimate benchmark routes using the standard SICE-TAC configuration for inter-municipal freight transport. Routing is conducted for all possible origin–cargo terminal pairs and repeated whenever a disruption occurs.

When SICE-TAC does not report a feasible route, or when additional comparable routes are available, I supplement the analysis using Google Maps. These alternative routes are selected to be similar in distance and travel time and reflect feasible options exporters could plausibly use to reach cargo terminals. All such routes are treated as potential benchmark routes when classifying exposure to road disruptions.
