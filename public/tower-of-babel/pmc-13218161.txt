Role of community pharmacy in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in minority ethnic groups in the UK including barriers and facilitators: a mixed-methods systematic review
Authors: Rumanveer Singh Duley, Gurkiran Kaur Birdi, Joseph Bush, Ahmad Shoaib, Clare J Taylor, Derek Connolly, Ian Maidment
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the role of community pharmacy in the prevention of cardiovascular disease (CVD) among minority ethnic groups and to identify key barriers and facilitators influencing engagement with pharmacy-based CVD prevention services.DesignSystematic review using thematic synthesis to analyse both qualitative data and narratively synthesised quantitative data taking a convergent data synthesis approach. Searches were conducted from inception to 16 October 2024 across CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCOhost), EMBASE (Ovid), PubMed (NLM), Scopus (Elsevier) and Web of Science (Clarivate), supplemented by Google Scholar for grey literature and backward and forward citation tracking to identify additional studies.SettingCommunity pharmacy settings across multiple countries, including the UK and international contexts.ParticipantsMinority ethnic groups accessing community pharmacy services and community pharmacy professionals involved in delivering CVD prevention interventions.InterventionsCommunity pharmacy-based CVD prevention interventions, specifically services addressing CVD risk factors.Outcome measuresOutcomes comprised qualitative insights, including barriers and facilitators to engagement with community pharmacy-led CVD prevention services.ResultsTwenty-three studies met the eligibility criteria. Five overarching themes were identified: trust, systemic barriers, culturally tailored language, cultural and religious influences and family and social networks. Community pharmacies undertook varied CVD-related roles, most commonly CVD screening and diabetes management with additional contributions to smoking cessation, weight and lipid management, health promotion and medication adherence. Community pharmacies showed potential to support CVD prevention among minority ethnic groups. However, engagement was limited by language barriers, cultural beliefs, lack of trust in healthcare providers and organisational constraints such as time pressures and limited training. Facilitators included culturally tailored interventions, language concordance and strong pharmacist-patient relationships.ConclusionsEvidence from the UK remains limited, highlighting a need for further research into the role of community pharmacy in reducing CVD disparities among minority ethnic groups. International evidence suggests significant potential for pharmacy-based interventions, but improved cultural competence, targeted service design and greater awareness of determinants of equitable healthcare are essential. Future research should focus on optimising culturally responsive pharmacy-led CVD prevention strategies.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024579766.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) encompasses conditions affecting the heart and circulatory system, including coronary heart disease, heart failure, heart valve disease, peripheral artery disease, transient ischaemic attack, stroke and vascular dementia.1 It is a leading global cause of death,2 with around 7.6 million people affected in the UK alone.3 CVD accounts for over a quarter of annual UK deaths, yet up to 80% of premature CVD-related mortality is preventable through risk factor management.46 Risk factors include age, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, ethnicity, family history, male gender, dyslipidaemia and hypertension, as well as modifiable behaviours such as excess alcohol consumption, obesity, inactivity, smoking and poor diet.1 4 5

Despite advances in prevention and treatment, CVD remains a significant public health concern in the UK, disproportionately affecting minority ethnic groups.4 7 South Asians, in particular, show higher CVD prevalence than both the White population and the national average, linked to elevated risk factors for diabetes, heart disease and stroke.4 8 In contrast, people who are Black experience higher stroke rates but lower heart disease risk.4 8 This pattern may be explained by lower cholesterol levels, despite higher rates of other CVD risk factors, such as hypertension, a risk factor for stroke.4 8 9 These disparities are attributed to physiological susceptibility, socioeconomic inequality, cultural and language barriers and limited healthcare access.4 10 Minority ethnic groups report gaps in access and greater exposure to negative healthcare experiences, including insensitivity and racism.4 11 12

Statistical narratives around differential risk are dominated by South Asian and Black ethnicities in the UK because of their relative population size—while smaller minority ethnic groups are often excluded from analyses due to low numbers and are therefore not represented in statistical outputs.13 14

People who are Black and those who are South Asian in England are more likely to live in deprived areas,15 where, notably, more pharmacies are located—a pattern described as the ‘positive pharmacy care law’.16 This contrasts with the ‘inverse care law’ typical of other healthcare providers, where deprived areas see reduced access.6 16 17 The strategic placement of pharmacies enhances access for minority ethnic groups and offers potential to reduce health inequalities.6 18 19

Events in recent years—including the COVID pandemic—have worsened pre-existing healthcare access barriers for minority ethnic groups, negatively impacting health-seeking behaviours,20 with barriers including mistrust—rooted in historical context2123—language barriers and limited health literacy.11 23 Recent NHS backlogs have further affected access to routine care, with ethnic disparities widely noted, though the consequences for public health and prevention receive less attention.20 Minority ethnic groups have seen worsening CVD risk trends, potentially widening health inequalities.1 CVD also imposes an economic burden—costing the NHS an estimated £7.4 billion annually, with a wider £15.8 billion impact from social care costs and reduced productivity.1 Preventative services could alleviate both the health and economic burden; one study estimated potential savings of £68 billion over 25 years through improved CVD detection and management.24

Successive NHS policies have aimed to reduce CVD-related mortality and morbidity, address inequalities and prioritise early detection and prevention—identifying community pharmacies as key service providers in achieving these goals.7 25 Pharmacists regularly offer lifestyle advice, including on diet, exercise and smoking cessation.18 The 2021 introduction of the hypertension case-finding service further elevated their role in CVD prevention, enabling blood pressure checks and referrals for undiagnosed individuals.26 Community pharmacies, as accessible local health hubs, are well positioned to address CVD and related inequalities.6 18 Patients value community pharmacy services, though greater public awareness and further research are needed to engage the most deprived, seldom-heard groups.27

This systematic review aims to identify factors influencing the role of community pharmacy in preventing CVD among minority ethnic groups, including barriers and facilitators from both patient and pharmacy staff perspectives, based on qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods literature. Guided by the UK context, minority ethnic groups are defined as all high-level ethnic groups excluding the majority ‘White’ group, including Asian, Black, Mixed and Other ethnicities.28 This review is significant because minority ethnic groups experience a disproportionate CVD burden yet remain under-represented in preventive interventions.12 As one of the most accessible healthcare settings, community pharmacy can help address this gap; understanding the barriers and facilitators shaping its role can guide service design, workforce development and policy to reduce health inequalities.12 16 29

Methods

This review was conducted according to both the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) guidelines30 and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.31 The protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024579766) and has been published.32

Information sources

Systematic searches of the following databases were conducted from inception to 16th October 2024: CINAHL Ultimate, EMBASE, PubMed, Scopus and Web Of Science. In addition to this, citation tracking was conducted to identify relevant studies missed by database searching alone. This entailed both backward tracking, where the included studies had their reference lists searched, and forward tracking, where studies that had cited the included studies were identified.33 A search of Google Scholar was conducted to identify further studies and grey literature.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were informed by an adapted population, intervention, comparison and outcomes (PICO) framework, excluding ‘Comparison’ to suit the qualitative review question.28 34

Population: The population of interest was minority ethnic groups or community pharmacy professionals. As such, studies exclusively involving healthcare professions other than community pharmacy professionals and studies not involving minority ethnic groups were excluded.

When considering minority ethnic groups in the UK, the review adopted an exclusionary approach by defining minority ethnic groups as all high-level ethnic groups besides the majority ‘White’ ethnic group.35 This includes the Asian, Black, Mixed and Other categories.35

While the primary focus was on the UK, studies from other countries were included when they were considered pertinent to understanding the UK context. Specifically, we included studies involving ethnic groups with an established presence in the UK as minority ethnic diaspora populations, as evidenced by national census data, to ensure cultural relevance to UK CVD prevention.35 Studies involving populations without a documented UK presence were excluded.

Interventions: Pharmacy interventions targeting CVD prevention were included, specifically services addressing risk factors such as lifestyle counselling, medication adherence, alcohol use, cholesterol levels, risk screening, diabetes, hypertension, smoking cessation and weight management.4 5

Comparator: No comparator groups were deemed necessary for inclusion.

Outcomes: Two outcomes were derived from the research question: the potential role for community pharmacy in CVD prevention for minority ethnic groups and the barriers and facilitators to the role of community pharmacy in CVD prevention.

The search identified primary research, in the English language, a restriction applied for feasibility and because publications in this research area are predominantly in English language, with no exclusions based on country of origin, publication date or study design.

Search strategy

Search terms were derived from the eligibility criteria with the assistance of an information specialist (GZ) from Aston University. The search strategy for each database is presented in online supplemental file 1.

Study selection

Identified records were exported to the reference management software EndNote 21, where duplicates were detected and subsequently removed. Initially, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance employing the Rayyan tool,36 followed by full-text screening. Reasons for excluding full-text reports were recorded (figure 1). Included studies underwent independent critical appraisal, using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).37 All screening and quality assessment was conducted independently by reviewers RSD and GKB, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted by RSD using a piloted data extraction form (online supplemental file 2), adapted from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) evidence tables.38 Extracted data was tabulated to provide a summary of the characteristics of the included studies (online supplemental file 3). In addition, data from the results and discussion sections of included studies—containing text relating to roles of pharmacy, barriers and facilitators—were extracted verbatim for thematic analysis. Extracted data were reviewed by GKB, and disputes were resolved with the input of the wider team.

Data synthesis

A data-based convergent synthesis approach was taken; both quantitative and qualitative findings were analysed together using the same synthesis method. Quantitative data were first transformed into qualitative themes before synthesis. Production of narrative statements reflecting the quantitative data from both quantitative and mixed-methods studies then formed the data transformation step. The resultant qualitised data were combined with the qualitative data from mixed-method and qualitative studies in the data synthesis.39 40

Thematic synthesis, as described by Thomas and Harden, using NVivo software, was employed to synthesise the review findings.41 Results and discussion sections of the included studies were coded line-by-line to generate initial ‘free codes’. These were examined for semantic and conceptual similarity and iteratively clustered into descriptive themes using constant comparison across studies. Through further interpretive refinement and mapping relationships between clusters, these descriptive themes were developed into ‘analytical themes’.41

Results

Database searches identified 3284 studies. Citation tracking and grey literature searches yielded a further 1064 studies. Twenty-three studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in data extraction (figure 1).

Study characteristics

Online supplemental file 3 provides a summary of the characteristics of the included studies. Across the 23 included studies,1829 4262 17 used quantitative methods,1842 43 45 47 4951 54 six used qualitative methods29 44 48 52 53 62 and one used mixed methods.46 Nine offered community pharmacy professional perspectives,4345 53 56 57 5962 11 offered minority ethnic patient perspectives1829 42 47 4952 54 55 58 and three captured both.44 46 48

Six studies were conducted in the UK,18 42 46 47 55 57 with the remaining 17 undertaken internationally.294345 48

Across these studies, community pharmacies contributed to CVD prevention through a range of roles, most commonly CVD screening and diabetes management, alongside smoking cessation, weight management, CVD-related health promotion, medication adherence support and dyslipidaemia management (online supplemental file 3).

Quality appraisal

No studies were rated as very low quality (MMAT < 25%). Two studies were rated low quality (25–<50%), six were moderate (50–<75%), seven were considerable (75–<100%) and eight were rated high quality (100%) (online supplemental file 4).

Results of thematic analysis

Five key themes emerged from the analysis and are presented within the thematic framework illustrated in figure 2.

Thematic analysis map.

Theme 1: trust in healthcare professionals

Four studies highlighted patient trust and relationships with HCPs.29 48 52 54 Distrust in pharmacists’ capabilities beyond dispensing was reported, particularly regarding diabetes management during Ramadan, further exacerbated by pharmacists’ lack of engagement and insufficient medication counselling.29 52

“…they did not trust pharmacists capability in dealing with these issues during Ramadan…dealing with the pharmacist was merely transactional.”52

In addition, cultural barriers influenced patient-pharmacist relationships, with English-speaking patients more likely to trust and follow pharmacists’ recommendations, while Arabic speakers were more likely to disagree with pharmacists about medications.54 Conversely, pharmacist relationships with South Asian patients were improved when pharmacists spoke the same language and understood the patients’ cultural values.

“…found a sense of belonging in the pharmacies where they often had the ability to communicate in the same language and by having their customs known.”48

South Asian patients valued pharmacists located near their communities, who provided frequent, supportive and non-judgemental care. Pharmacists reported that these relationships improved cardiac medication adherence and raised disease awareness.

“…patients were also drawn to pharmacists that would answer their questions and remain non-judgmental regarding a lack of previous non-adherence.”48

Theme 2: systemic barriers

Australian pharmacists highlighted time limitations as an organisational challenge, particularly hindering their capacity to provide tailored medication management for fasting Muslim patients with diabetes.

“…barriers…that prevented pharmacists from helping patients to take their medications and fast safely. Organizational barriers…included time shortage…”53

UK pharmacists reported rarely offering culturally tailored services that address patients’ religious and cultural needs.

“…special needs of patients with diabetes from different religions and cultures were rarely considered.”57

Community pharmacy services showed potential in promoting lifestyle changes like exercise and weight loss.51 Pharmacies also provided benefits such as extended hours, accessibility and informal support through open-door policies.46 55

“Perceived needs for a pharmacy open on Saturdays, Sundays or in the evenings were expressed more by respondents in work and of non-white ethnicity.”55

Theme 3: culturally tailored language

Language barriers impacted pharmacist-patient relationships, with patients preferring advice in their native language and some requiring interpretation. Cultural competence was equally important, prompting calls for education on practices like Ramadan. Effective communication—via translated materials, community seminars and personalised counselling—was seen as key to bridging gaps, with higher-educated patients being more proactive in seeking advice.

Subtheme: language and communication

Five studies29 44 48 49 53 found language influenced pharmacist-patient interactions. Patients reported a preference for pharmacists who could communicate in their native language.48 49

“…sense of belonging in the pharmacies where they often had the ability to communicate in the same language and by having their customs known.”48

Despite a preference for health information in their native language, patients were unsure of where to access such resources.

“…but I’m not quite sure where to access them (information written in other languages).”29

In contrast, some patients with limited literacy required additional support from family members to understand written health information, even when written in their native language, emphasising the need for tailored communication.

“…some Punjabi-speaking patients had difficulty reading in Punjabi and needed assistance (usually from family members).”48

Language barriers were more common in pharmacies than other healthcare settings.

“…patients’ language barriers posed more obvious challenges in the pharmacies…likely because patients who could not speak English were always accompanied to physician appointments or were treated by a physician who spoke their language.”48

Qatari pharmacists reported that Asian patients with limited English or Arabic comprehension faced language barriers that restricted access to smoking-cessation services.44 Similar patterns in other ethnically diverse settings showed that language was a major barrier to access, and communicating in a patient’s own language improved trust and service delivery.44 53 “…if we can speak the language it makes it easier as a technique to communicate with them, it gives them a bit more trust”53

Subtheme: health literacy and cultural competence

Australian pharmacists highlighted the need for cultural competency training and resources to help prevent CVD in minority groups, especially during culturally significant events like Ramadan, by addressing fasting-related health risks.

“…Training probably is needed. I’m not saying for other, non-Muslim pharmacists, but us as well, Muslim pharmacists…”

“…I think it’s a good idea [Pharmacist training], because then the pharmacists are obviously more equipped to help their patients, and patients are less likely to be confused…”53

Some pharmacists proposed integrating cultural competency training into pharmacy curricula and professional development, collaborating with religious scholars, and creating multilingual, tailored diabetes education for Ramadan to meet diverse patient needs.53 Similarly, patients requested guidance on managing diabetes during Ramadan to minimise risks, underscoring the need for culturally tailored advice.

“…need for more education provided to them and to other patients with diabetes who fast during Ramadan, to optimise diabetes control and minimise adverse health outcomes.”52

Additionally, patients cited the need for culturally tailored support in their native languages.29 52

“Participants were also appreciative of any cultural support initiatives, although these were not widely available. Translated diabetes education sessions could improve understanding of medication use, available information and lifestyle interventions.”29

Studies found ethnic minorities often preferred specific information formats—like radio, television campaigns, community seminars and multilingual materials—some of which occasionally conflicted with medical advice.29 48 52 55

In addition, minority ethnic patients struggled to interpret health information, with some valuing pharmacist support for clarity.

“It would be good to talk to other people and the pharmacist about the problems I have understanding my readings.”29

The studies found minority ethnic patients were less likely to seek pharmacist advice, but those with higher educational attainment showed greater health awareness and a higher likelihood of consulting pharmacists, highlighting the role of literacy in accessing health information.29 54

“Those graduating from high school were almost twice as likely as those who did not graduate to consider consulting a pharmacist when they had issues with health or medications.”54

Theme 4: cultural and religious influences

Eleven studies highlighted the significant cultural and religious influences on community pharmacy’s role in preventing CVD among minority ethnic groups, as patients’ beliefs and practices shaped health behaviours, medication adherence and engagement with pharmacists, with challenges like Ramadan fasting, cultural norms, social stigma and acculturation further complicating care.2944 48 49 5257 62

Subtheme: religious practices

Religious practices like Ramadan fasting may influence pharmacy’s role in diabetes management and, consequently, in CVD prevention.44 52 53 Some patients prioritised fasting over health, continuing despite diabetes complications and poor medication adherence.

“My sugar levels appear…very high and I fast…fasting is excellent.”52

In contrast, some patients acknowledged religious exemptions for chronic health conditions, demonstrating the complexity in the influence of culture and religion on CVD risk factors.52 Fear of receiving advice contrary to religious commitments also led to avoidance of HCPs, with patients instead opting for self-management, at times to the detriment of their health.52 Limited time due to religious commitments was a further barrier.

“In Ramadan I cannot go…to the pharmacist I don’t have time…I stay home, pray and worship.”52

Patients preferred discussing fasting-related health issues with pharmacists of the same faith, trusting them to offer culturally sensitive and informed advice.

“…non-Muslim pharmacist, do not advise your friends to listen to his/her advice because they do not know Ramadan.”52

Patients’ religious beliefs also influenced their likelihood of adopting lifestyle changes proposed by pharmacists to reduce CVD risk, such as quitting smoking.

“Patients’ religious beliefs…some patients believed that God gave them the power to quit were considered facilitators for quitting by patients…their religious activities, like performing Umrah (lesser Hajj)…motivated them to stop smoking.”44

Australian pharmacists highlighted various nuances surrounding religious beliefs, including intra-faith cultural diversity because of migration, varying observance levels, and the role of guidance from religious authorities, all of which influenced the degree to which tailored diabetes advice could be provided by the co-religious community pharmacists.53 Some pharmacists feared giving spiritually insensitive advice, feeling that such advice would be better coming from religious leaders.53

Subtheme: cultural norms

Cultural contexts surrounding weight and diet were discussed in several studies.29 52 62

Malaysian pharmacists reported the challenges of initiating discussions around weight management as it can be perceived as insensitive in the Malaysian culture.

“…in a lot of culture[s] weight is a sensitive issue, so you don’t suggest this kind of thing unless they ask you.”62

Patients reported difficulty in following dietary advice from HCPs due to cultural dietary habits.

“…we prepare many types of food…usually fatty as it is our culture.”52

Cultural norms around hospitality added to difficulties in following dietary advice.29 52

“I eat anything that is offered, to show my respect.”29

Patients avoided discussing cultural diets with HCPs over concerns of cultural insensitivity.

“I don’t tell my doctor about everything that I eat, because I don’t think they’d understand…our traditional diet.”29

Intersectionality, such as with gender, was reported.44 55 In Qatar, cultural norms and social stigma deter female patients from seeking smoking cessation.

“Cultural challenges…female patients…concerned about the social stigma associated with smoking…many female patients were very shy to approach the pharmacists to speak about smoking cessation freely.”44

In one study, minority ethnic patients reported a preference for same-sex pharmacists.

“non-white respondents were slightly more likely to prefer a pharmacy where the pharmacist is the same sex as them”55

Studies reported cultural influences on medication nonadherence, including language barriers, dosing misinterpretation and reliance on complementary medicine.29 48

“Patients’ medication adherence was challenged when they had language barriers or used complementary and alternative medicines…”48

Subtheme: impact of acculturation

Acculturation—the process of adapting to new cultural environments—was seen to have an impact. Adhering to cardiovascular medication is challenging for first-generation minority ethnic migrants due to international variations in pharmacy practices.

“Patients identified that the medications and the process of dispensing them were different in their home countries and this could negatively affect their ability to remain adherent.”48

Similarly, the stress of living abroad could foster health-damaging behaviours like smoking.

“…the main reason, the pressure…we live for a long period outside our home country…”44

Theme 5: family and social networks

Four studies highlighted the role of family and friends in health management.29 44 48 53 Pharmacists noted that in the Muslim communities of Australia and Qatar, friends and relatives support blood glucose management, particularly during religious fasting.44 53

“…most of Muslims here, their family surrounds them…they can see if they’re low on sugar or high so they will help them straight away”53

Younger family members may encounter fewer barriers than older generations, as they may be more acculturated to the broader society beyond their ethnic community.48 Family members often accompany patients to appointments, act as translators and contribute to medication adherence, healthcare access and culturally contextualising HCPs’ advice.48

“…doctor appoints are, my daughter always is with me…she talks to the doctors, (and asks) what are the pros and cons”.48

“My daughter takes me to the doctors, and makes sure I understand them.”29

Family involvement included relatives abroad, whose attitudes occasionally hindered medication adherence.

“…never here but it used to cross my mind in India…sometimes I feel that I should stop…people there tell us ‘You stop, you stop.’”48

Social networks influenced self-management and supported adherence to dietary recommendations.

“A lot of my friends are diabetics, so we respect each other’s meal choices when we eat out together.”29

Conversely, patients described instances of HCP avoidance because of peer influence.

“I don’t go to that doctor anymore, because I heard the medication they gave my friend stopped his kidneys working.”29

Discussion

This systematic review is the first to explore community pharmacy’s role in CVD prevention among minority ethnic groups. Despite limited UK-specific research, analysis of 23 studies—some drawn from comparable international contexts—identified five themes, highlighting the potential of pharmacists in CVD prevention in minority ethnic groups. Pharmacy-led interventions can effectively identify at-risk individuals and improve access to cardiovascular care, especially for underserved populations.18 47

Community pharmacy in the UK has contributed to CVD prevention and continues to do so, notably through the NHS hypertension case-finding service.26 The NHS aims to reduce ethnic disparities in cardiovascular health, recognising pharmacy’s role in achieving this.7 Research highlights the need for equitable targeting policies and culturally tailored health messaging, co-produced and communicated through trusted networks to improve accessibility.23 63 64 Holistic, culturally sensitive approaches have been shown to improve health outcomes by addressing group-specific differences and barriers.4 10 11 65 Existing services can be adapted to meet the needs of minority ethnic groups, rather than creating entirely new ones.66

Several barriers and facilitators were identified in relation to minority ethnic groups. Cultural and religious influences shape health behaviours, affecting the acceptability of health interventions among minority ethnic groups.2944 48 49 5257 62 While recognised in CVD prevention literature,10 66 this is less explored in the context of UK community pharmacy. Given the accessibility of pharmacies—particularly in areas of high deprivation, which are often home to larger populations of minority ethnic groups15—and their role as a frequent first point of contact for healthcare advice, community pharmacists must be equipped to understand and address diverse cultural needs.67 Additionally, understanding medical information is crucial for effective CVD prevention, and community pharmacists could play a greater role in educating patients, particularly those with lower health literacy.11

Language, a key aspect of cultural identity, is a barrier to pharmacy services.29 44 48 49 53 It hampers effective healthcare delivery and impacts trust in HCPs.53 Some patients preferred receiving medical information in their native language, both verbal and written.48 49 While migrants saw learning the host country’s language as essential,29 English literacy levels varied widely among first-generation migrants, with fewer barriers for later generations.48 These disparities lead to worse health outcomes for minority ethnic groups in the UK.11 Pharmacists also face challenges in addressing these issues, as some patients prefer those sharing their cultural, linguistic, or religious background.48 52

Knowledge gaps among pharmacists and patients underscore the need for cultural competency training and multilingual health information in diverse formats to enhance engagement.2948 5254 Studies frequently emphasised the need for additional pharmacist training and accessible patient resources.29 52 53 Integrating cultural competency into pharmacy education, tailored to national demographics, has been proposed to address health inequalities.10 53 67 Broader healthcare research has similarly highlighted gaps in cultural competence, with GPs reporting limited confidence in discussing health beliefs with minority ethnic patients.10 The need to embed cultural competency training within the education and training of healthcare students is widely acknowledged in the contemporary literature, with similar findings reported across other healthcare professions—including dentistry, medicine and nursing.67

Trust is crucial in pharmacist-patient relationships, with minority ethnic patients more likely to trust pharmacists who understand their culture and language.29 48 52 54 This trust is influenced by communication quality, cultural understanding and language familiarity, making patients feel valued and comfortable.68 A review found that over 65% of non-native speakers faced healthcare barriers, with 20% avoiding services due to fear of miscommunication and distrust.61 69 Research highlights the need for culturally competent approaches that acknowledge social, cultural and religious identities to build trust among South Asian minority ethnic groups.10 Reports indicate that prejudiced treatment in primary care settings has led to lower adherence to healthcare advice among these patients.10 Wider healthcare research has also highlighted the fundamental role of trust in HCP-patient relationships, particularly in determining medication adherence and subsequent health outcomes.70 This may be particularly important for minority ethnic groups, as trust is underpinned by vulnerability and uncertainty—factors that may be more pronounced among minority ethnic groups due to experiences of discrimination, including within healthcare settings.10 70

Systemic factors, such as time constraints and limited resources, hinder pharmacists from providing tailored services which are more resource intensive, especially during culturally significant periods like Ramadan.43 45 53 57 59 International literature emphasises the need for healthcare systems, policymakers and governments to prioritise tailored services for minority groups, addressing the current gap in targeted care.66 The included studies offered limited insight into systemic barriers, which were rarely examined in relation to minority ethnic groups. This gap restricts understanding of how structural factors influence the delivery and uptake of pharmacy services.

Family and social networks play a significant role in health management, supporting healthcare access—helping to overcome barriers with language, health literacy and navigating unfamiliar healthcare systems. In turn, this supports adherence and promotes greater self-management.29 48 67 However, shared misconceptions and cultural attitudes can hinder positive health behaviours.29 48 Social networks shape health-related attitudes, with ethnic enclaves sometimes reinforcing negative behaviours and limiting exposure to healthier choices and resources.71 These findings align with wider research by highlighting the value of leveraging social support to enhance engagement and counter shared misconceptions.12 By widening exposure to culturally adapted resources, we can strengthen culturally relevant health literacy within minority ethnic groups, reducing the reinforcing effects of social networks while preserving the social support that underpins access and adherence.12

The findings emphasise the need for further research into the contributory factors and nuances specific to minority ethnic groups in the UK, to support the need identified in the contemporary literature for culturally sensitive, pharmacy-based interventions to enhance CVD prevention efforts in minority ethnic groups.4 11 66

Strengths and limitations

Despite identifying 23 eligible studies, evidence directly addressing the research question remains limited. The synthesis underscores the scarcity of published work on community pharmacy’s role in preventing CVD among minority ethnic groups.

The included studies employed diverse methodologies, with many relying on cross-sectional designs that offered limited insights and faced quality issues like low response rates.43 45 47 55 57 58 60 Evidence on HCP perspectives regarding community pharmacy’s role in CVD prevention for minority ethnic groups was scarce, highlighting a need for further research.

Evidence on pharmacy-related barriers and facilitators for specific minority ethnic groups is limited, hindering efforts to address health disparities. Minority ethnic groups in the UK include Asian, Asian British, Asian Welsh, Black, Black British, Black Welsh, Caribbean or African, Mixed or Multiple and Other, each further divided into numerous distinct subgroups.35 Available studies mainly focused on South Asian and Arab ethnicities, offering minimal insight into group-specific nuances.

Limiting study eligibility to English may have excluded relevant non-English research. Although the research question is UK-focused, including international studies with varying definitions of ethnic groups was necessary to gain valuable insights, despite potential inconsistencies. International data, often reflective of first-generation and newly arrived migrants, offers valuable insights but must be interpreted cautiously to avoid generalising culturally specific findings to broader minority populations.10 71

The review applied broad inclusion criteria to capture relevant evidence. While many studies involved populations classified as minority ethnic groups in the UK, they primarily examined general populations rather than focusing specifically on UK minority ethnic communities. This limited the depth of analysis and specific insights into the unique healthcare needs of minority ethnic groups.

Additionally, some included studies were conducted in countries where the community pharmacy context differs markedly from the UK. These contextual differences were considered during interpretation, and findings from such settings were treated cautiously, with emphasis placed on transferable cultural or behavioural insights rather than system-specific practices.

Deviations from protocol

The review deviated from the protocol in not searching OpenGrey. The platform has been discontinued, reducing its value for identifying grey literature. Given the inclusion of Google Scholar, this deviation is unlikely to have substantially influenced the results.

Implications for future research

Tailoring preventative services to minority ethnic groups is increasingly emphasised,10 66 especially in the UK, where these populations face higher CVD risk.4 7 Community pharmacies, as accessible healthcare settings, offer potential to address such inequalities.6 18 Further research is needed to understand minority ethnic groups’ perspectives on facilitators and barriers to more targeted pharmacy services.66

The limited evidence on community pharmacy’s role in CVD prevention among UK minority ethnic groups points to a clear need for further qualitative research exploring the perspectives of these communities and healthcare professionals. In particular, future work should examine systemic factors largely absent from current evidence to understand how these may shape access and engagement for minority ethnic groups.

Conclusion

The scarcity of UK-based research on the role of community pharmacy in CVD prevention for minority ethnic groups underscores the need for further investigation. International studies indicate that community pharmacies may have a role in reducing CVD inequalities, but the evidence remains limited. Targeted interventions, cultural competence training and increased awareness are likely to be important for optimising impact in the UK context. Future research should explore strategies to enhance community pharmacy-based CVD prevention efforts in minority ethnic communities.
