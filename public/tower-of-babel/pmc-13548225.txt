Enhancing the cognitive, psychological, and social outcomes of adults undergoing surgery for focal drug-resistant epilepsy: protocol for a scoping review
Authors: Evelyn E Harvey, Honor Coleman, Deepa Bapat, Gloria Brigiari, Francesco Brigo, Sarah Brown, Sean M D Carnegie, Dario Englot, Michael W K Fong, Heather M Francis, Isabella Hogan, Nathalie Jetté, Andres Kanner, Aileen McGonigal, Bryce P Mulligan, Didier Vincent Oxenham, Piero Perucca, Sarah J Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAchieving seizure control remains a primary objective of epilepsy surgery but other important patient-centred outcomes should not be overlooked. Despite the need for more holistic approaches supporting well-being over and above seizure control, scant empirical research has identified effective interventions aimed at improving adults’ cognitive, psychological, and/or social outcomes following epilepsy surgery. This novel scoping review will be the first to map the evidence in this landscape.Methods and analysisThe review will be conducted with reference to the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. Databases searched will include MEDLINE, Embase, and CINAHL. Interventions of interest may be delivered preoperatively and/or postoperatively, and primary outcomes will be specific to cognitive, psychological, and social functioning after either resective or ablative epilepsy surgery conducted in adulthood. No limitations will be imposed on publication date, language, or country/region of origin. Selection will be based on title and abstract examination followed by full-text review. Data will be charted in duplicate and the results of descriptive and qualitative analyses will be presented using tabular and graphical formats, along with a narrative summary of the findings.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required for this review. The final review will be submitted for peer review in a medical journal and considered for dissemination via the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE). This review is intended as a first step towards informing the development of ILAE consensus-based recommendations on enhancing cognitive, psychological, and social outcomes after epilepsy surgery in adulthood.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Surgery for seizure control has a long history and is established as a safe and effective treatment for selected individuals with focal drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE).1–3 While seizure freedom is the single strongest determinant of improved health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and patient satisfaction with surgical treatment in adulthood,4 it guarantees neither of these results.5 6 Outcomes relating to social and emotional functioning are among the most frequently cited concerns by adults living with epilepsy7 and it has long been recognised that for many individuals ‘successful’ epilepsy surgery involves a great deal more than stopping seizures.8–11 Additional supports are often required for patients to capitalise on a favourable seizure outcome or, in contrast, adjust to an unfavourable or unexpected seizure outcome following surgery. Non-seizure outcomes, particularly those relating to cognition (eg, language and memory function), psychological adjustment and well-being (eg, mood, anxiety), and social functioning (eg, education, employment), are also very important to patients and play a critical role in their long-term HRQOL and perceptions of surgical success.9 12–14 Despite this, there is surprisingly limited empirical evidence, and no formal consensus, regarding the best methods for enhancing these outcomes following surgery for DRE.

Epilepsy surgery is a major event for many individuals with DRE and can be a critical turning point in their life trajectories.15 In adulthood, psychological and social expectations for surgery, such as increased independence, improved mental health, driving, employment, and opportunities to partner and have children, all frequently hinge on seizure freedom or a meaningful reduction in disabling seizures, yet the desire for seizure freedom must be weighed against potential risks to cognition and other neurological functions (themselves important contributors to psychological and functional status), as well as the inherent risk that seizures may recur in the long term. In such a context, it is not surprising that some adults experience a lengthy period of upheaval and psychological disequilibrium spanning several years after epilepsy surgery.16 Even for those patients who do achieve seizure freedom, social milestones are often achieved much later in the postsurgical timeline than might be expected; for example, vocational and family goals may not be achieved for a decade or more.17 Several factors, not least of all age at seizure onset and duration of epilepsy,18–20 might contribute to this variability—all highlighting the need for a person-centred approach to enhancing outcomes following epilepsy surgery.

Epilepsy aetiologies are varied and seizures can develop at different points across the lifespan. Outcome priorities necessarily differ between children and adults, with major implications for surgical management21; strategies effective for adults are not necessarily suitable for children and so a separate approach, specific to the paediatric context, is merited to enhance non-seizure outcomes after epilepsy surgery in childhood.

Among adults, the ‘burden of normality’ describes a complex phenomenon experienced by certain individuals as they adjust to living without seizures following epilepsy surgery.22 This seemingly paradoxical phenomenon highlights the psychological, affective, behavioural, and social challenges experienced by some people as they transition from a state of chronic ‘illness’ to one of ‘wellness’ after surgery—challenges that can present major barriers to them meeting expectations and achieving their postoperative goals.10 23 24 Conversely, a substantial minority of adults with DRE do not achieve complete relief from seizures following surgery.25 Some must inevitably deal with the challenge and disappointment of a suboptimal seizure outcome,26 27 including the prospect of further surgery or alternative interventions (eg, neuromodulation, drug trials). Other individuals—whether seizure free or not—must contend with acquired neurological deficits, including the cognitive costs of epilepsy surgery,28 and many must inevitably cope with the ongoing challenge of pre-existing dysfunction. This array of outcome scenarios highlights the importance of anticipating and optimising surgical patients’ cognitive, psychological, and social outcomes both in parallel with the primary goal of seizure control and with respect to their seizure status following surgery.

Along with surgery to stop or reduce seizures, targeted interventions aimed at adults’ cognitive, psychological, and/or social outcomes may have the potential to enhance their postoperative HRQOL and reported satisfaction with epilepsy surgery. Literature reviews29–33 and commentary articles34–38 have long flagged the need to better address these other outcomes by taking a more holistic approach to the preoperative and postoperative care of adults undergoing epilepsy surgery. A renewed call has been made to incorporate relevant aspects of the surgical outcome literature and develop a holistic and person-centred clinical framework for their care and rehabilitation.39 The push for more holistic care is also manifest in the increasing availability and use of epilepsy-specific patient-reported outcome measures,40 as reflected in the recent International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement consensus recommendations41 and the Multidimensional Outcome Reporting in Epilepsy framework,42 which aims to expand on existing treatment outcome measures that focus solely on seizure status (ie, the Engel43 and International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE)44 criteria) by including patients’ HRQOL and subjective experience in an overall, more comprehensive outcome metric.

With these considerations in mind, there is substantial scope to better integrate current approaches to epilepsy surgery with additional cognitive, psychological, and social interventions for enhancing long-term outcomes—particularly for individuals at risk of cognitive deficits, de novo or worsened psychiatric dysfunction, and/or difficulties with psychological and social adjustment after epilepsy surgery, irrespective of their seizure outcome.10 30 45 Patients often hold expectations for improvements in psychosocial functioning in the early months and years following surgery,12 46 and several social milestones (particularly driving and initiating/resuming work or study) will likely occur in this timeframe.17 47 Notably, a position paper by the Neuropsychology Task Force of the ILAE suggests that meaningful changes in HRQOL may take up to 5 years to emerge,48 which aligns with the timeframe of patient-reported postoperative adjustment difficulties commonly associated with the burden of normality.15 It follows, then, that interventions for enhancing outcomes might best be delivered preoperatively and/or early in the postoperative course to maximise the chance of positive long-term outcomes. It is the authors’ collective experience that following patients and delivering interventions, where indicated, in the 2 years after surgery is likely to be feasible for most specialised epilepsy surgical centres.

As with techniques commonly used in other neurological populations (eg, stroke49 and traumatic brain injury50), such interventions might take the form of rehabilitation—that is, postoperative programmes aimed at helping individuals compensate for new cognitive or other neurological deficits, develop or recover their social and occupational functions, and enhance their mood and well-being after epilepsy surgery. Increasingly, there is also recognition of the benefits of prehabilitation—for example, pre-emptive neuropsychological interventions delivered before surgery to prepare patients through psychoeducation, expectation management, and the early introduction of customised cognitive compensatory strategies in order to help maximise the success of rehabilitation processes delivered after surgery.51

To date, most of the work on enhancing outcomes in addition to seizure control has been done in the context of craniotomies and resective procedures, but newer and less invasive ablative techniques like stereotactic radiosurgery, radiofrequency thermocoagulation, and magnetic resonance-guided laser interstitial thermal therapy are on the rise, especially in high-resource countries,52 53 and can offer reasonable odds of seizure freedom, with lower morbidity, for carefully selected patients.54 55 When neither resection nor ablation is an option, neuromodulation with vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), deep brain stimulation (DBS), or responsive neurostimulation (RNS) is increasingly used, but seizure freedom is less likely and the aim is largely seizure alleviation rather than elimination.56 57 This review is aimed at understanding what methods of prehabilitation and rehabilitation are necessary for enhancing outcomes in addition to seizure control in the context of adult epilepsy surgery with a primary focus on seizure remission, so all such resective and ablative techniques will be considered whereas neuromodulation and disconnection procedures (eg, corpus callosotomy) will not. Furthermore, in acknowledgement of the known socio-economic, regional, racial, ethnic, and linguistic disparities in access to specialised epilepsy care,58–60 it is important to identify any such methods that might be feasible for enhancing surgical patients’ non-seizure outcomes regardless of country, world region, and/or treatment resources available. The setting of this review is accordingly wide-ranging.

The current context now warrants a formal review of existing methods—whether in the form of prehabilitation or rehabilitation—for enhancing focal DRE patients’ outcomes relating to their cognitive, psychological, and/or social functioning after epilepsy surgery in adulthood. Preliminary examination of the literature suggests the evidence base is small and heterogeneous, inclusive of multiple intervention types, diverse outcome domains, variable endpoints and measurement approaches, and a wide range of study designs. In expectation that a systematic search is likely to yield only a small number of relevant empirical studies, a scoping review is a necessary first step in establishing the range of interventions that have been attempted, how outcomes have been defined and measured, and where evidence clusters or gaps exist.61 62

Objectives

The overarching aim of this scoping review is to identify and characterise the evidence for prehabilitation and/or rehabilitation methods that have been designed to support adults undergoing resective or ablative epilepsy surgery and enhance their cognitive, psychological, and/or social outcomes as an adjunct to seizure control. In this context, ‘prehabilitation’ and ‘rehabilitation’ methods are defined as any clinical interventions, therapies, or practices provided in addition to routine neurological care (delivered before and after surgery, respectively) with the aim of enhancing patients’ outcomes beyond seizure control. The scope of the review is deliberately broad to ensure successful capture of the empirical intervention studies that have been published to date, which are likely to be few and disparate. Specifically, we seek to answer the following research questions:

In adults with focal DRE, what prehabilitation methods have been tested to improve their cognitive, psychological, and/or social outcomes following epilepsy surgery?

In adults with focal DRE, what prehabilitation methods have been tested to improve their cognitive, psychological, and/or social outcomes following epilepsy surgery?

Similarly, in adults with focal DRE, what rehabilitation methods have been tested to improve their cognitive, psychological, and/or social outcomes following epilepsy surgery?

Similarly, in adults with focal DRE, what rehabilitation methods have been tested to improve their cognitive, psychological, and/or social outcomes following epilepsy surgery?

What evidence is available regarding the effectiveness of prehabilitation and rehabilitation for improving these outcomes following epilepsy surgery?

Methods

Protocol development

This is an entirely novel protocol and does not update any prior review on this topic. After consideration of the research questions to be addressed, and some initial exploration of the available literature, a scoping review was identified as the most appropriate approach.62 63 The protocol has been developed a priori, in keeping with best practice for scoping reviews,64 and was created collaboratively by the research team with methodological input from medical librarians and members of the ILAE Standards and Best Practice Council.

Searches in MEDLINE, the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) and the Open Science Framework were performed on 8 July 2026 and no existing or ongoing systematic or scoping reviews on this topic were identified.

The review process will be performed with reference to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews65 and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.66

The review protocol has been registered with the Open Science Framework and is accessible here: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/ZB7XY.

Outcomes

There are three primary outcomes of interest:

Cognition.

Psychological well-being.

Patient-reported HRQOL and satisfaction with surgery are multifaceted constructs that frequently reflect an interplay between seizure status and aspects of the three primary outcomes listed above; for this reason, they will be considered as secondary outcomes for the purpose of this review and reported separately to endpoints that pertain specifically to postsurgical cognition, psychological well-being, or social functioning.

All forms of endpoints will be considered, not only validated instruments (eg, mood scales), and may include both patient-reported and carer-reported outcome or experience measures. The details of the endpoints identified will be described, whether compared individually (ie, pre-post) or between groups. For cognitive outcomes, anticipated endpoints might include neuropsychological scores (eg, change in measures of memory, language, and executive functions), self-reported use of cognitive compensatory strategies, or performance on functional occupational therapy measures. The endpoints for psychological outcomes might include differences in symptoms of depression and anxiety, prevalence of psychosis and other mental health conditions, and/or other markers of postoperative adjustment (eg, reduced reliance on carers, fewer features of the burden of normality). The endpoints for social outcomes might be reported with respect to practical or functional attainments (eg, driving, employment, further education, ability to travel) and/or more personal milestones (eg, new relationships or formal partnerships and first or subsequent children).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Within the primary research literature, studies will be considered for inclusion in the review provided they:

report on prehabilitation and/or rehabilitation delivered to adults with focal DRE undergoing resective or ablative surgery aimed at seizure control;

report on prehabilitation and/or rehabilitation delivered to adults with focal DRE undergoing resective or ablative surgery aimed at seizure control;

describe a specific intervention, therapy, or practice aimed at enhancing one or more of the three primary outcomes of interest;

describe a specific intervention, therapy, or practice aimed at enhancing one or more of the three primary outcomes of interest;

report on processes conducted not more than 12 months prior to surgery (for prehabilitation) and no later than 24 months after surgery (for rehabilitation).

report on processes conducted not more than 12 months prior to surgery (for prehabilitation) and no later than 24 months after surgery (for rehabilitation).

Various study designs will be included in this review. Experimental and quasi-experimental studies might potentially include randomised controlled trials, non-randomised controlled trials, before and after studies, and interrupted time-series designs. Analytical observational studies, including prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, and analytical cross-sectional studies will also be eligible for inclusion. This review will also consider descriptive observational study designs including case series and descriptive cross-sectional studies. Quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-method designs may all be included.

There will be no limits applied to sample size, language, world region, or resource level of the health settings described in the studies considered for inclusion.

Sources and search strategy

The search strategy and terms have been designed collaboratively by the authorship team and have been developed with methodological input from medical librarians and members of the ILAE Standards and Best Practice Council (online supplemental appendix A). The search terms have been subjected to review with explicit reference to the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies checklist.67 The databases searched will include MEDLINE, Embase and CINAHL. The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, will be adapted as necessary for each database. There will be no search of the grey literature. Covidence68 will be used for citation management. The reference list of all included papers will be screened for additional studies. Where necessary, Google Translate will be used to screen abstracts published in languages other than English and, if relevant, full texts will be considered for inclusion with input (where possible) from multilingual members of the research team.

Study selection and screening

The eligibility criteria will be used for screening of the search output. Following the search, all identified citations will be collated and uploaded into Covidence and duplicates removed. Following a pilot test of the screening process,62 titles and abstracts will be screened by two independent reviewers (EEH and HC) for assessment against the inclusion criteria of this review. Potentially relevant sources will be retrieved in full and then assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by the same two independent reviewers. If multiple reports from the same cohort are identified, then a hierarchy for inclusion will be employed (eg, only primary analyses from the same cohort will be included). Reasons for exclusion of any studies at full text that do not meet the inclusion criteria will be recorded and reported in the final scoping review. Any disagreements that arise between the reviewers at each stage of the selection process will be resolved with input from a third reviewer (DVO) to reach consensus. Cohen’s kappa (κ) will be calculated and reported to indicate inter-rater reliability.

Data extraction

A draft extraction form is provided (online supplemental appendix B). The form has been developed with input from several members of the research team. Consistent with scoping review methodology, the form will be pilot tested using two to three articles and may be iteratively modified during the data charting process to ensure comprehensive and accurate capture of relevant information. All modifications will be documented and applied retrospectively to previously extracted studies, where applicable, to maintain consistency. Data will be extracted from papers included in the scoping review by two independent reviewers (EEH and HC). These data will include specific details from the sources regarding the PICO framework for this review, along with study methods, key findings, and/or any recommendations relevant to the objective of the review. Any differences that arise between the two primary reviewers during the data extraction process will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (DVO).

Data charting and presentation

The results of the search and the study inclusion process will be reported in full in the final scoping review and presented in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.66 Analyses of any interventions identified by the literature search will depend on the types of data (eg, qualitative or quantitative) collected by the studies included in the review. No statistical synthesis will be conducted but all sources selected for inclusion will be considered for thematic narrative synthesis. The data will be presented in tabular and graphical forms, charting variables such as study design, sample size, patient characteristics, surgery type, comparator status, and outcome measurement tools (as per the data extraction process outlined above) to allow for some appraisal of the evidence. This will be accompanied by a narrative summary describing how the results relate to each objective of the review, with reference to the strength of evidence for intervention types and methodological limitations at the field level. Clinical implications and priorities for future research will be considered.

Data storage

The review will generate secondary data including screening logs, PRISMA flow information, data extraction tables, and charted descriptive summaries derived from the included evidence. On completion of the review, these materials will be deposited in the Macquarie University Research Data Repository and made openly accessible via a Digital Object Identifier.

Patient and public involvement

Individuals with lived experience of epilepsy surgery, along with representatives from epilepsy advocacy groups including the International Bureau for Epilepsy and Empowering Epilepsy, have been integral to the project and the design of this protocol from the outset (see working group members named in the ‘Acknowledgements’ section). Lived experience input has been crucial in exploring the context for, and developing the objectives of, this scoping review.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval is not required for this review. The final manuscript will be submitted for peer review in a medical journal and considered for dissemination via the website for the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), www.ilae.org. This scoping review is intended as a first step towards informing the development of ILAE consensus-based recommendations for enhancing cognitive, psychological, and social outcomes after epilepsy surgery.

Conclusion

This scoping review will provide a much-needed synthesis of the evidence for healthcare methods that have been described in the epilepsy literature for enhancing the postoperative outcomes of adults undergoing surgery for focal DRE, in addition to control of their seizures. Anticipating a relative dearth of empirical studies, this review has strong potential to identify important knowledge or feasibility gaps that might help guide the direction of future original research and will provide a foundation for the development of a novel set of ILAE consensus-based recommendations aimed at enhancing the cognitive, psychological, and social outcomes of adults following epilepsy surgery.
