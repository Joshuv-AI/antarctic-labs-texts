Rapid feed component assessment to enhance livestock productivity and reduce emissions
Authors: Ifeanyi Vincent Nwaneri, Juntae Kim, Tusan Park, Chukwuma Ume, Byoung-Kwan Cho, Daniel Dooyum Uyeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rising global demand for livestock products necessitates innovations in feed management to enhance efficiency and sustainability. Variability in livestock feed nutrient composition highlights the need for real-time monitoring to ensure animals receive proper nutrition. In this study, we first used Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to measure differences in moisture content (MC), crude protein (CP), and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) across eight feed types from seven factories in the Gyeongsang region, Republic of Korea. Then, we developed a non-destructive system to measure nutrient components using a Short-Wave Infrared (SWIR) Hyperspectral camera and Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR). The PLSR models, each optimized with a specific preprocessing method, showed strong predictive accuracy: maximum normalization was used for MC (R2p = 0.84, RMSEP = 0.49%, 23 LVs); mean normalization for CP (R2p = 0.86, RMSEP = 1.81%, 7 LVs); and a Savitzky-Golay first derivative for NDF (R2p = 0.79, RMSEP = 2.26%). At the same time, ANOVA was used to confirm significant nutritional differences among the factories, emphasizing that relying on standard book values is inadequate. Overall, these results demonstrate the potential of Hyperspectral Imaging (HSI) as an effective tool for real-time feed quality assessment. This ability enables more precise feed management, which is crucial for maximizing livestock productivity and reducing environmental impacts, such as methane emissions resulting from inefficient feed use.

Introduction

Rapid and steady increases in global demand for livestock products, such as beef, milk, and cheese, have been observed, with projections indicating a doubling by 2050 compared to 2012 levels1. Several factors contribute to this growing demand. Improved economic conditions and higher living standards in many areas of the world have led to changes in dietary habits, resulting in a shift toward diets that include more animal products. The livestock sector currently supplies 33% of the protein and 17% of the global kilocalorie intake for the 8.0 billion people comprising the global human population2. Other factors include enhanced living standards and evolving dietary patterns. Meeting this increasing demand presents significant challenges, particularly within livestock production, where feed constitutes a major expense, accounting for 60% to 80% of total production costs3,4.

Recently, there has been an intensified focus on sustainable livestock production. Livestock and crop production are interconnected, with one-third of the cereals harvested globally being used for livestock feed. This integrated relationship is further complicated by the impact of food production and consumption on the Earth’s climate, as well as how climate change, in turn, affects natural resources and the availability of quality feed crops and forages for livestock. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization, the agricultural sector contributes 10% to 12% of total anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions and is responsible for over 50% of phosphorus and nitrogen emissions5. Climate change is expected to have the most significant impact on feed, which is a crucial component of livestock production. The challenges of production costs and vulnerability to climate change indicate that conventional feed and livestock production systems are nearing their practical and ecological limits. This reality has spurred an urgent and intensified focus on identifying and implementing alternative production methods to secure sustainable protein sources from livestock, meeting the needs of a growing global population.

Over the years, a substantial body of research has focused on developing cost-effective and sustainable production methods6. Precision in livestock production, particularly in animal feed production, has become a critical component for achieving sustainability in the industry, as it enables the precise tailoring of nutrient supply to an animal’s specific needs, thereby optimizing resource utilization and minimizing environmental impact. Most studies on precision feed production have concentrated on developing various feed formulation methods to optimize feed costs while maintaining or enhancing productivity. These methods include least-cost feed formulation techniques, such as linear programming and stochastic programming7,8. More advanced approaches, such as Neuro-fuzzy systems, evolutionary algorithms, and goal programming, have also been explored1,9–11. These intelligent systems methods have been shown to increase productivity and have been thoroughly reviewed by Aderounmu et al. 1. Despite these extensive efforts, a pressing need remains for further investigation into integrated feed formulation approaches, which involve the precise blending of raw materials to create nutritionally balanced diets, along with material management approaches that can enhance precision and efficiency in addressing the complexities of modern livestock production.

A fundamental challenge in achieving precise feed formulation is the variability in conventional feed materials, particularly forages. This variability arises from numerous factors, including the chemical composition of different species, diverse growth conditions, and seasonal changes. Furthermore, nutrient composition in feed is increasingly subject to fluctuations anticipated under changing climatic conditions12. For instance, Lee et al.13 observed that rising temperatures can degrade forage quality, adversely affecting the nutritional value of grasses consumed by livestock. Compounding this issue, a study by St-Pierre and Weiss14 quantified sources of variation in feed nutrient composition across U.S. dairy farms. Their analysis revealed that sampling contributed significantly to within-farm variance, accounting for 30–81%, depending on the nutrient and feed type, particularly for nutrients such as neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and crude protein (CP). In contrast, for dry matter, the actual day-to-day variation was often larger. Total mixed rations (TMR), combined sampling, and analytical variability accounted for 40–70% of within-farm variance, highlighting the pressing need for more accurate and consistent sampling methods. This pervasive variability has a direct influence on nutrient availability, animal performance, and health.

The consequences of nutritional variability, particularly concerning essential nutrients such as MC, CP, and NDF, can compromise the health and productivity of livestock. Low-quality feeds, such as untreated crop residues, often fail to meet the nutritional needs of livestock, leading to reduced dry matter intake and, consequently, poorer growth and reproductive performance. Desta15 notes that using such inferior feeds increases feed consumption and operational costs, resulting in a higher amount of waste produced. Furthermore, insufficient nutrition weakens livestock’s immune systems, making animals more susceptible to diseases, which leads to higher veterinary expenses and increased mortality rates16. Climate change introduces additional challenges by affecting the availability and quality of feed, making it crucial for producers to employ flexible feeding strategies that can adapt to these changes to maintain consistent productivity17. The direct link between nutritional quality and livestock health also extends to environmental impacts, where poor feed quality affects productivity and increases methane emissions.

Feed quality can impact the amount of methane produced by livestock, particularly ruminants. Poorly digested feed remains in the digestive system longer, resulting in increased methane emissions due to prolonged fermentation processes. Roessler et al.18 noted that low-quality forage decreases livestock productivity and results in higher methane emissions per unit of digestible intake. Conversely, improving feed quality can significantly lower methane emissions by enhancing the efficiency of the digestive process. Two key components of feed quality that critically influence these fermentation dynamics are MC and NDF. Optimal moisture levels are crucial for effective microbial activity during fermentation; however, too much moisture can reduce nutrient availability and lead to spoilage19. In contrast, high MC can also hinder methane sequestration by blocking pore volume in feed particles through competitive adsorption with water molecules on fiber surfaces20. Therefore, achieving an optimal moisture balance in feed is crucial for attaining favorable fermentation outcomes and minimizing methane emissions. Similarly, NDF content affects rumen fermentation and methane production. Elevated NDF levels change fermentation dynamics, often extending fermentation times and increasing methane output21. A review by Ku-Vera et al.22 found that incorporating forages with specific fibrous characteristics and supplements can effectively reduce methane emissions by 6% to 20%, highlighting the impact of tailored fiber management. Ultimately, promoting well-utilized and efficiently digested feed accelerates its passage through the ruminant’s system, reducing the duration of methane-producing fermentation. Studies by Caicedo et al.23 and Lee et al.13 corroborate that optimizing feed for improved digestive efficiency leads to a notable decrease in methane emissions, thereby mitigating the environmental impact of livestock farming.

Various standardized analytical techniques are used in laboratories to determine the nutrient composition of feed materials, ensuring that formulations meet the dietary requirements of different animal species. While fundamental, obtaining accurate proximate analysis results presents several challenges that can affect data reliability and validity24. These challenges often arise from the methodologies themselves, the characteristics of diverse feed materials, and the environmental conditions during analysis. A limitation of current standard laboratory procedures is their time-consuming nature, often taking two or more days to return results. Furthermore, these analyses require high-level technical expertise for accurate performance, and existing techniques are often destructive, meaning the analyzed sample cannot be used for further study or subsequent analysis. As a direct consequence of these challenges, manufacturers and growers usually resort to using standard book values for estimating nutrient content.

For forage materials, this approach carries certain risks due to potential inaccuracies, often leading to suboptimal feed formulations that do not account for the actual, variable nutrient profiles of specific batches. The environmental implications of relying on such generalized data are becoming increasingly apparent. Inaccurate feed formulations can lead to overfeeding specific nutrients, thereby contributing to environmental pollution. Currently, the livestock sector is responsible for approximately one-third of all nitrogen emissions from human activities, contributing to global nitrogen pollution25. The consequences of these practices have become increasingly noticeable; Uwizeye et al.25 highlighted that the nitrogen contaminant emissions from global livestock farming exceed the planet’s capacity to manage. Their study suggested that reducing meat and dairy consumption could help mitigate these emissions. However, this will be viewed as a complex and potentially unsustainable policy, as it may encounter societal resistance26. Therefore, more efficient analytical methods are needed that provide rapid, precise, and practical data to support optimized feed management and mitigate environmental impact.

Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) is emerging as a promising solution for rapid, non-destructive analysis of feed materials. HSI combines conventional imaging with spectroscopy to provide detailed spectral and spatial information about each feed sample. Recent studies have demonstrated HIS’s ability to accurately monitor MC in livestock feed, a critical factor for maintaining feed quality and preventing spoilage27. Additionally, HSI has been proven effective in predicting the proximate composition of insect-based feed materials, such as black soldier fly larvae, which supports the development of sustainable feed production systems28. The data obtained from HSI are processed to remove noise, after which chemometric modeling is applied to identify and quantify the nutritional composition of the feed, enabling a precise and efficient assessment of feed quality in real-time. This innovative approach has the potential to revolutionize livestock feed production by allowing more accurate and timely adjustments to feed formulations, ultimately enhancing economic outcomes and environmental sustainability.

The goal of this research is to examine the variability of essential nutritional components, including MC, CP, and NDF, in eight feed materials collected from seven factories in the Gyeongsang region of the Republic of Korea. Additionally, we propose a non-destructive and rapid global measurement method that can be easily integrated into existing feed factories. This system, utilizing a single global model for each component, can accurately quantify the chemical composition of the eight feed materials used in this study. Our hypothesis posits that variability in these key nutritional components across different factories significantly affects feed quality and can be effectively modeled and predicted using hyperspectral imaging combined with Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR). This approach, compatible with existing factory setups, represents a significant advancement in the precision and efficiency of feed quality control, aiming to improve productivity and reduce methane emissions.

Methods

Sample collection

Feed materials (Fescues, Rice straw, Oat straw, Alfalfa, Annual ryegrass, Timothy grass, Italian rye grass, and Corn Silage) were collected from seven factories within the Gyeongsang region of the Republic of Korea, all of which share similar climate and geographic conditions. Three samples of each feed type were taken from each factory, with each factory recording the specific MC, CP, and NDF values for every sample. The samples were stored in airtight bags and transported to the laboratory for analysis within two hours of collection. At the lab, the feed was analyzed for proximate components, including MC, CP, and NDF. These laboratory results served as the ground truth for developing predictive models. The MC, CP, and NDF data collected from the factories were statistically analyzed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to assess the significance of differences among the factories and compared to standard values obtained from the Rural Development Administration of the Republic of Korea. Significant differences identified by ANOVA were further examined with Duncan’s Multiple Range Test to compare means and group factories based on their performance for each component.

HSI image acquisition

The samples were prepared in a circular petri dish (90 × 15 mm) with weights (50—100 g), depending on their densities. The surface of the samples was evenly distributed in the petri dish before HSI acquisition. A line-scan type short-wave infrared hyperspectral imaging system was used to acquire images of the samples, as shown in Fig. 1. The hyperspectral imaging system consisted of a camera (HyperspecSWR, Headwall Photonics, Fitchburg, MA, USA), a C-mount lens with a 25 mm f/1.4 aperture, and a moving stage. A computer-controlled motorized sample stage was integrated to move the samples into the camera’s Field of View (FOV). The camera was set up to capture a spectral wavelength range of 1050–2000 nm with a spectral interval of approximately 5.85 nm, resulting in a total of 162 spectral bands. Additionally, 100W tungsten-halogen lamps (Light Bank, Ushio Inc., Japan) were used to illuminate the feed samples.Fig. 1Hyperspectral imaging system for spectral image acquisition from roughage samples; Sensing unit consisting of lens, spectrograph, and camera (a); Light sources (b); Sample holder (c); conveyor system (d); and Computer unit (e).

The Petri dishes with the samples were placed on the sample holder mounted on the movable stage shown in Fig. 1. Preprocessing of spectral images

Background noises were eliminated from the calibrated feed sample images through various preprocessing steps. A single waveband image was chosen from the hypercube for preprocessing. The hyperspectral band image at 1140 nm was used to discard irrelevant background pixels, as the highest peak of the samples occurred at this band. A threshold value was applied to convert all sampled pixels to 1 and background pixels to 0 (Fig. 2). The final masked image was created using a morphological method (image erosion and filling), resulting in a masked image with the primary background removed for effective background elimination. The background-free band image was subsequently multiplied by each image in the hypercube to produce a hypercube with no background.Fig. 2Preprocessingtoeps of hrspeforral image for discarsding background areas.

Preprocessing of acquired HSI data

The acquired hyperspectral image spectrum contained numerous noises, including random noise, noise from length variation along the direction of light, leading to non-linearities from light scattering, and scattered noise produced by the device, which could significantly influence the spectra. Preprocessing spectral data is the most critical step before chemometric modeling to separate important wavelength information from unnecessary, redundant information contained in the data30. Several spectral preprocessing techniques were employed to enhance data quality. The aim was to mitigate various noise and distortion inherent in the spectral data. These methods included normalization (minimum, maximum, and range normalization) to standardize the data scale, Standard Normal Variate (SNV), and Multiplicative Scatter Correction (MSC) to correct for scatter effects, as well as Savitzky-Golay (SG) derivatives for smoothing and differentiation of the spectral data. Each of these preprocessing steps was chosen for its ability to improve the quality of the data, thereby enhancing the accuracy and reliability of models developed using PLSR.

Development of prediction models

Partial least squares regression

PLSR was used to model the relationship between the measured hyperspectral data and the laboratory reference values. PLSR is a multivariate analysis method that assesses the correlation between various independent variables (X) and dependent variables Y. The core principle of PLSR is to extract the most relevant information from the X-matrix and relate it to the Y-matrix. It transforms the original X variables into a new set of uncorrelated components, called latent variables (LVs), which are constructed to maximize the covariance with the Y-matrix. The PLSR model assumes that both the X and Y data matrices can be decomposed into two parts: a deterministic part that can be modeled (loading matrices M for X, Q for Y; score matrices T for X, U for Y) and a stochastic part (error matrices E for X, F for Y), Eqs. 3, 4.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{X}} = {\text{TM}}^{{{\text{T }} + }} {\text{E}}$$\end{document}4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{Y}} = {\text{ UQ}}^{{\text{T}}} + {\text{ F}}$$\end{document}

The process begins by estimating the loading matrix (M) of the X-matrix and the score matrix (T). This transformation condenses the X data into a smaller set of uncorrelated predictors that retain most of the variation related to the response variable in Y. In the context of our study, the spectral data matrix X which represents the spectral data is decomposed into its loading matrix M, score matrix T, and an error matrix E, which accounts for the variation in X that the model does not explain. Similarly, the Y-matrix, representing the lab analyzed result, is also decomposed into a loading matrix Q, a score matrix U corresponding to the X decomposition’s LVs, and its error matrix F. The relationship between the spectral data and the reference measurements is then defined by a linear regression model linking the X-matrix (T) scores to those of the Y-matrix (U) and their respective errors. The strength of PLSR lies in its ability to handle complex data structures often encountered in spectroscopic studies and provide a model that can predict the dependent variables, even when the predictors are numerous and highly intercorrelated31. By applying PLSR, we can reliably predict the specific chemical composition values of feed materials, cross-validated against reference methods determined using standard techniques as discussed earlier.

For model calibration and validation, the extracted total spectral data were randomly split into two distinct sets: 70% of the samples went to the calibration set. In contrast, the remaining 30% formed an independent validation set. This independent dataset was essential for evaluating the actual predictive performance of the developed models.

The optimal number of LVs for each PLSR model was determined based on the lowest Root Mean Square Error of Cross-Validation (RMSECV) obtained through a leave-one-out cross-validation process on the calibration set. Image visualization and MC distribution map of samples

The components of feed materials were calculated for each pixel to visualize the corresponding distribution associated with each pixel in a hyperspectral image that possesses a spectrum. While obtaining the exact quality parameters of every pixel within a sample through chemical analysis is practically impossible, optimal model calibration could predict them. The hyperspectral image was transformed into a two-dimensional (2D) matrix, multiplied by the regression coefficient (beta) derived from the best calibration model, and then applied to the selected wavelengths (Fig. 3). The resulting vector was then folded back to form a 2D image. A 3 × 3 median filter was applied to the 2D image to enhance its quality for visual display. Results

Forage materials and their characteristics

Feed materials, including fescues, rice straw, oat straw, alfalfa, annual ryegrass, timothy grass, Italian ryegrass, and corn silage, were collected from seven different factories in the Gyeongsang region of the Republic of Korea. The availability of specific feed materials varied across these factories, which affected the scope of the comparative analyses. Details on available collection sites and analysis results, including mean values, standard deviations, and p-values, are provided in Appendix A. For convenience, these feed materials will be referred to by letters A through H in the order listed above for subsequent discussions and all illustrative purposes diagrams.

Moisture content

The MC of the analyzed forage materials exhibited heterogeneous patterns of variation, both across different forage types and, in several notable cases, statistically significant differentiation among the surveyed factories. Forages such as A, F, and H demonstrated an absence of statistically significant inter-factory differences in MC (p > 0.05), indicating a relatively uniform moisture profile for these specific feedstuffs, regardless of their geographic or processing origin. Forage A yielded MC values that closely approximated its established reference book value. Forages B, C, D, E, and G consistently exhibited statistically significant variations in MC among their respective factories (p < 0.05). Forages C and D recorded MC levels notably below their corresponding book values. Furthermore, forage G displayed a particularly pronounced divergence in MC between its factories (p = 1.09e-07***), with one facility reporting an exceptionally elevated MC. Appendix A contains details about mean MC values, associated standard deviations, and corresponding p-values for each forage and related factory.

Crude protein

The CP content also showed different patterns of variability among both forage types and their factory sources. Statistically significant differences in CP levels were empirically determined between factories for forages A, C, D, E, and F (p < 0.05). Forage C generally recorded CP levels that exceeded its reference book value across all sampled factories. In stark contrast, forages A and G consistently reported CP concentrations that fell below their respective book values, regardless of the statistical significance of any inter-factory differences. Forages B and H did not show statistically significant variation in CP between factories (p > 0.05), suggesting a more uniform protein content for these specific sources.

Neutral detergent fiber

The NDF content, a critical quantitative metric for assessing roughage quality, also showed noticeable patterns of variation. Statistically significant differences in NDF levels among factories were noted for forages A, C, D, and F (p < 0.05). Notably, forages A, C, E, G, and H consistently recorded NDF values that surpassed their respective book values, indicating a generally higher fiber profile in the collected samples compared to established references. Forages B, E, G, and H did not exhibit statistically significant differences in NDF between factories (p > 0.05), suggesting a more consistent fiber content for these forage types across their various origins.

PLSR prediction models

Moisture regression model

The performance of the PLSR models in predicting MC across various livestock feed materials was evaluated using different spectral preprocessing techniques. As shown in Table 1, all preprocessing methods yielded consistent calibration results, with coefficients of R2C of 0.85 and RMSEC values between 0.44% and 0.45%. These values indicate that the models fit the calibration data well, irrespective of the preprocessing. However, differences in model robustness appeared during cross-validation and, notably, in predictions on the independent validation set. Among the evaluated preprocessing strategies, maximum normalization demonstrated the best predictive performance, achieving an R2P of 0.84 and an RMSEP of 0.49%. The optimal number of LVs varied slightly across the preprocessing methods, with the best model (max normalization) necessitating 23 LVs. This selection was made by minimizing the RMSECV during leave-one-out cross-validation. Discussion

Interpretation of key findings

This study successfully demonstrated the use of HSI with PLSR for rapid, non-destructive prediction of key nutritional components (MC, CP, and NDF) across various feed materials, achieving prediction coefficients of 0.84 (MC), 0.86 (CP), and 0.79 (NDF) as shown in Tables 1, 2, 3. While previous studies27,28,32 have reported higher prediction accuracies for single feed types, such as maize seeds, TMR, and black soldier fly larvae, their findings apply to individual feed types. The unique contribution of our study is the development of a single global model capable of accurately predicting these nutritional components across eight different feed varieties simultaneously. Although this adds more variability to the spectral signatures, it represents a significant step toward practical, in-factory quality control systems that do not require constant recalibration.

A key factor in achieving this strong performance was the component-specific optimization of spectral preprocessing. The preprocessing step is essential before chemometric modeling, as it affects how spectral features are represented and, consequently, how well the model captures chemical variability27,28,32. In this study, no single preprocessing method yielded an optimal solution for all nutritional components, underscoring the component-specific nature of spectral responses in feed materials. For MC estimation (Table 1), the Max normalization method achieved the highest prediction accuracy, likely because it enhanced intensity-related differences in reflectance that are sensitive to water absorption bands. Mean normalization yielded the best results for CP prediction (Table 2), indicating it effectively minimized baseline shifts and scattering caused by heterogeneous particle distributions. Conversely, the Savitzky–Golay first-derivative preprocessing method performed best for NDF (Table 3), as the derivative operation revealed subtle spectral features and reduced overlapping between cellulose- and lignin-related absorptions. These findings show that preprocessing should be tailored to each nutrient’s dominant absorption traits, rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

Regarding beta coefficients, which are regression coefficients in PLSR that measure the contribution and direction of each wavelength’s influence in predicting the analyte, our study identified significant positive and negative peaks for MC, CP, and NDF within specific spectral regions. For MC (Fig. 7), these regions lie in the near-infrared (NIR) spectrum, where absorption typically relates to overtone and combination vibrations of molecular bonds such as O–H, N–H, and C–H. Notably, absorption near 1000 nm corresponds to the second overtone of the O–H stretching vibration, while the peak around 1450 nm indicates the first overtone of the O–H stretch, which strongly suggests water content in organic materials33,34. These peaks occur at wavelengths where changes in light absorption closely correlate with the concentration of the respective nutrient, offering insights into the specific chemical bonds (e.g., C-H, N–H, O–H) and molecular structures responsible for the spectral signatures. Similarly, the significant peaks (Fig. 9) observed for CP are wavelengths generally associated with overtone and combination bands of C-H, N–H, and O–H bonds, characteristic of CP’s chemical composition35. Lastly, the prominent peaks (Fig. 11) within the NDF spectral regions are typically linked to the vibrations of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, the main components of NDF36. This chemical specificity confirms that our global model is robust because it leverages meaningful spectral information, rather than merely fitting noise, making it a reliable tool for addressing feed variability.

Implication for productivity and methane mitigation

The findings of this study showed notable differences in key nutrient parameters of livestock feed (MC, CP, and NDF) collected from factories in the Gyeongsang region, Republic of Korea (Appendix A). Variations in livestock feed composition are a well-known challenge in animal nutrition. While standardized processes aim to minimize variability, factors such as raw material quality, storage conditions, and handling practices often cause inconsistencies in the final feed product37. This indicates that sourcing feed materials from a seemingly standard supplier does not guarantee uniform nutritional content. Such variability heightens the need for quick testing on farms and across industry. Although established laboratory methods exist to analyze nutritional content, this study aims to improve these protocols by enabling more frequent, real-time monitoring with immediate feedback, especially during unexpected, significant deviations. As a result, this work marks a step forward in precision livestock nutrition, with meaningful implications for increasing productivity and reducing methane emissions.

Productivity of the livestock

Proper nutrition is fundamental for the growth, reproduction, and overall health of livestock, which is essential for sustainable agricultural systems and global food security. As livestock diets are often sourced from variable agricultural by-products, consistent quality assurance through feed analysis becomes even more critical to maintaining the animals’ health38. Accurately and quickly quantifying MC, CP, and NDF allows for the formulation of precise rations. This ensures that animals receive the exact balance of nutrients they need, avoiding deficiencies or excesses, thereby optimizing animal performance39. The HSI system developed in this study directly enhances livestock productivity and profitability by empowering producers to move from reactive problem-solving to proactive feed management. This is accomplished in two critical ways:

First, the system enables real-time quantitative adjustments to feed rations. For example, if a batch of soybean meal arrives at a farm with a CP content 5% below the specified level, our system would flag it immediately. This allows a manager to instantly reformulate the diet to compensate for the deficit, preventing the drop in milk yield or weight gain that would otherwise occur. This capability transforms costly feed inputs into predictable animal performance, directly protecting the operation’s bottom line by minimizing waste and maximizing nutritional efficiency. This real-time quality assurance minimizes nutritional deficiencies, improves animal health, reduces veterinary costs40,41, and ultimately ensures that costly feed inputs are utilized with maximum efficiency.

Second, and perhaps more powerfully, the system unmasks hidden risks through chemical imaging. As shown in Fig. 12, a chemical image provides a detailed nutrient concentration map that allows producers to identify and manage high-risk areas before they affect animal health, unlike point spectroscopy and fundamental wet chemistry, which provide a single average value for a feed sample. Chemical imaging can reveal localized wet spots — breeding grounds for toxic mold and mycotoxins — that can cause severe illness, feed refusal, and catastrophic drops in herd performance. This granular, visual insight provides a level of quality assurance and risk management that was previously impossible, ensuring the health and sustained productivity of the entire herd.

Methane mitigation

As stated earlier, obtaining an accurate, rapid assessment of essential nutritional components such as MC, CP, and NDF directly supports the formulation of balanced diets. Optimizing CP intake reduces excess nitrogen excretion and ammonia volatilization without compromising productivity42. Although not a direct methane precursor, this improved nitrogen utilization can indirectly affect rumen fermentation efficiency. Additionally, a precise understanding of NDF quality and digestibility enables dietary adjustments that can direct rumen fermentation pathways toward less methane-intensive outcomes. High NDF content often alters fermentation dynamics, potentially increasing methane production by prolonging fermentation times21. Accurate feed assessments will enhance feed efficiency, allowing animals to produce more with each unit of feed consumed. This reduces methane intensity (the amount of methane emitted per unit of product, such as milk or meat), a key metric in evaluating the environmental footprint of livestock43. Maintaining consistent feed quality through continuous monitoring also contributes to a stable rumen environment, potentially leading to more predictable and favorable methane emission profiles. As Króliczewska et al.44 note, interventions focused on improving feed quality have substantial potential to mitigate methane emissions from livestock systems, suggesting that optimizing the nutritional aspects of livestock feed is central to reducing emissions intensity. The predictive accuracy achieved in this study provides producers with the reliability needed to make real-time dietary adjustments. Hence, this study provides essential tools for a data-driven approach to feed management, directly supporting efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions from cattle.

Constraints to technology adoption

Despite the compelling benefits offered by hyperspectral technology for assessing feed quality, its widespread adoption may face practical constraints, primarily related to economic investment and the availability of specialized technical expertise. The initial capital required for hyperspectral imaging equipment, associated computing power, and sophisticated software presents a significant financial barrier, especially for smaller operations with limited financial resources. This high upfront cost can lead to a perceived low return on investment, hindering broader implementation within the livestock sector. To mitigate this challenge, strategies must focus on both financial accessibility and user empowerment. This includes developing more affordable, portable devices, potentially by leveraging this study’s beta coefficient analysis to design sensors that measure only the most crucial wavelengths. Additionally, offering flexible financing models, such as leasing or securing government subsidies, can help lessen the financial burden.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated a rapid prediction system for MC, CP, and NDF in livestock feed. Using SWIR Hyperspectral Imaging in the 1050–2000 nm range and PLSR, the algorithm was tested on eight diverse feed samples, including fescues, rice straw, oat straw, alfalfa, annual ryegrass, timothy grass, Italian ryegrass, and corn silage, collected from seven different factories in the Gyeongsang region of the Republic of Korea. The spectral data were processed using various methods, and results shows no single preprocessing technique was globally optimal; instead, the best performance was achieved with maximum normalization for MC, mean normalization for CP, and the Savitzky-Golay first derivative for NDF. Despite this component-specific optimization, the individual models showed acceptable results, indicating their potential for use in commercial settings. Statistical analysis comparing factory-reported proximate values with standard reference values showed significant variations in MC, CP, and NDF content in most cases. This highlights the importance of regular, accurate feed quality monitoring. This demonstrated capability has significant implications for sustainable livestock management, enhancing productivity by optimizing animal performance and health, and supporting methane reduction by enabling more efficient nutrient utilization and waste minimization. Although the current model was validated using eight feed types from a single geographic area, it provides a solid foundation for future expansion. The next step is to extend this model’s spectral library and test its effectiveness in real farm environments. Despite initial costs remain a challenge for widespread adoption, the insights from this study offer clear pathways for future progress implementation, especially regarding the development of more affordable and targeted sensors.
