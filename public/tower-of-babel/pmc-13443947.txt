Fostering psychological safety in classroom assessments: moderated-mediation effects of bullying, gender and counselling
Authors: Theresa Antwi, Patricia Mawusi Amos, Simon Ntumi, John Gabla, Sarah Vivian Davis Chintoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the growing concern about how school-based bullying undermines students’ academic success and emotional well-being, especially in high-stakes assessment environments, the study investigated the relationships between bullying frequency, psychological safety during assessments, counselling-based accommodations, and academic performance among senior high school (SHS) students in Ghana, with a focus on gender and regional variations. Guided by Edmondson’s (1999) theory of psychological safety, the research employed a quantitative correlational design involving 585 students from 12 public SHSs across Greater Accra, Central, and Ashanti regions. Multivariate statistical techniques were used to test three hypotheses related to direct, indirect, and interaction effects. Findings indicated that psychological safety significantly mediated the relationship between bullying and academic performance (indirect effect = − 0.775, 95% CI [− 1.23, − 0.43]), while the direct effect of bullying on performance became non-significant when psychological safety was controlled (B = 0.02, p = 0.921). Students who perceived higher psychological safety scored significantly higher in academic performance (B = 3.10, p < .001, η2 = 0.049). Counselling-based accommodations were found to moderate the negative impact of bullying on psychological safety (interaction term B = 0.22, p = 0.029, η2 = 0.008), suggesting that students with access to counselling reported greater resilience against bullying-induced threats to their assessment environments. Gender moderated the bullying performance link (B = − 0.88, p = 0.010), with female students more adversely affected. MANOVA results showed significant multivariate effects for gender (Wilks’ Λ = 0.934, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.039) and region (Wilks’ Λ = 0.882, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.043), and a smaller but significant interaction effect (Wilks’ Λ = 0.972, p = 0.012). Introduction

Bullying in senior high schools is a pervasive global concern with far-reaching consequences for students’ psychological well-being, academic performance, and school attendance1–5. Bullying is not a trivial or isolated event but a systemic issue that affects students across socioeconomic, cultural, and geographic boundaries. According to the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization6, nearly one in three students globally has been bullied by their peers at school at least once in the preceding month. Such behavior undermines the core values of inclusive and equitable education and jeopardizes the ability of schools to serve as safe and nurturing environments. Bullying is generally defined as repetitive and deliberate aggressive behavior involving a power imbalance between the perpetrator and the victim7–10. This imbalance can be physical (e.g., strength or size), social (e.g., popularity or group influence), or psychological (e.g., emotional manipulation), and it is this persistent inequality that makes bullying particularly harmful. Victims often find themselves unable to defend against the aggressor’s actions, leading to feelings of helplessness, fear, and isolation1,11–15. The manifestations of bullying are multifaceted, ranging from direct forms such as physical aggression (hitting, kicking, pushing) and verbal abuse (teasing, name-calling), to indirect or relational bullying such as social exclusion, rumor-spreading, and public humiliation.

In the last two decades, the advent of digital technologies and social media platforms has also given rise to cyberbullying, a form of psychological aggression carried out through electronic means16,17. This includes sending threatening messages, sharing embarrassing photos, or engaging in online harassment, all of which can reach a wide audience and persist indefinitely1,18–20. Cyberbullying, in particular, blurs the boundaries between school and home, making it difficult for victims to find safe spaces or periods of respite. Bullying thrives in school environments where protective structures are weak, inconsistent, or absent. Schools that lack clear anti-bullying policies, trained personnel, or a supportive culture often fail to detect, report, or address bullying incidents effectively1,21–24. In such settings, bystanders may be discouraged from intervening, victims may hesitate to report abuse for fear of retaliation, and perpetrators may act with impunity. Furthermore, institutional tolerance whether implicit or explicit can normalize aggressive behavior, erode trust in school authorities, and compromise the overall school climate12,25–27.

In Ghana, the situation is particularly worrying. Studies suggest that between 40 and 56% of senior high school (SHS) students have experienced some form of bullying, either as victims, perpetrators, or bystanders1,28–30. The 2008 Ghana Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS) reported that 40.1% of surveyed students had been bullied at least once in the past 30 days, and victims were nearly twice as likely to miss school compared to their non-bullied peers31. This trend continues to be evident in recent surveys, with the 2012 GSHS indicating a bullying prevalence of 41.3%, revealing associations with psychosocial problems such as loneliness, physical fights, injuries, and substance use2,11,31–33. The psychological impact of bullying on adolescents cannot be understated. Victims often report higher levels of depression, anxiety, social withdrawal, and suicidal ideation compared to their non-victimized counterparts34–36. Research also shows that chronic exposure to bullying compromises students’ concentration, academic self-efficacy, and motivation, leading to poor academic performance1,28,31,35,37. In Ghanaian studies, psychological distress particularly symptoms of depression and anxiety has been found to be significantly correlated with bullying victimization among senior high school students32,38–40. Importantly, gender differences play a notable role in both the experience and outcomes of bullying. Female students, for example, are more likely to experience emotional forms of bullying such as social exclusion or rumor-spreading, which can intensify feelings of isolation and emotional distress6,41–43. In contrast, males are often subjected to physical bullying but may report it less frequently due to social stigma around male vulnerability28,44,45.

While the psychosocial effects of bullying are well documented in global literature, Ghanaian senior high schools (SHSs) have not sufficiently translated this awareness into concrete, bullying-sensitive strategies within assessment environments. Much of the national discourse on bullying has focused on its prevalence and general impact on student well-being1,12,33,38, but little attention has been paid to how bullying affects students’ performance specifically during high-stakes academic assessments. This represents a critical oversight, as assessments in SHSs often determine progression, placement, and career prospects making them particularly high-pressure scenarios for students. Research shows that high-stakes examinations are inherently anxiety-inducing even for students who are not victims of bullying11,38,46–48. For those who have experienced persistent victimization, these pressures are often magnified. Victims may enter examination settings with heightened levels of psychological distress, including test anxiety, fear of teacher judgment, feelings of inadequacy, concentration difficulties, and even avoidance behaviors such as feigning illness or skipping school entirely7,28,49,50.

The cumulative impact of such stressors can lead to impaired cognitive functioning, reduced motivation, and ultimately, poor academic performance further entrenching educational inequalities. Moreover, current support systems in Ghanaian SHSs, particularly school counselling units, are under-resourced, reactive rather than preventive, and not fully integrated into the academic fabric of the school1,41,51,52. Although the Ghana Education Service mandates the provision of guidance and counselling in schools, these services often function without adequate personnel, professional development, or linkage to classroom and assessment practices36,53,54. This means that counselling-based accommodations such as psychological preparation for exams, designated safe spaces for vulnerable students, or teacher sensitization on trauma-informed assessment are rarely implemented systematically. Even where such accommodations exist in some private or urban schools, there is limited empirical evidence evaluating their effectiveness, particularly in public SHSs where bullying is reportedly more prevalent18,25,26,55. The absence of context-specific research on how to design, apply, and assess the impact of these interventions limits the ability of policymakers, school leaders, and mental health professionals to formulate responsive, evidence-based strategies. Therefore, this study sought to examine the frequency of bullying, its gendered dimensions, and the potential of counselling-based accommodations to create psychologically safe assessment conditions for bullied students in senior high schools across Ghana. By exploring this intersection, the study aimed to contribute to national education policy reform and improve both educational equity and student mental health.

Theoretical framework-Edmondson’s theory of psychological safety

Edmondson’s theory of psychological safety provides a foundational lens through which the findings of this study can be understood and interpreted, particularly regarding the mediating role of psychological safety in the relationship between bullying victimization and academic performance44. According to Edmondson, psychological safety refers to “a shared belief that the environment is safe for interpersonal risk-taking” where individuals feel they can express themselves without fear of embarrassment, ridicule, or punishment. While originally developed within the context of team dynamics and organizational learning, this theory has found increasing relevance in educational research, especially in understanding how students engage cognitively and emotionally within classroom environments. In the context of this study, psychological safety is extended to assessment environments, where students are required to perform under pressure, often in high-stakes situations. Students who experience bullying are likely to feel alienated, judged, and emotionally threatened within such settings, undermining their sense of safety.

Moreover, psychological safety in this study was operationalized through subscales such as Comfort in Assessment Settings, Sense of Threat or Judgment, and Peer Trust and Respect. These subcomponents mirror Edmondson’s notion that safety is not simply the absence of threat but also the presence of interpersonal trust and mutual respect. For instance, when students felt that they could take tests without being judged by peers or teachers despite prior victimization they were more likely to exhibit cognitive focus and self-efficacy, leading to improved performance. This aligns with findings from11,45, who similarly demonstrated that safe emotional climates in schools enhance academic engagement and outcomes. Crucially, the mediating role of psychological safety confirmed that the indirect psychological and emotional consequences of bullying such as heightened fear, reduced confidence, and withdrawal can be as damaging as the direct acts of aggression. Edmondson’s theory helps to explain why merely addressing bullying behavior at the surface level (e.g., through punitive measures) may be insufficient; rather, schools must also intentionally cultivate environments that foster emotional security and interpersonal trust, especially in assessment settings. In essence, the application of Edmondson’s theory in this study broadens its utility from workplace teams to school communities. It underscores the urgent need for systemic reforms in Ghanaian senior high schools that go beyond academic content delivery to create psychologically safe spaces where all students can thrive, even those with prior experiences of bullying. This involves not only anti-bullying initiatives but also the integration of emotionally supportive assessment policies and educator training that prioritize the socio-emotional well-being of learners.

Methodology

Study design

The study employed a correlational research design to examine the relationships between bullying frequency, perceived psychological safety during assessments, gender, counselling-based accommodations, and academic performance among senior high school (SHS) students in Ghana. The correlational design was selected because it enabled the investigation of statistical associations between naturally occurring variables without manipulating the study environment or influencing participants’ experiences7,12. Given that the primary goal was to explore both direct and indirect relationships including mediation and moderation effects a quantitative approach was particularly appropriate. It allowed for hypothesis testing, measurement of variable strength and direction, and generalization of findings across a larger student population56. The design further supported the use of multivariate statistical techniques such as Hayes’ PROCESS macro for regression-based mediation and moderation analysis, which assumes a correlational or observational dataset56. Importantly, the study was conducted in real-world school settings, enhancing ecological validity. The non-experimental nature of the research was ethically appropriate given the sensitivity of the topic (bullying) and the inability to manipulate variables such as victimization or psychological distress. This approach allowed the researchers to gain insight into how bullying and psychological factors interact within the academic assessment environment, reflecting real challenges that students face in the Ghanaian school system.

Population and sample

The target population for the study consisted of students in Senior High School Forms 1 to 3 (SHS 1–3) enrolled in public senior high schools across three diverse administrative regions of Ghana: Greater Accra, Central, and Ashanti. These regions were purposively selected due to their geographic spread, socio-economic diversity, and the consistently high prevalence of bullying reported in both national datasets and peer-reviewed research3. This broader focus across all grade levels allowed the study to capture a wider range of student experiences and developmental perspectives related to bullying, psychological safety, counselling access, and academic performance. While prior studies have often focused exclusively on final-year students due to their proximity to high-stakes examinations, the study intentionally included students from all three cohorts (SHS 1–3) to ensure a more comprehensive understanding of bullying experiences and assessment-related psychological factors. SHS 1 and SHS 2 students, although not in their final year, are regularly engaged in internal school-based assessments, and their exposure to bullying may significantly influence their emotional and academic adjustment throughout their secondary education. Including this broader age group also supported the analysis of grade-level differences in psychological safety and counselling needs, contributing to the study’s practical implications for early intervention. The accessible population was estimated at approximately 7800 students across the 12 selected schools.

To ensure methodological rigor and representation, a multi-stage sampling procedure was adopted: Stage One-School Selection: Schools were first stratified by region, and within each stratum, four public senior high schools were randomly selected using a computer-generated list. Selection criteria included school size, boarding/day status, and co-educational structure to reflect the diverse educational settings in Ghana’s public SHS sector. Stage Two-Class Selection: Within each selected school, classes were randomly selected from each grade level (SHS 1, SHS 2, and SHS 3) to ensure vertical representation. This approach allowed for intra-school variation to be captured and increased the robustness of the dataset. Stage Three-Student Selection: Within selected classes, students were chosen through systematic random sampling using class registers as the sampling frame. Every nth student on the register was selected, with replacement procedures used when students were absent or opted out. The target sample size was 600 students (n = 600), a number informed by statistical guidelines for mediation and moderation analysis. According to26, a sample of this size is adequate to detect small-to-medium mediation effects using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression models. It also exceeds the threshold required for moderation analysis, particularly when interaction terms are involved, which often require larger sample sizes to detect statistically significant differences7. Furthermore, the chosen sample size provided a statistical power level of β = 0.80 at a significance level of α = 0.05, which is standard in social science research. The final distribution of participants was proportionally stratified by region, school, and grade level, ensuring fair representation of key subgroups. This approach enhanced both the external validity and the generalizability of the findings across the wider SHS student population in Ghana.

Measures

Data for the study were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire composed of four main measurement scales, each carefully selected or developed to reflect the study’s core constructs: bullying victimization, perceived psychological safety during assessment, counselling-based accommodations, and academic performance. The questionnaire was designed to be student-friendly, culturally appropriate, and suitable for adolescents in Ghanaian senior high schools.

Bullying victimization scale This section assessed the frequency and forms of bullying experienced by students using a modified version of the Revised Olweus Bully/Victim Questionnaire (OBVQ-R) developed by Olweus. The scale consisted of 10 items, covering physical, verbal, relational, and cyberbullying behaviors. Sample items included: “I have been hit or pushed by other students,” “I have been excluded from group activities,” and “I have received threatening messages online.” Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = Never to 5 = Several times a week. The OBVQ-R is internationally recognized for its strong psychometric properties and has been successfully used in African contexts57. The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency in the present study (Cronbach’s α = 0.87). As shown in Table 1, McDonald’s ω was also computed to provide a more robust estimate of reliability, confirming the scale’s adequacy. The OBVQ-R has been widely validated across diverse cultural contexts, including Sub-Saharan Africa.

Perceived psychological safety during assessment scale This scale measured students’ subjective sense of emotional safety, trust, and non-judgment during examination periods. It was adapted from the Psychological Safety Scale originally developed by Edmondson for organizational settings and contextualized to fit the academic assessment environment. The adapted version included items such as “I feel safe to make mistakes during exams without being embarrassed” and “I trust my teachers to assess me fairly.” Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree. Higher scores indicated a greater sense of psychological safety in assessment contexts. This adaptation aligns with educational applications of psychological safety proposed by58 and supported in classroom contexts by28. Internal consistency for this scale was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α = 0.81). As reported in Table 1, McDonald’s ω was also estimated, further supporting the reliability of the construct in this context. Higher scores indicated greater perceived psychological safety during assessments.

Counselling-based accommodations index This index captured the availability and perceived usefulness of counselling interventions specifically related to assessments, such as pre-exam counselling sessions, relaxation techniques, emotional support, or alternative assessment arrangements for at-risk students. Students were first asked a Yes/No question: “Have you received any form of counselling or emotional support related to school assessments?” Those who responded “Yes” were asked to rate the frequency of support received on a 4-point scale (1 = Rarely to 4 = Always). The Counselling-Based Accommodations Index was scored using a conditional approach to account for the varying number of items answered by participants. Responses to the screening question were coded dichotomously (0 = No, 1 = Yes). Students who responded “No” were assigned a score of 0 on the index, indicating the absence of counselling-related accommodations. Those who responded “Yes” subsequently rated the frequency of support received, and mean scores across the applicable counselling-related items were computed to derive the final index score. This approach ensured comparability across participants while adequately capturing both the availability and intensity of counselling-based support received. This index was created based on empirical models of trauma-informed schooling and inclusive assessment accommodations22,38. The questionnaire was pre-tested with 60 SHS students from a non-participating school in the Eastern Region of Ghana to ensure clarity and cultural appropriateness. Feedback from the pilot led to minor adjustments in language and format. Construct validity was further supported through expert reviews from educational psychologists and school counsellors prior to final deployment. The index demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.79). As indicated in Table 1, McDonald’s ω was also computed to complement alpha, given the mixed item structure.

Academic performance Academic achievement was assessed using self-reported terminal examination average scores from the most recent school term. Students were instructed to provide their average percentage scores, which were subsequently cross-verified with teacher-reported class records to improve accuracy and reduce self-report bias. Although self-reported academic data may be subject to recall inaccuracies, previous studies have shown moderate-to-high correlations between self-reported and official grades among secondary school students23.

Socioeconomic status (SES) and selected demographics were included as control variables to account for background differences among students. SES was measured using a composite index combining parental education, household asset ownership, and housing conditions. Each indicator was standardized using z-scores and then averaged to form a single SES score, with higher values reflecting greater socioeconomic advantage. Additional controls included age, gender, and grade level. These variables were included to reduce confounding effects and strengthen the validity of the estimated relationships among the study variables.

Data collection procedure

Before commencing fieldwork, ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Education, Winneba. In line with ethical standards for research involving minors, permission letters were sent to the heads of the selected schools, and written informed consent was secured from all participants. For students under the age of 18, parental consent forms were distributed and retrieved in advance. The research adhered to the ethical principles of voluntary participation, anonymity, and confidentiality [British Educational Research Association BERA]22. Data collection took place over a three-week period, coordinated with school authorities to avoid disruptions to normal academic activities. The questionnaires were administered in designated counselling or free periods, minimizing pressure and ensuring a quiet, focused setting for completion.

A team of trained research assistants composed of graduate students in educational psychology was deployed to facilitate the administration process, explain instructions, and clarify student queries while avoiding any influence on their responses. Each administration session lasted approximately 10 to 15 min, depending on class size and reading speed. Students were instructed not to write their names or index numbers, ensuring anonymity. In addition, sealed envelopes were provided to allow for confidential submission of completed questionnaires. Across all participating schools, the average response rate was 97.5%, with only a few cases of incomplete or spoiled questionnaires. After data collection, all questionnaires were securely stored and transported to the research base, where they were coded, entered into SPSS Version 27, and prepared for statistical analysis. Special attention was given to data cleaning and verification, particularly for the academic performance scores, which were cross-checked against class registers and approved by class teachers.

Data analysis

Data collected from the questionnaires were first coded and entered into SPSS version 27 for statistical analysis. Prior to substantive analysis, data cleaning and screening were conducted to check for missing values, outliers, and violations of statistical assumptions such as normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity. No significant violations were observed, and missing data were minimal (< 2%) and handled using listwise deletion. All variables were properly labeled, and composite scores for the multi-item scales (bullying frequency, psychological safety, and counselling-based accommodations) were computed by calculating the mean of the items within each scale. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were computed to summarize demographic characteristics of the sample and describe key study variables, including the prevalence and frequency of bullying victimization. Cross-tabulations were also used to explore bullying rates by gender and region. These descriptive outputs provided a foundation for understanding the context of the inferential analyses. To explore bivariate associations, Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients were computed among all continuous variables, such as bullying frequency, psychological safety scores, counselling index scores, and academic performance. This allowed the researchers to identify the strength and direction of initial relationships and to check for multicollinearity among predictors. The core inferential analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (v4.2) developed by Andrew F. Hayes (2022), a widely used tool for testing mediation, moderation, and conditional process models using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. The macro was installed and run within SPSS. Three models were specified based on the study’s hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1 (Mediation Analysis): To test whether perceived psychological safety mediated the relationship between bullying frequency (predictor) and academic performance (outcome), Model 4 of the PROCESS macro was used. This model estimated the indirect effect of bullying frequency on academic performance through psychological safety. The a-path (bullying → psychological safety), b-path (psychological safety → academic performance), and c′-path (direct effect) were all evaluated. The indirect (mediated) effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% bootstrap confidence interval did not include zero. Hypothesis 2 (Moderation by Gender): To test whether gender moderated the relationship between bullying victimization and academic performance, Model 1 of the PROCESS macro was employed. Gender (coded as male = 0, female = 1) was entered as the moderator variable, and an interaction term between bullying and gender was automatically computed and included in the regression model. A statistically significant interaction term would indicate that the strength or direction of the bullying–performance relationship varies by gender. Hypothesis 3 (Moderation by Counselling-Based Accommodations): Similarly, Model 1 was again used to test whether counselling-based accommodations moderated the relationship between bullying frequency and perceived psychological safety. Here, the presence of counselling support (measured on a scale) served as the moderator. A significant interaction would suggest that students who received counselling were psychologically more resilient in the face of bullying. In all models, control variables were included to adjust for possible confounding influences.

These included ages, region (dummy-coded for Greater Accra, Central, and Ashanti), and socioeconomic status (proxied by parental education and access to basic needs). Including these covariates ensured that the effects observed were not spurious or due to external demographic factors. For inferential significance testing, bootstrapping procedures with 5000 resamples were applied to produce bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for the indirect and interaction effects. Bootstrapping was selected because it does not rely on normal distribution assumptions and is more robust in estimating standard errors for mediation and moderation effects33,56,59. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant across all models. Finally, interaction effects were probed using simple slopes analysis, and graphs were plotted to visually interpret how bullying impacted academic performance or psychological safety at different levels of the moderator variables (i.e., gender and counselling support). While regression-based PROCESS analyses and SEM might appear duplicative, their inclusion is justified. PROCESS provides robust estimates of indirect and interaction effects with bootstrapped confidence intervals, allowing precise hypothesis testing for mediation and moderation. SEM, on the other hand, offers a comprehensive evaluation of model fit and the interrelations among multiple latent constructs simultaneously. To minimize redundancy, the SEM was treated as a path model because the main structural component involved observed variables and the mediation-moderation framework, with latent constructs only where confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was required for scale validation.

Several model fit indices were used to assess the adequacy of the SEM/path model, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), model chi-square (χ2), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices in line with established SEM reporting standards. These included the chi-square statistic (χ2), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Given the sensitivity of χ2 to sample size, greater emphasis was placed on relative and approximate fit indices. A good model fit was determined using the following commonly accepted thresholds: CFI and TLI values of 0.90 or higher (preferably ≥ 0.95) indicate acceptable to excellent fit; RMSEA values of ≤ 0.08 indicate acceptable fit, with values ≤ 0.06 reflecting good fit; and a non-significant χ2 suggests good fit, although this criterion is rarely met in large samples48. The SEM/path analyses were conducted using [e.g., AMOS v27], which allowed simultaneous estimation of direct, indirect, and interaction effects, while providing overall model fit information. By combining PROCESS regression and SEM, the study balances robust hypothesis testing and theoretical validation, justifying the inclusion of both analyses while minimizing conceptual duplication. This approach strengthens confidence in both the statistical significance of effects and the structural coherence of the model.

Results

This aspect presents the findings of the study, which examined the relationships among bullying victimization, psychological safety during assessments, counselling-based accommodations, and academic performance among senior high school students in Ghana. The analysis was guided by three key hypotheses: (1) perceived psychological safety mediates the relationship between bullying and academic performance; (2) gender moderates the effect of bullying on academic performance; and (3) counselling-based accommodations moderate the impact of bullying on perceived psychological safety.

The overall bullying frequency mean score was 2.51 (SD = 0.92), indicating a moderate level of victimization on the 5-point scale. The distribution was positively skewed (Skewness = 0.42) with a slightly platykurtic shape (Kurtosis = – 0.51), suggesting that most students reported low-to-moderate levels of bullying, with fewer reporting high frequencies. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the mean ranged from 2.42 to 2.60, offering a precise estimate of central tendency. The Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.87) and McDonald’s omega (ω = 0.85) showed excellent internal consistency, confirming the reliability of this scale. Disaggregating the bullying variable, verbal bullying had the highest mean (M = 2.58, SD = 0.90), followed by relational/social bullying (M = 2.54, SD = 0.95), and physical bullying (M = 2.47, SD = 0.93). All subtypes demonstrated similar positive skewness (ranging from 0.38 to 0.45) and slight negative kurtosis, indicating consistent distribution patterns across bullying forms. The total psychological safety score had a mean of 3.08 (SD = 0.79), suggesting that students perceived a moderate level of emotional security during assessment situations.

Discussion

The findings of the study provide a comprehensive understanding of the relationships among bullying victimization, psychological safety during assessments, access to counselling-based accommodations, and academic performance among senior high school students in Ghana. The results align with and extend existing empirical research on school climate, student mental health, and performance outcomes within both African and global contexts. The analysis demonstrated that bullying frequency had a significant total negative effect on academic performance, but this relationship was indirectly mediated by perceived psychological safety60–62. In other words, the more frequently a student experienced bullying, the less psychologically safe they felt during assessment situations, which in turn negatively influenced their academic outcomes63–65. This is consistent with the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, which suggests that persistent exposure to stressors like bullying can erode students’ coping resources, reducing their ability to function effectively in academic contexts. These finding mirrors and extends the foundational work of18,32,38,66, whose studies on school bullying revealed that chronic exposure to peer victimization systematically undermines students’ sense of belonging, emotional security, and academic self-concept. When students feel alienated or targeted within their learning environment, they often experience internalized stress responses such as anxiety, low self-worth, and social withdrawal. These emotional disturbances ultimately result in decreased school engagement, absenteeism, and deteriorated academic performance patterns that are not only prevalent in Western contexts but have also been documented in Sub-Saharan Africa64,67.

In the Ghanaian setting38,68, provided robust evidence linking school-based violence and peer aggression to negative educational trajectories. Their study, which surveyed secondary school students across multiple regions, found that experiences of bullying were significantly associated with reduced school attendance, avoidance behaviors, and lower performance in core subjects such as English and Mathematics. These patterns confirm that bullying is not a peripheral behavioral issue but a central determinant of academic inequality and psychosocial dysfunction. The current study builds on this foundation by identifying psychological safety during assessments as a critical mediating mechanism in the bullying performance relationship. Students who reported greater psychological safety defined here as the perception of being emotionally secure, respected, and free from fear of ridicule during evaluative situations were more likely to perform well academically, even in the presence of bullying. This suggests that the impact of victimization is not uniformly deterministic; rather, it is significantly shaped by the emotional and relational climate within assessment environments20,38,66,69,70. This aligns closely with Edmondson’s theory of psychological safety, which, although originally conceptualized in organizational teams, has since been applied to educational settings. In this context, psychological safety enables students to take academic risks, ask questions, and participate in assessments without fear of embarrassment or failure, thereby enhancing cognitive engagement and perseverance. In essence, when students feel safe, they are more willing to perform to the best of their abilities, regardless of background stressors such as bullying.

Furthermore, this finding resonates with the work of11,35,38,69, who found that emotional safety within schools is directly correlated with improved mental health and academic motivation45 further demonstrated that students who feel emotionally supported by their peers and teachers report higher grade point averages, stronger intrinsic motivation, and greater optimism toward learning. Our study supports and expands this trajectory by emphasizing that psychological safety during high-stakes assessments often overlooked in traditional school climate research serves as a powerful protective factor against the cognitive disruptions caused by bullying. Crucially, these findings suggest that the psychological aftereffects of bullying may be more detrimental than its immediate behavioral manifestations. While bullying can manifest in overt acts of aggression, its subtler, more insidious impact lies in the erosion of students’ confidence, sense of safety, and academic identity. This is particularly alarming in high-stakes educational systems like Ghana’s, where assessments not only determine progression and certification but are also tied to national-level competition, scholarships, and future career opportunities. Therefore, fostering psychological safety should be a strategic priority in school intervention programs. It has the potential to buffer the academic consequences of bullying, reinforce student resilience, and promote equity by ensuring that every learner regardless of their social vulnerabilities can thrive in assessment environments.

This finding further highlights the need for integrated policies that address both behavioral and emotional dimensions of school safety, underscoring the interplay between psychosocial well-being and educational attainment. The moderation analysis revealed that gender significantly moderated the relationship between bullying and academic performance. Specifically, female students experienced a stronger negative impact of bullying on academic performance than male students. While both genders experienced bullying, the effects were more pronounced for girls likely due to the internalizing tendencies often observed in female students (e.g., anxiety, self-doubt), which can exacerbate the psychological damage of relational or verbal bullying1,12,29,38. This result is consistent with27,66,71,72 findings that girls are more likely to suffer emotional and academic consequences from bullying, especially when it involves social exclusion and verbal aggression. In Ghana38,68,73 reported similar findings, indicating that Ghanaian girls, particularly in coeducational schools, often face subtle yet harmful relational aggression that undermines their sense of self-worth and learning efficacy.

Conclusion

The study examined the complex interplay between bullying victimization, psychological safety during assessments, counselling-based accommodations, and academic performance among senior high school students in Ghana. The findings offer significant contributions to both educational research and practice. First, the study confirmed that bullying frequency is negatively associated with academic performance, but this relationship is indirectly mediated by perceived psychological safety. In other words, students who experience bullying are less likely to perform well academically, not solely because of the bullying itself, but due to the psychological distress it causes particularly in high-stakes evaluative contexts. This highlights the pivotal role of emotional security during assessments as a protective factor and reinforces the importance of nurturing psychologically safe learning environments. Second, the study revealed that gender significantly moderates the relationship between bullying and academic performance, with female students being more adversely affected than their male counterparts. This underscores the need for gender-sensitive intervention strategies that address the unique psychosocial vulnerabilities of girls in the school system, particularly in environments where verbal and relational aggression is prevalent. Third, counselling-based accommodations were shown to buffer the negative effects of bullying on students’ psychological safety. Students who had access to emotional support services such as counselling, alternative testing arrangements, or pre-assessment psychological preparation demonstrated greater resilience and higher levels of perceived safety, even when exposed to bullying. This finding points to the practical importance of institutionalizing school counselling frameworks, especially in resource-constrained contexts like Ghana, where support structures are often underutilized or inconsistently implemented. Collectively, these findings contribute to a more holistic understanding of how school climate, psychological well-being, and assessment-related stress intersect to influence student achievement. They emphasize that academic success cannot be divorced from emotional safety, and that anti-bullying policies must be tightly integrated with assessment reform and student support systems.

Policy and practical implications

The findings from this study provide persuasive evidence that psychological safety during assessments is a critical factor influencing students’ academic performance, particularly for those who have experienced bullying. As such, school administrators should take a proactive role in integrating psychological safety training into professional development programs for teachers, counsellors, and school leadership personnel. This training should be tailored to help staff recognize signs of emotional distress, foster inclusive classroom cultures, and employ assessment practices that minimize intimidation and fear of judgment. Emphasis should be placed on critical periods such as mock examinations, continuous assessments, and national exams, where performance pressures tend to heighten student anxiety and social vulnerability. In line with global best practices and the study’s evidence, the Ministry of Education in Ghana should institutionalize counselling-based accommodations as an essential component of national assessment policies. These accommodations might include the provision of pre-exam emotional preparedness sessions, availability of safe spaces for students needing short-term withdrawal from stressful environments, or even differentiated testing conditions for those identified as at-risk due to psychosocial factors. Implementation should not be left to the discretion of individual schools but should be mandated and monitored to ensure equitable access and consistency.

Furthermore, policies should be accompanied by adequate funding, training of qualified school counsellors, and routine evaluations to assess the impact of such support systems on academic outcomes. Additionally, the study underscores the need for gender-responsive and region-specific interventions in addressing the academic effects of bullying. The moderation analysis revealed that female students are more adversely affected by bullying in relation to their academic performance, suggesting the need for targeted support frameworks that recognize gender-based vulnerabilities. School-wide programs should include gender-sensitive anti-bullying campaigns, peer support groups, and trauma-informed counselling tailored to the experiences of girls. At the same time, the regional disparities identified particularly higher bullying rates in Ashanti and differing access to support services across Greater Accra, Central, and Ashanti regions suggest that a one-size-fits-all approach may be inadequate. Instead, localized interventions that take into account cultural, demographic, and institutional contexts should be prioritized. Clearly, addressing the psychosocial dimensions of assessment and bullying is not simply a welfare issue it is an academic one. Integrating psychological safety training, standardized counselling accommodations, and contextually relevant gender and region-specific interventions can collectively transform the assessment landscape in Ghanaian senior high schools, making it more inclusive, humane, and academically productive.

Limitations

One notable limitation of the study lies in its cross-sectional design, which restricts the ability to infer causal relationships between bullying, psychological safety, counselling support, and academic performance. While the structural equation modeling (SEM) and mediation/moderation analyses revealed significant associations among variables, the temporal order of these effects cannot be definitively established. Future longitudinal or experimental studies would be necessary to confirm the directionality and causal influence of bullying on psychological safety and academic outcomes over time. A second limitation involves the reliance on self-reported data, which is subject to various forms of response bias, including social desirability and recall inaccuracies. Again, while the sample was diverse across three administrative regions (Greater Accra, Central, and Ashanti), it may not fully represent the experiences of students in northern Ghana or private schools, where contextual factors such as cultural norms, resource availability, and institutional policies may differ. In particular, potential cultural or institutional biases in bullying reporting such as norms around respect, hierarchy, or conflict avoidance may have influenced how students perceived and reported incidents, which could affect the interpretation of the findings. Consequently, generalizing the results to all Ghanaian senior high school students should be done with caution. Finally, although some interpretations in the study imply causality for example, statements such as “bullying reduces performance” or “counselling improves outcomes” the correlational design does not allow definitive causal conclusions. Readers should interpret these associations cautiously, and future research using longitudinal or experimental designs is recommended to confirm causal effect. Further research should expand the geographical scope and include private and mission-based schools to increase external validity.
