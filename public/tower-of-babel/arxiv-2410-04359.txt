Efficient estimation of semiparametric spatial point processes with V-fold random thinning Journal: Biometrika
Xindi Lin, Hyunseung Kang

Abstract
We study a broad class of models called semiparametric spatial point processes where the intensity function contains both a parametric component and a nonparametric component. We propose a novel estimator of the parametric component based on random thinning, a common sampling technique in point processes. The proposed estimator of the parametric component is shown to be consistent and asymptotically normal if the nonparametric component can be estimated at the desired rate. We then extend a popular kernel-based estimator in i.i.d. settings and establish convergence rates that will enable inference for the parametric component. Next, we generalize the notion of semiparametric efficiency lower bound in i.i.d. settings to spatial point processes and show that the proposed estimator achieves the efficiency lower bound if the process is Poisson. Computationally, we show how to efficiently evaluate the proposed estimator with existing software for generalized partial linear models in i.i.d. settings by tailoring the sampling weights to replicate the dependence induced by the point process. We conclude with a small simulation study and a re-analysis of the spatial distribution of rainforest trees.

1
Introduction

1.1
Background and motivation

Parametric spatial point processes are widely used to quantify the relationship between the spatial distribution of events, for instance, the location of trees, violent crime, or gene expressions in a cell, and relevant spatial covariates, such as elevation, socioeconomic characteristics of a neighborhood, or cellular environment; see
Baddeley et al. 2015
for textbook examples. Specifically, the intensity function
\lambda(\cdot)
posits a parametric model between the spatial distribution of events and the spatial covariates, for example,
\lambda(u;\theta)=\exp\left(\theta^{\top}y_{u}\right)
where
u
is the spatial location in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
y_{u}
are the values of the spatial covariates at location
u
, and
\theta
quantifies the relationship between the spatial covariates and the spatial distribution of events.
Rathbun & Cressie 1994
showed that the maximum likelihood estimator of
\theta
is
consistent, asymptotically normal, and statistically efficient when the point process is Poisson, i.e., the number of spatial events observed in non-overlapping spatial regions is independent. Later,

Schoenberg 2005
,
Guan & Loh 2007
, and
Waagepetersen & Guan 2009

showed that the
maximum likelihood estimator in
Rathbun & Cressie 1994

remained consistent and asymptotically normal even if the process is not Poisson, but there is a loss in statistical efficiency. Also,
Berman & Turner 1992
,
Baddeley & Turner 2000
, and
Baddeley et al. 2014
proposed computationally efficient approaches to compute the maximum likelihood estimator. These methods for parametric spatial point processes are widely accessible with the R package
spatstat

(
Baddeley & Turner 2005
)
.

The main theme of the paper is to propose a more general class of models that study the same questions as above (i.e., the relationship between spatial events and spatial covariates), but with less restrictive modeling assumptions and more importantly, without sacrificing the aforementioned statistical and computational properties (i.e., consistency, asymptotic normality, statistical and computational efficiency). Formally, suppose we partition the set of spatial covariates into two types,
y_{u}
and
z_{u}
, and the intensity function takes the following form:

\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)=\Psi\left\{\tau_{\theta}(y_{u}),\eta(z_{u})\right\},\quad{}\theta\in\Theta,\eta\in\mathcal{H}.

(1)

In our generalization, we use
\lambda
and
\Psi

to distinguish two parametrizations of the intensity function, one that is a function of the spatial location
u
(i.e.,
\lambda
) and another that is a function of the spatial covariates
y_{u}
and
z_{u}
at the spatial location
u
(i.e.,
\Psi
). The latter parametrization is helpful to distinguish the theoretically distinct roles the two types of spatial covariates have for inference. The term
\tau_{\theta}(\cdot)
is an user-specified function of
y_{u}
and is parameterized by a finite-dimensional parameter
\theta
in a Euclidean space
\Theta
, for instance
\tau_{\theta}(y_{u})=\theta^{\top}y_{u}
and
\theta
is a vector of real numbers. The term
\eta(\cdot)
is an unknown, infinite-dimensional function of
z_{u}
in a linear space
\mathcal{H}
, for instance, the space of smooth functions in a Hilbert space. The function
\Psi(\cdot)
is a user-specified link function, and a popular choice is the exponential function.

The class of spatial point processes with an intensity function in equation (
1
), which we refer to as
a semiparametric spatial point process
, encompasses a variety of existing models. Some examples include (a) the parametric spatial point process mentioned above, (b) spatial point processes of the form
\lambda(u)=\lambda_{0}(u)\exp\left(\theta^{\top}y_{u}\right)
where
\lambda_{0}(u)
is a nonparametric, “baseline” rate/intensity function (e.g.,
Diggle 1990
;
Chu et al. 2022
;
Hessellund et al. 2022
), and recently, (c) spatial point process models for causal inference where
y_{u}
represents a spatial treatment/exposure and
z_{u}
represents measured, spatial confounders (e.g.,
Papadogeorgou et al. 2022
;
Jiang et al. 2023
). More broadly, equation (
1
) extends generalized partially linear models in i.i.d. settings (e.g.,
Robinson 1988
;
Severini & Staniswalis 1994
;
Härdle et al. 2000
) to spatial point processes. Similar to the motivation behind generalized partially linear models, semiparametric spatial point processes are designed to address questions about estimating and testing the partial effects of a small set of spatial covariates
y_{u}
on the spatial distribution of events without imposing parametric assumptions on all spatial covariates
y_{u}
and
z_{u}
.

We briefly remark that there is considerable work on nonparametric models of

\lambda(\cdot)
(e.g.,
Berman & Diggle 1989
;
van Lieshout 2012
;
Baddeley et al. 2012
;
Tang & Li 2023
). However, these methods generally do not enable statistical inference in the form of hypothesis testing or constructing confidence intervals.

1.2
Contributions

Under model (
1
), we propose a novel estimator of
\theta
that is consistent, asymptotically normal, and in some cases, semiparametrically efficient.
Our estimator is inspired by two ideas: (a) random thinning, a common technique to simulate point processes or to sample from posterior distributions in Markov chain Monte Carlo from Bayesian inference, and (b) the pseudo-likelihood framework in
Waagepetersen & Guan 2009
. Specifically, we use independent random thinning in
Cronie et al. 2024
to “correctly” partition spatial point processes into sub-processes so that the sub-processes have some desirable properties, such as first-order equivalence with respect to the pseudo-likelihood.
Also, if the process is Poisson, independent random thinning enables investigators to choose a wide array of nonparametric estimators of
\eta
without worrying about whether they affect downstream inference of
\theta
; see Section
3.2
.

Computationally, we show that our estimator is numerically equivalent to estimating a finite-dimensional parameter in a weighted generalized partially linear model in i.i.d. settings, for which many existing software are available (e.g., the R package
mgcv
). Importantly, the numerical equivalence allows investigators to use existing software from generalized partially linear models to estimate
\eta
without implementing a new estimator tailored to spatial point processes.

Finally, we propose a framework to study semiparametric efficiency lower bounds in spatial point processes where the data is not i.i.d. and the intensity function is not parametric, both of which make significant, non-trivial extensions of the parametric, Cramér-Rao lower bound in
Rathbun & Cressie 1994
to semiparametric spatial point processes and several existing works in generalized partial linear models (e.g.,
Severini & Wong 1992
) to non-i.i.d., spatial point processes. We then show that our estimator is optimal in the sense that it achieves the semiparametric efficiency lower bound if the process is Poisson.

While our estimator draws inspiration from cross-fitting techniques developed for i.i.d. data
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
, the methodology and theory we present are fundamentally distinct. Some important inherent differences between i.i.d. observations and spatial point processes are: (1) the entire observed spatial point pattern represents a single realization of the underlying process, unlike multiple independent samples; (2) the number of observed points within any given region is a random variable; and (3) the locations of these points exhibit spatial dependence. These differences require different tools and assumptions than those in
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
to establish inferential properties. More generally, we believe random thinning we describe below is a simple yet promising generalization of cross-fitting to handle more general data types (e.g., matrix data, points data, i.i.d. and non-i.i.d. data), and the properties we derive about random thinning may be of independent interest for future works.

2
Setup

2.1
Preliminaries from spatial point processes

Let
X
denote a spatial point process in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathrm{d}u
denote the Lebesgue measure of an infinitesimal neighborhood around a spatial location
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The first-order intensity function, often referred to as “the” intensity function, is the function

\lambda:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
such that for any bounded subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

E[\sum_{u\in X}\textit{I}(u\in A)]=\int_{A}\lambda(u)\mathrm{d}u
and
\textit{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function.
Also, the second-order intensity function is the function
\lambda_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
such that for any two bounded subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
E\left\{\sum_{u\in X,v\in X}^{u\neq v}\textit{I}(u\in A,v\in B)\right\}=\int_{A}\int_{B}\lambda_{2}(u,v)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v
. The pair correlation function of
X
is the normalized second-order intensity function, i.e.,
g(u,v):=\lambda_{2}(u,v)/\lambda(u)\lambda(v)
.

2.2
Semiparametric spatial point process

Consider a spatial point process
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that depends on two sets of bounded, spatial covariates,
y_{u}\in\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}

and
z_{u}\in\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
; note that the covariates can be the original spatial coordinates
u
. We assume the intensity function of
X
takes the form in equation (
1
). The target parameter of interest is
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
where
\Theta
is compact and
\eta(\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
is an infinite-dimensional nuisance parameter.
Mirroring the terminology for parameters, we refer to
y_{u}
as the target covariates and
z_{u}
as the nuisance covariates.

Consider an observational window
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
where the points can be realized. Following
Waagepetersen & Guan 2009
, in observational window
A
, the pseudo-log-likelihood function of a semiparametric spatial point process
X
is

\ell(\theta,\eta;X)=\sum_{u\in X\cap A}\log\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)-\int_{A}\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)\mathrm{d}u.

(2)

Next, for every fixed
\theta\in\Theta
, let
\eta_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}
to be the nuisance function that maximizes the expectation of the pseudo-likelihood in (
2
), i.e.,

\eta_{\theta}=\arg\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}}E\left\{\ell(\theta,\eta;X)\right\}.

(3)

Unlike existing works on parametric spatial point processes (e.g.,
Waagepetersen & Guan 2009
), the infinite-dimensional functional
\eta_{\theta}
is unique to our study of semiparametric point processes. More generally,
\eta_{\theta}
extends the notion of a “nuisance curve” in semiparametric models from i.i.d. settings (e.g.,
Severini & Wong 1992
) to spatial point processes and borrowing the terminology from this literature,
\eta_{\theta}
represents a particular map from the target parameter space
\Theta
to the nuisance parameter space
\mathcal{H}
.

Following
Waagepetersen & Guan 2009
,
Guan et al. 2015
and
Hessellund et al. 2022
, we assume that the pair correlation function is reweighted, isotropic, and parametrized by
\psi
(i.e.,
g(u,v;\psi)=g(0,u-v;\psi)
for any
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\psi
is a finite-dimensional parameter). We let
\theta^{*}
,
\eta^{*}
, and
\psi^{*}
denote the true values of
\theta,\eta
, and
\psi
, respectively. Our goal is to estimate and conduct inference on the true target parameter
\theta^{*}
.

We conclude by defining an important type of semiparametric spatial point process where the intensity function has a log-linear form:

\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)=\exp\left\{\theta^{\top}y_{u}+\eta(z_{u})\right\}.

(4)

Not only are log-linear intensity functions common due to their theoretical and practical conveniences (e.g.,
Baddeley et al. 2015
;
Hessellund et al. 2022
), we illustrate below that they possess some unique properties for our method.

3
Estimation with Spatial Cross-Fitting

3.1
V-fold random thinning

Historically, thinning has been used in point processes or Bayesian inference to efficiently draw samples from complex, often analytically intractable processes. In our paper, we re-purpose thinning as a general form of data splitting where thinning is used to obtain sub-processes
X_{1},...,X_{V}
and different subprocesses are used to estimate the nuisance and target parameters. We call this implementation of thinning as
V-fold random thinning
and it is formally defined below.

Definition 3.1
(
V-fold Random Thinning
).

Suppose
X
is a spatial point process and
V\geq 2
is an integer. For each point in
X
, we uniformly sample a number
v
from
\{1,\ldots,V\}
and assign the point to the sub-process
X_{v}
.

In Lemma
3.2
, we prove some important properties of V-fold random thinning.
These properties allow investigators to use a broad array of traditional and modern machine learning methods to estimate the nonparametric, nuisance parameter
\eta
without introducing bias when we estimate the target parameter
\theta
. This type of bias has recently been referred to as overfitting bias
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
and in i.i.d. settings, sample splitting is used to prevent overfitting bias when investigators use machine learning methods to estimate
\eta
.
More generally, Lemma
3.2
may be of independent interest for future methodologists who want to establish inferential properties of a low-dimensional parameter from point processes with a nonparametric, nuisance component.

Lemma 3.2
(Properties of V-fold Random Thinning).

For any set
I\subset[V]=\{1,2,...,V\}
, let
|I|
be the cardinality of
I
, and
X^{(I)}:=\bigcup_{j\in I}X_{j}
. Then,
X^{(I)}
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

The intensity function of
X^{(I)}
is
V^{-1}|I|\lambda(u)
.

(ii)

The pseudo-likelihood function of
X^{(I)}
has the same first-order properties as the original
X
. In other words, for any set
I
,
E\left\{\ell(\theta,\eta;X^{(I)})\right\}
is maximized by the same true values
\theta^{*},\eta^{*}
and for any fixed
\theta
,
E\left\{\ell(\theta,\eta;X^{(I)})\right\}
is maximized by the same
\eta_{\theta}
.

(iii)

If
X
is a Poisson spatial point process,
X^{(I)}
is independent with its complement
X\backslash X^{(I)}
.

We remark that
Cronie et al. 2024

used random thinning to split a point process into pairs of training and validation processes.
The pairs are used to choose the optimal bandwidth parameter in a nonparametric kernel estimator where the optimal bandwidth minimizes the “out-of-sample” prediction error.
In our setup, we use random thinning for a different purpose, specifically to conduct inference on the parameter
\theta
in the presence of an infinite dimensional nuisance parameter
\eta
in such a way that we end up with desirable statistical properties (e.g., consistency, asymptotic normality, and in some cases, semiparametric efficiency).

3.2
Spatial cross-fitting estimator

Our estimator of the target parameter
\theta^{*}
, which we refer to as a
spatial cross-fitting estimator
, is stated below and is broken into four steps.

Step 3.3
(V-fold random thinning).

Use V-fold random thinning in Definition
3.1
to partition the point process
X
into
V
sub-processes
X_{1},\ldots,X_{V}
.

Step 3.4
(Estimate
\eta_{\theta}
with sub-processes excluding
X_{v}
).

For every
v\in\{1,\ldots,V\}
, let
X_{v}^{c}:=\bigcup_{j\neq v}X_{j}
be the sub-processses that exclude
X_{v}
. Estimate
\eta_{\theta}
in equation (
3
) using the observations in
X_{v}^{c}
:

\hat{\eta}_{\theta}^{(v)}=\arg\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{H}}\ell(\theta,\eta;X_{v}^{c}).

Step 3.5
(Estimate
\theta^{*}
with sub-process
X_{v}
).

Estimate the target parameter
\theta^{*}
by plugging in the estimate
\hat{\eta}_{\theta}^{(v)}
from step
3.4
into the pseudo-likelihood and
maximizing it over the observations in
X_{v}
:

\hat{\theta}^{(v)}=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\ell(\theta,\hat{\eta}^{(v)}_{\theta};X_{v}).

Step 3.6
(Aggregation).

Repeat steps
3.4
and
3.5

for each
v
and return the aggregated estimate of
\theta^{*}
, i.e.,

\hat{\theta}=V^{-1}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\hat{\theta}^{(v)}
.

Roughly speaking, steps
3.4
and
3.5
resemble a profile-likelihood estimator where we maximize with respect to the nuisance parameter (i.e., step
3.4
), and then maximize with respect to the target parameter (i.e., step
3.5
). The important difference between the usual profile-likelihood estimator (e.g.,
Severini & Wong 1992
) and our method is that we use different observations at each maximization step. As mentioned above, this is critical to prevent over-fitting of the nuisance parameter; see Remark
3.7
and Section
4
for further discussions.

Remark 3.7
(Step
3.4
and using nonparametric estimators from i.i.d. settings).

In step
3.4
, we do not specify a particular nonparametric estimator of
\eta_{\theta}
, or more specifically the function class
\mathcal{H}
, to estimate
\eta_{\theta}
.
This is done on purpose and is a key defining feature of our procedure as it enables investigators to use a wide array of nonparametric estimators, including those developed for i.i.d. setting, to estimate
\eta_{\theta}
. Specifically, the next section shows that numerically solving step
3.4
is an instance of a problem in generalized partially linear model in i.i.d. settings. Thus, investigators can train their favorite nonparametric estimator (e.g., splines, generalized additive models, kernel regression) or even a machine learning estimator from i.i.d. settings with data from
X_{v}^{c}
and estimate
\hat{\eta}_{\theta}^{(v)}
. Investigators can even freely tune hyper-parameters associated with their nonparametric estimators, say choosing the bandwidth in kernel regression with cross-validation in
X_{v}^{c}
, and as long as
\hat{\eta}_{\theta}^{(v)}
is consistent at the desired rate (see Sections
4.2
and
4.3
), the estimator
\hat{\theta}
is consistent and asymptotically normal. Importantly, these rate conditions are “high-level” conditions that are agnostic to the function class of
\mathcal{H}
or how investigators fine-tuned their nonparametric estimators.
For more discussions on this agnostic framework for estimating infinite-dimensional nuisance parameters in semiparametric models, see
van der Laan & Rose 2011
,
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
and
Athey & Imbens 2019
.

Remark 3.8
(Step
3.6
and semiparametric efficiency).

Theoretically, any one of
\hat{\theta}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{\theta}^{(V)}
will be consistent and asymptotically normal under the assumptions laid out in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
. But, as we show in Section
5
, aggregating
\hat{\theta}^{(v)}
in step
3.6
and in doing so, using the sub-processes multiple times are critical to regain the full semiparametric efficiency of the estimator; see
Schick 1986
for a similar phenomena in i.i.d. settings.

Remark 3.9
(Choosing the number of folds
V
for random thinning).

As long as
V
is bounded, the choice of
V
has no asymptotic consequences for our estimator.
Practically speaking, while
V=2
was more than sufficient in our numerical experiments, we generally recommend choosing
V
based on both data and computational availability.

3.3
Numerical approximation and estimation of nuisance parameter

Directing maximizing the pseudo-log likelihood in equation (
2
) is numerically intractable because of the integral; this is in contrast to estimating low-dimensional parameters in partially linear models with i.i.d. data where no such integral exists. In this subsection, we propose a numerical, quadrature approximation based on
Berman & Turner 1992
.
Historically, the quadrature approximation has been used in point processes to
numerically approximate the first-order likelihood function. In our setup, we not only use the quadrature approximation for the same numerical purpose, but more importantly, we show how the approximation enables investigators to directly use existing R packages initially designed to estimate
\eta_{\theta}
in generalized partially linear models from i.i.d. data. Critically, these existing R packages are already optimized for computational efficiency, alleviating the burden on investigators to develop their own software.

Let
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
be the union of the data points in
X\cap A
and the points from a uniform grid on
A
. We use Dirichlet tessellation
(
Green & Sibson 1978
)
to generate the weight of each point
u_{j}
, denoted as
w_{j}>0
, and approximate the integral in equation (
2
) as

\int_{A}\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)\mathrm{d}u\approx\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\lambda(u_{j};\theta,\eta).

(5)

Then, the pseudo-log-likelihood in (
2
) can be numerically approximated by

\displaystyle\ell(\theta,\eta;X)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{u\in X\cap A}\log\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\lambda(u_{j};\theta,\eta)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=1}^{N}(y_{j}\log\lambda_{j}-w_{j}\lambda_{j}),\quad{}\lambda_{j}=\lambda(u_{j};\theta,\eta),\ y_{j}=\textit{I}(u_{j}\in X\cap A).

(6)

The right-hand side of (
6
) is equivalent to the log-likelihood of a weighted, semiparametric Poisson regression model of the form
y_{j}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\text{Poi}(\lambda_{j})
with weights
w_{j}
. Thus, we can use any existing methods developed for generalized partial linear models with i.i.d. data to estimate
\eta_{\theta}
in step
3.4
. In our numerical experiments below, we use the R packages
mgcv

(
Wood 2001
)
and
gplm

(
Mueller 2016
)
. By default,
mgcv
uses penalized splines regression to estimate the nonparametric portion of the generalized partially linear model and
gplm
uses kernel regression to estimate the nonparametric portion.

As a concrete example of step
3.4
, suppose we use the R package
gplm
to estimate
\eta_{\theta}
. Then, for each
\theta
and
q
-dimensional nuisance covariates
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we can rewrite step
3.4
as the solution to the following optimization problem:

\hat{\eta}_{\theta}(z)=\arg\max_{\eta(z)\in M}\left[\sum_{u\in X\cap A}K_{h}\left(z_{u}-z\right)\log\left[\Psi\left\{\tau_{\theta}(y_{u}),\eta(z)\right\}\right]-\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}K_{h}\left(z_{u_{j}}-z\right)\Psi\left\{\tau_{\theta}(y_{u_{j}}),\eta(z)\right\}\right].

(7)

The function
K_{h}(z)=h^{-q}K\left({z}/{h}\right)
is the rescaled kernel function with bandwidth
h>0
and the function
K(\cdot)
is a kernel function; see Section 13.1 of the Supplementary Material for a formal definition of a kernel function.
M
is the image of the nuisance function, i.e.,
M=\{\eta(z):z\in\mathcal{Z},\eta\in\mathcal{H}\}
. When the intensity function is log-linear, the solution to (
7
) has a closed-form solution; see Section 11.5 of the Supplementary Material. Also, in Section
4.5
, we formally show that under mild conditions, the estimator in equation (
7
) from the R package
gplm
can satisfy the desired rates of convergence to enable inference of
\theta^{*}
in our setting.

Remark 3.10
(Approximation based on logistic regression).

Baddeley et al. 2014
proposed an alternative approximation based on logistic regression and this approximation can also be implemented with the aforementioned R packages
mgcv
and
gplm
. Between the two approximations,
Baddeley et al. 2014
generally found that the logistic approximation is less biased at the expense of conservative standard errors. We also observe this pattern for semiparametric spatial point processes and we generally recommend the logistic approximation if obtaining valid coverage from a confidence interval is important. But, if statistical efficiency is important, we recommend the quadrature approximation discussed above. For further discussions, see Section 12.1 of the Supplementary Material.

Remark 3.11
(Boundary bias of the kernel estimator in
gplm
).

In our extended simulation studies (see Section 12.2 of the Supplementary Material), we found that the kernel estimator from
gplm

can sometimes be biased near the boundary of the support of the nuisance covariates. This is a well-recognized, finite-sample phenomenon among kernel-based estimators, and several solutions exist to mitigate this bias (e.g.,
Jones 1993
;
Racine 2001
). In contrast, we generally found that the the penalized spline estimator from the R package
mgcv
suffers less from boundary-related biases.

{assumption}

{assumption}

4.5
Convergence rate of the kernel-based nonparametric estimator

We derive the convergence rate of
\hat{\eta}_{\theta}
when the kernel regression in equation (
7
) is used to estimate
\eta_{\theta}
. Unlike the kernel regression estimator for semiparametric models in i.i.d. settings,
the observations in spatial point processes exhibit spatial dependence. At a high level, the spatial dependence violates a core assumption underlying the proof of the rates of convergence of kernel regression estimators in i.i.d settings, especially the upper bound for the variance of the kernel estimator. Also, as a subtle technical point, for a semiparametric spatial point process, the pseudo-log-likelihood in equation (
2
) consists of points in the spatial domain (i.e., the observation window
A_{n}
), whereas the nuisance parameter
\eta_{\theta}
is a function defined on the covariate domain
\mathcal{Z}
. These two spaces can exhibit different smoothness with respect to the pseudo-likelihood function. Furthermore, even if the nuisance covariates satisfy
z_{u}=u
so that the spatial domain and the covariate domain are identical, in the point processes literature, we are unaware of general results about the rates of convergence of nonparametric estimators that can estimate
\eta_{\theta}
in the presence of an unknown parametric component
\theta
.

We begin by
resolving the difference in the spatial domain and covariate domain. We define what we call the “spatial conditional expectation”
E\left[\ell(\theta,\gamma;X)\mid z\right]
over a push-forward measure.

Definition 4.5
(Spatial Conditional Expectation).

Suppose the push-forward measure induced by the map from the spatial domain and the covariate domain, i.e.,
u\mapsto(y_{u},z_{u})
, has the “joint” Radon-Nikodym derivative
f(y,z)
. We define the spatial conditional expectation as

E[\ell(\theta,\eta;X)|z]:=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\left(\log\left\{\Psi[\tau_{\theta}(y),\eta(z)]\right\}\Psi[\tau_{\theta^{*}}(y),\eta^{*}(z)]-\Psi[\tau_{\theta}(y),\eta(z)]\right)f(y,z)\mathrm{d}y.

With Definition
4.5
, we can re-write the expectation of the pseudo-log-likelihood in (
2
) as

\displaystyle E[\ell(\theta,\eta;X)]=

\displaystyle\int_{A}\left\{\log\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)\lambda(u;\theta^{*},\eta^{*})-\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)\right\}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\left(\log\left\{\Psi[\tau_{\theta}(y),\eta(z)]\right\}\Psi[\tau_{\theta^{*}}(y),\eta^{*}(z)]-\Psi[\tau_{\theta}(y),\eta(z)]\right)f(y,z)\mathrm{d}y\right\}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Z}}E[\ell(\theta,\eta;X)|z]\mathrm{d}z.

The above equality implies that for every
\theta
, the maximizer
\eta_{\theta}
in equation (
3
) is also the maximizer of the spatial conditional expectation
E[\ell(\theta,\eta;X)|z]
. Therefore, the kernel regression estimator in (
7
)
is also an estimator of the spatial conditional expectation
E[\ell(\theta,\eta;X)|z]
.

Next, we make the following assumptions about the smoothness of the intensity function, the “joint” Radon-Nikodym derivative, and the higher-order dependence of points in
X
.

{assumption}
[Regularity conditions for kernel regression estimator]
For some integers
l\geq 2
and
m\geq 2
, the following conditions hold.

5.1

(
Smoothness
)
\Psi\left\{\tau_{\theta^{*}}(y),\eta^{*}(z)\right\}
and
f_{n}(y,z)
are
l
-times continuously differentiable with respect to
z
. The kernel function
K(\cdot)
in equation (
7
) is an
l
-th order kernel.

5.2

(
Identification
)

\liminf_{n}\inf_{\theta,z}|A_{n}|^{-1}\partial^{2}/\partial\eta^{2}E\left\{\ell_{n}(\theta,\eta_{\theta,n})|z\right\}>0

5.3

(
Higher-order weak dependence
) There exists a positive constant
C
such that the factorial cumulant functions of
X
, denoted as
Q_{m}
, satisfy

\sup_{u_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|Q_{m^{\prime}}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m^{\prime}})\right|\mathrm{d}u_{2}\ldots\mathrm{d}u_{m^{\prime}}<C,\quad{}m^{\prime}=2,3,\ldots,m.

Condition
5.1
corresponds to the usual smoothness condition in kernel regression (e.g.,
Li & Racine 2023
). Condition
5.2
guarantees that
\eta_{\theta,n}
is unique.
Condition
5.3
assumes
m
-th order weak dependence between points.
If
X
is Poisson,
Q_{m}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})=0
for every
m
if at least two of
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
are different.
We remark that some works in the spatial point process literature assume a stronger
m\geq 4
(e.g., equation (6) in
Guan & Loh 2007
). A detailed discussion of the factorial cumulant function is in Section 13.4 of the Supplementary Material.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose
Assumptions
4.2
and
4.5
hold. Then, with an appropriately chosen bandwidth parameter (see Lemma 13 in the Supplementary Material), for
i=0,1,2
, the estimator of the nuisance parameter in equation (
7
) satisfies

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left|\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial\theta^{i}}\left\{\eta^{*}_{\theta,n}(z)-\hat{\eta}_{\theta,n}^{(v)}(z)\right\}\right|=o_{p}\left(|A_{n}|^{-\frac{m}{2(m+k+q+1)}\frac{l}{l+q+1}}\right),

(12)

where
k
is the dimension of
\theta

, and
q
is the dimension of the nuisance covariates
z
.

If the dimensions of both the target parameter
k
and the nuisance covariates
q
are large, we need weaker higher-order dependence (i.e., high

) and more smoothness (i.e., high

) to ensure that Assumptions
4.2
and
4.3
hold (i.e., the convergence rate of the estimated nuisance parameter is

). In particular, when the process is Poisson so that

4.6
Proof sketch for asymptotic normality and non-Poisson processes

As Theorem
4.2
stated, our estimator of the target parameter in the semiparametric point process remains asymptotically Normal even if the process
X
is not Poisson. However, in this setting, we have to assume that the intensity function is log-linear (i.e., equation (
4
)). This section illustrates why this is the case by explaining a key step of the proof for asymptotic normality. In doing so, we also explain why V-fold random thinning is important to enable asymptotic normality.

For an arbitrary, measurable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
\mathbb{G}^{X}_{n}[f]
denote the
|A_{n}|^{-1/2}
scaled innovations of spatial point processes from
Baddeley et al. 2008
:

\mathbb{G}^{X}_{n}[f]:=|A_{n}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\{\sum_{u\in X\cap A_{n}}f(u)-\int_{A_{n}}f(u)\lambda(u)\mathrm{d}u\right\}.

(13)

Equation (
13
) can also be thought of a spatial empirical process due to its resemblance to empirical processes in i.i.d. settings.
A central part of our proof for asymptotic normality relies on studying the Taylor expansion of the plug-in error from the estimated nuisance parameter, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left\{\ell_{n}(\theta,\hat{\eta}_{\theta,n}^{(v)};X_{v})-\ell_{n}(\theta,\eta_{\theta,n};X_{v})\right\}\big|_{\theta=\theta^{*}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\ell_{n}(\theta^{*},\eta_{\theta^{*},n};X_{v})\left(\hat{\eta}_{\theta^{*},n}^{(v)}-\eta_{\theta^{*},n}\right)\right\}}_{:=a}+\underbrace{O_{p}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\|\hat{\eta}_{\theta^{*},n}^{(v)}-\eta_{\theta^{*},n}\|^{2}|A_{n}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)}_{:=b}.

The second term is well behaved under Assumption
4.3
, obeying
b=o_{p}(1)
. The first term can be written as the sum of two “spatial empirical processes”
a=\mathbb{G}^{X_{v}}_{n}[f_{1}]+\mathbb{G}^{X_{v}}_{n}[f_{2}]
where

\displaystyle f_{1}(u)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta\partial\eta}\log\lambda(u;\theta,\eta_{\theta,n})\bigg|_{\theta=\theta^{*}}\left\{\hat{\eta}_{\theta,n}^{(v)}(z_{u})-\eta_{\theta,n}(z_{u})\right\},

\displaystyle f_{2}(u)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\log\lambda(u;\theta,\eta_{\theta,n})\bigg|_{\theta=\theta^{*}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left\{\hat{\eta}_{\theta,n}^{(v)}(z_{u})-\eta_{\theta,n}(z_{u})\right\}.

When the spatial point process is Poisson, the random thinning guarantees that the nuisance estimation
\hat{\eta}^{(v)}_{\theta,n}
, which is estimated using the subprocess
X_{v}^{c}
, is independent with the subprocess
X_{v}
so both
\mathbb{G}^{X_{v}}_{n}[f_{1}]
and
\mathbb{G}^{X_{v}}_{n}[f_{2}]
can be bounded with the point process central limit theorem (e.g.,
Biscio & Waagepetersen 2019
).

Now suppose the process is not necessarily Poisson, but the intensity function is log-linear. In this case, the second-order derivatives of
\log(\lambda;\theta,\eta)
equal to zero:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta^{2}}\log\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta^{2}}\left\{\theta^{\top}y_{u}+\eta(z_{u})\right\}=0,

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta\partial\eta}\log\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta\partial\eta}\left\{\theta^{\top}y_{u}+\eta(z_{u})\right\}=0.

(15)

Consequently, equation (
15
) implies that
f_{1}(u)\equiv 0
so that the spatial empirical process
\mathbb{G}^{X_{v}}_{n}[f_{1}]
is zero. Also, the second-order derivative of the pseudo log-likelihood in (
2
) depends on (
14
) and (
15
) via

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta^{2}}\ell(\theta,\eta;X)=-\int_{A}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta^{2}}\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)\mathrm{d}u,\quad\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta\partial\eta}\ell(\theta,\eta;X)=-\int_{A}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta\partial\eta}\lambda(u;\theta,\eta)\mathrm{d}u.

(16)

We now provide a sketch of the proof of the independence between
f_{2}
and
X\cap A
, allowing the second spatial empirical process term
G_{n}^{X_{v}}[f_{2}]
to be bounded using the point process central limit theorem. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the function class corresponding to a nonparametric estimator, and
\hat{\eta}_{\theta}=\arg\max_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}}\ell(\theta,\eta;X)
. By the push-forward measure and equation (
16
), the nuisance estimation
\hat{\eta}_{\theta}
satisfies:

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\ell(\theta,\hat{\eta}_{\theta};X)\right)=

\displaystyle\int_{A}\exp[\theta^{\top}y_{u}+\hat{\eta}_{\theta}(z_{u})]\left(y_{u}\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\hat{\eta}_{\theta}(z_{u})\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\exp[\theta^{\top}y+\hat{\eta}_{\theta}(z)]\left(y+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\hat{\eta}_{\theta}(z)\right)f(y,z)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z.

As a reminder,
f(y,z)
is the Radon–Nikodym derivative in definition
4.5
.
Then, the derivative of
\hat{\eta}_{\theta}(z)
is:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\hat{\eta}_{\theta}(z)=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}y)yf(y,z)\mathrm{d}y\right\}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\theta^{\top}y)f(y,z)\mathrm{d}y.

Since
f(y,z)
is determined by the map
u\mapsto(y_{u},z_{u})
, the derivative of
\hat{\eta}_{\theta}(z)
is independent with
X\cap A
and in doing so, we can bound
\mathbb{G}^{X}_{n}[f_{2}]
; see Sections 11.8 and 11.9 of the Supplementary Material for details. In particular, the independence can be achieved with both kernel and spline estimators of
\eta_{\theta}
.

5
Semiparametric Efficiency Lower Bound for Poisson Spatial Point Process

We derive the semiparametric efficiency lower bound of estimating
\theta
for semiparametric Poisson spatial point processes. Similar to the derivation of the semiparametric efficiency lower bound with i.i.d data, our derivation proceeds by (a) constructing a class of parametric submodels of Poisson spatial point processes and (b) finding the supremum of the Cramer-Rao bound over the class of submodels, which is the semiparametric efficiency lower bound. Most importantly, if the process is Poisson, the asymptotic variance of our estimator equals this lower bound.

First, consider a parametric submodel of
X
with intensity function
\lambda(u;\theta,\bar{\eta}_{\theta})
where
\bar{\eta}_{\theta}
is a twice continuously differentiable mapping from
\Theta
to
\mathcal{H}
. We denote such mappings by
\bar{\eta}_{\theta}
to distinguish from
\eta_{\theta}
in (
3
). When
\bar{\eta}_{\theta}
is known,
from
Rathbun & Cressie 1994
, the Cramer-Rao lower bound of estimating
\theta
in the submodel
is the inverse of the sensitivity matrix

S^{-1}(\theta^{*},\eta^{*},\nu)
:

S(\theta^{*},\eta^{*},\nu)=\int_{A}\lambda(u;\theta^{*},\eta^{*})\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log\lambda(u;\theta^{*},\eta^{*})+\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\log\lambda(u;\theta^{*},\eta^{*})\left\{\nu(z_{u})\right\}\right]^{\otimes 2}\mathrm{d}u.

(17)

An important observation from (
17
) is that
at the true
\theta^{*}
and
\eta^{*}
,
the Cramer-Rao lower bound depends on
\nu
, the derivative of
\bar{\eta}_{\theta}
with respect to
\theta
evaluated at
\theta^{*}
. In other words, finding the worst-case Cramer-Rao lower bound among all parametric submodels is equivalent to finding
v
that maximizes the Cramer-Rao lower bound and Theorem
5.1
derives the supremum of the Cramer-Rao lower bounds over all parametric submodels of
X
.

Theorem 5.1
(Semiparametric Efficiency Lower Bound for Semiparametric Poisson Process).

The supremum of the Cramer-Rao lower bound (i.e., the minimum of (
17
)) is attained at
\nu^{*}
defined in (
8
) and is equal to

\nu^{*}(z)=-\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta^{2}}E\left\{\ell(\theta^{*},\eta;X)\mid z\right\}\bigg|_{\eta=\eta^{*}}\right\}^{-1}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta\partial\eta}E\left\{\ell(\theta^{*},\eta;X)\mid z\right\}\bigg|_{\eta=\eta}.

(18)

The term
\nu^{*}
in Theorem
5.1

can be estimated by using any consistent estimator of the spatial conditional expectation
E\left\{\ell(\theta^{*},\eta;X)\mid z\right\}
in equation (
18
). One example estimator is the kernel regression in (
7
).

Theorem
5.2
shows that the proposed estimator achieves the semiparametric efficiency lower bound when
X
is a Poisson spatial point process.

Theorem 5.2
(Semiparametric Efficiency of Proposed Estimator).

Suppose Assumptions
4.2
-
4.3
hold. Then,
\hat{\theta}_{n}
is semiparametrically efficient if
X
is Poisson and the semiparametric efficiency lower bound is
S_{n}^{-1}(\theta^{*},\eta^{*},\nu^{*})
.

A natural follow-up question from Theorem
5.2
is whether the proposed estimator is efficient for non-Poisson processes. When the process
X
is Poisson, the pseudo-likelihood in (
2
) collapses to the true likelihood of
X
, and the efficiency bound is well-defined. However, the likelihood of a non-Poisson spatial point process typically does not have a closed form and can only be approximated by numerical methods; see Chapter 8 in
Moller & Waagepetersen 2003
for related discussions. Based on this, we conjecture that establishing a semiparametric efficiency lower bound for a general process
X
would likely require a different approach; see Section
7
for more discussions.

We apply our method to estimate the partial effect of elevation (i.e., the target covariate

z_{u}
). The dataset consists of a 1000 × 500 meters plot from a tropical rainforest on Barro Colorado Island; see
Hubbell 1983
and
Condit et al. 1996

for further details. The points on Figure

show the location of the tree species.

7
Discussion

As laid out in Table
1
, there are some remaining challenges when
X
is not Poisson and the intensity function is not log-linear. We conjecture that establishing asymptotic normality of
\hat{\theta}
is possible under additional weak dependence restrictions on
X
and the pair correlation function. For establishing the semiparametric efficiency lower bound, we believe that directly utilizing the
classical definition of the semiparametric efficiency lower bound (i.e. likelihood-based parametric submodels and taking the supremum of Cramer-Rao lower bounds) is insufficient. Instead, we believe either restricting the type of estimators (e.g., linear estimators) or developing a new efficiency criterion is most promising; see
Park & Kang 2022
for a related discussion under network dependence. Overall, we believe our work presents a new perspective on studying semiparametric point processes, and we hope the techniques in the paper are useful to both researchers in point processes and in semiparametric inference.
