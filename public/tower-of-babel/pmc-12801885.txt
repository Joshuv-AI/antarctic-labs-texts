Feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of a telehealth supported self-management intervention for adults with depression symptoms in Vietnam: a mixed-method pre-post study
Authors: Nhu Kieu Tran, Trang Thuy Ngo, Thu Khac Nguyen, Jill K. Murphy, Hoang-Minh Dang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTelehealth has been proven to be an effective means of delivering psychotherapy, yet there is a lack of evidence in Vietnam where access to psychotherapy is limited. We conducted a study to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of Tele-SSM—a mobile health-supported self-management intervention for depression in Vietnam.MethodsThis was a pre-post, mixed-methods study. Adults aged between 18 and 64 years old with symptoms of depression were recruited and participated in 10 individual weekly sessions delivered remotely by mental health para-professionals. Feasibility was assessed by recruitment capacity, retention, and participant compliance; acceptability by participant satisfaction ratings and in-depth interviews; and preliminary efficacy by changes in depression, anxiety, stress, suicidal ideation, self-esteem, and perceived social support scores from baseline to endline.ResultsSeventy-five adults with depression were enrolled and 58 of those completed the intervention. Feasibility was adequate as recruitment, retention, and compliance all exceeded the predefined progression criteria. Recruitment reached 93.75% (progression criterion of ≥70%), retention was 77% (progression criterion of ≥75%), and compliance was high, with 93.1% of participants completing the intervention within the expected number of sessions (progression criterion of ≥80%). The intervention was well-accepted by participants with 96% of participants reporting satisfaction with the materials and coaching sessions. The average number of coaching calls required to deliver 10 sessions was 10.81, suggesting that the intervention may need to be implemented over 11 calls. Additional emphasis should be placed on realistic thinking and non-violent communications skills as participants reported more difficulties with these components. Exploratory pre–post analyses suggested potential improvements across depression, anxiety, stress, suicidal ideation, self-esteem, and perceived social support; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given that the study was not powered to assess efficacy.ConclusionsThese results provide promising evidence for the adequate feasibility, acceptability and preliminary efficacy of Tele-SSM on depression. The intervention also has the potential to address anxiety and stress. These preliminary insights can inform the design of a future randomized hybrid effectiveness-implementation study, particularly with regard to recruitment, retention, adherence, and potential outcome trends.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov, NCT06456775. Introduction

Globally, depressive disorders are considered the largest factor contributing to Years Lived with Disability (YLD), and this burden resides mostly in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. Depression often has substantial negative impacts on people with the disorder and their families, and has been linked with enormous economic costs [2]. Depression has been associated with higher direct costs for treatment and higher indirect costs of lost or reduced productivity in all ages, both alone and as a comorbidity [3]. In a systematic review of suicide and depression in five South-East Asian countries, of all psychiatric issues, depressive disorders were the most closely associated with suicide attempts and/or suicide ideation [4, 5].

A range of treatment approaches is effective for depression in adults. Systematic reviews broadly confirm the efficacy of pharmacotherapy, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), counseling, and interpersonal psychotherapy [6, 7]. Despite the availability of clinic-based treatments, the majority of people with depression do not have contact with mental health professionals, in part because there is a lack of trained mental health professionals [8]. Supported self-management (SSM) for depression has the potential to overcome these barriers and has been used widely for patients with different levels of depression [9–11]. An SSM program is a psychosocial intervention based on CBT principles and employs a task-shifting approach which has been found to save costs and improve efficiency for healthcare activities in LMICs [9]. SSM has been used for both diagnosed and un-diagnosed patients, with varying program durations, ranging from 4 to 12 weeks, and by both mental health care professionals (psychologists) and health professionals (nurses, community health workers), lay counselors [9–14]. In the UK and Canada, low-intensity CBT in the form of guided self-help is now officially recommended for depression interventions in adults with mild to moderate depression [15, 16].

In limited resource settings, tele-mental health care is recommended alongside conventional treatments because of its flexibility and potential for increased cost-effectiveness [17]. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that computer-based CBT for depression with support from therapists, trainee therapists, other health professionals had a large effect size for self-reported depression level post-treatment (d = 1.35), with greater patient retention than self-help programs without support [18]. However, all studies included in this review/meta-analysis were conducted in developed countries and recommended that more research on computer-based CBT for depression in non-Western communities and LMICs should be conducted.

Recent studies in Vietnam have documented that the prevalence of depression was 12.7% among adults [19], 19.9% among adults with chronic diseases [20], and 26.4% among elderly people [21]. However, only a few pilot non-pharmacological interventions have been offered to people with depression [22]. For instance, a cluster-randomized controlled trial in Ha Nam province utilizing a task-shifting approach to depression management showed that the intervention was effective for moderate depression [23]. The intervention consisted of psychoeducation, counseling, and an 8-week yoga course delivered by nurses in a group setting. Another randomized controlled trial of an SSM intervention for depression in eight Vietnamese provinces demonstrated the effectiveness of community-based SSM in reducing symptoms in mildly to moderately depressed participants [14, 24]. The intervention was guided by commune-level lay social workers who provided assistance related to social work to residents in the commune. Despite encouraging efforts, depression care in Vietnam still suffers from limited availability of psychotherapy and psychosocial rehabilitation resources, shortages in human resources, and insufficient mental health care infrastructure [22].

Given the limited availability of interventions for depression in Vietnam and the strong potential for tele-health to improve accessibility to mental health care in LMICs [25], we conducted a mixed-method pre-post pilot study of a tele-SSM intervention for depression to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and the preliminary efficacy of this approach on depression and other mental health outcomes for adults with depression symptoms in Vietnam.

Method

This study was conducted from April 2021 through August 2023 in Vietnam by the Institute for Social Development Studies (ISDS), a local non-profit organization.

Participants and procedures

We recruited people ages 18–64 years old with depression symptoms and implemented a 10-week pre-post study in which participants received the phone-based intervention. The eligibility criteria included adults aged from 18 to 64 years, having a score on the PHQ-9 [26] greater than five. Any participants with severe mental health problems, i.e., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, recent suicide attempts, and/or with suicide plans, were excluded (current suicide ideation, but without recent suicide attempts and/or with suicide plans were not excluded). We excluded individuals with recent suicide attempts and/or with suicide plans for ethical and clinical safety reasons, as such cases require immediate crisis management and specialized care from mental health professionals. Tele-SSM was designed as a supported self-management program for depression delivered by para-professionals, not as an emergency intervention for acute suicide risk. This exclusion criterion was also intended to ensure participant safety and align with the task-shifting model employed. If during the assessment call, a research assistant identified signs and symptoms of other mental health problems but depression, they referred them to mental health services.

Participants’ ages 18–64 years were recruited online through social media from May 2022 to May 2023. Online announcements were posted on the Facebook page of the Institute for Social Development Studies (ISDS, the local NGO responsible for the research), in mental health community groups, and shared on personal Facebook pages by individuals interested in mental health. Facebook was chosen as a social media channel because in Vietnam, 84% of the population use Facebook, 51.1% of whom are women. The post included a brief description of the study, inclusion criteria, and a link to the online screening questionnaire on Kobotoolbox. Interested individuals first completed the PHQ-9 screening questionnaire online. Those meeting the inclusion threshold were subsequently contacted for a brief eligibility assessment via video call with a research assistant trained in the study protocol, who also explained the informed consent process. All research assistants had backgrounds in psychology and were not involved in the analysis of outcome quantitative data to minimize potential bias. Only participants who met the eligibility criteria during this second step were invited to complete the baseline survey and enroll in the study. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Participants then received the printed intervention manual and practice workbook through postal mail.

Screening and survey data were collected using Kobotoolbox, a secure, open-source platform for online and offline data collection widely used in research and humanitarian projects. It supports encrypted data transmission and is suitable for low-bandwidth environments, making it appropriate for remote mental health research in Vietnam.

Tele-SSM intervention

Intervention development process

Tele-SSM was developed based on the Live Happily intervention [27]. The Live Happily distance-delivered intervention is an individual-level psychosocial intervention developed based on an SSM approach to address stigma for youth living with HIV in Vietnam. In the Live Happily intervention, non-violent communication is integrated with CBT-based skills. In the study, depression was also reduced significantly among youth living with HIV [27]. To tackle depression more specifically, Tele-SSM made some modifications, including the addition of psychoeducation regarding depression and behavior activation skills. The principal investigator revised the participant manual based on the Live Happily manual. Five consultant experts in psychology, psychiatry, and community-based mental health care were involved in the refinement of the manual. The refined manual was pre-tested with 10 people experiencing depression before the intervention implementation.

The initial six sessions cover the foundational skills. Sessions 7 to 10 allow the participants to integrate previously learned skills into addressing common challenges and preventing relapse. Participants develop weekly practice plans with the support of the coach. The plans are customized to each patient (e.g., making a plan to practice relaxation skills, making a plan to practice realistic thinking).

The weekly criteria for progression are to (1) show an understanding of the skills by accurately responding to the questions given by the coach during each session; (2) create weekly plans for practicing the learned skills; and (3) successfully execute the weekly plans. If participants had difficulties in understanding the skill or practicing the skill in the previous week, the coach set up the skill again, and the participants have one more week to practice. A participant is considered to have completed the intervention after they completed 10 sessions. The duration of the program extends over 10 weeks, though the length of an individual’s participation may have been prolonged because of holidays or the participant’s need for additional time to improve their skills before proceeding to the next session.

To schedule the calls, we employed Google Calendar, utilizing only the participants’ study IDs to ensure confidentiality. The calls were recorded for quality assurance and saved in a double-encrypted computer system. To track the progress of the participants over the intervention, patient records were managed by a secured website using only the study ID and no identifiable information.

The intervention was designed to be delivered by para-professionals (so-called coaches) with health, public health, social sciences, or education backgrounds. Training and supervision were provided by a supervisor with a doctorate degree in social sciences and a master’s degree in public health, who had been trained as a Supported Self-Management (SSM) trainer and as a supervisor for tele-psychosocial interventions for parents of children with behavioral problems. Para-professionals completed a 140-h training program covering the intervention protocol, coaching techniques, and extensive role-play practice. Coaches listened to sample calls, learned the coaching protocols, and conducted mock calls with the supervisor. Supervisors reviewed all mock calls, provided detailed feedback, and granted approval only when coaches achieved a minimum adherence score of 80%. After being granted approval from the supervisor, the coaches delivered the intervention with 5 volunteer participants. At this stage, the supervisor listened to each call to provide feedback and quality assurance before the coaches progressed to the next session with a participant. Once coaches passed the testing phase, they were assigned study participants. All coaching calls were audio-recorded. A supervisor randomly audited recorded sessions as well as those flagged by the coaches, evaluating them against a structured fidelity checklist that included (1) adherence to session structure and manual content, (2) accuracy of skill delivery, (3) use of supportive communication techniques, and (4) participant engagement. A minimum 80% adherence score was required for fidelity. Weekly supervision meetings between the supervisor and the coaches were conducted to review cases, discuss challenges, and provide corrective feedback to maintain intervention fidelity.

Assessment

To assess the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of Tele-SSM, we used a mixed-methods approach.

Feasibility

Feasibility was assessed through the following outcome measures: (i) Recruitment capacity: the number of eligible patients that enrolled, the number of eligible patients that screened, the number that completed the intervention; (ii) Retention: the drop-out rate and reasons for drop-out, the completion rate; (iii) Compliance: the average number of coaching calls, the number of participants needing more than 10 coaching calls, reported difficulties during participating in the program, challenges in implementing the study.

Acceptability

For assessment of the acceptability of the intervention, we administered a satisfaction measurement tool at the endline survey. The tool assessed participants’ overall satisfaction with the program, satisfaction with the materials, coaching sessions, skills, and program duration.

Beyond that, ten participants who completed the intervention were purposively selected and invited to participate in in-depth interviews (IDIs) to capture a range of perspectives across age, gender, and personal circumstances. Outcome measure

Baseline

Upon enrollment, participants completed a self-administered baseline survey in Kobotoolbox. This survey encompassed socio-demographic information, such as age, gender, ethnicity, education, and job, as well as psychosocial well-being (depression, anxiety, stress, quality of health, self-esteem, and perceived social support).

To measure depression, we used the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [26], which has been validated in Vietnam [30, 31]. At baseline, this measure demonstrated good reliability in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.80).

Anxiety and stress were measured with the 21-item Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21) [32, 33]. The validity of the scale has been previously studied in Vietnam [34, 35] and demonstrated excellent internal reliability in our study at baseline (Stress subscale: Cronbach’s α = 0.90; Anxiety subscale: Cronbach’s α = 0.90).

Suicide ideation was measured using a single item in the PHQ-9 [26].

Self-esteem was measured using Rosenberg’s Self-esteem Scale [36]. At baseline, the full scale demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.82).

Perceived social support was measured by the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) [37]. At baseline, the full scale demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.90).

Endline

At the end of the intervention, the participants completed an endline survey in Kobotoolbox. In addition to the same questions as in the baseline survey, the follow-up survey included quantitative questions about the participants’ satisfaction with the intervention as mentioned above.

Our primary hypothesis was that the Tele-SSM intervention delivered by telephone is feasible and acceptable to implement in Vietnam and would result in the reduction of depression symptoms. Outcomes were the feasibility and the acceptability of the intervention and the difference between pre-and post-test depression, anxiety, stress, suicidal ideation, perceived social support, self-esteem.

Incentives

Participants received a small incentive (VND 500,000 (US$22)) at the end of the intervention when the post-assessment was completed.

All procedures performed in the study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional ethical committee and were approved by the Institutional Review Board at ISDS (IRB00011703) on 05th May 2021 in Hanoi.

Sample size considerations

As this study was designed as a pilot feasibility trial, the sample size was determined in accordance with the CONSORT Extension for Pilot and Feasibility Trials [38] to ensure adequate precision in estimating key feasibility parameters (recruitment, retention, and participant compliance) rather than to provide statistical power for detecting treatment effects. Based on available resources, expected recruitment capacity, and the need to generate stable estimates of feasibility indicators, we aimed to recruit approximately 70 participants. This target was considered sufficient to provide reasonably precise estimates of recruitment, retention, and completion rates for planning a future randomized trial. Ultimately, 75 participants were enrolled, which allowed us to assess the predefined feasibility criteria and obtain exploratory information on outcome trends.

Data management

Participants completed pre- and post-questionnaires online using Kobotoolbox. The in-depth interviews were audio recorded and typically lasted 60 min. Audio recordings were subsequently transcribed. Participant records were managed by a secured website.

Data analysis plan

Qualitative analysis methods

The 10 post-intervention IDIs were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim by two researchers, one of whom was the interviewer. Both researchers independently reviewed the transcripts and interpretations, and any discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached to ensure reliability. Coding combined inductive and deductive approaches, using pre-identified themes such as participants’ experiences with coaching calls, difficulties encountered during the intervention, evaluation of the program, and suggestions for improvement, while identifying new themes from within the dataset. The first author conducted the initial coding, and a second researcher reviewed a random subset of coded transcripts to verify coding consistency and ensure inter-coder reliability. Data were organized and coded using Microsoft Excel®. Excel was deemed appropriate given the manageable dataset size and was primarily employed for data organization and thematic grouping following standard qualitative analysis procedures. In-depth interviews did not contribute to the assessment of the hypothesis but facilitated the identification of numerous factors that influenced the feasibility, acceptability, and primary efficacy of the intervention.

Quantitative analysis method

We calculated descriptive statistics for all variables (means, ranges, standard deviations, percent data missing, and frequencies for categorical variables). To compare the characteristics between the group that completed the intervention and the dropped-out group and explore the changes in psychosocial well-being before and after the intervention of the group that completed the intervention, 95% confidence intervals were computed. The differences were considered significant if 95% confidence intervals did not overlap. Effect sizes of the changes in psychosocial well-being before and after the intervention were calculated by dividing the z value by the square root of the number of observations over the two time points [39]. Within-subject correlations were not used because the analysis focused on estimating group-level change and variability to inform future trial planning. This approach yields a more conservative estimate, consistent with the exploratory nature of this feasibility study. For the acceptability, frequencies for questions on satisfaction were calculated from endline data. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion for each analysis to maximize available data without imputation.

Retention

Ultimately, 58 participants completed the intervention, representing a retention rate of 77%, which exceeded the predefined progression criterion of 70%. The dropout rate was 23%, with 17 individuals discontinuing their participation. Among the reasons for dropping out, six participants were facing depression too severe to be addressed remotely by para-professionals. These were referred for specialist care; three participants felt that they had improved sufficiently and no longer required the intervention, one individual cited a busy schedule as the reason for discontinuation, two individuals were uncomfortable discussing their private life, two found specific skills of the intervention challenging, two desired more frequent contact with their coach than the weekly basis provided, and one individual was concurrently involved in more than one intervention.

Compliance

The average number of coaching calls is 10.81 per participant. Table 3 describes the practice of weekly plans of participants. Twenty-five participants (43.10%) succeeded in 10 weekly plans and were able to complete 10 sessions in 10 coaching calls. 24 participants (41.40%) have 1 weekly plan requiring 2 coaching calls to complete. Nine participants (15.52%) have more than 1 weekly plan requiring 2 coaching calls. Realistic thinking and non-violent communication are the two skills that usually need 2 coaching calls to complete. A total of 93.1% of participants completed the intervention within 10–12 coaching calls, exceeding the predefined progression criterion for participant compliance (80%). Depression, psychosocial outcomes (pre- vs. post-intervention)

Depression

The mean score for depression decreased from pre-intervention (M = 12.71, SD = 4.08, 95%CI: 11.66–13.76) to post-intervention (M = 4.29, SD = 2.71, 95%CI: 3.59–4.99). A significant reduction in depression following participation in the intervention was found, with a large effect size (r = 0.61).

Anxiety and stress

Similar to depression, we found a significant decrease in anxiety and stress after participating in the intervention, with a medium effect size for anxiety (r = 0.46) and a large effect size for stress (r = 0.55). The anxiety mean score decreased from 12.07 (SD = 7.59, 95%CI: 10.03–14.11) pre-intervention to 4.79 (SD = 4.61, 95%CI: 3.55–6.03) post-intervention and from 21.40 (SD = 8.95, 95%CI: 18.99–23.81) to 8.30 (SD = 5.84, 95%CI: 6.73–9.87) for stress. In both cases, the reductions were statistically significant as 95%CIs did not overlap.

Suicide ideation

Suicide ideation was reduced from pre-intervention (M = 0.48, SD = 0.10, 95%CI: 0.45–0.51) to post-intervention (M = 0.03, SD = 0.02, 95%CI: 0.02–0.04), with a large effect size (r = 0.55).

Self-esteem

Similar improvement was observed for self-esteem. The mean score increased from pre-intervention (M = 23.29, SD = 4.06, 95%CI: 22.25–24.33) to post-intervention (M = 28.38, SD = 3.51, 95%CI: 27.48–29.28), with a large effect size (r = 0.56).

Social support

There was a statistically significant increase in the overall social support measure from before the intervention (M = 3.93, SD = 1.40, 95%CI: 3.57–4.29) to after the intervention (M = 5.11, SD = 1.21, 95%CI: 4.80–5.42) with a large effect size (r = 0.51). Similar improvements were observed for family (pre-intervention: M = 3.72, SD = 1.72, 95%CI: 3.28–4.16; post-intervention: M = 4.74, SD = 1.63, 95%CI: 4.32–5.16, r = 0.44), friends (pre-intervention: M = 3.96, SD = 1.51, 95%CI: 3.57–4.35; post-intervention: M = 5.09, SD = 1.19, 95%CI: 4.78–5.40, r = 0.43), and significant others (pre-intervention: M = 4.11, SD = 1.83, 95% CI: 3.64–4.58; post-intervention: M = 5.49, SD = 1.47, 95%CI: 5.11–5.87, r = 0.47).

Discussion

This study is among the first studies in Vietnam to test the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of Tele-SSM, a telehealth-supported self-management intervention. The feasibility and acceptability indicators exceeded their predefined progression criteria among adults with symptoms of depression in Vietnam. Exploratory pre–post trends suggested potential improvements in symptoms of depression, anxiety, stress, and suicidal ideation, as well as self-esteem and social support; however, these trends are preliminary and should be interpreted cautiously.

Both quantitative and qualitative data on satisfaction showed that the intervention was well accepted by participants. Regarding the feasibility, the issue with internet stability could be resolved, especially with the development of future technology. The average number of coaching calls is 10.81 suggesting that the intervention may need to be delivered in 11 calls or more support could be provided especially with realistic thinking and non-violent communication skills, the two skills that participants had more difficulties with. The proportion of retention rate the Tele-SSM intervention with para-professional support was 77%, comparable with computer-based CBT interventions with therapist support in a meta-analysis of computer-based CBT interventions [18]. However, several reasons for discontinuation point out some areas for improvement of retention rate. Six participants were referred to specialist care due to severe depression, suggesting that more robust criteria and multi-step screening procedures are needed to better match participants to the level of support provided. A small number of participants reported discomfort in discussing their private life and some desired more frequent contact than the weekly schedule, underscoring the importance of clearly communicating during recruitment and orientation that sharing personal experiences is an integral part of the intervention and explaining the fixed structure of session frequency. These findings will inform adaptations to improve participant fit, expectation management, and intervention delivery in future trial.

Reflections on training and supervision highlight important considerations for scaling Tele-SSM. The 3-month training and intensive supervision model, including mock calls and fidelity audits, with a minimum 80% adherence score of coaching calls required for fidelity, ensured the quality of intervention delivery in this pilot. For larger-scale implementation, potential strategies include developing a tiered supervision structure and incorporating asynchronous digital training modules to maintain quality that could be solutions. Digital access was not a major barrier in this sample, which aligns with the rapid expansion of internet infrastructure in Vietnam; however, future trials should continue to account for potential connectivity challenges in rural areas. Feasibility findings—including a 77% completion rate, variability in coaching time, and the proportion of participants requiring more than 10 sessions—underscore the importance of building flexible session structures and robust digital infrastructure when planning for scale-up and integration into routine care.

Regarding preliminary efficacy, the mean post-intervention effect size on depression was 1.17, similar to the effect size of computer-based CBT interventions with therapist support in the meta-analysis (d = 1.35), and greater than the effect size of a computer-based CBT intervention with administrative support (d = 0.95) [18]. Given that the intervention is delivered remotely by para-professionals, the findings for the mental health outcomes are promising, although the results should be interpreted with caution as this was a small pilot study without a control group [28, 29].

One possible hypothesis to explain the effect size of Tele-SSM could be the integration of non-violent communication with CBT. The World Health Organization’s mhGAP Intervention Guide recommends interpersonal therapy as a first-line treatment for depression given that interpersonal challenges frequently trigger depressive episodes [40], and non-violent communication directly addresses interpersonal relationships. The Tele-SSM curriculum also covers topics in interpersonal therapy including grief, disputes, and life changes in the sessions on major events, stepping out of one’s comfort zone, and dealing with anger, which may have enhanced its impact. The significant improvement after the intervention of participants in the social scale suggests that the intervention positively affected interpersonal relationships. Regarding self-esteem, a meta-analysis has found that low self-esteem predicts depression/anxiety, and depression/anxiety further erodes self-esteem [41]. In this study, we found a significant improvement in self-esteem, which could also contribute to the improvement in symptoms of depression and anxiety. The combination of CBT and non-violent communication may therefore have produced a synergistic effect. However, the large effect size observed should be interpreted with caution as all outcomes were self-reported, and no control group was included; the observed change may also reflect measurement effects, natural recovery, or the drop outs of some severe cases.

The reduction of suicidal ideation is also notable, as suicide is a significant public health concern globally, but particularly challenging to address in limited resource settings. The literature on evidence-based suicide interventions is virtually nonexistent in Vietnam. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that CBT reduced both levels of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts [42]. Tele-SSM is therefore potentially valuable/relevant in that context.

Limitations

Although the findings are promising, the study has some limitations. First, the exploratory results should be interpreted with caution because the pre-post design without a control group inherently carries several sources of potential bias which might contribute to the changes in outcomes, such as exposure to information outside the intervention and self-resilience over time. A randomized controlled trial of the Tele-SSM intervention in the future could resolve the issue. Second, as all outcome data were based on self-report measures, the results may be subject to reporting bias, including recall and social desirability bias. Third, the sample was small and primarily female and from 18 to 49 years. Although the intervention is designed for adults from 18 to 64 years old, it was hard to reach the older group, and only 5.2% of the participants were 50–64 years old. This could be due to a limited understanding of depression and internet/technology literacy on the part of older persons. It was also challenging to recruit male participants, and 90% of participants were female. Although the documented risk of depression among females is about double that among males in Vietnam, gender norms in Vietnam hold that men should be strong, should not show weakness, and must provide for themselves and their families, all of which could inhibit the disclosure of mental health issues and mental health-seeking behaviors among men. Thus, our small sample may not be reflective of the true population at risk of depression in Vietnam, which could limit generalizability to adult populations with depression, particularly men and older individuals. Another limitation is that the sample was largely urban and well-educated, which also limits the generalizability of the study. However, with the high speed of technology development and globalization, which has significantly improved awareness of mental health in Vietnam, the intervention has high potential to reach a broader population.

Conclusion

The Tele-SSM intervention was feasible and highly acceptable, with recruitment, retention, and compliance exceeding their predefined progression criteria. Although the study was not powered to evaluate efficacy, exploratory pre–post findings suggested potential improvements in depression, anxiety, stress, suicidal ideation, self-esteem, and perceived social support; these results should be interpreted cautiously. The findings can inform the planning of a future randomized effectiveness-implementation hybrid type 2 study, though further formative research is needed to refine the design. The randomized effectiveness-implementation hybrid type 2 study could explore both the outcome effectiveness of the intervention as well as explore implementation strategies for the sustainability of the intervention. Feasibility findings suggest the need to strengthen screening procedures and to consider flexible session structures or additional support for skills such as realistic thinking and non-violent communication. The results also highlight the importance of maintaining intensive training and supervision while exploring tiered supervision and digital training modules, which future implementation research could further investigate. The study should emphasize recruitment and retention of men and older individuals, taking into account research on knowledge, attitudes, and practices about depression care among these populations, and potentially adapting the intervention accordingly.
