Abstract
Researchers often use specifications that correctly estimate the average treatment effect under the assumption of constant effects. When treatment effects are heterogeneous, however, such specifications generally fail to recover this average effect. Augmenting these specifications with interaction terms between demeaned covariates and treatment eliminates this bias, but often leads to imprecise estimates and becomes infeasible under limited overlap. We propose a generalized ridge regression estimator, regulaTE , that penalizes the coefficients on the interaction terms to achieve an optimal trade-off between worst-case bias and variance in estimating the average effect under limited treatment effect heterogeneity. Building on this estimator, we construct confidence intervals that remain valid under limited overlap and can also be used to assess sensitivity to violations of the constant effects assumption. We illustrate the method in empirical applications under unconfoundedness and staggered adoption, providing a practical approach to inference under limited overlap.



Key words:
heterogeneous treatment effects, limited overlap, ridge regression

JEL classification codes:
C12, C21, C51.

Introduction

In many empirically relevant settings for causal inference, correctly specifying the model requires allowing for fully flexible treatment effect heterogeneity as a function of covariates. However, estimating such rich models is often difficult or infeasible in practice due to limited overlap in covariate distributions. Consequently, estimating the fully flexible model may not be desirable in empirical applications, particularly when researchers are primarily interested in the average treatment effect rather than the full distribution of heterogeneous effects. Consider the unconfoundedness design, where the treatment is assumed to be as good as randomly assigned conditional on the covariates. Despite the availability of semi- and nonparametric estimators for the average treatment effect, such as matching estimators or the augmented inverse propensity score weighted estimator (AIPW), many applied studies rely on conventional linear regressions. In particular, it is common to estimate a “short” regression of the form

Y_{i}=D_{i}\beta_{\text{short}}+X_{i}^{\prime}\gamma_{\text{short}}+u_{i},

and interpret the estimated regression coefficient
\hat{\beta}_{\text{short}}
as an estimate for the average effect (e.g.,
Martinez-Bravo 2014
;
Favara and
Imbs 2015
;
Michalopoulos
and Papaioannou 2016
;
Bobonis et al. 2016
;
Xu 2018
).

While allowing for fully heterogeneous treatment effects, semi- and nonparametric estimators typically rely on strong overlap to perform well, which requires sufficient treated and untreated observations at each value of the covariates. However, in many empirically relevant settings, there may be limited overlap, or even complete lack of overlap for some covariate values, causing semi- and nonparametric estimators to become highly variable or even undefined. For example,
D’Amour et al. 2021
argues that with high-dimensional covariates, limited overlap is inevitable unless the covariates are highly correlated. One response is to redefine the estimand as the average effect for a subpopulation with sufficient overlap, as in
Crump
et al. 2009
. But when treatment effects are heterogeneous, this subpopulation estimand may differ substantially from the average effect in the full population, which is often the primary object of interest.

There is growing recognition that treatment effect heterogeneity affects the interpretation of the “short” regression. In an influential study,
Angrist 1998
shows that under unconfoundedness, the “short” regression estimates a convex average of treatment effects associated with each covariate value.
Słoczyński 2022
further shows that this convex average corresponds to a weighted average of treatment effects on the treated and untreated, with counter-intuitive weights. Therefore, the “short” regression estimate is generally biased for the average effect. Similar concerns have been raised in recent studies of “short” regression for staggered adoption designs
(
de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille 2020
;
Goodman-Bacon 2021
)
. However, as asserted by
Angrist and
Pischke 2009
, if treatment effect heterogeneity is limited, the “short” regression estimate may still be preferable to unbiased but noisier semi- and nonparametric estimators of the average effect. First, when treatment effects are constant, the “short” regression is unbiased and most efficient under homoskedasticity. Second,
Angrist and
Pischke 2009
informally argue that the bias of the “short” regression remains small when treatment effects do not vary too much, and the resulting efficiency gain can still justify its use. Consequently, the confidence intervals (CIs) based on the “short” regression should have coverage close to the nominal level, making it a pragmatic choice in applied work.

In this paper, we formalize the argument of
Angrist and
Pischke 2009
and analyze the “short” regression through the lens of a bias–variance tradeoff. Under an explicit bound on the variance of treatment effect heterogeneity, we show that this tradeoff can be optimally resolved using a generalized ridge regression, which coincides with the “short” regression when heterogeneity is absent but otherwise can depart from the “short” regression if its bias is large. This approach is feasible even when the average effect is not point identified due to a complete lack of overlap. As the bound varies from zero upwards, the associated CIs achieve correct coverage under each restriction while remaining tight, offering a practical tool for assessing the sensitivity of “short” regression estimates to violations of constant effects. The proposed estimator, which we term
regulaTE
, is computationally efficient and we provide an implementation in our accompanying R package.

Our theoretical contribution is to develop finite-sample valid confidence CIs, under a specific restriction on treatment effect heterogeneity, based on any linear (in the outcome) estimator of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Y_{i}
, where the weights
a_{i}
depend on the covariates and treatments in the full sample. To this end, we first assume that the regression errors are normally distributed with known variance and treat the treatment and covariates as fixed. This setup allows us to explicitly calculate the worst-case finite-sample bias of the estimator under the assumed bound on the variance of treatment effect heterogeneity. Following
Armstrong and
Kolesár 2021
, we construct CIs by inflating the standard critical value to account for this bias, yielding intervals with guaranteed coverage even when the average effect is not point identified due to limited overlap.

We then show how to choose the weights
a_{i}
optimally to minimize the length of the resulting bias-aware CIs, building on the bias-aware inference framework of
Armstrong
et al. 2023
. Solving this optimization problem yields a generalized ridge regression with a specific ridge penalty on the coefficients associated with the interaction between treatment and demeaned covariates. This generalized ridge regression exhibits intuitive behavior. As the penalty approaches infinity, the estimator of the average effect converges to the “short” regression coefficient estimator. In contrast, as the penalty approaches zero, the estimator increasingly interpolates the treatment effects for observations with limited overlap, effectively targeting the average effect in the subpopulation with overlap.

Related literature.
Since the seminal work of
Angrist 1998
, a growing body of literature has characterized the bias of the “short” regression estimator under treatment effect heterogeneity, including
Gibbons
et al. 2019
,
Słoczyński 2022
, and
Goldsmith-Pinkham
et al. 2024
, among others. When an unbiased and regular estimator of the average effect exists,
Armstrong
et al. 2025
proposes averaging the “short” regression estimator with the unbiased estimator to optimally adapt to the bias–variance tradeoff under treatment effect heterogeneity. We extend this literature by proposing estimators that account for worst-case bias in linear regression models under restrictions on treatment effect heterogeneity, and therefore do not require the existence of an unbiased estimator. Our framework also allows for an explicit tradeoff between worst-case bias and variance to minimize the length of the confidence interval centered on the proposed estimator.

There are semi- and nonparametric estimators, such as the inverse propensity score weighted estimator, that remain unbiased for the average treatment effect even under unrestricted treatment effect heterogeneity. However, these estimators rely on strong overlap for consistency and asymptotic normality. When propensity scores approach 0 or 1, they can become highly variable, making the conventional normal approximation unreliable, as demonstrated in
Rothe 2017
;
Heiler and
Kazak 2021
. To address extreme values of propensity scores,
Crump
et al. 2009
propose trimming the inverse propensity score weighted estimator by removing observations with estimated propensity scores outside the range
(0.1,0.9)
. Since this approach only estimates the average effect for a subpopulation with strong overlap, several papers have characterized the resulting bias and proposed bias correction methods, e.g.,
Ma and Wang 2020
and
Sasaki and
Ura 2022
, under certain smoothness conditions. Our method provides an alternative route for dealing with limited overlap: rather than focusing on a subpopulation with strong overlap, we restrict treatment effect heterogeneity and use this restriction to infer the worst-case heterogeneity for covariate cells with limited overlap.

Several papers have proposed estimators for the average effect under restrictions on treatment effects. In the context of the unconfoundedness design,
Lechner 2008
assumes outcomes are bounded,
Athey
et al. 2018
assume sparsity of effects,
Armstrong and
Kolesár 2021
assume smoothness of effects with respect to covariates, and
de Chaisemartin 2024
impose bounds on effects. In the context of difference-in-differences,
Manski and
Pepper 2018
impose bounds on the variation in outcomes over time and across states. In contrast to these papers, our method is tied directly to a measure of treatment effect heterogeneity by restricting the variance of treatment effects. Under this restriction, the optimal estimator is a generalized ridge regression, and we characterize its behavior as the ridge penalty varies.

2
Setup and treatment effect heterogeneity

This section introduces the fixed-design model, focusing on estimation of the average effect under the unconfoundedness assumption to illustrate the key ideas. The setup for staggered adoption is deferred to Section
5
. We present several common estimators and examine their bias due to treatment effect heterogeneity. We then discuss reasonable bounds on treatment effect heterogeneity.

2.1
Setup

We observe a random sample of
n
units. Each unit is characterized by a pair of
potential outcomes
(Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1))
under no treatment and treatment,
respectively; a flexible transformation of covariates
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}

that includes a constant; and a treatment indicator
D_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. We assume
unconfoundedness (also known as selection on observables):

{Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1)}\perp D_{i}\mid X_{i}
. We further assume that

\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)\mid X_{i}]
and
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)\mid X_{i}]
are both linear in
X_{i}
. Note
that this is without loss of generality when
X_{i}
is generated by saturating
discrete covariates.

Unless stated otherwise, we condition on the realized values
\{x_{i},d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of the covariates and treatment
\{X_{i},D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where lowercase denotes realizations. Probability statements are therefore with respect to the conditional distribution of
\{Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1)\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Under these assumptions, the DGP for the observed outcome
Y_{i}
can be written as

Y_{i}=d_{i}\tau(x_{i})+x_{i}^{\prime}\gamma+\varepsilon_{i},

(1)

where
\tau(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)\mid x_{i}=x]
is the covariate-specific average treatment effect (CATE), and
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a mutually independent noise term that is mean independent of
(X_{i},D_{i})
. This fixed-design framework follows, e.g.,
Rothe 2017
and
Armstrong and
Kolesár 2021
, to investigate finite-sample properties.

Our goal is to estimate a weighted average of the CATEs with known weights, with the weights reflecting a predetermined target population. The leading example is the sample average treatment effect (ATE)
\beta=\beta^{\text{ATE}}=\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tau(x_{i})]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau(x_{i})
as defined in
Imbens and
Wooldridge 2009
. Here
\mathbb{E}_{n}
denotes the “empirical” mean (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{n}a_{i}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
for any sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
). Given linearity, we can express
\tau(x_{i})=\alpha+x_{i,-1}^{\prime}\delta
where
x_{i,-1}
is the vector of covariates excluding the constant. Denoting
\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}=x_{i,-1}-\mathbb{E}_{n}[{x}_{i,-1}]
as demeaned covariates, we capture the deviation in treatment effects from the ATE by
\tau(x_{i})-\beta^{\text{ATE}}=\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\delta
. Therefore the DGP (
1
) can be reparameterized as the
long regression
:

Y_{i}=d_{i}\beta^{\text{ATE}}_{\text{long}}+(d_{i}\tilde{x}_{i,-1})^{\prime}\delta+x_{i}^{\prime}\gamma+\varepsilon_{i},\ \text{where }\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}=x_{i,-1}-\mathbb{E}_{n}[{x}_{i,-1}].

(2)

Other leading target parameters are the sample average treatment effect on the
treated (ATT) and the sample average treatment effect on the untreated (ATU).
For
\beta^{\text{ATT}}=\mathbb{E}_{n}[d_{i}\tau(x_{i})]/\mathbb{E}_{n}[d_{i}],
the deviation of
treatment effects from the ATT is

\tau(x_{i})-\beta^{\text{ATT}}=\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\delta
, where

\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}=x_{i,-1}-\mathbb{E}_{n}[d_{i}{x}_{i,-1}]/\mathbb{E}_{n}[d_{i}]
denotes
covariates demeaned by their average among the treated. The corresponding long
regression is

Y_{i}=d_{i}\beta^{\text{ATT}}_{\text{long}}+(d_{i}\tilde{x}_{i,-1})^{\prime}\delta+x_{i}^{\prime}\gamma+\varepsilon_{i},\ \text{where }\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}=x_{i,-1}-\mathbb{E}_{n}[d_{i}{x}_{i,-1}]/\mathbb{E}_{n}[d_{i}].

For
\beta^{\text{ATU}}=\mathbb{E}_{n}[(1-d_{i})\tau(x_{i})]/\mathbb{E}_{n}[1-d_{i}],
the deviation of
treatment effects from the ATU is

\tau(x_{i})-\beta^{\text{ATU}}=\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\delta
, where

\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}=x_{i,-1}-\mathbb{E}_{n}[(1-d_{i}){x}_{i,-1}]/\mathbb{E}_{n}[1-d_{i}]
denotes
covariates demeaned by their average among the untreated. The corresponding long
regression is

Y_{i}=d_{i}\beta^{\text{ATU}}_{\text{long}}+(d_{i}\tilde{x}_{i,-1})^{\prime}\delta+x_{i}^{\prime}\gamma+\varepsilon_{i},\ \text{where }\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}=x_{i,-1}-\mathbb{E}_{n}[(1-d_{i}){x}_{i,-1}]/\mathbb{E}_{n}[1-d_{i}].

When the covariates
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are generated by saturating discrete covariates, such as in a stratified RCT, these long regression coefficient estimators are algebraically equivalent to the familiar inverse probability weighted (IPW) estimators as shown in Lemma
A.2
.

We define the short regression as the specification that omits the interaction terms between treatment and covariates:

Y_{i}=d_{i}\beta_{\text{short}}+x_{i}^{\prime}\gamma_{\text{short}}+u_{i},

(3)

which is the most commonly used specification in empirical research.

2.2
Challenges under treatment effect heterogeneity

Even when treatment effects are fully heterogeneous, the long regression remains correctly specified for identifying the average effect. However, estimating this model can be practically challenging. To better understand the impact of limited overlap on the finite-sample performance of the long regression, consider the case where the covariates
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are generated by saturating discrete covariates. Let
p(x_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{n}[d_{i}\mid x_{i}]
denote the fitted values from the sample linear regression of
d_{i}
on
x_{i}
, that is, the empirical propensity score for covariate value
x_{i}
. In this special case, Lemma
A.2
shows that the long regression coefficient estimator is algebraically equivalent to the following IPW estimator:

\hat{\beta}_{\text{long}}^{\text{ATE}}=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\frac{d_{i}-p(x_{i})}{p(x_{i})\left(1-p(x_{i})\right)}Y_{i}\right].

Because
p(x_{i})(1-p(x_{i}))
appears in the denominator, the variability of

\hat{\beta}_{\text{long}}^{\text{ATE}}
is heavily influenced by observations for
which
p(x_{i})
is close to zero or one. Furthermore, when overlap fails entirely for a particular covariate value, meaning there exists
x_{i}=x
such that
p(x)\in\{0,1\}
, then the long regression
\hat{\beta}_{\text{long}}
is not even well-defined.

Similar issues arise when the target parameters are ATT and ATU instead. For
estimating the ATT, the long regression coefficient estimator is algebraically
equivalent to the following IPW estimator:

\hat{\beta}_{\text{long}}^{\text{ATT}}=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\frac{d_{i}-p(x_{i})}{\left(1-p(x_{i})\right)\mathbb{E}_{n}[d_{i}]}Y_{i}\right].

For estimating the ATU, the long regression coefficient estimator is equivalent to the following IPW estimator:

\hat{\beta}_{\text{long}}^{\text{ATU}}=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\frac{d_{i}-p(x_{i})}{p(x_{i})\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{n}[d_{i}]\right)}Y_{i}\right].

Therefore, the variability of
\hat{\beta}_{\text{long}}^{\text{ATU}}
is heavily influenced by observations where
p(x_{i})
is close to zero, and the variability
\hat{\beta}_{\text{long}}^{\text{ATT}}
is heavily influenced by observations where
p(x_{i})
is close to one.

On the other hand, the short regression estimator is less affected by limited
overlap. By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL) theorem, the short regression
estimator of (
3
) can be expressed as

\hat{\beta}_{\text{short}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(d_{i}-p(x_{i})\right)Y_{i}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(d_{i}-p(x_{i})\right)d_{i}\right]}.

This representation highlights that the short regression places little weight on observations with extreme propensity scores, and it remains well-defined even when overlap fails for some covariate values since such observations have no influence on the estimator. However, by omitting interactions between
d_{i}
and
\tilde{x}_{i,-1}
, the short regression is generally misspecified under treatment effect heterogeneity. As a result, it may suffer from omitted variable bias, with the bias measured relative to the average effect target parameters such as the ATE, ATT and ATU. To better understand the nature of the bias in the short regression, again consider the case where the covariates
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are generated by saturating discrete covariates. From
Angrist 1998
, the estimand is:

\beta_{\text{short}}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\text{short}}]=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\frac{p(x_{i})\left(1-p(x_{i})\right)}{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[p(x_{i})\left(1-p(x_{i})\right)\right]}\tau(x_{i})\right].

(4)

This estimand coincides with
\beta
only if one of the following holds:
\tau(x_{i})
is constant,
p(x_{i})
is constant, or
p(x_{i})
is orthogonal to
\tau(x_{i})
. In other words, the short regression recovers a weighted average of the heterogeneous effects, where the weights depend on the conditional variance of treatment assignment. As discussed in
Poirier and
Słoczyński 2024
, the subpopulation for which the short regression estimand corresponds to a true average treatment effect can be small, and thus the internal validity of the short regression may be low.

Nevertheless, when treatment effect heterogeneity is limited, the short regression remains nearly unbiased and provides more precise estimates. This
point is made informally by
(
Angrist and
Pischke 2009
, Chapter 3.3.1)
“Of course,
the difference in weighting schemes is of little importance if
\tau(x)
does
not vary across cells (though weighting still affects the statistical efficiency
of estimators).” This observation partly explains why the short regression is significantly more common than the long regression in empirical practice.

However, substantial empirical evidence documents the presence of treatment
effect heterogeneity (see, e.g.,
Athey and
Imbens 2016
and

Davis and
Heller 2017
), rendering the assumption that such heterogeneity is
negligible overly restrictive in many settings. Given the bias-variance
trade-off between the short and long regressions, a natural question is: what is
the optimal estimator for the average effect when treatment effect heterogeneity
is limited but nonzero? To formalize this trade-off, we construct the optimal
estimator and CI in Section
3
. A key assumption in our
approach is that we can place an upper bound on the variance of the CATE,
\tau(x_{i})
, also known as VCATE. VCATE provides a natural measure of treatment effect heterogeneity, capturing the variation of effects across covariates. Other related measures have been considered in the literature.
de Chaisemartin 2024
imposes bounds on
\tau(x_{i})
, constraining both heterogeneity and the magnitude of the average effect
\beta
. In contrast, our focus on VCATE is scale-invariant with respect to
\beta
. Similarly,
Armstrong and
Kolesár 2021
consider a Lipschitz condition on
\tau(x)
, which controls the smoothness of treatment effects but does not directly quantify their overall variation.

3
Optimal estimation and inference under bounded heterogeneity

In this section, we derive optimal estimation and inference methods under the
fixed design model with Gaussian noise. We begin by showing how to construct
valid CIs based on estimators that are linear in outcomes, and we provide a method for constructing an optimal CI under a bound on treatment effect heterogeneity. Moreover, we show that imposing an
a priori
bound on treatment effect heterogeneity is necessary for informative inference. We illustrate the performance using simulations based on a data generating process closely calibrated to the original analysis of
Angrist 1998
.

3.1
Proposed method

Building on (
2
), the regression model we consider is

Y_{i}=d_{i}\beta+x_{i}^{\prime}\gamma+d_{i}\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\delta+\varepsilon_{i},

(5)

where
Y_{i}
is the outcome variable,
d_{i}
the (non-random) treatment variable,
and
x_{i}=(1,x_{i,-1}^{\prime})^{\prime}
is the vector of (non-random) covariates, or a
dictionary of covariates, that includes a constant term. To derive finite
sample optimality results, we assume
\varepsilon_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^{2})
, an assumption we relax in Section
4.1
. Under this parametric specification, once we condition on
x_{i}
, no further covariates contribute to treatment effect heterogeneity.

Section
2.1
establishes that the choice of target parameter
\beta
,
whether it be the ATE, ATT or ATU, only affects how
\tilde{x}_{i,-1}
is
demeaned. Therefore, the subsequent discussion focuses on the estimation of the
ATE for simplicity, while the theoretical guarantees remain valid for any choice
of
\beta
, leaving the specific choice to the empirical researcher.
Under the assumption of linearity and the long regression (
2
), VCATE can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}[(\tau(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tau(x_{i})])^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{n}[(\tilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\delta)^{2}]=\delta^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{x}_{i,-1}\tilde{x}^{\prime}_{i,-1}]\delta.

Accordingly, imposing an upper bound on VCATE of the form

\mathbb{E}_{n}[(\tau(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tau(x_{i})])^{2}]\leq C^{2}

amounts to a weighted quadratic constraint on the parameter vector
\delta
given by

\delta^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{x}_{i,-1}\tilde{x}^{\prime}_{i,-1}]\delta\leq C^{2}.

For simplicity, write
V_{x}:=\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{x}_{i,-1}\tilde{x}^{\prime}_{i,-1}]
, which is the sample analogue of
\text{Var}(X_{i,-1})
.

Hence, in the fixed design setting, the problem of estimating the ATE under a bound
on the VCATE is equivalent to estimating
\beta
in (
5
)
subject to the constraint
\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta\leq C^{2}
. In other words, the problem is to
conduct inference on
\beta
under the restriction that

(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})^{\prime}\in\Theta_{C}
where

\Theta_{C}=\{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2+2k}:\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta\leq C^{2}\}
.

Let
Y=\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the vector of outcomes. We focus on linear
estimators of the form
\hat{\beta}_{a}=a^{\prime}Y
,
where
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is non-random, and on confidence intervals based on such
estimators. Since we treat
(d_{i},x_{i}^{\prime})^{\prime}
as fixed, the weights are allowed to
depend on these variables, and thus typical estimators such as
difference-in-means, the short regression, and the long regression (if defined) are included
in this class.Furthermore, we restrict attention to fixed-length confidence
intervals of the form
\hat{\beta}_{a}\pm\chi_{a,C}
, a restriction known
to have minimal effect in terms of efficiency (
Donoho 1994
;
Low 1997
;
Armstrong and
Kolesár 2018
).

The variance of the estimator is given as
\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{a})=\sigma^{2}a^{\prime}a

and from (
5
), the worst-case bias is given by

\maxbias_{a,C}=\sup_{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\in\Theta_{C}}a^{\prime}(D\beta+X\gamma+(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta)-\beta

(6)

where
D=\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denotes the treatment vector,
X=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

denotes the matrix of covariates, and each row of
D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1}
is

d_{i}\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}.
Given the weights
a
, we can derive the
worst-case bias of the corresponding estimators. For example,
Appendix
A.3
derives the worst-case bias for
the short regression estimator.

A fixed-length confidence interval (FLCI) is then formed
by taking

\chi_{a,C}=\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{a})^{1/2}\text{cv}_{\alpha}\left(\maxbias_{a,C}/\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{a})^{1/2}\right),

(7)

where
\text{cv}_{\alpha}(B)
denotes the
1-\alpha
quantile of the folded normal
distribution,
\lvert N(B,1)\rvert
. The critical value therefore reflects the
potential (worst-case) bias of
\hat{\beta}_{a}
. Importantly, the validity of
the CI does not rely on point identification of
\beta
. In cases of complete lack of overlap,
\beta
is set identified rather than point identified under a bound on the VCATE. As argued in Lemma
1
, which is an immediate consequence of
Armstrong and
Kolesár 2018
, this CI maintains correct coverage for every value of
\beta
within this identified set.

Lemma 1
.

Under the assumption that VCATE is bounded by
C^{2}
and error terms are Gaussian
in (
5
), the worst-case coverage of this FLCI satisfies

\inf_{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\in\Theta_{C}}P(\beta\in[\hat{\beta}_{a}-\chi_{a,C},\hat{\beta}_{a}+\chi_{a,C}])\geq 1-\alpha.

Proof.

Under the stated assumptions, the z-statistic

(\hat{\beta}_{a}-\beta)/\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{a})^{1/2}
follows a normal
distribution
N(b,1)
, where the bias term
b
is bounded by

\lvert b\rvert\leq\maxbias_{a,C}
.
∎

Since
\hat{\beta}_{a}\pm\chi_{a,C}
forms a valid CI for any choice of weights

a
, the remaining task is to choose
a
so as to minimize the length of the
CI. This problem can be simplified using arguments from
Donoho 1994
and
Armstrong and
Kolesár 2018
. Observing that

\chi_{a,C}
is increasing in both
\maxbias_{a,C}
and

\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{a})
, we first trace out weights on the bias-variance frontier by
minimizing variance subject to a bound
B
on the worst-case bias:

\min_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{\prime}a\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sup_{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\in\Theta_{C}}a^{\prime}(D\beta+X\gamma+(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta)-\beta\leq B.

(8)

Let
a_{B,C}
denote the weights that solve this optimization problem. Then,
the remaining task is to simply choose
B
such that
\chi_{a_{B,C},C}
is
minimized. Hence, once we obtain a solution to (
8
), the problem
of minimizing CI length reduces to a univariate optimization problem.

It follows from Theorem 2.1 of
Armstrong
et al. 2023
that solving
(
8
) can be further simplified by a two-step procedure. In the
first step, we solve a penalized propensity score regression:

\min_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}}\lVert D-X\pi_{1}-(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\pi_{2}\rVert^{2}+\lambda\pi_{2}^{\prime}V_{x}\pi_{2},

(9)

with solution

\begin{pmatrix}\pi_{1,\lambda}\\
\pi_{2,\lambda}\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}X^{\prime}X&X^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\\
(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}X&(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})+\lambda V_{x}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}X^{\prime}D\\
(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}D\end{pmatrix}.

(10)

In the second
step, we use the residuals

\tilde{D}_{\lambda}:=D-X\pi_{1,\lambda}-(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\pi_{2,\lambda}
from this penalized propensity score
regression as an instrument for the regression of
Y
on
D
, yielding the
optimal weights

a_{\lambda}:=\frac{\tilde{D}_{\lambda}}{\tilde{D}_{\lambda}^{\prime}D}
. It turns
out that
a_{\lambda}
solves the optimization problem
(
8
) with a particular choice of
B
determined by

\lambda
. Therefore, linear estimators lying on the bias-variance
frontier take the form

\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}:=a_{\lambda}^{\prime}Y=\frac{\tilde{D}_{\lambda}^{\prime}Y}{\tilde{D}_{\lambda}^{\prime}D}.

(11)

Theorem
1
formalizes this result, derives the worst-case
bias for the estimator, and relates it to a generalized ridge regression estimator.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
X^{\prime}X
and
V_{x}
are both invertible. Then,

\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
solves both: 1) the generalized ridge least squares problem

\min_{\beta,\gamma,\delta}\lVert Y-D\beta-X\gamma-(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta\rVert^{2}+\lambda\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta,

(12)

and 2) the bias-variance trade-off problem (
8
) with

B=\maxbias_{a_{\lambda},C}
, where

\maxbias_{a_{\lambda},C}=C\frac{a_{\lambda}^{\prime}(X\pi_{1,\lambda}+(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\pi_{2,\lambda})}{\pi_{2,\lambda}^{\prime}V_{x}\pi_{2,\lambda}}.

(13)

Consequently, the worst-case bias of
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
is
\maxbias_{a_{\lambda},C}
.

Proof.

Since the definition of
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
involves

(\pi_{1,\lambda},\pi_{2,\lambda})
, we first show that
(
10
) is well defined and indeed solves
(
9
). Note that when
\lambda>0
the inverse exists provided

X^{\prime}X
and
V_{x}
are both invertible. To see this, write

P_{X}=X(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}
and
H_{X}=I-P_{X}
, and note that

\displaystyle(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})+\lambda V_{x}-(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}X(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}H_{X}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})+\lambda V_{x}.

The first term in the last line is positive semidefinite and the second
term
\lambda V_{x}
is positive definite, so the sum is positive
definite. By the block matrix inverse formula, the inverse
in (
10
) therefore exists. The definition of

(\pi_{1,\lambda},\pi_{2,\lambda})
then follows from the first-order conditions
of a Lagrangian.

That
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
solves (
12
) is an
immediate consequence of Lemma
A.1
. Applying Theorem 2.1 of
Armstrong
et al. 2023
with the
penalty
\mathrm{Pen}((\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})^{\prime})=\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta
, it
follows that
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
solves (
8
) with
B=\maxbias_{a_{\lambda},C}
as in (
13
). Finally, since the constraint in
(
8
) must bind,
\maxbias_{a_{\lambda},C}
is indeed the
worst-case bias of
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
.
∎

Hence, estimators that lie on the optimal bias-variance frontier take the form of the regression coefficient estimator
\hat{\beta}_{{\lambda}}
from a generalized ridge regression. In Section
3.3
, we further examine the behavior of the generalized ridge estimator
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
as
\lambda
varies. Since the class of generalized ridge estimators spans the bias-variance frontier, their behavior is of independent interest.

Now, the only remaining task is to choose
\lambda
so that half-length of the
corresponding CI is minimized:

\lambda^{\ast}_{C}:=\text{argmin}_{\lambda}\,\,\sigma\lVert a_{\lambda}\rVert\cdot\text{cv}_{\alpha}\left(\maxbias_{a_{\lambda},C}/(\sigma\lVert a_{\lambda}\rVert)\right),

(14)

which gives the (near-)optimal estimator
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda^{*}_{C}}=a^{\prime}_{\lambda^{\ast}_{C}}Y
and the corresponding CI
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda^{*}_{C}}\pm\chi_{a_{\lambda^{\ast}_{C}},C}
. By construction, this is the shortest
fixed-length CI based on linear estimators. We refer to this estimator as the
regulaTE
estimator, for its ability to regularize treatment effect heterogeneity.

3.2
Necessity of bounding VCATE

The previous section demonstrates how to construct
regulaTE
CIs given a
bound on VCATE. One might instead wish to construct a “wider” CI that remains valid under unrestricted treatment effect heterogeneity, or a CI that adapts to the true underlying VCATE—shrinking in length according to the true bound while still guaranteeing coverage over a broader class of heterogeneity. We show that neither approach is feasible, thereby establishing the necessity of imposing an
a priori
bound on VCATE.

Intuitively, absent any restriction on the parameter space, the worst-case bias
of any linear estimator must be unbounded when overlap fails. The reason is that
the data contains no information about treatment effects for strata in which
only treated (or untreated) individuals are observed. Formally, recall
from (
6
) that the worst-case bias is linear in the
parameters. Thus, only unbiased estimators have bounded bias (in fact, zero)
when the parameter space is unrestricted. As evident from the expression,
unbiasedness requires
a^{\prime}D=1
,
a^{\prime}X=0
and
a^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})=0
. Suppose overlap fails for the
j
th covariate so that
X_{j+1}\leq D
,
where
\leq
is interpreted elementwise. Writing

\bar{x}_{j+1}:=\mathbb{E}_{n}[x_{i,j+1}]
, the conditions for unbiasedness
imply
a^{\prime}D=1
,
a^{\prime}X_{j+1}=0
and
a^{\prime}(X_{j+1}+D\bar{x}_{j+1})=0
, but it
is clear that no weight vector
a
can satisfy these conditions.
2
2

2

This
relies on the fact that
D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1}=X_{j+1}+D\bar{x}_{j+1}
,
established in the proof of Proposition
A.1
.
Hence,
no unbiased linear estimator exists, and the worst-case
bias of
any
linear estimator is unbounded. A formal statement and proof
of this result can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

Moreover, it turns out that it is impossible to construct a CI that adapts to the
true VCATE. By definition, an adaptive CI has length that automatically reflects the true
magnitude of VCATE while maintaining coverage under a conservative
a priori

bound on VCATE. However, Corollary 2.1 of

Armstrong
et al. 2023
implies that any valid CI must have expected length that
reflects the conservative
a priori
bound
C^{2}
, even when VCATE is much smaller
than
C^{2}
. In other words, one cannot automate the choice of the VCATE
bound
C^{2}
when constructing the CI.

Therefore, while
C
is an important input to our method, it cannot be set to
C=\infty
, nor can it be selected in a data-driven way with the goal of adapting to the true VCATE. In Section
4.3
, we discuss how to conduct sensitivity analysis with respect to
C
and approaches for estimating VCATE.

3.3
Generalized ridge regression

In this section, we provide some intuition for how our proposed estimator
attains the frontier for bias-variance tradeoff in the normal fixed-design
setting and compare its behavior with familiar alternatives. As shown in
Theorem
1
, our estimator is equivalent to the

generalized ridge
regression coefficient estimator
from (
12
) for a specific choice of
\lambda_{C}^{\ast}
.
3
3

3

Generalized ridge regression has also been analyzed recently in
Bruns-Smith et al. 2025
in the context of augmented balancing weights.
This
estimator penalizes the weighted
\ell_{2}
norm
\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta
implied by
the regression model. The form of penalty is intuitive: as discussed in
Section
3.1
, the VCATE bound
\mathbb{E}_{n}[(\tau(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{n}\tau(x_{i}))^{2}]\leq C^{2}
restricts the the parameter space for
\delta
to the
ellipsoid
\{\delta:\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta\leq C^{2}\}
. Optimality of ridge
regression under quadratic constraints traces back to

Li 1982
.

Theorem
2
characterizes several properties of

\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
in the case of discrete covariates.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
X
is generated from saturating discrete covariates. Then the
generalized ridge regression coefficient estimator
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
can
be written as

\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[a_{\lambda,i}Y_{i}\right],\quad\text{where }a_{\lambda,i}=\frac{(d_{i}-p(x_{i}))(1-\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\pi_{2,\lambda})}{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[(d_{i}-p(x_{i}))(1-\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\pi_{2,\lambda})d_{i}\right]}

(15)

with
\pi_{2,\lambda}
denoting the regression coefficient estimator from a ridge regression of
(d_{i}-p(x_{i}))
on
(d_{i}-p(x_{i}))\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}
. It satisfies the following properties:

1.

As
\lambda\to\infty
,
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
converges to the short regression estimator
\hat{\beta}_{\text{short}}
.

2.

For fixed
\lambda>0
, the estimand of
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
is given by

\beta_{\lambda}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}]=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\frac{(p(x_{i})(1-p(x_{i})))(1-\widetilde{x}^{\prime}_{i,-1}\pi_{2,\lambda})}{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[(p(x_{i})(1-p(x_{i}))(1-\widetilde{x}^{\prime}_{i,-1}\pi_{2,\lambda})\right]}\tau(x_{i})\right].

(16)

3.

As
\lambda\to 0
,
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
converges to

(a)

the long regression estimator
\hat{\beta}_{\text{long}}
if
overlap holds for all covariate values,

(b)

otherwise, the long regression estimator
\hat{\beta}_{\text{long}}
restricted to the trimmed subsample with overlap.

Proof.

The equivalence in (
15
) follows from Theorem
A.1
.
Case 1 and 3(a) are immediate. Case 3(b) follows from
Lemma
A.4
. For Case 2, plugging in (
15
) yields

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[(1-\widetilde{x}^{\prime}_{i,-1}\pi_{2,\lambda})\mathbb{E}\left[(d_{i}-p(x_{i}))Y_{i}|x_{i}\right]\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[(p(x_{i})(1-p(x_{i}))(1-\widetilde{x}^{\prime}_{i,-1}\pi_{2,\lambda})\right]}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[(1-\widetilde{x}^{\prime}_{i,-1}\pi_{2,\lambda})p(x_{i})(1-p(x_{i}))\tau(x_{i})\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[(p(x_{i})(1-p(x_{i}))(1-\widetilde{x}^{\prime}_{i,-1}\pi_{2,\lambda})\right]}.

∎

Expression (
15
) highlights that
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}

avoids the small-denominator problem of the long regression and thus exhibits
lower variance. Observations without overlap exert no influence, ensuring the
estimator remains well-defined under limited overlap.

Relative to the short regression estimand in (
4
), expression (
16
) shows that
\hat{\beta}{\lambda}
redistributes weights to reduce worst-case bias subject to the VCATE bound. As shown in Proposition
A.2
, the worst-case bias of the short regression can be derived, allowing for
ex post
bias correction via an adjusted critical value of
\chi_{a_{\text{short}},C}
in (
7
), with coverage guarantee by Lemma
1
. However, the resulting bias-aware confidence intervals are longer than those based on
regulaTE
, which is designed to minimize CI length.

Alternatively, one can improve the precision of the long regression by trimming observations with limited overlap, as proposed by
Crump
et al. 2009
. While trimming improves variance, it introduces bias by changing the estimand to the average treatment effect over a subpopulation. To see this, under the case of discrete covariates, the trimmed regression estimand is

\beta_{\text{trim}}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\text{trim}}]=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}\{p(x_{i})(1-p(x_{i}))>c\}}{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\mathbf{1}\{p(x_{i})(1-p(x_{i}))>c\}\right]}\tau(x_{i})\right].

where, for example,
c=0.1\cdot 0.9
corresponds to trimming to the interval

[0.1,0.9]
. Under a VCATE bound, the worst-case bias for the trimmed
estimator can also be derived (Proposition
A.3
), and a
bias-aware CI based on the trimmed estimator can again be constructed via
Lemma
1
. But like the short regression, such bias-corrected CIs are
longer than those of
regulaTE
.

However, trimming maintains certain efficiency advantages, as shown in
Theorem
2
. As
\lambda\to 0
,
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}

converges to the long regression estimator
\hat{\beta}^{\text{long}}
restricted
to the subsample with overlap, i.e., trimming the propensity score support to

(0,1)
. Intuitively, as
\lambda\to 0
, the estimator behaves like matching
for strata with overlap, while for strata without overlap, the optimal estimator
interpolates across groups with overlap in order to minimize VCATE.

4
Practical considerations

Our discussion in Section
3
focused on the idealized
setting of Gaussian, homoskedastic errors
\varepsilon_{i}
with known
variance. We now turn to the more realistic setting with
heteroskedastic (possibly clustered), non-Gaussian errors and unknown
variance. We also provide guidance on the choice of
C
, which is a central
input to our method.

4.1
Implementation under more general error distributions

We begin with an initial estimator

\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{init}}=(\hat{\beta}^{\mathrm{init}},\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{init}}{}^{\prime},\hat{\delta}^{\mathrm{init}}{}^{\prime})^{\prime}
for

\theta=(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})^{\prime}
, obtained either from the long regression
(when defined) or from a cross-validated generalized ridge regression that
penalizes the weighted
\ell_{2}
norm

\mathbb{E}_{n}[(\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\delta)^{2}]
. Define the residuals from this initial
estimator

\hat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{init},i}=y_{i}-d_{i}\hat{\beta}^{\mathrm{init}}-x_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{init}}-d_{i}\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\hat{\delta}^{\mathrm{init}}
, and the corresponding
initial variance estimator

\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\varepsilon}_{\text{init},i}^{2}.

For each
\lambda
, compute
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}
as in (
11
)
and
\maxbias_{a_{\lambda},C}
as in (
13
). Then, obtain

\lambda^{*}_{C}
via (
14
), and form the robust variance estimator

\hat{V}_{\lambda^{\ast}_{C},\text{rob}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{\lambda^{\ast}_{C},i}^{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{\text{init},i}^{2},\quad\text{where }a_{\lambda^{\ast}_{C}}=\left(W^{\prime}W+\lambda^{\ast}_{C}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&V_{x}\end{pmatrix}\right)^{-1}e_{1}.

The feasible CI is then
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda^{*}_{C}}\pm\text{cv}_{\alpha}\left(\maxbias_{a_{\lambda^{*}_{C}},C}/\hat{V}_{\lambda^{*}_{C},\text{rob}}^{1/2}\right)\cdot\hat{V}_{\lambda^{*}_{C},\text{rob}}^{1/2}
. Cluster-robust versions follow analogously.

Remark 1
.

Due to the heteroskedastic nature of the error terms, the exact optimality results
stated in Section
3
in general no longer hold. Nonetheless,
the procedure mirrors the common practice of using weights that are optimal under
homoskedasticity (i.e., OLS weights) combined with robust standard errors.

4.2
Conditions for validity of feasible CI

The asymptotic validity of the feasible CI is formally shown in
Appendix
A.4
, which follows from a
central limit theorem (CLT) applied to
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda^{*}_{C}}
. The key requirement is that
the maximal Lindeberg weight associated with the estimator,

\text{Lind}(a_{\lambda^{*}_{C}})\coloneqq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{a_{\lambda^{*}_{C},i}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{\lambda^{*}_{C},j}^{2}}

shrinks sufficiently quickly relative to the error of the initial estimator used to form the residuals. Due to limited overlap, the error may vanish at a rate slower than the parametric rate of
n^{-1/2}.

Using (
15
), the maximal Lindeberg weight for the ridge estimator is

\displaystyle\text{Lind}(a_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{(d_{i}-p(x_{i}))^{2}(1-\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\pi_{2,\lambda})^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(d_{j}-p(x_{j}))^{2}(1-\widetilde{x}_{j,-1}^{\prime}\pi_{2,\lambda})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(d_{i}-p(x_{i}))^{2}(1-\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\pi_{2,\lambda})^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}p(x_{j})(1-p(x_{j}))(1-\widetilde{x}_{j,-1}^{\prime}\pi_{2,\lambda})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(d_{i}-p(x_{i}))^{2}(1-\widetilde{x}_{i,-1}^{\prime}\pi_{2,\lambda})^{2}}{\max_{1\leq j\leq n}p(x_{j})(1-p(x_{j}))\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1-\widetilde{x}_{j,-1}^{\prime}\pi_{2,\lambda})^{2}}.

Thus, when
\lambda
is strictly positive,

\widetilde{x}_{j,-1}^{\prime}\pi_{2,\lambda}
is finite, and thus a necessary condition for the Lindeberg condition when
\lambda^{*}_{C}
is strictly positive is
\max_{1\leq j\leq n}p(x_{j})(1-p(x_{j}))
being bounded away from zero, which is satisfied as long as at least one covariate bin has strong overlap.

4.3
Sensitivity analysis

In light of Section
3.2
, the researcher must choose an
a priori
bound on VCATE to implement our method. We discuss a few approaches to
choose this bound.

Researchers often use the short regression to estimate the ATE under the
(frequently implicit) assumption that treatment effect heterogeneity is not too
large. Our method provides a formal way to assess this assumption. In this case,
rather than taking a definitive stand on
C
, one can begin with
C=0
(which
corresponds to using the short regression) and gradually increase
C
until the
results become insignificant. One should evaluate whether the corresponding
breakdown point is plausible, which is feasible since VCATE is a highly
interpretable quantity. This is analogous to the breakdown frontier analysis
considered in, e.g.,
Masten and
Poirier 2020
and
Li and
Müller 2021
. Our R
package provides a plotting functionality that aids such sensitivity analysis.

A second approach is to estimate VCATE directly, either on the subsample with overlap or using prior evidence on covariates most associated with treatment effect variation.
4
4

4

For instance, one can set
C^{2}
equal to twice the upper CI bound of the estimated VCATE, analogous to bounding smoothness using observed estimates in bias-aware regression discontinuity inference
(
Imbens and
Wager 2019
;
Noack and
Rothe 2024
)
.
Using the long regression coefficient, one can obtain a
plug-in estimator for VCATE based on
\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}V_{x}\widehat{\delta}

where
\widehat{\delta}
is the OLS estimator. However, as

Kline
et al. 2020
emphasize, this estimator is
biased upward and with bias

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}V_{x}\widehat{\delta}]-\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta=\text{trace}\left(V_{x}\text{Var}(\widehat{\delta})\right).

Because the dimension of
\delta
is
k
, the bias can accumulate when
k
is
large. Several recent papers propose consistent estimators for

\text{Var}(\widehat{\delta})
.
Sanchez-Becerra 2023
proposes a debiased
machine learning estimate for
VCATE.
de Chaisemartin and
Deeb 2024
uses
the empirical Bayes variance formula from Morris (1983) to construct a
bias-corrected estimator for the variance of site-specific effects, which can be
adapted to estimate the VCATE in our
setting. In our accompanying R package, we implement the bias-corrected estimator of

Kline
et al. 2020
,

\widehat{VCATE}=\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}V_{x}\widehat{\delta}-\text{trace}\left(V_{x}\widehat{\text{Var}}(\widehat{\delta})\right)

where
\widehat{\text{Var}}(\widehat{\delta})
is the leave-one-out variance estimator.

5.2
Staggered adoption

The recent literature on staggered adoption designs also emphasizes the
bias-variance trade-off arising from treatment effect heterogeneity; see

Roth
et al. 2023
for a review. Our framework naturally extends to this setting
and provides a principled way to navigate this trade-off. Consider a staggered
adoption setting where we observe a sample of i.i.d. draws

\left\{\{Y_{it},d_{it}\}_{t=0}^{T}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
over
\mathcal{T}=T+1

total time periods. Define
e_{i}:=\min\{t:d_{it}=1\}
as the period in
which unit
i
first receives treatment. Let
\mathcal{E}
denote the set of all
such first treatment periods, and define a cohort as the set of units treated
for the first time in the same period:
{i:e_{i}=e}
.

Under standard assumptions of parallel trends and no anticipation, the saturated difference-in-differences (DID) specification that allows for fully heterogeneous treatment effects across cohorts and event times is

Y_{it}=\alpha_{e}+\lambda_{t}+\sum_{\ell\geq 0,\,e\in\mathcal{E}}\tau_{e,\ell}\cdot d_{it}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{e_{i}=e,t-e_{i}=\ell\}+\varepsilon_{it},

(17)

where
\alpha_{e}
and
\lambda_{t}
are cohort and time fixed
effects respectively, and
\varepsilon_{it}
is independent of
d_{it}
. Here,

\tau_{e,\ell}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,e+\ell}(1)-Y_{i,e+\ell}(0)\mid e_{i}=e]

is the treatment effect for cohort
e
at relative time
\ell
, which allows the
average effect to vary across cohorts and over time in an unrestricted
fashion. Estimation of (
17
) corresponds to aggregating
cohort- and time-specific DID estimators
\hat{\tau}_{e,t-e}
for each

\tau_{e,\ell}
.

In practice, however, researchers often estimate a simplified specification, also known as the “static” two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regression

Y_{it}=\alpha_{e}+\lambda_{t}+\beta_{\text{short}}\cdot d_{it}+\varepsilon_{it}

(18)

which omits the interactions between
d_{it}
and cohort and relative-time
indicators. Analogous to the short regression in the unconfoundedness setting,
as argued in
de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille 2020
and
Goodman-Bacon 2021
, the “static”
specification (
18
) implicitly assumes constant effects across
cohorts and over time (i.e.,
C=0
). When the effects indeed vary, the “static” specification (
18
) can be severely biased for a class of reasonable estimands due to negative weighting of
\tau_{e,\ell}
for large
\ell
.

One reasonable estimand in this setting is the average effect over treated
cohorts (ATT), defined as

\text{ATT}=\frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{P}_{n}\{d_{it}=1\}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{n}\{e_{i}=e\mid d_{it}=1\}\cdot\tau_{e,t-e}}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{P}_{n}\{d_{it}=1\},}

where
\mathbb{P}_{n}
denotes empirical frequencies. To recover this estimand, we can reparametrize (
17
) as the “long” regression

Y_{it}=\alpha_{e}+\lambda_{t}+d_{it}\beta^{\text{ATT}}_{\text{long}}+\left(d_{it}\cdot\tilde{x}_{it}\right)^{\prime}\delta+\varepsilon_{it}

where
\tilde{x}_{it}
collects all but one the re-centered cohort and relative time indicators:

\tilde{x}_{it}=\begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\[4.0pt]
\mathbf{1}\{e_{i}=e,\,t-e_{i}=\ell\}-\dfrac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{P}_{n}\{d_{it}=1\}\mathbb{P}_{n}\{e_{i}=e\mid d_{it}=1\}\mathbf{1}\{t-e=\ell\}}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{P}_{n}\{d_{it}=1\}}\\[6.0pt]
\vdots\end{pmatrix}.

However, this long regression can be noisy or even infeasible in practice.

The original framework for bias-variance tradeoff, developed for settings of unconfoundedness, assumes
\tau(\cdot)
is a linear function of the confounders
x_{i}
. To see how the same framework applies to staggered adoption designs, note that compared to (
1
),
\tau(\cdot)
is now a linear function of group and relative time indicators, rather than the fixed effects that account for confounding. Therefore, once
\tilde{x}_{it}
is redefined appropriately to reflect this structure, the original framework continues to hold.
When few units are treated at a given time, their corresponding DID estimates
\hat{\tau}_{e,t-e}
are noisy. Moreover, if all units are treated after time

C>0
on the variance of treatment effect heterogeneity, restricting the deviation of
\tau_{e,t-e}
from the ATT. Under this assumption, we can construct valid confidence intervals using our
regulaTE
procedure, thereby extending the bias-variance framework to the staggered adoption setting.

6
Conclusion

Many specifications commonly used in empirical research implicitly restrict treatment effect heterogeneity, often favoring precision at the expense of robustness. While such models yield narrower confidence intervals, they may be substantially biased in the presence of heterogeneity. This paper develops a framework that makes this bias–variance trade-off explicit by incorporating an
a priori
bound on the variance of conditional average treatment effects (VCATE). This approach delivers valid and (near-)optimal inference even when overlap is limited or absent.

There are several directions for future work. One natural extension is to consider alternative forms of restrictions on treatment effect heterogeneity. While variance bounds naturally capture the dispersion in heterogeneous effects, one could instead impose an absolute bound on the deviation of individual treatment effects from the average effect if such prior knowledge is available. Another promising direction is to generalize the framework to accommodate multiple treatments or layered sources of heterogeneity, as in
Goldsmith-Pinkham
et al. 2024
and
Sun and
Abraham 2021
.

\min_{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(D_{i}-W_{i}^{\prime}\gamma\right)^{2}\right]+\gamma^{\prime}A\gamma.

A.1
.

denote the solution to the generalized ridge regression. The first-order

Lemma A.3

(Convergence as
\lambda\to 0
)
.

Assume that
S(\theta)
is quadratic and continuous in

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\theta}}
, and is strongly convex with unique minimizer

\theta^{\ast}
. Moreover, assume
P(\theta,\psi)\geq 0
is quadratic and continuous in

\theta
and
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\psi}}
, and is strictly convex. Let the
penalized objective be

F_{\lambda}(\theta,\psi)\;=\;S(\theta)\;+\;\lambda\,P(\theta,\psi),\qquad\lambda\geq 0.

Let
(\hat{\theta}_{\lambda},\hat{\psi}_{\lambda})
be the minimizer of
F_{\lambda}
for
\lambda>0
. Then
\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}\to\theta^{\ast}
as
\lambda\to 0.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Since
S(\theta)
is quadratic and strongly
convex, there exists a constant

such that

S(\theta)\;\geq\;S(\theta^{\ast})+c\|\theta-\theta^{\ast}\|^{2}\quad\text{whenever}\quad\|\theta-\theta^{\ast}\|^{2}\leq\epsilon.

By continuity of

on compact domain, there exists
M<\infty
such that

\sup_{\|\theta-\theta^{\ast}\|\leq\varepsilon}P(\theta,0)\;\leq\;M.

Now set

\delta:=c\,\varepsilon^{2}/2.

For
\lambda

, we have

\inf_{(\theta,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\theta}+p_{\psi}}}F_{\lambda}(\theta,\psi)\;\leq\;\inf_{\|\theta-\theta^{\ast}\|\leq\varepsilon}F_{\lambda}(\theta,0)\;\leq\;\inf_{\|\theta-\theta^{\ast}\|\leq\varepsilon}S(\theta)+\lambda M\;\leq\;S(\theta^{\ast})+\delta.

\|\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}-\theta^{\ast}\|>\varepsilon
. Then we reach a contradiction as

Therefore, for all sufficiently small

(\hat{\theta}_{\lambda},\hat{\psi}_{\lambda})
of

must satisfy

while overlap holds for all other groups. Then the generalized ridge regression
coefficient estimator satisfies

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}=\hat{\beta}_{\text{trim}}

where
\hat{\beta}_{\text{trim}}
denotes the long
regression estimator computed on the subsample with overlap, i.e. the sample
consisting of observations not in group
j
.

Proof.

With saturated discrete covariates, using reparameterization as in the proof for Lemma
A.2
, the generalized ridge problem is equivalent to

\min_{\bar{\gamma},\bar{\tau}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(Y_{i}-\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}-\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}D_{i}\right)^{2}\right]+\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}\right]\right)^{2}\right].

With this reparameterization, the ridge regression coefficient estimator that we are interested in is
\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}=\mathbb{E}_{n}[\hat{\bar{\tau}}_{\lambda}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}]
where
\hat{\bar{\tau}}_{\lambda}
minimizes the above ridge problem when the penalty is
\lambda
.

Case 1: Suppose all observations in group
j
are not treated. Then
D_{i}=0
whenever
\bar{X}_{ij}=1
, so
\bar{X}_{ij}D_{i}=0\quad\forall i
, and the ridge problem becomes

\min_{\bar{\gamma},\bar{\tau}}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(Y_{i}-\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}-\bar{\tau}_{-j}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i,-j}D_{i}\right)^{2}\right]}_{\eqqcolon S(\theta)}+\lambda\cdot\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}\right]\right)^{2}\right]}_{\eqqcolon P(\theta,\psi)}

where
\bar{\tau}_{-j}
is the subvector of
\bar{\tau}
deleting the
j
th
entry, and
\bar{X}_{i,-j}
is the subvector of
\bar{X}_{i}
deleting the
j
th
entry. Note that the coefficient
\bar{\tau}_{j}
does not enter the minimization of the squared errors.

Case 2: Suppose all observations in group
j
are treated. Then
D_{i}=1
whenever
\bar{X}_{ij}=1
, so
\bar{X}_{ij}D_{i}=\bar{X}_{ij}\quad\forall i
, and the ridge problem becomes

\min_{\bar{\gamma},\bar{\tau}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(Y_{i}-\bar{\gamma}_{-j}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i,-j}-(\bar{\gamma}_{j}+\bar{\tau}_{j})^{\prime}\bar{X}_{ij}-\bar{\tau}_{-j}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i,-j}D_{i}\right)^{2}\right]+\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}\right]\right)^{2}\right]

Writing

\tilde{\bar{\gamma}}_{j}=\bar{\gamma}_{j}+\bar{\tau}_{j}
,
then
\bar{\tau}_{j}
does not enter the minimization of the squared errors.

In both cases, by Lemma
A.3
, as
\lambda\to 0
, the limit of the estimators
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\hat{\bar{\gamma}}_{\lambda}
and
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\hat{\bar{\tau}}_{-j,\lambda}
are the OLS estimators that minimize

\min_{\bar{\gamma},\bar{\tau}_{-j}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(Y_{i}-\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}-\bar{\tau}_{-j}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i,-j}D_{i}\right)^{2}\right].

(A.1)

Note that the regressor
\bar{X}_{i,-j}D_{i}=\bar{X}_{i,-j}D_{i}\mathbf{1}\{X_{ij}=0\}
because these regressors all zero out on the non-overlapped sample where
X_{ij}=1.
The OLS problem (
A.1
) gives the OLS coefficient estimators

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{\tau}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[Y_{i}\mid X_{ik}=1,D_{i}=1\right]-\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[Y_{i}\mid X_{ik}=1,D_{i}=0\right]\text{ for }k\neq j

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{\gamma}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[Y_{i}\mid X_{ik}=1,D_{i}=0\right]\text{ for }k\neq j

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{\gamma}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[Y_{i}\mid X_{ij}=1\right]

At the same time, in both cases,
\bar{\tau}_{j}
enters the ridge problem only through the penalty term. Expanding the penalty term, the terms that depend on
\bar{\tau}_{j}
are

\bar{\tau}_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(\bar{X}_{ij}-\mathbb{E}_{n}[\bar{X}_{ij}]\right)^{2}\right]+2\bar{\tau}_{j}\sum_{k\neq j}\bar{\tau}_{k}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(\bar{X}_{ij}-\mathbb{E}_{n}[\bar{X}_{ij}]\right)\left(\bar{X}_{ik}-\mathbb{E}_{n}[\bar{X}_{ik}]\right)\right]

(A.2)

Plugging the limit ridge coefficient estimators
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{k,\lambda}=\hat{\bar{\tau}}_{k}
into (
A.2
) and solving the first-order condition for
\bar{\tau}_{j,\lambda}
gives

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{j,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k\neq j}\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{k}\Pr_{n}\{\bar{X}_{ik}=1\}\Pr_{n}\{\bar{X}_{ij}=1\}}{\Pr_{n}\{\bar{X}_{ij}=1\}\left(1-\Pr_{n}\{\bar{X}_{ij}=1\}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k\neq j}\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{k}\Pr_{n}\{\bar{X}_{ik}=1\}}{1-\Pr_{n}\{\bar{X}_{ij}=1\}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{-j}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i,-j}\mid\bar{X}_{ij}=0\right]

where
{\Pr}_{n}
is the empirical probability.
Thus we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{\lambda}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{\lambda}\right)^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{-j}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i,-j}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{j,\lambda}\bar{X}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\neq j}\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{k}{\Pr}_{n}\{\bar{X}_{ik}=1\}+{\Pr}_{n}\{\bar{X}_{ij}=1\}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{-j}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i,-j}\mid\bar{X}_{ij}=0\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\widehat{\bar{\tau}}_{-j}^{\prime}\bar{X}_{i,-j}\mid\bar{X}_{ij}=0\right]

which is the long regression estimator computed in subsample with
\bar{X}_{ij}=0
, i.e. the subsample with overlap.

∎

A.2
Impossibility of obtaining nontrivial CIs without a bound on heterogeneity

We now formally prove an impossibility result we discussed in Section
3.2
: any CI
\mathcal{C}
that has (uniformly) valid coverage under this unrestricted
parameter space must be trivial in
the sense that the worst-case expected length is unbounded,
\sup_{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2+2k}}\mathbb{E}_{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})}\mu(\mathcal{C})=\infty
. Here,

\mu(A)
denotes the Lebesgue measure of a measurable set
A
. This follows from applying the worst-case CI length bounds derived by
Low 1997
to the specific class of linear functions we consider.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a CI for
\beta
with nominal coverage probability

1-\alpha
(i.e.,

\inf_{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2+2k}}P(\beta\in\mathcal{C})\geq 1-\alpha
). If there is no overlap, then

\sup_{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2+2k}}\mathbb{E}_{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})}\mu(\mathcal{C})=\infty.

Remark 2
.

The proposition applies to any CI
\mathcal{C}
without restricting
it to be fixed length CIs based on affine estimators. Hence, the result
establishes that some restriction on the parameter space is necessary to obtain informative CIs when
overlap fails. Note that when the focus is on fixed-length CIs, which includes
many existing CIs, as is the case for this paper, the result implies that

\mu(\mathcal{C})=\infty
. That is, there does not exist any non-trivial CI
that has correct coverage if one were to restrict attention to CIs that
take the usual form of “linear estimator
\pm
critical value”

Proof.

By
Low 1997
, the worst case length is lower bounded by

(1-\alpha-\varepsilon/4)\omega(\varepsilon)
, where
\omega(\varepsilon)

is the
modulus of continuity
(see, e.g.,
Donoho 1994

or
Armstrong and
Kolesár 2018
) defined as

\omega(\varepsilon):=\sup_{(\beta,\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2+2k}}2\beta\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\lVert D\beta+X\gamma+(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}.

It suffices to show that
\omega(\varepsilon)=\infty
. Suppose to the
contrary that
\omega(\varepsilon)<\infty
. Then, there exists
\theta^{\ast}=(\beta^{\ast},\gamma^{\ast}{}^{\prime},\delta^{\ast}{}^{\prime})^{\prime}
such that
\theta^{\ast}
satisfies the
constraint and
\omega(\varepsilon)=2\beta^{\ast}
. Note that it must be the
case that
\beta^{\ast}>0
. Since there is no overlap, it must be the case
that there exists some
j
such that, without loss of generality,
X_{j+1}\leq D

where
X_{j+1}
denotes the (
j+1
)th column of
X
and
\leq
is interpreted
elementwise. That is, all individuals
i
with
X_{i,j+1}=1
are treated.

Now, consider the
j
th column of
D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1}
which is by definition
D\circ(X_{j+1}-\bar{x}_{j+1}\mathbf{1})
. We have
D\circ(\bar{x}_{j+1}\mathbf{1})=D\bar{x}_{j+1}
, and, due to lack of overlap,
D\circ X_{j+1}=X_{j+1}
. For any constant
c>0
, consider
\theta^{\ast}_{c}=(\beta^{\ast}+c\bar{x}_{j+1},\gamma^{\ast}-ce_{j+1},\delta^{\ast}+ce_{j})
. Note
that
\theta^{\ast}_{c}
satisfies the constraint because

\displaystyle D(\beta^{\ast}+c\bar{x}_{j+1})+X(\gamma^{\ast}-ce_{j+1})+(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})(\delta^{\ast}+ce_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D\beta^{\ast}+X\gamma^{\ast}+(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta^{\ast}+(c\bar{x}_{j+1})D-cX_{j+1}+c(D\circ(X_{j+1}-\bar{x}_{j+1}\mathbf{1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D\beta^{\ast}+X\gamma^{\ast}+(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta^{\ast}+(c\bar{x}_{j+1})D-cX_{j+1}+c(X_{j+1}-\bar{x}_{j+1}D)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D\beta^{\ast}+X\gamma^{\ast}+(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta^{\ast}.

Since
\beta^{\ast}+c\bar{x}_{j+1}>\beta^{\ast}
, this is a
contradiction. Hence, it must be the case that
\omega(\varepsilon)=\infty

∎

A.3
Worst-case bias for general weights

We derive the worst-case bias for the short regression and the trimmed regression under model (
5
) and the assumption that VCATE is bounded by
C^{2}.

Proposition A.2
.

The short regression estimator is
\hat{\beta}_{\text{short}}=a_{s}^{\prime}Y
, where

a_{s}=(D^{\prime}H_{X}D)^{-1}H_{X}D
. Its worst-case bias is given by
\maxbias_{a_{s},C}=C\cdot\sqrt{a_{s}^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})V_{x}^{-1}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}a_{s}}
.

Proof.

The expression for
a_{s}
holds by FWL. The worst case bias is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \maxbias_{a_{s},C}:=&\sup_{(\beta,\delta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime}\in\Theta_{C}}\mathbb{E}[a_{s}^{\prime}Y]-\beta\\
=&\sup_{(\beta,\delta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime}\in\Theta_{C}}a_{s}^{\prime}D\beta+a_{s}^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta+a_{s}^{\prime}X\gamma-\beta\\
=&\sup_{\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta\leq C^{2}}a_{s}^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta,\end{aligned}

(A.3)

where the last equality holds because
a_{s}^{\prime}D=1
and
a_{s}^{\prime}X=0
.
We optimize a linear objective function subject to a quadratic constraint
of
\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta\leq C^{2}
. Assuming the constraint binds,
KKT condition gives the solution:

\delta=V_{x}^{-1}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}a_{s}\sqrt{\frac{C^{2}}{a_{s}^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})V_{x}^{-1}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}a_{s}}}

and plugging in the solution gives the expression for the worst-case
bias.
∎

Proposition A.3
.

Suppose trimming is based on the values of
x_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{O}

denote the sample remaining after trimming. Let
\mathcal{J}
denote
the columns of
X
and
(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})
that remain after trimming.
Suppose
x_{i,j}=0
for all
j\not\in\mathcal{J}
and
i\in\mathcal{O}
.
The trimmed regression estimator is
\hat{\beta}_{t}=a_{t}^{\prime}Y
, where

a_{t,i}=\begin{cases}d_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}\pi_{1,t}-(d_{i}x_{i,-1})^{\prime}\pi_{2,t}&i\in\mathcal{O}\\
0&i\not\in\mathcal{O}\end{cases}
and
(\pi_{1,t}^{\prime},\pi_{2,t}^{\prime})^{\prime}
are the projection coefficients of

D
on
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X&(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\end{array}\right)
within the trimmed sample. Its worst-case bias is given by
\maxbias_{a_{t},C}=C\cdot\sqrt{a_{t}^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})V_{x}^{-1}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}a_{t}}
.

Proof.

The expression for
a_{t}
holds by FWL. The worst case bias is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \maxbias_{a_{t},C}:=&\sup_{(\beta,\delta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime}\in\Theta_{C}}\mathbb{E}[a_{t}^{\prime}Y]-\beta\\
=&\sup_{(\beta,\delta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime}\in\Theta_{C}}a_{t}^{\prime}D\beta+a_{t}^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta+a_{t}^{\prime}X\gamma-\beta\\
=&\sup_{\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta\leq C^{2}}a_{t}^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})\delta,\end{aligned}

(A.4)

where the last equality holds because of the projection, we have

a_{t}^{\prime}D=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}}a_{t,i}d_{i}=1
and
a_{t}^{\prime}X_{j}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}}a_{t,i}x_{i,j}=0

for
j\in\mathcal{J}
. For
j\not\in\mathcal{J}
, by the construction
of trimming, we have
a_{t}^{\prime}X_{j}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}}a_{t,i}\cdot 0=0

as well. However, note that for
j\not\in\mathcal{J}
, we do not
have
a_{t}^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1,j})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}}a_{t,i}d_{i}(x_{i,j}-\mathbb{E}_{n}x_{i,j})=0
.
Instead, we optimize a linear objective function subject to a quadratic
constraint of
\delta^{\prime}V_{x}\delta\leq C^{2}
. Assuming the constraint
binds, KKT condition gives the solution:

\delta=V_{x}^{-1}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}a_{t}\sqrt{\frac{C^{2}}{a_{t}^{\prime}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})V_{x}^{-1}(D\circ\tilde{X}_{-1})^{\prime}a_{t}}}

and plugging in the solution gives the expression for the worst-case
bias.
∎

A.4
Feasible CI with unknown error distribution

We adapt the results in Appendix B.2 of
Armstrong
et al. 2023
to our setting. We allow the distribution
Q
of
\varepsilon
to be unknown and possibly
non-Gaussian, and only maintain the assumption that
\varepsilon_{i}
is
independent across
i
. The extension to clustered errors is similar and omitted here for brevity.

The class of possible distributions for
Q
is denoted
by
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. We use
P_{\theta,Q}
and
E_{\theta,Q}
to denote
probability and expectation when
Y
is drawn according to
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}

and
\theta\in\Theta
, and we use the notation
P_{Q}
and
E_{Q}
for expressions
that depend on
Q
only and not on
\theta
.

Consider the estimator introduced in Section
4.1
. Let

V_{Q}=\text{Var}_{Q}(\hat{\beta}_{\lambda^{*}_{C}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{\lambda^{*}_{C},i}^{2}E_{Q}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}
the variance of the estimator.

Theorem A.2
.

Suppose that, for some
\eta>0
,
\eta\leq E_{Q}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}
and

E_{Q}\lvert\varepsilon_{i}\rvert^{2+\eta}\leq 1/\eta
for all
i
and all

Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. Suppose also that,
for some sequence
c_{n}
with
c_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
, we have

(i)

\max\left\{\sqrt{n}c_{n},1\right\}\cdot\operatorname{Lind}({\lambda^{*}_{C}})\to 0
; and

(ii)

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta,Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}}P_{\theta,Q}(\lVert W(\hat{\theta}^{\text{init}}-\theta)\rVert_{2}\leq c_{n})\to 1
.

Then, for any
\delta>0
,

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta,Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}}P_{Q}\left(\lvert(\hat{V}_{\lambda^{*}_{C},\text{rob}}-V_{Q})/V_{Q}\rvert<\delta\right)\to 1
. Furthermore,

\liminf_{n}\inf_{\theta\in\Theta,Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}}P_{Q}\left(\beta\in\left\{\hat{\beta}_{\lambda^{*}_{C}}\pm\text{cv}_{\alpha}(\maxbias_{a_{\lambda^{*}_{C}},C}(\hat{\beta}_{\lambda^{*}_{C}})/\sqrt{\hat{V}_{\lambda^{*}_{C},\text{rob}}})\cdot\sqrt{\hat{V}_{\lambda^{*}_{C},\text{rob}}}\right\}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(A.5)

The convergence rate
c_{n}
depends on whether
\hat{\theta}^{\text{init}}
is estimated via the long regression or the cross-validated generalized ridge regression.
