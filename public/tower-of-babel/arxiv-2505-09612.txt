Adaptively-weighted Nearest Neighbors for Matrix Completion
Tathagata Sadhukhan, Manit Paul, Raaz Dwivedi

Abstract
In this technical note, we introduce and analyze 𝖠𝖶𝖭𝖭 \mathsf{AWNN} – an adaptively weighted nearest neighbor method for performing matrix completion. Nearest neighbor (NN) methods are widely used in missing data problems across multiple disciplines such as in recommender systems and for performing counterfactual inference in panel data settings. Prior works have shown that in addition to being very intuitive and easy to implement, NN methods enjoy nice theoretical guarantees. However, the performance of majority of the NN methods rely on the appropriate choice of the radii and the weights assigned to each member in the nearest neighbor set and despite several works on nearest neighbor methods in the past two decades, there does not exist a systematic approach of choosing the radii and the weights without relying on methods like cross-validation. 𝖠𝖶𝖭𝖭 \mathsf{AWNN} addresses this challenge by judiciously balancing the bias–variance trade-off inherent in weighted nearest-neighbor regression. We provide theoretical guarantees for the proposed method under minimal assumptions and support the theory via synthetic experiments.

Nearest Neighbors based regression broadly is of two types,

The focus of our work is proposing a principled alternative to

We further leverage the convex optimization step for establishing finite-sample guarantees (

Our Contributions

(C1)

We propose the adaptively weighted nearest neighbors (
\mathsf{AWNN}
): a weighted nearest neighbor based algorithm for matrix completion that does not rely on any heavy parametric assumption on the data generation process.

(C2)

The requisite weights in
\mathsf{AWNN}
can be computed in closed form from the observed matrix itself. Because no iterative search or cross-validation is needed, the procedure not only runs quickly but also adapts the weights automatically to the data at hand.

(C3)

We provide theoretical guarantees on the performance of
\mathsf{AWNN}

without making any additional assumption (low rank/sparsity) on the structure of the ground truth matrix. To our knowledge, this is the first work on nearest neighbors to provide such a guarantee in the context of matrix completion.

Problem set-up and Motivation

Our objective is to estimate the entries of the ground truth matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
(whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
\theta_{i,j}
) using the data matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
whose
(i,j)
-th entry (for
(i,j)\in[n]\times[m]
) is given by,

\displaystyle X_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\theta_{i,j}+\epsilon_{i,j}&\mbox{ if }A_{i,j}=1,\\
*&\mbox{ if }A_{i,j}=0.\end{cases}

(2)

Here
\{A_{i,j}\}_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}
are binary random variables denoting which entries of the matrix are observed. The noise terms
\epsilon_{i,j}
are i.i.d. with variance
\sigma^{2}
. We propose the adaptively weighted row nearest neighbor algorithm (
\mathsf{AWNN}
) in this section to estimate
\theta_{i,j}
for
i\in[n],j\in[m]
. To explain the primary idea behind the algorithm we consider the simpler situation in this section when there is no missingness in the data i.e.
A_{i,j}=1
for all
i\in[n],j\in[m]
. Broadly speaking, this algorithm has two main steps: in the first step we obtain an estimate
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
of the error vaiance
\sigma^{2}
, in the second step we leverage the magnitude of the estimated error variance to construct a weighted nearest neighbors estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime}}X_{i^{\prime},j}
of
\theta_{i,j}
where the weight
w_{i^{\prime}}
on the entry from the
i^{\prime}
-th row is inversely proportional to the “distance" between the
i
-th row and
i^{\prime}
-th row.

We recall that the unweighted (vanilla) row nearest neighbor estimate (see
[
19
]
,
[
35
]
) identifies a neighboring set of rows
\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\mathsf{row}}
and estimates
\theta_{i,j}
by taking average on the
j
-th column over all rows in
\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\mathsf{row}}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}^{\mathrm{UW}}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime}}X_{i^{\prime},j}\quad\text{where}\quad w_{i^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\mathsf{row}}|}&\quad\text{if}\quad i^{\prime}\in\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\mathsf{row}},\\
0&\quad\text{otherwise}\quad.\end{cases}

(3)

However this does not provide ideal estimates in all situations. For instance if
\sigma^{2}=0
and we have the prior knowledge of this fact, then the most natural estimate of
\theta_{i,j}
is
X_{i,j}
. This suggests that we can use
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
to provide a more accurate estimate of the entries of the matrix by carefully weighing the neighboring rows provided
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
is a good enough estimate of
\sigma^{2}
. This is the main motivation behind the adaptively weighted row nearest neighbor algorithm.

2.1
Background and early insights

To rigorously formulate the idea behind the proposed algorithm we consider the following notion of distances between the rows,

\displaystyle\rho_{i^{\prime},i}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\theta_{i,j}-\theta_{i^{\prime},j})^{2}}{m}-2\sigma^{2}\quad\mbox{for all }i,i^{\prime}\in[n].

(4)

We also assume that we have reliable estimates
\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}
of the “true" distances between the rows
\rho_{i^{\prime},i}
. More formally, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1

(Concentration of NN distances)
.

For any fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
and for any pair of rows
i,i^{\prime}\in[n]
the event
\mathcal{E}_{dist}

\displaystyle|\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}-\rho_{i^{\prime},i}|\leq\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta),

(5)

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

We also make the following assumption on the noise terms to simplify the analysis.

Assumption 2

(Sub-gaussian noise)
.

The noise terms
\{\epsilon_{i,j}\}_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}
are independent and identically distributed sub-gaussian random variables with mean
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i,j}]=0
and
\mbox{Var}(\epsilon_{i,j})=\sigma^{2}
for all
i\in[n],j\in[m]
.

The next theorem provides data-dependent bound on the row-wise mean squared error
(1/m)\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}
for the
i
-th row (
i\in[n]
) where
\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime}}X_{i^{\prime},j}
and the weights
\{w_{i^{\prime}}\}_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}
are non-negative and satisfy
\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime}}=1
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime}}X_{i^{\prime},j}
where
w^{(i)}=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{n})
is the weight vector associated with the
i
-th row. Under assumptions
1
and
2
, the row-wise mean squared error for the
i
-th row (
i\in[n]
) has the following data-dependent upper bound with probability at-least
1-3\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\log(2m/\delta)\sigma^{2}\|w^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime}}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right\}+2\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta).

(6)

The proof of
Proposition

1
is discussed in
Appendix

S.1
. The second term in the upper bound on the row MSE,
\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)
captures the hardness in estimating the “true" distances
\rho_{i^{\prime},i}
between the rows and does not depend on how we weigh the rows to estimate the entries of the matrix. Therefore
Proposition

1
suggests that we can optimize the row-wise MSE by choosing weights
\widehat{w}^{(i)}=(\widehat{w}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{w}_{n})
that minimize the first term of the upper bound on the row MSE. During implementation, we replace
\sigma^{2}
by its consistent estimate
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
. We state the steps of the adaptively weighted row nearest neighbor algorithm (
\mathsf{AWNN}
) below when the matrix
X
comprises of no missing entries.

1.

Obtain a consistent estimate
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
of the variance of the noise terms.

2.

Compute the optimal weight vector
\widehat{w}^{(i)}=(\widehat{w}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{w}_{n})
that minimizes the following objective function,

\displaystyle\widehat{w}^{(i)}\coloneqq\mbox{arg min}_{w^{(i)}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2\log(2m/\delta)\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\|w^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime}}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right],

(7)

where
w^{(i)}=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{n})
is used to denote a candidate weight vector i.e.
w^{(i)}
is an elemeny-wise non-negative vector that satisfies
\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime}}=1
.

3.

Return the weighted nearest neighbor estimate,

\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime}}X_{i^{\prime},j}.

(8)

For showing the theoretical guarantees in this paper we assume that
\sigma^{2}
is known a priori for ease of explanation. All the derivations can be re-produced when do not know the true variance but have a consistent estimate
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
of
\sigma^{2}
. Returning back to the discussion of the noiseless setting (
\sigma^{2}=0
), we observe that our proposed weight vector
\widehat{w}^{(i)}
solves the following optimization problem,

\displaystyle\widehat{w}^{(i)}=\mbox{arg min}_{w^{(i)}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime}}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right].

(9)

Since
\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}=0
, it is easy to check that the objective function is minimzed at
\widehat{w}_{i}=1
and
\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime}}=0
for all rows
i^{\prime}\neq i
and the minimum value of the objective function is
0
. Hence for all
j\in[m]
, this algorithm yields the estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime}}X_{i^{\prime},j}=X_{i,j}
which is exactly same as
\theta_{i,j}
(as
\epsilon_{i,j}=0
). This demonstrates that, in the idealized noiseless setting, the adaptively weighted row nearest neighbor approach yields more accurate matrix entry estimates than the standard row nearest neighbor method.

Even when self-neighbor is not allowed by explicitly setting
\widehat{w}_{i}=0
in the above optimization, the adaptively weighted nearest-neighbor method still delivers more accurate estimates of the ground-truth matrix in the noiseless setting. To see this, we assume that the entries of the ground truth matrix come from a low rank factor model. In particular, we assume that
\theta_{i,j}=\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle
for all
(i,j)\in[n]\times[m]
where
u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}
are iid
d
-dimensional row latent factors with covariance matrix
\mathbb{E}u_{1}u_{1}^{T}=I_{d}
and
v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}
are iid
d
-dimensional column latent factors with covariance matrix
\mathbb{E}v_{1}v_{1}^{T}=I_{d}
. In noiseless setting, the estimated distance between the rows
i,i^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}=\rho_{i^{\prime},i}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle u_{i}-u_{i^{\prime}},v_{j}\rangle=(u_{i}-u_{i^{\prime}})^{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}v_{j}^{T}}}\right)(u_{i}-u_{i^{\prime}})=\|u_{i}-u_{i^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}+o_{P}(1).

(10)

If self-neighbor is not allowed, the objective function in (
9
) is minimized at
\widehat{w}_{i_{(1)}}=1
and
\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime}}=0
for all rows
i^{\prime}\neq i_{(1)}
where
i_{(1)}
is the closest row to the
i
-th row i.e.
\widehat{\rho}_{i,i_{(1)}}=\min_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}
. Observe that by (
10
), the index
i_{(1)}
corresponds to the row whose latent factor
u_{i_{(1)}}
is nearest in Euclidean norm to the latent factor
u_{i}
of the
i
-th row. Therefore when self-neighbor is not allowed, the adaptively weighted nearest-neighbor method estimates
\theta_{i,j}
by
\theta_{i_{(1)},j}
where
\|u_{i}-u_{i_{(1)}}\|_{2}=\min_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\|u_{i}-u_{i^{\prime}}\|_{2}
. It can be easily checked that this is the best one can do without any other additional information. We note that by cauchy-schwartz inequality,
|\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}w_{i^{\prime}}\theta_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j}|=|\langle\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}w_{i^{\prime}}(u_{i^{\prime}}-u_{i}),v_{j}\rangle|\leq\|v_{j}\|_{2}\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}w_{i^{\prime}}\|u_{i^{\prime}}-u_{i}\|_{2}
. Consequently, the weight should be allocated exclusively to the row whose latent factor lies nearest to
u_{i}
.

\eta_{i}^{2}=\max\{\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}:i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(i)\}
denote the distance of
i
-th row from the farthest row in the proximity set
\mathcal{R}(i)
. Using Von Szokefalvi Nagy inequality (see
[
29
]
) we can show that
\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(i)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}-\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i}}}\right)^{2}\geq\eta_{i}^{4}/2
. Consequently, the bias component of the error bound is no greater than,

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{AW}}\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\eta_{i}^{2}-\frac{\eta_{i}^{4}}{16\log(2m/\delta)\sigma^{2}}+\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)}}\right\}.

(15)

It can be shown that this is an improvement on the bias in the error bound of the unweighted row nearest neighbor estimate (
3
) that selects a set
\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\mathsf{row}}(\eta^{2})=\{i^{\prime}\in[n]:\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}\leq\eta^{2}\}
of nearest neighbors of row
i
. Let
k_{\eta^{2}}=|\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\mathsf{row}}(\eta^{2})|
be the cardinality of the nearest neighbor set i.e.
\eta^{2}
denotes the distance
\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}
of the
k_{\eta^{2}}
-th farthest row
i^{\prime}
from the row
i
. Under assumptions
1
and
2
, the row-wise mean squared error of the unweighted row nearest neighbor estimate (that thresholds at
\eta^{2}
) for the
i
-th row (
i\in[n]
) has the following upper bound with probability at-least
1-2\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}^{\mathrm{UW}}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}\leq\mathbb{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{UW}}(\eta^{2})+\mathbb{V}_{i}^{\mathrm{UW}}(\eta^{2})\quad\mbox{where},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{UW}}(\eta^{2})=2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\mathsf{row}}(\eta^{2})}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}}{k_{\eta^{2}}}+\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)}}\right\},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{i}^{\mathrm{UW}}(\eta^{2})=\frac{4\log(2m/\delta)\sigma^{2}}{k_{\eta^{2}}}.

(18)

See
[
18
]
for a proof of the above statement. It is clear from this decomposition that for
\eta^{2}=\eta_{i}^{2}
, the bias
\mathbb{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{UW}}(\eta_{i}^{2})
of the unweighted row nearest neighbor estimate is less than the bias
\mathbb{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{AW}}
of the
\mathsf{AWNN}
estimate because of the additional debiasing term
\eta_{i}^{4}/(16\log(2/\delta)\sigma^{2})
. More generally we can show the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

The
\mathsf{AWNN}
algorithm guarantees a lower row wise mean squared error than the best-performing unweighted row-nearest-neighbour estimator, even when the latter’s search radius
\eta^{2}
is optimally tuned.

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{AW}}+\mathbb{V}_{i}^{\mathrm{AW}}\leq\min_{\eta>0}\{\mathbb{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{UW}}(\eta^{2})+\mathbb{V}_{i}^{\mathrm{UW}}(\eta^{2})\}.

(19)

The proof of
Lemma

1
is illustrated in
Appendix

S.4
.
Lemma

1
shows that, by appropriately weighting rows within a selected neighborhood,
\mathsf{AWNN}
achieves a tighter row-wise mean-squared error bound than the unweighted row nearest neighbor method. To further unpack the statements in

1
and

we need additional conditions on the ground truth matrix. In particular, we assume that

Assumption 3

\displaystyle\theta_{i,j}=f(u_{i},v_{j})\quad\forall\quad(i,j)\in[n]\times[m],

Under assumptions

3.2
Matrix completion

When there is missingness in the data, we can not select a single proximity set
\mathcal{R}(i)
for the
i
-th row and estimate
\theta_{i,j}
(for any
j\in[m]
) by averaging over the entries in the
j
-th column of all the rows in the set
\mathcal{R}(i)
. This difficulty arises because rows within the selected neighborhood
\mathcal{R}(i)
typically contain multiple missing entries. To address this issue, when there are missing entries in the observed matrix, we do not use the same weighing to estimate all the entries in a given row. The algorithm
1
can be restated as follows when there is missingness in the data.

1.

Obtain a consistent estimate
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
of the variance of the noise terms.

2.

Identify the active set
\mathcal{A}(j)
of rows for the
j
-th column,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(j)=\{i\in[n]:A_{i,j}=1\}.

(23)

3.

Compute the optimal weight vector
\widehat{w}^{(i,j)}=(\widehat{w}_{1,j},\cdots,\widehat{w}_{n,j})
that minimizes the following objective function,

\displaystyle\widehat{w}^{(i,j)}\coloneqq\mbox{arg min}_{w^{(i,j)}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[2\log(2m/\delta)\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\|w^{(i,j)}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(j)}w_{i^{\prime},j}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right],

(24)

where
w^{(i,j)}=(w_{1,j},\cdots,w_{n,j})
is used to denote a candidate weight vector i.e.
w^{(i,j)}
is an elemeny-wise non-negative vector that satisfies
\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}w_{i^{\prime},j}A_{i^{\prime},j}=1
.

4.

Return the weighted nearest neighbor estimate,

\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}A_{i^{\prime},j}X_{i^{\prime},j}.

(25)

As in the previous sub-section, to simplify the discussion we show the theoretical guarantees in this sub-section assuming known
\sigma^{2}
. From
Proposition

2
we know that the optimal weight vector
\widehat{w}^{(i,j)}
that minimizes the objective function in (
24
) has the alternative representation,

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}=\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(i,j)}}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{K_{i,j}}-\frac{1}{4\log(2m/\delta)\sigma^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}-\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i;j}}}\right)}}\right]\quad\mbox{for all}\quad i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(j),

(26)

where
\mathcal{R}(i,j)\subset\mathcal{A}(j)
is a set of nearest neighbors of cardinality
K_{i,j}
automatically identified by
\mathsf{AWNN}
and
\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i;j}=(1/K_{i,j})\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(i,j)}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}
is the mean of the estimated distances of the rows in the set
\mathcal{R}(i,j)
from the
i
-th row. Note that higher the value of
\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}
, the lower the value of
\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}
. A salient feature of the
\mathsf{AWNN}
method is that it by design excludes rows distant from row
i
when constructing the neighbourhood
\mathcal{R}(i,j)
, thereby enhancing the reliability of the resulting matrix-entry estimates. The next lemma presents a formal statement of this property.

Lemma 2
.

In estimating
{\theta}_{i,j}
, any row
i^{\prime}
in the proximity set
\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)
satisfies,

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}\leq\begin{cases}4\log(2m\delta)\sigma^{2},&\quad\text{if $A_{i,j}=1$,}\\
4\log(2m\delta)\sigma^{2}+\min_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i},&\quad\text{if $A_{i,j}=0$.}\end{cases}

(27)

Additionally the average of the empirical distances over the proximity set
\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)
satisfy,

\displaystyle\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i;j}=\begin{cases}4\log(2m\delta)\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\widehat{w}^{(i,j)}}}}\right\|_{\infty}-\frac{1}{K_{i,j}}}}\right),&\quad\text{if $A_{i,j}=1$,}\\
4\log(2m\delta)\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\widehat{w}^{(i,j)}}}}\right\|_{\infty}-\frac{1}{K_{i,j}}}}\right)+\min_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i},&\quad\text{if $A_{i,j}=0$.}\end{cases}

(28)

The proof of
Lemma

2
uses the representation (
26
) and the fact that
\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}
lies in
[0,1]
for all
i^{\prime}\in[n],j\in[m]
. For the complete proof, see
Appendix

S.6
.
Lemma

2
establishes that if a row
i^{\prime}
belongs to the nearest neighbor set
\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)
of the
i
-th row, its estimated distance
\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}
from the
i
-th row can not be larger than
4\log(2m\delta)\sigma^{2}
when
A_{i,j}=1
. The following theorem discusses the performance of
\mathsf{AWNN}
in the general setup.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\widehat{w}^{(i,j)}=(\widehat{w}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{w}_{n})
is the optimal weight vector selected by
\mathsf{AWNN}
to estimate
\theta_{i,j}
in the presence of missing observations. Under assumptions
1
and
2
, the row-wise mean squared error for the
i
-th row (
i\in[n]
) has the following data-dependent upper bound with probability at-least
1-4\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}\leq\mathbb{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{AW}}+\mathbb{V}_{i}^{\mathrm{AW}}\quad\mbox{where},

(29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{AW}}=\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i;j}+\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(16M^{2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}(\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{2}/\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{1})+\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)}}\right),

(30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{i}^{\mathrm{AW}}=\frac{4\log(2m/\delta)\sigma^{2}}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{K_{i,j}}.

(31)

The proof of
Theorem

2
is discussed in

S.7
.
Theorem

2
generalizes

1
to cover arbitrary missingness patterns. The second component in the bias term

\displaystyle\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(16M^{2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}(\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{2}/\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{1})+\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)}}\right)=o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i;j}}}\right).

(32)

Analogous to the preceding subsection, we adopt assumptions
3
and
4

Assumption 5

(MCAR missingness)

The indicators
A_{i,j}
are drawn i.i.d.
\textrm{Ber}(p)

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i;j}}}\right)+\frac{4\log(2m/\delta)\sigma^{2}}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{K_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\min_{\eta>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\eta^{2}(1+o(1))+\frac{4\log(2m/\delta)\sigma^{2}}{(1-\delta)np\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\eta^{2}(1-o(1))}{L^{2}}}}\right)^{\frac{d_{1}}{2\lambda}}}}}\right\}.

selected by the unweighted row nearest neighbors with radius parameter
\eta
. It can be shown that under assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
with probability at-least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle k_{\eta^{2}}\geq(1-\delta)n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\eta^{2}-\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)}{L^{2}}}}\right)^{\frac{d_{1}}{2\lambda}}.

(E.69)

Therefore with probability at-least
1-4\delta
the row-wise mean squared error of
\mathsf{AWNN}
satisfies,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}\leq\min_{\eta>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\eta^{2}+\frac{4\log(2m/\delta)\sigma^{2}}{(1-\delta)n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\eta^{2}-\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)}{L^{2}}}}\right)^{\frac{d_{1}}{2\lambda}}}+2\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)}}\right\}.

(E.70)

From the expression of row-wise MSE it can be seen that under the regime
n=O(\{\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)\}^{-\frac{d_{1}+2\lambda}{2\lambda}})
(this ensures
\eta^{2}\gtrsim\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)

\eta=O(n^{-\lambda/(d_{1}+2\lambda)})
achieves the minimum value in the row-wise MSE upper bound,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{-2\lambda/(d_{1}+2\lambda)}}}\right).

(E.71)

Under the other regime since
\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)\gtrsim\eta^{2}
we get the trivial rate,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\max_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}\zeta_{i^{\prime},i}(m,\delta)}}\right).

(E.72)

This completes the proof of the corollary.

Appendix S.7
Proof of
Theorem

To aid the demonstration of the upper bound on the row wise MSE in the presence of missingness, we define the set
\mathcal{C}(i^{\prime})
for each row
i^{\prime}\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\in[m]:i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)}}\right\}.

(E.84)

The set
\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)
consists exactly of those columns
j
whose neighborhood
\mathcal{R}(i,j)
contains
i^{\prime}
. We begin the proof by stating the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

For 2 vectors
a
and
b
, we have the following identity
\forall\lambda\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\lambda a+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\lambda}}\right)b}}\right|^{2}=\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|a}}\right|^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\lambda}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|b}}\right|^{2}-\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\lambda}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|a-b}}\right|^{2}

(E.85)

Lemma

3
can be easily proved by expanding the square. We obtain the following bias-variance decomposition of the pointwise error,

\displaystyle(\widehat{\theta}_{i,j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}

(E.86)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}A_{i^{\prime},j}\theta_{i^{\prime},j}+\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}A_{i^{\prime},j}\epsilon_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j}}}\right)^{2}

(E.87)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\theta_{i^{\prime},j}+\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\epsilon_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j}}}\right)^{2}

(E.88)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i,j}^{(i_{0})}(\theta_{i,j}-\theta_{i_{0},j})}}\right)^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\epsilon_{i^{\prime},j}}}\right)^{2}

(E.89)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}(\theta_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}-2\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}<i^{\prime\prime}\leq n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime\prime},j}(\theta_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i^{\prime\prime},j})^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\epsilon_{i^{\prime},j}}}\right)^{2}

(E.90)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(\mathbb{B}_{1})_{i,j}-2(\mathbb{B}_{2})_{i,j}+2\mathbb{V}_{i,j}.

(E.91)

Here the step-
(i)
follows from the fact that
\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}A_{i^{\prime},j}=\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}
for all
i^{\prime}\in[n]
. In step-
(ii)
we use the property that for non-negative weights
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
which add up to
1
and for non-negative integers
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}^{2}-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}w_{i}w_{j}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}
from

3
. Since

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\epsilon_{i^{\prime},j}\sim\mbox{Sub-Gaussian}(\sigma^{2}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}(\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j})^{2})

(E.92)

\displaystyle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\epsilon_{i^{\prime},j}|>t}}\right)\leq 2\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{w^{(i,j)}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}}}\right),

(E.93)

This implies that with probability at-least
1-(\delta/m)
the following event holds,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{i,j}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i,j}^{(i_{0})}\epsilon_{i,j}}}\right)^{2}\leq 2\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{w^{(i_{0})}_{\cdot,j}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\log(2m/\delta)=C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{w^{(i_{0})}_{\cdot,j}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(E.94)

where

. Using the representation in
eq.

26
, we can bound the variance part as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{i,j}\leq

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{w^{(i,j)}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(E.95)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}}{K_{i,j}}+\frac{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}-\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i;j}}}\right)^{2}}{2C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1}{K_{i,j}}+\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)}\frac{\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}}{C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}}{2C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i;j}}{2C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}}}}\right)}}\right\}

\displaystyle\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{V}_{i,j}

\displaystyle\frac{2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}}{K_{i,j}}+\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)}\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\widehat{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}}{2C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\bar{\widehat{\rho}}_{i;j}}{2C_{\delta}\sigma^{2}}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\mathbb{B}_{1})_{i,j}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}(\theta_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}

(E.102)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}(\theta_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}

(E.103)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}(\theta_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}

(E.104)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}(\theta_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}}-\rho_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right)+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j})\rho_{i^{\prime},i}

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}-\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}(\theta_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}}-\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\theta_{i^{\prime},j}-\theta_{i,j})^{2}-\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right]}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}}

(E.111)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{l=1}^{T_{i^{\prime},i}}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)}\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)\leq m}}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\theta_{i^{\prime},t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)}-\theta_{i,t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)})^{2}-\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right]}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}}

(E.112)

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{l=1}^{T_{i^{\prime},i}}W_{l}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}},

t
as
\mathcal{F}_{t}
for

t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)

\mathcal{H}_{l}
for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{l}|\mathcal{H}_{l}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)}\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)\leq m}}}\right][(\theta_{i^{\prime},t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)}-\theta_{i,t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)})^{2}-\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i}]|\mathcal{H}_{l}]

(E.114)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)\leq m}}}\right]\mathbb{E}[\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)}|\mathcal{H}_{l}]\mathbb{E}[[(\theta_{i^{\prime},t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)}-\theta_{i,t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)})^{2}-\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i}]|\mathcal{H}_{l}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)\leq m}}}\right]\mathbb{E}[\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)}|\mathcal{H}_{l}](\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i}-\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(E.117)

Therefore we conclude that

Result 1

(Weighted Azuma martingale concentration)
.

Consider a bounded martingale difference sequence
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
i.e.
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
|S_{n}|\leq M_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the following event holds with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}}}\right|\leq\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}^{2}}.

(E.118)

Using
Result

1
on the bounded martingale difference sequence
\{W_{l}\}_{l=0}^{T_{i^{\prime},i}}
guarantees that the following event holds with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{l=1}^{T_{i^{\prime},i}}W_{l}}}\right|\leq\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)\sum_{l=1}^{{}^{T_{i^{\prime},i}}}64M^{4}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},t_{(l)}(i^{\prime},i)}^{2}}=8M^{2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{2}.

(E.119)

Therefore the following concentration holds with probability at-least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\tilde{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}-\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right|=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{l=1}^{T_{i^{\prime},i}}W_{l}}}\right|}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}}

(E.120)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 8M^{2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}(\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{2}/\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{1}).

(E.121)

Following the same steps we can show that with probability at-least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\rho_{i^{\prime},i}-\rho^{*}_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right|\leq 8M^{2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}(\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{2}/\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{1}).

(E.122)

Combining the two concentration bounds above we see that the following upper bound holds with probability at-least
1-2\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\tilde{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}-\rho_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right|\leq 16M^{2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}(\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{2}/\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{1}).

(E.123)

We use this concentration of
\tilde{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}
about
\rho_{i^{\prime},i}
to bound the row wise average bias with probability at-least

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\mathbb{B}_{1})_{i,j}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\rho}_{i^{\prime},i}-\rho_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right)+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j})\rho_{i^{\prime},i}

(E.124)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j})(16M^{2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}(\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{2}/\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{1}))+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j})\rho_{i^{\prime},i}

(E.125)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(16M^{2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}(\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{2}/\|\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},\cdot}\|_{1})+\rho_{i^{\prime},i}}}\right)(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right)}\widehat{w}_{i^{\prime},j}).

(E.126)

We put together all the prior analysis to show that the row wise mean squared error of
