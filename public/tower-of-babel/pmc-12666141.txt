The Health Survey of São Paulo – ISA-Physical Activity and Environment Longitudinal Study
Authors: Alex Antonio Florindo, Bianca Mitie Onita, Inaian Pignatti Teixeira, Andreia Machado Miranda, Pedro Curi Hallal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe main objective of the Health Survey of São Paulo or Inquérito de Saúde de São Paulo (ISA) in Portuguese, is to generate health indicators to support research and policy planning. The ISA-Physical Activity and Environment Longitudinal Study has the primary objective of examining built and social environmental determinants of leisure-time physical activity and active transportation.ParticipantsThe baseline (2014–2015) study included 4042 participants aged 12 years and older, men and women, living across the five regions of São Paulo city. Data were collected through household interviews. The second wave (2020–2021) used telephone interviews and included 1434 participants aged 18 or older, 58.6% female and representing 35% of the baseline sample. The third wave (2023–2024) included 1583 participants through household or telephone interviews, 58.6% of female and represented 39% of the original cohort.Findings to dateThe study has collected extensive individual-level data, including physical activity behaviours, health status and related behaviours, self-report of diseases and sociodemographic characteristics; built environment features such as public open spaces, transport infrastructure, schools and healthcare facilities, walkability index, sidewalks, traffic control and social environment features, such as crime occurrence and socioeconomic index. Analyses have identified changes in the built environment and their associations with physical activity and obesity. Infrastructure improvements, such as the increase of bike paths and outdoor gyms, have been more frequent in wealthier areas, reinforcing spatial inequalities. Increased availability of public open spaces has been associated with increased leisure-time walking. Obesity has shown a growing trend, particularly among specific sociodemographic groups, while physical activity has demonstrated protective effects against obesity. Cycling for transportation has remained stable over time, with disparities by gender and physical activity status.Future plansThe plan is to conduct the fourth wave in 2026 and 2027 and the fifth wave in 2029 and 2030.

Introduction

Physical inactivity is responsible for over five million deaths per year worldwide, positioning itself as one of the key public health challenges of contemporary societies. Despite proven progress in physical activity (PA) surveillance, research and policy,1 the key challenge for researchers and policy-makers continues to be making people move more.1 Recent trend data show that the prevalence of physical inactivity worldwide was unchanged between 2012 and 2024.2 The field typically divides PA according to the domain it is practised (leisure-time, transportation, occupational and housework). Previous studies have shown that the distribution of PA across these four domains varies considerably according to the income level of the country. For instance, Strain et al showed that the share of occupational PA on total activity levels was much higher in low-income and middle-income countries, as compared with high-income ones. The opposite trends were observed for leisure-time PA.3 More recently, Salvo et al proposed a new framework for classifying PA, dividing it into PA for choice versus PA for necessity.4

Leisure-time and transportation PAs have multiple determinants, including individual, social, environmental and policy.5 The proportion of the population practising PA in each domain varies according to the income of the countries, and inequities can be an important problem, not only in terms of practice, but also in terms of access to PA. Many environmental changes in the world can affect risk factors for chronic diseases, including PA.6 7 In addition, the social, natural and built environments and policies are very different according to country-income levels, which are determinants of PA.8 9

The Inquérito de Saúde de São Paulo (ISA)-Physical Activity and Environment longitudinal study was launched due to the lack of cohort studies exploring the associations between PA and the environment in low-income and middle-income countries. New policies were being implemented to reduce environmental inequities in cities like São Paulo, Brazil, where the study takes place. After 2014, the city launched its new Master Plan, its Urban Mobility Plan and a Pedestrian Statute, all considered promising to change health behaviours and to prevent diseases.9 In addition, most studies on associations between PA and the environment are cross-sectional,10 which makes them subject to neighbourhood self-selection bias. The ISA-Physical Activity and Environment cohort was started in 2014–2015 using the baseline (first wave) of the Health Survey of São Paulo. The main objective of the Health Survey of São Paulo is to produce health indicators for research and planning health policies, and it was a collaboration between professors from the University of São Paulo, Brazil and the Municipal Health Department in São Paulo city.11 The main objective of the ISA-Physical Activity and Environment cohort is to examine the relationship between the built and social environment, with leisure-time PA (LTPA) and active transportation as primary outcomes, and obesity, hypertension and mental health as secondary outcomes. The details of the ISA-Physical Activit and Environment protocol were described by Florindo et al.12

Cohort description

Who participated in the cohort?

São Paulo is the largest metropolitan area in South America, with a population of 11 451 999 million inhabitants and a high population density of 7528.26 inhabitants per square kilometre,13 with the highest concentration in the East and South regions14 (figure 1). São Paulo has 92.6% of households with adequate sewage, 74.8% of urban households on public roads with trees and 50.3% of urban households on public roads with adequate urbanisation (presence of drains, sidewalks, paving and curbs).13 The urbanisation rate is 99.1%, and 60% of São Paulo’s total area is urbanised.15 The population-based Health Survey of São Paulo in 2014–2015 was conducted using a clustered sampling approach, involving census tracts and households, and was stratified by five health departments (North, Midwest, Southeast, South and East). Further details about the study sampling strategy are available in Alves et al.16 The final sample included 4042 people aged 12 years or more. The ISA-Physical Activity and Environment cohort was proposed to follow up participants from the original survey 5 years (2020–2021) and 8 years (2023–2024) after the baseline data collection Health Survey of São Paulo. The study was possible with funds from the São Paulo Research Foundation.

The study sample was compared with data from the 2022 Demographic Census conducted by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics for the municipality of São Paulo. According to the Census, 53.0% of the population are women, and the median age is 37 years.17 Our cohort includes a higher proportion of women and older individuals. In terms of ethnicity, 54.3% of the population self-identifies as white, 33.4% as brown and 10.1% as black.17 The cohort shows a slightly lower proportion of white individuals (49.5%) and correspondingly fewer brown and black individuals. Regarding education, while 21.9% of the adult population completed college and 16.6% had no formal education or incomplete primary education,17 the cohort included a higher proportion of individuals with higher education. Overall, the cohort reflects the general population in some sociodemographic characteristics but includes more women, older adults and individuals with higher education levels.

To minimise selection bias due to loss to follow-up in the ISA-Physical Activity and Environment cohort, poststratification weights were constructed for the second (2020/2021) and third (2023/2024) waves using the rake method.18 This method adjusted the initial weights based on the distributions of age, sex and educational attainment collected at baseline (2015)—to align the weighted sociodemographic distributions of the follow-up samples with that of baseline population (n=4042). Comparisons between weighted and unweighted distributions were largely similar, but the use of weights helped attenuate minor imbalances observed in waves 2 and 3. Statistically significant differences across waves remained, likely reflecting real demographic changes over time, such as ageing and increased education, rather than selection bias alone. While poststratification weights are useful for reducing bias and enhancing internal validity, they are not always essential—especially when key sociodemographic variables are included as covariates, or when the goal is to estimate within-sample associations. Moreover, weights may complicate advanced statistical modelling, such as mixed-effects, Generalized Estimating Equations or Structural Equation Modeling approaches. In this study, the consistency of results with and without weights reinforces the robustness of the findings.

Waves of the cohort: follow-up visits

At baseline, participants aged 12 years or older were included in the study. For the follow-up waves, only participants aged 18 years or older were eligible for reinterview. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, wheelchair use, recent accidents or surgical procedures that limited mobility and cognitive impairments that prevented participation. Participants who had moved out of São Paulo or who were not interested or refused to participate were also excluded. Two follow-ups were conducted, 5–7 years and 8–10 years after the baseline data collection. During the 2020–2021 follow-up, conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, 1434 participants (35%) from the original cohort were successfully interviewed, and 1583 participants (39%) were followed up in 2023–2024. The ISA-Physical Activity and Environment cohort uses two types of interviews: household and telephone. At baseline (2014/2015), only household interviews were used. For the second wave (2020/2021), because of the COVID-19 pandemic, only telephone interviews were used. And for the third wave (2023/2024), both household and telephone interviews were conducted. Furthermore, a process to help locate participants with visits to the household before data collection was included in the data collection procedures of the second and third waves. All fieldwork was conducted by professional interviewers, and electronic questionnaires were used on mobile phones for household interviews. Computer-assisted telephone interviewing was used for telephone interviews and the interviews were recorded for data quality control. For the next waves, the plan is to maintain both types of data collection.

Self-reported PA

The primary outcome of this study is measured through the long version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which evaluates PA in different domains (leisure-time transportation, occupational and household activities). The IPAQ questionnaire was validated for application in face-to-face and telephone interviews, and the leisure-time and transportation modules are better suited for use in Latin American countries.1921

Self-reported weight and height

To assess body weight and height, the questionnaire included self-reported weight and height of the participant at the time of each data collection. A study by Teixeira et al22 used data of a subsample of participants with self-reported and measured height and weight to validate the self-reported measures collected in the longitudinal study.

Self-report of diseases

Information on chronic diseases was obtained through self-report, using a standardised questionnaire administered by trained interviewers. For each condition, participants were asked the following question: “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you have or had [name of disease]?”, referring specifically to hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases (such as myocardial infarction, arrhythmia or angina), cancer and mental health conditions including depression and anxiety. The available response options were yes, no, does not know or did not answer. The validity of this self-report approach, particularly for hypertension and diabetes mellitus, was previously assessed in subsamples by comparing questionnaire responses with clinical and laboratory measurements, showing acceptable sensitivity and specificity.23 24

Objective measures of movement behaviours

The use of accelerometer devices (Actigraph, model GT3X) was incorporated on the third wave of the cohort. The protocol included the use of the device on the wrist for 24 hours per day for 9 days to include valid data from week and weekend days, to reach data from at least seven valid days, including week and weekend days. All participants who were interviewed on the third wave were invited to use the device. They each received a card with instructions and possible doubts, as well as a contact number for emergencies or other questions related to the use of the device. A total of 1696 visits were conducted among 1583 participants, and 943 agreed to wear the accelerometer. Regarding individual and social variables, no differences in acceptance were found by sex; only participants with higher education levels showed a lower usage rate. The accelerometers collected objective measurements about the total daily PA, sedentary behaviour and sleep duration and quality. ActiLife was used to initialise the devices and download the data. The analyses were performed in R software using the GGIR package. Valid days were defined as those in which the device was worn for at least 16 hours, and light, moderate and vigorous PA was determined by the cut-off points: 30 to <100 mg, >100 to <400 mg and >400 mg, respectively.25

The data from those 19 features were processed at four levels: (1) city, (2) health administrative area (South, East, North, Southeast, Downtown and West)—not adjusted, (3) health administrative area—adjusted by the number of inhabitants and (4) buffers around the residences of the cohort members of the ISA-Physical Activity and Environment cohort. Regarding the buffers (item 4), for each wave, six different buffers were created around the residences of the cohort members, as follows: a 500 m radial buffer, 1000 m radial buffer, 1500 m radial buffer, 500 m network buffer, 1000 m network buffer and 1500 m network buffer.

We adopted all buffers (radial and network) because at baseline, associations were found between physical environment features and radial buffers.2628 For each environmental feature, wave and nested level, the quantity of the attribute was calculated respecting its unit characteristics (eg, map point, linear metres, square kilometres). Afterwards, the combination of presence or absence of the built environment attribute was calculated in the periods studied. All attributes were geocoded using QGIS V.3.40.4-Bratislava.

Micro variables

The Microscale Audit of Pedestrian Streetscapes-Global (MAPS) is used to evaluate the variability of pedestrian environment variables. This is an international instrument that originated in the USA and has already undergone validation studies to use on online platforms such as Google Earth.2931 The instrument is organised in four sections: routes, street segments, intersections and dead-end streets. The routes are divided into three main categories: residential and land-use mix; street usage; and aesthetic and social environment. The segments evaluate details about streets like the quality of sidewalks, tree cover, number of vehicle lanes and other characteristics that can affect pedestrian safety. The intersection evaluates traffic control, such as traffic lights, crosswalks, bicycle signals and accessibility.29

In the ISA-Physical Activity and Environment cohort, it was necessary to geocode the residences and addresses, which were considered as the starting points of the routes that were connected to the nearest shopping centres at least 400 m and no more than 725 m away from the residences. The ArcGIS Desktop software was used to prepare the routes. The evaluation was done on both sides of the streets. The first evaluation of MAPS was conducted between December 2020 and January 2023. More details can be obtained in Oliveira et al.32

Stakeholders, policy-makers and non-governmental organisations’ participation

This cohort involves the participation of stakeholders, policy-makers and non-governmental organisations since it started. In-depth interviews were conducted to know their perspective on the main challenges to promote and increase PA in the city, and to analyse which problems may be addressed in the study. The organisations were selected considering their representativeness in the areas of PA and sports, active transportation, urban mobility, public health, education and urban development, considering the primary outcomes of the research. We have annual meetings to present results and to collect new ideas for problem-solving by means of papers, and for new data collections of new waves. The main objective is that the knowledge produced in this study can be used to propose actions and policies for active and healthy cities.

Findings to date

The ISA-Physical Activity and Environment cohort was established to examine how urban environmental factors influence PA in São Paulo, Brazil, a highly urbanised city in a middle-income country.

In 2022, Teixeira et als33 described longitudinal changes in the built environment relevant to PA in the city of São Paulo between 2015 and 2020. An increase of 110% in the presence of outdoor gyms and of 67% in the presence of bike paths was observed.33 Interestingly, the increase in the availability of bike paths was more marked in rich regions as compared with poor ones.33 In 2023, Florindo et al34 showed that the highest public open spaces (POS) scores, like bike paths, public squares and parks availability around 500 m from subject living during 2015–2020 were associated with increased leisure-time walking.

Onita et al35 assessed the impact of obesity and found an almost 28% increase in its prevalence between 2014 and 2021. They highlighted that being black and middle-aged was associated with a fivefold increase in the odds of having obesity. Additionally, engaging in leisure-time and active transportation PA could reduce the odds of obesity by at least 50%.35 Knebel et al36 studied cycling as transportation in waves 1 (2014/2015) and wave 2 (2020/2021) and showed that the prevalence of this domain of PA did not change and was lower for women and higher among those engaged in leisure-time PA at baseline.

Also, Onita et al37 investigated whether exposure to changes in the neighbourhood crime-related violence could predict obesity incidence among adults between 2015 and 2021. Over 6 years, the incidence of obesity reached 14.8%, and exposure to intermediate (IR 2.74), increasing (IR 2.48) and persistent high levels of violence (IR 2.77) was significantly associated with higher obesity risk, independent of sociodemographic factors.37

Miranda et al (in press, Journal of Physical Activity & Health)38 investigated whether living near diverse POS reduces the risk of hypertension in adults in São Paulo (2014–2021) and whether LTPA mediates this association. Living near three or four POS types was associated with lower hypertension risk (RR 0.93), with an even greater reduction among those engaging in ≥150 min/week of LTPA (RR 0.88). These findings underscore the value of urban planning strategies that promote diverse recreational environments to support cardiovascular health in middle-income cities.38

Future plans

The plan is to conduct the fourth wave in 2026 and 2027 and the fifth wave in 2029 and 2030, in addition to the nutrition questionnaire for food consumption, food environment, global positioning system (GPS) and new data collection of accelerometers.

Strengths and limitations

There are several strengths to this cohort study. First, most studies on the association between PA and environmental features are derived from high-income countries, where the environments are typically markedly different from those observed in low-income and middle-income countries. A second strength is the use of a longitudinal design—most studies on PA and the environment are cross-sectional, failing to establish temporality in the associations between exposures and outcomes. Third, the incorporation of objective methods for assessing movement behaviours on the third wave should be highlighted, as most studies on this topic only rely on questionnaires. In addition, the sample includes participants from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and is widely distributed across different areas and neighbourhoods of São Paulo, Brazil. This heterogeneity is essential for identifying potential environmental determinants that shape behaviours and health outcomes.

The study also has some limitations. Notably, the follow-up rates were low, which can be attributed to several factors. First, the baseline study was not planned as a cohort study, and therefore, detailed contact information from each participant was not collected. Second, the long gap between the baseline survey and the first follow-up made it more likely for participants to have moved. Third, the first follow-up took place during the COVID-19 pandemic, when data collection was particularly challenging. However, the attrition analysis indicated that the remaining sample was largely comparable to participants lost to follow-up in key characteristics such as sex, age and educational level, suggesting that losses were not likely to have introduced substantial selection bias. Another limitation is the lack of dietary intake data, the ongoing process of food environment data extraction and the absence of geolocation information through a GPS. Future data collection efforts are planned to address these gaps.
