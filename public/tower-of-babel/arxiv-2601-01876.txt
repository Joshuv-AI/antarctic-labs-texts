Preface

Algebra, in its essence, is a language of symmetry and structure. From the ancient quest to solve polynomial equations to the modern exploration of abstract algebraic systems, the journey of algebra has been marked by profound insights and elegant theories. Among these, Galois theory stands as a crowning achievement, weaving together group theory, field theory, and the geometry of equations into a unified framework. This book aims to guide readers through this beautiful landscape.

Born from years of teaching undergraduate and graduate algebra courses at Chongqing University, this text is designed to introduce Galois theory while minimizing prerequisites. It seeks to reconnect the abstract machinery of modern algeba: groups, rings, and fields with the historical problem that inspired its creation: determining when a polynomial can be solved by radicals. By anchoring abstract concepts in concrete motivation, we hope to illuminate both the “how” and the “why” of algebraic structures.

The book is divided into four chapters, each building systematically toward the heart of Galois theory:

1. Basic Group Theory: From Lagrange’s pioneering work to Sylow’s theorems, we lay the group-theoretic foundation, emphasizing symmetric groups, solvability, and the simplicity of alternating groups.

2. Basic Ring and Field Theory: Polynomial rings, ideals, and field extensions are developed with an eye toward their role in splitting fields and separability.

3. Galois Theory: The fundamental theorem and its implications are rigorously proved, linking field extensions to group actions and paving the way for applications.

4. Applications: From solving polynomials by radicals to classical ruler-and-compass problems, we demonstrate how Galois theory resolves age-old questions with modern tools.

The book has the following features:

Minimalist Foundation: Only essential concepts from group, ring, and field theory are introduced, avoiding unnecessary abstraction while maintaining rigor.

Historical Motivation: The narrative frequently returns to the problem of solvability by radicals, grounding abstract ideas in their historical context.

Computational Insights: Explicit calculations of Galois groups and splitting fields bridge theory and practice.

Exercises and Examples: Over 150 exercises ranging from foundational proofs to exploratory problems—complement detailed examples, encouraging active learning.

This book is tailored for advanced undergraduates and beginning graduate students in mathematics. Familiarity with linear algebra and basic set theory is assumed, but no prior exposure to abstract algebra is required. Instructors will find it suitable for a one-semester course, with flexibility to emphasize theoretical depth or computational techniques.

Lemma 1.2.1
.

The order of an m-cycle is m.

Proof.

Consider the m-cycle
\sigma=(i_{1}\,i_{2}\,\cdots\,i_{m})
. One can see that
\sigma(i_{1})=i_{2},\sigma^{2}(i_{1})=i_{3},\cdots,\sigma^{m-1}(i_{1})=i_{m},\sigma^{m}(i_{1})=i_{1}

\sigma^{m}=e
. Moreover for any

∎

Theorem 1.4.3

(the third isomorphism theorem)
.

If

Proof.

1.6.
The simplicity of

Note that the alternating group

is simple since

\{e,(1\,2)(3\,4),(1\,3)(2\,4),(1\,4)(2\,3)\}\unlhd A_{4}.

However

.
This fact is the main theorem in the section and plays a crucial role for proving Abel-Ruffini’s theorem, that is, the polynomial

Theorem 1.6.1
.

A_{n}
is simple for all

.

Proof of Theorem

consists of two steps:

Step 1.

is simple;

Step 2. For

We need some preliminaries.

Lemma 1.6.1
.

If

is an m-cycle in

, then a conjugate of
\gamma
, say,
\sigma\gamma\sigma^{-1}
is an m-cycle of the form
(\sigma(i_{1})\,\sigma(i_{2})\,\cdots\,\sigma(i_{m}))
.

Proof.

Firstly
\sigma\gamma\sigma^{-1}(\sigma(i_{k}))=\sigma(i_{k+1})
for all

.
2
2

2

Here

.

If

Lemma 1.6.2
.

The alternating group

Proof.

It suffices to prove that the product of two transpositions is a product of 3-cycles.

There are two cases.

(a\,b)(a\,c)=(a\,c\,b)
.

Case 2:
(a\,b)(c\,d)=(a\,b)(b\,c)(b\,c)(c\,d)=(a\,b\,c)(b\,c\,d)

Lemma 1.6.3
.

If a normal subgroup

of

.

Proof.

Assume that

Suppose that
\gamma=(a\,b\,f)

1.7.
Solvable groups

In this section, we study the concept of solvable groups, which is, the group theoretic description of solvable polynomials.

Definition 1.7.1
.

A group
G
is called
solvable
if there exists a series of subgroups
\{G_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
of
G
such that

1=G_{n}\lhd G_{n-1}\lhd G_{2}\cdots G_{1}\lhd G_{0}=G,

and
G_{i}
is a normal subgroup of
G_{i-1}
with
G_{i-1}/G_{i}
being abelian for every
1\leq i\leq n
. We call
\{G_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
a
solvable sequence
of
G
.

Immediately we get the following from the definition.

Proposition 1.7.1
.

A subgroup of a solvable group is also solvable, therefore, if a group contains an unsolvable subgroup, then the group itself is unsolvable.

Examples 1.7.1
.

[Examples and nonexamples of solvable groups]

(1)

Every abelian group is solvable.

(2)

For
n\leq 4
,
S_{n}
is solvable. Let
H=\{e,(1\,2)(3\,4),(1\,3)(2\,4),(1\,4)(2\,3)\}
. Then
H
is a normal subgroup of
S_{4}
since
\sigma(a\,b)(c\,d)\sigma^{-1}=(\sigma(a)\,\sigma(b))(\sigma(c)\,\sigma(d))
for every
\sigma
in
S_{4}
. Moreover
1\lhd H\lhd A_{4}\lhd S_{4}
is a solvable sequence of
S_{4}
.

(3)

Every nonabelian simple group is unsolvable. So when
n\geq 5
,
A_{n}
and
S_{n}
are unsolvable.

Definition 1.7.2
.

The
commutator subgroup
of a group

is the subgroup generated by elements
[a,b]=ab(ba)^{-1}
for
a,b
in
G
. Denote the commutator subgroup of

by

.

The commutator subgroup

is the smallest normal subgroup of

such that the quotient is abelian. More precisely, the following hold.

Theorem 1.7.1
.

The commutator subgroup
[G,G]
is a normal subgroup of
G
such that
G/[G,G]
is abelian. Moreover if
H
is a normal subgroup of
G
with

is a subgroup of

.

For all
a,b,c
in
G
, one has
c[a,b]c^{-1}=[cac^{-1},cbc^{-1}]
. Thus
[G,G]
is normal.

Moreover if

is a normal subgroup of
G
with

in

, it’s true

, which means,
[a,b]
is in

. Hence

contains
[G,G]
.
∎

Denote
[G,G]
by

and the commutator subgroup of

by

for

.

Theorem 1.7.2
.

G
is solvable iff

for some

.

Proof.

Suppose that

.

Then

Note that

is abelian for all

is solvable.

Conversely assume that

Then there exists a sequence of subgroups

of

.

.

So

.

∎

.

(1)

If

(2)

Chapter 2
Basic Ring Theory

Ring theory, together with group theory and field theory are pillars of algebra. The abstract definition of ring were given only as late as the first decade of the 20th century and the abstract ring theory formed in the hands of E. Noether and E. Artin during the same period, though some special rings such as polynomial rings, rings of algebraic integers were studied quite thoroughly because of their importance in number theory and algebraic geometry.

In this chapter, we focus on commutative ring theory, in particular, theory of polynomial rings.

2.1.
Definition and examples

Definition 2.1.1
.

A

is a nonempty set
R
together with two binary operations: addition
``+"
and multiplication
``\cdot"

1
1

1

We write
a\cdot b
as
ab
for
a,b
in
R
.
such that

(1)

R
is closed under these two operations.

(2)

(R,+)
is an abelian group.

(3)

(ab)c=a(bc)
for all
a,b,c
in
R
.

(4)

(a+b)c=ac+bc
and
a(b+c)=ab+ac
for all
a,b,c
in
R
.

If
ab=ba
for all
a,b
in
R
, then
R
is called a

.

If
R
has an identity for multiplication, that is, an element

in

such that
1a=a1=a
for all

in

, then

is called a

.

If a ring

is unital and

is an abelian group, then

is called a

.

If a subset of a ring

is a ring, then it is called a

of

. If a subset of a field
\mathbb{F}
is a field, then it is called a

of

Below are some examples of rings.

.

(1)

,

and

,

(2)

For

(3)

(4)

Under matrix addition and multiplication, the set of

(5)

The set of polynomials over a unital commutative ring

(\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i})(\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}x^{j})=\sum_{k=0}^{m+n}(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}b_{k-i})x^{k}.

Theorem 2.1.1
.

Let

be a unital commutative ring. Then

is an integral domain iff

is an integral domain.

Proof.

Suppose
R
is an integral domain. For any nonzero
f=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}

If

∎

A nonempty subset

in

,
R
.

Given elements

\{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}\,|\,b_{i}\in R\,\,\text{for\,all}\,1\leq i\leq n\}

called the ideal generated by

. For example, the subset

Suppose

Proposition 2.1.1
.

If an ideal

of a ring

.

Proof.

Take a unit
a
in

. Every

in

can be expressed as

, hence is in

.
∎

Suppose
I
is an ideal of a ring
R
. Define the set
R/I=\{a+I\,|\,a\in R\}
, the addition on
R/I
by
(a+I)+(b+I)=a+b+I
and the multiplication on

by
(a+I)(b+I)=ab+I
for

in

Theorem 2.1.2
.

R/I
is a ring.

Proof.

If
a_{1}+I=a_{2}+I
and
b_{1}+I=b_{2}+I
, then
a_{1}+b_{1}+I=a_{2}+b_{2}+I
since
(a_{1}+b_{1})-(a_{2}+b_{2})
is in
I
. So the addition is well-defined.

Moreover under the addition,
R/I
is an abelian group:

•

(a+I)+(b+I)=a+b+I=b+a+I=(b+I)+(a+I)
.

•

((a+I)+(b+I))+c+I=(a+b)+c+I=a+(b+c)+I=a+I+((b+I)+(c+I))
.

•

(a+I)+I=a+0+I=I+(a+I)
.

•

(a+I)+(-a+I)=0+I=I
.

If
a+I=b+I
, then
(a+I)(c+I)=ac+I=bc+I=(b+I)(c+I)
since
ac-bc=(a-b)c
is in

. So the multiplication is well-defined.

Also we have the following:

•

((a+I)(b+I))(c+I)=(ab)c+I=a(bc)+I=(a+I)((b+I)(c+I))
.

•

((a+I)+(b+I))(c+I)=(a+b)c+I=(ac+I)+(bc+I)=(a+I)(c+I)+(b+I)(c+I)
.

•

(c+I)((a+I)+(b+I))=c(a+b)+I=(ca+I)+(cb+I)=(c+I)(a+I)+(c+I)(b+I)
.

So

is a ring under the defined addition and multiplication.
∎

R/I
is called the

of

over

.

Ring homomorphisms are defined similarly as group homomorphisms.

Definition 2.1.2
.

A

is a map
\varphi:R\to S
such that
\varphi(a+b)=\varphi(a)+\varphi(b)
and
\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)
for all
a,b
in
R
.

A bijective ring homomorphism is called a
ring isomorphism
.

The
kernel
of a ring homomorphism
\varphi:R\to S
, denoted by
\ker\varphi
is the set
\{a\in R\,|\,\varphi(a)=0\}
.

Like group theory, there are isomorphism theorems for rings.

Theorem 2.1.3
.

[1st ring isomorphism theorem]

Suppose

is a ring homomorphism. Then
\ker f
is an ideal of
R
,
\text{Im}f
is a subring of
S
, and

R/\ker f\cong\text{Im}f.

The proof of the 1st ring isomorphism theorem is similar to proof of the 1st group isomorphism theorems. We left it as an exercise.

Consider
f:\mathbb{Z}[x]\to\mathbb{Z}
given by
f(p)=p(0)
for all
p
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Then
\ker f=(x)
and
\text{Im}f=\mathbb{Z}
. The 1st ring isomorphism theorem says that
\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x)\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 2.1.4
.

[2nd ring isomorphism theorem]

Suppose
A
is a subring of
R
and

is an ideal of

. Then

is a subring of

,

is an ideal of

(A+I)/I\cong A/(A\cap I).

Proof.

For
a_{1},a_{2}
in

and

in

, one has that
(a_{1}+b_{1})+(a_{2}+b_{2})=(a_{1}+a_{2})+(b_{1}+b_{2})
is in

and
(a_{1}+b_{1})(a_{2}+b_{2})=a_{1}a_{2}+(b_{1}a_{2}+b_{2}a_{1}+b_{1}b_{2})
is in

. Hence

is a subring of
R
.

Moreover for any
a
in
A
and any
b
in

, it holds that

is in

. So

is an ideal of

.

Define
f:A+I\to A/(A\cap I)
by
f(a+b)=a+A\cap I
for all

in
A
and
b
in
I
.

•

If
a_{1}+b_{1}=a_{2}+b_{2}
for

in

and
b_{1},b_{2}
in
I
, then
a_{1}-a_{2}=b_{2}-b_{1}
is in

. So

is well-defined.

•

For
a_{1},a_{2}
in
A
and

f((a_{1}+b_{1})(a_{2}+b_{2}))=f(a_{1}a_{2}+a_{1}b_{2}+b_{1}(a_{2}+b_{2}))=a_{1}a_{2}+A\cap I=f(a_{1}+b_{1})f(a_{2}+b_{2})
since
a_{1}b_{2}+b_{1}(a_{2}+b_{2})
is in

, and
f((a_{1}+b_{1})+(a_{2}+b_{2}))=f((a_{1}+a_{2})+(b_{1}+b_{2}))=a_{1}+a_{2}+A\cap I=f(a_{1}+b_{1})+f(a_{2}+b_{2})
. So

is a ring homomorphism.

•

f

•

If

is in

2.2.
The fraction field of an integral domain

One can define a field, called the fraction field, out of an integral domain.

Suppose
R
is an integral domain. Define a relation
``\sim"
on
R\times R
as follows

(a,b)\sim(c,d)
if
ad=bc
.

One can check that
``\sim"
is an equivalence relation, that is, the relation
``\sim"
satisfies that

(1)

(a,b)\sim(a,b)
.

(2)

(a,b)\sim(c,d)
implies that
(c,d)\sim(a,b)
.

(3)

(a,b)\sim(c,d)
and
(c,d)\sim(e,f)
implies that
(a,b)\sim(e,f)
.

for
a,b
in
R
with
b\neq 0
,denote the equivalence class of
(a,b)
in
R\times R
by
ab^{-1}
, i.e.,

ab^{-1}=\{(c,d)\in R\times R\,|\,(c,d)\sim(a,b)\}.

Define
\text{Frac}(R)
to be the set of equivalence classes in
R\times R
. Define the multiplication and the addition on
\text{Frac}(R)
as
(ab^{-1})(cd^{-1})=ac(bd)^{-1}
and
ab^{-1}+cd^{-1}=(ad+bc)(bd)^{-1}
.

Theorem 2.2.1
.

For an integral domain
R
, the set
\text{Frac}(R)
is a field.

Proof.

First we check the addition and the multiplication on
\text{Frac}(R)
are well-defined.

Suppose that
(a_{1},b_{1})\sim(a_{2},b_{2})
and
(c_{1},d_{1})\sim(c_{2},d_{2})
. Then

(a_{1}c_{1})(b_{2}d_{2})=(a_{1}b_{2})(c_{1}d_{2})=(b_{1}a_{2})(c_{2}d_{1})=(a_{2}c_{2})(b_{1}d_{1}).

That is,
(a_{1}c_{1})(b_{1}d_{1})^{-1}=(a_{2}c_{2})(b_{2}d_{2})^{-1}
. So the multiplication is well-defined.

Moreover

\displaystyle(a_{1}d_{1}+b_{1}c_{1})(b_{2}d_{2})-(a_{2}d_{2}+b_{2}c_{2})(b_{1}d_{1})

\displaystyle=(a_{1}b_{2}d_{1}d_{2}+b_{1}b_{2}c_{1}d_{2})-(a_{2}b_{1}d_{2}d_{1}+b_{2}b_{1}c_{2}d_{1})

\displaystyle=(a_{1}b_{2}-a_{2}b_{1})d_{1}d_{2}+b_{1}b_{2}(c_{1}d_{2}-c_{2}d_{1})=0.

That is,
(a_{1}d_{1}+b_{1}c_{1})(b_{1}d_{1})^{-1}=(a_{2}d_{2}+b_{2}c_{2})(b_{2}d_{2})^{-1}
. So the addition is well-defined.

Also the following hold:

•

ab^{-1}+cd^{-1}=(ad+bc)(bd)^{-1}=(cb+da)(db)^{-1}=cd^{-1}+ab^{-1}
.

•

(ab^{-1}+cd^{-1})+ef^{-1}=(ad+bc)(bd)^{-1}+ef^{-1}=(adf+bcf+bde)(bdf)^{-1}=ab^{-1}+(cf+de)(df)^{-1}=ab^{-1}+(cd^{-1}+ef^{-1})
.

•

ab^{-1}+0d^{-1}=(ad)(bd)^{-1}=ab^{-1}
, i.e.,
0d^{-1}=0
in
\text{Frac}(R)
.

•

ab^{-1}+(-a)b^{-1}=0(b^{2})^{-1}=0
.

So
\text{Frac}(R)
is an abelian group under addition.

Notice that
ab^{-1}=0d^{-1}
iff
ad=0
iff
a=0
since
d\neq 0
and
R
is an integral domain.

Consider nonzero elements in
\text{Frac}(R)
. We have the following:

•

If
ab^{-1}\neq 0
and
cd^{-1}\neq 0
, then
(ac)(bd)^{-1}\neq 0
since
R
is an integral domain.

•

(ab^{-1})(cd^{-1})=(ac)(bd)^{-1}=(ca)(db)^{-1}=(cd^{-1})(ab^{-1})
.

•

((ab^{-1})(cd^{-1}))ef^{-1}=((ac)(bd)^{-1})(ef^{-1})=(ace)(bdf)^{-1}=(ab^{-1})((ce)(df)^{-1})=ab^{-1}((cd^{-1})(ef^{-1}))
.

•

(ab^{-1})(dd^{-1})=ad(bd)^{-1}=ab^{-1}
, i.e.,
dd^{-1}=1
in
\text{Frac}(R)
.

•

(ab^{-1})(ba^{-1})=ab(ab)^{-1}=1
.

These verify that
\text{Frac}(R)\setminus\{0\}
is an abelian group under multiplication.
∎

\text{Frac}(R)
is called the
fraction field
of
R
.

We left the proof the following theorem as an exercise.

Theorem 2.2.2
.

An integral domain
R
is isomorphic to the subring
\{a1^{-1}\,|\,a\in R\}
of
\text{Frac}(R)
. Moreover if
R
is a subring of a field
\mathbb{F}
, then
\text{Frac}(R)
is isomorphic to a subfield of
\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

Define
f:R\to\text{Frac}(R)
by
f(a)=a1^{-1}
for all
a
in
R
. Then

•

f
is well-defined.

•

f(ab)=(ab)1^{-1}=(a1^{-1})(b1^{-1})=f(a)f(b)
for all
a,b
in
R
. That is,
f
is a ring homomorphism.

•

If
f(a)=a1^{-1}=0
, i.e.,
a1^{-1}\sim 01^{-1}
, then
a=0
. Hence
f
is injective.

By the 1st ring isomorphism theorem,
R
is isomorphic to
\text{Im}f
, which is a subring of
\text{Frac}(R)
.

This ends the proof of part 1 of the theorem.

Proof of part 2 is left as an exercise.
∎

Corollary 2.2.1
.

For every

in
\text{Frac}(R)
, there is

in

such that

is in

. Here

Proof.

Denote

by

for

. That is,

2.3.
Prime ideals and maximal ideals

Definition 2.3.1
.

A proper ideal

of a ring

or

.

A proper ideal

of a ring

Examples 2.3.1

(1)

(2)

Theorem 2.3.1
.

Every proper ideal
I

.

Proof.

The proof relies on Zorn’s lemma, which says that if every completely ordered subset of a partially ordered set has an upper bound, then the partially ordered set has a maximal element.

Suppose
I
is a proper ideal of
R
.

Now we consider the set
\mathcal{A}
of proper ideals of
R
containing
I
which is ordered by inclusion, i.e., in
\mathcal{A}
, we define
J\prec J^{\prime}
by
J\subseteq J^{\prime}
. For a completely ordered subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{A}
, let
J_{0}=\displaystyle\bigcup_{J\in\mathcal{B}}J
.

For any
a,b
in
J_{0}
, their exist
J_{1},J_{2}
in
\mathcal{B}
such that
a\in J_{1}
and
b\in J_{2}
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is completely ordered, we have
J_{1}\prec J_{2}
or
J_{2}\prec J_{1}
. Both
a
and
b
locate in only one of
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
, so does
a+b
. For any
c
in
R
,
ac
is in
J_{1}
. Therefore
J_{0}
is an ideal of
R
. Note that
1\notin J_{0}
, so
J_{0}\subsetneqq R
. Thus
J_{0}
is in
\mathcal{A}
and
\displaystyle J\prec J_{0}
, that is,
J_{0}
is an upper bound of
\mathcal{B}
. So there is a maximal element in
\mathcal{A}
, which is a maximal ideal of
R
containing
I
.
∎

Corollary 2.3.1
.

Every nonunit in a unital commutative ring

is contained in a maximal ideal of

.

Proof.

Apply Theorem

to the ideal generated by the nonunit.

Theorem 2.3.2
.

Suppose

is a unital commutative ring.

(1)

I
is a prime ideal of

iff

is an integral domain.

(2)

Proof.

(1)

is in

implies

or

is in

.

(2) Suppose

. So

2.4.
Polynomials rings

In this section, we study the polynomial ring over a field
\mathbb{F}
. In particular, we introduce Eulidean algorithm for polynomials.

The Euclidean algorithm for polynomials, as its name indicates, is an analogue of Eulidean algorithm for integers.

The
degree
of a polynomial
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
is the largest nonnegative integer
n
such that
a_{n}\neq 0
. Denote it by
\deg f
. For example, in
\mathbb{R}[x]
,
\deg(x^{3}-1)=3
and
\deg(x^{2}-x+5)=2
.

Theorem 2.4.1
.

Given
f
and nonzero
g
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
, there exist unique
h
and
r
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
such that

f=gh+r
with
\deg(r)<\deg(g)
.

Proof.

We can assume that
\deg f\geq\deg g
otherwise
h=0
and
f=r
.

Denote
f
by
f=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
and
g
by
b_{m}x^{m}+b_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\cdots+b_{1}x+b_{0}
with
\deg f=n\geq m=\deg g
.

Let
h_{1}=a_{n}b_{m}^{-1}x^{n-m}
and
r_{1}=f-gh_{1}
. Then
\deg(r_{1})\leq n-1<n=\deg(f)
. If
\deg r_{1}<\deg g
, then we get that
h=h_{1}
and
r=r_{1}
.
If
\deg r_{1}\geq\deg g
, then repeat the above process for
r_{1}
and
g
to get
r_{2}
. After finite steps, say
k
steps, either
r_{k}=0
or
\deg r_{k}<\deg g
. Then we can get the required
h
and
r
by repeatedly plugging the identities from bottom to top. More precisely
h=h_{1}+h_{2}+\cdots+h_{k-1}
and
r=r_{k}
.

To summarize, what we do are the following:

\displaystyle f=gh_{1}+r_{1}

\displaystyle r_{1}=gh_{2}+r_{2}

\displaystyle\cdots\,\cdots\,\cdots

\displaystyle r_{k-1}=gh_{k-1}+r_{k}

and

f=g(h_{1}+h_{2}+\cdots+h_{k-1})+r_{k}.

We prove the existence of
h
and
r
.

For the uniqueness, if
f=gh+r=g\tilde{h}+\tilde{r}
with
\deg r<\deg g
and
\deg\tilde{r}<\deg g
, then
g(h-\tilde{h})=\tilde{r}-r
. It holds that
h=\tilde{h}
and
r=\tilde{r}
, otherwise
\deg(g(h-\tilde{h}))\geq\deg g>\deg(\tilde{r}-r)
which leads to a contradiction.

∎

The polynomials
r
is called the
reminder
of
f
divided by
g
.

If
f=gh
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
, then we say that
g
divides
f
or
g
is a
factor
of
f
denoted by
g|f
.

A
common divisor
of
f
and
g
is a polynomial
h
such that
h
divides both
f
and
g
.

If a common divisor
h
of
f
and
g
satisfies that any other common divisor of
f
and
g
is a factor of
h
, then it is called a
greatest common divisor
of
f
and
g
.

Up to scalar multiples, greatest common divisors of
f
and
g
are unique. So we call it the greatest common divisor of
f
and
g
and denote it by
\gcd(f,g)
.

If
\gcd(f,g)=1
, then we say that
f
and
g
are
coprime
or
f
is prime to
g
.

Lemma 2.4.1
.

Suppose
f
and
g
are in
\mathbb{F}[x]
. Then
\gcd(f,g)=fp+gq
for some
p,q
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
. In another word, the ideal
(f,g)
generated by
f
and
g
is
(\gcd(f,g))
, the ideal generated by
\gcd(f,g)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\deg g\leq\deg f
.

By Theorem
2.4.1
,
f=gh_{0}+r_{0}
with
\deg r_{0}<\deg g
. If
r_{0}\neq 0
, then
g=h_{1}r_{0}+r_{1}
with
\deg r_{1}<\deg r_{0}
. Again if
r_{1}\neq 0
, then
r_{0}=h_{2}r_{1}+r_{2}
. Do this inductively whenever
r_{i}\neq 0
, after finite steps, we get that
r_{k-2}=h_{k-1}r_{k-1}+r_{k}
and
r_{k-1}=h_{k}r_{k}
.

Note that
r_{0}
is in
(f,g)
. Inductively each
r_{i}
is in
(f,g)
for
0\leq i\leq k
. Moreover if
r
divides
f
and
g
, then
r
divides
r_{k}
since
r_{k}
is in
(f,g)
. Hence
r_{k}=\gcd(f,g)
and
r_{k}
is of the form
h=fp+gq
for some
p,q
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
.
∎

Remark 2.4.1
.

In the proof of Theorem
2.4.1
and Lemma
2.4.1
, algorithms for finding the reminder
r
of
f
divided
g
and
\gcd(f,g)
are called the
Euclidean algorithm
for polynomials.

2.5.
Principal ideal domains

A nonzero nonunit
f
in a unital commutative ring
R
is called
irreducible
if
f=gh
implies that
g
or
h
is a unit in
R
. Otherwise call
f

reducible
.

Definition 2.5.1
.

A
principal ideal domain
is an integral domain
R
in which every ideal is a
principal ideal
, that is, an ideal generated by a single element in
R
.

\mathbb{Z}
is a principal ideal domain.

\mathbb{Z}[x]
is not a principal ideal domain since the proper ideal
(x,2)
is not principal.

Theorem 2.5.1
.

\mathbb{F}[x]
is a principal ideal domain.

Proof.

Suppose
I
is a nonzero ideal of
\mathbb{F}[x]
. Assume that
f
is a nonzero element in
I
with the smallest degree.

Take any
g
in
I
. By Theorem
2.4.1
, there exist
h
and
r
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
such that

g=fh+r
with
\deg(r)<\deg(f)
. Then
r=0
otherwise
r
is a nonzero element in
I
with the degree less than the degree of
f
which is a contradiction. Hence
I=(f)
.
∎

Theorem 2.5.2
.

In a principal ideal domain, a nonzero ideal is prime iff it is maximal.

Proof.

The necessity follows from Corollary
2.3.2
.

We prove the sufficiency.

Assume that
I
is a prime ideal of a principal ideal domain
R
.

Suppose that an ideal
J=(b)\supsetneqq I=(a)
. Then there exists
c
in
R
such that
a=bc
. Since
I
is prime and
b
is not in
I
, the element
c
is in
I
. There is
d
in
R
such that
c=ad
. Hence
a(bd-1)=0
. The element
a
is nonzero since
I=(a)
is a nonzero ideal of
R
. Thus
bd=1
, which means,
b
is a unit. Therefore
J=R
which implies that
I
is a maximal ideal.
∎

Proposition 2.5.1
.

If a principal ideal
(a)
in an integral domain
R
is prime, then
a
is irreducible. If
a
is an irreducible element in a principal ideal domain
R
, then the ideal
(a)
is prime.

Proof.

Suppose
a=bc
for
b,c
in
R
. Then either
b
or
c
is in
(a)
. Assume that
b
is in
(a)
. Then
b=ad
for
d
in
R
. It follows that

a=bc=adc
, which implies that
dc=1
. So
c
is a unit and
a
is an irreducible in
R
.

Assume that
a
is an irreducible element in a principal ideal domain
R
. Suppose
(a)
is a proper subset of an ideal
J=(b)
. Then
a=bc
for some
c
in
R
. Since
a
is irreducible, either
b
or
c
is a unit. But
c
cannot be a unit otherwise
(a)=(b)
. Thus
b
is a unit and
J=R
. This shows that
(a)
is a maximal ideal, hence a prime ideal.
∎

As a consequence we get the following which says that irreducibles in a principal ideal domain
R
behave like primes in the ring
\mathbb{Z}
of integers.

Corollary 2.5.1
.

For an irreducible
p
in a principal ideal domain
R
, if
p|ab
, then either
p|a
or
p|b
.

Definition 2.5.2
.

A sequence
\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of ideals of a ring
R
is called
ascending
if
I_{n}\subseteq I_{n+1}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. We say that an ascending sequence
\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of ideals satisfies the
ascending chain condition
if there exists
K
such that
I_{k}=I_{k+1}
for all
k\geq K
.

Lemma 2.5.1
.

Every ascending sequence of ideals in a principal ideal domain satisfies the ascending chain condition.

Proof.

Suppose
\{I_{n}=(a_{n})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an ascending sequence of ideals in a principal ideal domain
R
. Define
\displaystyle J=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}
. Then
J
is an ideal and
J=(b)
for some
b
in
R
.

It follows that
b
is in
I_{K}
for some
K\geq 1
. Hence
(b)\subseteq I_{K}\subseteq I_{K+1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq(b)
. Consequently we obtain that
I_{k}=I_{k+1}
for all
k\geq K
.
∎

Lemma 2.5.2
.

Let
R
be an integral domain.

(1)

If
a
,
b
and
c
are nonzero nonunits in
R
and
a=bc
, then
(a)\subsetneqq(b)
.

(2)

(f)=(g)
iff
f=gh
for some unit
h
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose
(a)=(b)
. We get that
b=ad
for some
d
in
R
. Then
a=bc=adc
which implies that
c
is a unit. This is a contradiction.

(2)It follows from (1).
∎

Theorem 2.5.3
.

Up to a multiplication of units, every nonzero nonunit in a principal ideal domain can be written uniquely as a product of finitely many irreducibles.

Proof.

Suppose
a
is a nonzero nonunit in a principal ideal domain
R
.

Then by Corollary
2.3.1
, the element
a
is in a prime(maximal) ideal of
R
. By Proposition
2.5.1
, there is an irreducible
p_{1}
in
R
such that
a=p_{1}a_{1}
for some
a_{1}
in
R
. Inductively we get
a_{i}=p_{i+1}a_{i+1}
for all
i\geq 1
when
a_{i}
is a nonunit. However this process terminates after finite steps otherwise by Lemma
2.5.2
, we can get an ascending sequence of ideals

(a)\subsetneqq(a_{1})\subsetneqq(a_{2})\subsetneqq\cdots\subsetneqq(a_{n})\subsetneqq(a_{n+1})\subsetneqq\cdots

which does not satisfies the ascending chain condition, which contradicts to Lemma
2.5.1
.

Hence
a
can be written as a product of finitely many irreducibles.

If
a=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}=q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{l}
for
p_{i}^{\prime}s
and
q_{j}^{\prime}s
being irreducibles, then by Corollary
2.5.1
, each
p_{i}
divides some
q_{j}
, which means that
p_{i}=b_{i}q_{j}
for some unit
b_{i}
in
R
. Hence
k=l
and the decomposition of
a
into a product of irreducibles is unique up to a multiplication of units.
∎

2.6.
Eisenstein’s criterion and Gauss’s lemma

In this section, we introduce Eisentein criterion and Gauss’s lemma, which briefly speaking, are tools for finding irreducible polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[x]
.

Let
R
be a unital commutative ring. An irreducible in
R[x]
is called an
irreducible polynomial
.

Theorem
2.5.1
tells us that
\mathbb{F}[x]
is a principal ideal domain. Apply what we get for principal ideal domains, the following hold for
\mathbb{F}[x]
:

Theorem 2.6.1
.

(1)

For an irreducible
p
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
, if
p|fg
, then either
p|f
or
p|g
.

(2)

Up to scalar multiplications, every polynomial of degree greater than 0 in
\mathbb{F}[x]
can be uniquely written as a product of irreducible polynomials.

(3)

A nonzero ideal
(f)
of
\mathbb{F}[x]
is prime iff
f
is irreducible.

Eisenstein’s criterion together with Gauss’s lemma are sufficient conditions for a polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x]
, in particular, in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
to be irreducible.
[
6
]

Theorem 2.6.2

(Eisentein criterion)
.

Let
R
be an integral domain and
P
is a prime ideal of
R
. If
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
in
R[x]
satisfies that
a_{n}
is not in
P
,
a_{0},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n-1}
are in
P
and
a_{0}
is not in
P^{2}
, then
f
is irreducible in
R[x]
.

Proof.

Suppose
f(x)=g(x)h(x)
for
g,h
in
R[x]
with
\deg(g)=k<n
and
\deg(h)=l<n
. Assume that
g(x)=c_{k}x^{k}+c_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+c_{1}x+c_{0}
and

h(x)=d_{l}x^{l}+d_{l-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+d_{1}x+d_{0}
with
c_{i}
’s and
d_{j}
’s in
R
. We are going to prove that
c_{0}
and
d_{0}
are in
P
, which contradicts that
a_{0}=c_{0}d_{0}
is not in
P^{2}
.

Note that
R[x]/P[x]\cong(R/P)[x]
. See Exercise
2.21
.

Hence
f+P[x]=a_{n}x^{n}+P[x]=(a_{n}+P)x^{n}
by assumption. Moreover
g+P[x]=(c_{k}+P)x^{k}+(c_{k-1}+P)x^{k-1}+\cdots+(c_{1}+P)x+c_{0}+P
,
h+P[x]=(d_{l}+P)x^{l}+(d_{k-1}+P)x^{l-1}+\cdots+(d_{1}+P)x+d_{0}+P
and
f+P[x]=(g+P[x])(h+P[x])
.

Hence

\displaystyle c_{0}d_{0}+P=0

\displaystyle c_{1}d_{0}+c_{0}d_{1}+P=0

\displaystyle\cdots\,\,\,\cdots\,\,\,\cdots

\displaystyle c_{0}d_{l}+c_{1}d_{l-1}+\cdots+c_{l}d_{0}+P=0

If one of
c_{0}+P
and
d_{0}+P
, say,
c_{0}+P\neq 0
, then
d_{0}+P=0
and inductively
d_{1}+P=d_{2}+P=\cdots=d_{l-1}+P=d_{l}+P=0
. This contradicts that
c_{k}d_{l}+P=a_{n}+P\neq 0
. So
c_{0}+P
and
d_{0}+P
are zero, which means,
c_{0}d_{0}
is in
P^{2}
.
∎

Theorem 2.6.3

(Gauss’s lemma)
.

Suppose
R
is a principal ideal domain with the fractional field
\mathbb{F}
. Then
f
in
R[x]
is irreducible in
R[x]
iff
f
is irreducible in
\mathbb{F}[x]
.

Proof.

Suppose
f
is an irreducible polynomial in
R[x]
and
f=gh
for
g(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
and
h(x)=b_{m}x^{m}+b_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\cdots+b_{1}x+b_{0}
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
.

By Corollary
2.2.1
, there is
a,b
in
R
such that
g_{1}=ag
and
h_{1}=bh
are in
R[x]
. Denote
ab
by
c
, and
c=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
for
p_{j}
’s being irreducibles in
R
by Theorem
2.5.3
. We have

\displaystyle p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}f=g_{1}h_{1}=

\displaystyle(\tilde{a}_{n}x^{n}+\tilde{a}_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+\tilde{a}_{1}x+\tilde{a}_{0})

\displaystyle(\tilde{b}_{m}x^{m}+\tilde{b}_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\cdots+\tilde{b}_{1}x+\tilde{b}_{0}),

where
\tilde{a}_{i}=a_{i}a
and
\tilde{b}_{j}=b_{j}b
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq m
. Then
p_{1}
divides either all
\tilde{a}_{i}
’s or
\tilde{b}_{j}
’s by induction.

So
p_{1}
can be canceled out and we get
p_{2}\cdots p_{k}=h_{2}g_{2}
with
g_{2},h_{2}
in
R[x]
,
\deg g_{2}=\deg g
and
\deg h_{2}=\deg h
.

Repeat this process, we obtain that
f=g_{k}h_{k}
with
g_{k},h_{k}
in
R[x]
,
\deg g_{k}=\deg g
and
\deg h_{k}=\deg h
. Since
f
is irreducible in
R[x]
, we have either
\deg g_{k}=0
or
\deg h_{k}=0
. Hence either
\deg g=0
or
\deg h=0
. Therefore
f
is irreducible in
\mathbb{F}[x]
.
∎

Remark 2.6.1
.

in

.

(2) Gauss’s lemma holds for polynomial ring over a unique factorization domain, which is more general than principal ideal domain. Interested readers may consult

]

]

Chapter 3
Basic Field Theory

Though fields are special cases of rings, the definition of field appeared earlier than that of ring. In this chapter, we deal with field extension theory, in particular, finite extensions of the rational field
\mathbb{Q}
, serving for the fundamental theorem of Galois theory.

3.1.
Finite fields

Recall that a field is a unital commutative ring in which every nonzero is a unit.

Below are some examples of finite fields.

Examples 3.1.1
.

For a prime
p
,
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is a field. Denote it by
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Given an irreducible polynomial
f
of degree
n
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
. Then
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(f)
is a field since
(f)
is a maximal ideal of
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
. Moreover
|\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(f)|=p^{n}
since

\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(f)=\{a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+a_{n-2}x^{n-2}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}+(f)\,|\,a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\,\text{for\,all\,}1\leq i\leq n-1\}.

For instance
x^{2}+x+1
is an irreducible polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]
, then the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^{2}+x+1)
has 4 elements:
(x^{2}+x+1)
,
1+(x^{2}+x+1)
,
x+(x^{2}+x+1)
and
x+1+(x^{2}+x+1)
. So
|\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^{2}+x+1)|=4
.

Definition 3.1.1
.

The
characteristic
of a field
\mathbb{F}
is the smallest positive integer
n
such that
n=0
. Denote it by
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{F}
. If every positive integer is nonzero, then we say
\mathbb{F}
is of characteristic 0.

It is an exercise to check that for any field
\mathbb{F}
, either
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{F}=0
or
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{F}
is prime.

If
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{F}=p
, then
pa=0
for every
a
in
\mathbb{F}
.

A finite field is of finite characteristic. Later we will see examples of infinite fields which are of finite characteristic.

One can see that
\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
are all of characteristic 0, and
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{F}_{p}=p
.

Theorem 3.1.1
.

If
\mathbb{F}
is a finite field and
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{F}=p
, then
|\mathbb{F}|=p^{n}
for some positive integer
n
.

Proof.

Suppose
|\mathbb{F}|=p^{n}m
for some positive integer
m
prime to
p
. If
m>1
, then
m
has a prime factor
q
distinct from
p
. Apply Cauchy’s theorem to the additive group
\mathbb{F}
. There is an element
a
in
\mathbb{F}
of order
q
. So
qa=0
.

Since
q
is prime to
p
, we have
p=lq+r
with
0<r<q
. It follows that
0=pa=lqa+ra=ra
, which contradicts that the order of
a
is
q
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Vector spaces

In linear algebra, one deals with vector spaces over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Actually vector spaces could be defined over any field.

Definition 3.2.1
.

A
vector space

V
over a field
\mathbb{F}
is a nonempty set on which the operations of addition and scalar multiplication are defined. By this we mean that, with each pair of elements
x
and
y
in
V
, we can associate a unique element
x+y
that is also in
V
, and with each element
x
in
V
and each
\alpha
in
\mathbb{F}
, we can associate a unique element
\alpha x
in
V
. Moreover the following hold:

(1)

(V,+)
is an abelian group.

(2)

(\alpha\beta)x=\alpha(\beta x)
for each
x
in
V
and all
\alpha,\beta
in
\mathbb{F}
.

(3)

\alpha(x+y)=\alpha x+\alpha y
and
(\alpha+\beta)x=\alpha x+\beta x
for all
x,y
in
V
and all
\alpha,\beta
in
\mathbb{F}
.

(4)

1x=x
for each
x
in
V
.

An elements in a vector space is called a
vector
. The field
\mathbb{F}
is called the
scalar field
of
V
.

A
subspace
of a vector space is a subset which itself is a vector space.

For vectors
v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n}
in a vector space
V
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
in
\mathbb{F}
, the expression
\alpha_{1}v_{1}+\alpha_{2}v_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}v_{n}
is called a
linear combination
of
v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n}
.
The set of all linear combinations of
v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n}
is called the
linear span
of
v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n}
, denoted by
\text{Span}(v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n})
.

Vectors
v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n}
in a vector space
V
are called
linearly independent
if
\alpha_{1}v_{1}+\alpha_{2}v_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}v_{n}=0
implies that
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\cdots=\alpha_{n}=0
. Otherwise
v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n}
are called
linearly dependent
.

A subset
A
of a vector space is called linearly independent if any vectors
v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n}
in
A
are linearly independent. If a vector space
V
has a maximal linearly independent finite subset, then
V
is called
finite dimensional
, otherwise
infinite dimensional
.

A set of vectors
\{v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n}\}
in a finite dimensional vector space
V
is called a
basis
of
V
if they are linearly independent and
V=\text{Span}(v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n})
.

The proof of the following theorem is the same as the analogous result in linear algebra. We leave it as an exercise.

Theorem 3.2.1
.

Any two bases of a vector space have the same cardinality.

For a finite dimensional vector space
V
over a field
\mathbb{F}
, the cardinality of a basis of
V
is called the
dimension
of
V
, denoted by
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V
. Denote by
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}V=\infty
when
V
is infinite dimensional. For example,
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\{0\}=0
,

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}=2
,
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}=1
, and
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}=\infty
.

If
\mathbb{F}
is a subfield of
\mathbb{E}
, then
\mathbb{E}
is a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
. Denote
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{E}
by
[\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}]
.

Theorem 3.2.2
.

If
\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{E}\subseteq\mathbb{K}
are fields, then

[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]=[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{E}][\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}].

Proof.

We may assume that
[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{E}]
and
[\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}]
are finite since
[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]\geq[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{E}]
and
[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]\geq[\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}]
.

Let
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a basis of
\mathbb{E}
over
\mathbb{F}
and
\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be a basis of
\mathbb{K}
over
\mathbb{E}
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\sum\alpha_{ij}v_{i}w_{j}=0
for
\alpha_{ij}
’s in
\mathbb{F}
.
Then
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}v_{i})w_{j}=0
. Since
\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{E}
, we have
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}v_{i}=0
for every
1\leq j\leq m
. Furthermore since
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
, we have
\alpha_{ij}=0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq m
. So
\{v_{i}w_{j}\}_{1\leq i\leq n\,,1\leq j\leq m}
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
.

For every
v
in
\mathbb{K}
, one has
v=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}w_{j}
for
\beta_{j}^{\prime}s
in
\mathbb{E}
. Moreover each
\beta_{j}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{ji}v_{i}
for
\alpha_{ji}
’s in
\mathbb{F}
. Hence
v=\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ji}v_{i}w_{j}
, i.e., the set
\{v_{i}w_{j}\}_{1\leq i\leq n\,,1\leq j\leq m}
spans
\mathbb{K}
.

Therefore
\{v_{i}w_{j}\}_{1\leq i\leq n\,,1\leq j\leq m}
is a basis of
\mathbb{K}
over
\mathbb{F}
. Consequently

[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]=mn=[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{E}][\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}].

∎

3.3.
Fields extensions

3.3.1.
Fields extensions containing roots of polynomials

If
\mathbb{F}
is isomorphic to a subfield of
\mathbb{E}
, then
\mathbb{E}
is called an
extension
of
\mathbb{F}
. If
[\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}]<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}
is called a
finite extension
of
\mathbb{F}
, otherwise, an
infinite extension
of
\mathbb{F}
.

Assume that
\mathbb{F}
is a subfield of
\mathbb{E}
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
are in
\mathbb{E}
. Denote by

\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
the smallest subfield of
\mathbb{E}
containing
\mathbb{F}
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
. Call
\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
the field generated by
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
over
\mathbb{F}
.

For example, the field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})
is the subfield of
\mathbb{R}
generated by
\sqrt{2}
over
\mathbb{Q}
. One can see that
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})=\{a+b\sqrt{2}\,|\,a,b\in\mathbb{Q}\}
and
[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}]=2
.

Theorem 3.3.1
.

Suppose
p
is an irreducible polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x]
. Then there exists an extension
\mathbb{E}
of
\mathbb{F}
such that
\mathbb{E}
contains a root of
p
.

Proof.

Since
(p)
is a maximal ideal,
\mathbb{F}[x]/(p)
is a field. Denote it by
\mathbb{E}
.

Define
\varphi:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{E}
by
\varphi(a)=a+(p)
for all
a
in
\mathbb{F}
. Then
\varphi
is a nonzero homomorphism, hence injective. So
\mathbb{F}
is isomorphic to
\text{Im}\varphi=\varphi(\mathbb{F})=\{a+(p)\,|\,a\in\mathbb{F}\}
, which is, a subfield of
\mathbb{E}
.

Now we can identify
p=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
with
\tilde{p}=(a_{n}+(p))x^{n}+(a_{n-1}+(p))x^{n-1}+\cdots+(a_{1}+(p))x+(a_{0}+(p))
in
\varphi(\mathbb{F})[x]
.

Moreover

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(x+(p))=(a_{n}+(p))(x+(p))^{n}+(a_{n-1}+(p))(x+(p))^{n-1}+\cdots

\displaystyle+(a_{1}+(p))(x+(p))+(a_{0}+(p))=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}+(p)

\displaystyle=p+(p)=0.

So
\tilde{p}
has a root,
x+(p)
in
\mathbb{E}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3.1
.

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is a subfield of
\mathbb{E}
and
\alpha
in
\mathbb{E}
is a root of an irreducible polynomial
p
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
. Then

\mathbb{F}(\alpha)=\{f(\alpha)\,|\,f\in\mathbb{F}[x]\}
.

Proof.

Denote
\{f(\alpha)\,|\,f\in\mathbb{F}[x]\}
by
\mathbb{K}
. Clearly
\mathbb{K}
is a unital commutative subring of
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)
. Moreover if
f(\alpha)\neq 0
, then
f
is prime to
p
since
p
is irreducible. There exist
g,h
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
such that
fg+ph=1
. Hence
f(\alpha)g(\alpha)=1
. This shows that
\mathbb{K}
is a field. So
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)\subseteq\mathbb{K}
.

Therefore
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)=\mathbb{K}
.
∎

Theorem 3.3.2
.

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is a subfield of
\mathbb{E}
and
\alpha
in
\mathbb{E}
is a root of an irreducible polynomial
p
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
. Then
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)\cong\mathbb{F}[x]/(p)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3.1
,
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)=\mathbb{K}=\{f(\alpha)\,|\,f\in\mathbb{F}[x]\}
.

Define
\varphi:\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{F}[x]/(p)
by
\varphi(f(\alpha))=f+(p)
for all
f
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
.

•

If
f_{1}(\alpha)=f_{2}(\alpha)
, then
(f_{1}-f_{2})(\alpha)=0
. Either
f_{1}-f_{2}
is prime to
p
or
p
divides
f_{1}-f_{2}
. Since
p(\alpha)=0
, it’s impossible that
f_{1}-f_{2}
is prime to
p
. Thus
p
divides
f_{1}-f_{2}
, which means,
f_{1}+(p)=f_{2}+(p)
. So
\varphi
is well-defined.

•

\varphi(f_{1}(\alpha)f_{2}(\alpha))=\varphi(f_{1}f_{2}(\alpha))=f_{1}f_{2}+(p)=(f_{1}+(p))(f_{2}+(p))=\varphi(f_{1}(\alpha))\varphi(f_{2}(\alpha))
, and

\varphi(f_{1}(\alpha)+f_{2}(\alpha))=\varphi((f_{1}+f_{2})(\alpha))=f_{1}+f_{2}+(p)=(f_{1}+(p))+(f_{2}+(p))=\varphi(f_{1}(\alpha))+\varphi(f_{2}(\alpha))
. Hence
\varphi
is a ring homomorphism.

•

If
f+(p)=0
, then
f
is in
(p)
, i.e.,
p
divides
f
. So
f(\alpha)=0
. Hence
\varphi
is injective.

•

Obviously
\varphi
is surjective.

Therefore
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)\cong\mathbb{F}[x]/(p)
.
∎

We say a polynomial
f
in
\mathbb{F}[x]

split
s over a field
\mathbb{E}\supseteq\mathbb{F}
if all roots of
f
are in
\mathbb{E}
.

A polynomial
f
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
is called

if the leading coefficient of the polynomial is 1, i.e.,
f(x)=x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
.

Theorem 3.3.3
.

For every nonzero monic polynomial
f
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
, there exists an extension
\mathbb{E}
of
\mathbb{F}
over which
f
splits.

Proof.

We do induction on
\deg f
.

If
\deg f=1
, then
f
splits over
\mathbb{F}
.

Suppose the statement holds for any polynomial of degree less than
n
. Hence every polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x]
of degree less than
n
splits over an extension field of
\mathbb{F}
.

Assume that
f
is an irreducible monic polynomial with
\deg f=n
.

By Theorem
3.3.1
, there is an extension
\mathbb{K}
of
\mathbb{F}
which contains a root
\alpha
of
f
, which means,
f(x)=(x-\alpha)g(x)
with
\alpha
in
\mathbb{K}
and

in
\mathbb{K}[x]
. By assumption, there is an extension
\mathbb{E}
of
\mathbb{K}
over which

splits. Consequently

splits over
\mathbb{E}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.3.2.
Algebraic extensions

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is a subfield of
\mathbb{E}
. An element
\alpha
in
\mathbb{E}
is called
algebraic
over
\mathbb{F}
if it is a root of a nonzero polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x]
. For example,
\sqrt{2}
is algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}
since it is a root of
x^{2}-2
.

Theorem 3.3.4
.

If
\alpha
in
\mathbb{E}
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
, then there exists a unique monic irreducible polynomial
p
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
such that
p(\alpha)=0
.

Proof.

We first prove the existence of
p
.

Since
\alpha
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
, there is a monic polynomial
f
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
such that
f(\alpha)=0
. Write
f
as a product of irreducible monic polynomials:
f=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}
. Then
p_{i}(\alpha)=0
for some
1\leq i\leq m
.

Suppose
p
and
q
are distinct monic irreducible polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[x]
with a common root
\alpha
. Since
p
and
q
are coprime, there exist
g,h
in
\mathbb{F}
such that
pg+qh=1
. Then
0=p(\alpha)g(\alpha)+q(\alpha)h(\alpha)=1
, which is a contradiction. This proves the uniqueness of
p
.

∎

Call
p
the
minimal polynomial
of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{F}
and denote it by
m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}(x)
.

Corollary 3.3.1
.

Suppose
\alpha
in
\mathbb{E}
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
. If
f
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
satisfies that
f(\alpha)=0
, then
m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}(x)
divides
f
. That is, among all nonzero polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[x]
with
\alpha
being a root,
m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}(x)
has the smallest degree.

Proof.

Since
m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}(x)
is irreducible, either
m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}(x)
divides
f
or
m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}
is prime to
f
. As the argument in the proof of Theorem
3.3.4
shows, it’s impossible that
m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}
is prime to
f
.
∎

The degree of
m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}(x)
is called the
algebraic degree
of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{F}
.

Corollary 3.3.2
.

Suppose
\alpha
in
\mathbb{E}
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
. Then
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)\cong\mathbb{F}[x]/(m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}})
.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
3.3.2
and Theorem
3.3.4
.
∎

Theorem 3.3.5
.

\alpha
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
iff
[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]<\infty
. Moreover
[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]
equals the algebraic degree of
\alpha
when
\alpha
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\alpha
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
with the minimal polynomial
m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}
and
\deg m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}=n
.

By Lemma
3.3.1
,
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)=\mathbb{K}=\{f(\alpha)\,|\,f\in\mathbb{F}[x]\}
. So
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)=\text{Span}(1,\alpha,\cdots,\alpha^{n-1})
. It follows that
[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]\leq n<\infty
. Moreover
\{1,\alpha,\cdots,\alpha^{n-1}\}
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
otherwise one can find a nonzero polynomial
f
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
with degree smaller than
n
such that
f(\alpha)=0
, which contradicts that
\deg m_{\alpha,\mathbb{F}}=n
. Hence
[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]=n
.

Suppose that
[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]=n
. Then
\{1,\alpha,\cdots,\alpha^{n-1},\alpha^{n}\}
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
. Hence there is a nonzero polynomial
f
of degree no greater than
n
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
such that
f(\alpha)=0
, which means,
\alpha
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
.

∎

Theorem 3.3.6
.

If
\alpha,\beta
in
\mathbb{E}
are algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
, then
\alpha\pm\beta
,
\alpha\beta
and
\alpha^{-1}
are also algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
. That is, algebraic elements over
\mathbb{F}
form a subfield of
\mathbb{E}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{F}(\alpha+\beta)
is a subfield of
\mathbb{F}(\alpha,\beta)
and
[\mathbb{F}(\alpha,\beta):\mathbb{F}]=[\mathbb{F}(\alpha,\beta):\mathbb{F}(\alpha)][\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]<\infty
, by Theorem

,
\alpha+\beta
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
. Similarly
\alpha-\beta
and
\alpha\beta
are also algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
.

Moreover

Let

The following theorem is a characterization of finite extensions via algebraic extensions.

Theorem 3.3.7
.

\mathbb{E}
is a finite extension of
\mathbb{F}
iff in
\mathbb{E}
, there exist algebraic
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
over

such that
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbb{E}
is a finite extension of
\mathbb{F}
. Let
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a basis of
\mathbb{E}
over
\mathbb{F}
. Since
[\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{i}):\mathbb{F}]\leq[\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}]<\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq n

\alpha_{i}
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
. Moreover
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
.

Conversely if there exist algebraic

in
\mathbb{E}
such that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
are algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
and

\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
. Hence for every
1\leq i\leq n

is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{i})
, that is,
[\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1}):\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{i})]<\infty

\displaystyle[\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}]=[\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n}):\mathbb{F}]=[\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n}):\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n-1})]

\displaystyle[\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n-1}):\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n-2})]\cdots[\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}):[\mathbb{F}(\alpha_{1})]<\infty.

So

is a finite extension of

.
∎

\displaystyle x^{n}-1=\prod_{d|n}\Phi_{d}(x)=\Phi_{n}(x)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d<n\\
d|n\end{subarray}}\Phi_{d}(x).

It follows from the assumption that
\Phi_{n}(x)
is in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
.

Define
{\rm F}_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq d<n\\
d|n\end{subarray}}\Phi_{d}(x)
.

Then
x^{n}-1={\rm F}_{n}\Phi_{n}
. By assumption every
\Phi_{d}(x)
is in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
and has constant term
\pm 1
for all
1\leq d<n
and
d|n
, so is
{\rm F}_{n}
.

Hence the constant term of
\Phi_{n}(x)
is
\pm 1
. Inductively every coefficient of
\Phi_{n}(x)
is an integer.

(ii)According to Gauss’s lemma, it suffices to prove that
\Phi_{n}(x)
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

Suppose
f
is an irreducible monic polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
which divides
\Phi_{n}(x)
. We are going to show that every
\zeta_{n}^{m}
is a root of
f
which implies that
f=\Phi_{n}
and
\Phi_{n}
is irreducible.

For any root
\zeta
of
f
, we show that
f(\zeta^{p})=0
for any prime
p\nmid n
, which implies that
f(\zeta_{n}^{m})=0
for any
1\leq m\leq n
prime to n.

Suppose there exists
p\nmid n
such that
f(\zeta^{p})\neq 0
for some root
\zeta
of
f
. Since
\zeta^{p}
is a root of
\Phi_{n}
, there is
g
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that
\Phi_{n}=fg
and
g(\zeta^{p})=0
. That is to say that
\zeta
is a root of the polynomial
g(x^{p})
. Then
g(x^{p})=f(x)h(x)
for
h
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
since
f
is irreducible and
\zeta
is a root of
f
.

Consider the ring homomorphism from
\mathbb{Z}[x]
to
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
mapping
q=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{k}x^{k}
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
to
\bar{q}=\bar{a}_{0}+\bar{a}_{1}x+\cdots+\bar{a}_{k}x^{k}
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
. For
g(x)=b_{0}+b_{1}x+\cdots+b_{l}x^{l}
, one has

\bar{f}(x)\bar{h}(x)=\bar{g}(x^{p})=\bar{b}_{0}+\bar{b}_{1}x^{p}+\cdots+\bar{b}_{l}x^{lp}=\bar{b}_{0}^{p}+\bar{b}_{1}^{p}x^{p}+\cdots+\bar{b}_{l}^{p}x^{lp}=(\bar{g}(x))^{p}.

Here
\bar{b}_{i}^{p}=\bar{b}_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq l
follows from Fermat’s little theorem.

Hence
\bar{f}(x)
and
\bar{g}(x)
have a common root. Thus
\overline{\Phi_{n}}=\bar{f}\bar{g}
is inseparable in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
. However
\overline{\Phi_{n}}
is separable in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
since
x^{n}-1
is separable in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
. This is a contradiction.

∎

The field
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})
is called a
cyclotomic extension
of
\mathbb{Q}
.

3.5.
Separable extensions

In this section, we discuss separable extensions which paves the way for later discussions of Galois extensions.

Proposition 3.5.1
.

Assume that
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{F}=0
. Then every irreducible polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x]
is separable. Moreover a monic polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x]
is separable iff it is a product of distinct monic irreducible polynomials.

Proof.

If
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{F}=0
, then
D(p)\neq 0
and
\deg(D(p))<\deg p
for every irreducible polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x]
. Hence
p
is prime to
D(p)
.

Every
f
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
is a product of irreducibles and a root of
f
is multiple iff this root is a common root of at least two of these irreducibles. However distinct irreducibles have no common roots. This shows that
f
is separable iff
f
is a product of distinct irreducibles.
∎

Lemma 3.5.1
.

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is a field with
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{F}=p
. Then
\varphi:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
given by
\varphi(a)=a^{p}
for all
a
in
\mathbb{F}
is an injective ring homomorphism. Moreover if
\mathbb{F}
is a finite field, then
\varphi
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

For all
a,b
in
\mathbb{F}
, we have
\varphi(a+b)=(a+b)^{p}=\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}a^{i}b^{p-i}=a^{p}+b^{p}
since
p
divides
\binom{p}{i}
’s for all
1\leq i\leq p-1
, and
\varphi(ab)=(ab)^{p}=a^{p}b^{p}=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)
. So
\varphi
is a ring homomorphism. Moreover
\varphi(1)=1
, that is,
\varphi
is a nonzero ring homomorphism between fields, hence
\varphi
is injective.

When
\mathbb{F}
is finite, the injection
\varphi
is also a surjection, hence an isomorphism.
∎

Definition 3.5.1
.

A field
\mathbb{K}
is called
perfect
if
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{K}=0
or
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{K}^{p}
when
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{K}=p
, otherwise call it
imperfect
.

Except for fields with characteristic 0, by Lemma
3.5.1
, finite fields are also perfect fields.

The fraction field of
\mathbb{F}_{2}[t]
,
\rm{Frac}(\mathbb{F}_{2}[t])=\{\dfrac{p}{q}\,|\,p,q\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[t],\,q\neq 0\}
is imperfect since no
p,q
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[t]
satisfy that
(\frac{p}{q})^{2}=t
.

Theorem 3.5.1
.

Every irreducible polynomial over a perfect field is separable. Moreover a polynomial over a perfect field is separable iff it is a product of distinct irreducible polynomials.

Proof.

For fields of characteristic 0, we already prove the statements in Proposition
3.5.1
.

Assume that
{\rm Ch}\mathbb{K}=p
and
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{K}^{p}
, which means, the ring homomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
given by
\varphi(a)=a^{p}
for all
a
in
\mathbb{F}
is surjective.

Suppose
f
is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}
. If
f
is not separable, then
f
is not prime to
D(f)
, which means,
f
divides
D(f)
. This only happens when
D(f)=0
. Hence
f=a_{m}x^{mp}+a_{m-1}x^{(m-1)p}+\cdots+a_{1}x^{p}+a_{0}
. Since
\varphi
is surjective, we have
a_{i}=b_{i}^{p}
for all
0\leq i\leq m
. Therefore

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=a_{m}x^{mp}+a_{m-1}x^{(m-1)p}+\cdots+a_{1}x^{p}+a_{0}

\displaystyle=b_{m}^{p}x^{mp}+b_{m-1}^{p}x^{(m-1)p}+\cdots+b_{1}^{p}x^{p}+b_{0}^{p}

\displaystyle=(b_{m}x^{m}+b_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\cdots+b_{1}x+b_{0})^{p},

which contradicts that
f
is irreducible. Thus every irreducible polynomial over
\mathbb{F}
is separable.

The second statement follows from the first statement and the argument is similar to that in Proposition
3.5.1
.
∎

Definition 3.5.2
.

A field extension
\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}
is called a
separable extension
if every element of
\mathbb{K}
is a root of a separable polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x]
.

Corollary 3.5.1
.

Every finite extension of a perfect field is separable.

Over an imperfect field, there exist irreducible inseparable polynomials.

Let
\mathbb{F}=\rm{Frac}(\mathbb{F}_{2}[t])

in
\mathbb{F}[x]
is irreducible since

has no root in
\mathbb{F}

Definition 4.1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field.

(1)

An isomorphism
\sigma:\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{K}
is called an

of
\mathbb{K}

{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K})
the set of automorphisms of
\mathbb{K}
.

(2)

An automorphism

in
\mathbb{K}

\sigma(a)=a
.

(3)

Let
\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}
be a field extension.

{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F})=\{\sigma\,|\,\sigma(a)=a\,{\rm for\,all\,a\,in}\,\mathbb{F}\}
.

Clearly
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K})
is a group under composition of maps and
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F})
is a subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K})
. For a subgroup

of
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F})
, define
\mathbb{K}^{G}=\{a\in\mathbb{K}\,|\,\sigma(a)=a\,{\rm for\,all\,\sigma\,in}\,G\}
. It’s clear that
\mathbb{K}^{G}
is an intermediate field between
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{K}
.

The following says that elements of
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F})
are permutations of roots of polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[x]
.

Proposition 4.1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}
be a field extension and
\alpha
in
\mathbb{K}
be algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
. Then for any
\sigma
in
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F})
,
\sigma(\alpha)
is also algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
with the same minimal polynomial as
\alpha
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p(x)=x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
in
\mathbb{F}[x]
is the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle p(\sigma(\alpha))=\sigma(\alpha)^{n}+a_{n-1}\sigma(\alpha)^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}\sigma(\alpha)+a_{0}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\alpha^{n}+a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}\alpha+a_{0})=\sigma(p(\alpha))=0.

So
\sigma(\alpha)
is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}
and its minimal polynomial is also
p
.
∎

For Galois theory, one mainly cares about a special type of field extensions called Galois extension, which has many characterizations. Below we give some.

Theorem 4.1.1
.

Suppose
\mathbb{K}
is a finite extension of
\mathbb{F}
.

The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathbb{K}
is a splitting field of a separable polynomial
f(x)
in
\mathbb{F}[x]

(2)

|{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F})|=[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]

(3)

\mathbb{K}^{{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F})}=\mathbb{F}
;

(4)

\mathbb{K}
is a normal separable extension of
\mathbb{F}
, i.e., every element of
\mathbb{K}
is a root of a separable irreducible polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x]
which splits over
\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

We prove that (1)
\Rightarrow
(2)
\Rightarrow
(3)
\Rightarrow
(4)
\Rightarrow
(1).

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2).

We do induction on
[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]
.

Suppose
[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]=1
. Then
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}
. So
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F})=\{e\}
and
|{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F})|=1=[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]
.

Suppose whenever
[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{E}]<n
and
\mathbb{K}
is a splitting field of a separable polynomial

\mathbb{E}[x]

|{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{E})|=[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{E}]

Now assume that
[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]=n

Let
\alpha

be a root of

with

\mathbb{K}

For convenience, denote

by
G
and
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}(\alpha))

is a subgroup of

Next we construct a bijection
\psi_{\alpha}

Every

4.2.
The fundamental theorem of Galois theory

In this section, we prove the fundamental theorem of Galois theory.

Let

be a field and

Proof.

If

We conclude from the above that
