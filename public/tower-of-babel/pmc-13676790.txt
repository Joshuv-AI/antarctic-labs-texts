Loss of power by degree of heaping in covariables
Authors: Matthias Becher, Willi Sauerbrei, Heiko Becher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heaping or digit preference is frequently occurring in health research data as well as in deliberate data masking. We investigate the associated loss of power associated by degree of heaping by simulation and illustrate the effect with a real dataset. Moderate heaping leads to little power loss and may be tolerable. However, heaping in broad categories induces major power loss and should be avoided.

Background

In health research, a common source of information on exposures is the interview of study participants. There are various strategies to obtain the information as precisely as possible. Among the option are: (i) to ask directly for the variable of interest, e.g. the exact age when a certain event occurred, (ii) by giving predefined categories, e.g. various age ranges, or (iii) simply asking a binary question, e.g. occurrence of an event before a certain age yes/no. If asked directly, the response is often heaped, meaning that there is an answering preference of round numbers. In (ii), the heaping is inherent in the questioning, and (iii) represents the extreme form of heaping with two categories only. Another term often used in literature for “heaping” is digit preference (e.g.Pickering, [1]; [2]).

In a previous analysis, we investigated the bias of the regression coefficient when a covariable is heaped [3]. In that example we used data from the NAKO, a large cohort study performed in Germany [4]. The variable of interest was age when hypertension was diagnosed, and we observed heaping for this variable. The heaping in the age variable appeared visually high, however the percentage of heaped observations was 12.6% only. We showed with a simulation study that the bias is small unless the proportion of study participants which give heaped information is very large and the heaping is in broad categories. We concluded that in this cohort study the bias was small compared to other possible sources of bias and therefore can be neglected [3]. We did not, however, investigate the loss of power, which is associated with this kind of information reduction.

Discussions on data protection and related legislation have led to restrictions being placed on the sharing of data for research purposes. In particular, the aim is to ensure that it is not possible to re-identify individuals. One consequence of this, for example in the German National Cohort, is that a person’s exact age is only disclosed in justified exceptional cases; as a rule, only their placement within 10-year age groups is provided – in other words, a post-hoc heaping is introduced. (https://nako.de/en/participating/mynako/)

Some research has been done regarding heaping. However, most research focused on either quantifying the degree of heaping [5, 6] or investigating methods to deal with heaped data [7, 8]. Denic et al., [9] investigate the quality of age data in developing countries. They find many age endings with 0 and 5 and state that the heaped data may bias results and weaken the power of a study, but they do not investigate it. Nietert et al., [10] investigated electronic medical records from 85 practices around the United States. A number ending with zero was found in 44.6% and 47.5% for systolic and diastolic blood pressure. In a secondary data analysis, [11] included 852 nutrition surveys and assessed the normality of the Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) of children from 6 to 59 months. There were 533 surveys which showed departure from normal distributions, 294 (55.2%) of them showed high digit preference.

Despite its relevance in practice, little research has been done quantifying the effect of heaping. Augustin and Wolff, [12] and [7] looked at the resulting bias from heaping. However, [12] focused specifically on the Weibull model and [7] used inferred data to quantify the bias. Heaping occurs also in many other areas, e.g. in census and other surveys, and in blurring data to avoid identifiability of individuals with a so-called bucketing function [13].

Several approaches have been proposed to estimate this bias, although their applicability depends strongly on the type of data and the underlying model. For example, Jung et al., [8] propose a fuzzy heaping model for heaped count data, while Bar and Lillard (2011) develop a model-based approach to estimate the extent of heaping in retrospectively reported event data.

Although heaping differs from classical measurement error, some methods developed to address measurement error are probably transferable to mitigate biases introduced by heaping. A comprehensive discussion of measurement error and correction methods is provided in a two-part paper by the STRATOS initiative’s topic group “Measurement Error and Misclassification” [14, 15].

Regarding the application of these methods, the authors conclude in Part I: “However, our impression is that even when such quantitative information is available, the adjusted analyses are not being performed.” This observation likely also applies to biases arising from heaping.

In this paper, we investigate the loss of power for increasing degree of heaping which to the best of our knowledge has not yet been considered. Dichotomizing a continuous covariable can be considered as the extreme form of heaping which is still often done in the analysis of epidemiological and clinical data although it is known for decades that dichotomizing a continuous variable introduces several severe issues and should be avoided [16–19].

In this paper we demonstrate the loss of power induced by heaping by means of a simulation study in the context of the logistic regression model. For practical illustration we use data from a case-control study on stroke [20].

Loss of power by degree of heaping δ - Results of the simulation study

*D – Dichotomization at the median

We observe that with increasingly coarse heaping the p-value gets more and more distorted. For a moderate heaping (up to rounding to the next multiple of 5) the loss of power is mild. As expected, the largest power reduction is for the extreme case when the variable is dichotomized. Note that the change in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{\beta\:}$$\end{document} with increasing degree of heaping describes the bias through heaping. The simulation shows that a bias is apparent only if the heaping is rather coarse (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\delta\:}=10$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\delta\:}=20$$\end{document} ). There is no bias in the case of dichotomization if the expected value of X given < 0 resp. >0 is assigned to X. Note also that the p-value in case of dichotomization is independent of the assigned value. Thus, if the assigned values are 0 and 1, as commonly done for a dichotomized variable, the p-value remains unchanged.

Figure 1 shows the simulated power for our scenarios across the different rounding values as the proportion of observations with p-values smaller than 0.05 or 0.01 by degree of heaping. It further supports the observations made in Table 2. Loss of power is almost negligible up to heaping to the nearest multiple of 10. As expected, dichotomization is generally associated with a relevant drop in power in all scenarios.

Data example

We use data from a case-control study on stroke with the primary aim to investigate the effect of dental infections on stroke risk [20]. The study was performed in the years 1998–2000 in Heidelberg, Germany, and included 303 incident cases of ischemic stroke (208 males, 95 females) and 468 controls (302 males, 166 females). As main risk factors smoking and alcohol consumption were considered. In this analysis we excluded tea-totallers and never smokers. The analysis is then based on 181 cases and 221 controls. We use years of smoking and total amount of alcohol as quantitative variables. Assuming linear effects for both variables, the logistic regression analysis allows estimating the joint odds ratio function for the two main risk factors of stroke among those who were smokers and who consumed alcohol. Age (in years, linear effect) and sex are included as confounders. For both continuous risk variables the effect of heaping on the estimates and the p-values was investigated. As there was little heaping in the original data, we induced heaping for this analysis. Smoking was heaped by rounding to the next value of multiples of 2, 5, 10, and dichotomising at the median. The cutpoint here was 29 years. For alcohol we used 50, 100, 200, 500 and dichotomising at the median. The cutpoint here was 115.012 L. When dichotomizing, we assigned the observed mean given above resp. below the cutpoint to each individual regardless of case-control status. The means are 17.00 years and 39.68 years for smoking, and 37.75 L and 557.21 L for alcohol.

Figures 3 and 4 show the distribution of after heaping to the different rounding values for alcohol and smoking respectively. For dichotomization each value smaller than or equal to the median was set to the 25%-quantile and each value larger to the 75%-quantile.

The distribution of the alcohol variable is heavily right skewed. In contrast, the distribution of smoking is fairly symmetric. Discussion

Heaping or digit preference is common in observational data and can be considered as a special kind of measurement error. While the effect of heaping in terms of both precision and bias may often be small, it can lead to relevant problems such as distorted effect estimates, false decisions concerning inclusion of variables in a multivariable model and therefore seriously wrong conclusions, in particular when heaping is close to dichotomization as the extreme case of heaping. In this study we investigated the effect of different levels of heaping, and the associated information loss, on power. To that end we have conducted a simulation study. The results clearly show that an increasingly coarse heaping leads to higher p-values and thus to a loss in power. As expected, dichotomization leads to maximal power loss.

Although several critical issues are known for decades [16, 18], dichotomization of continuous variables is still very popular in many areas of research. For example, continuous biomarkers are often dichotomized to assess whether they have a prognostic effect [24] and in randomized trials interactions with continuous variables (e.g. age) are usually investigated by selecting a small number of values to create two, three or four subgroups [25]. In the latter context, increased type I error and loss of power was shown in simulation studies [26, 27].

It is important to note, that the simulation results represent a scenario in which all data is heaped. In contrast, in epidemiologic studies as in our data example, researchers are likely to encounter partial heaping which would reduce the associated power loss. In case of intended data blurring, heaping applies to all observations, and then the simulation results are directly applicable.

Our results may also be relevant for statistical disclosure control (SDC) and therefore for making data publicly available such that they are sufficiently anonymous and still allow an analysis with almost full power. SDC, discussed for decades, seeks to modify statistical data so that they can be published without giving away confidential information that can be linked to specific respondents. The challenge for SDC is to achieve this modification with minimum loss of the detail and accuracy sought by database users. The aforementioned application of a bucketing function which is not too broad could be suitable [13]. Further discussions on de-identification of individuals in other research areas in the context of data sharing is given e.g. in [28]. Here they use the term „microaggegation“ for heaping. They conclude that “This technique is very useful for de-identifying data where publicly available data that can be linked exist. “. No statement on the effect on the results are provided. We also conclude that the data protection policy of the NAKO cohort study is tolerable since the power loss induced by providing age in 10-year age groups is small. On the other hand, a re-identification is made considerably more difficult.

Our approach – heaping - considers a specific deviation from the true values. The results are, as expected, in line with established knowledge regarding information loss arising from measurement error, discretization, grouping, and dichotomization of continuous variables.

In our paper, we considered a binary outcome and a logistic regression model. We assume that our results generally hold for regression models [14, 29]. In order to further support this assumption, in appendix A.3 we report the results from the simulation based on a linear model. Here we use an equation which may resemble the relation between age and systolic blood pressure [30]. The results are totally in line with the results from the logistic regression.

We also illustrated the effect of heaping with real data from a case-control study. Overall, the results from the data example match the ones from the simulation. However, they also showed that the relationship between heaping and p-value is not strictly monotonous and depend on the distributions of the variables and their correlation. While the power generally decreases with increasing heaping, there were single instances in which the p-value increased. This is likely due to the data structure of this particular data set and means that in real data the effect can be unpredictable.

In summary, our results show that mild levels of heaping, no matter whether they result from direct responses from a fraction of study participants, or from deliberate data blurring, are acceptable from a statistical point of view, since it causes only negligible and tolerable power loss. According to our experience, this is the common case in practice. Extreme cases of heaping, however, such as dichotomization or rounding such that very few distinct levels remain should be avoided.
