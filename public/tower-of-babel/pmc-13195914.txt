YouTube as a tool for core stability exercise training a content evaluation study
Authors: Ali Zorlular, Rabia Zorlular
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYouTube® has become a widely used source of health-related information, and individuals with different health conditions increasingly rely on this platform to obtain exercise guidance. The aim of this study was to assess the content accuracy, reliability, and quality of YouTube videos related to core stability exercise training.MethodsA systematic search on YouTube using keywords related to core exercises identified relevant videos. The videos were classified according to their content characteristics and the professional identity of the uploader. Content reliability and video quality were assessed using the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) and the Global Quality Scale (GQS), respectively. Video popularity was quantitatively assessed using two commonly used metrics: the Video Power Index (VPI) and the View Ratio (VR). Each video was independently evaluated by two physiotherapists experienced in exercise and rehabilitation.ResultsA total of 136 videos were analyzed, of which 89% were classified as useful and 11% as misleading, with statistically significant differences in mDISCERN and GQS scores between the two groups (p<0.05). Based on the video source, the Kruskal–Wallis H test revealed statistically significant differences in scores among groups (p<0.05), with videos uploaded by healthcare professionals and organizations demonstrating higher quality and reliability. Furthermore, linear regression analysis demonstrated no significant associations between video popularity metrics and mDISCERN or GQS scores. Inter-rater reliability, assessed using Cohen’s kappa, indicated substantial agreement for mDISCERN (κ = 0.660) and GQS (κ = 0.610).ConclusionAlthough the majority of YouTube® videos on core stability exercise training were classified as useful, a notable proportion contained misleading information that may negatively influence users. Video popularity metrics do not reliably reflect content quality or reliability; therefore, users should avoid selecting videos based solely on popularity indicators. Greater attention to the professional identity of video uploaders is warranted, as content produced by healthcare professionals and organizations tends to provide more accurate, reliable, and higher-quality information.

Introduction

The term core refers to the lumbopelvic-hip complex, which includes the abdominal wall, paraspinal muscles, diaphragm, pelvic floor, and hip-stabilizing muscles [1]. This region functions as the central kinetic link that supports spinal alignment, controls segmental motion, and enables efficient force transfer between the upper and lower extremities [2]. Core stabilization denotes the neuromuscular capacity to maintain trunk and pelvic control during static and dynamic tasks, thereby preserving postural integrity and optimizing load distribution across the musculoskeletal system [3, 4]. Adequate core stability is essential for athletes, as it contributes to movement efficiency, balance, and power generation in multidirectional sport-specific actions [5]. According to a recent meta-analysis by Rodríguez-Perea et al., core stability training significantly improved balance (Effect Size (ES) = 1.17), vertical jump (ES = 0.69), and horizontal jump performance (ES = 0.84), indicating its positive effect on key components of athletic performance [6]. Core stability is essential not only for athletes but also for the general population, as adequate core stability has been associated with a decreased incidence of low back pain and improved functional capacity, highlighting its importance for overall health and quality of life in non-athletic individuals [7]. Furthermore, insufficient core strength or endurance is associated with compensatory movement patterns, altered lower-limb biomechanics, impaired shock absorption, and increased mechanical stress on the spine and peripheral joints. These deficits may predispose individuals to a range of musculoskeletal issues, including lumbopelvic dysfunction, patellofemoral stress, hip instability, and overuse injuries [8]. According to De Blaiser et al., in a cohort of 139 healthy university students followed over 1.5 years, athletes with reduced core muscle endurance and impaired postural control demonstrated a 24% incidence of lower-extremity overuse injuries, indicating that poor core stability is a significant risk factor for future lower-limb musculoskeletal injuries [9]. Maintaining robust core function represents a critical determinant of physical performance and long-term musculoskeletal health. The growing recognition of the role of core stability in athletic performance has led to an increased emphasis on core training among athletes. Consequently, many athletes are turning to online platforms such as the internet, social media, and YouTube to access information and guidance on various core exercise programs.

YouTube, which ranks as the second most visited and widely used video-sharing platform globally, attracts more than 22.8 billion visitors monthly and offers a wide array of videos containing specialized medical information and tutorials that may have practical applications in exercise training [10]. These resources are frequently accessed by diverse user groups, including athletes, patients seeking rehabilitation guidance, and the general public, who turn to such platforms to obtain information and support related to exercise and health. Although YouTube can serve as a valuable resource, individuals must ensure that they follow safe and effective exercise practices. The increasing interest in online platforms for health-related information, particularly YouTube, highlights the need for critical evaluation of the quality and reliability of the content provided [11, 12]. In this context, Zorlular et al. investigated the reliability and quality of dynamic balance exercise videos on YouTube and reported that approximately 25% of the videos contained inaccurate information, with reliability varying according to the type of content uploader [13]. Similarly, Karapınar et al. found that 22% of sarcopenia exercise videos available on YouTube were of low quality, underscoring the importance of assessing the accuracy and credibility of online exercise resources [14]. It is essential to critically examine the accuracy and trustworthiness of information presented on YouTube. Additionally, the reach and visibility of online exercise videos can be shaped by platform algorithms and user engagement indicators, which may favor popular content rather than scientifically accurate information. Recommendation systems typically rely on signals such as watch time, click-through rate, likes, comments, and users’ prior viewing behavior. As a result, these mechanisms may increase the spread of digital misinformation and amplify exposure to content that is not evidence-based [15]. The purpose of this study is to provide a comprehensive evaluation of YouTube content related to core stability exercise training. Specifically, the study aims to assess the quality, reliability, and accuracy of these videos and to examine how these characteristics differ according to the source of upload.

Method

This study is designed as a cross-sectional analysis. A cross-sectional research design was selected, particularly due to the dynamic nature of YouTube content. YouTube (www.youtube.com) was queried on October 5, 2025, using the search terms “Core exercises,” “Core workout,” and “Core trainings.” All exercise-related videos retrieved through these searches were subsequently reviewed and evaluated over a two-week period spanning October 5–20, 2025. All searches were conducted in anonymous mode, with browsing history and cookies cleared in advance to eliminate the influence of personalized recommendations. Regional filters (e.g., country-specific or global settings) were not applied; videos were identified using YouTube’s default search configuration and ranking order (“most relevant”) to simulate typical user behavior. However, algorithm-driven recommendations (e.g., homepage or suggested videos) were not included in the sampling process. An initial pool of 344 videos was screened for relevance. Videos that did not focus on core exercises, along with any duplicates, were excluded from further analysis. After applying the inclusion criteria—English language, the presence of audio, and the demonstration of exercise techniques—a total of 136 videos met the requirements and were retained for the final analysis (Fig. 1). The content, reliability, and quality of the selected videos were evaluated by independent physiotherapists with expertise in sports rehabilitation and exercise science. Before data extraction, two reviewers underwent a calibration process in which they jointly assessed a random sample of 10 videos to align their interpretations of the evaluation criteria and scoring methods. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were addressed through discussion and consensus to ensure consistency in the evaluation process. This process aimed to align their interpretations of the scoring system, clarify any ambiguities in the evaluation criteria, and minimize potential subjective differences between evaluators. This research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Since the study involved only the analysis of publicly available online content and did not include human participants or identifiable personal data, ethical committee approval was not required.

Assessing the videos

For each video, the following descriptive characteristics were documented: number of views, number of likes, upload date, duration, and number of comments. Video popularity was quantitatively evaluated using two established metrics: the Video Power Index (VPI) and the View Ratio (VR) [16]. The VR was determined by dividing the total number of views by the number of days since the video was uploaded, expressed as views per day (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{VR}=\frac{\mathrm{views}}{\mathrm{days\:since\:upload}}$$\end{document}) [17]. Following the review, the videos were categorized into four groups according to the source of upload: individual users, personal trainers, health professionals, and healthcare organizations. Individual users were defined as non-professional content creators or YouTubers who did not report any professional health or fitness credentials. Personal trainers included individuals who identified themselves as certified fitness trainers or exercise instructors. Health professionals referred to licensed healthcare providers such as physicians, physiotherapists, or other allied health professionals who explicitly stated their professional qualifications. Healthcare organizations included hospitals, clinics, universities, or other institutional healthcare channels that uploaded videos through official organizational accounts.

The assessed videos were categorized as either useful or misleading. Videos deemed useful provided clinically accurate and scientifically validated exercise information pertinent to core stability, offering dependable guidance. In contrast, videos classified as misleading either included clinically incorrect content, lacked adequate scientific backing, or presented information that was not supported by current evidence. Videos containing a mixture of accurate and misleading information were classified as misleading in order to adopt a conservative approach to content quality evaluation. The evaluation criteria were determined according to clinical guidelines and widely accepted professional resources related to core stability exercises. Two independent reviewers applied the established criteria following a calibration session to ensure consistency. Any discrepancies were addressed through discussion, and, in cases where consensus could not be achieved, a third evaluator was consulted. Inter-rater reliability for the subsequent independent assessments was determined using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. The Cohen’s κ values were interpreted as follows: <0.40 was considered to indicate fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 substantial agreement, and > 0.80 almost perfect agreement [18].

The reliability of YouTube videos was evaluated utilizing the modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) tool, as developed by Charnock et al. [19]. This tool consists of five key questions designed to assess the reliability and quality of health-related information. Each question is answered with a simple “yes” or “no,” with each “yes” response earning one point (Table 1). The maximum achievable score is 5, with a score of 1 indicating poor quality and a score of 5 denoting excellent quality.

The Global Quality Scale (GQS) is a widely employed 5-point tool for evaluating the clarity, comprehensiveness, and overall quality of health information. In this scale, videos are classified as low quality (scores 1–2), moderate quality (score 3), and high quality (scores 4–5). Originally developed by Bernard et al. for the assessment of health-related websites, the GQS evaluates factors such as the logical flow of information, ease of comprehension, and practical utility for the target audience (Table 1) [20]. Higher scores reflect greater informational value and reliability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while non-parametric data were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (25th–75th percentile). The Mann–Whitney U test was used to evaluate differences between two independent groups for variables that were not normally distributed. For comparisons involving more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons to pinpoint the specific sources of significant differences. A simple linear regression analysis was performed to assess the impact of the independent variable on the dependent variable. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, with 95% confidence intervals. Effect sizes were interpreted using Cohen’s d, where values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 indicate small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Statistical significance was established at a threshold of p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study examined the characteristics, reliability, and quality of YouTube videos featuring core exercises. Statistically significant differences were observed in mDISCERN and GQS scores according to the identity of the video uploader. Videos categorized as useful or misleading showed significant differences in their educational quality. No relationship was found between video popularity indicators and measures of quality and reliability.

YouTube provides an accessible platform for content creation and dissemination, resulting in increased user engagement, particularly within the health and exercise domains [21]. While many individuals use the platform to enhance visibility or generate income, the lack of regulatory oversight raises concerns regarding the accuracy and educational reliability of the uploaded materials. It is well documented that, especially during and following the COVID-19 pandemic, individuals have become more receptive to video exercise programs and health-related information [22]. Individuals seeking to participate in video exercise programs for recreational or professional purposes face several important concerns, including uncertainty about whether they are enrolling in an appropriate program, whether the exercises presented in the videos are safe, and whether these exercises are suitable for their individual needs and conditions. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons demonstrated that, in the present study, videos uploaded by healthcare professionals and healthcare organizations had significantly higher mDISCERN and GQS scores compared with those uploaded by individual users and personal trainers. These results align with previous studies indicating that content uploaded by healthcare professionals tends to demonstrate greater reliability and educational quality. Arıkan et al. reported that, in YouTube videos presenting exercise content for temporomandibular disorders, those uploaded by healthcare professionals achieved higher GQS and mDISCERN scores than videos uploaded by individual users [11]. Similarly, Zorlular et al., in their study evaluating the quality and reliability of dynamic balance exercise videos on YouTube, demonstrated that videos uploaded by healthcare providers had higher the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) and mDISCERN scores compared with those uploaded by general users [13]. These results highlight that the source of the content is a significant determinant in terms of reliability and educational value. Healthcare professionals’ strong background in exercise and health gives them an advantage over general users and fitness instructors in exercise planning and implementation. Prioritizing content created by healthcare professionals in online exercise training would be more beneficial for users. Clinicians and rehabilitation specialists may help users identify trustworthy and evidence-based video content. In addition, professional associations could contribute by creating structured and standardized educational resources for online dissemination.

Although usefulness, quality, and reliability may appear conceptually related, they represent distinct dimensions of video evaluation. In this study, the useful/misleading categorization reflected the accuracy of the exercise-related information, whereas the reliability and quality scores evaluated other characteristics of the videos, such as credibility, clarity, structure, and overall presentation. Consistent with prior literature, the majority of the analyzed videos (89%) were classified as useful. Comparable results have been reported in previous evaluations of exercise-related online content, with 88% of pelvic floor exercise videos [23] and 94% of rheumatoid arthritis exercise videos [24] being rated as useful. However, in the study by Sözlü et al. evaluating exercise videos for femoroacetabular impingement, useful videos were reported to be fewer in number than misleading ones [25]. Similarly, Arıkan et al. demonstrated that only 39% of videos related to temporomandibular disorder exercises were classified as useful [11]. These findings highlight variability in the quality and reliability of exercise-related video content across different clinical conditions. We consider that the primary reason for this discrepancy may stem from differences in the identities of the video uploaders across studies. Research focusing predominantly on content produced by accredited healthcare institutions and licensed healthcare professionals tends to present more accurate, comprehensive, and balanced information, thereby resulting in a higher proportion of useful videos. In addition, videos classified as useful achieved statistically significantly higher scores on the mDISCERN and GQS assessments. Although the classification of videos (useful/misleading) and the evaluation of reliability and quality were conducted based on predefined, evidence-based criteria rather than subjective judgments, the fact that all assessments were performed by the same group of researchers may still introduce a potential source of bias. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

On social media platforms, VPI and view ratio are frequently employed as measures of video popularity and user engagement [26]. As these metrics reflect the level of audience interaction and engagement achieved by video content creators, they are considered highly valuable from the perspective of content producers. However, it should be noted that the total view counts provided by YouTube do not represent unique viewers, as the same individual may watch a video multiple times. In the present study, no statistically significant associations were identified between video popularity indices and mDISCERN or GQS scores. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported no significant association between video popularity metrics and the reliability or quality of YouTube health-related content. In a cross-sectional study evaluating the reliability and quality of YouTube® videos on hallux valgus, it was demonstrated that popularity indicators such as view counts and engagement measures were not significantly correlated with DISCERN, JAMA, or GQS scores [27]. Similarly, Wang and colleagues, in their analysis of YouTube videos as a patient education resource for cervical spondylosis, found no meaningful relationship between video popularity and measures of informational quality or reliability [10]. Furthermore, in the study conducted by Sözlü et al., which assessed YouTube videos related to Otago exercises, popularity indicators were not associated with video quality or reliability scores [28]. However, in contrast to these findings, Baykara et al. demonstrated a significant correlation between video popularity metrics and content quality in their evaluation of YouTube videos recommending exercises for lower back pain [29]. These findings may be explained by the fact that video popularity metrics predominantly reflect user engagement rather than the scientific accuracy or educational depth of the content. Elevated levels of views or likes are often influenced by visual appeal, simplified narratives, or entertainment-oriented elements rather than evidence-based information. Additionally, videos produced by healthcare professionals may attract lower engagement due to the use of technical language and a more formal instructional style. Moreover, YouTube’s recommendation algorithms may not consistently prioritize content usefulness or reliability in their recommendation systems, as they typically rely on signals such as watch time, click-through rate, likes, comments, and users’ prior viewing behavior.

Several previous studies have analyzed and assessed YouTube videos addressing a wide range of health-related topics, including frozen shoulder exercises [30], Lateral Epicondylitis [31] COVID-19 [32], pelvic floor exercises [23], ankylosing spondylitis [33], rheumatoid arthritis [24], aerobic exercise training [34]. Notably, many of these investigations utilized evaluation scales (mDiscern, GQS) comparable to those applied in the present study. However, it should be noted that these assessments may still involve a certain degree of subjectivity, and therefore ensuring standardization and evaluating inter-rater agreement are of critical importance in such studies. In studies employing these scales, inter-rater reliability among researchers has been reported in the majority of the studies. In the study by Arikan et al., Cohen’s kappa coefficients were reported as 0.709 for mDISCERN and 0.753 for GQS, showing comparable inter-rater agreement to that found in our study (0.660 and 0.610, respectively) [11]. We believe that these minor variations in the findings may be attributed to differences in the evaluators’ professional backgrounds and areas of expertise. Nevertheless, the level of inter-rater agreement remains substantial across studies employing similar video quality assessment tools, supporting the reliability of these instruments for evaluating online health-related content.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive evaluation of the quality and content of YouTube videos related to core exercises. The findings of this study also have implications from a public health perspective. Misinformation in exercise-related videos may negatively influence individuals’ exercise practices, potentially increasing the risk of injury. However, this study has several limitations. Owing to its cross-sectional design, only videos available at the time of analysis were included, and videos uploaded thereafter were not considered. Given the dynamic and continuously evolving nature of YouTube, this may lead to variations in findings across studies conducted at different time points. In addition, the analysis was restricted to English-language videos, which may limit the generalizability of the results to non-English content. Furthermore, the inherently subjective nature of the assessment tools used may have introduced evaluator-related bias, potentially affecting the objectivity of the findings. A further limitation is that the total view counts reported by YouTube do not reflect unique viewers, as repeated views by the same user may occur. Consequently, the calculated view ratio should be interpreted as an approximate indicator of video popularity and engagement rather than an exact measure of unique audience exposure. Future studies would benefit from longitudinal designs with periodic reassessments, the inclusion of newly published and multilingual content, and the examination of temporal trends in online health-related video content.

Conclusion

In recent years, web-based exercise training has gained increasing prominence, driven by the rapid advancement of digital technologies. YouTube, as a widely used platform providing health- and exercise-related content, serves as an important information source for individuals seeking core exercise programs. Although the majority of the videos offer useful content, a subset appears to have the potential to be misleading for users. Critical consideration of the professional identity of video content creators may assist users in identifying more accurate and reliable information. In this context, videos produced by healthcare professionals and healthcare organizations are generally characterized by higher informational quality and credibility. Additionally, it can be stated that popularity metrics such as the VPI and View Ratio do not necessarily reflect the quality or reliability of video content, and users should therefore avoid relying on these algorithm-driven indicators when selecting videos.
