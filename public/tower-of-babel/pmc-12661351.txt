Piper chaba mediated synthesis of silver nanoparticles to control multidrug-resistant diarrheagenic fecal pathogens
Authors: Masuma Anzuman, Md. Abdullah Al Mashud, Rawnak Jahan Rakhy Moni, Shaharin Sultana, Rizone Al Hasib, Ismat Jahan, Muslima Rahman Shrabony, Labanya Aditi, Md. Rezuanul Islam, Jerin Alauddin, Mohammad Abu Hena Mostofa Jamal, Nilufa Akhter Banu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plant-mediated biosynthesis of nanoparticles has gained significant attention in Biomedical fields. This study focused on the isolation of multidrug-resistant diarrheagenic fecal pathogens and their control by green-synthesized silver nanoparticles. Furthermore, we assessed the antioxidant and cytotoxic properties of Piper chaba leaf-synthesized nanoparticles. We isolated and identified multiple antibiotic-resistant fecal pathogens, including Enterococcus gallinarum, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli, through biochemical assays and 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Then the synthesis of silver nanoparticles was visually confirmed by a color change in the sample solutions and further confirmed through UV–Vis spectroscopy, FTIR, TEM, DLS, and XRD analyses. UV–Vis spectroscopy revealed a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak at 440 nm. Meanwhile, FTIR analysis identified functional groups such as haloalkanes, alkenes, and alcohols contributing to the synthesis and stabilization of the nanoparticles. DLS showed the nanoparticles were 56.66 nm in size with monodispersity, and XRD confirmed the presence of face-centered cubic crystals with an average size of 16.06 nm. Furthermore, TEM images showed spherical-shaped nanoparticles at various resolutions. The DPPH (2, 2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay indicated antioxidant activity, with a maximum radical scavenging activity (% RSA) of 69.28 %. In addition, the brine shrimp lethality assay (BSLA) demonstrated low cytotoxicity with an LC50 value of 912.01 μg/mL. The nanoparticles effectively inhibited these multidrug-resistant pathogens at varying doses, with minimum inhibitory concentrations of 4 μg/mL against Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli, and 16 μg/mL against Enterococcus gallinarum. These findings suggest that green-synthesized silver nanoparticles could be a promising therapeutic approach for controlling antibiotic-resistant fecal pathogens.

Introduction

Every year, a large number of infants and children suffer from morbidity due to the outbreak of a common health hazard called diarrhea caused by bacterial pathogens, viruses, fungi, or the administration of certain antibiotics. Moreover, it substantially contributes to the rate of global child mortality [1]. Shigella, Campylobacter, rotavirus, enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC), adenovirus, and Cryptosporidium are the main etiological factors for diarrheal disease [2,3]. At least three or more unusually loose and liquid, or watery stools per day are referred to as diarrhea. Infection via bacteria, viruses, and protozoa causes acute diarrhea, challenges the total healthcare system, and acts as a hindrance to the overall health of a child by retarding child growth, enhancing malnutrition, impairing a child's vision, and so on [4,5]. There are several management approaches to control diarrheal prevalence, including early diagnosis, rehydration, dietary management, application of probiotics, pharmacological treatments (use of antimicrobials), etc [6,7]. The main treatment option for diarrhea is rehydration. Although antimicrobials (antibiotics) are not the first choice for diarrhea, some diarrheal conditions, including acute diarrhea, travelers' diarrhea, and prolonged diarrhea, require antibiotic treatment. A wide range of antibiotic groups, such as penicillin (amoxicillin), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin), macrolides (azithromycin), tetracycline, metronidazole, ceftriaxone, vancomycin, etc., are used to treat diarrhea [8,9]. However, the emergence of antibiotic resistance due to the overuse of antimicrobials has worsened the situation. In the case of antibiotic resistance, bacteria possess resistant genes and manage to conceal or avoid the inhibitory effects of antibiotics [9]. Therefore, an alternative approach is required to tackle this scenario.

The rapidly developing field of green nanotechnology is revolutionizing the sector of science and technology [10]. Particles with a size of 100 nm or less in a minimum one dimension are called nanoparticles (NPs) and have excellent physicochemical and optical features due to their nanosize (10−9 m) and a higher surface-to-volume ratio. Nanoparticles are categorized into three groups based on their chemical composition. These groups include organic, inorganic, and carbon-based nanoparticles [11,12]. Silver possesses antimicrobial activities, and before the discovery of the antibiotic penicillin, ancient people used it for treatment. Several microbial infections were treated with silver and its various salts. However, the development of a wide array of antimicrobials gradually replaced the medicinal use of silver [13,14]. However, the emergence of antimicrobial resistance incidents again gives a spotlight on the synergistic effects of silver and silver-doped drugs. Drugs, when doped with silver nanoparticles, can combat with antibiotic resistance bacteria far more efficiently than the drugs alone [15]. In recent years, the intersection of plant-mediated nanoscience and nanotechnology has led to the development of various nanoparticles, with silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) standing out due to their remarkable properties and wide-ranging applications. AgNPs have garnered significant attention for their potential uses in nanomedicine, ointments, data storage, cell biology, drug delivery, cosmetics, bone healing, cancer therapy, agriculture, textiles, chemical sensing, and as potent antimicrobial and antioxidant agents [16,17].

Physical, chemical, and biological synthesis methods are commonly employed for nanoparticle synthesis. Physical synthesis methods, such as laser ablation, ball milling, evaporation-condensation, etc., result in desired particle size and distribution; however, they require extensive energy, are costly, and have a low rate of synthesis. The chemical synthesis method employs chemical reagents as reducing and capping agents. These reagents are costly and highly toxic, thus posing a serious hazard to the environment and human health. On the other hand, biological synthesis methods employ plant parts, bacteria, fungi, and algae. The biological synthesis method is cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and successfully synthesizes several types of nanoparticles. Additionally, green synthesis of nanoparticles using plants is unique compared to all existing techniques [[18], [19], [20]]. In this approach, metal salts are reduced by the phytochemicals (bioactive compounds) present in plant leaves, stems, roots, fruits, flowers, bark, or latex. Alkaloids, flavonoids, phenols, saponins, and terpenoids are some examples of such phytochemicals that act as reducing agents to reduce Ag+ (silver ions) into Ag0 (silver nanoparticles) and as capping agents that increase the stability of the nanoparticles (Fig. 1). This synthesis technique not only saves money but also protects the environment from the release of toxic chemicals and thereby revolutionizes the field of nanotechnology [[21], [22], [23]]. A wide range of plants has already been employed to successfully synthesize nanoparticles. These plants include Citrus lemon, Piper nigrum, Phyllanthus emblica, Artemisia absinthium, Citrus maxima, Piper cubeba, Nigella sativa, Curcuma longa, Moringa oleifera, Carica papaya, Azadirachta Indica, etc. [18,24].Fig. 1Synthesis of silver nanoparticles mediated by phytochemicals present in plant extract.Fig. 1

Piper chaba, locally known as Choi Jhal or Chui Jhal, is a famous medicinal culinary spice in the Khulna region of Bangladesh, West Bengal, and Tripura [25]. Piper chaba is an edible flowering vine that is widely used in preparing many foods and as a traditional medicine. Since ancient times, it has been used by the local people to treat diarrhea, piles, dyspepsia, rheumatic pains, asthma, and gastralgia [26]. Apart from the Khulna region, farmers from northern parts of Bangladesh, including Nilphamari, Kurigram, and Panchagarh, are growing it as an economically important plant due to its low production cost and high market value [27]. This plant has remarkable biological activities. In recent times, phytochemical profiling of Piper chaba resulted in several phytoconstituents, including dimeric alkaloids that exhibit antimalarial, cytotoxic, and antituberculosis activities. Pungent alkaloids are also present in Piper chaba exhibits immunomodulatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, hepatoprotective, antitumor, and antimetastatic potential. Amide alkaloid, piplartine, has antitumor, antimetastatic, cytotoxic, antinociceptive, antidepressant, antifungal, antibacterial, antidiabetic, antiangiogenic, and genotoxic activities [25,28,29]. Sangjukta Yesmin and colleagues successfully synthesized iron oxide nanoparticles using Piper chaba stem extract [30]. Similarly, Md. Mahiuddin and his team synthesized silver nanoparticles using Piper chaba stem extract [31]. Although nanoparticle synthesis using Piper chaba leaf extract has not yet been reported, these previous studies indicate that the phytochemicals present in the plant serve as effective reducing and capping agents, suggesting their potential for facilitating nanoparticle synthesis using leaf extract.

This study aimed at the biosynthesis of metallic silver nanoparticles using Piper chaba leaf extract for the first time. It also focused on the characterization of the green-synthesized silver nanoparticles and evaluating their bactericidal activity against multiple drug-resistant fecal pathogens isolated from patients with diarrhea. Additionally, the study assessed the antioxidant and cytotoxic activity of the silver nanoparticles.

Materials and methods

Isolation of multiple antibiotic-resistant bacteria from diarrhea patients

Sample collection

Feces samples of ten pediatric diarrhea patients (aged 1–2 years) were collected from Kushtia General Hospital, Bangladesh, following all required ethical procedures and with informed consent from the patients’ parents. Additionally, the first five samples were obtained from girls, and the remaining five samples were collected from boys. All samples were carefully and aseptically collected in tryptic soy broth (TSB) and then incubated at 35oC for 24 h.

Isolation of multiple antibiotic-resistant bacteria

Bacteria were isolated, and their antibiotic susceptibility was assessed simultaneously. The incubated samples were spread onto tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates. Then those plates were impregnated with discs containing three generations of penicillin antibiotics (Phenoxymethylpenicillin, Cloxacillin, and Amoxicillin), incubated at 37 °C for 24 h prior to observation. Based on observations, antibiotic-resistant bacterial colonies were selected and streaked onto TSA to obtain pure cultures. Then, single colonies from each plate were collected and re-evaluated for their susceptibility to penicillin group antibiotics. Furthermore, the susceptibility of these isolates to four popular drugs usually used to treat diarrhea (Ciprofloxacin, Levofloxacin, Tetracycline, and Azithromycin) was examined. Finally, the isolated multiple antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains were cultured on TSA plates and incubated overnight for further investigations [32,33].

Biochemical characterization of isolated bacteria

For identifying and characterizing the isolates, several biochemical assays were performed, including the KOH test, oxidase test, motility test, catalase test, citrate utilization test, indole production test, MR (methyl red) test, and VP (Voges-Proskauer) test, following standard procedures described in Bergey's Manual of Systematic Bacteriology [[33], [34], [35], [36], [37]].

Molecular identification of bacteria

Extraction of bacterial genomic DNA

The genomic DNA of the isolates was extracted using the boiling method. Briefly, bacterial cultures grown in tryptic soy broth (TSB) were transferred to 2 mL Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 4500 rpm for approximately 5 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and 100 μL nuclease-free water was added to the pellet. Afterward, it was shifted to a water bath and kept for 5 min at 100 °C for incubation. Following incubation, the sample was centrifuged again at 3500 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected, mixed with absolute ethanol or Molecular Biology Grade ethanol, and centrifuged at a speed of 10,000 rpm for about 20 min. The resulting supernatant was discarded again, the debris was mixed with 500 μL of 70 % Molecular Biology Grade ethanol, and centrifuged for 15 min at 14,000 rpm. The supernatant was removed, and the remaining pellet was air-dried. 30 μl TE was used to dissolve the bacterial DNA, incubated in a water bath for about 1 h at 56 °C, and vortexed to dissolve the extracted DNA. Finally, these precipitates were re-dissolved in TE buffer and stored at −20 °C. The Thermo Fisher Scientific (NanoDrop 2000) spectrophotometer was used for quantifying DNA concentration [38].

PCR amplification of isolates’ 16S rRNA gene and sequencing of amplicons

Amplification of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene region was done using PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) with two primers-the forward primer: 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and the reverse primer 1492R (5′-CGGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). Furthermore, 2X Dream Taq PCR Master Mix (dNTPs, Taq DNA Polymerase, 4 mM MgCl2, and buffer) was added to the reaction vessel along with the previously mentioned primers and the sample with nuclease-free water. The thermal cycler was programmed for 30 cycles, each consisting of four stages: DNA denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing of strands with specific primers at 57 °C for 1 min, extension of strands at 72 °C for 2 min, and finally the extension of strands at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR amplicons were subsequently stored at −20 °C. Obtained amplicons of DNA were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis based on the size of the DNA fragments. Afterward, to purify the separated DNA fragments, the PureLink® (Thermo Fisher Scientific) PCR Purification Kit was used. Finally, the purified amplicons were partially sequenced using the Sanger sequencing method [39,40].

Sequence assembly and phylogenetic tree construction

The CodonCode Aligner software was used to align, assemble, and edit the previously obtained purified and partially sequenced PCR amplicons of 16S rRNA sequences [41]. Bacterial strains attributing those sequences were identified by nucleotide BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The corresponding accession numbers were retrieved from the NCBI submission portal (https://submit.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) by submitting the FASTA format of the sequences. Finally, the MEGA 11 software was used to construct the neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree.

Plant sample collection and leaf extract preparation

Green, disease-free, and mature leaves of Piper chaba were collected from Islamic University, Kushtia, Bangladesh. As the plants were cultivated by us following known and controlled cultivation practices under the supervision of Botanist Professor Dr. Nilufa Akter Banu, further taxonomical description was not necessary. After collecting, the leaves were washed several times with tap water to dispel all the dirt and then rinsed with double-distilled water. Afterward, the leaves were air-dried at room temperature, weighed about 5 g, and cut into medium-sized pieces using a clean scissor. Then, those leaf samples with 300 mL of double-distilled water were boiled in an electric heater for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature, it was filtered using 125 mm Grade-1 Whatman filter papers, and thus the leaf extract became ready and stored at 4 °C for future experiments (Fig. 2) [39].Fig. 2Preparation of Piper chaba leaf extract.Fig. 2

Green synthesis of silver nanoparticles

Silver nanoparticles’ biogenic synthesis was carried out employing leaf extract and silver nitrate solution. Freshly prepared plant extract was mixed with a 5 mM solution of AgNO3 at ratios of 1:2, 1:4, 1:6, and 1:8 and stirred in a magnetic stirrer at 800 rpm for about 120 min at room temperature (RT) without heating. Nanoparticle synthesis was confirmed by the conversion of a colorless solution (leaf extract and silver nitrate solution at previously mentioned ratios) to a deep brown color, and the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peaks of those stirred solutions were measured in a SHIMADZU-1700 UV–Vis Spectrophotometer at 300–600 nm [42].

In addition, spectroscopic analysis was employed to determine the most favorable ratio of plant extract to silver nitrate solution and the optimal stirring duration. Based on the obtained data, the 1:6 ratio was identified as the most effective among the four tested ratios. This ratio was subsequently prepared in large amounts and stirred separately for 60 min, 120 min, and 180 min at 800 rpm. Afterward, those nanoparticle solutions’ SPR peaks were recorded at 300–600 nm wavelengths [43].

Characterization of silver nanoparticles

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopic analysis

Functional groups corresponding to phytochemicals (present in leaf extract), which participate in the reduction of silver ions to silver nanoparticles, as well as capping and stabilization of nanoparticles, were identified using the FTIR study. Previously optimized 1:6 ratio of plant extract to silver nitrate solution was employed for recording FTIR spectra. The analysis was performed using a Perkin-Elmer-1600, USA (using ATR or attenuated total reflectance mode), and the infrared spectra from 4000 to 400 cm−1 were recorded [44,45].

Study of dynamic light scattering (DLS)

The DLS study was done in this research to find the average particle size (Size of hydrodynamic particles) and polydispersity index (PDI) of aqueous silver nanoparticles (1:6 ratio). A Malvern Zetasizer Nano Zs, ZEN3600 model with serial number MAL-1181676 instrument was used for the study [46,47].

Study of X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The XRD study was used to determine the structural characteristics of silver nanoparticles at the molecular level. For this study, an aqueous silver nanoparticle solution (1:6 ratio) was centrifuged at a rate of 10,000 rpm for 20 min, and the debris was collected in a small glass vial and dried in an oven. Bruker D8 Advanced XRD Spectroscopy was employed to assess the crystal structure and phase angle of biosynthesized nanoparticles at a 2θ range of 0-1000. The XRD pattern was recorded using CuKα radiation at 15 mA of electricity and 40 kV of voltage with a wavelength (λ) of 1.5406 Å. The Debey Scherrer equation was utilized to determine the size of crystalline silver nanoparticles [[48], [49], [50], [51]].

Study of the transmission electron microscope (TEM)

The Talos f200X transmission electron microscope (TEM) was used to examine the surface morphology and particle size of silver nanoparticles in an aqueous solution. An accelerated electric voltage of 120 kV was used, and high-resolution images of nanoparticles were obtained [52,53].

DPPH assay

As previously mentioned, Piper chaba possesses antioxidant properties. Considering this, the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl, 1-picrylhydrazyl) assay was performed following the modified Molyneux method [54].

100 mL DPPH reagent was prepared by dissolving 0.004 gm of DPPH in 100 mL of methanol. For better effectiveness, the DPPH reagent was kept in the dark for about 30 min 15 mL of 95 % methanol was prepared, and five doses of AgNPs (50 ppm, 100 ppm, 200 ppm, 400 ppm, and 500 ppm) were prepared by dissolving AgNPs in 95 % methanol. After dose preparation and completion of 30 min, 15 screw-capped test tubes were taken and 2700 μL of DPPH was transferred into each test tube. Subsequently, 300 μl of each dose was transferred into test tubes, placed those test tubes in a dark cabinet at 37 °C, and recorded their absorbance in a spectrophotometer at 517 nm wavelength after 1 h. The inhibitory concentration (IC50) and the percentage of radical scavenging activity (%RSA) of silver nanoparticles were also calculated [55].

Assessment of cytotoxicity

To assess the cytotoxicity level of green-synthesized silver nanoparticles, the Brine Shrimp Lethality Assay (BSLA) was performed [56]. Artemia salina, a brine shrimp species, was hatched into nauplii for about 24 h in artificial seawater (prepared by dissolving 36g of salt in 1 L of distilled water) in the laboratory setup [57]. Afterward, five different concentrations of silver nanoparticles (100 μg/mL, 500 μg/mL, 1000 μg/mL, 1500 μg/mL, and 2000 μg/mL) were made ready by dissolving oven-dried AgNPs in 1 % dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Dried powders of nanoparticles were utilized cause the exact amount (mg/mL, μg/mL, mg/L, or ppm) of nanoparticles is mandatory to confirm the credibility of the assay. DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide) is an appropriate solvent for silver nanoparticles, proven by recent investigations and thus used in this study [58]. This assay was performed in triplicate; therefore, 15 test tubes were taken, and 10 mL of 2 % saline water was transferred into each test tube. Ten alive nauplii were transferred into each test tube. Subsequently, the previously prepared doses of silver nanoparticles were added to the test tubes (each dose was added in three consecutive test tubes), kept in a place with enough aeration and light, and the number of alive nauplii was counted after 24 h % mortality was calculated by the formula:(i)% mortality = (number of dead nauplii/initial number of alive nauplii) x 100 % …. …. …. …In addition to calculating % mortality, lethal concentration 50 (LC50) was evaluated by probit analysis [59].

Antibacterial activity of silver nanoparticles

Bactericidal activity of silver nanoparticles was evaluated using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method with slight modifications. Five doses (4 μg/mL, 8 μg/mL, 16 μg/mL, 32 μg/mL, and 64 μg/mL) of silver nanoparticles were prepared by dispensing the dried nanoparticles in 1 % DMSO, followed by sonication to enhance solubility. The discs were prepared by impregnating filter paper with different doses of AgNPs. Isolated bacterial cultures were spread evenly onto the fresh tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates. Subsequently, the discs impregnated with AgNPs were placed on the agar surface. A commercial antibiotic disc (Amoxicillin 30 μg) served as a positive control, while a disc containing 1 % DMSO served as a negative control [60].

Analysis of statistical data

Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Office Professional Plus 2013) was used to plot the UV–Vis absorption and FTIR absorption spectra of silver nanoparticles. Malvern Nano Zs determined the hydrodynamic particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of nanoparticles and also plotted the obtained data to present the DLS spectrum. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) views the images and size of nanoparticles. The XRD pattern was recorded using CuKα radiation in Bruker D8 Advanced XRD spectroscopy. Experiments were conducted in triplicate for antibacterial, BSLA, and DPPH assays. Microsoft Excel was used to count the means and standard deviation. Finally, the data from these tests were also presented using Microsoft Excel.

Biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles from Piper chaba leaf extract

Initially, a color change of the sample mixture (AgNO3 and leaf extract solutions) from colorless to light brown and subsequently reddish brown was observed upon continuous stirring over time (Fig. 7A, B, and 7C). Fig. 7D represents the UV–Vis spectra of silver nanoparticles at ratios 1:2, 1:4, 1:6, and 1:8. Analysis of the data obtained from the UV–Vis spectroscopic study of those colloidal brown solutions, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peaks were found at 438 nm with an absorbance of 1.521 for 1:2, 436 nm with the absorbance of 1.426 for 1:4, 440 nm with the absorbance of 0.792 for 1:6, and 434 nm with the absorbance of 1.339 for 1:8. These peaks are characteristic of silver nanoparticles, confirming successful synthesis of AgNPs. Among the four ratios, the 1:6 synthesized nanoparticles with a more favorable rate and size. The 1:6 solution was subsequently prepared and stirred for different time durations as mentioned above (in the methodology section). Discussion

The prevalence of multidrug-resistant pathogens poses a significant challenge to the human race and the total healthcare facilities. In this medical crisis, nanoparticles have emerged as a good fit as an alternative medicine. Extracts of various plants have been shown to be promising alternatives for synthesizing silver nanoparticles in an environmentally friendly manner [64,65].

The feces of diarrhea patients are the reservoir of many multidrug-resistant bacterial pathogens. Three bacterial pathogens were isolated from the feces of ten diarrhea patients (children) that are resistant to seven antibiotics (Phenoxymethylpenicillin, cloxacillin, amoxicillin, tetracycline, azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin). Both the biochemical assays and 16S rRNA sequencing of amplified and purified bacterial genes identified the isolated bacterial strains as Enterococcus gallinarum (MEBTN4), Klebsiella pneumoniae (MEBTN5), and E. coli (MEBTN6). Previous research works suggest that Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli are the infamous multidrug-resistant pathogens causing diarrhea. However, diarrhea caused by Enterococcus gallinarum is quite rare, but it happens [66]. The phylogenetic tree revealed that Escherichia coli MEBTN6 clustered closely with Shigella dysenteriae (Fig. 6). It was supported by a high bootstrap value of 90, indicating a strong evolutionary relationship between the two bacterial strains. Our findings align with the previous studies demonstrating that Shigella species share extensive phylogenetic and genomic history with Escherichia coli [67,68].

This study facilitated the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles using Piper chaba leaf extract and determined their efficacy in controlling MDR fecal pathogens, which had not been investigated previously. Due to the in-situ reduction of silver ions (Ag+) to silver nanoparticles (Ag0), the sample solution became deep or radish brown and visually indicated silver nanoparticle synthesis. Subsequently, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peaks were recorded at 438 nm, 436 nm, 440 nm, and 434 nm for the 1:2, 1:4, 1:6, and 1:8 sample solutions, respectively. These peaks were observed due to the presence of SPR electrons on the nanoparticle surface, thereby confirming the nanoparticle synthesis. Based on the intensity and wavelength shift of the SPR peaks for the 1:6 solution, stirring for 180 min resulted in the most effective synthesis of silver nanoparticles. Previously Md. Mahiuddin, with his team, synthesized silver nanoparticles using the stem extracts of Piper chaba with an approximate SPR peak of 445 nm, which is consistent with our SPR values [31]. Another study regarding silver nanoparticles' green synthesis using Cinnamomum tamala leaf extract, following a similar synthesis process, resulted in an SPR peak at 425 nm [39].

FTIR spectrum of leaf extract confirmed the presence of the functional groups corresponding to several phytochemicals, including alcohols, phenols, alkanes, amines, and amides. These phytochemicals act as reducing agents and synthesize silver nanoparticles, and then, as a capping agent, stabilize the nanoparticles. Additionally, the FTIR spectrum of aqueous silver nanoparticles confirmed the presence of functional groups, including alkyl halide (C–Br and C–Cl bonds) and alcohol (O–H bond) that could play roles as reducing agents in the synthesis process (Ag + to metallic Ag0), and alkene or ethylene (CC bond) group that could act as capping and stabilizing agent [69,70]. From the results and data obtained from the DLS study, it is clear that the hydrodynamic particle size (average size of silver nanoparticles with a hydration layer on their surface) of nanoparticles is small (56.66 nm), and these particles were monodispersed (PDI is lower than 0.7). This small size and monodisperse nature led to its unique properties and vast applications [10,71]. Hemlata and team synthesized AgNPs using leaf extract of Cucumis prophetarum hydrodynamic particle size of 90 nm and a PDI of 0.3 [72]. An average crystal size of 16.06 nm was calculated from the XRD pattern for AgNPs. Furthermore, biosynthesized nanoparticles were face-centered cubic (FCC) crystals with peaks at 38.12 (111), 44.31 (200), 64.46 (220), 77.42 (311), and 81.56 (222) of 2θ° values [71,73]. XRD studies of Cucumis prophetarum leaf synthesized nanoparticles showed a face-centered cubic crystal structure of nanoparticles with peaks of 2θ° values of 32.18 (111), 38.04 (200), 46.13 (120), 54.63 (202), and 78.08 (311) [72]. TEM studies provided images of silver nanoparticles with their morphology, shapes, and sizes at the nanoscale in various resolutions. Spherical-shaped or mostly circular nanoparticles with soft edges were synthesized. These particles had close physical contact among themselves, and most of the particles were scattered uniformly [52].

This investigation optimizes the parameters for the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles, including stirring time, temperature, concentration of silver nitrate solution for a better bio-reduction process, and the most favorable ratio of silver nitrate and plant extract for better synthesis. These optimized parameters and successful synthesis of biologically active, green-synthesized silver nanoparticles set the seal on large-scale synthesis of silver nanoparticles. In addition, the synthesis step of nanoparticles was repeated several times, confirming its reproducibility on a large scale.

One of the aims of this study was to investigate whether green-synthesized silver nanoparticles possess antioxidant activity, and this was done via the DPPH assay. In the DPPH assay, the formation of yellow color from the violet color of DPPH indicates diphenyl picryl hydrazine formation and thus the scavenging of DPPH by silver nanoparticles. The results of this assay prove the dependency of antioxidant activity, implying that higher doses of silver nanoparticles have a higher %RSA value, thus higher antioxidant activity. IC50 is another important parameter to evaluate a compound's antioxidant activity, and it increases with decreasing IC50 value. At the highest dose (800 rpm) of silver nanoparticles, the %RSA is the highest (69.28 %) with an IC50 value of 67.45 ppm, indicating moderate antioxidant activity. Perilla frutescens leaf-synthesized silver nanoparticles showed the highest % RSA of 68.02 % with an IC50 value of 54.52 μg/mL. DPPH is advantageous over other assays due to its easier method, cheapness, speed, and accuracy. Furthermore, there is no need to generate radical compounds, and the stability of radical compounds made this assay far more trustworthy when compared to other assays [[74], [75], [76]].

The Brine Shrimp Lethality Assay is a well-established and reliable method for evaluating the cytotoxic potential of various products. From the results of BSLA, green-synthesized silver nanoparticles showed a very low level of cytotoxicity cause their value is near 1000 μg/mL (912.01 μg/mL) and were dose-dependent. The mortality rate of nauplii increased, and the survival rate decreased with increased concentration of nanoparticles. Our findings are in agreement with the work of Suree Tongwanichniyom et al. [77]. However, further toxicity studies on animal models and human cell lines are essential to ensure the safe use of nanoparticles as medicine or therapeutic drugs for humans.

The biosynthesized silver nanoparticles exhibited notable antibacterial activity against gram-positive (Enterococcus gallinarum) and gram-negative (Klebsiella pneumoniae and E. coli) multidrug-resistant fecal pathogens, with a stronger effect observed against gram-negative bacteria. Disk diffusion assay confirmed that E. coli was the most sensitive strain, and the antibacterial effect was dose-dependent. Similarly, Vajravathi Lakkim and colleagues in their recent study observed that silver nanoparticles synthesized using C. roseus and A. indica leaf extract showed potential bactericidal activities against both gram-positive (S. aureus) and gram-negative (K. pneumonia, E.coli, and P. aeruginosa) bacteria in a dose-dependent manner [78].

The antimicrobial activity of AgNPs depends on their morphology, dimensions or area, concentration, ionic strength, and pH of the medium. From previous investigations, a deep insight into the mechanisms by which silver nanoparticles inhibit bacterial populations is pointed out, although these are uncertain at some levels (Fig. 17). Silver ions (Ag+) are gradually released from the nanoparticles, and due to their toxic nature and ability to interfere with DNA replication, bacterial destruction happens. In addition to toxicity, Ag + ions interact with bacterial cell walls through electrostatic attraction, and their strong affinity for sulfur-containing proteins helps them establish bonds with cytoplasm and gradually causes the destruction of bacterial cell walls and cytoplasm [79]. Ag + ions disrupt the bacterial respiratory system immediately after entering the cell, leading to the generation of a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS, in turn, results in increased oxidative stress, interrupts the cell's ATP production, and alters DNA. Another mechanism regarding silver nanoparticles' bactericidal activity includes the denaturation of ribosomes via silver ions and thus interference in protein production. Nanoparticles with a size of 10 nm directly enter bacterial cells without releasing ions by altering the cell's permeability and killing bacteria directly. Gram-positive (G + ve) bacteria show comparatively more resistance to silver nanoparticles than gram-negative (G-ve) bacteria due to their cell wall's thick layer of peptidoglycan, which tries to restrict the entry of silver ions and silver nanoparticles. Owing to this circumstance, silver nanoparticles show higher bactericidal activities against gram-negative bacteria rather than gram-positive bacteria, and it perfectly complies with our finding of antibacterial assay [80,81].Fig. 17Schematic representation of the mechanistic overview of bactericidal activities of silver nanoparticles.Fig. 17

Schematic representation of the mechanistic overview of bactericidal activities of silver nanoparticles.

Several recent studies have demonstrated the synergistic effects of silver nanoparticles and nanoparticle-doped therapeutic agents as effective antimicrobials against multidrug-resistant pathogens. The control of multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii using silver-doped hydroxyapatite is an example of this synergistic approach [15].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study reporting the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles using Piper chaba leaf extract. A wide range of drugs that are generally used to treat diarrhea were used to observe the antibiotic-resistance patterns of the isolated bacterial strains. Furthermore, BSLA provided a preliminary evaluation of the cytotoxicity level of nanoparticles.

However, there are some limitations of the current study. It included only ten patients, and a sample size calculation was not performed before recruitment because this is a preliminary or pilot study. We aimed to check the feasibility of green-synthesized silver nanoparticles to control multiple drug-resistant diarrheagenic fecal pathogens. Furthermore, resource availability, ethical considerations, and limited recruitment time also resulted in lower patient numbers. However, we strictly maintained inclusion and exclusion criteria, standard sample collection and analysis procedures to minimize bias and variability. The study findings are just preliminary insights, and further study with a large sample size is a must to validate these findings. We just performed the FTIR analysis of the plant extract to find the presence of phytochemicals, as it is a preliminary study. However, phytochemical profiling using in-vitro assays and mass spectrometry techniques (e.g., GC-MS or LC-MS) would be more informative. Our study is the preliminary investigation of the bactericidal activity of green-synthesized silver nanoparticles; thus, we just measured the zone of inhibition. Evaluation of minimum lethal and inhibitory concentrations is beyond our scope for this study. However, we plan to calculate these in the future. Additionally, we did not determine the shelf life of biosynthesized silver nanoparticles.

Although variations in previous studies complicate direct comparisons of antibacterial efficacy, the findings of this study highlighted the potential bactericidal properties of silver nanoparticles synthesized from Piper chaba leaf extract. These results suggest that green-synthesized silver nanoparticles could emerge as a promising alternative to conventional drugs for managing multidrug-resistant pathogens shortly.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated the isolation and identification of multiple drug-resistant diarrheagenic fecal pathogens, Enterococcus gallinarum, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli. Furthermore, successful synthesis of silver nanoparticles was attained for the first time using Piper chaba leaf extract. Comprehensive characterization confirmed that the synthesized AgNPs were monodisperse, face-centered cubic crystals and exhibited high purity. The bactericidal activity of the synthesized silver nanoparticles was rigorously tested against the three drug-resistant isolates, and a dose-dependent antibacterial activity was observed, with a particularly pronounced effect against E. coli. Additionally, the cytotoxicity test indicated that these AgNPs are safe for human use, while their antioxidant properties further enhanced their therapeutic potential. Our study aligns with several United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). It contributed to the development of alternative antimicrobial agents, supporting SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being); demonstrated successful synthesis of silver nanoparticles, aligning with SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure); adopts an environmentally friendly synthesis approach thus aligning with SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), and avoids the use of environmentally hazardous chemicals thus aligning with SDG 13 (Climate Action). Based on these findings, it can be concluded that AgNPs synthesized using Piper chaba leaf extract hold promise as future antibiotics, offering a cost-effective, low-toxicity alternative for combating multidrug-resistant bacterial infections. Optimizing parameters for large-scale synthesis of silver nanoparticles, and exploring green-synthesized nanoparticles’ potential roles in nanotechnology, environmental remediation, and medicine should be the future focus of this study.
