A learning health systems approach to scaling up an evidence-based intervention for integrated primary mental healthcare case finding and referral in South Africa
Authors: André Janse van Rensburg, Nikiwe Hongo, Londiwe Mthethwa, Merridy Grant, Tasneem Kathree, Zamasomi Luvuno, Alim Leung, Arvin Bhana, Deepa Rao, Inge Petersen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite progress in the development and evaluation of evidence-based primary mental health interventions in low-income and middle-income countries, implementation and scale-up efforts have had mixed results. Considerable gaps remain in the effective translation of research knowledge into routine health system practices, largely due to real-world contextual constraints on implementation and scale-up efforts. The Southern African Research Consortium for Mental Health Integration (S-MhINT) programme used implementation research to strengthen the implementation of an evidence-based integrated collaborative depression care model for primary healthcare (PHC) services in South Africa. To facilitate the scale-up of this model from a testing site to the whole province of KwaZulu-Natal, a capacity building programme was embedded within the Alliance for Health Policy and Systems Research (AHPSR) learning health systems (LHS) approach. The paper discusses efforts to scale up and embed case finding and referral elements of the S-MhINT package within routine PHC. Data from semistructured interviews, a focus group discussion, proceedings from participatory workshops and outputs from the application of continuous quality improvement (CQI) cycles were thematically analysed using the AHPSR LHS framework. Learning particularly occurred through information sharing at routine participatory workshops, which also offered mutual deliberation following periods of applying CQI tools to emergent problems. Individual-level, single-loop learning seemed to be particularly observable elements of the AHPSR LHS framework. Ultimately, our experience suggests that successful scale-up requires strong and sustained relationships between researchers, policy-makers and implementers, investments into learning platforms and organisational participation across all levels to ensure ownership and acceptance of learning processes.Keywords: Global Health, Other study design, Health systems, Mental Health & Psychiatry

Background

Globally, there has been progress in the development and evaluation of evidence-based primary mental health interventions using task-sharing approaches in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in the last decade.1 However, integrating and scaling up of mental health service interventions within routine primary healthcare services (PHC) have had mixed results, and implementation efforts are often met with a range of complex challenges.2,5 Among a range of factors, limited programme acceptability, appropriateness and credibility; fragmented delivery care systems; issues in programme planning, coordination and management and lack of coordination and collaboration among stakeholders, pose significant barriers to effective and sustainable implementation of integrated primary mental health services.4 6 7 The high proportion of evidence-based interventions not effectively being put to scale is a critical, enduring gap in knowledge to action, research to practice and quality—gaps that are in large part manifestations of failures in implementation.8

While implementation research has been widely accepted as a foundational part of global health planning and practice,9,12 its potential is often inhibited because research questions that are not contextual or of practical relevance for stakeholders or produced through meaningful engagement rooted in local geographies and guided and informed by theory and led by community preference is lacking.13,15

Given the importance of contextual relevance, this paper reports on how iterative learning was used as an implementation strategy to help scale up an evidence-based mental health service intervention in South Africa. The Southern African Research Consortium for Mental Health Integration (S-MhINT) programme used implementation research to strengthen the implementation strategies of an evidence-based integrated collaborative depression care model for PHC services in South Africa.16 The evidence-based package (the Mental health INTegration Programme—MhINT) includes PHC psyoeducation talks to increase mental health literacy; improvements on PHC clinical guidelines to promote nurse-led mental health assessment; adherence support and lay-counselling provided by lay health workers under supervision; improved referral pathways through educational meetings and healthcare management orientation. PHC nurses independently assessed, diagnosed and referred patients. Referral for treatment was independently associated with substantial improvements in depression symptoms 3 months later. Crucially, the package was shown to increase nurse-led assessment, diagnosis and referral for depression in PHC clinics, while participants who were referred showed improvements in depression symptoms after 3 months. The implementation and trail outcomes of MhINT are described more comprehensively elsewhere.16,18

To facilitate the scale up of the resulting strengthened implementation package from the testing district site17 to all the health districts in the province of KwaZulu-Natal in South Africa, a participatory capacity building programme was embedded within a learning health systems (LHS) approach. The programme was designed to empower mid-level district health managers, responsible for developing mental health services in each of their districts (a defined area-based management system), to implement the implementation package incrementally in each of their respective districts. The programme was guided by an LHS approach, an emerging conceptualisation through which the effectiveness of implementation, adoption and scale-up can be increased. LHSs are thought to improve healthcare over time, by embedding evidence-based knowledge in care platforms through continuous quality improvement (CQI) cycles.19 Recently, a multidimensional LHS framework was developed by the Alliance for Health Policy and Systems Research (AHPSR).20 Here, learning is conceptualised to occur at multiple interconnected levels, characterised by distinct learning loops, via interconnected means (figure 1).

AHPSR LHS framework.20 AHPSR, Alliance for Health Policy and Systems Research; LHS, learning health system.

Focusing on how learning occurs within an LHS, the framework posits that learning occurs interactively at an individual level (gaining knowledge through experience, gathering information from different sources and making sense of these data), group level (collective interpretation of knowledge through dialogue and exchange) and organisational level (routine integration of both knowledge and understanding towards driving coordinated action on a wider scale). Further, learning unfolds in loops—single-loop learning influences routine actions through an adaptation of novel ways of working; double-loop learning extends these behavioural adaptations to also include underlying assumptions, models and frameworks through which problems and solutions are approached; while triple-loop learning can be understood as ‘learning how to learn’, the altering of fundamental assumptions about change in order to improve how learning occurs in a system. Finally, learning is understood to occur cyclically through the collection of information, collective deliberation and action/praxis.20 The application of LHS in real-life contexts remains limited, particularly in LMICs. In a recent example, the AHPSR LHS framework was applied to better understand how learning occurred in Guinea in response to infectious disease outbreaks, suggesting that successive learning processes led to improved responses to COVID-19, Ebola and Marburg virus outbreaks.21 Further, LHS approaches are increasingly being used to inform and guide policy development and implementation.22 23

In this paper, we discuss our efforts to scale up and embed the S-MhINT integrated depression service package within routine PHC in the province of KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, by applying the principles of LHS in an ongoing learning collective.

Methods

Study area

The study was undertaken in the province of KwaZulu-Natal, on the east coast of South Africa. The province has an estimated population of 11 million people, spread over an area of 94 361 km². Health services are provided through 11 districts of varying sizes, with public mental health services being delivered in PHC facilities, secondary and tertiary hospitals. A learning collective was set up between a research team at the University of KwaZulu-Natal, the provincial KwaZulu-Natal Department of Health (KZN DoH) Directorate for Mental Health and Substance Abuse and district programme coordinators. The primary role of the directorate and the coordinators is to provide technical support and training in the delivery of district mental health services. The 11 coordinators all had a background in nursing, and, while the portfolio of most were mental health and substance use, some were also tasked with coordinating non-communicable disease management. Variation exists between districts, with a wide range of geographies and population densities, urban, periurban and rural areas, as well as slight variations in demographic and socioeconomic features as well as disease burden. It should be highlighted that very little of the overall health budget is reserved for district mental health services, with no ringfencing of mental health funding for PHC and community care.24

Description of the capacity building programme

This paper focuses on the South African scale-up dimension of the S-MhINT programme,25 a multicountry initiative funded by the National Institute of Mental Health (U19MH113191-01), which included the development and implementation of a capacity-building programme aimed at key stakeholders, towards creating more enabling environments for the uptake of mental health interventions in Mozambique, South Africa and Tanzania. The South African approach entailed the codevelopment of a programme curriculum, to be implemented across 3 years (2019–2022). Three broad phases were followed, namely set up and initiation (2019), tool development and refinement (2020–2021), and application, analysis and translation (2021–2022).

As depicted in figure 2, during the first phase, a number of meetings were held between the research team and the KZN DoH, towards the formalisation of a learning collective. Participants were provided with laptop computers and data, and, following a series of workshops, a codeveloped set of goals and associated work plans were accepted. During the second phase, a set of tools was developed in a further series of participatory workshops, which included the development of a situational analysis toolkit and CQI tools. COVID-19 caused disruption during this phase, and much of the learning collaborative process and associated tools had to be adapted to public health service reprioritisation and infection prevention and control measures. Finally, an application phase followed during which a range of CQI techniques were introduced to participants, after which they were requested to apply these techniques in their districts, focusing on mental health identification in PHC clinics. Three learning sessions were held following the application of these techniques, in order to improve mutual understanding of the operationalisation of the techniques, as well as to illustrate the utility of the iterative approach to system improvement. It should be noted that, due to time constraints within the boundaries of a project funding cycle, the focus of the programme was limited. The codeveloped curriculum included a cascade of mental health services, starting at PHC-level case finding and referral for common mental disorders, followed by treatment at PHC and specialist levels. The same programmatic themes were included for severe mental disorders, as well as for child and adolescent mental health. However, in this paper, we report on learning and improvements in PHC-level case finding and referral for common mental disorders, using a programmatic intervention that included a validated screening instrument (Brief Mental Health Screening Tool).26 Ultimately, several tools were codeveloped during the duration of the programme, namely a situational analysis tool and accompanying data elements table; as well as a CQI package consisting of templates for process maps, fishbone diagrams, root-cause analysis and a Plan-Do-Study-Act analysis. By following the principles of an LHS approach—data-driven, collaborative working with continuous learning—the codeveloped capacity building programme fell naturally within the parameters of the AHPSR LHS framework.20 The learning-through-action approach, as well as cointerpretation of findings, ensured individual-level learning; the routine communal workshops ensured group-level learning; while organisational-level learning would be ensured through the continued inclusion of provincial policy-makers throughout the duration of the programme. Due to the bottom-up nature of the programme, planning for learning loops was limited, though it was assumed that single-loop learning would be central given its prominence in previous research. That being said, the CQI approach was designed to alter the ways in which participants approach problem-solving, by highlighting collective working and learning. Finally, the routine workshops across the 3-year period, as well as allowing for time to try out CQI tools by participants, set up a structure for iterative learning through information collection, deliberation and action.

Design

A qualitative observational study was undertaken to describe learning in the S-MhINT capacity building programme, drawing from primary and secondary data sources. In terms of primary data, semistructured interviews (between 30 and 40 min) were conducted with each of the district participants (n=11), via phone and Zoom calls (depending on internet connectivity and preference), digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim. A focus group discussion was undertaken with eight participants who were able to attend, digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim. One-on-one interviews were used to capture individual reflections on the capacity building process, while the focus group discussion was meant to capture the collective sense-making of the programme in the spirit of the programme design. Both data collection processes were semistructured in nature, and a basic topic guide was developed that explored the experiences of and assessment by participants of the capacity building programme. This included exploring recollections of the programme, assessments of the utility of the codevelopment approach, the necessity and worth of the situational analysis and CQI aspects of the programme, and reflections on learning during the duration of the programme. The transcribed data were transferred into NVivo V.19 for management, and two researchers undertook a thematic analysis, using the different sections of the AHPSR LHS framework as themes. The analyses conducted by the two researchers were compared during analysis meetings, where differences were addressed. The final combined analysis was written up in narrative form, drawing from direct quotations from the two analytical sets. In addition to primary data, the outputs of the CQI process from participants, as well as from the CQI cointerpretation workshops, were analysed according to LHS themes and combined in order to summarise key findings from which to draw conclusions on learning.

Findings

Learning across levels

Learning across levels includes learning at the individual, team/group, organisational and cross-organisational levels. Learning at an individual level was relatively straightforward to determine, as the capacity building process involved individual district mental health coordinators attending a participatory programme. In this way, the CQI elements were particularly useful:

It (the Capacity Building Programme) assisted me a lot ‘cause… those processes and stages that (I) have mastered like the situational analysis, …I'm now able to do my own living situational analysis and try and have activities. (IP1)

However, some elements were harder to comprehend during the CQI process. While the situational analysis and fishbone processes were relatively easy to apply, some expressed difficulty comprehending the later stages of the CQI cycle. This period coincided with a shift to online learning workshops due to COVID-19 restrictions. The logic on continuous improvement also affected a change in approaching challenges emerging in PHC facilities. For instance, in the focus group discussion, some participants noted that issues emerging in clinics such as infrastructure challenges were not definitive, and by workshopping the problem with clinic stakeholders, innovative (and, often simple) solutions could be found, using minimal additional resources. Similarly, a change in perception of working in the mental health programme was reported, in that some realised that they were not necessarily expected to solve problems individually, but that stakeholder involvement and team-driven working are key. For others though, there was still an insistence that many of the problems outlined could only be solved by employing more psychologists in districts, despite this option not being viable due to mental health specialist resource constraints. Learning by doing was instrumental, as the utility of the CQI skills and tools only became a reality once coordinators used CQI to analyse problems in screening for mental health in PHC clinics.

Then it also assisted us too, we learned how to do a process mapping where you go into the clinic, and you understand clinics are different. You understand how this one works. You go with a client in each station or in each step and identify mental health issues or gaps within the whole flow of the client. Then this also assisted …in identifying these gaps and coming up with root cause analysis where then we had to categorise the gaps that we have identified into whether they're client, whether they're policy, staff related, environmental and all. And then we analyse them. (IP4)

Further, the systematic and inclusive approach brought about a stronger sense of person-centredness, a realisation that the surrounding contexts of clients are crucial—specifically, family dynamics, socioeconomic challenges and cultural beliefs and practices.

At the team or group level, the importance of group-based learning was highlighted through the disruption of COVID-19, which necessitated much more isolated working. The process of developing the CQI toolkit together, piloting the different tools within different parts of the CQI cycle, and then collectively making sense of the process in a participatory workshop platform was perceived to be especially helpful:

…we go and practise, try and collect the data layer, come back and give some feedback. And then COVID came and we were no longer meeting regularly any focus shifted from CQI to COVID. (IP1)

We went through a very good program, capacity building. And we… we gained some knowledge, although it was not enough, time was not on our side. And we were able to work together and we developed the brief mental health screening tool. We rolled out trainings. We orientated…. operational lower-level managers (FGDP3).

As mentioned, participants were requested to assemble teams in their districts to assist them during the CQI process. While this was recognised as challenging in some respects, it also brought awareness of the importance of team work when applying CQI. In this vein, it was suggested that there is a need to capacitate the team being assembled in the districts, in order to ensure smooth application. This, however, requires more investment into the understanding of CQI processes on the part of the mental health coordinators:

…when we go back (workplace), we have to have this group we have to work with. So, if you are not clear yourself, having undergone this (Capacity Building programme) alone, so that means you are going to feed…them with something that is not clear. (FGDP4).

A degree of comradery and mutual collaboration was both an asset and a platform for group learning, and the preference for meeting face to face and use of the communal WhatsApp group was an important additional support. Coordinators expressed how they were able to depend on other coordinators for assistance:

I think with other coordinators we have good relations if there’s something that we're not getting or want to find out, how are they doing it? It’s not a challenge to maintain such a relation so that you can assist each and learn from them. If there’s something that is good (that’s) working for other districts. (IP6).

Group discussions further lessened feelings of isolation and put problems into perspective:

…by having presentations from other districts, you also find out you are not in a problematic district alone. Even other districts maybe have the same challenges. Then you also learn how are they trying and solve those challenges that they have. (IP4).

An important learning from the capacity building programme was enhanced understanding of the bureaucratic structure of the DoH. It became clear during the group format of the learning process that the Directorate for Mental Health and Substance Abuse is mandated to provide technical support to district health systems, without the required authority to enforce any policy guidelines. In this way, it became clear that district service management actors from the respective districts should have been included in the capacity building process, in order to facilitate uptake of emerging innovations. This could further have added to broader organisational learning.

While cross-organisational learning was outside the scope of this project, limited organisational learning could be inferred. Learnings of the CQI process were workshopped and led to changes in the indicators as well as in the SOP of the BMH. Importantly, group meetings led to learnings being translated into a key change in the provincial district health information system (DHIS), by adding the identification and adoption of presumptive cases as an additional PHC routine mental health indicator.

Learning loops

Single-loop learning—adapting routines and practices within the system without checking assumptions or underlying root causes—occurred in terms of participants understanding and applying the content of the workshops in their respective districts. In this way, participants established teams and used the different tools in the CQI toolkit. However, despite learning how to use the different tools, the undertaking was approached by some as a task that they had to perform—in a similar way to homework for school learners—without necessarily interrogating the underlying logic.

Participants understood the necessity for each part of the CQI process, even when not completely grasping the operationalisation for some of the tools later in the cycle. The initial problem identification section of the CQI process was found to be particularly helpful:

Yea, it assisted us to identify a lot of problems that we were not aware of. Like when we say, take it that screening will be done at the observation room. But each clinic, each facility has its things or dynamics. So, you will go to this one, it’s easy, she’s got a room. You’ll go to the next one. They’ll just have a screen in the corner and there are clients here. So, it’s not easy to converse and discuss with the client. You go to the other one who’s doing everything and end up not doing mental health. So, it taught us where we will be able to add, okay, this clinic can do this, this cannot because of infrastructure problems. Or this one cannot ‘cause of HR issues. (FGDP4).

In addition, the process helped participants identify potential resources to draw on, as well as helping to identify a range of stakeholders in the improvement of PHC mental health screening. Further, the stakeholder identification process helped participants identify a need for facility-level mental health champions (dedicated mental health nurses in PHC clinics were absorbed into chronic diseases management as part of reform towards integrated care).

In terms of double-loop learning—the questioning of fundamental frameworks and assumptions around problems and their solutions—was ultimately a central goal of the capacity-building programme. In place of traditional training involving a transfer of knowledge, a key goal was to influence how mental health coordinators approach problems and to provide them with a systematic quality improvement package to help address these problems in an iterative way:

It is useful. It has broadened my thinking. It has broadened my knowledge. Yeah. I just think that I would always refer to it not only in mental health, it’s broad. It can assist me in most things. (IP3).

This requires ‘looking deeper’ for underlying root causes and to work with a range of stakeholders to find practical and durable solutions. The following quotations illustrate the realisation of a new approach to problem-solving:

Sometimes you oversee the problem, you know that there’s a problem but you don't zoom in to check how deep is the problem. Yeah, you'll try and solve the problem that you see in front of you, but you don't go down and say but what is the real (cause) of this problem? So, I have learned to say yes if you see there is a problem, go even more deeper to see what is the root cause of the problem. So that when you treat the problem, you treat the root cause not the problem that is in front of you only. (IP5).

Importantly, going through the process, practically, helped participants to illuminate the gaps in the implementation of PHC mental health screening. It highlighted the necessity of having strong implementation strategies in place while scaling up a screening tool and new indicators in a system:

…our structures are not in place. It gave us our situational analysis. Gave a clear picture… now we thought, oh yes, we are supposed to be having screening tools, we're supposed to be having SOPs. It just gave them directions as well to know what is needed for the lower (level) carry on (implementation) (FGDP4).

Another form of double learning was the realisation that training in the traditional sense was ineffective for a scale-up of an intervention to a province. Participants described frustrations with training facility staff, only for staff to not employ the tool to screen, even after multiple training sessions. A different model of data-driven quality improvement alongside sustained mentorship was suggested:

when we are doing the PDSA in planning, we did the objective and predictions and what change we can do. We plan all those. Then we do, in my case then my doing was to do trainings (and) to orientate OMs (Operational Managers). Then we have to study, see if whatever you have planned (to) put in place—is it happening? You manage to train the target group and yeah, manage to do all your trainings and what was the result? Yes. Then at the end you asked yourself (if) the change that we have implemented—is it (good) enough…? Like the trainings, do I … continue with the trainings or … wasn't (it) good enough? So, we need to adapt it (referring to adopting a trainer as a facility mental health champion to do mentoring and support) (IP4).

An additional insight was that the capacity building approach to scale up would be a suitable approach for other programmes and provinces, given the dynamism within and across PHC clinics:

…like I've said, clinics are dynamic. They're not the same. Even the provinces won't be the same. I think if you go with what you have (CQI skills), then you'll be able to identify, they are going to tell you what works in the area, what does. (IP3).

Triple-loop learning was more difficult to pin down. However, as mentioned in some interviews, there was a willingness to adapt to a participatory, learning-driven way of working and consensus was reached that the KZN DoH and its partners should pursue a culture of improvement through learning, especially in an area as dynamic and challenging as integrated mental healthcare.

Means of learning

Learning through information occurs in two related ways. First, the situational analysis provided routine quantitative data that helped identify challenges. Starting with mental health screening at PHC clinics, identification of key bottlenecks was workshopped in a group setting, ultimately leading to changes in the SOP for PHC mental health screening. Further interrogation of routine data resulted in changes to data elements in the DHIS. Both of these amendments were ultimately ratified by the provincial government. Using routine data to monitor progress also provided feedback on the learnings that were implemented:

I think so far what I've realised is that victory wise, those clinics that have enough capacity (because), since I started the (mental health) screening is growing…the referral and the diagnosis is improving. Looking at the substance abuse disorders, …more …clients are coming up. And then I see quite a number of people that are coming for detox services. (IP2).

Implementation of screening in the vital signs room was less than functional as the level of training of enrolled nurses precluded management of follow-up questions from clients about their mental health, resulting in the screening being moved to the consultation room. While the use of routine data led to improvements, it was accepted that the data should be cautiously used given questionable accuracy in some respects. Further, the relative lack of mental health indicators across the care cascade would make future CQI processes harder to implement.

Learning through deliberation was a key feature of the capacity building programme. As described several times above, regular workshops to discuss findings and challenges, assess progress and clarify concepts, were a cornerstone of the process. However, face-to-face meetings were preferred to online meetings, especially in workshopping the last sections of the CQI process:

I feel sitting around the table helps because I feel that that’s where everyone is more likely to open up as compared to virtual. Because (with) virtual (meetings)…, we have a network issue. So, others do get cut out. Either they’re just sitting there hoping that this meeting will end soon. But then when it’s a table (face-to-face) method, you can ask one by one …where you work, what is your problem, where you are, and how do we work around it?…Then it also assisted us… in grouping the change ideas whether we can achieve this. If we do take training, is it going to yield anything good? If you take mentoring, it’s going to yield, (does it have)…a high impact or low impact?… (IP2).

Why I like it, is (be)cause with the face presentation it it’s easy to see somebody’s emotion. I'm somebody who want(s) to see the emotions. (IP2)

The process of learning through action, whereby workshops were followed by periods of application, followed up by further workshops, was generally perceived to be helpful. There was a conceptual understanding of the practical value of applying the various tools at various stages, for instance:

The fish(bone). (Be)cause we do use it… and then we get to a problem and then …how do we then address this problem that we have discovered? (IP2)

Participants highlighted how actively visiting facilities allowed them to observe certain dynamics which they would have otherwise missed. Embeddedness in the PHC facility sphere allowed participants to observe key challenges in the screening process, for instance, finding that conducting screening by an enrolled nurse in view of waiting clients partly explained resistance to employing the mental health screening tool. Further, engagement with staff and clients made participants aware of important contextual issues for consideration, illustrated by the following quotation:

And that is also where I learned about those cultural practises. Even though I saw it in the unit where I was at being practised, but I didn't see the extent. (IP4)

This referred to a tendency in the region of seeking care for mental health problems from traditional health practitioners before submitting to screening at PHC clinics, leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment.

Discussion

In this paper, we describe scaling up and embedding common mental disorder case findings and the referral dimension of the S-MhINT package through an LHS approach. While there is an increased focus being placed on the need for investment in capacity building efforts towards increasing the effectiveness of mental health service implementation and scale-up globally, critical gaps remain.27 Capacity-building is a common strategy in programme implementation, and learning content usually involves problem-solving, human resource and financial management, and leadership development.28 Nonetheless, very little evidence exists on how learning occurs towards building an LHS, capable of swiftly and accurately take up evidence-based interventions with iterative, data-driven quality improvement.

Our analysis of learning dimensions during the capacity building programme highlighted that individual-level, single-loop learning, was the most prominent learning method in terms of the AHPSR framework. Single-loop learning is perhaps that most readily observable of the levels because it is the most empirically measurable feature of learning, that is, the degree to which an individual learns now knowledge. This finding is in step with previous research using the AHPSR framework21 and underlines the relative rarity of double and triple-loop learning in practice. When double-loop learning does occur, the reflexivity that it generates within a health system context could gradually drive organisational transformation.29 Triple-loop learning is a rare and not fully documented in the recent literature. For both double and triple-loop learning, there are crucial requirements for it to emerge, particularly in complex environments fraught with uncertainty. This includes leadership, commitment and involvement of people across the healthcare spectrum, investment into integrated knowledge-sharing structures across healthcare levels, and adequate staff and dedicated budgets to ensure that knowledge is being harnessed and looped around in the health system.29 30

Our experience suggests that effective capacity building requires multilevel development that includes individual, group and organisation learning, as well as moving beyond project-level capabilities to higher-order, generic capabilities.31 The group-based learning platform used in the capacity building programme helped to achieve group-level learning to some degree. Inevitably, much more time and investment are required before a learning culture can be cultivated at an organisational level, which in our example was bedevilled by COVID-19. Aside from COVID-19, the expansion from individual to organisation-level learning is complex and the learning outcomes of health managers are open to a range of organisational dynamics.32 This being noted, more investment into fostering learning climates on organisational level could potentially yield substantial returns. It has been suggested that positive attitudes towards a learning culture within an organisation correlate with a willingness to transfer training,33 and that a learning climate may improve transformational leadership, as well as moderating its relationship with knowledge absorption.34 These aspects of health system strengthening are yet to be fully explored, even though it is known that learning capacity building should include mid-level and lower level management and local policy-makers and decision-makers within health systems.35 Participation on all levels of the organisation is required to ensure ownership of interventions and scale-up processes.36 Indeed, implementation strategies of task-sharing interventions in mental healthcare should seek to address barrier along mesolevel and macrolevel—organisational and broader system levels—and align with facilitators on these levels.4 Investments in management learning capacity building are likely to take some time to show evidence and require constant technical support and, in some cases, institutional redesign.37

Finally, capacity building requires a careful alignment of the needs of participants, policy and practice, while also considering the non-linear ways in which new interventions are adapted and taken up into routine practice.32 In this vein, the use of CQI was particularly useful to help contextualise learnings, by cyclically driving the information-deliberation-action heuristic. It also provides higher order learning—double-loop learning—by imparting a participatory method beyond the conventional didactic introduction of tools through training.31 CQI techniques and the ways of approaching system strengthening that are embedded within it, therefore, proved to be a critical element in the generation and uptake of learnings. This allowed the process to identify important gaps in the implementation of the S-MhINT depression intervention, leading in turn to fine-tuning how it is operationalised in PHC facilities. Further, the CQI process and group learning platform led to the identification and adoption of presumptive cases as an additional PHC routine mental health indicator in the DHIS, thereby strengthening the system capabilities to monitor implementation of depression services.

Lessons learnt

Our experiences in the codevelopment and enactment of a capacity-building programme to facilitate the scale-up of an integrated depression care implementation package from a testing site to a provincial level provide important lessons:

A critically important consideration in any health systems strengthening undertaking is a strong and sustained partnership, built on mutual goals and trust, between stakeholders. Time, mutual investments and a shared agenda between academics, government management (at a provincial level and district level) and district coordinators were crucial in driving a highly dynamic and complex scaling up process forward.

The intervention to be scaled up requires a balance between (1) fidelity and robust evidence, generated pragmatically in real-life contexts and (2) flexibility to adapt to changing needs and external shocks. COVID-19 is a well-described example of external shock, with a need to shift to virtual communication and shifting of resources away from health programmes to battle an emerging threat. However, technical difficulties may emerge, especially a lack of access to dependable email infrastructure, weak data coverage for internet connections and limited IT capacity. In the South African case, a provincial programme directorate does not strictly have direct authority over district coordinators. In turn, district programme managers have no authority over facility staff, who report to district managers, who also control the budget and other regulatory matters, who, therefore, also need to play a more central role in driving quality improvement strategies in order to effectively operationalise an integrated mental healthcare package.

Conclusion

Implementation and scale-up of EBPs are notoriously difficult to achieve in real-world settings, and this is also the case in mental health system strengthening in LMICs. The development of an LHS, through a participatory, bottom-up capacity building process, could potentially help ensure more sustained uptake of such practices. The S-MhINT intervention package illustrates how integrated, collaborative depression case identification and referral can be expanded from a test site to broader uptake through sustained and iterative learning, guided by the principles of LHS design.
