Baseline onchocerciasis prevalence, community perceptions and attitudes around the Singrobo-Ahouaty hydroelectric dam before impoundment, South-Central Côte d’ivoire

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHydroelectric dams are one of the major development projects designed to meet the requirements of sustainable development and improve living conditions and living standards. While contributing to socio-economic development, these dams profoundly alter ecosystems, leading to the proliferation of several vectors of water-linked diseases, including onchocerciasis transmitted by black fly bites. This study aims to assess baseline onchocerciasis prevalence, as well as community perceptions and attitudes in the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam area during its construction.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in six villages between February and March 2021. Consenting participants, selected from households, were aged five years and older. They underwent a clinical examination and skin scrapings were obtained to assess symptoms and detect microfilariae. A semi-structured questionnaire targeting heads of households was used to assess their perception, knowledge, and attitudes regarding onchocerciasis transmission. Data were double-entered into an Excel sheet, cross-checked, and analysed using STATA software 14.2.ResultsA total of 522 participants were included, of whom 274 were men (52.49%), yielding a sex ratio of 1.10. Farming was the most common occupation among the population, with 32.75%. Symptoms of onchocerciasis were observed in 30 (5.75%) participants. The most frequently reported symptoms were blindness (23.68%) and itching (16.62%). Among the participants included, 0.77% were carriers of microfilariae, and all were male farmers. The community microfilarial load (CMFL) obtained was 0.05 microfilariae/ skin snip. Out of 307 people interviewed, 48.86% were aware of onchocerciasis as a disease that causes blindness, and 36.48% were aware it was transmitted by black fly bites. 66.28% of those interviewed irregularly participated in the Community-Directed Treatment with Ivermectin, and 33.72% had stopped taking ivermectin.ConclusionsThe results show a very low prevalence of onchocerciasis and CMFL, associated with low community awareness of the disease. However, the water filling of the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam could promote a resurgence of transmission by creating environmental conditions conducive to black flies and increasing human-vector contact. It would therefore be necessary to strengthen and maintain epidemiological surveillance and implement vector control and awareness-raising measures.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12718-8.Keywords: Singrobo-Ahouaty hydroelectric dam, Prevalence, Perceptions and attitudes, Onchocerciasis, Côte d’ivoire

Background

Onchocerciasis, known as river blindness, is a parasitic, dermal, debilitating, and cumulative disease caused by the filarial Onchocerca volvulus [1]. Onchocerca volvulus is a nematode, transmitted to humans through the bite of female black flies belonging to the genus Simulium [2]. This disease is characterised by three types of lesions: onchocercomas, acute or chronic pruritic skin lesions, and ocular lesions that can progress to total blindness, causing a state of distress in the populations affected [3]. Onchocerciasis affects around 37 million people worldwide, over 99% of whom live in sub-Saharan Africa. Around 270,000 people lose their sight, and over 500,000 live with major visual impairment [4]. Most infections are reported in 31 countries in sub-Saharan Africa.

Côte d’Ivoire is an endemic country for onchocerciasis, with fast-flowing rivers that provide ideal breeding sites for Simulium flies. Before the launch of the Onchocerciasis Control Programme (OCP) in 1974, the disease was a major public health problem. Control efforts, led first by the OCP and later by the National Onchocerciasis Control Programme (PNLO), were adapted to regional epidemiology and included two main strategies: vector control via aerial larvicide spraying and mass treatment with ivermectin. Aerial spraying began in 1975 in the initial OCP area (Volta Noire, Haute Comoé, Haute Bagoé) and was extended in 1979 to the savannah-forest transition zones (Sassandra, N’Zi-Bandama and Comoé basins), with ivermectin distribution introduced in 1988. In the southern forest zone, only ivermectin was used [5]. These interventions successfully interrupted transmission and led to the elimination of onchocerciasis as a public health problem by 2002, with prevalence falling below 5% [6]. However, the end of OCP activities coincided with the 2002 military-political crisis, which disrupted surveillance systems [3]. Today, control relies on Community-Directed Treatment with Ivermectin (CDTI). Despite this, a 2007 survey in western forested areas revealed that 14.3% of children under five were still infected, indicating ongoing transmission [7].

The prevalence of onchocerciasis within populations results from the interaction between multiple factors. It depends both on the dynamics of parasite transmission and the host’s immune status. In addition, socio-demographic characteristics and occupational activities play an important role in the occurrence, distribution and persistence of the disease [8]. Development projects such as hydroelectric dams further exacerbate the risk by altering hydrology, geodynamics [9], and the ecosystem, creating favourable conditions for vector proliferation [10] and the spread of water-linked vector-borne diseases [11], including onchocerciasis, lymphatic filariasis [9], malaria, and schistosomiasis [12]. A hydroelectric dam causes artificial acceleration of water flow through the creation of spillways and river beds downstream of the dam. This promotes the establishment and proliferation of extremely large populations of Simulium damnosum [13]. The vector species for onchocerciasis in West Africa belong to the Simulium damnosum complex [14]. These black flies reproduce in fast-flowing streams (rivers, etc.). These specific river portions are suitable for the vectors along riverbanks. Black flies cause a nuisance that is difficult for humans to tolerate. This nuisance severely disrupts agricultural and fishing activities, leading to a decrease in annual productivity. It also contributes to the abandonment of riverside areas by young people and the gradual breakdown of the rural social fabric [1]. In Côte d’Ivoire, studies conducted in the Taabo region, particularly in Ahouaty in 1977, revealed the presence of larval habitats and black flies belonging to the Simulium soubrense and Simulium sanctipauli species [15]. In addition to these ecosystem changes, hydroelectric dams, due to their size and location, create socio-economic opportunities and conditions for human population migration for better living conditions. These populations are exposed to diseases, particularly vector-borne diseases [16]. When human communities settle, pathogen carriers can be a potential source of vector infestation. Despite these risks, few studies have examined the impact of hydroelectric infrastructure on onchocerciasis transmission in Côte d’Ivoire, both before and after dam impoundment. This study aims to help fill this gap by providing baseline data relevant to the health surveillance and monitoring system in the context of a hydroelectric dam. This study is part of the “Epidemic Risk, Awareness, Demographic Monitoring” component of the 44 MW Singrobo-Ahouaty hydroelectric project, construction of which began in 2021 on the Bandama river, downstream of the Taabo hydroelectric dam built between 1975 and 1979. Given the increased potential for human–black flies contact, this dam may raise the risk of onchocerciasis transmission. The objective of this study is to determine the prevalence of onchocerciasis and to explore community perceptions in the area influenced by the Singrobo-Ahouaty hydroelectric dam project, after 26 years of Community-Directed Treatment with Ivermectin, to identify key health risks before impoundment.

Methods

Study area

The study was carried out around the Singrobo-Ahouaty hydroelectric project, located in the Taabo department, south-central Côte d’Ivoire. Data were collected in six villages: N’Dénou, Ahouaty, and Sokrogbo in the Taabo sub-prefecture, and Singrobo, Pacobo, and Ahéremou 2 in the Pacobo sub-prefecture (Fig. 1).

Geographical location of study sites in the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam impact zone, in Côte d’Ivoire

The study area experiences a humid subtropical climate, characterised by four distinct seasons based on rainfall patterns: a long dry season from December to March, a long rainy season from April to mid-July, a short dry season from mid-July to mid-September, and a short rainy season from mid-September to November. The average annual rainfall is approximately 1235.03 mm. The mean monthly temperature and relative humidity are 28.4 °C and 76.4%, respectively. The vegetation consists of a mosaic of forest and savannah ecosystems. The area is traversed from north to south by the Bandama River, which dominates the local hydrographic network. The population comprises both indigenous (Baoulé, Souamlin, and Dida) and non-indigenous (Burkinabe and Malians) communities, with most residents relying on subsistence activities such as farming and fishing.

Selection of study villages

Prior to the dam construction, the local operator, namely Ivoire Hydro Energie (IHE) has conducted an environmental and social impact assessment of the Singrobo-Ahouaty hydroelectric dam before it began. The study villages were selected because of their location within the impact zone of the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam project. An important part of their territory was used for the dam construction, staff housing, access roads, power lines and temporary construction sites. The distances between the villages and the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam are as follows: Ahouaty, 1 km (km), Singrobo, 3.5 km, and Sokrogbo, Pacobo, N’Dénou and Ahéremou 2, each less than 10 km. In addition to their location in the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam impact zone, the populations of these villages are located downstream of the Taabo hydroelectric dam, also constructed on the Bandama river. All communities receive a mass treatment with ivermectin under the Community-Directed Treatment with Ivermectin (CDTI) managed by the national neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) control program.

Study design and sampling

A cross-sectional survey was carried out from February 3 to March 3, 2021, in households in the Taabo department. A household was defined as all individuals residing in the same dwelling and contributing to its economic functioning. A total of 60 households per village was estimated based on the following sample size formula: n = D [(Zα + Zβ) 2*(P1 (1 - P1) + P2 (1 - P2)) / (P2 - P1) 2], where n= number of households; D= design effect, conventionally estimated at 2; P1 = estimated level of the proportion of households in the project area with an acceptable state of health (0.40); P2 = expected proportion of household health improvements at the end of the project (0.75); Zα = the Z-score corresponding to the level of confidence we wish to have in concluding that a change has been observed and that it is not due to chance (1.645); Zβ = the Z-score corresponding to the degree of confidence we wish to have in detecting the change with certainty if a change has taken place (1.282).

In each household, up to five individuals aged five and older were selected for biological sample collection. Individuals were excluded if they presented with poor general health or severe illness at the time of the survey. Pregnant and breastfeeding women were excluded from the study for ethical and safety reasons, as skin biopsy is an invasive procedure that may present potential risks such as infection or complications. In accordance with the World Health Organization recommendations, these procedures are avoided in pregnant and breastfeeding women to minimise any potential risk for both mother and child.

Participants from each household went to the local health centre on the day of the screening. After signing the informed consent and/or assent forms, each participant was identified according to the following characteristics: household number, individual number, name, sex, age, profession, ethnicity and origin. The participants were all asked about the ivermectin distribution campaign and the use of these medicines.

Clinical examination

The participants all underwent clinical examinations to identify the various signs of onchocerciasis. Each participant was carefully examined in a voting booth by a nurse. Symptoms such as nodule location, skin lesions and obvious visual disturbances were noted on clinical forms.

Palpation

Participants were palpated to check for the presence of nodules (onchocercomas) harbouring adult O. volvulus worms. Palpation was performed on the bony prominences of the ribs, iliac crests, sacrum and upper leg. The nodules are firm, round or oval, generally non-tender, and can grow to several centimetres in diameter.

Visual impairment tests

All participants were screened for visual impairment potentially linked to past or current onchocerciasis infection. The examination began with a gross vision acuity test, in which participants were asked to count the health examiner’s fingers at three meters. If unsuccessful, the test was repeated at one meter. Inability to see fingers at either distance was classified as severely reduced visual acuity. A basic ocular inspection was then performed using a flashlight to detect corneal opacity, nodules on the eyelid or conjunctiva, visible scars or signs of ocular inflammation (e.g., redness and pain). Ocular onchocerciasis was suspected in cases of reduced visual acuity accompanied by corneal opacity or nodules.

Physical examinations to detect skin rash

Participants underwent physical examinations to identify dermatological signs suggestive of onchocerciasis. Attention was given to skin condition in individuals reporting pruritus, a common symptom of the disease. Pruritus may occur in isolation or association with characteristic skin lesions.

Parasitological examination

Parasitological assessments were performed following clinical examination. Dermal microfilariae were detected using the skin snip technique with Holth 2.3 mm forceps [17]. Two skin snips were collected from the right and left iliac crests and placed in a drop of physiological water on pre-labelled ringed slides. Individual skin samples were collected in the morning between 7:30 and 10:00 a.m. The emergence of microfilariae was observed using an optical microscope at 30-minute intervals for each skin sample, from the time of collection until the end of the day (around 6 p.m., when observations ended in each village). The microfilariae detected were counted and recorded on individual data collection sheets.

Collection, morphological identification and dissection of female black flies

The human landing collection technique was used in this study [17]. Collectors, who had been previously trained, sat with their legs exposed to the knees, taking turns every hour. Each female black fly that landed on the skin was immediately covered and trapped in a labelled haemolysis tube before it began to bite. Black flies’ collections were carried out in November 2021, on two consecutive days from 7 a.m. to 6 p.m., at the capture points in each study site: Ahouaty, N’dénou, Sokrogbo, Ahéremou 2, Pacobo and Singrobo. These capture points were located at 5 m from the Bandama river, at points with fast-flowing waters where their larvae and pupae can perform their life cycle.

The black flies collected were identified in the laboratory based on the morphological characteristics [18]. Following the identification step, the dissection of female black flies captured allowed detailed examination of the head, thorax, and abdomen for the detection of the larval stages of Onchocerca volvulus [19].

Household questionnaire

A semi-structured questionnaire was developed specifically for this study and uploaded as an additional file. The survey questionnaire was pre-tested and administered to the heads or representatives of households screened for onchocerciasis in each locality. It aimed to assess knowledge of onchocerciasis and community attitudes and practices related to the disease.

Statistical analysis

Data collected were double-entered into Microsoft Access 2013 software and checked for consistency. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 14.2 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Proportions and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated. The Chi-square test was used to compare proportions across categories of onchocerciasis symptoms, microfilaria carriers, and community perceptions of the disease. When expected numbers were < 5, the Fisher-Pearson test was applied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Prevalence and distribution of onchocerciasis symptoms

Clinical examination showed that 5.75% of the 522 participants had symptoms of onchocerciasis. Among these symptomatic individuals, visual impairment was the most common manifestation (43.33%), followed by skin rashes (36.67%) and nodules (20%). No statistically significant difference in symptom prevalence was observed between sexes or occupational categories. However, women represented a higher proportion of symptomatic cases compared to men (6.85%, 95% CI: 4.04–10.74% vs. 4.74%, 95% CI: 2.55–7.97%). The number of farmers (15) presenting symptoms was higher than in other professions. In terms of age group, the 36–65 age group exhibited all three types of clinical signs. No significant differences were observed between age groups. Regarding origin, no significant difference was observed between indigenous and allogenous participants (6.73%, 95% CI: 4.26-10.00% vs. 4.10%, 95% CI: 1.78–7.92%) (Table 2).

Prevalence and distribution of microfilaria carriers

Among the 522 individuals examined, 4 participants tested positive for Onchocerca volvulus, representing a prevalence of 0.77%. However, those who tested positive for microfilariae were all male and farmers. The 19–35 and 36–65 age groups were carriers of microfilariae, with prevalences of 1.34% and 0.81%, respectively. The majority of infected individuals were indigenous at 0.92% (Table 2).

Microfilariae were detected in two of the six study villages. Although no significant difference was observed between these villages, the prevalence in the village of Ahéremou 2 was slightly higher, at 3.33%, than that in Singrobo (0.85%) (Fig. 2b).

Characteristics of the population interviewed

A total of 307 heads of household were interviewed in 354 households screened. Of the 307 participants interviewed, the majority were male gender (59.61%), particularly in the village of Ahouaty, where the proportion (79.27%) was higher than in the other villages. In terms of age group, the 36–65 age group was the most represented (58.31%) in all villages. The occupational activities of the people interviewed were dominated by farmers (46.25%) in all villages, particularly numerous in the village of Sokrogbo (66.67%). The majority of the population interviewed in the study area was indigenous (61.89%). In terms of education, most interviewees had attended school (57%). Among those with formal education, primary schooling (32.25%) was the most common level, except in Pacobo, where secondary education (36.36%) was more prevalent (Table 5).

Knowledge and perception of onchocerciasis

Of the 307 heads of households surveyed, 48.86% had heard of onchocerciasis and described it as a disease that causes blindness, while 51.14% had no idea about the disease. Awareness levels varied significantly between villages (p < 0.0001), with proportions ranging from 29.27% in Ahouaty to 72.73% in Pacobo. Regarding transmission, 36.48% correctly identified the blackfly (locally known as the “midge”) as the vector of onchocerciasis, where as 62.53% were unable to identify the cause. The villages of Ahéremou 2 and N’Dénou had the highest levels of awareness of the blackfly vector, with 63.64% and 51.06% respondents, respectively, citing it. The knowledge of the disease’s cause differed significantly between villages (p < 0.001). When asked about symptoms, responses also varied significantly by village (p < 0.001). The most cited symptoms were blindness (23.78%), itching (16.62%) and skin lesions (3.58%), while 51.14% of respondents were unaware of any symptoms (Table 6). Knowledge of preventive measures also differed significantly between villages (p < 0.001). Ivermectin was mentioned by 25.08% of the respondents, with proportions ranging from 13.41% in Ahouaty to 38.18% in Ahéremou 2. However, most of the respondents could not identify any preventive method (Table 6). As for perceptions of who is most at risk of infection, 18.24% believed that everyone is exposed to onchocerciasis, while 11.40% thought it affects mainly older individuals. The majority (68.08%) stated they did not know who was affected by the disease (Table 6).

Attitudes and adherence to onchocerciasis chemoprevention

These results are based on responses from participants screened via skin biopsy in each village. Of the 522 participants, 96.93% were aware of the Community-Directed Treatment with Ivermectin campaign, generally reporting that it occurs once or twice per year. Awareness of the campaign was high across all villages (Fig. 3a). Among the 506 participants aware of the CDTI campaign, 68.38% reported adhering to ivermectin intake, with village-specific rates ranging from 50% in Pacobo to 80% in Ahouaty. Conversely, 31.62% did not adhere to treatment. This variation revealed a significant difference between adherence (66.28%, 95% CI: 62.04–70.33) and non-adherence (33.72%, 95% CI: 29.67–37.95%) across villages (Fig. 3b). Notably, even among adherents, compliance with the recommended ivermectin intake schedule during campaigns was irregular. Several socio-demographic factors influence adherence. Gender was significantly associated with adherence (p < 0.05): men adhered more frequently (55.78%) than women (44.22%), whereas non-adherence was more common among women (53.98%). Age was also significantly associated (p < 0.001), with the highest adherence observed in the 36–65 age group (50.29%). Professionally, farmers showed the highest adherence (36.71%) compared to other occupations (p < 0.001). Origin was another influential factor: indigenous participants had significantly higher adherence (65.90%) than allogenous individuals (34.10%) (p < 0.05). No significant association was observed between education level and adherence to ivermectin (p > 0.05) (Table 7).

Discussion

Hydroelectric dams are known for their modifications, which create environmental conditions conducive to the proliferation of several vectors of waterborne diseases. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of onchocerciasis, as well as the population’s level of knowledge about onchocerciasis and their adherence to ivermectin treatment in the impact zone of the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam during its construction phase, to provide baseline data before impoundment for sustainable and effective control.

The study reported an onchocerciasis prevalence of 0.77%, below the 5% elimination threshold, likely reflecting the effectiveness of community-directed ivermectin treatment (CDTI). Among the six villages surveyed, positive cases were detected only in Singrobo and Ahéremou 2. The presence of onchocerciasis in these two villages may be associated with individual risk behaviour, including absence during CDTI campaigns and refusals of ivermectin. Ahouaty, Sokrogbo, N’Dénou and Pacobo showed an absence of positive cases. This represents a marked decline compared with WHO-reported prevalences in Ahouaty, 9.5% in 1997 and 8.3% in 2007 [18]. Such results may reflect strong community to mass ivermectin administration and the sustained efforts of the national control programme.

Study results also showed that the participants with microfilaria were all male gender and farmers in the 19–35 and 36–65 age groups. These observations corroborate those from Kuunifaa (2013) in certain communities in the environment of the Bui dam, Ghana, showing that Onchocerca volvulus infection was observed only in males in the 15–29 age group [19]. The presence of the infection in males in these age groups could be explained by the fact that these people are the most active in the village and in field work, which makes them more exposed to Simulium bites through their daily activities.

The study made possible the current black fly fauna identification in the study area and the estimation of the overall parous rate (41.46%) and infection rate (0.53%). The village Sokrogbo harboured a higher rate of parous (55%) than that observed at the other capture sites, associated with an infection rate of 3.03%. The presence of black flies in this area could be explained by ecological conditions that are particularly suitable to their reproduction, notably the proximity of the Bandama River with a portion of fast-flowing waters and the Taabo hydroelectric Dam, which provide habitats conducive to larval development. The high rate of parous observed in Sokrogbo could be linked to more intense biting activity and increased longevity of the vectors, two factors that are essential to maintaining the transmission cycle in an environment. The presence of infection in the area, particularly in the village of Sokogbo, could be explained by the persistence of the parasite transmission cycle. This could lead to this area becoming a residual hotspot for active transmission.

The study shows a variation in the frequency of clinical manifestations of onchocerciasis in the different villages. In the study area, all the people carrying microfilariae showed no symptoms of the disease. Skin rash, visual impairment and nodules were the main clinical symptoms. The 36 to 64 age group is the only one to present with onchocerciasis nodules, mainly on the iliac crest, elbow, knee and costal grid. This result could be explained by the fact that in elderly subjects, the nodules, although evidence of infestation, often no longer harbour any living filariae, and there is no longer any emission of microfilariae.

The study also determined a CMFL of 0.05 mf/ss. This result differs from the results observed in the reach on the lower Bandama at Ahouaty in Côte d’Ivoire. The study showed a CMFL of 0.28 mf/ss in 2007 compared with a study conducted in 1997 with a CMFL of 0.30 mf/ss [18]. The reduction in CMFL could be explained by the effectiveness of the fight devoted to CDTI over 26 years of treatment.

The study showed a low level of knowledge of onchocerciasis in the population in the study area. This study showed that 48.86% of people questioned knew about onchocerciasis as a disease that causes blindness, and 36.48% of these people associated the cause of the disease with the bite of black flies (belonging to the genus Simulium). These results differ from those obtained in Kafolo in the north of Côte d’Ivoire [7], which showed that the majority of the population surveyed (78%) knew about onchocerciasis as a ‘blind’ disease and that 92% of the population thought that it was transmitted by black flies (belonging to the genus Simulium) bites. However, a lack of awareness of symptoms related to onchocerciasis was observed in the study area. The symptoms cited were itching (16.62%) and nodules (1.95%). These results differ from those found in south-west Nigeria, where respondents had a good knowledge of the symptoms of onchocerciasis - itching (64.4%), nodules (34%) [20]. The low level of knowledge about onchocerciasis could be explained by the lack of population awareness campaigns and training of community health workers by the national program. Insufficient knowledge among communities could lead to risky behaviours within these populations.

The study showed that participants had a good knowledge of CDTI campaigns. This good knowledge could be explained by the regularity of these campaigns carried out by community health workers. The majority of participants (68.38%) informed about the campaigns adhere to taking ivermectin, while 31.62% do not. But the frequency of participants adhering to treatment with ivermectin is not consistent during CDTI. This irregularity could be explained by the absence of participants (travel for agricultural work and trips). Farm activities and travel could be major factors contributing to irregularity in the administration of ivermectin during mass treatment campaigns. Indeed, field work due to agricultural activities and professional or personal travel results in the absence of many people from mass distribution campaigns with ivermectin. This absence causes irregularity in the administration of treatment. The issue of hard-to-reach area mainly during the rainy season, needs to be considered.

Certain socio-demographic characteristics such as gender, age, profession and origin influence adherence to treatment with ivermectin. These results could be explained by the fact that men in the 36–65 age group are more active in the communities and are more interested in their health because of their daily activities (field work). Pregnant women are excluded from mass drug administration of ivermectin during pregnancy and breastfeeding, in accordance with WHO recommendations. However, this exclusion may contribute to maintaining a reservoir of parasites among women of reproductive age, particularly in the case of successive pregnancies. Non-adherence to ivermectin in the participants could be explained by the painful onset of side effects such as body swelling and intense vomiting. Participants reported that these pains cause significant discomfort and, in some cases, may lead to other health problems. However, it should be noted that side effects are a factor in the reluctance of people in the communities to take ivermectin. These results are similar to those obtained in Nigeria, with 57.2% of people not complying with CDTI, including 26.7% with low compliance and 30% with average compliance [17]. The irregular use and refusal of ivermectin by the population represent a limiting factor in the eradication of onchocerciasis in this study area, as these people could constitute reservoirs for the transmission of onchocerciasis.

The limitations of this study include the reluctance of some households to provide skin samples, the absence of some household members at the time where the questionnaires were administered, and the assessment of knowledge about onchocerciasis and CDTI intake based solely on the statements from household heads or their representatives.

Conclusion

The study conducted in the context of the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam revealed a low prevalence of onchocerciasis (0.77%), a CMFL (0.05 mf/ss) and an infection rate of 0.53% after several years of CDTI. At community level, it also showed a lack of sufficient knowledge about onchocerciasis and non-compliance with the use of ivermectin. However, the filling of the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam could promote a resurgence of transmission by creating environmental conditions conducive to black flies and increasing human-vector contact. It would therefore be necessary to strengthen and maintain the epidemiological surveillance system and implement vector control measures and appropriate awareness campaigns before and after mass treatment with ivermectin. This will ensure sustainable and effective control of the disease in the region, mainly in communities living in the impact zone of the Singrobo-Ahouaty dam.

The study protocol was approved by the Côte d’Ivoire National Ethics Committee for Life Sciences and Health (149 − 22/MSHPCMU/CNESVS-kp) and was conducted in accordance with the protocol and the current version of the Declaration of Helsinki (ICH GCP Guidelines E6/R2). Data collection was conducted with strict confidentiality. Individuals who tested positive for onchocerciasis received treatment confidentially at the village health centre, where they were monitored by a nurse or physician and administered appropriate doses of ivermectin.
