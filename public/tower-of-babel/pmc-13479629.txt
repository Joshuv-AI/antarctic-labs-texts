KRAS-driven protein disulfide isomerase family A member 6 expression suppresses PRKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase-mediated immunogenic cell death to desensitise pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma to immune checkpoint blockers
Authors: Ronglin Wang, Junqiang Li, Danjie Su, Jing Yang, Peixiang Ma, Lei Hua, Jing Luo, Jingyi Liu, Rui Yang, Liang Zhang, Xiangjing Shen, Hongrui Wang, Hong Li, Ting Zhao, Jie Min, Lili Liu, Chenggong Liao, Yang Song, Haichuan Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is characterised by a dismal prognosis and insensitivity to immune checkpoint blockers (ICBs); however, the underlying mechanism remains elusive.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify tumour cell-intrinsic regulators that promote immune evasion and ICB resistance in PDAC.DesignMulti-omics analysis and clinical cohort studies identified protein disulfide isomerase family A member 6 (PDIA6) as a regulator of the immune microenvironment. Flow cytometry, multiplex immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy and Glutathione S-Transferase (GST) pulldown assays confirmed that PDIA6 repressed PRKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) activation and immunogenic cell death (ICD). Chromatin immunoprecipitation confirmed that KRASG12D and YY1 modulated PDIA6 transcription. LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-Trp53R172H/+;Pdx-1-Cre (KPC) mouse models showed that PDIA6 inhibition improved ICB response.ResultsMulti-omics screening identified PDIA6 as a biomarker of CD8+ T-cell paucity and poor prognosis in patients with PDAC. High PDIA6 levels predicted poor ICB response in the PDAC cohorts. PDIA6 inhibition reprogrammed the immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment and hindered mouse PDAC growth in the presence of CD8+ T-cell, which is attributed to enhanced ICD. PDIA6 interacted with cysteine 453 of PERK, abrogating the disulphide bond-mediated dimerisation and activation of PERK, an ICD inducer. Oncogenic KRASG12D potently upregulated PDIA6 via YY1-mediated transcriptional activation. We identified a small-molecule inhibitor of PDIA6, PACMA31, and demonstrated that targeting PDIA6 with PACMA31 improved ICB efficacy in a PDAC mouse model with KRAS mutations.ConclusionsPDIA6, driven by KRASG12D, alleviates ICD and promotes immune evasion, functioning as a predictive biomarker to screen ICB-sensitive patients and a therapeutic target to improve ICB efficacy in PDAC with KRAS mutations.Keywords: PANCREATIC CANCER, CANCER GENETICS, CANCER IMMUNOBIOLOGY, CELL BIOLOGY

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer, predominantly the pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), has the highest rate of KRAS mutation and is the third leading cause of cancer-related death.1 As a ‘cold’ tumour, PDAC displays poor infiltration and activation of CD8+ T-cells and is therefore intrinsically refractory to immune checkpoint blocker (ICB) treatment.2 Cumulative evidence reveals complex mechanisms underlying CD8+ T-cell inhibition, involving, among others, the cancer cell-intrinsic regulators.3–6 By modulating cancer cell membrane ligands or immunomodulatory factors to induce CD8+ T-cell inhibition, these regulators drive immune evasion and ICB resistance.7–9 However, their roles in PDAC remain incompletely defined.

According to the cancer-immunity cycle, antigens or immune adjuvants released by dying cancer cells induce antigen-presenting cell maturation and subsequent CD8+ T-cell infiltration and activation, which are essential for antitumour immunity and related immunotherapies.10 Immunogenic cell death (ICD), as a type of regulated cell death, represents a critical step in the cancer-immunity cycle to elicit an adaptive immune response. By releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) including Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP), calreticulin (CRT) and some other factors, cancer cells undergoing ICD promote maturation and antigen presentation of dendritic cells (DCs), especially conventional DC 1 (cDC1), leading to CD8+ T-cell activation and proliferation.11 Previous studies have shown that the endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) initiates ICD12 13 and pancreatic acinar cell neoplastic transformation.14 As one of the major ERS sensors, excessive or inhibited PRKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) activation triggers ICD, mounting antitumour immune responses.12 15 Therefore, a moderate level of PERK activation is essential for cancer cell survival and immune evasion, and cancer cells likely use a strategy to stabilise PERK and evade ERS-induced ICD. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear.

This study aimed to reveal how protein disulfide isomerase family A member 6 (PDIA6) drives immune evasion in PDAC, explore its role in CD8+ T-cell suppression and resistance to immunotherapy, elucidate its regulatory mechanism and assess whether targeting PDIA6 could serve as a novel therapeutic strategy for improving ICB efficacy.

Results

PDIA6 was a negative indicator of prognosis and ICB response associated with poor CD8+ T-cell infiltration in patients with PDAC

To identify the potential regulators of PDAC immune evasion, patients with PDAC were divided into high-CD8+ T-cell or low groups (figure 1A,B and online supplemental figure S1A–D), and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in cancer cells between the two groups were determined (online supplemental tables S1 and S2). These CD8+ T-cell deficiency-related DEGs were intersected with DEGs indicating a poor prognosis (figure 1C and online supplemental tables S3 and S4), which identified PDIA6 and CLDN1 as candidates. Given established roles of CLDN family members in cancer progression, we prioritised PDIA6 for further investigation.16 PDIA6 mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated in PDAC tumour tissues (figure 1D–F and online supplemental table S5). PDIA6 expression was correlated with higher clinical stages and poor prognosis in patients with PDAC (figure 1G,H and online supplemental figure S1E–G).

We delineated the relationship between PDIA6 and CD8+ T-cell infiltration. Analysis of TIMER and a scRNA-seq dataset (GSE212966) showed a decreased CD8+ T-cell proportion in the PDIA6-high group (online supplemental figure S1H–M). High PDIA6 levels were associated with increased CK19 and decreased CD8a levels in PDAC biopsied tissues (figure 1I–K).

We examined the correlation between PDIA6 and ICB response. In the PDAC-ICB dataset (GSE179351), PDIA6 levels decreased in patients who responded to ICB treatment (figure 1L). Patients with lower PDIA6 expression had longer median progression-free survival (PFS) (48.5 vs 7.0 months; HR, 0.2728; 95% CI 0.06086 to 1.223; p=0.0110) and overall survival (OS) time (109.0 vs 25.0 months; HR, 0.2454; 95% CI 0.05253 to 1.146; p=0.0051) after ICB treatment (figure 1M,N). The result was confirmed in the Tangdu PDAC-ICB cohort constructed by our centre (online supplemental tables S6–S9 for demographic data). In patients treated with adjuvant therapy and ICB, median PFS time (5.5 vs 3.5 months; HR, 0.4746; 95% CI 0.1857 to 1.213; p=0.0384) was prolonged in the PDIA6-low group receiving ICB treatment (figure 1O). Median OS time (17.0 vs 9.0 months; HR, 0.4419; 95% CI 0.1594 to 1.225; p=0.0691) improved in the PDIA6-low group, though not statistically significant (figure 1P). The median disease-free survival (DFS) (16.0 vs 4.5 months; HR, 0.3239; 95% CI 0.08684 to 1.208; p=0.0235) and OS (32.0 vs 25.0 months; HR, 0.3240; 95% CI 0.06439 to 1.631; p=0.0419) were prolonged in the PDIA6-low group versus PDIA6-high group in patients treated with postoperative adjuvant therapy and ICB (figure 1Q,R). Together, these results suggest that PDIA6 is highly expressed in PDAC and correlates with advanced disease stage, reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration and unfavourable prognosis and ICB response.

PDIA6 knockdown suppressed PDAC progression in an immune-dependent manner

We assessed the effect of PDIA6 knockdown on tumour progression and CD8+ T-cell infiltration. Results showed that PDIA6 knockdown had little effect on growth (online supplemental figure S2A–M) or apoptosis (online supplemental figure S2N–P) of mouse PDAC cells and organoids from pancreatic tumours in KPC mice. Animal studies showed that PDIA6 knockdown hindered orthotopic tumour progression in immunocompetent mice (figure 2A–D, online supplemental figure S2Q–S). Flow cytometry showed increased CD8+ T-cell proportion (figure 2E) and enhanced proliferation and activation of CD8+ T-cells in PDIA6-knockdown tumours (figure 2F,G and online supplemental figure S3A). cDC1, a key DC subset, activates CD8+ T-cells and promotes antitumour immunity via antigen cross-presentation. The results showed that PDIA6 knockdown did not alter cDC1 proportion but elevated CD86, correlating with enhanced CD8+ T-cell activation (figure 2H,I and online supplemental figure S3C). And PDIA6 knockdown had little effect on NK1.1+, CD19+, CD8a+ and CD4+ cells and cDC1 in the spleen (online supplemental figures S3B,D and S4). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses confirmed increased CD8a, decreased Ki-67 and unchanged cDC1 in PDIA6-knockdown tumours (figure 2J–L). Consistently, PDIA6 knockdown repressed subcutaneous tumour progression in immunocompetent mice but not in immunocompromised mice (online supplemental figure S5A–I). Flow cytometry confirmed increased proportion (online supplemental figure S5J) and enhanced activation (online supplemental figure S5K–O) of CD8+ T-cells in PDIA6-knockdown tumours, while other immune cells remained unchanged in spleen (online supplemental figure S6A–E). These results suggest that PDIA6 knockdown might repress tumour growth in the presence of CD8+ T-cells. To confirm this, immunocompetent mice were injected with anti-CD8a or anti-CD8β antibodies to deplete CD8+ T-cells (figure 2M and online supplemental figure S6F). Results showed that the tumour-suppressive effect of PDIA6 knockdown was abrogated by CD8+ T-cell depletion (figure 2N,O and online supplemental figure S6G,H). Therefore, PDIA6 knockdown in cancer cells suppresses PDAC progression in an immune-dependent manner.

PDIA6 knockdown sensitised PDAC to ICD to thereby suppress PDAC progression

Prompted by Western blot (online supplemental figure S7A) and data analysis (online supplemental figure S7B) showing weak PDIA6-immune checkpoint correlation, we analysed PDAC datasets for other potential mechanisms. Patients in the scRNA-seq dataset (GSE242230) were stratified into PDIA6-high or low groups based on PDIA6 levels in tumour cells. ICD-related genes were differentially expressed between groups (figure 3A). Gene set enrichment analysis of DEGs showed that the PDIA6-low group was enriched for ICD-related pathways (figures 3B,C, online supplemental figure S8 and online supplemental table S10). Previous studies have shown that PDIA6 negatively regulates ERS and autophagy, which initiate ICD in tumours. These findings suggest that PDIA6 likely blunts ICD to counteract CD8+ T-cell-mediated immune elimination. To examine whether PDIA6 modulates ICD, PDIA6 knockdown and control PDAC cells (online supplemental figure S2B–G) were treated with ICD inducer oxaliplatin (OXP), commonly used in PDAC treatment. Results showed that OXP promoted cell death in PDIA6-knockdown cells (figure 3D,E and online supplemental figure S9A–F), primarily through apoptosis and pyroptosis-like cell death (online supplemental figure S9G,H). Further analyses showed that PDIA6 downregulation enhanced CRT translocation to cytoplasmic membrane, autophagy induction and ATP release, which are ICD characteristics (figure 3F–I and online supplemental figure S10A–H). We then co-cultured bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) with PDIA6 knockdown and control PDAC cells treated with or without OXP. Results showed that BMDCs phagocytosed and internalised more OXP-treated PDIA6 knockdown cells, showing mature features through enhanced phagocytic ability (figure 3J,K and online supplemental figure S10I), more extensive protrusions (figure 3L), upregulated CD80 and CD86 co-stimulation molecules and cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6 and IL-12 (figure 3M,N and online supplemental figure S11A–C). Additionally, pre-immunisation with OXP-treated PDIA6 knockdown cells led to the highest tumour-free proportion in mice, suggesting superior immunogenic potential (figure 3O,P and online supplemental figure S11D–G). Our results confirmed that PDIA6 knockdown sensitises PDAC cells to ICD, compromising PDAC progression.

PDIA6 cleaved intermolecular disulphide bond and alleviated dimer formation and activation of PERK

PDIA6 is associated with negative regulation of ERS, which initiates ICD. To investigate how PDIA6 regulates ERS, PDIA6 knockdown and control PDAC cells were treated with thapsigargin (TG), an ERS inducer (online supplemental figure S12A–H). CA2+ analysis (online supplemental figure S12I–L) and ER tracker staining (online supplemental figure S12M–P) showed that PDIA6 knockdown intensified the TG-induced ERS. Among three major ERS sensors, PERK, which interacts with PDIA6 and induces ICD, was most influenced by PDIA6 knockdown (figure 4A and online supplemental figure S13A–C). In addition, PDIA6 expression suppressed PERK activation and ERS (figure 4B, online supplemental figure S13D–F).

PDIA6 trap mutant (mu1) has high affinity for substrates when cysteine residues 58 and 193 in the disulphide isomerase domain are replaced with alanine (C58A, C193A). When all four cysteine residues are mutated into alanine (C55A, C58A, C190A and C193A), the PDIA6 mutant (mu2) loses affinity to its substrates (figure 4C and online supplemental figure S14A).17 Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis showed that PDIA6-wt interacted with endogenous PERK, with enhanced interaction between PDIA6-mu1 and PERK but reduced interaction between PDIA6-mu2 and PERK (online supplemental figure S14B), indicating involvement in disulphide bond-related reactions. A similar conclusion emerged when PERK was overexpressed in PDIA6-mu1 HEK293T cells (online supplemental figure S14C,D). We explored PERK-binding sites of PDIA6. We generated PERK mutants with four cysteines in the ER luminal domain substituted with serine (C216S, C221S, C336S and C453S), while threonine 982 of the phosphorylation site was replaced with alanine (T982A). Co-IP results showed that the interaction between PERK C453S and PDIA6-mu1 was the weakest among PERK mutants, suggesting that PDIA6 primarily binds to PERK via Cys453 (figure 4D,E). The GST pull-down assay showed consistent results (figure 4F and online supplemental figure S14E). We next assessed the role of PDIA6 in regulating PERK. Immunoblotting showed PDIA6 did not influence PERK protein stability (online supplemental figure S14F,G).

PERK forms dimers or oligomers, followed by autophosphorylation, leading to activation.18 To examine whether PDIA6 regulates intermolecular disulphide bond formation, dimer formation and activation of PERK, immunoblotting was performed under the non-reducing condition. The non-reducing WB results showed that TG treatment induced PERK dimerisation (figure 4G, lanes 3 and 5), and PDIA6 knockdown promoted PERK dimerisation compared with nonsense control with or without TG treatment (figure 4G, lanes 3–6). PERK formed more dimers in PDIA6-wt cells than in PDIA6-mu1 cells, which showed higher affinity for PERK, suggesting increased PDIA6-PERK interaction suppresses PERK dimerisation (figure 4H, lane 2 and 4). Compared with wt PERK, PERK C453S almost completely lost dimerisation ability in PDIA6-wt or -mu1 cells, indicating that Cys453 is critical for PERK dimerisation (figure 4H, lanes 3 and 5).

As a disulphide isomerase, PDIA6 primarily affects disulphide bonds of target proteins. Protein docking analysis indicated C453S site was shifted away from the disulphide isomerase catalytic site of PDIA6 (online supplemental figure S15A). Mass spectrometry results showed that PDIA6-mu1 co-culture cleaved intermolecular disulphide between cysteine 424 of PERK proteins’ endoplasmic reticulum luminal segment (corresponding to the cysteine 453 of full-length PERK) (online supplemental figure S15B). Although both PERK wt and PERK C453S underwent autophosphorylation and activation, C453S mutation and PDIA6 expression both significantly weakened PERK activation (figure 4I, online supplemental figure S15C,D). Consistently, in vitro kinase assay indicated that PERK C453S showed diminished kinase function compared with PERK wt (online supplemental figure S15E). These results indicated that an intermolecular disulphide bond between Cys453 is targeted by PDIA6 and critical for PERK dimerisation and activation. C453S mutant appears to attenuate PERK activation and function. However, the structural or biochemical validation of the PERK C453S mutant is still lacking and awaits future investigation.

To verify whether PDIA6 controls ICD by modulating PERK, PERK inhibitor GSK2606414 was used to treat PDIA6 knockdown and control cells. Results showed GSK2606414 pretreatment partly reversed the cell death (online supplemental figure S16A,B), CRT exposure (online supplemental figure S16C) and ATP release (online supplemental figure S16D) induced by OXP treatment. Collectively, our results suggested that PDIA6 interacts with PERK and may cleave intermolecular disulphide bond between Cys453. This could lead to reduced PERK dimerisation and activation (figure 4J), thereby attenuating ICD.

KRASG12D upregulated PDIA6 via YY1-mediated transcriptional activation in PDAC

Using the Chip-Atlas, ENCODE, GTRD, hTFtarget and the TCGA-PAAD datasets, YY1 was identified as the most potent transcription factor regulating PDIA6 expression (figure 5A and online supplemental table S11). KRASG12D is the most prevalent oncogenic mutation in PDAC, and YY1 has been reported to be the downstream target of KRAS.19 Thus, we analysed the relationship among KRASG12D, YY1 and PDIA6. The results showed high correlations of expression between each pair of KRAS, YY1 and PDIA6 in patients with KRASG12D in the PAAD-CPTAC dataset (figure 5B and online supplemental table S12). In 14 patients who underwent microarray-based mutation screening in the Tangdu PDAC-ICB cohort, KRASG12D expression was positively correlated with PDIA6 expression (figure 5C). In addition, the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 was adopted to inhibit KRASG12D function and explore the relationships among these three proteins. The results showed that MRTX1133 reduced KRAS downstream targets activation and downregulated the mRNA and protein levels of YY1 and PDIA6 in a dose-dependent manner (figures 5D,E and online supplemental figure S17A-J). We also examined the effect of KRASG12V and KRASG12R on YY1 and PDIA6 expression. The results showed that knockdown of KRASG12V and KRASG12R decreased the mRNA and protein expression levels of YY1 and PDIA6 (online supplemental figure S17K–R). KRAS has been implicated in regulating ER sensors. This study showed that KRAS knockdown decreased IRE1α protein levels but had a limited effect on PERK and ATF6 (online supplemental figure S17S,T).

Next, we determined whether YY1 directly regulates PDIA6. YY1 knockdown decreased while its overexpression upregulated PDIA6 mRNA and protein expression in PDAC cells, respectively (figure 5F–L and online supplemental figure S18A). Six potential YY1 binding sites were identified in the promoter sequence of PDIA6 (online supplemental figure S18B). The luciferase reporter assay showed that promoter truncations drove luciferase expression, unless the PDIA6-5 binding site was deleted (figure 5M and online supplemental figure S18C–E). ChIP-qPCR verified YY1 binding to PDIA6-4 and PDIA6-5 sites (figure 5N and online supplemental figure S18F). Mutation of the PDIA6-5 site reduced luciferase activity compared with wild-type PDIA6-5 site, showing that YY1 primarily binds to the PDIA6-5 site to transactivate the PDIA6 promoter (figure 5O and online supplemental figure S18G). These findings established that KRASG12D induces YY1 expression, which promotes PDIA6 transcription in PDAC cells.

We further verified our findings in PDAC tissue microarray. We found that PDIA6 was positively correlated with YY1 and negatively correlated with p-PERK and CD8a (online supplemental figure S19A,B). PDIA6 was correlated with T stage (online supplemental table S13 for demographic data) and poor median OS time (8.0 vs 26.0 months; HR, 1.868; 95% CI 1.084 to 3.219; p=0.0157) in patients with PDAC (online supplemental figure S19C). PDIA6 indicated decreased CD8+ T-cell infiltration (online supplemental figure S19D,E). CellChat analysis showed that DCs and CD8+ T-cell interaction increased in the PDIA6-low group (online supplemental figure S19F–H).

Given PDIA6’s established role in proteostasis, we investigated whether the immune-regulatory role of PDIA6 is mechanistically distinct. The expression (online supplemental figure S20A) and the correlations with ICB response (online supplemental figure S20B) of proteostasis-related genes were further examined in PDAC and normal tissues. Results showed not all proteostasis-related genes regulated PDAC progression and ICB response, and the immune-regulatory function of PDIA6 was relatively specific.

Repressing PDIA6, combining OXP, synergised with PD-1 blocker in treating PDAC

We examined PDIA6 inhibition’s effect on ICB efficacy in PDAC (figure 6A). PDIA6 knockdown with OXP treatment reduced the tumour size and weight compared with OXP treatment alone. PDIA6 knockdown combined with OXP treatment was synergistic with PD-1 blocker in inhibiting tumour progression versus control IgG2a (figure 6B–D). Flow cytometry showed that PDIA6 knockdown with OXP and PD-1 blocker maximised the induction of CD8+ T-cell accumulation and activation compared with the other groups (figure 6E–H). Consistently, the maturation level of tumour-infiltrating cDC1 in the combination therapy group was the highest among all groups (figure 6E,I,J). CD8+ T-cells, cDC1, CD4+ T-cells, NK cells and B cells in the spleen remained consistent across all groups (figures 6K,L and online supplemental figure S21). In subcutaneous PDAC models, the combination therapy most potently suppressed tumour growth (online supplemental figure S22A–D), enhanced activation of tumour-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S22E–K), without significantly altering splenic cell subsets (online supplemental figure S23). This study indicates that with OXP, inhibiting PDIA6 synergises with PD-1 blocker in PDAC treating, which could be a promising strategy for PDAC.

Identification of a PDIA6 inhibitor that repressed tumour progression of KPC mice by synergising with OXP and PD-1 blocker

We identified small-molecular inhibitors for PDIA6. Using virtual screening and DARTS assay, PACMA31 showed potent inhibitory activity against PDIA6 but not PDIA1 and PDIA3 (online supplemental figure S24). The equilibrium association constant of PACMA31 and PDIA6 was 1.147E-6M (figure 7A and online supplemental figure S25A–C). PACMA31 repressed disulphide reductase activity of PDIA6 (figure 7B) and enhanced PERK-eIF2α pathway activation with or without TG treatment (figure 7C and online supplemental figure S25D). To verify PACMA31’s specificity for PDIA6, shNC and shPDIA6 KPC cells were used to develop the orthotopic models treated with PACMA31. Results showed that PACMA31 repressed shNC tumour growth, with tumour size similar to shPDIA6 tumours. In shPDIA6 tumours, PACMA31 had limited effect compared with solvent group, suggesting that PACMA31 partly repressed tumour growth by targeting PDIA6 in tumour cells (online supplemental figure S25E–G). Body weight, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen levels (online supplemental figure S26A–C), and major organ staining were comparable between mice receiving solvent and PACMA31 (online supplemental figure S26D), suggesting PACMA31’s safety.

Next, we examined the effect of PACMA31 on PD-1 blockade in orthotopic PDAC models (online supplemental figure S27A). PD-1 blocker monotherapy had little effect on tumour progression, while the combination with PACMA31 repressed tumour growth compared with PD-1 blocker or PACMA31 treatment only (online supplemental figure S27B–D). Discussion

Stromal components and immune cells contribute to shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment and erode the ICB therapy.20–22 Intervention strategies based on these findings effectively activate CD8+ T-cells and augment ICB efficacy in preclinical translational research. Although these microenvironmental features are hallmarks of PDAC, they are derived from intrinsic clues in tumour cells, with genetic and epigenetic alterations influencing their gene expression networks, which remain poorly understood. In this study, we systematically profiled cancer cell-intrinsic regulators related to poor CD8+ T-cell infiltration and poor prognosis, leading to the identification of PDIA6 as a candidate. PDIA6 has been implicated in modulating thiol-disulphide interchange reactions, suppressing ERS and autophagy and promoting tumour progression in an immune-dependent manner.23–27 In this study, PDIA6 was shown to correlate with advanced clinical stages, CD8+ T-cell deficiency and poor prognosis in patients with PDAC. PDIA6 inhibition effectively impeded tumour progression in the presence of CD8+ T-cell. These results demonstrated that PDIA6 is an authentic target for enhancing immune surveillance and augmenting ICB efficacy in PDAC.

PDIA6 catalyses protein folding and regulates thiol-disulphide interchange reactions, exhibiting dual chaperone and isomerase activity.23 28 Here, we establish PDIA6 as a disulphide isomerase specifically targeting PERK in PDAC cells - a previously unidentified function. PERK is one of the three critical sensors of the ER stress pathway. Previous studies demonstrate that both sustained over-activation and ablation of PERK disrupt homeostasis and induce ICD.12 29–31 Phosphorylated eIF2α, a downstream target of PERK, has been identified as a hallmark of ICD.32 Thus, maintaining a moderate PERK activation state is vital for cellular homeostasis and survival. HSPA5, which is a versatile chaperone for sensors of all three ER stress pathways, binds to and prevents PERK dimer formation and activation.33 In addition, studies have indicated that intermolecular disulphide bonds promote dimer formation and activation of ERS sensor proteins.17 18 34 Moreover, PDIA6 has also been validated to bind with PERK, although the binding site and function are not clear.17 The underlying mechanisms controlling PERK activation remain to be fully explored. Consistent with previous studies, we demonstrated that PDIA6 inhibition intensified ERS and activated the PERK-eIF2α signalling pathway in PDAC cells. We also found that PDIA6 directly bound to PERK. In addition, PDIA6 mainly interacted with cysteine 453 of PERK and cleaved the intermolecular disulphide bond, alleviating the PERK activation and preventing ICD. Collectively, our findings expand the understanding of PDIA6 function and reveal previously unrecognised regulatory layer controlling PERK activity and ICD induction in PDAC.

Hypoxia, oxidative stress, post-transcriptional factors and ER sensors, including IRE1 and ATF6, have been implicated in modulating PDIA6 expression, although the regulatory mechanism governing PDIA6 in PDAC remains poorly characterised.35–37 Our results demonstrated that PDIA6 was a downstream target of KRASG12D in PDAC. KRASG12D is the most prevalent KRAS mutation in PDAC, correlating with increased nodal disease, distant recurrence and poor OS.38–40 Emerging KRAS-targeting strategies, including vaccination, TCR, protein degrader and small-molecule inhibitors, have been proven to reprogram the tumour microenvironment, reverse ICB resistance and improve the prognosis in patients with PDAC.9 41–43 Nevertheless, limitations persist, particularly the rapid development of acquired resistance whose mechanisms require further elucidation.44 Notably, IRE1α reactivation restores proteostasis to confer KRAS inhibitor resistance, establishing IRE1α inhibition as a promising resistance-reversal strategy.45 Similarly, PDIA6 inhibition disrupts proteostasis of PDAC cells with KRAS mutation, which converts immunologically ‘cold’ tumours to ‘hot’ phenotypes and hinders the progression of PDAC. These results identify PDIA6 as a critical mediator of KRASG12D-driven immune evasion. Targeting PDIA6 represents a potential strategy to eliminate the immune-regulatory function of KRASG12D and reverse resistance to KRASG12D inhibitors.

Induction of ICD has been validated to reverse immunosuppressive microenvironment, and a variety of ICD inducers are used to elicit antitumour immunity and potentiate ICB efficacy.46–49 However, the intrinsic initiators of ICD resistance are largely unknown, substantially limiting the therapeutic potential of ICD inducers and ICB. Our study identifies PDIA6 as a critical regulator of ICD resistance in PDAC and demonstrates that PDIA6 inhibition synergises with OXP to robustly induce ICD. PDAC barely benefits from PD-1 blocker monotherapy in either animal models or clinical trials.50 51 Our study found that PACMA31, a small-molecule inhibitor of PDIA6, enhanced the efficacy of PD-1 blocker. A combination regimen containing a PDIA6 inhibitor, OXP and a PD-1 blocker represents a potential therapeutic strategy for PDAC treatment. In addition, PDIA6 predicted ICB response in PDAC cohorts, suggesting that PDIA6 could screen ICB responders in all patients with PDAC. Detecting PDIA6 expression can help develop personalised and precise ICB treatment for patients with PDAC. Collectively, we demonstrated a screening strategy for selecting patients with PDAC sensitive to ICB and discovered a novel combination regimen that could hinder immune evasion and inhibit PDAC progression, alleviating the dilemma of limited treatment options for patients with PDAC.

In this study, GM-CSF-treated bone marrow cells are a heterogeneous mixture of macrophage-like and DCs, which do not represent bona fide cDCs.52 Purified cDCs are required to precisely evaluate the effect of shPDIA6 tumour cells on DC function and subsequent CD8+ T-cell activation. Modulated by NK cells, cDC1 represents one of the most critical DC subsets for cross-presenting tumour antigens and initiating CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumour immune responses.53 Therefore, we mainly examined the cDC1 subset in tumour and spleens. Future investigations delineating how ICD affects other DC subsets would provide critical insights into how ICD activates antitumour immunity. Additionally, the evidence for the PDIA6-PERK interaction remains preliminary. And definitive structural or biochemical validation of the PERK C453S mutant is not yet available and requires future exploration.

Here, we established that PDIA6 is a critical downstream effector of KRASG12D that cleaves intermolecular disulphide bonds and attenuates PERK activation, repressing ICD and promoting immune evasion. PDIA6 is identified as both a predictive biomarker and therapeutic target for ICB response in patients with PDAC, and a PDIA6 inhibitor may serve as an initial step for novel PDAC therapy.
