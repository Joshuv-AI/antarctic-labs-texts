Factors associated with suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students: Dynamics of mental health vulnerabilities and protective resources
Authors: Shin Ling Wu, Pei Nee Mah, Soon Aun Tan, Jia Yuin Fam, Chin Choo Yap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicidal ideation is a major public health concern among university students, especially in recent years. Guided by Beck’s cognitive model of suicidal behaviour and the dual-factor model of mental health, this study examined the association between mental health vulnerabilities (i.e., depression, anxiety, stress and pessimism), protective resources (i.e., social support and optimism) and suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students.MethodsData were collected in 2023 using a cross-sectional survey design. A total of 547 university students aged 18 to 24 years old (M = 21.23, SD = 1.82) completed the Suicidal Ideation Attributes Scale (SIDAS), Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21, Revised Life Orientation Test, and Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine relationships among the study variables, while binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with the presence of suicidal ideation. The SIDAS score was dichotomised as 0= no suicidal ideation and > 0 as the presence of any suicidal ideation.ResultsNearly half of the participants reported some degree of suicidal ideation. Correlation analyses showed that depression, anxiety, stress, and pessimism were positively correlated with suicidal ideation, while optimism and social support were negatively correlated with suicidal ideation. In the logistic regression model, pessimism, depression, anxiety and social support remained significant factors associated with suicidal ideation.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students is associated with both psychological vulnerabilities and interpersonal resources. Interventions should not focus solely on reducing psychological distress but should also address pessimistic thinking patterns and strengthen perceived social support. Given the cross-sectional design and convenience sampling approach, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships.

Introduction

Suicide represents a critical public health concern as it is the third leading cause of death among teenagers and young adults globally [106]. More than 720,000 people die by suicide every year, with 73% of these deaths occurring in low and middle-income countries [106]. In Malaysia, the situation is equally concerning, with suicide rate ranking second highest among Muslim countries in the Middle East and fifth among ASEAN countries [63]. Despite the challenge of underreporting due to cultural taboos [11, 107], Malaysia experienced an alarming 78% increase in suicide rates from 2019 to 2023 [62]. These trends indicate the need for greater empirical attention to populations that may be especially vulnerable to suicidal thoughts and behaviours.

University students represent one such vulnerable group due to the developmental demands of emerging adulthood, which often align with heightened psychological vulnerability. In Malaysia, empirical evidence has shown that 47.4% of university students reported experiencing suicidal thoughts [72]. This vulnerability may be partly explained by the developmental and contextual demands faced during emerging adulthood. University students are navigating the transition from adolescence to adulthood while assuming new academic, social, and personal responsibilities [79, 91]. At the same time, they may encounter academic pressure, family difficulties, peer pressure, interpersonal challenges, financial constraints, and bullying experiences [20, 59]. Although they are increasingly expected to demonstrate mature decision-making and problem-solving abilities, their still-developing neurological and socioemotional systems may restrict emotion regulation compared with older adults [23, 41]. Consequently, under intense distress, some students may become more vulnerable to impulsive or risky behaviours, including suicide attempts, due to an underdeveloped cognitive control system [41, 97, 101]. Suicidal ideation, defined as thoughts and planning related to suicide, is therefore important to examine because it may serve as an early indicator of later suicidal behaviour [15, 100]. It also warrants attention because it may involve not only contemplation of suicide attempts but also deliberate self-harm [72].

To provide a comprehensive explanation of suicidal ideation, the present study integrates Beck’s cognitive model of suicidal behaviour with the dual-factor model of mental health. Beck’s cognitive model proposes that suicidal ideation may emerge when dispositional vulnerabilities, such as depression and anxiety, interact with maladaptive cognitive processes, such as negative expectations about the future [103]. The continued relevance of this cognitive perspective is reflected in recent research showing that suicide-specific rumination, attentional fixation, thought suppression, and maladaptive metacognitions are associated with suicidal ideation and suicide attempts [31]. From this perspective, pessimism may heighten suicidal vulnerability by shaping how students interpret stressors, future possibilities, and their perceived capacity to cope. Life stressors may further increase the likelihood of suicidal ideation when individuals simultaneously experience negative cognitive processes and psychological vulnerabilities [44, 73]. For example, university students who experience pessimistic thinking alongside symptoms of depression and anxiety may become more susceptible to thoughts of death when confronted with multiple life stressors [41, 65]. Beck’s model, therefore, provides a basis for examining depression, anxiety, stress, and pessimism as vulnerability factors associated with suicidal ideation.

However, focusing only on psychological distress may provide an incomplete understanding of students’ mental health. The dual-factor model of mental health proposes that mental health involves both the presence or absence of psychopathology and the presence or absence of positive psychological and social resources [37, 98]. In this model, good mental health is defined not merely by low levels of depression or anxiety but also by positive indicators such as positive emotions and social well-being [69, 98]. This framework is especially relevant to suicidal ideation because students with similar levels of distress may differ in their cognitive resources and perceived social support. Thus, the present study adopts an integrated risk-and-protection framework by examining not only depression, anxiety, stress, and pessimism as potential risk factors, but also optimism and social support as protective resources. Taken together, these trends underscore the importance of identifying both risk and protective factors associated with suicidal ideation among university students.

Mental health factors

Depression, anxiety, and stress are among the most common mental health difficulties experienced by university students [38]. Among Malaysian students, prevalence rates of depression, anxiety, and stress have been reported at 53.9%, 66.2%, and 44.6%, respectively [105]. Stress, as defined by Lazarus and Folkman [61], involves individuals’ perception of events, including appraisals of potential risk, harm and challenges, alongside evaluations of their capability to address these circumstances. This transactional and appraisal-based conceptualisation continues to be applied in contemporary university student research [42]. Meanwhile, depression is described as the constant feeling of sadness and inability to experience joy for at least two weeks [5], while anxiety involves persistent worry and tension for at least six months. Although these constructs are conceptually distinct, they are often interrelated and may collectively impair students’ academic, social, and emotional functioning.

For university students, stress may arise when academic demands, developmental challenges, and societal pressures accumulate beyond their perceived coping capacity [20, 59]. Such stressors may contribute to frustration and irritation [72], which may increase the likelihood of adopting maladaptive coping strategies (i.e., substance use, avoidance coping style, [102]. When stress becomes prolonged or overwhelming, suicidal thoughts may emerge as a perceived escape from distressing circumstances [13]. Students who are unable to cope effectively with repeated stressors may also experience depressive and anxious symptoms [45].

Depression may be especially relevant to suicidal ideation because it is commonly associated with negative automatic thoughts, helplessness, and impaired daily functioning [45, 107]. When students experience persistent sadness and reduced ability to experience pleasure, they may become more likely to interpret their circumstances as unchangeable or intolerable. This may increase vulnerability to suicidal thoughts, particularly when depressive symptoms are accompanied by academic or interpersonal difficulties. Consistent with this, Ladi-Akinyemi et al. [59] found that 53.8% of university students with depression reported suicidal thoughts, highlighting the association between depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation.

Anxiety may also contribute to suicidal ideation through persistent worry [36]. University students may worry about others’ perceptions, future uncertainty, regret over decisions, and possible disappointment from people around them [36, 77]. These concerns may be particularly salient among graduating students who are expected to make important decisions about their future career pathways [77]. Social sensitivity may further increase students’ tendency to imagine negative consequences of their decisions, thereby increasing vulnerability to suicidal ideation [36]. Taken together, depression, anxiety, and stress are examined in the present study as mental health risk factors associated with suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students. Although depression, anxiety, and stress are important indicators of psychological distress, emotional difficulties alone may not fully explain why some students develop suicidal thoughts while others facing similar challenges do not. Cognitive orientations, particularly individuals' expectations about future outcomes, may influence how stressors are interpreted and managed. Therefore, examining optimism and pessimism may provide additional insight into the cognitive processes associated with suicidal ideation among university students.

Pessimism and optimism

Pessimism and optimism are opposite but independent factors [40]. Individuals can be partially optimistic and pessimistic simultaneously [22, 40]. Individuals with greater optimism often expect positive outcomes, while individuals with greater pessimism often expect negative outcomes [40, 84]. Optimism helps individuals enhance their motivations and perceive challenges as opportunities for growth, especially under uncontrollable situations. Optimistic university students tend to accept challenges, strive to improve their conditions and persevere in goal achievement [52, 64, 87]. Optimism serves as a buffer for university students to restore their energy while facing obstacles [52, 64, 87]. This is associated with better psychological adjustment and lower risk of inducing suicidal ideation [21, 58]. Hence, optimism acts as a predictor of suicidal ideation by eliminating suicidal thoughts [58].

In contrast, pessimism may function as a cognitive vulnerability. Pessimistic students may view university life as torturous, monotonous, and unfulfilling, while overlooking its meaningful and positive aspects [52, 64]. When facing difficulties, they may be more likely to give up or adopt maladaptive coping mechanisms to escape from negative emotions [52, 64]. Miranda et al. [74] found that pessimistic university students developed negative cognitive thoughts that were strongly associated with depressive symptoms. Such negative thinking may contribute to hopelessness and subsequently increase vulnerability to suicidal ideation. Therefore, pessimism is examined as a potential risk factor for suicidal ideation because it may intensify negative automatic thinking and future-oriented vulnerability [80, 90]. While cognitive dispositions may influence how students perceive and respond to adversity, these internal processes do not function independently. Students' psychological well-being is also shaped by the quality of their interpersonal relationships and the support available within their social environment. Consequently, social support represents another important resource that may affect vulnerability to suicidal ideation.

Social support

According to the American Psychological Association, social support is described as the subjective availability of care and guidance from social relationships, including family, friends and significant others [6, 54]. It includes instrumental support (e.g., personal and medical care), emotional support (e.g., encouragement and listening support) and informational support (e.g., guidance and advice, [43, 54]). Although university students are developing their sense of independence, they still have a strong need for support and mutual understanding from their close ones [23].

Family and peer support may be especially relevant during university life. Family support has been found to have an important role in reducing suicidal ideation, as students may spend considerable time with their family members [51]. They prioritise help and advice from their family members, who are more emotionally connected and experienced, when facing obstacles [51]. Peer support is also important as friends are often undergoing similar phases of growth and may share comparable academic directions and stress experiences [1]. Through these relationships, students may receive emotional reassurance, practical assistance, and advice that help them cope with stressful situations.

Conversely, limited social support may increase vulnerability to suicidal ideation. University students with suicidal ideation often perceive their family members and friends as less engaged and affectionate [45, 50]. Social isolation and loneliness may exacerbate their mental health and reduce social connectedness [45], while reluctance to express feelings or discuss difficulties with others may lead to emotional suppression [45, 50]. Such suppression may further diminish mental health outcomes and increase psychopathological symptoms [17]. In contrast, students who receive more social support may feel loved, supported, and more capable of coping with stressful situations, thereby reducing the likelihood of suicidal ideation [25, 45]. Hence, social support is examined in this study as a protective factor associated with lower suicidal ideation [89].

Collectively, the literature indicates that suicidal ideation in university students is shaped by an intersection of psychological, cognitive, and social factors. Specifically, psychological distress and pessimism increase vulnerability by amplifying negative future expectations, whereas optimism and social support function as protective mechanisms that build resilience. Examining these contrasting variables together yields a more complete understanding of suicidal ideation than analysing risk or protection alone.

The current study

University students are an important population to examine because they represent critical human resources for future societal development [66] and are also vulnerable to multiple academic, interpersonal, developmental and psychological stressors [59]. In Malaysia, the continued increase in suicide rates [62], together with the high prevalence of suicidal thoughts among Malaysian university students [72], highlights the need for more focused research on factors associated with suicidal ideation in this population.

Previous studies among university students have examined psychological distress, cognitive vulnerabilities, and social support in relation to suicidal ideation, although these factors are often investigated separately or within different theoretical frameworks [45, 111]. Research applying the dual-factor model has also examined the coexistence of psychopathology, positive mental health, and suicidal ideation among university student [16]. Based on our review of the literature, we identified limited previous studies that simultaneously examined affective symptoms, future-oriented cognitive tendencies, and interpersonal resources within a single explanatory model of suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students. The present study therefore extends existing research by integrating Beck’s cognitive model of suicidal behaviour with the dual-factor model of mental health to examine depression, anxiety, stress, pessimism, optimism, and social support together among Malaysian university students.

Based on Beck’s cognitive model of suicidal behaviour, and Greenspoon’s and Sasklofske’s dual-factor model of mental health, this study investigates the associations of risk factors (depression, anxiety, stress, and pessimism) and protective resources (optimism and social support) with suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students (See Fig. 1). It is hypothesised that depression, anxiety, stress, and pessimism will be positively correlated with suicidal ideation, while optimism and social support will be negatively correlated with suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students. It is also hypothesised that depression, anxiety, stress, pessimism, optimism, and social support will collectively be associated with the odds of reporting suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students. Methods

Design and respondents

This cross-sectional study was conducted in 2023 among Malaysian university students aged 18 to 24 years. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling because the target population was geographically dispersed across universities in Malaysia, and the sensitive nature of the topic required voluntary participation. This approach enabled access to eligible students from multiple university contexts, although it may introduce selection bias and limit national representativeness. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 for binary logistic regression [29]. Assuming an odds ratio of 1.50, a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.90, the minimum required sample size was 409 participants. G*Power has been used in recent suicidal ideation studies involving binary logistic regression to estimate required sample size [24]. To account for a potential 20% attrition or incomplete response rate, the target sample size was increased to 500 participants.

A total of 550 respondents were recruited. The inclusion criteria were Malaysian university students aged 18 to 24 years and currently enrolled in any tertiary education in Malaysia. After removing the participants who did not fulfil the inclusion criteria, the remaining valid respondents were 547 (218 males, 329 females), with an average age of 21.23 years (SD = 1.82). Most of the respondents were of Chinese ethnicity and full-time students studying in Malaysia, with 48.8% of the participants experiencing some degree of suicidal ideation. Measures

Suicidal Ideation Attributes Scale (SIDAS; [96]

Several measurements have been developed to assess suicidal ideation, such as Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSI; [14]), Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS, [35]) and Suicidal Ideation Attributes Scale (SIDAS, [96]). The SIDAS was selected for the present study to assess participants’ suicidal ideation in this study due to a) being suitable for an online survey because of fewer items, b) being more applicable in an Asian setting, and c) being validated in non-clinical populations such as college students. The SIDAS comprises only five items using a 10-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 to 10 [96]. Each item represents the attribute of suicidal thoughts, including frequency, controllability, closeness to attempt, level of distress associated with the thoughts and impact on daily functioning. The total score of SIDAS is calculated by reversing the score of Item 2 (Controllability) and summing it with the remaining four items. A higher total score indicates more severe suicidal thoughts. If the participants score zero (never) for the first item (i.e., In the past month, how often have you had thoughts about suicide?), a total score of zero is awarded.

The SIDAS showed good validity and internal consistency in the original study (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91; [96]). Its continued relevance is supported by recent studies that have used the SIDAS and reported good reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.80 to 0.83 [33, 94]. In addition, Nazari et al. [76] validated the SIDAS in an Asian college student sample and reported good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80). Although both BSI and C-SSRS are well-established measures of suicidal ideation [14, 35], the BSI has been described as particularly relevant for clinical populations at risk of suicide [7, 19]. Meanwhile, the C-SSRS was originally developed as an interviewer-administered tool and is commonly used with trained assessors [7, 28]. Given the online self-report design of the present study and the non-clinical Malaysian university student sample, the SIDAS was considered appropriate for assessing suicidal ideation. In the present study, the SIDAS had a good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.74.

Depression Anxiety Stress Scale −21 (DASS-21; [68]

DASS-21 was selected to measure participants’ depression, anxiety and stress levels in this study. The DASS-21 is a well-known and validated measure that assesses all three factors simultaneously. It comprises 21 items using a four-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (did not apply to me at all) to 3 (applied to me very much, or most of the time). This scale comprises three subscales (i.e., depression, anxiety and stress) with seven items for each subscale. The total score of each subscale was calculated by summing the scores of each item in the subscale and multiplying by two. A higher total score indicated a greater depression, anxiety or stress level.

The DASS-21 was considered suitable for this study because it provides an efficient assessment of depression, anxiety, and stress within a single instrument, rather than requiring separate measures such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; [56]), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7, [92]) and Perceived Stress Scale (PSS, [26]). Recent research has continued to support the use of brief validated screening tools for psychological distress, including the DASS-21, in survey-based research [70]. The DASS-21 showed good validity and internal consistency for depression (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.94), anxiety (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87) and stress subscale (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91, [8]). In Malaysia, Thiyagarajan et al. [95] examined the psychometric properties of the DASS-21 among university students and reported excellent reliability for each subscale: depression (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92), anxiety (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87), and stress (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89). Therefore, the DASS-21 is appropriate for assessing depression, anxiety and stress levels in the present sample. In this research, the scale had a good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.91 for depression, 0.89 for anxiety, and 0.82 for the stress subscale.

Revised Life Orientation Test (LOT-R; [86]

Two validated measures related to trait optimism are the Revised Life Orientation Test (LOT-R; [86]) and the Life Engagement Test (LET, [88]). The Revised Life Orientation Test was selected because it directly assesses dispositional optimism and pessimism, whereas LET assesses purpose in life and life engagement more broadly. The LOT-R has also been used in the Malaysian setting [104]. It comprises 10 items using a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). After removing the four filler items, the remaining six items were included in this study. All items were summed up to obtain a total score, where a higher score indicates greater optimism.

The LOT-R has demonstrated validity and internal consistency in earlier research [85], and its continued relevance is supported by recent psychometric evaluation [40]. In this study, the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) displayed a two-factor structure for LOT-R to fit the data well by separating them into optimism and pessimism factors [108]. Past studies (e.g., [22, 40, 47]) supported that optimism and pessimism are bidimensional, where individuals can possess both at the same time. Hence, a two-factor structure (Item 1, 3 and 6 under the optimism factor, Item 2, 4 and 5 under the pessimism factor) is applied in this study. The total score of each factor was calculated by summing up the items in the respective factors. A total higher score indicated greater optimism or pessimism. LOT-R scale had an acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.60 for optimism and 0.70 for pessimism factor.

Multidimensional Survey of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; [113]

Several measurements were designed to assess individuals’ social support level, including the Multidimensional Survey of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; [114]), Perceived Social Support Questionnaire (F-SozU, [55]) and Social Support Scale (SSS, [18], pp. 201–217), all of which have applied in recent studies [2, 39, 83]. To the best of our knowledge, published psychometric validation studies of the F-SozU and SSS among Malaysian university students are limited or unavailable. In contrast, Malaysian validation research on perceived social support has primarily focused on the MSPSS, which has demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability for assessing perceived social support among Malaysian university students [46]. The MSPSS was therefore selected for the present study.

The MSPSS comprises 12 items using a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree). This scale comprises three subscales (significant other, family, and friend) with four items for each subscale. The total score of the scale was calculated by summing up all the items and dividing by 12. A higher total score indicates greater social support. MSPSS showed good validity and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88; [114]. Hsu et al. [46] also reported excellent internal consistency across Malaysian student samples (Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.89 to 0.94). In this study, the scale had a good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.92.

Procedure

Ethical approval was obtained from Sunway University Institutional Research Board (Ethics approval code: SUREC 2023/013). Participants were recruited in 2023, both virtually through social media platforms and in person around universities in Malaysia. A brief overview of the study was provided before participation. Participants were then given an information sheet and informed consent form, which explained the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality of responses and their right to withdraw at any point in time without penalty. Once informed consent to participate and for publication was obtained, participants completed a demographic section containing questions about gender, age, nationality, race, and university category. The survey was followed by the Suicidal Ideation Attributes Scale, Depression Anxiety Stress Scale, Revised Life Orientation Test, and Multidimensional Survey of Perceived Social Support. Upon completion, participants were provided with a debriefing sheet and compensated with a RM15 (USD4) voucher. Given the sensitive nature of the study, the debriefing sheet included information on available mental health support services and encouraged participants experiencing distress or suicidal thoughts to seek help from mental health professionals or emergency support services. This procedure was implemented to minimise potential psychological risk and to ensure that participants had access to appropriate support after completing the survey.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to determine the relationships between depression, anxiety, stress, pessimism, optimism, social support and suicidal ideation. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to explore the odds ratios of the predictive factors contributing to suicidal ideation. For this analysis, the SIDAS total score was dichotomised, where participants who scored “zero” were classified as “having no suicidal ideation”, while participants who scored above zero were classified as “having the presence of any suicidal ideation”. This categorisation enabled the identification of factors associated with the occurrence of suicidal ideation and facilitated the estimation of interpretable odds ratios. Logistic regression was therefore considered appropriate because the outcome variable was binary. Although suicidal ideation exists on a continuum and dichotomisation may result in some loss of information, this approach was adopted to address the study's primary research question concerning the presence versus absence of suicidal ideation.

Multicollinearity among the predictors was examined using the variance inflation factor (VIF) values. As logistic regression does not directly provide collinearity diagnostics, a supplementary linear regression was conducted using the same set of predictors for diagnostic purposes. The VIF values ranged from 1.13 to 2.55, indicating that multicollinearity did not exceed conventional thresholds. Model performance was assessed using the likelihood-ratio chi-square test, Nagelkerke R-squared, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and classification accuracy. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were reported for all predictors.

Discussion

Framed within Beck’s cognitive model of suicidal behaviour and the dual-factor model of mental health, the observed pattern of findings clarifies how psychological vulnerabilities, cognitive dispositions, and protective resources jointly influence suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students. The correlation analysis showed that the risk factors (depression, anxiety, stress, and pessimism) were significantly positively correlated with suicidal ideation, while the protective factors (optimism and social support) were negatively correlated with suicidal ideation. In the regression model, pessimism, depression, anxiety and social support remained significant, whereas stress and optimism did not. appeared as significant factors of suicidal ideation. The findings, therefore, partially supported the study hypotheses and should be interpreted as cross-sectional associations rather than evidence of causal prediction. In addition, 48.8% of participants reported some degree of suicidal ideation, which exceeds the rates reported by Ghazali et al. [34] and Kee et al. [53] among Malaysian youth. The difference may be partly explained by differences in measurement tools, operational definitions and age groups. Nevertheless, this highlighted the severity of suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students.

The correlation analysis revealed significant positive relationships between depression, anxiety, stress, pessimism and suicidal ideation among university students. These factors constitute elements of psychopathology that negatively impact mental health [69, 98]. These findings are consistent with Beck’s cognitive model of suicidal behaviour, which proposes that the maladaptive cognitive process related to psychological disturbance are associated with suicidal ideation [103]. Negative cognitive appraisals of challenges, such as constant pessimistic thinking and worry, together with life stress, may exacerbate diminished well-being [90, 102] and intensify feelings of hopelessness [107]. Hence, university students who exhibited higher levels of depression, anxiety, stress and pessimism may be more vulnerable to suicidal ideation.

Pessimism emerged as a significant cognitive vulnerability associated with suicidal ideation, consistent with Shen et al. [90]. This finding is theoretically meaningful because suicidal ideation is often future-oriented. Pessimistic university students tend to anticipate negative outcomes and demonstrate reluctance to overcome obstacles [52, 64]. When confronting stressors, these students tend to employ maladaptive coping mechanisms (e.g., avoidance, reactive emotion-focused strategies) and experience profound powerlessness [90]. The resulting negative automatic thoughts would lead students to perceive their lives as worthless, subsequently increasing the likelihood of suicidal thoughts [21, 74]. This interpretation is consistent with Beck’s cognitive model of suicidal behaviour [103], which emphasises maladaptive cognitive processes in the emergence of suicidal thinking. Pessimism acts as a stable negative thinking pattern that believes bad things will happen and are unchangeable through personal efforts [99]. Therefore, pessimism is a cognitive risk factor of negative emotions, such as depression and anxiety, and suicidal ideation.

Depression was also significantly associated with suicidal ideation, aligning with previous studies [45, 59, 110]. This outcome is consistent with Zhou et al.’s [112] study, which similarly found that depression exerted a stronger effect than anxiety in predicting suicidal ideation among university students, as supported by De La Cruz-Valdiviano et al. [27]. Individuals experiencing depressive symptoms typically focus on negative emotions while disregarding positive aspects of their experiences and struggling with emotion regulation [102]. The constant experience of sadness and inability to enjoy their life heavily impair their daily functioning [45]. This will then potentially intensify their desire for death based on the belief that death holds more value than their current existence [32].

Among the risk factors examined, anxiety demonstrated the smallest yet significant association with suicidal ideation, which is consistent with past studies [20, 45, 77]. University students experience multiple stressors, including academic challenges, family problems and financial constraints [20]. Constant worry about others’ perceptions and fear of social rejection increase their vulnerability to suicidal ideation [36]. Additionally, university students may experience career anxiety [77] and fear of uncertainty regarding their future [4] as they navigate the transition to adulthood. Intolerance of uncertainty and challenges could trigger anxiety-related distress [4], which may increase suicidal risk as a form of escape mechanism.

Contrary to our expectations, stress was not significantly associated with suicidal ideation in the regression model, despite being significantly correlated with suicidal ideation, diverging from some previous findings [20, 45]. One possible explanation is the severity of the stress, as Alfakeh et al. [3] found that only severe stress levels were related to suicidal ideation, while normal to moderate stress levels did not demonstrate significant associations. In addition, the non-significant regression finding may reflect the shared variance among depression, anxiety, and stress, which are conceptually and empirically related. Stress may contribute to suicidal ideation indirectly through depressive symptoms, anxiety, or pessimistic appraisal, rather than exerting a strong direct association after these variables are considered. According to Lovibond and Lovibond’s [68] DASS-21 scoring interpretation, participants in our study displayed moderate stress levels on average, which may be insufficient to explain suicidal ideation uniquely when included in the regression model with other risk factors. Similarly, Ashari et al. [9] and Ibrahim et al. [49] found that stress predicts depression and anxiety directly, but exhibits a limited direct effect on suicidal ideation, suggesting that other factors could mediate the relationship between stress and suicidal ideation. This pattern corresponds with Beck’s cognitive model [103], which posits that life stressors may increase the likelihood of engaging in suicidal ideation particularly when accompanied by negative cognitive processes and dispositional vulnerabilities [44, 73].

Our study identified significant negative relationships between the protective factors (social support and optimism) and suicidal ideation. University students with greater social support demonstrate greater willingness to share difficulties and express opinions with close contacts (e.g., family members, friends and trusted individuals; [51]. This openness prevents them from suppressing their emotions [67], which mitigates suicidal ideation. Similarly, optimistic thinking enables university students to maintain motivation when confronting challenges and anticipate positive outcomes [52, 64, 87]. Therefore, optimistic students are more capable of managing stressful situations and are less likely to experience hopelessness [58].

Social support demonstrated a substantial protective association with suicidal ideation among university students, aligning with previous research [23, 45, 50, 89]. In the Malaysian university context, social support may be especially important because students often negotiate academic demands alongside family expectations, financial concerns, and help-seeking stigma. Malaysian university students also experience multiple stressors [20], thus, social support from family, friends and romantic partners is important to facilitate adaptation to stressful circumstances [10] and promote proactive responses to challenges [23]. With help and guidance, their confidence in handling difficulties could be enhanced [45, 50], which would protect them against suicidal ideation [45]. In contrast, university students with limited social support typically experience greater social isolation and reduced social connections [45]. They displayed a lower sense of belongingness within their social networks and society [75], which may potentially increase vulnerability to suicidal ideation.

Although optimism significantly correlated with suicidal ideation, it did not remain significant in the regression model. This finding contrasts with some previous research [58, 74] yet aligns with others suggesting that optimism may exert a more direct effect on depression and anxiety, while having limited predictive power on suicidal ideation [82]. Lai et al. [60] and Marshall et al. [71] explained that optimism is more effective in enhancing mental health among university students when psychological issues are minimal. However, its protective effects may diminish when students experience severe depression, anxiety or pessimistic thinking. Consistent with Greenspoon and Sasklofske’s [37] dual-factor of mental health, optimism alone may not be sufficient to maintain psychological well-being when psychopathology is high [69]. This condition is described as “externally maladjusted” in the model. Our correlation analysis revealed that optimism showed the weakest, although still significant, relationship with suicidal ideation among the variables examined. The relationship between optimism and suicidal ideation may be mediated by other variables such as depression, stressor quantity, or self-efficacy [48, 57, 60], potentially explaining why optimism was not significantly associated with suicidal ideation in our regression model.

Taken together, the findings provide partial support for Beck's cognitive model of suicidal behaviour [103] and the dual-factor model of mental health [37]. Consistent with Beck’s model, pessimism, depression, and anxiety were significantly associated with suicidal ideation, suggesting that suicidal thoughts among university students may be linked to both maladaptive cognitive processes and psychological vulnerabilities. In particular, pessimism may reflect a negative future-oriented cognitive style that intensifies the effects of depressive and anxious symptoms. At the same time, the findings are consistent with the dual-factor model, which emphasises that mental health involves both psychopathology and positive resources. Social support remained significant even after psychological symptoms and cognitive orientation were included in the model, suggesting that protective interpersonal resources may play an important role in reducing vulnerability to suicidal ideation. This interpretation aligns with Magalhães’ [69] systematic review, which highlighted that well-being and psychopathology are independent but related dimensions within the dual-factor model of mental health. Therefore, reducing risk factors alone may be insufficient,suicide prevention efforts should also strengthen protective resources, particularly perceived social support.

The finding that nearly half of the participants reported some degree of suicidal ideation underscores the need for preventive action among Malaysian university students. However, this prevalence should be interpreted cautiously because the present study used a broad operational definition, where any SIDAS score above zero was classified as the presence of suicidal ideation. From a practical perspective, university students may benefit from programmes that strengthen proactive problem-solving skills, emotional regulation, and help-seeking skills, while educational institutions should provide accessible counselling services, regular mental health screening, and structured peer or social support programmes. Families may also play a role by providing consistent support to foster belonging and connection. Nevertheless, the model explained 36.4% of the variance in suicidal ideation, indicating that a substantial proportion of variance remains unexplained. Other relevant factors may include sociodemographic characteristics, personal and family health history, childhood experiences, lifestyle habits, trauma exposure, hopelessness, parent–child conflict, and other cognitive or psychological vulnerabilities. For example, Ghazali et al. [34] found that among Malaysian university students aged 18 to 29 years, females and individuals with lifetime trauma and depressive symptoms were more vulnerable to suicidal ideation. They also noted that childhood bullying and neglect may contribute to maladaptive thought suppression as a form of emotional protection. Similarly, Yosep et al. [109] identified parent–child conflict, hopelessness, perceived interpersonal burden, substance use, low socioeconomic status and educational level as important risk factors of suicidal ideation. These findings are consistent with Baldini et al. [12], who suggested that both personal and environmental factors contribute to the latent profiles of suicide risk among university students.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents conclusions about causal relationships among depression, anxiety, stress, pessimism, optimism, social support and suicidal ideation [30]. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence that these factors directly cause suicidal ideation. Although the model explained 36.4% of the variance in suicidal ideation, suicide is a complex and multifactorial phenomenon, and a substantial proportion of variance remained unexplained. Future research should explore other potential variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the risk and protective factors of suicidal ideation [12, 109]. Second, the study relied on self-reported measures in assessing participants’ suicidal ideation, which may have introduced potential biases such as social-desirable bias, recall bias, and respondent bias [93]. These factors could affect the reliability and validity of the findings and should be considered when interpreting the results. Future studies would benefit from multiple assessment methods, including semi-structured interviews, clinician-administered assessments, or mixed-method approaches, to enhance validity and minimize bias. Third, the LOT-R optimism subscale displayed a borderline internal consistency (Cronbach’s a = 0.60), which may have attenuated its association with suicidal ideation and may partly explain why optimism did not emerge as a significant factor in the regression model [78]. Therefore, the relationship between optimism and suicidal ideation warrants further investigation, particularly using measures with stronger psychometric properties or alternative approaches to assessing dispositional optimism. Future research may benefit from further validating the LOT-R in similar populations or employing alternative measures with stronger psychometric properties. Fourth, the dichotomisation of SIDAS scores for logistic regression analysis facilitated the identification of factors associated with the presence of suicidal ideation and enabled straightforward interpretation of odds ratios. However, this approach may have reduced statistical power and obscured differences in ideation severity [81]. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting factors associated with the presence rather than the severity of suicidal ideation. Future studies may analyse SIDAS scores as a continuous outcome to capture the full spectrum of suicidal ideation and assess the robustness of these findings.

Conclusion

The findings partially supported the study hypotheses. Depression, anxiety, stress, and pessimism were positively correlated with suicidal ideation, while optimism and social support were negatively correlated with suicidal ideation. However, in the logistic regression model, only pessimism, depression, anxiety, and social support remained significant factors associated with suicidal ideation. These findings suggest that suicidal ideation among Malaysian university students is associated with psychological distress, pessimistic thinking, and perceived social support. Interventions should therefore prioritise strengthening social support networks and addressing pessimistic thought patterns, reducing depressive and anxiety symptoms, rather than solely targeting stress reduction or optimism enhancement. Despite its limitations, this study emphasises the need for universities to implement targeted mental health interventions that enhance social support and address underlying psychological factors, thereby fostering a supportive and resilient environment for students’ well-being. Ultimately, the findings highlight that suicidal ideation among university students is not solely a consequence of emotional distress but is also shaped by how students view their future and the support they perceive from others. Strengthening social connectedness while addressing pessimistic thinking and psychological distress may therefore represent a key strategy for reducing suicide risk and promoting student well-being within Malaysian higher education institutions.
