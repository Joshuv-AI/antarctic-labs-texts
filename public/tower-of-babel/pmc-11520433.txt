Cardiac corin and atrial natriuretic peptide regulate liver glycogen metabolism and glucose homeostasis
Authors: Wenguo Li, Xianrui Zhang, Zibin Zhou, Wenjun Guo, Mengting Wang, Tiantian Zhou, Meng Liu, Qingyu Wu, Ningzheng Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular function and metabolic homeostasis are closely linked, but the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. Corin is a protease that activates atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), an essential hormone for normal blood pressure and cardiac function. The goal of this study is to investigate a potential corin and ANP function in regulating liver glycogen metabolism and glucose homeostasis.MethodsLiver glycogen and blood glucose levels were analyzed in Corin or Nppa (encoding ANP) knockout (KO) mice. ANP signaling was examined in livers from Corin and Nppa KO mice and in cultured human and mouse hepatocytes by western blotting.ResultsWe found that Corin and Nppa KO mice had reduced liver glycogen contents and increased blood glucose levels. By analyzing conditional KO mice lacking either cardiac or renal Corin, we showed that cardiac corin and ANP act in an endocrine manner to enhance cGMP-protein kinase G (PKG)-AKT-GSK3 signaling in hepatocytes. In cultured hepatocytes, ANP treatment stimulated PKG signaling, glucose uptake, and glycogen production, which could be blocked by small molecule PKG and AKT inhibitors.ConclusionsOur results indicate that corin and ANP are important regulators in liver glycogen metabolism and glucose homeostasis, suggesting that defects in the corin and ANP pathway may contribute to both cardiovascular and metabolic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-024-02475-w.Keywords: ANP, Corin, Hepatocytes, Glucose homeostasis, Liver glycogen metabolism, PKG signaling

Introduction

Glucose homeostasis is essential for cardiovascular health. Individuals with impaired glucose metabolism, e.g., type 2 diabetes, have increased risks of cardiovascular diseases, such as hypertension and heart disease. Glucose metabolism is controlled by distinct mechanisms in diverse organs, including the pancreas, liver, kidney, skeletal muscle, and brain [1, 2]. Corin is a membrane-bound protease that activates atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), a cardiac hormone that controls sodium homeostasis, body fluid balance, and normal blood pressure [3, 4]. The ANP function is mediated by natriuretic peptide receptor-A (NPR-A) (also known as guanylyl cyclase-A, GC-A) to increase intracellular cGMP, leading to downstream events in target tissues, including salt and water excretion in the kidney and muscle relaxation in blood vessels [5]. In the heart, ANP modulates cell morphology and function, inflammation, and stress responses [6]. Genetic defects in CORIN and NPPA (encoding ANP precursor) may cause cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure [7–12].

In addition to their function in cardiovascular homeostasis, corin and ANP also play a key role in metabolism [13, 14]. In skeletal muscles and adipose tissue, for example, ANP promotes lipid hydrolysis and oxidation by enhancing phosphorylation and activity of the hormone-sensitive lipase in a cGMP-protein kinase G (PKG)-dependent pathway [15–19]. In mice, disrupting the Nprc gene (encoding the natriuretic peptide clearance receptor) in adipose tissue enhances natriuretic peptide activity and lipid metabolism, preventing diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance [20]. Moreover, ANP increases adipose tissue browning and thermogenic activities via a PKG and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway [21, 22]. In mice, Corin or Nppa deficiency reduces brown adipose tissue and inhibits thermogenic gene expression [23, 24]. Under cold conditions, the body temperature in Corin and Nppa knockout (KO) mice dropped more rapidly than in wild-type (WT) mice, an indication of cold intolerance [23, 24]. These data indicate a critical role of corin and ANP in thermoregulation.

In mammals, increased blood glucose level upon cold exposure is an acute thermoregulatory response to provide more fuel to generate heat. Unlike in cold-exposed WT mice, blood glucose levels in cold-exposed Corin and Nppa KO mice did not increase, but instead decreased [23, 24], indicating that corin and ANP deficiency may impair glucose homeostasis. In line with the findings in mice, low plasma ANP levels are linked to type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome in humans [25, 26]. Conversely, high plasma ANP levels are associated with metabolic protection against obesity and type 2 diabetes [27–29]. High levels of proteolytically cleaved corin fragments in circulation have also been reported in individuals with hyperglycemia and obesity [30, 31]. In rats, reduced corin and ANP expression in the heart contributes to diabetic cardiomyopathy [32]. These data indicate a role of corin and ANP in regulating glucose homeostasis. To date, however, the mechanism underlying such a potential corin and ANP function has not yet been elucidated.

In this work, we conducted studies in Corin and Nppa KO mice to understand if and how corin and ANP regulate glucose homeostasis. Moreover, we analyzed conditional KO mice, lacking either cardiac or renal corin, to determine if the corin and ANP function in glucose metabolism is mediated via a cardiac endocrine mechanism. Finally, we did molecular and biochemical studies in cultured liver cells to verify our findings in mice and to understand the corin and ANP-mediated signaling pathway. Our findings provide important new insights into the function of corin and ANP in the regulation of glucose homeostasis and liver metabolism.

Results

Corin KO mice have increased blood glucose levels

To examine a potential role of corin in regulating glucose homeostasis, we measured blood glucose in Corin KO mice housed in regular room temperature. Compared to those in fed WT mice, blood glucose levels were higher in fed Corin KO mice (8.94 ± 0.91 vs. 10.10 ± 0.93 mmol/L, p = 0.001) (n = 16) (Fig. 1A). Similarly increased blood glucose levels were found in fasted Corin KO mice (9.45 ± 1.97 vs. 7.08 ± 0.75 mmol/L in fasted WT, p = 0.003) (n = 9–11) (Fig. 1B). In glucose tolerance test, blood glucose levels increased in both WT and Corin KO mice after glucose injection, which peaked after 30 min and gradually returned toward the pre-injection levels after 2 h. Throughout the test, blood glucose levels in Corin KO mice were higher than those in WT mice. Values of area under the curve (AUC) were higher in Corin KO mice than in WT mice (Fig. 1C). In insulin tolerance test, blood glucose levels decreased in both WT and Corin KO mice after insulin injection, which slowly returned toward the pre-injection levels after 2 h (Fig. 1D). Like in the glucose tolerance test, blood glucose levels remained higher in Corin KO mice than in WT mice throughout the insulin tolerance test. AUC values were also higher in Corin KO mice than in WT mice (Fig. 1D). In ELISA, plasma insulin (Fig. 1E) and glucagon (Fig. 1F) levels were comparable between WT and Corin KO mice. These results showed that Corin KO mice had increased blood glucose levels, which was unlikely caused by altered plasma insulin and glucagon levels.

Corin KO mice have decreased liver glycogen levels

The liver plays a key role in glucose metabolism [33–35]. We measured liver glycogen in Corin KO mice. Compared to WT mice, Corin KO mice had markedly reduced liver glycogen levels (97.71 ± 26.40 vs. 17.64 ± 8.19 μg/mg liver, p < 0.001) (n = 13) (Fig. 1G). By contrast, glycogen levels in skeletal muscles were similar between WT and Corin KO mice (0.24 ± 0.13 vs. 0.26 ± 0.18 μg/mg muscle, p = 0.741) (n = 12) (Fig. 1H). Additionally, WT and Corin KO mice had similar liver weights, liver to body weight ratios, and liver insulin sensitivity (Fig. S1A-C). Liver cholesterol and triglyceride levels in Corin KO mice appeared higher than those in WT mice (Fig. S1D, E). These results indicated a corin function in liver glycogen metabolism.

Nppa KO mice have increased blood glucose and decreased liver glycogen levels

The corin function in cardiovascular biology is mediated via ANP activation [3, 36]. If the corin function in regulating blood glucose and liver glycogen metabolism is also mediated by ANP activation, Corin and Nppa deficiency should have similar effects on blood glucose and liver glycogen levels. We created and analyzed Nppa KO mice (Fig. S2A, B). Compared to WT mice, Nppa KO mice had similar body weights, but increased blood pressure, total body fat mass, and fat/body weight ratio (Fig. S2C-F). Like in Corin KO mice, increased blood glucose levels were observed in fed and fasted Nppa KO mice (9.95 ± 0.98 vs. 8.13 ± 0.78 mmol/L in fed WT, p < 0.001) (n = 12–13) (Fig. 2A) and (10.37 ± 0.97 vs. 8.38 ± 0.50 mmol/L in fasted WT, p = 0.003) (n = 8-9) (Fig. 2B). In the glucose and insulin tolerance tests, blood glucose levels changed parallelly in Nppa KO and WT mice after glucose (Fig. 2C) and insulin (Fig. 2D) injections. AUC values were higher in Nppa KO mice than in WT mice in both the tests (Fig. 2C, D). Compared to WT mice, Nppa KO mice had similar plasma insulin (Fig. 2E) and glucagon (Fig. 2F) levels. Moreover, liver glycogen levels were markedly decreased in Nppa KO mice, compared to those in WT mice (30.16 ± 8.15 vs. 87.91 ± 13.98 μg/mg liver, p < 0.001) (n = 8) (Fig. 2G). By contrast, glycogen levels in skeletal muscle were similar between Nppa KO and WT mice (0.22 ± 0.05 vs. 0.23 ± 0.10 μg/mg muscle, p = 0.840) (n = 6-7) (Fig. 2H). When the mice were challenged with cold exposure at 4 °C, levels of blood glucose and liver glycogen in Corin and Nppa KO mice decreased further, compared with those in similarly treated WT mice (Fig. S3A, B). These data were consistent with impaired glucose and liver glycogen metabolism in Corin and Nppa KO mice. The similar findings in Corin and Nppa KO mice indicated that the corin function was likely mediated via ANP activation.

Npra, but not Corin and Nppa, is expressed in the liver

Corin and ANP are highly expressed in the heart. Corin-mediated ANP activation also occurs in non-cardiac tissues, including the kidney and skin eccrine glands, to regulate sodium homeostasis in an autocrine manner [37, 38]. To understand if corin and ANP regulate liver glycogen metabolism via an autocrine mechanism, we examined Corin, Nppa, and Npra (encoding natriuretic peptide receptor a) in the mouse liver. In RT-PCR, cardiac Corin mRNA expression was detected in WT and Nppa KO mice, but not in Corin KO mice (Fig. 3A), whereas cardiac Nppa mRNA expression was detected in WT and Corin KO mice, but not in Nppa KO mice (Fig. 3A). By contrast, no Corin or Nppa mRNA expression was detected in livers from WT, Corin KO, and Nppa KO mice, whereas Npra mRNA expression was detected in hearts and livers from WT, Corin KO, and Nppa KO mice (Fig. 3A). These results showed that corin and ANP were not expressed in the liver, suggesting that the corin and ANP function in liver glycogen metabolism may be mediated in an endocrine manner. We also compared Npra mRNA expression levels in the liver and skeletal muscles from WT and Corin KO mice. In RT-PCR and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), we found that Npra mRNA expression levels the skeletal muscles were much lower than those in the liver (Fig. S4A, B). There were no apparent differences between WT and Corin KO mice in Npra mRNA expression levels in the liver or skeletal muscles (Fig. S4B). These results indicated that corin-ANP-mediated function may not be a dominant regulator in the glycogen metabolism of skeletal muscles.

Increased blood glucose and decreased liver glycogen levels are detected in heart-, but not kidney-, conditional Corin KO mice

Both corin and ANP are expressed in the heart and kidney [39, 40]. To assess the potential role of cardiac and renal corin in regulating liver glycogen metabolism, we analyzed Corin heart-conditional KO (hcKO) and kidney-conditional KO (kcKO) mice, in which the Corin gene was selectively disrupted in the heart or kidney (Fig. S5A, B). Both Corin hcKO and kcKO mice had increased blood pressure, as reported previously [38]. As shown by ELISA, plasma mature ANP levels were reduced in Corin KO and hcKO mice, but not in Corin kcKO mice, compared to those in WT mice (Fig. 3B), indicating that circulating ANP was generated primarily by cardiac corin. Like in Corin KO mice, blood glucose levels were increased in Corin hcKO, but not in kcKO, mice (Fig. 3C). Urinary glucose levels appeared lower in Corin KO and hcKO mice than those in WT and Corin kcKO mice (Fig. S5C), suggesting a potential role of renal corin in regulating glucose reabsorption in the kidney. In the glucose and insulin tolerance tests, Corin KO and hcKO mice had similar responses to glucose and insulin challenges, and AUC values were higher than those in WT mice (Fig. S5D, E). Moreover, liver glycogen levels were decreased in Corin KO and hcKO, but not in Corin kcKO, mice (Fig. 3D), whereas muscle glycogen levels were similar among WT and Corin KO, hcKO, and kcKO mice (Fig. 3E). These results indicated that cardiac corin-activated ANP regulated liver glycogen metabolism in an endocrine manner.

Liver cGMP and PKG levels are reduced in Corin and Nppa KO mice

ANP binding to NPR-A stimulates intracellular cGMP production and downstream effectors, including PKG, AKT, and glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) [5]. Possibly, a cGMP-PKG-AKT-GSK3 signaling pathway may mediate the corin and ANP function in liver glycogen metabolism (Fig. 4A). In line with this hypothesis, ELISA measurements found reduced cGMP levels in liver homogenates from Corin (Fig. 4B) and Nppa (Fig. 4C) KO mice, compared to those in WT mice.

Increased cGMP levels have been reported to upregulate PKG expression [41]. In mammals, there are two PKG genes, i.e., PRKG1 and PRKG2, encoding PKGI and PKGII, respectively. Based on gene database analysis, mouse liver appeared to express mostly Prkg1 and little Prkg2, which was confirmed by RT-PCR (Fig. S6). In qRT-PCR, decreased Prkg1a and Prkg1b (splicing variants encoding Pkg1α and Pkg1β isoforms, respectively) mRNA expression was observed in livers from Corin (Fig. 4D) and Nppa (Fig. 4E) KO mice. Reduced PkgI protein levels were confirmed by western blotting in livers from Corin (Fig. 4F, G) and Nppa (Fig. 4H, I) KO mice, indicating that Corin and Nppa deficiency impaired the cGMP-PkgI pathway in the mouse liver.

Liver PkgI signaling is impaired in Corin and Nppa KO mice

AKT and GSK3 are PKG downstream effectors [42]. AKT-GSK3 signaling is critical in liver metabolism [43, 44]. We examined Akt and Gsk3 expression and phosphorylation in livers from Corin and Nppa KO mice. In western blotting, decreased Akt, Gsk3α, and Gsk3β phosphorylation, but not protein expression, was observed in Corin KO mice (Fig. 5A, B). Similarly reduced Akt, Gsk3α, and Gsk3β phosphorylation, but not protein expression, was observed in the liver from Nppa KO mice (Fig. 5C, D). These results indicated that Corin and Nppa deficiency impaired cGMP-PkgI-Akt-Gsk3 signaling in the mouse liver.

By qRT-PCR, we analyzed selected genes in liver glycogen metabolism, including glycogenolysis (glycogen phosphorylase L, Pygl; phosphoglucomutases, Pgm1 and Pgm2; glycogen debranching enzyme, Agl), glycogenesis (UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase 2, Ugp2; glycogen synthases, Gys1 and Gys2), glycolysis (glucokinase, Gck; glucose-6-phosphate isomerase1, Gpi1; phosphofructokinase, Pfkl; phosphoglycerate kinase 1, Pgk1), and glyconeogenesis (glucose 6-phosphatase catalytic subunit, G6pc; phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, Pepck; pyruvate carboxylase, Pcx). We found that WT, Corin KO, and Nppa KO mice had similar expression levels of Pygl, Pgm1, Pgm2, Agl, Ugp2, Gys1, Gys2, Gck, Gpi1, Pfkl, Pgk1 and G6pc genes (Fig. 6A, B). However, Pepck and Pcx expression levels were increased in Corin and Nppa KO mice, compared to those in WT mice (Fig. 6A, B), which may indicate enhanced gluconeogenesis.

ANP increases cGMP production, AKT and GSK3 phosphorylation, and glycogen levels in HepG2 cells

To verify the above findings in mice and to determine if ANP acts directly on hepatocytes, we conducted studies in cultured human hepatoma HepG2 cells. In RT-PCR, we detected Corin, NPPA, and NPRA mRNA expression in control HEK293 cells (Fig. 7A). In HepG2 cells, NPRA, but not CORIN or NPPA, mRNA expression was detected (Fig. 7A), consistent with the findings in the mouse liver (Fig. 3A). When HepG2 cells were treated with ANP, intracellular cGMP levels were increased (Fig. 7B). Similarly increased intracellular cGMP levels were observed in ANP-treated control baby hamster kidney (BHK) cells, as reported previously [45]. In western blotting, enhanced AKT, GSK3α, and GSK3β phosphorylation was detected in the ANP-treated HepG2 cells (Fig. 7C, D). Increased glucose uptake (Fig. 7E) and glycogen levels (Fig. 7F) were also observed in HepG2 treated with ANP or insulin (positive control). These results indicated that ANP acted directly on hepatocytes to stimulate cGMP signaling, thereby increasing glycogen levels in hepatocytes.

PKG and AKT inhibitors block the effects of ANP on HepG2 cells

To verify that the enhanced AKT and GSK3 phosphorylation in ANP-treated HepG2 cells was PKG-dependent, we tested effects of KT5823, a selective small molecule PKG inhibitor [46], in ANP-treated HepG2 cells. HepG2 cells were treated with ANP or insulin (control) in the absence or presence of KT5823. As shown in western blotting, the ANP-enhanced AKT, GSK3α, and GSK3β phosphorylation was inhibited by KT5823 (Fig. 8A, B). We next tested MK2206, an allosteric inhibitor that blocks AKT phosphorylation [47]. In western blotting, we found that the ANP-enhanced AKT, GSK3α, and GSK3β phosphorylation was inhibited in MK2206-treated HepG2 cells (Fig. 8C, D). Moreover, ANP-increased glycogen levels in HepG2 cells were inhibited upon KT5823 (Fig. 8E) or MK2206 (Fig. 8F) treatment. These results indicated that the ANP function in elevating glycogen levels in hepatocytes was likely mediated by the cGMP-PKG-AKT-GSK3 pathway.

ANP increases cGMP-Pkg signaling and glycogen levels in mouse AML12 hepatocytes

HepG2 cells were derived from human hepatoma. We verified our findings in mouse AML12 hepatocytes derived from a normal liver [48]. Like in WT mouse liver, Npra, but not Corin or Nppa, mRNA expression was detected in AML12 cells by RT-PCR (Fig. S7). When AML12 cells were treated with ANP, intracellular cGMP levels (Fig. S8A) and Akt, Gsk3α, and Gsk3β phosphorylation (Fig. S8B, C) were increased. The ANP-enhanced Akt, Gsk3α, and Gsk3β phosphorylation was blocked when AML12 cells were treated with KT5823 (Fig. S8D, E). Like in HepG2 cells, ANP and insulin (control) treatment increased glucose uptake (Fig. S8F) and glycogen levels (Fig. S8G) in AML12 cells. These results were consistent with the findings in human HepG2 cells, indicating a direct ANP function in hepatocytes to increase cGMP-PkgI-Akt-Gsk3 signaling and glycogen production.

Discussion

Corin and ANP are key components in the heart endocrine system that connects cardiovascular and metabolic homeostasis [13, 21]. In this study, we identified an important function of corin and ANP in regulating glucose homeostasis and liver glycogen metabolism. We show that Corin deficiency increased blood glucose concentrations and lowered hepatic glycogen levels in mice. A similar phenotype was found in Nppa KO mice, indicating that the corin function is mediated via ANP activation. These findings may provide a mechanistic explanation for the inverse relationship between circulating ANP levels and the risk of type 2 diabetes observed in human populations [25–29], highlighting the importance of the corin and ANP pathway in metabolic health. In line with our findings, a recombinant form of ANP was shown to lower blood pressure and glucose levels in patients with hypertension and metabolic syndrome [49].

Previously, increased plasma insulin levels were reported in healthy individuals receiving ANP infusion [18, 50]. In experiments with isolated rat or mouse pancreatic islets, ANP was shown to have an insulinotropic effect in the presence of glucose stimulation [51, 52]. An inhibitory effect of ANP on glucagon release from isolated rat pancreatic islets was also reported, when the experiment was done in the presence of 3 mM glucose and 1 nM ANP [53]. These data suggest a potential role of ANP in regulating insulin and glucagon release from the pancreas. In our study, however, we found similar plasma insulin and glucagon levels between WT and Corin or Nppa KO mice, indicating that corin and ANP deficiency does not impair insulin or glucagon secretion in mice. In the glucose and insulin tolerance tests, Corin and Nppa KO mice also responded to glucose or insulin challenge. It is unlikely, therefore, that the increased blood glucose levels in Corin and Nppa KO mice were due to an abnormal pancreatic function. In line with our findings, WT and GC-A (Npra) KO mice were reported to have similar plasma insulin levels and indistinguishable responses in glucose and insulin tolerance tests [52]. GC-A KO mice also had increased blood glucose levels under fasted conditions [52]. Under fed conditions, increased blood glucose levels were detected when GC-A KO mice reached 10 months of age [52]. Further studies are required to directly compare the phenotypes of Nppa and GC-A KO mice under similar experimental settings.

Hepatic glycogen metabolism is a major regulator in glucose homeostasis [33–35]. Consistently, we found markedly decreased liver glycogen levels in Corin and Nppa KO mice, an indication of reduced glycogenesis and/or increased glycogenolysis. The decreased Gsk3 phosphorylation observed in Corin and Nppa KO mouse livers was consistent with impaired glycogenesis. The finding of low hepatic glycogen levels in Corin and Nppa KO mice may explain the previous observations that blood glucose levels failed to increase when the mice were exposed to cold [23, 24], which was confirmed in this study. In a previous study, ANP was shown to stimulate hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in perfused livers and isolated hepatocytes in rats [54], which appeared inconsistent with our findings of low liver glycogen levels in Corin and Nppa KO mice. In cultured human HepG2 and mouse AML12 hepatic cells, we showed that ANP treatment increased GSK3 phosphorylation, glucose uptake, and glycogen levels, indicating enhanced glycogenesis but not glycogenolysis. We also detected upregulated Pepck and Pcx genes, encoding gluconeogenic phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase and pyruvate carboxylase, respectively, in Corin and Nppa KO mouse livers. Previously, Pepck overexpression in the liver increased glucose levels and reduced glycogen levels in hepatocytes, causing hyperglycemia in mice [55]. In humans, increased pyruvate carboxylase expression correlated with high plasma glucose levels [56]. Inhibition of pyruvate carboxylase expression in the liver decreased hepatic glucose production and plasma glucose levels in rats [56]. These results do support the idea that ANP increases hepatic glycogen synthesis and that dysregulated hepatic glycogen metabolism may contribute to hyperglycemia in Corin and Nppa KO mice. Additional studies are required to define the specific pathways by which ANP regulates liver glycogen metabolism and to understand if corin and ANP also modulate glycogen metabolism in other non-hepatic tissues.

Corin and ANP are expressed in the heart and non-cardiac tissues, including the kidney, skin eccrine glands, and uterus. In the kidney and skin eccrine glands, corin and ANP act in an autocrine manner to promote salt and water excretion [37, 38]. In the uterus, corin and ANP enhance spiral artery remodeling in a similar autocrine mechanism [57, 58]. As shown in this study, corin and ANP expression was undetectable in the liver, indicating that the corin and ANP function in liver glycogen metabolism is likely mediated by an endocrine mechanism. Corin is a transmembrane protease that activates ANP in tissues where it is expressed. This characteristic could help us to identify the tissue origin of circulating ANP and the associated endocrine function. By analyzing Corin KO, hcKO, and kcKO mice, we showed that cardiac, but not renal, corin was responsible for the ANP function in liver glycogen metabolism and glucose homeostasis. These results highlight distinct roles of corin in the heart and kidney in regulating physiological homeostasis. As reported previously [38], both Corin hcKO and kcKO mice were hypertensive. These findings also indicated that impaired glucose homeostasis and liver glycogen metabolism in Corin KO mice were unlikely due to increased blood pressure.

ANP activates NPR-A to increase intracellular cGMP, enhancing down-stream signaling [5]. PKGI, also known as cGMP-dependent protein kinase (cGKI), is a major effector in cGMP-mediated pathways [5]. Consistently, we found reduced cGMP levels and Pkg1 mRNA and protein expression in livers from Corin and Nppa KO mice. Reduced hepatic Akt, Gsk3α, and Gsk3β phosphorylation was also detected in the KO mice. These results indicate that the corin and ANP function in the liver is mediated, at least in part, via a cGMP-PkgI-Akt-Gsk3 pathway. Gsk3 is a major glycogen synthase regulator [59, 60]. Reduced Gsk3 phosphorylation is expected to reduce glycogen production. Together with the findings of increased Pepck and Pcx expression in Corin and Nppa KO mouse livers, these results indicate that corin and ANP deficiency may reduce glycogen synthesis and increase gluconeogenesis in the liver. Our studies in cultured hepatocytes with PKG and AKT inhibitors were consistent with an ANP-enhanced cGMP-PKGI-AKT-GSK3 pathway in stimulating glycogen synthesis. The direct ANP function in cultured hepatocytes also indicates that the phenotype of reduced liver glycogen levels in Corin and Nppa KO mice is unlikely due to secondary effects of corin and ANP deficiency, e.g., impaired adipose tissue function. Possibly, ANP-stimulated intracellular cGMP production in hepatocytes may activate additional signaling pathways, PKG-dependent and independent. Additionally, we observed increased hepatic cholesterol and triglyceride levels in Corin KO mice, suggesting that corin/ANP-mediated signaling may regulate other metabolic pathways in the liver. Further studies will be important to determine if other signaling mechanisms contribute to the corin and ANP function in the regulation of liver metabolism.

Conclusion

Glucose homeostasis is of physiological and medical significance. Here we report an important function of cardiac corin and ANP in regulating liver glycogen metabolism and glucose homeostasis via a cGMP-PKGI-AKT-GSK3 pathway. Conceivably, defects in the cardiac corin and ANP pathway may impair both cardiovascular and metabolic homeostasis, contributing to major diseases, such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome. Our study should encourage further investigations to verify our findings in humans and to test if enhancing the corin and ANP pathway may be of therapeutic value for individuals with impaired liver glycogen metabolism and glucose homeostasis.

Limitations

There are several limitations of our study. Our findings in mouse models indicate a role of cardiac corin/ANP in regulating glucose homeostasis and liver glycogen metabolism. Additional investigations are required to understand if our findings in mice may apply to humans and if defects in the corin and ANP pathway may contribute to major metabolic diseases, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. It will be important to test if enhancing the corin/ANP pathway may improve cardiovascular and metabolic health in humans. By analyzing tissues from Corin and Nppa KO mice and molecular studies in cultured human and mouse liver cells, we show that the function of corin and ANP in the liver is mediated, at least in part, via a cGMP-PKGI-AKT-GSK3 pathway. More studies are needed to determine if the corin/ANP signaling interreacts additional pathways that are critical in liver metabolism and homeostasis.

Methods

Mice

The experiments in mice were approved by the Animal Use and Care Committee of Soochow University (protocol number: 202111A0071). All mice were housed in a special-pathogen-free facility with 12-h dark-light cycles and free access to water and a regular chow diet. Corin KO, hcKO, and kcKO mice were reported previously [37, 38]. Briefly, two loxP sites were inserted into the Corin gene to generate Corflox mice. Corin KO, hcKO, and kcKO mice were made by crossing Corflox mice with CMV-Cre (ubiquitous), Myh6-Cre (heart), and Ggt1-Cre (kidney) mice, respectively. Corin KO mice were hypertensive and developed progressive cardiac dysfunction with cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis after 9 months of age [38, 61]. The KO mice also had increased body fat weights [23], but did not exhibit apparent pathological changes in the kidney, such as necrosis, fibrosis, inflammation, or ischemia. Water intake and urine volume were similar between WT and Corin KO mice [38].

Nppa KO mice were created using CRISPR/Cas9 methods to delete exons 1-3 of the Nppa gene (GemPharmatech, Nanjing, China). PCR genotyping, DNA sequencing, and mRNA expression analyses were used to verify the gene deletion. All the KO mouse strains were back crossed (> 10 generations) into the C57BL/6 J background. WT C57BL/6 J mice were used as controls. Age-matched mice of different genotypes (males, ~ 4 months of age) housed in the same room were used in the experiments. Investigators were not blinded to mouse genotype. Sample sizes were based on observed variations in these mice in previous or preliminary studies and statistical significance level (p = 0.05). After euthanasian, blood and tissue samples were collected for further analyses. No data were excluded.

Blood glucose measurement and glucose and insulin tolerance tests

Blood samples were collected via the tail vein of fed or fasted mice. Blood glucose levels were measured using a glucometer (OneTouch VerioVue, Life Scan Inc.). Glucose and insulin tolerance tests were conducted in 16-week-old mice fasted for 6 h (glucose test) or 5 h (insulin test). A glucose solution (2 mg of glucose/gram of body weight) or insulin (Sigma, I0908, Burlington, MA, USA) (0.75 U/kg of body weight) was injected intraperitoneally. Blood samples were taken via the tail vein at different time points after the injection. Blood glucose levels were measured. Values of AUC were calculated using Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).

Plasma insulin and glucagon measurements

Insulin and glucagon levels in plasma samples were measured using ELISA kits (ml001903 and ml057708, Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Shanghai, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Liver insulin sensitivity

Blood samples were collected from mice fasted for 5 h. Blood glucose levels (G0, mg/dL) were measured, as described above. Following euthanasia, blood samples were collected, and plasma insulin levels (I0, μU/mL) were determined using an ELISA kit (10-1247-01, Mercodia, Uppsala, Sweden). Liver insulin sensitivity was calculated using the formula: 1/(logG0 + logI0), based on a published method [62].

Hepatic triglyceride and cholesterol measurements

Mouse livers (100 mg) were homogenized in 0.9 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged at 1,200 g, 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and lipid levels were measured using assay kits for triglyceride and total cholesterol (A110-1-1, A111-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Tissue and cell glycogen levels

Mouse liver or skeletal muscle tissues (100 mg) were isolated and placed in 250 μL of 30% KOH. After boiling for 1.5 h, the samples were put on ice. Crude glycogen was precipitated with 650 μL of 100% ethanol and centrifuged at 6,000 g, 4°C for 10 min. The glycogen pellets were dissolved in 10 µN HCl and precipitated with an equal volume of ethanol. The procedures were repeated twice to remove glucose background. The final precipitates were washed with ethanol, air-dried, and re-suspended in distilled water. To measure cellular glycogen levels, cells were washed with cold PBS, suspended in distilled water on ice, and homogenized. The samples were boiled for 10 min and centrifuged at 6,000 g, 4 °C for 10 min. Glycogen levels in the supernatant were measured with a kit (K646-100, BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cold exposure experiment

Male WT, Corin KO and Nppa KO mice were placed at room temperature (RT) (22-25 °C) or in a cold room (4 °C) with free access to water without food. Blood glucose levels were monitored at 0, 30, 150, and 300 min in samples obtained via tail vain. At the end of the experiments, the mice were euthanized. Liver samples were collected for glycogen level analysis.

RT-PCR and qRT-PCR

Total RNAs were extracted from tissues or cells using Trizol agents (15596018, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNAs were used to synthesize cDNAs with a kit (K1622 Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for RT-PCR and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). qRT-PCR was conducted using the SYBR Green master mix (A25742 Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the QuantStudio 6 Real-Time PCR System. The specific primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Plasma ANP and cGMP measurements

Mature ANP levels in mouse plasma were measured using an ELISA kit (LV30662, Shanghai Animalunion Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Tissue and cellular cGMP levels were assessed by ELISA (ml001887, Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co.). All procedures in the ELISA were conducted according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Western blotting

Mouse livers were homogenized in RIPA solution (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) with pooled protease and phosphatase inhibitors (1:100, 78442, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cultured cells were lysed in a solution with 1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 10% (v/v) glycerin, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and a protease inhibitor mixture (04693116001, 1:100 dilution, Roche Life Sciences, Basel, Switzerland). Protein concentrations in samples were determined using a BCA assay (23,227, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein samples were analyzed with SDS-PAGE and western blotting.

The primary antibodies used in this study included those against PkgI (1:1000, 3248S, Cell Signaling), Akt (1:1000, 4691, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), phospho (p)-Akt (1:1000, 4060, Cell Signaling), Gsk3α (1:1000, 4337, Cell Signaling), p-Gsk3α (1:1000, 9316, Cell Signaling), Gsk3β (1:1000, 12,456, Cell Signaling), p-Gsk3β (1:1000, 5558, Cell Signaling), and GAPDH (1:10,000, MB001H, Bioworld, Nanjing, China). A horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000, BS13278, Bioworld) was used for detection. GAPDH was used as a loading control. Protein bands were visualized using the Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) and quantified with ImageJ software (NIH).

Cell culture and ANP treatment

HepG2, HEK293, and BHK cells originally from ATCC (authenticated by STR profiling) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (10-013-CVRC, Corning, Suzhou, China) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (900-108, GeminiBio, West Sacramento, CA, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Murine AML12 liver cells (NingBoMingZhou Tech, Ningbo, China) were cultured in a specialized medium (NingBoMingZhou Tech, MCM-0602) with 10% FBS at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2.

Upon reaching ~ 90% confluency, HepG2 and AML12 cells were serum-starved for 16 h and treated with ANP (100 nM) (AS-20648, Anaspec, Fremont, CA, USA) over time. To test the effects of PKG and AKT inhibitors on ANP-treated HepG2 and AML12 cells, the serum-starved cells were treated with ANP (100 nM) and KT5823 (PKG inhibitor, 1 μM, ab120423, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or MK2206 (AKT inhibitor, 2 μM, SF2712, Beyotime Biotechnology). After 30 min at 37 °C, the cells were isolated. Levels of cGMP and glycogen or protein expression were analyzed, as described above.

Cellular glucose uptake assay

An indirect assay was used to assess glucose uptake in cultured cells. HepG2 and AML12 cells (2 × 105/well) in 6-well plates were serum-starved for 4 h and then treated with ANP (100 nM) or insulin (20 ng/mL) in DMEM with 25 mmol/L glucose at 37 °C for 4 h. The conditioned media were collected at different time points. Glucose levels in the medium were measured using a glucometer (OneTouch VerioVue). Glucose uptake was estimated based on the depletion of glucose in the medium.

Statistics

Data were analyzed using Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad). The normal distribution of the data was verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were done using two-tailed Student’s t test or nonparametric Mann-Whitney test. Comparisons among three or more groups were done using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc analysis or the Kruskal-Wallis test, or two-way ANOVA and Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Data are presented as mean ± SD. P values of < 0.05 were considered significant.
