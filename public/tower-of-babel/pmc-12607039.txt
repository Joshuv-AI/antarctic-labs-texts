Metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses provide insights into metabolic networks during Fragaria nilgerrensis fruit development and ripening
Authors: Ai-Hua Wang, Bao-Hui Zhang, Chao Ma, Jie Wang, Chun-Ying Kang, Xing-Tao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFragaria nilgerrensis is a wild diploid strawberry species that represents a rich source of genetic variations with potential for enhancing fruit quality traits. However, the transcriptional regulation of changes in fruit quality relevant metabolites during F. nilgerrensis fruit development and ripening has not been investigated. Thus, this study analyzed the changes and accumulation of sugars, organic acids, phenolic acids and flavonoids at the four developmental stages of F. nilgerrensis fruit.ResultsD-sucrose, raffinose, D-trehalose, melibiose and isocitric acid increased as fruit developed. In terms of phenolic acids and flavonoids, cinnamic acid, hydroxycinnamic acid, coumarin, coniferin, anthocyanidins, rutin, and nicotiflorin accumulated as fruit developed. Conversely, contents of sinapoyl malate, coniferaldehyde, sinapinaldehyde, coniferyl alcohol, quercetin, gallocatechin, eriodictyol, luteolin, phloretin, and naringenin were decreased. The expression levels of key structural genes that corresponded with metabolite changes were identified. These genes included RFS (LOC101297814) and IDH(LOC101296705) in saccharide and organic acid metabolism, PAL (LOC101315259), BG (LOC101313585), F5H (LOC101307828), CCR (LOC101315149), CAD (LOC101306416 and LOC101309917), GT5 (LOC101296671), CHS(LOC101298162 and LOC101298456), LAR(LOC101306809), FLS(LOC101303260, LOC101309876, and LOC101302485), and ANR (LOC101292386) in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. Correlation analysis revealed that multiple transcription factor families were involved in the saccharide, phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, among which, AUX/IAA (LOC101298379), WRKY (LOC101302596), and AP2/ERF(LOC101295372)TFs were significantly correlated with saccharide synthesis. The effects of AP2/ERF (LOC101291560), AUX/IAA (LOC101298379), MYB (LOC105352442), and WRKY (LOC101302596 and LOC101295677) TFs were significantly correlated with cinnamic acid accumulation.ConclusionsThis study identified key metabolites, structural genes, and transcription factors influencing fruit quality-related metabolic changes during fruit development in F. nilgerrensis. These findings may facilitate the utilization of wild strawberry resources for breeding novel cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07590-8.

Background

Cultivated garden strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa) is one of the most economically important fruit species worldwide [1]. However, decades of intensive breeding focused on yield and disease resistance have inadvertently narrowed genetic diversity in key quality-related traits [2]. To address this limitation, wild strawberry germplasm resources offer a promising strategy to enhance traditional breeding programs [3]. In particular, Fragaria nilgerrensis, a wild diploid species native to eastern and southeastern Asia, offers substantial genetic diversity for trait improvement [4]. To capitalize on this potential, it is crucial to characterize the dynamic changes in quality-related metabolites during fruit ripening and elucidate their biosynthetic mechanisms in Fragaria nilgerrensis.

Strawberry fruit quality is influenced by multiple dimensions: appearance (e.g., color, shape, and size), texture (e.g., firmness), flavor and aroma, and nutritional components, etc. Among these, the nutritional profile comprises macronutrients (e.g., sugars), micronutrients (e.g., vitamins and minerals), and bioactive compounds (e.g., flavonoids and phenolic acids) [5], all of which collectively contribute to sensory attributes and health-promoting properties [6, 7]. In strawberry, sucrose is the major form of carbohydrate translocated from the leaves, and its metabolism is usually associated with the activities of several sucrose metabolism-related enzymes: sucrose phosphate synthase (SPS), sucrose synthase (SUS), and invertase (INV) [8]. Furthermore, the biosynthesis process of the phenylpropanoid pathway, which usually lead to the production of phenolic acids, has also been well studied [9, 10]. The key enzymes of this pathway, phenylalanine ammonialyase (PAL) and cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H), participate in the conversion of phenylalanine to several phenolic acids [11–14]. These phenolic acids can be converted to coumarin, chlorogenic acid, and caffeic acid in reactions catalyzed by 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL) [15, 16]. Flavonoids in strawberries mostly exist as proanthocyanidins, as well as flavonols, anthocyanins and various other phenolics [17]. Enzymes in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, including chalcone isomerase, chalcone synthase (CHS), and anthocyanin synthase (ANS), etc., catalyze reactions that lead to the production of different intermediates and flavonoid classes [18–21]. The expression of structural genes encoding enzymes in pathways is largely regulated by TFs. The initiation and progression of fruit ripening are highly modulated by multiple TFs [5]. For example, several TFs involved in fruit ripening have been identified, including NAC TFs affecting tomato fruit ripening [22], differentially expressed WRKY TFs influencing banana fruit development and ripening [23], and MYB TFs contributing to papaya fruit ripening [24]. In strawberry, the ripening-inducing factor gene (FaRIF), a NAC TF, is highly expressed in floral tissues during ripening, while FaMYB10 acts as another critical regulator of fruit ripening [25, 26].

Despite the aforementioned understanding of strawberry ripening mechanisms, in-depth research on the diploid strawberry F. nilgerrensis remains limited. Although recent studies have compared the differences and the biosynthesis of flavonoids, lipids, sugars, and amino acids between ripe fruit of F. nilgerrensis and F. pentaphylla [2], and identified key metabolites related to flavonoids, sugars, and amino acids in the mature fruit of Fragaria nilgerrensis and 12 other diploid wild Fragaria species [27], significant knowledge gaps persist. Specifically, the dynamic changes and accumulation patterns of crucial metabolites (e.g., sugars, organic acids, phenolic acids, and flavonoids) during F. nilgerrensis fruit development and ripening remain uncharacterized. Moreover, many key genes, including transcription factors (TFs) and structural genes, involved in this process have yet to be identified. Given that fruit quality is determined by the content and composition of metabolites, which are tightly regulated during ripening [19, 28], elucidating these metabolic and transcriptional dynamics is essential for a comprehensive understanding of quality trait formation in strawberry.

Metabolome and transcriptome association analyses are useful for clarifying gene regulatory networks. To elucidate the transcriptional regulation underlying the fruit development of F. nilgerrensis, we analyzed the metabolome and transcriptome of F. nilgerrensis fruit to detect the differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) affecting fruit quality (sugars, organic acids, phenolic acids, and flavonoids) during development. Furthermore, the metabolic regulatory networks influencing four fruit developmental stages were constructed and the regulatory genes associated with key metabolites were identified. The study results also revealed the changes in quality-related metabolic pathways and candidate genes during the development of F. nilgerrensis fruit, with possible implications for enhancing the use of wild strawberry resources and the breeding of cultivated strawberry varieties.

Liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis resulted in the identification of 1,242 metabolites in 12 samples (Table S1, Fig. 2b). These metabolites were classified in 11 primary classes and 36 secondary classes. The most common metabolites were flavonoids (252 metabolites, accounting for 20.29% of all metabolites), followed by phenolic acids (246, 19.81%), lipids (146, 11.76%), amino acids and derivatives (94, 7.57%), terpenoids (94, 7.57%), organic acids (79, 6.36%), alkaloid compounds (69, 5.56%), lignans and coumarins (58, 4.67%), nucleotides and derivatives (56, 4.51%), tannins (35, 2.82%), and other compounds (113, 9.1%).

The BG vs. SG, T vs. BG, and R vs. T comparisons revealed 578 DAMs, of which 23 were specific to one of the comparisons (mainly organic acids, phenolic acids, and amino acids and derivatives). The comparison with the most DAMs was BG vs. SG (412 in total, 185 upregulated and 227 downregulated), followed by the R vs. T comparison (227 in total, 159 upregulated and 68 downregulated) and the T vs. BG comparison (135 in total, 83 upregulated and 52 downregulated) (Fig. 2c, Table S2). These results reflected the substantial changes in metabolite contents during the transitions from SG to BG and from T to R.

The K-means analysis grouped the 578 DAMs into eight clusters (Fig. 2d, Table S2). During fruit development, the trend of the metabolites in clusters 4 and 7 continued to increase, which was in contrast to the continual decrease in the trend of cluster 2 metabolites. The trend of the metabolites in clusters 3, 6, and 8 initially increased and then decreased, whereas the trend of the metabolites in clusters 1 and 5 initially decreased and then increased. The metabolites that continued to accumulate were mainly lipids, phenolic acids, organic acids, and saccharides, suggesting that these metabolites have important roles related to F. nilgerrensis fruit ripening.

Identification and functional analysis of DEGs

To investigate the transcriptional differences among fruit developmental stages, 7,163 DEGs were identified and analyzed (Fig. 3a, Table S3). The BG vs. SG comparison had the most DEGs (4,547), followed by the R vs. T comparison (3,322) and the T vs. BG comparison (1,993) (Fig. 3a). Therefore, the transcriptional changes at BG may be the main reason for the subsequent fruit quality changes, indicative of their important effects on strawberry fruit development. A K-means analysis was conducted to further examine DEG expression patterns in each sample (Fig. 3b). Cluster 1 contained 2,503 DEGs, with expression levels that continued to increase during the fruit developmental process. Conversely, the 4,660 DEGs in cluster 2 had expression levels that continued to decrease as fruit developed. Transcription factor family analysis

The DEGs included 44 TF-encoding genes belonging to 22 TF families, among which AP2/ERF, WRKY, AUX/IAA, and TIFY (related to plant growth and development) and MYB and bHLH (related to fruit coloration) were the main families. According to their expression patterns (Fig. 4), some TF genes were highly expressed in BG (e.g., LOC105349847, LOC101306109, LOC101301342, and LOC101290854), while others (e.g., LOC101291560, LOC101295677, LOC105352442, LOC101298379, LOC101294043, LOC101314073, LOC101301353, and LOC101307102) peaked in R. Notably, LOC101302596 was highly expressed in both the S and R. Furthermore, for the majority of these TF genes, the highest expression levels were observed in the SG, being significantly greater than those in the other three stages. Analysis of saccharide and organic acid metabolism

In terms of sugar and organic acid metabolism (nine DAMs and 36 DEGs), most DAMs accumulated as fruit developed, including D-sucrose, raffinose, galactinol, D-trehalose, melibiose, isocitric acid, and α-ketoglutaric acid. In contrast, succinic acid and uridine 5′-diphospho-D-glucose levels decreased in BG and T (Tables S4; Fig. 6). Among these compounds, D-sucrose, galactinol, D-trehalose, raffinose, and melibiose contents were 39.17-, 32.28-, 12.03-, 3.98-, and 3.31-times higher in BG than in SG, respectively (Tables S4), indicating that this key early-accumulating sugars that persist throughout development and may contribute to F. nilgerrensis fruit flavor. The structural genes encoding raffinose synthase (RFS; LOC101297814) and invertase (INV; LOC101302788), both involved in raffinose metabolism, along with those for isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH; LOC101296705; related to isocitric acid) and trehalose-phosphate synthase (TPS; LOC101291216; involved in D-trehalose biosynthesis), all showed peak expression in the R stage. Identification of saccharide synthesis correlation networks

To identify the TFs involved in regulating saccharide accumulation in F. nilgerrensis fruit, the correlations between 29 TF DEGs and 30 structural genes as well as four up-regulated saccharides were analyzed (Table S10, S11). The four saccharides (D-sucrose, D-trehalose, raffinose, and melibiose) were highly correlated with the TF DEGs and structural genes. More specifically, D-sucrose was significantly correlated with AUX/IAA, AP2/ERF, TIFY, WRKY, bHLH, and LOB TF genes. Discussion

Saccharide and organic acid metabolism

Previous studies have demonstrated that fruit sweetness and acidity are predominantly governed by the accumulation of soluble sugars and organic acids, respectively [29, 30]. In cultivated strawberry varieties fruit, the primary sugar components include sucrose, glucose, and fructose, which collectively contribute to the characteristic sweet flavor profile [8, 31]. Our current investigation of F. nilgerrensis fruit during development and ripening revealed dynamic alterations in sugar composition, with sucrose, raffinose, D-trehalose, and melibiose exhibiting significantly more pronounced fluctuations compared to glucose and fructose (Table S1, S5; Fig. 6). Notably, D-sucrose and D-trehalose accumulated dramatically during ripening, reaching levels over 10-fold higher in BG compared to SG. This accumulation correlated with upregulated expression of RFS (LOC101297814), a key gene in raffinose biosynthesis, suggesting its critical role in sugar metabolism during ripening (Fig. 6). Similar to the recent studies on F. nilgerrensis sugar, D-sucrose may be the key sugar in the fruit development and quality formation of F. nilgerrensis [2, 27]. In contrast, D-trehalose may have been overlooked in prior literature due to sample differences. Trehalose is known for its diverse biological applications, like in drug development, research application, natural scaffold, stem cell preservation, food, and various other industries [32]. In addition, the Raffinose family has the potential role of prebiotics [33]. Therefore, they can serve as potential breeding targets for strawberries. The main organic acids in cultivated strawberry varieties are citric and malic acid [34, 35], which can exerted a stronger influence on taste perception can suppressed the perception of sweetness [36]. In comparison, F. nilgerrensis has a distinct organic acid profile, primarily characterized by isocitrate, succinic acid, and methylmalonic acid [2]. In this study, most organic acids remained stable across all four developmental stages of F. nilgerrensis fruit, with the exception of succinic acid, isocitric acid, and α-ketoglutaric acid, which exhibited significant fluctuations. This distinct pattern likely occurred because these acids served as key substrates in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which underwent dynamic metabolic shifts during fruit maturation.

Phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways

The antioxidant capacity of phenolic compounds, particularly their ROS scavenging and oxidative stress mitigation properties, is well documented [37, 38]. Cultivated strawberries accumulate significant quantities of phenolic acids, including ferulic, p-coumaric, vanillic, caffeic, syringic, and sinapic acids, which are biosynthesized through the phenylpropanoid pathway [39, 40]. Notably, ellagic acid and p-coumaric acid constitute approximately 85% of total phenolic content, with ellagic acid demonstrating strong radical scavenging activity and p-coumaric acid exhibiting UV-protective properties [41]. In this study, cinnamic acid and coumarin progressively accumulated, correlating with the upregulation of PAL (LOC101315259) and BG (LOC101313585) (Fig. 7). Ellagic acid levels remained stable, consistent with its role as a terminal metabolite in the hydrolyzable tannin pathway (Table S1) [42]. Notably, coniferin, a monolignol glucoside traditionally used in Chinese medicine for its anticancer properties, accumulates unexpectedly, hinting at potential stress-induced lignification or defense responses. Conversely, several phenylpropanoid derivatives declined, including, sinapoyl malate (a UV-absorbing compound linked to photoprotection) and lignin precursors (coniferyl alcohol, coniferaldehyde, sinapinaldehyde). These reductions may reflect developmental shifts in carbon allocation, favoring soluble phenolics (e.g., anthocyanins) over cell wall-bound metabolites as ripening progresses.

Anthocyanins, which are derived from the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, are the major compounds responsible for the red coloration of strawberry fruit [43]. In addition, flavonols protect fruit skins from the harmful effects of UV light [44], whereas flavan-3-ols, such as catechin and epicatechin, contribute to defense responses and stress resistance [45]. Flavonols in cultivated strawberry fruit are mainly glycosides of quercetin and kaempferol. The most common flavan-3-ols are catechin and epicatechin as well as their monohydroxylated equivalents afzelechin and epiafzelechin and glycosylated derivatives [6]. In this study, flavonoids in F. nilgerrensis exhibited distinct accumulation patterns during fruit development. Anthocyanins and flavonol glycosides (e.g., rutin and nicotiflorin) increased progressively, suggesting coordinated activation of both anthocyanin biosynthesis and flavonoid glycosylation during ripening. In contrast, gallocatechin peaked in SG and declined at R, likely due to CHS (LOC101298162 and LOC101298456), FHT (LOC101300182) and LAR (LOC101292386) downregulation and competition for the shared precursor dihydroquercetin (Fig. 8). This shift suggests a transition from defense (gallocatechin) to pigmentation (anthocyanin) during ripening.

Transcription factors controlling strawberry fruit development and ripening

Non-climacteric fruit ripening is primarily regulated by abscisic acid (ABA). In strawberry, ABA and sucrose cooperatively modulated ripening while suppressing glycolytic activity [8]. Mechanistically, ABA was perceived by specific receptors, triggering downstream signaling cascades that activated key transcription factors and ripening-associated genes [46]. Several TFs have been identified as regulators of sugar metabolism in strawberry fruit. MYB family TFs (including FaMYB44.2 and FaGAMYB) promote sugar biosynthesis by regulating the expression of genes encoding sucrose metabolism enzymes (FaSUS, FaSPS, and FaSUT) [47, 48]. Additionally, FvNAC073, FaRIF and bZIP TFs have been shown to influence sugar metabolism [49, 50]. Recent research has established that ABA signaling regulates sugar accumulation through the FaRIPK1-FaTCP7-FaSTP13/FaSPT signaling pathway [51]. In the current study, we identified three putative sugar metabolism regulators in F. nilgerrensis: AUX/IAA (LOC101298379), WRKY (LOC101302596), and AP2/ERF (LOC101295372). However, their potential position downstream of ABA signaling and precise functional roles in strawberry fruit ripening require further validation.

Among the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, most identified TFs primarily regulate anthocyanin synthesis, with MYB TFs being the most extensively studied ((52, 53). Other key regulators of anthocyanin biosynthesis include MdNAC33 ([54], the MBW complex ((55, 56), bHLH ([57], TIFY [58], MdLBD37 ([59], AP2/ERF ([60], and StWRKY13 ([61]. Notably, a novel WRKY34-bZIP3 module has been demonstrated to specifically regulate phenolic acid biosynthesis inSalvia miltiorrhiza ([62]. Phenolic acids are mainly synthesized synergistically through the phenylpropanoid pathway and part of the shikimic acid pathway ([63]. The phenylpropanoid pathway is extremely complex. Yang et al. identified 40 pairs of TFs in the phenylpropanoid pathway, which are composed of members bHLH, TUB, WRKY, MYB and NAC ([64]. These transcription factors provide crucial insights into the regulatory mechanisms of flavonoid and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. However, significant functional divergence exists both for the same transcription factor across different species and among different members within the same TF family. A critical research gap remains in systematically cataloging known regulatory proteins (particularly transcription factors) that directly control specific structural genes and their transcriptional regulation ([65]. To date, relatively few studies have investigated TFs associated with the phenylpropanoid pathway in strawberry fruit, particularly those regulating cinnamic acid and coumarin biosynthesis. Beyond the previously mentioned TF families, our findings suggest that AUX/IAA family members may also participate in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis. These identified TFs represent promising candidates for further research on fruit development regulation inF. nilgerrensis.

In summary, during fruit development and ripening in F. nilgerrensis, D-sucrose, raffinose, D-trehalose, melibiose, and isocitric acid increased, while key phenolic and flavonoid metabolites exhibited dynamic shifts: cinnamic acid, hydroxycinnamic acid, coumarin, coniferin, anthocyanidins, rutin, and nicotiflorin accumulated, whereas sinapoyl malate, coniferaldehyde, sinapinaldehyde, coniferyl alcohol, quercetin, gallocatechin, eriodictyol, luteolin, phloretin, and naringenin declined.These metabolic shifts correlated with the expression patterns of sugar/organic acid genes (RFS, IDH) and phenylpropanoid/flavonoid pathway genes (PAL, BG, F5H, CCR, CAD, CHS, GT5, LAR, FLS, ANR). Transcriptional regulation involved AUX/IAA, WRKY, and AP2/ERF TFs in sugar synthesis, while AP2/ERF, AUX/IAA, MYB, and WRKY TFs correlated with cinnamic acid accumulation. While this study identified key genes and metabolites associated with fruit quality traits in F. nilgerrensis, further validation across distinct populations and accessions is imperative. These investigations will assess the stability and predictive value of the proposed biomarkers under diverse genetic and environmental conditions, which is crucial for their reliable application in marker-assisted breeding programs.

Materials and methods

Fruit materials and sample collection

Fruits of Fragaria nilgerrensis were collected from the Strawberry Germplasm Repository of Guizhou University, located in Guiyang (26.492310° N, 106.653870° E), Guizhou Province, China. Fruits were considered mature at approximately 30 days after flowering (DAF). Sampling was conducted at 7, 15, 22, and 30 DAF, corresponding to the SG, BG, T, and R stages, respectively. For each developmental stage, three independent biological replicates were analyzed. Each biological replicate was constructed as a composite sample by pooling fifteen fruits collected from five individual plants (three fruits per plant), with all plants randomly selected from distinct cultivation rows. This design resulted in each biological replicate representing a composite of fifteen fruits, yielding a total of n = 3 biological replicates per developmental stage. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until subsequent analysis.

Analyses of phenotypes and firmness during F. nilgerrensis fruit development and ripening

A GY-1 firmness tester (Top Instrument Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) was used to determine fruit firmness. Fruit color attributes at each developmental stage were monitored using the NF555 spectrophotometer (Nippon Denshoku Industries Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) calibrated using a white plate and the CIE L*, a*, b* color space, where L* indicates brightness, a* indicates the axis from green to red, and b* indicates the axis from blue to yellow. Fruit diameter (cm) was measured using a digital vernier caliper (Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan). Images of fruit were used to compare phenotypes. Analyses were completed using three biological replicates. Significant differences were assessed by a one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc Duncan’s multiple range tests (P < 0.05). The data are here in expressed as the mean ± standard error.

Metabolite extraction and analysis

Biological samples were lyophilized and then crushed using the MM400 mixer mill (Retsch, Haan, Germany) and a zirconia bead (1.5 min at 30 Hz). For each sample, 50 mg lyophilized powder was resuspended in 1.2 mL 70% methanol solution and vortexed for 30 s every 30 min (six times in total). Samples were centrifuged (1,432 × g for 3 min) and then the supernatants (extracts) were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Filtrates retained were analyzed using an ultraperformance liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI-MS/MS) system (UPLC, SHIMADZU Nexera X2; MS, Applied Biosystems 4500 QTRAP). The chromatographic and mass spectrometry conditions were used according to the methods of previous works [66].

The metware database (MWDB) was used for metabolites annotation. The qualitative analysis of metabolites was carried out according to the secondary mass spectrometry information, and the quantitative analysis was accomplished by using the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) of triple quadrupole mass spectrometry. Then, perform peak area integration for the mass spectrometry peaks of all metabolites, and perform integration correction for the mass spectrometry peaks of the same metabolite in different samples.

The PCA was performed using the statistics function prcomp within R (www.r-project.org). The data were unit variance-scaled before the unsupervised PCA. VIP values were determined on the basis of the orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) results, which included score plots and permutation plots generated using the R package MetaboAnalystR. Data were log-transformed (log2) and mean-centered before the OPLS-DA. To avoid overfitting, a permutation test (200 permutations) was conducted. For each comparison (BG vs. SG, T vs. BG, and R vs. T), DAMs were identified using the following thresholds: variable importance in projection (VIP) value ≥ 1.0, absolute log2(fold-change) (|log2(fold-change)|) ≥ 1.0 and P < 0.05. To study the changing trends of the DAMs in the four comparisons, the relative contents of all DAMs were subjected to UV (unit variance scaling) processing, and then K-Means clustering analysis was conducted.

Illumina sequencing and RNA-seq data processing

Total RNA was extracted from the SG, BG, T, and R fruit using the TRIZOL kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). RNA concentration and integrity assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Co. Ltd., Santa Clara, CA, USA) respectively. RNA libraries were constructed for each sample and sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq-2000 platform [66]. For RNA-seq data processing, The clean reads were mapped to the downloaded Fragaria vesca L. (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/?term=Fragaria+vesca+L.) genome assembly using HISAT (v2.1.0) with default parameters. DESeq2 (v1.22.1)/edgeR (v3.24.3) were used to screen for DEGs in each comparison (BG vs. SG, T vs. BG, and R vs. T), with the P value corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (Wang et al., 2023). Corrected P < 0.05 and |log2(fold-change)| ≥ 1were used to detect significant DEG. The FPKM values of differentially expressed genes were then used for clustering heat map plotting using the TBtools software (http://www.tbtools.org/). To study the changing trends of the DEGs in the four comparisons, the relative contents of all DEGs were subjected to UV (unit variance scaling) processing, and then K-Means clustering analysis was conducted.

Quantitative real-time PCR validation

The quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis was completed using a BioRad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., USA) and TSINGKE® Master qRT-PCR Mix (SYBR Green I) (Tsingke, Beijing, China). The PCR program was as follows: 95 °C for 3 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The internal reference gene was FaActin (accession No. AB116565). Details regarding the qRT-PCR primers are listed in Table S18. The comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method was used to calculate the relative gene expression levels [67]. All samples were analyzed using three biological replicates, each with three technical replicates.

Combined transcriptome and metabolome analysis

For the combined analysis, all obtained DEGs and DAMs were simultaneously annotated according to the KEGG pathway database to identify common enriched pathways. The major biochemical pathways associated with both DAMs and DEGs were identified. Histograms of the top 10 significantly enriched metabolic pathways in each comparison were plotted using the OmicShare tool (https://www.omicshare.com/tools). The correlations between TFs with DAMs and DEGs were analyzed on the basis of Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs) calculated using the cor() function from R’s stats package. Correlation network plots were generated using Cytoscape (v3.9.1). The following thresholds were used to detect strong correlations: PCC ≥ 0.8 and P ≤ 0.05.
