Nonparametric Smoothing of Directional and Axial Data
Lutz Dümbgen, Caroline Haslebacher, [1ex] University of Bern

Abstract




Abstract.

We discuss generalized linear models for directional data where the conditional distribution of the response is a von Mises–Fisher distribution in arbitrary dimension or a Bingham distribution on the unit circle. To do this properly, we parametrize von Mises–Fisher distributions by Euclidean parameters and investigate computational aspects of this parametrization. Then we modify this approach for local polynomial regression as a means of nonparametric smoothing of distributional data. The methods are illustrated with simulated data and a data set from planetary sciences involving covariate vectors on a sphere with axial response.

AMS subject classification:

62J12, 62H11, 62G05

Key words:

Bingham distributions, exponential family, Galileo space mission, generalized linear model, local polynomial modelling, stereographic projection, von Mises–Fisher distributions.

When thinking about smaller subregions of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}

\mathbb{R}^{2}

to the task of smoothing directional data, given by pairs
(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, where the conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}
, given
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}
, is a von Mises–Fisher distribution with parameters depending smoothly on
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}
. More generally, one could think about observations
(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i})

\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}

\mathcal{X}

\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

Two general references about directional data, including regression methods, are the monograph of
Mardia and Jupp 2000
and the review paper of
Pewsey and García-Portugués 2021
. These references describe and cite numerous approaches to regression with directional response. Some of these approaches involve transformations of the directional response into points in some Euclidean space, but the choice of this transformation is somewhat ad hoc. Let us restrict our attention to models in which
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
has a von Mises–Fisher distribution conditional on
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}
. Many authors characterize a von Mises–Fisher distribution via a direction parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and a concentration parameter
\kappa\geq 0

\boldsymbol{\beta}
is the parameter of interest. We propose to replace such a parameter pair
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\kappa)
with the vector
\boldsymbol{z}=\kappa\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since
\kappa=\|\boldsymbol{z}\|
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\kappa^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}
, this description is equivalent, and dealing with parameters in a Euclidean space is more convenient, particularly in generalized linear models.

\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}

\mathbb{R}^{2}

\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}^{\perp}

\mathbb{S}^{1}

2
Directional and axial Gaussian distributions

For a given dimension
d\geq 2
, we consider the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with standard Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
. A directional distribution describes a random unit vector
\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\boldsymbol{y}\|=1\}
. An axial distribution describes a random unit vector
\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with symmetric distribution, where
\boldsymbol{V}
represents the random line
\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{V}
. In what follows, let
M
denote the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

2.1
Von Mises–Fisher distributions

The von Mises–Fisher distribution with parameter
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
\mathrm{vMF}(\boldsymbol{z})
, is given by the density

f_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{y})\ :=\ e^{\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\gamma(\boldsymbol{z})}

with respect to
M
, where
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the cumulant-generating function of
M
,

\gamma(\boldsymbol{z})\ :=\ \log\int e^{\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}}\,M(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}).

Some authors rather talk about the vMF distribution with concentration parameter
\|\boldsymbol{z}\|
and center
\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}
, see
Mardia and Jupp 2000
. But in view of the regression methods introduced later, we stick to the parametrization in terms of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The family
(\mathrm{vMF}(\boldsymbol{z}))_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is a natural exponential family
(
Barndorff-Nielsen 2014
)
, and it is well-known that for a random vector
\boldsymbol{Y}\sim\mathrm{vMF}(\boldsymbol{z})
, its mean vector and covariance matrix are given by the gradient and Hessian matrix of
\gamma
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{z}):=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\boldsymbol{Y})\

\displaystyle=\ \nabla\gamma(\boldsymbol{z}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{z}):=\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{Y})\

\displaystyle=\ D^{2}\gamma(\boldsymbol{z}).

Other well-known facts are that the mapping
\boldsymbol{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|<1\}
is a diffeomorphism. Precisely,

\boldsymbol{\mu}(t\boldsymbol{v})\ =\ \tilde{\gamma}_{d}^{\prime}(t)\boldsymbol{v}\quad\text{for}\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

where
\tilde{\gamma}_{d}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an even, strictly convex and analytic function such that
\tilde{\gamma}_{d}(0)=0
, and
\tilde{\gamma}_{d}^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}\to(-1,1)
is bijective, see Appendix
A
. By means of a Lagrange argument one can show that the density
f_{\boldsymbol{z}}
maximizes differential Shannon entropy

-\int f(\boldsymbol{y})\log f(\boldsymbol{y})\,M(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y})

among all probability densities
f
with respect to
M
satisfying

\int f(\boldsymbol{y})\boldsymbol{y}\,M(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y})\ =\ \boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{z}).

For that reason, some authors refer to the vMF distributions as Gaussian directional distributions, alluding to the fact that any Gaussian density maximizes entropy among all densities with the same first and second moments.

2.2
Bingham distributions

A random axis
\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{V}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, given by a random unit vector
\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with symmetric distribution, can be identified with the symmetric matrix
\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
describing the orthogonal projection of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto
\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{V}
. Maximizing differential Shannon entropy over all densities
f
with respect to
M
such that
\int f(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\,M(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v})
is a given symmetric matrix with trace
1
leads to the following type of distribution: For a symmetric matrix
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with trace
0
, the Bingham distribution
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{W})
has density

f_{\boldsymbol{W}}^{\rm B}(\boldsymbol{v})\ =\ e^{\langle\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top},\boldsymbol{W}\rangle-\gamma^{\rm B}(\boldsymbol{W}}\ =\ e^{\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{v}-\gamma^{\rm B}(\boldsymbol{W})},

where

\gamma^{\rm B}(\boldsymbol{W})\ :=\ \log\int e^{\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{v}}\,M(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}).

Bingham 1974
introduced these distributions for dimension
d=3
, but the extension to arbitrary dimensions
d\geq 2
is straightforward. Again, since the density of
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{W})
maximizes differential Shannon entropy among all probability densities
f
such that
\int f(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\,M(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v})
is a given symmetric matrix with trace
1
, some authors refer to the Bh distributions as Gaussian axial distributions.

2.3
The special case of
d=2

In the special case of the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, there is a useful connection between vMF and Bh distributions. Starting from an axis
\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{v}
with
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we rescale and center the corresponding projection matrix
\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}
similarly as
Arnold and Jupp 2013
and write

2\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{I}_{2}\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}2v_{1}^{2}-1&2v_{1}v_{2}\\
2v_{1}v_{2}&2v_{2}^{2}-1\end{bmatrix}\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}v_{1}^{2}-v_{2}^{2}&2v_{1}v_{2}\\
2v_{1}v_{2}&v_{2}^{2}-v_{1}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}y_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})&y_{2}(\boldsymbol{v})\\
y_{2}(\boldsymbol{v})&-y_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})\end{bmatrix},

where

\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{v})\ :=\ [v_{1}^{1}-v_{2}^{2},2v_{1}v_{2}]^{\top}\ \in\ \mathbb{S}^{1}.

(2)

If we identify any vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a complex number, then
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{v}^{2}
. In particular, this shows immediately that
\|\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{v})\|=1
. Hence there is a one-to-one correspondence between axes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and specifying the expectation
\int\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\,Q(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v})
for a distribution
Q
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is equivalent to specifying the expectation
\int\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{v})\,Q(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v})
. Note further that any symmetric matrix
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
may be written as

\boldsymbol{W}\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}z_{1}&z_{2}\\
z_{2}&-z_{1}\end{bmatrix}

for some
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and elementary algebra reveals that

\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{v}\ =\ \boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{v}).

Furthermore, if
\boldsymbol{V}\sim M
, then
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{V})\sim M
too, and these findings imply the very useful fact that

\boldsymbol{V}\sim\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{W})\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{V})\ \sim\ \mathrm{vMF}(\boldsymbol{z}).

(3)

To visualize a Bingham distribution
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{W})
, let us resort to random angles. One may write

\boldsymbol{W}\ =\ \kappa\begin{bmatrix}\cos(2\beta)&\sin(2\beta)\\
\sin(2\beta)&-\cos(2\beta)\end{bmatrix}

for some
\kappa\geq 0
and some angle
\beta\in[0,\pi)
. If
\boldsymbol{v}=[\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta)]^{\top}
for some angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, then
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{v})=[\cos(2\theta),\sin(2\theta)]^{\top}
and

\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{v})^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}\ =\ \kappa\cos(2(\theta-\beta)).

Hence,
\boldsymbol{V}\sim\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{W})
means that
\boldsymbol{V}=[\cos(\tilde{V}),\sin(\tilde{V})]^{\top}
with a random angle
\tilde{V}\in[0,2\pi)
having density

\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\theta)\ :=\ e^{\kappa\cos(2(\theta-\beta))-\tilde{\gamma}(\kappa)}

(4)

with respect to the uniform distribution on
[0,2\pi)
, where

\tilde{\gamma}(\kappa)\ :=\ \log\int e^{\kappa y_{1}}\,M(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y})\ =\ \log\Bigl(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{\kappa\cos(2(\theta-\beta))}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\Bigr).

This shows that in case of
\kappa>0
, the preferred axis direction is
\pm[\cos(\beta),\sin(\beta)]^{\top}
, and
\kappa
measures the strength of this preference. Thus we reparametrize the Bingham distribution
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{W})
with the parameter vector

\boldsymbol{w}\ :=\ \kappa[\cos(\beta),\sin(\beta)]^{\top}

and write
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{w})
instead of
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{W})
.

Figure

depicts the distribution
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{w})
for two different vectors
\boldsymbol{w}=\kappa[\cos(\beta),\sin(\beta)]^{\top}
. One possible representation is a graph of the angular density
\tilde{f}
in (
4
). Another possibility is to draw an “axial histogram”, that is, the region surrounded by the curve
\theta\mapsto\tilde{f}(\theta)^{1/2}[\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta)]^{\top}
, leading to a symmetric but non-circular “pie” (unless
\kappa=0
). The area of any “slice” of this pie with boundary angles
0\leq\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}\leq 2\pi
equals
2\pi
times the probability that
\tilde{V}\in[\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
.

for

As a final remark, it follows from (
1
) that

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{V}))\ =\ \tilde{\gamma}_{2}^{\prime}(\kappa)[\cos(2\beta),\sin(2\beta)]^{\top}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top})\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{I}_{2}+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}(\boldsymbol{V})&y_{2}(\boldsymbol{V})\\
y_{2}(\boldsymbol{V})&-y_{1}(\boldsymbol{V})\end{bmatrix}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{I}_{2}+\tilde{\gamma}_{2}^{\prime}(\kappa)\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\beta)^{2}-\sin(\beta)^{2}&2\cos(\beta)\sin(\beta)\\
2\cos(\beta)\sin(\beta)&\sin(\beta)^{2}-\cos(\beta)^{2}\end{bmatrix}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\ 2^{-1}(1+\tilde{\gamma}_{2}^{\prime}(\kappa))\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}+2^{-1}(1-\tilde{\gamma}_{2}^{\prime}(\kappa))\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}

with the orthonormal basis vectors
\boldsymbol{u}:=[\cos(\beta),\sin(\beta)]^{\top}
, the preferred axis direction, and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}:=[-\sin(\beta),\cos(\beta)]^{\top}
. For that reason, in the context of regression with Bingham-distributed response vectors, we visualize
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{w})
with
\boldsymbol{w}=\kappa\boldsymbol{u}
simply by the vector pair
\pm\tilde{\gamma}_{2}^{\prime}(\kappa)\boldsymbol{u}
.

3
Regression methods for directional data

Consider observation pairs
(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i})
,
1\leq i\leq n
, consisting of a covariate (vector)
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}
in an arbitrary set
\mathcal{X}
and a response vector
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}\,|\,\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\ =\ \mathrm{vMF}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}))

for some unknown regression function
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The task is to estimate
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
from the given data.

Generalized linear models (GLMs).

A possible parametric approach is to assume that the unknown regression function
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
belongs to a given finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{F}
of functions
\boldsymbol{f}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Conditioning on the covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, and assuming that the responses
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}
are conditionally independent, the resulting negative log-likelihood function
\ell=\ell(\cdot\,|\,\mathrm{data}):\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\ell(\boldsymbol{f})\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\gamma(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}))-\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\bigr).

In our specific settings, any
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}
can be written as
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{k})_{k=1}^{d}
with functions
f_{k}
in a given finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{F}^{o}
of functions
f^{o}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. For this particular case, Appendix
B
provides technical details about the first and second derivatives of the function
\ell(\cdot)
after a suitable parametrization of
\mathcal{F}_{o}
. These formulae enable us to minimize
\ell(\boldsymbol{f})
over all
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}
via a Newton-Raphson procedure.

Smoothing via local GLMs.

A possible extension of parametric GLMs are nonparametric analyses, where the unknown regression function
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
is only assumed to be “smooth” and estimated via local parametric models in the spirit of
Fan et al. 1998
. The value of
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
at a particular point
\boldsymbol{x}^{o}\in\mathcal{X}
is estimated by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{o})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\!\boldsymbol{x}_{o}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{o})
, where

\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\!\boldsymbol{x}_{o}}(\cdot)\ =\ \mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{\boldsymbol{x}_{o}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\bigl(\gamma(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}))-\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\bigr)

with a certain weight function
w_{\boldsymbol{x}_{o}}(\cdot):\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
. In principle, one could even let the model
\mathcal{F}
depend on
\boldsymbol{x}_{o}
, but for our specific settings this is not done.

Specifically let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{q}
. For a given number
N\in(0,n)
, one could set

w_{\boldsymbol{x}_{o}}(\boldsymbol{x})\ =\ \exp(-S_{\boldsymbol{x}^{o},N}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{o}\|^{2}),

(5)

where
S_{\boldsymbol{x}^{o},N}>0
is chosen such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\bigl(-S_{\boldsymbol{x}^{o},N}\|\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}-\boldsymbol{x}^{o}\|^{2})=N
.

If
\mathcal{F}
consists of all constant functions with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then the estimation task is easily solved by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{o})\ =\ \boldsymbol{\mu}^{-1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o}))

with the local mean

\bar{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o})\ :=\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{\boldsymbol{x}_{o}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}\Big/\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{\boldsymbol{x}_{o}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})

and the inverse
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{-1}:\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\boldsymbol{z}\|<1\}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the mean function
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\cdot)
for vMF distributions.

If
\mathcal{F}
consists of all linear functions
\boldsymbol{f}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then a suitable local basis of
\mathcal{F}^{o}
is given by the
r=q+1
functions
f^{o}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}):=1
and

f^{o}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\ :=\ x_{j}-x_{o,j},\quad 1\leq j\leq q,

with
\boldsymbol{x}_{o}=(x_{o,j})_{j=1}^{q}
.

If
\mathcal{F}
consists of all quadratic functions
\boldsymbol{f}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then these
d+1
local basis functions are complemented with the
q(q+1)/2
functions

f^{o}_{jk}(\boldsymbol{x})\ :=\ (x_{j}-x_{o,j})(x_{k}-x_{o,k}),\quad 1\leq j\leq k\leq q,

so
\mathcal{F}^{o}
has dimension
r=(q+1)(q+2)/2
.

Analyzing ice cracks on Europa

We return to a data set consisting of points
(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i},\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i})
,
1\leq i\leq n
, where
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}^{\perp}
representing the axis
\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}
in the tangent plane of the sphere at
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}
. By means of these observations, we want to fit for any point
\boldsymbol{x}_{o}\in\mathbb{S}^{3}
a Bingham distribution
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o},\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o}))
, where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o})\in\boldsymbol{x}_{o}^{\perp}
. For any vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{x}_{o}^{\perp}
, the Bingham distribution
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o},\boldsymbol{w})
is defined as follows: Let
\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2}
be an orthonormal basis of
\boldsymbol{x}_{o}^{\perp}
such that the orthogonal matrix
[\boldsymbol{x}_{o},\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2}]
has determinant
1
. Writing
\boldsymbol{w}=\kappa\cos(\beta)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\kappa\sin(\beta)\boldsymbol{e}_{2}
for some
\kappa\geq 0
and
\beta\in[0,\pi)
, the distribution
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o},\boldsymbol{w})
describes the distribution of the random vector

\boldsymbol{V}\ =\ \cos(\tilde{V})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\sin(\tilde{V})\boldsymbol{e}_{2}\ \in\ \boldsymbol{x}_{o}^{\perp},

where
\tilde{V}\in[0,2\pi)
is a random variable following the density (
4
). To relate the observations
(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i},\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i})
to the point
\boldsymbol{x}_{o}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and its tangent plane
\boldsymbol{x}_{o}^{\perp}
, we need a suitable transformation which is described in the next two paragraphs.

A stereographic projection.

We start with the particular reference point
\boldsymbol{x}_{o}=[1,0,0]^{\top}
. Any point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
x_{1}>-1
is mapped to a point
P(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as follows: One moves
\boldsymbol{x}
along the straight line connecting the reference point’s antipode
[-1,0,0]^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
such that it hits the hyperplane
\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:z_{1}=1\}
. That is, we need
\nu(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(1-\nu(\boldsymbol{x}))[-1,0,0]^{\top}+\nu(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{x}=[1,P(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}]^{\top}
. This leads to

P(\boldsymbol{x})\ :=\ \nu(\boldsymbol{x})\begin{bmatrix}x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{with}\quad\nu(\boldsymbol{x})\ :=\ \frac{2}{1+x_{1}}.

If restricted to
\mathcal{X}:=\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{[-1,0,0]^{\top}\}
, the mapping
P:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a diffeomorphism, and its inverse mapping
P^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathcal{X}
is given by

P^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z})\ :=\ \begin{bmatrix}2\omega(\boldsymbol{z})-1\\
\omega(\boldsymbol{z})z_{1}\\
\omega(\boldsymbol{z})z_{2}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{with}\quad\omega(\boldsymbol{z})\ :=\ \frac{4}{4+\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}}.

For
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, any vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}
can be viewed as the derivative of a smooth curve in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
passing through
\boldsymbol{x}
, so it is natural to consider
DP(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{v}
with the Jacobian matrix
DP(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}
. Elementary calculations show that

DP(\boldsymbol{x})\ =\ \nu(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\ :=\ \begin{bmatrix}-x_{2}/(1+x_{1})&1&0\\
-x_{3}/(1+x_{1})&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

It is well-known that
P
is a conformal mapping in the sense that
DP(\boldsymbol{x}):\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
preserves angles. Precisely, one can show that for arbitrary
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp}
,

\|\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\ =\ \|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}.

Thus we relate the pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})
to the reference point
[1,0,0]^{\top}
and its tangent plane by mapping it to the pair

\bigl(P(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{v}\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Figure
8
illustrates this mapping. The upper left panel shows an artificial set of points
(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i},\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i})
,
1\leq i\leq n
, where
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}^{\perp}
. The axes correspond to the second and third components of all vectors. Note that the data points
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}
(black dots) are situated on a finite collection of circles on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and each axis
\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}
(indicated by a green line connecting
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}\pm 0.1\cdot\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}
) is perpendicular to the circle containing
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}
. The other panels show the projected points
\bigl(P(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}),\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}\bigr)
in squares of different size centered around
\boldsymbol{0}
. One sees clearly the conformal nature of this projection and the well-known fact that circles are mapped onto circles.

Smoothing the data for an arbitrary reference point.

For an arbitrary reference point
\boldsymbol{x}_{o}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we choose an orthogonal matrix
\boldsymbol{B}_{o}
with determinant
1
such that
\boldsymbol{B}_{o}\boldsymbol{x}_{o}=[1,0,0]^{\top}

(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i},\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i})

\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}\neq-\boldsymbol{x}_{o}
are transformed into the pairs

\bigl(P(\boldsymbol{B}_{o}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}),\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{B}_{o}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\boldsymbol{B}_{o}\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}\bigr)\in\ \mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}.

Since
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{B}_{o}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\boldsymbol{B}_{o}\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}
represents an axis in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we replace it by means of the mapping
\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot)

\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{B}_{o}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\boldsymbol{B}_{o}\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i})\in\mathbb{S}^{1}

to the data pairs

and
\boldsymbol{0}

\widehat{\kappa}\geq 0

\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}

This leads to the estimate
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o},\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o}))
of
\mathrm{Bh}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o},\boldsymbol{f}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{o}))
. Note that any choice of

Final comments.

The starting point for the present manuscript was the data from Europa. But as mentioned before, analogous questions arise with observations from Ganymede
(
Rossi et al. 2020
)
. In the latter case, the axes refer to grooves (i.e. tectonic deformations) rather than cracks. We are currently experimenting with data from Ganymede and planning to compare our results with previous ones. There are also potential applications for planets with non-icy surfaces, e.g. faults in the solid surface of Mercury
(
Watters et al. 2001
)
or Venus
(
Sabbeth et al. 2023
)
.

In the derivation of the regression and smoothing methods, we used the standard setting of stochastically independent responses, given the covariates. For the application to Europa or Ganymede, this assumption is certainly not satisfied, for instance, because very long ice cracks lead to several observations
(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i},\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i})
with different locations
\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}
but similar axis directions
\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!i}
. Thus, the methods presented here are merely exploratory and serve to find and visualize patterns in the data, without statistical conclusions such as standard errors, p-values or confidence bounds.

The simulations and data analyses were carried out with the programming language
\mathsf{R}

(
R Core Team 2023
)
. The code we used is availabe upon request.

The function
\gamma
.

If
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!\boldsymbol{0}}
is a random vector with uniform distribution
M=\mathrm{vMF}(\boldsymbol{0})
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, then for any unit vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, the random variable
U_{0}:=\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!\boldsymbol{0}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}\in(-1,1)
has density

,

h_{d}(u)\ :=\ C_{d}(1-u^{2})^{a_{d}-1},\quad u\in(-1,1),

(6)

where
a_{d}:=(d-1)/2
and
C_{d}:=1/B(1/2,a_{d})
with the beta function
B(\cdot,\cdot)
. This implies that
\gamma(\boldsymbol{z})=\log\int e^{\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}}\,M(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y})
can be written as

\gamma(\boldsymbol{z})\ =\ \tilde{\gamma}_{d}(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|),

where

\tilde{\gamma}_{d}(t)\ :=\ \log G_{d}(t),\quad G_{d}(t)\ :=\ \int_{-1}^{1}e^{tu}h_{d}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The function
G_{d}
can also be written as
G_{d}(t)=t^{1-d/2}J_{d/2-1}(t)
, where

stands for the modified Bessel function of the first kind, see
Mardia and Jupp 2000
. In the special case

,
h_{3}\equiv 1/2
and
G_{3}(t)=\sinh(t)/t
.

Proof of (
6
).

Let
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a standard Gaussian random vector. Then,
\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}
has the same distribution as
Z_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|
, and
Z_{1}^{2}/\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}
follows
\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,a_{d})
. Denoting the density of the latter distribution with
b_{d}
, it follows from the symmetry of the distribution of
Z_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|
that for
u\in(0,1)
,

h_{d}(u)\ =\ -\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|>u)\ =\ -\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}2^{-1}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{1}^{2}/\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}>u^{2})\ =\ ub_{d}(u^{2}),

whereas for
u\in(-1,0)
,

h_{d}(u)\ =\ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|<u)\ =\ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}2^{-1}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{1}^{2}/\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}>u^{2})\ =\ |u|b_{d}(u^{2}).

In both cases the result equals
C_{d}(1-u^{2})^{a_{d}-1}
, because
b_{d}(x)=C_{d}x^{-1/2}(1-x)^{a_{d}-1}
for
x\in(0,1)
.
∎

With our regression applications in mind, we decided to calculate

directly, rather than using the detour via Bessel functions. A Taylor series for

G_{d}(t)\ =\ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{d,k}t^{2k}\quad\text{with}\quad c_{d,k}\ :=\ \frac{2^{-2k}\,\Gamma(d/2)}{k!\,\Gamma(k+d/2)}.

(7)

Proof of (

Since

\displaystyle G_{d}(t)\

\displaystyle=\ C_{d}\int_{-1}^{1}\cosh(tu)(1-u^{2})^{a_{d}-1}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\ 2C_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\cosh(tu)(1-u^{2})^{a_{d}-1}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\ 2C_{d}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}\int_{0}^{1}u^{2k}(1-u^{2})^{a_{d}-1}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\ C_{d}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}\int_{0}^{1}s^{k-1/2}(1-s)^{a_{d}-1}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle(s=u^{2},2\,\mathrm{d}u=s^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}s)

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{d,k}t^{2k},

where

\displaystyle c_{d,k}\ :=\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2k)!}\,\frac{B(k+1/2,a_{d})}{B(1/2,a_{d})}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2k)!}\,\frac{\Gamma(k+1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)}\,\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(k+d/2)}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{2^{-(2k-1)}\,\Gamma(2k)}{(2k)!\,\Gamma(k)}\,\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(k+d/2)}

\displaystyle=\

where the second step is a consequence of Legendre’s well-known duplication formula for the gamma function.

\displaystyle G_{d}^{\prime}(t)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2kc_{d,k}t^{2k-1}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2k2^{-2k}\,\Gamma(d/2)}{k!\,\Gamma(k+d/2)}\,t^{2k-1}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{-2k+1}\,\Gamma(d/2)}{(k-1)!\,\Gamma(k+d/2)}\,t^{2k-1}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{t\,\Gamma(d/2)}{2\,\Gamma(d/2+1)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{-2(k-1)}\,\Gamma((d+2)/2)}{(k-1)!\,\Gamma((k-1)+(d+2)/2)}\,t^{2(k-1)}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{t}{d}\,G_{d+2}(t).

∎

Computation of
G_{d}(t)
,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(U_{t})
and
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(U_{t}^{2})
for large values of
t
.

For large values of
t
, computing
G_{d}(t)
,
G_{d+2}(t)
and
G_{d+4}(t)
via their series expansion becomes problematic. Here we resort to the following approximation formulae.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{z}=t\boldsymbol{v}
with
t>0
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Then as
t\to\infty
and uniformly in
\boldsymbol{v}
,

\displaystyle\gamma(\boldsymbol{z})\

\displaystyle=\ \log\Bigl(\frac{2^{a_{d}-1}\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)}\Bigr)+t-a_{d}\log(t)-\frac{a_{d}(a_{d}-1)}{2t}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{2t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{z})\

\displaystyle=\ \Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}}{t}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{2t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr)\Bigr)\,\boldsymbol{v},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{z})\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{a_{d}}{t^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr)\,\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}+\frac{1}{t}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr)(\boldsymbol{I}_{d}-\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}).

The formulae in this lemma, without the terms
O(t^{-2})
, provide excellent approximations as soon as
t=\|\boldsymbol{z}\|
is larger than, say,
100d
.

For the proof of Lemma
1
, we use a general result about expansions of moments for distributions on
(-1,1)
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
h:(-1,1)\to\mathbb{R}
be integrable such that for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and constants
a>0
and
b_{0},\ldots,b_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,

h(1-r)\ =\ \sum_{j=0}^{k}b_{j}r^{a+j-1}+O(r^{a+k})

as
r\downarrow 0
. Then, for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u)^{\ell}e^{tu}h(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\ =\ e^{t}t^{-(\ell+a)}\Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{k}b_{j}\Gamma(\ell+a+j)t^{-j}+O(t^{-(k+1)})\Bigr)

as
t\to\infty
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

We may write

\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u)^{\ell}e^{tu}h(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\ =\ e^{t}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u)^{\ell}e^{-t(1-u)}h(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\ =\ e^{t}\int_{0}^{2}r^{\ell}e^{-tr}h(1-r)\,\mathrm{d}r.

For any fixed
\delta\in(0,2)
,

\int_{0}^{2}r^{\ell}e^{-tr}h(1-r)\,\mathrm{d}r\ =\ \int_{0}^{\delta}r^{\ell}e^{-tr}h(1-r)\,\mathrm{d}r+R_{1}(\delta,t)

with

|R_{1}(\delta,t)|\ \leq\ 2^{\ell}\int_{-1}^{1}|h(u)|\,\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-t\delta}\ =\ O(e^{-t\delta}).

Moreover,

\int_{0}^{\delta}r^{\ell}e^{-tr}h(1-r)\,\mathrm{d}r\ =\ \sum_{j=0}^{k}b_{j}\int_{0}^{\delta}r^{\ell+a+j-1}e^{-tr}\,\mathrm{d}r+R_{2}(\delta,t),

where

|R_{2}(\delta,t)|\ \leq\ D(\delta)\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\ell+a+k}e^{-tr}\,\mathrm{d}r\ =\ D(\delta)\Gamma(\ell+a+k+1)t^{-(\ell+a+k+1)},

and

D(\delta)\ :=\ \sup_{r\in(0,\delta)}r^{-(a+k)}\Bigl|h(1-r)-\sum_{j=0}^{k}b_{j}r^{a+j-1}\Bigr|

is finite for sufficiently small
\delta>0
.

Finally,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta}r^{\ell+a+j-1}e^{-tr}\,\mathrm{d}r\

\displaystyle=\ t^{-(\ell+a+j)}\int_{0}^{t\delta}y^{\ell+a+j-1}e^{-y}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\ \Gamma(\ell+a+j)t^{-(\ell+a+j)}+O(t^{-(\ell+a+j)}e^{-\delta t/2}),

because for any
m>0
and a random variable
Y
with distribution
\mathrm{Gamma}(m,1)
, it follows from Markov’s inequality that

\int_{t\delta}^{\infty}y^{m-1}e^{-y}\,\mathrm{d}y\ =\ \Gamma(m)\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(G_{m}\geq t\delta)\ \leq\ \Gamma(m)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(e^{G_{m}/2})e^{-t\delta/2}\ =\ \Gamma(m)2^{m}e^{-t\delta/2}.

∎

Lemma
2
will be applied to the particular density
h_{d}
, noting that

\displaystyle h_{d}(1-r)\

\displaystyle=\ C_{d}(2r-r^{2})^{a_{d}-1}

\displaystyle=\ 2^{a_{d}-1}C_{d}r^{a_{d}-1}(1-r/2)^{a_{d}-1}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{2^{a_{d}-1}\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(a_{d})\Gamma(1/2)}r^{a_{d}-1}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{2}r+\frac{[a_{d}-1]_{2}}{8}r^{2}+O(r^{3})\Bigr)

(11)

as
r\downarrow 0
. Here and throughout this section, we use the notation
[s]_{j}:=\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}(s-i)
for real numbers
s
and integers
j\geq 1
. Applying Lemma
2
to
h_{d}
with the latter expansion leads to expansions for
G_{d}(t)
and
\log G_{d}(t)
.

Corollary 3
.

As
t\to\infty
,

G_{d}(t)\ =\ \frac{2^{a_{d}-1}\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)}\,e^{t}t^{-a_{d}}\Bigl(1-\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{2t}+\frac{[a_{d}+1]_{4}}{8t^{2}}+O(t^{-3})\Bigr)

and

\log G_{d}(t)\ =\ \log\Bigl(\frac{2^{a_{d}-1}\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)}\Bigr)+t-a_{d}\log(t)-\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{2t}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{2t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr).

This corollary implies the first expansion in Lemma
1
.

Proof of Corollary
3
.

By means of (
11
) we can apply Lemma
2
with
h=h_{d}
,
a=a_{d}
,
k=2
and

\displaystyle(b_{0},b_{1},b_{2})\

\displaystyle=\ 2^{a_{d}-1}C_{d}\Bigl(1,-\frac{a_{d}-1}{2},\frac{[a_{d}-1]_{2}}{8}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{2^{a_{d}-1}\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(a_{d})\Gamma(1/2)}\Bigl(1,-\frac{a_{d}-1}{2},\frac{[a_{d}-1]_{2}}{8}\Bigr).

Then Lemma
2
implies that

\displaystyle G_{d}(t)\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{2^{a_{d}-1}\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(a_{d})\Gamma(1/2)}\,e^{t}t^{-a_{d}}\Bigl(1-\frac{\Gamma(a_{d}+1)(a_{d}-1)}{2t}+\frac{\Gamma(a_{d}+2)[a_{d}-1]_{2}}{8t^{2}}+O(t^{-3})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{2^{a_{d}-1}\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)}\,e^{t}t^{-a_{d}}\Bigl(1-\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{2t}+\frac{[a_{d}+1]_{4}}{8t^{2}}+O(t^{-3})\Bigr)

where we used the identities
\Gamma(a_{d}+1)/\Gamma(a_{d})=a_{d}
and
\Gamma(a_{d}+2)/\Gamma(a_{d})=[a_{d}+1]_{2}
. The previous and all subsequent expansions are meant as
t\to\infty
. Moreover,

\log G_{d}(t)\ =\ \log\Bigl(\frac{2^{a_{d}-1}\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)}\Bigr)+t-a_{d}\log(t)+\log\Bigl(1-\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{2t}+\frac{[a_{d}+1]_{4}}{8t^{2}}+O(t^{-3}\Bigr),

and the standard expansion
\log(1+x)=x-x^{2}/2+O(x^{3})
as
x\to 0
implies that

\displaystyle\log\Bigl(1-\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{2t}+\frac{[a_{d}+1]_{4}}{8t^{2}}+O(t^{-3})\Bigr)\

\displaystyle=\ -\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{2t}+\frac{[a_{d}+1]_{4}}{8t^{2}}-\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}^{2}}{8t^{2}}+O(t^{-3})

\displaystyle=\ -\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{2t}-\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{4t^{2}}+O(t^{-3})

\displaystyle=\ -\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{2t}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{2t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr),

because
[a_{d}+1]_{4}-[a_{d}]_{2}^{2}=-2[a_{d}]_{2}
.
∎

Another consequence are expansions for moments of
U_{t}
. Precisely, we can express moments of
U_{t}
in terms of moments of
1-U_{t}
, and the latter may be approximated by means of Lemma
2
.

Corollary 4
.

As
t\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(U_{t})\

\displaystyle=\ 1-\frac{a_{d}}{t}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{2t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(U_{t})\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{a_{d}}{t^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr),

\displaystyle\frac{1-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(U_{t}^{2})}{d-1}\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{t}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr).

This corollary, applied to (
8
) and (
9
), yields the second and third expansion in Lemma
1
.

Proof of Corollary
4
.

We apply Lemma
2
with
h=h_{d}
,
a=a_{d}
,
k=1
and
(b_{0},b_{1})=C(1,\tilde{b}_{1})
for some constant
C>0
and
\tilde{b}_{1}=-(a_{d}-1)/2
. Note that Lemma
2
implies that for any integer
\ell\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[(1-U_{t})^{\ell}]\

\displaystyle=\ \int_{-1}^{1}(1-u)^{\ell}e^{tu}h_{d}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\Bigr/\int_{-1}^{1}e^{tu}h_{d}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\ \frac{\Gamma(a_{d}+\ell)}{\Gamma(a_{d})\,t^{\ell}}\,\frac{1+\tilde{b}_{1}(\ell+a_{d})t^{-1}+O(t^{-2})}{1+\tilde{b}_{1}a_{d}t^{-1}+O(t^{-2})}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{[a_{d}+\ell-1]_{\ell}}{t^{\ell}}\Bigl(1+\frac{\tilde{b}_{1}\ell}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{[a_{d}+\ell-1]_{\ell}}{t^{\ell}}\Bigl(1-\frac{\ell(a_{d}-1)}{2t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr),

Specifically, for
\ell=1,2
we obtain the expansions

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(1-U_{t})\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{a_{d}}{t}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{2t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr),

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[(1-U_{t})^{2}]\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{[a_{d}+1]_{2}}{t^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr),

and this implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(U_{t})\

\displaystyle=\ \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[(1-U_{t})^{2}]-[\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(1-U_{t})]^{2}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{[a_{d}+1]_{2}}{t^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr)-\frac{a_{d}^{2}}{t^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{2t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{[a_{d}+1]_{2}}{t^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr)-\frac{a_{d}^{2}}{t^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{a_{d}}{t^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}-1}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr),

\displaystyle 1-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(U_{t}^{2})\

\displaystyle=\ 2\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(1-U_{t})-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[(1-U_{t})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\ \frac{2a_{d}}{t}-\frac{[a_{d}]_{2}}{t^{2}}-\frac{[a_{d}+1]_{2}}{t^{2}}+O(t^{-3})

\displaystyle=\ \frac{2a_{d}}{t}-\frac{2a_{d}^{2}}{t^{2}}+O(t^{-3})

\displaystyle=\ \frac{d-1}{t}\Bigl(1-\frac{a_{d}}{t}+O(t^{-2})\Bigr),

because
2a_{d}=d-1
.
∎

Appendix B
Derivatives of the negative log-likelihood functions

In the regression settings of Section
3
, suppose that each function
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}
is equal to
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{k})_{k=1}^{d}
with all components
f_{k}
belonging to the same finite-dimensional linear space
\mathcal{F}^{o}
of functions
f^{o}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. If
f^{o}_{1},\ldots,f^{o}_{r}
is a basis of
\mathcal{F}^{o}
, then any function
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}
can be represented as

\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\ =\ \boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})

with a parameter matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x}):=(f^{o}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}))_{j=1}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. This leads to the negative log-likelihood function
L:\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\to\mathbb{R}
,

L(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\ :=\ \ell(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o})\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\,\bigl(\gamma(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}))-\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\bigr).

Here
W_{i}=1
for a parametric GLM, and
W_{i}=w_{\boldsymbol{x}_{o}}(X_{i})
for the local GLMs. Since
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{z})
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{z})
are the gradient and Hessian matrix of
\gamma
at
\boldsymbol{z}
, for
\boldsymbol{\Theta},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
,

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\Theta}+\boldsymbol{\Delta})\ =\

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\Theta})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\,(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}))-\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}

\displaystyle+\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\,\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})+O(\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{3})

as
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\to 0
. Here
\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm of a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
. If we define
\langle\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\rangle=\operatorname{\mathrm{trace}}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B})
for matrices
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}
of the same size, then
\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}=\langle\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{A}\rangle^{1/2}
, and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\,(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}))-\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\ =\ \langle\boldsymbol{\Delta},\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\rangle

with the gradient matrix

\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\ :=\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\,(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}))-\boldsymbol{Y}_{\!\!i})\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}^{\top}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}.

(12)

Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=[\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}]
with
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\rm sym}
,

\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\ =\ \mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})^{\top}\bigl(\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\,\mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}),

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\

\displaystyle:=\ [\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{2}^{\top},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r}^{\top}]^{\top}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{dr},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\otimes\Sigma\

\displaystyle:=\ \begin{bmatrix}f_{1}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{1}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}&f_{1}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{2}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}&\ldots&f_{1}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{r}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\\
f_{2}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{1}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}&f_{2}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{2}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}&\ldots&f_{2}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{r}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f_{r}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{1}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}&f_{r}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{2}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}&\ldots&f_{r}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{r}^{o}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\end{bmatrix}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{dr\times dr}_{\rm sym}

Hence, the Hessian matrix for
L(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
, viewed as a function of
\mathrm{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
, equals

\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\,\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})\boldsymbol{F}^{o}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i})^{\top}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{X}_{\!i}).

(13)

These formulae are useful to minimize
L(\cdot)
over
\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
via a Newton–Raphson procedure.
