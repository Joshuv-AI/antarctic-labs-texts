Surrounding space associations affect attentional orienting and visual discrimination in frontal field
Authors: Rébaï Soret, Pom Charras, Vsevolod Peysakhovich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAttentional orienting is essential for the optimal processing of sensory information. In orienting studies, the visual modality related to surrounding space is often overlooked because visual cues are not directly available to individuals in those regions. In this study, we propose to address the methodological challenge of presenting visual cues associated with surrounding space by adapting the classic Posner paradigm to the use of multiple screens. Participants performed a discrimination task (orientation of a Gabor patch) with predictable cues on the main screen, where different areas of this screen corresponded to computer monitors placed around the participant environment. Four configurations were tested: without additional screens (main screen only), with screens positioned in front of the person (up and down to the right and left), and with screens arranged around the person (front and rear to the right and left). The condition without additional screens serves as a reference. The analysis of reaction times reveals a stronger attentional orienting downwards in the reference condition (downward orienting bias). In terms of accuracy, the data suggest a prioritization of the stimuli bound to the lower-associated space when screens are placed in front (bias towards the bottom) and to the front- associated space when screens are arranged around (bias towards the front). These results illustrate our ability to orient ourselves towards information located in the surrounding environment, the effectiveness of this orientation being influenced by the region of space to be processed. All in all, our work opens new perspectives for studying how surrounding space influence attentional orienting through an innovative method derived from the Posner paradigm. Introduction

Human cognitive system continually receives much sensory information. All the information available in the surroundings cannot be processed. For efficient information processing, it is necessary to select critical details and to ignore irrelevant distractors. This selection is particularly critical in situations that require rapid information gathering and efficient response, such as high-speed driving or emergencies. Attentional orienting reflects our ability to prioritize information contained or likely to appear in a specific region of visual space to optimize its processing1. This attentional shift improves, under specific conditions, the processing speed and accuracy. This shift is not restricted to the visual modality but also occurs in other perceptual modalities such as hearing or haptic perception2, and between perceptual modalities (crossmodal) under certain conditions3. In many activities, we have the choice to prioritize one sensory modality instead of another. For example, car drivers or aircraft pilots can rely on engine noise or visual information provided by the cockpit’s multiple instruments. However, it appears that we tend to rely more on visual input, an effect known as Colavita visual dominance4,5.

We represent the environment around us using vision, audition, and touch. Starting from two-dimensional space in front of us6, through adding the depth dimension7 to multisensory integration in peripersonal and extrapersonal space8, theoretical knowledge in 3D attentional orienting is still lacking. One can indeed wonder whether we can orient visual attention in rear space? Since visual cues are not available to humans in the rear space, multisensory integration is studied in audio-tactile modalities9,10. However, it is possible to have an internal visual representation of an object behind us without visual feedback. As the internal representation of auditory information11,12, working memory allows us to have a stable multi-sensory representation of the space around us. Attentional orienting and visual working memory are complex intertwined mechanisms, and it is possible to direct spatial attention to internal representations13. This internal attentional orienting facilitates search within the working memory. We believe that this mechanism can also account for situational awareness in the rear space and spatial orienting to “invisible” cues behind us, and, therefore, modulate human behavior.

Furthermore, it has been shown that visual context strongly influences early visual cortical areas even in the absence of specific stimulation14,and that attention, based on object characteristic (color, shape, etc.), can be deployed even in unstimulated parts of the visual field15. Finally, it has been suggested that there is an independent coding of invisible egocentric space (beyond the field of view) and visual space (within the field of view) which may allow for a flexible combination of the two16. All these studies suggest that it is possible to direct attention outside of visual space and in particular, behind us. Therefore, we can suppose that it is possible to use visual cues in the field of view to direct human attention in the surrounding space.

Guiding people’s attention across the entire perceived visual field has been addressed in visual search studies17,18. A recent study investigated the spatiotemporal characteristics of basic 360° attention19. The authors used a visual search task for stimuli that could appear at different locations in the space around the subject (360°). The results show that attention is most efficiently deployed in the useful field of view, then progressively deteriorates with increasing eccentricity, and is least efficiently distributed in the rear space. This study shows the importance of considering the whole visual field of the subject called the “field of regard”. The absence of control of the subjects’ movements, who could move their head and body freely, does not allow us to attribute the results exclusively to attentional processes. When the target is outside the visual field, it takes longer to find it, however, we cannot infer mechanisms of attentional orientation without constraining body and head movements.

A classic paradigm to study visual attentional orienting, the Posner task, was proposed in 19801. Two locations in front of a participant can be cued while the subject is fixating a central cross. Classical results indicate faster responses after a valid cue compared to neutral and invalid ones. This difference between valid and invalid (or neutral) trials is an indicator of cue effectiveness in orienting attention. If the response times to valid trials are lower than to invalid trials, then the cue is effective in directing attention. If there is no difference, then the cue fails to orient attention. And if the response times to valid trials are higher than to invalid trials, the cue disrupts orienting, an effect known as inhibition of return (IOR). The effect is attributed to the shift of covert attention to the cued location. Different factors, such as cue predictivity, position, type (endogenous/exogenous), the time interval between the cue and the target, modulate this effect20. All of the studies used standard computer screens in front of the participants with targets located within the oculomotor range. However, visual attention is not limited by our saccadic reach21, and it is important to scrutinize the limits of visual attention. Posner’s task can be extended to a more ecological environment with natural stimuli and unconstrained head movements. For instance, Soret et al.22 showed that Posner’s classical results apply in a virtual environment with a head-mounted display.

The investigation of visual attentional orienting in the rear space faces obvious methodological constraints: the impossibility of providing visual cues in the rear space or presenting visual targets without introducing head and body movements. Few examples approaching this research question using crossmodal orienting exist. For instance, Ho and Spence23 used auditory cues (warning signals or verbal indications) and visual targets to direct the attention of car drivers. The rear information was visible through a rear-view mirror in front of the driver and the front information through the windshield. Thus, all targets were presented in the front space. The results showed that frontal cues induced faster response times. Spence and Lee24 obtained different results using the "orthogonal cueing paradigm" with spatialized auditory cues and frontal lateralized (left or right) targets. The results indicated a processing benefit when the cue predicts the lateral position (left/right) regardless of its transverse location (front/back). The authors suggested that auditory cues did not necessarily draw attention to the orienting direction but rather to a specific visual field (left/right). However, in their study, the target could only appear in front of the subjects. Therefore, the cue’s transversal spatial location (front/back) is irrelevant to the task and might be ignored by the participants.

It is still unknown how human attentional orienting operates when a person’s attention requires shifting out of the visual field, especially, in the rear space. We know that there are differences in crossmodal interaction in front and rear spaces using auditory cues (see25, for a review), but many questions remain open. The methodological conundrum is that it is difficult to present visual cues that associate with real objects in rear space. We propose to address this challenging issue with a modified version of Posner’s paradigm using multiple screens. It could open up a new field of investigation in the study of attentional orienting, the rear-space orienting, to further investigate the neural bases of orienting mechanisms.

While some authors suggested using a rear-view mirror to study the processing of attentional orienting in the rear space23,26, its content was never associated with real-world information behind the participant. We propose to use visual cues and visual targets presented on a central screen in front but representing four screens around the participant in the horizontal plane (front and rear, left and right). The participants will perform a discrimination task (Gabor patch orientation) with predictable cues. The sense of spatial location will be further induced in the subjects by asking them to verify whether the content of the corresponding position on the central screen is identical to a monitor around them. Two control experiments with Surrounding Screens in the vertical plane and without Surrounding Screens would ensure that the results are attributable to the spatial aspect of the modified Posner paradigm and not to other factors.

We hypothesized that the covert orienting of attention to frontal targets would be modulated by whether those targets were bound to the rear versus the front surrounding direction. The shifting of attention in the rear-associated space could involve an additional treatment step indexed by the subjects’ reaction times. To test this assumption, following the additive factor method27, if two factors affect subject response times independently (main effect without interaction), then it can be assumed that they represent two stages of treatment and involve processes that operate separately28. If attentional orienting in front- and rear-referenced spaces includes different processing steps, we should observe a principal effect of validity and a principal effect of the orienting direction (front/rear).

Average response time and accuracy should be better in response to valid cues than to invalid cues, especially if the target appears in the front-referenced space rather than in the rear-referenced space (additive effect). However, this additive effect should only be observed for the experiment with horizontal Surrounding Screens. Thus, we should observe a significant interaction between our three experimental factors: validity of the cue (valid/invalid), orienting direction (front/rear or top/bottom), and Surrounding Screens (horizontal/vertical/without). This interaction should show a Cue Validity effect in all conditions2. However, the orienting direction effect should be observed between the front- and rear-referenced spaces when horizontal screens around the subject are associated with the main screen. With faster response times and greater accuracy in front-referenced than in the rear-referenced. When there are no screens, or when the screens are all placed in front of the subject, we should observe an orienting bias toward the bottom, as observed many times in the literature29,30. We therefore expect an orienting direction effect between top and bottom, with faster response times and greater accuracy at the bottom than at the top (See Table 1 for more details about the hypothesis).

Thus, when we perceive information in front of us, the spatial framework used is an egocentric frame of reference; the information can be directly deduced from our own position. By contrast, when we perceive information through a mirror, we must adopt an allocentric frame of reference with the mirror itself as the reference point to determine the object’s position in relation to the mirror. Then we must switch back to an egocentric frame of reference to deduce the object’s position in relation to ourselves. Thus, if when directing our attention in front of us, we only use an egocentric frame, and when information is perceived through a mirror we have to go through an allocentric frame to determine the position of the object to be processed, then an additional processing step is added to the processing steps which should impact the processing of the target. However, in our study, mirror information intervention is also present in the front space. Therefore, it is possible that this manipulation prevented our experiment from highlighting processing differences between the front and rear spaces. Therefore, there would not necessarily be a difference in the process of orienting between the front and rear spaces. Rather, it is a difference at the level of selecting the spatial representation necessary to select the region of the visual space to be prioritized (especially if there is a main effect of the presence of a screen around the person)

Methods

Ethics information

Following the Declaration of Helsinki, the written Informed consent was obtained from all the study before the experiment. The study was approved by Comité d'Éthique de la Recherche (CER) of the University of Toulouse under number 2022–539. Participants have not received any contributions for their participation.

Design

Materials

The modifier Posner task was developed using MATLAB and Psychtoolbox. We use the remote eye-tracking system EyeLink 1000 Plus with a 2 kHz sampling rate, 0.25°-0.5° accuracy, and a resolution of 0.01° RMS.

The screens surrounding participants were four Dell P2417H2 of 23.8″ with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels and 60 Hz refresh rate. The main screen was a BENQ Zowie XL2430 of 24″ with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels and a refresh rate of 144 Hz.

The answers were given via a Black Box Toolkit response box. Participants respond with the left hand for the left key and with the right hand for the right key. The hands were still on the table and were not in the direct visual field of view. The key assignments were counterbalanced between the subjects.

The computer for stimuli presentation was an SR Research PC 11,807 working under Windows 7. The eye-tracker has a dedicated computer for data acquisition Dell OptiPlex XE2 SFF connected to the computer used for the MATLAB program.

Stimuli

The targets were Gabor patches produced with an online patch generator. With an orientation of 0° (vertical) or 90° (horizontal) and with a black background and white line. The Gabor patch characteristics were the following : Size: 96 pixels, Envelope: Gaussian, Standard deviation: 12 pixels, Frequency (in cycles/ pixel): 0.1, Phase (in cycles (0–1)): 0, Background color (RGB values): 128, 128, 128, Color 1 (RGB values): 255, 255, 255, Color 2 (RGB values) : 0, 0, 0. The stimuli were displayed on the screen according to the pixel coordinates (jn,hn) provided in Fig. 3. The boxes containing the stimuli have a size of 200 × 200 pixels (7° visual angle). The background was black, and the stimuli were white.

Coordinates in pixels and position of the squares that will contain the targets. Coordinates in pixels and position of the squares that has contain the targets. The fixation point was positioned at the center of the screen (A). The squares were defined by their upper left corner (jn) coordinates (x,y) and their lower right corner (hn) coordinates (x, y). Each square is 600 pixels away from its two closest neighbors.

Procedure

In the experimental phase, the subjects had to identify as quickly and as accurately as possible the occurrence of a visual stimulus with the help of a previously provided cue. This visual stimulus could have appeared in one of the 4 randomly numbered boxes from 1 to 4 (Fig. 1). Each box was associated with a screen located around the subject (front left, front right, rear right, or rear left, cf. Fig. 2). For half of the participants, the top boxes were associated with the screens in front of the participants, and the bottom boxes with the screens behind (Fig. 4). For the other half, the top boxes were associated with the screens behind, and the bottom boxes with the screens in front (Fig. 5). The cue provided to the participant was an arrow in the boxes’ direction where the target was expected to appear. After the target appeared, if the participant was instructed to “check” (30% of the time), he/she had to look at the target on the screen around him/her, which was associated with the box where the target appeared on the main screen (mirrored information). Then, he/she had to answer again according to this target. The front screens were hidden by occlusive panels to ensure that the target on these screens was not directly visible to the participants and therefore required a head movement to be seen, in the same way as for the rear screens.

Position of the screens in relation to the subject for the horizontal phase. The participant was sitting on a chair. The main screen was positioned 57 cm in front of the participant. The Surrounding Screens were all equally spaced from the subject.

Position of the screens in relation to the subject for the vertical phase. The participant was sitting on a chair in front of all screens (57 cm).

Before the experimental phase, a training session with systematic checks allowed each subject to become familiar with the cues and associate them with a specific spatial location around them. The cue had a predictivity of 75%. In 75% of the trials, the cue was valid and indicated the target’s correct position, while in 25% of the cases, the cue indicated a wrong position of the target.

All the trials had been randomized. When the cue was invalid, the target appeared half the time in the box vertically opposite to the indicated box and half the time in the box horizontally opposite. The target never appeared in a position diametrically opposite to the box indicated by the cue. The targets were a Gabor patch with vertical or horizontal lines associated with a specific answer button on the answer pad. The answer buttons were counterbalanced between subjects. Response time recordings were relative to the target that appeared on the main screen. The response times to the target on the Surrounding Screens were not recorded. They were intended to create a mental association between the target present on the central screen and the target present on the Surrounding Screens. In other words, to create a spatial awareness of the situation. The target on the Surrounding Screens, during a “check trial”, was identical to the target on the main screen in 80% of the cases. The information was therefore reliable enough to be interpreted by the participants as a reflection of the information on the main screen, while requiring special attention by the participant, because this information could differ in rare cases. Indeed, if the information was always reliable (100% identical), then participants might simply not perform a real verification of the information on the Surrounding Screens. Whereas if it was only identical half the time, it did not truly reflect the information on the main screen and would be judged as different information.

The time interval between the end of cue occurrence and the beginning of target appearance (ISI) was 500 ms. We chose this interval because it allows the observation of an endogenous orientation’s effects, usually observed as occurring at ISIs of 300 to 1000 ms (see20). We did not manipulate the ISI to avoid intricate experimental design.

To validate the hypothesis that there was a difference in target processing between the front- and rear-referenced spaces, a second experimental phase was incorporated. In this phase, all the Surrounding Screens were placed in front of the person on a vertical plane (and no longer horizontal). The procedure remained identical to the experimental phase described above. The main difference was that the Surrounding Screens were no longer associated with the front and rear space but with the upper and lower space (see Fig. 5).

The horizontal experimental phase of the main experiment consisted of 168 trials per participant: 126 valid trials (63 front and 63 rear trials) and 42 invalid trials (21 front and 21 rear trials). The trials were divided into 3 blocks of 56 trials including all experimental conditions, with a break between the blocks if the participant wished. The vertical phase had the same number of trials. The training phase consisted of 24 trials per participant: 18 valid trials (9 front and 9 rear trials) and 6 invalid trials (3 front and 3 rear trials). The vertical experimental phase was the same as the horizontal experimental phase, with the only difference being that the front and rear trials became top and bottom trials. The order of the experimental phases was counterbalanced: half of the participants performed the vertical phase followed by the horizontal phase, and the other half performed the horizontal phase followed by the vertical phase. The control phase without screens was performed before the experimental phases. The number of trials was the same as the other phases. Altogether, 528 trials were performed by each participant. The number of trials was calculated following the recommendations of Chica et al. in 2014 in their article: "The Spatial Orienting paradigm: How to design and interpret spatial attention experiments".

Eye-tracking was used to check that participants did not move their eyes before the target appeared (a classic Posner task in the covered version). A drift control was performed at the beginning of each block to correct any change of placement by the participants when checking the information on the screens around them.

A posteriori analysis will be performed on eye movements to explore whether any difference in saccadic latency or fixation time can indicate the involvement of orienting processes in the front space or the rear space. It would then be possible to determine by eye measurements whether individuals are focused on their front space or their rear space. This eye-tracking could also help us to identify any intervention of overt attention in our study. Due to a technical error after drift correction, the eye-tracking recording was stopped. The planned analysis of eye saccades could not be carried out.

Results

Filter and exclusion criteria

For all phases: response times below 50 ms and response times greater than 3 standard deviations above average were discarded from the data analysis. Besides, we assume that participants with an average accuracy of less than 80% have failed the task and were therefore excluded from the analyses. No data will be replaced; if a participant’s data contains too many missing values according to our exclusion criteria, the participant will be excluded from the analysis.

Analysis

As the experimental designs were intra-subject designs, i.e., all participants were confronted with all the modalities of our experimental factors, the statistical analyses were all Repeated Measures Analysis of Variance (RM-ANOVA) on mean response time (RT) and accuracy (ACC). The statistical analyses were a three-way RM-ANOVA on subject response time (RT) and response accuracy (ACC) to observe the effects of the manipulated variables: Cue Validity (valid/invalid), Orienting Direction (top/bottom or front/rear), and Surrounding Screens (without/vertical/horizontal). HSD Tukey tests were used for post-hoc comparisons.

It should be noted that the term “Orienting Direction” is defined based on the condition of Surrounding Screens. In the “without screens” condition, “top” and “bottom” refer to the upper and lower parts of the main screen. In the “vertical” condition, “top” and “bottom” correspond to the associated screens placed above and below. For the “horizontal” condition, “front” and “rear” related to the associated screens placed in front of and in rear of the participants, respectively.

Finally, to better understand the influence of the attentional direction and the presence of associated screens on attention orientation, we also conducted RM-ANOVA on the Cueing effect. The cueing effect is the difference in response times or accuracies between valid and invalid trials; i.e., cueing effect = RT invalid – RT valid. (This analysis was not planned in stage 1).

The repeated measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) table for reaction times (RTs) shows results both with and without correction (see Supplementary Information, Table 1.3). As expected, the analysis revealed a main effect of Cue Validity on mean response time, F(1, 69) = 150.474, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.294. Participants’ reaction times were significantly faster when the cue was valid rather than invalid (MD = 75 ms). We also observed a main effect of Surrounding Screens, F(1.639, 113.117) = 25.188, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.119. The post-hoc comparison shows that the mean reaction times are significantly slower without screens than with vertical screens (p < 0.001, MD = 55 ms) or horizontal screens (p < 0.001, MD = 46 ms). The difference between vertical and horizontal screens is not significant (p = 0.248).

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate how covert attentional orienting to frontal targets is influenced by their association with the surrounding space, in particular front- versus rear-referenced mappings. For this, we used a modified version of the Posner paradigm under three conditions: without associated screens, with screens associated in the vertical plane (in front), and with screens in the horizontal plane (around). Participants were asked to perform a Posner task with an endogenous cue (predictive directional arrow) to discriminate a target (vertical or horizontal Gabor patch) as quickly and accurately as possible. The cue correctly indicated the target’s position in 80% of the trials (valid cue) and was incorrect in 20% of the trials (invalid cue). In the screen conditions, subjects had to mentally map the squares on the central screen to the corresponding screens around them, verifying the target perceived in the square on the central screen and in the associated peripheral screen during specific ‘check’ trials. The target therefore appeared both on the central screen and on its associated peripheral screen, making it clear to participants that both corresponded to the same linked piece of information.

Effect on response time

The results show a main effect of cue validity on mean reaction times. Participants detected the target faster when the cue indicated the correct position of the target rather than an incorrect one. This cue validity effect demonstrates that the cue allowed participants to orient their attention to the indicated location, and that they successfully used it to respond more quickly. We also observe a main effect of the presence of screens on reaction times. On average, subjects are slower to respond when there are no associated screens compared to when there are (either vertically or horizontally). This effect is likely due to a learning effect, as participants always start with the control condition. Response times are therefore necessarily slower for the first block, while participants familiarize themselves with the task, and then decrease progressively with each block. Another factor may also have contributed to the main effect of screen presence. In the control condition, the top and bottom positions on the main screen are fixed, whereas in the experimental conditions these same positions are associated with surrounding screens through different mappings across participants. For half of the participants, the top boxes of the main screen were linked to the upper and front surrounding screens, and the bottom boxes to the lower and rear screens; for the other half, this mapping was reversed. Thus, differences between the control and experimental conditions may reflect not only the presence or absence of surrounding screens, but also this structural difference in spatial coding. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our design, since no equivalent counterbalancing is possible in the absence of surrounding screens.

Finally, we observe a three-way interaction between our experimental factors, as expected. This interaction shows a significant simple effect of validity in all conditions, in line with our initial hypothesis. Participants achieve significantly faster reaction times when the cue accurately predicts the target’s location across all conditions. However, the simple effect of orienting direction is only significant in the valid condition without screens. Participants detect the target faster downwards than upwards only when correctly oriented. No differences were observed in other conditions, especially between front and rear-referenced spaces in the horizontal screen’s configuration, contrary to expectations. To recall, we expected a simple effect of orienting direction (front vs. rear) in the horizontal condition in both valid and invalid conditions. More specifically, we anticipated two simple effects: a simple effect of cue validity across all conditions and a simple effect of orienting direction only in the horizontal condition; with faster response times for valid forward cues rather than invalid backward cues (additive effect).

Studying the effect of cue validity on participants’ reaction times sheds further light on these unexpected results. The cue validity effect is calculated by subtracting the mean reaction time of valid trials from the mean reaction time of invalid trials. This measure therefore reflects the cue’s ability to orient attention. The larger this effect, the more the cue has helped participants to orient their attention to the indicated location; conversely, the smaller it is, the less effective the cue. In other words, the larger the cueing effect, the greater the orienting effect. This analysis reveals an effect of the presence of screens and the orienting direction on the cueing effect. Firstly, in the condition without screens, the cueing effect is larger when the orienting direction is in bottom compared to the top. The orienting effect on participants’ reaction times is then stronger when they are oriented towards the bottom rather than the top. These results are consistent with a bias in favour to the lower visual field, previously observed in literature29,30.

Secondly, in the condition with vertically associated screens (all in front), there is no longer a significant difference in the cueing effect on reaction times depending on the orienting direction (top and bottom). This absence of difference can be attributed to the experimental design, which counterbalanced the ‘top’ and ‘bottom’ areas of the main screen with the surrounding screens. Consequently, statistical analyses mix the ‘top’ and ‘bottom’ zones of the main screen within the ‘top’ and ‘bottom’ modalities of the surrounding screens. This mixing masks the downward bias observed in the condition without surrounding screens. Therefore, the mental association between the areas of the main screen and the surrounding screens did not generate a bias towards the lower surrounding screens, as one might have expected. Multiple explanations could be at play here: either the association did not work sufficiently, or the downward bias observed in the direct visual field does not generalize to situations in which the same visual information must be interpreted through its associated position in the surrounding space (i.e., when the target on the central screen is “relocated” cognitively to an upper, lower, front, or rear external screen). For the horizontal condition, the results are similar. Participants achieve a similar cueing effect in the front space as in the rear space. Therefore, the cue affects participants’ performance similarly in the front- referenced and rear- referenced directions, at least in terms of reaction times.

Effect on accuracy

The results on accuracy show a main effect of cue validity on subjects’ mean accuracy. Subjects better identify the target when the cue accurately predicted the target’s location compared to when it did not. However, the analysis of response accuracy shows a difference in accuracy based on several experimental factors. First, we note an interaction between cue validity and the presence of screens, which shows better accuracy when the cue accurately predicted the target’s location compared to when it did not, in conditions with screens (vertical and horizontal), but not in the condition without screens. It therefore seems that participants identify the target differently when they are accurately oriented compared to when they are not, only when screens are associated with the main screen. Moreover, we note a significant interaction between the presence of screens and the orienting direction which shows that subjects’ accuracy is higher in the rear-referenced space than in the front referenced space in the horizontal condition, but no difference is observed between top and bottom in the other conditions.

Lastly, an interaction among our three experimental factors is observed. However, the decomposition of this interaction does not reveal the expected results (additive effect, simple effect of Cue Validity, and simple effect of Orienting Direction). This interaction shows that the effect of cue validity on the mean accuracy of responses varies depending on the direction of orienting and the presence of screens. Participants achieve lower response accuracy on invalid trials only in the vertical condition for bottom-referenced targets, and in the horizontal condition for front-referenced targets. Participants therefore seem to make more identification errors when they are poorly oriented rather than accurately oriented, suggesting the presence of an identification cost specifically in these referenced directions — only at the bottom in the condition where the information on the main screen was associated with screens in front of them (vertical, top and bottom), and only at the front in the condition where the information on the main screen was associated with the surrounding screens (horizontal, front and rear). No other effect of cue validity on response accuracy is observed in the other conditions.

It would therefore seem that a bias towards the bottom and the front exists respectively in these two conditions. This interpretation is consistent with previous findings reporting a preferential processing of the lower visual field29,30 and a forward attentional bias, with faster and more efficient processing for front-referenced information compared to rear-referenced information32,33. In fact, in comparison to other conditions where the accuracy is similar in both cases. It would seem, then, that there is a processing cost impacting downward and forward target identification that does not exist upward and backward. Several explanations are possible. Firstly, we could assume that this impact on identification is due to an additional disorientation cost. Indeed, if a bias in orienting favors these regions of space, then attention may be more strongly oriented there. Thus, in case of poor orientation (invalid cue), the cost of reorientation is stronger. Participants may then prioritize processing speed over accuracy, resulting in a drop in accuracy. However, if attention is more strongly oriented in these areas of space, why don’t we observe it on response times and therefore detection? Moreover, why don’t these costs exist above and behind?

Another possible explanation could be due to the strength of the association between the main screens and the external screens and the induced cognitive load. Indeed, the results do not indicate a difference in terms of response accuracy based on the validity of the cue when there were no associated screens. However, no differences are observed either at the top or in the rear referenced space when screens are present. This might indicate that the integration of the main screen areas with the external screens in these conditions was not effective, leading us back to the results found in the baseline condition, which had no screens. Conversely, the association may have worked at the bottom and front, since these are spaces naturally favored by the participants (bias towards the bottom and the front). This effective association may have led to a higher cognitive load in these conditions. This cognitive load induced by the association may in turn have led to a stronger reorientation cost, impacting target discriminations.

A further possibility is that these selective drops in accuracy may be partly influenced by the congruence of the mappings used in the experimental phases. In the vertical and horizontal conditions, half of the participants experienced a congruent mapping (e.g., top of the main screen bound to the top external screen or to the front), while the other half experienced an incongruent mapping (e.g., top of the main screen bound to the bottom or to the rear). This incongruence may have introduced additional cognitive load, selectively reducing accuracy in the favored directions (downward and forward). Future studies should examine congruent versus incongruent mappings separately, in order to determine more precisely how mapping structure contributes to accuracy effects.

Conclusion

In summary, we observe an orienting effect (cueing effect) on response times, which exists independently of other experimental variables. Interestingly, this effect varies in strength based on the presence of additional screens and the direction of attention. Specifically, the cueing effect is significantly stronger when attention is oriented towards the bottom, as compared to the top, in the condition without screens. However, this directional bias is absent when screens are present, either vertically or horizontally arranged. In addition, we observe a main effect of cue validity on response accuracy, with lower accuracy in response to invalid cues than valid cues. However, a significant interaction among our three experimental factors shows that this effect only exists when attention is oriented downwards in the condition with associated vertical screens, and forwards when screens around the person are associated with the main screen. No effect of validity on accuracy is observed without associated screens, nor for bottom-referenced targets in the front-linked condition, nor for rear-referenced targets in the around-linked condition.

In conclusion, at the level of target detection, participants detect the target faster when they are accurately oriented compared to when they are poorly oriented, regardless of the presence or absence of additional screens and the specific region where their attention is directed. However, the strength of this cueing effect is notably higher when participants are oriented downwards compared to upwards, without screens only. On the other hand, at the level of target identification, overall good response accuracy is observed (average = 95%). However, in conditions with screens, a decrease in accuracy is observed to the bottom and to the front when participants are oriented in the wrong direction. Identification of the target is then weaker when participants have been oriented downwards and the target appears elsewhere when screens are associated on the vertical plane (top and bottom), and worse when they are oriented forwards when screens are associated on the horizontal plane (front and rear). Additionally, identification of the target remains high, and no difference is observed when participants are well or poorly oriented, at the top with vertical screens and at the rear with horizontal screens. These results suggest that participants did not consider the associated information in these spaces and prioritized the associations of the main screen zones that corresponded to the bottom screens when they were all placed in front, and to the front screens when they were placed around them.

In other words, when analyzing response times, we only observe a bias towards the bottom in the condition without screens. Additionally, when analyzing accuracy, we find a bias towards the bottom and a bias towards the front in the condition with screens. However, these biases manifest themselves differently in our study. In the condition without screens, it manifests in a difference in the orientation of attention in the lower space (observed on the cueing effect). Conversely, in the condition with screens, the bias is manifested through the effective or ineffective mental association of information on the main screen with the external screens. Participants favor the association in naturally prioritized spaces (the bottom and the front) while neglecting the association in other spaces (the top and the rear). These results demonstrate that we do not only process information in our direct visual field without accounting for its relation to the surrounding space—meaning, our entire perceptual space, especially that which exists around us. Our study shows that we can orient our attention beyond our direct visual field by using ‘mirrored’ information, i.e., information perceived in front but connected to other spaces around us. However, the strength of these associations seems to depend on the peripheral space with which the information on the main screen is associated, with participants seeming to prioritize the space below and the space in front of themselves.

This study lays the groundwork for the fundamental exploration of 360-degree attentional orienting mechanisms. Considering this, it is now essential to further investigate attentional orienting differences in more depth. Specifically, it would be interesting to replicate this experiment with a simple detection task, to see if we can observe similar results to those obtained on accuracy. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to explore if the absence of an effect in the upper part of the peripheral field and at the rear is due to a weaker cognitive association between the main screen and the external screens. To this end, the number of ‘checks’ during the experiment could be increased to improve the strength of mental association, or longer training could be conducted beforehand. The number of screens and task difficulty could also be manipulated to refine results on accuracy or to explore the cost of cognitive load, as suggested. Finally, it would be interesting to explore whether the forward bias is stronger than the backward bias, and whether information perceived in front and upwards is processed faster than information perceived behind and downwards. Moreover, exploring the neural correlates could help better understand the cognitive mechanisms involved in the observed differences.

Once the mechanisms of 360-degree orientation are better understood, we can then design guidance systems that could direct an individual’s attention to any location in the surrounding space through an information association system, like navigation maps. After training designed to create associations between zones in the navigation system and other zones in the surrounding space, it would be possible to guide an individual toward the desired location. Moreover, our findings have practical implications for attention guidance in complex settings such as airplane cockpits or cars. For instance, a centralized display system could use a schematic representation of the vehicle to signal to the pilot or driver where to focus their attention. In a car, this could take the form of a “proximity radar” on the central screen, flashing to indicate the presence of an obstacle or pedestrian in a specific area around the vehicle. This would effectively guide attention without cognitively overloading the individual, taking advantage of our ability to associate visual information with different surrounding spaces. Such an approach could help optimize decision-making and responsiveness in scenarios where speed and precision are essential.
