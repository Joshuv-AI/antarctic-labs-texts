Estimating standard deviation via sample mean extended quantile estimation
Authors: Mediya Bawakhan Mrakhan, Tamás Kói
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is common practice in meta-analysis to estimate the mean and standard deviation (SD) when only the median and other ordered statistics are published in an included study. There are several methods for this purpose. Often, the advantages of including additional studies in the meta-analysis of means by using these methods outweigh the disadvantages of estimation errors. Sometimes, in addition to the median and other ordered statistics, the sample mean is also available, e.g. it can be displayed in box plots as additional information. In this case, only the standard deviation should be estimated.MethodsThe popular quantile estimation (QE) method was modified by incorporating the known mean into the standard deviation (SD) estimation. We analysed the performance of the original and the mean-extended quantile estimation (MEQE) methods with extensive simulations. A wide range of visual and quantitative tools, including average relative error (ARE) with and without absolute value and root mean squared error, were used to evaluate the performance of the methods across various simulated scenarios. ARE was calculated both against the theoretical and the sample SD. Besides simulations, we also compared the methods on real dataset.ResultsIn the scenario where only the median, lower and upper quartiles were used, for gamma Weibull and lognormal distributions, the mean-boosted version resulted in an absolute ARE decrease of 43%-65%, 28%-40%, and 8%-23%, respectively, depending on the sample size. For the normal distribution, the improvement was negligible (1%-3%). When both the QE and MEQE methods also used the minimum and maximum, their performance was essentially the same. Interestingly, in most cases, the joint performance was worse than the performance obtained without using the extreme values.ConclusionsWe incorporated the mean into the QE method. When only the median, lower and upper quartiles, and the mean are available, the MEQE method may provide more accurate SD estimates. This study may encourage the modification of other algorithms used in meta-analysis to incorporate additional information that may occasionally become available. The results suggest that the usage of the minimum and maximum may decrease the performance of both QE and MEQE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-025-02711-1.

Background

Both the mean and the median can be critical to the decision-making process, depending on the nature of the problem. The theoretical (or population) mean provides researchers with the long-term average of future measurements. The theoretical median, on the other hand, has the property that in the long run, approximately one half of the future observations will be larger and the other half smaller. Of course, for symmetric distributions, such as the normal distribution, the two quantities coincide. However, for non-symmetric distributions, such as the lognormal distribution, the difference between the mean and the median can be substantial.

In research studies, it is common practice to report only the mean and standard deviation when the assumption of normality appears valid; otherwise, typically only the median and the lower and upper quartiles are presented. This practice poses a challenge for comprehensive meta-analyses, which aim to combine results from previously published studies to strengthen evidence, identify controversies, or uncover meaningful patterns. The traditional approach to handling the problem is to estimate the mean and the standard deviation using median, quartiles and/or minimum and maximum, see [1, 2, 4, 7, 10, 12, 15, 18] and Table 1 below. Previous methods

MEQE Mean- Extended Quantile Estimation

The median, lower, and upper quartiles are frequently visualized in box-plot figures. Sometimes, the mean is also indicated in the published box plots. Thus, researchers can use image digitizer tools such as the Webplotdigitizer [14] to obtain the sample mean, even in studies that focus on the quartiles. However, the standard deviation is still needed for meta-analysis. The natural question arises: Can we improve the estimation of the standard deviation using the available mean? As far as we are aware, this is the first study to investigate this question.

Motivated by the above question, we slightly modified the method called quantile estimation (QE) introduced in McGrath et al. [12] by incorporating the mean into the optimization objective function. We evaluated the performance of this mean-extended QE (MEQE) method against the original QE approach through simulations. The results were evaluated using several metrics and visualization techniques using different families of distributions in the simulations. As the ultimate goal is to estimate the theoretical (population) standard deviation, comparisons were conducted in terms of both the theoretical SD and the sample SD. In addition, we also investigated the ability of the methods to correctly identify the underlying distribution family. The diverse evaluation approach provides novel simulation insights, even for the original QE method. Moreover, we also evaluated the performance on a real dataset.

Using the median alongside the lower and upper quartiles (Scenario S2) showed that both the QE and MEQE methods performed well. A comparison of the two methods clearly shows that incorporating the mean into the estimation procedure is beneficial whenever the mean is available. However, in scenario S3, where minimum and maximum values were used in addition to the median and quartiles, the standard deviation estimates provided by the QE and MEQE methods coincided and were less accurate in most cases. On the real dataset, improvement was substantial in scenario S2. However, unlike most of the simulation results, performance was better under the S3 scenario than under the S2 scenario.

Additionally, we observed an interesting—though practically less significant—result consistent with findings reported by Finney [6] in a lognormal case, both QE and MEQE estimated the population standard deviation more accurately than the sample standard deviation.

An alternative or supplementary approach, when both mean-focused and median-focused studies are available, is to pool these outcomes separately. A recent study McGrath et al. [11] provides a methodology specifically designed for meta-analyzing medians. Recognizing the distribution family is crucial in this approach. The improved distribution detection ability of the MEQE approach suggests that incorporating the mean, when available, can also improve the performance of this methodology.

The literature about mean and SD estimations using ordered statistics is vast and extensively used in meta-analysis. Table 1 summarizes the existing approaches. None of these methods use the mean to estimate the standard deviation. However, the methods of Kwan et al. [8] and Balakrishnan et al. [1] may incorporate statistics other than the median, lower and upper quartiles, minimum, and maximum into the estimation. After careful modification, Kwan et al. ‘s [8] method can be used when only the differences between the minimum, maximum, lower, and upper quartiles are known, i.e., when only the range and interquartile range are available. Balakrishnan et al.‘s [1] technique can incorporate any known percentiles into the estimation.

The MEQE algorithm in scenario S2 first determines the vector of best-fitting parameters in each family by minimizing [7]. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{N}^{\text{*}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{LN}^{\text{*}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{G}^{\text{*}}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{W}^{\text{*}}$$\end{document} denote the parameters achieving the optimum within the families. From these four candidates, the algorithm chooses the parameter family and the parameter vector for which [7] is minimal. Finally, the algorithm outputs the population standard deviation corresponding to the optimal parameter vector of the optimal family using [2, 5].

Remark 1 The original QE algorithm introduced in McGrath et al. [12] is identical with two exceptions. As opposed to [7] and [8], the objective functions in McGrath et al. [12] do not contain the last term associated with the mean. Moreover, the beta distribution family is also considered there. For technical reasons, we omitted the beta family. To ensure a fair comparison, we compared QEME with a version of the original QE algorithm that does not consider the beta family.

Implementation of the extension

The implementation of the MEQE algorithm is entirely based on the functions of the estmeansd() R package. The qe.fit() function performs the optimization in this package, while the set.qe.fit.control() package provides initial values for the optimizer. We made only three minor modifications. We added an extra term for the mean to the objective functions in the qe.fit() function. In the original set.qe.fit.control() function, a preliminary mean estimate is provided using the methodology of Luo et al. [9] Instead, we used the known mean. The optimization did not work properly for the beta distribution. For this reason, we deleted all rows related to the beta distribution family.

The complete R code that produces the results in the main manuscript is available in the supplementary files.

Simulation and evaluation

To facilitate the comparison between MEQE and the original QE methods and to ensure transparency, we followed McGrath et al. [12]. In the choice of the parameters. As one of our goals was to understand even the original QE method better, besides average relative error (ARE) and relative mean squared error (RMSE), we also calculated absolute ARE and visualized the results in a box plot. Bearing in mind that the real goal was to be as close to the theoretical standard deviation as possible, we evaluated the performance in terms of both the theoretical SD and the sample SD. In the former case, we also added the performance of the sample SD to the plot. The estimation may be acceptable even if the true distribution family is not detected. For this reason, we evaluated the performance in detecting the underlying distribution family using bar plot visualizations.

Design for simulation

We conducted a simulation study to systematically compare the performance of the original and mean extended QE methods.

We used the following sample sizes in our simulations: 25, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, 400, 450, 500, 550, 600, 650, 700, 750, 800, 850, 900, 950; and 1000. We used normal, log-normal, gamma, and Weibull distributions with parameters specified in Table 2. For each sample size and distribution, we simulated the data, calculated summary statistics and performed QE and MEQE estimations 1000 times. Discussion

Discussion of the results

Using the mean almost always improved the accuracy of the standard deviation estimation in scenario S2. Consistent with common sense, in the case of the normal distribution, when the theoretical mean and median coincided, the performance of the methods was about the same. The gain was especially large for gamma and Weibull distributions.

It was interesting to see that for scenario S3, the performance of the QE and MEQE methods was essentially identical. More interestingly, for most parameter settings, the performance in scenario S3 was worse than the performance in scenario S2. In other words, the additional information provided by the minimum and maximum was actually detrimental. A plausible explanation is that the variance of the maximum is asymptotically larger than that of the lower and upper quartiles. It is well known that the asymptotic variances of the median, lower, and upper quartiles are constant times the reciprocal of the sample size, see, e.g. Walker [17]. Meanwhile, it follows from extreme value theory that for normal, lognormal, gamma, and Weibull distributions the asymptotic variance of the maximum is larger. All these distribution families belong to the domain of the Gumbel distributions, see, e.g., Embrechts et al. [5] and Resnick [13] . For these families, the asymptotic variance is equal to the normalizing constants. In section 8 of the Supplementary Material, we analyzed the performance of the QEME method in two additional scenarios. When only the maximum is added to the S2 scenario, the performance is similar to the performance in the S3 scenario. In contrast, when only the minimum is added to the S2 scenario, the performance is only slightly worse than in the S2 scenario. These results support the hypothesis that the large variability of the maximum is one of the reasons for the performance loss in the S3 scenario.

We emphasize that in real word applications, the use of minimum and maximum can be even more harmful. In the simulation setting of this study, the minimum and maximum are known exactly. However, in practice, the presence of outliers can provide the algorithm with false extreme values.

In the log-normal (5,1) scenario, we obtained an interesting result. Fig. 7 shows that in scenario S2, when the sample size is at least 100, the MEQE method provides a better estimate for the theoretical standard deviation than the sample standard deviation. For larger sample sizes, even the original QE method outperforms the sample standard deviation. To eliminate the possibility of random fluctuation, we repeated the simulation with 10,000 replicates instead of the usual 1000 for sample size 1000. We obtained the same results. The somewhat surprising result is partly explained in Finney [6], where it is shown that the sample SD is not the most efficient for the standard deviation of the lognormal distribution.

The median pooling approach uses the QE method to determine the distribution family and uses the detection result to provide an estimate for the variance of the median statistic. The improvement shown in Fig. 6 is also likely to increase the estimate of the variance of the median statistic.

Future research directions

According to Bono et al. [3], the considered distribution families frequently arise in real-world applications. In particular, the gamma and Weibull distributions are common in survival analysis. However, a serious limitation of the QE and MEQE methods is that they only consider the normal distribution when fitting if the minimum or lower quartile is negative, since the other possible distribution families are concentrated in the nonnegative half of the real line. Therefore, extending the methods to other nonnegative distribution families is important. Additionally, incorporating the mean into the other approaches listed in Table 1 would be interesting.

Conclusion

One of the main questions in a meta-analysis is whether it is better to estimate the unreported statistic or omit the corresponding studies. The choice, of course, depends on the topic and the number of available studies. The results indicate that the QE and MEQE methods are valid options for Scenario S2. When the mean is available, including the mean in the estimation using the MEQE method improves the accuracy of the SD estimation. However, simulation results under Scenario S3 warrant caution: Using the minimum and maximum can be detrimental due to the high variability of extreme order statistics.
