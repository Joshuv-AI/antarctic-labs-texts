Time-splitting methods for the cold-plasma model using Finite Element Exterior Calculus
Elena Moral Sánchez, Martin Campos Pinto, Yaman Güçlü, Omar Maj

Abstract
In this work we propose a high-order structure-preserving discretization of the cold plasma model which describes the propagation of electromagnetic waves in magnetized plasmas. By utilizing B-Splines Finite Elements Exterior Calculus, we derive a space discretization that preserves the underlying Hamiltonian structure of the model, and we study two stable time-splitting geometrical integrators. We approximate an incoming wave boundary condition in such a way that the resulting schemes are compatible with a time-harmonic / transient decomposition of the solution, which allows us to establish their long-time stability. This approach readily applies to curvilinear and complex domains. We perform a numerical study of these schemes which compares their cost and accuracy against a standard Crank-Nicolson time integrator, and we run realistic simulations where the long-term behaviour is assessed using frequency-domain solutions. Our solvers are implemented in the Python library Psydac which makes them memory-efficient, parallel and essentially three-dimensional. Keywords: Cold-plasma model, full wave solver, splitting methods, Hamiltonian systems, Silver-Müller boundary conditions, B-Spline Finite Elements Exterior Calculus, isogeometric analysis.



1
Introduction

The cold-plasma model describes the propagation of a high-frequency electromagnetic wave in a magnetized plasma, by coupling Maxwell’s system with a linearized current density equation corresponding to the response of the plasma close to an equilibrium state. Simulation codes constitute an important tool to study the various phenomena involved in the plasma-wave interaction. Turbulent magnetized plasma is an inhomogeneous medium, and highly anisotropic due to the strong background magnetic field. For references, see the classical books
[
37
,
38
,
8
]
.

Despite its apparent simplicity, the model is able to accurately simulate physical scenarios relevant to reflectometry diagnostics on fusion plasmas
[
35
,
32
]
and plasma wave heating
[
22
]
, which consists of using electromagnetic waves to heat up the plasma in magnetic-confinement fusion devices. For instance, Electron Cyclotron Resonance Heating (ECRH) is one of the heating systems of the ITER Tokamak
1
1

1

https://www.iter.org/machine/supporting-systems/external-heating-systems
. For the simulations of plasma wave heating, thermal effects are relevant in the core of the plasma where the wave interacts with the plasma particles, but the cold-plasma model remains valid for most of the domain and can be used to study the interaction of the wave with turbulent plasma fluctuations
[
19
]
.

In simplified regimes, reduced models such as the Helmholtz equation or ray-tracing methods have been used, however some applications require the use of so-called full-wave codes, which solve the full vector-valued electromagnetic field
[
19
,
33
]
. Certain full-wave codes solve the problem in the frequency-domain
[
14
]
, i.e. they solve for time-harmonic solutions. Frequency domain codes usually rely on iterative solvers
[
5
]
which may have convergence issues due to the bad conditionning
of the operators, especially for high frequencies
[
15
]
or close to plasma resonances
[
21
]
. Finding good preconditioners is an ongoing research topic. Domain Decomposition Methods (DDM)
[
13
]
have been the main line of research
[
12
]
. However, as the number of subdomains increases, the convergence deteriorates and, unlike for elliptic problems, the construction of coarse solvers is challenging. Another preconditioning approach has been the shifted-Laplacian method
[
16
]
, but the trade-off between dissipation and preconditioner accuracy is difficult to assess.

In recent years, the Finite-Difference Time-Domain method (FDTD) has been typically used to run the time-domain problem
[
39
,
33
]
. A priori this method based on finite differences can be very fast since the computations only involve a small stencil. However, FDTD codes often require a very fine mesh and very small time-steps. The cost is critical for three-dimensional realistic scenarios
[
34
]
, especially for high frequency.
Furthermore, long-time stability is often an issue which can lead to the development of unphysical effects
[
20
]
. Long-time stable FDTD schemes exist, but at the price of being either first order
[
36
]
or implicit
[
39
]
, which mitigates their intrinsic efficiency. Another drawback of FDTD codes is that their application to complex geometries is not straightforward
[
20
]
. However, the interaction of the wave with the complex structures in the Tokamak vessel and the wave launcher can be essential for reflectometry diagnostics
[
33
,
30
]
.

Our contribution is a full wave code for the time-domain cold-plasma model based on high-order B-spline structure-preserving Finite Element Exterior Calculus (FEEC)
[
9
,
17
]
that applies to general domains of curvilinear geometry and is intrinsically stable on very long time ranges. In particular, we present three methods based on geometric integrators
[
18
]
. Two of the methods combine time-splitting techniques
[
26
]
with a treatment of the non-Hamiltonian terms, motivated by the preservation of incoming wave boundary conditions.
The third method, used for the purpose of comparison, is a standard energy preserving Crank-Nicolson integrator which does not involve time-splitting.

The presented methods are implemented in the
Psydac
library
2
2

2

https://github.com/pyccel/psydac
, which provides its own sparse stencil matrix format. As a result, our code is memory-efficient, it runs in parallel and is essentially three-dimensional. Besides, block Kronecker mass matrices are used to precondition the implicit steps. Complex geometries can be directly included in the simulation using mappings,
and physical parameters can be either interpolated from general data or set symbolically thanks to the
SymPDE
library
3
3

3

https://github.com/pyccel/sympde
, so that the code easily adapts to different plasma profiles and background magnetic fields.

The paper is organized as follows. Firstly we present a theoretical study of the properties of the problem and the derivation of the three methods. In particular, we study the long-time stability of two of the proposed schemes. The second part involves a numerical study, where we check the properties of the implemented methods (convergence order, numerical stability and preserved quantities) and compare their performance in terms of cost and accuracy.
The third part includes three realistic two-dimensional numerical simulations. We compute the solutions for long times and track the convergence to the frequency-domain solution.

2
The cold-plasma model

In a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the cold-plasma model

[
8
]
describes the time-evolution of electric and magnetic fields
\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
,
\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, together with the current density
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, and it amounts to the system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{E}-c\ \textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=-4\pi\boldsymbol{J}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}+c\ \textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{J}+\omega_{c}\boldsymbol{J}\times\boldsymbol{b}_{0}

\displaystyle=(\omega_{p}^{2}/4\pi)\boldsymbol{E}-\nu_{e}\boldsymbol{J}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(1c)

where

\omega_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sqrt{\cfrac{4\pi e^{2}n_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})}{m_{e}}},\qquad\omega_{c}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\cfrac{e|\boldsymbol{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})|}{m_{e}c}>0\qquad\text{(in CGS units)}

are the electron plasma and cyclotron frequencies respectively.
Here,
c
is the speed of light in vacuum,

e>0
the elementary charge,
m_{e}
the electron mass,

n_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})
denotes the background electron plasma density
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})
, the background magnetic field with point-wise modulus
|\boldsymbol{B}_{0}|=\sqrt{(B_{0})_{1}^{2}+(B_{0})_{2}^{2}+(B_{0})_{3}^{2}}
and unitary vector field
\boldsymbol{b}_{0}=\boldsymbol{B}_{0}/|\boldsymbol{B}_{0}|
. The background electron plasma density and magnetic field are assumed to be bounded and constant in time.
Lastly,
\nu_{e}\geq 0
denotes the electron-collision frequency, which introduces damping in the system.

Observe that the model is written in Gaussian units, so that
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
are of the same order, and only the electron species have been considered. In addition the initial condition must satisfy the constraint
\mathrm{div}(\boldsymbol{B})=0
, which is preserved by
Faraday’s law (
1b
).

In this article we will complement this model with an impedance
boundary condition corresponding to an incoming time-harmonic
field of angular frequency
\omega_{0}>0
.

2.1
Normalization

In practice, the time-scale of the problem is normalized with the angular frequency
\omega_{0}
, yielding
\tilde{t}=\omega_{0}t
. The spatial scale is normalized with
\omega_{0}/c=2\pi/\lambda_{0}
, where
\lambda_{0}
is the wave-length in vacuum. This yields the normalized variable
\boldsymbol{\tilde{x}}=(\omega_{0}/c)\boldsymbol{x}
.
For simplicity of notation, the tildes are dropped in the rest of the paper, and the quantities correspond to normalized quantities. The normalized cold plasma model reads then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{E}-\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=-\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{Y}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}+\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{Y}+\hat{\omega}_{c}\boldsymbol{Y}\times\boldsymbol{b}_{0}

\displaystyle=\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{E}-\hat{\nu}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2c)

where the current density has been rescaled as
\boldsymbol{J}=(\omega_{p}/4\pi)\boldsymbol{Y}
and the normalized parameters are given by
\hat{\omega}_{p}=\omega_{p}/\omega_{0}
,
\hat{\omega}_{c}=\omega_{c}/\omega_{0}
and
\hat{\nu}_{e}=\nu_{e}/\omega_{0}
.

The well-posedness of (
2
) is usually studied with
the Hille-Yosida theorem, which relies on the fact that the evolution operator is maximal monotone.

2.2
Boundary conditions

For simplicity we consider a cubic domain,
with a boundary partitioned into

\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{A}\cup\Gamma_{P}
.
On
\Gamma_{P}
we gather faces with periodic boundary conditions
while
\Gamma_{A}
is an artificial boundary where we impose a type of
impedance conditions
known as Silver-Müller boundary conditions in time-domain

[
2
]
. In normalized units
they take the form

\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{x})\times\left(\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\times\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{x})\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\qquad\text{ for }\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma_{A},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is the outward unit normal vector on
\Gamma_{A}
, and

\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}
is a boundary source which may be used to prescribe a given incoming field.
In this paper we consider an incoming wave on a face

\Gamma_{A}^{\mathrm{inc}}:=\Gamma_{A}\cap\{x=x^{\mathrm{inc}}\}
,

\boldsymbol{x}=(x,y,z)
, and an absorbing boundary condition
on
\Gamma_{A}\setminus\Gamma_{A}^{\mathrm{inc}}
.
Specifically, our incoming wave corresponds to a
time-harmonic circular cross-section Gaussian beam, launched from
x^{\mathrm{inc}}=0

and propagating in the
x
-axis, with expression

\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{\mathrm{gb}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{e}\frac{w_{0}}{w(x)}\exp\left[-\frac{r^{2}}{w(x)^{2}}+i\left(x+\frac{\kappa(x)}{2}r^{2}-\psi(x)\right)\right]

(4)

where
r^{2}=(y-y_{0})^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}
is the radial distance
to
(0,y_{0},z_{0})
the position of the focus,
w_{0}>0
is the waist radius,
\kappa(x)=x/(x^{2}+x_{R}^{2})
is the curvature of the wavefronts,
\psi(x)=\arctan(x/x_{R})
is the Gouy phase and
w^{2}(x)=w_{0}^{2}\big(1+(x/x_{R})^{2}\big)
, where
x_{R}=w_{0}^{2}/2>0
is the normalized Rayleigh range in vacuum.
The unit vector
\boldsymbol{e}
indicates the polarization,
for which we will consider different configurations.
Thus, our boundary source reads

\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\Re\{\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}(\boldsymbol{x})e^{-it}\}\qquad\text{ with }\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{\mathrm{gb}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\mathrm{gb}}(\boldsymbol{x})\times\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{x})&\text{on }\Gamma_{A}^{\mathrm{inc}}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\text{on }\Gamma_{A}\setminus\Gamma_{A}^{\mathrm{inc}}\end{cases}

(5)

where we have denoted
\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\mathrm{gb}}=-i\,\textrm{curl}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{\mathrm{gb}}
.
We remind that the the associated field

\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{gb}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\Re\{\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{\mathrm{gb}}(\boldsymbol{x})e^{-it}\}
is an exact solution of the paraxial wave equation in vacuum, but only an
approximate solution of Maxwell’s equations
[
28
]
.

2.3
The frequency-domain problem

Assuming that the solution of (
2
) with the boundary conditions (
3
) is time-harmonic of the form

\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{th}}(t)=\Re\{\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}e^{-it}\},\quad\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{th}}(t)=\Re\{\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}e^{-it}\},\quad\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{th}}(t)=\Re\{\hat{\boldsymbol{Y}}e^{-it}\},

(6)

then
\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}=-i\textrm{curl}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{Y}}=i/\hat{\omega}_{p}(\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}-\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}})
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}
must be the solution of the associated frequency-domain problem

\displaystyle\textrm{curl}\textrm{curl}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}-\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\qquad\text{ on }\Omega,

(7a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times(\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}+i\textrm{curl}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}\qquad\text{ on }\Gamma_{A},

(7b)

where
\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}=S\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}-iD\boldsymbol{b}_{0}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}+(P-S)\boldsymbol{b}_{0}(\boldsymbol{b}_{0}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{E}})
is the dielectric tensor defined with the Stix parameters

S(\boldsymbol{x})=1-\cfrac{(1+i\hat{\nu}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x}))\hat{\omega}_{p}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})}{(1+i\hat{\nu}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}-\hat{\omega}_{c}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})},\qquad D(\boldsymbol{x})=\cfrac{\hat{\omega}_{c}(\boldsymbol{x})\hat{\omega}_{p}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})}{(1+i\hat{\nu}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}-\hat{\omega}_{c}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})},\qquad P(\boldsymbol{x})=1-\cfrac{\hat{\omega}_{p}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})}{1+i\hat{\nu}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})}.

In the following we proceed with the dispersion relation analysis,
for which we assume that
\hat{\nu}_{e}=0
. In the case of homogeneous plasmas where
\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
is constant, the analysis resorts to the dispersion relation
[
8
]
for plane waves of
the form
e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{A}
. In our case, the plasma is inhomogeneous and we resort to the local dispersion relation in the WKB approximation

[
23
,
6
,
25
]
.
This relation can be obtained from the symbol

[
25
, Eq. (3.15)]

of the partial differential operator in (
7a
),
which reads

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{p}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k})=-\boldsymbol{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{k}+\|\boldsymbol{k}\|^{2}\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{I}}}-\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{Char}(\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{p}}})=\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k})\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}|\det(\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{p}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k})=0\}

(8)

is its characteristic variety. Note that since
\hat{\nu}_{e}=0
,
\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
is Hermitian and
\det(\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{p}}})
is real. Following the Stix frame of reference
[
37
]
, we align the
z
-axis with
\boldsymbol{b}_{0}
. By symmetry we can assume that
\boldsymbol{k}
is contained in the
x
-
z
plane, thus
\boldsymbol{k}=n(\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)
, where
\theta
is the angle from
\boldsymbol{b}_{0}
to the
x
-axis and
n
is the refractive index (since
\boldsymbol{k}
is normalized to
\omega_{0}/c
). In this work we are only interested in perpendicular propagation, i.e.
\theta=\pi/2
, for which there are two eigenmodes corresponding to

•

the O-mode polarization, also known as Transverse Magnetic (TM), where
\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}\parallel\boldsymbol{b}_{0}
,

•

the X-mode polarization, also known as Transverse Electric (TE), where
\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}\perp\boldsymbol{b}_{0}
.

In this reference frame the dielectric tensor amounts to

\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}=\begin{bmatrix}S&-iD&0\\
iD&S&0\\
0&0&P\end{bmatrix}

(9)

and the symbol defined in (
8
) becomes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{p}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k})=\begin{bmatrix}-S&iD&0\\
-iD&n^{2}-S&0\\
0&0&n^{2}-P\end{bmatrix}\qquad(\theta=\pi/2).

(10)

The symbol consists of two decoupled blocks, corresponding to the X-mode and O-mode symbols, respectively,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{p}}}_{X}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k})=\begin{bmatrix}-S&iD\\
-iD&n^{2}-S\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{O}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k})=n^{2}-P.

Each symbol defines a different branch of the characteristic variety,
\mathrm{Char}(\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{p}}})=\mathrm{Char}(\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{p}}}_{X})\cup\mathrm{Char}(p_{O})
. A simple calculation shows that the points
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k})
within
\mathrm{Char}(\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{p}}}_{X})
and
\mathrm{Char}(p_{O})
satisfy

n^{2}=S(\boldsymbol{x})-D^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})/S(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\text{(X-mode)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad n^{2}=P(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\text{(O-mode)},

where we remind that
n=|\boldsymbol{k}|
. These equations are known as the local dispersion relations. Regions with real solutions
n
are called propagative, whereas regions with complex solutions are called evanescent. Points
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
where
n=0
are called cutoffs and points where
n^{2}\to\pm\infty
are called resonances.

From the local dispersion relation it is clear that the O-mode has a cutoff at
P=0
, i.e. when
\hat{\omega}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=1
, and does not have any resonances. In the case of the X-mode, there is a cutoff at
S^{2}=D^{2}
and a resonance when
S=0
, namely,

\hat{\omega}_{p}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})+\hat{\omega}_{c}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\pm\hat{\omega}_{c}(\boldsymbol{x})\hat{\omega}_{p}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=1\text{ (X-mode cutoffs)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{\omega}_{p}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})+\hat{\omega}_{c}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=1\text{ (upper hybrid resonance)}.

Since in our setup the wave is launched from vacuum, we only consider the lower density cutoff (plus sign).

Remark 2.1
.

(Cutoffs and the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
)
From (
9
), it is clear that the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
at every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
are

\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})=S(\boldsymbol{x})+D(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=S(\boldsymbol{x})-D(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})=P(\boldsymbol{x}).

(11)

Therefore, for both polarizations, at least one eigenvalue is zero at the cutoff position.

We conclude this section by discussing the well-posedness of the frequency-domain problem (
7
).
References
[
31
]
,
[
3
]
show, under the assumption of
\hat{\nu}_{e}>0
(this assumption regularizes the problem), the well-posedness of (
7a
) with different boundary conditions. In
[
31
]
, the result is shown for perfect electrical conductor boundary conditions, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\partial\Omega
. In
[
3
]
, it is proved in the case of Dirichlet boundary conditions
\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}=\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}_{A}
for a prescribed field
\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}_{A}
and the case of Neumann boundary conditions
\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\textrm{curl}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}=\boldsymbol{j}_{A}
for a prescribed
\boldsymbol{j}_{A}
.

In
[
21
]
the well-posedness of (
7
) with
\hat{\nu}_{e}=0
is shown under certain assumptions.
Among others, all the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
must be either strictly positive or strictly negative in the domain. As it is shown in remark
2.1
, this excludes the cutoffs and the resonance. Another assumption is that
\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
must be uniformly bounded, which excludes the points where
\hat{\omega}_{c}^{2}=1
which corresponds to the fundamental cyclotron resonance.

In the present work, we assume that there are no resonances in the domain, but allow the existence of cutoffs.

2.4
Weak form

Throughout this paper we denote by

\langle\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}d\boldsymbol{x}

the
L^{2}
-inner product on the domain
\Omega
,
and by

\langle\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}=\int_{\Gamma_{A}}\boldsymbol{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}d\boldsymbol{S}

the product on the artificial boundary
\Gamma_{A}
.
We want to enforce the Silver-Müller boundary conditions in (
3
) to the time-domain problem (
2
). For that we impose the Ampère-Maxwell equation (
2a
) weakly, while Faraday (
2b
) and
the current density (
2c
) equations are required to hold strongly.
We consider

\boldsymbol{Y}\in H(\textrm{curl},\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{B}\in H(\mathrm{div},\Omega)
.
Given the impedance boundary condition,
as discussed in
[
27
]
, a specific space is required for
\boldsymbol{E}
,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{imp}}(\textrm{curl},\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{U}\in H(\textrm{curl},\Omega)|\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{U}\in L^{2}_{t}(\partial\Omega)\}

\displaystyle\text{where }\quad L^{2}_{t}(\partial\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{U}\in(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))^{3}|\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}.

Consequently the phase space is
V:=H_{\mathrm{imp}}(\textrm{curl},\Omega)\times H(\mathrm{div},\Omega)\times H(\textrm{curl},\Omega)
,
and the solution

(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{Y})
in
C^{1}(\mathbbm{R}_{+};V)
satisfies
for every
(\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{C},\boldsymbol{G})\in V
and every
t>0
,

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{F},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{E}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{B}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{E}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}+\langle\boldsymbol{F},\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{Y}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}},

(12a)

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{C},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{C},\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{E}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

(12b)

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{G},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{Y}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{G}\times\boldsymbol{Y},\hat{\omega}_{c}\boldsymbol{b}_{0}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\boldsymbol{G},\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{E}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{G},\hat{\nu}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0.

(12c)

Using proper test functions and integration by parts,
one can check that equation (
12a
) is consistent
with the boundary conditions (
3
).

2.5
Incoming and scattered fields

Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{\mathrm{inc}},\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\mathrm{inc}}
be the complex-valued
solution to the time-harmonic Maxwell equations in vacuum
with Silver-Müller boundary conditions,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} -i\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{\mathrm{inc}}-\textrm{curl}\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\mathrm{inc}}=0\\
-i\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\textrm{curl}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{\mathrm{inc}}=0\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{ on }\Omega

(13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times(\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{\mathrm{inc}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\mathrm{inc}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu})=\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}\qquad\text{ on }\Gamma_{A}

where the boundary source term
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}

is defined in (
5
).
The corresponding (real-valued) time-dependent incoming field

(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B})^{\mathrm{inc}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\Re\{(\hat{\boldsymbol{E}},\hat{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\mathrm{inc}}(\boldsymbol{x})e^{-it}\}

satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{F},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}},

(14a)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{inc}},\boldsymbol{C}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{inc}},\boldsymbol{C}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

(14b)

for all
\boldsymbol{F}\in H_{\mathrm{imp}}(\textrm{curl},\Omega)
,

\boldsymbol{C}\in H(\mathrm{div},\Omega)
.
We next decompose the solution as

\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{sca}},\qquad\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{sca}},\qquad\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{sca}},

(15)

where the scattered field is characterized by the system

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{F},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{sca}}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{sca}}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{sca}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}+\langle\boldsymbol{F},\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{sca}}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

(16a)

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{C},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{sca}}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{C},\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{sca}}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

(16b)

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{G},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{sca}}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{G}\times\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{sca}},\hat{\omega}_{c}\boldsymbol{b}_{0}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\boldsymbol{G},\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{sca}}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{G},\hat{\nu}_{e}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{sca}}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{G},\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Omega}

(16c)

for
(\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{C},\boldsymbol{G})
arbitrary in
V
.
In particular we observe that the scattered field solves the cold-plasma model with purely absorbing boundary conditions, its only source being the interaction of the incoming electric field with the electron plasma density.

Remark 2.2
.

(boundary data for the incoming field)
In (
12
) the boundary condition has been defined using the Gaussian beam profile
(
4
) for the sake of simplicity. If specific values are available for the
incoming wave, e.g., as provided by an experiment or a separate numerical solver, these values
could be used as well to define the boundary source term
\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}
.

2.6
Hamiltonian structure of the ideal case

In the case of the
ideal
model, that is, when
\Gamma_{A}=\emptyset
and
\hat{\nu}_{e}=0
, the problem has a non-canonical Hamiltonian structure
[
29
]
in the sense that it can be rewritten in the form

\cfrac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{Y})=\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{H}\}(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{Y})

(17)

for an arbitrary functional
\mathcal{F}\in C^{\infty}(V)
.
Here,
\mathcal{H}
is the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{Y})=\cfrac{1}{2}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{Y}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

(18)

and the bracket
\{\cdot,\cdot\}

is defined for any smooth functionals
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}

on
V
such that the functional derivatives are also in
V
by

\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\}:=\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}+\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\}_{p}+\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\}_{c}\in C^{\infty}(V),

(19)

where

\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}=\Big\langle\textrm{curl}\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\boldsymbol{E}}\right),\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta\boldsymbol{B}}\Big\rangle_{\Omega}-\Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\boldsymbol{B}},\textrm{curl}\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta\boldsymbol{E}}\right)\Big\rangle_{\Omega}

(20)

is the Poisson bracket for Maxwell’s equations
[
1
]

and

\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\}_{p}=\Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\boldsymbol{Y}},\hat{\omega}_{p}\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta\boldsymbol{E}}\Big\rangle_{\Omega}-\Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\boldsymbol{E}},\hat{\omega}_{p}\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta\boldsymbol{Y}}\Big\rangle_{\Omega},\qquad\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\}_{c}=\Big\langle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\boldsymbol{Y}}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta\boldsymbol{Y}},\hat{\omega}_{c}\boldsymbol{b}_{0}\Big\rangle_{\Omega}

(21)

account for the plasma response and the cyclotron rotation.
The bracket in (
19
) is skew-symmetric and verifies the Leibniz rule and the Jacobi identity, hence it is a Poisson bracket

[
24
]
.

From the skew-symmetry, it is clear that the Hamiltonian (
18
) is preserved by the ideal system.
An advantage of the form (
17
) is that it simplifies
the study of invariants. In particular, one easily sees that the Casimirs
of the bracket, i.e., the functionals
\mathcal{C}\in C^{\infty}(V)
such that

\{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{G}\}=0\qquad\forall\mathcal{G}\in C^{\infty}(V),

(22)

are also invariants of the ideal system
[
29
]
, indeed
taking
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{H}
in (
22
) yields

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{Y})=0
.

2.7
Non-ideal case and energy balance

In the general case, (
17
) becomes

\cfrac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{Y})=\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{H}\}(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{Y})+(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{H})(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{Y})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\boldsymbol{E}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}\right\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}\qquad\forall\mathcal{F}\in C^{\infty}(V),

(23)

where the Poisson bracket
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
and Hamiltonian are as above, and where

(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}):=-\left\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\boldsymbol{E}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta\boldsymbol{E}}\right\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}-\left\langle\hat{\nu}_{e}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\boldsymbol{Y}},\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta\boldsymbol{Y}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}

(24)

is a symmetric, non-positive bilinear form (sometimes called a metric bracket),
see
[
24
]
.
In the homogeneous case (
\nu\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}=0
),
system (
12
) has the form of a metriplectic system, however
we note that this metriplectic form dissipates the Hamiltonian.

In the presence of an incoming source,
we compute the variation in time of the Hamiltonian by considering
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{H}

(i.e., by testing against the solution itself in (
12
)), which yields

\partial_{t}\mathcal{H}=(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\boldsymbol{E}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}\right\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}=-\|\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{E}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{A})}^{2}-\langle\hat{\nu}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}.

(25)

In the case of the ideal model, this shows again that the Hamiltonian is constant. Otherwise, since
\hat{\nu}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0
, it holds that
\|\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{E}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{A})}^{2}+\langle\hat{\nu}_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{Y},\boldsymbol{Y}\rangle_{\Omega}\geq 0
, hence the first two terms can only dissipate the energy.
In addition, the third term may introduce fluctuations of energy.

Finally, as a direct consequence of Faraday’s law (
2b
), we have the invariant

\partial_{t}(\mathrm{div}\boldsymbol{B})=0.

(26)

In addition, Ampère’s equation (
12a
) implies that the charge density

\rho=\mathrm{div}\boldsymbol{E}
satisfies a continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{Y})=0\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

(27)

Using Gauss’ law, the total charge is
Q(t):=\int_{\Omega}\rho d\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}\boldsymbol{E}d\boldsymbol{x}

By integration by parts we thus have

\frac{d}{dt}Q(t)=\int_{\Gamma_{A}}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{E}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}d\boldsymbol{S}=\int_{\Gamma_{A}}\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{Y}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}d\boldsymbol{S}\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

(28)

In the case where
\Gamma_{A}=\emptyset
the total charge is trivially preserved, but not in the presence
of an artificial boundary.

\displaystyle=N_{i_{z,1}}^{p_{1}}(x)N_{i_{z,2}}^{p_{2}}(y)D_{i_{z,3}}^{p_{3}}(z),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{i}_{x}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=N_{i_{x,1}}^{p_{1}}(x)D_{i_{x,2}}^{p_{2}}(y)D_{i_{x,3}}^{p_{3}}(z),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{i}_{y}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=D_{i_{y,1}}^{p_{1}}(x)N_{i_{y,2}}^{p_{2}}(y)D_{i_{y,3}}^{p_{3}}(z),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{i}_{z}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=D_{i_{z,1}}^{p_{1}}(x)D_{i_{z,2}}^{p_{2}}(y)N_{i_{z,3}}^{p_{3}}(z).

In the above tensor-product spline bases, the differential matrices
\mathbb{G},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D}
are
essentially connectivity matrices made of 0 and
\pm 1
, thanks to the univariate derivative
formula
(N_{i}^{p})^{\prime}=D_{i}^{p}-D_{i+1}^{p}
for any degree
p
and index
i
.

3.2
Discretization of the equation

For our discrete phase space we take
V_{h}:=V_{h}^{1}\times V_{h}^{2}\times V_{h}^{1}
. Since
V_{h}^{1}\subset H_{\mathrm{imp}}(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)\subset H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega)
, this discretization is conforming in the sense that
V_{h}\subset V
.
In particular, we can define a semi-discrete solution

\boldsymbol{E}_{h}(t)\in V_{h}^{1},\quad\boldsymbol{B}_{h}(t)\in V_{h}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{Y}_{h}(t)\in V_{h}^{1}

(31)

characterized by the same equations as (
12
) but
with discrete test functions
(\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{C},\boldsymbol{G})\in V_{h}
.
In terms of (column) vectors of spline coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1}(\boldsymbol{E}_{h})
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}(\boldsymbol{B}_{h})
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{h})
, these equations read

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}\\
\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}\\
\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{A}_{1}&\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}&-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}\\
-\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}&\mathbb{0}&-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\left(\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}+\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}\right)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}(t)\\
\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},

(32)

where
\mathbb{0}
denotes the zero matrix,
\mathbb{M}_{k}
is the mass matrix of
V_{h}^{k}

and

\displaystyle(\mathbb{A}_{1})_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}

\displaystyle:=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}},

\displaystyle(\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}})_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}

\displaystyle:=\langle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\rangle_{\Omega},

\displaystyle(\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}})_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}

\displaystyle:=\langle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\hat{\nu}_{e}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\rangle_{\Omega},

\displaystyle(\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}})_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}

\displaystyle:=\langle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{j}},\hat{\omega}_{c}\boldsymbol{b}_{0}\rangle_{\Omega}.

The source term is

({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}})_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}=(\cos(t){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{R}+\sin(t){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{I})_{\boldsymbol{i}}

with
time-independent boundary source arrays given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} ({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{R})_{\boldsymbol{i}}&:=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\Re\{\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}\}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}\\
({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{I})_{\boldsymbol{i}}&:=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\Im\{\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}\}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(33)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}
is given in (
5
).
Lastly, we remark that
\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}
is skew-symmetric and
\mathbb{A}_{1}
is symmetric positive semi-definite.

3.3
Discrete Hamiltonian structure

Gathering the coefficients in a large column array
of the form

{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}=[{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}]^{T}
in

C^{1}(\mathbbm{R}_{+};C_{h})
with
C_{h}=C_{h}^{1}\times C_{h}^{2}\times C_{h}^{1}
,
system (
32
) rewrites as

\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}=\mathbb{P}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{H}+\mathbb{N}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{H}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}(t),

(34)

where
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{N}
are the Poisson and “metric” matrices corresponding to
the continuous metriplectic system (
23
),
namely

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{C}^{T}&-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\\
-\mathbb{C}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}&\mathbb{0}&-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{A}_{1}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(35)

Here,
the source is
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}(t):=[\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}(t),\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}]^{T}

and the discrete Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{E}_{h},\boldsymbol{B}_{h},\boldsymbol{Y}_{h})=\cfrac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}),

(36)

so that
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{H}=[\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},\mathbb{M}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}]^{T}
.
Another way of writing (
34
) is to
consider a general functional
\mathsf{F}\in C^{\infty}(C_{h})
and apply a chain rule:

\cfrac{d}{dt}\mathsf{F}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}})=\{\mathsf{F},\mathsf{H}\}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}})+(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{H})({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}})+(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{F})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}(t).

(37)

Since
\mathbb{P}
is constant and skew-symmetric it represents a Poisson bracket, moreover

\mathbb{N}
is symmetric and negative semi-definite matrix.
Therefore the homogeneous (
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}}
) equation admits
a metriplectic structure with discrete Poisson and metric brackets

\displaystyle\{\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G}\}:=(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{F})^{T}\mathbb{P}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{G},\qquad(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G}):=(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{F})^{T}\mathbb{N}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{G}\qquad\text{ for }\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G}\in C^{\infty}(C_{h}).

(38)

In the ideal case (
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{0}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}}
), system (
37
)
preserves
\mathsf{H}
(by antisymmetry of
\mathbb{P}
) and the Casimirs
[
18
]
,
i.e. every functional
\mathsf{C}\in C^{\infty}(C_{h})
such that

(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{C})^{T}\mathbb{P}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
In the general case, the semi-discrete energy balance involves both non-Hamiltonian terms of
(
34
), namely

\partial_{t}\mathsf{H}=(\mathsf{H},\mathsf{H})+(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{H})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}(t)=-({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{T}\mathbb{A}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}})+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}(t),

(39)

where
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{T}\mathbb{A}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}\geq 0
only contributes to energy dissipation, while the last term may introduce energy oscillations.

4
Time discretization

Geometric integrators are methods that preserve certain geometric quantities of the solutions, such as the Hamiltonian or the Casimirs of the Poisson bracket
[
18
]
. Thus, they are of special interest for long times simulations. Splitting methods are a type of geometric integrators consisting of solving certain subsystems sequentially, instead of the whole system at once. Usually this approach leads to smaller subsystems which are easier to solve.
For Hamiltonian systems, geometric splitting methods have been thoroughly studied in
[
26
]
.
Here, we design two methods based on such time-splitting schemes where the non-Hamiltonian part
is handled in a stable way.

The resulting splitting schemes will then be compared with a Crank-Nicolson scheme, which is a standard second-order implicit, stable (energy preserving) time integrator based on the trapezoidal rule.

4.1
Time splittings for Hamiltonian systems

In the ideal case, that is when
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{0}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}}
, the ideal problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}=\mathbb{P}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{H}

can be split following two standard approaches
[
26
]
:

•

Poisson splitting which consists in decomposing the Poisson matrix into skew-symmetric submatrices
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}_{1}+\mathbb{P}_{2}
, leading to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{1}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{H}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{2}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{H}.

If these two subsystems can be solved exactly
the resulting integrator preserves the Hamiltonian.

•

Hamiltonian splitting which consists in decomposing the discrete Hamiltonian into
\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{H}_{1}+\mathsf{H}_{2}
, leading to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{H}_{1}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}\mathsf{H}_{2}.

If these two subsystems can be solved exactly
the resulting integrator preserves the Poisson bracket, and in particular its Casimir invariants.

In practice these subsystems are solved alternatively, leading to numerical flows which are composed to yield potentially high order time integration schemes. In this article we will consider Strang splitting, which is second-order.

4.2
Splitting of the non-Hamiltonian terms

For the Hamiltonian part, we can use the splitting methods described above.
We then need a criterion to decide in which subsystem to place the non-Hamiltonian terms
from (
34
)–(
35
),
namely the two boundary terms (associated with
\mathbb{A}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}
) and the collision term
(associated with
\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}
).

For the latter we do not see a particular constraint, but for the boundary terms there seems to be only
one correct configuration, given some splitting of the Hamiltonian terms.
Consider indeed one equation in a subsystem containing the (weak) “
\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{B}
” term and some (possibly vanishing)
contributions from other terms, that is an equation of the form

\langle\boldsymbol{F},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{E}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{B}\rangle_{\Omega}+\alpha\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{E}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}+\gamma\langle\boldsymbol{F},\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{Y}\rangle_{\Omega}=\beta\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}},

for some parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}
.
Then the corresponding boundary condition reads

\boldsymbol{\nu}\times(-\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{\nu}+\alpha\boldsymbol{E})=\beta\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}

By comparison with (
3
)
it appears that the choice
\alpha=\beta=1
is the only option that ensures that the field satisfies the physically correct boundary conditions.
We confirmed numerically that splitting these boundary terms with
\alpha\neq\beta
resulted in a very large error originating from the boundary. Indeed these terms are very large and they compensate only when they remain together.

This choice is also physical because it guarantees that in the case of
\hat{\nu}_{e}=0
the energy-balance (
39
) is preserved, i.e. there is no mismatch between energy input and dissipation.

Another justification is based on guaranteeing the physical behaviour of the scattered field.
As we can see in (
16
), the source of the scattered field
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{sca}}
is basically the interaction of the incoming field with the plasma. Thus, initially the scattered field is zero near the incoming boundary
\Gamma_{A}^{\mathrm{inc}}
.
Splitting the terms mentioned above, i.e.
\alpha\neq\beta
, introduces a boundary source in (
16a
). As a result, a very large unphysical error appears initially at the incoming boundary, where the scattered field is supposed to be zero.

4.3
Poisson splitting time scheme

Following subsection
4.1
, in this approach we split the Poisson matrix into two
skew-symmetric matrices. Here a natural choice is to separate the curl-curl operators from the
magnetic and plasma terms:

\mathbb{P}_{1}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{C}^{T}&\mathbb{0}\\
-\mathbb{C}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbb{P}_{2}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}&\mathbb{0}&-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

According to our discussion above the boundary contributions are kept together with the first (Maxwell) step which contains the
\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{B}
term.
Moreover the collision term associated to
\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}
is kept together with
\mathbb{P}_{2}
,
to avoid having to solve for
\boldsymbol{Y}
in the first subsystem.

The splitting consists of solving the following subproblems:

•

a Maxwell subsystem, associated to
\mathbb{P}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{1}\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}+\cos(t){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{R}+\sin(t){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{I}-\mathbb{A}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},

(40a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},

(40b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}},

(40c)

and

•

a plasma subsystem, associated to
\mathbb{P}_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{1}\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}},

(41a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}},

(41b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{1}\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}-(\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}+\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}.

(41c)

4.3.1
Time integration

Each problem is integrated numerically in the interval
(t^{n},t^{n+1}=t^{n}+\Delta t)

using a second-order trapezoidal rule, leading to the following two discrete flows.

\bullet

A first flow
({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1})=\Phi_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}^{\mathrm{P}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n};\Delta t,t^{n})

obtained by integrating the Maxwell subsystem (
40
):

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{M}_{1}+\cfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{4}\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}\mathbb{C}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{A}_{1}\right]{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n}+\cfrac{s^{n}}{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}_{R}^{\mathrm{inc}}-\cfrac{c^{n}}{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}_{I}^{\mathrm{inc}},

(42a)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=2{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1/2}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},

(42b)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n}-\Delta t\mathbb{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1/2},

(42c)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n},

(42d)

where
s^{n}=\sin(t^{n+1})-\sin(t^{n})
and
c^{n}=\cos(t^{n+1})-\cos(t^{n})
. The system (
42a
) is solved using PCG (Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient)
[
5
]
. For the preconditioner we use a Kronecker mass solver.

\bullet

A second flow
({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1})=\Phi_{\mathrm{plasma}}^{\mathrm{P}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n};\Delta t)
obtained by integrating the plasma subsystem (
41
):

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}&\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}\\
-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}&\mathbb{M}_{1}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\left(\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}+\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}\right)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1}\\[10.00002pt]
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n}\\
\Big(\mathbb{M}_{1}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\left(\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}+\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}\right)\Big){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n}\end{bmatrix},

(43a)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n}.

(43b)

Since the matrix is not symmetric, we use PBiCGStab (Preconditioned Biconjugate Gradient stabilized method)
[
40
]
to solve the system. The method is preconditioned with a block diagonal matrix, where each block is a Kronecker mass solver.

4.3.2
Poisson splitting scheme

Lastly, we compose the above flows using a Strang splitting scheme. Given the initial condition
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{0}
at
t^{0}=0
, for each time-step
n=0,...,N-1
, compute

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1/3},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1/2},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}^{\mathrm{P}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n};\Delta t/2,t^{n}),

(44a)

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+2/3},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1/2},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mathrm{plasma}}^{\mathrm{P}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1/3},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1/2},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n};\Delta t),

(44b)

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}^{\mathrm{P}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+2/3},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1/2},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1};\Delta t/2,t^{n}+\Delta t/2),

(44c)

\displaystyle t^{n+1}

\displaystyle=t^{n}+\Delta t,

(44d)

where we note that
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1/3},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1/2},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+2/3}
are intermediate solutions and not
proper approximate solutions for intermediate times.

4.4
Hamiltonian splitting time scheme

We now split the discrete Hamiltonian (
36
) as described in subsection
4.1
,
Here we choose to separate
\mathsf{H}
into two parts:

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}}

\displaystyle:=\cfrac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}}

\displaystyle:=\cfrac{1}{2}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}\right).

(45)

The alternative splitting given by
\mathsf{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}}=(1/2){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}
and
\mathsf{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}}=(1/2)({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}})
yields a large subsystem involving the three fields, which is expensive to solve. In contrast, the choice in (
45
) yields small subsystems which are less costly to solve.
Again, following subsection
4.2
we place the boundary terms with the second subsystem
(
\mathsf{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}}
) since it will contain the
\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{B}
term. The splitting consists of solving the following subproblems:

•

the electric subsystem, corresponding to
\mathsf{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}}
, which reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}},

(46a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},

(46b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{1}\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},

(46c)

and

•

the magnetic-plasma subsystem, corresponding to
\mathsf{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}}
, which reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{1}\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}+\cos(t){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{R}+\sin(t){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{I}-\mathbb{A}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}-\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}},

(47a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}},

(47b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{1}\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}-\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}.

(47c)

Note that here
the collision term associated to
\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}
is kept for simplicity with the second subsystem but
could alternatively be placed differently, such as in (
46
) or in both subsystems with some weighting.

4.4.1
Time integration

Each problem is integrated numerically in the interval
(t^{n},t^{n+1}=t^{n}+\Delta t)
yielding the following discrete flows.

\bullet

A first flow
({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1})=\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}}^{\mathrm{H}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n};\Delta t)
obtained by integrating the electric subsystem (
46
):

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},

(48a)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n}-\Delta t\mathbb{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},

(48b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n}+\Delta t\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},

(48c)

which corresponds to an exact integration and therefore
\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}}^{\mathrm{H}}
preserves the Poisson structure. Equation (
48c
) is solved using PCG with a Kronecker mass solver as preconditioner.

\bullet

A second flow
({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1})=\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}}^{\mathrm{H}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n};\Delta t,t^{n})
obtained by integrating the magnetic-plasma subsystem (
47
):

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{A}_{1}&\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{M}_{1}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\left(\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}+\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}\right)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1}\\[10.00002pt]
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{A}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n}+\Delta t\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n}+s^{n}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}_{R}^{\mathrm{inc}}-c^{n}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}_{I}^{\mathrm{inc}}\\
\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\left(\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}+\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}\right){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n}\end{bmatrix},

(49a)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n},

(49b)

where
s^{n}=\sin(t^{n+1})-\sin(t^{n})
and
c^{n}=\cos(t^{n+1})-\cos(t^{n})
. Here we used a second-order trapezoidal rule for the integration. The system (
49a
) is solved with PBiCGStab, since the matrix is not symmetric. Again, the preconditioner is a block diagonal matrix, where the blocks are Kronecker mass solvers.

4.4.2
Hamiltonian splitting scheme

Lastly, we compose the flows using Strang splitting. Given an initial condition
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{0}

at
t^{0}=0
, for each time-step
n=0,...,N-1
, compute

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1/2},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1/3})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}}^{\mathrm{H}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n};\Delta t/2),

(50a)

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1/2},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+2/3})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}}^{\mathrm{H}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1/2},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1/3};\Delta t,t^{n}),

(50b)

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}}^{\mathrm{H}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1/2},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+2/3};\Delta t/2),

(50c)

\displaystyle t^{n+1}

\displaystyle=t^{n}+\Delta t,

(50d)

where again the fractional superscripts just indicate intermediate solutions and not proper approximate solutions for intermediate times.

4.5
Crank-Nicolson time scheme

For the purpose of comparison we finally consider an implicit numerical integration of the full system
using a Crank-Nicolson method, which consists in integrating (
32
) with the second-order trapezoidal rule.
This yields a flow
({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1})=\Phi^{\mathrm{CN}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n};\Delta t,t^{n})
defined by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{A}_{1}&-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}&\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}\\
\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{0}\\
-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{M}_{1}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}+\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1}\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1}\\
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{A}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n}+s^{n}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{R}-c^{n}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{I}\\
-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n}\\
\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{p}}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n}+\mathbb{M}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\left(\mathbb{R}_{1,\hat{\omega}_{c}}+\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}\right){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n}\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathbb{I}
denotes the identity matrix,
s^{n}=\sin(t^{n+1})-\sin(t^{n})
and
c^{n}=\cos(t^{n+1})-\cos(t^{n})

as above.
Note that in the ideal case (
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{0}
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}}
), this flow also preserves the Hamiltonian

\mathsf{H}
. Since the matrix is not symmetric, the system is solved using PBiCGStab. For the preconditioner we construct a block diagonal matrix, where each block is a Kronecker mass solver.

4.5.1
Crank-Nicolson scheme

Given an initial condition
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{0}
at
t^{0}=0
, for each time-step
n=0,...,N-1
, compute

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n+1})

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\mathrm{CN}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{n};\Delta t,t^{n}),

(51)

\displaystyle t^{n+1}

\displaystyle=t^{n}+\Delta t.

5
Long-time stability

In this section we show that the solution cannot grow infinitely large after long times. We study the long-time stability at three levels: the continuous case, the semi-discrete case and the fully discrete case.

5.1
Continuous level

Here we show the long-time stability of the solution of the time-domain problem (
12
) in terms of the evolution of the distance to the time-harmonic solution (
6
), which involves solving the frequency-domain problem (
7
).

The corresponding weak form consists of finding
\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}\in H_{\mathrm{imp}}(\textrm{curl},\Omega)
such that

\langle\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{F},\textrm{curl}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}\rangle_{\Omega}-i\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}=-i\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{F}\in H_{\mathrm{imp}}(\textrm{curl},\Omega).

(52)

In the frequency-domain the underlying field of the spaces is
\mathbb{C}
, but for simplicity we maintain the same notation.

Given
\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}\in H_{\mathrm{imp}}(\textrm{curl},\Omega)
the solution to (
52
), the triplet
\boldsymbol{U}^{\mathrm{th}}=(\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{th}},\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{th}},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{th}})
defined in (
6
) with the fields
\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}=-i\textrm{curl}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}
and
\hat{\omega}_{p}\hat{\boldsymbol{Y}}=i(\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}-\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}})

is a time-harmonic solution of the time-domain problem (
12
) with initial condition
\boldsymbol{U}^{\mathrm{th}}(0)
. As a result,
(\boldsymbol{E}-\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{th}},\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{th}},\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{th}})
is a solution of (
12
) with
\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\boldsymbol{0}
and thus, from the energy balance (
25
) we can deduce that

\partial_{t}\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{E}-\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{th}},\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{th}},\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{th}})\leq 0
, i.e.

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{U}(t)-\boldsymbol{U}^{\mathrm{th}}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\boldsymbol{U}(0)-\boldsymbol{U}^{\mathrm{th}}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall t>0,

(53)

where
\|\boldsymbol{U}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|\boldsymbol{E}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{Y}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.

5.2
Semi-discrete level

The long-time stability also holds after applying the space discretization. In terms of the spline coefficients the weak form of the frequency-domain problem (
52
) becomes

(\mathbb{C}^{T}\mathbb{M}_{1}\mathbb{C}-\mathbb{M}_{1,\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}}-i\mathbb{A}_{1})\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}}=-i\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}}^{\mathrm{inc}},

(54)

where

(\mathbb{M}_{1,\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}})_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\rangle_{\Omega},\qquad(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}}^{\mathrm{inc}})_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}.

Again, the coefficient array
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th}}=[{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{\mathrm{th}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{\mathrm{th}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{\mathrm{th}}]^{T}
with

{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{\mathrm{th}}(t)=\Re\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}}e^{-it}\},\quad{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{\mathrm{th}}(t)=\Re\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}}e^{-it}\},\quad{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{\mathrm{th}}(t)=\Re\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}}e^{-it}\}

is a solution of (
32
) and therefore
[{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{\mathrm{th}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{\mathrm{th}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{\mathrm{th}}]^{T}
is also a solution with
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}}
. From (
39
) we deduce that
\partial_{t}\mathsf{H}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{\mathrm{th}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}^{\mathrm{th}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}^{\mathrm{th}})\leq 0
, i.e. for every
t>0
,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{U}_{h}(t)-\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\boldsymbol{U}_{h}(0)-\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall t>0,

(55)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1}(\boldsymbol{E}_{h})={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}(\boldsymbol{B}_{h})={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{h})={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}
are the coefficients of the solution
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{E}_{h},\boldsymbol{B}_{h},\boldsymbol{Y}_{h})
.

5.3
Fully discrete level

At the fully-discrete level we show the long-time stability only for the Crank-Nicolson (
51
) and the Poisson splitting (
44
) schemes. For the Hamiltonian-splitting scheme (
50
), a similar result could be obtained by performing a backward error analysis and exhibiting a conserved modified Hamiltonian that is preserved and controls the true Hamiltonian under a CFL condition as it is done in
[
36
]
. This analysis will not be performed here.

We derive a similar result to (
55
), but with
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}
being a solution of the Crank-Nicolson or the Poisson splitting scheme. Given a solution
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{E}_{h},\boldsymbol{B}_{h},\boldsymbol{Y}_{h})
with spline coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}=[{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}]^{T}=[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}(\boldsymbol{E}_{h}),\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}(\boldsymbol{B}_{h}),\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{h})]^{T}
, we define the norm

\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}\|_{\mathbb{M}}=\sqrt{({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n})^{T}\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}}=\|\boldsymbol{U}_{h}(t^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad\text{with}\quad\mathbb{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{M}_{1}&0&0\\
0&\mathbb{M}_{2}&0\\
0&0&\mathbb{M}_{1}\end{bmatrix},

for every
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}(t^{n})\in C_{h}
and
n\geq 0
.

For the following analysis, we assume that the time-domain problem (
12
) and the frequency-domain problem (
52
) are well-posed.

5.3.1
Crank-Nicolson scheme

We recall the full evolution equation for the Crank-Nicolson scheme given in (
32
), that is,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}(t).

(56)

The Crank-Nicolson scheme consists of the update rule

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}=\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n})+\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n}+\Delta t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}(t)dt,

(57)

In the case
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}}
, we can define the discrete evolution operator
\mathbb{K}
satisfying
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}=\mathbb{K}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}
for every
n
, that is,

\mathbb{K}=\left(\mathbb{I}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{I}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}\right).

(58)

The discrete evolution operator is well defined since the matrix
\mathbb{I}-(\Delta t/2)(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}
is invertible.

Proof.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}
be an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
, then

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}^{T}\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}=\left[\left(\mathbb{I}-(\Delta t/2)(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}\right){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}\right]^{T}\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}^{T}\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}-(\Delta t/2)\left[(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}\right]^{T}\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(1-\lambda_{k}){\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}^{T}\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}=(\Delta t/2)\left(\mathbb{N}\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}\right)^{T}\mathbb{M}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{W}}}_{k}\leq 0\quad\Rightarrow\lambda_{k}\geq 1.

In particular,
\lambda_{k}\neq 0
, thus
\mathbb{I}-(\Delta t/2)(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}
is invertible.
∎

Furthermore,
\mathbb{K}
is nonexpansive, in the sense that
\|\mathbb{K}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\|_{\mathbb{M}}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\|_{\mathbb{M}}
.

Proof.

Assume
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}}
, then
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}=\mathbb{K}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}
and it holds that

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{K}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{M}}-\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{M}}

\displaystyle=({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n})^{T}\mathbb{M}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n})=\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\left[(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n})\right]^{T}\mathbb{M}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n})

\displaystyle=\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\left[\mathbb{N}\mathbb{M}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n})\right]^{T}\mathbb{M}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n})\leq 0,

since
\mathbb{P}
is skew-symmetric and
\mathbb{N}
is negative semi-definite.
∎

Next we consider the source term
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}
and show the long-time stability result.
We consider the coefficients corresponding to a time-harmonic solution given by
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},n}=\Re\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}_{\Delta t}e^{-in\Delta t}\}
, where the complex
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}_{\Delta t}
depends on
\Delta t
but not on
n
. The corresponding sequence in
n
yields a discrete evolution of the Crank-Nicolson scheme if for any
n
it holds

{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},n}=\mathbb{K}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},n-1}+\left(\mathbb{I}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}\right)^{-1}\int_{(n-1)\Delta t}^{n\Delta t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}dt.

(59)

Equation (
59
) forces the definition of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}_{\Delta t}
, which reads

\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}_{\Delta t}=i(e^{-i\Delta t}-1)\left(e^{-i\Delta t}\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{K}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{I}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}\right)^{-1}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}},

(60)

where
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}}=[\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}},\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}]^{T}
.
Here we assume that the matrix
(e^{-i\Delta t}\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{K})
is invertible, which is a natural consideration following the assumption that the frequency-domain problem is well-posed.

With this construction, for any iterates of the Crank-Nicolson scheme
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}
, the difference
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},n}
solves a discrete evolution with
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}}
, i.e.

{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},n}=\mathbb{K}^{n}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{0}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},0}\right).

(61)

Since
\mathbb{K}
is nonexpansive, we conclude with the long-time stability result

\|\boldsymbol{U}_{h}(t^{n})-\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}(t^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\boldsymbol{U}_{h}(0)-\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{for every }n\geq 0,

(62)

where
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},n}

are the spline coefficients of
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}(t^{n})
.

5.3.2
Poisson splitting scheme

First we recall the subproblems of the Poisson splitting scheme defined in (
40
) and (
41
), that is,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}(t),

(63)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}_{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}},

with
\mathbb{T}_{1}=(\mathbb{P}_{1}+\mathbb{N}_{1})\mathbb{M}
,
\mathbb{T}_{2}=(\mathbb{P}_{2}+\mathbb{N}_{2})\mathbb{M}
,

\mathbb{N}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{A}_{1}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{N}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{0}&-\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{M}_{1,\hat{\nu}_{e}}\mathbb{M}_{1}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

The Poisson splitting scheme consists of the update rule

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1/3}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}

\displaystyle=\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1/3}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n})+\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n}+\Delta t/2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}dt,

(64)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+2/3}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1/3}

\displaystyle=\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{T}_{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+2/3}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1/3}),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+2/3}

\displaystyle=\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}+{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+2/3})+\int_{t^{n}+\Delta t/2}^{t^{n}+\Delta t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}dt.

Hence any discrete evolution of the Poisson splitting scheme (
44
) must satisfy

{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n+1}=\mathbb{K}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}+\left(\mathbb{I}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}\right)^{-1}\int_{t^{n}+\Delta t/2}^{t^{n}+\Delta t}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}dt+\mathbb{K}\left(\mathbb{I}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}\right)^{-1}\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n}+\Delta t/2}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}dt,

(65)

where the discrete evolution operator is given by

\mathbb{K}=\left(\mathbb{I}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{I}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}\right)\left(\mathbb{I}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{T}_{2}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{I}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{T}_{2}\right)\left(\mathbb{I}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{I}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}\right),

(66)

where the matrices
\mathbb{I}-(\Delta t/4)\mathbb{T}_{1}
and
\mathbb{I}-(\Delta t/2)\mathbb{T}_{2}
are invertible. The proof is analogous to the proof for the inverse of
\mathbb{I}-(\Delta t/2)(\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{N})\mathbb{M}
in (
58
).

In addition,
\mathbb{K}
is nonexpansive, in the sense that
\|\mathbb{K}{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\|_{\mathbb{M}}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\|_{\mathbb{M}}
for every
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\in C_{h}
. This is due to the fact that the operator is the composition of three nonexpansive operators (for each step). The proof that each step is nonexpansive is analogous to the proof showing that (
58
) is nonexpansive.

Following we show the form of the solution considering the source. As before, we consider the coefficient sequence given by
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},n}=\Re\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}_{\Delta t}e^{-in\Delta t}\}
, where the complex
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}_{\Delta t}
depends on
\Delta t
but not on
n
. They yield a discrete evolution of the Poisson splitting scheme (
44
) if for every
n
it holds

\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}}_{\Delta t}=i\left(e^{-i\Delta t}\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{K}\right)^{-1}\left[(e^{-i\Delta t}-e^{-i\Delta t/2})\left(\mathbb{I}-\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}\right)^{-1}+(e^{-i\Delta t/2}-1)\mathbb{K}\left(\mathbb{I}+\cfrac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{T}_{1}\right)^{-1}\right]\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}}}.

(67)

As in (
60
), here we assume that
(e^{-i\Delta t}\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{K})
is invertible.
For any discrete evolution
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}
, the difference yields an evolution of the Poisson splitting scheme (
44
) without source. Consequently, it holds that

{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{n}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},n}=\mathbb{K}^{n}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{0}-{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}^{\mathrm{th},0}\right),

(68)

which shows the same long-time stability result as in (
62
), since
\mathbb{K}
is also nonexpansive.

6
Benchmarking and performance study

In this section we verify the accuracy of our scheme on exact univariate solutions. For that we consider the domain
\Omega=[0,3\pi]\times[0,2\pi]\times[0,2\pi]
and the physical parameters
\hat{\omega}_{c}=0.5
,
\boldsymbol{b}_{0}=(0,0,1)
and
\hat{\omega}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=x/100
. In addition, periodic boundary conditions are imposed along the
y
and
z
directions.
We study the accuracy of the proposed methods in two test cases. The first one follows an O-mode polarization (Transverse Magnetic), i.e.
\boldsymbol{E}\parallel\boldsymbol{b}_{0}
, and the second one follows an X-mode polarization (Transverse Electric), i.e.
\boldsymbol{E}\perp\boldsymbol{b}_{0}
.
The simulation of an X-mode wave is in general more complex than an O-mode wave. Firstly, in (
12c
) the term
\langle\boldsymbol{G}\times\boldsymbol{Y},\hat{\omega}_{c}\boldsymbol{b}_{0}\rangle_{\Omega}
is zero in the O-mode case. Secondly, as we have shown in the dispersion relation analysis of section
2.3
, the X-mode configuration has a resonance. In our tests we tune the parameters in order to avoid the resonance. Since the X-mode case is more involved, we include a study on the numerical stability, conservation of physical quantities and performance.
A solution with a certain polarization can be manufactured following the steps:

1.

Choose a current density
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{ex}}
either polarized along
z
(O-mode) or in the
x-y
plane (X-mode).

2.

Find electric field
\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{ex}}
using the current density equation (
2c
).

3.

Find magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{ex}}
using Faraday equation (
2b
).

4.

Find the trace
\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\boldsymbol{\nu}\times(\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{ex}}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{ex}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu})
so that the boundary conditions (
3
) hold.

5.

Add a volume source
\boldsymbol{S}:=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{ex}}-\textrm{curl}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{ex}}+\hat{\omega}_{p}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{ex}}
to the Ampère-Maxwell equation (
2a
) corresponding to the residual.

For convenience, we construct the above solution as the real part of a time-harmonic solution, that is

\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{ex}}=\Re\{\hat{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{\mathrm{ex}}e^{-it}\}
,
\dots
,
\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\Re\{\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}e^{-it}\}
.
We further decompose the volume source as
\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{S}_{R}\cos(t)+\boldsymbol{S}_{I}\sin(t)
and redefine the arrays in (

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}_{R})_{\boldsymbol{i}}:=\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\Re\{\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\mathrm{inc}}\}\rangle_{\Gamma_{A}}+\langle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}},\boldsymbol{S}_{R}\rangle_{\Omega},

6.1
Numerical errors and discrete numerical parameters

We denote the Courant number
\mathrm{CFL}=\Delta t/\Delta x
, where
\Delta t
is the normalized time-step and
\Delta x
is the diameter of a cell in the
x
direction.
Each simulation is run for three periods, i.e.
t\in[0,3T]
, where
T=2\pi
. The grid size and time-step are controlled by the parameters

\mathrm{PPW}=\cfrac{2\pi}{\Delta x},\qquad\mathrm{PPP}=\cfrac{2\pi}{\Delta t},

which correspond to the number of points per wavelength and the number of time-points per period, respectively.
Since the parameters and exact solution only depend on
x
we use a single cell in the
y
and
z
directions.
The B-spline degree of the space
V^{0}_{h}
is fixed as
\boldsymbol{p}=(3,1,1)
, thus the degree of the basis functions for spaces
V^{1}_{h}
and
V^{2}_{h}
is set, as it is shown in (
30
).
We distinguish the following three errors:

•

Projection errors
defined as

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{proj}}_{E}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{ex}}-P_{1}(\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{ex}})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{proj}}_{B}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{ex}}-\Pi_{2}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{ex}})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{proj}}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{ex}}-P_{1}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{ex}})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(70)

where
P_{1}:(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}\rightarrow V_{h}^{1}
denotes the
L^{2}
-projection. For consistency reasons with discrete Gauss laws, it is natural to define the reference discrete fields using the
L^{2}
-projection for
\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{ex}}
,
\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{ex}}
and a commuting projection
\Pi_{2}
for
\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{ex}}
.
These errors only depend on the space discretization, i.e.
\mathrm{PPW}
and
\boldsymbol{p}
.

•

Total errors
defined as

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{total}}_{E}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{ex}}-\boldsymbol{E}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{total}}_{B}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{ex}}-\boldsymbol{B}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{total}}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{ex}}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(71)

which depend on the space and time discretizations, thus
\mathrm{PPW}
,
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\mathrm{PPP}
.

•

Errors of the time-evolution solver defined as

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{solver}}_{E}

\displaystyle=\|P_{1}(\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{ex}})-\boldsymbol{E}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{solver}}_{B}

\displaystyle=\|\Pi_{2}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{ex}})-\boldsymbol{B}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{solver}}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\|P_{1}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\mathrm{ex}})-\boldsymbol{Y}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(72)

Since the discrete operators are approximations of continuous operators, the solver errors also depend on the space and time discretizations, thus
\mathrm{PPW}
,
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\mathrm{PPP}
.

These errors are defined pointwise in time. In practice, we study their maximum value in time.

6.4.1
Operation metrics

Since the operators have different sizes and they are applied a different number of times per time-step, the number of iterations is not enough to compare the performance of the schemes. Consequently we consider the metric

\mathrm{MVBP}=\text{number of Matrix-Vector Block Products (per time step)},

where a block correponds to a field:

{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}}
or
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}
. The blocks do not have the same size, since the fields belong to different spaces, however the size difference is very small and has a negligible effect on the performance.

The
\mathrm{MVBP}
metric does not depend on the number of coefficients within each block and therefore it cannot be used to compare different costs as the grid is refined. The number of time steps, or points per period
(
\mathrm{PPP}
), may also vary between two simulations.
For these reasons we introduce a second metric,
namely

\mathrm{LFOps}=\text{number of Local Field Operations}:=\mathrm{PPP}\times\mathrm{MVBP}\times\mathrm{dim}

where
\mathrm{dim}
is the dimension of the space
V^{1}_{h}
(close to that of
the space
V^{2}_{h}
).
Our performance study is based on these two metrics:
\mathrm{LFOps}
which accounts for the number of operations in one period of a given simulation, and
\mathrm{MVBP}
per time step which indicates how well the linear systems
are conditionned.

\mathrm{MVBP}
per time-step. For the experiments we manufacture two density patterns. The first pattern is made of the superposition of Gaussian functions centered at different points. The superposition is multiplied by an envelope of the form
\xi(\boldsymbol{x})=x
, to ensure that the incoming wave is launched from vacuum. Besides, the pattern is scaled so that it contains the cutoff contour but not the resonances. The second pattern consists on a single blob of high density, which is placed slightly below the beam axis. In total we run three experiments. The first involves an O-mode polarization and the complicated density pattern. The second consists of an X-mode polarization with the single blob density. Finally, the third one involves an X-mode polarization with the complicated density pattern. {\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}^{\mathrm{th}}=\Re\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}}}e^{-it}\}

To our present knowledge there is not a proved limited amplitude principle that ensures the convergence
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\boldsymbol{R}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
in the case of a general cold plasma. Physically, the wave goes through a transient phase until it reaches an equilibrium, where the behaviour is similar to
\boldsymbol{E}^{\mathrm{th}}_{h}

\boldsymbol{R}
as an indicator of the qualitative behaviour of the solution after long simulation times.

Remark 7.1

(Relation to the long-time stability)
.

The long-time stability result shown for the Poisson splitting scheme in (
68
) does not imply that
\boldsymbol{R}
decreases in time.
The analysis shows that under certain assumptions, we can construct a time-harmonic solution
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{U}_{h}(t^{n})-\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}(t^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
cannot increase as
n
increases. Here
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}
is a solution of the time-domain problem (
12
) and not the frequency-domain problem (
52
). In fact, we note that the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}
given in (
67
) depend on
\Delta t
. In other words, the electric field of
\boldsymbol{U}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}
is an approximation of
\boldsymbol{E}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}
using the time-evolution problem and therefore it involves the errors related to the time discretization. In contrast, the frequency-domain problem (
54
) does not involve the time discretization and therefore it provides a more reliable reference solution.

In fact, there are certain reasons to suspect that in practice
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\boldsymbol{R}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\neq 0
. Firstly, the source spectrum in the time-domain and frequency-domain problem is not the same. The time-domain problem has an initialization, which introduces high frequencies in the spectrum. Besides, the envelope for the Gaussian beam given in (
78
) introduces certain artificial frequencies in the first time-steps. Unless the corresponding modes are part of the transient regime and propagate outwards through the boundary
\Gamma_{A}
, they inevitably contribute to
\boldsymbol{R}
.
A secondary factor that may influence the convergence of
\boldsymbol{R}
is that, contrary to the numerical solution,
\boldsymbol{E}_{h}^{\mathrm{th}}
is not affected by the time-splitting error.

8
Conclusions

In the first part of this work, we have studied the Hamiltonian structure of the ideal problem and the effect of the boundary terms in the energy-balance. We derived two methods based on time-splitting geometric integrators for non-canonical Hamiltonian systems and proposed a treatment of the boundary terms, that guarantees the enforcement of the Silver-Müller boundary conditions.
In particular, one method uses Poisson splitting (
44
) and the other method is based on Hamiltonian splitting (
50
). For comparison in the analysis we included a method involving no splitting (
51
), which amounts to the Crank-Nicolson method. For the Poisson splitting and Crank-Nicolson schemes we showed that under certain assumptions the methods are long-time stable.

In the second part of this work, the properties of the proposed schemes were tested in a series of numerical experiments. We checked the convergence order, the numerical stability and compared the performance in terms of cost and accuracy. Furthermore, we showed that the proposed methods yield accurate approximations of the energy, total charge and zero divergence of magnetic field. In the study we found that the Poisson splitting scheme has the best cost/accuracy ratio by one order of magnitude: for a given accuracy it is the cheapest,
and for a given cost it is the most accurate.
Furthermore, it is unconditionally stable and our findings also hold with larger Courant numbers.

Finally, we used the Poisson splitting method to run three two-dimensional experiments of physical interest.
We tracked the convergence of the solution to the time-harmonic regime,
by using the same spatial discretization of the frequency-domain problem.
As expected, our experiments showed that in different scenarios the solutions converge towards the time-harmonic solution, with a residual error that is consistent with our
long-time stability analysis.
