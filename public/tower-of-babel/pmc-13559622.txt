Context-dependent roles of HtrA2/Omi in ovarian cancer: mitochondrial apoptotic function linking platinum sensitivity and acquired chemoresistance—a narrative review
Authors: Yao Wu, Yu Gu, Xuemei Wang, Hongjing Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveEpithelial ovarian cancer often responds to platinum therapy but frequently relapses with acquired chemoresistance. HtrA2/Omi is a mitochondrial serine protease that can promote apoptosis after cytosolic release. This narrative review synthesizes ovarian-cancer-specific and mechanistic evidence and asks whether HtrA2/Omi is better understood as a static expression marker or as a stress-activated apoptotic effector linked to platinum response.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and reference lists were searched through April 30, 2026 and updated through June 25, 2026 using terms related to HtrA2/Omi, PRSS25, ovarian cancer, platinum resistance, apoptosis, X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP), mitochondrial release, cytosolic release and invasion. A structured narrative approach was used because the ovarian HtrA2/Omi literature is limited, heterogeneous, and unsuitable for pooled quantitative synthesis.Key Content and FindingsThis review proposes a two-layer model. Baseline HtrA2/Omi abundance is context dependent and varies with histotype, assay platform, treatment state, and compartment resolution. In contrast, under platinum or mitochondrial stress, cytosolic HtrA2/Omi release more consistently supports XIAP relief, caspase activation, apoptosis, and platinum sensitivity. Persistent post-treatment HtrA2/Omi loss may mark an apoptosis-deficient resistant state.ConclusionsHtrA2/Omi should not be framed as a simple oncogene or tumor suppressor in ovarian cancer. Its clearest value is as a candidate response-linked, compartment-aware marker of platinum-induced apoptotic competence. Clinical use requires longitudinal, histotype-stratified and microenvironment-aware validation before treatment selection.

Introduction

Epithelial ovarian cancer remains one of the most lethal gynecologic malignancies because many patients present with advanced intraperitoneal disease and because relapse is common even after an initial platinum-responsive phase (1-6). Contemporary treatment is no longer limited to surgery plus chemotherapy. Maximal-effort cytoreductive surgery or neoadjuvant platinum-taxane chemotherapy followed by interval debulking remains central, while bevacizumab, poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor maintenance and folate receptor-alpha (FR-alpha)-directed antibody-drug conjugate therapy have expanded options in selected frontline, recurrent and platinum-resistant settings (2,3,7-21). These advances have not eliminated the central biological problem: tumors that initially undergo platinum-induced DNA damage and mitochondrial apoptosis frequently evolve toward apoptosis escape, recurrent disease and acquired chemoresistance. The clinical distinction between platinum-sensitive and platinum-resistant relapse remains prognostically important, but it is an imperfect surrogate for the deeper biology of cell death competence, residual DNA-repair capacity, tumor evolution and the anti-apoptotic state of recurrent disease (22-24). HtrA2/Omi is relevant to this transition because it sits at the interface between mitochondrial stress sensing and apoptosis execution. The protein is synthesized as a mitochondrial precursor, matures in the intermembrane space, exposes an inhibitor-of-apoptosis-protein (IAP)-binding motif after processing, and can be released into the cytosol after apoptotic injury. Once cytosolic, HtrA2/Omi can antagonize or degrade IAP proteins, particularly X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP), thereby facilitating caspase-9 and caspase-3/7 activation (25-37). At first glance, the ovarian-cancer literature on HtrA2/Omi appears inconsistent. Mixed expression studies report slight decreases, no significant differences or higher bulk tumor expression. At the same time, mechanistic studies repeatedly place cytosolic HtrA2/Omi on the pro-apoptotic side of platinum response: cisplatin-sensitive cells mobilize HtrA2/Omi into the cytosol; low HtrA2/Omi is associated with lower response rates and poorer survival in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC); and repeated cisplatin exposure can be selected toward a low-HtrA2/Omi, more invasive resistant phenotype (38-46). This review therefore uses a two-layer framework. Layer I asks why HtrA2/Omi looks different across baseline or cross-sectional cohorts. Layer II asks what HtrA2/Omi does once platinum or mitochondrial injury engages apoptosis signaling. The goal is not to force the literature into a single direction, but to separate static abundance from dynamic function and to identify which conclusions are directly supported, which are inferential and which remain unresolved. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1129/rc).

Methods

Review type and search strategy

This manuscript is a structured narrative review with transparent retrieval procedures and study-level evidence classification. It is not a formal systematic review, scoping review or meta-analysis. PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus were searched through April 30, 2026 and updated through June 25, 2026. Reference lists of core ovarian-cancer and HtrA2/Omi papers were manually screened to identify additional mechanistic and clinical context papers. The core search logic was: (HtrA2/Omi OR Omi OR PRSS25 OR high-temperature-required protein A2) AND (ovarian cancer OR ovarian carcinoma OR epithelial ovarian cancer OR serous ovarian cancer OR high-grade serous) AND (cisplatin OR carboplatin OR platinum resistance OR chemoresistance OR apoptosis OR XIAP OR caspase OR mitochondrial release OR cytosolic release OR invasion). Eligibility criteria

Included studies comprised: original ovarian-cancer studies directly examining HtrA2/Omi expression, localization, function, platinum response, recurrence, prognosis or invasion; mechanistic apoptosis studies relevant to HtrA2/Omi maturation, mitochondrial release, XIAP/IAP antagonism, protease activity, caspase activation or mitochondrial apoptosis; clinically informative ovarian-cancer studies linking HtrA2/Omi to chemotherapy response, progression-free survival, overall survival, post-treatment status or acquired resistance; and key background reviews, guidelines and major ovarian-cancer treatment trials used only for clinical context. Excluded sources were non-ovarian studies that mentioned HtrA2/Omi only incidentally and did not add mechanistic relevance, conference abstracts without full text, duplicate or obviously overlapping datasets, and HtrA-family papers without a direct HtrA2/Omi interpretive link.

Evidence classification and quality evaluation

To avoid overstating a limited and heterogeneous literature, studies were classified before synthesis. Level A evidence comprised direct ovarian-cancer clinical correlative studies. Level B evidence comprised direct ovarian-cancer cell-line or mechanistic studies. Level C evidence comprised ovarian expression-profiling studies without treatment-stress function. Level D evidence comprised non-ovarian mechanistic studies used as biological scaffolding. Level E evidence comprised general ovarian-cancer treatment or public-database context. No pooled quantitative estimate was attempted because the available studies differ substantially in histotype, treatment state, assay platform and outcome definition.

Nomenclature and biological basis

Throughout this review, HTRA2 denotes the human gene and HtrA2/Omi denotes the protein or mature protease. The alternative ordering HtrA2/Omi is retained only when it appears in article titles or historical descriptions. This distinction is important because public transcriptomic analyses measure HTRA2 mRNA, whereas most mechanistic and translational questions concern protein maturation, subcellular release and protease activity. HtrA2/Omi is one of four human HtrA serine proteases. It differs from secretory HtrA1, HtrA3 and HtrA4 because its canonical location is the mitochondrial intermembrane space. HtrA2/Omi is synthesized as an approximately 49-kDa precursor with an N-terminal mitochondrial localization sequence and membrane-associated region. After import and proteolytic maturation, the processed protein exposes an N-terminal IAP-binding motif (IBM) that resembles the motifs used by Smac/DIABLO and Drosophila IAP antagonists (25-31,37). This maturation step is biologically important. In intact mitochondria, HtrA2/Omi contributes to protein-quality control and mitochondrial homeostasis. After stress-induced release into the cytosol, the same molecule becomes an apoptosis amplifier. Therefore, total HtrA2/Omi protein abundance is not equivalent to active death signaling. A tumor can contain measurable mitochondrial HtrA2/Omi yet fail to execute apoptosis if upstream mitochondrial permeabilization, cytosolic release, IAP antagonism or caspase activation is blocked (33,37,47,48). This compartment-dependent mechanism is summarized in Figure 1. Cytosolic HtrA2/Omi promotes apoptosis through at least two partially overlapping mechanisms. First, the exposed IBM binds IAP proteins, particularly XIAP, thereby relieving direct inhibition of caspase-3, caspase-and caspase-9. Second, the protease domain can cleave IAPs or additional substrates, making anti-apoptotic restraint less reversible. The combination of IBM-mediated sequestration and protease-mediated degradation helps explain why HtrA2/Omi can contribute to both caspase-dependent and caspase-independent cell death (27-37). HtrA2/Omi should be interpreted as part of a mitochondrial death module rather than as a solitary determinant of cell fate. Under cisplatin stress, cytochrome c, Smac/DIABLO and HtrA2/Omi can be released together. Cytochrome c promotes apoptosome formation; Smac and HtrA2/Omi relieve IAP-mediated caspase suppression; and caspase activation executes apoptosis. In ovarian cancer, this integrated module is critical because platinum sensitivity depends less on one protein than on whether DNA damage is successfully converted into mitochondrial apoptosis (38,39,49-53). Upstream regulation is especially relevant. Mitochondrial p53 engagement can promote release of pro-apoptotic mitochondrial mediators, while Akt activation can preserve mitochondrial integrity and blunt the pathway. This has direct implications for ovarian cancer, where p53 protein accumulation does not necessarily indicate successful mitochondrial p53 action, and where PI3K-Akt signaling is frequently implicated in survival and chemoresistance (38,39,50,51).

Horizontal comparison within the HtrA family

A family-level comparison helps explain why HtrA2/Omi should be interpreted differently from HtrA1, HtrA3 and HtrA4 in ovarian cancer. HtrA1 and HtrA3 are generally discussed as secretory or non-mitochondrial HtrA-family proteases with tumor-suppressive, extracellular-matrix, invasion, chemotherapy-sensitivity or prognostic associations in gynecologic malignancies (40,41,46,54-59). HtrA4 is also a secreted HtrA-family protease, but its ovarian-cancer literature is much thinner and it is more often treated as a pregnancy/placental or extracellular-context molecule than as a mitochondrial apoptosis effector. HtrA2/Omi is distinct because its canonical location is the mitochondrial intermembrane space and because its pro-apoptotic function depends on precursor import, proteolytic maturation, mitochondrial outer-membrane permeabilization, cytosolic relocation, IAP interaction and protease activity (25-44). Therefore, the most clinically relevant HtrA2/Omi question is not simply whether total expression is high or low. The critical question is whether a tumor can convert platinum-induced mitochondrial injury into cytosolic release of mature HtrA2/Omi and downstream XIAP/caspase engagement. This makes HtrA2/Omi a compartment- and treatment-state marker candidate, whereas HtrA1, HtrA3 and HtrA4 mainly provide comparison points for extracellular, stromal or non-mitochondrial HtrA biology. HtrA2/Omi in ovarian cancer

Baseline heterogeneity (Layer I)

The first layer of the model addresses a simple but frequently blurred question: what does HtrA2/Omi abundance look like before treatment-state and compartmental function are considered? Direct ovarian expression studies show three patterns: slight decrease, no significant difference and higher bulk expression. These patterns should not be treated as interchangeable, because each derives from different cohorts, histotypes and assay platforms (40,41,43,46). The slight-decrease branch is best represented by Narkiewicz et al. This mixed cohort included normal ovaries, benign tumors, borderline tumors, malignant tumors and Krukenberg tumors. HtrA2/Omi mRNA and protein were only modestly lower in tumor tissues, whereas HtrA1 and HtrA3 showed stronger downregulation. Thus, the study did not support uniform HtrA2/Omi loss as a pan-ovarian-cancer event. Instead, it suggested that any HtrA2/Omi baseline drift is modest and easily confounded by tissue composition (40). The no-major-difference branch is represented by Singh et al., who examined HtrA-family expression in primary ovarian tumors and cancer cell lines. HtrA2/Omi was detected in ovarian control and tumor samples without significant differences, whereas HtrA3 was substantially reduced in serous and granulosa-cell ovarian tumors. This negative result argues against using baseline HtrA2/Omi abundance as a broad diagnostic marker across ovarian histotypes (41). The higher-bulk-expression branch is represented by Wang et al., who reported that HtrA2/Omi mRNA and protein were higher in epithelial ovarian carcinoma than in paired adjacent tissue and were associated with advanced stage and distant metastasis. However, the same study showed that HtrA2/Omi overexpression increased apoptosis, increased caspase-3 and caspase-9 expression, and restrained xenograft growth. This internal tension is central to the review: higher bulk abundance does not necessarily mean pro-tumor HtrA2/Omi activity. It may reflect precursor, mitochondrial, stromal, treatment-state or compensatory signals rather than active cytosolic apoptotic release (43). Five variables likely explain why cohorts disagree. First, histotype mixing is a major confounder. Second, several cohorts are limited and become underpowered when subgrouped. Third, mRNA-protein discordance is expected for a processed mitochondrial protease. Fourth, total expression is not equivalent to cytosolic release or protease activation. Fifth, pre-treatment, post-neoadjuvant, recurrent and post-platinum samples represent different biological states. A sixth technical factor is platform heterogeneity: reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), Western blotting, immunohistochemistry (IHC), tissue microarray scoring and public-omics mining measure different levels of biology. The safest conclusion is therefore not that baseline HtrA2/Omi is low or high in ovarian cancer. Rather, baseline HtrA2/Omi abundance is context dependent and insufficient by itself to infer function. This conclusion prevents overinterpretation of static expression and prepares the ground for the more consistent treatment-stress literature.

Stress-induced functional activation (Layer II)

Upstream triggering: platinum injury, mitochondrial priming and Akt/p53 gating

The second layer asks what HtrA2/Omi does once platinum or mitochondrial stress is engaged. In contrast to baseline abundance studies, treatment-stress studies are more directionally coherent. The central pattern is that successful mitochondrial priming permits HtrA2/Omi release, and released HtrA2/Omi contributes to apoptosis rather than survival (38,39,42,44,45). Yang et al. showed that cisplatin-sensitive ovarian cells accumulated p53 in mitochondria after cisplatin exposure, whereas resistant variants did not. This sensitive-state response included release of Smac, cytochrome c and HtrA2/Omi. Akt activation blunted mitochondrial p53 accumulation and suppressed release of these pro-apoptotic mediators, while Akt inhibition partially restored the pathway and sensitized resistant cells in a p53-dependent manner (39). This finding has two implications. First, HtrA2/Omi release is a downstream readout of successful mitochondrial commitment, not an isolated event. Second, total p53 expression alone is insufficient; the decisive factor is whether p53 engages mitochondria under drug stress. This is particularly relevant for HGSOC, where TP53 alteration is common and p53 immunostaining may not reflect mitochondrial apoptotic function (6,23,39,50,60).

Mitochondrial release and cytosolic activation

The pivotal event is HtrA2/Omi translocation from mitochondria to cytosol. Yang et al. demonstrated that cisplatin-sensitive A2780 and COC1 cells responded to cisplatin with XIAP downregulation, increased cytosolic HtrA2/Omi, increased caspase-3 activity and apoptosis. Their resistant derivatives failed to mount the same response. Downregulation of XIAP increased caspase-3 activity and sensitized resistant cells to cisplatin, while cytosolic HtrA2/Omi increased as XIAP was suppressed (38). The non-platinum lapatinib model is supportive but should be weighted carefully. In SKOV-3 cells, lapatinib-induced mitochondrial fragmentation increased mitochondrial ROS, reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and promoted HtrA2/Omi liberation into the cytoplasm, followed by caspase-3/9 activation and apoptosis. This supports a broader role for HtrA2/Omi as a mitochondrial stress effector, but it does not prove platinum-specific function (45).

XIAP antagonism, caspase activation and apoptosis amplification

Classical HtrA2/Omi studies established that the mature protein binds XIAP through its IBM and can proteolytically disable IAP proteins, thereby facilitating caspase activation (27-37). In ovarian cancer, the XIAP-HtrA2/Omi-caspase axis is most directly supported by Yang et al. 2005. However, the pathway should not be drawn as XIAP-only. Miyamoto et al. showed that HtrA2/Omi knockdown reduced cisplatin sensitivity in serous ovarian cells without a corresponding change in XIAP expression, indicating that HtrA2/Omi may have XIAP-dependent and XIAP-independent outputs (38,42). This nuance matters for therapeutic interpretation. XIAP is a major partner, but HtrA2/Omi also exists within a network containing Smac/DIABLO, cytochrome c, Bcl-2 family proteins, stress-activated kinase signaling, XIAP regulation and caspases (52,53,61-65). Therefore, HtrA2/Omi should be described as an apoptosis-amplifying effector within a mitochondrial death module, not as the sole determinant of platinum sensitivity (39,49,52,53,63).

Clinical association with platinum response: value and caution

Miyamoto et al. provide the strongest direct clinical bridge. In pure HGSOC cases, 25% were HtrA2/Omi-negative by tissue microarray IHC. HtrA2/Omi-negative tumors had lower primary chemotherapy response rates and worse progression-free and overall survival; in vitro, lower endogenous HtrA2/Omi correlated with higher cisplatin IC50, and HtrA2/Omi knockdown reduced cisplatin sensitivity (42). This study supports HtrA2/Omi as a candidate response-linked marker, but it should not be overinterpreted as practice-changing evidence. The cohort spanned 1984–2008 and had a substantial imbalance in chemotherapy regimen: HtrA2/Omi-negative cases were much more likely to receive conventional platinum-based therapy rather than taxane-platinum therapy. Although multivariable models were used, treatment era and regimen imbalance remain important limitations. The result is therefore hypothesis-generating and suitable for prospective validation, not immediate clinical implementation (42).

Acquired cisplatin resistance and invasion

Soyama et al. moved the field from baseline chemosensitivity into treatment-evolution biology. In ovarian serous carcinoma samples and the KF28 to KFr13 acquired-resistance model, repeated cisplatin treatment downregulated HtrA2/Omi. Low post-treatment HtrA2/Omi was associated with worse outcome, while HtrA2/Omi loss accompanied both cisplatin resistance and increased invasion. HtrA2/Omi knockdown in parental cells recapitulated resistance and invasive phenotypes (44). This is the key ovarian paper linking apoptosis escape with invasive progression. Nevertheless, the downstream invasion mechanism is not fully closed. Non-ovarian evidence suggests that p53-mediated HtrA2/Omi activation can suppress invasion by regulating beta-actin cleavage and p130Cas signaling, offering a plausible but unproven framework for ovarian models. The ovarian field still needs rescue studies that test whether restored HtrA2/Omi, protease-dead HtrA2/Omi or IBM-deficient HtrA2/Omi can reverse both resistance and invasion (44,66,67).

Candidate upstream mechanisms of HtrA2/Omi downregulation during resistance

The mechanisms responsible for HtrA2/Omi downregulation during acquired resistance can be organized into three linked levels rather than listed as independent possibilities. At the first level, platinum exposure may select cell states with lower mitochondrial apoptotic priming. Such cells can survive DNA damage because p53-dependent mitochondrial engagement, Bax/Bak activation, and mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) are insufficient to release cytochrome c, Smac/DIABLO and HtrA2/Omi together (38,39,50-53). At the second level, HTRA2 expression and HtrA2/Omi protein availability may be reduced by transcriptional or epigenetic repression, post-transcriptional regulation, altered mitochondrial import, impaired proteolytic maturation, or increased turnover of the mature mitochondrial pool (27,31,35,37,60,68-72). These mechanisms would not be captured reliably by bulk mRNA alone because the clinically relevant output is mature, releasable, cytosolic HtrA2/Omi. At the third level, survival signaling can block functional deployment of HtrA2/Omi even when total protein remains detectable. PI3K-Akt activation can preserve mitochondrial integrity and blunt p53-dependent mitochondrial signaling; autophagy and mitochondrial quality-control adaptation can remove damaged mitochondria before MOMP; and proteostasis remodeling may shift HtrA2/Omi from apoptotic execution toward stress tolerance (39,47,48,73-75).

These upstream events intersect directly with other resistance pathways. Reduced HtrA2/Omi release leaves XIAP and related IAP proteins less opposed, raising the caspase-activation threshold after platinum exposure (27-38,52,53,61-63). Autophagy-rich resistant states may further buffer mitochondrial injury and delay cytosolic death signaling (73,74). Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-like remodeling, p130Cas/actin signaling and invasion-associated cytoskeletal changes may couple apoptosis escape to peritoneal dissemination (44,66,67). The mechanistic prediction is therefore specific: resistant cells should show a discordance between total HtrA2/Omi abundance and cytosolic, protease-active HtrA2/Omi after platinum challenge. Future studies should test this by comparing wild-type, protease-dead, IBM-deficient, mitochondria-retained and forced-cytosolic HtrA2/Omi rescue constructs, while measuring MOMP, cytosolic HtrA2/Omi, XIAP binding, caspase-3/9 activation, autophagy flux and invasion in parallel.

Why total HtrA2/Omi abundance is not equivalent to function

The strongest conceptual correction in this review is the explicit distinction among total expression, mature mitochondrial protein, cytosolic release and protease-active HtrA2/Omi. Public-database analysis

Public databases can strengthen a narrative review only when their limitations match the biological question. For HtrA2/Omi, public resources can evaluate HTRA2 mRNA, copy-number or mutation status, and in some datasets total protein abundance. They cannot directly measure mature cytosolic HtrA2/Omi release, XIAP engagement or protease-active apoptosis signaling. Therefore, public-database findings should be presented as hypothesis-generating support rather than independent validation of the compartment-aware model (60,68-72). A reproducible public-database analysis should use The Cancer Genome Atlas ovarian cancer cohort (TCGA-OV) for bulk HTRA2 mRNA and survival exploration, Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) HGSOC proteogenomic data for total protein-level context, Human Protein Atlas for tissue/subcellular expression overview, and cBioPortal for mutation/copy-number context. If Kaplan-Meier Plotter or GEPIA2 is used, analyses should specify probe/gene model, histotype filters, debulking and chemotherapy covariates when available, and should avoid interpreting mRNA survival curves as evidence of cytosolic HtrA2/Omi function (60,68-72). Cell-model limitations and model-modernization needs

A major limitation of the HtrA2/Omi ovarian literature is its reliance on classic cell-line models. A2780, SKOV-3, OVCAR-3, COC1 and related resistant derivatives are useful for pathway dissection, but they do not fully reproduce the genomic, histologic, stromal and treatment-history complexity of HGSOC. Some models are not high-grade-serous-like by contemporary molecular criteria, and most do not reflect the post-PARP inhibitor or post-bevacizumab states now encountered clinically. Isogenic acquired-resistance models remain valuable because they isolate treatment selection pressure and allow rescue experiments. However, resistance generated by repeated drug exposure in vitro may not reproduce immune pressure, hypoxia, stromal protection, intraperitoneal dissemination or clonal heterogeneity in patients. For HtrA2/Omi, this limitation is especially relevant because mitochondrial stress, apoptosis threshold and invasion are strongly shaped by microenvironmental context. Future validation should therefore prioritize patient-derived organoids, organoid-drug testing paired with matched clinical response, patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, spatial proteomics and serial biopsies (76,77) from primary, interval, relapse and post-platinum-resistant disease. Genomic annotation should include breast cancer susceptibility gene 1/2 (BRCA1/2), homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), TP53 pattern, prior PARP inhibitor exposure, platinum-free interval, residual disease and histotype. Without such modernization, HtrA2/Omi cannot be confidently moved from a mechanistic candidate to a clinically useful response-linked marker.

Detection methods and translational path

The most defensible translational role for HtrA2/Omi today is as a candidate marker of apoptosis competence, not as an established standalone biomarker. The strongest use-case is platinum-treated serous/HGSOC, where lower HtrA2/Omi has been associated with poorer primary chemotherapy response and worse survival. However, prospective validation is required before clinical decision making can rely on HtrA2/Omi status (42,44). A clinically useful assay would likely be multiplex and compartment aware. Rather than measuring only baseline total HtrA2/Omi, it should consider total HtrA2/Omi, mature or cytosolic HtrA2/Omi, XIAP, cleaved caspase-3/9 and timing relative to platinum exposure. This would align the biomarker with the underlying biology: the critical event is not mere abundance, but conversion of mitochondrial injury into apoptosis execution (38,39,42).

Intraperitoneal microenvironmental stress and HtrA2/Omi function

A tumor-cell-intrinsic apoptosis model is necessary but incomplete for ovarian cancer because most advanced disease evolves within the peritoneal cavity, where ascites, stromal contact, extracellular-matrix remodeling, hypoxia, nutrient limitation, inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, mesothelial-cell interaction, cancer-associated fibroblasts and immune-cell pressure shape metastatic survival and treatment response (4-6,78-82). These stresses can regulate HtrA2/Omi function by acting upstream of mitochondrial commitment. Hypoxia, nutrient stress and ascites-derived cytokines may activate PI3K-Akt, NF-kappaB, STAT3, autophagy and antioxidant programs that preserve mitochondrial membrane integrity, increase Bcl-2-family anti-apoptotic buffering, suppress Bax/Bak-dependent MOMP, and thereby reduce cytosolic release of mature HtrA2/Omi after platinum injury (38,39,52,53,61-63,73,74). Conversely, microenvironmental oxidative stress or inadequate mitochondrial quality control may prime mitochondria for MOMP, making HtrA2/Omi release more likely once platinum damage crosses the apoptotic threshold. Thus, the key microenvironmental question is not merely whether HtrA2/Omi is expressed, but whether peritoneal stress signals shift the balance between mitochondrial retention and cytosolic release.

This mechanism also provides an explanation for compartment-specific resistance. In ascites-rich or stromal-protected niches, tumor cells may maintain measurable total HtrA2/Omi while preventing its apoptotic deployment through MOMP blockade, enhanced IAP/XIAP buffering, autophagy-mediated mitochondrial cleanup, or EMT/invasion programs that favor survival during detachment and implantation (44,52,53,61-63,66,73,74,78-80). This would produce the exact pattern that static assays miss: total protein present, but low cytosolic HtrA2/Omi, weak XIAP engagement, low caspase activation and poor platinum response. Direct ovarian evidence for every step remains limited, so the revised model should be tested mechanistically. Ascites-conditioned media, hypoxia/nutrient-stress models, mesothelial or cancer-associated fibroblast co-culture, patient-derived organoids, PDX models with matched ascites/peritoneal implants and serial pre/post-platinum samples should measure mitochondrial retention, cytosolic release, HtrA2/Omi-XIAP interaction, caspase activation, MOMP markers and platinum response in the same system (38,39,42,44,76-82).

What HtrA2/Omi cannot yet do clinically

At present, HtrA2/Omi should not be used to select treatment, omit platinum-free interval, choose or withhold PARP inhibitor maintenance, guide bevacizumab decisions, guide mirvetuximab soravtansine decisions, or classify a patient as platinum-sensitive or platinum-resistant outside a research protocol. It should not be presented as a diagnostic marker for ovarian cancer or as a validated prognostic marker across all ovarian histotypes. Its current value is best framed as a candidate response-linked marker of mitochondrial apoptosis competence in platinum-treated serous/HGSOC. This boundary protects the manuscript from clinical overreach. HtrA2/Omi may ultimately become useful as part of a multiparameter apoptosis-competence panel, but only after analytical validation, prospective clinical validation and demonstration of incremental value beyond breast cancer susceptibility gene (BRCA)/HRD status, residual disease, platinum-free interval, prior PARP inhibitor exposure, FR-alpha expression and histotype.

Translational implications and therapeutic opportunities

HtrA2/Omi is not currently a direct therapeutic target in ovarian cancer. However, it may help define a biologic state: whether a tumor retains the ability to convert platinum-induced mitochondrial stress into caspase execution. This state could be especially relevant in platinum-treated serous/HGSOC, acquired platinum resistance and recurrent disease in which apoptosis competence is uncertain. The most plausible translational use is therefore panel-based. HtrA2/Omi-low, XIAP-high, cleaved-caspase-low tumors may represent apoptosis-deficient states. HtrA2/Omi-released, XIAP-relieved, cleaved-caspase-positive tumors may represent apoptosis-competent states. Such a framework could inform trial stratification for IAP antagonists or SMAC mimetics, but it does not justify treatment selection outside research settings (63,83-87). HtrA2/Omi should also be evaluated within the modern biomarker ecosystem of ovarian cancer. BRCA/HRD status, prior PARP inhibitor exposure, bevacizumab exposure, FR-alpha status, residual disease, platinum-free interval and histotype all shape treatment selection. HtrA2/Omi would need to demonstrate incremental value beyond these variables and should therefore be tested in well-annotated, contemporary serous/HGSOC cohorts (2,3,15,24).

Limitations of the current evidence and of this review

The main strengths of this review are the explicit separation of baseline abundance from stress-activated function, conservative evidence grading, and a translational assay framework. Its limitations are summarized below. First, the direct ovarian HtrA2/Omi literature is limited. The most persuasive clinical evidence comes from retrospective HGSOC analyses and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing (42,44). Second, human tissue studies usually measure total expression rather than mature, cytosolic or protease-active HtrA2/Omi. This matters because HtrA2/Omi function depends on compartmental movement and interaction with anti-apoptotic proteins. Third, the mechanistic literature relies heavily on cell lines. These models are useful for testing XIAP, Akt/p53 and mitochondrial-release pathways but do not capture the full clinical heterogeneity of HGSOC or the modern treatment sequence. Fourth, the public-database component is limited by the biology of available data. Bulk HTRA2 mRNA and total protein can be explored, but public databases do not directly measure cytosolic release or HtrA2/Omi protease activity. Fifth, this review is structured and transparent but remains narrative. It does not provide a PRISMA flow count, formal risk-of-bias scoring instrument or pooled effect estimate. This choice reflects the current heterogeneity of the literature rather than an attempt to claim systematic-review completeness.

Specific translational recommendations

The immediate translational step is not clinical use of HtrA2/Omi as a treatment-selection marker, but disciplined assay development. Specifically, the validation path includes retrospective assay development, compartment-aware IHC/immunofluorescence, paired pre/post-platinum samples, organoid validation, and PDX validation. A practical validation path would begin with archived HGSOC cohorts with known platinum response, residual disease status, BRCA/HRD status, treatment regimen, recurrence interval and survival outcomes (42,44,76,77). Total HtrA2/Omi IHC should be compared with multiplex or compartment-aware readouts that include cytosolic HtrA2/Omi, XIAP, cleaved caspase-3 or caspase-9, MOMP markers and tumor-cell markers (38,39,42,44,52,53,61-63). The staged validation plan is summarized in Table 9. The key question is whether an HtrA2/Omi-centered apoptosis-competence panel adds predictive value beyond established clinical and molecular variables.

Prospective studies should then use paired pretreatment, interval, relapse or platinum-resistant samples where feasible. Patient-derived organoids and PDXs could be used for mechanistic co-validation, including platinum-response testing and HtrA2/Omi rescue experiments. Only if these analytical and clinical validation steps are successful should HtrA2/Omi be considered for biomarker-guided trial stratification or combination strategies involving IAP antagonists, apoptosis-enhancing agents, or treatments designed to restore mitochondrial death execution (16-20,63,83-85,88). Conclusions

HtrA2/Omi in ovarian cancer is best interpreted through a two-layer framework. At baseline, cross-sectional studies show genuine heterogeneity: HtrA2/Omi can appear slightly decreased, unchanged or increased depending on cohort composition, histotype, assay platform and treatment state. This heterogeneity makes total abundance alone insufficient for functional interpretation. The second layer is dynamic and more biologically decisive. When cisplatin or mitochondrial stress engages the death program, HtrA2/Omi functions predominantly as a pro-apoptotic protease that helps relieve IAP-mediated restraint, activate caspases and promote platinum-induced apoptosis. Persistently low or treatment-downregulated HtrA2/Omi is associated with poorer platinum response, acquired resistance and increased invasion in serous ovarian models. The field should now move beyond simple abundance claims. Future work should use compartment-aware assays, longitudinal patient samples, modern HGSOC models and conservative evidence grading. Only then can HtrA2/Omi be judged as a clinically useful candidate marker or therapeutic vulnerability in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer.
