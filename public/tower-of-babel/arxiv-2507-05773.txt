Abstract
. We consider the wave scattering and inverse scattering in an inhomogeneous medium embedded a homogeneous droplet with a small size, which is modeled by a constant mass density and a small bulk modulus. Based on the Lippmann-Schwinger integral equation for scattering wave in inhomogeneous medium, we firstly develop an efficient approximate scheme for computing the scattered wave as well as its far-field pattern for any droplet located in the inhomogeneous background medium. By establishing the approximate relation between the far-field patterns of the scattered wave before and after the injection of a droplet, the scattered wave of the inhomogeneous medium after injecting the droplet is represented by a measurable far-field patterns, and consequently the inhomogeneity of the medium can be reconstructed from the Helmholtz equation. Finally, the reconstruction process in terms of the dual reciprocity method is proposed to realize the numerical algorithm for recovering the bulk modulus function inside a bounded domain in three dimensional space, by moving the droplet inside the bounded domain. Numerical implementations are given using the simulation data of the far-field pattern to show the validity of the reconstruction scheme, based on the mollification scheme for dealing with the ill-posedness of this inverse problem. Keywords : Helmholtz equation, domain integral, Bessel functions, acoustic wave scattering, Newtonian potential, integral equation, body-wave resonances, numerics. AMS subject classification : 35J05; 35R30; 65D12; 65D30; 65R20; 65R32.



1.
Introduction

The wave propagation in inhomogeneous medium corresponding to given incident wave is an important physical phenomenon. For different kinds of waves, the governed equation may be in different forms, say, the Helmholtz equation for acoustic wave, the Maxwell equations for electromagnetic wave, and the elastic wave equations for elastic waves. In general, the inhomogeneity of the medium is represented by some physical parameters such as medium density and wave velocity arising in the governed equations. The reconstruction of the medium parameters using some information about the scattered wave such as its far-field pattern, which are called the inverse medium problems, have been thoroughly studied, see
[
9
]
and the references therein. It has been proven in
[
9
, Theorem 10.5]
that the far-field pattern of the scattered wave using all observation points excited by incident plane waves of the form

u^{inc}(x,\theta):=e^{ikx\cdot\theta}=e^{ik\left|x\right|\hat{x}\cdot\theta},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\;\;\theta,\hat{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|=1\},

where
k
is the fixed wave number,
\theta
is the incident direction, and
\hat{x}
is the unit observation direction, from all incident directions
\theta
can uniquely determine the refraction index with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Of course, the requirement for the unique determination of the medium inhomogeneity that both
\hat{x}
and
\theta
should be full of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is not practical from the engineering point of view.

Except for the wave propagation in general inhomogeneous media, the other important physical situations are the wave propagation in inhomogeneous media embedded with some bubbles, or droplets. Many interesting physical works have been devoted to the acoustic bubble problems, see

[
10
,
7
,
5
]
. In the case that the bubbles are of small size, they will yield extra scattering behaviors like the point-source.
Then by moving bubbles in the inhomogeneous medium, the corresponding scattering waves depending on the bubbles will involve information about the background media, which provide a new way to the inhomogeneity detection for the background media. That is, by applying the far-field patterns of the scattered waves in inhomogeneous background medium embedded with various bubbles, it is possible to recover the refraction index of the medium using finite number of incident directions and observation points in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, instead of all incident directions and all observation directions. Such a configuration with finite number of incident directions and observations for the inverse medium problems based on the wave scattering is of crucial importance.

For
\rho_{0}(\cdot),k_{0}(\cdot)\in C(\mathbb{R}^{3})
representing the parameters of the inhomogeneity of the background medium, as the usual situations, we restrict the inhomogeneity in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. More precisely, we assume that

\rho_{0}(x)=1
and
k_{0}(x)=1
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega
,
where
\rho_{0}(\cdot)
(respectively,
k_{0}(\cdot)
) is the density function (respectively, bulk modulus function) of the background medium.

The effective equations for wave propagation in bubbly liquids have been derived in the quasi-static regime, see
[
6
,
22
]
for low frequency regime. In this paper, however, we are more concerned with wave propagation in the resonant regime, i.e., moderate frequency regime.
We consider the scattering of acoustic waves in inhomogeneous media in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
embedded a homogeneous droplet
D_{z}
given by the form

(1.1)

D_{z}:=z+\varepsilon B({\bf{0}},1),

where
B({\bf{0}},1)
is the unit ball with center
{\bf 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with its boundary
\partial B({\bf{0}},1)\equiv\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and
z
specifies the location of the droplet with radius
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, i.e.,
D_{z}=B(z,\varepsilon)
. The small parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
means that the used droplet is of small size, and consequently acts like a particle, or, a point source. Then the mass density and bulk modulus
(\rho(\cdot),k(\cdot))
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are of the form

(1.2)

\rho(\cdot):=\begin{cases}\rho_{0}(\cdot)&\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D_{z}\\
\rho_{1}&\text{in}\;{\overline{D}_{z}}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad k(\cdot):=\begin{cases}k_{0}(\cdot)&\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D_{z}\\
k_{1}&\text{in}\;{\overline{D}_{z}}\end{cases},

where
\rho_{1}
and
k_{1}
are known positive constants indicating the constant density and the bulk modulus of the homogeneous droplet
D_{z}
.

For a given incident plane wave

u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega):=e^{i\omega x\cdot\theta}
, with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, solving

(1.3)

\Delta u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega)+\omega^{2}u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega)=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

the total wave
u(\cdot,\theta,\omega):=u^{s}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)+u^{inc}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
, propagating in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
embedded a droplet scatter
D_{z}
with media parameters given by (
1.2
),
obeys the governed system

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\left[\dfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla u\right]+\dfrac{\omega^{2}}{k_{0}}u=0&\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{D}_{z},\\
\nabla\cdot\left[\dfrac{1}{\rho_{1}}\nabla u\right]+\dfrac{\omega^{2}}{k_{1}}u=0&\text{in}\;D_{z},\\
u|_{-}-u|_{+}=0&\text{on}\;\partial D_{z},\\
\dfrac{1}{\rho_{1}}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}|_{-}-\dfrac{1}{\rho_{0}}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}|_{+}=0&\text{on}\;\;\partial D_{z},\end{cases}

where
\nu
denotes the outward unit normal to
\partial D_{z}
,
and
u^{s}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
is the corresponding scattered wave satisfying the Sommerfield radiation condition at infinity, i.e.,

(1.5)

\frac{\partial u^{s}}{\partial\left|x\right|}(x,\theta,\omega)-i\omega\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{0}}{k_{0}(x)}}u^{s}(x,\theta,\omega)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\left|x\right|}\right),\quad\left|x\right|\rightarrow+\infty.

The problem (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) is well-posed, see
[
3
,
4
]
. The scattered wave exhibits the asymptotic behavior

u^{s}(x,\theta,\omega)=\frac{e^{i\omega\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{0}}{k_{0}(x)}}\left|x\right|}}{\left|x\right|}u^{\infty}(\hat{x},\theta,\omega)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\left|x\right|^{2}}\right),\quad\left|x\right|\rightarrow+\infty,

where
\hat{x}:=\frac{x}{\left|x\right|}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
u^{\infty}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
is the far-field pattern corresponding to
u^{s}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
. Of course, both
u^{s}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
and
u^{\infty}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
depend on
D_{z}
, which will be denoted by
u^{s}_{z}(\cdot,\theta,\omega),u^{\infty}_{z}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
in the sequel. Moreover,
when we move
z\in\Omega
and keep
D_{z}\subset\subset\Omega
, the scattered wave
\{u^{s}_{z}(x,\theta,\omega):\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\}
involves the information about
k_{0}(x)
in
\{x\in\Omega:\;\hbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\geq\varepsilon>0\}
. So an important inverse problem is to recover
k_{0}(\cdot)
inside
\Omega
using the information about
u^{s}_{z}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
for given fixed incident direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and all possible
D_{z}\subset\subset\Omega
. By this configuration, for a known domain
\Omega
where the background medium is inhomogeneous, we can detect its inhomogeneity.

In the absence of a droplet,
(\ref{Eq0758})
in the case
\rho_{0}(x)\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is reduced to the Helmholtz equation

(1.6)

\Delta v(x,\theta,\omega)+\frac{\omega^{2}}{k_{0}(x)}v(x,\theta,\omega)=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)=v^{s}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)+u^{inc}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
is the total field before injecting the droplet
D_{z}
. The scattered wave
v^{s}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
is also of the asymptotic behavior

v^{s}(x,\theta,\omega)=\frac{e^{i\omega\sqrt{\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}}\left|x\right|}}{\left|x\right|}v^{\infty}(\hat{x},\theta,\omega)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\left|x\right|^{2}}\right),\quad\left|x\right|\rightarrow+\infty,

where
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
is the far-field pattern corresponding to the scattered field
v^{s}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
.

The inverse problem we are facing is the recovery of the bulk modulus function
k_{0}(\cdot)
in
\Omega
from
u_{z}^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
corresponding to all
D_{z}\subset\subset\Omega
and
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
, the far-field pattern of
v^{s}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
in free space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
without any injected droplet for some incident and observation directions in
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
. In fact, in our imaging model, we will apply one incident direction
\theta
and one observation direction along
-\theta
, i.e., the back-scattered direction.

This paper is arranged as follows. In section
2
, we set up a reduced acoustic model corresponding to a constant mass density and variable bulk modulus. In section
3
, we will write done the computations related to the far-field patterns
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and
u_{z}^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
. In subsection
3.1
, we explain how we can estimate the total field
v(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and the far-field
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
for the unperturbed medium cases. In subsection
3.2
, we explain how we can estimate the total field
u_{z}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and the far-field
u_{z}^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
for the perturbed medium cases. The objective of section
4
is to numerically support the derived results in section
3
. Finally, section
5
is devoted to the numerical reconstruction of the bulk modulus function
k_{0}(\cdot)
in interior domain of
\Omega
, where the mollification scheme is applied to deal with the ill-posedness of this inverse problem, due to the numerical differentiations in the reconstruction algorithm.

2.
Reduced Acoustic Model

To recover
k_{0}(\cdot)
in
\Omega
, we introduce a droplet scatter
D_{z}\subset\subset\Omega
for
z\in\Omega
and consider the wave scattering restricted in
\Omega
. Under the assumption that the density function
\rho(\cdot)
given by (
1.2
) is constant, which means the inhomogeneity of the scattering media and the droplet is independent of the media density, the corresponding mathematical model
(\ref{Eq0758})
becomes

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u+\frac{\omega^{2}}{k_{0}}u=0&\mbox{in}\ \Omega\setminus\overline{D}_{z},\\
\Delta u+\frac{\omega^{2}}{k_{1}}u=0&\mbox{in}\;D_{z},\\
u|_{-}-u|_{+}=0&\mbox{on}\;\partial D_{z},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial_{\nu}}|_{-}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial_{\nu}}|_{+}=0\quad&\mbox{on}\;\partial D_{z},\end{cases}

where we have assumed

\rho(\cdot)\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The notation
u(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the total field after injecting a droplet
D_{z}
. To establish our reconstruction scheme, we assume that the droplet is of a small bulk modulus of order
\varepsilon^{2}
, that is,

(2.2)

k_{1}=\overline{k}_{1}\varepsilon^{2},

where
\overline{k}_{1}
is some constant independent of
\varepsilon
, and
\varepsilon
is the radius of
D_{z}
.
The following results about the far field patterns of
u^{s}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and
v^{s}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
have been established in
[
10
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Denote by
\{(\lambda_{n},e_{n}),\;n\in\mathbb{N}\}
the eigensystem related to the Newtonian operator
N_{B}
, defined from
\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)
to
\mathbb{L}^{2}(B)
by

(2.3)

N_{B}(f)(x):=\int_{B}\frac{1}{4\pi\left|x-y\right|}f(y)dy,\quad x\in B:=B({\bf 0},1),

i.e.,
N_{B}(e_{n})=\lambda_{n}e_{n}
in
B
. Then, for
z\in\Omega
such that
D_{z}\subset\subset\Omega
, the far-filed patterns admit

(2.4)

u^{\infty}_{z}(\hat{x},\theta,\omega)=v^{\infty}(\hat{x},\theta,\omega)-\frac{1}{4\pi\overline{k}_{1}}\frac{\omega^{2}\omega_{n_{0}}^{2}}{\left(\omega^{2}-\omega_{n_{0}}^{2}\right)}\left(\int_{B}e_{n_{0}}(x)dx\right)^{2}\varepsilon v(z,-\hat{x},\omega)v(z,\theta,\omega)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{\min(1,2-2h)}\right)

uniformly for all
\theta,\hat{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, under the condition
\omega^{2}-\omega^{2}_{n_{0}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{h}\right)
for
h\in(0,1)
, where
\omega^{2}_{n_{0}}
is the eigen-frequency given by

(2.5)

\omega^{2}_{n_{0}}\,=\,\overline{k}_{1}\,\lambda_{n_{0}}^{-1}.

Taking the observation direction
\hat{x}
as the back scattered direction, (
2.4
) yields

(2.6)

u_{z}^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega)=v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega)-\frac{1}{4\pi\overline{k}_{1}}\frac{\omega^{2}\omega_{n_{0}}^{2}}{\left(\omega^{2}-\omega_{n_{0}}^{2}\right)}\left(\int_{B}e_{n_{0}}(x)dx\right)^{2}\varepsilon\left(v(z,\theta,\omega)\right)^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{\min(1,2-2h)}\right).

Obviously,
v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega)
is independent of
z
, representing the far-field of the inhomogeneous background medium before injecting the droplet
D_{z}
. However,
u_{z}^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega)
from the droplet
D_{z}
depends on

. The identity
(\ref{BSD})
gives us an approximate scheme to compute

from the contrast

, which will be taken as our inversion input for recovering
k_{0}(\cdot)
in
\Omega
.

For given
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, we will choose the incident frequency
\omega
in the sequel such that

(2.7)

\omega^{2}=\omega_{n_{0}}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{h})\quad\text{ for }0<h<1,

with
\omega_{n_{0}}^{2}
being the eigen-frequency defined by
(\ref{Def-Eigen-Freq})

\overline{k}_{1}=1
, i.e.,

is given by

(2.8)

\omega_{n_{0}}^{2}\;=\;\lambda_{n_{0}}^{-1}.

Notice, in
(\ref{eq1039})
and
(\ref{Eigfreq})
, the small radius
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and
n_{0}
should ensure
\lambda_{n_{0}}^{-1}\gg\varepsilon^{h}
for numerical computations. This condition is always satisfied as
\lambda_{n_{0}}\sim 1
,

(\ref{eq1039})

u^{\infty}_{z}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})-v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\lambda_{n_{0}}^{-2}\left(\int_{B}e_{n_{0}}(x)dx\right)^{2}\varepsilon^{1-h}\left(v(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})\right)^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{\min(1,2-2h)}\right).

Next, to ensure that the first term on the right hand side of the above equation dominates the error term, i.e.,
\varepsilon^{1-h}\gg\varepsilon^{\min(1,2-2h)}
, we need to choose
h\in(1/2,1)
. Then (
2.6

(2.9)

u^{\infty}_{z}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})-v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\lambda_{n_{0}}^{-2}\left(\int_{B}e_{n_{0}}(x)dx\right)^{2}\varepsilon^{1-h}\left(v(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})\right)^{2}.

Based on the above relations, the algorithm we propose to reconstruct the bulk function
k_{0}(\cdot)
in
\Omega
is stated as the following

Algorithm 2.3
.

(1)

Step (1)
. We start by collecting our inversion input

(2.17)

\xi(z):=\xi(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}}):=v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})-u^{\infty}_{z}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}}),

with
z\in\Omega
, which corresponds to the left hand side of
(\ref{BSD-1-Algo})
.

(2)

Step (2)
. Based on
(\ref{BSD-1-Algo}),(\ref{KK})
and
(\ref{DefCn0})
, we compute
v(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})
, which is the point-wise value of the total field before injecting the droplet
D_{z}
into
\Omega
.

(3)

Step (3)
. We use numerical differentiation techniques (mollification method) to compute
\Delta v(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})
from the already reconstructed value
v(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})
, as shown in
Step
(\ref{Stepb})
.

(4)

Step (4)
. We use the Helmholtz equation
(\ref{Heq})
to obtain a set of point-wise values of
k_{0}(\cdot)
by

(2.18)

\frac{1}{k_{0}(z)}=-\frac{1}{\omega_{n_{0}}^{2}}\frac{\Delta v(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})}{v(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})},\quad z\in\Omega,

where, in the right-hand side,
v(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})
is already reconstructed from
Step
(\ref{Stepb})
, and
\Delta v(z,\theta,\omega_{n_{0}})
is already reconstructed from
Step
(\ref{Stepc})
. Hence, by moving the droplet
D_{z}
in
\Omega
, we can recover
\left\{k_{0}(z_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
, the point-wise values of
k_{0}(\cdot)
.

Based on this algorithm, the continuous values of
k_{0}(\cdot)
can be finally approximated from
\left\{k_{0}(z_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
obtained in
Step (
4
)
from the expansion

(2.19)

k_{0}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\gamma_{j}f_{j}(x),\quad x\in\Omega,

where
(f_{1}(\cdot),\cdots,f_{N}(\cdot))
is the specified radial basis functions, for example, see
subsection
3.1
for the choice of the radial basis functions. The expansion coefficient
\vec{\gamma}:=(\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{N})^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
can be determined from the following linear algebraic system

(2.20)

(f_{i}(z_{j}))_{N\times N}\cdot\vec{\gamma}=\vec{k_{0}}

with known
\vec{k_{0}}:=(k_{0}(z_{1}),\cdots,k_{0}(z_{N}))^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
.

becomes

(2.23)

\frac{1}{k_{0}(z)}=-\frac{1}{\omega_{1}^{2}}\frac{\Delta v(z,\theta,\omega_{1})}{v(z,\theta,\omega_{1})},\quad z\in\Omega.

Concerning the numerical differentiation involved in
Step (

.

To handle the numerical differentiation of

such as
\Delta v(z,\theta,\omega_{1})
, see for instance
Step
(\ref{Stepc})
, we express the complex-valued inversion input data as

(2.24)

\xi(z)=C_{1}\left(v(z,\theta,\omega_{1})\right)^{2},\quad z\in\Omega,

see

, with

is estimated by

. Then, from
(\ref{liu0201})
, we deduce that

(2.25)

v(z,\theta,\omega_{1})=\frac{\sqrt{\xi(z)}}{\sqrt{C_{1}}},\quad z\in\Omega.

Hence, for
z\in\Omega
, from
(\ref{liu03})
and
(\ref{Equa1129})
, it follows that

(2.26)

\frac{1}{k_{0}(z)}=-\frac{1}{\omega_{1}^{2}}\frac{\Delta\sqrt{\xi(z)}}{\sqrt{\xi(z)}}=-\frac{1}{\omega_{1}^{2}}\left(\frac{\Delta\xi(z)}{2\xi(z)}-\frac{|\nabla\xi(z)|^{2}}{4\xi(z)^{2}}\right).

A very important observation in (
2.26
) is that, once we determine
\xi(z)
from (
2.21
), the right-hand side of
(\ref{rek0})
is independent of
C_{1}
, i.e., we do not need
C_{1}
approximated by (
2.22
) for our reconstruction. On the other hand, the right-hand side of
(\ref{rek0})
requires numerical differentiations, which are ill-posed for
\xi(z)
given by
(\ref{BSDI})
, since
u_{z}^{\infty}\left(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{1}\right)
are approximated by solving truncated integral equations for small
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, and consequently
\xi(z)
is of unavoidable noise. To address this issue, we utilize the mollification method to deal with the ill-posedness, see
[
8
,
24
]

for related regularization schemes. More precisely, for given
\xi(\cdot)
on a domain
\mathfrak{U}
containing the point
z
where we want to compute its Laplacian, we compute its second order partial derivatives along three directions, respectively. We firstly take
\mathfrak{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
to be a cube and introduce

\eta(x):=\begin{cases}\textbf{c}\exp\left(\dfrac{1}{\left|x\right|^{2}-1}\right),&\text{if $\left|x\right|<1$,}\\
0,&\text{if $\left|x\right|\geq 1$,}\end{cases}

with

\textbf{c}:=\left(\int^{1}_{-1}e^{\frac{1}{\left|x\right|^{2}-1}}dx\right)^{-1}
, which is a
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{1})
function satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{1}}\eta(x)dx=1
. For kernel function
\eta_{\delta}(x):=\frac{1}{\delta}\eta(\frac{x}{\delta})
with specified
\delta>0
, if
f\;:U\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally integrable, define its mollification
[
13
]

f^{\delta}(x):=(\eta_{\delta}*f)(x):=\int_{U}\eta_{\delta}(x-y)f(y)dy=\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\eta_{\delta}(y)f(x-y)dy,\qquad x\in U_{\delta},

where
U_{\delta}:=\left\{x\in U\mid\hbox{dist}(x,\partial U)>\delta\right\}
. Additionally, from
[
2
, Chapter 2, Page 37]
, we have

(2.27)

D^{\alpha}f^{\delta}(x)=\int_{U}D^{\alpha}\eta_{\delta}(x-y)f(y)dy,\quad x\in U_{\delta}\subset U\subset\mathbb{R}^{1},

where
D=\frac{d}{dx}
and
\alpha=1,2
is the order of derivative. According to
[
18
, Theorem 4.1.1]
, the formula
(\ref{FARNJB})
makes sense under the condition
\left\|D^{\alpha}f\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}\left(U_{\delta}\right)}\leq\mathrm{M}(\alpha,p,\delta)
for
1\leq p\leq\infty
, with some positive constant
\mathrm{M}(\alpha,p,\delta)
, and it holds that

(2.28)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\left\|Df^{\delta}-Df\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}\left(U_{\delta}\right)}\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\delta\to 0}\left\|D^{2}f^{\delta}-D^{2}f\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}\left(U_{\delta}\right)}\to 0.

To compute
\nabla\xi(z)
and
\Delta\xi(z)
, we first use (
2.27
) to compute all the partial derivatives in

\displaystyle\nabla\xi^{\delta}(z)=\left(\frac{\partial\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{1}}(z),\frac{\partial\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{2}}(z),\frac{\partial\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{3}}(z)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta\xi^{\delta}(z)=\frac{\partial^{2}\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{1}^{2}}(z)+\frac{\partial^{2}\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{2}^{2}}(z)+\frac{\partial^{2}\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{3}^{2}}(z)

with specified mollification constant
\delta>0
,
and then get the approximations to
\nabla\xi(z)
and
\Delta\xi(z)
in the sense (
2.28
).
Assuming
\xi(\cdot,z_{2},z_{3}):U\to\mathbb{R}
is locally integrable for fixed
(z_{2},z_{3})
, we have

\displaystyle\xi^{\delta}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}):=\left(\eta_{\delta}*\xi(\cdot,z_{2},z_{3})\right)(z_{1})=\int_{U}\eta_{\delta}(z_{1}-t)\xi(t,z_{2},z_{3})dt,\quad z_{1}\in U_{\delta}.

Then, it holds that

\partial_{z_{1}}\left(\xi^{\delta}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\right)=\int_{U}\partial_{z_{1}}\eta_{\delta}(z_{1}-t)\xi(t,z_{2},z_{3})dt,\quad z_{1}\in U_{\delta}

from (
2.27
).
In a similar manner, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{2}}\left(\xi^{\delta}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{U}\partial_{z_{2}}\eta_{\delta}(z_{2}-t)\xi(z_{1},t,z_{3})dt,\quad z_{2}\in U_{\delta},

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{3}}\left(\xi^{\delta}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{U}\partial_{z_{3}}\eta_{\delta}(z_{3}-t)\xi(z_{1},z_{2},t)dt,\quad z_{3}\in U_{\delta}.

Hence, we can compute the approximation to
\nabla\xi(z)
by

(2.29)

\nabla\xi^{\delta}(z)\,=\,\left(\frac{\partial\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{1}},\frac{\partial\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{2}},\frac{\partial\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{3}}\right)(z).

In addition, we also have the approximation to
\Delta\xi(z)
by

(2.30)

\Delta\xi^{\delta}(z)=\frac{\partial^{2}\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{1}^{2}}(z)+\frac{\partial^{2}\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{2}^{2}}(z)+\frac{\partial^{2}\xi^{\delta}}{\partial z_{3}^{2}}(z),

where

\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}\left(\xi^{\delta}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\right)=\int_{U}\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}\eta_{\delta}(z_{1}-t)\xi(t,z_{2},z_{3})dt,\quad z_{1}\in U_{\delta},

and

\partial_{z_{2}}^{2}\left(\xi^{\delta}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\right),\partial_{z_{2}}^{2}\left(\xi^{\delta}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\right)

are of similar representations.

Thus, from
(\ref{rek0})
, we deduce

(2.31)

\frac{1}{k_{0}(z)}=-\frac{1}{\omega_{1}^{2}}\left(\frac{\Delta\xi^{\delta}(z)}{2\xi(z)}-\frac{|\nabla\xi^{\delta}(z)|^{2}}{4\xi(z)^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}Error(z),\quad z\in\mathfrak{U}_{\delta},

where the error term
Error(z)
is given by

\displaystyle Error(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\left(\xi-\xi^{\delta}\right)(z)}{2\xi(z)}-\frac{|\nabla\left(\xi-\xi^{\delta}\right)(z)|^{2}}{4\xi(z)^{2}}-

\displaystyle\frac{\langle Re\left(\nabla\xi(z)\right);Re\left(\nabla\xi(z)-\nabla\xi^{\delta}(z)\right)\rangle+\langle Im\left(\nabla\xi(z)\right);Im\left(\nabla\xi(z)-\nabla\xi^{\delta}(z)\right)\rangle}{2\xi(z)^{2}},

which can be estimated as

\left\|Error\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}\left(\mathfrak{U}_{\delta}\right)}\lesssim\left\|\Delta\xi^{\delta}-\Delta\xi\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{p}\left(\mathfrak{U}_{\delta}\right)}+\left\|\nabla\xi^{\delta}-\nabla\xi\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2p}\left(\mathfrak{U}_{\delta}\right)}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0

from (
2.28
).
Hence, by referring to
(\ref{XC-Equa0850})
, we have

\dfrac{1}{k_{0}(z)}\approx-\frac{1}{\omega_{1}^{2}}\left(\frac{\Delta\xi^{\delta}(z)}{2\xi(z)}-\frac{|\nabla\xi^{\delta}(z)|^{2}}{4\xi(z)^{2}}\right),\quad z\in\mathfrak{U}_{\delta}\subset\mathfrak{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
\xi(z)
is given by (
2.21
), while
\nabla\xi^{\delta}(z),\Delta\xi^{\delta}(z)
are given by (
2.29
) and (
2.30
), respectively.

3.
The direct scattering for inhomogeneous medium

The goal of this section is to derive the far-field patterns of the scattered waves for inhomogeneous medium with and without the droplet denoted by
D_{z}
with small size and high contrasting bulk modulus, see
(\ref{DzepsB}),(\ref{defrhodefk})
and
(\ref{Ctek1})
, which is necessary for our inversion scheme, see
Step
(\ref{Stepa})
.

Our basic scheme, to determine the far-field waves
u_{z}^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
, is to firstly generate the total waves
u(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and
v(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
in
B({\bf 0},1)
for known
k_{0}(\cdot)
by simulation process, and then to compute
u_{z}^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
by their integral expressions, respectively. Consequently, we will fix the artificial domain
\Omega\equiv B({\bf 0},1)
where we want to recover
k_{0}(\cdot)
, without loss of generality. In fact, if we want to recover the inhomogeneous function
k_{0}(\cdot)
in general domain
\Omega
not necessarily in
B({\bf 0},1)
, we can always take
B({\bf 0},R_{0})
with radius
R_{0}
large enough such that
\Omega\subset B({\bf 0},R_{0})
. For this domain
B({\bf 0},R_{0})
, our implementation schemes for both direct and inverse scattering in
B({\bf 0},1)
are still applicable, except for the introduction of the amplifying factor
R_{0}
in the computational process.

3.1.
Generated fields for the unperturbed medium

We recall from
(\ref{Heq})
that, the total field
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
inside
\Omega\equiv B({\bf 0},1)
for fixed
(\theta,\omega)
, before injecting the droplet
D_{z}
, is the solution to

(3.1)

{\Delta}v(x,\theta,\omega)+\frac{\omega^{2}}{k_{0}(x)}v(x,\theta,\omega)=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\;\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

We decompose our simulation process into two steps. The first step is to compute the total field
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
from
(\ref{HeqI})
, while the second step is to compute its corresponding far-field
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

(1) Computation of the total field
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
.

Using
(\ref{PDE-u-inc})
, we can rewrite
(\ref{HeqI})
as

{\Delta}v(x,\theta,\omega)+\frac{\omega^{2}}{k_{0}(x)}v(x,\theta,\omega)={\Delta}u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega)+\omega^{2}u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\;\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

Multiplying the above equation by the fundamental solution

(3.2)

\Phi_{\omega}(x,y):=\dfrac{e^{i\omega\left|x-y\right|}}{4\pi\left|x-y\right|},\quad x\neq y

to the Helmholtz equation, which meets the equation
\Delta\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)+\omega^{2}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)=-\delta(x,y)

in the distributional sense, we derive the following Lippmann-Schwinger equation

(3.3)

v(x,\theta,\omega)-\omega^{2}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-1\right)\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)v(y,\theta,\omega)dy=u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

in terms of the radiation conditions at infinity. From
(\ref{Eq0823})
, we observe that
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is completely determined by
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
in
B({\bf 0},1)
. Thus, the computation of
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
in
B({\bf 0},1)
is of great importance. To this end, by restricting the Lippmann-Schwinger equation
(\ref{Eq0823})
into
B({\bf 0},1)
, and denoting its corresponding solution by
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)|_{B({\bf 0},1)}
, we get for
x\in B({\bf 0},1)
that

(3.4)

v(x,\theta,\omega)|_{B(0,1)}-\omega^{2}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-1\right)\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)v(y,\theta,\omega)|_{B({\bf 0},1)}dy=u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega).

Once we solve
(\ref{liu04})
, which is a Fredholm integral equation of the second kind, the total field
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be computed from
(\ref{Eq0823})
.

There are many schemes in the literature to solve the Lippmann-Schwinger equation
(\ref{liu04})
, such as the Born and Rytov approximations for linearized models
[
1
,
11
]
, the contrast source-inversion method
[
28
]
, and the recursive Born approximation for nonlinear model with heavy computational costs
[
14
]
. The challenging numerical issue is the computation of the volume integral with weak singular kernel, due to the fundamental solution
(\ref{FundSolHelmoltzEq})
appearing on the left-hand side of
(\ref{liu04})
. To deal with this issue, according to
[
27
,
15
]
, the dual reciprocity method (DRM) is the most commonly used method for converting volume integrals into surface integrals based on the Green’s formula. Then the boundary element method (BEM) can be applied to compute the corresponding surface integrals
[
25
,
12
]
, which weakens the computational cost for the Lippmann-Schwinger equation
(\ref{liu04})
.
To apply the DRM to solve
(\ref{liu04})
efficiently, we firstly introduce the functions
v^{\star}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
and
u^{\star,inc}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
defined by

(3.5)

v^{\star}(x,\theta,\omega):=\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}-1\right)v(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

(3.6)

u^{\star,inc}(x,\theta,\omega):=\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}-1\right)u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

respectively.
Then, multiplying both sides of
(\ref{liu04})
by
\left(\dfrac{1}{k_{0}(\cdot)}-1\right)
and using
(\ref{defvstar})
and
(\ref{Uincstar})
, we obtain

(3.7)

v^{\star}(x,\theta,\omega)-\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}-1\right)\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)v^{\star}(y,\theta,\omega)dy=u^{\star,inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

Now, inspired by
[
29
, Section 1]
, the volume integral appearing on the left hand side of
(\ref{liu05})
can be equivalently transformed to a surface integral if and only if the density function over the volume integral, i.e.,
v^{\star}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
, is a source of a given Helmholtz problem. To handle this transformation, we will use the basic concept of DRM, which consists in approximating
v^{\star}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
by a linear combination of radial basis functions in the form

(3.8)

v^{\star}(x,\theta,\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}f_{k}(x),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

with
\left(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
the expansion coefficients to be determined, and
\left(f_{1}(\cdot),\cdots,f_{n}(\cdot)\right)
is the suitably chosen radial basis functions,
see
[
15
,
23
,
16
]
. For notations’ simplicity, we omit the dependency of
\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k}(\theta,\omega)
on
(\theta,\omega)
. Clearly, if each basis function
f_{k}(\cdot)
is chosen as a source term for a given Helmholtz problem,
v^{\star}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
can be approximated by a function which is a source for a given Helmholtz problem. There are already thorough studies on the choice strategy for the radial basis function in the Dual Reciprocity Method, see
[
15
,
23
,
16
]
and the references therein, such as

f_{k}(\cdot)=1,\quad f_{k}(\cdot)=1+\left|\cdot-x_{k}\right|,\quad f_{k}(\cdot)=e^{\left|\cdot-x_{k}\right|^{2}},\quad f_{k}(\cdot)=\left|\cdot-x_{k}\right|^{2}\ln\left(\left|\cdot-x_{k}\right|\right),

where
\{x_{k}\in B({\bf 0},1):k=1,\cdots,n\}
is a set of randomly collocated points. Now, by plugging (
3.8
) into (
3.7
), we obtain

(3.9)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\left[f_{k}(x)-\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}-1\right)\int_{B(0,1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)f_{k}(y)dy\right]=u^{\star,inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

To numerically handle the integral appearing on the left hand side of
(\ref{Eq0936II})
given by

(3.10)

u_{k}(x):=\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)f_{k}(y)dy,\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

it is crucial to select a good radial basis function
f_{k}(\cdot)
to obtain good numerical quadrature
(\ref{Equa0500})
. In many instances, as demonstrated in
[
15
]
, the radial basis function

(3.11)

f_{k}(x):=1+r_{k}(x),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

where
r_{k}(\cdot):=\left|\cdot-x_{k}\right|
for
k=1,\cdots,n
and
x_{k}\in B({\bf 0},1)
, is the most frequently used one due to its satisfactory outcomes.
Thanks to
[
29
, Proposition 1.1]
, and the expression of
u_{k}(\cdot)
, defined by
(\ref{Equa0500})
, we know that
f_{k}(\cdot)
will be a source term for the following Helmholtz equation

(3.12)

\Delta u_{k}(x)+\omega^{2}u_{k}(x)=f_{k}(x),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

and
u_{k}(\cdot)
satisfies the following boundary condition
*
*

*

p.v
stands for the Cauchy principal value.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u_{k}(x)+\int_{\partial B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\partial_{\nu}u_{k}(y)d\sigma(y)-p.v.\int_{\partial B({\bf 0},1)}\partial_{\nu}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)u_{k}(y)d\sigma(y)=0,\quad x\in\partial B({\bf 0},1).

To solve
(\ref{PT-Equa0918})
, we start by splitting
u_{k}(\cdot):=\hat{f_{k}}(\cdot)+\hat{f}_{k,H}(\cdot)
in
B({\bf 0},1)
, where
\hat{f}_{k}(\cdot)
satisfies

(3.13)

\Delta\hat{f_{k}}(x)+\omega^{2}\hat{f_{k}}(x)=f_{k}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{RBF})}}{{=}}1+\left|x-x_{k}\right|,\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

and
\hat{f}_{k,H}(\cdot)
is the solution to the homogeneous Helmholtz equation

(3.14)

\Delta\hat{f}_{k,H}(x)+\omega^{2}\hat{f}_{k,H}(x)=0,\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

Then, by plugging
(\ref{PT-Equa0918})
into
(\ref{Equa0500})
, and using
(\ref{fk})
and
(\ref{PT-Equa09180})
, we deduce

(3.15)

u_{k}(x)=\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\left(\Delta\hat{f_{k}}(y)+\omega^{2}\hat{f_{k}}(y)\right)dy,\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

Furthermore, in
(\ref{Eq0936II+})
, to handle the volume integral with weak singular kernel, we apply the Green’s formula to change the volume integral into surface integral. Hence,

(3.16)

u_{k}(x)=-\hat{f_{k}}(x)+\int_{\partial B({\bf 0},1)}\left(\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\frac{\partial\hat{f_{k}}}{\partial\nu(y)}(y)-\hat{f_{k}}(y)\frac{\partial\Phi_{\omega}}{\partial\nu(y)}(x,y)\right)d\sigma(y),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

To compute numerically the surface integral in the right hand side of
(\ref{Eq0936II++})
, we need the analytic expression of
\hat{f_{k}}(\cdot)
. To keep things simple, we initially consider the case
x_{k}=0
, and due to the Helmholtz equation being invariant when rotating, it’s recommended to look for radial solution first, i.e., we take
\hat{f}_{k}(x)=g\left(\left|x\right|\right)
for
x\in B({\bf 0},1)
. Then
(\ref{fk})
becomes

(3.17)

\left|x\right|g^{\prime\prime}(\left|x\right|)+2g^{\prime}(\left|x\right|)+\omega^{2}\left|x\right|g(\left|x\right|)=\left|x\right|+\left|x\right|^{2}.

By the function transform

\varphi(\left|x\right|)=\left|x\right|g(\left|x\right|)
, the equation
(\ref{Eq1134})
admits the solution

\varphi(\left|x\right|)=\frac{\left|x\right|^{2}+\left|x\right|}{\omega^{2}}-\frac{2}{\omega^{4}}+C_{1}e^{-i\omega\left|x\right|}+C_{2}e^{i\omega\left|x\right|}\quad\text{for}\quad C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{C}.

This suggests, by the construction of
\varphi(\cdot)
, the solution to (
3.13
) for general source point
x_{k}
is

(3.18)

\hat{f}_{k}(x)=\frac{\left|x-x_{k}\right|}{\omega^{2}}+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}-\frac{2}{\omega^{4}\left|x-x_{k}\right|}+\frac{C_{1}e^{-i\omega\left|x-x_{k}\right|}}{\left|x-x_{k}\right|}+\frac{C_{2}e^{i\omega\left|x-x_{k}\right|}}{\left|x-x_{k}\right|},\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are such that

(3.19)

C_{1}+C_{2}=\frac{2}{\omega^{4}}.

To avoid complex solution to
(\ref{Equahatfk})
, we take
C_{1}=C_{2}=\omega^{-4}
and then get

(3.20)

\hat{f_{k}}(x)=\frac{\left(1+\left|x-x_{k}\right|\right)}{\omega^{2}}-\frac{2\left(1-\cos(\omega\left|x-x_{k}\right|)\right)}{\omega^{4}\left|x-x_{k}\right|}.

Since
\hat{f_{k}}(x)\to\omega^{-2}
as

,
\hat{f_{k}}(\cdot)
in

is of the removable singularity, which is a significant advantage for numerical computations. Now, by using
(\ref{fk})
in
(\ref{Eq0936II})
, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\left[f_{k}(x)-\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}-1\right)\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)(\Delta\hat{f_{k}}(y)+\omega^{2}\hat{f_{k}}(y))dy\right]=u^{\star,inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

which, by an integration by parts, yields

(3.21)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\left[f_{k}(x)-\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}-1\right)\left(\mathcal{J}_{k}(x,\omega)-\hat{f_{k}}(x)\right)\right]=u^{\star,inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

where

\mathcal{J}_{k}(x,\omega):=\int_{\partial B({\bf 0},1)}\left[\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\frac{\partial\hat{f_{k}}(y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}\hat{f_{k}}(y)\right]d\sigma(y),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

Notice, for
\partial B({\bf 0},1)=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the single and double surface potentials with known
\hat{f}_{k}(\cdot)
given by (
3.20
) involved in
\mathcal{J}_{k}(x,\omega)
can be computed efficiently using the spherical harmonic functions expansions in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, see
[
9
]
for more details. For any
x\in B({\bf 0},1)
, the integrand

\mathcal{I}_{k}(y,x,\omega):=\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\frac{\partial\hat{f_{k}}(y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}\hat{f_{k}}(y),\quad y\in\partial B({\bf 0},1)=\mathbb{S}^{2},

is an analytic function
\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
, thus we have the Gauss trapezoidal product rule

(3.22)

\mathcal{J}_{k}(x,\omega)\approx\frac{\pi}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{m=0}^{2N-1}\mu_{j}\mathcal{I}_{k}(y_{jm},x,\omega),

where
\mu_{j}:=\frac{2(1-t_{j}^{2})}{[NP_{N-1}(t_{j})]^{2}}
for
j=1,\cdots,N

are the weights of the Gauss-Legendre quadrature rule, with

-1<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{N}<1
the zeros of the Legendre polynomial
P_{N}(\cdot)
, while
y_{jm}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
are given in polar coordinates by

y_{jm}:=(\sin\theta_{j}\cos\phi_{m},\sin\theta_{j}\sin\phi_{m},\cos\theta_{j})
,
where
\theta_{j}=\arccos t_{j}
for
j=1,\cdots,N
and
\phi_{m}=\pi m/N
for
m=0,\cdots,2N-1
.
Thus, by taking
x
in
(\ref{dis})
as a random distributed collocation point
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset B({\bf 0},1)
, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\left[f_{k}(x_{i})-\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x_{i})}-1\right)\left(\mathcal{J}_{k}(x_{i},\omega)-\hat{f_{k}}(x_{i})\right)\right]=u^{\star,inc}(x_{i},\theta,\omega)\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\cdots,n.

Then we derive the following algebraic system

(3.23)

\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{\alpha}=\mathbf{b},

where the elements of the interpolation matrix
\mathbf{A}=(A_{ik})\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and the vector
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1}
are given by

A_{ik}:=f_{k}(x_{i})-\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x_{i})}-1\right)\left(\mathcal{J}_{k}(x_{i},\omega)-\hat{f_{k}}(x_{i})\right),\quad b_{i}=u^{\star,inc}(x_{i},\theta,\omega)\quad\text{for}\quad i,k=1,\cdots,n,

and
\mathbf{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the expansion coefficients.

Finally, by solving
(\ref{alg})
, we get the expansion coefficients. Hence, the function
v^{\star}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
can be simulated in
B({\bf 0},1)
using the expression
(\ref{Vexpres})
. Therefore, since we have assumed that
k_{0}(\cdot)
is a-priori known function, we deduce the reconstruction of
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)|_{B({\bf 0},1)}
by using
(\ref{defvstar})
. Consequently, from
(\ref{Eq0823})
, we deduce the reconstruction of
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

(2) Computation of the total field
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
.

Since
v(\cdot,\theta,\omega)=v^{s}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)+u^{inc}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
, the equation (
3.3
) says

v^{s}(x,\theta,\omega)=\omega^{2}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-1\right)\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)v(y,\theta,\omega)dy\overset{(\ref{defvstar})}{=}\omega^{2}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)v^{\star}(y,\theta,\omega)dy,\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Thanks to
[
9
, Theorem 2.6]
, it follows that
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
has the following expression

(3.24)

v^{\infty}(\hat{x},\theta,\omega)=\frac{\omega^{2}}{4\pi}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}e^{-i\omega\hat{x}\cdot y}v^{\star}(y,\theta,\omega)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{Vexpres})}}{{=}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{4\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}e^{-i\omega\hat{x}\cdot y}f_{k}(y)dy,\quad\hat{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

where
f_{k}(\cdot)
is given by (
3.11
). That is, we can compute
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
using
(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
directly. In particular, by taking
\hat{x}=-\theta
in (
3.24
), we obtain

(3.25)

v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega)=\frac{\omega^{2}}{4\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}e^{i\omega\theta\cdot y}f_{k}(y)dy,

which can be computed numerically.

3.2.
Generated fields for the perturbed medium

Similarly to the unperturbed medium case stated in the above subsection, we also generate the scattered wave related to the perturbed medium by the presence of the droplet
D_{z}
in two parts. However, since the domain
\Omega\equiv B({\bf 0},1)
involves the presence of a droplet
D_{z}
with small size of order
\varepsilon^{3}
and high contrasting bulk coefficient, see
(\ref{defrhodefk})
and
(\ref{Ctek1})
, the treatment for the volume integral should be careful.

In the presence of an injected droplet
D_{z}
, the total wave
u(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
solving
(\ref{Eq1})
satisfies the following Lippmann-Schwinger equation

(3.26)

u\left(x,\theta,\omega\right)-\omega^{2}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\left(\frac{1}{k(y)}-1\right)\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)u(y,\theta,\omega)dy=u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

where the bulk modulus function
k(\cdot)
is defined by (
1.2
). Notice, the solution to (
3.26
) depends on
z
, which is denoted by
u_{z}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
, since
k(\cdot)
in
B({\bf 0},1)
given by (
1.2
) depends on
D_{z}
. In the same way as the unperturbed medium cases, we split the study into two steps.

(1) Estimation of the total field
u_{z}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
solving (
3.26
).

By restricting (
3.26
) into the unit ball
B({\bf 0},1)
, using (
1.2
) and the fact that
\overline{k}_{1}=1
, we obtain

(3.27)

\displaystyle u_{z}\left(x,\theta,\omega\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)\setminus D_{z}}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-1\right)\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)u_{z}(y,\theta,\omega)dy-

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\int_{D_{z}}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)u_{z}(y,\theta,\omega)dy=u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

To deal with the second term on the left hand side of
(\ref{MLSA})
which is an integral over a domain with a hole
D_{z}
, where the quadrature process is highly complicated and may cause numerical instability
[
20
]
, we rewrite
(\ref{MLSA})
as

(3.28)

\displaystyle u_{z}\left(x,\theta,\omega\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-1\right)u_{z}(y,\theta,\omega)dy+

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\int_{D_{z}}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)u_{z}(y,\theta,\omega)dy=u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

We solve the above equation using DRM directly. To do this, we start by approximating the function
u_{z}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
, the solution to
(\ref{comp_u})
, by

(3.29)

u_{z}(x,\theta,\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}f_{k}(x),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

where
f_{k}(\cdot)
is the radial basis function defined by
(\ref{RBF})
. For notations’ simplicity, we omit the dependency of
\beta_{k}=\beta_{k}(z,\theta,\omega)\in\mathbb{C}
on
(z,\theta,\omega)
.
By plugging (
3.29
) into (
3.28
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}f_{k}(x)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-1\right)f_{k}(y)dy+

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}\int_{D_{z}}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)f_{k}(y)dy=u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

which can be rewritten as

(3.30)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}(x)\beta_{k}=u^{inc}(x,\theta,\omega),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

where

(3.31)

A_{k}(x):=f_{k}(x)-\omega^{2}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-1\right)f_{k}(y)dy+\omega^{2}\int_{D_{z}}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)f_{k}(y)dy.

The evaluations of
(\ref{Equa0249})
at collocation points
\left\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\right\}
leads to the following algebraic system

(3.32)

\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}(x_{1})&\cdots&A_{n}(x_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{1}(x_{n})&\cdots&A_{n}(x_{n})\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u^{inc}(x_{1},\theta,\omega)\\
\vdots\\
u^{inc}(x_{n},\theta,\omega)\end{pmatrix}.

To generate the interpolation matrix appearing on the left hand side of
(\ref{A.beta=uinc})
, it is necessary to numerically compute the function
A_{k}(\cdot)
given by
(\ref{DefAk(x)})
at the collocation points
\left\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\right\}
. To do this, by using the fact that the bulk modulus function
k_{0}(\cdot)
, which is assumed a-priori known in simulation process, is a smooth function in
B({\bf 0},1)
, we have the following expansion

(3.33)

\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)f_{k}(y)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\ell}(y),\quad y\in B({\bf 0},1),

where
f_{\ell}(\cdot)
is the radial basis function given by (
3.11
). Besides, for known
k_{0}(\cdot)
in
B({\bf 0},1)
, the coefficients
(c_{1,k}^{(\varepsilon)},\cdots,c_{n,k}^{(\varepsilon)})
are also known, which can be expressed by

c_{l,k}^{(\varepsilon)}=\begin{cases}d_{l,k},&l\not=k,\\
d_{l,k}-\varepsilon^{-2},&l=k,\end{cases}

where
\left(d_{1,k};\cdots;d_{n,k}\right)
is solution of

\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}(x_{1})&\cdots&f_{n}(x_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f_{1}(x_{n})&\cdots&f_{n}(x_{n})\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}d_{1,k}\\
\vdots\\
d_{n,k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{1}{k_{0}(x_{1})}f_{k}(x_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\dfrac{1}{k_{0}(x_{n})}f_{k}(x_{n})\end{pmatrix},

by taking the points
y=x_{j}\in B({\bf 0},1)
in (
3.33
) for
j=1,\cdots,n
.
It’s worth noting that the component
c_{k,k}^{(\varepsilon)}
is of a sharp jump
\varepsilon^{-2}
for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, compared with
c_{l,k}^{(\varepsilon)}
for
l\not=k
.

Now, by plugging (
3.33
) into (
3.31
), we deduce for

x\in B({\bf 0},1)
that

(3.34)

A_{k}(x)=f_{k}(x)-\omega^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(1)}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)f_{\ell}(y)dy+\omega^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(\varepsilon)}\int_{D_{z}}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)f_{\ell}(y)dy,

where
(c_{1,k}^{(1)},\cdots,c_{n,k}^{(1)})
are the expansion coefficients, such that

\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-1\right)f_{k}(y)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(1)}f_{\ell}(y),\quad y\in B({\bf 0},1).

By using
(\ref{fk})
, the equation
(\ref{EM-Equa1204})
can be written as

\displaystyle A_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{k}(x)-\omega^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(1)}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\left(\Delta\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)+\omega^{2}\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)\right)dy+

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(\varepsilon)}\int_{D_{z}}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\left(\Delta\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)+\omega^{2}\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)\right)dy,\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

Thanks to Green’s formula, the above equation becomes

(3.35)

\displaystyle A_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{k}(x)+\omega^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(1)}\hat{f_{\ell}}(x)-\omega^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(\varepsilon)}\hat{f_{\ell}}(x)\chi_{D_{z}}(x)-

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(1)}\int_{\partial B({\bf 0},1)}\left[\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\frac{\partial\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)\right]d\sigma(y)+

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell,k}^{(\varepsilon)}\int_{\partial D_{z}}\left[\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\frac{\partial\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)\right]d\sigma(y),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

For
x\in B({\bf 0},1)\setminus\partial D_{z}
, we can compute the single and double potentials defined on
\partial D_{z}
in the above expression of
A_{k}(\cdot)
using the formulas developed in
[
31
]
. As for
x\in\partial D_{z}
, we can take some
\tilde{x}\in B({\bf 0},1)\setminus\overline{D}_{z}
such that
\left|\tilde{x}-x\right|\to 0
. Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D_{z}}\left[\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\frac{\partial\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)\right]d\sigma(y)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D_{z}}\left[\Phi_{\omega}(\tilde{x},y)\frac{\partial\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial\Phi_{\omega}(\tilde{x},y)}{\partial\nu(y)}\hat{f_{\ell}}(y)\right]d\sigma(y)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\hat{f_{\ell}}(x),\quad x\in\partial D_{z},

due to the jump relation of the double layer potential, see
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
. The integrals in the right hand side can also be computed in terms of the formulas developed in
[
31
]
.
Finally, the matrix appearing on the left hand side of (
3.32
) can be numerically computed for a set of collocation points
\{x_{j}:\;j=1,\cdots,n\}\subset B({\bf 0},1)
with specified point
z\in B({\bf 0},1)
determining the center of
D_{z}
. Solving (
3.32
) enables us to determine the expansion coefficients
(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})
, and using (
3.29
) allows us to reconstruct the values of
u_{z}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
, with
u_{z}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
being the solution to (
3.26
). To avoid the
\varepsilon^{-2}-
sharp jump for
x\in D_{z}
from the third term in the expression of
A_{k}(x)
, see the right hand side of (
3.35
), all the collocation points
\{x_{j}:j=1,\cdots,n\}
are taken outside of
D_{z}
. Since the size of
D_{z}
is of order
\sim\varepsilon^{3}
, such a missing of collocation points in
D_{z}
does not cause too much error for the approximation of
u_{z}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
in the whole domain
B({\bf 0},1)
.

(2) Estimation of the far field
u_{z}^{\infty}\left(\cdot,\theta,\omega\right)
.

Similarly to the expression of
v^{\infty}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
given by (
3.24
), the far-field
u_{z}^{\infty}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
associated to

, with
u_{z}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
solving (
3.26
), is given by

\displaystyle u_{z}^{\infty}(\hat{x},\theta,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\left(\frac{1}{k(y)}-1\right)\frac{e^{-i\omega\hat{x}\cdot y}}{4\pi}u_{z}(y,\theta,\omega)dy

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{liu011})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\left(\frac{1}{k(y)}-1\right)\frac{e^{-i\omega\hat{x}\cdot y}}{4\pi}f_{k}(y)dy,

where the expansion coefficients
(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})
are solved from (
3.32
). Besides, by using (

), (
2.2
), and the face that

, the above equation for

becomes

(3.36)

u_{z}^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega)=\frac{\omega^{2}}{4\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}\left[\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}e^{i\omega\theta\cdot y}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-1\right)f_{k}(y)dy-\int_{D_{z}}e^{i\omega\theta\cdot y}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(y)}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)f_{k}(y)dy\right].

The expression
(\ref{MS1036})
(respectively,

) construct an efficient scheme for direct scattering problem for an inhomogeneous medium without (respectively, with) droplet, which also provide us the simulated data for inverse scattering problem,
see (

.

4.
Numerical implementations for direct scattering

The goal of this section is to test our implementation schemes for direct scattering problem in an inhomogeneous medium embedded droplet. Such a realization process also provides us the simulation data for the far fields patterns
u_{z}^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega)
and
v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega)
. Then by moving
D_{z}
inside
\Omega
, we collect the data
\{u_{z}^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega):\ D_{z}\subset\subset\Omega\}
and
v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega)
from which we can recover
k_{0}(z)
in
\Omega
, as explained in
Algorithm
2.3
. In the recovery process, we take
\varepsilon=0.01
,
h=0.95
, and we only need to use one fixed incident direction
\theta=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\left(1,2,1\right)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

4.1.
The unperturbed medium case

To check our algorithm, we recall from
(\ref{liu05})
that

(4.1)

v^{\star}(x,\theta,\omega)-\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}-1\right)\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)v^{\star}(y,\theta,\omega)dy=u^{\star,inc}\left(x,\theta,\omega\right),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1).

The exact solution
v^{\star}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
for (
4.1
) for general
k_{0}(\cdot)
, although exists, is very hard to be expressed analytically. Then, our goal is to obtain its approximation using our algorithm, which we refer to as
v^{\star}_{num}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
. To verify the validity of our scheme, we replace the right hand side of (
4.1
) by a known source function
\Theta(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
generated from some assumed a priori known function
v^{\star}_{ass}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
with analytical expression, that is,

(4.2)

\Theta(x,\theta,\omega):=v^{\star}_{ass}(x,\theta,\omega)-\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}-1\right)\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)v^{\star}_{ass}(y,\theta,\omega)dy.

Hence, in (
4.1
), by replacing the right hand side with
\Theta(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
and using our algorithm, we obtain the numerical solution
v^{\star}_{ass,num}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
associated to the known function
v^{\star}_{ass}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
. The objective is to justify the small difference between
v^{\star}_{ass}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
, which is known, and
v^{\star}_{ass,num}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
which will be computed using our numerical scheme, see
Algorithm
2.3
. To this end, we consider the case that
v^{\star}_{ass}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
and
k_{0}(\cdot)
are given by the following expressions

v^{\star}_{ass}(x,\theta,\omega)=\left|x-x_{1}^{*}\right|^{2}+i\left|x-x_{2}^{*}\right|^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad k_{0}(x)=\frac{2}{1+\left|x\right|^{2}}\quad\text{for}\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),

respectively, where
x_{1}^{*}=(1,-1.5,1.5)
and
x_{2}^{*}=(1.5,0,1.5)
. Then
(\ref{DefTheta})
becomes

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Theta(x,\theta,\omega)=\frac{\left|x\right|^{2}+1}{2}(\left|x-x_{1}^{*}\right|^{2}+i\left|x-x_{2}^{*}\right|^{2})-\frac{(\left|x\right|^{2}-1)(3+i3)}{\omega^{2}}+\frac{\left|x\right|^{2}-1}{2}\left[\mathcal{F}_{1}(x)-\mathcal{F}_{2}(x)\right]

from the Green’s formula,
where the boundary integrals
\mathcal{F}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}(\cdot)
are given by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{F}_{1}(x):=\int_{\partial B({\bf 0},1)}\frac{\partial\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}\left(\left|y-x_{1}^{*}\right|^{2}+i\left|y-x_{2}^{*}\right|^{2}-\frac{(6+6i)}{\omega^{2}}\right)d\sigma(y),\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),\\
\mathcal{F}_{2}(x):=\int_{\partial B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)\frac{\partial\left(\left|y-x_{1}^{*}\right|^{2}+i\left|y-x_{2}^{*}\right|^{2}\right)}{\partial\nu(y)}d\sigma(y),\qquad\qquad\quad\quad x\in B({\bf 0},1),\end{cases}

which can be calculated numerically with high accuracy like
\mathcal{J}_{k}(x,\omega)
, see (
3.22
). Hence, the source term
\Theta(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
given by
(\ref{FFJM})
can be numerically computed. Consequently, by using our proposed algorithm scheme, the numerical solution
v_{ass,num}^{\star}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
for
x\in B({\bf 0},1)
associated to such a known function
v_{ass}^{\star}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
can be derived by solving the following equation

(4.4)

v_{ass,num}^{*}(x,\theta,\omega)-\omega^{2}\left(\frac{1}{k_{0}(x)}-1\right)\int_{B({\bf 0},1)}\Phi_{\omega}(x,y)v_{ass,num}^{*}(y,\theta,\omega)dy=\Theta(x,\theta,\omega),

where
\Theta(x,\theta,\omega)
is given by (
4.3
).

The accuracy of our proposed algorithm scheme can be tested by comparing the disparity between the assumed known function
v_{ass}^{\star}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
and its associated numerical solution
v_{ass,num}^{\star}(\cdot,\theta,\omega)
, solution of
(\ref{v_num})
. In order to do this, for both
v_{ass}^{\star}(x,\theta,\omega)
and
v_{ass,num}^{\star}(x,\theta,\omega)
, we represent the argument
x\in B(0,1)
by spherical coordinates, i.e.,

5.
Recovering the bulk modulus function
k_{0}(\cdot)
inside
\Omega\,\equiv\,B(0,1)

The goal of this section is to numerically recover the bulk modulus function
k_{0}(\cdot)
in local domain of
\Omega\,\equiv\,B({\bf 0},1)
, using our proposed scheme described by
Algorithm
2.3
from the data

(5.1)

\xi(z)=v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{1})-u^{\infty}_{z}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{1}),

see (
2.17
). It is important to note that in engineering situations, the above inversion input data
\xi(\cdot)
are obtained through physical measurements for
\left(v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{1}),\,u^{\infty}_{z}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{1})\right)
. To test our reconstruction algorithm, we use the far-field data
v^{\infty}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{1})
and
u^{\infty}_{z}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{1})
generated from the simulation process. The effectiveness of the simulation process has been checked in section 4. We assume the bulk modulus function
k_{0}(\cdot)
to be identified is give by

(5.2)

k_{0,exact}(x)=\frac{2}{1+\left|x\right|^{2}},\quad x\,\in B({\bf 0},1)

for the simulation. Using the arguments in section 3, we can obtain
\xi(z)
from (
5.1
) for
z\in B({\bf 0},1)
.

As explained in section 3, the simulation of
u^{\infty}_{z}(-\theta,\theta,\omega_{1})
for
z\in\mathfrak{U}
where
k_{0}(z)
is recovered, requires to solve a 3-dimensional linear integral equation for every
z\in\mathfrak{U}
, which need large computational cost for large domain
\mathfrak{U}
. To check the numerical performances of our proposed reconstruction
Algorithm
2.3
with acceptable computational costs, we take
\mathfrak{U}
as a cube
\mathfrak{Q}:\equiv\mathfrak{Q}({\bf 0};0.5)\subset\subset B({\bf 0},1)
, which is centered at the origin with the side length
0.5
. In this cube, we take
61
-points along each direction as observation points.
More precisely, the periodically distributed discrete points
x_{i,j,k}=\left(x^{1}_{i},x^{2}_{j},x^{3}_{k}\right)\in\mathfrak{Q}
are specified by

x^{1}_{i}=-0.25+\frac{0.5(i-1)}{N-1},\quad x^{2}_{j}=-0.25+\frac{0.5(j-1)}{N-1}\quad\text{and}\quad x^{3}_{k}=-0.25+\frac{0.5(k-1)}{N-1}

for
i,j,k=1,\cdots,N
with
N=61
, and the corresponding noisy data
\xi^{\tau}(\cdot)
are obtained by shifting
\xi(\cdot)
using a random variable
\mathcal{X}(\cdot)
following a uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}\left(\cdot\right)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}\sim\left(\dfrac{\tau}{0.25}\right)^{3}\mathcal{U}\left(\mathfrak{Q}\right)
, with
\tau
being a small positive number indicating the noisy level. For
\left\{\tau_{i,j,k}:=\mathcal{X}\left(x_{i,j,k}\right):\,i,j,k=1,\cdots,N\right\}
, we synthesize the noisy data by

\xi^{\tau}\left(x_{i,j,k}\right):=\xi\left(x_{i,j,k}\right)+\tau_{i,j,k}\xi\left(x_{i,j,k}\right)\quad\text{for}\quad i,j,k=1,\cdots,N\quad\text{and}\quad 0<\tau_{i,j,k}\ll 1,

which fulfills the inequality

\left\|\xi^{\tau}(\cdot)-\xi(\cdot)\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}\left(\mathfrak{Q}\right)}\leq\tau\left\|\xi(\cdot)\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}\left(\mathfrak{Q}\right)}.

Although we only check our reconstruction algorithm in an interior local domain
\mathfrak{Q}=\mathfrak{Q}({\bf 0},0.5)\subset B({\bf 0},1)
due to the restrictions on the computational cost for yielding the inversion input by simulation process, our algorithm can be applied to any interior domain of
\Omega
, if we do not consider the simulation cost for yielding
u^{\infty}_{z}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
, or we assume that
u^{\infty}_{z}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
are given directly in
\Omega\equiv B({\bf 0},1)
.

The crucial step for numerically computing
k_{0}(z)
by (
2.26
) from noisy inversion input is the computational scheme for
\Delta\xi^{\tau}(z),\nabla\xi^{\tau}(z)
with noisy level
\tau>0
. By our mollification
scheme (
2.29
) and (
2.30
), we are essentially to compute
\Delta\xi^{\delta,\tau}(z)
and
\nabla\xi^{\delta,\tau}(z)
with specified
\delta>0
and noisy level
\tau
.

However, we only have the noisy values
\xi^{\tau}
at
61
discrete points, due to the restriction on the computational cost in simulating these inversion input by solving 3-dimensional integral equations for each point
z
. These values are not enough for computing the derivatives accurately from (
2.29
) and (
2.30
), since the integrations in (
2.29
) and (
2.30
) need very fine grids of
\xi^{\tau}
for yielding good approximation. To this end, we refine the computational grids by interpolation process using
\{\xi^{\tau}(x_{i,j,k}):i,j,k=1,\cdots,61\}
to yield a smooth function
\tilde{\xi}^{\tau}
, which is taken as the approximation to
\xi^{\tau}(x)
. This process is finished by the standard interpolation function
griddedInterpolant
in Matlab with cubic spline function as base functions. Then we have the values of

\tilde{\xi}^{\tau}
at fine grids
\{\tilde{x}_{i,j,k}:\;i,j,k=1,\cdots,201\}\subset\mathfrak{Q}
. Finally we have the reconstruction

(5.3)

\frac{1}{k_{0,num}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})}\approx-\frac{1}{\omega_{1}^{2}}\left(\frac{\Delta\tilde{\xi}^{\delta,\tau}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})}{2\tilde{\xi}^{\tau}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})}-\frac{|\nabla\tilde{\xi}^{\delta,\tau}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})|^{2}}{4\tilde{\xi}^{\tau}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})^{2}}\right),\quad\tilde{x}_{i,j,k}\in\mathfrak{Q}_{\delta}.

Since we have the values of
\tilde{\xi}^{\tau}(\cdot)
at very fine grids
\{\tilde{x}_{i,j,k}:\;i,j,k=1,\cdots,201\}
, the right-hand side can be computed accurately from (
2.29
) and (
2.30
).

To indicate the reconstruction performance of our proposed
Algorithm
2.3
quantitatively,
we introduce the global relative error (GRE) and the point-wise relative error (PRE) in
\mathfrak{Q}
related to the
a-prior
known bulk modulus function
k_{0,\text{exact}}(\cdot)
given by
(\ref{k0GRE})

GRE:=\frac{\left\|k_{0,exact}-k_{0,num}\right\|_{\ell^{2}\left(\mathfrak{Q}\right)}}{\left\|k_{0,exact}\right\|_{\ell^{2}\left(\mathfrak{Q}\right)}}:=\frac{\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{201}\sum_{j=1}^{201}\sum_{k=1}^{201}\left|k_{0,exact}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})-k_{0,num}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{201}\sum_{j=1}^{201}\sum_{k=1}^{201}\left|k_{0,exact}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}},

and

PRE(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k}):=\frac{\left|k_{0,exact}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})-k_{0,num}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})\right|}{\left|k_{0,exa}(\tilde{x}_{i,j,k})\right|},\quad\tilde{x}_{i,j,k}\in\mathfrak{Q},

respectively,
where
k_{0,num}(\cdot)
is the numerical solution obtained from inverting the data (
5.1
). Since the mollification scheme treating the numerical differentiations for noisy data is of only average convergence by (
2.28
), we can also expect the good approximation under the convergence norm, instead of the point-wise approximation.
