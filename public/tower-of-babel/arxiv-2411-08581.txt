1.
Introduction

We will let all groups be finite throughout this paper. Let
d
be the degree of a complex irreducible character of
G
. Since
d
must divide
|G|
and
|G|\geq d^{2}
, we may write
|G|=d(d+e)
for some nonnegative integer
e
. If
e=0
, then
G
must be trivial since the sum of squares of the irreducible character degrees of a group are equal to the order of that group. Berkovich
[
1
]
showed that when
e=1
,
G
is a cyclic group of order
2
or that
G
is a
2
-transitive Frobenius group. This is an interesting case, because this is the only value of
e
for which
|G|
has no upper bound. Snyder
[
12
]
showed that for
e\geq 2
, there is an upper bound on
|G|
in terms of
e
. Moreover, in the same paper, Snyder classified groups with
e=2
or
3
, and as a result, showed that
|G|\leq 8
when
e=2
and
|G|\leq 54
when
e=3
. Finally, Snyder showed that whenever
e>1
, we have that
|G|\leq((2e)!)^{2}
.

Isaacs
[
4
]
went on to improve Snyder’s (non-polynomial) bound to a polynomial bound of the form
Be^{6}
for some, large enough, not explicitly known, universal constant
B
. This proof relied on a result of Larsen et al.
[
7
]
, which relied on the classification of finite simple groups.

Following this, Durfee and Jensen
[
3
]
improved this bound to strictly less than
e^{6}-e^{4}
without using the classification of finite simple groups. Moreover, in the same paper, Durfee and Jensen showed that if
G
has a non-trivial normal abelian subgroup, then
|G|\leq e^{4}-e^{3}
if
e
is prime, and
|G|<e^{4}-e^{3}
if
e
is divisible by two distinct primes. However, it still needed to be shown that this is the correct bound in the case where
G
is solvable and
e
is a prime power that is not prime.

Following the work of Durfee and Jensen, Lewis
[
8
]
proved that
|G|\leq e^{4}-e^{3}
for groups with a nontrivial, abelian normal subgroup, without any prime-related restrictions on
e
. Finally, in 2015, Nguyen, Lewis, and Schaeffer Fry
[
10
]
proved the general case. That is, if
d
is the degree of a complex irreducible character of
G
where
|G|=d(d+e)
for some integer
e>1
, then
|G|\leq e^{4}-e^{3}
. Moreover, they show this bound is the best possible bound.

The authors in
[
10
]
asked, when does
G
have an irreducible character of degree
d
? Snyder
[
12
]
classified all groups with
e=2
and
e=3
. Durfee and Jensen, in the same paper referenced above, fully classified all groups with
4\leq e\leq 6
, as well as all possible
d
values which can occur with
e=7
. Next, Sambale
[
11
]
extended this classification of
d
to
e\leq 11
. Much of the previous work has relied on computer algebra systems. However, as the value of
e
gets larger, these computer algebra systems become more difficult to use.

We seek to answer this question for all groups of order
d(d+e)
by finding an equivalent condition for when
d\in\text{cd}(G)
. As we begin our work, for this paper, we will handle the case when
d
and
d+e
are coprime, and
d
is square-free. The goal of this paper is to show an equivalence between
d\in\text{cd}(G)
and a sequence of congruences between the prime power factors of
d+e
and the factors of
d
. In particular, we will prove the following:

Main Theorem
.

Let
G
be a finite solvable group such that
|G|=d_{1}\cdots d_{m}p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{n}^{a_{n}}
where the
d_{j}
’s and
p_{i}
’s are distinct primes, and
a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
for all
i
. Let
d=d_{1}\cdots d_{m}
.

Then there exists a group
G
with
d\in{\rm cd}(G)
if and only if there exist
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

p_{c_{1}}^{e_{c_{1}}}\equiv 1({\rm mod}\text{ }b_{1}),\dots,p_{c_{k}}^{e_{c_{k}}}\equiv 1({\rm mod}\text{ }b_{k}),

where
b_{1}\cdots b_{k}=d
and primes
p_{c_{1}},\dots,p_{c_{k}}
such that
p_{c_{1}}^{e_{c_{1}}}\dots p_{c_{k}}^{e_{c_{k}}}\mid p_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots p_{n}^{a_{n}}
.

2.
Background

We will need to make use of Frobenius groups throughout the course of this paper. We begin with some background information on such groups.

Let
H\subseteq G
, with
1<H<G
. Assume that
H\cap H^{g}=1
whenever
g\in G\setminus H
. Then we define
H
to be a
Frobenius complement
in
G
. A group which contains a Frobenius complement is defined to be a
Frobenius group
. Next, we state a classical result of Frobenius:

Lemma 1

(Frobenius)
.

Let
G
be a Frobenius group with complement
H
. Then there exists
N\trianglelefteq G
with
HN=G
and
H\cap N=1
.

Proof.

This is Theorem 7.2 in
[
5
]
.
∎

The subgroup
N
in the above lemma is called the
Frobenius kernel
. A useful consequence is that Frobenius kernels and Frobenius complements have necessarily coprime orders. In particular,

Lemma 2
.

Let
G
be a Frobenius group with Frobenius complement
H
and Frobenius kernel
N
. Then

|N|\equiv 1({\rm mod}\text{ }|H|)

Proof.

This is Lemma
6.1
in
[
6
]
.
∎

Irreducible characters of Frobenius groups have been extensively studied.
They are of two types: ones which are induced from nontrivial irreducible characters of
N
, and others whose kernels contain
N
. Formally,

Lemma 3
.

Let
G
be a Frobenius group with Frobenius complement
H
and Frobenius kernel
N
. Then

(1)

For
\phi\in{\rm Irr}(N)
with
1_{N}\neq\phi
, we have
{\rm I_{G}}(\phi)=N
and
\phi^{G}\in{\rm Irr}(G)
.

(2)

For
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)
with
N\not\subseteq{\rm ker}\chi
, we have
\chi=\phi^{G}
for some
\phi\in{\rm Irr}(N)
.

Proof.

This is Theorem
6.34
in
[
5
]
.
∎

When considering Lemma
3
, we have that there exists
\phi\in\text{Irr}(N)
such that
\chi=\phi^{G}
. Then
\phi^{G}(1)=|G:N|\phi(1)
and so
\phi^{G}(1)=|H|\phi(1)
. Therefore,
|H|\mid\phi^{G}(1)
and if
\phi
is a linear character, then
|H|=\phi^{G}(1)
. In particular,
|H|\in\text{cd}(G)
.

By Thompson’s classical result, we know
N
is nilpotent, thus
N^{\prime}\lneq N
where
N^{\prime}
is the derived subgroup of
N
. Therefore,
N
must have at least one linear character which is non-principal. Let
\phi\in\text{Irr}(N)
be such a character. We know that
\phi
lies in an orbit of size
|H|
, and
\phi^{G}\in\text{Irr}(G)
by Lemma
3
. Hence
\phi^{G}(1)=|H|
. In particular, we will always be guaranteed such a
\phi
such that
|H|\in\text{cd}(G)
.

Next, we will need the Ito-Michler Theorem and so we state it here for convenience:

Lemma 4

(It
\hat{\text{o}}
-Michler)
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
p
a prime, and
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)
. Then
p\nmid\chi(1)
if and only if
G
has a normal, abelian Sylow
p
-subgroup.

Proof.

See Theorem 2.3 on page 132 in
[
9
]

∎

Consider a group of order
d_{1}\cdots d_{m}p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{n}^{a_{n}}
, where
d=d_{1}\cdots d_{m}
and
d+e=p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{n}^{a_{n}}
, as in the Main Theorem. Let
D_{j}\in\text{Syl}_{d_{j}}(G)
. We define the Fitting subgroup of
G
, denoted
\textbf{F}(G)
, to be the unique largest normal nilpotent subgroup of
G
. We then know that
d_{j}\nmid\textbf{F}(G)
for all
j
, otherwise
D_{j}\trianglelefteq G
, and so
d_{j}\nmid\chi(1)
for all
\chi\in\text{Irr}(G)
. In particular,
d\not\in\text{cd}(G)
. This will be a useful contradiction when considering the backwards direction of the Main Theorem.

The Chinese Remainder Theorem will play a crucial role in proving our main theorem, but it is typically presented in terms of rings. Though we will not need the ring-theoretic version for our result, we will state it here for convenience to the reader, and then give a useful corollary which will be used in the proof of the main result.

Lemma 5

(Chinese Remainder Theorem)
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}
be ideals in the ring
R
. The map

R\rightarrow R/A_{1}\times R/A_{2}\times\cdots\times R/A_{k}\text{ defined by }r\mapsto(r+A_{1},r+A_{2},\dots,r+A_{k})

is a ring homomorphism with kernel
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap\cdots\cap A_{k}
. If for each
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
with
i\neq j
the ideals
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
are comaximal, then this map is surjective and
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap\cdots\cap A_{k}=A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{k}
, so

R/(A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{k})=R/(A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap\cdots\cap A_{k})\cong R/A_{1}\times R/A_{2}\times\cdots\times R/A_{k}.

Proof.

This is Theorem 7.6.17 in
[
2
]
.
∎

An immediate corollary of the Chinese Remainder Theorem is the following:

Lemma 6
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2},\dots n_{k}
be integers such that
(n_{i},n_{j})=1
for all
i\neq j
. Then, for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
there is a solution
x\in\mathbb{Z}
to the simultaneous congruences

x\equiv a_{1}({\rm mod}\text{ }n_{1}),x\equiv a_{2}({\rm mod}\text{ }n_{2}),\dots,x\equiv a_{k}({\rm mod}\text{ }n_{k})

and that the solution
x
is unique modulo
n_{1}n_{2}\cdots n_{k}
.

Proof.

This is Exercise 5.a of Section 7.6 in
[
2
]
.
∎

For our main result, we will consider the case where
d=n_{1}\cdots n_{k}
. Note that any
n_{i}
in Lemma
6
may be a product of prime factors of
d
. Moreover, we will be in the case where
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{k}=1
. Suppose we are in the case where
x
is some power of a prime
p
, say
p^{b}
where
b\in\mathbb{N}
, and we have that
p^{b}\not\equiv 1(\text{mod }n_{i})
, where
n_{i}
is the product of more than one prime factor of
d
. Since all the prime factors of
d
are unique, and
d
is square free, we have that
n_{i}
is coprime to any other prime factor of
d
not dividing itself. Therefore, we may reduce case above from
p^{b}\not\equiv 1(\text{mod }n_{i})
, to
p^{b}\not\equiv 1(\text{mod }d_{j})
, where
d_{j}
is a single prime divisor of
d
such that
d_{j}\nmid n_{i}
.

Next, we need a bit of background on Hall subgroups, as we will use their properties in the proof of Lemma
7
below. As in
[
6
]
, let
\pi
be a set of primes, and let
\pi^{\prime}
be the complement of
\pi
in the set of all prime numbers.
Then we define a
\pi
-group to be a finite group such that all primes dividing its order lie in
\pi
, and we define a
\pi
-subgroup of
G
to be a subgroup of
G
that happens to be a
\pi
-group. Next, we define a Hall
\pi
-subgroup of a finite group
G
to be a
\pi
-subgroup of
G
whose index in
G
involves no prime of
\pi
, and so if
H
is a Hall
\pi
-subgroup of
G
, then
|H|
is a
\pi
-number and
|G:H|
is a
\pi^{\prime}
-number. Note that when
\pi
consists of a single prime, then a Hall
\pi
-subgroup of
G
is just a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
, and such a subgroup of
G
exists for every finite group. If
|\pi|>1
, then an arbitrary finite group can fail to have a Hall
\pi
-subgroup. However, Theorem 3.13 in
[
6
]
, commonly called Hall’s Existence Theorem, guarantees that if
G
is a finite
solvable
group, then
G
has a Hall
\pi
-subgroup for any set of primes,
\pi
. Therefore, it is important that we assume solvability in our results below.

3.
Main Result

We begin by proving the backwards direction of our main theorem.

Lemma 7
.

Let
G
be a finite solvable group such that
|G|=d_{1}\cdots d_{m}p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{n}^{a_{n}}
where the
d_{j}
’s and
p_{i}
’s are distinct primes,
a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
for all
i
. Let
d=d_{1}\cdots d_{m}
.

If there exist
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
and
b_{1},\cdots,b_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

p_{c_{1}}^{e_{c_{1}}}\equiv 1({\rm mod}\text{ }b_{1}),\dots,p_{c_{k}}^{e_{c_{k}}}\equiv 1({\rm mod}\text{ }b_{k}),

where
b_{1}\cdots b_{k}=d
and primes
p_{c_{1}},\dots,p_{c_{k}}
such that
p_{c_{1}}^{e_{c_{1}}}\dots p_{c_{k}}^{e_{c_{k}}}\mid p_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots p_{n}^{a_{n}}
.

Then there exists a group
G
with
d\in{\rm cd}(G)
.

Proof.

Given the list of congruences above, we may construct a group
G
which can be written as a direct product of Frobenius subgroups, say,
H_{1},\cdots,H_{k}
such that each of these Frobenius subgroups have a Frobenius complement of order
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}
, respectively, and abelian subgroups,
K_{1},\dots,K_{q}
such that

\prod_{s=1}^{q}|K_{s}|=\frac{p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{n}^{a_{n}}}{p_{c_{1}}^{e_{c_{1}}}\dots p_{c_{k}}^{e_{c_{k}}}}.

We have that
b_{i}\in\text{cd}(H_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. Hence the product
b_{1}\cdots b_{k}=d\in\text{cd}(G)
, as wanted.
∎

Next, we will need a lemma that will serve as the base case for an inductive argument used in the proof of our main result. Specifically, this is the case when
d
is composed of a single prime, and our Main Theorem will extend this result to the case where
d
is composite (while still being square-free).

Lemma 8
.

Let
G
be a finite solvable group such that
|G|=dp_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{n}^{a_{n}}
, where
d
and the
p_{i}
’s are distinct primes, and
a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
for each
i
. Then there exists a group
G
such that
d\in{\rm cd}(G)
if and only if

p_{i}^{b_{i}}\equiv 1({\rm mod}\text{ }d),

where
b_{i}\leq a_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq n
.

Proof.

The backwards direction is immediate from Lemma
7
above.

For the forward direction, we will proceed by proving the contrapositive. Suppose
p_{i}^{b_{i}}\not\equiv 1(\text{mod }d)
for all
b_{i}\leq a_{i}
where
1\leq i\leq n
. Since
G
is solvable,
G
has a Hall
dp_{i}
-subgroup of
G
for each
p_{i}
. Let
D\in\text{Syl}_{d}(G)
and
P_{i}\in\text{Syl}_{p_{i}}(G)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Since
p_{i}^{b_{i}}\not\equiv 1(\text{mod }d)
for all
b_{i}\leq a_{i}
where
1\leq i\leq n
, we may choose
\hat{P_{i}}\in\text{Hall}_{dp_{i}}(G)
such that
D\trianglelefteq\hat{P_{i}}
.
P_{i}
will then normalize
D
for each
1\leq i\leq n
, and so
P_{i}\subseteq N_{G}(D)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Certainly
D\subseteq N_{G}(D)
and so
|G|=|N_{G}(D)|
. Therefore
D\trianglelefteq G
, and so
d\not\in\text{cd}(G)
, by Lemma
4
.
∎

We are now ready to proceed with the proof of the Main Theorem.

proof of Main Theorem.

The backwards direction is Lemma
7
above.

For the forward direction, without loss of generality, suppose
d_{1}<d_{2}<\dots<d_{m}
. We may assume that
d_{j}\nmid|\textbf{F}(G)|
for all
j
, otherwise
D_{j}\trianglelefteq G
and so
d\not\in\text{cd}(G)
, by Lemma
4
. Thus,
|\textbf{F}(G)|
divides
p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{n}^{a_{n}}
. We will induct on
m
.

For the base case, when
m=1
, we may apply Lemma
8
. Now, suppose the result holds for
m-1
.

Let
E/\textbf{F}(G)
be the Fitting subgroup of
G/\textbf{F}(G)
. Consider
|E:\textbf{F}(G)|
. We know that
|E:\textbf{F}(G)|
divides
d_{1}\cdots d_{m}
. Suppose that
|E:\textbf{F}(G)|=d_{j}
, for some
j<m
. Then,

\frac{N_{\frac{G}{\textbf{F}(G)}}\big(E/\textbf{F}(G)\big)}{C_{\frac{G}{\textbf{F}(G)}}\big(E/\textbf{F}(G)\big)}=\frac{G/\textbf{F}(G)}{E/\textbf{F}(G)}=G/E.

And so,
|G:E|
divides
|\text{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}_{d_{j}})|=d_{j}-1
.
Hence,
d_{m}\mid d_{j}-1
which implies that
d_{j}>d_{m}
, a contradiction. Therefore,
|E:\textbf{F}(G)|
is equal to
d_{m}
, or some product of the
d_{j}
’s.

If
|E:\textbf{F}(G)|=d_{m}
, then
|E|=d_{m}|\textbf{F}(G)|
, and so

|G:E|=d_{1}\cdots d_{m-1}\Big|\frac{p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{n}^{a_{n}}}{\textbf{F}(G)}\Big|.

By our inductive hypothesis, the result holds in
G/E
. Moreover, as
|E|=d_{m}|\textbf{F}(G)|
, Lemma
8
gives the result in
E
. Taking our congruences in both
G/E
and
E
, we obtain the result in
G
.

Let
|E:\textbf{F}(G)|=\gamma
, where
\gamma
is some arbitrary placeholder for some product of the
d_{j}
’s. To obtain a contradiction, suppose that for all
a_{i}
and for all
b_{a_{i}}
, where
1\leq i\leq n
, we have
p^{a_{i}}\not\equiv 1(\text{mod }b_{a_{i}})
. Without loss of generality, we may choose
b_{a_{i}}\mid\gamma
. Moreover, since the prime divisors of
d
are unique, by Lemma
6
, we may take
b_{a_{i}}
to be equal to
d_{\alpha}
for some
1\leq\alpha\leq m
.

We have that
E/\textbf{F}(G)
is a square-free nilpotent group, and so is cyclic. Thus,
E/\textbf{F}(G)
has a subgroup of index
\gamma/b_{a_{i}}=\gamma/d_{\alpha}
. And so,
E
has a subgroup of order
d_{\alpha}|\textbf{F}(G)|
. Let
\bar{E}
be the aforementioned subgroup of
E
. Since
d\in\text{cd}(G)
, we must have
d_{\alpha}\in\text{cd}(\bar{E})
, which is true if and only if
p^{f}\equiv 1(\text{mod }d_{\alpha})
, for some
1\leq f\leq l\leq k
, by Lemma
8
, a contradiction, as wanted.
∎
