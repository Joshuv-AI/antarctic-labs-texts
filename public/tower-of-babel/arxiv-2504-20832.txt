Approximate Quantum Fourier Transform in Logarithmic Depth on a Line
Elisa Bäumer ,, David Sutter, Stefan Woerner

Abstract
The approximate quantum Fourier transform (AQFT) on n n qubits can be implemented in logarithmic depth using 8 ​ n 8n qubits with all-to-all connectivity, as shown in [Hales, PhD Thesis Berkeley, 2002]. However, realizing the required all-to-all connectivity can be challenging in practice. In this work, we use dynamic circuits, i.e., mid-circuit measurements and feed-forward operations, to implement the AQFT in logarithmic depth using only 4 ​ n 4n qubits arranged on a line with nearest-neighbor connectivity. Furthermore, for states with a specific structure, the number of qubits can be further reduced to 2 ​ n 2n while keeping the logarithmic depth and line connectivity. As part of our construction, we introduce a new implementation of an adder with logarithmic depth on a line, which allows us to improve the AQFT construction of Hales.

Introduction

It is widely believed that quantum computers can solve problems that no classical algorithm can solve in a reasonable amount of time. Arguably, the most prominent example is Shor’s algorithm
[
1
]
to factor an arbitrary integer in polynomial time, whereas the most efficient known classical factoring algorithms have a sub-exponential runtime
[
2
]
.

To quantify the cost of implementing a quantum algorithm represented by a quantum circuit, typically three quantities are considered: (i) the width, i.e., the number of qubits, (ii) the depth, i.e., the number of consecutive time steps necessary to execute the circuit, where a time step is a single application of the maximum number of gates such that all applied gates can be executed simultaneously, and (iii) the size, i.e., the total number of gates.
Width and depth are arguably the most operationally relevant metrics, as they quantifiy the required number of qubits and resulting runtime of an algorithm.
While occasionally only the size and width of an algorithm are analyzed, this only gives a lower bound on the depth. In contrast, analyzing the width and depth directly results in an upper bound on the size.
Here, we assume that gates on distinct subsets of qubits that are applied in the same time slice can be executed in parallel.

One crucial building block in Shor’s algorithm is the quantum Fourier transform (QFT). In
[
3
]
, Cleve and Watrous showed that the approximate quantum Fourier transform (AQFT) on
n
qubits can be implemented in depth
O(\log n)
with all-to-all connectivity between the qubits and
O(n\log n)
ancilla qubits. They also reduced the circuit size of the exact QFT from
O(n^{2})
to
O(n(\log n)^{2}\log\log n)
, still with a depth of
O(n)
though. Later in
[
4
]
, Hales showed that the AQFT can be implemented in the same depth, however, requiring only
8n
qubits. There have been further improvements regarding the size of the AQFT but with linear depth
[
5
]
. The depth can be further reduced to constant when using
O(n^{3}\log n)
qubits and all-to-all connectivity
[
6
,
7
]
.
Here, we focus on logarithmic depth constructions that require only
O(n)
qubits on a line.

A natural question is whether the width or connectivity requirements to implement the AQFT can be further improved while preserving logarithmic depth. In this work, we show that

(1)

we can relax the connectivity requirements from all-to-all to a minimum, i.e., to a line of qubits with nearest-neighbor connectivity (see
Table

1
).

(2)

we can reduce the number of qubits to
4n
for a line of qubits with nearest-neighbor connectivity and to

3n
for an all-to-all connectivity (see
Table

2
).

We refer to
Theorem

1.2
for the mathematically rigorous statement.
Our algorithm builds on the results from
[
4
]
and we use the power of dynamic circuits, i.e., mid-circuit measurements and feed-forward operations, to implement the circuits on a line without increasing the required depth.
In particular, we show how to implement a quantum adder in logarithmic depth on a line of qubits using dynamic circuits, enabling our main result.
For states with specific structure, we do not even require dynamic circuits and can implement the AQFT on a line of only
2n
qubits.

Quantum Fourier transform on a line

In this section we formally introduce the building blocks for the QFT, i.e., the QFS, FPE and ADD operations mentioned in
Section

1

2.1
Preliminaries

Let
\mathrm{S}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of density matrices on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Furthermore, for

we denote its trace norm by
\left\lVert L\right\rVert_{1}:=\mathrm{tr}[\sqrt{L^{\dagger}L}]
and its operator (or spectral) norm by
\left\lVert L\right\rVert_{\infty}

The diamond-norm distance between two unitary channels
\mathcal{U}(\cdot):=U(\cdot)U^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{V}(\cdot):=V(\cdot)V^{\dagger}
for two unitaries

and

can be written and bounded as

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\diamond}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{V}\right\rVert_{\diamond}

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\max_{\rho_{AE}\in\mathrm{S}(\mathcal{H}_{AE})}\left\lVert((\mathcal{U}_{A}-\mathcal{V}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{I}_{E})(\rho_{AE})\right\rVert_{1}

(12)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\phi\in[0,2\pi]}\left\lVert\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}U-V\right\rVert_{\infty}

(13)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert U-V\right\rVert_{\infty}

(14)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert U\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}-V\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}\right\rVert_{\infty}

(15)

\displaystyle=\max_{|\psi\rangle_{AE}\in\mathcal{H}_{AE}}\left\lVert(U\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}-V\otimes\mathds{1}_{E})|\psi\rangle_{AE}\right\rVert_{2}

(16)

\displaystyle=:\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{H}_{AE}}(U,V)\,,

(17)

where
A\simeq E

3
Improved implementation of the QFT

For a more efficient implementation, we can leverage that the ancilla registers are (ideally) uncorrelated in the end together with the principle of deferred measurement. This allows to measure some of the ancilla registers during the uncomputation and apply classically conditioned gates. Similarly to the advantage we get when implementing only classically conditioned QFS and ADD operations in the general construction of
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}
, we can reduce the depth for
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
, first considering the implementation presented above, and then showing a further optimized way by reversing the order of the operations.

Forward implementation.

Noting that the FPE essentially just unentangles and resets the first register, allows with the principle of deferred measurement to measure (and reset) it already after the QFS in the
X
-basis (i.e. applying a Hadamard gate before the measurement). The
n
CNOT gates of the FPE operation can then be replaced by classically controlled Z-gates applied to the second register. As these gates are classically controlled, they are not even executed if the measured qubit is in the
|0\rangle
state, so on average we can cut down half of those operations which allows to further simplify the FPE circuit (depending on the measurement outcomes). In addition, due to the early measurements there would be a smaller idling error on the first register.
Another possibility would be to measure the first register after the FPE has been applied and post-select on the output state
|0\rangle_{A}
, thereby reducing the error of the final (post-selected) state.

Backward implementation.

Let us first note that
\mathrm{QFT}^{\dagger}|j\rangle=\sum_{k}\omega_{N}^{-jk}|k\rangle=|\phi(-j)\rangle
and let us define an operation
\overline{\mathrm{FPE}}
which performs

\displaystyle|b\rangle|\phi(-j)\rangle\overset{\overline{\textnormal{FPE}}}{\longrightarrow}|b\oplus j\rangle|\phi(-j)\rangle.

(30)

Note that we can implement
\overline{\mathrm{FPE}}^{(\varepsilon)}
similarly to
\mathrm{FPE}^{(\varepsilon)}
, but with all QFTs in the quantum circuit in
Figure

4
replaced by QFT
†
and vice-versa, such that
\mathrm{dist}_{\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathrm{uni}}}(\mathrm{FPE}_{AB},\mathrm{FPE}^{(\varepsilon)}_{AB})\leq\varepsilon
, where
\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathrm{uni}}
is a restricted set of states, such that any
|\psi\rangle_{ABE}\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathrm{uni}}
is a uniformly distributed input state of the form
|\psi\rangle_{ABE}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\beta_{j,m}|j\rangle_{A}|\phi(-j)\rangle_{B}|m\rangle_{E}
, with
|\sum_{m}\beta_{j,m}\beta_{\ell,m}^{\ast}|\leq\frac{p(n)}{N}\delta_{j,\ell}
, where
p(n)
denotes a polynomial in
n
with fixed degree independent of
n
and some environment
E
with dimension
\dim(E)=M\geq 2^{2n}
.

We can now see that when replacing the QFS with QFS
†
and the FPE with
\overline{\mathrm{FPE}}
in the implementation of
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
, we get

\displaystyle|j\rangle|0\rangle\!\overset{\textnormal{H}}{\longrightarrow}\!|j\rangle|\phi(0)\rangle\!\overset{\textnormal{QFS}^{\dagger}}{\longrightarrow}\!|j\rangle|\phi(-j)\rangle\!\overset{\overline{\textnormal{FPE}}}{\longrightarrow}\!|0\rangle|\phi(-j)\rangle\overset{\textnormal{SWAP}}{\longrightarrow}\!|\phi(-j)\rangle|0\rangle\,,

(31)

i.e. we have implemented
(\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}})^{\dagger}
. This directly implies that if we implement the quantum circuit in the opposite direction with all gates transposed and complex conjugated, we can again implement
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
, but this time with a reversed order of the operations. The benefit is that in this reversed order the final operation that essentially just unentangles and resets the ancilla register is the QFS. As in the forward implementation, we can use the principle of deferred measurement to measure and reset this register and apply classically conditioned gates. As the QFS consists solely of controlled phase gates, the total phase applied to each of the unmeasured qubits can be classically determined and applied in parallel. In particular,
all

O(n^{2})
controlled rotations can be taken into account, so the
exact
QFS can be implemented in constant depth. While this does not change the scaling of the implementation of
\mathrm{QFT}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{uni}}
, it still drastically improves the depth, mainly by decreasing the depth of the QFS from logarithmic to constant. In addition, as the exact QFS would reduce the total error by a constant factor, alternatively one could keep the total error fixed and thus decrease the depth of
\mathrm{FPE}^{(\varepsilon)}
as well.
This backward implementation works also for general inputs, as the general
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}
construction can simply be reversed as well.

Another potential optimization would be to recursively replace the small QFT operations in the construction of the FPE (see
Figure

4
) by the approximate QFT presented here. As the inputs are already by construction uniform, the simpler
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
could be applied here and thus the depth of the FPE reduced to
O(\log\log n)
. However, this requires another
n
qubits and a larger prefactor, and one would need to carefully analyze the total error to determine in which regime of
n
this trade-off could become beneficial.

4
Implications for Shor’s algorithm

The main ingredient of Shor’s algorithm
[
1
]
to factor a number
M
is the controlled modular exponentiation, which prepares the state

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x=0}^{2^{2n}-1}|x\rangle|a^{x}\bmod M\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{z=0}^{r-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\frac{2^{2n}}{r}-1}|z+mr\rangle|a^{z}\bmod M\rangle\,,

(32)

where we used that
a^{x}\bmod M
is periodic with period
r
and for simplicity assumed that
r
divides
2^{2n}
. This is followed by a QFT on the first register, which in practice is often implemented on a single qubit via successive measurements and feed-forward operations though
[
12
,
13
]
. But as the multiplication steps for the modular exponentiation are in most implementations performed in the Fourier basis
[
14
]
, typically another
2n
QFT and QFT
†
applications are required. Here, the input states are of the form
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}|a^{x}\bmod M\rangle
, i.e., they are not uniformly distributed according to
Equation

5
. However, since we can easily check if the result is correct, we can always try the simplified protocol with
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
first and if not successful implement the general
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}
. Note, that slightly varying the block number
k
for the FPE changes the set of bad states
B
for a given input state and thus may also still allow a successful implementation of
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
.
We leave it for future research to rigorously understand the set of input states that guarantee a successful implementation of
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
and in particular to understand whether the input states in Shor’s algorithm are of that form for any
a
and
M
.

Using a logarithmic depth protocol for the QFT together with a sublinear depth multiplication operation, such as the Toom-Cook quantum multiplication circuit from
[
15
]
, leads to a sublinear depth implementation of the controlled modular multiplication step
|c\rangle|b\rangle\longrightarrow|c\rangle|a^{c}b\mod M\rangle
, which is the main ingredient in Shor’s algorithm. This also implies a sub-quadratic depth implementation of Shor’s using only
O(n)
qubits.
Unfortunately, the recursive “PhaseProduct” operation presented in
[
15
]
requires an adder applied to consecutive (not interleaved) registers of order
O(n)
, for which a logarithmic depth implementation is only known assuming all-to-all connectivity.
Thus, finding a sublinear multiplication circuit that can be implemented on a line of qubits remains an important open question, as it would immediately imply a sub-quadratic implementation of Shor’s algorithm on a line.

(75)

with

|\perp^{\prime}_{n}{}^{\phi(j)}\rangle
another garbage state orthogonal to

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j}=1-\prod_{m=0}^{\frac{n}{k}-1}(1-\varepsilon_{j_{m}})\leq 1-(1-\frac{1}{2^{k/2}})^{\frac{n}{k}}\leq\frac{n}{k2^{k/2}}\,.

(76)

Note, that the approximate FPE in

\displaystyle=\max_{|\psi\rangle_{ABE}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(p)}}\left\lVert(\mathrm{FPE}_{AB}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E})|\psi\rangle_{ABE}-(\mathrm{FPE}^{(\varepsilon)}_{AB}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E})|\psi\rangle_{ABE}\right\rVert_{2}

Proof of

In this section we compose the operations QFS and FPE to prove the assertion of

.
Let us recall that
\forall j

Thus,

(89)

for operations acting on
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(p)}
as defined above in
Equation

5
.
Then

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76219pt\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(p)}}(\mathrm{QFT},\mathrm{QFT}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(\varepsilon^{\prime},\bar{\varepsilon})})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(p)}}\big(\mathrm{SWAP}\cdot\mathrm{FPE}\cdot\mathrm{QFS}\cdot(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes H^{\otimes n}_{B}),\mathrm{SWAP}\cdot\mathrm{FPE}^{(\bar{\varepsilon})}\cdot\mathrm{QFS}^{(\varepsilon^{\prime})}\cdot(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes H^{\otimes n}_{B})\big)

(90)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(p)}}\big(\mathrm{FPE}\cdot\mathrm{QFS},\mathrm{FPE}^{(\bar{\varepsilon})}\cdot\mathrm{QFS}^{(\varepsilon^{\prime})}\big)

(91)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(p)}}\big(\mathrm{FPE}\cdot\mathrm{QFS},\mathrm{FPE}^{(\bar{\varepsilon})}\cdot\mathrm{QFS}^{(\varepsilon^{\prime})}\big)

(92)

\displaystyle{\leq}\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(p)}}\big(\mathrm{FPE}\cdot\mathrm{QFS},\mathrm{FPE}^{(\bar{\varepsilon})}\cdot\mathrm{QFS}\big)+\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(p)}}\big(\mathrm{FPE}^{(\bar{\varepsilon})}\cdot\mathrm{QFS},\mathrm{FPE}^{(\bar{\varepsilon})}\cdot\mathrm{QFS}^{(\varepsilon^{\prime})}\big)

A.4
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

As we have shown in
Section

A.3
that we can implement the unitary
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
which approximately performs the QFT on uniform input states with an error
\varepsilon
. Here, we show how this result can be lifted to work for arbitrary input states by constructing a circuit that first enforces the uniformity, then applies the
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
, and finally decodes such that the correct output state is reached.
To see how this construction (also indicated in
Figure

2
) works, consider

\displaystyle|j\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|c_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|c_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{QFS}_{A,C_{1}}}{\longrightarrow}\omega_{N}^{jc_{1}}|j\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|c_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|c_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}

(97)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{ADD}_{A,C_{2}}}{\longrightarrow}\omega_{N}^{jc_{1}}|j+c_{2}\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|c_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|c_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}

(98)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{QFT_{A,B}}}{\longrightarrow}\omega_{N}^{jc_{1}}|\phi(j+c_{2})\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|c_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|c_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}

(99)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{QFS}^{\dagger}_{A,C_{2}}}{\longrightarrow}\omega_{N}^{jc_{1}}|\phi(j)\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|c_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|c_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}

(100)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{ADD}^{{\dagger}}_{A,C_{1}}}{\longrightarrow}|\phi(j)\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|c_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|c_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}\,.

(101)

As this holds
\forall|j\rangle_{A}
and
\forall|c\rangle_{C}:=|c_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}\otimes|c_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}
, by linearity it also holds for an arbitrary superposition state
\sum_{j,c}\beta_{j,c}|j\rangle|c\rangle
.
Ensuring that
\sum_{c}|\beta_{j,c}|^{2}=\frac{1}{N}
allows to apply
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
in the second step.

Let us consider an arbitrary input state
|\psi\rangle_{ABE}\in\mathcal{S}
and an ancillary equal superposition state
|c\rangle_{C}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{c_{1},c_{2}=0}^{N-1}|c_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|c_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}
on
2n
qubits. Then, we have

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle_{ABE}|c\rangle_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j,m,c}\alpha_{j,m}|j\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|m\rangle_{E}|c\rangle_{C}

(102)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{ADD}_{A,C_{2}}\cdot\,\mathrm{QFS}_{A,C_{1}}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j,m,c}\alpha_{j,m}\omega_{N}^{jc_{1}}|j+c_{2}\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|m\rangle_{E}|c\rangle_{C}

(103)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j,m,c}\alpha_{j-c_{2},m}\omega_{N}^{(j-c_{2})c_{1}}|j\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|m\rangle_{E}|c\rangle_{C}

(104)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,m^{\prime}}\beta_{j,m^{\prime}}|j\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|m^{\prime}\rangle_{E^{\prime}}

(105)

\displaystyle=:|\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{ABE^{\prime}}\,,

(106)

with
|m^{\prime}\rangle_{E^{\prime}}:=|m\rangle_{E}|c\rangle_{C}
and
\beta_{j,m^{\prime}}:=\frac{\alpha_{j-c_{2},m}}{N}\omega_{N}^{(j-c_{2})c_{1}}

\displaystyle\sum_{m^{\prime}}\beta_{j,m^{\prime}}\beta_{\ell,m^{\prime}}^{\ast}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{m,c_{1}}\alpha_{j-c_{2},m}\alpha_{\ell-c_{2},m}^{\ast}\underbrace{\sum_{c_{2}}\omega_{N}^{(j-\ell)c_{2}}}_{\delta_{j,\ell}N}=\frac{\delta_{j,\ell}}{N}\sum_{m,c_{1}}|\alpha_{c_{1},m}|^{2}=\frac{\delta_{j,\ell}}{N}\,.

(107)

Thus, for any input state
|\psi\rangle_{ABE}\in\mathcal{S}
, the construction yields a state
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{ABE^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(1)}
which allows the application of
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
. Following the construction described in
Equation

101
then yields for arbitrary states

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle_{ABE}|c\rangle_{C}

\displaystyle{\longrightarrow}\sum_{j,m^{\prime}}\beta_{j,m^{\prime}}|j\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|m^{\prime}\rangle_{E^{\prime}}

(108)

\displaystyle{\longrightarrow}\!\sum_{j,m^{\prime}}\!\beta_{j,m^{\prime}}\!|\phi(j)\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|m^{\prime}\rangle_{E^{\prime}}\!=\!\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{j,m,c}\!\alpha_{j,m}\omega^{jc_{1}}|\phi(j+c_{2})\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|m\rangle_{E}|c\rangle_{C}

(109)

\displaystyle{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j,m,c}\alpha_{j,m}|\phi(j)\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}|m\rangle_{E}|c\rangle_{C}=\mathrm{QFT}_{AB}|\psi\rangle_{ABE}|c\rangle_{C}\,.

(110)

Note, that the ancilla register
C
is uncorrelated in the end, which allows to be measured, and that all operations that involve register
C
are only controlled on it. Thus, we can use the principle of deferred measurement to measure already in the very beginning and apply only classically conditioned gates. This, on the other hand, can be replaced by just classically drawing two random numbers
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, adding them to the initial state and phase via classically controlled ADD and QFS operations, and later subtracting the corresponding states and phases again via the classically controlled QFS
†
and ADD
†
. This procedure yields the exact same statistics as using an equally distributed state
|c\rangle_{C}
and allows to implement the classically controlled
\mathrm{QFS}
and
\mathrm{QFS}^{\dagger}
without any approximation error, as it simply corresponds to classically controlled single qubit phase rotations. It further substantially reduces the depth as compared to the regular QFS and ADD operations on
2n
qubits, as all two-qubit gates controlled on
C
can be replaced by classically controlled single-qubit rotations, which have no connectivity constraints anymore.

For
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{AB}=\mathrm{ADD}_{A,C_{1}}^{\dagger}\mathrm{QFS}_{A,C_{2}}^{\dagger}\cdot\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}\cdot\mathrm{ADD}_{A,C_{2}}\mathrm{QFS}_{A,C_{1}}
we can now bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{QFT}_{AB},\mathrm{QFT}_{AB}^{(\varepsilon)})

\displaystyle=\max_{|\psi\rangle_{ABE}\in\mathcal{S}}\left\lVert\big((\mathrm{QFT}_{AB}-\mathrm{QFT}_{AB}^{(\varepsilon)})\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}\big)|\psi\rangle_{ABE}\right\rVert_{2}

(111)

\displaystyle=\max_{|\psi\rangle_{ABE}\in\mathcal{S}}\!\left\lVert\Big(\mathrm{ADD}^{\dagger}\mathrm{QFS}^{\dagger}(\mathrm{QFT}\!-\!\mathrm{QFT}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(\varepsilon)})\mathrm{ADD}\,\mathrm{QFS}\big)\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}\big)|\psi\rangle_{ABE}\right\rVert_{2}

(112)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{|\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{ABE^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(1)}}\left\lVert(\mathrm{QFT}-\mathrm{QFT}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(\varepsilon)})\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}\big)|\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{ABE^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{2}

(113)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(1)}}(\mathrm{QFT},\mathrm{QFT}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(\varepsilon)})

(114)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\,,

(115)

where the final step uses
Lemma

1.1
.
The circuits required to perform the approximate QFT with error
\varepsilon
as explained above are visualized in
Figure

2
and can be implemented according to
Lemmas

2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
in 1D with depth

\displaystyle\underbrace{O(1)}_{\mathrm{QFS}}+\underbrace{O(\log n)}_{\mathrm{ADD}}+\underbrace{O(\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}})}_{\mathrm{QFT}_{\mathrm{uni}}}+\underbrace{O(1)}_{\mathrm{QFS}^{\dagger}}+\underbrace{O(\log n)}_{\mathrm{ADD}^{\dagger}}=O\Big(\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\,.

(116)

Note that
\mathrm{QFT}_{\mathrm{uni}}
is applied to states in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{uni}}^{(1)}
, thus its depth is bounded by
O(\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
and as the QFS is classically controlled here, its depth is constant, as explained above.

Remark A.1
.

Note, that the construction described above adds a random state and phase to the input state before applying
\mathrm{QFT}_{\mathrm{uni}}
and shows that the expectation value of the error can be bounded. This implies, that for most states and phases the error can be bounded with the same scaling. In particular, also for
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
, i.e.,
\mathrm{QFT}_{\mathrm{uni}}
is likely to work for a random input state without adding a state and phase. However, the success probability is state-dependent, and thus it cannot be guaranteed to work for an arbitrary input state and we do not know if it works without the general construction for relevant states. As the set of bad states
B
depends on the block size
k
though, varying
k
may also remove a certain input state from
B
and thus allow for a successful implementation.

Remark A.2
.

Unlike in
[
4
]
, the presented
\mathrm{QFT}
acts only on two registers of
n
qubits each, instead of four. However, for general input states that require the construction described in
Equation

101
, we require a quantum adder. As shown in
Lemma

2.3
, this can be implemented on a line in logarithmic depth, but it requires in general
4n
qubits. In our specific case, where a classical number is added to a quantum register, we can apply classically conditioned operations and reduce the number of qubits for the adder to
3n
. However, given the local connectivity, yet another
n
qubits are required for teleporting the long-range entangling gates. As the adder is only applied when register
B
is in state
|0\rangle_{B}
, these
n

4n

To adhere to the connectivity constraints for the different operations, all four registers need to be “meshed”, meaning that the registers the qubits are part of are alternating. As this implies that two qubits of the same register are separated by three qubits of the other registers, these qubits often need to be “skipped”, which increases the depth and size of the circuit only by a constant factor though. Still, this makes the implementation of
\mathrm{QFT}^{(\varepsilon)}_{\mathrm{uni}}
substantially cheaper.
