Dietary supplementation of lysolecithin improves growth performance of piglets fed a low-nutrient density diet by modulating nutrient utilization and metabolism
Authors: Huaqi Song, Zhiming Zhang, Renjie Yao, Qiang Zhou, Yang Liu, Jin Jin, Junqi Jin, Zheng Luo, Jiayong Tang, Bin Feng, Shengyu Xu, Yan Lin, Lun Hua, Yong Zhuo, De Wu, Lianqiang Che
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reducing dietary nutrient density may lower the feed cost but compromise the growth performance in piglets. Given the ability of lysolecithin (LPI) to enhance nutrient utilization, its supplementation may help piglets adapt to a low-nutrient (LN) diet. This study aimed to investigate the effects of dietary LPI supplementation on growth performance, nutrient utilization, metabolism, and muscle development in piglets fed a LN diet. A total of 96 nursery piglets (17.0 ± 0.2 kg in initial body weight [BW] and 50 d of age) were randomly assigned to three groups in a randomized complete block design and received either the normal-nutrient diet (CON), a LN diet, or the LN diet supplemented with 400 mg/kg lysolecithin (LN-LPI) for 28 d. Each dietary treatment included 8 replicates of 4 piglets each. Results showed that the LN-LPI diet improved the average daily gain (ADG; P = 0.040) during the first two weeks, feed-to-gain ratio (F:G; P = 0.032), and BW (P = 0.065) throughout the experimental period, reaching levels comparable to those of piglets in the CON group. Compared with the LN diet, the growth-promoting effect of LPI may be attributed to its role in increasing the digestibility of ether extract (EE; P < 0.001) and gross energy (GE; P = 0.022) in diets, as well as increasing the relative weight of psoas major muscles (PMM) and the EE (P = 0.049) and crude protein (CP) content (P = 0.007) in muscle. Accordingly, compared with the LN diet, LPI supplementation decreased the blood urea nitrogen (P = 0.003) and total cholesterol (TC) concentrations (P = 0.017), and increased total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD) levels in plasma (P = 0.005) and liver (P = 0.006), along with the lower malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in blood (P < 0.001) and liver (P = 0.003). In addition, microarray analysis indicated that dietary LPI supplementation promoted expression of lipid synthesis and transport-related genes in the liver (FAS, P = 0.040; FABP5, P = 0.001; and SLC27A6, P = 0.025), and amino acid (AA) transport and synthesis-related genes (LAT, P = 0.021; LAMC3, P = 0.004), compared with the LN diet in muscle. In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the LN-LPI diet mitigated the compromised growth performance commonly observed under nutrient restriction, potentially mediated by improving nutrient utilization and redox status, and modulating lipid and protein metabolism.

Introduction

A sufficient nutrient supply is essential for optimal growth performance and health of piglets. However, feed costs continue to increase due to the rising prices of feed ingredients. To address this problem, one strategy is to reduce the nutrient levels of the feed (Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2021), especially the energy and protein, which account for the largest portion of feed costs (Hussain et al., 2015). Of note, previous research has demonstrated that reducing dietary nutrient levels decreases nutrient deposition, thereby compromising the growth performance of piglets (Dong et al., 2020; Ishida et al., 2023). Given these concerns, the balance must be struck between the high cost of feed and the nutrient requirements for piglets.

Lysolecithin (LPI) has been reported to improve the growth performance of weaned piglets via enhancing nutrient absorption and lipid metabolism (Liu et al., 2023). Following hydrolysis by phospholipase, which cleaves one fatty acid from lecithin, LPI acquires hydrophilic characteristics and demonstrates improved emulsification properties, thereby enhancing the efficiency of fat digestion (Weng et al., 2022). After hydrolysis by phospholipase, which removes one fatty acid from lecithin, LPI exhibits hydrophilicity and emulsification properties, and consequently improves the digestion efficiency of fats (Bhattarai et al., 2022; Cloft et al., 2021). Lysolecithin contains mixtures of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE), and lysophosphatidylinositol (Lyso-PI) (Liu et al., 2023). Among them, dietary supplementation with LPC has been demonstrated to improve fat digestibility, augment the absorption of amino acids (AAs) and cholesterol, and up-regulate the expression of genes associated with nutrient transport (Allahyari-Bake and Jahanian, 2017). The Lyso-PI has been demonstrated to stimulate the mTOR (the full names of the abbreviations of proteins can be found in Table S1) signaling pathway by activating Akt, thus promoting protein synthesis and muscle growth (Menon et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2022). In addition, LPE promotes lipid droplet formation and modifies the triacylglycerol (TAG) profiles (Yamamoto et al., 2022), and LPA has promoted the proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells and maintained the balance among the gut-associated microbiome (Jang et al., 2022).

As such, it is reasonable to hypothesize that LPI could be incorporated into diets with reduced nutrient levels for weaned piglets, thereby mitigating the effects of nutrient insufficiency. In this study, a low-nutrient (LN) diet was used to investigate the effect of dietary LPI supplementation on nutrient availability, protein and lipid metabolism, and redox status in weaned piglets. The findings of this study may provide a potential avenue for balancing feed cost with the nutritional requirements of piglets.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Sichuan Agricultural University (SICAU 2023314113). All institutional and national regulations governing laboratory animal care and utilization were strictly followed. The animal experiment was performed in accordance with the National Research Council’s Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Animals and dietary treatments

A total of 96 Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire (DLY) piglets (50 d of age) with an average body weight (BW) of 17.0 ± 0.2 kg were allocated into three dietary groups in a randomized complete block design, with BW and sex as blocking factors. Each dietary treatment was replicated eight times, with four piglets per replicate. The piglets either received the normal-nutrient diet (CON), the LN diet or the LN diet supplemented with 400 mg/kg LPI (LN-LPI) for a period of 28 d. The LPI complex used in this study was provided by Kemin (China) Technologies Co., Ltd. (Zhuhai, Guangdong, China), containing LPC ≥ 2.5%, Lyos-PI ≥ 1.2%, LPE ≥ 1.2%, and LPA ≥ 0.5%, with corn and soy oil as the carriers, and the optimal dosage was determined and recommended by the manufacturer according to the product’s properties and existing application data. The CON diet was formulated according to the nutrient requirements for nursery piglets, as estimated by the NRC (2012). The LN diet had 2% reduction in gross energy (GE) and 7% reduction in crude protein (CP), compared with the CON diet (Table 1). The ambient temperature was controlled between 24 and 25 °C, and piglets had ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the experiment. Sample collection

To determine nutrient digestibility in piglets, 0.3% chromic oxide (Cr2O3) was incorporated into the diets as an indigestible marker during the fourth week of the experiment. After a 4-d adaptation period, fresh fecal samples were collected from piglets during the final three days and pooled as representative fecal samples. After the 4-week dietary intervention, individual piglet weights were recorded at 06:00 after an overnight fast. The piglet with the BW closest to the average BW of each pen was selected for sampling and 10 mL blood samples were collected from the jugular vein into sodium heparin tubes. The blood samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min to separate the plasma, which was then stored at −20 °C. After blood sampling, the piglets were administered an intramuscular injection of anesthetic (Su Mianling II, 0.1 mL/kg BW; Animal Disease Laboratory, Beijing, China) and were subsequently euthanized. The ileal tissue samples of approximately 4 cm in length were opened longitudinally, washed with physiological saline to remove chyme, snap frozen and stored immediately in liquid nitrogen. Samples of the partial right-side longissimus dorsi muscles (LDM), right psoas major muscles (PMM), and liver tissue were also collected and stored in liquid nitrogen.

Chemical analysis and muscle nutrient composition

During the digestion trial, fecal, diet, and muscle tissue samples were collected, freeze-dried, equilibrated to room humidity, and then ground through a 0.42-mm screen. These samples were analyzed for dry matter (DM) following the methods outlined by method 930.15 (AOAC, 2007). Crude protein was analyzed by Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (B-877 Titrino Plus, Buchi Labortechnik AG, Flawil, Switzerland) according to method 984.13. Amino acid, except for tryptophan, were analyzed after a 24-h acidolysis using an automatic AA analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) according to method 982.30. Gross energy was analyzed with an automatic adiabatic oxygen bomb calorimeter (6400, Parr Instrument, Moline, IL, USA) according to GB/T 45104-2024 (China National Standard, 2024) and Cr2O3 was quantified via flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry (CONTAA 700, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) according to GB/T 13088-2006 (China National Standard, 2006). The apparent total tract digestibility (ATTD) was calculated according to the following equation:ATTDNutrient=1-(CrFeed×NutrientFeces)/(CrFeces×NutrientFeed).

Determination of plasma metabolites

After thawing frozen plasma samples on ice and centrifuging them at 3000 × g for 5 min, 200 μL the supernatant was collected to determine the concentrations of total cholesterol (TC; CH0101152), high-density lipoprotein (HDL; RB30412), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA; RB30951), triglycerides (TG; CH0101151), and urea (CH0101051) using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Model 7020, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The assay kits for TC, TG, and urea were purchased from Maker Biotechnology Inc. (Chengdu, Sichuan, China), whereas the assay kits for NEFA and HDL were obtained from Beijing Century Ward Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Oxidative stress parameters

Protein concentration of liver samples was determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein kit (A045-3, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Activities of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD; A015-1), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A001-1), malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1), and reduced glutathione (GSH; A006-2-1) in plasma and liver samples (supernatant) were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, according to the manufacturers’ instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) with a microplate reader (SpectraMax 9, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

The relative expression levels of genes in the ileum, liver, and muscle tissues of piglets were determined by RT-qPCR following a previously reported protocol (Hussain et al., 2025). The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were determined by nucleic acid analyzer (Beckman DU-800, Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA). Reverse transcription of RNA and RT-qPCR were performed by PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser and TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (RR820 A/B, Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan), respectively. The total PCR reaction system was 10 μL, consisting of 5.0 μL TB Green Premix Ex TaqII, 1.0 μL forward primer, 1.0 μL reverse primer, 0.2 μL ROX reference dye II and 2.8 μL cDNA. The PCR thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 30 s (annealing and extension). β-Actin was treated as a housekeeping gene to normalize the expression of target genes according to the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in Table S1. The PCR primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Transcriptome analysis

After total RNA extraction from liver and PMM tissues, eukaryotic mRNA was enriched using oligo (dT) magnetic beads. The PMM was selected for transcriptome analysis because recent multi-omics studies have characterized it as a “representative red muscle,” whose fiber-type composition and unique AA/lipid metabolic network reflect whole-body muscle development and pork quality more faithfully than other muscles (Yang et al., 2025). The enriched mRNA was fragmented and reverse-transcribed into double-stranded complementary DNA (cDNA) by a reverse transcription system. The cDNA was purified with a QiaQuick PCR Purification Kit (28104, QIAGEN GmbH, Hilden, Nordrhein-Westfalen, Germany), and Illumina sequencing adapters were ligated. RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeqTM 2500 platform (HiSeq 2500, Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The library preparation and sequencing analysis were done by Guangzhou Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). After sequencing, raw data were filtered, and quality control, assembly, and alignment to the pig reference genome were conducted. Gene expression levels were calculated using the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) method. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with a fold change ≥ 2 and P < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were used to explore DEGs’ functions between groups. This methodology has been validated and reported in studies (Ma et al., 2022).

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test procedures of SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) were used to evaluate the variance homogeneity and normality, respectively. For growth performance and nutrient digestibility, pens served as the experimental unit, and data were reported as the mean of the pen. Data was analyzed using a linear mixed model with the dietary treatment as fixed effect and initial BW as random effect, using the following statistical model:Y=μ+αi+υj+εij,where Y is the parameter to be tested; μ is the overall mean; αi is the fixed effect of diet (i = 1, 2); υj is the random effect of BW and sex; and εij is the residual error. The results are presented as means and standard error of the mean (SEM). A significant difference is considered at P < 0.05, with a tendency noted for 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10.

Results

Growth performance

From d 1 to 7 and d 8 to 14, the ADG of piglets in the LN group was lower than that in the CON and LN-LPI groups (P = 0.026 and P = 0.040, respectively; Table 2), with a trend toward lower ADG over the entire experimental period (P = 0.085). In addition, the F:G of the LN group was higher than that in the CON group during the entire period (P = 0.032). Accordingly, BWs of piglets in the LN group were lower on d 8, 15, and 22 compared with those in the CON and LN-LPI groups (P = 0.022, P = 0.006 and P = 0.028, respectively, on d 7, 14, and 21), with a decreasing trend on d 28 (P = 0.065). Discussion

Reducing dietary nutrient levels results in an insufficient nutrient supply, which compromises the growth performance of piglets (Grove et al., 2024; Law et al., 2019). In this study, however, the addition of LPI to the LN diet improved growth performance, nutrient deposition, redox status, and metabolism in piglets. These findings suggest that LPI supplementation mitigates the adverse effects of LN intake by improving nutrient utilization.

In this study, the LN diet was formulated to have 2% reduction in GE and 7% reduction in CP as compared with the CON diet. Notably, during the initial 2-week period, the relatively lower ADG (−21% and −93 g/d) was observed in LN piglets compared with the CON group. Although the LN group showed a transient increase in growth during 3 weeks, likely due to increased feed intake following dietary adaptation, ADG declined again after d 21, presumably as a consequence of insufficient nutrient supply. In addition, the LN diet also decreased the nutrient digestibility. The underlying reasons for the reduced feed intake and nutrient digestibility induced by the LN diet remain unclear. Lysophosphatidylcholine can affect mucosal barrier function by increasing membrane permeability, which impacts nutrient transport and absorption (Allahyari-Bake and Jahanian, 2017). Despite this, the considerable decreases in GE and CP intake in the LN piglets likely contributed to the poor growth performance. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the decreased growth performance and nutrient digestibility were largely reversed by LPI supplementation. Previous study support that LPI improved feed intake and nutrient absorption of weaned piglets (Liu et al., 2023). As the byproduct of protein degradation, urea reflects AA metabolism and protein turnover (Schutz, 2011). In this study, dietary LPI supplementation decreased plasma urea concentration, indicating improved AA utilization for protein synthesis. These results suggest that LPI might improve the growth performance of LN piglets by increasing nutrient availability.

The LPI has been identified as an agonist of GPR55, which modulates lipid metabolism and LPE has been found to promote lipid droplet formation and modify the TAG profiles (Moreno-Navarrete et al., 2012; Yamamoto et al., 2022). In this study, therefore, the effects of LPI supplementation on lipid metabolism were explored. Dietary LPI supplementation increased the mRNA expression of FAS, which catalyzed fatty acid synthesis. In contrast, the genes (FABP1, PPARGC1A, and SIRT1) related to lipolysis and energy consumption were inhibited by LPI. In addition, the plasma TC level reflects lipid metabolism (Talbot et al., 2018), and the lowered TC level in the LN-LPI group further confirms that LPI supplementation activated lipid anabolism while inhibiting lipid catabolism in weaned piglets, thereby promoting BW gain.

To further elucidate the effects of LPI on lipid metabolism, the transcriptomic profiling of liver tissue was conducted. The PPAR-γ signaling pathway was found to be enriched. As one of the key sensors and regulators in lipid metabolism, the PPAR-γ signaling pathway is responsible for promoting energy storage by enhancing lipogenesis and adipogenesis (Changizi et al., 2023). Within this signaling pathway, the markedly regulated PPAR-γ genes, such as FABP5, FAS, and CPT2, play important roles. Among them, FABP5 is an intracellular lipid-binding protein that facilitates the transport of fatty acids to PPAR-γ, thereby participating in lipid metabolism and signal transduction (O’Sullivan and Kaczocha, 2020). Meanwhile, CPT2 and CYP4A24 regulate fatty acid oxidation (Smith et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2020). Lysolecithin supplementation significantly increased hepatic mRNA expression of FABP5 and FAS, whereas it decreased the expression of CPT2 and CYP4A24, further demonstrating a shift toward enhanced lipid anabolism and reduced catabolism in the liver. However, excessive lipid synthesis might lead to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. The SLC25A47 is involved in regulating the balance between lipid synthesis and oxidation. This study found that the mRNA expression of SLC25A47 was significantly increased by LPI supplementation in the LN diet, indicating that the addition of LPI could prevent excessive fatty acid accumulation, consistent with a previous study (Cheng et al., 2023). Lipids are converted into TG and very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) through anabolic pathways, and then transported via apolipoproteins to peripheral tissues (Sun et al., 2020; Yiu et al., 2020). Validation of these effects was supported by higher EE content in the muscle of the LN-LPI piglets, indicating enhanced energy storage and availability to support growth in piglets.

The growth of skeletal muscle is associated with nutrient intake and utilization (Blum et al., 2021). In this study, piglets fed the LN diet had a lower relative weight of PMM, along with 36% lower deposition of nutrients (CP, DM, and EE) in muscle, indicating that an insufficient nutrient supply compromised muscle development. To validate, CCL2, the gene that inhibits protein synthesis via inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway (Luciano-Mateo et al., 2020), was increased in the LN group. However, LPA has activated LPA1-6 GPCRs to regulate various physiological and pathological processes and has thereby promoted cell-cycle progression and inhibited apoptosis (Wang et al., 2020). Lysolecithin supplementation reversed the impaired muscle development observed in piglets fed the LN diet, as evidenced by the increased relative weight of PMM and nutrient deposition. The GO enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs were mainly linked to cytokine-mediated signaling, with enrichment in chemokine and cytokine activity as well as myofibril structures. This suggests that LPI may enhance myofiber development and organization via receptor-dependent pathways, as indicated by differential gene expression. In addition, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway was significantly enriched. And LPI supplementation decreased the mRNA expression of CCL2, the expression of LAT, SLC5A1, LAMC3, and FHL1 were upregulated by LPI supplementation. LAT and SLC5A1 facilitate AA uptake and utilization, while LAMC3 and FHL1 regulate cell proliferation and differentiation (Eve and Smith, 2017; Nguyen et al., 2020; Kurtyka et al., 2024). Growth factor-activated PI3K-Akt signaling pathway regulates AAs transport through LAT (Zhang et al., 2019). Taken together, these findings suggest that LPI supplementation improved nitrogen-use efficiency, directing a greater proportion of AAs toward utilization and deposition in muscle and thereby enhancing muscle development, a finding consistent with the observed reduction in blood urea concentration.

Oxidative stress results from the imbalance between pro-oxidant systems, typically represented by reactive oxygen species (ROS), and antioxidant defenses (Sies et al., 2017), which negatively affect the growth performance of piglets. As a major product of ROS-induced lipid peroxidation, MDA was considered as a marker of oxidative damage (Cordiano et al., 2023). Conversely, T-AOC, T-SOD, and GSH were important antioxidant indicators that scavenged ROS (Pedone et al., 2020). However, there is currently no direct evidence that LPI exerts an influence on the redox status of organisms. Its observed effects were likely mediated indirectly through the regulation of lipid metabolism and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis, which may collectively enhance the antioxidant capacity (Wang et al., 2025). In this study, LPI supplementation in the LN diet consistently increased T-SOD activity but decreased MDA concentration, indicating its role in maintaining redox homeostasis.

Conclusion

The LN-LPI diet improved muscle development and growth performance in piglets, mitigating the growth deficits caused by nutrient restriction. These effects were associated with enhanced lipid and protein metabolism, increased digestibility of energy and fat, and improved antioxidant capacity (Fig. S1). Overall, dietary LPI effectively supports growth and metabolic adaptation in piglets fed LN diets, offering a practical strategy to optimize the efficiency of LN feeding.
