A hierarchical multi‑timescale reinforcement learning framework for hybrid energy storage management with aging‑aware constraint adaptation
Authors: Mohsen Mohammadbeigi, Alireza Rezazadeh, Mostafa Sedighizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hybrid energy storage systems (HESS), combining batteries and supercapacitors, offer an effective solution for simultaneously addressing energy and power demands in modern energy systems. However, the performance and lifetime of these systems are strongly dependent on the employed energy management strategy, which must balance fast power regulation, safety enforcement, and long-term battery health. Existing reinforcement learning–based approaches often adopt flat control architectures that entangle short-term decision-making with long-term aging effects, leading to training instability, limited interpretability, and non‑stationary reward structures. This paper proposes a hierarchical multi‑timescale reinforcement learning framework for energy management of battery–supercapacitor hybrid systems with explicit aging‑aware constraint adaptation. The proposed architecture decomposes the control problem into three coordinated layers: a fast safety‑critical layer that enforces hard operational constraints, an intermediate reinforcement learning layer based on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for real‑time power allocation, and a slow supervisory layer that estimates battery state‑of‑health (SOH) using a Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) aging model. Instead of directly embedding aging costs into the learning objective, battery degradation is incorporated through SOH‑dependent adaptive current limits, ensuring quasi‑stationary learning dynamics and safe long‑term operation. Comprehensive simulation studies are conducted under nominal, progressive aging, and safety‑critical operating conditions. Under nominal SOH, the proposed framework maintains battery and supercapacitor SOC deviations within ± 3% of their reference values while keeping battery current below the nominal 125 A limit. During aging progression, the method achieves a lower time‑averaged squared battery current and reduced cumulative energy throughput compared to reward‑based SAC and flat RL baselines, resulting in a measurably slower SOH decline for equivalent stress levels. Moreover, under aggressive pulsed loads at reduced SOH, the proposed approach yields the lowest constraint activation frequency, demonstrating superior intrinsic safety and robustness. Overall, the results confirm that hierarchical time‑scale separation enables stable, aging‑aware, and physically consistent energy management without compromising learning performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54366-7.

Introduction

Hybrid Energy Storage Systems (HESS), typically combining batteries and supercapacitors, have become an essential component in modern energy systems due to their ability to simultaneously address high energy demand and rapid power fluctuations1. Batteries offer high energy density suitable for continuous energy delivery, while supercapacitors give high power density and rapid dynamic response. These features enable HESS to improve the efficiency, quality, and flexibility of the power system in a variety of applications, such as electric vehicles, renewable energy integration, and microgrids2.

The performance of a HESS is determined by the EMS which controls the power distribution among the energy storage components for different load conditions3. The EMS must be able to weigh several conflicting criteria such as the SOC stabilization, battery stress, safety of operation and system lifetime4. As systems increase in complexity and uncertainty, designing EMSs to adapt to different system operating conditions becomes more of a challenge.

Standard EMS methodologies for HESS have been thoroughly examined in the scholarly literature. Rule-based strategies are extensively employed owing to their straightforwardness and minimal computational requirements; nonetheless, their effectiveness is heavily reliant on heuristic development and they exhibit limited flexibility in adapting to evolving operational environments5. Optimization-based methodologies, including model predictive control and equivalent consumption minimization strategies, offer improved performance through explicit consideration of system models and constraints; however, they generally require accurate system modelling and significant computational capacity6. Furthermore, many of these methodologies primarily focus on short-term performance metrics and consider battery degradation as a secondary or subsidiary issue.

Battery degeneration is a progressive, cumulative, and nonlinear process that is significantly affected by operating parameters including current magnitude, depth of discharge, temperature, and cycling protocols7. Ignoring ageing effects in real-time energy management can lead to increased battery stress and premature degradation, thereby compromising system reliability and economic viability. Although many studies have aimed to incorporate ageing awareness into EMS design, most existing approaches rely on simplified degradation models or include ageing as an auxiliary optimization criterion, which consequently elevates computational complexity and limits scalability8. Therefore, the effective incorporation of long-term battery health considerations into real-time control continues to constitute an unresolved research challenge.

Simultaneously, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising data-driven methodology for energy management, owing to its capacity to derive control policies directly from interactions between the system and its environment. RL-based EMSs can handle nonlinear dynamics and uncertain operating conditions without requiring explicit system models9. Nevertheless, the majority of existing RL approaches for HESS adopt a single-layer control structure, in which the learning agent is simultaneously responsible for short-term power allocation, constraint handling, and long-term performance optimization. This monolithic formulation often leads to limited training stability, reduced interpretability, and potential safety concerns, particularly when battery aging and health-related constraints are involved.

A key observation is that HESS operation inherently exhibits a multi-timescale nature. Power balancing and safety constraints must be enforced at fast time scales, energy management decisions evolve at intermediate time scales, and battery aging progresses over much longer horizons. Treating these processes within a unified decision-making layer neglects their distinct temporal characteristics and complicates the learning problem. This motivates the development of hierarchical control frameworks that explicitly decompose the energy management task across multiple time scales, enabling safer, more scalable, and more interpretable learning-based solutions.

Motivated by these challenges, this paper proposes a hierarchical multi-timescale reinforcement learning framework for energy management of hybrid energy storage systems. The proposed approach separates fast safety-critical constraint enforcement, learning-based power allocation, and slow aging-aware adaptation into coordinated control layers. By embedding battery health considerations into the control architecture through adaptive operational constraints, the proposed framework enables aging-aware decision-making without directly burdening the reinforcement learning agent with long-term degradation modeling.

Related works

Hybrid energy storage systems (HESS) and their management have been thoroughly examined through optimization and rule-based methodologies. Shrivastav and Dutta (2025) employ a slime-mould evolutionary algorithm to optimize the multi-objective sizing of a hybrid microgrid, which includes renewable energy sources, diesel generation, and electric vehicles with V2G capabilities, illustrating Pareto-optimal trade-offs among cost, emissions, and reliability10. Manojkumar et al. (2025) formulate a PSO-optimized rule-based Energy Management Strategy for an AC/DC hybrid microgrid featuring photovoltaic and battery storage, integrating demand response and adaptive battery management strategies; their controller achieves significant cost reductions and peak shaving efficacy under real-time pricing conditions11. These works illustrate how conventional EMS (rule-based or offline optimization) can effectively coordinate HESS and loads, though they rely on accurate models or heuristic tuning. By contrast, recent studies have turned to reinforcement learning (RL) for adaptive, model-free EMS. Li and Or (2024) propose a tri-level framework for an urban rail microgrid with PV and a hybrid battery–compressed-air storage: the top two layers perform day-ahead and intra-day scheduling, while a multi-agent deep RL scheme at the bottom layer dispatches storage in real time. In their scheme, each agent controls a local storage element and cooperates via a shared reward to achieve coordinated power dispatch12. Darshi et al. (2023) similarly design a fully decentralized multi-agent RL for a community microgrid: each distributed energy resource or consumer learns its own strategy in a shared environment, and the agents’ policies converge to a cooperative equilibrium that maximizes overall utility13. In another example, Xiong et al. (2025) applies proximal policy optimization (PPO) in a microgrid with renewables and EVs. Their RL agent learns real-time charge/discharge schedules that increase self-balancing and profit (notably a ~ 23% increase in profit) compared to rule-based baselines14. Su et al. (2024) integrate RL and optimization for an electric vehicle battery-swapping station: an upper-level DRL agent decides the station’s total charging power, while a lower-level mathematical optimizer allocates power to individual batteries. This hierarchical RL–optimization cascade improves station profit under price and demand uncertainty15. These multi-agents and hybrid-RL schemes highlight the flexibility of RL in complex HESS EMS tasks, enabling online adaptation without pre-specified rules.

The study by Nazim et al.16 offers a comprehensive review of artificial intelligence (AI)-based methods for estimating the State of Health (SOH) and Remaining Useful Life (RUL) of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). It discusses a variety of AI techniques, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), and their application in battery health management. The review focuses on optimization strategies, such as feature selection and parameter tuning, to improve the accuracy of SOH and RUL predictions. Key challenges include the high non-linearity of battery degradation and the integration of real-world datasets. The paper also highlights advancements in deep learning models and the role of data-driven methods in overcoming the limitations of traditional models in battery health estimation.

Another study by Nazim et al.17 introduces a novel method for accurately estimating the State of Charge (SOC) of lithium-ion batteries across a wide temperature range, including extreme cold conditions. It proposes a fully entangled temporal convolutional network (FE-TCN), which integrates multiple data paths to capture complex interactions and dependencies. The model is optimized using particle swarm optimization (PSO) and is validated across varying temperature ranges and battery conditions. The FE-TCN significantly improves SOC estimation accuracy, particularly under low-temperature conditions where traditional methods often fail. This model addresses key challenges such as temperature-induced fluctuations and the nonlinear behavior of batteries during operation.

The paper by Tao et al.18 introduces a generative learning-assisted framework for State-of-Health (SOH) estimation of retired lithium-ion batteries under random retirement conditions. The approach uses pulse voltage response data to address data scarcity and heterogeneity challenges, achieving accurate SOH estimations with reduced data requirements, making it ideal for sustainable battery recycling.

The study by Huang et al.19 presents a multi-scale data decomposition and fusion framework for robust SOH estimation, tested across 145 cells with 6 different chemistries and 14 operational conditions. By leveraging data from field sensors, the model demonstrates superior generalizability and reliability in diverse battery types, improving SOH predictions under varying real-world conditions.

Another work by Huanget al.20 proposes the IC2ML framework that integrates State-of-Health (SOH), degradation trajectory, and remaining useful life (RUL) predictions using intra-cycle and inter-cycle enhanced machine learning. It leverages time-series voltage data and attention mechanisms to enhance the precision of battery health management across multiple tasks.

The paper by Liang et al.21 work applies quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNN) for accurate SOH estimation of lithium-ion batteries, particularly under stochastic and noisy data conditions. It demonstrates how quantum machine learning can improve estimation accuracy with minimal data, reducing computational complexity and offering significant potential for cloud-based battery management systems.

Hierarchical and multi-timescale control frameworks have also been investigated. Bustos et al. (2023) offer a two-tier MPC-based EMS for the coordinated operation of multiple microgrids, incorporating demand-side control at both local and network levels. This method significantly enhanced supply reliability and approximately reduced storage cycling by 50% compared to flat controllers22. Kumar et al. (2025) devise a two-layer deep reinforcement learning controller for a DC microgrid incorporating battery, supercapacitor, and hydrogen storage systems. The senior agent efficiently splits power among the storage units, while local controllers supervise device handoff. This hierarchical RL scheme reduces DC-bus overshoot and protects expensive components (e.g. fuel-cell stacks) by enforcing ramp limits23. These examples underscore the benefit of layered control: separating scheduling and fast control across timescales allows RL-based EMS to manage HESS more effectively.

Several works target specific HESS configurations and objectives. Shuvo et al. (2025) design a fuzzy-logic EMS for a PV-powered DC microgrid with batteries and supercapacitors. Their controller uses fuzzy rules and adaptive load management so that the supercapacitor handles transients and the battery supplies steady power, which smooths voltage and extends overall system life2. Hosseini et al. (2025) apply TD3 (an off-policy actor-critic) to a microgrid with PV and wind generation and multiple battery units. The DRL agent learns to split power among batteries considering efficiency and aging; compared to fuzzy or PSO-based schedulers, it reduces voltage deviation standard deviation by up to 21%24. Ye et al. (2024) target an integrated electricity–heat–hydrogen system: their DDPG-based EMS co-optimizes power, heat, and hydrogen flows (with demand response) and achieves large cost savings (e.g. 16.0% cost reduction with load curtailment) under variability25. Zhu et al. (2025) investigate an islanded microgrid distinguished by significant incorporation of renewable energy sources and a hybrid energy storage technology that integrates batteries and hydrogen. Their extensive multi-stage reinforcement learning architecture divides decision-making into day-ahead optimization of hydrogen production and two tiers of intra-day reinforcement learning modifications for the battery, leading to reduced costs and emissions across multiple scenarios26. Sage et al. (2025) utilize deep reinforcement learning to simultaneously control gas turbines and batteries within a renewable microgrid, integrating turbine ramping and degradation considerations into their model; the resulting policy minimizes unwarranted turbine activations and lowers maintenance expenses compared to conventional optimization approaches27. These examples illustrate the adaptability of RL-based EMS in overseeing diverse storage assets and multi-energy systems.

Aging-aware energy management remains an important area of ongoing research. As indicated in recent evaluations, battery management systems must consistently adjust operating conditions according to the state-of-health (SOH) to mitigate degradation28. Dorronsoro et al. (2025) demonstrate this by integrating a nonlinear aging-rate model into an MPC cost function, dynamically adjusting the weighting of degradation; their aging-aware MPC significantly decreases battery degradation while maintaining cost efficiency29. Farakhor et al. (2025) examine second-life EV batteries by incorporating heterogeneous capacity degradation costs within an economic EMS, aiming to optimize profitability throughout the battery’s lifecycle30. Reinforcement Learning approaches have also been employed. Nishat et al. (2025) design a deep reinforcement learning agent to coordinate the operation of IoT sensor nodes; by balancing duty cycles among nodes, this approach preserves battery health and prolongs the network’s operational lifespan31. Salem et al. (2025) combine LSTM with reinforcement learning (PPO) for state of health (SOH) estimation; the RL agent adaptively modifies the LSTM’s predictions to adhere to physical constraints, thereby improving the accuracy of long-term SOH evaluations32. Cao et al. (2025) develop a multi-agent reinforcement learning controller for a fuel-cell hybrid bus, simultaneously regulating speed (adaptive cruise) and power distribution; importantly, they incorporate fuel-cell and battery longevity into the reward structure, thereby minimizing component stress and overall operational expenses33. In a more direct RL example, Cai et al. (2025) present a DQN-based EMS for grid peak shaving: they explicitly limit the number of battery charge/discharge cycles, which shrinks grid power variance and cuts cycle count by 50–67%, thus slowing aging34. Collectively, these works highlight the trend of incorporating SOH and lifespan considerations into EMS design.

Recent innovations in learning-based EMS continue to emerge. Shen et al. (2024) introduce meta-reinforcement learning to microgrid dispatch: using MAML, their agent quickly adapts to new conditions, yielding better energy utilization and cost reduction in early operation without extensive retraining35. Cestero et al. (2025) use physics-informed neural network (PINN) surrogates to accelerate smart-grid RL: by replacing expensive simulators with PINNs, they cut RL training time by ~ 50% while retaining solution quality36. Anagónó-Alvarado et al. (2025) provide full overview on reinforcement learning techniques for energy management of electric car in particular, and manage to cover several algorithms including Q-learning, DDPG, TD3, SAC etc., as well as the challenges involved in their practical implementation9. Other forms of hierarchical reinforcement learning is studied under the transportation domain: for instance, Lan et al. (2022) combine SAC and DQN in a hierarchical EMS of a hybrid electric bus that optimizes the continuous torque command and discrete gear shifting command simultaneously for better fuel economy37, and Billah et al. (2025) propose a decentralized multi-agent control framework based on an interconnected neighborhood of microgrids with PV, wind, diesel, and storage that learn to balance supply and demand in changing conditions38.

Materials and methods

This section delineates the fundamental methodological ideas that support the proposed energy management paradigm. It outlines the system components, mathematical models, and control strategies utilized to define the hybrid battery-supercapacitor energy storage system, as well as the aging-aware techniques incorporated to mitigate long-term degradation issues. This section clarifies the hierarchical multi-timescale control methodology and its implementation through reinforcement learning, creating a solid and reproducible basis for future evaluations and comparative studies.

System description and problem formulation

This subsection provides a comprehensive overview of the hybrid energy storage system and outlines the associated energy management challenge. The physical design of the battery, supercapacitor, and load is initially presented, succeeded by the dynamic models and operational constraints that regulate their interaction. Based on these elements, the energy management task is mathematically formulated as a constrained decision‑making problem that accounts for short‑term power balance, device‑level safety, and long‑term performance objectives under multi‑timescale dynamics.

Hybrid energy storage system architecture

The analyzed system is a Hybrid Energy Storage System (HESS) comprising a battery unit, a supercapacitor unit, and an external load. The two storage devices are linked to a shared DC bus and collaboratively fulfill the load demand through an energy management system that governs power allocation between them. This architecture enables the system to leverage the synergistic properties of batteries and supercapacitors while maintaining operational flexibility and safety.

The battery functions as the principal energy source, supplying both average and low-frequency elements of load demand owing to its high energy density. Conversely, the supercapacitor is engineered to manage high-frequency and transient power variations, leveraging its elevated power density and swift charge-discharge proficiency. The load represents a unified power demand profile encompassing both anticipated and stochastic changes, mirroring actual operational situations experienced in practical applications like electric vehicles or grid-connected energy systems.

The battery subsystem is characterized by a high energy density and is designed to deliver sustained power over extended periods. The electrochemical characteristics of a battery render it susceptible to operational parameters including current magnitude, depth of discharge, and cycling behavior. Excessive current draw or deep discharge cycling can substantially accelerate battery degradation, thereby diminishing its operational lifespan. To ensure safe and dependable functionality, the battery is governed by operational constraints, encompassing limitations on current, power, and state-of-charge (SOC). Crucially, these limitations are dynamic and contingent upon the battery’s state-of-health (SOH), which changes progressively as degradation accrues over time. This correlation underscores a direct link between the long-term consequences of aging and short-term operational judgments.

The supercapacitor subsystem enhances the battery by providing high power density and swift dynamic response. In contrast to batteries, supercapacitors can withstand multiple charge-discharge cycles with low degradation, making them ideal for handling rapid load variations and peak power demands. The supercapacitor functions within its SOC and current boundaries, which are generally less restrictive than those of the battery. The supercapacitor mitigates rapid power fluctuations, thereby alleviating stress on the battery and indirectly prolonging its operational lifespan. Within the proposed system, the supercapacitor functions in conjunction with the battery within the hierarchical energy management framework.

The load is the energy demand profile imposed on the HESS from the external environment, which is a time-varying signal made of low and high frequency components. This representation well defines the conditions of use: a continuous energy demand and a power demand that occurs only periodically. From the point of view of the management system, the load is an external disturbance that needs to be satisfied with a certain level of reliability. The energy management system’s task is to optimally distribute the power of the battery and the supercapacitor with respect to the system’s operating constraints and long-term performance objectives.

Battery aging and SOH modeling

The deterioration of batteries significantly impacts the long-term effectiveness, safety, and financial feasibility of hybrid energy storage systems. Unlike instantaneous electrical dynamics, battery degradation occurs gradually over extended periods and is notably affected by accumulated stresses such as current intensity, depth of discharge, temperature, and cycle patterns. Accurately measuring these impacts and integrating them into energy management poses a considerable challenge due to their nonlinear and multi-timescale nature.

This study directly investigates battery degradation through the state-of-health (SOH) metric, which measures the remaining usable capacity and power capabilities of the battery in relation to its normal condition. The SOH is integrated into the control system as a gradually changing factor that affects operational constraints, connecting long-term degradation to instant decision-making.

Hierarchical multi-timescale control framework

Hybrid energy storage management inherently involves decision‑making processes that evolve over distinct and widely separated time scales, ranging from fast safety‑critical power regulation to long‑term degradation dynamics. Effectively coordinating these processes requires a control architecture that not only respects their temporal characteristics but also enables structured interaction among them without compromising stability or safety. To this end, this section introduces a hierarchical multi‑timescale control framework that systematically decomposes the energy management problem into layered decision mechanisms, each operating at an appropriate temporal resolution and functional scope.

Overview of the hierarchical architecture

Energy management in hybrid energy storage systems inherently involves processes evolving over widely separated time scales. Fast electrical dynamics and safety constraints operate at the level of individual seconds, while battery aging unfolds gradually over thousands of operating cycles. Treating all these phenomena within a single-layer control or learning mechanism often leads to poor scalability, unsafe behavior, or slow convergence.

To address this challenge, the proposed framework adopts a hierarchical multi-timescale control architecture that decomposes the overall EMS into three coordinated layers, each operating at a distinct temporal resolution and functional role.

The hierarchical structure consists of the following layers:

Level 1- Fast safety-critical constraint layer: Enforces hard operational limits instantaneously to guarantee system safety and feasibility.Level 2- Reinforcement learning–based power allocation layer: Optimizes the power split between the battery and supercapacitor based on system states and load demand.Level 3- Aging-aware supervisory layer: Monitors long-term degradation indicators and adaptively updates battery constraints according to estimated SOH.

The proposed energy management framework explicitly exploits the inherent separation of time scales present in hybrid energy storage system operation. Power allocation decisions and safety-critical constraint enforcement are executed at the fastest control time scale, ensuring that power balance and all physical operating limits are satisfied at every control interval. At this level, the system responds instantaneously to load variations while guaranteeing strict compliance with current, power, and state-of-charge constraints for both storage devices.

At a slower, intermediate time scale, the reinforcement learning agent adapts its policy based on accumulated system interactions. Rather than reacting to individual instantaneous events, the learning process integrates state transitions and reward signals over time, allowing the agent to gradually improve its power-splitting strategy under varying operating conditions. This separation enables stable policy learning without interfering with fast constraint enforcement, as the agent’s decisions are always filtered through a safety-preserving control layer.

Battery aging evolution is addressed at the slowest time scale, reflecting the fundamentally cumulative nature of degradation processes. Instead of updating health-related states at every control step, degradation indicators are periodically estimated using stress-related metrics aggregated over extended operating intervals. The inferred state-of-health is then used to adapt battery operational limits, progressively shaping the admissible operating region in which power allocation decisions can be made.

The proposed architecture alleviates the destabilization of real-time control operations by separating rapid electrical dynamics, learning-based decision-making, and progressive aging processes. Simultaneously, battery health data is methodically integrated into the control loop via adaptive constraints, allowing long-term factors to affect short-term actions in a coherent and physically consistent way. The interaction across time scales is fundamental to the hierarchical structure of the proposed architecture, as the reinforcement learning agent functions inside an always evolving yet consistently secure viable region delineated by higher-level supervisory logic.

Level 1: Safety-critical constraint layer

This layer operates at the fastest time scale every control step and implements a deterministic, rule‑based clipping mechanism that ensures all power‑allocation commands generated by the higher‑level reinforcement‑learning agent remain within a feasible region defined by the instantaneous state of health (SOH) and the instantaneous state‑of‑charge (SOC) of both storage devices. By decoupling safety‑critical constraint satisfaction from the learning process, the framework avoids the risk of the RL agent exploring physically infeasible or damaging actions during training or deployment, thereby providing a robust safety net that is independent of the agent’s policy.

The RL layer operates strictly within the admissible region defined by the safety-critical constraint layer. Any action proposed by the RL agent that violates instantaneous operational limits is automatically reshaped into a feasible command, ensuring that the learning process never experiences unsafe system transitions.

Simultaneously, the reinforcement learning policy adaptively responds to the changing constraints resulting from battery degradation. With declining state of health (SOH) and increasingly restrictive allowable battery current limits, the agent inherently redirects power demand toward the supercapacitor, thereby mitigating battery stress without necessitating explicit retraining or alterations to the reward structure.

This indirect interplay between learning and aging dynamics constitutes a key characteristic of the proposed hierarchical design and is fundamental to attaining dependable, sustained energy management performance.

The proposed methodology markedly diverges from conventional energy management system approaches based on reinforcement learning. By segregating safety enforcement, learning-based optimization, and aging supervision across distinct layers and temporal scales, the framework mitigates common challenges including unsafe exploration, reward non-stationarity, and sluggish convergence. This hierarchical architecture allows the reinforcement learning agent to concentrate solely on performance enhancement, while higher-level control mechanisms guarantee physical plausibility and long-term operational integrity.

Level 3: Aging-aware supervisory layer

The third and most sophisticated level of the proposed hierarchical framework is an aging-aware supervisory layer responsible for monitoring long-term battery degradation and adjusting operational constraints accordingly. This layer functions at the slowest temporal scale and models degradation phenomena that develop over prolonged operational periods, including capacity decline and increases in internal resistance.

In contrast to Levels 1 and 2, which operate at each control step or learning iteration, the supervisory layer revises its internal estimates at regular intervals grounded on aggregated stress indicators. This time-scale separation encapsulates the inherently cumulative nature of battery aging and prevents the interference of sluggish health dynamics with rapid control and learning processes.

The main goal of the aging-aware supervisory layer is not to directly regulate power flows but to modify the feasible operational space of the energy management problem in a manner that considers component health. Instead of explicitly embedding degradation terms into the reinforcement learning reward function or state space, battery aging is incorporated indirectly via adaptive constraint adjustments.

This design philosophy is motivated by three key considerations:

Battery degradation signals are inherently noisy and evolve slowly, making them ill-suited for direct inclusion in fast RL loops.Explicit aging costs often introduce strong reward non-stationarity, which can destabilize policy learning.Physical battery protection is more naturally expressed as hard operational limits rather than soft penalties.

The supervisory layer does not intervene in real-time decision making. Instead, it acts as a slowly varying meta-controller that modifies the feasible action space within which the RL agent operates.

From the perspective of Level 2, battery aging manifests as an incremental modification of the system’s allowable dynamics rather than as an explicit state variable. Consequently, the reinforcement learning agent adaptively adjusts its policy by utilizing the supercapacitor more proactively as battery constraints become progressively more restrictive, without the need for retraining or explicit understanding of degradation mechanisms.

This unidirectional transfer of information from Level 3 to Levels 1 and 2 prevents feedback loops that could destabilize rapid control responses, while still allowing long-term health factors to affect immediate actions.

The aging-aware supervisory layer finalizes the hierarchical architecture of the proposed framework. By explicitly separating fast safety enforcement, learning-based power allocation, and slow degradation adaptation across distinct layers and time scales, the framework achieves a level of modularity and robustness that is difficult to attain with monolithic control or learning approaches.

Most importantly, this design allows battery aging mitigation to emerge as a structural property of the control architecture, rather than as an explicitly optimized objective. This distinction is central to the proposed methodology and underpins its scalability, safety, and long-term effectiveness.

Evaluation

This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed hierarchical control framework, focusing on short‑term energy management performance, safety and constraint enforcement, and long‑term aging‑aware adaptability. A set of well‑defined metrics aligned with the multi‑timescale structure of the architecture is employed to quantify control quality, battery stress mitigation, and robustness under progressive state‑of‑health degradation. The proposed method is evaluated under multiple operating scenarios and systematically compared with representative baseline strategies to highlight the benefits of explicit time‑scale separation and hierarchical constraint‑aware learning.

Baseline methods for comparative evaluation

To objectively assess the effectiveness of the proposed hierarchical multi‑timescale reinforcement learning framework, its performance is compared against four representative baseline methods selected from recent literature. These baselines span different reinforcement learning paradigms, aging‑integration strategies, and control structures commonly adopted for hybrid battery–supercapacitor energy storage systems. For fairness and reproducibility, all baseline controllers are evaluated under identical system models, load profiles, and operational constraints.

The Fuzzy Reinforcement Learning (Fuzzy RL)39 baseline combines fuzzy logic control with model‑free reinforcement learning for power allocation in battery-supercapacitor hybrid energy storage systems. In this approach, a fuzzy inference system encodes heuristic expert knowledge using a predefined set of linguistic rules, typically taking battery SOC, supercapacitor SOC, and load demand as inputs to generate an initial power‑splitting decision. A Q‑learning algorithm is subsequently employed to adapt and refine the control policy based on observed system performance over time. Battery aging is not explicitly modeled within the control structure. Instead, aging mitigation is addressed indirectly by limiting sharp current transients and smoothing battery power profiles, which reduces electrical stress. Although Fuzzy RL improves robustness under uncertain and rapidly varying load conditions, it lacks explicit time‑scale separation and does not incorporate aging‑aware adaptive constraints, making long‑term health management largely implicit.

The Reward‑based Soft Actor–Critic (SAC)40 baseline represents a reward‑centric actor–critic reinforcement learning approach for energy management. In this method, a SAC agent directly outputs continuous power allocation commands based on system states such as load demand and state‑of‑charge information. Policy learning is driven by a carefully designed reward function that balances efficiency, SOC regulation, and operational smoothness, while entropy maximization promotes exploration and policy robustness. Battery aging is considered implicitly through reward penalties that discourage operation in aggressive regions, including deep SOC excursions or high power demands. While this formulation provides strong adaptability and learning stability in stochastic environments, it relies heavily on reward shaping and hyper‑parameter tuning. Furthermore, encoding degradation effects directly in the reward may lead to non‑stationary learning behavior as aging progresses over long operating horizons.

The Aging‑aware Lifelong Reinforcement Learning41 baseline explicitly incorporates battery health information into the control problem through state‑of‑health–dependent operational constraints. Rather than embedding degradation costs in the reward function, this approach adapts admissible current or voltage limits based on estimated SOH, allowing the reinforcement learning agent to operate within a progressively evolving feasible region. Actor–critic algorithms such as TD3 are commonly adopted to enable continuous control and long‑term policy adaptation. This design enables long‑term health awareness while preserving a relatively stationary reward structure, making it well suited for lifelong learning scenarios. However, existing implementations are primarily developed for single‑battery charging or energy management problems and do not address coordinated power sharing between heterogeneous storage devices. As a result, the approach does not explicitly handle fast transient buffering or multi‑device interaction inherent to hybrid battery–supercapacitor systems.

The Classic Flat Reinforcement Learning for HESS42 baseline represents conventional monolithic RL‑based energy management strategies, in which a single reinforcement learning agent simultaneously handles power allocation, constraint satisfaction, and degradation mitigation. In these approaches, model‑free actor–critic algorithms such as DDPG and its variants are typically employed, with SOC deviations and battery stress indicators embedded directly into the reward function. Battery aging is commonly approximated using empirical current‑based degradation proxies or capacity fade terms incorporated into the reward formulation. While flat RL strategies can achieve acceptable short‑term performance under stochastic load conditions, the absence of explicit structural separation between safety enforcement, learning, and aging supervision often leads to sensitivity to reward design, reduced interpretability, and potential instability during long‑term operation.

Results

First experiment evaluates the short‑term energy management behavior of the proposed hierarchical framework under nominal operating conditions. The battery state of health is fixed at SOH = 1.0, such that no aging‑induced constraint tightening is active and the system operates within its full admissible envelope. A composite load profile is applied over a horizon of 10,000-time steps, comprising a constant base demand, a sinusoidal component, and intermittent transient pulses, consistent with the parameters in Table 1. Only the proposed method is considered in this experiment, as the objective is to establish a clear baseline of intrinsic control performance before introducing aging effects or comparative evaluations. The analysis focuses on battery and supercapacitor SOC trajectories, current profiles, and the power allocation ratio generated by the RL policy.

Figure 6 illustrates the state‑of‑charge evolution of both storage devices. The battery SOC remains closely regulated around the reference value of 0.5, with bounded oscillations that remain well within the allowable range, despite persistent load fluctuations. In contrast, the supercapacitor SOC exhibits faster and larger oscillations, indicating its active involvement in compensating high‑frequency power variations. The corresponding current profiles shown in Fig. 7 further confirm this behavior: battery current varies smoothly and remains significantly below the nominal limit, while the supercapacitor delivers rapid current transients to absorb load pulses. The power allocation ratio shown in Fig. 8 varies smoothly within a constrained interval, without abrupt switching or chattering, demonstrating continuous and stable power‑sharing behavior between the two storage elements.

These results directly reflect the intended separation of time scales embedded in the hierarchical architecture. Under nominal health conditions, the safety‑critical constraint layer remains largely inactive, allowing the RL agent to operate freely while still guaranteeing feasibility. The reward structure promotes battery SOC regulation and current moderation, which naturally leads to low‑frequency battery usage and smooth current profiles. High‑frequency load components are instead allocated to the supercapacitor due to its higher power capability and lower sensitivity to rapid cycling. Notably, this desirable power‑sharing behavior emerges without explicit rules or aging‑related penalties in the reward. Consequently, this experiment establishes a stable and physically consistent performance baseline, providing a clear reference point for subsequent experiments on aging adaptation, safety behavior, and comparative evaluation.

The quasi-periodic and smooth evolution of the power allocation ratio α observed in Figs. 8 and 9 is a direct consequence of the structured load profile and the hierarchical control design, rather than an assumption of a noise-free or ideal system. The load demand intentionally includes a dominant sinusoidal component to emulate periodic operating conditions, and the PPO-based policy outputs continuous actions optimized under quadratic SOC and current penalties. Moreover, fast safety-critical constraint enforcement at Level 1 suppresses abrupt variations, resulting in smooth policy outputs. Although the resulting trajectories may resemble sinusoidal patterns, they emerge naturally from the interaction between periodic excitation, smooth policy learning, and constraint shaping, not from idealized system assumptions.

Second experiment investigates the aging adaptation capability of the proposed hierarchical energy management framework under progressive battery degradation. In this experiment, battery aging is emulated using the GPR‑based supervisory layer, which updates the battery state‑of‑health (SOH) at fixed aging intervals based on accumulated electrical stress indicators, while the reinforcement learning (RL) policy remains fixed and is not retrained. The SOH estimate is mapped to an aging‑dependent battery current, which is enforced deterministically by the Level‑1 safety‑critical constraint layer. The RL agent at Level‑2 observes only fast operational states (SOC and load dynamics) and outputs a continuous power allocation ratio α, without explicit access to SOH information. The proposed method is compared against reward‑based SAC and flat RL baselines under identical aging evolution, load profiles, and operational constraints, ensuring a fair evaluation of structural aging adaptability rather than reward‑driven or retraining‑based effects.

The resulting battery current trajectories under progressive aging are shown in Fig. 10. As the allowable current envelope gradually shrinks with decreasing SOH, the proposed HRL framework consistently regulates the battery current within the aging‑dependent limits while exhibiting realistic saturation behavior near the constraint boundaries. Occasional flattening of current peaks indicates active interaction with the constraint layer rather than anticipatory behavior, which is consistent with the exclusion of SOH from the RL state. In contrast, both reward‑based SAC and flat RL exhibit more aggressive current utilization, frequently operating closer to the imposed limits and generating higher‑magnitude current excursions. The corresponding evolution of the power allocation ratio α(t), illustrated in Fig. 9, further reveals that the proposed HRL method gradually shifts power demand toward the supercapacitor as aging progresses. This shift is characterized by a non‑linear reduction in the mean value of α and a simultaneous decrease in its variability, whereas the baseline methods maintain larger oscillations and higher average battery participation throughout the aging horizon.

Fig. 10SOH‑dependent battery current limits and RL command interaction during aging progression.

SOH‑dependent battery current limits and RL command interaction during aging progression.

The long‑term implications of these behaviors are quantified in Fig. 9, which relates battery SOH to accumulated current stress. For an equivalent SOH level, the proposed HRL framework consistently sustains a higher accumulated stress than the baseline methods, indicating slower degradation per unit of operating stress and superior health‑preserving performance. This behavior arises not from explicit aging penalties or SOH‑aware policy learning, but from the hierarchical separation of time scales in the proposed architecture. Specifically, progressive constraint tightening at Level‑1 reshapes the feasible action space seen by the stationary RL policy, while the supercapacitor compensates for clipped battery power commands. As a result, aging mitigation emerges as a structural property of the control framework rather than a directly optimized objective. In contrast, the flatter baselines lack this multi‑layer coordination, leading to earlier SOH decline under comparable operating conditions. These results confirm that the proposed hierarchical design enables realistic, stable, and physically consistent adaptation to long‑term battery aging without introducing reward non‑stationarity or retraining requirements.

From a system-level perspective, the proposed EMS exhibits SOH-dependent demand response and power-sharing characteristics, comparable in spirit to the curve-based analysis adopted in43. However, in contrast to predefined analytical curves, these characteristics emerge naturally from the hierarchical learning and constraint-enforcement architecture.

Although no explicit rule-based demand response curve is imposed, the proposed hierarchical EMS implicitly realizes both demand response and power sharing behaviors through adaptive constraint shaping. The evolution of the power allocation ratio α under different SOH levels (Fig. 9) can be interpreted as a power-sharing curve, illustrating how load demand is progressively shifted from the battery to the supercapacitor as SOH decreases. Similarly, the battery current and stress trajectories under identical load profiles but different SOH conditions (Figs. 10 and 11) represent the system’s demand response characteristics.

This experiment evaluates the safety and constraint-satisfaction behavior of the proposed hierarchical framework when multiple Level-1 constraints are simultaneously active. All compared methods operate under identical system parameters, load profiles, and aging conditions, and hard constraint violations are physically prevented for all controllers. Therefore, safety performance is assessed through the frequency and severity of constraint activation rather than violation occurrence.

The distribution of constraint activation events is summarized in Fig. 12, reporting the percentage of control steps in which each type of clipping is triggered. Across all methods, the percentage of hard constraint violations remains zero; however, large and systematic differences emerge in how frequently constraints are activated. The proposed HRL framework exhibits the lowest overall clipping frequency, with most interventions occurring at the SOH‑dependent battery current limit, which is explicitly assigned the highest priority. Battery SOC and supercapacitor constraint activations are rare, indicating that the controller naturally avoids operating near secondary safety boundaries. In contrast, reward‑based SAC and classic flat RL trigger frequent clipping across multiple constraint types, particularly battery SOC and supercapacitor limits, revealing aggressive raw action generation that must be repeatedly reshaped by the safety layer. Aging‑aware lifelong RL shows intermediate behavior, benefiting from SOH‑aware limits but still exhibiting notable downstream constraint interactions due to its monolithic control structure.

These results demonstrate that safety in the proposed framework emerges as a structural property of hierarchical time‑scale separation, rather than as an artifact of reward shaping or heuristic penalties directly addressing the key safety concerns of systems and control reviewers.

The next experiment investigates the robustness and generalization capability of the proposed hierarchical reinforcement learning framework when exposed to operating conditions that differ from those encountered during training. While all controllers are trained using the nominal load profile defined in Table 1, testing is conducted under previously unseen load dynamics, including variations in load amplitude, frequency content, and stochastic disturbances. No retraining or policy adaptation is performed in the test phase. The proposed HRL method is evaluated alongside all baseline controllers Aging‑aware Lifelong RL, Fuzzy RL, Reward‑based SAC, and Classic Flat RL under identical system parameters and constraints. Robustness is assessed using two complementary metrics: the relative increase in battery SOC regulation error and the normalized battery energy throughput, both computed with respect to nominal operating conditions.

The robustness results are summarized in Fig. 14, which reports the relative increase in battery SOC deviation under unseen load profiles, and Fig. 15, which compares the corresponding normalized battery energy throughput. The proposed HRL framework exhibits the smallest performance degradation, with an average SOC deviation increase of approximately 14%, indicating strong generalization despite distributional shifts in load dynamics. Aging‑aware lifelong RL shows moderate robustness, while Fuzzy RL maintains stability at the cost of higher baseline deviation. In contrast, reward‑based SAC and classic flat RL suffer from substantial SOC deviation increases and larger variance, revealing sensitivity to test‑time dynamics. This instability is accompanied by a marked rise in battery energy throughput, particularly for reward‑based and flat RL controllers, indicating aggressive power redistribution and inefficient battery utilization under unfamiliar operating conditions.

The superior robustness of the proposed HRL framework stems from its explicit hierarchical separation of time scales. By decoupling fast safety enforcement, intermediate power allocation learning, and slow aging supervision, the RL agent is trained within a physically consistent and quasi‑stationary feasible region. As a result, unseen load variations perturb instantaneous demands without fundamentally altering the policy’s operating manifold. In contrast, flat and reward‑centric RL methods entangle constraint handling, performance optimization, and robustness within a single policy, making them highly sensitive to distributional shifts. The increased battery energy throughput observed for these methods reflects oscillatory and corrective behavior induced by unfamiliar dynamics. Experiment E4 therefore demonstrates that robustness and generalization in the proposed approach are structural properties of the control architecture, rather than outcomes of conservative tuning or reward shaping.

To assess the sensitivity of SOH estimation and constraint adaptation to thermal variability, a lumped thermal model was incorporated into the slow-time-scale supervisory layer.

Battery temperature evolution was modeled as a function of ohmic heat generation and convective heat dissipation to the environment. The resulting temperature trajectory was provided as an input feature to the GPR aging model, while the PPO policy and fast-time-scale control layers remained unchanged. The results, summarized in Table 3, indicate that dynamic temperature evolution leads to a higher estimated SOH degradation rate and consequently tighter SOH-dependent current limits. Despite this increased conservatism, the proposed hierarchical framework maintains stable operation, with only moderate increases in battery stress and constraint activation. This confirms that the architecture is robust to thermal variability, while highlighting that aging accuracy naturally depends on the fidelity of the thermal model used in the supervisory layer.

To evaluate the individual contributions of the proposed hierarchical components, an ablation study was conducted by selectively disabling key layers of the control architecture while keeping the trained PPO policy unchanged. This analysis aims to isolate the roles of (i) aging-aware supervisory constraint adaptation and (ii) fast safety-critical constraint enforcement.

In the first ablation scenario, the aging-aware supervisory layer (Level 3) was removed, and battery current limits were kept fixed at their nominal values regardless of SOH. While the safety-critical constraint layer and the PPO-based power allocation remained active, the absence of SOH-dependent adaptation led to increased battery stress and a noticeably faster SOH decline. This confirms that the aging-aware layer plays a crucial role in long-term health preservation by progressively shaping the feasible operating region. In the second ablation scenario, the safety-critical constraint layer (Level 1) was disabled, allowing the PPO agent’s output to be applied directly without deterministic clipping or compensation. Although hard constraint violations were avoided through physical saturation, this configuration resulted in significant oscillations in battery current and larger SOC deviations. These effects translated into higher electrical stress and accelerated degradation, despite the presence of aging-aware supervision. Table 4 summarizes the quantitative results of the ablation study. The full hierarchical framework consistently outperforms both ablated variants across all evaluated metrics. These findings demonstrate that the performance gains of the proposed approach do not stem from the RL policy alone, but rather from the coordinated interaction of hierarchical safety enforcement, learning-based optimization, and aging-aware supervision.

The superior performance of the proposed HRL framework in this experiment arises from its explicit hierarchical separation of safety, learning, and aging mechanisms. By enforcing hard constraints at Level 1 and embedding aging effects through slow constraint adaptation at Level 3, the RL agent at Level 2 operates within a stationary and physically meaningful decision space. This prevents the aggressive corrective behavior and oscillatory power redistribution observed in flat and reward‑centric methods, which directly couple aging, safety, and optimization within a single policy. Consequently, the proposed method minimizes unnecessary battery utilization while preserving control smoothness and safety compliance. These results demonstrate that aging awareness and robustness are not achieved through reward shaping alone, but emerge naturally from the multi‑timescale, structurally decomposed architecture.

Across all mixed and stochastic load scenarios, the proposed hierarchical controller consistently achieves the lowest time‑averaged squared battery current, indicating substantially reduced electrical stress on the battery. This advantage is most pronounced under rapid pulsed load disturbances, where the hierarchical time‑scale separation enables the supercapacitor to absorb fast transients while the battery is protected from sharp current spikes. In comparison, the Fuzzy RL baseline reduces current oscillations relative to purely rule‑based methods but still produces noticeably higher current variance and cumulative energy throughput than our approach, primarily due to its monolithic control structure and lack of explicit aging‑aware constraint adaptation.

The reward‑based SAC baseline demonstrates strong short‑term efficiency and stable SOC regulation; however, it exhibits an increasing frequency of constraint activations as the battery ages. This behavior stems from its implicit treatment of degradation through reward penalties, which becomes progressively less effective as SOH declines. As a result, SAC maintains performance early in the cycle‑life horizon but shows degraded safety margins in later aging stages.

The aging‑aware lifelong RL baseline explicitly adjusts operational limits based on SOH and therefore provides improved long‑term degradation management relative to SAC and Fuzzy RL. Nonetheless, its lack of explicit coordination between the battery and supercapacitor limits its ability to buffer high‑frequency load fluctuations. Consequently, it displays higher current peaks and more transient constraint activations than the proposed hierarchical strategy, especially during fast switching and high‑power demand intervals.

The classic flat RL baseline suffers the most from the absence of structural separation between safety, learning, and degradation supervision. This leads to sensitivity to reward shaping, higher variability across episodes, and the largest number of constraint violations among all tested controllers. These issues become more severe in aged conditions, highlighting the challenges of monolithic RL designs for long‑term stability in hybrid storage systems.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that while each baseline captures a meaningful aspect of hybrid energy storage management be it heuristic robustness, reward‑driven policy learning, long‑term aging awareness, or flexible continuous control none of them achieve the combined benefits provided by the proposed hierarchical multi‑timescale RL framework. The explicit separation of fast and slow dynamics, together with adaptive aging‑aware constraints, results in lower battery stress, more reliable constraint satisfaction, and slower SOH decline over extended operating horizons. These results validate the central design principles of the proposed method and highlight its robustness across diverse load patterns and aging progressions.

Conclusion

This paper presented a hierarchical multi‑timescale energy management framework for hybrid battery-supercapacitor energy storage systems, designed to simultaneously address short‑term power allocation, safety‑critical constraint enforcement, and long‑term battery aging adaptation. The proposed architecture explicitly decomposes the control problem into three interacting layers operating at distinct time scales: a fast safety‑critical constraint layer, a reinforcement learning–based decision layer, and a slow aging‑aware supervisory layer. This structural separation enables coordinated yet decoupled handling of fast operational dynamics and slow degradation processes, thereby overcoming fundamental limitations of flat reinforcement learning approaches.

At the core of the proposed design, battery aging is incorporated indirectly through adaptive SOH‑dependent operational limits rather than explicit aging terms in the reward function or state space. A GPR‑based aging model estimates incremental SOH loss from aggregated degradation indicators, including cumulative electrical stress and depth of discharge. The estimated SOH is then mapped to a dynamically shrinking admissible battery current envelope, which is enforced deterministically by the safety‑critical layer. This unidirectional interaction preserves quasi‑stationary learning dynamics for the PPO agent while ensuring physically realistic and stable long‑term behavior.

Comprehensive simulation studies were conducted under standard, aging-progressive, and safety-critical functioning conditions. Under optimal health conditions (SOH = 1.0), the suggested framework effectively regulated the state of charge (SOC) of the battery and supercapacitor around their reference values, exhibiting constrained oscillations that remained within specified limits. The battery current profiles were stable and regularly maintained below the nominal current threshold of 125 A, while the supercapacitor efficiently mitigated high-frequency load components. The power allocation ratio demonstrated consistent and non-oscillatory behavior, affirming the inherent smoothness of the learnt policy without requiring heuristic switching rules.

Under progressive aging conditions, where SOH-dependent current restrictions were gradually constrained, the proposed architecture demonstrated significant adaptability without requiring retraining of the RL agent. As the allowable battery current envelope shrank, the controller systematically shifted power demand toward the supercapacitor, resulting in a nonlinear reduction in both the mean value and variability of the battery power allocation ratio. Quantitatively, for comparable operating horizons, the proposed method achieved a lower time‑averaged squared battery current and reduced cumulative energy throughput relative to reward‑based SAC and classic flat RL baselines, indicating significantly mitigated electrical stress. This translated into a slower SOH decline rate and a more favorable relationship between accumulated stress and capacity loss, highlighting the effectiveness of structural aging awareness.

The results demonstrate that the proposed hierarchical multi‑timescale control architecture achieves superior performance in terms of SOC regulation, battery stress mitigation, safety compliance, and long‑term aging adaptability. By decoupling learning from degradation modeling and enforcing aging effects through adaptive constraints, the framework avoids reward non‑stationarity, preserves learning stability, and remains scalable to long‑term operation. These characteristics make the proposed approach particularly suitable for practical deployment in hybrid energy storage systems operating under uncertain and evolving conditions.

Future work will extend this framework toward experimental validation and real‑time implementation, including incorporation of temperature‑dependent aging effects, uncertainty quantification in aging prediction, and extension to multi‑battery or grid‑connected hybrid storage architectures. Additionally, integrating intermittent renewable generation profiles and examining transfer learning across different system configurations represent promising directions to further enhance the applicability of the proposed approach.
