Powering Neurology Drug Development Trials to Reduce False Negative Results
Authors: Hiromasa Mori, Stig Johan Wiklund, Vikram Ramanarayanan, Fatta B. Nahab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSensitive and reliable measurement of therapeutic outcomes in neurology drug trials remains challenging. Clinical rating scales and their total scores are often considered the gold standard and used as primary efficacy endpoints in studies because of their acceptance by the clinical community and regulators. However, rating scales have limitations when used in drug development, and we hypothesize that their lower statistical power is one possible reason leading to false negative results. This paper proposes a framework to address four systemic components of statistical power—size of the treatment effect, variability, sample size, and statistical threshold—to minimize the odds of observing false negatives, and thereby maximize the odds of trial success. Drawing from the literature and biopharmaceutical anecdotal evidence, we identify key factors that affect each of these power components and assess their influence. We also highlight challenges with rating scale endpoints associated with total scores and large sample sizes. We identify potential causes of rating scale endpoint failure and provide an indication‐agnostic framework for understanding the relationship between clinical rating scale performance and study power. The proposed methods can be applied to both study design and post hoc analysis.

Introduction

Neurology drug development remains difficult. Hopeful new discoveries fill the news but few disease‐modifying therapies (DMT) reach regulatory approval. Like any other therapeutic area, neurology drug trial endpoints must establish both clinically meaningful improvements and their statistical significance. Well‐designed primary endpoints satisfy the two key requirements at once. Neurology DMTs alter the biopathological processes in the nervous system with the goal of slowing or halting disease progression and maintaining function; this is challenging in the context of an incomplete understanding of these biological cascades and manifested functional symptoms. Neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders are consequential health and societal challenges that demand the best efforts from the biopharmaceutical industry. Patient‐Focused Drug Development guidelines and advances in biomarker development offer promise [1], but lower success rates discourage industry investment [2].

Revised Airlie House consensus guidelines state that ALS Phase 2 studies should not be designed to establish clinical efficacy as the primary goal [3]. But in reality, many neurology DMT Phase 2 studies adopt regulator‐endorsed clinical rating scale endpoints to quantify functional improvements, with the goal of serving as “mini Phase 3” studies. While the approach to traditional clinical development includes a series of confirmatory trials, an initial proof‐of‐concept, followed by a Phase 2b, and leading to a larger pivotal Phase 3 trial, this approach is understandably costly and frequently abbreviated. Instead, sponsors often design a Phase 2b study as a mini Phase 3 with the goal of demonstrating early success and accelerating the path to a regulatory submission. These sequential validation steps are unfortunately not interchangeable, and Phase 2 studies are better suited for signal‐finding and endpoint optimization that can then be used to properly design a successful pivotal Phase 3. In the end, a fragmented clinical development strategy does not serve either the needs of the patient community nor the industry. More innovative trial designs are required to turn the tide, particularly in slowly progressive neurological disorders.

Clinical rating scales are validated clinical outcome assessment (COA) instruments, developed to standardize disease severity and progression monitoring. Use of these rating scales is supported by precedent and psychometric validation data demonstrating at least modest levels of internal consistency, test–retest reliability, inter‐rater reliability, construct and content validity. Based on these validations, researchers and regulators accept them as evidence and study sponsors use them routinely as the primary endpoints in neurology drug trials, despite their known insensitivity and variability [4, 5]. We hypothesize that some neurology DMT Phase 2 studies result in false negatives (i.e., efficacious DMTs failing to meet trial endpoints and producing inconclusive results) due to ineffective trial designs and underpowering [6].

False negatives can be defined as “effective treatments wrongly eliminated, leading to missed healthcare and economic opportunities” [6]. Denying patients access to potentially effective therapies has serious consequences in the future, especially for life‐shortening rare diseases with no approved cure. A simulation by Burt et al. indicates greater missed economic opportunities from false negatives in Phase 2 than the excessive testing of the false positives; however, the false positive outcomes are often given more attention [6, 7]. Quantitative evaluations of false negatives are often made under the, arguably simplistic, assumption that the definition and measurement of efficacy are unambiguous [6, 7]. In this article, we acknowledge that the definition and measurement of efficacy is in itself a complex and ambiguous issue. While a negative study outcome may be a “true negative” on the basis of a chosen insensitive endpoint and study design, we hypothesize that other choices of design and endpoints might nonetheless provide a positive result. In such a scenario, if the intervention is actually an effective treatment (as measured by a more appropriately defined endpoint), then a negative outcome could in fact be a false negative.

Disease modification is a shared aspiration in the neuroscience community, but it is elusive. Clinical characterization of disease modification is poorly understood with few therapeutic proofs, and often defined by changes in rating scale total scores. Without prior clinical evidence of what therapeutic functional effect(s) are expected or the time course of the effect, the disease stage and patient‐specific nuance of disease modification may be lost in a single composite measure of progression.

Variability deserves more attention in drug development. Neurological conditions have biological and phenotypic variability. Patients live with the uniqueness of their diseases and face unpredictability in their daily lives [8]. Additionally, comparability of neurological drug trials is limited by variability in design, operational execution, and the use of endpoints. Controlling and minimizing these sources of variability is crucial for improving clinical trial outcomes.

In this article we propose a generalizable theory on how current study designs, especially the reliance on rating scale total scores, may lead to systematic underpowering and consequently failure of neurology drug trials. Hobart et al. in their 2007 publication articulated the psychometric shortcomings of rating scales in neurology drug trials. Scale scores are ordinal and the intervals are unequal; therefore, it is argued that the scores cannot be treated arithmetically. Confidence intervals are too large to quantify effects for individual patients; therefore, the scores are only suitable for group‐level comparisons. Classical test theory that underpins the psychometric methods for rating scale analysis cannot be tested or verified [5].

We theorize that one can improve the chances of study success by understanding and leveraging the mathematics of statistical power. We aim to establish a set of general principles that will improve DMT trials in neurology and beyond.

Growing Size of Neurology Drug Trials

We note that the sample size of neurology studies has increased in the recent decades, while few have achieved statistically significant results. For example, Parkinson's Disease DMT Phase 2/3 studies by pharmaceutical sponsors using MDS‐UPDRS as a primary endpoint grew significantly in sample size between 2006 and 2026 (Figure 1) [9].

Neurology Drug Development and Limitations of Clinical Rating Scales

In the United States, regulatory approval by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) may be based on a Clinical Outcome Assessment (COA) that directly measures how a patient feels, functions, or survives. In contrast, biomarker‐based approval relies on biological or physiological indicators (e.g., tumor size, blood chemistry) that are expected to predict a meaningful clinical benefit rather than proving it directly [10]. Scales have predefined assessments and questions covering multiple disease‐relevant functional domains (e.g., speech, walking and tremor in Parkinson's disease (PD), vs. swallowing, handwriting, and respiratory function in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)). Scoring is typically based on verbally characterized grading anchors and score categories of normal, slight, mild, moderate and severe that correspond to the severity scores 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4. Assessment score points are added together to calculate total scores that represent overall severity, while changes over time are intended to reflect disease progression. Clinical rating scales can consist of clinician‐reported outcome (ClinRO) and/or patient‐reported outcome (PRO), both of which are human interpretations of disease‐related feeling and function. While rating scales are accepted by clinical communities and regulators (arguably due to the lack of better alternatives at the time of their introduction), many recognize their limitations as trial endpoints for DMTs [4, 5, 11]. Here we list key challenges of clinical rating scales for the context of use of detecting changes that is, establishing a clinically‐meaningful degree of improvement above a statistical significance threshold, in disease progression in neurology DMT Phase 2 or 3 trials.

Insensitivity

Clinical rating scale assessments and questions have coarse, ordinal severity grades (e.g., 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4) and rely on subjective interpretation by the assessor and/or patient. At best, the scores are semi‐quantitative, which is suboptimal for quantitative evidence of effects. Many assessments have mean annual change of less than 0.5, which implies the natural progression in a 52‐week study is more likely to be 0 than 1 [4]. This necessitates combining multiple scores to create a composite capable of showing meaningful change. We will discuss the challenges of sum scores later.

Subjectivity

Clinician raters are expected to score their evaluations against predefined verbal or video‐based descriptions of severity, leading to subjective interpretation. For example, the MDS‐UPDRS question 3.10 for assessing gait assigns a score of 1 (Slight) for “Independent walking with minor gait impairment” while the score of 2 (Mild) is “Independent walking but with substantial gait impairment” [12]. It can be difficult for clinicians to categorize patient functions consistently this way and the nuances can be lost. When it comes to sensitivity and objectivity, rating scale scores are quite different from glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) for diabetes or body weight in obesity, both of which are machine‐generated, quantitative, and precise biomarker measurements [13]. While similar biomarkers are currently lacking in neurology trials, there are numerous opportunities to improve measurement precision.

Low Frequency Snapshots

Many neurological conditions fluctuate within and across days to weeks while progressing slowly. Conversely, clinical assessments are normally conducted during infrequent scheduled visits, and the scores represent a snapshot view at that time. Connecting a few snapshots may either over‐ or underestimate the true disease progression curve. More frequent in‐clinic administrations of rating scale would be costly and burdensome for the patients. Studies comparing in‐clinic to at‐home measures of Parkinson's, which are more representative of real‐world performance, have shown moderate correlations at best [14], while in‐clinic within subject variability for MDS‐UPDRS subscales achieved reliability of only 0.5 suggesting that only half of the true signal of change was captured at a clinic visit [15].

Nonlinearity, Ceiling and Floor Effects

Despite sequential integers having an appearance of quantitativeness, rating scale scores do not have equal intervals and the scores cannot be treated arithmetically [5]. Moreover, some assessments are inconsistent in how much disease progression each grade change represents. Clinical rating scales may only function appropriately in the mid stages of disease progression. A study of 70 multiple system atrophy (MSA) patients noted a ceiling effect, with none of the patients exceeding ~90% of total available points in both UMSARS Part I and II [4]. Floor effects also emerge as an outcome sensitivity issue if early or prodromal cohorts are studied, as is often the case in DMT trials when intervention is most likely to be effective. The respiratory insufficiency assessment (item 12) in ALSFRS‐R starts with “None (+4)”, and the next anchor is for “Intermittent use of Bilevel Positive Airway Pressure (BiPAP) (+3)” which points to advanced respiratory muscle involvement [16].

For a single item, the issue of unequal intervals might be handled with statistical methods specifically designed for ordinal data, for example, ordinal logistic regression. However, with rating scales commonly being defined as the arithmetic sum of individual item scores, the issue of unequal intervals remains in the aggregation of the rating scale, and the issue is not remedied by the choice of statistical analysis method for the total score.

Limits in Informativeness

For each individual patient, only a subset of measurements are relevant and sensitive. Informative measures explain large proportions of the concept we are interested in. The item response analysis conducted on 577 MSA patients at the French Reference Centre showed all the UMSARS part I and II items had different levels of informativeness across disease stages, except for speech (Item I.1) which explained about 5% of the total disease progression consistently [17].

Imbalance of Functional Domains

Imbalance in assessment items can also bias total scores. An ideal total score should reflect the overall level of disease progression encompassing all the relevant aspects of life equally, but actual total scores may instead reflect the number of assessments that can be done for each function. For example, MDS‐UPDRS Part III is overrepresented by 11 tremor items compared to a single assessment of speech, whereas studies have shown that tremor is less disabling than non‐tremor features [18, 19].

Multidimensionality of Total Scores

Clinical rating scales incorporate multiple functional domain assessments by design, and total scores represent the overall severity and progression. Van Eijk et al. write: “The ALSFRS‐R total score may be insensitive to detecting treatment benefit when a treatment affects only some of its subscales, or benefits subscales in varying degrees, resulting in potentially higher false negative rates and an increased risk of missing important treatment clues” [11]. We believe the same problem statement applies to all neurological conditions with functional heterogeneity and disease stage‐dependent progression variability [20]. Early‐stage ALS patients on Pridopidine in HEALEY ALS trial showed statistically significant improvement in ALSFRS‐R respiratory function by Week 16, and within the domain dyspnea signal was consistently stronger than orthopnea and respiratory insufficiency assessments [21].

Limitations of Total Scores

A composite measure (e.g., total score) provides a single measure of disease severity that can be used to track progression over time, and it may be interpreted more easily in long‐term care. For these reasons drug regulators often suggest total scores to be the primary endpoints [22]. A primary endpoint must satisfy two requirements at once. It must capture change that is clinically meaningful—change anchored to a defined, patient‐derived within‐patient threshold rather than a merely statistically detectable difference—and it must be sufficiently responsive to detect a treatment effect of the anticipated magnitude, which falls outside of measurement error and biological variability, with adequate power at a feasible sample size and trial duration. Total scores can trade the second against the first, while aggregation can dilute signal density if progression over the observation window is confined to a subset of contributing features. For example, if progression is confined to one item out of 10 assessments the signal density is diluted to one tenth, whereas the measurement error of the nine unchanging items is carried into the total in full. This dilution is most consequential in early, de novo disease, where change is slow and confined to few domains—precisely the setting in which disease‐modification claims are pursued. The practical implication is that endpoint selection should be justified prospectively with responsiveness (e.g., minimal detectable change) and minimal‐clinically‐important‐difference data from the target population.

MDS‐UPDRS Part I, II, and III are commonly used in early Parkinson's Disease DMT trials. Part I and II are patient‐reported outcomes of non‐motor and motor aspects of daily living, whereas Part III is a clinician‐rated motor examination. Many studies set Part I, II, and III total score as their primary efficacy endpoint with their main emphasis on Part III. Despite such common practice, Goetz et al. show that combined MDS‐UPDRS parts fail validation because the parts are “apples and oranges” [22].

We analyzed recent neurology drug studies that used rating scales as their primary endpoints. Many trials reportedly failed to reach statistical significance, yet clinical development programs continued presumably due to the presence of promising signals. We aim to uncover the underlying causes of such “near misses” and we explore the potential approaches to overcome them.

Framework to Address Key Components of “Statistical Power”

For a new drug to be approved and made available in healthcare systems around the world, an applicant needs to show clinically meaningful therapeutic benefits with statistical significance. “Near miss” trial results are often presented visually as a gradual separation between the treatment and placebo arms along the axis of rating scale‐based primary endpoints with their confidence intervals overlapping significantly [39]. These are weak and statistically insignificant results that complicate sponsors' decision‐making (Figure 2).

Illustration of common results in neurology Phase 2 studies.

Power calculation is a critical part of clinical study design and investment decisions. Drug trial sponsors design studies to achieve a balance between the risk of false positive outcomes (Type I error, α) and false negative outcomes (Type II error, β). Typically, α is set as 5% (p value of 0.05) and β is 20%. Statistical power is a function of the assumed size of treatment effect Δ (e.g., differential therapeutic effect measurement compared to the control), α for the statistical significance threshold, standard deviations of the measurements σ, and the sample size N. It may be noted that the term “effect size” is sometimes used to represent the ratio d = Δ/σ, also referred to as Cohen's d. We have chosen to discuss the two components of this ratio separately as we see that issues pertaining to the size of the treatment effect, Δ, and the variability, σ, may have different causes (Figure 3).

Statistical components in study power calculation.

Statistical power can also be defined as 1 − β, and its relationships with the factors can schematically be described in the following simplified expression. Power│α~Δ·Nσ

Underpowering can occur when the size of treatment effect (Δ) is overestimated, variance (σ) is underestimated, the sample size (N) is too small and/or the statistical threshold (α) is set unnecessarily strict. We identified many drivers of underpowering and suspected false negative study results, and we suspect multiple factors cause a compounding impact (Figure 4).

Key challenges in powering neurology clinical studies.

Before investigational therapies are tested for efficacy in the target disease population, true effect size and endpoint variability are not known. Therefore, they are assumed in the sample size calculation. Underpowering only comes to light at the end of the study, which may be several years down the road after all the patient volunteer contributions and study investments had been made. We need to view these elements to be an interconnected system where change in one element affects other elements. Policy and strategy decisions need to be made with others in mind.

Size of Treatment Effect

Size of treatment effect (Δ) is the difference in patients' functional outcomes attributed to the studied therapies. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT), the size of treatment effect is the difference between the means of the treatment arm and the placebo arm.

Size of treatment effect is impacted by insensitive measurements. As we have shown, clinical rating scales have coarse severity grades and rely on subjective interpretation by the assessors and patients. Total scores can weaken or diminish signals of progression and therapeutic effects. Ceiling and floor effects as well as disease stage‐specific items reduce the size of treatment effect further.

Biases can set the predicted size of treatment effect larger than what studied drugs can achieve. In vitro and imaging biomarkers have made remarkable contributions to neuroscience in recent decades, but changes in intermediate biology and physiology do not always translate to functional improvements. Some functional improvements take time, and some tissue damages are irreversible even after the pathological causes are eliminated (Figure 5).

Improving signal sensitivity to achieve statistical significance.

We can measure the size of therapeutic effects either by sharpening the instrument (scales), applying them to the right daily functions or increasing the quantity of data. Variability

While the size of treatment effect of an investigational therapy is always reported, variability of the outcomes is given less attention. Power simulation models may be adopting outcome variability estimates from publications and assume them to be applicable and constant. Statistical power is sensitive to variability, and statistical insignificance is often due to an overlap in confidence intervals (i.e., insufficient separation of the study arms).

Variability management is important and we must learn from other industries. This has been a major topic in industrialization and is synonymous with quality improvement. In automotive manufacturing, reduction in component variability enabled assembly lines for Ford Model T and led to kaizen practice at Toyota. Six Sigma improvements drove commercial success of General Electric and many other industrial processes including pharmaceutical manufacturing. Simply, reduction in variability directly contributed to improvement in quality and enabled mass production of everything we use. However, this thinking is not yet adopted in neurology clinical development.

While variability is represented in the above equation as one factor, actual variability is a product of each and all of the components. As we focus on variabilities in neurology drug development, we first need to establish a systematic framework (Category break‐down described in Supplement). One broad category is with the natural heterogeneity of the target population, in their disease biology, stage and functional presentation. In addition, patients face intra‐ and inter‐day fluctuations and variability of the disease [8]. These differences are likely to give rise to varied treatment responses. But until neurological conditions are meaningfully subtyped to the level of precision oncology, neurology trial sponsors must accept these as natural population heterogeneity (Figure 6).

Reducing variability to achieve statistical significance.

Inter‐Rater Variability

Arises from clinicians conducting disease diagnosis and monitoring differently. Human judgment is intrinsically noisy, even among trained clinicians. Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman and the co‐authors of Noise talk about the inconsistency of the physician and inadequacy of nomenclature [56]. While scientific publications, conferences, and training encourage uniformity, we are aware of variability among countries, sites, and clinicians. Study investigators may also interpret inclusion/exclusion criteria slightly differently and administer clinical rating scales in their own ways despite certification requirements. Central reading is commonly applied for radiological assessments, and equivalent review of video recording of clinical rating should be considered.

Investigator and site selection can impact study endpoint variability. There may be operational differences between teaching hospitals and regional clinics that may introduce certain biases in addition to variability. They must be familiar and well trained in the inclusion/exclusion criteria and assessments for monitoring progression. Thorough site setup as well as site‐to‐site practice alignment are recommended. Those sites that are sensitive to financial incentives may pose different challenges; without adequate supervision and control, they may be tempted to interpret study enrolment criteria more liberally in borderline cases.

Intra‐rater variability can arise from workday rhythms and what each day brings, for example, immediately after lunch to the very last patient of the day, or from external influences and anchoring. Patients can be given different scores by the same neurologist just by being seen at different times of the day, or standards can creep over time [14].

Intra‐Patient Variability

Patients can have their own variability due to external influences, daily rhythm and seasonality [8]. Infrequent rating scale assessments are snapshots that may be influenced by the travel, waiting, and other factors of in‐clinic visits. Immediate care givers' engagement before and during the assessment can also impact patients.

Instrument Variability

Reduction in measurement variability starts with consistent instrumentation. Rating scale items must be calibrated to generate consistent information. One example is that asking about patients' abilities to “cut food” would have different functional implications in Western culture using knife and fork and in Asia where chopsticks are more frequently used. For digital devices, sources of variability need to be addressed [57], especially bring‐your‐own‐devices need to be verified for technical consistency.

Trial Operations to Manage Variability

Clinical operations are critically important for reducing variability. Site clinicians and teams need to be set up and trained to recruit and monitor study participants to an appropriate and consistent level of knowledge and skill depending on the study design. Timely and compliant clinical assessments should be monitored. Standards may drift over time and clinical rating scale assessments need an equivalent to the re‐calibration of laboratory testing equipment.

Statistical Thresholds

Drug approval requires proof that a positive treatment outcome is because of the drug, rather than by chance or placebo effects. Both α and β are set to achieve scientific confidence for benefit–risk balance and optimum investment of the resources. In neurology DMT Phase 2 trials, statistical thresholds seem to be set to fail studies. These studies should be designed as proof‐of‐concept, not a small version of a pivotal study [3]. α is almost always set as 0.05 and we see suspected false negative results too often. False positives in Phase 2 can lead to massive economic loss in Phase 3 and industry sponsors often prefer to fail early [58]. However, failed trials disappoint the participating patients and erode trust. Reducing the probability of false negatives should be the primary focus of efficacy proof‐of‐concept studies.

False positives and false negatives put burden on different stakeholders, and the regulators face a delicate balance. Especially for breakthrough treatment for rare diseases with no approved cure, it may be better to shift the thresholds to allow more false positives [6, 58]. Furlong et al. cite that the FDA can draw different conclusions on product efficacy from the same set of data, and they demonstrate Objective Participatory Patient Advocacy can guide regulatory rigor and patient needs to a better balance [59]. On the economics of drug development, Burt et al. argue that investing more in Phase 2 to increase the power combined with lenient α leads to higher profits in totality [6]. In that context, the recent case of approval and subsequent market withdrawal of Amylyx's sodium phenylbutyrate and taurursodiol (Relyvrio, AMX0035) presents an alternative scenario where the ALS patients' voice led the FDA to err on the side of hope when faced with weak efficacy evidence [60, 61, 62].

Sample Size

Sample size increases statistical power generally, but we argue for better designs first, then enlarging the size. Kaplan et al. warn that large sample size does not overcome biases, and generalizability to the studied populations is more important than sample size [63]. We often witness unrestrained reliance on larger sample sizes where the clinical operation partners are asked to set up more sites and recruit patients faster. Patient volunteers should be considered as the most valuable and expensive resource. Sponsors must do everything to increase the size of treatment effect and reduce the variability before relying on the power of the sample size.

Complications and Trade‐Offs in Neurology Study Designs

In neurology DMT development we face additional trial design challenges due to outcome insensitivity, variability, symptomatic heterogeneity, polypharmacy, and the rating scales. We need to adopt more careful and systematic thinking to come up with effective and practical designs.

Sample Size Penalty Hypothesis

Larger scale studies may increase variability in hard‐to‐differentially‐diagnose rare neurological conditions. The expert investigator pool is finite. Specialist neurologists who attend medical congresses in a particular rare disease would be better and more consistent in diagnosis and monitoring, but they are concentrated in key academic centers. It is plausible that clinicians with less familiarity with the disease will get involved as study sites are broadened to recruit more patients. This variability may lead to a potential sample size penalty (Figure 7).

Sample size penalty hypothesis.

DMT Adjunctive Benefits Over Standard‐of‐Care

Effective symptomatic therapies exist in some of the neurological conditions we discussed above. DMT trials need to show either superiority or adjunctive benefits to the standard‐of‐care (SoC). Immediate and strong efficacy of those symptomatic therapies can mask DMT efficacy.

Levodopa works well on most PD patients, and it creates marked few‐hour ON/OFF effect cycles. PD DMTs aim to slow the underlying disease progression, and their efficacy appears over months, not hours. From the point of view of DMT outcome assessment, SoC symptomatic therapies create enormous fluctuations that mask the underlying disease trajectory changes. Many sponsors start PD DMT trials in the Levodopa naïve population, but this signal detection challenge becomes critical in later phase studies.

Co‐Primary Endpoints

Neurological conditions have heterogeneous functional presentations and rates of progression; therefore, therapeutic responses are expected to be heterogeneous. In this context, co‐primary endpoint strategies offer advantages [64]. Multiple endpoints weaken power if they are positioned as conjunctive (A AND B) co‐primary. Requirement of statistical significance in every co‐primary endpoint stated in the FDA guideline document Multiple Endpoints in Clinical Trials Guidance for Industry decreases study power. Disjunctive (A OR B) co‐primary endpoints (FDA refers to them as “primary endpoint family”) may be acceptable to establish therapeutic efficacy, but appropriate controls for Type I errors need to be incorporated [65]. Careful regulatory consultation incorporates the patients' perspectives and balanced scientific rationale.

Potential Solutions for Better DMT Trial Designs

We must accept that much of the mechanistic links between the physiological and functional changes caused by neurological drug treatment are not clearly understood. The best information on drug effects that are available to us are biomarkers and COAs independently. Correlating them would help us understand the invisible therapeutic process and aid in designing better studies. Balanced reliance on biomarkers and COAs will reduce both Type I and Type II errors and the evidence will be much stronger when both are supportive [66].

In recent years, many disease‐specific biomarkers have been evaluated and developed. Neurofilament light (NfL) is now well established as a predictive biomarker for progression of ALS, and the FDA granted an accelerated approval to tofersen for SOD1‐ALS based on the reduction in plasma NfL that would reasonably be likely to predict clinical benefits [67]. CReATe Consortium also reported NfL as a powerful prognostic biomarker, predicting survival and ALSFRS‐R decline [68].

Developing and validating new COAs will need concerted strategic efforts by disease communities. Qualified digital functional endpoints like SV95C can improve measurement sensitivity and increase statistical power, but their development and validation require several years of foresighted planning and commitment [69]. For now we assume qualitative clinical rating scales will remain essential, and that realistic solutions combine thoughtful application of established assessments with digital and AI technologies and operational improvements.

We recommend this framework to be applied to future clinical studies with a clear understanding of the challenges associated with each rating scale and therapeutic indication. We have included a root cause analysis structure (See Supporting Information).

Discussion

During the drafting of this manuscript, the authors heard many neurology researchers and clinical trialists acknowledge the limitations of clinical rating scales for drug development. At the same time, we witnessed many DMT studies conducted with total scores as their primary endpoints. There seems to be mutually reinforcing inertia among the stakeholders to continue using total scores despite loud and clear warnings coming from researchers, including those involved in creating rating scales [11, 22]. We should accept that rating scales are here to stay as clinical assessment tools and explore how we apply them as trial endpoints to serve the needs of the patients, regulators, and economic stakeholders.

Compared to sensitivity being framed clearly in the context of meaningful clinical effects, our community seems to have less of a clear idea about variabilities involved in drug development. Neurological conditions have biological and phenotypic variability within and across individuals over time. Additionally, comparability of neurological drug trials is limited by variability in design, operational execution, and the use of endpoints. Controlling and minimizing these sources of variability is crucial for improving clinical trial outcomes.

Along with the heterogeneity and variability in neurological conditions, absence of clear understanding of disease modification effects is a major challenge. Slowing or halting of disease progression is equally lacking in functional specificity and nuance for disease staging. Statisticians and executive management prefer clear‐cut success or failure, and unified primary endpoints are attractive. But heterogeneous efficacy is visible in individual patient data in specific functional assessments, not in total score or study arm summary statistics.

In this context we should use specific fit‐for‐purpose measurements; a “Swiss army knife” approach of rating scale total score is tempting but the endpoint performance suffers and we cannot tell if the drug worked from the study results. FDA's Patient‐Focused Drug Development Guideline offers a clear steer towards measuring what matters to the patients [70]. Patients express the felt benefits in specific functions, for example, “I can go up the stairs more easily” or “speech is less slurred.” They do not express how they are feeling and functioning in total scores.

Knowing the measurements that matter also helps deploy relevant digital solutions. Sensor and algorithm‐based solutions to detect and characterize patients' functional burden have made enormous progress in recent years and they can be readily deployed once their context of use are defined. Specific digital measurements such as gait parameters can be validated against the corresponding functional domains rather than total scores or alternatively developed to predict meaningful events (e.g., falls, dysphagia, or respiratory dysfunction).

Statistical significance threshold should be optimized to balance patient needs and benefit–risk certainty. Current probability of success of neurology DMTs is too low for every stakeholder. ALS community expressed their patients' view of benefit/risk clearly to the FDA after the Relyvrio Phase 2 study. Its conditional approval highlighted an innovative model that addressed the patients' unmet needs sooner and accumulated real‐world understanding of disease modification. Life‐shortening rare diseases especially should be given that flexibility.

Neurology communities must learn from previous drug trials, even if the sponsors terminated development programs. We can build clearer pictures of disease modification for each DMT class with cumulative shared data. Failed studies are published less frequently. Laviolle et al. found that 34.8% of published neurology RCTs had negative results [71]. This is counter‐intuitive given more neurology trials fail than succeed. Data sharing initiatives like Vivli (http://vivli.org/) have made great progress in enabling secure data access to researchers. Patients and the research community should continue to demand study sponsors to make data available.

Past therapeutic trials contain the best insights on potential disease‐modifying effects and rating scale performance. We must learn from existing clinical data, especially the failed DMT trial datasets that contain valuable and quantifiable insights related to statistical power that is, overestimation in the effect size, enrichment, endpoint insensitivity and variability and placebo effects. Analyze failures and build disease community‐wide knowledge base to advance future drug development efforts.

We should re‐evaluate failed DMT programs when we suspect their Phase 2 or 3 results to be false negatives. Analysis of the previous studies will highlight the signal, responder subpopulation, and sources of variability. We would be able to assess the statistical power and probability of success in much higher confidence, and turn‐around trials will become investable propositions. It should be emphasized, however, that in such re‐evaluations, selection bias might be an issue and there is a risk that spurious signals are identified.

Lastly but not least, deep disease insights and effective study designs should belong to the community rather than being proprietary know‐how of individual sponsors. As the world population ages rapidly, efficient therapeutic development capabilities are critical for our future neurological health.

Limitations

This review is primarily a theoretical and opinion‐based framework rather than an empirical study. Our main objective was to propose a perspective that helps connect various statistical elements contributing to study success, and to invite the neurology research community to test if the hypotheses hold in actual drug trials. This theoretical nature limited our ability to make specific and quantitative assertions about the endpoint insensitivity and variability in actual study designs. We mention in the article some examples where studies have failed to meet the primary endpoint. It should be acknowledged that when further trials have not been run, it is generally not possible to reliably determine whether the study outcome was a false negative or an appropriate failure of a futile drug.

We also acknowledge that trial implications of these factors are specific to each disease indication, investigational therapy and product strategy. Other limitations include potential selection bias of examples, distinguishing false negatives from ineffective therapy (true negatives), broad claims for potentially indication‐specific situations, lack of quantitative evidence from, for example, post hoc power or meta‐analysis, conjectural claims such as larger sample sizes can worsen variability, current insufficient operationalization of digital endpoints, lack of discussion of other important causes of trial failure (e.g., missing data, multiplicity handling, nonadherence, concomitant medications, site quality issues, placebo response), and possible lack of generalizability. We recognize that robust clinical evidence must be built on scientifically rigorous processes, for example, separation of hypothesis generation from testing that hypothesis, and therefore insights generated from post hoc analysis alone are not substitutes. Future work should examine these aspects with actual trial datasets in areas of community interest.

Conclusions

This paper proposes a new theoretical lens for improving statistical power in neurology drug trials where clinical rating scales are used as primary endpoints. Clinical rating scales are well‐established and validated assessment instruments for the functional aspects of many neurological diseases. We were motivated to understand why so many clinical studies failed to achieve statistical significance but had promising signals, suggesting false negative results.

Our analysis started from the key criteria for study success: meaningful improvement compared to control and its statistical significance. Based on the formula for power calculation, we focused on the size of treatment effect, variability, statistical threshold, and to a lesser extent, sample size. We recognized that numerous factors could influence the therapeutic effect and variability in neurology drug trials. The categorized list of variability sources we provided in Supplement should aid study sponsors to tackle the known unknowns in upcoming studies.

Neurology trial designs for disease‐modifying treatments require more attention than those indications that have simple, objective, and quantitative endpoints. We highlight a mismatch between the heterogeneity and the current use of rating scale total scores. Neurological conditions are biopathologically heterogeneous, which manifests in varied functional symptoms. It is natural to assume DMT efficacy will also be heterogeneous in the functional domains and the magnitude of effects. Raw sum scores may capture multiple functional changes in a patient but are less effective than specific assessments, a case of a jack of all trades, master of none. Domain subscores, for example, offer concrete alternative methods we can explore. Study arm averages of total scores could further weaken signals by offsetting one patient's improvement by a worsening of another within the arm. We should be cautious using total scores as primary endpoints unless we know that disease modification in the target indication improves all rating scale items equally.

We warned against relying on a larger sample size to achieve statistical power. Especially in rare and atypical diseases involving more sites may increase the variability, which undermines the power. Study sponsors should first improve the endpoint sensitivity and contain variability, after which they can calculate the necessary sample size to achieve appropriate power.

We also need to set statistical thresholds to embrace heterogeneity and achieve optimum results for all stakeholders. Disjunctive co‐primary endpoints can accommodate heterogeneous populations with smaller sample sizes, and such an approach may be suitable for rare diseases with no approved cure. Early regulatory discussions on appropriate tolerance for Type I and II errors are needed.
