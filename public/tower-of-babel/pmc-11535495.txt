Genome-wide analysis of the PYL gene family and identification of PYL genes that respond to cold stress in Triticum monococcum L. Subsp. Aegilopoides
Authors: Xin Liu, Xin Zhao, Yue Yan, Mang Shen, Ruizhang Feng, Qin Wei, Lianquan Zhang, Minghu Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abscisic acid (ABA) is a key plant hormone that regulates plant growth and response to stress . Pyrabactin resistance 1-like (PYR/PYL) proteins are ABA receptors involved in the initial steps of ABA signaling. Triticum monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides is an important germplasm resource for wheat. In this study, we identified 15 PYL genes from T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides and found that they were distributed across five chromosomes. Based on phylogenetic analysis, we classified these genes into three subfamilies. Members of each subfamily have similar gene structures and contain a common motif. Further analysis revealed that the promoters have multiple hormone-related elements. We found 7, 33, and 49 collinear gene pairs in three different ploidy wheat species (T. urartu, T. turgidum and T. aestivum), indicating that PYL genes are relatively conserved during the process of wheat polyploidization. Additionally, interaction networks and miRNA targets were predicted, revealing interactions between PYL proteins and key components of the abscisic acid signaling network. miR9666b-3p may serve as a central factor in PYL involvement in the abscisic acid network. Through RNA-seq analysis and qPCR validation, three genes (TbPYL2, TbPYL5, and TbPYL12) were found to potentially play a role in cold stress. These findings lay the groundwork for further research on PYL genes in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77962-x.

Introduction

Modern breeding has made significant advancements in enhancing yield and quality; however, it has also led to a reduction of genetic diversity in crops, resulting in the loss of many valuable genes1. In recent years, wild relatives and landraces of wheat have gained considerable attention for their potential to reveal novel alleles that confer stress resistance2,3. T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides (2n = 2x = 14, AbAb) is an ancient grain that is believed to be among the earliest domesticated crops. T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides exhibits robust adaptability and stress resistance in natural environments, enabling it to survive and reproduce in adverse conditions such as drought and high temperatures. This high ecological adaptability and genetic diversity make it a valuable component of wheat germplasm resources4–7. Although T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides is not extensively cultivated in the present day, it continues to be a significant resource for researchers and wheat breeders aiming to create new wheat strains. Its genetic diversity offers valuable material for the development of superior wheat varieties.

Abscisic acid (ABA) plays a crucial role in plant growth and development, including cell elongation and division, seed desiccation tolerance, seed dormancy, germination, leaf senescence, and fruit ripening8–10. Moreover, it is the primary hormone responsible for plant cell tolerance to various biotic and abiotic stresses11,12. In the abscisic acid (ABA) signal transduction pathway, ABA receptors play a crucial role. These receptors are generally composed of the regulatory components, including regulatory of ABA receptor (RCARs), pyrabactin resistance 1 (PYR1), and PYR1-like protein (PYL). They initiate the first step in the ABA signaling pathway and activate downstream ABA signal cascades13. PYLs are an important component of the core signaling pathway PYLs-PP2Cs-SnRK2. Their binding to ABA alters their conformation, thereby inhibiting the enzymatic activity of protein phosphatase 2 C (PP2C), which subsequently leads to the release of serine/threonine protein kinase SRK2 (SnRK2)14–16. Activated SnRK2 phosphorylates downstream transcription factors, such as abscisic acid response elements binding factors (ABRE/ABF), thus activating the abscisic acid signaling pathway and stress response processes17,18.

Plants rapidly accumulate ABA in response to non-biological stresses such as drought, high temperatures, low temperatures, salinity, and heavy metals. The excess synthesized ABA binds to PYL, initiating abscisic acid signal transduction and prompting plants to respond to stress19. PYL has been identified as a core regulatory factor in ABA signaling across various species, including Arabidopsis20, wheat21, rice22, maize23, soybean24, and cotton25. Overexpression of AtPYL4 significantly enhances drought tolerance in A. thaliana26. Overexpression of OsPYL5 enhances drought and salt tolerance in rice27. The overexpression of VaPYL9 enhanced cold tolerance in tomatoes by reducing membrane damage and improving the activities of enzymatic antioxidant defense systems28. ApPYL1 and ApPYL2 interact with ApMYB44 to participate in the cold signaling pathway, and their overexpression in A. thaliana enhances cold tolerance29. These findings demonstrate the role of PYL genes in enhancing tolerance to abiotic stresses. Therefore, identifying and characterizing PYLs in these plants is crucial role in understanding their function and the ABA signaling transduction pathway. However, T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides as an important germplasm resource of wheat, has limited information available regarding its PYL genes.

Based on the latest genomic information, this study conducted comprehensive genome-wide identification and characterization of the PYL gene family in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides, identifying 15 TbPYLs. Computational methods were employed to analyze gene structure, motif composition, subcellular localization, protein 3D models, miRNA targets, and expression analysis. The collinearity of PYL genes in wheat with different ploidy levels was investigated, along with the role of cis-regulatory elements in the promoter regions during stress response and development. By subjecting T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides to cold stress treatment, the study elucidated the response of the gene family under cold stress, providing profound insights into the PYL gene family in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides.

Results

Characterization of TbPYL gene family

Using the methods described below, 15 PYL genes were identified in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides. The protein lengths range from 176 (TbPYL4) to 232 (TbPYL14) aa, molecular weights from 18.89 (TbPYL5) to 24.97 (TbPYL14) kDa, and isoelectric points from 4.5 (TbPYL4) to 8.99 (TbPYL13). Among them, 66.67% of the proteins are acidic (pI < 7). The instability index ranges from 27.84 (TbPYL7) to 58.76 (TbPYL12), and the aliphatic index ranges from 70.22 (TbPYL7) to 90.24 (TbPYL12). Except for proteins TbPYL2, TbPYL5, and TbPYL8, all others are hydrophilic (GRAVY < 0) (Table.S1, Fig. 1). These genes are unevenly distributed across 5 chromosomes in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides, with 6 PYL genes identified on chromosome 3Ab, while chromosomes 5Ab and 6Ab do not harbor any PYL genes (Fig. 2a). Based on the results from the three subcellular localization prediction tools, it was determined that among the identified PYL proteins, 12 are localized in the cytoplasm (80%) and 3 are localized in the chloroplast (20%). Gene structure and conserved motifs analysis

Comparative analyses of exon-intron structures can reveal conserved and divergent features, providing clues about the evolutionary history and functional diversification of gene families. The sequence logo indicates that the PYL gene family has high protein sequence similarity, especially within the red box areas (Fig. 3a). Our findings indicate that the exon-intron structure of the wheat PYL genes varies across the different subfamilies. Specifically, genes in Group I and II have a single exon, while those in Group III, TbPYL2, and TbPYL14 contain three exons (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, TbPYL11 is an exception, as it contains two exons. To identify conserved motifs within the TbPYL family, we analyzed the full-length protein sequences of the 15 TbPYLs using the MEME suite. Our analysis revealed a total of 7 distinct motifs. Motif 1 is common to all TbPYL genes, while motifs 2 and 3 are shared by Group II and III. For Group I, motif 4 and 6 are specific. Additionally, motif 5 is present in all members of Group III and in Group II members except for TbPYL9 and TbPYL15 (Fig. 3b). Additionally, we predicted the motifs of PYL genes in A. thaliana, T. urartu, T. turgidum, and T. aestivum. The results indicate that motifs 1–3 were conserved across all five species, suggesting that these motifs play a crucial role in the functional regulation of PYL genes. Furthermore, we identified 4, 5, and 6 motifs similar to those in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides in T. urartu, T. turgidum, and T. aestivum, respectively. Promoters analysis of TbPYL genes

To further understand the transcriptional regulation and potential functions of TbPYL genes, the cis-regulatory elements in the promoters were predicted. This analysis identified a total of 46 distinct cis-elements within the 2000 bp upstream region from the transcription start sites of the TbPYL genes, in addition to the core cis-elements (Table.S2). These cis-elements play important roles in biological processes such as stress responses, hormone responses, metabolic regulation, and growth and development. Among the identified elements, transcription factor binding sites are the most numerous, followed by light responsive elements and stress responsive elements (Fig. 4a). The G-box is the most abundant light-responsive element, present in all TbPYLs. All PYL genes contain at least one hormone responsive element. Among them, abscisic acid responsive element (ABRE) is the most abundant element, with TbPYL8 containing as many as 14 ABRE elements (Fig. 4b), which is consistent with the function of PYL as an abscisic acid receptor. Meanwhile, auxin-responsive elements (AuxRR-core, TGA-element), MeJA-responsive (CGTCA-motif, TGACG-motif), gibberellin-responsive (GARE, P-box), and salicylic acid-responsive elements (TCA-element) were found in TbPYL genes. Additionally, this analysis also revealed various types of stress response elements, such as anoxic specific inducibility (GC-motif), low-temperature-responsive (LTR) elements and drought-inducibility (MBS), defense, and stress responsiveness (TC-rich repeats). The findings suggest that TbPYL genes may play a role in the transcriptional control of plant growth, hormone regulation, and stress responses. To identify key cis-regulatory elements, we calculated the difference between the expected random number of CREs and the identified number. Fourteen PYL genes exhibited an increase in the number of ABRE elements compared to the expected values, with a range of 6.79 to 95.17 times higher. Certain PYL genes contain specific cis-elements. Notably, the promoter region of TbPYL1 harbors seven GC-motif elements, which is 610 times the expected random occurrence (Table.S3). Prediction of protein 3D structure

In this study, three-dimensional structural homology modeling was performed on the amino acid sequences of 15 TbPYL gene family members from T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides (Fig. 5a). The analysis of Phyre2 indicated that all 15 TbPYL proteins exhibited α-helices and β-sheets as their main structural elements. The polyketide_cyc2 domain (PF10604) constitutes a substantial portion of 15 PYL proteins and encompasses crucial structures such as α-helices. Moreover, motif 1, which is conserved across all PYL proteins, is situated within a β-sheet structure, suggesting that it may contribute to the overall stability and functional integrity of the PYL proteins (Fig. 5a). For further verification, the ProSA server was used to evaluate potential errors within the protein models. The analysis revealed negative z-values within a confidence zone, deviations that could be experimentally discerned through X-ray and NMR spectroscopy (Table.S4). In T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides, the predicted number of protein channels ranges from 1 to 7, with TbPYL5 having the highest number of channels (Table.S4). We predicted the protein binding pocket (Fig. 5b), whose surrounding amino acid residues determine its physicochemical properties, morphology, and function. Many functionally important residues are located in this pocket, including tyrosine (TYR), phenylalanine (PHE), asparagine (ASN), lysine (LYS), aspartic acid (ASP), glutamic acid (GLU), isoleucine (ILE), and leucine (LEU).Fig. 5The 3D structure modeling (a) and ligand binding site prediction (b) in TbPYLs.

Interaction networks analysis of TbPYLs

To gain a better understanding of the biological functions and regulatory networks of TbPYLs, we performed a prediction of their protein-protein interactions (PPIs). Consistent with our expectations, the majority of proteins predicted to interact with TbPYLs are important components of the ABA signaling pathway, such as PP2C, ABI1 and DDA1 (Fig. 7a). Based on function annotation, these TbPYLs interacting proteins can be divided into phosphatase 2 C family proteins (ABI1, ABI2, AHG1, AIP1, HAB1, HAB2, HAI3, PP2C and SAG113), DET1- and DDB1-associated protein (DDA1), and transcription factors (MYB44, MYB77 and MYC2). We also identified transcription factor binding sites for the PYL gene family and found that ERF was the most abundant class of transcription factors, followed by MYB, bZIP, C2H2 and LBD, among others, indicating that multiple transcription factors are involved in the regulation of PYL genes (Fig. 7b). The ERF family is primarily involved in the ethylene signaling pathway, while the PYL family plays a key role in ABA signaling. To investigate the potential association between ABA signaling pathways and miRNA regulation, we predicted putative miRNA targets of TbPYLs using the wheat miRNA database. Our analysis identified three miRNAs (tae-miR9666b-3p, tae-miR530 and tae-miR9780) that may target four TbPYL genes (Fig. 7c). miR9666b-3p may target three PYL genes, suggesting its potential importance in ABA signaling. To determine whether these miRNAs have homologous sequences in wild einkorn wheat, we used BLAST to search the genome. The results indicated that 64, 1, and 2 sequences (Table.S6) with high homology (homology ratio > 85%) and capable of forming hairpin structures were found, respectively.

Expression analysis of the PYL gene family

The expression profiles of TbPYLs in different tissues, including aerial parts, flag-leaf, spike, glume, grain and roots, were studied using RNA-seq data (Fig. 8a). The results indicate that these PYL genes exhibit tissue specificity. TbPYL1, TbPYL6, TbPYL7, TbPYL8 and TbPYL10 have higher expression levels in flag leaves compared to other tissues. TbPYL2 displays high expression levels in most tissues, while TbPYL4, TbPYL5, TbPYL9, TbPYL12, TbPYL13, and TbPYL15 are predominantly expressed at high levels in the glume. Only TbPYL14 is preferentially expressed in the spike. Additionally, consistent with previous studies, most PYL genes also exhibit varying levels of expression in grains. These results suggest that the TbPYLs play different roles in the growth and development of various tissues in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides.

The expression patterns of wheat TbPYLs under cold stress were also investigated based on RNA-seq data. TbPYL4, TbPYL6, and TbPYL14 were not expressed in the control and treatment groups, indicating that they were not involved in this process. Five PYL genes (TbPYL2, TbPYL5, TbPYL9, and TbPYL12) are induced by cold stress, with their expression levels showing varying degrees of increase compared to the control (Fig. 8b). TbPYL2 shows the most significant difference (TPM: 2.95–35.21). Based on the transcriptome data analysis, four T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides PYL genes (TbPYL2, TbPYL5, TbPYL9 and TbPYL12) were selected for further validation. qPCR analysis was conducted to examine their expression levels after 24 h of 4 °C treatment in seedlings. Among the four genes tested, the expression levels of TbPYL2, TbPYL5, and TbPYL12 significantly increased under cold stress treatment (Fig. 8c). Discussion

Abscisic acid (ABA) is a critical regulatory factors in regulating plant growth, development, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. The PYL receptor family perceives abscisic acid, which is a crucial component of the core abscisic acid signaling pathway that plays an indispensable role in plant growth and development8,10. T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides is an important wheat genetic resource that exhibits strong adaptability and stress tolerance in natural environments. Investigating the role of PYL family in abscisic acid metabolism and signaling pathways can provide valuable insights into its stress resistance mechanisms. Based on the genome-wide search method, we identified 15 PYL genes in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides. A phylogenetic tree was constructed with wheat and Arabidopsis, classifying the TbPYL family into three groups based on phylogenetic relationships, which is consistent with the classification of PYL genes in other plant species21,32. The groupings in the phylogenetic tree were supported by the exon-intron structure (Fig. 3b). The number and composition of conserved motifs vary among the PYL subfamilies. Motif 1 is common to all TbPYL genes, indicating a relatively conserved function within the family. Additionally, motif 4 and 6 are unique to group I, while motif 7 is found only in group II. This indicates that TbPYL genes within the same group may have similar functions.

The cis-elements present in plant PYL play a crucial role in responding to environmental stress and signaling pathways during plant growth and development and has been widely studied in various plants29,32. This analysis identified, in addition to the core cis elements, a total of 46 distinct cis-elements within the 2000 bp upstream region from the transcription start sites of the TbPYL genes. These cis-elements are essential for various biological processes. As a key factor in ABA signaling pathway, the PYL gene contains a large number of ABRE elements in its promoter region. Notably, TbPYL8 contains up to 14 ABREs, which may enhance its ability to better perceive ABA. Whole-genome duplication (WGD) and small-scale duplication events are not only major features of eukaryotic genome evolution but also primary drivers for the emergence of new functions in genomic and genetic evolutionary systems33. Three pairs of tandemly duplicated genes and two segmental duplication events were identified in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides. One round of WGD occurred during the origin of T. turgidum and two rounds occurred in the origin of T. aestivum34. Consequently, most collinear PYL genes in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides correspond to two or three genes in these two species. T. urartu is the donor of the A genome in common wheat35. Interestingly, there are only seven collinear genes between T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides and T. urartu, but there are 13 collinear genes with T. turgidum and T. aestivum.

The interaction network of PYL proteins with other genes was predicted using the STRING database. Studies have found that TbPYL proteins interact with several phosphatase 2 C family proteins. Among them, PP2CA plays a crucial role in controlling ABA signaling and negatively regulates plant growth, development, and responses to various biotic and abiotic stresses36. ABI1 and ABI2 encode homologous protein phosphatase 2 C, which are involved in abscisic acid signaling37. HAB1 is homologous to ABI1 and ABI2 and acts as a negative regulator of ABA signaling38. the LOB domain gene DDA1 suggests possible functions in auxin signalling and photomorphogenesis39. MYB77 and MYB44 can interact with PYL8 and enhance the ability to activate the transcription of auxin-responsive gene IAA1940,41. We also found that two TbPYLs (TbPYL6 and TbPYL13) interact with the aforementioned three auxin-related genes, suggesting that they may play an important role in linking abscisic acid and auxin signaling pathways in wheat. Additionally, this study predicted miRNA-targeted TbPYLs, with 4 PYL genes being targeted by 3 miRNAs. miR9666b-3p may be a central regulatory factor mediating the abscisic acid signaling network in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides, consistent with findings in wheat21. Integrating the protein interaction network and miRNA-mediated TbPYL pathways will help to better understand the ABA signaling pathway in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides.

Many plant PYL genes are reported to be expressed in various tissues, including seeds, roots, leaves, flowers, and fruits21. Transcription levels of PYLs were much higher in the seeds of oilseeds during germination compared to other tissues42. The transcription levels of PYLs were highly abundant in the latex of rubber trees43. In this study, TbPYL2 shows higher expression potential across six different tissues. Six genes (TbPYL4, TbPYL5, TbPYL9, TbPYL12, TbPYL13, and TbPYL15) are highly expressed in glumes, while five genes (TbPYL1, TbPYL6, TbPYL7, TbPYL8, and TbPYL10) are highly expressed in flag leaves. These results indicate that TbPYLs play distinct roles in the growth and development of various tissues of T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides. Cold stress can negatively impact seed germination, seedling development, and growth during the seedling stage, ultimately resulting in decreased grain yield44. The role of PYL genes in cold stress has been well documented. In Acer palmatum, ApPYL1 and ApPYL2 interact with ApMYB44 to respond to cold signaling pathways, thereby enhancing the plant’s cold tolerance43. Overexpression of VaPYL9 improves cold tolerance in tomato by regulating key genes in hormone signaling and antioxidant enzymes28. In this study, transcriptome data and qPCR validation indicated that cold treatment can induce the expression of certain PYL genes, such as TbPYL2, TbPYL5 and TbPYL12. This suggests that these genes may be involved in the cold stress response of T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and cold stress treatments

The experimental material used was T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides G52, provided by Professor Lianquan Zhang from the State Key Laboratory of Crop Gene Resources and Utilization at Sichuan Agricultural University. Twenty seeds of wild einkorn wheat G52 were placed in a 4 °C refrigerator for 24 h to break dormancy. The seeds were then transferred to culture dishes and incubated at room temperature for 7 days. After germination, ten seedlings were subjected to a constant temperature 4 °C incubation for 24 h, while the remaining seedlings were kept at room temperature. Leaf samples from seedlings subjected to room temperature and cold treatment were collected with three biological replicates per group, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was extracted from the leaves using the Plant RNA Extraction Kit-V1.5 (Chengdu Biofit Biotechnologies Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China). The concentration and purity of the RNA were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE).

Identification and chromosomal location of PYL gene family in T. Monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides

The reference genome of T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides was downloaded from DYRAD (10.5061/dryad.v41ns1rxj). The typical domain of PYL (Pfam: PF10604) was obtained from the PFAM database (http://pfam.xfam.org/). HMMER 3.045 was used for hidden markov model (HMM) search queries with the expected value (E-value) of 1e-5. Further confirmation of the PYL domain in potential PYL protein sequences was conducted using the NCBI-CDD web server (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/), SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/), and Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/). The biochemical parameters of TbPYLs proteins were computed using the ExPASy online tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The subcellular localization of TbPYLs proteins was predicted using WoLF-PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/), Cell-PLoc 2.0 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/) and DeepLoc 2.1(https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.1/). Finally, the chromosome locations of the 15 TbPYLs were extracted from the gff file and the chromosomal distribution map of these genes was created using Tbtools (v2.097)46.

Phylogenetic relationship, gene structure and conserved motifs analysis

The neighbor-joining (NJ) method in MEGA X47 was used to construct a phylogenetic tree for PYL proteins, with a bootstrap analysis for resampling set to 1000. The phylogenetic tree included PYL proteins from A. thaliana, wheat, and T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides. The phylogenetic tree file (*.nwk) from MEGA was uploaded to the iTOL website (http://itol.embl.de/) for better visualization of the phylogenetic tree. The gene structure information of PYL genes was extracted from the genome annotation gff3 file of T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides and visualized using TBtools (v2.097)46. Using the MEME online website (http://meme-suite.org/meme)48, the conserved motifs of TbPYLs proteins were analyzed, with the maximum number of motifs set to 10.

Synteny analysis for PYL genes

The reference genome files and gff3 annotation files for Triticum urartu, Triticum turgidum, and Triticum aestivum were downloaded from the EnsemblPlants database (http://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). Analyze the gene collinearity among T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides, T. urartu, T. turgidum and T. aestivum based on their amino acid sequences and chromosome locations using MCScanX software49. A synteny circos plot was created using TBtools (v2.097)46 and the Venn diagram was generated using EVenn (http://www.ehbio.com/test/venn/)50 to show the genes that exhibit synteny with wild einkorn wheat PYL genes across several species.

Prediction of protein pocket binding sites

The three-dimensional structures of the candidate TbPYLs in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides were predicted through Phyre2 server (http://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/phyre2/html/page.cgi?id=index). Protein molecular voids and pocket/channel numbers were estimated using the BetaCavity Web server (http://voronoi.hanyang.ac.kr/betacavityweb/). The ProSA server51 (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php) was employed to calculate errors, generate plots for protein structure validation, and validate the 3D models. Docking analysis of ligand-binding regions in the predicted protein models was performed using the P2Rank tool (https://prankweb.cz/), followed by visualization in PyMOL.

Prediction of protein networks and miRNAs targeting the TbPYL gene

Using the STRING database (http://string-db.org/cgi), identify the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of PYL genes based on orthologous genes between T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides and A. thaliana. Utilize the psRNATarget Server (https://www.zhaolab.org/psRNATarget/) with its default settings to search the T. aestivum miRNA sequence reference, using all cDNA sequences of the TbPYL gene family as candidate genes for predicting potential miRNAs. The sequences of predicted wheat miRNAs interacting with PYL genes will be retrieved from the miRBase database (https://www.mirbase.org/). Next, a BLAST search will be conducted in T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides genome. Sequences with over 85% similarity will be retained, and miRNAFold (https://tanuki.ibisc.univ-evry.fr/evryrna/mirnafold/) will be used to predict the hairpin structures. The predicted interaction network was displayed through Cytoscape software52.

Analysis of gene expression profiles

Download the transcriptome data of different tissues and cold stress treatments of T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides from SRA database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/) with accession numbers: PRJEB61155 and PRJEB21284. Use Trimmomatic53 to filter adapter sequences, Kallisto54 to align reads to the T. monococcum L. subsp. aegilopoides reference genome and count them, obtain expression data, and use TBtools (v2.097)46 to create a heatmap of gene expression levels.

Real-time RT-PCR

Actin was used as the reference gene to determine relative expression values, and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the CFX96 PCR System. The 10 µl reaction mixture included 5 µl of 2× TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus), 0.4 µl of each primer (10 µM), 1 µl of cDNA, and sterile double-distilled water to reach the final volume. The qRT-PCR cycling conditions were 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 39 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 58 °C for 30 s. A melt curve was obtained after the last qRT-PCR cycle: 95 °C for 5 s, followed by a gradual temperature increase of 0.5 °C from 65 °C to 95 °C. RT-PCR primers were designed on Primer Premier Software (version 5.0) and are listed in Table S7. A t-test was performed on the qPCR results using GraphPad Prism (v7.04), and a bar graph of expression levels was created.
