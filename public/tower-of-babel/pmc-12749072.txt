Integrative transcriptomics and network analysis reveals core genes driving meningioma pathogenesis and clinical outcomes
Authors: Youssef M Zohdy, Ali M Alawieh, Arman Jahangiri, Benjamin Siciliano, Leonardo Tariciotti, Alejandra Rodas, Justin Maldonado, Kimberly Hoang, Edjah Nduom, Brian M Howard, Daniel L Barrow, Aliaksandr Aksionau, Stewart G Neill, Zhexing Wen, Gustavo Pradilla, Tomas Garzon-Muvdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Meningiomas are the most common primary CNS tumors, often managed conservatively due to their benign histology. Molecular phenotyping is considered the current frontier in meningioma classification to predict progression and guide clinical management of adjuvant therapies. While several biomarkers have been studied, a definitive molecular prognostic panel has yet to be established—a gap this study seeks to address. Using patient-resected meningioma tumor tissues paired with normal meninges, transcriptomic analysis identified a subset of significantly differentially-expressed-genes(DEG) linked to cellular proliferation and immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment. Graph-theory analysis revealed a rich-club organization in the gene networks, with highly connected core nodes. Core gene expression levels significantly correlated with tumor WHO grading, achieving an ROC-AUC of 0.8 for distinguishing low-grade(grade-1) from high-grade(grades-2&3) tumors(P < 0.001). For clinical outcomes, core gene expression achieved an AUC of 0.96 for predicting tumor recurrence(P < 0.001) and 0.74 for patient survival(P = 0.018). This study employs an analytical pipeline integrating transcriptomic analysis of meningiomas with a graph-theory approach to identify core regulators within pathological tumor networks. This refined method facilitated the discovery of phenotype-encoding biomarkers, which accurately predicted histological grade and clinical outcomes. The identified core markers provide insights into meningioma-specific pathophysiological pathways and offer potential targets for therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29334-2.

Introduction

Meningiomas are the most common primary central nervous system tumors, accounting for 39.7% of primary intracranial tumors1. Given their typically benign histology, the primary management approach is often watchful waiting, reserving surgical intervention for growing or symptomatic tumors. Classically, meningiomas have been classified based on their histopathological appearance using the World Health Organization (WHO) grading system into 3 grades2. The consensus is that benign grade 1 tumors have an optimal prognosis with a low probability of recurrence. In contrast, grades 2 and 3 tumors tend to attain invasive phenotypes and are more likely to recur, necessitating postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy3. However, large cohort multicenter studies have shown that a subset of grade 1 tumors can recur or exhibit higher-grade-like behavior despite their histopathological appearance3–5. Conversely, patients with grade 2 and 3 tumors may experience postoperative quiescence, with or without adjuvant therapy3–5. Consequently, the exact utility of histological classification systems in guiding decision on post-operative management remains limited.

Based on accumulating molecular findings and advances in bioinformatic analyses, the WHO revised the meningioma grading criteria in 2021 to incorporate genetic fingerprinting in tumor classification, such as CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion and TERT promoter mutations6. Additional follow-up research has demonstrated the added benefit of comprehensive molecular analysis in meningioma classification and patient management based on various DNA mutations, copy number variations (CNV), methylation profiling, and cellular transcriptomics3,7–10. Translational work showed that targeted gene analysis could be used in patient risk stratification and management planning, allowing for better allocation of patients into treatment arms based on molecular and genetic profiling leading to better outcomes compared to histopathological classification systems3,7,8,11. However, despite the accuracy of subcellular tumor profiling, most studies have relied on statistical regression models and mathematical correlations. This has created a gap between the identified markers and the understanding of the exact mechanistic pathways, particularly within tumor transcriptomic research. Consequently, despite the high predictive ability of the identified tumor markers, their utility as therapeutic targets often remain unjustified.

In this study, we aimed to characterize meningioma gene expression dysregulation using graph theory concepts to identify core regulators within the pathological network and their correlation with tumor histopathological morphology and postoperative outcomes. Using differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified from a cohort of meningiomas from various anatomical locations, compared with normal meninges, we constructed a tumor-specific pathological network. Network analysis of the DEGs revealed a scale-free network topology with a subset of genes upon which the up- and down-regulated networks are mechanistically dependent. We then investigated the impact of the expression of these genes on tumor histopathological morphology. Finally, the findings were externally validated to confirm the stability of the identified core genes expression in meningiomas, in correlation with WHO grade, and clinical outcomes including tumor recurrence and overall patient survival. Ultimately, this study provides a reproducible pipeline for deciphering the mechanistic pathways of tumor transcriptomics and highlights the potential for therapeutic targeting of the disease (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Patient cohort and specimens

Samples were obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection of meningioma at Emory University Hospital between 2023 and 2024. All collected tissues exceeded the quantity required for pathological diagnosis and were obtained specifically for research purposes. Inclusion criteria were histologically confirmed meningioma, availability of sufficient high-quality tissue for molecular analyses, and complete clinicopathologic data. Consecutive sampling within the specified time frame, based on the inclusion criteria and tissue availability, was employed to minimize selection bias and ensure representativeness of the treated population. All samples were processed promptly and transported to the laboratory within 15 min of resection to preserve tissue integrity.

Prior to surgery, all patients underwent preoperative magnetic resonance imaging, which aided in intraoperative navigation and guided the surgeons during tumor resection and tissue collection. Resected tumor tissues were histopathologically analyzed and graded according to the WHO classification by an independent expert neuropathologist2. A total of 11 meningiomas were included in this study, resected from 9 patients; in two patients, two tumors were simultaneously resected from different anatomical locations (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, 3 normal meninges were resected and included in the analysis: 2 samples were obtained during meningioma resection away from the tumor site, and 1 sample was obtained during a cerebrovascular lesion surgery. Collected tissue was stored at -80° C in RNAlater (Cat. #AM7020) until processing.

The study protocol received approval by Emory University Institutional Review Board, and all research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participating patients provided informed consent for the collection of tumor tissue.

RNA extraction, purification

The frozen samples stored were thawed, and immediate steps were taken for RNA extraction. To ensure consistency, all samples were processed simultaneously to avoid any potential batch effects. RNA was extracted and purified from the patient specimens using the Direct-zol RNA Microprep Kit (Cat. #R2063), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Nuclease-free commercially available water was used during the RNA isolation process. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were immediately assessed using Nanodrop UV measurements. Following the measurements, all isolated RNA samples were stored at -80° C until sequencing.

Library preparation and next generation RNA sequencing

An RNA-seq library was generated utilizing the Illumina mRNA sample prep kit (Cat. #RS-100-0801) according to the manufacturer’s protocol in 3 technical replicates for each sample. The library constructs’ size and concentration were verified with a bioanalyzer before sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq6000. The raw reads’ quality was evaluated to confirm the success of the library preparation and sequencing processes.

RNA sequencing data analysis

Raw RNA reads processing was conducted following the validated nf-core bulk RNA-seq bioinformatics pipeline12. Adapter sequences and low-quality sequences were analyzed using FastQC13 and trimmed from the reading using TrimGalore14. The RNA-seq reads were aligned to the human reference genome hg38 using STAR v2.7.1115 and quantified using Salmon16. Data normalization and differential expression values were calculated with DESeq217 standard parameters in R (R Core Team, 2022). Genes with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P-value < 0.05 and a log2 Fold-Change (Log2FC) absolute value > 1 were identified as DEGs. Functional annotation and interactome analysis were conducted using the clusterProfiler18 package for Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes and cellular components.

Network construction and analysis

An interaction network was constructed using protein-protein interactions (PPI) data from the STRING database, which provides interaction information for proteins/genes within various species. A list of node-node interactions was used to construct a network for the identified DEGs, where the edges present PPIs from STRING database. Edges were then weighted based on gene co-expression data using normalized gene expression results from our study, calculated using the Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) algorithm19. The topology of this network was then analyzed using established graph theory principles, which were first described in social networks and have been recently applied to biological systems20. This analysis was performed with the igraph packages21 in R, and the networks were visualized using Cytoscape 3.2. We calculated the node-specific degrees (k), representing the number of edges (interactions) for each node. To determine if the resulting interaction network follows a scale-free, non-random pattern, we studied the power-law degree distributions of the networks and compared them to random networks with the same degree distribution following Viger and Latapy’s method22.

Rich-club analysis

A rich-club (RC) organization refers to a network structure where a group of high-degree nodes, known as the RC, have more interconnections among themselves than would be expected by random. To test for a RC organization, the RC coefficient ϕ(k) is calculated across the range of degrees in the network, according to a previously described method by Colizza et al.23. For a given degree distribution [k1, k2, …, kn], the RC coefficient for each degree k is determined by computing the ratio of the actual number of edges among nodes with degrees higher than k to the maximum possible number of edges among those nodes.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varphi\:\left(k\right)=\:\frac{2{E}_{>k}}{{N}_{>k}({N}_{>k}-1)}$$\end{document}

where N > k is the number of nodes with a degree higher than k, and E > k is the number of edges among those nodes.

To compute the normalized RC coefficient (ρ(k)), we generated 1,000 random networks as previously described and the average RC coefficient for those random networks was computed and used to calculate ρ(k).\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:\left(k\right)=\:\frac{\varphi\:\left(k\right)}{{\varphi\:}_{rand}\left(k\right)}$$\end{document}

A normalized RC coefficient ρ(k) greater than 1 indicates that the RC organization in the network is significant and not merely a result of the network’s degree distribution. Nodes with ρ(k) > 1 are classified as part of the RC i.e. hub nodes.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

To thoroughly assess the impact of DEGs on the overall network and RC genes, we utilized normalized expression values as inputs for PCA. This allowed us to identify the components that explain the variance in the data. We selected the up to three PCs that collectively explained 90% of the variance across all measures. The Eigengene, which measures the eigenvector for gene expression, was then calculated for each sample. PCA and eigengene computation were conducted using the PCAtools24 and tmod25 packages in R, respectively.

External validation data

For external validation of the results, data was obtained from the National Center of Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GEO database, accession numbers GSE1658426, GSE13666127, GSE16181, GSE4780, GSE580378, GSE4329028, GSE7438529. This accession’s data included microarray gene expression profiles of 68 meningiomas. The study metadata also included the tumor histopathology, WHO grade, local recurrence and overall survival (OS) for each patient. No batch correction was applied, as each dataset was analyzed independently, with discriminatory and predictive metrics reported separately.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using R and GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.1) software. Parametric testing was the default method, except where otherwise specified. Numerical data and histograms were presented as the mean ± SD. Group comparisons utilized one-way ANOVA (or two-way ANOVA for analyses spanning multiple time points), followed by Bonferroni’s test for multiple comparisons. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Student’s t-test (one- or two-tailed) was employed for comparing two groups. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to evaluate correlations. Proportional statistics were analyzed using the chi-square test. Fisher’s exact test was utilized to determine the p-value for the enrichment of annotation terms between the network and the RC. Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) were constructed to analyze correlations between tumor characteristics and gene expression and coefficient of determination were computed using Nagelkerke R2 Value. The eigengene, derived from PC1, PC2 and PC3, served as the gene expression input for both the overall network of DEGs and RC genes. Tumor grade was not included in the models predicting clinical outcomes, allowing assessment of the predictive value of network-based transcriptomic analysis independent of histopathological tumor characteristics at the time of resection. To assess model fitness, tumor WHO grades were dichotomized into low grade meningiomas (WHO grade 1) versus higher grade meningiomas (WHO grad 2 and 3), and ROC analysis was conducted and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was measured. Optimal ROC cut-off was determined using Youden’s index. Confusion matrices were employed to compute the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV). The Hazard Ratio (HR) was calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel method to assess the relative risk associated with differential genes expression.

Results

Identification of meningioma pathological differentially expressed genes

To identify genes of pathological significance in meningiomas, transcriptomic data from patient-resected tumors from different anatomical locations was compared with normal meninges. Initially, PCA plot was constructed showing clear separation of meningiomas versus normal meninges clusters (Fig. 2a). Interestingly, differential clustering was observed between cranial and skull base meningiomas recapitulating previous findings30 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Clusterogram and heatmap analysis then revealed clear separation of meningioma DEGs into two major up- and down-regulated clusters (Fig. 2b). At an adjusted P-value of < 0.05 and an absolute log2FC of 1, a total 725 up-regulated genes and 928 down-regulated genes were identified (Fig. 2c). GO functional annotation of the DEGs was then conducted. The up-regulated biological processes included cell growth, cell-membrane transport, and vascularization, among others, all of which indicated increased cellular activity (Fig. 2d). On the other hand, down-regulated processes showed a tumor immunosuppressed microenvironment with down-regulation of leukocyte migration, proliferation, and activation (Fig. 2e). Focused analysis of the down-regulated DEGs GO, showed a highly significant suppression of leukocyte mediated immunity and leukocyte migration with normalized enrichments scores of -2.25 and − 1.98, respectively (adjusted P-value < 0.01) (Fig. 2f).

Analysis of the meningioma pathophysiologic network

Given that the main goal of this work was to leverage disease network topology for hypothesis-driven tumor stratification, we first constructed the interaction networks for the identified up- and down-regulated DEGs using PPI data from the STRING database. The up-regulated network comprised 702 gene nodes and 1,468 interactions, while the down-regulated network included 876 nodes and 1,764 interactions (Fig. 3a, b). Analysis of the power-law distribution curve revealed that both networks exhibited a scale-free topology, characterized by a structured arrangement with central hub nodes having high connectivity and a decreasing connectivity towards the network periphery (Supplementary Fig. 2a, c). Additionally, the networks displayed significantly higher clustering coefficients compared to randomly generated networks with similar degree distributions (Supplementary Fig. 2b, d), indicating a small-world organization with central connectivity facilitating interactions among the nodes.

Based on the presence of a small-world organization within the networks, we investigated whether they also exhibit a RC organization, indicating highly interconnected central nodes that are well-linked with each other and with other non-core nodes. The constructed up- and down-regulated networks of meningioma DEGs demonstrated a RC organization, as evidenced by an increased RC coefficient (φ(k)) with rising node degrees (Fig. 3e, f). To assess the significance of this RC structure, we compared the observed RC coefficient to that of randomly generated networks with similar degree distributions using a normalized RC coefficient (ρ(k)). This analysis revealed a significant RC effect between degrees 18–110, peaking at 85 degrees for the up-regulated and between 20 and 240 degrees with a peak at 150 for down-regulated network (Fig. 3e, f). The most prominent component of the RC was identified where the φ(k) plateaus, coinciding with the highest normalized RC coefficient, as detailed in the methods. The subnetworks of nodes forming the RC core are highlighted in Fig. 3c & d and a full list of gene with differential expression analysis is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Comparing the connectivity of RC core genes to non-RC components showed a significantly higher degree for RC genes, with significant increase compared to other nodes in both up- and down-regulated networks (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3c, d) (Supplementary Table S3).

External validation of rich-club genes expression for predicting meningioma WHO grade and clinical outcomes

To analyze the generalizability of our findings, we then analyzed the expression of the RC genes in external datasets. Initially, a dataset including 68 meningiomas (43 WHO grade 1, 19 grade 2, and 6 grade 3) was analyzed. The computed eigengenes, derived from PC1, PC2, and PC3 of the up- and down-regulated RC genes, demonstrated high accuracy in classifying tumors according to WHO grade (Supplementary Fig. 3). A GLM incorporating the expression of both up- and down-regulated RC genes effectively distinguished lower-grade meningiomas (grade 1) from higher-grade tumors (grades 2 and 3), achieving an AUC of 0.798 (95% CI: 0.686–0.911, P < 0.0001) and an R² value of 0.356 (Fig. 4a).

We then assessed the robustness of this approach in predicting disease progression, including local tumor recurrence and overall patient survival, using the same external validation dataset. For meningioma recurrence, the up- and down-regulated RC genes independently demonstrated significant predictive ability (Supplementary Fig. 3). The combined GLM (both up- and down-regulated RC genes) further improved prediction, achieving an AUC of 0.959 (95% CI: 0.908–1, P < 0.0001), an R² value of 0.53 (Fig. 4b), and a PPV of 80% (95% CI: 44–97) (Supplementary Table S4).

For the prediction of OS, the up- and down-regulated RC genes individually did not achieve statistical significance. However, the combined model significantly predicted OS, with an AUC of 0.736 (95% CI: 0.56–0.91, P = 0.018) and R² value of 0.18 (Fig. 4c), and a PPV of 66.7% (95% CI: 35–90) (Supplementary Table S4). Finally, at an optimal eigengene cut-off, patients with high differential expression of RC genes had a significantly higher risk of mortality (log-rank test, P = 0.013), with a HR of 6.1 (95% CI: 1.443–25.67) (Fig. 4c).

We then expanded the validation of the identified RC genes’ ability to classify WHO tumor grade and predict local tumor recurrence by applying them to additional external validation datasets. Each dataset was analyzed independently, enabling robust multi-center, multi-cohort validation (Supplementary Table S5). The average ROC–AUC for distinguishing low-grade (WHO grade 1) from high-grade (WHO grades 2 and 3) tumors was 0.812, while the average ROC–AUC for predicting local tumor recurrence was 0.819.

Discussion

In this study, we present an analytical framework that integrates transcriptomics with graph theory to examine the molecular characteristics of meningiomas and their role in prognostic stratification. By constructing a network of pathological gene interactions in meningiomas, we identified a scale-free topology with central genes in both up- and down-regulated networks, forming a core subnetwork that drives the meningioma network pathology. The existence of such organization in the networks suggests a hierarchical structure where a few hub genes exert significant control over network functionality, supporting the idea that these hubs could be used in predicting tumor behavior and strategically targeted for therapeutic intervention. In fact, the identified core genes effectively classified meningiomas according to the WHO grading system in external validation datasets. Moreover, the core genes demonstrated discriminative ability for clinical outcomes, including tumor recurrence and OS.

In the past decade, the value of classifying meningiomas based on molecular and genetic signatures has gained significant attention due to its higher accuracy of stratifying patients and predicting clinical outcomes compared to traditional histopathology3,7–10. This shift is based on the understanding that histopathological morphology provides a snapshot of the tumor’s current state, while molecular signatures at the subcellular level reflect the tumor’s overall trajectory31. In depth genetic and molecular analysis of meningiomas through DNA mutations8 and methylation10, CNV analysis32, and RNA sequencing3 have significantly advanced our knowledge of meningioma tumor biology. Integrating both unsupervised and supervised3,27 bioinformatics has led to the identification of therapeutic targets based on distinct biological mechanisms9,10 and improved the evaluation of tumor recurrence and OS3,27. The robustness of meningioma cellular transcriptomics has been demonstrated through its utility in tumor classification33, predicting recurrences27, response to radiotherapy3 and OS34. Nonetheless, given the quantitative nature of transcriptomic data, most studies have primarily focused on the use of statistical methodologies and mathematical modeling to identify the most significant tumor markers3,27,34. This approach restricted the ability to fully capture the biological complexity of cellular gene expression, treating RNA abundance as merely numerical data rather than considering its functional role within cellular biology. Consequently, this constrains the predictive capacity of the models and limits the translatability of the identified markers in therapeutic medicine, restricting their applicability beyond predictive modeling within the same population.

In the field of cellular biology, the application of graph theory has greatly enhanced our understanding of PPIs by uncovering network organization23,35,36. Biological networks typically exhibit a scale-free topology, characterized by an uneven distribution of nodes, with some nodes having higher connectivity and influence. Identifying these highly connected nodes is vital for understanding disease pathophysiology and identifying effective drug targets, particularly in cancer research. The application of network analysis to advance our understanding of meningiomas has been explored in previous research, highlighting the robustness of these methods30,37–40. These studies have demonstrated the scale-free topology of the meningioma pathophysiological networks, confirming its stability and reliability. However, none of these studies implemented a robust, replicable method for identifying network hubs to better guided prognostic testing or therapeutic targets. Most studies also relied on co-expression correlations rather than confirmed gene-gene or protein-protein interactions, which can lead to inaccurate interpretations of the biological implications. Furthermore, there was a tendency to combine up-regulated and down-regulated genes into a single network. From a biological standpoint, this approach limits the understanding of the results and the identification of pathway-specific targets.

To identify key nodes in a network, RC analysis exploits the tendency of certain nodes in a scale-free network to form a highly interconnected ‘club,’ which serves two key functions: enabling rapid signal transmission among club members and creating a resilient network where the loss of one RC node can be compensated by others, thereby minimizing overall disruption23,35,36. These highly connected nodes not only interact with each other but also with less central nodes, ensuring efficient signal propagation across the network. Understanding the structure of the RC is therefore crucial. Given the central role of the RC genes, their expression levels reflect the trajectory of the overall network, enabling the prediction of tumor behavior and, consequently, clinical outcomes. While from a therapeutic perspective, drugs that target the majority of RC nodes can leverage their high connectivity to spread the therapeutic effect to other core nodes, while the RC’s broad links with the rest of the network ensure that this effect disseminates throughout the entire system, resulting in a widespread impact23,35,41.

The use of RC analysis in our study approaches meningioma transcriptomics from a biological perspective while overcoming the limitations of previous meningioma network-analysis studies. After identifying the tumor DEGs, we constructed two separate networks for up-regulated and down-regulated genes followed by network functional annotation. This segregation allowed the identification of network-specific cores based on gene directionality. We then enhanced network construction by applying graph theory concepts, which provide a robust and replicable method for isolating network cores. This approach demonstrated promising discriminative ability for tumor grade classification and for predicting clinical outcomes, including recurrence and OS. The robustness of identified RC gene panel is evidenced by the fact that, despite the small size of the discovery cohort, the results were still generalizable during external validation. Although tumor location (e.g., cranial versus skull base) is known to influence molecular subtypes and clinical outcomes in meningiomas, our study was designed to identify shared, location-independent network signatures. By including tumors from a range of anatomical sites—cranial, skull base, intraventricular, and spinal—we aimed to uncover a unifying molecular signature, thereby enhancing the generalizability of our findings and establishing an anatomically agnostic molecular framework.

To better understand the impact of the constructed networks, we examined the up- and down-regulated RC gene panels from a biological gene-level perspective. Functional annotation of the up-regulated network revealed that the primary roles of the network genes were centered on enhancing cellular proliferation and promoting tumor vascularity. This finding prompted further investigation into the key players within the network, which could serve as vital therapeutic targets. The homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B is crucial for classifying meningiomas as WHO grade 3 by promoting increased cell division through the disinhibition of the CDK4/6-CCND1 complex6,42. Notably, CCND1 was significantly increased and identified as a RC gene in the up-regulated network, alongside CDH1, which was also recognized as a key gene. Prior biological studies and mathematical modeling have shown that a cyclic increase in CDH1 is essential for the pathological activity of the CDK4/6-CCND1 complex in driving cell division43,44. Accordingly, previous data combined with our findings emphasize the value of biological insights in tumor transcriptomics over statistical correlations for outcome predictions, patient stratification, and therapeutic strategies. The analysis of the CKD4/6-CCND1 axis is one example of the utility of network analysis in biologically annotating the disease pathological network. Nonetheless, several other identified up-regulated RC genes have been independently reported throughout previous meningioma studies including BMP45, FOXM146, ERBB247, VIM31, and TOP2A48, among others. The ability of the RC analysis to independently recover previously validated meningioma-specific genes underscores its biological relevance and highlights its effectiveness in identifying mechanistically important genes. In addition to confirming known markers, the analysis revealed several understudied genes that may represent contributors to meningioma pathophysiology and potential therapeutic targets, warranting further investigation.

Conversely, the down-regulated network was analyzed to identify genes with reduced activity, which may contribute to tumor progression. Tumor microenvironment immune inhibition has been well-documented across various solid tumors and has led to the successful use of immunomodulation therapies49,50. Similarly, the immune infiltration and characterization of meningiomas have been also documented in the literature30,33,40. From a transcriptomic perspective, previous studies have demonstrated a significant reduction in immune response-associated genes in grade 2 and 3 meningiomas compared to grade 133. These findings highlight the potential of targeting these pathways as a novel therapeutic strategy for high-grade meningiomas and underscore the need for a more comprehensive biological analysis of the immune expression network within these tumors. A recent study observed a trend toward decreased expression of IL-6, IL-10, and CCL2 in meningiomas—genes that were identified as RC nodes in our network analysis51. Collectively, these insights reinforce the importance of further exploring immune-related pathways to improve the understanding and treatment of meningiomas.

Using transcriptomic network analysis, our study identifies a panel of meningioma-specific genes that show potential for predicting patient outcomes. By employing a two-tiered approach—a small, mechanistically informed discovery phase followed by independent validation—our study presents a robust, meningioma-specific gene panel with clear clinical relevance that complements large-scale predictive modeling efforts. These findings provide valuable insights into postoperative meningioma biology and suggest potential avenues for identifying those at higher risk of recurrence and poorer outcomes. Limitations

The main limitation of this study is the small sample size of the discovery cohort, which may affect the robustness of the findings. Additionally, the number of control meninges samples was limited, and one was derived from a cerebrovascular case, which could affect differential gene expression analyses. Nonetheless, while control selection followed previously published pipelines, and the control cohort size is comparable to the discovery tumor cohort; this limitation should be considered when interpreting the results. Although cross-referencing our differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with previously published meningioma DEGs from multiple GEO datasets demonstrated strong concordance, the limited sample size may still influence differential expression and network structure. Additionally, Tumor samples from different anatomical sites in the same patient were treated as independent samples. While their transcriptomic profiles differed, this approach may introduce potential bias in the differential expression. While our network-based systems biology approach and validation in larger, multi-center external datasets support the robustness and generalizability of our findings, larger prospective studies will be required to confirm and extend these results. Notably, the RC gene panel demonstrated promising predictive performance in the external dataset, suggesting potential relevance across different gene expression platforms, though its clinical utility requires further validation. However, precision at clinically relevant thresholds (e.g., PPV) remains uncertain due to the limited number of outcome events, emphasizing the need for larger, prospective validation studies.

The use of network-based methods allowed the focus on biologically connected gene clusters, which are often more resilient to sample size constraints when derived from high-quality data. Still, large-scale, multicenter validation studies will be necessary to confirm and extend these findings across broader patient populations. Due to technical differences between the discovery and validation datasets—RNA-Seq versus microarray—a common GLM could not be directly transferred. Therefore, to assess predictive performance, we trained a new GLM on the external dataset using only the predefined RC genes, without further feature selection. While this maintains biological consistency, it introduces a limitation regarding true out-of-sample generalizability of a single, locked model. Another limitation is that our analysis focused on predicting overall tumor recurrence rather than the more standardized 5-year progression-free survival (PFS). This was due to limitations in the clinical annotations of the external dataset, which did not provide exact timing of recurrence. While not equivalent to 5-year PFS, the model’s ability to accurately predict recurrence during the follow-up period suggests the gene panel may still be relevant for more standardized endpoints, pending future validation. Furthermore, our analysis is based on transcriptomic data, which does not capture post-transcriptional or post-translational modifications that can significantly influence protein function. Additional research is needed to explore causative roles and therapeutic implications of the identified genes beyond their association with clinical outcomes.
