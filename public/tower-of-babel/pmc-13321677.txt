Real-world effectiveness and safety of CGRP monoclonal antibodies in elderly migraine patients: a Korean multicenter study
Authors: Soohyun Cho, Mi-Kyoung Kang, Heui-Soo Moon, Mi Ji Lee, Soo-Kyoung Kim, Hong-Kyun Park, Woo-Seok Ha, Min Kyung Chu, Young-Eun Gil, Byung-Su Kim, Soo-Jin Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have demonstrated significant efficacy in general migraine populations, real-world data specifically focusing on elderly patients remain limited. This multicenter study aimed to evaluate the long-term effectiveness and safety of CGRP mAbs in Korean migraine patients aged 65 and older.MethodsThis multicenter, retrospective real-world study analyzed 107 patients aged ≥ 65 years initiated on CGRP mAbs (galcanezumab or fremanezumab) at nine Korean university hospitals between January 2022 and April 2025. Effectiveness was measured by the proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 30%, ≥ 50%, or ≥ 75% reduction in monthly headache days (MHD) at 3, 6, and 12 months. Response rates were reported using an intention-to-treat (ITT) framework in which non-attendees were conservatively classified as non-responders, with the observed-case analysis presented as a sensitivity analysis. Safety was ascertained at each follow-up visit through open-ended clinical interview and patient self-report; adverse events were adjudicated for drug relationship by the treating clinician. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors of ≥ 50% response at 3 months.ResultsOf 107 enrolled patients, 103 reached the 3-month time point, 93 reached 6 months, and 61 reached 12 months by the data lock. On an ITT basis, the ≥ 50% response rate was 38.8% (40/103) at 3 months, 40.9% (38/93) at 6 months, and 31.1% (19/61) at 12 months. The corresponding observed-case rates were 48.2%, 67.9%, and 70.4%, respectively. A linear mixed-effects model confirmed a significant reduction in MHD over time (F = 48.912, p < 0.001). Female sex was a significant positive predictor of ≥ 50% response at 3 months (adjusted OR 3.21, 95% CI 1.04–9.93, p = 0.043), while prior failure of OnabotulinumtoxinA was a negative predictor (adjusted OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.03–0.95, p = 0.043). One all-cause death (ruptured dissecting aneurysm) occurred during follow-up and was adjudicated as unrelated to study drug; no other serious adverse events were reported. Documented adverse events were mild and included constipation and injection site reactions.ConclusionAlthough long-term follow-up data were available for only a limited number of patients, a substantial proportion of those who continued treatment demonstrated a response of at least 50%. These results imply that CGRP-targeted therapy could be an effective and well-tolerated preventive treatment option for older migraine patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02410-2.

Introduction

Migraine is a leading cause of disability globally, and as populations age, its management in older adults has become an increasingly critical clinical challenge [1, 2]. Although migraine prevalence typically decreases after the fifth or sixth decade of life, it remains significant, affecting approximately 7% of women and 3% of men over the age of 65 [3, 4]. In the elderly, migraine often presents with distinct clinical features such as reduced pain intensity, decreased attack frequency, or the late-onset aura which can complicate accurate diagnosis and effective management [5].

Managing migraine in older adults is exceptionally complex due to age-related physiological changes, such as decreased drug clearance, and a higher prevalence of comorbidities including hypertension, cardiovascular disease, small vessel disease, and diabetes [6]. Traditional oral preventive medications, such as beta-blockers, tricyclic antidepressants, and anti-epileptic drugs, are often poorly tolerated in this population due to the risk of adverse effects like cognitive impairment, sedation, and orthostatic hypotension [7, 8]. Furthermore, triptans for acute treatment are generally contraindicated in patients with ischemic heart disease or stroke, which are common in the elderly [9]. However, delayed treatment or underuse of medication for migraines can lead to an exacerbation of migraines and associated conditions, as well as reduced effectiveness of treatment [10]. Consequently, there is a profound unmet need for safe and effective migraine-specific therapies for older adults.

The development of monoclonal antibodies targeting the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) or its receptor (CGRP mAbs) has represented a paradigm shift in migraine prophylaxis [2, 11]. These therapies offer high target specificity, minimal drug-drug interactions, and favorable safety profiles, making them particularly advantageous for older patients managing polypharmacy [12]. However, most pivotal randomized clinical trials (RCTs) for CGRP mAbs restricted enrollment to patients aged 65 years or younger or included only a small proportion of older participants, leaving a gap in evidence-based guidelines for the elderly [12, 13]. While recent real-world studies have begun to support the effectiveness and safety of CGRP mAbs in older adults, evidence in Asian populations remains scarce, particularly regarding long-term outcomes and predictors of treatment response [2, 12, 14].

To address the paucity of real-world long-term data on CGRP mAbs in older Asian adults, we conducted a multicenter retrospective study of patients aged ≥ 65 years treated at nine Korean university hospitals. The objectives were to evaluate the real-world effectiveness and safety of galcanezumab and fremanezumab in this population and to identify baseline clinical factors associated with achieving a ≥ 50% reduction in monthly headache days at 3 months.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a multicenter, retrospective, real-world study at nine university hospitals. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Uijeongbu Eulji Medical Center (IRB No. UEMC 2024-12-007-001) and the ethics committees of all other participating multicenter institutions. Given the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived, and all patient data were anonymized. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

The inclusion criteria specified adult patients aged 65 years or older with a confirmed diagnosis of migraine, based on the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition, who received CGRP mAbs between January 2022 and April 2025 [15]. Patients were eligible only if the index CGRP mAb (galcanezumab or fremanezumab) represented their first exposure to any anti-CGRP therapy; prior treatment with another CGRP mAb or with a CGRP receptor antagonist was an exclusion criterion. Analyses were performed on patients who completed follow-up assessments at 3, 6, and 12 months and had available data on monthly headache days (MHD). The use of MHD instead of monthly migraine days reflects the real-world clinical setting and accommodates the atypical presentation of migraine in older adults, in whom attacks frequently lack one or more classical features for example, bilateral or non-pulsatile pain, milder pain intensity, and reduced or absent accompanying symptoms such as nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia leading clinicians in routine practice to record such episodes as headache days rather than migraine days.

Data collection

We collected demographic data (age, sex, and body mass index [BMI]), as well as lifestyle factors including smoking and alcohol consumption. All comorbidity data were ascertained from each center’s electronic medical record. Medical comorbidities, including vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia), cardiovascular conditions (angina), and cerebrovascular events (infarction, hemorrhage, and transient ischemic attack [TIA]), were recorded from documented diagnoses in the medical records. Anxiety and depression were diagnosed by the treating headache physicians using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 questionnaires, which were administered as part of routine clinical practice at each headache clinic.

Regarding headache profiles, we recorded the age of onset and detailed migraine phenotypes, including pain laterality (unilateral), quality (pulsatile), and aggravation by physical exertion. Accompanying symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia were quantified by the number of concurrent symptoms. MHDs were primarily recorded by patients using their headache diaries (paper or app-based) during each one-month interval. At each follow-up visit, the treating headache specialist reviewed the diary together with the patient and entered the headache day count into the electronic medical record (EMR); for this retrospective analysis, we extracted the final EMR-recorded counts. Patients were not re-contacted for the purposes of this study. Baseline MHD was assessed based on the one-month period immediately prior to the first administration of CGRP mAbs. Disease burden was measured using MHD at baseline. Furthermore, treatment history and concomitant therapies were analyzed, including the specific type of CGRP mAb (galcanezumab or fremanezumab), prior or concurrent OnabotulinumtoxinA (BoNT-A) treatment, and the use of traditional oral preventives.

Treatment outcomes

We analyzed the effectiveness and safety of CGRP mAbs in older adults with migraine. Effectiveness outcomes included changes in MHD from baseline at 3, 6, and 12 months, as well as the proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 30%, ≥ 50% and ≥ 75% reduction in MHD following the initial injection at each follow-up visit. Safety and tolerability of CGRP mAbs were evaluated by monitoring adverse events at the 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month follow-up visits. Adverse events were collected through systematic clinical interviews and patient self-reports during each visit. Active safety probing was documented for 44 of 83 measured charts at 3 months and 17 of 56 at 6 months; in the remaining records adverse events were neither described as present nor explicitly recorded as absent, and absence of documentation cannot be equated with absence of an event. Furthermore, treatment persistence and patient survival were monitored throughout the study period to identify mortality.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized for the total population and compared between the ≥ 50% responder and non-responder groups at the 3-month follow-up. The normality of the continuous variable distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on the data distribution, and analyzed using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate based on the normality of the data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To evaluate treatment effectiveness, the proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 30%, ≥ 50% and ≥ 75% reduction in MHD from baseline was calculated at each follow-up visit (3, 6, and 12 months) using the following formula: [(Baseline MHD – Follow-up MHD) / Baseline MHD] × 100. A ≥ 50% reduction in MHD at the 3-month follow-up was used as the responder definition for both the baseline responder/non-responder comparison and the multivariable logistic regression analysis; the ≥ 30%, ≥ 50%, and ≥ 75% reduction thresholds were applied at 3, 6, and 12 months for the age-stratified subgroup analyses (60s, 70s, and 80s).

To identify clinical factors associated with a ≥ 50% reduction in MHDs at 3 months, univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Variables included in the analysis were age, sex, BMI, onset age, medical comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes), psychiatric symptoms (anxiety, depression), baseline MHDs, and treatment history (concomitant or previous BoNT-A treatment). Variables showing a p-value < 0.2 in the univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors. Results were reported as odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0.2.0.

For the effectiveness analysis, response rates were calculated under an intention-to-treat (ITT) framework. Patients who had reached a given follow-up time point by the data lock (April 2025) were retained in the denominator; patients whose index CGRP mAb date was too recent to have reached a given time point (administrative censoring) were excluded from that time point’s denominator only. Within the eligible cohort, any patient without observed headache-diary data at a given visit (“lost to follow-up”) was conservatively classified as a non-responder at that and all subsequent time points. Because the retrospective design did not permit reliable reconstruction of the individual reason for each case of loss to follow-up — i.e., treatment discontinuation due to inefficacy, treatment discontinuation due to adverse events, withdrawal of follow-up because the patient was doing well, or true loss of contact — all such patients were collapsed into a single “lost to follow-up” category. As a sensitivity analysis, response rates were also calculated using only patients with observed data at each time point (observed-case analysis), and a linear mixed-effects model with time as a fixed effect and a random intercept per participant was fitted to all available longitudinal MHD measurements to provide unbiased estimates under the missing-at-random assumption.

Results

The flow diagram of the study is illustrated in Fig. 1. Of the 107 patients originally enrolled, 103 had reached the 3-month time point, 93 had reached the 6-month time point, and 61 had reached the 12-month time point by the data lock of April 2025. The remaining patients at each time point (4, 14, and 46, respectively) had index CGRP mAb dates too recent to have reached that time point and were administratively censored. Within the eligible cohort, observed headache-diary data were available for 83 patients at 3 months (median follow-up 91 days), 56 patients at 6 months (median follow-up 182 days), and 27 patients at 12 months (median follow-up 471 days). Among the 27 patients with 12 months of follow-up, 12 have reached 18 months, and 7 have completed at least 24 months of observation. The corresponding numbers of patients without observed follow-up data (“lost to follow-up”) were 20 at 3 months, 36 at 6 months, and 34 at 12 months. One all-cause death occurred during follow-up: a patient in the 70s subgroup died of a ruptured dissecting aneurysm between the 3- and 6-month visits, adjudicated by the treating clinician as unrelated to study drug.

Patient characteristics

Among the 83 patients with observed 3-month follow-up data, 40 (48.2%) were classified as responders (≥ 50% reduction in MHD) and 43 (51.8%) as non-responders (Table 1). The cohort was predominantly female (77.1%) with a mean age of 69.7 ± 4.8 years. Vascular risk factors were prevalent, including hypertension and dyslipidemia, and approximately 20% of patients had a history of anxiety or depression. The baseline MHD was high (median 30 days, IQR 22–30), and a small proportion of patients had been previously treated with BoNT-A. Detailed baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, along with comparisons between the responder and non-responder groups, are presented in Table 1; no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups for variables entered into the univariable analysis.

Of the 107 patients enrolled, 61 were eligible for 12-month assessment by the April 2025 data lock; of these eligible patients, 27 (44.3%) had observed follow-up data and 34 (55.7%) did not return for evaluation.

Clinical effectiveness over time

Among patients who remained on treatment and were evaluated at each visit, the proportion achieving a ≥ 50% reduction in MHD tended to be maintained or further improved over time (Table 2; Fig. 2). Under the ITT framework, the ≥ 50% response rate was 38.8% (40/103) at 3 months, 40.9% (38/93) at 6 months, and 31.1% (19/61) at 12 months; the corresponding ≥ 30% rates were 54.4%, 49.5%, and 39.3%, and the ≥ 75% rates were 22.3%, 17.2%, and 14.8%. The corresponding observed-case rates restricted to patients with available data were 48.2% (40/83), 67.9% (38/56), and 70.4% (19/27) for the ≥ 50% threshold. A linear mixed-effects model with time as a fixed effect and a random intercept per participant confirmed a significant overall reduction in MHD over time (F = 48.912, p < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis by age category

Analysis by age subgroups (60s, 70s, and 80s) showed that response was apparent across all elderly age categories among patients with observed data (Fig. 3), although denominators differed because of eligibility and attrition. Of the 10 patients aged ≥ 80 years, 10 were eligible at 3 months, 10 were eligible at 6 months, and 8 were eligible at 12 months by the data lock; within these eligible numbers, observed data were available for 6, 5, and 2 patients respectively. Under the ITT framework, ≥ 30% response rates in the 80s subgroup were 60.0% (6/10), 50.0% (5/10), and 25.0% (2/8) at 3, 6, and 12 months, and ≥ 50% response rates were 30.0% (3/10), 40.0% (4/10), and 12.5% (1/8).

Predictors of 3-month treatment response

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with a ≥ 50% responder rate at 3 months (Table 3). In the multivariable model, female sex was independently associated with a favorable treatment response (OR 3.21, 95% CI 1.04–9.93, p = 0.043). Conversely, a history of previous BoNT-A treatment was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of achieving a 50% response at 3 months (OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.03–0.95, p = 0.043). Age, BMI, baseline MHD, comorbid anxiety or depression and CGRP mAb type (galcanezumab vs. fremanezumab) did not significantly predict treatment response in the final model.

Safety and tolerability

The treatments were generally well-tolerated throughout the 12-month study period. There were no treatment-related deaths; one all-cause death (ruptured dissecting aneurysm) occurred during the study period and was adjudicated by the treating clinician as unrelated to study drug, given the patient’s pre-existing vascular risk factors. At the 3-month follow-up (n = 44), reported adverse events included constipation (n = 2), injection site reaction (n = 1), dizziness (n = 1), and insomnia (n = 1). At the 6-month visit (n = 17), adverse events were limited to constipation (n = 2) and injection site reaction (n = 1). None of the documented adverse events led to permanent treatment discontinuation. No serious cardiovascular events were observed.

Discussion

This multicenter, retrospective study yielded several key findings regarding the real-world application of CGRP mAbs in elderly migraine patients: (1) Among patients who remained on treatment and continued to be evaluated, response rates tended to be maintained or further improved over time. Under the ITT framework, ≥ 50% response rates were 38.8% (40/103) at 3 months, 40.9% (38/93) at 6 months, and 31.1% (19/61) at 12 months, with corresponding observed-case rates of 48.2%, 67.9%, and 70.4%; the true population-level response likely lies between these bounds, and a linear mixed-effects model confirmed a significant overall reduction in MHD over time (F = 48.912, p < 0.001), (2) ITT response rates in patients in their 60s and 70s broadly mirrored those of the overall cohort, while findings in the small 80s subgroup were exploratory only, (3) Multivariable logistic regression identified female sex as an independent positive predictor of a ≥ 50% response at 3 months (OR 3.21), whereas previous experience with BoNT-A treatment was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of achieving this response (OR 0.18), and (4) The treatments were generally well-tolerated; documented adverse events were mild and included constipation and injection site reactions, none of which led to permanent treatment discontinuation, and no treatment-related deaths occurred.

Under our ITT analysis, the ≥ 50% response rate in our cohort was 38.8% at 3 months, 40.9% at 6 months, and 31.1% at 12 months; in the parallel observed-case analysis, the corresponding rates were 48.2%, 67.9%, and 70.4%. The true population-level response likely lies between these two bounds, with the linear mixed-effects model providing an intermediate, statistically appropriate estimate that is robust to missing data under the missing-at-random assumption. Our findings are broadly aligned with prior real-world evidence in older adults receiving CGRP mAbs. Muñoz-Vendrell et al. reported a 6-month ≥ 50% response rate of 57% in a multicenter Spanish cohort of 162 patients aged ≥ 65 years; in their analysis, patients who discontinued treatment for inefficacy or intolerance at month 3 (n = 28) were classified as non-responders at month 6, while frequency variables were calculated among the 134 patients with available month-6 data and a last-observation-carried-forward sensitivity analysis was performed [12]. Our observed-case 6-month rate (67.9%) is comparable to theirs, while our ITT rate (40.9%) is somewhat lower, reflecting differences in the analytical handling of patients without observed follow-up data. Gonzalez-Martinez et al. similarly reported a 50% response rate of 57.5% in older patients (n = 73 of 114 with available data at weeks 20–24) under CGRP mAb therapy, with 30%, 50%, and 75% response rates of 68.5%, 57.5%, and 21.9%, respectively, at this time point [16]. They observed that the ≥ 50% response rate at 8–12 weeks was significantly lower in elderly patients than in younger controls (31.6% vs. 48.2%, p = 0.015) but became comparable by weeks 20–24 (57.5% vs. 60.8%, p = 0.811). One plausible interpretation, applicable to patients who remained on treatment and continued to be evaluated, is that older adults may take longer to reach peak response on CGRP mAb therapy, possibly because of age-related differences in monoclonal antibody pharmacokinetics or slower stabilization of the CGRP pathway in a brain characterized by long-standing chronicity and structural aging [16, 17]. Notably, the Gonzalez-Martinez cohort showed substantial follow-up loss at later time points (73/114 at 20–24 weeks and 44/114 at 44–48 weeks), an attrition pattern similar to ours; this similarity reinforces the need for cautious interpretation of long-term real-world outcomes in this population and supports our parallel reporting of ITT and observed-case results.

Our age-stratified analyses provide preliminary observations on CGRP mAb response across elderly age strata. Response rates in patients in their 60s and 70s broadly mirrored those of the overall cohort under the ITT framework, suggesting a consistent treatment effect across these age strata. The apparent increase in response rate at 12 months in the 70s subgroup should be interpreted with caution, as this estimate was derived from only 11 eligible patients and most plausibly reflects selective retention of responders rather than population-level cumulative benefit. Findings in patients aged ≥ 80 years are reported as exploratory and hypothesis-generating only; with only 2 of 8 eligible patients providing observed 12-month data. Larger prospective registries enriched for patients aged 80 years and older will be required.

Our study identified two key clinical predictors that significantly influenced the likelihood of achieving a ≥ 50% response rate at 3 months: female sex and prior failure of BoNT-A. In our multivariable model, female sex was associated with a higher likelihood of achieving a ≥ 50% response at 3 months (adjusted OR 3.21, 95% CI 1.04–9.93, p = 0.043). The wide confidence interval and moderate absolute sample size warrant caution in interpreting this association. The direction of this association is consistent with emerging evidence of sexual dimorphism in CGRP-targeted therapy response. In a recent FDA-data subpopulation analysis pooling over 2,000 women and 380 men across all approved CGRP mAbs and atogepant trials, Porreca et al. reported a male-to-female treatment-effect ratio of 0.55 in episodic migraine prevention, indicating that the preventive treatment effect in men was approximately half as large as that in women [18]. These observations suggest that CGRP-dependent pathophysiology may play a more dominant role in women than in men. Because most participants in our cohort were postmenopausal, our finding is consistent with the hypothesis that CGRP-dependent mechanisms remain a durable feature of the migrainous brain in older women, even as hormonal influences are expected to be substantially attenuated. To our knowledge, our cohort provides one of the first direct observations of sex-related differential CGRP mAb response specifically in elderly migraine patients. Mechanistically, females exhibit higher levels of circulating CGRP and increased sensitivity of CGRP receptors in the meningeal vasculature compared with males [19, 20], and the underlying architecture of the trigeminovascular system, characterized by a higher density of CGRP-containing fibers and distinct receptor expression patterns, may remain a durable feature of the migrainous brain in older age [16]. The female-sex association observed in our study should be considered hypothesis-generating until confirmed in larger prospective cohorts with adequate male representation and stratification by menopausal status.

A history of prior BoNT-A treatment was associated with a lower likelihood of achieving a ≥ 50% response at 3 months (adjusted OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.03–0.95, p = 0.043). Because patients who had previously received BoNT-A are likely to represent a subgroup with greater overall disease burden or refractoriness to multiple preventive strategies, prior BoNT-A exposure in our cohort should be interpreted as a marker of treatment-refractory disease rather than as a causal negative predictor of CGRP mAb response [21]. A specific limitation of our retrospective design is that the number and type of all prior preventive failures was not uniformly available, which precluded us from formally controlling for prior-preventive burden in the regression model.

Safety is a paramount concern in the geriatric population due to age-related physiological changes and polypharmacy. In this study, treatments were well-tolerated with no treatment-related mortalities or serious cardiovascular events. Although one death due to a ruptured aneurysm occurred, it was considered unrelated to the CGRP mAb treatment given the patient’s pre-existing vascular risk factors. The most common adverse events were constipation and injection site reactions, which are consistent with the known safety profile of the CGRP class. Given that traditional preventives like beta-blockers or tricyclic antidepressants often cause sedation or orthostatic hypotension in the elderly, CGRP mAbs offer a significantly safer alternative due to their non-systemic action and minimal drug-drug interactions. Beyond CGRP mAbs, recent evidence on gepants further supports the clinical utility of CGRP-targeted therapies, particularly given their favorable long-term tolerability profile and minimal risk of vasoconstriction [22].

Limitations

The primary strength of this study is its multicenter design and the inclusion of long-term follow-up data exceeding 12 months for a significant subgroup, providing rare insights into the durability of CGRP mAb treatment in Asian elderly populations. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the primary endpoint for evaluating treatment effectiveness was based on changes in MHD rather than monthly migraine days. This selection was necessitated by the incompleteness of monthly migraine days data within the retrospective records. Furthermore, elderly migraine patients frequently present with distinct clinical features, such as reduced pain intensity or fewer accompanying symptoms, which often lack the classic diagnostic markers of migraine. Second, the retrospective design did not permit reliable reconstruction of the individual reason for each case of loss to follow-up from the medical record. We were unable to systematically distinguish among (i) treatment discontinuation due to inefficacy, (ii) treatment discontinuation due to adverse events, (iii) withdrawal of clinical follow-up because the patient was doing well, and (iv) true loss of contact. Each of these has different implications for both effectiveness and safety interpretation. Because individual cases could not be classified, all patients without observed follow-up data were conservatively treated as non-responders in the ITT analysis. This approach is likely to underestimate the true population-level response rate (because some non-attendees were probably patients who stopped attending because they were doing well), while the parallel observed-case analysis is likely to overestimate it because of selective retention of responders. The true response rate likely lies between these two bounds, and the linear mixed-effects model which provides unbiased estimates under the missing-at-random assumption represents an intermediate, statistically appropriate estimate of the longitudinal MHD trajectory. The convergence of these three analyses on a clinically meaningful effect lends some robustness to our overall conclusions, but the absolute magnitude of long-term effectiveness should be interpreted within this uncertainty range. Third, the sample size decreased substantially at successive follow-up intervals. Of the 107 patients enrolled, 103, 93, and 61 patients were eligible for assessment at 3, 6, and 12 months, respectively, based on their index treatment date relative to the April 2025 data lock. Within these eligible cohorts, observed data were available for 83, 56, and 27 patients. This progressive reduction in the cohort with available data, combined with the inability to classify reasons for non-attendance, limits the statistical power and generalizability of the results observed at later time points, particularly within the age-stratified subgroups (most notably patients aged ≥ 80 years, of whom only 2 had observed data at 12 months). Subgroup findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating only. Fourth, the accuracy of our safety data is constrained by missing information. Adverse events were systematically probed and documented for 44 of 83 measured patients at 3 months and 17 of 56 at 6 months; in the remaining records, adverse events were neither described as present nor explicitly recorded as absent, and absence of documentation cannot be equated with absence of an event. Because safety outcomes were not uniformly recorded for the entire cohort, the incidence of mild adverse events, such as constipation or dizziness, may be underreported. In particular, we cannot exclude the possibility that some patients classified as “lost to follow-up” had in fact discontinued treatment because of an undocumented adverse event. The reported adverse event frequency in our cohort should therefore be regarded as a lower bound, and the absence of treatment-related serious adverse events, although reassuring, requires confirmation in larger prospective cohorts with systematic adverse event ascertainment. Finally, there remains a paucity of ultra-long-term data in our analysis. While a small subset of participants was followed for up to 24 months (n = 7), this sample is too limited to draw definitive conclusions regarding the multi-year durability and cardiovascular safety of CGRP mAbs in a frail elderly population. Further prospective research with larger, standardized cohorts is necessary to establish robust evidence-based guidelines for long-term treatment in this age group.

Conclusion

Despite the constraints of a limited long-term follow-up cohort, the data reveal that a significant majority of patients maintaining the regimen achieved a clinical response of 50% or greater. These findings underscore the potential of CGRP-targeted therapies as both a potent and tolerable prophylactic strategy for the elderly migraine population.
