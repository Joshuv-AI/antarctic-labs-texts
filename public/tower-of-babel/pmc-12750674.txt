ICOSL: more than a trigger of ICOS function
Authors: Ian Stoppa, Alessia Provera, Sara Sacchetti, Nausicaa Clemente, Deepika Pantham, Foteini Christaki, Cristina Vecchio, Anteneh Nigussie Sheferaw, Roberta Rolla, Chiara Dianzani, Salvatore Sutti, Umberto Dianzani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInducible T-cell costimulator (ICOS) and its ligand ICOSL are members of the CD28 and B7 receptor families that play central roles in immune regulation. While the role of ICOS in T-cell activation and differentiation has been widely studied, increasing evidence indicates that ICOSL, which is broadly expressed across both immune and nonimmune cell types, also functions as a signaling receptor. This review focuses on ICOSL-mediated reverse signaling and its ability to modulate the behavior of ICOSL-expressing immune and stromal cells. In vitro, ICOSL activation induces cell type-specific responses, most notably regulating cell migration. In vivo, ICOSL signaling influences key processes such as tumor growth, bone remodeling, wound healing, liver regeneration, and sepsis. Taken together, these findings expand the conventional view of ICOSL as a passive ligand and suggest that ICOSL is a versatile immune modulator with therapeutic relevance in cancer, inflammatory, and fibrotic diseases.Keywords: Reverse signalling, Immune checkpoints, Tumor microenvironment, Angiogenesis

Introduction

Inducible T-cell costimulator (ICOS, also known as CD278 or AILIM) and its ligand (ICOSL, also referred to as CD275, ICOS-L, ICOSLG, LICOS, B7H2, B7RP1, or GL50) are immune receptors belonging to the CD28 and B7 families, respectively, and serve as critical regulators of adaptive immunity [1].

Initially, identified by Redoglia et al. [2] as H4 and subsequently cloned by Hutloff et al. [3], ICOS is a T-cell costimulatory molecule selectively expressed on activated T cells, with more limited expression observed in subsets of dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages [2–5]. In contrast, ICOSL is constitutively expressed across a broader range of immune and nonimmune cell types, particularly under inflammatory conditions.

While ICOS and CD28 are members of the same receptor family and share considerable structural and functional homology, their downstream signaling outputs are distinct. CD28-mediated costimulation effectively induces the high levels of IL-2 required for full T-cell activation and expansion. In contrast, ICOS stimulation alone does not elicit high-level IL-2 expression; rather, its costimulatory efficacy in naive T cells is contingent upon the presence of exogenous IL-2 [6]. Hence, ICOS is generally regarded as a costimulatory signal that sustains T-cell responses following initial CD28-mediated priming. In addition to secondary lymphoid organs, ICOS signaling also plays a crucial role at peripheral inflammatory sites, where inducible ICOSL expression on resident and infiltrating cells may promote and maintain effector T-cell activity and is involved in retaining resident memory CD8+ T cells in peripheral tissues [7, 8].

Under homeostatic conditions, deficiency of either ICOS or ICOSL leads to defective formation of germinal centers and reduced production of class-switched immunoglobulins in both mice and humans, resulting in a clinical picture similar to common variable immunodeficiency. In ICOS deficiency, this is also associated with an increased incidence of autoimmune diseases [9].

The functional relevance of the ICOS/ICOSL axis has been primarily examined in the context of ICOS-mediated signals delivered to T cells. These studies have elucidated key roles for ICOS in the differentiation and function of multiple T helper subsets, regulatory T (Treg) cells, cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and innate lymphoid cells and have been the subject of extensive prior reviews (Fig. 1) [10–19]. However, less attention has been given to the signaling capacity of ICOSL itself. Indeed, emerging evidence indicates that ICOSL is not merely a passive ligand but can transduce intracellular signals upon ligation, a phenomenon referred to as “reverse signaling”, which modulates the activity of ICOSL-expressing cells, including antigen-presenting cells and other stromal and immune elements [5, 20–23]. More recently, ICOSL has been shown to interact with osteopontin (OPN), a multifunctional protein with both cytokine-like and matrix-associated properties. Importantly, ICOS and OPN bind to nonoverlapping regions on ICOSL, eliciting divergent, often antagonistic, cellular responses [24].

Despite the growing interest in this dual-ligand model, the role of ICOSL in transducing reverse signals that influence the behavior of ICOSL-expressing cells remains underappreciated and has yet to be examined in an integrated context. Understanding the mechanistic and functional implications of this signaling axis constitutes a primary objective of the present review.

Structural insights into the ICOSL interaction with ICOS and OPN

ICOS and ICOSL are transmembrane glycoproteins of the immunoglobulin superfamily, each comprising an extracellular, a transmembrane and an intracellular portion. Moreover, soluble forms (sICOS and sICOSL), consisting of the extracellular portion, can be detected in plasma and are increased in several autoimmune diseases. Their production has been ascribed to alternative transcription of the gene, alternative splicing of the mRNA, or proteolytic cleavage of the membrane form [25–27]. OPN is an intrinsically disordered protein abundant in the extracellular matrix and organic bone matrix; however, it is also produced as a soluble protein acting as an inflammatory cytokine, which can be detected in plasma. ICOS/ICOSL interaction

ICOS comprises an extracellular IgV-like domain, a transmembrane segment, and a 35-amino-acid cytoplasmic tail. The 199-amino acid sequence of ICOS shares 24% and 17% identity with those of CD28 and CTLA-4, respectively, and includes a 41-residue stretch that is highly conserved across species [28]. ICOS is expressed as a disulfide-linked homodimer and binds to ICOSL in a 1:1 receptor‒ligand stoichiometry. The binding interface is located on the apex of the ICOS IgV-like domain (Fig. 2) and is formed by the peptide motifs 114FDPPPFK120 and 66TKTKGS71, alongside the glycan linked to N110 [29].

The ICOSL extracellular portion consists of two domains: a membrane-distal IgV-like domain and a membrane-proximal IgC-like domain, followed by a transmembrane region and a cytoplasmic tail [19, 29]. ICOSL shares approximately 20% sequence homology with B7.1 and B7.2, the canonical ligands for both CD28 and CTLA-4. While its IgC-like domain harbors a cysteine residue, ICOSL does not appear to form disulfide-linked dimers on the cell surface. Instead, it tends to organize into noncovalent oligomers, both in solution and on the membrane—an oligomerization pattern reminiscent of B7.1 but not B7.2 [19, 29].

The binding site for ICOS on ICOSL resides in its membrane-distal IgV-like domain and involves aromatic and hydrophobic residues clustered along the GFCC’C’’ strand. Specifically, residues Y51, Y53, Q55, H66, L114, L116, and F122 are located in close proximity to the 114FDPPPFK12 motif of ICOS. Y53, in particular, plays a key role in this binding and is highly conserved across species. Interestingly, while Y53 is found in both human and murine ICOSL, as well as B7.1, it is replaced by phenylalanine—another aromatic residue with similar properties—in B7.2. In contrast, the IgC-like domain of ICOSL does not participate directly in ICOS binding, but it is required for the structural integrity and oligomerization state of ICOSL, which in turn affects the binding capacity of this ligand [19, 30].

The ability of ICOSL to oligomerize suggests that preassembled dimers or higher-order complexes on the surface of antigen-presenting cells may enhance its interaction with disulfide-linked dimers of ICOS on T cells [30]. Notably, the monovalent binding of ICOS to ICOSL has been implicated in promoting T follicular helper (Tfh) cell motility in germinal centers, facilitating the scanning of B-cell surfaces for cognate antigens. On the other hand, the bivalent binding of ICOS to ICOSL is approximately two orders of magnitude stronger than the monovalent interaction and likely drives the formation of highly organized and sustained contact zones (i.e., synapses) between T cells (typically Tfh cells) and B cells, as well as the delivery of robust costimulatory signals to T cells [19, 29, 30].

ICOSL is abundantly expressed on naïve B cells, and this expression is sustained when B cells are activated through simultaneous engagement of the B-cell receptor (BCR) and CD40, which are typically provided by T helper cells. However, stimulation through the BCR alone leads to a marked downregulation of ICOSL [31]. Upon ICOS ligation, most ICOSL is removed from the B-cell surface through proteolytic shedding mediated by ADAM family metalloproteases. In parallel, ICOS itself can be internalized from the cell surface following engagement with ICOSL [26, 31–34]. This reciprocal modulation is functionally relevant, as transgenic mice overexpressing ICOS in T cells exhibit a defective antibody response resembling that observed in ICOSL-deficient mice [34]. In cell lines, the interaction between ICOS and ICOSL promotes the downregulation of both molecules via endocytosis and trans-endocytosis, with ICOS being transferred into the cytoplasm of ICOSL+ cells [35].

OPN/ICOSL interaction

Recent studies have shown that ICOSL can also bind to OPN, a multifunctional protein secreted by a variety of immune and nonimmune cell types, which contributes to inflammation, T helper cell differentiation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis [24]. It is a highly acidic protein composed of 314 amino acids (aa), and its molecular weight ranges from 44 to 75 kDa, depending on alternative splicing events and extensive post-translational modifications (PTMs) [36, 37].

OPN binds to several receptors and is proteolytically cleaved by thrombin into two bioactive fragments, an N-terminal fragment (aa 17–167) and a C-terminal one (aa 168–314), each with distinct functional profiles. The N-terminal portion contains an RGD motif that mediates binding to several integrins, such as αvβ1, αvβ3, αvβ5, and α5β1. Thrombin cleavage also exposes two additional integrin-binding sites specific for α4β1, α9β1, and α4β7. The C-terminal fragment includes a CD44 binding site (Fig. 2) [36, 37].

Structurally, OPN lacks a well-defined secondary structure and can adopt either an extended, flexible conformation or a more compact globular structure comprising 8 𝛼-helices and 6 𝛽-sheets. This structural adaptability enables OPN to interact with multiple binding partners and assume diverse functional states [38]. Among others, one proposed role for OPN is to act as a molecular scaffold, simultaneously interacting with several distinct cell-surface receptors and modulating their functional interplay [38].

The potential interaction between OPN and ICOSL was first proposed by C. Dianzani, who found that ICOSL expression is required for the migratory response of tumor cells to OPN [23, 24]. This finding was later corroborated by ELISA-like and pull-down assays, which revealed a direct physical association between recombinant OPN and ICOSL. Subsequent conformational analysis of the OPN/ICOSL complex, performed via limited proteolysis and chemical cross-linking, revealed that the ICOSL binding site on OPN spans an extended region overlapping with the trypsin cleavage site (Fig. 2) [24]. Conversely, the OPN binding site on ICOSL is contained in its membrane-proximal IgC-like domain and does not overlap with the ICOS binding site, which is confined to the IgV-like domain. Upon binding to ICOSL, OPN undergoes a conformational change that exposes a positively charged segment downstream of the RGD domain—including residues K170, K172, and K173—which may influence the accessibility of the adjacent thrombin cleavage site [24]. Furthermore, ICOSL binding to OPN does not appear to sterically compete with ICOS binding, although it remains unclear whether it affects ICOSL oligomerization [24].

Interestingly, ICOSL also harbors a conserved RGD motif that selectively binds to integrin αvβ3 [39]. This motif is localized in the IgV-like domain in humans and in the IgC-like domain in mice. As OPN also binds to αvβ3, the capacity of both OPN and ICOSL to interact with this integrin raises the possibility of functional convergence or competition, particularly given reports that αv integrin activation on podocytes confers protection against kidney damage [39].

Another point of convergence between OPN and the ICOS/ICOSL pathway emerges from studies on Tfh cells, where ICOS costimulation facilitates the interaction between the p85α regulatory subunit of PI3K and an intracellular form of OPN (OPN-i), inducing its translocation to the nucleus. Once in the nucleus, OPN-i forms a complex with Bcl-6, protecting it from ubiquitin-dependent degradation [40].

Cell type-specific effects of ICOSL triggering

Reverse signaling refers to the process whereby ICOSL, which is typically considered a ligand, functions as a signaling receptor upon engagement by its partner, ICOS, thus reversing the usual direction of signal transmission [20]. This phenomenon has been investigated in vitro via the use of recombinant ICOS-Fc, a fusion protein composed of the extracellular domain of ICOS fused to the Fc portion of IgG1. The results demonstrate that the downstream effects of ICOSL triggering are cell type dependent, with inhibition of cell migration being the more frequently observed outcome. In contrast, ICOSL engagement by OPN seems to predominantly induce cell migration (Fig. 1; Table 2).

Dendritic cells

The first evidence that ICOSL stimulation by ICOS induces functional changes in ICOSL-expressing cells was provided by Tang et al. [20] in immature mouse DCs differentiated in vitro from bone marrow mononuclear cells. In this context, ICOS-mediated ICOSL triggering activates a p38-dependent reverse signaling pathway that enhances IL-6 production, endocytosis, antigen presentation, and T helper cell-mediated secretion of IFN-γ. Notably, these effects occurred independently of LPS-mediated stimulation, as they were also observed in TLR4-deficient mice [20]. Conversely, human mature DCs differentiated in vitro from monocytes and activated with LPS seem to respond differently. ICOSL triggering by ICOS-Fc led to reduced secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α, whereas it increased the production of IL-23 and IL-10 [22]. Functionally, ICOSL engagement enhances the ability of DCs to induce IL-17 secretion in allogeneic CD4+ T cells and to activate syngeneic MHC class I-restricted T cells in response to endocytosed antigens. These effects were attributed to increased antigen cross-presentation efficiency. Furthermore, ICOSL triggering impaired the migration of both immature and mature DCs and was associated with reduced expression of β-Pix, a guanine nucleotide exchange factor involved in cytoskeletal remodeling [22]. Subsequent studies by Hedl et al. [5] reported partially different results, showing that human monocyte-derived DCs express both ICOS and ICOSL and that the homotypic ICOS-ICOSL interaction potentiates Toll-like receptor (TLR)-induced secretion of TNF-α, IL-8, IL-1β, IL-23, and IL-10. This reverse signaling cascade involves a signaling complex consisting of protein kinase C (PKC), receptor for activated C kinase 1 (RACK1), and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK). Finally, a single-nucleotide polymorphism in ICOSL, rs7282490, was associated with decreased ICOSL expression and increased susceptibility to Chron’s disease due to impaired DC responses to the gut microbiota [5].

Taken together, these data suggest that ICOSL triggering by ICOS enhances the activity of mature DCs by increasing the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, particularly those involved in type 3 responses mediated by IL-17; by potentiating antigen cross-presentation involved in the activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells; and by inhibiting cell migration, which may favor DC interaction with T cells. OPN can also support DC activity by increasing cell motility and secretion of IL-12 involved in type 1 responses mediated by IFNγ, but the involvement of the OPN interaction with ICOSL in these activities is not known [41].

Macrophages

In human monocyte-derived macrophages polarized toward classical (M1) or alternative (M2) activation states, ICOSL is expressed at significantly higher levels in M2 than in M1 cells, whereas ICOS expression remains low in both subsets [42]. Surprisingly, ICOS-mediated triggering of ICOSL exerts opposing effects depending on the macrophage polarization state [42]. In M2 macrophages, it inhibits cell migration and β-Pix expression. In contrast, the same stimulus enhances both parameters in M1 macrophages. Furthermore, upon LPS stimulation, ICOSL engagement in M1 cells selectively upregulates IL-23 production while diminishing IL-6, TNF-α, IL-10, CCL3, and CCL4 expression. On the other hand, ICOSL signaling in M2 macrophages, under similar conditions, enhances the secretion of IL-6, IL-10, CCL3, and CCL4 while decreasing TNF-α. In the absence of LPS, ICOSL stimulation induces CCL3 and CCL4 secretion in both M1 and M2 cells, suggesting a context-dependent modulatory role [42].

Mouse spleen-derived macrophages have contrasting expression profiles, with ICOSL being expressed at higher levels in M1 cells than in M2 cells. In this model, ICOS-mediated ICOSL stimulation increases the motility of M2 cells while suppressing the migratory capacity of M1 macrophages [43].

These data suggest that, in macrophages, triggering ICOSL tends to increase cytokine production without influencing the polarization of M1 and M2 cells, and modulates their recruitment in tissues with different effects on macrophage types. OPN can also support macrophage activity by increasing cell motility, phagocytosis, and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, but the involvement of the OPN interaction with ICOSL in these activities is not known [44].

Osteoclasts

Osteoclasts (OCs), which are derived from monocytes in response to macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL), act as the main mediators of bone resorption and operate in concert with osteoblasts (OBs) to maintain skeletal homeostasis through tightly regulated remodeling cycles.

During the in vitro differentiation of human OCs, ICOS-mediated stimulation of ICOSL inhibits osteoclastogenesis in a reversible manner. In mature OCs, this same signaling pathway transiently reduces both cell and nuclear size, downregulates OC-specific gene expression, and decreases resorptive function. These phenotypic changes are accompanied by increased phosphorylation of p38 MAPK, implicating this kinase pathway in the negative regulation of OC activity [45].

These data indicate that, in OCs, triggering ICOSL inhibits cell differentiation and osteolytic function and this effect is reversed by removal of ICOSL stimulation. By contrast, OPN increases OC osteolytic function; however, this activity has been mainly ascribed to its interaction with CD44 [46, 47].

Tumor cell lines

Several tumor cell types express ICOSL, and this expression has been associated with increased invasiveness and poor prognosis in breast carcinoma and glioblastoma [48, 49]. This correlation has been attributed primarily to the ability of ICOSL to potentiate the suppressive function and accumulation of ICOS-expressing Treg cells infiltrating the tumor. However, recent findings suggest that the promigratory effect of OPN on ICOSL+ tumor cells may also contribute to this phenotype [23]. In contrast, ICOS-mediated stimulation of ICOSL exerts antithetical effects on tumor cell behavior. Specifically, it inhibits tumor cell migration induced by fetal calf serum (FCS) or OPN, reduces FAK phosphorylation and β-Pix expression, and inhibits cellular adhesiveness as well EMT [21, 23, 24]. The inhibitory effect on tumor cell migration depends on the expression level of ICOSL, since it is not detectable on cells expressing low levels. Furthermore, ICOSL expression impairs the anchorage-independent growth of tumor cells in soft agar, with no apparent effect on adhesion-dependent growth under standard culture conditions (Fig. 1) [24].

These data show that triggering ICOSL has key effects on tumor cell adhesiveness and migration, with opposite effects induced by the binding of ICOS or OPN. These interactions may play a role in tumor dissemination and metastasis.

Endothelial cells

A similar phenomenon is observed in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), where ICOS-mediated stimulation of ICOSL inhibits migration induced by either OPN or vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and impairs tubulogenesis initiated by OPN but not that driven by VEGF [21, 23, 24, 43, 50]. Moreover, ICOSL engagement on HUVECs diminishes the adhesion of various cell types, including polymorphonuclear cells that lack ICOSL expression, suggesting a potential role in modulating neutrophil recruitment to inflamed tissues. At the molecular level, ICOSL triggering downregulates ERK phosphorylation induced by either OPN or E-selectin stimulation [21].

These data suggest that, in endothelial cells, triggering ICOSL has key effects on cell adhesiveness, migration and tubulogenesis, with opposing effects induced by the binding of ICOS and OPN. These interactions may play a role in inflammation, tissue repair, tumor growth, and metastasis.

Tumors

Several clinical observations underscore the importance of the ICOS/ICOSL axis in oncologic immunity. In melanoma, immune checkpoint blockade with anti-CTLA-4 antibodies promotes the expansion of ICOS+ effector T cells, but the therapeutic benefit of this approach is lost in ICOS-deficient mice [51], indicating that ICOS is essential for antitumor immune responses. Similarly, in ICOSL-expressing colorectal carcinoma, high ICOS expression on tumor-infiltrating T cells is correlated with improved patient survival [52]. Conversely, high ICOSL expression levels have been reported in invasive breast carcinoma and glioblastoma, where it is associated with poor prognosis, possibly due to its role in supporting Treg expansion [48, 49]. Nevertheless, data from the mouse mammary carcinoma model 4T1 complicate this narrative: tumors engineered to overexpress ICOSL exhibit reduced growth and angiogenesis in vivo compared with their ICOSL-negative counterparts, although metastasis formation remains unchanged [24]. This dualistic role of ICOS can probably be attributed to its ability to increase both antitumor effector responses—mediated by Th1, Th17, Tfh, cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), and natural killer (NK) cells—and immunosuppressive pathways via protumor Tregs, thus contributing to a context-dependent balance between tumor rejection and immune tolerance.

Given the dual role of ICOS in enhancing both effector and regulatory T-cell functions, pharmacological modulation of this pathway may constitute a promising therapeutic approach: anti-ICOS agonist antibodies can indeed boost antitumor immunity, whereas antagonistic antibodies are known to limit Treg-mediated immunosuppression [13, 53–55]. OPN, likewise, supports Th1 and Th17 activity, but it can also suppress antitumor immunity by promoting the accumulation and function of tumor-associated myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Thus, targeted inhibition of ICOS and OPN may represent a novel checkpoint blockade strategy that is comparable to established therapies directed against CTLA4 and PD1 [55–59].

Consistent with in vitro data, systemic administration of ICOS-Fc in mouse models significantly reduces lung metastases following tail vein injection of tumor cells, which act on both exogenous tumor cells and the host vascular endothelium [23]. Moreover, endogenous ICOS appears to restrain metastatic progression, as B16 melanoma cells overexpressing ICOSL generate fewer lung metastases than their low-ICOSL counterparts do, and wild-type mice develop fewer metastases than ICOS-deficient mice do [60].

The effect of ICOS-Fc on primary tumor growth has primarily been studied in transplantable tumor models. Initial studies revealed no significant effect when free ICOS-Fc was systemically administered to mice bearing established subcutaneous melanomas. However, when ICOS-Fc was encapsulated in biocompatible biodegradable nanoparticles, enhancing tumor targeting via the “enhanced permeability and retention” (EPR) effect, it displayed pronounced antitumor activity in the same models. Comparable results have been reported in a subcutaneous model of multiple myeloma [50, 61].

Mechanistic studies suggest that ICOS-Fc exerts its antitumor effect primarily by disrupting tumor angiogenesis, a process likely mediated through ICOSL expressed on endothelial cells. This conclusion is supported by the persistence of antivascular effects in ICOS-deficient mice, ruling out any antagonistic effects on endogenous ICOS. In addition, ICOS-Fc has been shown to suppress the expression of the immunoregulatory markers FoxP3 and IL-10 under specific experimental conditions (Fig. 3) [50].

ICOS-Fc has also shown promise as part of combination treatment regimens. Compared with formulations containing only chemotherapeutic agents or ICOS-Fc alone, nanoemulsions containing subtherapeutic doses of the cytotoxic alkylating drug temozolomide, the kinase inhibitor sorafenib, and ICOS-Fc demonstrate superior antitumor activity against established melanomas [62].

These data suggest that ICOSL may play a role in tumor growth by modulating immune response, tumor angiogenesis, tumor metastasis, all of which may be inhibited by ICOS and promoted by OPN. Therefore, ICOS-Fc might act as an anti-tumor agent by affecting all these aspects of tumor biology.

Bone metabolism

Bone homeostasis relies on tightly regulated interplay between OBs, which deposit the bone matrix, and OCs, which resorb it. This balance is influenced by immune-derived signals, as RANKL—a key osteoclastogenic factor—can be expressed by activated lymphocytes and macrophages, particularly during inflammation. Indeed, proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, and IL-17, upregulate RANKL expression, leading to increased bone resorption. Excessive OC vs. OB activity is responsible for pathological bone loss, which manifests either systemically, such as in osteoporosis, or locally, as observed in rheumatoid arthritis, periodontitis, and osteolytic tumors (Fig. 4) [63].

Since ICOS is expressed by activated and effector T cells, it is plausible that the antiosteoclastogenic effects of ICOSL reverse signaling observed in vitro [45] may counteract proosteoclastogenic signals from RANKL, CD40L, and IL-17 expressed by activated T cells in vivo. The net effect likely reflects the balance between these opposing signals [64, 65].

Abundantly present in the extracellular matrix, where it contributes to hydroxyapatite deposition, OPN is a key regulator of bone remodeling. While predominantly produced by OBs, OPN is also expressed in OCs, where it directly influences their differentiation and resorptive functions. Evidence from OPN-deficient mice, which are resistant to ovariectomy-induced osteoporosis, supports a role for OPN in OC-mediated bone resorption [66, 67].

Consistent with the in vitro data in OCs, the treatment of mice with ICOS-Fc significantly reduced the expression of OC-specific markers and the development of both acute (RANKL-induced) and chronic (ovariectomy-induced) osteoporosis. Thus, the observation that ICOS-Fc reversibly inhibits OC activity makes this compound a promising candidate for antiresorptive therapy. Unlike conventional antiosteoporotic agents, such as bisphosphonates and anti-RANKL antibodies, which are toxic to OCs, ICOS-Fc appears to exert reversible inhibitory effects without compromising cell viability [45].

The dual effects of ICOS-Fc on tumor growth and osteolysis further highlight its potential use in the treatment of osteolytic tumors. In multiple myeloma, for example, malignant plasma cells display high ICOSL and low ICOS expression levels. In these patients, the serum levels of sICOS and sICOSL correlate with the tumor burden, and sICOS levels serve as independent prognostic factors for overall survival [61].

In addition to pharmacologic applications, ICOS-Fc could also be used to functionalize bone implants and cements aimed at mechanically supporting pathologic bone fractures and promoting bone healing. This approach could be particularly advantageous in patients with severe osteoporosis or osteolytic tumors, where ICOS-Fc may combine its antiosteolytic and antineoplastic properties to enhance both bone regeneration and tumor management [68–70].

These data suggest that ICOSL may play a role in bone metabolism, and its triggering on OCs by ICOS may counteract pro-osteolytic signals that cause pathological local and systemic osteolysis and may be involved in bone fracture repair. By contrast, OPN generally promotes bone formation, but its effect on ICOSL in this context is not known.

Liver damage

ICOS/ICOSL signaling is involved in liver tissue repair, with opposite effects in acute and chronic liver injury.

Acute liver injury

The liver is highly susceptible to chemical insults, including drug-induced toxicity from agents such as paracetamol, a common cause of acute liver injury [71, 72]. Hepatocellular damage, in this context, triggers a robust inflammatory response and the recruitment of activated T cells, leading to increased expression of both ICOS and ICOSL across several hepatic cell types [73]. In murine models of carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver injury, the ICOS/ICOSL axis appears to play a protective role by mitigating tissue damage and promoting tissue repair [73, 74]. More specifically, ICOS-deficient mice exhibit delayed recovery and increased mortality due to impaired liver repair, likely as a result of a deficient interaction between activated cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and ICOSL+ reparative macrophages [73]. Finally, exogenous administration of ICOS-Fc in ICOS-deficient mice prevents the loss of reparative macrophages and promotes hepatic regeneration, suggesting that the observed protection is driven by ICOSL reverse signaling rather than by classical ICOS-mediated costimulation [73].

Chronic liver diseases: metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)

MASLD is characterized by the accumulation of triglycerides within hepatocytes, and it can progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), a more severe condition where hepatocellular injury, inflammation, and fibrosis coexist [75]. MASH typically follows a chronic, progressive course culminating in cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [75].

As MASH worsens, the interplay between T cells and macrophages becomes more evident. This crosstalk is mediated, in part, by ICOS, ICOSL and OPN activity. Indeed, ICOSL-deficient mice exhibit reduced hepatic OPN expression and attenuated steatohepatitis [76]. The upregulation of OPN in both hepatic tissue and plasma correlates with liver fibrosis severity [77, 78], and MASH-associated macrophages acquire a unique OPN-expressing, profibrogenic phenotype that actively contributes to the scarring process [79].

The first study to investigate the mechanistic role of the ICOS/ICOSL system in MASH was by Torres-Hernandez et al. [80], who reported increased infiltration of ICOS+ γδT cells and ICOSL+ macrophages in the liver and showed that the ICOS/ICOSL interaction supported IL-17 A production by these γδT cells [80]. In this context, our recent work revealed that ICOS/ICOSL mediates crosstalk between CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and TREM2+ reparative macrophages, which strongly influences the functional properties of these macrophages and the development of experimental MASH [76]. Genetic ablation of ICOSL in this model disrupts the development of this reparative macrophage subset, mitigates steatohepatitis, and reduces the expression of profibrogenic mediators, thereby limiting the progression of fibrosis (Fig. 4) [76]. Consistently, additional data indicate that triggering ICOSL-mediated reverse signaling through ICOS-Fc exacerbates steatohepatitis in mice by modulating macrophage activity and the inflammatory response (unpublished data).

HCC development is also associated with an expansion of infiltrating ICOS+ activated T cells [73, 81, 82], as well as immunosuppressive ICOS+ Tregs, the latter of which are correlated with a worse prognosis and decreased overall survival [82, 83]. Spatial profiling of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) revealed frequent colocalization of ICOS+ Tregs with exhausted CD8+PD1+ T cells and ICOS+ monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) within the tumor microenvironment [82]. These findings suggest that ICOS signaling may contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive niche that promotes tumor progression and limits effective antitumor immunity.

These data suggest that ICOS-mediated triggering of ICOSL plays a role in liver repair during acute liver damage by promoting the involvement of reparative macrophages. By contrast, in chronic liver damage, this effect may result in chronic inflammation and fibrosis, which might favour the development of HCC. By contrast, the effect of ICOSL triggering by OPN in these contexts is not known, but in general, OPN has been shown to exert a profibrotic effect on the liver.

Skin wound healing

Wound healing is a complex, multiphase process that begins with the hemostatic phase, characterized by platelet aggregation, degranulation, and fibrin deposition. This is followed by an inflammatory phase involving the infiltration of neutrophils, M1 macrophages, and T lymphocytes. The subsequent repair phase consists of re-epithelialization, angiogenesis, macrophage polarization to the M2 phenotype, and the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, followed by collagen synthesis and extracellular matrix deposition. The final remodeling phase includes collagen and vascular maturation and regression [84]. The ICOS/ICOSL axis plays a significant role in this process. Mice lacking ICOS, ICOSL, or both display delayed wound healing characterized by defective angiogenesis, diminished T cell and macrophage infiltration, and reduced IL-6 levels at the injury site [43, 85]. Among these alterations, the decrease in IL-6 production is especially consequential, as local IL-6 administration restores tissue repair. These findings suggest that the healing defect may be driven, at least in part, by impaired ICOS-dependent costimulation of T helper cells, which are a major source of IL-6. Indeed, the adoptive transfer of wild-type T cells into ICOS-deficient mice but not ICOSL-deficient mice rescues wound healing [85]. Nonetheless, the observation that topical application of ICOS-Fc accelerates early wound repair in both wild-type and ICOS-deficient mice but not in ICOSL-deficient mice suggests that this healing impairment may also involve defective reverse signaling through ICOSL. Notably, this ICOS-Fc-mediated effect is also observed in immunodeficient mice lacking T cells, indicating a T cell-independent mechanism [43, 86].

In wild-type mice, ICOS-Fc treatment is accompanied by reduced neutrophil infiltration and increased migration of T cells, M2 macrophages, and fibroblasts at the wound site. Given the lack of excessive collagen deposition, these features are indicative of a proregenerative rather than a profibrotic response. Strikingly, this treatment elicits a robust angiogenic response accompanied by elevated IL-6 and VEGF levels, which contrasts sharply with the established antiangiogenic activity of ICOS-Fc observed in tumor models [43, 86]. This discrepancy suggests that angiogenesis is driven by different mechanisms in skin wound healing and cancer growth, mechanisms that may be modulated differently by ICOS-Fc. Specifically, while ICOS-Fc inhibits OPN-induced tubulogenesis in HUVECs, it does not appear to oppose VEGF-mediated tubulogenesis. As both VEGF and OPN are produced by vascular endothelial cells via a positive feedback loop, in which VEGF appears to be the main angiogenic signal [87–89], tissue-specific modulation of this axis may lead to different outcomes. For example, in tumors where angiogenesis is strongly dependent on OPN activity, ICOS-Fc may disrupt vascular expansion by curbing OPN signaling. On the other hand, in wound healing, IL-6-mediated upregulation of VEGF may be sufficient to sustain angiogenesis despite the presence of ICOS-Fc, effectively overriding any inhibitory influence on OPN.

Taken together, these findings underscore the requirement of a finely tuned balance within the ICOS/ICOSL/OPN network for effective wound healing. Interestingly, mice deficient in both ICOS and OPN, or ICOSL and OPN, do not exhibit the impaired wound healing observed in single knockout (KO) or ICOS/ICOSL double KO mice, suggesting that the absence of OPN can compensate for the loss of ICOS and/or ICOSL [86]. This compensatory effect may be due to enhanced angiogenesis, a feature consistently observed in ICOS/OPN and ICOSL/OPN double-KO mice but absent in the other genotypic combinations. Finally, local administration of ICOS-Fc promotes both wound healing and angiogenesis in strains expressing ICOSL, whereas these effects are completely absent in ICOSL-KO mice, confirming the requirement for ICOSL in mediating wound repair [86].

Sepsis

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition caused by a dysregulated systemic immune response to infection. Its pathophysiology is only partially understood, but a key mechanism involves an initial phase of systemic hyperinflammation, characterized by excessive production of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6, followed by a prolonged phase of immunoparalysis, typified by elevated levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10. This biphasic immune imbalance results in widespread tissue damage and severe multiorgan failure, with more than 70% of sepsis-related deaths occurring during the immunosuppressive phase [90, 91].

Several clinical studies have implicated OPN in the early hyperinflammatory phase of sepsis in humans. OPN serum levels rise sharply during the acute phase and decrease during resolution. These levels correlate with the severity of subsequent multiorgan dysfunction and serve as predictors of both early and late mortality. Furthermore, OPN levels are directly correlated with those of IL-6, likely due to the direct stimulatory effects of OPN on monocytes and macrophages [90, 92].

In murine models of polymicrobial sepsis induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP), OPN-deficient mice exhibit a reduction in disease severity, proinflammatory cytokine production, tissue damage marker expression, and overall mortality rates. Similarly, treatment with neutralizing anti-OPN antibodies decreases proinflammatory cytokine levels and tissue damage markers, further substantiating the proinflammatory role of OPN in sepsis pathogenesis [92–94].

With respect to the ICOS/ICOSL axis, patients with sepsis have reduced expression of ICOS and other T cell-associated genes, indicating a putative immunosuppressive state [95, 96]. Compared with wild-type mice, ICOS-deficient mice in the murine CLP model presented elevated IL-10 and, to a lesser extent, TNF-α levels. Moreover, treatment with ICOS-Fc ameliorated clinical disease severity and reduced the levels of proinflammatory cytokines and tissue damage markers in both wild-type and ICOS-deficient mice. These findings suggest that the therapeutic benefits of ICOS-Fc are likely mediated through stimulation of ICOSL rather than blockade of endogenous ICOS. Moreover, in ICOS-deficient mice, ICOS-Fc administration normalizes IL-10 levels, suggesting that it may help restore the immune balance between the hyperinflammatory and immunosuppressive phases in sepsis [97].

These data suggest that ICOS-mediated triggering of ICOSL may play a role in counteracting the systemic inflammation that causes sepsis, possibly by acting on macrophages and other inflammatory cells. Conclusions

The collective evidence presented in this review frames the ICOS/ICOSL/OPN signaling axis as a central regulator of immune homeostasis, tissue regeneration, and inflammatory resolution. Their complex interplay appears to determine the behavior of both innate and adaptive immune cells in a context-dependent manner, and their dysregulation may contribute to pathological microenvironmental remodeling that promotes chronic inflammation, tumor growth, and metastasis.

In the early defensive phase of inflammation, ICOS activation by ICOSL drives the differentiation of proinflammatory Th1 and Th17 cells, enhancing macrophage and neutrophil activity, respectively. Simultaneously, ICOS costimulation promotes the differentiation of CTLs, NK cells, and tissue-resident memory CD8+ T cells. These proinflammatory responses are potentiated by OPN, which induces Th1/Th17 polarization and amplifies cytokine production.

The role of DCs is equally shaped by ICOSL reverse signaling. Specifically, ICOSL stimulation by ICOS halts DC migration, thereby prolonging interactions with T cells to facilitate immune activation. Furthermore, it enhances antigen cross-presentation—promoting cytotoxic T cell activation—and modulates cytokine secretion by DCs, with context-dependent effects.

ICOSL reverse signaling also appears to regulate macrophage activity, influencing both cytokine secretion and cell migration. Notably, M1 and M2 macrophages exhibit opposing migratory responses upon ICOSL activation, which may reflect their differential ability to arrest within inflamed tissues under distinct conditions. In human resting M1 and M2 macrophages, ICOSL engagement increases the secretion of CCL3 and CCL4, which contributes to the recruitment of neutrophils and other immune cells. In activated macrophages, ICOSL engagement modulates the secretion of key cytokines characteristic of M1 and M2 cells without altering their functional polarization.

An integrated model emerges in which Th1 and Th17 cells infiltrating inflamed tissues play a major role in the acute phase of inflammation, producing proinflammatory cytokines, such as IFN-γ and IL-17, while engaging ICOS to activate ICOSL on M1 macrophages. This interaction enhances IL-23 secretion, thereby sustaining the Th17 response and promoting neutrophil recruitment. During the transition to resolution and repair, Th2 and Treg cells inhibit the defensive response and coordinate tissue regeneration by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10 and TGFβ, and engaging ICOS to activate ICOSL on M2 macrophages to stimulate the secretion of IL-10 and IL-6, two cytokines essential for angiogenesis and tissue repair. This model is even better exemplified in acute liver injury, where ICOS- and ICOSL-mediated signaling pathways promote regenerative macrophage activity. However, under chronic conditions, the persistence of this signaling contributes to fibrotic remodeling.

The regulation of cell motility is another critical aspect of the ICOS/ICOSL/OPN network. In ICOS+ T cells, low-avidity ICOS/ICOSL interactions with bystander B cells facilitate the recruitment of Tfh cells into lymphoid follicles, whereas high-avidity contacts with cognate B cells induce T cell arrest. ICOS also contributes to the retention of CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells in peripheral tissues. On the other hand, ICOSL+ cells display cell type-dependent migratory responses to ICOS or OPN stimulation, indicating lineage-specific functional adaptations.

Another key target of these interactions is IL-6. OPN enhances IL-6 secretion in monocytes, whereas ICOS-mediated ICOSL activation increases IL-6 production in DCs and M2 macrophages and represses it in M1 macrophages. These regulatory effects have divergent consequences: in wound healing, IL-6 facilitates VEGF-mediated angiogenesis—a process amplified by ICOS-Fc—whereas in tumors, where angiogenesis depends on the positive feedback loop between OPN and VEGF, ICOS-Fc disrupts this crosstalk to exert an antiangiogenic effect. In sepsis, the elevated IL-6 levels observed during the inflammatory phase, potentially supported by OPN, are suppressed by ICOS-mediated ICOSL activation in M1 macrophages, thereby reducing systemic inflammation.

Despite these advances, a fundamental question remains: how does ICOSL discriminate between its two ligands—ICOS and OPN—and translate their engagement into distinct signaling outputs tailored to specific tissue contexts and immunological states? Understanding how ICOSL transduces ligand-specific reverse signals and cooperates with cell-intrinsic transcriptional programs will be essential for fully mapping its immunoregulatory potential. Addressing these gaps will be critical for developing targeted therapies aimed at modulating this axis in diseases characterized by persistent inflammation, tissue injury, or tumor growth.
