Behavioral and physiological responses of the aphelinid parasitoid, Encarsia formosa, to interspecific competitors
Authors: Ze-Yun Fan, Muhammad Musa Khan, Xiao-Lu Lv, Han Gao, Zhen-Huai Fan, Li-He Zhang, Peng-Jun Zhang, Bao-Li Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The inter- and intra- specific competition is a key factor influencing population dynamics, ecosystem stability, and adaptive evolution in invertebrates, especially within parasitoid communities. Numerous parasitoid species can detect cues left by other parasitoid species, yet it remains unclear whether these cues influence parasitism behavior and competitive outcomes. Here, we show that the endo-parasitoid Encarsia formosa adjusts reproductive, behavioral and physiological strategies when detecting competition cues from Eretmocerus hayati, which shares the same whitefly host. The results indicate that En. formosa produces an increased number of mature eggs and exhibits more frequent parasitic behavior, changes that likely enhance its competitive ability. Transcriptomic analyses indicate that visual perception of competition alters neurotransmitter and hormone expression, promoting vitellogenin production and egg formation. However, probably due to energy and resource constraints, these additional eggs are smaller and exhibit higher developmental mortality, ultimately leading to a reduction in number of viable offspring. This reproductive adjustment may ultimately increase lifetime fitness under actual competition in the field. Our findings clarify how parasitoids integrate sensory cues into physiological responses, offering a framework for understanding competition-driven reproductive strategies and informing future ecological and evolutionary research.

Introduction

Competition is a fundamental ecological interaction that occurs when individuals vie for limited resources such as food, space, or mates. It plays a vital role in regulating population dynamics and shaping community structure1. Competition can be broadly classified into intraspecific competition, which occurs among individuals of the same species, and interspecific competition, which arises between individuals of different species2,3. While both forms of competition influence ecological and evolutionary processes, interspecific competition often involves more complex interactions due to differences in species’ physiological traits, behaviors, and ecological niches4. Interspecific competition may drive more efficient resource allocation through ecological niche differentiation or behavioral adaptations5, as individuals adjust their behavior, habitat use, or reproductive strategies to reduce direct interference or resource competition, thereby contributing to population regulation and potentially promoting ecosystem stability under certain conditions6. Additionally, interspecific competition serves as a driver of adaptive evolution, compelling species to develop survival strategies under competitive pressures, thereby facilitating their adaptation to changing environmental conditions7,8.

Hymenopteran parasitoids exhibit free-living behavior as adults, while their larvae derive all necessary resources for development from a single arthropod host. Interspecific competition is both widespread and particularly critical in parasitoid wasp communities as well as other parasitic species9. Compared to other consumer guilds, the majority of parasitoids demonstrate a high degree of specialization, targeting only one or a few host species. While such specialization may reduce competition through host partitioning in some systems, in many cases it results in overlapping host use, particularly when host availability is limited, thereby intensifying interspecific competition among parasitoids10.

In order to survive when faced with interspecific competition, the oviposition strategies employed by female parasitoids are pivotal in determining their reproductive success. The outcome of such competition is influenced by several factors. These include differences in host location efficiency, dispersal abilities, reproductive capacities, and phenological synchronization with the hosts11. Host location efficiency is particularly critical in shaping the extrinsic competitive abilities between parasitoid species12. Parasitoids with superior host-finding capabilities may gain a competitive advantage when contesting with antagonistic species within the same host patch13. Foraging efficiency, characterized by the time spent locating hosts (search time), the duration of the oviposition process (handling time), and the quality of hosts accepted, further influences competitive outcomes. Adult parasitoids exhibiting more efficient foraging behaviors are expected to be stronger extrinsic competitors9, meaning they outcompete rivals for host access during the adult stage. Numerous studies have demonstrated that parasitoids adjust their foraging and patch residency decisions based on the depletion rate of healthy hosts and the presence of intra- and interspecific competitors14–16. Despite long-standing interest and ongoing debate, the detailed mechanisms underlying parasitoid interspecific competition remain largely unexplored, limiting a comprehensive understanding of its complex ecological dynamics.

The whitefly Bemisia tabaci (Hemiptera: Aleyrodidae) is a highly polyphagous pest that has emerged as a major threat to vegetable, cotton, and ornamental crops globally over the past several decades17. Encarsia formosa is a parthenogenetic, obligate endo-parasitic wasp that lays eggs directly into the body of whitefly nymphs, preferentially parasitizes older nymphal stages (3–4 instars) and thrives in relatively low-temperature environments18,19. By comparison, Eretmocerus hayati is an ecto-endo-parasitoid, females lay their eggs externally, between the leaf surface and the abdomen of whitefly nymphs. Upon hatching, the larvae burrow into the body of B. tabaci nymphs, where they continue to develop until adulthood20. Meanwhile, Er. hayati prefers younger host stages (2-3 instars) and exhibits better adaptation to higher temperature environments21. Both En. formosa and Er. hayati are dominant parasitoids of B. tabaci in China, and in principle, the combined use of both could maximize the biological control of whiteflies due to their complementary ecological traits, including differences in host-stage preference (older vs. younger nymphs) and thermal tolerance (cool vs. warm temperatures). These differences allow the two parasitoids to exploit B. tabaci populations more completely across developmental stages and environmental conditions, thereby enhancing pest suppression. However, since En. formosa and Er. hayati partially share the same host, their simultaneous use may still lead to resource competition and interspecific interference, which could reduce the overall effectiveness of biological control. Understanding how En. formosa responds to cues of interspecific competition from Er. hayati is therefore critical for improving the efficacy and stability of biological control strategies, conversely, the same applies.

Previous studies on the competitive interactions between parasitoids have primarily focused on direct competition, such as physical interference or superparasitism when females encounter the same host, and the implications for parasitoid population dynamics22. However, responses to risk of competition, defined here as behavioral and physiological changes triggered by the presence of a competitor rather than direct contact or resource contest, remain underexplored despite their potential to influence parasitoid efficacy. This interspecific competitive stress may be especially important in multi-species release strategies, where long-term success depends on the compatibility and performance of the involved natural enemies. In this study, we exposed En. formosa to the competition cues from Er. hayati to simulate the risk of competition. We aimed to investigate how En. formosa responds to these cues, with a particular focus on changes in parasitism behavior, reproductive output, and physiological regulation. We designed a series of exposure experiments to assess changes, including potential stress-induced compensatory effects under varying levels of competition cues, aiming to better understand how parasitoids adjust their survival strategies in response to the competition cues. Our findings showed that En. formosa produced significantly more offspring under lower levels of competition cues from Er. hayati, but fewer offspring under higher levels of competition cues compared to the control (with no cues for interspecific competition). Further physiological analyses revealed that this alteration was associated with suppressed neurotransmitter levels, altered hormonal secretion, and an accumulation of mature eggs, as determined by ELISA-based quantification of juvenile hormone titers and microscopic analysis of ovarian development. Although En. formosa exhibited more frequent parasitic attempts under competitive stress, energy trade-offs led to compromised egg quality and ultimately a decline in offspring viability. These findings provide mechanistic insights into how indirect interactions among parasitoids can influence reproductive physiology and behavioral plasticity, with important implications for optimizing the combined use of multiple natural enemies in biological control programs.

Results

Effects of interspecific competition cues on the development of Encarsia formosa F1 progeny

Results demonstrated that the number of B. tabaci nymphs parasitized by En. formosa increased significantly when they faced the cues of interspecific competition from Er. hayati at low ratios (Er vs. En = 2:6 to 3:6) compared to the control (with no cues for interspecific competition). However, as the competitive stress increased, the number of the pupae parasitized by En. formosa decreased gradually (One-way ANOVA, F5,24 = 9.98, P = 0.0001, η2 = 0.675; Fig. 1B). Although there were no significant changes in the developmental time (One-way ANOVA, F5,24 = 0.71, P = 0.624, η2 = 0.1284; Fig. 1C), the cues of interspecific competition led to a significant reduction in the emergence rate of En. formosa F1 adults (One-way ANOVA, F5,24 = 7.46, P = 0.0002; η2 = 0.6085; Fig. 1D), their longevity (One-way ANOVA, F5,24 = 5.36, P = 0.002, η2 = 0.528; Fig. 1E) and body size (Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared = 16.406, df = 5, P = 0.0058, η2 = 0.029; Fig. 1F). In order to confirm whether En. formosa experiences oviposition failure or not, B. tabaci nymphs that were parasitized by En. formosa for more than 120 s were dissected. Results displayed that parasitoid eggs were found in most of the parasitized B. tabaci nymphs. Effects of interspecific competition cues on the transcriptome of Encarsia formosa

RNA-Seq profiling was performed to identify the transcriptome changes that mediate the response of En. formosa. In total, 48,162 unigenes were identified, which were mainly concentrated in the 200–1000 bp range from a total of 12 sample groups (three replicates in each of the 2:6, 6:6, 18:6 and control experiments). Afterwards, 13 of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were further analyzed by qRT-PCR to validate the RNA-Seq results. Results demonstrated that the expression change trend of the selected genes by qRT-PCR was consistent with the transcriptome sequencing results (Fig. S3).

Different numbers of genes were significantly induced in the 2:6, 6:6 and 18:6 levels of interspecific competition (Fig. 5A). For instance, a total of 2205 genes were upregulated and 495 genes were downregulated in response to 2:6 treatment; 956 genes were upregulated and 583 genes were downregulated in the 6:6 treatment; while 1575 genes were upregulated and 1030 genes were downregulated in the 18:6 treatment (Fig. 5B). There were 199 DEGs shared in the three levels of interspecific competition (Figs. 5C and S4). Notably, most of the 199 overlapping DEGs exhibited concordant expression directions across stress levels, indicating a consistent transcriptional response to interspecific competition. Hierarchical clustering of the 199 DEGs based on normalized FPKM values under the stress regimes produced a distinct correlation pattern between control and the three levels of stress treatment. Among them, 61 genes were annotated (Table S4). Further analysis of the annotated genes showed that eight genes related to visual signaling, neurotransmitter transmission, hormone secretion, and reproduction were selected for clustering analysis. Discussion

Competition is a pervasive interaction within ecosystems22,23, and as a result, competitive pressures are therefore widespread among insect populations. Interspecific competition is often regarded as one of the key biological factors shaping the distribution, abundance, and diversity patterns within ecological systems24. It influences a wide range of ecological processes, including foraging behavior, population reproduction25–27, survival, and spatial distribution of insects28. In addition, competition can impact the overall balance and stability of entire ecosystems29,30.

Parasitoids can reduce the negative effects of interspecific competition by temporally allocating resources through variations in life history traits; a strategy that may play a critical role in enabling species coexistence4. Parasitoid species with superior host location efficiency may gain an advantage when facing the cues of interspecific competition from antagonistic species sharing the same habitat31. When multiple female parasitoids encounter the same host patch, they may engage in interspecific competition through contest behavior, which includes aggressive interactions such as biting, stinging, and striking the intruder32. The outcome of these competitive interactions is influenced by several factors, including host-finding efficiency, dispersal ability, reproductive capacity, and fighting ability12. Our current study revealed that, in addition to direct competition, the cues of interspecific competition significantly increased the number of mature eggs in En. formosa without leading to oviposition failure. This adaptive response may enhance the ability of En. formosa to secure more hosts under competitive stress.

The synthesis of vitellogenin and its uptake by maturing oocytes are essential processes for successful reproduction in insects, including En. formosa. In most insect species, juvenile hormone or ecdysone acts as a gonadotropin to regulate yolk production33,34. These results suggest that hormonal regulation may be associated with increased vitellogenin levels, potentially promoting oocyte maturation and thereby facilitating more frequent parasitic behavior. This stress response has been observed across multiple species, as energy and resources available for reproduction are inherently limited. A well-documented trade-off exists between progeny size and number, with progeny fitness generally increasing with greater investment per offspring35,36. Goubault et al.37 demonstrated that under intense intraspecific competition, the parasitoid Goniozus nephantidis produced fewer but larger offspring. Negative correlations between egg number and mass have been observed in other insects, such as the European earwig Forficula auricularia38. In our study, En. formosa produced more eggs under competitive stress, though the eggs were smaller in size. This trade-off is likely to have major implications for offspring fitness, as a positive correlation between offspring size and fitness is typically found, and this relationship is largely influenced by environmental factors39,40. Previous research indicates that in many insect species, including parasitoid wasps, egg size affects offspring survival, growth, competitive ability, and developmental success41. These findings align with our current results, where a greater number of smaller eggs led to lower survival rates, thus reducing the total number of offspring. Unlike some species where larger offspring are prioritized under stress to enhance individual survival37,38, En. formosa, as a synovigenic and highly fecund parasitoid, may adopt a quantity-over-quality strategy in response to perceived competition, possibly to ensure reproductive success in unpredictable environments. Based on biological and transcriptomic data, we propose a molecular model that outlines the mechanisms leading to reduced offspring numbers under competitive stress, as illustrated in Fig. 9. This model is yet to be experimentally validated and is intended to guide future research efforts. Although the number of viable offspring is reduced, this response may represent an adaptive strategy under anticipated resource competition. By increasing egg production and accelerating reproduction, En. formosa may prioritize rapid reproductive output over individual offspring quality, and although this leads to fewer surviving offspring under our experimental conditions. Firstly, our model elucidates how environmental stress is perceived by insects in natural settings. The ability to gather and process environmental information is crucial for invertebrates to optimize survival and reproduction. Life-threatening enemies, such as predators, parasites, and resource competitors, represent important environmental challenges. Thus, the ability to identify such threats, evaluate the level of competition, and formulate appropriate defensive responses is vital for both survival and reproductive success. Invertebrates have evolved various methods to recognize biological stressors, employing multiple sensory systems to detect odors, shapes, behaviors, and other cues42,43. For instance, Pang et al.44 demonstrated that fruit flies (Drosophila) use visual signals to detect predator risk. Our model suggests that transcriptional changes triggered by stress are likely initiated through visual perception, which subsequently leads to associated changes in the brain. Specifically, we observed significant differences in the expression of Prim1 and RHO genes across the three treatment groups (Fig. 5D). Notably, Prim1 plays a role in the fat facets signaling pathway during eye development in Drosophila45, while RHO is involved in establishing ommatidia polarity in the Drosophila eye46. Additionally, our transcriptome data revealed that the GSEA results were significantly enriched in vision-related categories (Fig. 6), indicating that visual cues enable En. formosa to perceive competitive stress from Er. hayati.

Secondly, our model suggests that neurotransmitter activity is modulated in response to biotic stimuli, such as competitive stress. We observed expression changes in the Akt1 and GFA1 genes across the three treatment groups (Fig. 5D). Akt1 has been shown to protect against the loss of dopamine activity associated with 1-octen-3-ol exposure47, while GFA1 is an allele of the Da7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) in Drosophila48. Moreover, our subsequent analyses of biogenic amine content revealed significant differences between the control and competition groups. Biogenic amine signaling is known to be activated in insects under adverse environmental conditions, such as biological stress49, and it plays a role in regulating behaviors related to food search, intake, and responses to starvation50. For instance, octopamine administration allows Western honeybees (Apis mellifera) to restore their ability to increase body temperature under cold conditions51. Similarly, changes in dopamine or serotonin levels are closely associated with the solitary and gregarious phases of locusts (Schistocerca gregaria and Locusta migratoria)52,53. Together with these previous studies, our findings suggest that biogenic amine signaling may be a key component of the stress response mechanism, influencing alternative reproductive pathways in response to competition.

Thirdly, our model posits that biogenic amines regulate hormone release from the ovaries. Biogenic amines mediate the interaction between juvenile hormone (JH) and ecdysteroids (20E)54. Vellichirammal et al.55 demonstrated that biogenic amines likely exert their polyphenic effects by regulating hormones such as JH and ecdysteroids. Sts1 has been revealed to be involved in the formation of substrate ecdysteroid phosphates in Drosophila melanogaster56, in our current study, the Sts1 gene exhibited significant changes under competitive stress (Fig. 5D). GSEA results further revealed enrichment in pathways related to hormone secretion in the 2:6 and 6:6 groups, and hormone binding in the 18:6 group (Fig. 6). Our hormone assays showed significant changes in JH and 20E levels in En. formosa under varying degrees of competitive stress. Additionally, previous studies have reported that biogenic amines can regulate JH or molting hormone levels, contributing to phenological phenomena57. Octopamine and 5-HT have been shown to induce JH release in bees58, while serotonergic neurons link external environmental cues with the internal endocrine system by modulating steroid hormone biosynthesis in D. melanogaster59. In our study, competitive stress also induced changes in rps2 and Tbpl1 genes, along with varying degrees of vitellogenin content under different levels of stress (Fig. 5D). Previous research has shown that rps2 (a ribosomal protein) plays a role in oogenesis in D. melanogaster60, while Tbpl1 is essential for embryogenesis in Drosophila61.

In summary, our findings support the hypothesis that JH and 20E are critical regulators of reproduction in En. formosa, and that biogenic amine signaling in the brain acts upstream of these hormone differences. Through visual perception, En. formosa detects interspecific competitive stress from Er. hayati, which triggers neurotransmitter production in the brain. This neurotransmitter activity signals the pharyngeal body or thymus to modulate JH and 20E levels, thereby regulating ovarian function and leading to the production of more mature eggs. Concurrently, En. formosa exhibits increased parasitic behavior, but possibly due to limited energy and resources, egg quality decreases, resulting in smaller eggs and higher developmental failure rates. While this model remains to be experimentally validated, it provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying stress responses and reproduction in parasitoid wasps. This model can also serve as a basis for future research to further explore the functional relationships between gene expression changes and competitive stress.

From the perspective of biological control, these findings have important implications. Interspecific competition is inevitable when multiple parasitoid species are released together in integrated pest management programs. Our results suggest that under competitive stress, En. formosa adopts a high-egg-output strategy that comes at the cost of individual egg quality. This could influence its long-term population stability and host suppression efficiency in the field. Therefore, when designing multi-species release strategies, it may be necessary to consider the compatibility of parasitoid species and the potential trade-offs induced by competition. In practice, it may also be beneficial to release a small number of competitors simultaneously to stimulate oviposition while avoiding excessive stress. Furthermore, understanding the sensory and endocrine mechanisms that mediate this shift provides valuable opportunities to optimize rearing and release conditions, such as minimizing unnecessary interspecific encounters during mass production, to maintain or enhance parasitoid performance in pest control applications.

Methods

Plants and insects

Cotton plants (Gossypium hirsutum L. var. Lumianyan no. 32) were used to rear B. tabaci in the current study. Cotton seeds were sown in plastic pots (12 cm diameter × 15 cm height) containing a soil-sand mixture (10% sand, 5% clay, and 85% peat). The plants were reared in an environment-controlled glasshouse located at Chongqing Normal University, devoid of any arthropod infestation and maintained under ambient temperature (15–30 °C) and photoperiod conditions (10–14 h light). The plants were adequately maintained until they reached the stage of 6–8 fully expanded leaves for use in experimental trials.

Whitefly B. tabaci Middle East-Asia Minor 1 cryptic species (MEAM1, formerly known as biotype B) was initially collected from eggplant (Solanum melongena) in August 2017 at the training farm of South China Agricultural University in Guangzhou, China. A sub-colony was taken from this culture and maintained on cotton plants within growth chambers in Chongqing Normal University at 26 ± 1 °C, RH 65–75% and L:D 16 h:8 h photoperiod for experimental use, and the purity of the whitefly MEAM1 population was monitored monthly using the mitochondrial DNA COI gene of whitefly B. tabaci62.

The parasitoid En. formosa was first collected from parasitized B. tabaci nymphs on tomato plants in the Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences in May 2016. The parasitoid Er. hayati was obtained from the Institute of Plant Protection, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, in October 2017. Both the progeny populations were separately reared on cotton plants infested with B. tabaci MEAM1 within a condition-controlled laboratory environment of 26 ± 1 °C, RH 65–75% and a L:D cycle of 16 h:8 h photoperiod. The female adults of En. formosa and Er. hayati used in the experiments were 3–4 days old after elusion. The parasitoids were never given hosts to parasitize before experiments and were never reused between experiments.

Experimental apparatus I

A two-layer leaf cage with overlapping spaces (Fig. 1A) as described by Fan et al.63 was used in the competitive experiments. The leaf cage was clipped onto the cotton leaves and En. formosa female adults were introduced into the upper layer that directly covered the cotton leaf. As a result, they could directly feed and parasitize whitefly nymphs on the leaves. Meanwhile, Er. hayati female adults were introduced into the lower layer of the leaf cage to induce interspecific competition. Er. hayati is unable to access the upper section of the leaf cages through the screen, thereby eliminating direct contact and resource competition, resulting in solely interspecific competitive stress (Fig. 1A).

Biology assay of Encarsia formosa

Five to six leaves of similar size were meticulously chosen from cotton plants that exhibited robust growth. About 50 pairs of whitefly adults were then introduced onto one leaf enclosed within a single-layer leaf cage. Following 24 h of oviposition, the adults were removed and the leaves were subsequently covered with nylon bags (70 mesh cm−2). The whitefly progeny was allowed to feed and develop on the original leaves until reaching to 3rd nymphal instars from which approximately 150 nymphs were randomly selected. The remaining whitefly nymphs were removed with a camel hairbrush before the leaves were clamped into a two-layer leaf cage (Fig. 1A).

There were 6 experimental setups, in which 6 En. formosa adults (one-day-old) were uniformly introduced into the upper layer of leaf cages, while 0, 2, 3, 6, 12, and 18 individuals of Er. hayati parasitoids (3–4 days old) were introduced into the lower layer of the leaf cages, respectively. All the parasitoids were then removed following 24 h. Subsequently, the parasitized nymphs of whitefly were observed daily, the number of black pupae on cotton leaves was counted. The development of En. formosa F1 nymphs was monitored daily using a hand microscope. The emergence rate of En. formosa F1 adults was calculated, and their longevities were measured by supplying them with ample whitefly nymphs to feed within insect-rearing cages (60 × 60 × 60 cm) under laboratory conditions until their death.

Individual size is an important index of insect quality. For a parasitoid, their hind tibia is heavily ossified, and its length correlates favorably with individual size64. Therefore, the length of the hind tibia (HTL) was used to indicate the individual size of En. formosa in the current study. Ninety En. formosa F1 female adults that emerged from the above experiments (one-day-old) were randomly selected and put into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes and killed with alcohol. Then, individually, their hind feet were dissected and photographed under the microscope (Zeiss, Stemi 508, German) in order to record the hind tibia length.

Behavior analysis of Encarsia formosa

A water agar gel was prepared on one side of a double-space behavior observation box (Fig. 2A). Preliminary experimental analyses indicated that there were very few differences between the 2:6 and 3:6 treatments, same between 12:6 and 18:6 treatments, thus, we focused on the four representative interspecific stress (0, 2, 6, and 18) in subsequent experiments. When the B. tabaci nymphs developed to 3rd instar on cotton leaves, 100 of them were randomly selected, while the remaining whiteflies were removed, after which the cotton leaves were laid flat closely on the agar gel.

In each observation box, there are 6 En. formosa adults (one-day-old) introduced through the catch hole on the side with whitefly 3rd instar nymphs, while 0, 2, 6, or 18 Er. hayati were introduced to the other side respectively, which were named as Ctrl (0:6), 2:6, 6:6, and 18:6. Then the leaf cages boxes were placed under a camera (CCTV Lens 3 mp, Sunny, China), and the parasitic behavior (detailed descriptions of searching, probing, parasitizing, cleaning, feeding, sucking water and honey, resting and walking are listed in Table S1) of the En. formosa parasitoids were observed. Behavioral videography was conducted for a total duration of 3 h on each occasion (9:00 AM–12:00 PM). To avoid any potential bias caused by initial disturbance, we excluded the first 15 min from each recording session before randomly selecting a 1 h segment for behavioral analysis, and the proportion of time spent on each action in an hour analyzed. As van Lenteren et al.65 suggested, successful parasitizing time of whitefly nymphs should last for more than 2 min, within our experiments we therefore considered that the egg-laying times of shorter than 2 min were deemed non-effective. Each parasitoid was tested only once, with 30 replicates per treatment.

Anatomy of the Encarsia formosa reproductive system

During dissection, one dissecting needle was used to gently press the dorsal plate of the thorax, while the other needle held the external genitalia at the end of the abdomen and was used to gently pull them outwards. This process allowed for the careful removal of the attached digestive system and fat body while leaving the reproductive system intact.

Anatomy of the En. formosa reproductive system and newborn egg statistics from B. tabaci adults were investigated by introducing whitefly adults into a common single layer leaf cage (5 cm in diameter and 2 cm in height) to lay eggs on healthy, clean cotton leaves for 24 h. Once sufficient nymphs had developed through to the 3rd instar nymphal stage, 6 En. formosa adults (one-day-old) were subjected to the 4 intensities of competitive stress as outlined above for 24 h oviposition using the apparatus illustrated in Fig. 1. After the treatments, all the Encarsia parasitoids were collected in 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, frozen at −20 °C for 10 min and then placed on a microscope slide for dissection in 1× PBS buffer (PH 7.4). Using a microscope (Zeiss, Stemi 508, German), the morphological structure of En. formosa reproductive system was observed and photographic documentation taken for further analysis. The number of mature eggs in the ovaries was counted (minimum 90 parasitoids per treatment). Simultaneously, to verify whether En. formosa experiences oviposition failure, the Encarsia parasitoids following interspecific competition were introduced into common leaf cages to lay eggs for 3 h. Subsequently, parasitized whitefly nymphs (oviposition time exceeds 120 seconds) were dissected and the egg laying rates were calculated. In addition, the newborn eggs were photographed under a microscope (Zeiss, Stemi 508, German), and the length and width of the eggs were measured (minimum 140 eggs per treatment). The egg volume was calculated according to Church et al.66 as volume = π × width2 × length/6, and aspect ratio = length/width.

RNA extraction and sequencing for GSEA analysis

After 24 h of interspecific competition, En. formosa parasitoids were collected from each treatment (Ctrl, 2:6, 6:6, 18:6). For each biological replicate, approximately 300 individuals were collected. To obtain this number, parasitoids were pooled from multiple two-layer leaf cages (Fig. 1A), with approximately 80 cages maintained simultaneously, allowing completion of collections within 9–12 days. Parasitoids from different cages but belonging to the same treatment and collection period were combined to constitute one biological replicate. The collected parasitoids were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Before RNA extraction, the frozen parasitoids were first disrupted and homogenized, then the mixture was oscillated by vortex blending for 2 min. Total RNA of parasitoids was extracted with a Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA). RNA was checked for purity and integrity using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA).

After total RNA was extracted, eukaryotic mRNA was enriched by Oligo (dT) beads. This enriched mRNA was then fragmented into short fragments using fragmentation buffer and reversely transcribed into cDNA by using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB #7530, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina novaseq 6000 system (Gene Denovo, Guangzhou, China). There were three biological replicates per treatment (Ctrl, 2:6, 6:6, and 18:6) sequenced.

Raw reads were processed in order to obtain clean reads by filtering and removing reads with adaptors, short reads, and low-quality reads. Following this, clean reads were produced and assembled into unigene using StringTie v1.3.1. For each transcription region, a FPKM (fragment per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) value was calculated to quantify its expression abundance and variations, using RSEM software. A gene was considered significantly differentially expressed if its corresponding False Discovery Rate (FDR) corrected P value was ≤0.05 and it had a fold change equal or greater than 2.0.

Subsequently, GSEA was performed using the GSEA software v3.0 to assess whether predefined gene sets, such as Gene Ontology (GO) terms or pathway categories, were significantly enriched in the expression profiles between four interspecific competition treatments. Following the standard GSEA procedure67, all genes from the expression matrix were ranked according to the Signal-to-Noise ratio. Enrichment scores (ES) for each gene set were calculated based on 1000 permutations of the phenotype labels, with a minimum gene set size of 15 and a maximum of 500. Significance thresholds were set at nominal P-value < 0.05 and FDR q-value < 0.25 to identify significantly enriched gene sets. Among all significantly enriched gene sets, we focused our analysis on those related to visual signal transduction, biogenic amine and neurotransmitter processes, hormonal signaling, and germ cell development, as these biological pathways are directly relevant to the physiological responses under stress conditions in our model. The selection of these gene categories was based on prior biological hypotheses and known stress-associated phenotypes in related organisms.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) was used to verify the RNA-Seq results. Thirteen DEGs were selected for validation based on their putative biological roles. One microgram of RNA from the En. formosa parasitoid samples used to prepare the RNA-Seq library was reverse transcribed using random hexamer primers (Invitrogen) and superscript II reverse transcriptase (Life Technologies Inc.). The qRT-PCR reactions were performed on a Real-Time PCR system 7500 (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) (TaKaRa, RR820A) (primers are shown in Table S2, Supplementary Information, all primers were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). 18S was selected as the reference gene for qRT-PCR. Each biological replicate was measured with three technical replicates, and the fold change was calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method68 for further log2 transformed. The qRT-PCR thermocycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min; denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s; annealing/extension at 60 °C for 15/30 s; these two steps were repeated for a total of 40 cycles. A melt-curve analysis was performed (95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 95 °C for 15 s) to confirm amplification specificity. Data were analyzed using the software provided with the real-time PCR instrument.

Determination of hormone and neurotransmitter

For the physiological assays (e.g., hormone and neurotransmitter quantification), En. formosa individuals were also collected from each treatment (Ctrl: 6:0, 2:6, 6:6, 18:6) after 24 h of interspecific competition. For each biological replicate, approximately 1600 parasitoid individuals (approximately 50 mg) were collected. Each treatment group was sampled daily using 40 two-layer leaf cages (Fig. 1A), collecting approximately 200–240 individuals per day. Each treatment included six biological replicates. All required samples were obtained within a 40–50 day period. Parasitoids from different cages but within the same treatment and collection period were pooled to constitute one biological replicate. The collected Encarsia parasitoids were immediately snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C before downstream physiological analyses.

For the measurement of hormones and neurotransmitter, 300 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to each of the frozen tubes and the parasitoids thoroughly homogenized using a plastic rod. Then the tubes were mixed for 2 min and centrifuged at 10,000 r/min for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to a new centrifuge tube, which served as the test fluid.

To measure the contents of vitellogenin (Vg), juvenile hormone (JH), 20-ecdysone (20E), dopamine (DA), serotonin (5-HT) and acetylcholine (ACh), commercial ELISA kits from SinoBestBio were employed (Shanghai, China). The analysis of these hormones and neurotransmitters was undertaken following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments included biologically independent replicates. For the biological experiments, each group contained five biological replicates, and hind tibia length was measured on at least 60 individuals per group (specific numbers are indicated in the Fig. 1F). For parasitization behavior experiments, each biological replicate consisted of 30 parasitoids, and parasitization frequency and effective parasitization frequency were repeated at least 14 times per group (specific numbers are indicated in the Fig. 2). Ovarian dissection to count mature eggs included at least 94 individuals per group (specific numbers are indicated in the Fig. 3), and egg measurements were performed on at least 138 eggs per group (specific numbers are indicated in the Fig. 4). Egg-laying rate was measured with three biological replicates per group. Transcriptome analyses were performed with three biological replicates per treatment. Hormone and biogenic amine assays were performed with six biological replicates per group.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (v8.01). Normal distribution of the data was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Bartlett chi-square test was used to test the homogeneity of variance of the data, which was consistent with the normal distribution. For parameters including length of hind tibia, parasitization frequency, effective parasitization frequency, number of mature eggs, egg length, egg width, egg volume, and egg length-width ratio, the data did not follow a normal distribution (The test results are shown in Table S5) and were therefore analyzed using non-parametric tests, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons. Behavioral data were analyzed using GLMs with appropriate link functions. Other data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Duncan’s Multiple Range Test was used as a post hoc test to further investigate differences between specific group pairs when significant variations were detected in the one-way ANOVA. Box plots show the median, interquartile range (IQR), and whiskers representing 1.5 × IQR. For other datasets, values are shown as mean ± SE. Different letters in figures indicate statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software) created the graphs for all parameters.
