e-Profits : A Business-Aligned Evaluation Metric for Profit-Sensitive Customer Churn Prediction Journal: Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services
Awais Manzoor, M. Atif Qureshi, Etain Kidney, Luca Longo

Abstract
Retention campaigns in customer relationship management often rely on churn prediction models evaluated using traditional metrics such as AUC and F1-score. However, these metrics fail to reflect financial outcomes and may mislead strategic decisions. We introduce e-Profits , a novel business-aligned evaluation metric that quantifies model performance based on customer-specific value, retention probability, and intervention costs. Unlike existing profit-based metrics such as Expected Maximum Profit, which assume fixed population-level parameters, e-Profits uses Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to estimate personalised retention rates and supports granular, per-customer evaluation. We benchmark six classifiers across two telecom datasets (IBM Telco and Maven Telecom) and demonstrate that e-Profits reshapes model rankings compared to traditional metrics, revealing financial advantages in models previously overlooked by AUC or F1-score. The metric also enables segment-level insight into which models maximise return on investment for high-value customers. e-Profits is designed as an understandable, post hoc tool to support model evaluation in business contexts, particularly for marketing and analytics teams prioritising profit-driven decisions. All source code is available at: https://github.com/matifq/eprofits .

Introduction

Customer churn, the end of a relationship between a customer and a business, presents a serious risk to a company’s sustainability. Whether customers choose to go to a competitor or stop using the service altogether, churn results in immediate revenue loss and a gradual decline in customer equity. Since attracting new customers is much more expensive typically five times more than keeping current ones
[
1
,
2
]
, many companies now focus on customer retention strategies enhanced by predictive analytics
[
3
,
4
]
. Loyal customers may gradually acquire more goods and services over time and are associated with reduced marketing costs
[
5
]
, and they spread positive sentiment which indirectly attract new customers. Therefore, higher customer retention rates result in increases in company profits .

Businesses increasingly rely on predictive models trained on behavioural and transactional data to address churn and identify customers at risk of leaving
[
6
,
7
]
. Customer churn prediction (CPP) is typically framed as a binary classification task in the machine learning literature
[
8
]
. The goal is to identify would-be churners early enough to intervene, usually via targeted retention campaigns offering incentives. However, while predictive accuracy is important, most models are evaluated using traditional metrics such as F1-score or AUC, which do not reflect real-world business impact
[
9
,
10
]
. This issue is especially problematic in profit-sensitive domains, where not all customers are equally valuable, and not all classification errors have the same cost
[
11
,
12
]
. For instance, targeting low-value customers who were unlikely to churn wastes retention resources, while failing to identify high-value churners results in substantial lost revenue. Studies have shown that an inverse relationship may exist between customer lifetime value (CLV) and churn probability
[
13
,
14
]
, meaning that models can systematically overlook high-value customers optimised for statistical accuracy.

To bridge this gap, profit-based evaluation metrics have been proposed, including the Maximum Profit Criterion (MPC) and Expected Maximum Profit (EMP)
[
15
,
16
]
. These metrics represent important progress but are often constrained by fixed assumptions, such as uniform CLV and constant offer acceptance probabilities, which rarely hold in real-world business contexts characterised by customer heterogeneity.

In this study, we introduce
e-Profits
, a business-aligned evaluation metric designed to assess churn models based on financial relevance rather than statistical precision. By incorporating customer-specific CLV, intervention cost, and retention probability, estimated through Kaplan-Meier survival analysis,
e-Profits
offers a granular, per-customer evaluation approach. We empirically evaluate six machine learning classifiers across two real-world telecom datasets (IBM Telco and Maven Telecom) and show that
e-Profits
reveals model rankings that diverge significantly from those based on traditional or existing profit-based metrics.

2
Related Works

Customer churn prediction has been extensively studied across domains such as telecommunications, banking, and subscription-based services
[
17
]
. Traditionally, churn is formulated as a supervised binary classification problem, with models trained to distinguish between customers who stay and those who leave. Early approaches employed logistic regression and decision trees, gradually evolving toward advanced techniques, including artificial neural networks
[
18
]
, support vector machines
[
19
]
, ensemble methods
[
20
]
, and feature selection pipelines
[
21
]
. Despite these advances, the dominant focus remains on improving predictive performance measured through conventional metrics such as Accuracy, F1-score, and AUC
[
22
]
.

These conventional metrics, however, are ill-suited for business settings where the cost of misclassification varies across customers. For example, AUC treats false positives and false negatives symmetrically
[
9
]
, ignoring the financial impact of retention decisions. As Elkan noted in his foundational work on cost-sensitive learning
[
10
]
, a classifier’s utility must be assessed based on domain-specific cost matrices rather than classification error alone. In churn prediction, misclassifying a high-value customer as a non-churner could lead to significant revenue loss, while misclassifying a low-value, loyal customer could result in wasted marketing resources.

Profit-based evaluation metrics have been introduced to address this gap. Verbeke et al. introduced the Maximum Profit Criterion (MPC)
[
15
]
, which estimates profit based on classification outcomes. This was extended by Verbraken et al. through the Expected Maximum Profit (EMP) metric
[
16
]
, which incorporates uncertainty in retention success and introduces the profit-maximising churn fraction, the optimal subset of customers to target. These metrics marked an important shift from accuracy-focused to business-aligned evaluation. Table
1
summarises the key characteristics of traditional and profit-aware evaluation metrics, highlighting the business relevance and flexibility offered by
e-Profits
compared to widely used alternatives.

Nevertheless, limitations persist. EMP assumes constant values for customer lifetime value (CLV), contact cost, and offer acceptance probability
[
23
]
. It models retention success using an unimodal Beta distribution with fixed hyperparameters, which may not capture the heterogeneity of real-world customers. Additionally, while some studies have explored embedding profit functions directly into model optimisation via profit-aware decision trees or evolutionary strategies
[
22
,
24
]
, these approaches often introduce complexity and reduce interpretability, limiting their uptake in business environments.

Some recent works have considered time-dependent churn risk using survival analysis models
[
25
,
26
]
. These approaches explicitly demonstrate the value of incorporating tenure and retention time, but they seldom translate into actionable profit-based evaluation metrics.

While the academic literature contains several profit-aware metrics, their adoption in operational churn platforms remains rare. Surveys such as García et al.
[
27
]
and our own review
[
28
]
highlight that many practitioners still default to traditional metrics like AUC or lift, due to limitations in available tools and ease of understanding. This disconnect reinforces the need for practical and business-aligned evaluation metrics that can bridge the research-practice divide.

Conventional evaluation metrics such as Accuracy, F1-score, and AUC focus on statistical correctness but neglect the financial implications of churn prediction. Although profit-based metrics such as the EMP
[
16
]
represent an important step toward business-aligned evaluation, they often rely on population-level assumptions: fixed CLV, fixed intervention cost parameters (such as cost of contact and retention offer), and no integration of individualised retention rates. Such assumptions ignore the heterogeneity of real-world customer bases, where profit potential and intervention costs vary significantly across individuals.

Despite these advances, existing profit-aware approaches generally fail to capture customer heterogeneity. Most rely on fixed cost parameters or make strong assumptions about offer acceptance rates. By contrast, our proposed
e-Profits
is aimed at leveraging individual-level cost, revenue, and survival-based retention to provide a metric that is granular, understandable, and practically deployable, without retraining the model.
e-Profits
responds to these limitations by offering a modular evaluation metric that aligns directly with business impact, empowering practitioners to make financially sound model selection decisions.

3
Methodology and Experimental Setup

This section introduces our proposed evaluation metric,
e-Profits
, and explains how it aligns churn prediction with business objectives by incorporating customer-specific profitability and cost structure. We also introduce the details of a primary empirical study including the datasets selected, the classification techniques for churn prediction, and the comparative metrics for the purpose of validation.

3.1
Overview and Motivation

As established in the previous section, conventional evaluation metrics fail to capture the financial implications of churn misclassification, and existing profit-based alternatives often rely on strong population-level assumptions. In response, we introduce
e-Profits
, a modular evaluation metric that supports granular, business-aligned model comparison.

The
e-Profits
metric evaluates model performance by aggregating per-customer profit outcomes under realistic operational conditions. It incorporates:

•

Customer-specific lifetime value (CLV):
Estimated using revenue and retention projections

•

Personalised retention probabilities:
Derived via Kaplan-Meier survival analysis based on customer tenure

•

Individualised intervention costs:
Including both contact and retention offer costs per customer

This design enables profit-based comparison of churn models without altering training pipelines, ensuring both understandability and practical deployment feasibility.

3.2
e-Profits
Formulation

We define
e-Profits
as the total estimated profit that would be realised if a retention campaign was executed based on the model’s churn predictions:

\text{{e-Profits}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Pi_{i}

(1)

Where
\Pi_{i}
is the estimated profit or loss associated with customer
i
, computed as:

\Pi_{i}=\begin{cases}\text{CLV}_{i}-C_{\text{offer},i}-C_{\text{contact},i}&\text{if }y_{i}=1\text{ and }\hat{y}_{i}=1\quad\text{(True Positive)}\\
\\
-C_{\text{offer},i}-C_{\text{contact},i}&\text{if }y_{i}=0\text{ and }\hat{y}_{i}=1\quad\text{(False Positive)}\\
\\
0&\text{otherwise (no retention action)}\end{cases}

(2)

Where:

•

y_{i}
is the true churn label for customer
i

•

\hat{y}_{i}
is the predicted label (1 = predicted to churn)

•

\text{CLV}_{i}
is the estimated customer lifetime value

•

C_{\text{offer},i}
and
C_{\text{contact},i}
are the intervention costs for customer
i

In this framework, retention actions are only initiated when
\hat{y}_{i}=1
(i.e., the model predicts churn). True positives generate profit, while false positives incur unnecessary cost. No intervention, and hence no financial consequence, is applied to customers predicted as non-churners.

3.3
Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)

The CLV for each customer is calculated using the revenue, the estimated retention probability, and a fixed profit margin:

\text{CLV}_{i}=\frac{R_{i}\times M}{1-\min(r_{i},0.995)}

(3)

Where:

•

R_{i}
is the monthly revenue from customer
i

•

M
is the profit margin

•

r_{i}
is the customer-specific retention probability

The minimum function ensures numerical stability by capping the retention probability at 0.995. This avoids division by values close to zero, which could lead to unrealistically large CLV estimates, especially in cases where long-tenured customers receive extremely high retention probabilities from survival models like Kaplan-Meier.

3.4
Retention Rate Estimation via Survival Analysis

To estimate the probability that a customer remains subscribed beyond a given tenure, we employ Kaplan-Meier survival analysis
[
25
]
. This method is non-parametric, meaning it does not assume any specific distribution of churn times, making it well-suited for business datasets where churn dynamics are irregular or unknown
[
29
]
. Compared to parametric or neural survival models, Kaplan-Meier offers a transparent and computationally efficient approach
[
30
]
that facilitates easy integration into evaluation workflows such as
e-Profits
.

We consider two forms of retention probability:

•

Average Retention Rate (ARR):
A global retention probability calculated from the full dataset. This rate reflects the overall likelihood of a randomly selected customer staying subscribed and is computed using the aggregate churn durations in the dataset. In our case, this is based on each dataset’s customer records available at the time of data collection.

•

Tenure-based Retention Rate (TRR):
A personalised retention estimate for each customer, derived from Kaplan-Meier survival curves conditioned on the customer’s current tenure (i.e., account length). This allows us to model retention more granularly, accounting for the fact that long-tenured customers are generally less likely to churn.

The CLV formula uses both
ARR
and
TRR
to compute expected profitability. To prevent unrealistically large CLV values due to extremely high retention estimates, we cap the retention rate at a maximum value of 0.995, ensuring numerical stability in the denominator, as discussed in Eq.
3
.

3.5
Cost of Intervention

Intervention cost comprises two components: the cost of making contact with the customer and the cost of a retention offer. Both are proportional to the customer’s CLV.

C_{\text{offer},i}=\text{CPO}\times\text{CLV}_{i}

(4)

C_{\text{contact},i}=\max(c_{0},c_{1}\times C_{\text{offer},i})

(5)

Where:

•

CPO = Cost-per-offer ratio (e.g., 0.1)

•

c_{0}
= fixed minimum contact cost

•

c_{1}
= proportional multiplier for contact cost

This setup reflects realistic business conditions where retention costs scale with customer value. Complete workflow of
e-profits
is illustrated in figure
1
.

3.6
Comparison with EMP

While the EMP metric also seeks to optimise profit, it differs from
e-Profits
in several key ways:

•

EMP assumes fixed CLV and global retention parameters
[
23
]
;
e-Profits
uses customer-specific inputs;

•

EMP models offer acceptance with a Beta distribution;
e-Profits
applies empirically estimated retention probabilities via survival analysis;

•

EMP is analytically optimised for a specific target fraction;
e-Profits
enables direct computation of total and top-segment profit without restrictive assumptions;

These features make
e-Profits
more understandable and suitable for operational decision-making in customer-centric businesses.

3.7
Implementation Guidance

e-Profits
can be applied to any churn classifier without modifying the model architecture. It requires:

1.

Customer-level predictions
\hat{y}_{i}
and true labels
y_{i}

2.

Monthly revenue and tenure information to compute
\text{CLV}_{i}

3.

Retention rate estimation using Kaplan-Meier survival curves

4.

Cost parameters for contact and offers

This modular design makes
e-Profits
readily integrable into standard evaluation pipelines and dashboards used by marketing and customer analytics teams.

3.11
Evaluation Metrics

We benchmark
e-Profits
against both traditional and profit-oriented evaluation metrics, including:

•

Accuracy:
Proportion of correctly classified instances. It is a simple metric, but it ignores class imbalance.

•

F1-score:
Harmonic mean of precision and recall, appropriate for better handling imbalanced binary classification.

•

AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve):
Measures ranking performance independent of decision threshold.

•

Top-Decile Lift:
Measures how concentrated churners are in the top 10% of predicted probabilities.

•

Lift Index:
Weighted index of churners across ranked deciles; values close to 1 indicate effective prioritisation.

•

Expected Maximum Profit (EMP):
A profit-based metric assuming fixed CLV and offer acceptance probabilities
[
16
]
.

•

e-Profits (ours):
Our proposed metric that evaluates models based on customer-specific CLV, retention, and intervention cost.

Metrics are reported in two scenarios: the
full population evaluation
, which assesses the total profit across all predicted churners, and the
top-segment evaluation
, focusing on the profit generated from the top 20% of the most profitable customers based on predicted risk. This dual perspective allows us to understand both global model performance and targeted campaign efficiency
4
4

4

Further mathematical formulations for traditional evaluation metrics can be found in our earlier review work
[
28
]
.

Results

This section presents and analyses the performance of several machine learning classifiers for churn prediction using the two benchmark datasets: IBM Telco and Maven Telecom. Each classifier is evaluated using both traditional metrics (e.g., Accuracy, F1-score, AUC) and business-aligned metrics (e.g., Top-Decile Lift, Lift Index, EMP, and the proposed
e-Profit

Results are organised in two parts. First, we report and discuss the results for the IBM Telco dataset, followed by the Maven dataset. We examine both full-population and top-segment outcomes for each dataset, focusing on how different tuning objectives (e.g., optimising for AUC vs.
e-Profits
) influence downstream profit impact.

5
Discussion

Our findings across the IBM and Maven datasets reveal consistent patterns that underscore the practical value of using
e-Profits
as an evaluation metric for churn prediction models. Traditional performance metrics such as AUC and F1-score did not consistently identify the most financially effective models. For instance, classifiers like LGBM and CatBoost, which did not top the rankings under accuracy or AUC, emerged as highly profitable when evaluated using
e-Profits
. This reordering highlights a critical discrepancy between predictive correctness and financial value, underscoring the limitations of relying solely on statistical metrics for real-world business decisions.

Moreover,
e-Profits
proved especially effective in identifying models that excel within high-value customer segments. In both datasets, CatBoost and GBC generated the highest profits when analysis was focused on the top 20% of customers sorted by predicted churn risk and estimated profitability. These models were not necessarily the best according to traditional metrics, reinforcing the importance of adopting evaluation criteria that prioritise return on investment (ROI) over aggregate accuracy. The ability to tailor model evaluation towards strategic, high-impact interventions makes
e-Profits
a valuable tool for resource-constrained retention campaigns.

A key insight from our analysis is the limited utility of commonly used lift metrics in reflecting actual business outcomes. While lift remains a popular heuristic in industry, it often fails to account for the heterogeneity in customer value and the variable cost of retention. For example, Table
7
illustrates scenarios where models with strong lift scores delivered zero
e-Profits
, indicating that churn likelihood alone is an insufficient criterion for profitable targeting. By combining churn risk with revenue potential and cost structure,
e-Profits
offers a more holistic and financially meaningful basis for model selection.

In addition to these strategic insights, we also note several practical considerations in the implementation of
e-Profits
. First, accurate estimation of customer lifetime value and retention probabilities requires access to detailed data, including monthly revenue, tenure, and timestamped churn labels. Organisations may need to enrich existing customer datasets to support such granular modelling. Second,
e-Profits
is sensitive to cost parameters such as contact cost and the cost of providing retention offers. While our experiments employed domain-informed heuristics for offer costs (e.g., proportions of CLV informed by CRM practices), businesses may need to calibrate these parameters based on internal financial policies and strategic goals. Third, our current approach assumes a binary intervention model, deciding whether or not to contact a customer based on predicted churn. Future extensions could consider multi-tiered or continuous intervention strategies to capture more complex business realities.

We also justified the use of Kaplan-Meier survival analysis for retention rate estimation, citing its simplicity, non-parametric nature, and understandability. These qualities make it suitable for business contexts where model transparency and ease of implementation are key. Although more complex methods such as DeepSurv
[
26
]
and DRSA
[
38
]
may offer improved predictive accuracy, their integration typically requires changes to model training workflows and increased computational overhead. For the purposes of post hoc model evaluation, Kaplan-Meier offers a robust and practical trade-off, delivering reliable customer-level retention estimates without excessive complexity.

In summary,
e-Profits
extends beyond predictive performance to deliver insights directly aligned with financial decision-making. Its application enables marketing and analytics teams to select models that maximise business impact rather than statistical precision alone, positioning it as a valuable addition to the model evaluation toolkit in customer retention contexts.

6
Conclusion

Traditional churn prediction models are often assessed using statistical performance metrics such as Accuracy, AUC, and F1-score. While these metrics serve well in quantifying classification performance, they frequently fail to capture the real financial consequences of misclassification, particularly in profit-sensitive applications like customer retention.

In this study, we introduced
e-Profits
, a business-aligned evaluation metric that integrates customer-specific lifetime value (CLV), intervention costs, and retention probabilities derived from Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Unlike traditional metrics and even profit-based alternatives such as EMP,
e-Profits
evaluates models based on individualised cost-revenue dynamics, facilitating better alignment between predictive analytics and financial outcomes. Our empirical evaluation on two benchmark datasets demonstrated that
e-Profits
significantly reshapes classifier rankings. Models that were suboptimal under Accuracy or AUC often emerged as the most financially effective under
e-Profits
, particularly when focusing on the top 20% of customers by predicted profitability. This shift underscores the potential for misaligned decision-making if only conventional metrics are used. The metric’s post hoc nature and lightweight implementation make
e-Profits
practical for real-world deployment. It requires no retraining and can be integrated easily into existing evaluation workflows, making it accessible to business analysts and operational stakeholders alike. Moreover, its understandable structure enhances communication between technical teams and decision-makers.

Future directions for
e-Profits
include embedding it as an objective function during model training and experimenting with advanced survival models for improved retention prediction. Overall,
e-Profits
introduces a financially meaningful evaluation paradigm that empowers practitioners to make data-driven decisions rooted in return on investment.
