Exploratory study of the oral microbiota in pregnant women with hypothyroidism and their infants
Authors: Mayu Ohmichi-Tomiwa, Nahoko Kato-Kogoe, Asako Kudo, Daisuke Fujita, Shoichi Sakaguchi, Kouji Tsuda, Michi Omori, Emi Hayashi, Shota Nakamura, Takashi Nakano, Masahide Ohmichi, Junko Tamaki, Takaaki Ueno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypothyroidism is a metabolic disorder associated with potentially adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. Emerging evidence suggests a link between thyroid function and the microbiota; however, little is known about the oral microbiota of pregnant women with hypothyroidism and its potential impact on that of their offspring. This study aimed to characterize the oral microbiota of pregnant women with hypothyroidism and their children as part of the Oral Microbiome Prospective Unicenter Cohort Study (OMPU-CS).MethodsPregnant women with hypothyroidism (Hypothyroid group, n = 31) and those with normal thyroid function (Control group, n = 30) were selected from participants in the ongoing OMPU-CS. Oral samples were collected from the women during pregnancy and at one month postpartum, and from their one-month-old infants. Microbiota composition was analyzed using 16 S rRNA metagenomic sequencing.ResultsCompared with pregnant women in the Control group, those in the Hypothyroid group exhibited significantly reduced richness and evenness of the oral microbiota (observed operational taxonomic units, p = 0.034; Shannon index, p = 0.034). The overall structure of the oral microbiota differed significantly between groups at all phases—in pregnant women, postpartum women, and their infants (unweighted UniFrac distances, p = 0.002, p = 0.049, and p = 0.019, respectively). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) identified several differentially abundant taxa, including a consistently reduced abundance of members of the Rhizobiaceae family in the Hypothyroid group across all three phases compared with that in the Control group.ConclusionsThe oral microbiota of pregnant women with hypothyroidism and their one-month-old infants exhibited disease-specific characteristics. These findings suggest that maternal hypothyroidism may influence the oral microbiota of offspring, underscoring the importance of monitoring oral microbiota in mothers with hypothyroidism and their children.

Background

Hypothyroidism is a prevalent endocrine disorder, and maternal hypothyroidism poses significant risks to both maternal and fetal health [1, 2]. During pregnancy, increased demand for thyroid hormones induces anatomical and physiological changes in the thyroid gland [3]. As thyroid hormones are essential for pregnancy maintenance and fetal development, maternal hypothyroidism is associated with adverse outcomes such as miscarriage, preterm birth, gestational hypertension, and low birth weight [4–8], and may influence the health of the child [8].

Emerging evidence indicates that the gut microbiota is an important environmental factor influencing the pathogenesis of thyroid disease, and thyroid dysfunction may in turn disrupt gut microbial communities [9–11]. While the relationship between thyroid function and the oral microbiota is less well studied, thyroid hormone alterations, such as those occurring in hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism, have been shown to be potentially associated with the oral microbiome [12, 13]. Furthermore, observational studies suggest a bidirectional relationship between periodontal disease and thyroid dysfunction [14, 15], and periodontal pathogens may exert systemic effects that interfere with endocrine function, including that of the thyroid gland [16].

The oral microbiota is altered by immunological, endocrinological, and metabolic changes during pregnancy [17, 18]. These changes increase susceptibility to oral infections such as periodontal disease [19, 20]. The altered maternal oral microbiota and periodontal disease may serve as potential risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth, preeclampsia, and low birth weight [17, 18, 20–22]. However, little is known about how hypothyroidism during pregnancy affects the maternal oral microbiota [23].

The maternal microbiota is considered a major determinant of microbial colonization in newborns [24], and several studies have demonstrated similarities between the oral microbiota of mothers and their infants [25, 26]. Maternal and neonatal microbiota are also thought to influence early-life development and may have long-term physiological consequences [27, 28]. These findings underscore the importance of investigating how maternal health and oral microbiota during pregnancy affect the establishment of the infant oral microbiota. Maternal metabolic conditions such as gestational diabetes and obesity have been shown to alter the microbiota of offspring [29–33], but no studies have examined the influence of maternal hypothyroidism on the microbiota of their offspring.

Therefore, the present study focused on the oral microbiota of pregnant women with hypothyroidism and their one-month-old infants. Using samples from participants in the Oral Microbiome Prospective Unicenter Cohort Study (OMPU-CS), we evaluated the characteristics and associations of the oral microbiota in this population.

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its latest revision. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University, Takatsuki, Japan (Approval No. 2410). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The OMPU-CS is a prospective cohort study designed to investigate the associations among maternal oral microbiota, lifestyle factors, and health status during pregnancy, as well as their impact on pregnancy outcomes and infant growth, development, and oral microbiota composition. Between June 2018 and January 2022, 601 community-dwelling pregnant women were enrolled in the OMPU-CS. Data about medical histories and medication use as well as maternal and infant characteristics and pregnancy outcomes were collected from medical records. The pre-pregnancy weight and the feeding practices at one month infant were obtained via questionnaire.

Participants in this study were selected from pregnant women and their infants enrolled in the OMPU-CS. The inclusion criteria were as follows: for the Hypothyroid group, pregnant women receiving treatment for hypothyroidism at the time of saliva collection; for the Control group, pregnant women with no history of thyroid dysfunction or medication use, with blood test data available throughout the pregnancy period, and with serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and free thyroxine (FT4) levels within the reference ranges during pregnancy. The exclusion criteria were not defined. For pregnant women in the Hypothyroid group, we confirmed through questionnaires and medical records that they were receiving regular treatment for hypothyroidism although information regarding thyroid status such as TSH level during pregnancy was not obtained in this study. For participants in the Control group, thyroid function was assessed immediately after pregnancy diagnosis, and we confirmed through medical records that no abnormalities in thyroid function were present throughout the pregnancy period. A total of 61 pregnant women and their infants were included in the analysis: 31 in the Hypothyroidism group and 30 in the Control group.

Oral sample collection

Oral samples were collected from 61 mothers during pregnancy (between 22 and 35 weeks of gestation), and at one month postpartum, and from their one-month-old infants. Collection was performed as previously described [34]. Briefly, saliva samples were obtained using the SalivaBio® oral swab and swab storage tube system (Salimetrics, Irvine, PA, USA). Saliva samples from the mothers were collected by having each of them hold an oral swab (length 3 cm, diameter 1 cm) in their mouth for 1 min to allow saliva to penetrate, and then placing the swab in a dedicated tube and recovering the saliva using centrifugation. Saliva samples from infants were collected using oral swabs designed for infants. Specifically, the tip of an infant’s swab stick (length 9 cm, diameter 6.5 mm) was placed in each infant’s mouth for 1 min to allow saliva to penetrate, after which the saliva was collected using centrifugation. Samples were immediately frozen and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction.

DNA extraction, 16 S rRNA sequencing, and taxonomic classification

DNA extraction, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, and taxonomic classification were performed as previously described [35], with minor modifications. Briefly, homogenized samples underwent DNA extraction, and the V1–V2 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using PCR. Indexed libraries (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) were prepared, and 250 bp paired-end reads were generated using 500 cycles on the MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 (Illumina). An average of 29,224 sequence reads were denoised and quality-filtered using DADA2 within Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2) version 2020.08. Rarefaction was applied with a minimum depth threshold of 10,000 reads, and operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were assigned using the Silva-138-99 Reference Database.

Statistical analysis

Between-group comparisons of patient characteristics were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Statistical analyses were conducted using the SAS statistical package version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). All probability values for statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Alpha diversity, indicating richness and evenness of bacterial communities, was assessed using the observed OTU index and Shannon index, and group differences were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Beta diversity was assessed using phylogenetic tree-based metrics (unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances) and visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Differences in microbial community composition among groups were tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). All analyses were conducted in QIIME2. Differentially abundant taxa were identified using the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) algorithm, with a significance threshold of α = 0.05 and a logarithmic LDA score cutoff of 2.5.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

The mean age at delivery for the participating pregnant women was 35 (range: 20–46) years, mean pre-pregnancy BMI was 20.5 (range: 15.8–28.7), and mean gestational age at delivery was 38 (range: 33–40) weeks. There were no significant differences between the Hypothyroid and Control groups in maternal characteristics (Table 1). In the Hypothyroid group, there was a tendency for gestational diabetes mellitus, preterm birth, and low-birth-weight infants to be more common than in the Control group. Infant characteristics at birth showed no significant differences between the groups. Among the one-month-old infants, age at oral sample collection and feeding method were comparable between the groups; however, weight, length, and head circumference were significantly lower in the Hypothyroid group than in the Control group (p < 0.05; Table 1).

Oral microbiota composition

In women during their pregnancy (M0), at one month postpartum (M1), and in their one-month-old infants (C), oral bacteria with relative abundances > 0.1% in at least one participant were classified into 11 phyla, 17 classes, 50 orders, 88 families, and 177 genera. At the class level, the predominant taxa (accounting for > 1% of total sequences in any group) in M0, M1, and C were Bacilli, Gammaproteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidia (Fig. 1a). These four classes comprised 78.0% (Hypothyroid group) and 75.3% (Control group) of the total at M0; 77.6% (Hypothyroid group) and 77.2% (Control group) at M1; and 92.8% (Hypothyroid group) and 92.9% (Control group) in C (Fig. 1a).

Differences in oral microbiota between Hypothyroid and Control groups

The alpha diversity analysis revealed significantly lower richness and evenness of oral microbiota at M0 in the Hypothyroid group than in the Control group (observed OTU index, p = 0.034; Shannon index, p = 0.034; Fig. 2a). No significant differences in alpha diversity were observed in M1 or C (Fig. 2b, c). The beta diversity analysis showed that PCoA based on unweighted UniFrac distances demonstrated distinct clustering of oral microbiota between the Hypothyroid and Control groups in three-dimensional space at M0 and M1 and in C. These group-level differences in microbial community structure were confirmed by PERMANOVA (999 permutations) using unweighted UniFrac distances (M0: p = 0.002; M1: p = 0.049; C: p = 0.019; Fig. 3). No significant differences were observed using the weighted UniFrac distance metric, which accounts for both the presence/absence and abundance of taxa, at any phase (Fig. 3).

Bacteria with differential abundance between Hypothyroid and Control groups

The LEfSe analysis identified taxa with significantly different abundances between the Hypothyroid and Control groups. The cladogram in Fig. 4 illustrates these differences across taxonomic levels from phylum to genus. At the family level, at M0, Leptotrichiaceae, Clostridia, and Rhizobiaceae were significantly more abundant in the Control group, whereas Lactobacillaceae was more abundant in the Hypothyroid group (Fig. 4a). At M1, Fusobacteriaceae, RF39, and Rhizobiaceae were enriched in the Control group, while no specific family was abundant in the Hypothyroid group (Fig. 4b). In C, Lactobacillaceae and Rhizobiaceae were significantly more abundant in the Control group, whereas Enterococcaceae was more prevalent in the Hypothyroid group (Fig. 4c).

Notably, members of the family Rhizobiaceae were consistently more abundant in the Control group than in the Hypothyroid group across all phases (M0, M1, and C), as indicated by blue arrows in Fig. 4. A comparison of the relative abundance of Rhizobiaceae between the groups revealed that it was significantly higher in the Control group than in the Hypothyroid group at each phase (p < 0.05; Fig. 5a). The proportion of individuals with detectable Rhizobiaceae in the oral cavity was 54.8% in the Hypothyroid group and 96.7% in the Control group at M0, 35.5% and 76.7% at M1, and 38.7% and 83.3% in C, respectively, all higher than the proportion in the Control group.

The results of comparing the relative abundance of Rhizobiaceae for the M0, M1, and C pairs are shown in Fig. 5b. Mothers with no detectable Rhizobiaceae in the oral cavity during pregnancy (M0) tended to remain negative at one month postpartum (M1) (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001). Infants (C) of mothers with no Rhizobiaceae detected in M0 or M1 also tended to lack detectable Rhizobiaceae (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001). However, greater maternal abundance of Rhizobiaceae at M0 or M1 did not consistently correspond to higher abundance in the infant’s oral microbiota (Fig. 5b).

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the oral microbiota of pregnant women with hypothyroidism, as well as that of the same individuals at one month postpartum and their one-month-old infants. The results showed that the oral microbiota of women with hypothyroidism during pregnancy and at one month postpartum differed from that of women without abnormal thyroid function at the corresponding phases. Moreover, the oral microbiota of infants born to hypothyroid mothers also differed from that of infants born to mothers with normal thyroid function. Notably, Rhizobium spp. was consistently less abundant in the Hypothyroid group across all phases than in the Control group. These findings suggest a disease-specific oral microbial signature in pregnant women with hypothyroidism that may influence the oral microbiota of their offspring.

The alpha diversity analysis in the present study revealed significantly reduced richness and evenness of oral bacterial species in the Hypothyroid group compared with those in the Control group during pregnancy. This trend is consistent with a previous study of Han Chinese pregnant women with hypothyroidism, although statistical significance was not achieved in that cohort [23]. No significant differences in alpha diversity were found at one month postpartum (M1) or in the infants (C) of women in the Hypothyroid group. Furthermore, beta diversity analysis demonstrated significant differences in microbial composition the corresponding between groups. The differences in the microbial composition of the oral microbiota of pregnant women with hypothyroidism corroborate earlier research finding [23], but to our knowledge, no other studies have investigated the oral microbiota of pregnant women with hypothyroidism. Regarding the association between thyroid function and oral microbiota, previous reports indicate that serum TSH, free triiodothyronine, FT₄, and thyroglobulin levels are associated with the oral microbiota of non-pregnant individuals [12, 13]. Therefore, the results of this study warrant further validation, as dynamic changes in thyroid hormone levels during pregnancy in women with hypothyroidism may influence the oral microbiota.

The LEfSe analysis in the present study identified a consistently lower abundance of Rhizobiaceae in the Hypothyroid group across pregnant women, their one-month postpartum samples, and their infants. Although the role of Rhizobiaceae in human health remains unclear, previous work has shown that it is more abundant in healthy individuals than in patients with thyroid cancer [24], suggesting a potential association with thyroid function. However, the specific mechanisms linking Rhizobiaceae to hypothyroidism, particularly in pregnancy and early life, require further investigation. Nevertheless, the results of this study suggest that this maternal oral bacterium can colonize the infant’s oral cavity. Our M0, M1, and C pair analyses showed similar trends in lack detectable Rhizobiaceae in the oral microbiota. The mechanism of vertical transmission of specific oral bacteria from mother to infant requires further exploration, as little is currently known about this process [25, 26].

In the present study, the oral microbiota of infants born to hypothyroid mothers showed lower abundances of not only Rhizobiaceae but also Lactobacillus, and higher abundances of Enterococcus, compared with controls, based on LEfSe analysis. To date, there are no reports on characteristic oral bacteria in infants born to hypothyroid mothers. However, similar findings have been reported in studies of the gut microbiota in preterm infants, with decreased Lactobacillus and increased Enterococcus observed [36, 37]. In the gut microbiota, Lactobacillus is generally considered a beneficial bacterium, while Enterococcus is potentially pathogenic [38, 39]. The mechanism by which maternal metabolic states influence the infant microbiota remains unclear. Specifically, it is uncertain whether the mother’s disease-specific microbiota contributes to the establishment of the infant’s microbiota, or whether systemic factors in the infant of a mother with disease affect microbial colonization [40, 41]. Maternal metabolic conditions, such as gestational diabetes mellitus, have been associated with alterations in both the gut [29, 30, 42] and oral microbiota of offspring [31–33], supporting the plausibility of our findings. It will be important to assess how the oral microbiota of one-month-old infants evolves as they grow. In future studies, we plan to follow the development of the oral microbiota in children born to mothers with hypothyroidism over a longer period.

It is noteworthy that in this study, infants in the Hypothyroidism group showed no significant difference in birth weight compared with those in the Control group, but were found to have significantly lower weight, height, and head circumference at one month of age. It is known that maternal hypothyroidism affects birth weight; however, the participants in this study received management and treatment for hypothyroidism, which likely prevented any effect on birth weight. It remains unclear whether maternal hypothyroidism affects children’s growth, and the findings of this study would be worth verifying through future large-scale longitudinal research.

This study has some limitations. First, although the design included paired sampling from pregnant women and their infants at one month postpartum, the sample size was relatively small. To confirm and validate our findings, a larger-scale study is required. For example, using a larger sample size would be beneficial for confirming whether the observed differences in infant characteristics—such as low birth weight, short stature, and reduced head circumference—in the Hypothyroid group represent potentially relevant findings. Second, we used 16 S rRNA metagenomic sequencing, which provides insights into community structure and relative bacterial abundance but does not capture absolute abundance or detailed functional capacity. To better characterize specific taxa and their roles, future studies should incorporate more comprehensive approaches, such as shotgun metagenomics, long-read sequencing, and multi-omics analyses. Third, this was an observational study; thus, causal relationships cannot be established. In particular, the pregnant women with hypothyroidism in this study were managed with thyroid medication, but their condition was not evaluated. The effects of the drugs and the state of control may have influenced the results of this study. In addition, we did not evaluate potential confounding factors such as diet, hygiene practices, or medication use that could influence the oral microbiota. We believe that future large-scale studies should consider these factors. Further mechanistic investigations, including animal studies, are required to elucidate the biological pathways linking maternal hypothyroidism to changes in the oral microbiota of mothers and their offspring. Despite these limitations, this preliminary study provides foundational evidence for an association between hypothyroidism and alterations in the oral microbiota during pregnancy and early infancy.

Conclusions

We characterized the oral microbiota of pregnant women with hypothyroidism and their infants at one-month postpartum. The oral microbiota of pregnant women with hypothyroidism differed from that of pregnant women with normal thyroid function and may influence the oral microbiota of their offspring. These findings provide a foundation for future research aimed at developing diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to support oral health in mothers with hypothyroidism and their children.
