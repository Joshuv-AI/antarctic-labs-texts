Predictors of male partners’ behavioral intentions and cognitive readiness toward maternal health service utilization in rural Tanzania: an application of the theory of planned behavior
Authors: Fabiola V. Moshi, Walter C. Millanzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMen are not only heads and decision-makers in most families, but they also heavily influence the healthcare access of their female partners and/or children. However, low male spousal accompaniment at health facilities remains a persistent public health challenge in rural Tanzania. While structural barriers are well documented, there is a lack of evidence regarding the behavioral intention factors that drive this men's physical disengagement from clinical spaces. Understanding these internal drivers is critical for designing effective targeted interventions to improve maternal service utilization in rural settings. Applying the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), this study assessed the predictors of male partners’ behavioral intentions and cognitive readiness toward spousal accompaniment in maternal health service utilization in rural Tanzania.MethodsA baseline community-based assessment from a longitudinal interventional study was adopted to evaluate 546 randomly selected male partners from June to October 2017 was conducted using a quantitative research approach. A multi-stage sampling technique was employed to select participants. While male accompaniment broadly encompasses emotional and domestic support, this study specifically operationalized male spousal accompaniment as men's physical accompaniment of their expectant mothers to the health facility for clinical maternal health services. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 25. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors (α = 0.05).ResultsParticipants’ mean age was 31 ± 7.726 years, and 71.8% were married. Findings revealed that 61.7% of male partners had never physically accompanied their spouses to health facilities for maternal services. High rates of negative behavioral constructs were observed, with 59.0%, 57.0%, and 57.0% of male partners reporting negative attitudes, demonstrating negative perceived subjective norms, and negative perceived behavior control, respectively. Multivariable analysis showed that positive attitude (AOR = 3.616; p < 0.05; 95%CI: 1.333, 6.138), positive perceived subjective norms (AOR = 2.374; p < 0.05; 95%CI: 1.669, 5.984), and positive perceived behavioral control (AOR = 1.759; p < 0.05; 95%CI: 1.004, 4.373) were significantly associated with increased prospective intention to accompany partners to care.ConclusionA significant proportion of male partners in rural Tanzania remain physically disengaged from clinical maternal spaces, driven primarily by negative cognitive constructs and traditional social norms that designate pregnancy care as an exclusively female domain. Policy and programmatic actions should prioritize community-based social and behavior change communications (SBCC) that challenge restrictive subjective norms, foster joint family decision-making, and reshape male attitudes toward spousal accompaniment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09427-0.

Introduction

Male partner accompaniment (MPA) is a pivotal determinant in Maternal Health Services (MHS) utilization and positive birth outcomes as well, yet its prevalence remains a significant global challenge [1]. While participation is nearly universal in the Global North, it remains unacceptably low across the Global South [2]. In Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), male accompaniment in antenatal care (ANC) is often fragmented, with many men acting as financial providers but remaining physically absent from clinical spaces [3]. For instance, while some SSA regions report rising trends, others still see less than 30% of men accompanying their partners to health facilities [4]. In Tanzania, although institutional delivery has improved to approximately 78% nationally by 2024, male attendance in clinical settings lags significantly, particularly in rural areas where gender-defined roles are more rigid [5]. Moreover, in the Rukwa region, recent data suggest that skilled birth attendance and postnatal care utilization remain below national targets, often exacerbated by the lack of active male partnership [6]. Addressing this gap is central to achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1, which aims to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio to less than 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030 by shifting from individual maternal focus to a couple-centered approach [7, 8].

Tanzania has made commendable strides, reducing its maternal mortality ratio (MMR) from 980 in 2000 to approximately 276 per 100,000 live births by 2023 [9]. The government has integrated male accompaniment into the National Road Map Strategic Plan to improve Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child, and Adolescent Health, which explicitly advocates for male accompaniment [3]. Internationally, these efforts align with the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines and the 1964 International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD) framework, yet the transition from policy to community-level practice in rural Tanzania remains low [10]. The risk is unevenly distributed; for example, institutional MMR in regions such as Mtwara and Iringa has reached as high as 174 and 105 per 100,000 live births in 2024 [11]. In the Rukwa region, where this study is situated, maternal service utilization is impacted by geographical and socio-economic barriers [6]. In rural Tanzania, traditional gender roles often define maternal health as a female domain, where a man’s presence at an antenatal clinic is perceived as a sign of weakness or a violation of cultural norms [12].

While existing literature has extensively documented structural barriers, such as distance to facilities, long waiting times, unfriendly healthcare staff, unfriendly health infrastructure, and the dominance of female healthcare providers, there is a profound gap in understanding the psychological and behavioral drivers of male disengagement in maternal health services [1, 13]. Most studies in the Rukwa region have focused on the “what” (prevalence) and socio-demographic indicators rather than the “why” (behavioral intent) [14]. Do men with access to services choose not to engage? There is an urgent need to understand the psychological drivers, attitudes, and social pressures that influence a man’s decision-making process during his partner’s pregnancy. This study is designed to fill this gap by examining the men's internal cognitive factors that precede the decision to participate in maternal care.

The study was grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which provides a robust framework for understanding health-seeking actions [15]. The TPB posits that an individual’s behavior is driven by their behavioral intention, which is shaped by three constructs, including Attitude (personal evaluation of the favorable or unfavorable feelings of being involved), Subjective Norms (perceived social pressure from peers or elders), and Perceived Behavioral Control (the man’s belief in their ability to overcome barriers such as work or clinic costs) [15]. In this study, the TPB is utilized to move beyond descriptive statistics, allowing the researchers to quantify how a man’s personal beliefs and his perception of community expectations directly influence his accompaniment in maternal services, providing a scientific basis for behavioral change interventions.

Research in the Rukwa region is particularly timely as the government seeks to decentralize maternal services to the community level and to find cost-effective ways to increase maternal health services uptake [16]. By quantifying whether negative attitudes or restrictive social norms through the lens of TPB are the primary barriers, this study will provide the policy-relevant evidence needed to design targeted behavioral change communications. This study does not merely document low accompaniment; it provides a baseline for interventional strategies that can reshape the behavioral intentions of men, thus improving the utilization of maternal health services and reducing preventable deaths in rural Tanzania. Nevertheless, this study fills a methodological gap by providing a baseline for interventional studies aimed at transforming men from passive financial providers into active, informed partners in maternal health.

Methods and materials

Study design and target population

This study presents the baseline assessment of the cross-sectional phase of a larger quasi-experimental interventional design evaluated through a quantitative research approach [17–21]. Data collection was executed between June 1 and October 30, 20,217. The target population comprised male partners of pregnant women residing within the selected clusters. To be eligible for inclusion, participants had to be male partners cohabiting with a pregnant woman whose gestational age was documented at ≤ 24 weeks at the time of recruitment, and both partners had provided informed consent. If a female partner reported amenorrhea for at least two consecutive months but had not confirmed her pregnancy status, a urine human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) test was administered on-site; a positive result granted eligibility for household enrollment. Exclusion criteria applied to male partners who were transitionally resident in the village for less than six months, those with cognitive impairments that restricted communication, or couples where the female partner presented with immediate obstetric emergencies requiring urgent referral.

Study setting

The study was conducted across 45 randomly selected villages within the Kalambo and Sumbawanga Rural Districts of the Rukwa Region, situated in the Southern Highlands of Tanzania. The Rukwa Region is predominantly rural, with the economy anchored heavily on subsistence agriculture, livestock keeping, and small-scale fishing, occupations that separate men from their households for extended daytime hours. The region features a fragmented healthcare infrastructure, consisting of approximately two regional referral hospitals, three district hospitals, and a network of scattered rural health centers and dispensaries. On average, communities in these rural districts reside at a distance of 7 to 15 km from the nearest facility offering comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care (CEmONC). This geographical terrain, characterized by poor road infrastructure and seasonal sub-optimal transport coverage, severely limits access to maternal care. These structural factors, coupled with the dominance of female healthcare providers in maternal clinics and long facility waiting times (frequently exceeding three hours), heavily predispose men to avoid accompanying their partners to health facilities.

Sample size determination

The sample size for this study was calculated based on the estimated prevalence of skilled delivery utilization. A two-tailed formula comparing two independent proportions (intervention vs. control groups) in a quasi-experimental design by West et al., [22]. Fixed statistical parameters were used, including a 95% confidence interval/level (Z = 0.196), a 5% standard margin of error (e = 0.05), and a design effect of 1.5 to account for cluster sampling variations. The primary baseline parameter was drawn from the national prevalence of skilled delivery utilization in the Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS) (NBS, 2016). Crucially, because the active fieldwork and data collection phase of this study was conducted from June to October 2017, the 2015–16 TDHS represented the most current, legally mandated, and contemporary national dataset available at the time of this study. Adjusting for a non-response safety margin of 10%, the final conservative sample estimate was established at 546 male partners. While recognizing that every arm of the maternal continuum of care is critical, skilled delivery was selected as the reference indicator for sample estimation because it represents the primary bottleneck in rural maternal health utilization. Because its baseline utilization rate is closer to the maximum statistical variance threshold (50%) compared to early ANC indicators, calculating the sample based on skilled delivery yielded a larger, more conservative sample size. This approach effectively ensured that the study remained fully powered to evaluate the secondary maternal health utilization metrics analyzed throughout the text.

Data collection instrument

Data collection procedures

Data collection was conducted by the principal investigator and a team of six trained research assistants who held bachelor’s degrees in nursing or public health and were fluent in both Swahili and local vernacular languages. Research assistants underwent an intensive three-day training workshop covering ethical protocols, unbiased questioning techniques, and cluster management. To guarantee maximum visual and auditory privacy, data collection was conducted in neutral, unoccupied community spaces (such as designated classrooms or community centers/public offices) outside the earshot of spouses or peers. Participants were seated at a minimum distance of approximately 3 m apart to prevent peer dialogue or cross-contamination of responses. Before initiating the interview, brief standardized instructions were read aloud. The average time for questionnaire administration ranged between 30 and 45 min. Completed questionnaires were assigned unique alphanumeric codes rather than personal identifiers to maintain strict confidentiality, and physical copies were stored in secure, locked cabinets accessible only to the principal investigator.

Validity

To establish robust content and face validity, the initial draft of the tool was submitted to a panel of reviewers, including two biostatisticians, two public health specialists, and two senior academic experts in reproductive health. These experts evaluated each item for its thematic relevance, linguistic clarity, and structural alignment with the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) constructs. The Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated at both the item level (I-CVI) and the scale level (S-CVI). Items yielding an I-CVI below 0.80 were eliminated or revised. Based on expert recommendations, item phrasings were adapted to suit the lower literacy levels characteristic of rural populations, and abstract theoretical jargon was replaced with concrete examples of maternal support behaviors (e.g., replacing “maternal service utilization” with “accompanying your spouse to the antenatal clinic”).

Reliability

To verify the psychometric stability and internal consistency of the translated instrument, a pilot pre-test was conducted among ten (10) couples residing in an independent ward exhibiting identical sociodemographic features but excluded from the final study sample [6, 14, 16, 31]. Inter-observer reliability was assessed among the data collectors, achieving a high consensus rate (> 90%). The pilot data were subjected to an Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) to ensure proper factor loading structure. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy yielded a value of 0.78, and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity demonstrated highly significant Sphericity (p < 0.001), confirming that the data matrix was appropriate for factor extraction. Internal consistency was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha (α) coefficients for each independent domain. For the actual field data, the instrument achieved highly dependable reliability scores: α = 0.81 for the Attitude scale, α = 0.79 for Perceived Subjective Norms, and α = 0.84 for the Perceived Behavioral Control scale, exceeding the internationally recognized threshold of ≥ 0.70 for behavioral sciences [33, 34].

Variable measurement and psychometric evaluation

To capture the cognitive constructs of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) before the completion of obstetric events, items were designed to measure prospective behavioral intentions and attitudes (e.g., “My intention to accompany my wife to the health center for her upcoming deliveries is …”). To ensure methodological rigor and respect temporal ordering, male accompaniment was operationalized into two distinct dimensions. These included: (1) Retrospective experience, assessing the male partner’s reported accompaniment in previous pregnancies as a baseline behavioral profile, and (2) Prospective cognitive readiness, measuring current attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and explicit behavioral intentions to accompany their partners during the current ongoing pregnancy and upcoming childbirth. The variable “Intention to accompany a female partner to childbirth” was assessed at baseline by asking male partners whose spouses were in their first trimester whether they intended or were willing to physically accompany their partners into the labor and delivery room during childbirth. This was measured as a binary baseline indicator (“Yes” vs. “No”) of future behavioral intention, rather than an observed retrospective practice.

This dual-operationalization was opted for as it aligns directly with the predictive structure of the Theory of Planned Behavior. This aligns with the psychometric architecture of the TPB, which posits that prospective behavioral intention is the most proximate cognitive precursor to actual future behavior. Construct validation was executed using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA). Initially, each of the three behavioral domains (Attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control) comprised 25 raw items (Table 3). This ensured an objective, criteria-based classification independent of the sample distribution.

Discussion

According to the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), an individual’s health-seeking behavior is fundamentally driven by a cognitive constellation of personal attitudes, perceived social pressure, and self-efficacy [15]. This study utilized a consolidated TPB framework to map how structural barriers and deeply entrenched socio-cultural dimensions independently shape male partners’ engagement in maternal health service utilization. By analyzing these constructs simultaneously, the study successfully captured both the internal mindsets and external systemic realities that govern spousal accompaniment in rural reproductive health networks. The findings of the descriptive analysis of this study revealed a profound deficit in active male participation, with the significant majority of male partners reporting they had never been involved in maternal health services utilization. This behavioral passivity corresponds directly with a dominant negative trend across all three underlying TPB constructs, including attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control [39].

This widespread negative orientation contrasts sharply with findings from similar maternal health evaluations in Iran and India, where male partners demonstrated overwhelmingly positive attitudes and routinely participated in pregnancy care without facing social stigma [15]. In the context of this rural study community, these negative cognitive constructs persist because pregnancy and childbirth remain culturally constructed as exclusively female domains [40]. While men are socially expected to act as financial providers, physically entering maternal health spaces is often locally interpreted as a violation of traditional gender roles [41]. Men who attempt to break this boundary often face subtle social sanctions, domestic ridicule, or peer disapproval, which systematically depresses their enthusiasm to participate [42]. To dismantle these entrenched normative barriers, the Ministry of Health should leverage these findings to design targeted social and behavioral change communication (SBCC) programmes that engage local traditional gatekeepers to reframe clinical spousal accompaniment as an essential aspect modern partner.

Findings of the multivariable logistic regression models demonstrated that ethnicity acts as a powerful, cross-cutting independent predictor across all three TPB constructs. Specifically, male partners from the Mambwe ethnic group exhibited significantly higher odds of holding positive attitudes, reporting positive subjective norms, and demonstrating positive perceived behavioral control compared to their Fipa counterparts. This significant variation aligns with wider implementation science literature from Sub-Saharan Africa, which establishes that maternal health-seeking behaviors are rarely uniform across a single geographical region; instead, they are highly fragmented by sub-district tribal governance and lineage systems [32, 43]. In the study’s immediate context, this divergence suggests that Mambwe socio-cultural frameworks and contemporary community practices place a much stronger and more progressive emphasis on shared spousal responsibility during reproductive milestones than neighboring tribes. Fipa cultural structures, by contrast, appear to enforce a more rigid domestic division of labor that distances men from clinical spaces. Consequently, reproductive health planners should reject one-size-fits-all strategies and instead implement culturally tailored peer-education interventions that utilize traditional elders from progressive cohorts to diffuse positive behavioral models to adjacent, conservative communities [44].

Exposure to target health information emerged as a vital entry point for reshaping personal mindsets. Men who had previously been exposed to messages regarding birth preparedness had significantly higher odds of maintaining a positive attitude toward maternal accompaniment than those who had not. This finding strongly reinforces existing maternal health literature across East Africa, which consistently demonstrates that structured reproductive health literacy directly correlates with increased physical and emotional spousal support during the perinatal period [29]. Possible reasons for this are grounded in the fact that when men are exposed to Birth Preparedness and Complications Readiness (BPCR) frameworks, they undergo a cognitive shift [45]. They begin to view physical accompaniment not as an unnecessary intrusion into a female space, but as a functional, protective marital obligation designed to safeguard their family against unexpected obstetric emergencies [46]. Formal knowledge effectively demystifies the clinical environment, bridging the traditional gap between passive financial provision and active physical presence [47]. This clear link signifies an immediate programmatic need to transition to couple-friendly Antenatal Care (ANC) protocols where health facilities structurally integrate male partners into joint BPCR educational consultations.

A robust, identical predictive relationship was established between a man’s explicit willingness to accompany his partner during childbirth and his positive subjective norms and perceived behavioral control. This finding provides empirical support for the core theoretical assumptions of the TPB, which indicate that strong internal self-efficacy and perceived social alignment are the direct precursors to behavioral intentions and ultimate practice [32]. In this rural environment, men who possess an intrinsic desire to witness childbirth are better psychologically equipped to withstand localized stigma or traditional taboos. Their high perceived behavioral control functions as a cognitive shield because they feel highly capable of navigating the healthcare system and believe their immediate inner circle approves, they actively choose to ignore broader community disapproval [48]. To reinforce this internal self-efficacy, hospital managers should remove institutional disincentives by redesigning delivery wards to feature private spaces that accommodate and structurally validate male presence during intrapartum care.

Additionally, physical access to health infrastructure emerged as a critical structural bottleneck, whereas living a moderate distance away from a healthcare facility significantly reduced the odds of a male partner holding a positive attitude toward maternal accompaniment compared to those living in proximity. This finding aligns seamlessly with the geographical dimensions of the classic three delays model in maternal health, which identifies physical distance and transit logistics as primary structural impediments to service utilization [15]. When healthcare facilities require long walking distances over difficult rural terrain, the opportunity cost of male accompaniment skyrockets [49]. Men have to weigh the physical burden and the loss of a productive working day against a behavior that their culture already tells them is low-priority [42]. This finding highlights that as distance increases, men are highly likely to relegate pregnancy care back to the custody of women, viewing the journey as an inefficient use of male domestic labor [42]. To mitigate these spatial barriers and lower the threshold for male participation, local governments should prioritize decentralized infrastructure by expanding dispensary-level maternity wings closer to peripheral villages [15].

By applying the TPB framework, this study contributes an empirical model demonstrating how socio-cultural identities and structural barriers independently shape male partner configurations, providing a blueprint for shifting behavioral paradigms from passive financial providers to active spousal accompaniment in rural maternal healthcare [50]. These findings are highly instrumental for Tanzanian policymakers shifting focus from purely financial interventions toward behavioral barriers, offering regional health administrators a framework to design targeted, couple-centric, and culturally tailored health programmes. However, the contributions of this study must be considered alongside its limitations, including the cross-sectional design that restricts the ability to establish definitive causal relationships over time, and the reliance on self-reported data from a single rural district introduces potential social desirability bias and limits the immediate generalizability of the behavioral patterns of urban or vastly different socio-cultural regions across Tanzania.

Conclusion

The findings of this study uncovered that male partner accompaniment in maternal health service utilization remains profoundly low in this rural community, a behavioral deficit that is tightly mirrored by predominantly negative personal attitudes, oppressive subjective norms, and low perceived behavioral control. Guided by the Theory of Planned Behavior, this study demonstrates that male participation is not merely an individual choice but a complex intersection of sociocultural identity, structural access, and health literacy. Ethnicity stands out as a powerful independent predictor, with the Mambwe partners exhibiting far greater cognitive readiness and positive behavioral intentions toward maternal care than their Fipa counterparts. Furthermore, exposure to birth preparedness messaging and closer geographical proximity to health facilities significantly optimize positive spousal attitudes and self-efficacy. Based on these conclusions, the following recommendations are made:i.Implement culturally tailored SBCC campaigns: The Ministry of Health, in collaboration with local government authorities, should design targeted social and behavior change communication (SBCC) programs. Rather than using generic regional messages, these campaigns should engage Mambwe traditional elders and community champions to model and diffuse progressive norms of spousal collaboration to neighboring Fipa and other conservative ethnic groupsii.Institutionalize couple-centric ANC: Reproductive health facility management should transition away from female-exclusive care spaces. The ANC guidelines should be structurally updated to mandate joint, couple-friendly consultations where birth preparedness and complication readiness educational packages are delivered to both partners simultaneously, effectively converting passive financial providers into active physical participantsiii.Decentralize maternal health infrastructure: To mitigate the restrictive opportunity costs imposed by long walking distances, local health departments must prioritize the expansion of community dispensary-level maternal wings and optimize mobile reproductive health clinics. Reducing the physical distance barrier directly lowers the structural threshold for male accompanimentiv.Adapt clinical environments for delivery accompaniment: To support and reinforce the high perceived behavioral control found among those willing to witness childbirth, healthcare facilities should adapt labor ward infrastructures to offer private, role-specific, dignified delivery spaces that safely accommodate male presence.
