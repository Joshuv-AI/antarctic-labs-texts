Prevalence and associated factors of public stigma toward people living with HIV: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhihua Ye, Jin Peng, Jiang Yuan, Fengjun Li, Yuan Li, Yi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHIV-related public stigma remains a critical barrier to ending the AIDS epidemic, yet its global prevalence remains poorly quantified. Existing evidence is fragmented, with no comprehensive synthesis of temporal trends, regional patterns, or population subgroups. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the global prevalence of public stigma toward people living with HIV (PLHIV) in the general population and to identify factors associated with such stigma.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the DHS Program website, among others, from inception to June 30, 2025. We included observational studies reporting the prevalence of public stigma toward PLHIV in general populations aged 15 years or older. Two reviewers independently screened records, extracted data, and assessed study quality. The overall and subgroup prevalence estimates were pooled using a three-level mixed-effects meta-analytic model. For associated factors reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR), we applied random- or fixed-effect models based on heterogeneity.ResultsWe included 59 studies comprising 171 data points and 2438150 participants. The overall pooled prevalence of public stigma was 69.09% (95% CI 60.81–76.30). In subgroup analyses, prevalence declined over time, from 71.82% (1991–2010) to 49.15% (2020–2024), and varied substantially by region, ranging from 46.04% in Eastern and Southern Africa to 92.06% in the Middle East and North Africa. Prevalence was highest in lower‑middle‑income countries (75.98%) and lowest in high‑income countries (54.68%). In analyses of factors associated with stigma, higher education (AOR 0.43, 95% CI 0.25–0.73), greater wealth (AOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.32–0.69), HIV knowledge (AOR 0.55, 95% CI 0.36–0.86), HIV testing history (AOR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85–0.98), and personal proximity to PLHIV (AOR 0.68, 95% CI 0.55–0.85) were each associated with lower odds of stigma.ConclusionsPublic stigma toward PLHIV remains pervasive globally, affecting nearly seven in ten people and far exceeding the UNAIDS target of < 10%. Although modest declines in recent years offer cautious optimism, current progress is inadequate. Stigma reflects structural inequities and sociocultural norms, underscoring the need for sustained multilevel strategies beyond information campaigns. Without intensified efforts, the 2030 goal of ending AIDS will remain out of reach.RegistrationPROSPERO, CRD420251055912.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28723-0.Keywords: Public stigma, HIV, Prevalence, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

HIV-related stigma remains a critical barrier to ending AIDS as a public health threat. Remarkable progress has been achieved in the global HIV/AIDS response: widespread availability of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV from a fatal diagnosis into a manageable chronic condition [1], contributing to substantial declines in new infections and AIDS-related mortality [2]. By the end of 2024, global progress toward the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets had reached 87%, 89%, and 94%, respectively, bringing these goals closer than ever before [3]. Nevertheless, an estimated 9.2 million people living with HIV (PLHIV) remained without treatment in 2024 [3], and persistent HIV-related stigma remains a recognized barrier to closing this gap [4, 5]. Stigma discourages testing, hinders linkage to and retention in care, reduces treatment adherence, and undermines the health and well-being of PLHIV [6–8].

Addressing this barrier necessitates a clear understanding of stigma as a social process [9], which operates through two interrelated but distinct dimensions. Public stigma refers to the negative beliefs, prejudices, and discriminatory intentions held by society toward PLHIV [10–12]. Self-stigma, in contrast, occurs when PLHIV internalize these societal attitudes and represents a harmful consequence of public stigma [10–12]. Longitudinal evidence indicates that public stigma predicts increases in self-stigma over time [13] and that its negative effects on health-related quality of life are largely mediated through this internalization [14]. Thus, public stigma functions as an upstream driver of the overall stigma experience and its adverse outcomes, making it a critical target for intervention.

Despite decades of effort to improve public attitudes, stigma reduction interventions have achieved only limited success [4, 8], and public stigma remains pervasive. Recent UNAIDS data show that in 33 countries, a median of 53% of people aged 15–49 years hold discriminatory attitudes toward PLHIV, far exceeding the global target of less than 10% [3]. Large-scale studies, including one across 64 low- and middle-income countries, report prevalence ranging from 12.9% to 90.6%, highlighting substantial variation across nations [15]. Such studies, however, are often restricted to countries with major health surveys [16], leaving a gap in the comprehensive synthesis of the global distribution of public stigma over time, across regions, and among population subgroups.

Evidence on the factors associated with public stigma remains fragmented and inconsistent. Some studies attribute stigma primarily to gaps in HIV knowledge [15, 17, 18], whereas others demonstrate that stigma persists even among well-informed individuals, suggesting deeper sociocultural and structural influences [19–21]. This inconsistency reflects a theoretical divide between individual-level cognitive models and societal-level frameworks in explaining stigma [22, 23]. Existing systematic reviews have provided important insights but have largely concentrated on self-stigma among PLHIV [24–27]. A dedicated synthesis that quantifies the global prevalence of public stigma and clarifies its associated factors in the general population is currently lacking.

This evidence gap hampers the development of targeted, evidence-based interventions designed to reduce stigma at the community level. Accordingly, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to (1) estimate the global prevalence of public stigma toward PLHIV among the general population and (2) identify factors associated with such stigma.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [28]. The protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251055912).

Eligibility criteria

We structured the eligibility criteria according to the PECOS framework (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome, and Study design) [29].

Regarding the population, we considered studies that reported quantitative data on public stigma toward PLHIV in general population samples aged 15 years or older. We excluded studies that sampled healthcare workers, medical or health profession students, high‑risk groups (e.g., sex workers, people who inject drugs), or PLHIV themselves, as these populations do not reflect community‑level attitudes.

Regarding the exposure and comparison, eligible studies examined factors potentially associated with public stigma towards PLHIV. Since this is a prevalence‑focused review, no independent comparator group was required; instead, associations were examined through comparisons between subgroups within individual studies using multivariable logistic regression models.

Regarding the outcome, we focused on two types of outcomes. First, the prevalence of public stigma: included studies were required to report the prevalence of stigmatizing attitudes as a dichotomous outcome, defined as endorsement of any stigmatizing response across measurement items, consistent with the UNAIDS stigma measurement approach [30]. This uniform dichotomous operationalization provides a common metric that facilitates pooling across different instrument formats. Studies were also required to provide measures of uncertainty or sufficient data (sample size and number endorsing stigma) for its calculation. Eligible studies used validated stigma measurement tools or single standardized items with documented sources or reported psychometric properties, including reliability. Studies reporting stigma only as a continuous score without a defined cut‑off for dichotomization were excluded. Second, factors associated with stigma: studies were required to report adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% CIs from multivariable logistic regression analyses.

Regarding study design, we considered observational studies (cross‑sectional, cohort, or case‑control) and mixed‑methods studies that reported quantitative data. Studies conducted in closed or non‑generalizable settings (e.g., prisons, military bases) were excluded. Qualitative‑only studies, randomized trials, case reports, reviews, opinion pieces, and conference abstracts were not eligible. No restrictions were placed on publication date or language.

Information sources and search strategy

On June 30, 2025, we searched PubMed, Embase, The Cochrane Library, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, CNKI, SinoMed, WanFang Data, and VIP. Gray literature was identified through The DHS Program website, a key source of population‑based stigma indicators. The reference lists of the included articles were also examined. Search strategies combined terms related to HIV (“HIV”, “human immunodeficiency virus”, “AIDS”, “acquired immunodeficiency syndrome”), stigma (“social stigma”, “public stigma”, “misinformation”, “prejudice”, “discrimination”), and epidemiology (“prevalence”, “risk factors”, “associated factors”, “determinants”). Complete search strategies are provided in Appendix S1.

Study selection and data extraction

Records were managed in EndNote. Duplicates were removed first automatically and then verified manually. Two reviewers (Zhihua Ye and Jin Peng) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full‑text assessment. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or, when needed, by a third reviewer (Yi Yang). Potentially relevant studies not published in English or Chinese were translated using DeepL Translate. For studies with missing or unclear data, corresponding authors were contacted by email; if no reply was received within two weeks, a reminder was sent. When multiple publications used overlapping datasets, the version with the most comprehensive data was retained.

Data extraction was performed independently by the same two reviewers using a standardized Excel form. Extracted data included study characteristics (authors, publication year, investigation year, country, region, data source, study design, stigma measurement tool); counts for effect‑size calculation (total sample size, number endorsing stigma); and, where available, prevalence stratified by sample‑level characteristics such as gender, age group, marital status, residence, education level, and wealth quintile. For studies reporting multivariable logistic regression analyses of factors associated with stigma, we extracted the definition and effect measure (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] with 95% confidence interval [CI]) for each factor.

Risk of bias assessment

The same two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias for each included study. Risk of bias was assessed using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) checklist for cross‑sectional studies and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort and case–control studies (details and specific scoring criteria in Appendix S2) [31, 32]. The cut-off values for classifying studies as low, moderate, or high risk were adopted from previous studies applying these tools [33, 34]. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by the third reviewer.

Data synthesis and analysis

Because many studies contributed multiple prevalence estimates (data points), a three‑level mixed‑effects meta‑analytic model was used to account for clustering: level 1 represented random sampling variance, level 2 within‑study variance, and level 3 between‑study variance [35]. Prevalence estimates were logit‑transformed to stabilize variances, and pooled estimates were back‑transformed to the original scale for reporting. The global pooled prevalence of public stigma (primary outcome) and its 95% CI were derived using a random‑effects model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation. Heterogeneity was assessed with the Cochran Q test and quantified using the I2 statistic. Within the three‑level framework, total heterogeneity was further decomposed into within‑study (I2level 2) and between‑study (I2level 3) components based on variance estimates (σ2). Prediction interval (PI) was calculated for the primary estimate to account for the overall heterogeneity across studies. For analyses fitted with standard two‑level models, the overall I2 is reported. Statistical homogeneity for subsequent analyses was predefined as I2 < 50% with a non‑significant Q test (p > 0.10).

When significant heterogeneity was present, pre‑specified moderator analyses were conducted. Study‑level subgroups included investigation period (1991–2010, 2011–2015, 2016–2019, 2020–2024), chosen to align with key phases in the HIV/AIDS response: pre-treatment as prevention, the 90-90-90 targets era, the U = U consensus period, and the COVID-19 pandemic and post-pandemic period, respectively; country income level (low, lower‑middle, upper‑middle, high) according to World Bank classification; UNAIDS region; data source (large‑scale standardized surveys, study‑specific surveys); stigma measurement tool (UNAIDS scale, other validated scale, single standardized item); and risk of bias (low, moderate). The influence of study‑level moderators was formally tested using multivariable multilevel meta‑regression. Country‑specific pooled prevalence estimates were calculated for nations with available data and are presented in a map. Sample‑level subgroups (gender, age group, marital status, residence, education level, wealth quintile) were analyzed where data permitted.

For synthesizing factors associated with stigma, we included factors previously established in the literature and reported as AOR in at least three studies, a threshold consistent with prior methodological practice and adopted to ensure stable estimation of between-study heterogeneity. For quantitative pooling, we included only factors with comparable operational definitions across studies, or whose original categorisations could be aligned with our pre‑specified classification scheme. Factors with substantial definitional heterogeneity that could not be harmonised, as well as factors reported in fewer than three studies, were summarised narratively. Each synthesis employed a two‑level model. When statistical homogeneity was indicated, a fixed‑effect model (Mantel–Haenszel method) was applied; otherwise, a random‑effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) was used. The certainty of evidence for pooled associations was evaluated. The criteria for grading evidence credibility are detailed in Appendix S3.

Sensitivity analyses of the main prevalence estimate involved (1) excluding studies identified as outliers (standardized residuals > 3); (2) excluding studies with extreme sample sizes (below the 5th or above the 95th percentile); and (3) excluding studies that used single‑item measures. Publication bias was assessed for the primary meta‑analysis via funnel plots, Egger’s regression test, and the trim‑and‑fill method as a sensitivity analysis. All analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 with the ‘metan’ command and R version 4.3.0 with the ‘meta’ and ‘metafor’ packages. Maps were generated using the 'rnaturalearth' package in R.

Results

Study selection

Our search yielded 1158 records from PubMed, 13498 from Web of Science, 4642 from Embase, 1508 from ScienceDirect, 111 from The Cochrane Library, 518 from CNKI, 1016 from WanFang Data, 700 from VIP, 1592 from SinoMed, and 86 from The DHS Program website. During duplicate removal and screening, we contacted corresponding authors for four studies with missing or unclear data; one responded, while the remaining three did not. Ultimately, 59 studies were included in the systematic review and meta‑analysis (Fig. 1). A full list of included studies is provided in Appendix S4.

Flow diagram of the study selection process

Study characteristics

The 59 studies contributed 171 data points, encompassing 2438150 participants, of whom 1394641 endorsed stigmatizing attitudes. The included sample sizes ranged from 254 to 1172841, with a median of 11814 participants. They included 30 journal articles and 29 Gray literature reports, with data from 100 countries. Of the included studies, 58 were cross‑sectional, and one was a cohort study. Geographically, they covered all UNAIDS regions: 22 from Eastern and Southern Africa, 18 from Asia and the Pacific, 11 from Western and Central Africa, eight from Western and Central Europe and North America, seven from Eastern Europe and Central Asia, four each from Latin America and the Middle East and North Africa, and three from the Caribbean; two additional studies provided global multi‑country estimates. The data sources comprised 44 large‑scale standardized surveys and 15 study‑specific surveys, with investigation years ranging from 1991 to 2024. The stigma measurement tool was the UNAIDS scale in 46 studies, another validated scale in 9 studies, and a single standardized item in 4 studies. Consistent with the UNAIDS approach, stigma was predominantly defined as endorsing any stigmatizing response across items in multi‑item scales. In studies using a single item, stigma was defined as agreement with a stigmatizing statement or a stated unwillingness to live near a PLHIV. Detailed study characteristics are summarized in Appendix S5.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality assessment rated 34 studies as having a low risk of bias and 25 as having a moderate risk, with detailed results presented in Appendix S6. These moderate-risk ratings were primarily due to insufficient details on quality assurance, handling of missing data, and participant response rates.

Prevalence estimates and sensitivity analysis

Based on 171 data points from 59 studies, the global pooled prevalence of public stigma toward PLHIV was 69.09% (95% CI 60.81–76.30) (Table 1). Significant heterogeneity was observed (Cochran Q = 409,335.34, p < 0.001), with variance decomposition indicating that 44.46% of total variance arose from within‑study differences (I2level 2) and 55.40% from between‑study differences (I2level 3). Reflecting this heterogeneity, the corresponding 95% PI ranged from 10.55% to 97.69%, indicating that the prevalence in a given setting could deviate substantially from this global average. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of this estimate after excluding outliers (68.04%, 95% CI 60.79–74.52), studies with extreme sample sizes (69.20%, 95% CI 60.02–77.07), or studies using single‑item measures (72.53%, 95% CI 65.00–78.97) (Table 1). Visual inspection of the funnel plot and Egger’s regression test (z = 1.41, p = 0.161) did not suggest substantial publication bias (funnel plot in Appendix S7). This was corroborated by a trim-and-fill analysis, which estimated zero missing studies, with adjusted estimates virtually identical to the original findings (funnel plots before and after adjustment in Appendix S8). Country‑specific pooled estimates for 100 nations showed considerable variation (Fig. 2), ranging from 98.77% (95% CI 98.16–99.17) in Egypt to 5.50% (95% CI 4.25–7.10) in Uruguay. In addition to Uruguay, the estimates for Argentina, the Netherlands, Brazil, and Sweden were also below 10% (complete estimates in Appendix S9).

Global prevalence of public stigma toward people living with HIV in 100 countries

Subgroup and meta‑regression analyses

To explore potential sources of the significant heterogeneity, we examined the associations of prespecified moderators at the study and sample levels. At the study level, prevalence varied significantly across country income level, region, and stigma measurement tool, with a borderline significant variation across investigation period (Table 1). Prevalence varied by investigation period (QM = 7.54, p = 0.056): 71.82% (95% CI 61.06–80.54) for 1991–2010, 75.28% (95% CI 59.99–86.08) for 2011–2015, 52.42% (95% CI 35.70–68.62) for 2016–2019, and 49.15% (95% CI 36.75–61.65) for 2020–2024. This declining temporal trend was also observed in multivariable meta‑regression, which showed significantly lower odds of stigma in 2016–2019 (AOR 0.19, 95% CI 0.10–0.39, p < 0.001) and 2020–2024 (AOR 0.23, 95% CI 0.11–0.46, p < 0.001) than in 1991–2010. Prevalence also differed by country income level (QM = 17.05, p < 0.001), with the highest estimate in lower‑middle‑income countries (75.98%, 95% CI 60.61–86.67) and the lowest in high‑income countries (54.68%, 95% CI 24.48–81.79). Meta‑regression indicated significantly lower odds in high‑income versus low‑income countries (AOR 0.36, 95% CI 0.17–0.77, p = 0.009). Significant regional differences were observed (QM = 98.82, p < 0.001), ranging from 46.04% (95% CI 33.68–58.90) in Eastern and Southern Africa to 92.06% (95% CI 58.01–98.98) in the Middle East and North Africa. Meta‑regression showed that all regions except Latin America had significantly higher odds of stigma than Eastern and Southern Africa, with the strongest association for the Middle East and North Africa (AOR 10.62, 95% CI 5.70–19.81, p < 0.001). No significant differences were found by data source (QM = 1.57, p = 0.210) or risk of bias (QM = 2.35, p = 0.125). However, prevalence varied by measurement tool (QM = 11.21, p = 0.004): 73.93% (95% CI 65.38–80.99) for the UNAIDS scale, 64.51% (95% CI 52.88–74.65) for other validated scales, and 33.61% (95% CI 25.29–43.10) for single items. Accordingly, meta‑regression indicated that studies using single items had significantly lower odds of stigma than those using the UNAIDS scale (AOR 0.09, 95% CI 0.04–0.24, p < 0.001). In study-level moderator analyses, residual heterogeneity remained substantial after accounting for these factors, with I2level 2 ranging from 36.10% to 55.31% and I2level 3 from 44.51% to 63.74%. Full results of the multivariable meta‑regression are provided in Appendix S10.

To further explore the heterogeneity at the individual level, we examined sample‑level sociodemographic strata (Table 2). Women showed a higher prevalence than men (73.98% [95% CI 64.30–81.77] vs 69.09% [95% CI 58.91–77.70]; QM = 6.31, p = 0.012). The prevalence was highest among adolescents and young adults aged 15–24 years (77.66%, 95% CI 66.67–85.81), followed by those aged 25–49 years (74.39%, 95% CI 63.37–82.98) and those aged 50 years or older (70.79%, 95% CI 55.86–82.27; QM = 7.98, p = 0.019). No significant difference emerged across marital status categories (QM = 3.42, p = 0.181). Individuals in rural areas reported a higher prevalence than those in urban areas (77.82% [95% CI 67.44–85.59] vs 71.13% [95% CI 59.77–80.34]; QM = 24.17, p < 0.001). A strong inverse gradient was observed with education level (QM = 102.48, p < 0.001): prevalence decreased from 84.39% (95% CI 74.47–90.92) among those with no formal education to 55.02% (95% CI 40.50–68.72) among those with more than secondary education. Similarly, a clear inverse wealth gradient was found (QM = 169.36, p < 0.001), with prevalence declining from 86.12% (95% CI 77.77–91.67) in the lowest wealth quintile to 71.29% (95% CI 57.62–81.93) in the highest quintile. In sample-level analyses, within-study heterogeneity (I2level 2) dominated, ranging from 90.45% to 97.49%, while between-study heterogeneity (I2level 3) was relatively low (1.96% to 8.94%).

Factors associated with public stigma

The synthesis of factors from multivariable models indicated associations for several factors (Table 3). Most sociodemographic factors were not consistently associated with stigma: gender (women vs men: AOR 1.01, 95% CI 0.90–1.13) and age (25–49 vs 15–24 years: AOR 0.87, 95% CI 0.50–1.50) showed no significant association. Regarding marital status, being married or cohabiting was associated with slightly higher odds of stigma compared to being never married (AOR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02–1.22), whereas no significant association was found for divorced, separated, or widowed status (AOR 1.06, 95% CI 0.92–1.21). Rural residence was also not significantly associated (AOR 1.12, 95% CI 0.72–1.76). Higher socioeconomic status was associated with lower odds of stigma. Compared to having no education, the AOR was 0.64 (95% CI 0.42–0.98) for secondary education and 0.43 (95% CI 0.25–0.73) for more than secondary education. Similarly, being in the highest wealth quintile was associated with lower odds relative to being in the lowest quintile (AOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.32–0.69). Greater HIV-related knowledge, service utilization, and personal contact were each inversely associated with stigma: awareness of HIV (vs unaware: AOR 0.55, 95% CI 0.36–0.86), having a history of HIV testing (vs untested: AOR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85–0.98; a weak but significant association), and personal proximity to PLHIV (yes vs no: AOR 0.68, 95% CI 0.55–0.85). The certainty of evidence for these pooled associations was graded as class III (suggestive) for all significant factors. For these meta-analyses, the Cochran Q test indicated significant heterogeneity in most pooled analyses, with I2 estimates ranging from 0.00% to 97.03%, and most exceeding 50%, for which random-effects models were applied. Beyond the factors quantitatively synthesized, other examined variables demonstrated inconsistent or context-specific relationships with public stigma. Concerning socioeconomic indicators beyond wealth and education, food insufficiency was associated with higher stigma (AOR 1.29, 95% CI 1.06–1.59) [36], while occupational status showed no clear association [18, 37]. For religious factors, denominational affiliations yielded mixed results across studies [37–40]; neither general religiosity (AOR = 0.98, 95% CI = 0.78–1.22) [41] nor frequency of church attendance (AOR 1.07, 95% CI 0.86–1.33) was significantly associated with stigma [38]. However, endorsing specific beliefs, such as that HIV is a divine punishment (AOR 1.46, 95% CI 1.11–1.95) or that PLHIV violated God’s word (AOR 1.92, 95% CI 1.41–2.66), was consistently associated with higher stigma [38]. Findings on media exposure were heterogeneous: frequent newspaper reading was correlated with substantially lower stigma in one study (almost daily vs none, AOR 0.07, 95% CI 0.01–0.53) [37], whereas other forms of media use generally showed nonsignificant associations, as did a composite media-use measure in another study (AOR 1.01, 95% CI 0.38–1.96) [18]. Regarding health services, the perceived local availability of HIV treatment was strongly associated with lower stigma (AOR 0.42, 95% CI 0.24–0.74) [42], whereas the actual use of HIV-related health services was not (AOR 0.85, 95% CI 0.60–1.21) [43]. Relevant sociocultural attitudes included acceptance of gender-based violence (AOR 1.49, 95% CI 1.02–2.17) [37] and holding gender-discriminatory attitudes (AOR 1.11, 95% CI 0.85–1.44) [42], which were associated with higher stigma. Additionally, the experience of intimate partner violence was associated with higher stigma in one study (AOR = 1.40, 95% CI = 1.08–1.82) [36].

Discussion

Our central finding is that almost seven in ten people worldwide hold stigmatizing attitudes toward PLHIV, with a pooled prevalence of 69.09% (95% CI 60.81–76.30). This estimate starkly exceeds the UNAIDS target of less than 10% [3], underscoring the profound and persistent nature of HIV-related stigma as a critical barrier to ending the AIDS epidemic. Of note, the global estimate should not be interpreted as a uniform prevalence across all settings. The substantial heterogeneity observed, with nearly equal contributions from within-study and between-study variance, reflects both methodological diversity and true contextual differences across settings. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the estimate, and no substantial publication bias was detected. Country-specific estimates ranged from 5.50% in Uruguay to 98.77% in Egypt, revealing striking global variation. This striking variation, together with the wide prediction interval, reinforces that the global estimate is a summary measure of central tendency, and the pre‑specified subgroup analyses provide more context‑specific benchmarks for interpretation. These findings affirm that public stigma remains a normative social attitude in much of the world, despite advances in treatment and prevention. Stigma reduction must therefore be prioritized as a central, monitored component of the global HIV/AIDS response, with strategies tailored to local epidemiological and sociocultural realities [4].

A central and encouraging finding is the temporal decline in public stigma suggested by period-stratified estimates. After adjustment for other study-level characteristics, the odds of stigma were significantly lower in 2016–2019 and 2020–2024 than in 1991–2010. The period 2011–2015, which saw landmark developments such as the HPTN 052 trial results proving treatment as prevention [44] and the launch of the UNAIDS 90-90-90 targets [45], did not show a similar reduction in adjusted analyses. This lag suggests a delay between scientific discovery, policy translation, and measurable shifts in public attitudes. The more pronounced decline post-2016 coincides temporally with the genesis and global dissemination of the U = U scientific consensus, a message that is conceptually linked to challenging fear-based narratives about casual transmission through its decoupling of HIV infection from infectiousness [46, 47]. The continued decline into 2020–2024, despite the profound disruptions of the COVID-19 pandemic [48, 49], suggests resilience in this attitude change. However, even among studies conducted during 2020–2024, the pooled prevalence remained near 50%, which is still unacceptably far from global targets, and progress is unevenly distributed across regions and social groups.

The geographical and economic patterning of stigma reveals consistent and instructive disparities. Prevalence was highest in lower-middle-income countries and in the Middle East and North Africa region, whereas Eastern and Southern Africa, the region with the highest HIV burden, reported comparatively lower prevalence. This pattern aligns with previous cross-national studies documenting a negative ecological association between HIV prevalence and public stigma [50–52]. In settings where HIV is more common, greater population-level exposure to HIV information, more substantial investment in anti-stigma policies and programmes, and a higher likelihood of personal contact with PLHIV may all be linked to less stigmatizing attitudes [51–53]. The lower odds of stigma observed in high-income countries are consistent with expectations given their greater resources for public health infrastructure, universal education, and legal protections against discrimination compared to low- and middle-income countries [54–57]. Conversely, the elevated stigma in lower-middle-income countries compared with low-income countries warrants further exploration; it may reflect a specific stage in the epidemic response where awareness has increased but comprehensive normalization and health system strengthening are not yet fully established, a context in which fear and misinformation may remain salient. These findings illustrate a mutually reinforcing relationship between regional disparities and economic inequalities, underscoring that public stigma is associated not only with disease burden but also with broader structural, policy, and informational environments [58]. Our analysis further showed that both regional location and national income level were significantly associated with this heterogeneity, underscoring that stigma relates not only to local HIV epidemiology but also to broader structural context.

At the individual level, the contribution of within‑study variance to total heterogeneity far exceeded that of between‑study variance, indicating that the strength of sociodemographic associations varies considerably across local population contexts rather than reflecting systematic differences across study designs. Public stigma prevalence was higher among women, younger age groups, and rural residents and showed steep inverse gradients by education and wealth. After multivariable adjustment, however, gender, age, and rural residence were no longer independently associated with stigma. In contrast, the associations of higher education and greater wealth with lower odds of stigma remained robust, while being married or cohabiting was associated with modestly higher stigma odds. These patterns indicate that the sociodemographic disparities observed in crude analyses are largely correlated with structural determinants, particularly educational attainment and economic status. Education is consistently associated with lower stigma, possibly through enhanced cognitive skills, greater capacity to challenge misconceptions, and more egalitarian social values [4, 59]; wealth shows a similar pattern, potentially via better access to accurate information, diverse social networks, and health-related resources [58, 60]. The modest association with marriage may reflect unmeasured contextual factors such as a heightened sense of familial protection or stronger adherence to traditional social norms and merits further investigation.

Beyond socioeconomic factors, HIV-related knowledge, a history of HIV testing, and personal proximity to PLHIV were each independently associated with lower odds of stigma. Accurate knowledge is linked to fewer misconceptions about transmission risks and a lower overestimation of threats, which are often accompanied by less avoidant attitudes; it is also associated with viewing HIV as a more comprehensible and manageable condition [59, 61]. Personal contact is associated with greater normalization of the condition, less endorsement of stereotyped beliefs, and reduced social distance, often in the context of positive intergroup encounters [4, 53]. HIV testing, although the association was modest, may indicate prior health service engagement or a sense of personal relevance that is linked to viewing HIV as a more normal condition [62, 63]. Narrative synthesis further revealed two additional patterns. First, perceived local availability of HIV treatment was also linked to lower stigma, which is consistent with the notion that visible and accessible health systems may convey that HIV is a treatable and ordinary condition [64, 65]. Second, specific punitive religious beliefs, such as viewing HIV as divine punishment and acceptance of gender-based violence, were strongly associated with higher stigma, whereas general religiosity showed inconsistent associations, highlighting that stigma corresponds more closely with specific moral narratives than with faith itself [4]. Together, these findings clarify that stigma is not merely linked to inadequate information but is also deeply embedded in structural inequities and sociocultural schemas [58, 66]. The substantial heterogeneity observed in most pooled meta-analyses underscores that these pooled associations should be interpreted as general tendencies rather than precise constants applicable across all settings.

A critical methodological finding is the substantial variation in prevalence estimates associated with the measurement instrument, which our analysis identified as an additional significant source of heterogeneity. Studies using the UNAIDS-recommended scale reported a significantly higher prevalence than those using a single standardized item, a difference that remained significant in multivariable meta-regression. This discrepancy is inherent in the measurement approaches. Multi-item instruments capture a broader range of stigmatizing beliefs and offer greater sensitivity, aligning with UNAIDS surveillance guidance, but like all survey measures, they are subject to social desirability bias and assess hypothetical attitudes rather than observed discrimination [30]. Single-item measures are feasible in space-constrained surveys but may underestimate true prevalence, possibly because they capture only a narrow attitudinal domain. This measurement‑related variation should be borne in mind when comparing prevalence estimates across studies or over time, because apparent differences may partly reflect instrument choice rather than true changes in attitudes. Importantly, sensitivity analyses excluding single-item studies confirmed the robustness of the global pooled estimate, indicating that this methodological variation does not undermine the overall findings.

Strengths

This review has several methodological strengths. To our knowledge, it provides the most comprehensive quantitative synthesis of global HIV-related public stigma to date, integrating 59 studies from 100 countries with more than 2.4 million participants across three decades. The inclusion of gray literature from the DHS Program website, alongside peer-reviewed journal articles, enhances representativeness beyond published literature alone. Methodologically, the use of a three-level mixed-effects meta-analytic model appropriately accounted for the clustering of multiple data points within studies. We systematically explored potential sources of heterogeneity through prespecified subgroup analyses and multivariable meta-regression, examining temporal, geographic, economic, and measurement-related moderators. To assess the robustness of our primary estimate, we conducted three complementary sensitivity analyses: excluding studies identified as statistical outliers, excluding studies with extreme sample sizes, and excluding studies that relied on single-item measures. The consistency of the pooled prevalence across these sensitivity analyses supports the stability of the global estimate. The calculation of prediction intervals, together with country-specific estimates for 100 nations, provides contextual benchmarks that account for the wide variation across settings.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, limitations in the body of evidence should be acknowledged. The predominance of cross-sectional designs strictly precludes causal inference; all identified associations are inherently correlational and remain susceptible to reverse causality and residual confounding. Thus, these findings should be viewed as identifying correlates, not causes, of public stigma. Data coverage was uneven across regions, particularly in Eastern Europe, Central Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa, which may affect the precision and generalisability of regional estimates, and estimates from regions with few studies deserve particular scrutiny. By design, we excluded healthcare workers, key populations, and PLHIV to isolate community-level attitudes; consequently, this review does not address stigma dynamics within these groups. Furthermore, the synthesis of associated factors was constrained by heterogeneity in variable definitions across primary studies; quantitative pooling was feasible only for factors defined comparably in at least three studies, whereas many potentially relevant determinants could only be narratively summarized. In addition, these pooled estimates were graded as suggestive rather than conclusive, and thus warrant cautious interpretation when considering policy or practice implications. Second, limitations in the review processes also warrant consideration. Despite contacting corresponding authors for missing data, the response rate was low (one of four, 25%), potentially resulting in missing information that could have informed more nuanced subgroup analyses. While Egger's test did not indicate significant publication bias for the primary analysis, such bias cannot be definitively ruled out, particularly for smaller studies or findings from regions with less established research infrastructure. Finally, despite systematic exploration through study-level and sample-level moderators, substantial residual heterogeneity remained, implying that unmeasured cultural, political, or historical factors are also associated with stigma and may limit the precision of the global estimate as a universal benchmark. Despite these limitations, the review provides a robust benchmark for monitoring broad temporal trends and highlighting persistent disparities that warrant targeted policy attention.

Recommendations

Based on these findings, we propose the following recommendations for policy, practice, and research. For policymakers, the persistently high prevalence of public stigma signals that current efforts remain insufficient. Stigma reduction must become a core, resourced, and monitored pillar of the global HIV/AIDS response. National monitoring systems should adopt validated multi-item instruments, such as the UNAIDS-recommended scale, within large-scale surveys to enable accurate tracking. The stark regional and socioeconomic disparities we documented mandate targeted investment, particularly in lower-middle-income countries and communities with low educational attainment. However, our findings caution against one-size-fits-all approaches; effective tailoring requires formative research and community engagement.

For practitioners and programme implementers, our synthesis offers actionable insights. The consistent association between personal proximity to PLHIV and lower stigma supports interventions that safely amplify the visibility of PLHIV, including community-based testimonial programmes. The U = U message is a potent evidence-informed tool, and its consistent dissemination should be prioritized. Educational interventions must evolve beyond biomedical facts to confront the moral narratives surrounding HIV, potentially through participatory methods that engage social norms. Partnerships with faith-based organisations could prove valuable. The associations of HIV testing history and perceived treatment availability with lower stigma suggest that integrating HIV services into primary healthcare platforms could normalize HIV as a manageable chronic condition. The linkage between stigma and acceptance of gender-based violence further suggests that HIV stigma reduction efforts may achieve greater impact when integrated with broader programmes addressing gender inequality.

This review also identifies priorities for future research. First, studies should examine intersectional stigma, exploring how HIV stigma overlaps with stigma related to gender, sexuality, and poverty to inform more holistic interventions. Second, implementation research is needed to compare the effectiveness of different strategies across varied settings, including contact-based versus education-based interventions. Third, research efforts must extend to countries not covered by large-scale national health surveys, particularly in underrepresented regions such as Eastern Europe, Central Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa. Fourth, qualitative research is essential to understand how macro-level factors translate into individual attitudes in specific cultural contexts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis confirms that public stigma toward PLHIV remains a pervasive global phenomenon, affecting nearly seven of every ten people and far exceeding internationally agreed targets. While a modest decline in stigma prevalence in the most recent period offers a cautiously optimistic signal, the current pace of change is inadequate. Stigma is not merely a reflection of ignorance and misunderstanding; it is a manifestation of structural inequity and sociocultural norms. Reducing it will require moving beyond brief information-based campaigns to implement sustained, multilevel strategies. Without such a response, the goal of ending AIDS as a public health threat by 2030 will remain out of reach.
