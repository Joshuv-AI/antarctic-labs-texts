Temporal Changes in Hospital-Captured 90-Day Care After Venous Thromboembolism: A 16-Year Electronic Health Record Cohort Study
Authors: Yonghui Wang, Yan Wang, Xuemin Zhang, Wei Li, Weihao Li, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDirect oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have simplified treatment for venous thromboembolism (VTE), but fewer mandatory monitoring visits may reduce opportunities for structured reassessment. We evaluated temporal changes in hospital-captured documentation of post-VTE care processes during the transition to DOAC-based treatment, including initial anticoagulation, clinical follow-up, laboratory monitoring, and selective imaging reassessment.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective electronic health record cohort study using routinely collected vascular surgery outpatient and inpatient records from Peking University People’s Hospital between 2009 and 2024. Patients with VTE-related evidence and a definable hospital-captured index date were included. Treatment eras were prespecified as 2009–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2024. The study-defined documentation measure comprised therapeutic anticoagulation within 14 days and clinical follow-up, laboratory monitoring, and selective VTE-related ultrasound or imaging reassessment within 15–90 days.ResultsAmong 5998 patients with a definable hospital-captured index VTE-related event, the median age was 64.0 years, and 55.3% were female. Documented anticoagulation within 14 days increased from 34.1% in 2009–2014 to 58.0% in 2020–2024. Within 15–90 days, clinical follow-up was documented in 1483 patients (24.7%), laboratory monitoring in 366 (6.1%), and VTE-related ultrasound or imaging reassessment in 578 (9.6%). All four prespecified domains were documented in 149 patients (2.5%) overall and in 3.2% during 2020–2024. In multivariable analysis, the 2020–2024 era, pulmonary embolism presentation, and upper-extremity or catheter-related VTE were associated with higher odds of four-domain documentation completion.ConclusionIn this 16-year electronic health record cohort, hospital-captured documentation of early anticoagulation increased, whereas documentation of 90-day follow-up processes remained infrequent; incomplete documentation or care delivered outside the hospital system may have contributed. These findings highlight opportunities to strengthen continuity and traceability of post-VTE management through timely clinical review and individualized decisions regarding treatment duration, safety monitoring, and selective imaging when clinically indicated.Keywords: venous thromboembolism, direct oral anticoagulants, anticoagulation management, follow-up care, vascular disease management, real-world evidence

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a common vascular condition associated with both acute complications and long-term sequelae. In addition to preventing early thrombus propagation and embolization, VTE management requires reassessment of recurrence risk, bleeding risk, treatment duration, persistent symptoms, post-thrombotic syndrome, and chronic thromboembolic complications.1 Cardiovascular diseases are a leading cause of sudden adult death, underscoring the importance of comprehensive vascular management and longitudinal follow-up.2 For this reason, the quality of VTE care cannot be evaluated solely by whether anticoagulation is initiated. Follow-up, safety monitoring, and reassessment after the index event are also important parts of care.

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have changed routine VTE treatment by providing fixed-dose oral therapy without the need for regular international normalized ratio (INR) monitoring.3,4 Current guidelines recommend DOACs as preferred treatment options for many patients with DVT or PE and recommend that treatment duration be individualized based on each patient’s risk factors for VTE recurrence and bleeding.5,6 However, easier anticoagulant use does not necessarily ensure reliable follow-up. In the warfarin era, monitoring requirements often created repeated clinical contact. In the DOAC era, fewer mandatory monitoring visits may improve convenience but may also reduce opportunities to confirm adherence, review renal or hepatic function, reassess bleeding risk, and determine the intended duration of treatment. This issue is increasingly relevant as follow-up after PE, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, post-VTE symptoms, and patient-centered outcomes receive greater attention.7,8

Although DOAC prescribing trends are well described, whether simplified treatment pathways have altered the documentation and continuity of post-VTE care remains unclear. This question is particularly relevant in outpatient and inpatient vascular surgery settings, where patients with VTE may enter care through heterogeneous pathways and follow-up may depend on local documentation systems. Using vascular surgery electronic health records from 2009 to 2024, our primary objective was to evaluate temporal changes in hospital-captured documentation of 90-day post-VTE care. Secondary objectives were to describe trends in anticoagulation strategies and individual documentation domains and to identify factors associated with four-domain documentation completion.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This single-center retrospective electronic health record cohort study used routinely collected vascular surgery outpatient and inpatient records from Peking University People’s Hospital between January 1, 2009, and December 31, 2024. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE and RECORD statements.9,10 The patient was the unit of analysis, and only the first definable VTE-related index event during the study period was included for each patient.

Deidentified structured and semi-structured data were extracted from the hospital information system, including available vascular surgery outpatient and inpatient encounters, demographics, diagnoses, medication orders, laboratory tests, ultrasound and imaging reports, procedures or interventions, and relevant clinical text. All extracted laboratory records were linkable at the patient level using anonymized identifiers and, where applicable, encounter-sequence information. The extract included records generated within the hospital system; completeness relative to the underlying source systems could not be independently quantified, and visits, prescriptions, laboratory tests, or imaging performed outside the hospital were not systematically captured. Accordingly, absence of a record was interpreted as absence of documentation in the available database rather than definitive absence of care.

Study Population and VTE Ascertainment

Patients were eligible if they had at least one VTE-related record in the vascular surgery database and a definable index date. Candidate records were identified from diagnosis records, ultrasound or imaging reports, procedure or intervention records, and relevant clinical text. Anticoagulant prescriptions alone were not used to define VTE eligibility.

VTE ascertainment used a hierarchical record-based approach. Objective VTE evidence was defined as VTE documentation in ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), or procedure/intervention records. Diagnosis-based or text-based evidence was accepted when the record clearly indicated DVT, PE, other vein thrombosis, catheter-related thrombosis, or another clinically relevant VTE presentation. Records suggesting only previous, chronic, old, organized, post-thrombotic, or follow-up VTE were not considered new index events unless accompanied by wording suggesting new diagnosis, progression, recurrence, or treatment escalation. Detailed search terms and classification rules are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Patients were excluded if no confirmable VTE-related evidence was present or if an index date could not be defined. Because objective diagnostic reports were not uniformly available in the extracted database across the study period, patients with database-captured objective VTE evidence were analyzed as a prespecified sensitivity subgroup to assess the robustness of the findings to potential diagnosis- or text-based misclassification. Formal inter-review agreement and independent sample validation were not performed.

Index Date, Treatment Era, and Initial Anticoagulation

The index date was defined as the earliest date of a qualifying VTE-related record during the study period. The index date represented the first hospital-captured VTE-related event and may not have reflected the true date of symptom onset or diagnosis outside the hospital system.

Treatment eras were prespecified before outcome analysis as 2009–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2024. The 2015 cut point corresponded to the May 2015 approval of rivaroxaban for DVT treatment in China, whereas the 2020 cut point represented contemporary practice following publication of the 2018 Chinese pulmonary thromboembolism guideline and the 2019 ESC pulmonary embolism guideline.7,11 The boundaries were not selected according to observed outcome differences.

Initial anticoagulation strategy was classified using anticoagulant records captured within 14 days after the index date. Patients were classified hierarchically as DOAC-based treatment, warfarin-based treatment, parenteral anticoagulation only, or no anticoagulant record identified. DOAC-based and warfarin-based treatment included use of the oral agent alone or parenteral anticoagulation followed by the oral agent within the 14-day window. Parenteral anticoagulation only was assigned when low-molecular-weight heparin, unfractionated heparin, or fondaparinux was identified without a DOAC or warfarin record. This classification reflected hospital-captured medication documentation and did not determine whether anticoagulation had been prescribed or taken outside the hospital system.

Hospital-Captured 90-Day VTE Documentation Measure

A study-defined hospital-captured 90-day VTE documentation measure was constructed as a process-of-care measure rather than a validated clinical outcome. Its four domains were selected based on contemporary VTE treatment and follow-up guidance. Anticoagulation represented treatment initiation;5,6 clinical follow-up represented reassessment of symptoms, treatment adherence, bleeding risk, and treatment duration;5–7,12,13 laboratory testing represented safety assessment when clinically appropriate;4,14 and imaging represented selective reassessment for persistent or recurrent symptoms, suspected thrombus progression or recurrence, or other clinical indications.7,13 Imaging reassessment was not considered routinely required for every patient.

Four components were assessed: documented therapeutic anticoagulation within 14 days after the index date; clinical follow-up within 15 to 90 days; laboratory monitoring within 15 to 90 days; and VTE-related ultrasound or imaging reassessment within 15 to 90 days. Clinical follow-up was defined as any vascular surgery outpatient visit, inpatient encounter, or VTE-related clinical record after the index period. Laboratory monitoring was defined as documentation of at least one potentially safety- or VTE-relevant laboratory test, including complete blood count, liver or renal function, coagulation testing, or D-dimer. VTE-related reassessment was defined as ultrasound or imaging related to DVT, PE, thrombus progression, thrombus resolution, recurrence, or post-treatment reassessment.

Each domain contributed one point to a study-defined, unweighted 0-to-4 documentation score. Equal weighting was used for descriptive transparency in the absence of a validated weighting scheme and did not imply equal clinical importance. Documentation of all four prespecified domains was selected as the primary process outcome to summarize within-system documentation, while each domain was also analyzed separately. The score was not a validated clinical outcome or a universal standard for every patient. Because laboratory testing and imaging reassessment were clinically selective, absence of a laboratory record, including a complete blood count, was not considered equivalent to absence of documented clinical follow-up.

Covariates and Outcomes

VTE phenotypes were derived from diagnosis records, imaging and ultrasound reports, procedure or intervention records, and relevant clinical text. Phenotypes included lower-extremity DVT, proximal lower-extremity or iliocaval DVT, distal lower-extremity DVT, upper-extremity DVT, catheter-related thrombosis, DVT with PE, and PE only. Proximal lower-extremity or iliocaval DVT was defined as thrombosis involving the popliteal, femoral, common femoral, or iliac veins or the inferior vena cava; distal lower-extremity DVT was defined as thrombosis confined to the infrapopliteal deep veins, including the anterior tibial, posterior tibial, peroneal, gastrocnemius, or soleal veins. These categories were assigned only when the venous level could be identified, and phenotype categories were not necessarily mutually exclusive.

Prespecified covariates included age, sex, index encounter setting, treatment era, PE presentation, and upper-extremity or catheter-related VTE. Additional database-derived covariates included cancer-related state, prior VTE, and surgery- or trauma-related state. Detailed definitions are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table 1.

The primary outcome was documentation of all four prespecified domains of the hospital-captured 90-day VTE documentation measure. Secondary outcomes included documentation of each individual domain, distribution of the unweighted 0-to-4 documentation score, treatment-era differences in the individual documentation domains, and temporal trends in initial anticoagulation strategies.

Sensitivity Analyses

Three prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed. First, analyses were restricted to patients with database-captured objective VTE evidence. Second, patients whose index records suggested previous, chronic, old, organized, post-thrombotic, or follow-up VTE were excluded unless records also suggested new diagnosis, progression, recurrence, or treatment escalation. Third, a three-domain documentation measure was evaluated by excluding the anticoagulation domain and retaining clinical follow-up, laboratory monitoring, and VTE-related ultrasound or imaging reassessment within 15 to 90 days.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median with interquartile range or mean ± standard deviation, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics, initial anticoagulation strategies, and 90-day documentation domains were described overall and by treatment era.

Treatment-era differences were evaluated using regression models with era modeled as an ordinal variable. Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes and linear regression for continuous outcomes. Annual trends in initial anticoagulation strategies were evaluated with calendar year modeled as a continuous variable.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with four-domain documentation completion. The primary model was prespecified and intentionally parsimonious because the number of outcome events was limited. Covariates included treatment era, age per 10-year increase, sex, index encounter setting, PE presentation, and upper-extremity or catheter-related VTE. An extended model additionally included database-derived cancer-related state, prior VTE, and surgery- or trauma-related state. Initial anticoagulation strategy was not included because documentation of anticoagulation was one domain of the outcome.

Analyses used complete covariate data. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All tests were two-sided, and P <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2.

Results

Cohort Derivation and Baseline Characteristics

Between 2009 and 2024, 5999 candidate patients were retrieved from vascular surgery outpatient and inpatient records. After exclusion of one patient without confirmable VTE-related evidence, 5998 patients with a definable index VTE-related event were included in the primary analysis. Among them, 717 patients (12.0%) had database-captured objective VTE evidence. After excluding patients whose index records contained wording suggestive of prior, chronic, or follow-up VTE without evidence of a new or progressive event, 5702 patients remained in the corresponding sensitivity analysis (Figure 1).

The three-domain documentation measure excluded the anticoagulation domain.

Temporal Changes in Hospital-Captured Anticoagulation Documentation

Hospital-captured initial anticoagulation strategies changed over the study period. Documented anticoagulation within 14 days increased from 474 of 1391 patients (34.1%) in 2009–2014 to 435 of 880 (49.4%) in 2015–2019 and 2160 of 3727 (58.0%) in 2020–2024. DOAC-based treatment increased after 2015, whereas warfarin-based treatment declined. No therapeutic anticoagulant record was identified within 14 days in 1567 patients (42.0%) during 2020–2024 (Figure 2).

Temporal trends in documented initial anticoagulation strategies from 2009 to 2024. Initial strategies were classified using therapeutic anticoagulant records captured within 14 days after the index VTE event. “No anticoagulant record identified” indicates absence of a therapeutic anticoagulant record in the hospital database, not necessarily absence of anticoagulation outside the hospital system.

Abbreviations: DOAC, direct oral anticoagulant; VTE, venous thromboembolism.

Hospital-Captured Post-Index Documentation Within 90 Days

Improvements in documented early anticoagulation were not accompanied by comparable increases in hospital-captured documentation of follow-up and reassessment. In the primary cohort, 3069 patients (51.2%) had documented therapeutic anticoagulation within 14 days after the index date. Within 15 to 90 days, clinical follow-up was documented in 1483 patients (24.7%), laboratory monitoring in 366 patients (6.1%), and VTE-related ultrasound or imaging reassessment in 578 patients (9.6%). Documentation of all four prespecified domains was observed in 149 patients (2.5%) (Figure 3).

Domains were not mutually nested, and absence of a record did not necessarily indicate absence of care outside the hospital system.

Factors Associated with Documentation of All Four Domains

In the primary multivariable logistic regression model, the 2020–2024 era was associated with higher odds of four-domain documentation completion compared with 2009–2014 (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 2.41; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.41–4.44). The 2015–2019 era was not clearly associated with completion compared with 2009–2014 (aOR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.45–2.03). PE presentation (aOR, 3.13; 95% CI, 2.16–4.49) and upper-extremity or catheter-related VTE (aOR, 3.84; 95% CI, 2.64–5.60) were associated with higher odds of completion. Age per 10-year increase showed a modest association with completion (aOR, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.05–1.30), whereas sex was not associated with completion (Figure 4).

The primary model included treatment era, age per 10-year increase, sex, index encounter setting, PE presentation, and upper-extremity or catheter-related VTE.

In the extended model additionally adjusted for cancer-related state, prior VTE, and surgery- or trauma-related state, associations for the 2020–2024 era, PE presentation, and upper-extremity or catheter-related VTE remained directionally similar. Prior VTE was also associated with higher odds of four-domain documentation completion (aOR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.70–3.94) (Supplementary Table 2).

The association for the 2020–2024 era remained in the same direction as the primary analysis, although the estimate was imprecise because of the small number of events in the earlier eras.

After excluding patients whose index records suggested prior, chronic, or follow-up VTE without evidence of a new or progressive event, four-domain documentation completion occurred in 146 of 5702 patients (2.6%). In this cohort, the 2020–2024 era remained associated with higher odds of completion compared with 2009–2014 (aOR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.49–4.88). When the anticoagulation domain was excluded, documentation of all three remaining domains remained low at 3.1% (184 of 5990 patients), and the 2020–2024 era again showed higher odds of completion compared with 2009–2014 (aOR, 2.49; 95% CI, 1.53–4.32).

Discussion

In this 16-year vascular surgery cohort, hospital-captured initial VTE treatment shifted toward DOAC-based strategies, and documented early anticoagulation increased over time. Documentation of follow-up, laboratory monitoring, and VTE-related reassessment within 90 days increased less markedly, with all four prespecified domains documented in 2.5% of patients overall. These findings show a divergence between treatment initiation and subsequent within-system documentation and provide a basis for considering how structured follow-up can be maintained as anticoagulation pathways evolve.

These findings should be interpreted in the context of changing VTE treatment pathways. DOACs have simplified anticoagulation for many patients with DVT or PE and are recommended as preferred treatment options in contemporary guidelines.3–6 Nevertheless, simplified drug administration does not remove the need for clinical reassessment. Patients treated with DOACs may still require review of adherence, renal and hepatic function, bleeding risk, drug interactions, symptom evolution, and intended treatment duration.12,14 In the warfarin era, INR monitoring often created repeated clinical contact; in the DOAC era, fewer mandatory monitoring visits may improve convenience but may also reduce routine opportunities for reassessment. Our data are consistent with this concern at the health-system level: although documented early anticoagulation increased, hospital-captured laboratory monitoring and VTE-related reassessment within 90 days remained infrequent. The absence of a hospital-captured anticoagulant record in 42.0% of patients during 2020–2024 may partly reflect prescriptions issued outside the hospital, treatment at referring institutions, or medication records not included in the available extract. The documentation measure used in this study should not be interpreted as a validated clinical outcome or a universal standard requiring every domain for every patient. Rather, it identifies whether patients remained visible to a documented follow-up process after the index VTE event.

The association between VTE presentation and four-domain documentation completion provides clinically plausible insight into which presentations were more likely to generate complete within-system documentation. PE presentation and upper-extremity or catheter-related VTE were associated with higher odds of completion. Patients with PE may be more likely to undergo reassessment because of persistent dyspnea, concern for post-PE syndrome, or evaluation for chronic thromboembolic complications.7,13,15,16 Patients with upper-extremity or catheter-related thrombosis often have central venous devices, malignancy, or treatment-duration decisions related to catheter management, which may create additional contact points with the healthcare system.17–20 Prior VTE was also associated with higher completion in the extended model, suggesting that an established thrombotic history may similarly generate more opportunities for documented reassessment. Overall, these findings suggest that clinically visible or management-intensive presentations may create additional contact points and consequently more complete within-system documentation.

Contemporary guidance emphasizes individualized follow-up after VTE, including review of anticoagulant selection and intended treatment duration, bleeding risk, renal and hepatic function when appropriate, symptom evolution, and selective imaging when clinically indicated.5,6,12–14 Observational studies suggest that systematic PE follow-up may increase detection of chronic thromboembolic complications and that anticoagulation stewardship can facilitate structured medication review.16,21 In practice, VTE follow-up clinics, electronic reminders, pharmacist-supported anticoagulation services, and stewardship programs may help make these decisions explicit and trackable.21–23 Such approaches may be particularly relevant in the DOAC era, when routine laboratory-based contacts are less frequent. Recent work also emphasizes patient-centered outcomes, functional recovery, and long-term care value after VTE.8,24–27 Together, the available literature and the documentation patterns observed in this study provide a rationale for further evaluation of structured follow-up models; however, they do not establish that completing all four documentation domains improves care or clinical outcomes.

This study has limitations. First, it was a single-center vascular surgery cohort based on records from one hospital system and may not represent follow-up pathways in hematology, internal medicine, oncology, primary care, anticoagulation clinics, or other institutions. Hospital documentation is an imperfect surrogate for overall healthcare utilization; follow-up delivered through shared-care or out-of-system settings may not have been captured, and changes in documentation systems over the 16-year period may have contributed to treatment-era differences. VTE ascertainment, phenotype classification, and index-date assignment relied on heterogeneous structured and semi-structured records and were not independently validated, although sensitivity analyses restricted to objective VTE evidence and excluding historical or follow-up records showed similar overall patterns. The study-defined 90-day documentation measure was process-based, and laboratory monitoring and imaging reassessment were not clinically indicated for every patient. Mortality, recurrent VTE, bleeding, medication adherence, post-thrombotic syndrome, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, and patient-reported outcomes were not evaluated as study outcomes; accordingly, documentation completion could not be linked to care quality or clinical outcomes. Finally, because documentation during days 15–90 could accrue only while patients remained alive and observable, differential opportunity for documentation and survivor bias were possible.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a real-world description of how hospital-captured post-VTE documentation evolved during the transition to DOAC-based treatment and a basis for further evaluation of trackable, individualized follow-up approaches.

Conclusion

In this 16-year vascular surgery cohort, hospital-captured documentation of early anticoagulation increased during the transition toward DOAC-based treatment, whereas hospital-captured documentation of 90-day follow-up processes remained limited. These real-world patterns highlight opportunities to strengthen the continuity and traceability of post-VTE management through explicit documentation of individualized decisions regarding treatment duration, safety monitoring, symptom reassessment, and selective imaging when clinically indicated.
