Real World Patterns of Sexually Transmitted Infection Testing in the US Reveal Substantial Under Testing and Wide Geographic and Inter-Provider Variation
Authors: Jason M. Baron, Denise LaMarche Heaney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Testing for Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) and Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) is indicated for diagnostic purposes in symptomatic individuals and for screening purposes in certain populations including sexually active females younger than 25 and partners of individuals who test positive.Methods:Using 2023 insurance claims data from 2 million US individuals in the Marketscan database with commercial insurance or with Medicaid, we quantified rates of CT/NG testing and patterns of co-ordering of tests for additional infections. We assessed variation in testing rates and patterns using mixed effects logistic regression models.Results:The overall testing rate for CT/NG in 2023 was 23.7% (23.4–24.0%, 95% CI) in females 16 to 24 and 6.9% (6.7–7.1%) in males 16 to 24; rates were lower in older males and older females. Among females 16 to 24 with at least 1 outpatient obstetrician-gynecology visit, the testing rate was only 56%. Testing rates varied by US state with a median odds ratio of 1.34 in females 16 to 24. Symptomatic females tested for CT/NG were also tested for Trichomonas vaginalis, Mycoplasma genitalium and HSV in 62%, 6%, and 4% of cases, respectively. Patterns of test co-ordering varied widely by reason for testing and specific provider.Conclusions:CT/NG testing rates were low. Differences between rates of testing and sexual activity in females under 25 suggest guideline nonadherence (sexual activity estimated based on our analysis of NHANES survey data and prior literature including Halpern, 2012 and Rimsza, 2005). Wide geographic and interprovider variation in testing patterns argues for improved testing systems and practice.

Timely clinical laboratory testing is critical for the identification and diagnosis of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) and Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG). Suboptimal test ordering can lead to delayed or incorrect diagnosis and, in turn, delayed or inappropriate treatment, potentially compromising both individual patient care and public health. Nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT)-based tests1 are commercially available for CT and NG diagnosis. NAAT-based tests are also available for many other STIs, including Trichomonas vaginalis (TV), Mycoplasma genitalium (MG) and herpes simplex virus (HSV). Mpox (while not strictly an STI) and varicella zoster virus (VZV), which is not an STI but may be in the differential diagnosis of a patient presenting with lesions, can also be diagnosed with NAAT-tests. Syphilis is most commonly diagnosed using serologic-based tests (rapid plasma reagin and Treponema pallidum particle agglutination). Because it is often not possible to limit the differential diagnosis to a single infection on clinical grounds, multiple tests are usually co-ordered for diagnostic purposes; likewise, patients may be co-infected with multiple STIs.2,3

In addition to diagnostic testing, guidelines recommend screening in certain asymptomatic patients for infections, including CT, NG and in some cases syphilis. The US Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC),4 US Preventive Services Task Force,5 the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and the American Association of Family Physicians all recommend screening for CT and NG in sexually-active women under 24 (the CDC specifically specifies annual testing).6 Relevant guidelines also recommend CT/NG screening in additional populations including men who have sex with men individuals, pregnant women and women over 24 at increased risk and recommend syphilis screening in specific populations including men who have sex with men and pregnant individuals.6

Unfortunately, STI tests may be substantially underutilized in RWD practice.7–10 For example, Kuritzky et al. find underuse of CT and NG testing in patients with LGUTS.7 Likewise, Lillis et al. find that in patients with complications potentially caused by CT and NG (including pelvic inflammatory disease and prostatitis), frequently receive empiric treatment without adequate testing.8 Keddem et al. find poor rates of CT and NG testing among female veterans cared for at Veterans Administration Sites10 and Zeidan et al. find low rates of STI testing at outpatient physician offices.9

However, general, real-world patterns of STI testing, including population-level screening rates, the specific infections tested, and the factors driving variation in practice, remain undercharacterized in the literature. To address this gap and identify potential opportunities to improve STI test utilization, we analyzed real-world claims data. Specifically, this study aims to evaluate 2 key aspects of STI testing: (1) the overall rates and geographic variation of CT and NG screening across broad populations and (2) the clinical patterns of additional STI tests co-ordered for individuals receiving a CT/NG test.

METHODS

This study includes 2 related, but distinct, sets of analyses. First, we look at overall rates of CT/NG testing in the overall population and among various subgroups; in these analyses, we include individuals regardless of testing or health care received. Second, we look at patterns of test co-ordering in individuals who had CT/NG testing; in these analyses, we only include individuals with CT/NG testing. Both analyses evaluate sources of variation in test ordering.

Here we evaluate 2 aspects of STI testing: (i) rates of CT and NG testing and geographic variation in key populations and (ii) patterns of additional infections tested alongside CT and NG. Key methodology is described below with additional details in Supplemental Appendix A, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378.

Dataset

Primary study data was derived from the Merative Marketscan Database including Commercially-insured and Medicaid covered individuals.9 Rates of sexual activity used in supplemental analyses were estimated from the 2015 CDC NHANES Survey.11 We extracted state-based CT prevalence from 2021 CDC epidemiologic data (downloaded from the AtlasPlus tool12); empiric calculation of STI prevalence was outside the scope of this study.

Ethics Statement

Because all claims data was fully deidentified, this study was not considered human subjects research and IRB approval was not needed.

Cohort Rationale and Definitions

Starting with all individuals 16 to 64 years of age within the Marketscan (Commercial or Medicaid database) during calendar year 2023 (representing on the order of 10% of the US population in this age range), we define 3 key cohorts (Supplemental Fig. S1, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378). Our methodology required the development of multiple distinct cohorts because some analyses considered all individuals within the population and others looked specifically at individuals who were tested. In addition, random sampling was used to limit cohort sizes to make model fitting time more practicable. Finally, we developed a specific cohort for analyses by ordering physician; this used a sampling schema intended to ensure sampled physicians had a sufficient number of cases. We limited our analysis to individuals aged 16 and older to align with established clinical metrics (e.g., Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set [HEDIS]13,14) and to minimize confounding interstate variation related to minor consent laws for STI testing that might have come into play had we not used a minimum age.

“Cohort 0” and “Cohort 1” were used in analyses related to the proportion of the population tested. Cohort 0 was based on a random sample of 1 million individuals with Marketscan enrollment in 2023; Cohort 1, used in our primary analyses represented the subset of Cohort 0 with full year coverage. While we require full year coverage in our primary analysis to facilitate inclusion of outpatient visits as a subgrouping variable, we evaluate certain testing rates of individuals with full or partial year coverage (“Cohort 0”) as a sensitivity analysis. “Cohort 2” was used for most analyses looking at which tests were ordered. Cohort 2 was based on a random sample of individuals with 1 or more STI testing events in 2023 in the database. We defined an STI testing event as a unique patient–date of service combination in which CT and/or NG was billed (additional detail below). “Cohort 3” was used to estimate inter-provider variation. Cohort 3 was based on a sample of 25,000 ordering providers with all STI testing events from the sampled providers included.

For Cohort 2 and Cohort 3, we only considered each enrollee’s first testing event during the calendar year; this was intended to reduce inclusion of repeat tests which may be more likely to be tests of cure and may not follow the same patterns are initial testing. (Since Cohort 0 and Cohort 1 analyses considered only whether an enrollee was tested 1 or more times vs not tested, this first test exclusion was not relevant to these cohorts.) Formally, the unit of analysis for Cohort 0 and Cohort 1 analyses was the enrollee and the unit of analysis for Cohort 2 and Cohort 3 based analyses was the testing event.

Attribute Definitions

We captured testing for CT, NG, TV, MG, HSV, VZV, syphilis, and Mpox using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes included on outpatient claims (Supplemental Table S1, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378); with the exception of syphilis, we considered only molecular test for these infections. CPT code lists were manually compiled based on a review of many sources including code definitions, billing codes used by reference labs (based on online test menus), lists derived by internal colleagues for other projects and relevant literature (e.g.,8,15). In all analyses, individuals needed to have at least CT and/or NG to meet our definition of an STI testing event; other infections were only included in analyses of additional infections testing alongside CT and NG. All tests collected on a patient with the same date of service were assumed to be co-ordered.

We infer reasons for STI testing based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes (Supplemental Table S2, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378) and classify reasons as screening, pregnancy (asymptomatic testing in pregnant individuals), symptomatic (individuals presenting with lower genitourinary tract symptoms or other symptoms concerning for CT or NG), exposure (individual potentially exposed to an STI) and infection (individual diagnosed with an STI). We considered all ICD-10 codes used to bill the testing or other outpatient services the same day as the testing. Tests in individuals without ICD-10 codes corresponding to any defined category were placed into a category of “Undefined.”

We categorized individuals ages as age 16 to 24, age 25 to 44, and age 45 to 64 based on age as of December 31, 2023. We extracted US state (not available for Medicaid), and race/ethnicity (not available for commercially insured). For each individual in Cohort 1, we captured whether they had an outpatient encounter with a provider in each of four specialties or settings (primary care [PCP], obstetrician-gynecology [ObGyn], emergency room [ER], and urgent care) during 2023; we based this on outpatient claims throughout the year. For Cohorts 2 and 3, we inferred the ordering provider and ordering provider specialty (if PCP, ObGyn, ER, and urgent care) for each STI testing event based on claims from outpatient visits occurring on the same day as the testing event.

Statistical Analysis

For individuals covered in the database for the entirety of 2023 (Cohort 1), we estimated STI testing rates by subgroup as the number of individuals with 1 or more testing events (CT and/or NG ordered) during 2023 divided by the total number of individuals in the subgroup. We also performed supplemental analysis looking at STI testing rates in individuals regardless of full year enrollment (Cohort 0) using Kaplan–Meier time to event models (see Supplement, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378).

We estimated inter-state variation in testing rates (summarized as median odds ratios [MORs]) by fitting mixed effects logistic regression models with a target variable denoting whether each enrollee had 1 or more testing events, US state as a random intercept and state CT prevalence as a fixed effect. Analysis of NHANES survey data used to provide supplemental results around rates of sexual activity is provided in the Supplemental Methods, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378.

We estimated, by patient subgroup, the proportion of STI testing events in which each relevant infection was tested. We looked at factors impacting which tests were co-ordered using fixed effects and mixed effects logistic regression models with dependent variables, fixed effects and random intercepts as denoted in the relevant figure for each analysis.

Analysis was conducted in the R statistical scripting language.16 Survival modelling was performed using the R “survival” package17; random effects modeling was conducted using the R lme4 package.18 Median odds ratios (MORs) for random intercepts were calculated from the variance of the parameter using standard formulas.19,20 Confidence intervals for parameter estimates from fixed effects and mixed effects logistic regression models were computed using a bootstrapping procedure (200 replicates). Binomial confidence intervals were calculated using the R binom package and plots were generated using the R ggplots package.

RESULTS

Our study included 3 main cohorts as defined in the methods and illustrated in Supplemental Figure S1, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378. Characteristics of these cohorts is shown in Table 1. Cohort 1 (used for analysis of whether individuals had an STI testing event during the year) included 689,235 unique individuals; these individuals were 53% female and had median (IQR) age of 40 (27-53). Cohort 2 (used to assess which infections are tested alongside CT and NG) included 89,160 unique individuals; the individuals in this cohort were 78% female and had median (IQR) age of 30 (23–38). Cohort 3 was demographically similar to Cohort 2.

Rates of Testing, Variation, and Missed Opportunities

In Cohort 1, 23.7% (23.4–24%), 20% (19.8–20.2%), and 5% (4.9–5.1%) (est [95% CI]) of females aged 16 to 24, 25 to 44, and 45 to 64, respectively, were tested for CT and/or NG in 2023. Likewise, 6.9% (6.7–7.1%), 5.8% (5.6–5.9%), and 2.3% (2.2–2.3%) of males aged 16 to 24, 25 to 44, and 45 to 64, respectively, were tested (Fig. 1A). However, our analysis of NHANES survey data11 revealed that 85% (80–89%) of females 18 to 24 and 70% (64–77%) of males 18 to 24 were sexually active (Supplemental Table S3, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378), findings that fall generally within the range of prior literature.21,22 Thus, most sexually active females 24 and under are likely not tested, despite guidelines calling for sexually active females 24 and under to be screened.

Individuals not tested often had visits with ObGyn, PCP, ER and/or urgent care providers (Fig. 1B), suggesting that the health care system may have frequently overlooked opportunities to test individuals who could have benefitted from testing. For example, even in females 16 to 24 with a known visit with an ObGyn provider during the year, only 56.3% (55.4–57.2%) had testing (i.e., 44% did not have testing, suggesting a potentially missed opportunity).

As a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the possibility that individuals with full year coverage in the database (Cohort 1) were fundamentally different than the overall population, we looked at testing rates in individuals within the database regardless of length of time enrolled in the database (Cohort 0). This analysis (Supplemental Table S4, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378) demonstrates that testing rates are similar in individuals with full year coverage compared to the overall population.

Medicaid insured individuals were tested at a slightly higher rate compared to those with Commercial Insurance (Supplemental Table S5, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378); however, since the database only includes Medicaid data for select states and includes commercial data nationally, we cannot exclude the possibility of geographic confounding contributing to this difference.

Testing rates in the commercially-insured population varied widely by US state in both sexes and all age groups (Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table S6, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378).

Variation in Co-ordering of additional tests alongside CT and NG

The ordering of additional STI (TV, MG) or lesion related tests (Mpox, HSV, and syphilis) alongside CT and NG, as expected, varied by inferred reason for testing (Fig. 3). Overall, in Cohort 2, 24.4% (23.8–25%) and 40.7% (40.4–41.1%) of males and females, respectively, who were tested for CT/NG were also tested for TV, with the rate reaching 62% among symptomatic females. Rates of co-testing for other infections were lower; MG and Mpox were not-surprisingly rarely tested alongside CT/NG. (Mpox was tested in 18 out of 88,635 testing events, but counts were too low to include for individual indications in Fig. 3.) CT and NG were nearly always co-ordered (>97% of orders containing one of these tests contained both tests).

In multivariate analysis (Fig. 4), rates of testing for other infections alongside CT and NG varied by age, sex, insurance type and inferred reason for testing. Moreover, as shown (Fig. 4A), rates also varied substantially by ordering provider specialty; for example, in comparison to PCP, urgent care providers were more likely to order TV (OR = 2.7 [2.4–3]) and lesion-related tests such as HSV (OR = 1 [0.8–1.3]) and VZV (OR = 1.3 [1.2–1.6]) but were less likely to test for syphilis serologies (OR=0.6 [0.5–0.7]). ObGyn physicians (compared to PCPs) were more likely to order TV (OR=1.3 [1.2–1.3]) but less likely to order syphilis serologies (OR=0.8 [0.7–0.8]). Fewer additional tests are generally ordered in the ER. Ordering varied somewhat by the patient’s state of residence and dramatically by the specific ordering provider (Fig. 4B). MOR by state ranged from 1.4 for syphilis to 1.9 for VZV. The MOR across providers was 8.3 (6.07–8.78) for TV ordering, 4.98 (4.1–5.25) for syphilis and 12.73 (6.73–12.04) for VZV. Although we limited to inter-provider variation analysis shown in Figure 4 to include only providers with at least 50 STI tests included in the analysis, overall rates of testing for each infection (stratified by indication for testing) were similar between providers with fewer than vs. at least 50 (Supplemental Table S7, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378).

DISCUSSION

Here we show that CT and NG testing are grossly under-utilized at least within women aged 16 to 24 and likely also within older women and in men of all ages. Indeed, only about 1 in 4 women aged 16 to 24 received an STI test in 2023; in comparison, more than 3 in 4 women in this age group should have been tested per guidelines, given estimated rates of sexual activity. Testing rates are also widely variable geographically. Even within individuals who are tested for CT and NG, we find that co-ordering of tests for other infections is variable and highly dependent on the specific physician the patient sees.

The overall CT/NG testing rates presented in this manuscript complement those provided through HEDIS quality measures.13,14 Health plans report the HEDIS Chlamydia Screening measure as the percentage of women aged 16 to 24 identified as sexually active (and, more recently, transgender men and gender-diverse individuals assigned female at birth) who were tested for chlamydia. However, because HEDIS measures are intended primarily for health plan quality assessment, the HEDIS Chlamydia Screening measure, while highly useful in that context, has limitations for broader public health and clinical improvement tracking. For example, HEDIS primarily relies on medical and pharmacy claims to identify individuals as sexually active. This proxy inherently misses sexually active individuals who do not seek medical care, or who seek care but do not generate claims indicating sexual activity (e.g., those who use over-the-counter contraception or no contraception). In contrast, our study considers all individuals in the relevant age range within the denominator. Moreover, we offer a granular view by provider type and US state.

Of note, HEDIS benchmarks indicate that approximately 48% (Commercial Insurance) and 58% (Medicaid) of claims-identified sexually active women aged 16 to 24 were screened in 2020.13 These rates are understandably higher than those we report here, with the major difference being the HEDIS measure’s exclusion of individuals without documented sexual activity. Notably, our screening results for women with an ObGyn visit, a population presumably more likely to be sexually active and when sexually active, to generate corresponding claims data, more closely align with the HEDIS measures.

Prior studies by Keddem et al.10 and Zeidan et al.9 have similarly demonstrated low STI testing rates among female populations; however, their analyses were based on system-specific cohorts. Specifically, Keddem et al. evaluated female patients presenting to Veterans Health Administration facilities, while Zeidan et al. focused on outpatients presenting to US physician offices. Because these prior studies relied on facility-level data, they inherently miss individuals who do not present for care and may not capture patient movement between different clinical practices. Our study addresses this limitation by utilizing a large national database to explore CT and NG testing rates in a broader population, regardless of whether or where they received care. Furthermore, we extend their foundational work by evaluating interstate variation, identifying missed clinical opportunities, and examining patterns of test co-ordering. Notably, to the extent that our cohorts are comparable, they lead to the same overarching conclusion: testing is grossly underutilized. For example, Keddem et al. found that 21% of women aged 15 to 24 received testing, a figure strikingly similar to the overall rate we estimated for 16 to 24-year-old women in our national cohort.

Similarly, while we directly estimate rates of sexual activity from NHANES survey data for individuals aged 18 and older to provide a comparison for CT testing rates, prior literature offers valuable context. For example, using National Survey of Family Growth data, Copen et al.23 estimated that approximately 86% of individuals aged 18 to 24 have engaged in sexual activity, a figure which is generally supportive of our NHANES-derived estimates of sexual activity over a 1-year period.

Regardless of the underlying causes, undertesting for CT and NG leads to suboptimal outcomes for both individual patients and public health. At the individual level, delayed diagnosis increases the risk of severe downstream complications, such as pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility. At the population level, missed screening opportunities allow asymptomatic infections to persist, directly contributing to sustained transmission networks and rising incidence rates. Infrastructural and clinical process improvements, such as the utilization of targeted clinical decision support, may support improved testing practices and guideline adherence.

Broadly, strengths of our study include the large sample size from a national dataset and the robust statistical approaches employed and the public health implications of the findings. This study is also subject to limitations, including that the database used may not perfectly represent the country at large and does not include uninsured people. In addition, testing not billed to insurance even within the insured population included will not be captured. Our use of CPT codes to capture testing may introduce imprecision; interpretation of VZV testing requires caution as the CPT code used to define VZV for this study is nonspecific and may sometimes be used to bill testing for other infections. Likewise, CPT code 87801 was assumed to always include CT and NG, but this may not be true in 100% of cases. Indications for testing were inferred based on ICD-10 coding using a grouper that we developed (Supplemental Table S2, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B378); developing this grouper and approach was necessarily subjective and may not perfectly capture true testing indications. We were unable to capture detailed clinical information from this claims data (and adjust our analyses based on such details); thus, some apparent variation in infections tested may be due to confounding. For example, our findings of inter-provider variation (Fig. 4B) could conceivably be due in part to different providers tending to see patients with different sets of clinical characteristics. If these clinical characteristics impact test ordering practice and are not accounted for in our model, this could lead to apparent (but not real) inter-clinician variation. The Medicaid Data (extracted from Marketscan) included only a subset of states and may not optimally represent the overall population of individuals covered through Medicaid. We cannot exclude the possibility that some of the empirically identified geographic variation was driven by variation between states in Marketscan enrollment (e.g., sampling bias) as opposed to underlying differences in population and testing patterns. Finally, although we compare testing rates to rates of sexual activity, these were derived from different data sources.

Although the start of our study period coincided with the tail end of the COVID-19 public health emergency, it is possible that residual pandemic effects influenced our testing rates. Specifically, a “rebound” in healthcare utilization, driven by patients completing screenings that were deferred earlier, may have temporarily impacted observed testing rates and patterns.

Taken together, these findings highlight critical opportunities to improve STI screening through practice changes at both the provider and health-system levels. Prior work demonstrates that redesigning electronic test menus and ordering templates can significantly improve test utilization24,25 an intervention that holds strong potential here. Furthermore, implementing panel-based testing and establishing clear clinical guidelines could effectively standardize test co-ordering across diverse patient scenarios. Future research correlating these testing patterns and screening rates to actual test positivity rates may be informative. Ultimately, we hope this study serves as a foundational step for quality improvement initiatives aimed at optimizing STI testing.
