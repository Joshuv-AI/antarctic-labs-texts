‘Road safety is no accident’: building efficient road safety lead agencies, strategies and targets in the world, 2009–2023
Authors: Matts-Åke Belin, Meleckidzedeck Khayesi, Nhan Tran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this study is to examine the pattern in building road safety lead agencies, national strategies and national quantified targets in the world in the period 2009-2023. This was done through an analysis of reported presence of road safety lead agencies, national strategies and national quantified targets based on data collected through a questionnaire for five global status reports on road safety. The results show that there has been a steady growth in road safety lead agencies, national strategies and quantified targets globally and by WHO regions in the period 2009-2023. While, on the one hand, substantial increases in these three governance features were observed in Africa and the Americas, on the other hand, slight declines were observed in these features in Europe and some countries in the Pacific. In conclusion, there has been a steady growth in the number of road safety lead agencies, national strategies and quaantified targets globally and by WHO regions in the period 2009-2023. However, there were declines in these governance tools in some countries. Further resarch into how efficiently these tools are being used is needed to provide insights into the effectiveness of road safety policies, organisations and institutions worldwide.Keywords: Planning, Policy, Policy analysis, Global

Introduction

The slogan for the World Health Day 2004 was ‘Road safety is no accident’.1 The logic underlying this slogan was that improvement of road safety does not happen accidentally or on its own but requires deliberate efforts by governments, communities and other partners. This logic is founded on scientific research and practice that reveal the importance of organisations and institutions in delivering results in diverse social, economic and environmental areas.2,5 Designing organisations to be fit for purpose is a growing area of research and practice.6

Though road safety organisations, strategies and quantified targets have been developed by several countries, research has not paid attention to them as it has done to an analysis of trends in road traffic fatalities, risk factors and technical interventions.7 8 Institutions form a smaller percentage of the interventions synthesised and presented in comprehensive literature reviews on road safety.9,11 At the global level, there is scant institutional analysis of road safety policy and planning. However, studies have shown that institutional factors such as target setting, national strategies and lead agencies can have a significant influence on road safety outcomes. For example, five studies have demonstrated a consistent association between the adoption of quantified targets and reductions in road traffic fatalities.12,16 Two other studies have explored the likelihood of achieving road safety targets and the sustainability of such efforts.17 18 In addition, two studies have emphasised the importance of lead agencies and strategic planning in enabling effective policy coordination and delivery.19 20 These findings support the need for sustained analysis of institutional arrangements for achieving road safety improvements. This study addresses this gap in knowledge by examining the pattern in building road safety lead agencies, national strategies and national quantified targets in the world in the period 2009–2023.

Conceptual model

Road safety policy development and implementation take place within a governance system, consisting of several organisations, institutions, strategies and programmes at different geographical and administrative units in a country.21,25 Sharing responsibility is an important principle in road safety policy.26 27 The well-documented effective road safety interventions9 11 require an efficient organisational framework for implementation and delivery of expected outcomes and impact.

Road safety is a multifaceted issue, involving an intricate interplay of socio-political, economic and institutional factors in shaping a nation’s road safety landscape.25 28 Despite variations in organisational structures for road safety policy and planning, a common thread in key reports is the establishment of a dedicated traffic safety agency in each country.25 29 30 The lead agency is expected to have the authority to make decisions, control resources and coordinate efforts across various government sectors, including health, transport, education and police. In addition to the lead agency, the need for countries to develop comprehensive and multisectoral national road safety strategies with quantified targets for road traffic fatality reduction was also expected of countries.29 Figure 1 shows the governance mechanisms that road safety organisations which include lead agencies, national strategies and quantified targets form the processes and tools through which to plan and implement road safety policies.

Methods

A questionnaire used to collect data for global reports on the status of road safety covers several items, and one of them is on the existence of a road safety lead agency, a road safety strategy and a quantified road safety target. For this article, we selected only those countries that participated in all five surveys conducted for these reports.31,35 Collection of data for the global status reports has involved many countries, but the exact number of participating countries has varied across the years. We have compiled data from a subset of 144 countries that participated in all five reporting years. This approach increases the likelihood that the patterns identified are based on a consistent set of countries across the different time periods.

Results

As shown in table 1, the number of countries reporting about the presence of road safety lead agencies grew steadily between 2009 and 2015, reaching a peak of 135 countries (93.8%), but then slightly declined in 2018 and 2023 to 132 countries (91.7%). Similarly, the adoption of national road safety strategies increased substantially from 94 countries (65.3%) in 2009 to 124 countries (86.1%) in 2015. However, this number started to decline thereafter, reaching 110 countries (76.4%) in 2023. Quantified targets followed a similar pattern, with a notable rise from 69 countries (47.9%) in 2009 to 109 countries (75.7%) in 2015, before decreasing slightly in 2018 and 2023. Despite the fluctuations after 2015, the overall trend from 2009 to 2023 shows a positive trajectory in the establishment of lead agencies, strategies and quantified targets.

Distribution of road safety lead agencies by WHO regions, 2009–2023

Growth in road safety lead agencies varied from one region to another (figure 2). In Africa, the proportion of countries with lead agencies slightly increased from 87.9% in 2009 to 93.9% in 2013, but then slightly dropped to 90.9% in 2015 and 2018, before returning to 93.9% in 2023.

Evolution of road safety lead agencies by WHO regions, 2009–2023.

The Americas exhibited steady growth in the presence of lead agencies, increasing from 80.8% in 2009 to 96.2% by 2018, where it remained in 2023.

The Eastern Mediterranean region shows a peak in 2015, where 93.3% of countries had lead agencies, but this declined to 80% by 2023.

Europe had 91.1% of countries reporting lead agencies in 2009, reaching 95.6% in 2015. However, by 2023, this figure had dropped to 88.9%, corresponding to five countries that no longer have lead agencies.

Southeast Asia has remained largely stable, with 100% of countries having lead agencies in all years except 2013, where it dropped to 70%.

The Western Pacific region has also experienced consistent reporting, with 100% of countries having lead agencies from 2009 to 2015. A slight decline occurred in 2018 and 2023, dropping to 93.3%.

Distribution of national strategies by WHO regions, 2009–2023

A general upward trend is observed in the adoption of national road safety strategies across WHO regions, although some regions exhibit fluctuations over time (figure 3). In Africa, there was a substantial rise from 48.5% of countries with national strategies in 2009 to 75.8% in 2015. However, this rise was followed by a slight decline in 2018 (66.7%), before increasing again to 78.8% by 2023. The Americas followed a similar trajectory, rising from 57.7% in 2009 to a peak of 84.6% in 2018, only to drop slightly to 73.1% by 2023.

Growth of national strategies by WHO regions, 2009–2023.

In the Eastern Mediterranean region, there was a notable increase from 40% in 2009 to a high of 93.3% in 2013. However, the proportion of countries with national strategies dropped to 66.7% in both 2018 and 2023, indicating some challenges in maintaining momentum. Europe showed a consistent rise until 2015, when 95.6% of countries had strategies in place, but this proportion declined to 73.3% in 2023.

Southeast Asia demonstrated a steady upward trend, with the proportion of countries adopting national strategies increasing from 70% in 2009 to 100% in 2018, though it dropped slightly to 90% by 2023. The Western Pacific region maintained very high levels of strategy adoption, reaching 100% in 2015 and 2018 before experiencing a slight decrease to 86.7% in 2023.

Distribution of quantified national road safety targets by WHO regions, 2009-2023

A general increase is observed in the adoption of quantified road safety targets across WHO regions, although there are some regional fluctuations (figure 4). In Africa, the proportion of countries with quantified targets increased from 27.3% in 2009 to 66.7% in 2015 but dropped to 51.5% in 2018, before increasing to 66.7% in 2023. The Americas show a similar pattern of growth, with the proportion of countries adopting quantified targets rising from 38.5% in 2009 to 76.9% in 2015, followed by a slight decline to 69.2% in 2018 and remaining stable through 2023.

Evolution of national quantified targets by WHO regions, 2009–2023.

The Eastern Mediterranean region experienced significant growth in the adoption of quantified targets, increasing from just 20% of countries in 2009 to 53.3% in both 2013 and 2015. After a slight dip to 46.7% in 2018, the proportion rose again to 60% by 2023. Europe, which started with a relatively high adoption rate of 73.3% in 2009, saw a peak of 88.9% in 2015. before gradually declining to 77.8% by 2023.

In Southeast Asia, the adoption of quantified targets increased steadily from 40% in 2009 to 80% in 2023, showing consistent progress over time. Meanwhile, the Western Pacific region witnessed steady growth from 66.7% in 2009 to 93.3% in 2018, before dropping slightly to 86.7% in 2023 .

Discussion

The results presented in the preceding section show a steady growth in road safety lead agencies, national strategies and quantified targets temporally and by WHO regions in the period 2009–2023. There are fluctuations in the adoption of national strategies and quantified targets in some regions, particularly after the initial high growth, perhaps due to the push for these initiatives during the first Decade of Action for Road Safety 2011–2020.36 This trend is particularly noticeable in the drop in the number of national strategies and quantified targets after 2015, which may be a consequence of countries having completed their strategies for the first Decade of Action without updating or renewing them for the second Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021–2030.37

These developments in building governance structures for road safety policy and planning are encouraging steps towards taking action to improve road safety in countries. However, there is a need for research to examine not just whether or not these agencies, strategies and quantified targets exist but also how effectively and efficiently they are being used to demonstrate that ‘road safety is not an accident’. Are the lead agencies fit for purpose? This study is not able to answer this question because of its scope and data available. This question needs to be considered in future research. Much of the current research on road safety focuses on outcomes like fatalities and injuries, and interventions like speed limits and infrastructure changes but there is a growing need for more upstream research that examines governance structures to answer such questions as: How is road safety improvement produced in each country? What are the processes and mechanisms through which road safety policies are developed and implemented? While intervention-level evaluations remain essential, there is increasing recognition that sustainable and large-scale improvements in road safety require upstream governance mechanisms that enable and institutionalise effective implementation.38 Future research should also explore the consequences of institutional weakening—such as the removal of targets or lead agency downgrading—on national road safety performance, including whether such changes correspond with stagnation or reversal in outcome trends.

Conclusion

There has been a steady growth in the number of road safety lead agencies, national strategies and quantified targets temporally and by WHO regions in the period 2009–2023. However, there were declines in these governance tools in some countries. Further research into how these tools are being applied in practice, as well as into governance and management processes, is needed to provide insights into the effectiveness of road safety policies, organisations and institutions worldwide.
