Cancer-associated fibroblasts drive immune resistance in MSS colorectal cancer via the FAIM2-CES1-ER stress pathway
Authors: Ren Tang, Hongtao Wan, Menglan Zhong, Kangpeng Yan, Bin Xu, Jianyong Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrosatellite-stable (MSS) colorectal cancer (CRC) shows limited responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors, often in the context of a fibrotic and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are central to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, yet the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we identify Fas apoptotic inhibitory molecule 2 (FAIM2) as a stromal regulator that is downregulated in MSS CRC and associated with immune infiltration and patient prognosis. Single-cell RNA sequencing localizes FAIM2 predominantly to CAFs, where it inversely correlates with collagen production. Functional studies in L-929 cells and primary CAFs show that FAIM2 overexpression suppresses endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress signaling and reduces collagen I and matrix metalloproteinase 2 (MMP2) expression; these effects are attenuated by the ER stress inducer Tunicamycin. Conversely, FAIM2 knockdown enhances ER stress and collagen synthesis, which are mitigated by the ER stress inhibitor tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA). Mechanistically, carboxylesterase 1 (CES1) is identified as a downstream effector negatively regulated by FAIM2, mediating ER stress and ECM remodeling. In vivo, FAIM2 overexpression restrains tumor growth, reduces collagen deposition, and promotes tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation, whereas CES1 co-overexpression counteracts these effects. Together, these findings reveal a potential FAIM2-CES1-ER stress axis in CAFs associated with ECM remodeling and immune contexture in MSS CRC, providing a rationale for future studies exploring its therapeutic potential in immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11010-026-05705-9.Keywords: Microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer, FAIM2, Immunotherapy resistance, Cancer-associated fibroblasts, Endoplasmic reticulum stress, Tertiary lymphoid structures

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1], accounting for more than 1.9 million new cases and 900,000 deaths annually [2]. Although surgery, chemotherapy [3], and targeted therapies [4] have improved CRC management, outcomes in advanced disease remain limited because of recurrence and therapeutic resistance [5, 6]. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) or its ligand (PD-L1) have revolutionized cancer treatment, offering durable clinical benefits across a variety of malignancies [7, 8]. However, the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs in CRC is highly heterogeneous and largely determined by the molecular subtype of the tumor. CRC can be broadly divided into microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) and microsatellite stable (MSS) subtypes. MSI-H CRC, which accounts for approximately 15% of all cases [9], arises from defective DNA mismatch repair (MMR) and is characterized by high tumor mutational burden, abundant neoantigen generation, and dense immune infiltration [10]. Accordingly, MSI-H CRC responds well to PD-1 blockade, for which ICIs are approved as first-line therapy [11]. By contrast, MSS CRC, which comprises nearly 85% of CRC cases [12], is characterized by low mutational burden and an immune-excluded microenvironment [13]. Numerous clinical trials have demonstrated that MSS CRC is intrinsically resistant to PD-1 blockade [14], and effective strategies to overcome this resistance remain a major unmet need. Defining the stromal mechanisms driving immune exclusion in MSS CRC may facilitate the development of more effective immunotherapeutic strategies.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are abundant in CRC and regulate tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapy resistance [15, 16]. They remodel the extracellular matrix (ECM) by producing collagens, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and soluble factors, thereby altering tissue architecture and immune cell infiltration [17, 18]. Excessive collagen deposition induces fibrosis and establishes physical and biochemical barriers that impede immune cell infiltration [19]. CAFs have also been linked to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress induced by protein misfolding and metabolic stress [20, 21]. Persistent ER stress activates the unfolded protein response (UPR), promoting collagen synthesis [22] and ECM stiffening [23], thereby contributing to a pro-tumorigenic and immunosuppressive microenvironment.

The effectiveness of anti-tumor immunity and immunotherapy is influenced by the spatial organization of immune cells within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [24]. The presence of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) is strongly associated with responses to anti-PD-1 therapy. TLSs are ectopic aggregates resembling secondary lymphoid organs, composed of B-cell follicles, T-cell zones, dendritic cells, and stromal cells. They act as local immune hubs that enable antigen presentation, T-cell priming, and B-cell activation [25, 26]. Clinical and preclinical studies show that TLS density correlates with better prognosis and response to ICIs [27], whereas poor TLS formation is linked to immune evasion and therapy resistance [28]. Recent evidence indicates that CAFs and ECM remodeling influence TLS organization [29]. Modugno et al. reported that distinct hMENA11a isoforms differentially regulate TLS formation by modulating LTβR signaling and fibronectin production in tumor cells and CAFs [30]. Thus, CAF-driven ECM remodeling emerges as a critical stromal checkpoint that shapes TLS organization and ultimately influences the efficacy of immunotherapy.

However, the molecular basis for the ECM remodeling and immunomodulatory activities of CAFs in MSS CRC remains largely undefined. In this study, we found that Fas apoptotic inhibitory molecule 2 (FAIM2) is selectively downregulated in MSS relative to MSI CRC. Transcriptomic and single-cell analyses revealed that FAIM2 is enriched in CAFs and negatively correlated with collagen synthesis. Functionally, FAIM2 knockdown in CAFs upregulated carboxylesterase 1 (CES1), a downstream mediator of ER stress and collagen synthesis, thereby promoting fibrotic remodeling and impairing TLS formation. Together, these findings uncover a stromal mechanism that may contribute to immune resistance in MSS CRC and nominate the FAIM2-CES1-ER stress axis as a potential therapeutic target for improving immunotherapy responsiveness.

Methods

Cell culture and reagents

Mouse fibroblast cell line L-929 (YDT-0488), murine MSS CRC cell line CT26 (YDT-0147), and human embryonic kidney 293 T (YDT-0019) cells were obtained from INDIT Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). L-929 cells were cultured in MEM medium (BDBIO, Hangzhou, China, C11095500BT) supplemented with non-essential amino acids (NEAA). CT26 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (BDBIO, CA, USA, L103-500), while 293 T cells were gsrown in DMEM medium (BDBIO, Hangzhou, China, L100-500). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; SERANA, Brandenburg, Germany, s-FBS-SA-015) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, NY, USA, 15140-122). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Primary CAFs were isolated from mouse MSS CRC xenografts. 1 × 10⁶ CT26 cells were subcutaneously injected into BALB/c mice. Tumors were excised, minced into ~ 1 mm3 fragments, and digested in DMEM containing 160 µg/mL collagenase and 25 µg/mL hyaluronidase at 37 °C for 4 h. The suspension was filtered through a 100 μm strainer, centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min, and the pellet was cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS. CAFs were purified by differential adhesion: cells were allowed to attach for 20 min, and non-adherent cells were transferred to a new dish; this process was repeated. CAF identity was confirmed by positive expression of α-SMA, FAP, and PDGFR-β.

For functional assays, Tunicamycin (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA, HY-A0098), an inducer of ER stress, was applied at 1 µg/mL for 24 h. For ER stress inhibition experiments, Tauroursodeoxycholate (TUDCA; MedChemExpress, NJ, USA, HY-19696), a chemical ER stress inhibitor, was used to treat primary CAFs at a concentration of 50 µM for 24 h.

Total protein was extracted from L-929 and primary CAFs cells using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA, 87787) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors and quantified using a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA, 23227). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg/lane) were resolved on 8 ~ 12% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% BSA (Solarbio, Beijing, China, A8020), and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized with an ECL substrate (Biosharp, Hefei, China, BL520B), and signal intensities were normalized to β-actin. Antibodies used are listed in Table 1.

Stable cell lines were generated using lentiviral transduction. Lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting 293 T cells with psPAX2, pMD2.G, and FAIM2 alone or a combination of FAIM2 and CES1 plasmids (pcDNA3.1 as control) using Lipofectamine 2000. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 h, filtered, and used to infect L-929 cells. After 24 h, cells were selected with puromycin (1 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher, MA, USA, A1113802). Overexpression efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR and WB analysis.

Construction of xenograft tumor models in vivo

All animal experiments were performed following the “Guiding Principles in the Care and Use of Animals” (China) and approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Cancer Hospital (Approval No. 2025ky019). Eighteen 6-week-old male BALB/c mice (SLAC ANIMAL, Shanghai, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 22 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 10% humidity, with a 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. Mice underwent a 7-day acclimatization period before the experiment. To assess the effects of FAIM2 and CES1 on CRC progression, mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6): Lenti-control, Lenti-FAIM2, and Lenti-FAIM2 + Lenti-CES1. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 1 × 10⁶ CT26 cells mixed with 1 × 10⁶ L-929 cells overexpressing FAIM2 alone or FAIM2 plus CES1 in 100 µL PBS. Tumor growth was measured every 3 days once volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, and volume was calculated as length × width² × 0.5. Mice were euthanized when any tumor approached 1000 mm³, and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

TLSs in xenograft tumor tissues were assessed by H&E staining. Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (BioSharp, Hefei, China, BL529A), dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were dewaxed in xylene (SinoPharm, Beijing, China, 10023418), rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (5 min each). After dehydration and clearing, slides were mounted with neutral resin and imaged under a bright-field microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan, E100).

H&E-stained whole-tumor sections were digitally scanned and independently evaluated by two blinded investigators. TLSs were identified as well-organized lymphocytic aggregates with clear boundaries within or adjacent to tumors and distinguished from diffuse inflammatory infiltrates based on morphological criteria. TLS-positive rate was defined as the proportion of mice with at least one TLS, and TLS density was calculated as the number of TLSs per mm² of tumor area using ImageJ software. Quantitative analyses were performed using one representative tumor section per mouse, with each mouse considered an independent biological replicate (n = 5).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Human CRC specimens used for IHC analysis were histologically confirmed as MSS CRC based on routine clinical MSI/MMR assessment, as described previously [31]. Target protein expression in clinical and animal tumor tissues was assessed by IHC, including 6 tumor and 6 normal clinical samples. Paraffin sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by blocking with 3% H₂O₂ and 3% BSA. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 50 min. Signals were developed with DAB solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, G1212), counterstained with hematoxylin, and mounted. Images were captured using a microscope. Antibodies are listed in Table 1.

Immunofluorescent (IF) staining

IF staining was performed to assess CES1 subcellular localization. Primary mouse CAFs derived from murine MSS CRC xenografts were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Cells were first incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against the ER marker Calnexin, followed by incubation with the corresponding fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After extensive washing, cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against CES1, followed by incubation with a second fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and co-localization analysis was performed to evaluate CES1 distribution. Antibodies are listed in Table 1.

Masson’s trichrome staining

Tissue sections were stained with Masson’s trichrome kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, G1006). Briefly, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin (5 ~ 10 min), followed by differentiation, blueing (5 min), and counterstaining with Ponceau S solution (5 ~ 10 min). Sections were then treated with 0.3% acetic acid and phosphomolybdic acid, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. Images were acquired using a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan, E100).

Bioinformatics analysis

CRC transcriptomic data were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), including 26 MSI and 11 MSS samples. Differential expression analysis was performed on raw count data using the R package “DESeq2” (v1.48.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. To evaluate the association between gene expression and immune infiltration, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the R package “GSVA” (v2.2.0) with 28 immune-related gene sets. Spearman correlation analysis was applied to determine the relationship between the expression of SLC26A2 or FAIM2 and immune cell infiltration. Survival analysis was performed using the R package “survminer” (v0.5.0). Based on the optimal cutoff values of SLC26A2 and FAIM2 expression, patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Additionally, based on survival outcomes, samples were categorized into “Alive” (n = 18) and “Death” (n = 19) groups. Gene expression differences of FAIM2 and SLC26A2 between groups were analyzed by the Wilcoxon test using the R package “ggpubr” (v0.6.1), and bar plots were visualized with “ggplot2” (v3.5.2).

Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data of human CRC were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE205506, comprising eight samples (anti-PD-1 treatment, n = 4; untreated, n = 4). Low-quality cells were filtered, and datasets were integrated using the “IntegrateLayers” function of “Seurat” (v5.1.0) (https://satijalab.org/seurat). The top 30 principal components were selected, and cell clustering was visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). Major cell lineages were identified based on canonical markers. Copy number variation analysis was performed with “inferCNV” (v1.18.1) (https://github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV) to distinguish malignant cells. FAIM2 expression patterns across cell clusters were visualized using the DotPlot function. To assess differential expression in fibroblasts between anti-PD-1 and untreated groups, cells were divided into FAIM2+ and FAIM2− subgroups, and expression of collagen-related genes and MMP2 was compared. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of fibroblast DEGs (anti-PD-1 vs. untreated; p-value ≤ 0.05, |avg_log2FC| ≥ 0.25) were performed using the R package “clusterProfiler” (v4.10.1) (https://github.com/YuLab-SMU/clusterProfiler).

Bulk RNA-seq data of CRC from TCGA were further stratified according to FAIM2 expression into high (n = 192) and low (n = 191) groups. DEGs were identified using “DESeq2” (|log2FC| > 1, p-value < 0.05). “GSVA” (v2.2.0) was applied to calculate enrichment scores for ER stress-related gene sets. Overlapping DEGs between TCGA (high vs. low FAIM2) and fibroblast (anti-PD-1 vs. untreated) datasets were identified, and Venn diagrams were generated using “ggvenn” (v0.1.10). Correlation between FAIM2 and CES1 expression was assessed using “ggplot2” (v3.5.2).

Statistical analysis

To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were performed at least three times. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Comparisons between two groups were assessed with Student’s t-test, while one-way ANOVA was applied for multiple group comparisons. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with statistical significance defined as *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.

Results

FAIM2 downregulation in MSS CRC correlates with immune microenvironment alterations and poor prognosis

To identify key genes associated with immunotherapy resistance in MSS CRC, transcriptomic analysis was performed on 37 human CRC samples (26 MSI and 11 MSS) from the TCGA dataset. Differential expression analysis revealed 309 upregulated genes (including SLC26A2) and 732 downregulated genes (including FAIM2) in the MSS subgroup compared with the MSI subgroup, as shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 1A). A heatmap of the top 33 DEGs ranked by p-value (Fig. 1B) further identified SLC26A2 and FAIM2 as candidate genes among the significantly altered transcripts, consistent with their differential expression patterns between MSI and MSS CRC (Fig. 1C). Further RT-qPCR validation in clinical CRC specimens demonstrated that SLC26A2 was upregulated, whereas FAIM2 was downregulated in MSS compared with MSI CRC tissues, consistent with the bioinformatics analysis (Fig. 1D).

To understand their potential role in shaping the TIME, we performed immune correlation analysis. FAIM2 expression showed a significant positive correlation with the infiltration of monocytes (cor = 0.403, p = 0.013) and Type 17 T helper cells (cor = 0.343, p = 0.038) (Fig. 1E), while SLC26A2 correlated positively with activated B cells and CD8+ T cells and negatively with neutrophils (Fig. 1F). These immune correlations suggest a potential association between FAIM2/SLC26A2 expression and TLS-related immune features [32]. Survival analysis further indicated that low SLC26A2 or FAIM2 expression was associated with poorer overall survival (p = 0.02 and p = 0.00068, respectively) (Fig. 1G and H). In a survival-stratified cohort, FAIM2 was significantly lower in deceased patients (p = 0.046), while SLC26A2 showed a non-significant downward trend (Fig. 1I). Given its more pronounced and statistically robust association with patient survival, we selected FAIM2 for further mechanistic investigation. Collectively, these findings suggest that FAIM2 is downregulated in MSS CRC and is correlated with immune infiltration characteristics and patient prognosis.

FAIM2 exhibits predominant expression in CAFs and negatively modulates collagen biosynthesis

To elucidate the impact of anti-PD-1 therapy on the tumor microenvironment (TME) and fibroblast function in CRC, scRNA-seq data from 8 CRC patients (4 anti-PD-1-treated and 4 untreated) were analyzed. Cellular composition was visualized by t-SNE, and major cell lineages were identified based on established markers (Fig. 2A and B). The fibroblast markers were primarily COL1A2 and COL3A1, which encode major structural components of type I and type III collagen that are critical for ECM organization and fibrotic remodeling [33]. Furthermore, comparison of cell type proportions before and after anti-PD-1 treatment revealed that post-treatment tumors exhibited reduced proportions of B cells, macrophages, monocytes, epithelial cells, and fibroblasts, accompanied by increased proportions of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, endothelial cells, and tumor cells (Fig. 2C), indicating dynamic immune and stromal remodeling following immunotherapy.

Additionally, analysis of FAIM2 expression across different cell clusters indicated that FAIM2 is primarily expressed in fibroblasts (Fig. 2D). Notably, FAIM2 expression was significantly higher in CAFs from anti-PD-1-treated patients compared with untreated ones (Fig. 2E), suggesting a potential role in treatment response. Stratification of fibroblasts according to FAIM2 expression (FAIM2+ vs. FAIM2−) demonstrated that CAFs with high FAIM2 expression exhibited markedly reduced expression of genes associated with collagen synthesis, including COL3A1 (type III collagen), COL1A1 and COL1A2 (type I collagen), as well as MMP2, a matrix metalloproteinase involved in ECM degradation and remodeling (Fig. 2F). These findings indicate that FAIM2-high CAFs are characterized by reduced expression of collagen synthesis- and ECM remodeling-related genes.

To experimentally validate these findings, we analyzed MSS CRC tissues with differential FAIM2 expression. IHC staining distinguished MSS CRC tumor tissues with low and high FAIM2 expression (Fig. 3A). Masson’s trichrome staining suggested that FAIM2− MSS CRC tumors exhibited increased collagen deposition and fibrosis compared with FAIM2+ MSS CRC tumors (Fig. 3B). Functional assays in L-929 fibroblasts were consistent with these observations. FAIM2 overexpression was validated by RT-qPCR and WB analyses (Fig. 3C and D). Notably, FAIM2 overexpression was associated with reduced expression of collagen I and MMP2, whereas collagen III and MMP9 levels showed only modest changes (Fig. 3E). Together, these results suggest that FAIM2 may function as a negative regulator of collagen synthesis and fibrosis in CAFs.

FAIM2 suppresses ER stress and collagen synthesis in fibroblasts by downregulating CES1

To explore the downstream pathways regulated by FAIM2 in CRC, we next performed transcriptomic and functional analyses. Bulk RNA-seq analysis from the TCGA dataset identified 83 upregulated and 104 downregulated genes in FAIM2-high CRC samples (Fig. 4A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of these DEGs revealed significant enrichment of the “protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum” pathway (Fig. 4B and C). At the protein level, overexpression of FAIM2 in L-929 fibroblasts markedly reduced phosphorylation of canonical ER stress mediators, including PERK, eIF2α, and IRE1α (Fig. 4D). Importantly, pharmacological induction of ER stress with Tunicamycin attenuated the effects of FAIM2 overexpression and increased the phosphorylation levels of ER stress markers (Fig. 4E). Concomitantly, Tunicamycin partially restored collagen I and MMP2 expression in FAIM2-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4F), suggesting that ER stress may contribute to the effects of FAIM2 on collagen synthesis-related processes.

To further investigate the underlying mechanisms, pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using DEGs from CAFs in anti-PD-1-responsive and non-responsive CRC patients. GO and KEGG analyses of scRNA-seq data indicated enrichment in ER protein processing, cellular senescence, and Hippo signaling in FAIM2-associated fibroblasts (Fig. S1). An integrative analysis combining TCGA-derived DEGs stratified by FAIM2 expression (high vs. low) with fibroblast-specific DEGs from anti-PD-1-treated versus untreated samples identified CES1 as the only overlapping gene (Fig. 5A). Correlation analysis revealed a negative association between FAIM2 and CES1 expression in CRC samples (R = −0.18, p = 0.00041) (Fig. 5B). This inverse correlation was further supported at the protein level in clinical samples. IHC staining showed reduced FAIM2 expression and increased CES1 expression in MSS CRC tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, FAIM2⁺ MSS CRC tumors displayed lower CES1 expression compared with FAIM2− MSS CRC tumors (Fig. 5D). Next, functional experiments were performed in L-929 fibroblasts using cells with confirmed CES1 overexpression (Fig. 5E and F). Co-overexpression of CES1 and FAIM2 partially restored ER stress marker phosphorylation (p-PERK, p-eIF2α, p-IRE1α) and collagen-related protein expression (collagen I and MMP2) (Fig. 5G and H), suggesting that CES1 is involved in the effects of FAIM2 on ER stress and ECM-related processes. These findings support CES1 as a potential downstream component of the FAIM2-associated pathway in regulating ER stress and ECM remodeling in CAFs.

The FAIM2-CES1 axis regulates ER stress and collagen synthesis in primary CAFs

Although L929 cells are widely used as a CAF model due to their stable phenotype, ease of culture, and fibroblast-like characteristics [34, 35], primary CAFs provide greater physiological relevance. To this end, CAFs were isolated from a murine MSS CRC model, and WB analysis confirmed the expression of canonical CAF markers, including α-SMA, FAP, and PDGFR-β (Fig. 6A). Co-overexpression of FAIM2 and CES1 in CAFs was validated by WB (Fig. 6B and C). In CAFs, FAIM2 overexpression alone reduced CES1 protein levels (Fig. 6D). Subsequent WB analysis showed that FAIM2 overexpression alone reduced ER stress markers (p-PERK, p-eIF2α, p-IRE1α) and collagen production (collagen I, MMP2), whereas co-overexpression of CES1 partially restored these effects (Fig. 6E and F). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis revealed similar alterations in ATF4, CHOP, and XBP1s expression (Fig. 6G), suggesting that the FAIM2-CES1 axis is associated with activation of the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway and IRE1-mediated XBP1 splicing during ER stress [36].

Rescue experiments were performed using the ER stress inhibitor TUDCA. WB analysis showed that TUDCA markedly suppressed the upregulation of ER stress-related proteins induced by FAIM2 knockdown (Fig. 7G), as well as the increase in collagen I and MMP2 expression (Fig. 7H). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that the elevated expression of ATF4, CHOP, and XBP1s following FAIM2 knockdown was reduced upon TUDCA treatment (Fig. 7I). Together, these findings indicate that FAIM2 knockdown contributes to ECM remodeling in CAFs through CES1-dependent activation of ER stress.

FAIM2 overexpression suppresses tumor growth and promotes TLS formation in a CES1-dependent manner in vivo

To explore the functional relevance of FAIM2 in vivo, we established syngeneic CRC models by co-injecting CT26 cells with L-929 fibroblasts engineered to overexpress FAIM2 alone or in combination with CES1. Stable overexpression of FAIM2 and CES1 was confirmed by RT-qPCR and WB analysis (Fig. 8A and B). Xenograft tumors derived from the FAIM2-overexpressing group exhibited significantly reduced growth, as evidenced by smaller tumor size, weight, and volume compared with controls. Notably, co-expression of CES1 partially reversed these inhibitory effects (Fig. 8C-F). In addition, H&E staining revealed increased TLS formation in FAIM2-overexpressing tumors, characterized by a higher TLS-positive rate and increased TLS density compared with controls. These effects were partially attenuated by CES1 co-expression, as demonstrated by quantitative TLS analysis (Fig. 8G), further supporting the role of the FAIM2-CES1 axis in regulating TLS formation in vivo. Histological analyses further corroborated these findings. IHC analysis demonstrated an inverse association between FAIM2 and CES1 expression in vivo. In addition, FAIM2-overexpressing tumors showed decreased levels of ER stress markers, while CES1 co-overexpression attenuated these changes (Fig. 9A). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed reduced collagen deposition and fibrosis in the FAIM2-high group, effects attenuated by CES1 co-expression (Fig. 9B). Collectively, these in vivo results support a role for the FAIM2-CES1 axis in shaping the TME through alterations in CAF-driven ER stress responses, collagen deposition, and TLS formation, thereby potentially influencing tumor progression.

Discussion

The treatment of CRC remains a major challenge in oncology. Standard management typically involves surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and targeted therapies [37], including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors (cetuximab) and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) inhibitors (bevacizumab) [38]. Immunotherapy has revolutionized cancer care by reactivating T cells suppressed within the TME [39]. Tumors evade immune surveillance primarily through inhibitory pathways such as PD-1/PD-L1 and cytotoxic T lymphocytes-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), which can be blocked by ICIs to restore T-cell activity and achieve durable responses across multiple cancer types [40]. However, the clinical benefit of ICIs in CRC is largely restricted to patients with MSI-H tumors [41]. By contrast, most CRC patients with MSS disease tend to derive limited benefit [42], as their tumors are often associated with a fibrotic, immune-excluded microenvironment that may contribute to primary resistance to immunotherapy [43]. The urgent need for novel strategies to overcome this therapeutic barrier underlies the significance of our study.

Herein, we identify FAIM2 as a pivotal regulator of CAF-mediated ECM remodeling and immune suppression. In MSS CRC, FAIM2 expression is markedly reduced. Given that FAIM2 is predominantly enriched in CAFs, its loss alleviates the normal inhibition of ER stress, leading to excessive collagen deposition and stromal remodeling. The resulting fibrotic microenvironment impedes TLS formation and promotes immune exclusion, which in turn compromises the response to immunotherapy. Consistent with this notion, our scRNA-seq analysis revealed that FAIM2-high CAFs exhibited reduced expression of collagen synthesis-related genes, while in vivo FAIM2 overexpression decreased collagen deposition and enhanced TLS formation. These findings support a model in which FAIM2 restrains CAF-mediated stromal fibrosis to maintain a TME permissive for immune cell organization and infiltration.

Our findings provide evidence linking FAIM2-mediated stromal remodeling with TLS formation in MSS CRC. We first found that FAIM2 is selectively downregulated in human MSS CRC tissues, and its loss correlates with poor patient survival and impaired immune cell infiltration. Mechanistically, FAIM2 appears to suppress ER stress, as indicated by reduced phosphorylation of PERK, eIF2α, and IRE1α upon its overexpression, whereas FAIM2 knockdown shows the opposite trend. In addition, the concordant changes in ATF4, CHOP, and XBP1s expression further suggest that both the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 axis and the IRE1-XBP1 branch participate in FAIM2-mediated regulation of CAF activation. This observation is consistent with previous evidence that sustained activation of these UPR pathways contributes to excessive ECM production and fibroblast pathogenicity in chronic inflammatory conditions [44]. Modulation of ER stress with Tunicamycin or TUDCA correspondingly attenuated the effects of FAIM2 on collagen production, supporting ER stress as an important mediator of FAIM2-regulated collagen remodeling. These results align with previous studies implicating ER stress in fibroblast activation and fibrosis. Druso et al. reported that ER oxidative stress drives S-glutathionylation of collagen, generating a degradation-resistant extracellular matrix that sustains fibroblast proliferation and profibrotic gene expression [45]. Similarly, Ferreira et al. showed that ER stress in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) induces fibroblast activation through direct cell-to-cell contact and PERK signaling, uncovering a novel immune-stromal interaction in fibrosis [46]. Our findings extend these insights by identifying FAIM2 as an upstream regulator of this pathway in the tumor stroma, where its loss amplifies ER stress in CAFs, triggering excessive collagen secretion and reinforcing a self-sustaining fibrotic loop.

Unexpectedly, we identified CES1 as a critical downstream effector mediating FAIM2 function. CES1, classically known for roles in lipid and drug metabolism [47], has not been widely implicated in stromal biology. Our analyses revealed a significant inverse correlation between FAIM2 and CES1 expression across clinical datasets, and functional assays showed that enforced CES1 expression restored ER stress signaling and collagen production in FAIM2-overexpressing fibroblasts. These findings indicate that CES1 functions as a key downstream mediator of FAIM2-associated ER stress signaling and collagen remodeling in CAFs. Although CES1 has primarily been studied in the context of cancer cell metabolism [48], our results identify it as a potential therapeutic target in MSS CRC by demonstrating its novel function in remodeling the TME-associated ECM.

Our findings substantially extend the current understanding of CAF biology and TLS formation in MSS CRC. TLS serve as local immune hubs that coordinate T- and B-cell activation, and their paucity in MSS CRC has been linked to resistance to immunotherapy [49]. Although previous studies have established the immunosuppressive roles of certain CAF subsets [50], the molecular determinants of their profibrotic activity have remained elusive. Herein, we identify FAIM2 as an important suppressor of ER stress-associated collagen remodeling in CAFs. FAIM2 loss enhances stromal fibrosis and is associated with changes in TLS formation and immune cell infiltration in MSS CRC. In line with this, FAIM2 overexpression alleviated fibrosis and promoted TLS formation in vivo, supporting a functional link between ECM remodeling and adaptive immune organization. Mechanistically, our data highlight the FAIM2-CES1-ER stress axis as a previously unrecognized stromal pathway regulating CAF activation and collagen deposition. Collectively, these findings provide new insight into the stromal mechanisms underlying immune exclusion in MSS CRC and suggest that modulation of this pathway may improve responses to immunotherapy.

Despite these insights, our study has certain potential limitations. Firstly, the clinical sample size was modest, and validation in larger patient cohorts will be required to confirm the generalizability of FAIM2-CES1 expression patterns. Secondly, although our fibroblast models and syngeneic tumor experiments support a functional relationship between FAIM2 and CES1, the precise biochemical mechanism by which FAIM2 suppresses CES1 expression remains to be elucidated. Finally, the contribution of other stromal and immune cell subsets to TLS regulation was not comprehensively investigated, and TLS formation was primarily assessed based on histological lymphoid aggregation; therefore, future studies incorporating multiplex spatial imaging and TLS maturation markers are warranted to further define the functional status of these immune structures. Future studies should therefore focus on validating the clinical relevance of the FAIM2-CES1 axis in larger CRC cohorts, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying FAIM2-mediated CES1 suppression, and applying spatial multi-omics and TLS maturation analyses to further characterize fibroblast-immune interactions and the functional organization of TLS in MSS CRC. Such investigations may further refine the mechanistic basis of stromal-mediated immune exclusion and facilitate the development of potential therapeutic strategies targeting the FAIM2-CES1-ER stress pathway.

Conclusion

Our study uncovers a potential mechanism whereby FAIM2 loss in CAFs of MSS CRC upregulates CES1, activating ER stress and promoting collagen deposition. This process disrupts TLS formation, establishes an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and confers immunotherapy resistance. Targeting the FAIM2-CES1-ER stress axis may offer new strategies to enhance immune responses in MSS CRC.
