Improved immediate postoperative T4-L1 hip axis with use of pre-contoured patient-specific rods versus surgeon-contoured rods in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis
Authors: Sahir S Jabbouri, Peter Joo, Anthony Seddio, Dominick Tuason
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFewer mechanical failures in adult spine deformity patients undergoing long construct deformity correction occur when postoperative T4-L1 hip axis is optimized. To our knowledge, these parameters have not been extensively investigated in the pediatric population. In addition, despite the increased popularity of pre-contoured patient-specific rods and preoperative planning software, it is not clear if their use is associated with improved postoperative T4-L1 hip axis compared with standard surgeon-contoured rods. This study investigates the radiographic outcomes, with a focus on the T4-L1 hip axis, and mechanical failures in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) patients who underwent posterior spinal fusion with either pre-contoured patient-specific rods or surgeon-contoured rods.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study identified AIS patients treated with pre-contoured patient-specific rods or surgeon-contoured rods by a single academic pediatric spine surgeon. Demographics, Lenke classification, fused levels, osteotomies, and complications were obtained. Radiographic measurements including T4-L1 pelvic angle (T4-L1PA) were obtained pre-operatively, postoperatively, and at last follow-up. Comparison between cohorts was made based on standardized postoperative alignment goals including T4-L1PA difference within 4 degrees and deviation from ideal L1PA (=0.5 × pelvic incidence − 21).ResultsThere were 40 patients in the surgeon-contoured cohort and 33 patients in the pre-contoured cohort with an average follow-up of 24.1 and 16.9 months, respectively. No significant differences were identified with regards to demographics, Lenke classification, preoperative radiographic measurements, number of fused levels, and osteotomies. A T4-L1PA difference within 4 degrees in the immediate postoperative period was achieved in 81.8% of patients in the pre-contoured cohort vs. 42.5% of patients in the surgeon-contoured cohort (P<0.001). At the most recent follow-up, attainment of the T4-L1PA goal was more frequent in the pre-contoured cohort (72.7%) than in the surgeon-contoured cohort (57.5%); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.18). Our study sample was insufficient to detect a difference in complications/mechanical failures and there was no difference in deviation from ideal L1PA between cohorts (P=0.77).ConclusionsUse of pre-contoured patient-specific rods was associated with improved immediate postoperative T4-L1 hip axis compared with surgeon-contoured rods in AIS patients. However, this difference was not statistically maintained at the last follow-up.Keywords: Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), pre-contoured rods, patient-specific rods, surgeon-contoured rods, T4-L1 pelvic angle (T4-L1PA)

Key findings

• T4-L1 pelvic angle (T4-L1PA) goal within 4 degrees immediately postoperative was achieved in 81.8% of patients in the pre-contoured cohort vs. 42.5% of patients in the surgeon-contoured cohort (P<0.001).

• There was no difference in deviation between ideal L1PA between cohorts (P=0.77).

• Our study sample was insufficient to detect a difference in complications/mechanical failures between cohorts.

What is known and what is new?

• Fewer mechanical failures occur in adult spine deformity patients undergoing long construct deformity correction when postoperative T4-L1 hip axis is optimized. The use of artificial intelligence (AI) preoperative planning software and patient-specific rods has grown in popularity for spine deformity patients.

• This study highlights the potential benefits of patient-specific rods in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) patients, as evidenced by improved immediate T4-L1 hip axis alignment compared with surgeon-contoured rods. However, within the current follow-up period, no mechanical differences were observed.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• It is critical to optimally align pediatric spine deformity patients to reduce the reliance on compensatory mechanisms and the risk of mechanical complications as they age.

• Use of AI-based preoperative planning software and patient-specific rods may better achieve ideal T4-L1 hip axis alignment in AIS patients than traditional rod contouring techniques.

• However, additional studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, inclusion of patient-reported outcome measures, and investigation of different rod materials are needed to further evaluate the potential benefits of pre-contoured rods and to better assess differences in mechanical failure rates.

Introduction

Optimizing sagittal plane correction for adult and pediatric spine deformity patients has been associated with improved patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) (1-3). Thus, increased effort has been placed toward the development of strategies to improve sagittal plane correction. Use of predictive modeling software and pre-contoured patient-specific rods has grown in popularity and has been shown in previous studies to improve postoperative alignment and intraoperative efficiency, as surgeon contouring of rods can be subjective, prone to error and may increase operative time (4-7). A previous study investigating use of pre-contoured rods versus surgeon-contoured rods in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) found high preoperative planning accuracy with improved postoperative thoracic kyphosis (TK) restoration and pelvic incidence (PI) to lumbar lordosis (LL) mismatch (PI-LL mismatch) in a pre-contoured cohort that was compared to a surgeon-contoured cohort (8).

Recently, additional sagittal alignment targets that can be easily measured and modified in surgery have been explored. Hills et al. demonstrated the concept of the T4-L1 hip axis in a disease-free international cohort (9). This study identified L1 pelvic angle (L1PA) to be a critical measure for lumbar spinopelvic alignment and demonstrated that T4PA was nearly equivalent to the L1PA (often within 4 degrees) in normal disease-free patients. In a separate study, Hills et al. demonstrated fewer mechanical failures in adult spinal deformity correction when there was less deviation from normal L1PA (= 0.5 × PI − 21) and when T4-L1PA alignment was within 4 degrees postoperatively (10). Other adult spinal deformity studies have similarly demonstrated reduced rates of rod fracture/pseudoarthrosis, reoperation and improved clinical outcomes when ideal L1PA is achieved (11,12). To our knowledge, these parameters have not been investigated extensively in the pediatric population.

This study investigates the radiographic outcomes and mechanical failures, with a focus on the T4-L1 hip axis, in a group of AIS patients who underwent posterior spinal fusion (PSF) with rods contoured by the surgeon versus rods that are pre-contoured and patient-specific. We hypothesize that AIS patients treated with pre-contoured patient-specific rods have more optimal T4-L1 hip axis alignment postoperatively with fewer mechanical failures than those treated with surgeon-contoured rods. This article is presented in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0076/rc).

Methods

The study was conducted at Yale New Haven Hospital. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yale University (No. 2000041100), and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. A retrospective cohort study was carried out to investigate the radiographic outcomes, particularly, L1PA and T4PA, and complications/mechanical failures between two cohorts of patients with AIS who underwent PSF with surgeon-contoured rods (December 2019–February 2023) or pre-contoured patient-specific rods (September 2021–October 2022). Surgery was performed by a single orthopedic spine surgeon at Yale New Haven Hospital who was transitioning from surgeon-contoured rods to the use of patient-specific rods for AIS patients. There was no patient randomization and patients were excluded if they had <6 months of postoperative follow-up and lack of preoperative and postoperative full spine standing anteroposterior and lateral radiographs.

Patient demographics, Lenke classification, Risser stage, number of fused levels and osteotomies, rod material, and complications were obtained via chart review. Radiographic measurements were obtained by an orthopaedic surgeon unblinded utilizing Orthoview software and included T4PA, L1PA, T5-T12 Kyphosis angle, PI-LL mismatch, and T1PA preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, and at the most recent follow-up. The primary outcome measures were rate of achievement of T4-L1PA within 4 degrees and determination of deviation from normal L1PA as described by Hills et al. (9,10). Other outcome measures included achievement of established spinal alignment goals including PI-LL mismatch <10 degrees, T1PA <14 degrees, and TK within 20–40 degrees (3,13-17).

Statistical analysis

Various patient characteristics and radiographic measurements were compared between the two cohorts. A subgroup analysis of patients in the pre-contoured and surgeon-contoured cohorts who only had cobalt chromium rods utilized was performed to address the potential confounding effect of rod material. Student’s and paired t-tests were used to determine significant mean differences for continuous variables. Chi-squared analysis was performed for categorical variables. A priori power analysis was performed, and a sample size of 39 per group was determined to adequately detect proportional differences in the main radiographic outcome. Data normality was tested and assumed via the Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plots, and parametric tests were utilized under this assumption. SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 28.0. Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses with an alpha set at 0.05 and beta set at 0.20.

Results

There were 40 patients in the surgeon-contoured cohort and 33 patients in the pre-contoured cohort with an average most recent follow-up of 24.1 and 16.9 months, respectively. No significant differences were identified with regards to demographics, Lenke classification, preoperative radiographic measurements, number of fused levels, and osteotomies (Table 1, Figure 1). The predominant rod material utilized in the pre-contoured cohort was cobalt chromium (97% of patients) and in the surgeon-contoured cohort was titanium (85% of patients) (P<0.001). Table 2 demonstrates radiographic measurements between cohorts with noted statistical differences in immediate postoperative PI-LL mismatch (average 0.1 for pre-contoured and 10.9 for surgeon-contoured, P=0.001). However, there was no difference in deviation between ideal L1PA between cohorts (P=0.77). As noted in Table 3 and demonstrated in Figure 2, 81.8% of patients in the pre-contoured cohort (vs. 42.5% of patients in the surgeon-contoured cohort) achieved a T4-L1PA within 4 degrees postoperatively (P<0.001). At most recent follow-up, attainment of the T4-L1PA goal was more frequent in the pre-contoured cohort (72.7%) than in the surgeon-contoured cohort (57.5%); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.18).

Discussion

Sagittal plane optimization in adult and pediatric patients undergoing spinal deformity corrective surgeries is essential (1,2,3,10). AIS patients typically have a much longer post-surgery life expectancy than adult patients with degenerative spinal deformity and therefore, it is even more critical to optimally align pediatric patients to reduce the risk of mechanical complications as they reach adulthood and age further. Achieving global sagittal spinopelvic balance can minimize the risk of junctional issues and development of disk degeneration, flat back syndrome, and even cervical kyphosis which can occur due to postoperative compensation from iatrogenic thoracic hypokyphosis (18,19). Furthermore, with a more normalized sagittal plane, there is less reliance on compensatory mechanisms and reduced risk of late compensatory decompensation (19).

A prior investigation evaluating pre-contoured and surgeon-contoured rods in AIS patients who underwent PSF demonstrated high preoperative planning accuracy and improved immediate postoperative sagittal alignment (TK and PI-LL mismatch) with utilization of patient-specific rods (8). However, given recent adult spinal deformity studies that associate deviations from the ideal L1PA and failure to achieve a T4-L1PA within 4 degrees with an increased risk of mechanical complications, this study evaluated these same parameters in the pediatric AIS population (9,10). Representative case examples from the current study of patients treated with either surgeon-contoured or patient-specific pre-contoured rods are highlighted in Figures 4,5. At the immediate postoperative follow-up, T4-L1PA within 4 degrees was achieved in 81.8% of patients in the pre-contoured cohort versus 42.5% of patients in the surgeon-contoured cohort (P<0.001), although this was not maintained at last follow-up (P=0.18). There was also no difference in deviation between ideal L1PA between cohorts (P=0.77). In contrast to the referenced study by Hills et al., which investigated adult patients with constructs to the sacrum or pelvis, thereby fixing measurements intraoperatively, our cohort did not include patients with instrumentation to the pelvis, as this is typically avoided in the AIS population to preserve motion in young patients (10,20). Thus, there may be more variability when comparing intraoperative versus postoperative measurements once compensatory mechanisms take effect with patients standing (21,22). In addition, there may be more remodeling at unfused segments over time as noted by the lack of maintenance of T4-L1PA and PI-LL mismatch difference from immediate postop to most recent follow-up (23,24). This finding could also be secondary to the differences in the Young’s modulus of the rod material between cohorts and/or technical intra-observer measurement differences (25,26). However, a multicenter randomized controlled study in AIS patients treated with titanium or cobalt chromium rods with 1-year follow-up demonstrated not only similar spinal correction rate but also demonstrated no difference in radiographic parameters at any time point between the cohorts (27).

Current literature in pediatric patients suggests targeting an ideal LL (LL = 0.62 PI + 17.59) and TK [TK=2 × (PT+ Ideal LL – PI)] (19,28,29). However, the latter formula may not be applicable to many pediatric AIS patients as a prior study demonstrated decreased accuracy for patients less than 15 years of age (30). For this current study, a TK goal within 20–40 degrees was set based on a prior study by Marya et al. investigating pre-contoured rods in AIS patients as well as other historic studies (14-17). TK within 20–40 degrees postoperatively was achieved in 90.9% of patients in the pre-contoured cohort versus 55% in the surgeon-contoured cohort (P<0.001) and was maintained at final follow-up (P=0.002). This is an important finding as achieving optimal TK allows for appropriate postural adaptations/reciprocal changes whereas a lack of restoration of normal kyphosis can be associated with compensatory deleterious changes in the cervical and lumbar spine (19,24).

Achieving a PI-LL mismatch <9 degrees has been shown in a cohort study with 46 years of average postoperative follow-up to be associated with improved patient-reported outcomes (3). In the current study, PI-LL mismatch was within 10 degrees postoperatively in 69.7% of patients in the pre-contoured cohort versus 32.5% of patients in the surgeon-contoured cohort (P=0.002), but this was not maintained at final follow-up (P=0.054). However, recent studies have highlighted that the use of PI-LL mismatch <10 degrees goal may not be appropriate for all patients, given that this recommendation was based off adult patients with spinal deformity and not a normal disease-free population (9,10). Furthermore, there is a wide 20-degree range (i.e., ±10 degrees) for the traditionally accepted “ideal” PI-LL mismatch goal which therefore may not be the most appropriate sagittal alignment parameter for every patient (31). Further, Inami et al. demonstrated that the PI-LL mismatch goal is not a constant value and depends on individual PI (32). Bourret et al. also demonstrated that the PI-LL mismatch is negative in normal adult disease-free subjects with an average of −5.4°±10.7° (31).

The use of L1PA as a sagittal parameter accounts for both lordosis and shape and can be easily measured intraoperatively. This parameter has been shown to have a stronger association with the PI than the LL in the normal adult population (9). In addition, the T4PA has been shown to have a near perfect correlation with the L1PA (9). More recently, a study on preoperative AIS patients found the T4-L1PA relationship to be maintained despite significant coronal deformity and the authors advocate for utilization of the T4-L1 hip axis parameters as a sagittal plane alignment framework not just for adult patients, but also for AIS patients (33). However, precise achievement of these targets as well as other established parameters (i.e., ideal TK and T1PA <14 degrees) and accounting for potential reciprocal changes in the unfused spine can be difficult and time-consuming with standard intraoperative surgeon rod contouring and purely visual subjective techniques (7,24). Thus, utilizing patient-specific plans with pre-contoured rods that target these alignment goals may improve postoperative radiographic alignment and potentially help reduce operative time and blood loss (34).

Machine learning algorithms have been shown in the adult population to accurately predict non-instrumented TK and pelvic compensation/reciprocal changes after fusion from the lower thoracic spine to the sacrum (35). The referenced model describes utilizing a database with more than 4,000 patients and incorporates planned/targeted LL with preoperative sagittal parameters to anticipate pelvic tilt and TK. The target alignment that achieves global sagittal balance is selected when producing the pre-contoured patient-specific rod. A similar industry-created proprietary machine learning algorithm was utilized for this study in patients in the pre-contoured cohort that typically incorporates planned/targeted TK correction along with preoperative sagittal alignments to predict postoperative reciprocal changes in the unfused lower lumbar spine and pelvic tilt. Although the algorithm did not intentionally seek to minimize deviation from ideal L1PA and achieve a T4-L1PA within 4 degrees, more patients in the pre-contoured rods cohort achieved a more aligned T4-L1 hip axis (<4 degrees) postoperatively than the surgeon-contoured cohort which highlights the benefits of utilizing preoperative planning software and patient-specific rods. Despite this difference, this study did not demonstrate a statistical difference in mechanical complications (P=0.64). However, further study with a larger sample size and long-term follow-up in pediatric patients may show a difference in mechanical complications in the future. Incorporation of the above parameters with preoperative planning may further optimize sagittal plane correction for AIS patients.

Limitations of this study include its retrospective design, small sample size, and relatively limited follow-up with follow-up length asymmetry. In addition, although there were differences in rod material between the cohorts, subgroup analysis of patients with only cobalt chromium rods still statistically favored the pre-contoured cohort with regards to T4-L1PA and PI-LL mismatch at immediate postop as well as TK at immediate and most recent follow-up. However, these findings should still be interpreted carefully given the limited sample size in the surgeon-contoured cohort. Our study sample was also insufficient to detect a difference in complications/mechanical failures. Another limitation in this study is the inability to associate radiographic measures with patient quality of life given lack of completely available patient-reported outcome scores for both groups. It should also be highlighted that while restoration of sagittal alignment is clearly affected by appropriate and carefully planned rod contouring, the shape of the implanted rods is not the sole factor in determining the postoperative sagittal alignment of the fused spine. Pedicle screws allow for three-dimensional correction maneuvers, including direct vertebral rotation for correction of axial plane deformity. While such techniques can effectively reduce the axial rotation and associated rib prominence seen in thoracic scoliosis, they have also been shown to reduce TK and LL (36). As this was a retrospective study, we were not able to assess for differences in how the spine was manipulated following initial rod implantation (i.e., whether direct vertebral rotation was performed in each cohort and at how many levels), which is another limitation of the study. Also, the present study approached the threshold of 39 patients per group determined by the a priori power analysis, suggesting adequate power for detecting larger effect sizes but limited power for smaller differences. Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates that careful preoperative consideration and planning for appropriate rod contouring can help contribute to more effective sagittal plane restoration following surgical fusion for AIS.

In summary, pre-contoured patient-specific rods in AIS patients may improve postoperative sagittal plane alignment as evidenced by improved immediate postoperative T4-L1 hip axis compared with surgeon-contoured rods. We also demonstrate an increased rate of achieving target immediate postoperative TK and PI-LL mismatch within 10 degrees with use of pre-contoured rods compared with surgeon-contoured rods.

Conclusions

Use of pre-contoured patient-specific rods was associated with improved immediate postoperative T4-L1 hip axis compared with surgeon-contoured rods in AIS patients who underwent PSF. However, our sample size and follow-up was insufficient to detect a potential difference in mechanical failures between cohorts. Further study with long-term outcomes and comparison of cohorts with similar rod materials is needed to further understand the utility of these additional radiographic parameters in the AIS population and the potential benefits of pre-contoured rods on patient-reported outcomes.
