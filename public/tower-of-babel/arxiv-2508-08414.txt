Derivation from classical Majorana–Bloch equation to quantum von Neumann equation for any angular momenta in coherent states
Lihong V. Wang

Abstract
After publishing the derivation from the classical Bloch equation to the quantum von Neumann equation to the Schrödinger–Pauli equation for spin- 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} , we proposed renaming the Bloch equation to the Majorana–Bloch equation because Majorana’s work predated Bloch’s in the presentation of the Bloch equation by 14 years. Here, we generalize our previous derivation to higher spins or angular momenta in coherent pure states. Using the polynomial representation of the coherent-state projector, we derive an invertible mapping from the Majorana–Bloch equation to the von Neumann equation, establishing a one-to-one correspondence between these two formalisms. Application of the Ehrenfest theorem also shows that expectation values in these states reproduce the classical equation of motion.



Keywords:

Bloch equation; Majorana–Bloch equation; von Neumann equation; spin coherent state; coherent‐state projector; density operator polynomial; quantum–classical correspondence; higher‐spin dynamics; angular momentum

We begin with the classical Majorana–Bloch equation,

\frac{d\vec{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}}{dt}=\gamma\,\vec{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}\times\vec{B},

(1)

where
\vec{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}
denotes the classical spin pseudovector,
\gamma
the gyromagnetic ratio,
\vec{B}
the magnetic flux density pseudovector, and
t

\hat{n}\equiv\frac{\vec{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}}{\hbar\,s},

(2)

where
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant,
s
is the spin angular momentum quantum number, and
\hat{n}
is a unit pseudovector (i.e.,
|\hat{n}|=1
, Fig.
1
). Substitution gives

\frac{d\hat{n}}{dt}=\gamma\,\hat{n}\times\vec{B}.

(3)

Multiplying both sides from the right by the spin‑
s
angular momentum operator pseudovector
\vec{\mathcal{S}}=(\mathcal{S}_{x},\mathcal{S}_{y},\mathcal{S}_{z})
yields

\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)=\gamma\,\bigl(\hat{n}\times\vec{B}\,\bigr)\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}.

(4)

From the commutator identity valid for any vectors
\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
(see Appendix
A
),

\bigl(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\,\bigr)\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\bigl[\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}},\;\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr],

(5)

we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)=\frac{\gamma}{i\hbar}\,\bigl[\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}},\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr].

(6)

We define the Zeeman-type Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}\equiv-\,\gamma\;\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}

(7)

and the dimensionless projection of
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
along
\hat{n}

\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}\equiv\frac{1}{\hbar}\,\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}.

(8)

Consequently, Eq.
6

\frac{d\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}}{dt}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,[\mathcal{H},\,\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}].

(9)

For the coherent state along
\hat{n}
, we find its density operator (see Appendix
B
),

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}=\prod_{m^{\prime}=-s}^{s-1}\frac{\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}-m^{\prime}\mathcal{I}}{s-m^{\prime}},

(10)

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the identity operator. We write Eq. (
10
) with fixed coefficients
a_{k}
, which depend only on
s
, as

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}=\sum_{k=0}^{2s}a_{k}\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{k},

(11)

a polynomial of degree
2s
. Then,

\frac{d\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}}{dt}=\sum_{k=0}^{2s}a_{k}\frac{d\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{k}}{dt}=\sum_{k=0}^{2s}a_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{j}\,\frac{d\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}}{dt}\,\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{k-1-j}.

(12)

Inserting (
9
) inside the sum produces

i\hbar\,\frac{d\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}}{dt}=\sum_{k=0}^{2s}a_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{j}\,[\mathcal{H},\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}]\,\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{\,k-1-j}.

(13)

Using (see Appendix
C
)

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{j}\,[\mathcal{H},\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}]\,\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{\,k-1-j}=[\mathcal{H},\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{k}],

(14)

we obtain

i\hbar\,\frac{d\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}}{dt}=\sum_{k=0}^{2s}a_{k}\,[\mathcal{H},\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}^{k}].

(15)

Substituting Eq.
11
yields

i\hbar\,\frac{d\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}}{dt}=[\mathcal{H},\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}],

(16)

which is the space‑independent von Neumann equation for arbitrary spin
s
or any angular momentum. For the pure coherent‑state manifold, the derivation establishes an exact correspondence from the classical (real‑space) Majorana–Bloch dynamics to the quantum (Hilbert‑space) operator formalism.

Since each step is reversible on the coherent-state manifold for linear Zeeman dynamics, the derivation is bijective. Hence, the von Neumann equation and the Majorana–Bloch equation are equivalent descriptions of the same rigid-precession dynamics on this manifold.

3
Expectation‑value derivation of the classical Majorana–Bloch equation

The von Neumann equation can also be recast into the Majorana–Bloch form by taking expectation values. First, we prove that the expectation value of the quantum spin operator
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
under the coherent‐state density operator recovers the classical spin pseudovector:

\Tr\bigl(\rho_{\hat{n}}\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)=\vec{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}.

(17)

Note that

\Tr\bigl(\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)=\bigl(\Tr(\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\mathcal{S}_x),\,\Tr(\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\mathcal{S}_y),\,\Tr(\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\mathcal{S}_z)\bigr).

(18)

For brevity, we define

\lvert\hat{n}\rangle\equiv\lvert s\rangle_{\hat{n}}.

(19)

From Eq.
57
, we obtain

\Tr\bigl(\rho_{\hat{n}}\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)=\langle\hat{n}\rvert\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\lvert\hat{n}\rangle.

(20)

Eq.
52
gives

\bigl(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)\,\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=\hbar\,s\,\lvert\hat{n}\rangle,

(21)

leading to

\langle\hat{n}\rvert\,\bigl(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)\,\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=\hbar\,s.

(22)

One can show that the perpendicular components vanish using ladder operators in the local coordinates aligned with
\hat{n}
,

\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},\pm}=\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},x}\pm i\,\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},y},\qquad\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},z}\equiv\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}.

(23)

For the highest-weight eigenstate
\lvert\hat{n}\rangle
,

\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},+}\,\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\hat{n}|\,\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},-}=0.

(24)

Hence,

\langle\hat{n}|\,\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},x}\,\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\,\langle\hat{n}|(\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},+}+\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},-})\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=0,

(25)

\langle\hat{n}|\,\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},y}\,\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{2i}\,\langle\hat{n}|(\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},+}-\mathcal{S}_{\hat{n},-})\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=0.

(26)

Therefore, we reach

\langle\hat{n}\rvert\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=\hbar\,s\,\hat{n}.

(27)

Substituting Eq.
20
and
\hat{n}=\vec{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}/(\hbar\,s)
(Eq.
2
), we have proven Eq.
17
.

Second, using the von Neumann equation (Eq.
16
) and the cyclic property of the trace, we compute the time derivative:

\frac{d}{dt}\Tr\bigl(\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\Tr\bigl([\mathcal{H},\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}]\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\Tr\bigl(\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,[\vec{\mathcal{S}},\mathcal{H}]\,\bigr).

(28)

Note that

[\vec{\mathcal{S}},\mathcal{H}]\;=\;\bigl([\mathcal{S}_{x},\mathcal{H}],\,[\mathcal{S}_{y},\mathcal{H}],\,[\mathcal{S}_{z},\mathcal{H}]\bigr).

(29)

Substituting the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}=-\gamma\,\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}
, using the commutator identity
[\vec{\mathcal{S}},\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}]=-i\hbar\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\times\vec{B}
(see Appendix
D
), then inserting Eq.
17
, we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\Tr\bigl(\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr)=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\Tr\bigl(\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,[\vec{\mathcal{S}},-\gamma\,\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,]\bigr)=\gamma\,\Tr\bigl(\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\times\vec{B}\,\bigr)=\gamma\,\vec{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}\times\vec{B}.

(30)

Finally, recognizing
\hat{n}=\vec{S}_{\mathrm{cl}}/(\hbar\,s)
(Eq.
2
) gives

\frac{d\hat{n}}{dt}=\gamma\,\hat{n}\times\vec{B},

(31)

which is the classical Majorana–Bloch equation (Eq.
3
).

Alternatively, we may recover the classical Majorana–Bloch equation by applying the Ehrenfest theorem,

\frac{d}{dt}\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\langle[\mathcal{O},\mathcal{H}]\rangle,

(32)

to the spin operator, where the general operator
\mathcal{O}=\vec{\mathcal{S}}
. Again, substituting the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}=-\gamma\,\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}
and using the commutator identity
[\vec{\mathcal{S}},\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,]=-i\hbar\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\times\vec{B}
(see Appendix
D
), we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\langle\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\rangle=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\langle[\vec{\mathcal{S}},-\gamma\,\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,]\rangle=\gamma\,\langle\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\times\vec{B}\,\rangle=\gamma\,\langle\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\rangle\times\vec{B}.

(33)

Since
\langle\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\rangle=\hbar\,s\,\hat{n}
(Eq.
27
) for coherent states, we recover exactly the classical Majorana–Bloch equation (Eq.
3
).

4
Applicability to coherent states

The derivation presented above applies to coherent pure states or statistical mixtures thereof only. A coherent state is a particular family of pure states constructed to mimic classical behavior as closely as quantum mechanics allows.

Any direction in space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
may be specified by the polar angle
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and the azimuthal angle
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
as

\hat{n}(\theta,\phi)=\bigl(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\;\sin\theta\sin\phi,\;\cos\theta\bigr).

(34)

Solving the eigenvalue equation

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\lvert\hat{n}(\theta,\phi)\rangle=\lvert\hat{n}(\theta,\phi)\rangle

(35)

yields the coherent‑state ket in the
\lvert s,m\rangle
basis with quantization along
\hat{z}

[
10
,
11
]
,

\lvert\hat{n}(\theta,\phi)\rangle=\sum_{m=-s}^{+s}\sqrt{\binom{2s}{s+m}}\,e^{i(s-m)\phi}\,\Bigl(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\Bigr)^{s+m}\Bigl(\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\Bigr)^{\,s-m}\;\lvert s,m\rangle.

(36)

For
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
,

\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\lvert\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle+e^{i\phi}\,\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\lvert\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle.

(37)

For
s=1
,

\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=\cos^{2}\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\lvert 1,1\rangle+\sqrt{2}\,e^{i\phi}\,\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\lvert 1,0\rangle+e^{2i\phi}\,\sin^{2}\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\lvert 1,-1\rangle.

(38)

Note that
\lvert 1,0\rangle
is a non-coherent pure state because no values of
\theta,\phi
yield
\lvert\hat{n}\rangle=\lvert 1,0\rangle
. The spin-1 state
\lvert 1,0\rangle
is represented on the Majorana sphere by two antipodal stars, meaning they lie on opposite sides of the sphere. This configuration reflects a state with zero average spin and purely quadrupolar character. In contrast, a coherent state corresponds to two coincident stars at the same point on the sphere, indicating maximal polarization along a single direction.

The set of all spin-
s
coherent-state rays

\bigl\{[\,\lvert\hat{n}(\theta,\phi)\rangle\,]\colon(\theta,\phi)\in S^{2}\bigr\}\;\subset\;\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{H})

(39)

is homeomorphic to the coset manifold
\mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1)
, which is homeomorphic to
S^{2}\ni(\theta,\phi)
. Here, the square brackets denote the ray in projective Hilbert space associated with the ket—that is, the equivalence class of all vectors differing by a global phase. The homeomorphisms can be written as

\bigl\{[\,\lvert\hat{n}(\theta,\phi)\rangle\,]\colon(\theta,\phi)\in S^{2}\bigr\}\;\cong\;\mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1)\;\cong\;S^{2}\,.

(40)

Put differently, the SU(2) coherent-state rays form a submanifold of the projective Hilbert space
\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{H})
for any spin
s
, which is homeomorphic to the two-sphere
S^{2}
, a manifold of real dimension two. By contrast, the full pure-state space of a
(2s+1)
-level system is the complex projective space
\mathbb{CP}^{2s}
with real dimension
4s
(complex dimension
2s
).

In the special case
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
, one has

\mathbb{CP}^{1}\cong S^{2}
; thus, every pure state is an SU(2) coherent state. Stated otherwise, for spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
, no pure state lies outside the coherent-state manifold, and the Bloch sphere parametrizes all pure states.

One may extend from coherent states to mixed states that specifically originate from a combination of coherent states:

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}^{(i)}}\;=\;\bigl|\hat{n}^{(i)}\bigr\rangle\!\bigl\langle\hat{n}^{(i)}\bigr|,\quad\mathscr{\rho}\;=\;\sum_{i}p_{i}\,\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}^{(i)}},

(41)

subject to the convexity conditions of

\sum_{i}p_{i}=1,\;p_{i}\geq 0.

(42)

From Eq.
16
for coherent states, we obtain the associated von Neumann equation for this special set of states:

i\hbar\,\frac{d\mathscr{\rho}}{dt}=[\mathcal{H},\mathscr{\rho}].

(43)

Although the von Neumann equation governs the evolution of any density operator, extending our approach to arbitrary states demands caution. A noncoherent pure state lacks a single classical spin pseudovector representation; except in the trivial pure‐state case, a mixed-state density operator possesses an uncountable continuum of distinct decompositions into pure-state ensembles.

5
Conclusion

We have extended our earlier work on the Majorana–Bloch‐to‐von Neumann derivation from the spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
case to systems of arbitrary angular momentum for the pure coherent‑state manifold. By exploiting the polynomial form of the coherent‐state projector of the angular momentum, we have shown that the time evolution of the projector satisfies the von Neumann equation. This derivation establishes a bijective correspondence between the classical Majorana–Bloch equation on the real-space sphere and its coherent-state quantum analog in the Hilbert-space operator formalism; therefore, classical and quantum angular-momentum dynamics on the pure coherent-state manifold are in one-to-one correspondence. As implied by the Ehrenfest theorem, taking the expectation value of the angular‐momentum operator in a coherent state exactly reproduces the classical Majorana–Bloch equation. We further generalized the derived von Neumann equation to encompass mixed states that originate from convex combinations of coherent‐state projectors.

Appendix A
Proof of the commutation relation for
[\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}},\;\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,]

We show that for any vectors
\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

[\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}},\;\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,]=i\hbar\;(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\,)\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}.

(44)

Substituting

\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}=\sum_{i}a_{i}\,\mathcal{S}_{i},\quad\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}=\sum_{j}b_{j}\,\mathcal{S}_{j}

(45)

into the left side, we reach

[\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}},\;\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,]=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}b_{j}\,[\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{S}_{j}].

(46)

Resorting to

[\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{S}_{j}]=i\hbar\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{ijk}\,\mathcal{S}_{k},

(47)

where
\epsilon_{ijk}
is the Levi–Civita symbol, we obtain

[\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}},\;\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,]=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}b_{j}\,\biggl(\sum_{k}i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\mathcal{S}_{k}\biggr)=i\hbar\sum_{i,j,k}a_{i}b_{j}\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\mathcal{S}_{k}.

(48)

By definition of the cross‐product, one has

(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\,)_{k}=\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{ijk}\,a_{i}\,b_{j}.

(49)

Substitution yields

[\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}},\;\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,]=i\hbar\sum_{k}(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\,)_{k}\,\mathcal{S}_{k}=i\hbar\;(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\,)\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}.

(50)

Appendix B
Proof of the density operator

\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}
is a Hermitian operator on a
(2s+1)
‑dimensional space with simple (nondegenerate) spectrum

\{\,m:m=-s,\,-s+1,\dots,s\}

(51)

and eigenvectors
\{\lvert m\rangle_{\hat{n}}\}
satisfying

\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}\,\lvert m\rangle_{\hat{n}}=m\,\lvert m\rangle_{\hat{n}},\quad\langle m\mid m^{\prime}\rangle_{\hat{n}}=\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}.

(52)

We prove (Eq.
10
)

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}=\prod_{m^{\prime}=-s}^{s-1}\frac{\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}-m^{\prime}\mathcal{I}}{s-m^{\prime}},

(53)

which by construction omits the factor with
m^{\prime}=s
. We claim
\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}
is the rank‑one projector onto the eigenspace of
\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}
with eigenvalue
s
, i.e., the density operator. It is a specific realization of Löwdin’s general projection operator
[
8
,
9
]
.

Since each factor
\bigl(\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}-m^{\prime}\mathcal{I}\bigr)/(s-m^{\prime})
commutes with
\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}
, the operator
\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}
is diagonal in the basis
\{\lvert m\rangle_{\hat{n}}\}
. It follows that

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\lvert m\rangle_{\hat{n}}=\prod_{m^{\prime}=-s}^{s-1}\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{s-m^{\prime}}\,\lvert m\rangle_{\hat{n}}.

(54)

If
m=s
, we have

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\lvert s\rangle_{\hat{n}}=\lvert s\rangle_{\hat{n}}.

(55)

If instead
m\neq s
, then

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}\,\lvert m\rangle_{\hat{n}}=0.

(56)

Namely,
\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}
acts as the identity on
\lvert s\rangle_{\hat{n}}
and annihilates all other
\lvert m\neq s\rangle
. Therefore, we have

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}=\lvert s\rangle_{\hat{n}}\langle s\rvert_{\hat{n}},

(57)

which is the unique rank‑one projector onto the eigenspace of
\mathcal{X}_{\hat{n}}
with the eigenvalue
s
and is hence idempotent (i.e.,
\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}^{2}=\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}
).

For
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
, the density operator (Eq.
10
or
53
) truncates at degree one:

\mathscr{\rho}_{\hat{n}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}+\frac{1}{\hbar}\,\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}},

(58)

which is a pure qubit state. The state points in the direction
\hat{n}
in the Bloch sphere.

Appendix C
Proof of the commutator expansion

The identity

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{X}^{j}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}^{k-1-j}=[\mathcal{X}^{k},\mathcal{H}]

(59)

for any operators
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{H}
can be proven by induction.

Step 1. Base case
k=1
:

We have

[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]=[\mathcal{X}^{1},\mathcal{H}],

(60)

and the sum on the left side becomes

\sum_{j=0}^{0}\mathcal{X}^{0}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}^{0}=[\mathcal{X}^{1},\mathcal{H}].

(61)

Therefore, the identity holds for
k=1
.

Step 2. Inductive hypothesis:

Assume the identity holds for integer
k\geq 1
:

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{X}^{j}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}^{k-1-j}=[\mathcal{X}^{k},\mathcal{H}].

(62)

Step 3. Inductive step:

From the product rule,

[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}]=\mathcal{A}[\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}]+[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C}]\mathcal{B},

(63)

we reach

[\mathcal{X}^{k+1},\mathcal{H}]=[\mathcal{X}^{k}\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]=\mathcal{X}^{k}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]+[\mathcal{X}^{k},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}.

(64)

Applying the inductive hypothesis to
[\mathcal{X}^{k},\mathcal{H}]
yields

[\mathcal{X}^{k},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}=\bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{X}^{j}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}^{k-1-j}\bigr)\mathcal{X}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{X}^{j}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}^{k-j}.

(65)

Substitution into Eq.
64
yields

[\mathcal{X}^{k+1},\mathcal{H}]=\mathcal{X}^{k}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}^{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{X}^{j}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}^{k-j}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathcal{X}^{j}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]\mathcal{X}^{k-j}.

(66)

Therefore, the identity holds for
k+1
. By induction, the formula is true for all integers
k\geq 1
.

Appendix D
Proof of the commutation relation for
[\vec{\mathcal{S}},\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,]

For the
i
th component of
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
, we have

\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{i},\;\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr]=\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{i},\;\sum_{j}B_{j}\mathcal{S}_{j}\bigr]=\sum_{j}B_{j}[\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{S}_{j}].

(67)

The spin operators obey

\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{i},\,\mathcal{S}_{j}\bigr]=i\hbar\,\sum_{k}\varepsilon_{ijk}\,\mathcal{S}_{k},

(68)

where
\epsilon_{ijk}
is the Levi–Civita symbol. Substituting into Eq.
67
yields

\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{i},\;\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigr]=\sum_{j}B_{j}\,[\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{S}_{j}]=i\hbar\,\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{ijk}\,B_{j}\,\mathcal{S}_{k}=i\hbar\,\bigl(\vec{B}\times\vec{\mathcal{S}}\bigr)_{i}.

(69)

Thus, we reach

\bigl[\vec{\mathcal{S}},\,\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}}\,\bigl]=i\hbar\,\vec{B}\times\vec{\mathcal{S}}=-\,i\hbar\,\vec{\mathcal{S}}\times\vec{B}.

(70)
