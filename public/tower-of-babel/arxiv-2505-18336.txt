Sampled-data Systems: Stability, Contractivity and Single-iteration Suboptimal MPC
Yiting Chen, Francesco Bullo, Emiliano Dall’Anese

Abstract
This paper analyzes the stability of interconnected continuous-time (CT) and discrete-time (DT) systems coupled through sampling and zero-order hold mechanisms. The DT system updates its output at regular intervals T > 0 T>0 by applying an n n -fold composition of a given map. This setup is motivated by online and sampled-data implementations of optimization-based controllers – particularly model predictive control (MPC) – where the DT system models n n iterations of an algorithm approximating the solution of an optimization problem. We introduce the concept of a reduced model , defined as the limiting behavior of the sampled-data system as T → 0 + T\to 0^{+} and n → + ∞ n\to+\infty . Our main theoretical contribution establishes that when the reduced model is contractive, there exists a threshold duration T ⁡ ( n ) T(n) for each iteration count n n such that the CT-DT interconnection achieves exponential stability for all sampling periods T < T ⁡ ( n ) T<T(n) . Finally, under the stronger condition that both the CT and DT systems are contractive, we show exponential stability of their interconnection using a small-gain argument. Our theoretical results provide new insights into suboptimal MPC stability, showing that convergence guarantees hold even when using a single iteration of the optimization algorithm—a practically significant finding for real-time control applications.

II-B

Contractivity and stability of dynamical systems

Consider a dynamical system
\dot{x}=\mathsf{F}(x)
, where
\mathsf{F}:\mathcal{C}\mapsto\mathcal{C}
is continuous. Assume that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex, and that it is forward invariant for the dynamics
\dot{x}=\mathsf{F}(x)
. Let
t\mapsto\phi(t;x_{0})
be the flow map of the system from an initial point
x(0):=x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
. We give the following definitions of stability and contractivity (and refer the reader to
[
39
, Ch. 4]
and
[
4
, Ch. 3]
for background and additional details).

Definition 1
(Exponential stability)

Let
x^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
be such that
\mathsf{F}(x^{*})=0
. The system
\dot{x}=\mathsf{F}(x)
renders the equilibrium
x^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
globally exponentially stable if there exists a norm
\|\cdot\|
defined on
\mathcal{C}
and constants
a>0
and
r>0
such that
\|\phi(t;x_{0})-x^{*}\|\leq r\operatorname{e}^{-at}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|,~\forall~t\geq 0
, and
\forall~x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
.
\Box

Definition 2
(Strongly infinitesimally contracting system)

Given a norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a compatible weak pairing
\llbracket\cdot;\cdot\rrbracket
, the vector field
\mathsf{F}
is strongly infinitesimally contracting on
\mathcal{C}
with
contraction rate
c>0
if
\mathsf{osLip}_{x}(\mathsf{F})\leq-c
.
\Box

By
[
4
, Theorem 3.7]
, the vector field
\mathsf{F}
is
c
-strongly
infinitesimally contracting if and only if any two trajectories
\phi(t;x_{0})
and
\phi(t;y_{0})
of the system
\dot{x}=\mathsf{F}(x)
satisfy
\|\phi(t;x_{0})-\phi(t;y_{0})\|\leq\operatorname{e}^{-ct}\|x_{0}-y_{0}\|
, for all
t\geq 0
. In
addition, if
\mathsf{F}
is
c
-strongly infinitesimally contracting, then there
exists a unique equilibrium
x^{*}
that is globally exponentially stable at
rate
c
.

For a given
T\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, consider the sampled trajectories
\phi(kT;x_{0})
and
\phi(kT;y_{0})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, of the system
\dot{x}=\mathsf{F}(x)
for initial conditions
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
. We state the following definition.

Definition 3
(Discrete-time contracting flow map)

Given a norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
T>0
, the continuous-time system
\dot{x}=\mathsf{F}(x)
is discrete-time contracting on
\mathcal{C}
if

\exists~b\in[0,1)
such that
\|\phi(kT;x_{0})-\phi(kT;y_{0})\|\leq b^{k}\|x_{0}-y_{0}\|
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
.
\Box

Definition
3
considers samples of a trajectory
\phi(t;x_{0};y_{0})
at times
t=kT,k\in\mathbb{N}
. This is a key distinction relative to Definition
2
, since the transient bound applies to only the time instants
t=kT,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and not on the distance between trajectories
\phi(\tau+kT;x_{0})-\phi(\tau+kT;y_{0})
during the open interval
\tau+kT,\tau\in(0,T)
. Finally, we have the following definition for discrete-time dynamical systems.

Definition 4
(Contracting discrete-time system)

Consider a discrete-time dynamical system
x_{k+1}=\mathsf{G}(x_{k})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathsf{G}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Lipschitz continuous. Given a norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the system

is contracting (in discrete time) if
\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G})<1
.
\Box

Remark 1
(Link to optimization-based control)

In several optimization-based control frameworks such as MPC

The sub-system (

\Box

Given this setup, the goal of this paper is threefold:

(Research goal #1)
First, inspired by classical formulations of two-time-scale CT-CT systems
[

, we aim to formalize the notion of a

(RM) associated with (

, and

is distributed over time.

(Research goal #2)
A second goal is to unveil conditions on the RM and the CT subsystem (

) to ensure contractivity or stability of the interconnected system (

1
–
3
), while also elucidating how these conditions depend on the parameters

and

.

(Research goal #3)
Finally, we aim to extend classical network contraction theorems for interconnected CT systems
[

to the system (

III-B

The reduced model

We define the
reduced model
associated with the interconnected system (
1
) as:

\textsf{RM:~~~~}\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t),z^{*}(x(t))),\,\,\,\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},

(4)

x(0)\in\mathcal{X}
. Comparing (
4
) and (
1
), and in light of Assumption
3
, (
4
) can be interpreted as a limiting system where:

•

n\rightarrow+\infty
, so that

is computed for any

; and

•

T\rightarrow 0^{+}
, so that
z^{*}(x)
is applied at all times.

To further motivate the
(Research goals #2 and #3)
and to highlight challenges and the inherent limitations imposed by discrete sampling, we start by examining the role of
T
, which determines the update frequency of
z(t)
. While the map
x\mapsto z^{*}(x)
may be designed so that the reduced model

f(x,z^{*}(x))
enjoys stability or contractivity properties under
continuous-time feedback, such properties are not necessarily preserved under sampled-data
implementations, thereby requiring restriction on

.
To see this, we consider the system

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=f(x(t),z(t))

(5a)

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=z^{*}(x(kT)),\,\,t\in[kT,(k+1)T)

(5b)

for some

and

.
This is a version of (

. The following simple example shows that (

is too large.

Example III.1
(Large

may not preserve stability)

Consider a special case of (

) where
\dot{x}=ax+bz
,

,
\mathsf{G}(x,z)=cx+dz

. Suppose that

and, based on Assumption

. Assume that
a+bc/(1-d)<0
; this ensures that the RM
\dot{x}=(a+bc/(1-d))x
renders the origin GES. As in system (

), suppose now that
z^{*}(x)
is updated every

due to underlying computational and sampling constraints; the dynamics are then
\dot{x}(t)=ax(t)+bz(t)

\displaystyle x(kT)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{e}^{aT}x((k-1)T)+\int_{(k-1)T}^{kT}\operatorname{e}^{a(kT-\tau)}bz~d\tau

\displaystyle=\left(\operatorname{e}^{aT}+\frac{bc}{1-d}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{aT}-1}{a}\right)x((k-1)T)

\displaystyle=\left(\operatorname{e}^{aT}+\frac{bc}{1-d}\frac{\operatorname{e}^{aT}-1}{a}\right)^{k}x(0).

Then, for any

\Box

This example shows that, even if the RM

. In the ensuing Section

alongside

Remark 2
(Connections with classical CT-CT systems)

Consider a classical two-time-scale CT system of the form
[
1
]
:

\dot{x}=f(x,z),~~~\epsilon\dot{z}=g(x,z)

[

Our CT–DT interconnection (

For fixed

satisfying

under Assumption

In analogy with the classical CT–CT singular perturbation framework

\Box

III-C

Supporting results: a fundamental limitation, discrete-time contractivity, and continuous-time stability

Before presenting the main technical results associated with the implication diagram in Fig.
2

of the CT–DT system: continuous-time contraction cannot be
expected under sampled-data implementations. This motivates a shift in perspective, whereby contraction properties are instead characterized in discrete time and combined with continuous-time stability arguments.

To begin with, we note that the interconnected system (

each open interval
(kT,(k+1)T)

any solution

\dot{y}=F(y):=\begin{bmatrix}f(x,z)\\

(7)

IV-A

Batch and online MPC

Consider an LTI system
\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t)
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{u}}
. To formulate an instance of MPC for this system, let
\Delta>0
be an interval used to discretize the dynamics of the LTI system, and let
H
be the length of the MPC horizon (here,
\Delta>0
is an MPC design parameter that is used to build the predictive horizon). Define the matrices:

\displaystyle\bar{A}:=\operatorname{e}^{A\Delta},~~~\bar{B}:=\left(\int_{0}^{\Delta}\operatorname{e}^{A(\Delta-s)}~ds\right)B\,.

(22)

At a given time
kT
, instances of the MPC problem can then be built based on the discretized dynamics

x_{k,i+1}=\bar{A}x_{k,i}+\bar{B}z_{k,i}
,
i=1,\ldots,H
, with
x_{k,1}=x(kT)
. We first consider the following unconstrained MPC formulation
[
7
]
(and comment on constrained versions in Section
VI
) that is solved at each time
t=kT
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

:

\displaystyle z^{*}(x(kT))\!=\!

\displaystyle\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}H}\\
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}(H\!+\!1)}\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\!\left(U_{i}(z_{i})\!+\!J_{i}(x_{i})\right)\!+\!J_{H+1}(x_{H+1})

\displaystyle\text{s. to:~}x_{i+1}=\bar{A}x_{i}+\bar{B}z_{i},~~i=1,\ldots,H\!

\displaystyle~~~~~~~x_{1}=x(kT),

(23)

where
z_{i}\mapsto U_{i}(z_{i})
is a cost function associated with the input
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
and
x_{i}\mapsto J_{i}(x_{i})
is a cost function associated with the state of the system at time
kT+(i-1)\Delta
. We note that the functions
U_{i}(z_{i})
and
J_{i}(x_{i})
can also include (soft) barrier functions associated with input and state constraints, respectively. Once (
23
) is solved (i.e., a
batch
solution), a sampled-data implementation of the MPC amounts to:

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=Ax(t)+B\Pi_{1}z(t),

(24a)

\displaystyle z_{k}

\displaystyle=z^{*}(x(kT)),

(24b)

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=z_{k},\,\,t\in[kT,(k+1)T),

(24c)

where
\Pi_{1}=[\mathbf{I}_{n_{u}\times n_{u}},\mathbf{0}_{n_{u}\times n_{u}(H-1)}]
is a matrix that extracts the block
z_{1}
from
z^{*}(x(kT))
. Here, at each time instant
kT
, the MPC problem is solved over the predictive interval
kT,kT+\Delta,\ldots,kT+H\Delta
, and the input
z_{1}^{*}(x(kT))=\Pi_{1}z^{*}(x(kT))
is applied over the interval
[kT,(k+1)T)

[
7
]
.

We consider now the ideal case where the MPC problem can be solved continuously in time (i.e., for
T\rightarrow 0^{+}
)
[
17
]
; that is, given the state
x(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the following problem is solved instantaneously over a rolling predictive horizon
[t,t+H\Delta]
:

\displaystyle z^{*}(x(t))\!=\!

\displaystyle\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}H}\\
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}(H\!+\!1)}\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\!\left(U_{i}(z_{i})\!+\!J_{i}(x_{i})\right)\!+\!J_{H+1}(x_{H+1})

\displaystyle\text{s. to:~}x_{i+1}=\bar{A}x_{i}+\bar{B}z_{i},~~i=1,2,\ldots,H\!

\displaystyle~~~~~~~x_{1}=x(t),

(25)

where the input to the MPC problem is now
x(t)
. In this case, one has the closed-loop system:

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)+B\Pi_{1}z^{*}(x(t))\,,~~t\geq 0

(26)

for
x(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
. The system (
26
) corresponds to the RM in our framework.

Note that the optimization problem (
23
) can be reduced to the following form:

\displaystyle z^{*}(x(kT))\!=\!\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}H}}\tilde{U}(z)+\tilde{J}(z,x(kT))

(27)

where we define
\tilde{U}(z):=\sum_{i=1}^{H}U_{i}(z_{i})
,
\tilde{J}(z,x(kT)):=\sum_{i=1}^{H+1}\tilde{J}_{i}(z,x(kT))
and each of the functions
\tilde{J}_{i}(z,x(kT))
is obtained from
J_{i}(x_{i})
by repeatedly substituting the dynamics for the state; see, for example,
[
6
, Section 14.2]
. Assuming that the cost in (
27
) is
\mu
-strongly convex and
\ell
-smooth in
z
uniformly in
x
, one can utilize the following gradient descent map:

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}_{\textsf{mpc}}(x,z):=z-\eta\left(\nabla_{z}\tilde{U}(z)+\nabla_{z}\tilde{J}(z;x)\right)

(28)

where
\eta\in(0,2\mu/\ell^{2})
is the step size. With this choice of
\eta
, the map
z\mapsto\mathsf{G}(x,z)
is contractive with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. Then, a sampled-data and
sub-optimal
implementation of the MPC is:

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=Ax(t)+B\Pi_{1}z(t),

(29a)

\displaystyle z_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{G}_{\textsf{mpc}}^{n}(x(kT),z_{k-1}),

(29b)

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=z_{k},\,\,t\in[kT,(k+1)T).

(29c)

When the MPC is designed so that the closed-loop system (
26
) is strongly infinitesimally contractive, the results of Theorem
III.6
and Corollary
III.7
can be readily applied to the sub-optimal MPC (
29
) to identify requirements on
n
and
T
in order to preserve stability.

Interestingly, an important aspect of Theorem
III.6
in the context of sub-optimal MPC is that stability can be preserved even when
n=1
, so long as
T
is chosen sufficiently small.
This guarantee relies on the contractivity of the reduced model induced by
the MPC controller, which is conceptually different from and not directly comparable to the classical
assumptions adopted in time-distributed or online MPC frameworks; see,
e.g.,
[
19
,
20
]
.
Moreover, existing works typically require
n>1
and therefore do not
address the single-iteration case considered here.

In this subsection, we provide some results that will be leveraged in the subsequent development.

Lemma V.1
(
{\mathsf{G}^{n}}(x,z)
is Lipschitz)

Consider the map
{\mathsf{G}}(x,z)
and let
Assumptions
2
and
3

hold. Then, the map
{\mathsf{G}^{n}}(x,z)
is Lipschitz in both arguments and the
associated (minimal) Lipschitz constants
\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G}^{n})
and

\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G}^{n})
satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G})\frac{1-[\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})]^{n}}{1-\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})},

(31a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq[\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})]^{n},

(31b)

respectively.
\Box

Proof:

In this proof, norm subscripts are omitted whenever the underlying space is clear from the argument. We prove the bounds by induction. The bounds in (
31
) are trivially satisfied for
n=1
. Then, suppose the bounds hold for
n=k
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Then, focusing first on (
31a
), compute for
n=k+1
:

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt\|\mathsf{G}^{n}(x,z)-\mathsf{G}^{n}(\bar{x},z)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{G}(x,\mathsf{G}^{k}(x,z))-\mathsf{G}(\bar{x},\mathsf{G}^{k}(\bar{x},z))\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{G}(x,\mathsf{G}^{k}(x,z))-\mathsf{G}(\bar{x},\mathsf{G}^{k}(x,z))\|

\displaystyle+\|\mathsf{G}(\bar{x},\mathsf{G}^{k}(x,z))-\mathsf{G}(\bar{x},\mathsf{G}^{k}(\bar{x},z))\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G})\|x-\bar{x}\|+\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})\|\mathsf{G}^{k}(x,z)-\mathsf{G}^{k}(\bar{x},z)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G})+\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G}^{k}))\|x-\bar{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G})+\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G})\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}[\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})]^{i}\right)\|x-\bar{x}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G})\sum_{i=0}^{k}[\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})]^{i}\right)\|x-\bar{x}\|,

where we use the inductive argument that
\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G}^{k})\leq\mathsf{Lip}_{x}(\mathsf{G})\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}[\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})]^{i}
.

Consider now (
31b
); following similar steps as before, we have that:

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{G}^{n}(x,z)-\mathsf{G}^{n}(x,\bar{z})\|=

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{G}(x,\mathsf{G}^{k}(x,z))-\mathsf{G}(x,\mathsf{G}^{k}(x,\bar{z}))\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})\|\mathsf{G}^{k}(x,z)-\mathsf{G}^{k}(x,\bar{z})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G}^{k})\|z-\bar{z}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle[\mathsf{Lip}_{z}(\mathsf{G})]^{k+1}\|z-\bar{z}\|,

where the last inequality follows from the inductive assumption. By the definition of minimal Lipschitz constant, we immediately get that the bounds in (
31
) also hold for
n=k+1
. This completes the proof by induction.
∎

Lemma V.3
(Conditions for

)

Define the matrix

c&d\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\hskip-39.83368pt\left(a+d+\sqrt{(a-d)^{2}+4bc}\right)/2<1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow~~

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow~~

\displaystyle(1-a)(1-d)>bc,~2-a-d>0.

VI
Conclusions

This paper examined sampled-data systems, formed by the interconnection of a CT dynamical system and a DT system that updates its output at each interval
T\!>\!0
based on an
n
-fold composition of a given map. We formalized the notion of reduced model associated with such a sampled-data system, which was defined as the limiting system for
T\!\rightarrow\!0^{+}
and
n\!\rightarrow\!+\infty
. Our main results (Theorem
III.6
) showed that, if the reduced model is contractive, then for each
n
there exists a
T(n)
such that the sampled-data system is exponentially stable for each
T<T(n)
; the theorem provides an explicit (possibly conservative) expression for
T(n)
as a function of relevant Lipschitz constants.
This result requires a weaker condition than the exponential‐stability conditions derived via the small‐gain arguments (Theorem
III.4
), which constitute an additional contribution of this work.
Our findings were then applied to MPC frameworks, offering new insights into the stability of suboptimal MPC. Importantly, our results provide conditions for stability even for a single-iteration suboptimal MPC algorithm; to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that such an MPC result is established. Future research will further investigate local versions of our results (expanding on Corollary
III.7
), exploring conditions for local contractivity and exponential stability, with an emphasis on MPC formulations with input and state constraints. Another avenue for future research involves
the design of observers and output-feedback controllers for interconnected CT–DT
systems, extending ideas from related work such as
[
42
]
.
