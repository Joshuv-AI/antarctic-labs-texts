Surgical excision of an infraorbital nerve schwannoma via a transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy: a case report
Authors: Jonathan Andrés Panchi-Lasluisa, Virgen María Acuña-Peña, Sebastián Álvarez-Razo, Juan Marcos Parise-Vasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Schwannoma is a benign, encapsulated, slow-growing tumor that arises from Schwann cells. Approximately 25%–45% of extracranial schwannomas occur in the head and neck region, accounting for 1%–8% of all neoplasms. However, involvement of the infraorbital nerve is exceptionally rare. This report describes the surgical management of an infraorbital nerve schwannoma using a transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy, and documents the clinical, functional and aesthetic outcomes at 12 months. A 32-year-old woman with a history of hypochromic microcytic anemia presented with a progressively enlarging mass in the right infraorbital region that had been present for four years, associated with mild paresthesia in the nasolabial region. The main reason for consultation was aesthetic concern due to facial protrusion. Physical examination revealed a firm, painless subcutaneous mass without local inflammatory signs. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a solid, lobulated lesion measuring approximately 60 mm in maximum diameter in the right infraorbital region. Imaging features suggested an origin from the infraorbital foramen and involvement of the corresponding nerve; there was no evidence of osteolysis. Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) suggested a mesenchymal neoplasm. Surgical excision was performed under general anesthesia via a transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy. The infraorbital nerve was identified and preserved intraoperatively. Histopathological examination confirmed a schwannoma, showing spindle-shaped cells with Verocay bodies. The patient had a favorable clinical course. At the 12-month follow-up, facial symmetry and preserved sensory function were confirmed, as was the absence of recurrence.

Introduction

Schwannoma is a benign, encapsulated, slow-growing neoplasm arising from Schwann cells of the peripheral nerve sheath (1). It may develop in any myelinated nerve, including cranial nerves and spinal nerves, and is characterized by a well-circumscribed morphology and indolent clinical behavior (1, 2). Clinically, it presents as a solitary mass with progressive growth, usually asymptomatic, although it can cause compression of adjacent structures or sensory disturbances depending on the affected nerve pathway (2).

Schwannomas of the head and neck account for approximately 25%–45% of all extracranial schwannomas and represent 1%–8% of head and neck neoplasms (3–5). Although the cephalic region is a common site for extracranial schwannomas, involvement of the infraorbital nerve is exceptional, with only a few cases documented in the literature (6). This rarity has prevented the establishment of precise epidemiological data for this specific location (3, 6). Moreover, the limited number of reports on infraorbital schwannomas has restricted the available evidence regarding the optimal surgical approach and long-term outcomes.

When a schwannoma is located in the infraorbital region, complete surgical excision is the treatment of choice, requiring the selection of an approach that ensures adequate exposure, preservation of neurovascular structures, and acceptable aesthetic outcomes (3, 6). The transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy is an established technique in maxillofacial surgery to access the orbital rim and floor, as well as the anterior wall of the maxilla, with the advantage of avoiding visible cutaneous scars (6, 7). It consists of an incision in the inferior conjunctival fornix, in the preseptal plane, followed by blunt dissection and subperiosteal elevation to the infraorbital rim. The addition of lateral canthotomy optimizes the exposure and retraction of the surgical field (3, 7). Closure with absorbable sutures allows favorable aesthetic results, although complications such as eyelid retraction, epiphora, or conjunctival inclusion cysts may occur (8, 9).

The scarcity of reports on infraorbital schwannomas limits the availability of information on the optimal surgical approach and long-term results. The objective of this case report is to describe the surgical management of an infraorbital nerve schwannoma using a transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy, documenting the clinical, functional and aesthetic outcomes at 12 months of follow-up.

Case report

A 32-year-old woman with a history of hypochromic microcytic anemia presented with a progressively expanding mass in the right infraorbital region of 4 years’ duration. The patient reported mild tingling in the nasolabial region, without an objective sensory deficit at the time of evaluation; however, the main reason for the consultation was aesthetic concern secondary to facial protrusion. Physical examination revealed a firm subcutaneous mass, painless on palpation, without local inflammatory signs (Figure 1).

Preoperative clinical photographs. Frontal and lateral views demonstrating increased volume in the right infraorbital and malar region secondary to the tumoral lesion.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a solid, serpiginous, lobulated lesion measuring approximately 60 mm in maximum diameter and 19 mm in thickness, located in the right infraorbital region. Post-contrast enhancement and a slight impression on the zygomatic arch were observed, with no signs of osteolysis. The lesion appeared to arise from the infraorbital foramen, with a widening of the infraorbital canal and involvement of the corresponding nerve. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was not performed preoperatively, as it was unavailable at the treating institution and the patient was unable to undergo it at a private center for financial reasons. Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) revealed spindle-shaped cells with mild anisonucleosis and intracytoplasmic vacuoles, findings suggesting a mesenchymal neoplasm (Figure 2).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography with three-dimensional reconstruction. A solid, serpiginous, lobulated lesion in the right infraorbital/malar region, with apparent origin at the infraorbital foramen and extension into the adjacent facial soft tissues; no osteolysis is observed.

Under general anesthesia, the conjunctival fornix was infiltrated with 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine. Ocular protection was achieved using temporary tarsorrhaphy and malleable retractors. The transconjunctival incision was made 3 mm from the inferior tarsal border. Blunt dissection was then performed to the infraorbital rim, followed by a periosteal incision and elevation of a subperiosteal flap. To optimize surgical field exposure, a lateral canthotomy was performed through a 1.5 cm horizontal skin incision placed along the natural lateral canthal crease; the incision was carried sequentially through skin, subcutaneous tissue, orbicularis oculi muscle, and lateral canthal tendon, allowing adequate mobilization of the lower eyelid and wider exposure of the infraorbital region. The lesion was then released by blunt dissection and completely resected. After excision, the infraorbital nerve was identified and preserved; a solution containing dexamethasone (8 mg), 2% procaine, and 0.25% caffeine was infiltrated over the nerve for neuroprotection. Closure was performed using 6-0 absorbable polyglactin sutures for the conjunctiva and lateral canthal tendon, and 6-0 nylon sutures for the skin. Compressive dressings were applied (Figure 3).

Histopathological analysis showed the proliferation of spindle-shaped cells arranged in fascicles, with nuclear palisading and the formation of Verocay bodies, findings compatible with schwannoma (Antoni A areas). Immunohistochemical studies such as S-100 and SOX10 were not performed, as they were not available at the institution; therefore, the diagnosis was established based on the characteristic morphological findings on hematoxylin–eosin staining.

Postoperative follow-up was performed at 8 days and at 1, 3 and 12 months. At each visit, sensory function within the infraorbital nerve distribution was objectively assessed by pinprick mapping using a sterile 30G needle to identify any area of hypoesthesia or sensory deficit; periocular function was evaluated by asking the patient to perform active eyelid movements, including gentle and forceful eye closure, to confirm lower-eyelid mobility and integrity. Throughout this period, the patient showed a favorable clinical course, characterized by progressive restoration of facial symmetry, adequate healing of the conjunctival and cutaneous planes, and no detectable hypoesthesia or sensory deficit in the territory of the infraorbital nerve at any time, including the 12-month assessment. The patient reported sustained satisfaction with the aesthetic and functional outcomes achieved (Figure 4). Recurrence was not documented at the end of follow-up.

Discussion

The present case documents the successful surgical excision of an infraorbital nerve schwannoma in a woman using a transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy. Complete resection of the lesion, with preservation of the infraorbital nerve and absence of recurrence at 12 months of follow-up, supports the feasibility of this technique for the management of tumors of the infraorbital region. The choice of a transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy for the excision of the schwannoma was based on both technical and aesthetic considerations. Compared to the transfacial or subciliary routes, this approach provides optimal visualization of the surgical field through the inferior conjunctival fornix, allowing for a precise dissection that minimizes the risk of infraorbital nerve injury, while the addition of a lateral canthotomy expands access to the lateral orbital floor and malar region without an external incision. In addition, it avoids extensive cutaneous incisions in visible areas, which is particularly relevant in the facial region, where aesthetic result is a priority for the patient.

The available evidence, although of low certainty, suggests that the transconjunctival approach offers advantages over the subciliary approach, particularly by reducing the risk of ectropion and achieving superior aesthetic results (10–12). Similarly, this approach is associated with a lower incidence of intraoperative and postoperative complications, including diplopia, transient paresthesia, ecchymosis, scleral show, and lower eyelid lacerations. However, a possible increase in the risk of entropion has been reported, although the certainty of this evidence is also low (10–12).

Lateral canthotomy was initially described in 1980 as part of a unified transconjunctival approach for the treatment of zygomatico-orbital fractures. The original technique consisted of a 3 to 5-mm incision in the natural crease of the lateral canthus, with division of the upper and inferior limbs of the lateral canthal tendon (13). This method represented an advance by allowing simultaneous access to the orbital floor and lateral orbital wall without additional eyelid or eyebrow incisions. However, retrospective studies have reported a 34.8% incidence of aesthetic complications with this combined technique, compared with 0.3% in patients treated exclusively through a transconjunctival approach (14). Subsequent technical modifications have proposed releasing only the inferior limb of the tendon, preserving part of the tarsal plate and avoiding formal reattachment of the lateral canthus. This approach maintains adequate surgical exposure, facilitates anatomical closure, and significantly reduces the risk of eyelid malposition, entropion, ectropion, and lateral canthal angle deformities (10, 11, 13–15). However, most of the available evidence on the complications and aesthetic outcomes of the transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy derives from orbital trauma and fracture repair rather than from tumor surgery (10, 11, 13–15). The applicability of these complication estimates to oncologic resections—which may involve larger lesions, wider dissection, and longer operative fields—therefore remains uncertain, and the present single case does not permit independent estimation of these risks in the tumor setting.

The optimal surgical route for an infraorbital nerve schwannoma is dictated by the size, depth, and anatomical extent of the lesion, and the cases summarized in Table 1 illustrate a clear evolution and stratification of the approaches. Historically, management has shifted from extensive transfacial procedures toward more conservative scar-sparing techniques. Extensive lesions invading the maxillary sinus, pterygopalatine fossa, or infratemporal fossa have generally required wide transfacial exposure, such as the Weber–Fergusson incision with osteoplastic maxillotomy (16); when intracranial extension was present, an endoscopic endonasal corridor has been used (17). Conversely, superficial, well-encapsulated lesions confined to the infraorbital or malar soft tissues have been managed through conservative routes that avoid a visible facial scar, including intraoral sublabial or vestibular incisions (18, 19) and incisions placed within natural skin creases (5). For lesions centered on the orbital floor and the inferior orbital rim, periorbital approaches—such as the subciliary approach (1, 20) and transconjunctival inferior orbitotomy (2)—have provided adequate access. Regardless of route, complete or near-complete excision has been consistently associated with an excellent prognosis and absence of recurrence during the available follow-up; less invasive approaches, however, appear to offer superior cosmetic outcomes by avoiding visible facial scars while maintaining adequate exposure in appropriately selected lesions (21). More broadly, the management of rare cranial-nerve schwannomas consistently centers on balancing complete tumor removal against preservation of nerve function, a principle illustrated by systematic reviews of schwannomas at other rare sites such as the glossopharyngeal nerve (22). In the present case, the tumor affected the inferior orbital rim, the orbital floor, and the anterior maxillary wall, spreading to the malar region. This is an intermediate extent between purely superficial tumors and deeply invasive ones; the transconjunctival approach reinforced by lateral canthotomy provided the wide exposure required to address this distribution while avoiding an external cutaneous scar, thus combining the access of the periorbital routes with the cosmetic advantage of conservative approaches. The use of this combined approach for an infraorbital nerve schwannoma has only been reported rarely. Together with the favorable functional outcome documented herein, this constitutes the main contribution and clinical relevance of this report.

Infraorbital nerve schwannoma remains exceptionally rare: a consolidated review of the last two decades identified approximately 19 reported cases (23), to which the present case adds another example (Table 1). In all of these reports, complete or near-complete excision was consistently associated with the absence of recurrence during the available follow-up, irrespective of the surgical route.

The postoperative sensory outcome in previously reported cases appears to depend largely on the relationship of the tumor to the infraorbital nerve and the dissection strategy adopted. In several reports, the nerve was found to be intimately involved by or to be the origin of the tumor and was deliberately sacrificed, with consequent persistent hypoesthesia or anesthesia in the infraorbital territory (16, 28, 31, 32). In the case reported by Righini et al., complete sensory loss in the maxillary division was an anticipated and accepted consequence of resecting a nerve whose trunk had been destroyed by the tumor (31). By contrast, sensory function was frequently retained or recovered during follow-up when the lesion could be separated from the nerve by intracapsular enucleation or microsurgical preservation of the nerve fascicles, which is typically achieved in more superficial or well-encapsulated tumors (1, 5, 18, 23). Transient hypoesthesia that improved gradually was also described (17, 29). In the present case, the lesion was suitable for nerve-sparing dissection and objective pinprick mapping demonstrated no sensory deficit in the infraorbital distribution in any follow-up assessment, an outcome consistent with the more favorable results reported when the nerve can be anatomically preserved.

Intraoperative infiltration of dexamethasone, procaine, and caffeine on the preserved nerve was used as an adjunctive neuroprotective measure to minimize neural injury during tumor dissection (33). Functional recovery after peripheral nerve manipulation depends not only on meticulous surgical techniques, but also on strategies aimed at reducing inflammation, limiting secondary neuronal damage, and promoting axonal regeneration. Although no standardized intraoperative neuroprotective protocol has been established for peripheral nerve surgery, several pharmacological agents have been proposed as complementary measures (33). Dexamethasone exerts anti-inflammatory and anti-edematous effects that may reduce perineural inflammation and secondary compression of nerve fibers (34). Procaine, in addition to its local anesthetic action, has been reported to stabilize neuronal membranes and attenuate inflammatory responses associated with surgical manipulation (35). Caffeine has shown neuroprotective effects in experimental models through modulation of adenosine receptors, reduction of oxidative stress, and preservation of neuronal function (36). Consequently, this combination was intended to provide a favorable microenvironment for nerve preservation during surgery; however, we emphasize that this was an adjunctive, non-standardized measure, that the supporting evidence, particularly for caffeine, derives largely from experimental and non-surgical settings, and that its specific contribution to the favorable outcome in this case cannot be established.

Among the limitations of this case is the lack of access to advanced preoperative and postoperative imaging. Although contrast-enhanced computed tomography provided sufficient information for surgical planning, the absence of preoperative magnetic resonance imaging prevented a more precise characterization of the lesion, a more detailed assessment of its relationship with the infraorbital nerve, and a better visualization of the extent of soft-tissue involvement. Similarly, immunohistochemical confirmation could not be obtained, as these studies were unavailable at the institution; consequently, the diagnosis was based on the characteristic histopathological findings, which were considered sufficient to establish the diagnosis of schwannoma. The inability to perform follow-up computed tomography precluded an early assessment of the extent of resection and the prompt detection of potential alterations in adjacent anatomical structures. Nevertheless, clinical follow-up at 12 months showed no signs of recurrence. A major strength of this case is the extended 12-month clinical follow-up, which allowed us to document the absence of recurrence and confirm functional and aesthetic preservation. In addition, the detailed description of the surgical technique, including intraoperative neuroprotective measures, provides reproducible information for similar cases.

From a clinical perspective, this case reinforces the importance of considering schwannoma in the differential diagnosis of slow-growing infraorbital masses, particularly when they are associated with paresthesia in the corresponding nerve territory. The transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy may represent a valid alternative when a broad exposure of the orbital rim and floor is required while maintaining favorable aesthetic outcomes. However, multicenter studies or systematic reviews comparing different surgical approaches for infraorbital schwannomas are needed, with an emphasis on nerve preservation rates, complications, and long-term results, to strengthen evidence in this field.

Conclusions

Infraorbital nerve schwannoma is an exceptionally rare neoplasm whose surgical management should ensure complete resection while preserving neurovascular structures and achieving acceptable aesthetic outcomes. The present case shows that the transconjunctival approach with lateral canthotomy may represent an effective and safe alternative for this indication, allowing adequate exposure of the surgical field, complete excision of the lesion, and functional preservation of the infraorbital nerve, with satisfactory aesthetic outcomes and no recurrence at 12 months of follow-up. However, the choice of approach should be individualized according to the extent of the tumor, the anatomical characteristics of the patient, and the aesthetic expectations. Factors such as age and physiological changes in periorbital tissues can influence susceptibility to complications such as ectropion or entropion and should therefore be considered during surgical planning.
