Diagnostic and therapeutic gaps in recurrent stroke management: real-world insights from a Ghanaian hospital-based registry
Authors: Priscilla Abrafi Opare-Addo, Fred Stephen Sarfo, Minas Aikins, Enock Asante, Maxwell Nyanaah, Nana Osei Asibey Derkyi, Jessey Mahama Holu, Gordon Manu Amponsah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecurrent strokes account for up to 1 out of 4 strokes in Low-and-Middle-Income-Countries (LMICs), culminating in worsening of outcomes. International guidelines recommend subtype-specific etiological workup to help tailor strategies for prevention of recurrence after an index stroke. However, huge gaps in secondary stroke prevention persist in LMICs.ObjectivesWe aimed to assess the extent of diagnostic evaluation (for risk-factor and underlying etiological identification) and treatment approaches among recurrent stroke patients in a Ghanaian tertiary hospital.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study among recurrent stroke cases admitted to Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital between 2023 and 2025. The data was analyzed using SPSS Version 28.ResultsRecurrent strokes accounted for 17.8% of all stroke admissions over 2 years, with ischemic strokes comprising 76.2% of these recurrent events. Hypertension was the most dominant modifiable risk factor among both ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes, 97.0% and 92.1% respectively, (p = 0.297). Only a minority of ischemic stroke patients (12.6%) had sufficient diagnostic evaluation to allow definitive etiological classification according to the TOAST framework. Only 7.9% of hemorrhagic strokes underwent Computed tomography angiography (CTA)/ Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) to evaluate for structural causes. Most ischemic stroke patients were prescribed a statin (93.7%) and an antiplatelet, while anticoagulant therapy was used only in a minority of patients (3.91%). Carotid endarterectomy or carotid stenting was not performed for any of the ischemic stroke patients.ConclusionOur findings highlight significant gaps in recurrent stroke evaluation and management in the Ghanaian setting. Standardized algorithms for secondary stroke prevention and treatment are urgently needed to mitigate recurrence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05013-1.

Introduction

Up to 25% of stroke survivors in Low-Middle-Income-Countries (LMICs) suffer recurrence within the first 5 years of an index stroke [1, 2]. Stroke recurrence is frequently associated with dismal functional outcomes, longer hospital stays, greater healthcare costs, and higher case fatality rates [3]. This has serious global health and economic implications [4]. Structured systems for prevention are required to avert such dire repercussions.

The specific strategies for secondary prevention after stroke heavily depend on the particular stroke subtype. International guidelines, therefore, recommend an extensive diagnostic workup after index stroke, where possible, to define associated risk and etiological factors [5, 6]. This approach informs the tailoring of treatments to effectively target and interrupt the underlying pathophysiological mechanism. However, despite holding the highest population of stroke survivors globally, huge gaps in secondary stroke prevention persist in LMICs [7, 8]. Considering that recurrent strokes are highly preventable if appropriately mitigated, understanding the routine practices in real-world LMIC settings could inform future initiatives to improve and standardize secondary stroke prevention [2, 5].

This study sought to assess the extent of diagnostic evaluation (for risk-factor and underlying etiological identification) and treatment approaches to mitigation among recurrent stroke patients in a Ghanaian tertiary hospital.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study among recurrent stroke cases admitted to Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital.

Study settings

This study was conducted at the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH), which is the second-largest tertiary referral institution in Ghana, serving an estimated population of over 12 million and receiving referrals from 10 out of 16 administrative regions in Ghana. KATH is located in Kumasi, the capital city of the Ashanti region.

Study population

The cases included patients with recurrent strokes, aged 18 years and above. Cases were recruited from September 2023 to September 2025.

Data collection

Demographic variables included age and sex, type of domicile, and National Health Insurance (NHIS) status, while clinical features included frequency of stroke recurrence, stroke severity, presenting symptoms, outcome of admission, and complications. Stroke severity was assessed with the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score [9].

Stroke risk factors included hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, cigarette smoking, alcohol use, and the number of previous strokes.

Hypertension was based on a self-reported history of hypertension, or use of antihypertensive before stroke onset, or with confirmed medical records, or a BP cut-off of at least 140/90 mm Hg.

Diabetes mellitus was defined based on a history of diabetes mellitus, use of antidiabetic medications, confirmed medical records, or an HbA1c > 6.5% [10].

Dyslipidaemia was defined as fasting total cholesterol ≥ 5.2 mmol/L, HDL-C (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol) ≤ 1.03 mmol/L, triglyceride ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, or LDL-C (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol) ≥ 3.4 mmol/L or use of lipid-lowering drug prior to stroke onset [11].

Lacunar stroke was defined by the presence of a lacunar syndrome or subcortical stroke measuring < 1.5 cm in diameter on CT or MRI [12].

Etiological classification for ischemic stroke was based on the TOAST Classification (Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) [13]. Etiologies included cardio-embolic, large-artery atherosclerosis (LAA), small vessel disease, ischemic stroke of other determined etiology and stroke of undetermined pathogenesis [13].

Large Artery Atherosclerosis required clinical findings suggestive of cerebral cortical impairment or brain stem or cerebellar dysfunction, infarct areas > 1.5 cm in diameter on CT or MRI and supportive evidence by duplex imaging or arteriography of a stenosis of greater than 50% of an appropriate intracranial or extracranial artery. Additionally at least an ECG and TTE/TEE should have excluded potential sources of cardiogenicembolism.

Small Vessel Disease required one of the traditional clinical lacunar syndromes and no evidence of cerebral cortical dysfunction, infarct areas were < 1.5 cm in diameter on CT or MRI. Additionally at least evidence of the absence of cardiac sources of embolism and large extracranial arteries stenosis of < 50% in an ipsilateral artery.

Cardioembolic strokes required at least one cardiac source for an embolus, from diagnostic studies including at least and ECG and/ or TTE/TEE clinical and brain imaging findings are similar to those described for LAA and the absence of significant (≥ 50%) stenosis or ulcerated plaques in the proximal large-artery supply on angiogram or ultrasonogram.

Patients with stroke of other determined pathogenesis had rare causes of stroke, including nonatherosclerotic vasculopathies, hypercoagulable states, or hematologic disorders.

Stroke of undetermined pathogenesis was defined when any of the following criteria were met: (a) no cause was found despite an extensive evaluation; (b) a most likely cause could not be determined because > 1 plausible cause was found; or (c) undetermined pathogenesis attributable to incomplete evaluation.

For ICH, etiological classification was based on SMASH U, comprising Structural, Medication, Amyloid Angiopathy, Systemic diseases, Hypertension, and Undetermined [14].

Confirmed etiologies refer to cases with etiological classification based on adequate evidence from diagnostic evaluation, while presumed etiologies refer to cases with etiological classification assigned solely based on infarct or hemorrhage location, clinical context and vascular risk factors without evidence from vascular imaging, cardiac investigations, or structural imaging. All patients with presumed etiologies were classified as undetermined pathogenesis attributable to incomplete evaluation.

Stroke treatment instituted during the current admission for the recurrent stroke including antihypertensives, antiplatelets, statins, oral hypoglycaemics, and anticoagulants, and interventions such as carotid endarterectomy and stenting, were retrieved from the patient’s hospital records. Secondary preventive medications already in use at the time of stroke recurrence were self-reported. Therefore, findings should be interpreted bearing the risk of recall bias in mind.

Recurrent stroke was defined as a new focal neurological deficit occurring after an index stroke with imaging evidence of infarction consistent with symptoms, OR lasting > 24 h without imaging evidence, AND not attributable to a non-ischemic etiology; AND acute extravasation of blood into the brain parenchyma, judged to be non-traumatic and identified as the cause of new neurologic symptoms [15].

Untyped stroke refers specifically to cases where a stroke diagnosis was made based on clinical diagnosis such as SUDDEN onset of focal neurological deficit but was not confirmed by neuroimaging (CT scan or MRI) [16].

Index stroke events were verified using a hierarchical approach. For patients previously managed at our institution, index stroke characteristics were confirmed through review of hospital medical records and neuroimaging reports. For patients treated elsewhere, verification was based on available neuroimaging reports or imaging films provided by the patient. When imaging documentation was unavailable, information was obtained from patient or caregiver report, including details of the clinical event and prior hospitalization. Medication history, particularly long-term antiplatelet use, was also reviewed as supportive evidence of prior ischemic stroke.

Data analysis

The data extracted from case report forms was entered into a predesigned database using Microsoft Excel 2021. The data was subsequently cleaned and double-checked for errors and then exported to SPSS Version 28 for further validation and analysis.

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Mean with standard deviation will be used for numerical variables with a normal distribution, while median with interquartile range will be used for those that were skewed. To compare variables across stroke types, the ANOVA-test or Kruskal Wallis test was used for normally or non-normally distributed continuous variables, respectively. Chi-square/Fisher’s Exact tests were used for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the predictors of lack of etiological evaluation. P-values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance for all statistical analyses.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of recurrent stroke patients

Recurrent strokes accounted for 17.8% (302 out of 1701) of all stroke admissions over the 2 years, with ischemic strokes comprising 76.2% of these recurrent events. The mean age of participants was 63.20 ± 13.26 years, and 52.3% were males. Most patients were enrolled on the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) (68.9%). The majority were semi-urban (64.2%) and urban dwellers (25.5%). The median NIHSS score at presentation for recurrent ischemic stroke was 15 (IQR 11), compared to 18 (IQR 13.5) for recurrent hemorrhagic strokes (p = 0.027). Among patients with recurrent ischemic strokes, most of the index events were also ischemic (78.7%). In contrast, only 44.4% of patients with recurrent hemorrhagic strokes had an index hemorrhagic event (p < 0.001). The median length of hospital stay was 9 days (IQR 8). Aspiration pneumonia was the most common in-hospital complication, occurring in 43.9% of patients with ischemic, 46.0% of patients with hemorrhagic, and 66.7% of patients with recurrent untyped strokes (p = 0.491). Generally, in-hospital case fatality among patients with recurrent stroke was 25.8%; however, this was highest among patients with untyped recurrent events (57.1%), p = 0.000 (Table 1).

Risk profile of recurrent stroke patients

Among the recurrent stroke patients, 90.1% were admitted with the first episode of recurrence, while 8.9% had experienced two recurrent strokes, and the rest had experienced at least two. Hypertension was the most dominant modifiable risk factor among both ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes, 97.0% and 92.1%, respectively (p = 0.297). Diabetes was identified as a modifiable vascular risk factor in the majority of untyped recurrent stroke (77.8%), while dyslipidemia was mainly identified among patients with recurrent ischemic stroke (p < 0.001).

Atrial fibrillation was diagnosed in 5.7% of patients with ischemic strokes (p = 0.003). Alcohol use was reported in 21.9%, while cigarette smoking (2.7%) and recreational drug use (0.3%) were uncommon. Compared to recurrent hemorrhagic strokes (69.8%), the majority of recurrent ischemic (86.5%) and untyped stroke patients (66.7%) engaged in < 150 min of low-moderate intensity/ week physical activity per week (p = 0.013) (Table 2).

Gaps in diagnostic workup

The extent of risk and etiologic diagnostic evaluation varied substantially across stroke types. Among patients with recurrent ischemic strokes, 94.8% had a lipid profile, while HBA1c was measured in 69.1% (p = 0.000). For ischemic non-lacunar strokes, only 28.9% had 12-Lead ECG, 16.4% had transthoracic echocardiograms, 14.1% underwent carotid doppler, 1.96% had Computerized tomographic (CT) or Magnetic resonance (MR) angiography. No participant had 24-72-hour Holter ECG, transesophageal echocardiography, nor implantable loop recorder studies performed (Supplementary Tables S1). Only 7.9% of hemorrhagic strokes underwent Computed tomography angiography (CTA)/ Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was not performed for any hemorrhagic stroke patients. Gradient echo sequences (GRE)/ Susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI) sequence MRI were not done for any of the hemorrhagic strokes to evaluate for Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy (CAA) (Table 3).

In the bivariate analysis, NIHSS score at the time of admission (OR 1.53 (1.36–1.71), p < 0.001), being aphasic (OR 1.97 (1.19–3.26), p = 0.008), presence of aspiration pneumonia (OR 2.80 (1.79–4.61), p < 0.001), and being hypertensive (OR 1.96 (1.21–3.18) p = 0.006) were predictive of lack of etiological evaluation, as shown in Supplementary Table 4. However, in multivariable analysis only NIHSS score remained as an independent predictor of lack of etiological evaluation aOR 1.57 (1.38–1.78), p < 0.001).

Etiological designation of recurrent strokes based on level of diagnostic certainty

As shown in Fig. 1, among patients with recurrent ischemic stroke, 128 (55.7%) were classified as non-lacunar infarcts, while 102(44.3%) were lacunar infarcts based on neuroimaging characteristics. Only a minority of ischemic stroke patients (12.6%) had sufficient diagnostic evaluation to allow definitive etiological classification according to the TOAST framework. Of the 128 non-lacunar ischemic strokes, only 15 (11.7%) were confirmed to be cardioembolic stroke, while (7.8%) had confirmed large artery atherosclerosis. The remaining 103 (80.5%) fell under stroke of undetermined etiology due to incomplete evaluation. Few lacunar strokes 3 (2.9%) were confirmed to be secondary to small vessel disease, 98 were classified as undetermined due to the absence of comprehensive vascular and cardiac evaluation to completely ruled out alternative etiologies. One case of lacunar stroke was confirmed to be secondary to vertebral dissection.

Secondary preventive medications in use at the time of stroke recurrence

As shown in Supplementary Table 3, the majority of patients were already receiving antihypertensive therapy at the time of recurrent stroke onset, including (55.6%) calcium channel blockers (CCBs), 16.2% on angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), 5.6% on thiazides, and 3% on beta blockers. Among patients with prior ischemic stroke, only 3.3% of were on statin therapy, 5.1% were on aspirin, and 0.9% were on dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) comprising aspirin and clopidogrel. Anticoagulant use was observed in only 3.3% of patients with prior ischemic stroke. None of the patients with prior ischemic stroke had undergone Carotid endarterectomy or Carotid stenting following the index ischemic event.

Secondary prevention treatments and interventions during recurrent stroke

The majority of recurrent stroke patients received antihypertensive treatments, including (90.7%) calcium channel blockers (CCBs), and 72.9% on angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB) during the recurrent stroke admission. Among ischemic stroke, 93.9% were prescribed a statin during admission, 81.30% received aspirin, and 14.8% were on clopidogrel. 10.9% of ischemic stroke patients received dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT). Anticoagulant therapy was used in 3.9% of ischemic strokes. None of the patients underwent Carotid endarterectomy or Carotid stenting (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study has evaluated critical diagnostic and treatment gaps in recurrent stroke management, in a real-life setting in Ghana. On a backdrop of an existing National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), our findings provide very important insights for LMICs.

Among our cohort of stroke patients, we found that almost a fifth of admitted stroke cases were recurrent strokes, which is much higher than what was reported in the SIREN study in 2022 [17]. This highlights a rising burden of recurrent strokes in Ghana, contrary to the trend in High-Income Countries (HICs) like the United Kingdom, where recurrent strokes were found to be on a decline from 1995 to 2005 and stabilization afterwards till 2018 due to intensification of secondary prevention measures [18]. Ischemic strokes accounted for 76.2% of the recurrent events in our study, which is consistent with the patterns in other SSA countries [7, 17, 19, 20]. We observed a high prevalence of modifiable vascular risk factors, particularly hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, consistent with other studies in LMICs [2, 21]. These findings suggest that these risk factors may be the major drivers of stroke recurrence in our setting, underscoring an urgent need to strengthen approaches to mitigation.

Additionally, recurrent strokes were associated with a longer length of hospital stay (median of 9 days (IQR 8) compared to that reported to the index stroke in our setting (median of 6 days (IQR 5) [22]. Aspiration pneumonia remained the predominant in-hospital complication, occurring in almost half of patients [22]. In-hospital case fatality among patients with recurrent stroke was 25.8%, which is comparatively lower than rates reported in other LMICs [21, 23]. This may reflect incremental improvements in stroke services in our setting over recent years. However, the markedly high Case Fatality (57.1%) observed in patients with untyped recurrent strokes highlights persistent challenges in their management. These challenges extend beyond the lack of neuroimaging confirmation, which often precludes definitive stroke classification and hence limited specific therapy. Notably, this group also had the highest median NIHSS and also had the greatest proportion of (66.67%) aspiration pneumonias, further contributing to their elevated mortality.

Recurrent strokes constitute a very high-risk population, requiring careful evaluation to inform strategies for prevention [24]. A key finding that stood out in our study was the limited adherence to recommended etiological and risk evaluation [5, 25] Although basic laboratory tests such as lipid profiles and HbA1c were performed, the workup was remarkably sparse for cardioembolic etiologies. Though readily available, only a minority received 12- lead ECGs and transthoracic echocardiograms. Also, in cases where ECGs were negative, holter monitoring and implantable cardiac loop monitors were never utilized. Transesophageal echocardiograms were not done in any of the patients. Advanced neuromaging modalities such as CT/ MRI angiograms were sparingly used, and DSA were nonexistent. These gaps underscore substantial constraints in diagnostic capacity, including financial limitations, clinician inertia, absence of structured protocols and systems. Of note is the fact that NIHSS score at admission emerged as an independent predictor of lack of etiological evaluation. This highlights additional constraints associated with the management of severe stroke in resource limited settings.

We also observed limited etiological classification in our cohort of recurrent strokes, which underscores very crucial diagnostic gaps that often compel clinicians to rely heavily on clinical judgement rather than objective evidence. In particular, cardiac evaluation was very limited, with very few patients undergoing echo cardiography, olter monitoring or other advanced cardiac assessments. This raises concerns about the potential underdiagnosis of cardioembolic strokes in our setting [26]. Consequently, the unexpectedly low prevalence of atrial fibrillation in our population, is most likely attributable to detection bias arising from incomplete diagnostic evaluation rather than a epidemiologic profile. Similarly, vascular imaging was sparingly performed to assess for intracranial and extracranial large vessel atherosclerosis. Given the limited depth of diagnostic evaluation, it is difficult to meaningfully apply the concepts of cryptogenic or Embolic Strokes of Undetermined Source (ESUS) in our setting. Both diagnoses require a thorough minimum workup, including extended cardiac monitoring and vascular evaluation [5]. Ideally individuals with inadequate diagnostic evaluation should be classified as undetermined due to incomplete diagnostic investigation, instead of being presumptively assigned an etiology. This strategy may prompt other clinicians to perform additional diagnostic evaluation during subsequent clinical encounters. All non-lobar bleeds in hypertensive patients were designated as hypertensive bleeds. The majority of Lobar hemorrhages were attributed to CAA, without any GRE/SWI sequence MRI. These findings highlight the urgent need for improved diagnostic infrastructure to enable accurate classification, guide appropriate interventions and ultimately improve outcomes.

Additionally, we observed that the rates of secondary prevention medication use at the time of stroke recurrence were alarmingly low, which raises concern. However, this finding must be interpreted with caution, as the information on medication use at the time of stroke recurrence was based on patient self-report, as most index strokes were managed at other healthcare facilities making verification through prescription records, pharmacy refill or hospital documentation infeasible for majority of patients. There was also a clear mismatch between stroke risk factors, etiologies, and the secondary prevention strategies implemented for patients with recurrent strokes, prior to discharge [18, 27]. Anticoagulant use among our cohort was strikingly low, including even atrial fibrillation patients with recurrent stroke. Similarly no one underwent carotid endarterectomy or carotid stenting. This highlights important challenges in accessing evident based interventions. Ghana’s existing National Health Insurance Scheme offers an opportunity to strengthen diagnostic infrastructure and subsidize the cost of stroke evaluation and treatment.

Limitations

This study employed a cross-sectional design, which limits its ability to draw causal inferences. It, however, has some limitations. It was also a single-center study, which limits its external validity. Use of self-reported medication and hierarchical approach for verifying index strokes increase the risk of recall bias and potential misclassification.

Future directions

Pragmatic initiatives incorporating structured risk/ etiological profiling and standardized therapeutic protocols, driven by strong national policies, are required for improved recurrent stroke prevention in Ghana and other resource constrained setting. Substantial effort is required to strengthen stroke care services, expand diagnostic capacity, and build the human resources to adequately mitigate recurrent stroke risk in LMICs.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight significant gaps in recurrent stroke evaluation and management in Ghana. Standardized prevention models are urgently needed to overcome existing bottlenecks.
