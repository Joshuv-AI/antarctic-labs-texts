KGLGANSynergy: knowledge graph-based local and global attention network for drug synergy prediction
Authors: Chaokun Yan, Menglei Chu, Ge Zhang, Caili Fang, Nian Liu, Huimin Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug combination therapy offers an effective strategy for treating complex diseases by leveraging synergistic multi-target effects. However, conventional experimental approaches for identifying synergistic drug pairs are costly and inefficient. Graph representation learning has shown strong potential for modeling complex biomolecular interactions and is widely applied to drug synergy prediction. Despite this progress, existing knowledge graph–based methods primarily focus on node-level relational features while overlooking edge attributes and global semantic context, limiting predictive capability.MethodsWe propose KGLGANSynergy, a knowledge graph–based deep learning framework for drug synergy prediction. The model integrates a Local Graph Attention Network (LGAT) to learn fine-grained neighborhood features as local features, and a Global Graph Attention Network (GGAT) to capture global structural information in the drug knowledge graph as global features. These learned features are fused through a Mutual Cross Attention (MCA) mechanism to obtain representations of drugs and cell lines. The final synergy score is computed by combining the feature vectors of the two drugs and the cell line and applying a sigmoid function to generate the predicted probability.ResultsExperimental results on two benchmark datasets, DrugCombDB and Oncology-Screen, demonstrate that KGLGANSynergy achieves AUPR scores of 0.9360 and 0.9472, respectively, outperforming existing baselines. Case studies further validate the model’s ability to accurately identify novel synergistic drug combinations.ConclusionThis study provides a more accurate computational tool for predicting drug synergy and introduces a multi-level graph representation learning paradigm, offering new perspectives for biomedical data modeling and showing considerable potential for clinical applications.

Introduction

Drug combinations are a more effective strategy for treating complex diseases by disrupting disease progression through the synergistic action of multiple drugs targeting distinct molecular pathways [1]. For cancer therapy, monotherapy is often limited by issues such as drug resistance and suboptimal efficacy [2]. Mounting evidence suggests that multi-drug combination therapies can substantially improve therapeutic outcomes, mitigate resistance, and reduce toxicity through synergistic interactions, offering significant advantages in oncology [3, 4]. However, current clinical strategies for developing combination therapies are typically time-consuming, labor-intensive, and costly. While high-throughput screening (HTS) [5] enables the generation of large-scale experimental data at a relatively lower cost and within a shorter time frame, it remains impractical to exhaustively evaluate the entire drug combination space. Furthermore, cancer cell lines commonly used in HTS may not accurately reflect the biological complexity of in vivo tumor environments [6, 7]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for the development of efficient and cost-effective computational approaches to facilitate large-scale drug combination screening.

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, machine learning (ML) has been widely employed to tackle the challenges associated with drug combination prediction. Common ML approaches include forest-based models, matrix factorization techniques, and Bayesian frameworks [8]. Among these, forest-based models have been extensively explored. For example, Li et al. proposed a random forest–based method that integrates multi-source data to predict drug synergy [9]. Meng et al. extracted features from protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks using network embeddings, and combined drug and cancer cell line information to predict synergy using XGBoost [10]. More recently, these approaches have been extended to incorporate deep forest models for enhanced performance in synergy prediction [11–13]. Moreover, matrix factorization–based methods have also demonstrated promising results. Nafshi and Lezon employed probabilistic matrix factorization to infer the efficacy of untested drug combinations using only partially observed outcome data, applying matrix decomposition and optimization techniques [14]. Pinoli et al. proposed a non-negative matrix tri-factorization framework that integrates drug synergy measurements with cell line genomic profiles to predict context-specific synergy scores and drug–cell line interactions [15]. For Bayesian methods, Li et al. utilized a Bayesian network to integrate multidimensional drug features and applied probabilistic ensemble learning to predict combination outcomes [16]. Rønneberg et al. adopted a Bayesian framework based on Gaussian processes to model the dose–response behavior of drug pairs, explicitly capturing uncertainty and noise in experimental measurements to improve the prediction of synergistic and antagonistic interactions [17, 18]. While these machine learning approaches offer valuable insights, they often depend heavily on manual feature engineering and struggle to model complex nonlinear relationships. These limitations have spurred increasing interest in the development of deep learning–based methods for drug synergy prediction.

Recently, deep learning and large language model (LLM)–based approaches have shown promising performance in drug combination prediction, contributing significantly to the advancement of computational drug development. For example, Preuer et al. introduced DeepSynergy [7], a model that integrates chemical features of drugs and genomic features of cell lines, represents them as one-dimensional vectors, and inputs them into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to predict synergistic effects. This work marked a key breakthrough in applying deep learning to predict anticancer drug synergy. Beyond one-dimensional feature inputs, drug structural information and biological knowledge have also been incorporated into deep learning models [19]. Drug molecules are typically represented as graph structures, where nodes correspond to atoms and edges represent chemical bonds. Graph neural networks (GNNs) have been used to extract structural representations from molecular graphs [20–24]. Additionally, drug synergy prediction has been improved by constructing graphs based on protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks or by integrating relationships among drugs, proteins, and cell lines. GNNs are then applied to learn comprehensive feature representations of both drugs and cell lines within these heterogeneous graphs [25–29]. In parallel, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated exceptional capabilities in natural language understanding and generation across a wide range of domains [30]. Xu et al. utilized a BERT model fine-tuned with SimCSE (Simple Contrastive Learning of Sentence Embeddings) to encode SMILES strings of drugs, generating more accurate molecular embeddings and thereby improving synergy prediction performance [31]. Li et al. reformulated few-shot drug synergy prediction as a natural language processing (NLP) task and leveraged pretrained LLMs to extract prior knowledge from unstructured text corpora. This enabled effective prediction of drug synergy in rare tissue types, where structured data and curated features are often limited [32].

Among the aforementioned deep learning methods, graph representation learning–based methods have been widely adopted due to their strong ability to model topological structures and capture complex biological relationships [33]. For instance, Yang et al. proposed GraphSynergy [28], which integrates the topological information of drug targets and cell line–associated proteins in protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks using graph convolutional networks (GCNs) combined with an attention mechanism. However, this model does not incorporate drug molecular structures or gene expression data. To address this limitation, Wang et al. introduced DeepDDS [20], which employs graph neural networks (GNNs) to learn structural features from molecular graphs of drugs and combines them with gene expression profiles of cell lines to predict drug synergy. Nonetheless, DeepDDS does not consider the biological correlations between drugs and cell lines, such as those involving proteins or tissues. Zhang et al. proposed KGANSynergy [29], which constructs a knowledge graph to represent relationships among drugs, cell lines, proteins, and tissues, and utilizes graph attention networks (GATs) to learn entity embeddings. However, this model relies on random initialization of entity features, which may lead to drug and cell line representations that deviate from their true biological properties. To mitigate this issue, Cheng et al. proposed HANSynergy [26], which initializes drug features using pretrained BERT embeddings of SMILES strings and cell line features using gene expression data. It employs a heterogeneous graph attention network to aggregate neighborhood information. Nevertheless, this approach does not incorporate drug–drug synergy graphs and is limited to single-view learning. Hao et al. introduced GNNSynergy [25], a multi-view GNN framework that constructs drug synergy graphs from a main view (representing specific cancer cell lines) and subviews (representing other cell lines from the same tissue). The model learns entity representations and fuses multi-view information via attention mechanisms, significantly enhancing prediction performance. Despite these advancements, current graph-based drug synergy prediction methods still face two major challenges. First, while models such as KGANSynergy and HANSynergy learn representations for both nodes and edge types, others like GraphSynergy, DeepDDS, and GNNSynergy focus exclusively on node-level features, completely ignoring edge attributes. This simplification may result in the loss of important local interaction information during representation learning. Second, most existing GNN-based models are limited by the local aggregation scope imposed by message-passing mechanisms, preventing them from effectively capturing graph-wide structural dependencies. As a result, node representations often lack global semantic context. These two limitations jointly restrict the ability of current models to accurately capture the complex interactions underlying drug synergy.

To address the limitations of existing graph neural network–based methods for drug combination prediction, this study proposes a knowledge graph–driven deep learning framework termed Knowledge Graph Local and Global Attention Network (KGLGANSynergy). The framework consists of three core components designed to enhance prior models. The Local Graph Attention Network (LGAT) precisely captures neighborhood interactions in the knowledge graph by assigning a learnable feature vector to each edge, enabling fine-grained aggregation of local information and effectively addressing the challenge of missing edge features. The Global Graph Attention Network (GGAT) establishes graph-wide dependencies and learns global semantic representations for each entity, thereby compensating for the absence of global contextual information in previous approaches. To fuse the learned local and global representations, the framework introduces the Mutual Cross Attention (MCA) mechanism [34], which enables adaptive and complementary integration of multi-scale features. This hierarchical architecture not only mitigates the issue of inadequate edge feature modeling but also overcomes the limited receptive field inherent in traditional message passing networks through a coordinated local–global representation mechanism. As a result, the enriched feature representations significantly improve the accuracy and robustness of drug synergy prediction.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:We propose a knowledge graph–based framework for drug combination prediction, which effectively captures both local and global information of entities within the knowledge graph, thereby improving predictive performance.We leverage Transformer-based models to learn entity representations from the knowledge graph by capturing long-range dependencies across nodes, enabling the integration of global semantic information for entity representation.We introduce a mutual cross-attention mechanism to adaptively fuse local and global features of knowledge graph nodes, which significantly enhances the accuracy of synergy prediction.

Dataset description

To evaluate the applicability of KGLGANSynergy on both large-scale and small-scale datasets, we conducted experiments on two widely used benchmark datasets: DrugCombDB and Oncology-Screen.DrugCombDB [35] is a comprehensive database of drug combinations that integrates data from high-throughput screening (HTS) assays, external databases, and literature curated from PubMed. Drug synergy scores in this dataset are computed using the ZIP (Zero Interaction Potency) model. The full dataset contains 764 drugs and 76 cancer cell lines, comprising a total of 69,436 drug combinations.Oncology-Screen [36], curated by O’Neil et al., is derived from large-scale tumor cell line screening and is specifically designed to evaluate drug combination synergy in cancer contexts. This dataset includes 21 drugs and 29 cell lines, covering 4176 drug combinations. Additionally, Preuer et al. [7] processed the dataset by calculating Loewe additivity scores to quantify drug synergy.

The framework design

In this study, we design a deep learning framework named KGLGANSynergy. The overall architecture of KGLGANSynergy is illustrated in Fig. 1. The model consists of the following key components:Knowledge Graph Construction. We construct a drug knowledge graph using four types of biological associations: drug–protein, cell line–protein, cell line–tissue, and protein–protein interactions. Each entity and relation in the knowledge graph is randomly initialized as a vector representation.Local Graph Attention Network (LGAT). LGAT is designed upon the Edge Enhanced Local Attention (EELA) mechanism [37]. For a given entity, LGAT combines message passing and local attention to learn its vector representation from neighboring nodes in the knowledge graph. In this paper, LGAT concatenates the edge features with the source node features and combines them with the target node features, using an attention mechanism to perform local information aggregation. This design enables the simultaneous fusion of node and edge information in computation, thereby facilitating the learning of richer local representations for each node.Global Graph Attention Network (GGAT). GGAT leverages a Transformer-style attention mechanism [38] and applies it to the knowledge graph to learn global semantic representations of entities. By utilizing global attention, GGAT can capture dependencies between any pair of nodes in a single aggregation step, which is particularly advantageous when applied to large graphs. Through multi-layer global attention, GGAT gains a deeper understanding of the overall graph structure, thereby learning more informative global representations for each node.Mutual Cross-Attention (MCA) Feature Fusion. MCA is a parameter-free feature fusion mechanism that improves model performance without introducing learnable parameters [34]. In this work, we adopt MCA to support the fusion of local and global features in the knowledge graph. Specifically, the local features generated by LGAT and the global features produced by GGAT are used as the input to MCA. Through mutual attention computation between these two representations, MCA enables them to complement and reinforce each other, effectively capturing their mutual dependencies and generating the final feature representation for each entity.Drug Synergy Prediction Layer. The fused entity representations are used to predict whether a given drug pair exhibits synergistic effects on a specific cell line. The features of the two drugs and the cell line are first integrated using max pooling and inner product operations. The resulting feature vector is then passed through a sigmoid activation function to compute the final synergy prediction score.Fig. 1The architecture of KGLGANSynergy. (1) Knowledge graph construction: a knowledge graph is built based on interactions among drugs, cell lines, proteins, and tissues. (2) Local graph attention network (LGAT): local feature representations are generated by aggregating neighborhood information through message passing and attention mechanisms. Local graph attention network

In a knowledge graph, entities and their associations are of various types. For a given entity, its semantic meaning and feature representation should vary depending on the types of entities it is connected to. To accurately capture entity embeddings, we employ LGAT, a message-passing module based on attention mechanisms, designed to enhance the capacity for local information aggregation by taking into account the relationships among all neighboring entities. This module assigns attention weights to each neighbor in the entity’s neighborhood set, reflecting their relative importance. By incorporating both node features and edge features, LGAT enables more accurate modeling of the local structural information within the knowledge graph. As a result, it effectively learns meaningful embedding representations for drugs and cell lines.

For drug combination prediction, LGAT constructs local feature representations for drugs and cell lines through multi-level neighborhood aggregation. For drug nodes, a single-layer LGAT dynamically aggregates information from their directly associated target proteins, assigning higher attention weights to the most relevant proteins to emphasize their influence. By stacking multiple LGAT layers, deeper hierarchical context can be captured through paths such as drug–protein–cell line–tissue, allowing drug nodes to incorporate contextual information from related cell lines and tissues, thereby enriching their representations. Similarly, for cell line nodes, a single-layer LGAT aggregates information from associated proteins and corresponding tissues. Deeper LGAT layers enable hierarchical aggregation along paths such as cell line–protein–drug, allowing cell lines to incorporate relevant features from related drugs. Through this hierarchical aggregation mechanism, LGAT incrementally enriches the feature representations of both drugs and cell lines within the knowledge graph, ultimately providing high-quality local representations that support accurate drug synergy prediction.

Global graph attention network

In knowledge graph representation learning, shallow graph neural network (GNN) methods aggregate node information via hierarchical message passing mechanisms. The receptive field of these models is limited by network depth. As illustrated in Fig. 2, taking the drug entity Nilotinib as an example, a single-layer GNN can only integrate information from its directly connected neighbors in the knowledge graph, such as P00519, P10721, and O15197. When using a two-layer GNN, the model can further incorporate second-order neighbors, such as CAOV3, P42684, and SW620, thus expanding the receptive field. However, knowledge graphs often contain long-range dependencies, such as the connection between Nilotinib and lung. Models like KGANSynergy and LGAT, which are based on message passing GNNs, rely on layer-wise propagation to aggregate information. When the number of layers becomes excessively deep, these models tend to introduce additional noise and suffer from performance degradation, which typically limits their architectures to a shallow depth in practice. Consequently, the multi-hop information they capture is confined to local neighborhoods, and long-range dependencies in knowledge graphs cannot be effectively modeled. These long-range relations are critical in knowledge graph representation learning. Due to their inherent architectural constraints, GNNs are limited in their ability to model potential associations between arbitrary entity pairs in the graph. As a result, they struggle to effectively capture the global structural information embedded in knowledge graphs. To address the inability of models such as KGANSynergy and LGAT to capture potential associations between arbitrary nodes in the knowledge graph, this study employs a Global Graph Attention Network (GGAT).Fig. 2Hierarchical partial subgraph of Nilotinib in the knowledge graph, illustrating first-order, second-order, and long-range multi-hop relationships

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^gX^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times F}$$\end{document} denotes the global feature matrix of all entities at the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L$$\end{document}-th layer, with each row representing the global representation of an entity. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N$$\end{document} is the total number of entities, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F$$\end{document} is the feature dimension, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} denotes the total number of GGAT layers used to control the depth of global information propagation

In the drug combination prediction task, GGAT overcomes the limitations of traditional hierarchical message passing mechanisms by establishing dynamic dependencies across the entire graph. This allows drug and cell line nodes to adaptively capture feature information from any relevant entity in the knowledge graph, thereby compensating for the lack of global contextual information in their representations. Specifically, drug and cell line nodes in the knowledge graph attend to other related entities via attention mechanisms. The attention scores are used to weight and aggregate information from these connected entities, enabling the model to learn global feature representations of drugs and cell lines based on the full structure of the knowledge graph.

Mutual cross-attention feature fusion

Mutual Cross Attention (MCA) is a feature fusion mechanism designed based on the concept of cross-attention. In this study, MCA is employed to integrate the local and global features of drugs and cell lines learned by the LGAT and GGAT modules, respectively. Unlike traditional cross-attention mechanisms, MCA eliminates the need for learnable linear projection layers. MCA performs parameter-free computations to achieve adaptive fusion between the two types of features, allowing for efficient and effective integration without increasing model complexity.

Conventional approaches, such as feature concatenation or linear fusion, typically combine features in a simple way and fail to capture deeper relevance between them. MCA performs bidirectional attention calculations, allowing local and global features to mutually guide each other in identifying their most informative components. This bidirectional mechanism captures feature complementarity more effectively than simple aggregation strategies, enabling the model to adaptively emphasize critical information. Through this parameter-free attention mechanism, MCA can achieve dynamic feature fusion while avoiding the additional complexity introduced by linear fusion methods.

Specifically, MCA first performs a directional attention operation, where the local features attend to the global features to selectively aggregate complementary global information. This enables the local representations to be enhanced by the global semantic context. In the opposite direction, the global features attend to the local features, allowing the global representations to incorporate fine-grained local information. The outputs from both attention directions are then combined through element-wise summation to generate the fused representation. This fused representation integrates both local and global information and is used as the final node representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X$$\end{document} in the knowledge graph. The detailed computation is defined as follows:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Atten}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,$$\end{document}12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ X = Atten\left(^lX^{(\alpha)},\ ^gX^{(\beta)},\ ^gX^{(\beta)}\right) + Atten\left(^gX^{(\beta)},\ ^lX^{(\alpha)},\ ^lX^{(\alpha)}\right),$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^lX^{(\alpha)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times F}$$\end{document} denotes the local feature matrix of knowledge graph entities output by the final layer of LGAT, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^gX^{(\beta)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times F}$$\end{document} represents the global feature matrix output by the final layer of GGAT. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times F}$$\end{document} is the fused feature matrix, which serves as the final representation of entities in the knowledge graph after MCA-based fusion.

The drug and cell line nodes obtain their local and global feature representations through the LGAT and GGAT modules, respectively. The MCA module adaptively fuses these two types of features via a mutual cross-attention mechanism, producing final representations that capture both local structural information and global semantic dependencies. These fused representations are then directly used as input features for the downstream drug synergy prediction layer.

This design offers three key advantages. First, the max pooling layer preserves the most salient dimensions of the drug feature vectors, thereby emphasizing the critical information that contributes to drug synergy. Second, the inner product operation enhances the similarity measurement between the drug combination and the cell line features, improving the model’s ability to capture meaningful relational patterns. Third, the sigmoid function produces an interpretable probabilistic output, enabling threshold-based classification for downstream tasks such as binary synergy prediction.

Objective function

The drug synergy prediction task in this study is formulated as a binary classification problem, for which we adopt the Binary Cross-Entropy Loss (BCELoss) as the training objective. To determine the optimal classification threshold, we employ a data-driven dynamic thresholding strategy based on Youden’s J statistic [39], which identifies the threshold that maximizes the combined performance of sensitivity and specificity. Specifically, the model’s predicted probabilities are evaluated on the training set across a range of thresholds, and the threshold that best balances the identification of positive and negative samples is selected as the final decision threshold. If the predicted synergy probabilities exceed this threshold, the drug pairs are considered synergistic and labeled as 1 (synergistic), while those below are labeled as 0 (antagonistic). Compared with fixed-threshold methods, this adaptive approach is more robust to variations in data distribution and helps mitigate classification bias resulting from class imbalance or task-specific requirements.

Considering that parameters in different modules may require different degrees of regularization, we adopt a parameter grouping strategy for the Adam optimizer. Specifically, we apply different weight decay coefficients to the parameters of the LGAT and GGAT modules, respectively. This differentiated optimization scheme helps mitigate the potential conflicts that may arise from applying a single regularization setting to structurally heterogeneous components. The overall loss function of the model is defined as follows: 15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCELoss}}(y,\hat{y})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N[y_i\log(\hat{y}_i)+(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)],$$\end{document}16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta_1,\theta_2)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCELoss}}(y,\hat{y})+\frac{\lambda_1}{2}\|\theta_1\|_2^2+\frac{\lambda_2}{2}\|\theta_2\|_2^2,$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y,\hat{y}\in\{0,1\}$$\end{document} represent the true label and the predicted value of each sample, respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta_1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta_2$$\end{document} denote the model parameters of LGAT and GGAT, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_2$$\end{document} are the corresponding weight decay coefficients applied to LGAT and GGAT, respectively.

Parameter sensitivity analysis

The number of layers directly influences the receptive depth of the model. In this section, we evaluate the performance of KGLGANSynergy on the DrugCombDB and Oncology-Screen datasets by varying the number of layers in the LGAT and GGAT modules. Experiments are conducted under both the leave-one-drug-pair-out and random split settings. The results are presented in Fig. 4, where subfigures (A) and (B) correspond to the leave-one-drug-pair-out setting, and (C) and (D) to the random split setting. The number of layers is varied within the range {1, 2, 3, 4}. The results indicate that the optimal configuration varies across datasets. On the DrugCombDB dataset, the best performance is achieved when both LGAT and GGAT are set to two layers, whereas on the Oncology-Screen dataset, the optimal results are obtained with a single-layer LGAT and a two-layer GGAT. Compared to the DrugCombDB dataset, the Oncology-Screen dataset is more sensitive to parameter variations. For the LGAT module, a single layer captures information only from direct neighbors, while a multi-layer design enables the aggregation of indirect neighborhood information. For the GGAT module, although a single layer can theoretically aggregate information from all nodes in the knowledge graph, it is limited in its ability to capture indirect dependencies, thereby constraining the model’s expressive capacity. A multi-layer GGAT alleviates this limitation by enabling the integration of higher-order global dependencies and enriching representation learning. However, as the number of layers of LGAT or GGAT increases, additional noise is introduced into the aggregated features, which may degrade performance. Conclusion

In this study, we proposed KGLGANSynergy, a knowledge graph–based deep learning framework designed to address the limitations of existing graph-based approaches in drug synergy prediction. The model introduces three core components that contribute to its effectiveness: The Local Graph Attention Network (LGAT) leverages fine-grained edge feature modeling and a neighborhood aggregation mechanism to precisely capture local interaction patterns between drugs and cell lines. The Global Graph Attention Network (GGAT) overcomes the limitations of traditional message-passing schemes by establishing graph-wide dependencies and integrating long-range semantic information. The Mutual Cross Attention (MCA) module enables adaptive fusion of local and global features in a novel and effective manner. This architecture effectively addresses two major limitations of existing methods: insufficient edge feature learning and limited ability to capture long-range dependencies. Experimental results demonstrate that KGLGANSynergy consistently outperforms existing baseline models on the DrugCombDB and Oncology-Screen datasets. Furthermore, the case study confirms the model’s capability to identify unknown yet biologically meaningful drug combinations. Overall, this work provides a more powerful computational tool for drug synergy prediction and holds broad potential for applications in biomedical research and drug development.

While knowledge graphs are effective in representing pairwise interactions between biological entities such as drugs, their inherent graph structure limits their ability to directly model higher-order associations, such as those between synergistic drug pairs and specific cell lines. In contrast, hypergraphs are naturally suited for representing complex interactions among multiple entities. They enable joint modeling of a drug pair and its associated cell line as a unified relational unit. Based on this insight, future work could explore dual-view representation learning by integrating knowledge graphs and hypergraphs. Specifically, incorporating a hypergraph attention mechanism could help quantify the synergistic impact of different drug combinations on cell lines, providing a more powerful framework for predicting effective therapies. This direction holds promise for overcoming the representational limitations of traditional binary relation graphs and may introduce a novel computational paradigm for combinatorial drug screening and precision therapy design.
