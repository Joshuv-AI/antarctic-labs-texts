Perceived barriers and opportunities to the continuous distribution of ivermectin in the Cameroon primary health care system: an exploratory study of major stakeholders
Authors: Guy Sadeu Wafeu, Valerie Makoge, Loirette Simo Simo, Hugues Clotaire Nana-Djeunga, Joseph Kamgno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOnchocerciasis, a debilitating parasitic disease transmitted by blackflies, remains a significant public health concern in Cameroon. While mass drug administration (MDA) campaigns with ivermectin offer success in controlling transmission, logistical and financial challenges threaten long-term sustainability. Integrating ivermectin distribution into the existing primary healthcare system (PHC) could offer a promising alternative. We aimed to explore challenges and opportunities associated with this integration.MethodsThis study employed a mixed-methods approach conducted across five regions in Cameroon. Qualitative methods (in-depth interviews and focus group discussions) were used to explore stakeholder perceptions at all levels of the distribution ladder. Quantitative data on program costs and drug coverage were extracted from district campaign reports.ResultsStakeholder perspectives on Ivermectin Integration (n = 130) revealed strong support for partial integration within the primary healthcare system. Stakeholders from various levels (health system, healthcare workers, and community members) perceived this approach as simple and readily implementable. Most stakeholders viewed this approach as viable to ensure a continued ivermectin administration despite funding shortfalls. However, concerns were raised regarding management of adverse events and a potential misuse of ivermectin. Additionally, our analysis revealed an estimated cost of 53 CFA francs (0.1 USD) per person treated.ConclusionBy exploring the partial integration of ivermectin distribution within Cameroon’s primary healthcare system, this research paves the way for sustainable onchocerciasis control. Clinical trials and community education initiatives are crucial next steps to validate and optimize this promising strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14863-x.

Introduction

Onchocerciasis, also known as river blindness, is a debilitating parasitic infection caused by the filarial nematode Onchocerca volvulus. It is the second leading infectious cause of blindness worldwide, surpassed only by trachoma [1]. The disease manifests through various clinical signs and symptoms that significantly affect survival and socioeconomic conditions, particularly impacting women and children. Associations with conditions such as dwarfism (Nakalanga syndrome), cognitive impairments, epilepsy, nodding syndrome, increased mortality, diminished productivity, and social stigma have been documented [2–6]. As of 2017, approximately 21 million people were infected globally, with over 99% residing in 31 African nations, including Cameroon [7].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has endorsed community-directed treatment with ivermectin (CDTI) as the primary control strategy against onchocerciasis in Africa [7]. This approach has successfully interrupted or eliminated transmission in several regions of Latin America and specific African foci, including Uganda, Sudan, Senegal, Mali, and Nigeria, demonstrating its efficacy. Nonetheless, despite over two decades of CDTI implementation in Cameroon, onchocerciasis persists in 60% (113 out of 189) of the health districts across the country’s ten regions, with some districts reporting a prevalence exceeding 60% [8]. Challenges to eradication include high transmission rates due to significant vector presence, suboptimal treatment coverage attributed to absenteeism during campaigns, fear of adverse effects, and potential suboptimal ivermectin efficacy or emerging drug resistance [9]. Most studies reported that programmatic treatment coverage often falls below 80%, with around 10% of individuals consistently opting out of ivermectin administration (called systematic non compliers). Factors such as absence during mass drug administration campaigns, primarily among individuals under 40 who are likely to be at work, notably affect compliance rates. Additional barriers include logistical challenges in drug delivery by community drug distributors (CDDs) and resource constraints at the operational level of the National onchocerciasis control program (NOCP) [9–11]. These observations highlight limitations in IVM campaign planning and delivery that are hindering its potential as an effective onchocerciasis intervention. Given that absence during the campaign represents a significant barrier to this intervention, extending the duration for which medication remains available in the community may serve as a potential strategy to address this challenge.

This study aims to explore the challenges and opportunities associated with partial integration of ivermectin distribution into the primary healthcare (PHC) system to address these gaps, as suggested by Bhatnagar et al. [12]. This partial integration would involve maintaining a stock of ivermectin at local health centres post-CDTI campaign for ongoing distribution by health workers to those who missed the initial distribution. Such a strategy could enhance program coverage and outcomes by reducing resource strain, promoting local involvement in planning, and ensuring continuous availability of treatment.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study used a mixed-methods approach, utilizing both qualitative and quantitative data collection methods. The research was conducted from September 2021 to July 2022, spanning five regions in Cameroon: Centre, Littoral, Adamawa, South, and West.

For the qualitative component, in-depth interviews were conducted with stakeholders from non-governmental organizations, as well as central, regional, district, and community-level representatives. Additionally, focus group discussions were held within the communities. In-depth interviews and focus group discussion were led by LSS, GSW and VM. The determinants of successful implementation of an intervention or strategy, such as integrating a campaign into the PHC system, were conceptualized using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) modified by Means et al. for low- and middle-income countries [13]. This framework was selected as it is widely used and synthesizes prior evidence from various disciplines, encompassing various constructs that affect the successful implementation of an intervention or strategy. Table 1 summarizes the domains and constructs evaluated in this study, along with the corresponding levels of assessment. At the end of the data analysis, a deliberative forum was organized, involving community members, local health workers, and regional and central stakeholders. Domains and constructs with corresponding levels of evaluation during data collection

For the quantitative component, data on resources for the implementation of the most recent MDA campaign were obtained from existing district reports.

Study population and sampling method

The study population included stakeholders at the central (Ministry of Health), regional, and district levels who were involved in the IVM distribution campaign and/or PHC activities. A purposive sampling method was used to select participants. To be included, a participant must have participated at least once in the conception, planning, implementation, and/or evaluation of IVM distribution campaigns or PHC activities in one of the targeted regions. Prior to inclusion, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the purpose of the study as well as the researchers’ background was clearly presented to all of them.

At the central level, stakeholders were selected from the NOCP and the Directorate of the Organization of Health Care and Sanitary Technology, which are responsible for planning and implementing activities related to onchocerciasis control and promoting/implementing PHC activities, respectively. Participants from non-governmental organizations (NGOs) involved in the implementation of IVM MDA campaigns in Cameroon were also included.

At the regional level, two participants were selected: the regional delegate of public health and the regional coordinator of NTD control programs. At the district level, two districts were randomly selected from those where IVM is distributed; in the selected districts, district medical officers, chiefs of the health bureau, IVM distribution coordinators, and one representative of the district dialogue structure were included. In each district, two communities belonging to different health areas were selected based on their reported IVM coverage during the previous campaign: the health area with the highest and the one with the lowest coverage. In each of these selected communities, one local health worker (head of the health area), one community health worker, and one member of the local dialogue structure were selected. Furthermore, a focus group discussion (FGD) was organized in each community with 6–8 participants selected according to their involvement in community health activities, considering their age range and gender. All community members were selected in accordance with community leaders, based on their involvement in health activities and their leadership in the community.

Qualitative data collection and study procedures

The qualitative data was collected through in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, lasting 30–60 minutes. Participants were asked to share their views and reflect on their perceptions regarding the partial integration of IVM distribution into the PHC system, drawing from their experiences in IVM distribution campaigns and/or other health interventions they had been part of. They were specifically asked to focus on factors that could constitute barriers to the integration, as well as potential opportunities for successful integration. The partial integration of IVM into PHC here consists of leaving a stock of medication in health facilities for several weeks or months after mass treatment, so that anyone who did not take the medication during the campaign can come and get it at the health facility. Thus, the distribution would be integrated into the primary health care services available in health facilities.

An interview guide was used, with questions derived from the research framework, model, and theories (see Additional file 1). Each interview was conducted in French or English and audio-recorded, with participants informed that the interview was being recorded. For participants who were unable to speak French or English, a local translator was used to translate the interview from French to the local language, and vice versa. The interviews were carried out in the participant’s office, the local health centre, or another convenient location agreed upon by the participants. Confidentiality measures were observed during the interviews and focus group discussions to ensure that only the research team members and participants had access to the content of the discussions.

Quantitative data collection and study procedures

The quantitative data were extracted from the health district reports and entered into a form designed for this purpose. Upon arrival at the health district, a working session was organized with the district’s data manager. The data from the last ivermectin distribution campaign, held 6–10 months prior to our study, were presented in an Excel file. This allowed us to extract the data necessary for our study. The main items of the questionnaire included the workload related to IVM MDA (number of community health workers involved per 1,000 inhabitants during the last campaign, working time of each community health worker dedicated to the task, resources used for campaign preparation, implementation, monitoring, and reporting), drug supply, storage and stock management, adverse events management (frequency and severity of adverse events, resources needed for their management), the cost of the campaign at the district level, and drug distribution coverage.

Data analysis

The interviews and FGD records were transcribed and analysed using a thematic framework analysis approach, with a deductive, iterative process grounded in the aim of the study. Firstly, the interviews were transcribed verbatim, and the researcher read and reread the transcripts to become familiar with the data and identify patterns, which were then used to generate codes. The transcripts were then imported into the software and coded by GSW and VM. Similar codes were brought together in themes relative to the objective of the study. Codes and subsequent themes were reviewed and interpreted by considering the participants’ perceptions of challenges and barriers to the partial integration of IVM distribution into the PHC system. Thematic analysis was carried out following the protocol of Braun and Clarke [14]. Qualitative data was handled and analysed using ATLAS.ti qualitative data analysis software. Findings are reported following the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) guidelines (Supplementary Material 1).

All quantitative data collected from the districts were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0. Categorical data were presented as counts and percentages while Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation with ranges. The coverage rate was calculated as the number of people who actually received treatment divided by the number of eligible people expected, based on estimates provided by the Ministry of Public Health for each district. The economic evaluation method was used in cost-outcome description analysis, where the cost and outcome of only one intervention are assessed. The total direct cost was calculated, encompassing expenses related to campaign planning, implementation, supervision, reporting, and adverse events management. The number of person-days, representing the sum of working days for each Community Drug Distributor (CDD), was also quantified. To determine the cost-effectiveness of the intervention, the cost per person treated was calculated by dividing the total direct cost by the total number of individuals treated within the district.

Opportunities to enhance partial integration of IVM distribution into PHC

Most of the participants had average to good knowledge about onchocerciasis and CDTI as the recommended strategy against the disease. The demand and willingness to take the drug was very high in almost all regions where data were collected, with some participants asking for several treatments per year. A head of a health area reported,We are in an area where people are in great need of Mectizan. It’s a real problem […]. The CHWs who work in the field also tell me that people need it.

We are in an area where people are in great need of Mectizan. It’s a real problem […]. The CHWs who work in the field also tell me that people need it.

Moreover, the idea of integrating IVM distribution into the PHC system was well received by almost all the participants, from the communities to NGOs at the central level. An advantage reported was the opportunity to catch up those who were absent, sick or not willing to take the drug during the campaign and later on changed their minds.

The partial integration was also seen as a simple and easy- to- implement approach. People were highly motivated and ready to try it. It was also described as a sustainable activity especially if the required inputs were available, and other authorities (administrative, traditional, religious, etc.…) were involved in the implementation. A participant reported:It will last if certain parameters are ensured, i.e., the medicine must be available for the activity to be sustainable, […]. The actors must be available. If they are available and accessible, it will work.

It will last if certain parameters are ensured, i.e., the medicine must be available for the activity to be sustainable, […]. The actors must be available. If they are available and accessible, it will work.

This partial integration was also seen as an opportunity for sustainability of IVM distribution by some NGOs program managers, as funding may not be available in future years for campaigns; IVM may therefore be shared only through the PHC system.

Challenges to be addressed while integrating IVM distribution into PHC system

The first perceived challenge identified was the strategy to manage adverse events (AE) that may occur after the treatment. Talking about the partial integration, a health area chief stated:It [IVM integration to the primary health care system] really needs to be accompanied by measures to manage severe adverse events.

It [IVM integration to the primary health care system] really needs to be accompanied by measures to manage severe adverse events.

The strategy suggested to address this challenge was to involve CDDs in follow-up after treatment as it is done during campaigns, and to provide resources for management of AEs at health centres. However, this challenge will be rare due to the very low incidence rate of adverse events, with less than 2 cases of mild adverse events for 10 000 persons treated, and no moderate nor severe adverse events during the last.

The second perceived challenge was an increased risk of mis-use of IVM for purposes other than treatment of onchocerciasis – It was reported that IVM may be used by some people with the perception that it is a medication that can induce abortion, a medication to treat animals or to be used for skin care, as well as the possibility that some health workers might decide to sell the drug instead of normal free distribution. Respondents suggested that these can be addressed by a close monitoring and follow-up of the activity: a role that stakeholders at district and regional levels were willing to play as they do for other activities and other drugs and vaccines offered in health facilities, as well as regular reporting on program data. Health workers suggested that tools (patient-registers, reporting forms, drug accountability forms, etc. …) need to be adjusted (increased) to take into consideration this new approach.

The third perceived challenge reported was the lack of motivation (remuneration or incentives) of CDDs.There is not good coverage (during campaigns) due to the lack of motivation of CDDs, if they were motivated […] the result would be very satisfactory,

reported a community actor. CDDs are key actors for sensitization in NTDs programs, and according to most stakeholders, they must be involved in sensitization after integrating IVM distribution into the PHC system. However, their motivation decreases over time, especially as there are no financial incentives for the activity. One approach suggested to address this was to use the same community worker for IVM distribution as working with the insecticide treated net campaigns or HIV/AIDS programs, for which there is remuneration. In other words, CDDs who work voluntarily for CDTI should be prioritized for involvement in malaria and HIV/AIDS control programs. CDTI activities should be included in their contracts, although separate from the distribution of mosquito nets and HIV/AIDS-related activities. Thus, same CDD to handle several paid campaign activities to avoid demotivation.

Deliberative forum summary and outputs

After data analysis, a deliberative forum was organized with 50 people invited to attend (randomly selected among the participants). A total of 31 participants were present, including two participants from the Ministry of Health, one from a non-governmental organization, one from a regional delegation, 16 from District level and 11 from health areas. Participants validated the results, and confirmed that the conceptual framework describing the responsibilities of different actors in the partial integration that emerged from the study was feasible to act on (Fig. 1). They were ready to get involved in this new approach. They also emphasized the need for involving local community radio for sensitization of the population. Discussion

This study explored stakeholder perceptions regarding the potential for integrating IVM distribution for onchocerciasis control into the existing PHC in Cameroon. Our findings provide insights into the perceived benefits and challenges to this partial integration strategy.

We found that the community’s willingness to accept ivermectin is a pivotal factor that could significantly facilitate the successful implementation of its integration into PHC. This readiness for engagement suggests that community involvement might be easily achieved, which is crucial for the success of public health programs. As highlighted by Lemoine et al. [15]. community engagement has been essential for the success of NTD programs. Therefore, leveraging this willingness could substantially benefit the partial integration of ivermectin into PHC.

Participants from various levels of the health system perceived the partial integration as a straightforward intervention to implement and a sustainable method for delivering the medication to communities. Although the sustainability of MDA programs has been previously criticised, there are concerns about the long-term effectiveness on disease dynamics and the potential for increased aid-dependency, which could lead to the discontinuation of the intervention if funding ceases [16]. The economic analysis revealed that the cost per person treated was 50 CFA Francs (approximately 0.1 USD), which, while lower than costs reported by Mow et al. for similar MDA efforts against scabies [17], remains a substantial financial burden for a lower-middle-income country like Cameroon, especially given the millions requiring treatment annually. Despite these economic challenges, the partial integration was widely supported at a stakeholder forum, indicating a strong readiness to adopt this strategy.

Community drug distributors were identified as a fundamental component for the success of the partial integration of ivermectin into PHC. They are crucial for sensitization efforts before, during, and after MDA, as well as for monitoring adverse events after treatment. Their role is consistent with findings from other studies that emphasize the effectiveness of CDD in promoting health while considering local contexts [18, 19]. The involvement of CDD in the follow-up of adverse events is expected to enhance community confidence in the integration process. Even though the workload for monitoring adverse events is relatively low, due to the minimal occurrence of moderate to severe incidents in past campaigns, there is a noticeable decline in motivation over time among CDD. Some participants proposed that CDD engaged in other health initiatives could also handle post-treatment follow-ups. However, to sustain these efforts, it is essential to establish mechanisms that enable the community to actively support and maintain the responsibilities carried out by CDD.

A significant challenge highlighted by our study is the potential misuse of ivermectin, as observed during the COVID-19 pandemic when premature reports of its efficacy against the virus led to widespread unauthorized use [20]. This risk of misuse could be more acute during partial integration compared to standard campaigns because the medication’s presence time within the community is much longer (2–3 weeks vs. 3–4 months).Finally, it could deter future donations if not adequately addressed. Proposed mitigation strategies include strict controls on drug distribution and enhanced supervision at healthcare facilities to ensure proper usage and reporting.

Although integration of IVM into PHC system represents a valuable opportunity, it is important to highlight that access to PHC system is not guaranteed for the entire population. A report from the Africa Health Agenda International Conference Commission indicated that 48% of Africans access their healthcare needs through primary healthcare services [21]. This suggests that the integration into primary healthcare may also encounter the barrier of low utilization by the population. Therefore, it is crucial to conduct further research to identify the main reasons for low utilization of primary healthcare in onchocerciasis-endemic areas, in order to associate integration with interventions that can help overcome these barriers. This is particularly important as the integration of ivermectin into primary healthcare also serves as an asset for the sustainability of this intervention and other campaign-based initiatives.

Our study’s primary limitation is its reliance on participant perceptions, which introduces potential biases and does not provide empirical evidence of the challenges and opportunities reported. No trial or comparative analysis was conducted, underscoring the need for further research to validate these findings and assess their real-world impact. Additionally, the reliance on district reports for campaign data may introduce inaccuracies, affecting the precision of our findings related to resource allocation. Despite these limitations, this research is among the first to evaluate the feasibility of integrating ivermectin into the PHC system in Cameroon, presenting an interesting opportunity for sustainable onchocerciasis control. Future studies should aim to empirically test the partial integration strategy and explore practical solutions for the challenges outlined to enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of ivermectin distribution within public health frameworks.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings from this study represent an initial exploration into the feasibility and implications of incorporating ivermectin into the primary health care system in low- and middle-income countries. These results underscore the promising potential for such integration to enhance the sustainability and reach of ivermectin mass drug administration. Furthermore, the data illuminate the necessity for future trials to evaluate the effects of partial integration on drug coverage and cost-effectiveness. These trials could assess of the number of individuals who missed the campaign but received treatment through this partial integration strategy. Additionally, they could evaluate of the optimal duration of integration, the appropriate quantity of medication to provide, and the specific population groups to target. Equally important, this research has identified critical areas within current campaigns that require improvement, particularly the motivation and engagement of community drug distributors, which represent a key component of the fight against NTDs. These insights are pivotal in refining strategies to better integrate ivermectin into health systems and maximize its public health benefits.
