Multiphoton imaging for quantification of mesenchymal stem cell survival and distribution in PEG granular hydrogel scaffolds post-implantation into rat cranial bone defects
Authors: Auden P Balouch, Alexandra Z Francis, Varsha V Rao, Samantha J Wojda, Kristi S Anseth, Seth W Donahue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are promising candidates for cellular therapies aimed at promoting bone regeneration due to their secretory properties and osteoblastic differentiation capacity. However, typically < 5% of delivered MSCs are retained at the healing site within days of delivery via injection. In this work, granular PEG hydrogel scaffolds were used to deliver MSCs, labeled with fluorescent Quantum Dots, into critical-sized rat calvarial bone defects. The presence, survival, and distribution of MSCs within the hydrogel scaffold were evaluated with multiphoton microscopy at 3- and 7-days post-implantation. Additionally, endogenous cell infiltration into scaffolds was quantified, and markers for M1 and M2 macrophages were identified with immunohistochemistry. This multiphoton microscopy technique provides a quantitative analysis of exogenous MSC presence and survival and allows for micron-level spatial resolution of cell distribution throughout the implanted scaffolds. When ~750,000 MSCs were implanted in a calvarial bone defect via PEG granular hydrogel scaffolds, ~27% and ~8% survived 3- and 7-days post-implantation, respectively. At 3- and 7-days post-implantation, exogenous MSCs and infiltrating endogenous cells, including M1 and M2 macrophages, were well distributed throughout the scaffolds. This multiphoton microscopy technique could be used to assess biomaterial delivery systems that can improve exogenous MSC presence and survival, facilitate endogenous cell infiltration, and investigate exogenous-endogenous cell interactions for bone regeneration therapies.Keywords: Stem cell delivery, MSC, Cell survival, Hydrogel, Bone healing

Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are one of the most investigated cell types in clinical trials and have been evaluated for their use in aiding bone healing [1,2]. Various studies investigating the use of MSCs to treat critical-size bone defects indicate they have the capacity to modulate inflammation [1], aid in tissue preservation [3], secrete angiogenic and osteogenic factors [2,4], and differentiate into bone forming osteoblasts [4,5]. Considerable investments have been made in MSC clinical therapies; however, only a small degree of efficacy has been observed in preclinical and clinical studies [2]. When exogenous MSCs are injected directly to a site of injury, less than 5% of cells are retained at the injection site within 7 days post-transplantation [3,6]. This is due both to clearance of the cells from the area as well as deleterious effects of shear stress from the injection process [7,8]. To enhance the survival of exogenous MSCs and improve regenerative outcomes, biomaterial scaffolds have been engineered as MSC delivery systems. The use of biomaterial scaffolds can increase MSC efficacy by reducing shear induced cell damage during delivery and slow the clearance of cells by the circulatory system or migration to non-target organs [6].

Synthetic hydrogels, such as those derived from poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) precursors, are biocompatible, highly tailorable, and can be designed to protect implanted cells from the shock of an inflammatory microenvironment, improve cell retention, and allow for endogenous cell attachment onto scaffolds at the delivery site [2,3,9–11]. In contrast to natural polymers, synthetic polymers allow users to design hydrogels with nearly identical macromers to precisely control scaffold properties, such as moduli and degradation rate [11]. In this study, microgel creation relied on an inverse phase suspension polymerization, which necessitated the use of rapid bio-click chemistry, such as a strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) reaction between dibenzocylcooctyne (DBCO) and azides. Multi-arm PEG macromers were selected as they can be easily functionalized with DBCOs and azides to create rapidly gelling microgels of controlled sizes [12]. MSC encapsulation in bulk hydrogels, where pores are on the nanoscale, can increase MSC retention in the injured area from hours to weeks [6,13]. However, bulk encapsulation slows diffusion of MSC secreted growth factors, can impede nutrient exchange, limits cell-to-cell interactions, and impedes endogenous cell infiltration, often resulting in fibrous capsule formation and a pro-inflammatory immune response [13–15]. To circumvent some of these limitations, porous granular hydrogels have been designed for cell delivery, where the pore spaces are on the microscale to allow for endogenous cell infiltration, as well as cell-cell interactions that enhance secretory properties, without compromising efficacy [16–19]. Previous studies have shown cells had improved viability when cultured in vitro in 3D granular hydrogel scaffolds (60 ± 6%) compared to bulk hydrogel scaffolds (40 ± 5%) for 3 days [20]. However, due to limitations in current imaging techniques, whether this improved viability translates to an in vivo environment is largely uncharacterized. In the in vivo environment, delivered MSCs are exposed to inflammatory cytokines and numerous cell types. Thus, to continue to improve upon the therapeutic efficacy of biomaterial scaffolds and understand exogenous-endogenous cell interactions, techniques for quantification of exogenous cell survival and endogenous cell infiltration in vivo models are crucial.

Methods for labeling exogenous MSCs for in vivo tracking include ultra-small ion particles, radioactive labels, organic fluorescent dyes, or fluorescent proteins naturally expressed by cells [21]. However, these methods have limitations with respect to chemical and metabolic degradation, as well as reduced photo stability and signal quality over time. Thus, alternatives like red-fluorescent quantum dot (QD) nanoparticles, have been designed for long term in vivo tracking [22]. Here we present a novel approach to track QD-labeled MSCs, delivered in PEG granular hydrogel scaffolds to rat calvarial bone defects, using three-dimensional multiphoton microscopy. In combination with multiphoton imaging, exogenous MSC retention and distribution within implanted scaffolds were quantified with high resolution and limited photobleaching compared to in vivo imaging systems and confocal microscopy techniques, respectively [23]. Additionally, we quantified the infiltration of pro- and anti-inflammatory macrophages using immunohistochemistry (IHC). The combination of these methods allowed for quantification of both the delivered exogenous MSCs and endogenous infiltrating cells within the same hydrogel scaffold post-implantation. QD-labeled MSCs were delivered in hydrogel scaffolds that were synthesized from granular PEG hydrogel spheres (i.e., microgels), with diameters of ~200 µm, fabricated from bio-click chemistry and inverse phase suspension polymerization. Hydrogel scaffolds were delivered into 6 mm rat calvarial defects and left for 3- and 7-days. Ex vivo staining and multiphoton imaging allowed for the quantification and distribution of live exogenous MSCs, dead exogenous MSCs, and infiltrating endogenous cells at the wound site. Secondary staining for CD68+, iNOS+, and CD206+ cell surface markers identified endogenous pro- and anti-inflammatory macrophages.

Methods

Rat MSC isolation and QD labeling.

MSCs were isolated from the bone marrow of 14-week-old female Sprague Dawley rats in accordance with the NIH guide for the care of laboratory animals and with approval from the University of Massachusetts Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol #2154). The MSCs were isolated from the femurs and humeri of 8 rats as previously described [16]. Briefly, the ends of the long bones were cut and the marrow cavities were flushed with PBS. The bone marrow mixture was plated onto tissue culture plastic in media comprised of alpha modified Eagle medium (alpha-MEM) with 1% (v/v) Gibco antibiotic-antimycotic (10,000 units/mL of penicillin, 10,000 μg/mL of streptomycin, and 25 μg/mL of Amphotericin B) and 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS). After 24 h, the plates were washed with fresh media and the attached MSCs were left to proliferate for an additional 10 days, with media changes occurring every 3 days. Passage 1 (P1) MSCs were frozen down in 80% FBS with 20% DMSO. All experiments were conducted with P2 MSCs. MSCs demonstrated ability to produce mineralized matrix nodules after 21 days of culture in osteogenic media, verifying their potential to differentiate into bone forming osteoblasts.

For both in vitro and in vivo experiments (Fig. 1a&b), MSCs were labelled with QD 655 (ThermoFisher, Q25021MP) (Fig. 1c1) before seeding in hydrogel scaffolds, according to the manufacturers’ protocol. Briefly, 4 nM of the QD working solution was prepared and 0.2 mL of the solution was placed on a T-75 flask of ~70% confluent MSCs for 1 hour at 37 °C. The plate was washed twice with media. MSCs were labeled with fluorescent QDs 24 h prior to seeding in scaffolds.

Schematic of methods. a) In vitro timeline of events. b) In vivo timeline of events. c) Overview of procedures. MSCs were labeled with fluorescent QD 655 (c1) 1 day prior to seeding in hydrogel scaffolds (c2). Following a 3-day incubation period, MSCs-laden scaffolds were implanted into a 6 mm rat calvarial bone defect (c3) and left to heal for either 3 or 7 days. Upon explant, scaffolds were well fused with the periosteum and new connective tissue within the defect space (c4) and all tissue within the defect was extracted and stained for live cells with Calcein AM (c5). Immediately following staining, hydrogels were imaged on a multiphoton microscope (c6) across the scaffold diameter to quantify amounts of live endogenous cells, live exogenous MSCs, and dead exogenous MSCs. Next, hydrogel scaffolds were fixed (c7), embedded in OCT, and sectioned along the scaffold diameter (c8). Sectioned slides were immunostained for CD68+, iNOS+, and CD206+ cells (c9) to evaluate M1- and M2-like cell infiltration into the middle and edge regions of hydrogel scaffolds post-implantation.

QDs (10–20 nm in diameter) are typically delivered into the cell cytoplasm by nonspecific endocytosis, making them applicable to a variety of cell types. Once inside cells, QD nanocrystals are released and concentrate in intracellular vesicles around the nucleus and are passed on to daughter cells. Previous in vitro studies demonstrated this QD concentration does not impair viability of MSCs [21,24] or other cell types [25]. We verified this finding by labeling MSCs with QD 655 (4 nM), as described above, and quantifying cell death (%) after 3 and 7 days in 2D culture in comparison to non-labeled MSCs (n = 3 per group). Briefly, MSCs were plated in a 24 well plate (1.5 × 104/mL) and at 3- and 7-days post-plating, MSCs were stained with NucBlue (1 mM) and Image-IT Dead Green (100 nM). Three random ROIs per sample were selected for analysis where the total number and the number of dead cells was quantified. Cell death was calculated as the number of dead cells normalized to the number of total cells (%). Evaluation of cell viability in response to QD exposure at a concentration of 4 nM showed no cytotoxic effect, where there was no difference in the percentage dead cells between QD labeled and non-labeled MSCs after 3 (p = 0.21) or 7 days (p = 0.75) of culture.

Macromer synthesis and granular hydrogel scaffold fabrication.

Multi-arm poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) polymers, specifically 8-arm 20 kDa PEG alcohol and 4-arm 10 kDa PEG amine (Jenkem) were used to make PEG-DBCO and PEG-azide (PEG-N3), respectively, as previously described [16]. C6-DBCO-acid was purchased from Click Chemistry Tools. PEG-DBCO was synthesized via an esterification reaction using an EDC (N-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride) coupling. The resulting ester linked PEG-DBCO is susceptible to hydrolysis and cell secreted esterases. End-group functionalization was confirmed by 1H NMR to be >85%. PEG-azide was prepared by first synthesizing a PEG-mesylate using triethylamine and methanesulfonyl chloride. Then, the resulting tetra-arm PEG-mesylate was reacted with sodium azide to create PEG-azide. Complete disappearance of end-group mesyl protons on 1H NMR was interpreted as quantitative conversion of tetra-PEG-mesylate to tetra-PEG-azide. An azide modified lysine analog (Fmoc-azide-l-lysine, ChemImpex) was used to synthesize an azide-labeled RGD (N3-KGRGDS) peptide using standard Fmoc chemistry and a Rink Amide MBHA resin (Chempep Inc, USA) on a Protein Technologies Tribute Peptide Synthesizer. The peptides were purified using reverse phase High Pressure Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) as previously described [12,26].

Individual microgels were synthesized as previously described [16]. Briefly, individual microgels were created using an inverse suspension polymerization under shear (magnetic stirring) with PEG-DBCO and PEG-N3 macromers in hexanes with Span-80 (2.25% v/v) and Tween-20 (0.75% v/v). Two distinct sets of microgels, with diameters of 190 ± 100 μm, one with excess DBCO and the other with excess azide, were prepared with 11 mM excess of either functional group to allow for subsequent scaffold assembly. 1 mM of N3-GRGDS was included in all microgels. Microgels were washed with 100% isopropanol (IPA) (4x) and placed on a shaker overnight in IPA in 15 mL conical tubes. For in vitro and in vivo experiments, the microgels were shipped in IPA to Amherst for later assembly. Upon arrival, they were stored at −80 °C until use.

After equilibrating the conical tubes containing microgels in IPA to RT, a probe sonicator was used to break up any aggregates of microgels. The microgels were washed again with 100% IPA, centrifuged, and then washed twice with sterile PBS in a biosafety cabinet. To create MSC-laden hydrogel scaffolds, 4.5 million QD labelled MSCs were mixed with DBCO-excess and N3-excess microgels (~100 µL polymer mix each) swollen in PBS. The suspension was then centrifuged at 1000 rcf for 10 min, followed by 3000 rcf for 3 min in a syringe mold (~10 mm in diameter). After centrifugation, scaffolds were removed from the mold, stamped using a 6 mm biopsy punch, and immediately placed in MSC media. This procedure created hydrogel scaffolds that were ~600 µm thick and 6 mm in diameter loaded with MSCs at a density of ~750,000 cells/scaffold (Fig. 1c2). As previously assessed [12,16], the compressive modulus for the fully assembled scaffolds was 1.9 ± 0.3 kPa, the average pore diameter was 210 ± 260 μm, and the void fraction was 30.5 ± 0.2%. After incubation for three days in media, the scaffolds were either implanted in rat calvarial bone defects or used for in vitro experiments.

Calvarial defect surgery.

Animal experiments were completed in accordance with the NIH guide for the care of laboratory animals and with approval from the University of Massachusetts Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol #3197). Six female Sprague Dawley rats underwent surgery to create a 6 mm unilateral calvarial defect in the parietal bone following established protocols [27]. Rats were anesthetized to effect via isoflurane inhalation (2–3%). Animals received buprenorphine SR (0.65 mg/kg) subcutaneously 30 min prior to surgery for pain management, cefazolin (20 mg/kg) as a prophylactic antibiotic, and sterile saline (5 mL/kg) for loss of fluids. Under anesthesia, the incision site was shaved and disinfected with isopropyl alcohol and chlorohexidine. A midline incision 2–3 cm in length was made through skin and underlying periosteum along the sagittal suture of the calvaria. The periosteum was laterally retracted from the defect site and a trephine bur operating at 1500 RMP was used to create a 6 mm diameter unilateral circular defect in the left parietal bone. Sterile saline irrigation and gauze were used throughout the procedure to maintain bleeding and prevent thermal damage to bone tissue. Caution was taken to not damage the underlying dura mater. The defect was completed by removing the segmented bone using a surgical elevator. MSC-laden hydrogel scaffolds were then removed from incubators and immediately placed into the defect space (Fig. 1c3). The periosteum and skin were separately sutured closed using 4–0 monofilament absorbable sutures, helping keep the scaffold in place. Wound clip staples were placed over the incision. The wound site was cleaned with hydrogen peroxide and animals were left to recover on a heating pad until fully awake. Animals were monitored daily for signs of distress and evidence of wound problems.

Multiphoton imaging

Sample Collection and Preparation.

For in vitro work, two hydrogel scaffolds were removed from incubators on each of days 1-, 3-, and 7-post-seeding and imaged as described below (Fig. 1a). For in vivo work, following euthanasia, hydrogel scaffolds were explanted from rat calvarial defects on days 3 and 7 post-implantation (days 6 and 10 post-seeding with MSCs, Fig. 1b). Four 8 mm incisions were made through the periosteum surrounding the defect space to extract the hydrogel for evaluation (Fig. 1c4). Hydrogels were well integrated with new connective tissue within the defect space and fused with the periosteum. Therefore, the periosteum and entire scaffold-tissue construct within the defect space was extracted and left intact. Hydrogels were stained, immediately, with acetoxymethyl ester Calcein AM (ThermoFisher, C3099) to identify all live cells in the scaffold (Fig. 1c5). Individual scaffolds were fully submerged in 0.5 µM of Calcein AM for 30 min at 37°C. Hydrogel scaffolds were then submerged in fresh PBS and positioned for imaging with the periosteal surface superiorly and dura mater surface inferiorly, and topped with a cover glass.

Imaging.

Immediately following staining, hydrogel scaffolds were imaged using an A1RMP Resonant Scanning Multiphoton Confocal (Nikon) with an excitation wavelength of 750 nm, power of 5, gain of 30, Fluor LWD 25x water immersion objective (Nikon), at a resolution of 0.5 µm/pixel. Z-stacks in 5 positions (XY: 515 µm × 515 µm) across the 6 mm diameter of each scaffold were captured (Fig. 1c6) for as much height as there was detectable fluorescence, with a Z-stack step of 5 µm. The presence of variable amounts of connective tissue in the in vivo samples led to variability in imaging depth into scaffolds between samples. To account for this variability, all quantified imaging parameters were normalized and reported as a percentage of the total imaging volume of interest (VOI).

Fluorescent volume and total volume were reported for each fluorescent channel (FITC and TRITC) where the TRITC channel quantified QD fluorescence and the FITC channel quantified Calcein AM fluorescence. Binary volume renderings were created by thresholding each fluorescent channel to exclude autofluorescence (NIS Elements, v 5.21). Autofluorescence level was established by imaging unstained periosteal tissue extracted from the calvaria outside the defect space. Comparison of animal tissue between stained and unstained samples served as a resource for establishing the fluorescent intensity threshold to minimize autofluorescence in cell volume quantifications, where Calcein AM and QD fluorescent emission intensity was 2–3x that of background fluorescence. Binary thresholding was then set to only include fluorescence > 2x average fluorescent intensity of unstained animal tissue. Additionally, Z-stacks were processed to increase fluorescent contrast and exclude objects less than 2 µm from binary thresholding to reduce error from unspecific staining in cell volume quantifications. For in vitro experiments, the volume of binary QD fluorescence indicated the amount of all MSCs. The amount of live MSCs (yellow) was quantified as the volume of overlapping QD fluorescence and Calcein AM fluorescence. The amount of dead MSCs (red) was quantified as the volume of QD fluorescence without Calcein AM. In vitro, the viability of MSCs was approximated as the amount of live MSCs normalized by the total amount of MSCs. For in vivo experiments, the binary volume of Calcein AM fluorescence indicated the amount of all live cells, both endogenous and exogenous. The binary volume of QD fluorescence indicated the amount of all exogenous MSCs, both alive and dead. The amount of live exogenous MSCs (yellow) was quantified as the volume of overlapping QD fluorescence and Calcein AM fluorescence. The amount of dead exogenous MSCs (red) was quantified as the volume of QD fluorescence without Calcein AM. The amount of live endogenous cells (green) was quantified as the volume of Calcein AM fluorescence without QD fluorescence.

For all in vitro quantification of MSC presence (live and dead), variables were normalized to the first imaging time point to report changes in MSC population over time. For in vivo data, MSC presence (live and dead) was normalized to the total amount of MSCs delivered (sum of live and dead MSCs in vitro on day 3; the time of implantation). To compare exogenous MSC clearance from the injury site post-implantation to previous literature [28–33], the presence of implanted MSCs (% present) in vivo was quantified as the sum of live and dead MSCs normalized to the total amount of MSCs delivered. Cell volumes (live MSC, dead MSC, and endogenous cell) in relation to the imaging position within hydrogel scaffolds (i.e., varying distance along the diameter) was used to assess cell distribution within scaffolds (Fig. 1c6).

Immunohistochemistry.

Immediately following multiphoton imaging, samples were fixed in 10% formalin for 30 min followed by 3 washes with PBS (Fig. 1c7). Scaffolds were embedded in Optimal Freezing Compound (OCT) and stored at −80 °C until sectioning. Samples were sectioned using a cryostat (Leica) along the scaffold diameter (Fig. 1c8) to a thickness of 15–20 µm and placed on ColorFrost Plus Microscope Slides (Fisher Scientific). Slides were stored at −80 °C until immunohistochemical (IHC) staining.

In preparation for staining, slides were allowed to thaw for 15 min at room temperature. After 5 min, a PapPen was used to create a hydrophobic barrier on the edges of the slides. At 15 min, slides were washed three times using 400–500 µL of tri-buffered saline (TBS, ThermoFisher) with 0.025% Triton X (five minutes for each repetition). After the final wash and aspiration were completed, slides were blocked for 2 h using 400–500 µL of 10% Normal Goat Serum (ThermoFisher) with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Fisher Scientific). After blocking serum was aspirated, the primary antibodies were added. Primary antibodies were added in a 1:50 dilution with 5% BSA in TBS overnight at 4 °C in a humidity chamber with parafilm. RbCD68 (Abcam, ab125212) was used to stain all macrophages, RbiNOS (Bio-Rad, AHP2399) and RbCD206/Mannose (Abcam, ab64693) were used to differentiate M1- and M2-like macrophages, respectively. In order to verify MSC proliferation in hydrogels in vitro, staining for Ki67+ cells was performed three days after seeding MSCs into scaffolds. The percentage of Ki67+ cells, normalized to all DAPI stained cells, was quantified to indicate the percentage of proliferating MSCs in hydrogel scaffolds.

The next day, parafilm was removed carefully to ensure slices were not removed from the slides. Three washes with 400–500 µL of TBS with 0.025% Triton X for 5 min were completed, as before. Secondary antibodies (Goat anti-rabbit 555 and DAPI, ThermoFisher) were added in a 1:250 dilution with 5% BSA in TBS for 1 hour at room temperature. The slides were washed another three times using TBS with 0.025% Triton X and mounted with 100 µL of Prolong Glass (ThermoFisher). Slides were imaged using a widefield microscope (Nikon) and image analysis was performed in Fiji ImageJ.

For image analysis, the outermost regions of the scaffold were defined as edges (two per slide) and the central region between these two edge regions was defined as the middle region (one per slide) (Fig. 1c9). Maximum intensity projections were generated, and brightness, contrast, and thresholding were adjusted using the auto feature of Fiji. Thresholding was deemed appropriate when cells had clearly defined oval or spherical regions. While proper thresholding of the secondary antibody channel could often be attained using the auto feature, background in middle regions of some slices elicited a need for manual thresholding. If use of the auto feature resulted in too much background signal infiltration, the lower threshold limit was increased until cell regions aligned with DAPI channel cell regions. The percent area measurement tool was used to obtain values for both channels. Percent area of the secondary antibody was normalized to DAPI for each primary antibody.

Statistical Analysis.

All fluorescent cell volumes were normalized to total VOI volume (% VOI) to account for variability in imaging depth. To quantify changes in MSC populations over time in vitro, data was normalized to the first imaging time point (day 1 post-seeding) and a nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn post-hoc test were used. To quantify changes in exogenous MSC populations over time in vivo post-implantation, data was normalized to the in vitro day 3 time point (representative of the time of implantation) and a Wilcoxon test was used. To quantify changes in endogenous cell infiltration into scaffolds post-implantation, a Wilcoxon test was used; data are presented % VOI given there was no original time point to normalize data. For assessment of in vivo cell distribution throughout hydrogels, linear regression was used where the cell volume was the dependent variable and the position in the hydrogel (radial distance to the nearest hydrogel border) was the independent variable, evaluated separately for day 3 and day 7 post-implantation. For comparison of IHC fluorescent area between scaffold explant time points (days 3 and 7), an unpaired t-test was used. For comparison of IHC fluorescent area to imaging position (scaffold edge vs. middle), a paired t-test was used. To quantify differences in cell death between QD labeled and non-labeled MSCs, an unpaired t-test was used for day 3 and 7 data. All analyses were performed in JMP Pro v.15.1. All data are presented as mean ± SE and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Multiphoton imaging

In Vitro Cell Survival.

The VOI imaging depths spanned the height of hydrogel scaffolds, 515–650 μm. MSCs viability in hydrogel scaffolds was ~82% at day 7 post-seeding. There was no difference in the amount of live MSCs between days 1 and 3 (p = 0.401) and days 3 and 7 (p = 0.056, Figs. 2a and 3a). However, between days 1 and 7, there was overall a 232% increase (p = 0.0003) in live MSCs, suggesting cells were proliferating in the scaffolds over the 7-day study (Figs. 2a and 3a). Additionally, IHC staining verified 2.8% of MSCs in hydrogels were Ki67+ on day 3 post-seeding, indicating low amounts of proliferation over this period. There was no difference (p = 0.918) in the amount of dead MSCs between days 1 and 3, but an increase in amount (p < 0.001) between days 3 and 7 post-seeding in vitro (Figs. 2b and 3b). 3D volume renderings showed that within a VOI, cells appeared to be highly clustered in the pore spaces (Fig. 2c) around microgel spheres (Fig. 2d) at day 7 compared to a more uniform distribution at day 1 post-seeding. The relationship between live MSCs and radial position within scaffolds was not significant at day 1 (p = 0.542), 3 (p = 0.096), or 7 (p = 0.496) post-seeding, indicating these cells were uniformly dispersed throughout the diameter of scaffolds. Similarly, dead MSCs were well dispersed throughout scaffolds at day 1 (p = 0.229), 3 (p = 0.611), and 7 (p = 0.124) post-seeding.

In Vivo Cell Survival.

Hydrogel scaffolds contained extracellular matrix and were well fused with periosteal tissue upon explant from calvarial defects, which limited imaging depths to a range of 100–245 µm from the dura mater side of the scaffolds. Relative to the total number of MSCs implanted, 131% (live and dead cells) were present within scaffolds at 3 days post-implantation and 13% (live and dead cells) were present within scaffolds 7 days post-implantation. Of the total MSCs implanted, an estimated 27% survived to 3 days post-implantation and 8% and survived to 7 days post-implantation (Fig. 4a). Between days 3 and 7 post-implantation, the amount of live MSCs decreased (p = 0.019, Fig. 4a). Similarly, between days 3 and 7 post-implantation, the amount of dead MSCs within scaffolds decreased (p < 0.001, Fig. 4b). By day 3, numerous endogenous cells had infiltrated hydrogel scaffolds, but there was no change (p = 0.396) in the amount of endogenous cells between days 3 and 7 post-implantation (Fig. 4c). Unlike observations in vitro (Fig. 2d), cell clustering around individual microgels was not observed in the scaffolds that were implanted in calvarial defects (Fig. 4d, 4e).

Immunohistochemistry.

More CD68+ cells (p = 0.021) were observed at the hydrogel scaffold edge, near the native bone interface, compared to the radial center at day 3 (Fig. 6a, 6b). Over time (day 3 to 7), the cell distribution became more homogenous, and there was no difference (p = 0.605) between the amount of CD68+ cells at the edge versus the middle of the scaffold at day 7.

IHC staining analysis. a) Representative images for cell markers CD68+ (green), iNOS+ (magenta), and CD206+ (cyan) to predict amounts of all macrophages, M1, and M2 phenotypes, respectively. DAPI stain (blue) was co-stained for each condition. Day 3 (upper) and day 7 (lower) images represent macrophage populations at the edge (E) and middle (M) regions of scaffolds. Percent area of b) CD68+, c) iNOS+, and d) CD206+ normalized to total cellular area (% area DAPI). All graphs are mean ± SE.

To examine the macrophage polarization, day 3 and day 7 explant sections were immunostained for the M1 macrophage marker iNOS and the M2 macrophage marker CD206. With respect to the M1 macrophages, there was no difference in the amount of iNOS+ cells at the scaffold edges compared to the center region at day 3 (p = 0.676) or day 7 (p = 0.905). Additionally, there was no change in the amount of iNOS+ cells between day 3 and day 7 samples for either the middle (p = 0.631) or edge (p = 0.441) regions of the scaffolds (Fig. 6a and c). Day 3 post-implantation samples showed infiltration of cells labelled with the M2 macrophage marker CD206 into scaffolds, with more CD206+cells in the edge regions than the middle regions of the scaffold (p = 0.003). Between day 3 and 7 samples, the amount of CD206+ cells at the scaffold edge were unchanged (p = 0.438), but the amount of CD206+ cells in the middle region of scaffolds increased (p < 0.0001), with more CD206+ cells in the middle region of scaffolds compared to the edge (p = 0.0006) at day 7 post-implantation (Fig. 6a, 6d).

Discussion

Maintaining the survival of exogenous stem cells delivered in vivo has proven challenging [3], and has limited the efficacy of MSC therapies for clinical applications [1,3,8], where cells are most commonly delivered via injection. Beyond the challenges of the adverse pro-inflammatory environment following injury, delivering cells via injection can further reduce viability due to the shear stress of the injection. Additionally, many of the MSCs delivered systemically are cleared from the circulation before they can home to the injury site [29]. Implantable bioengineered scaffolds for exogenous cell delivery can aid in cell survival in vivo, however, methods to track and quantify the survival of exogenously delivered MSCs are critical for making further advances in this field. In this work, an innovative PEG granular hydrogel scaffold designed to promote the survival of exogenously delivered MSCs while also allowing for the infiltration of endogenous cells, including macrophages, during the early healing stages of bone healing was assessed. By day 3 post-implantation, endogenous cells had infiltrated and were homogenously distributed throughout scaffolds, with no change by day 7 post-implantation. Of the ~750,000 exogenous MSCs implanted into calvarial defects, we found ~131% and ~13% of MSCs (including both live and dead cells) persisted in the scaffolds at 3- and 7-days post-implantation, respectively. Of those MSCs persisting in the scaffolds, ~27% and ~8% survived at 3- and 7-days post-implantation, respectively. These are favorable survival rates compared to locally administered MSCs via injection, which typically have < 5% cell retention at the injury site 7 days after delivery [3,28,31–33], with a majority accumulating in organs including the lungs, liver, and spleen [29,31–33].

In this work, single cell tracking of fluorescently labeled exogenous MSCs was not possible due to the intense clustering of cells both in vitro and in vivo, preventing distinction of individual cell boundaries. However, the relative fluorescent volume served as a reliable measure for the quantification of the amount of cells at each time point. Additionally, autofluorescence and newly formed connective tissue penetrating scaffolds in vivo limited imaging depth. However, we were still able to capture fluorescent data to a depth of 100–245 µm on the inferior side of scaffolds, an advantage over traditional confocal microscopy [34]. The amount of live MSCs was quantified as the overlapping fluorescence of QD-labeled MSCs and Calcein AM staining, however, it’s likely that colocalization of these two fluorophores is not 100%, raising the possibility the quantification of live MSCs populations were underestimated and quantification of dead MSCs and endogenous cell populations were overestimated. To address this limitation, colocalization was maximized [35] by choice of fluorophores and microscope parameters (i.e., well-separated emission spectra, optimized emission filters, and sequential scanning of fluorescent channels). Future studies utilizing this imaging technique could be improved by utilizing a longitudinal design with the use of a cranial window that allows non-invasive repeated imaging events over time on individual animals [36].

In contrast to bulk hydrogels, the use of porous granular hydrogel scaffolds allows for the infiltration of endogenous cells, endogenous-exogenous cellular interactions, ingrowth of new blood vessels, and increased nutrient exchange. Exogenous MSCs have the capacity not only to differentiate into bone forming osteoblasts to aid in osteogenesis, but also the ability to regulate immune cells by secreting anti-inflammatory factors [37,38]; specifically macrophages, which are crucial for tissue homeostasis after injury [37]. MSCs can regulate the polarization, phagocytosis, and metabolism of macrophages. MSCs can increase polarization of macrophages to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [37,39]. Additionally, macrophages have been shown to increase the proliferation of MSCs [37,40], likely aiding in the survival of exogenously delivered cells. In this work, 3 days post-implantation, endogenous cells had infiltrated scaffolds, including M1- and M2-like macrophages identified by IHC (iNOS+ and CD206+ cells, respectively). Interestingly, at day 3 post-implantation there were more CD206+ cells near the edge of scaffolds compared to the middle and at day 7 post-implantation there were more CD206+ cells in the middle compared to the edge. Although no interrelation was observed between endogenous M2 macrophages and exogenous MSC position within hydrogel scaffolds in vivo, this demonstrates the multiphoton technique combined with IHC can be used to assess endogenous cell infiltration into porous hydrogel scaffolds and endogenous-exogenous cellular interactions. Future studies can build on this by evaluating infiltration of other endogenous cell types of interest via IHC of other specific cell markers.

Previous studies have tracked delivered stem cells for organ repair, including the heart [6,28,41], kidney [29], and liver [31,42] with imaging modalities including BLI [6,29,31], MRI [28,29,42], PET [41], and confocal microscopy [43]. One study tracked the retention of delivered bone marrow MSCs in a calvarial bone defect [30]. That study utilized BLI to track 5 × 106 MSCs locally injected into the calvarial defect space 2 weeks after defect creation. Exogenous cells were retained at the site of injection for 12 days. This is a longer retention period than other studies (< 10 days) [3] and may be attributed to local injection of a large number of cells (5 × 106) possibly resulting in decreased clearance by the circulation and/or reduced immunological stress since the cells were delivered 2 weeks after surgery, which is after the end of the typical inflammatory response. Both BLI and MRI have limitations for tracking exogenous cell delivery including poor spatial resolution and inability to distinguish live from dead cells. The labeling of MSCs with QDs coupled with multiphoton imaging is advantageous because it provides micron-level resolution and QDs have resistance to fluorescent bleaching over long periods of time as well as chemical and metabolic degradation [21], where QDs have been tracked up to several months following implantation in vivo [44,45]. QDs have narrow band emission, broadband excitation, and high emission wavelengths (525–800 nm) to avert artifact from tissue autofluorescence, a common hindrance with in vivo and ex vivo imaging. The technique used here also provides a way to distinguish live from dead exogenous cells using secondary staining with Calcein AM, allowing quantification of MSC survival.

Despite the convenience of cell delivery via injection, poor cell retention reduces the therapeutic effect of treatment leading to poor clinical outcomes [1,3,8]. The use of bioengineered scaffolds, like those utilized in this study, can aid in the retention and survival of delivered stem cells by mitigating effects of adverse biochemical, mechanical, and immunological stress upon implantation. Multiphoton microscopy allowed three-dimensional tracking of QD-labeled MSCs and endogenous cell infiltration into hydrogel scaffolds at 3- and 7-days post-implantation, where ~27% and ~8% of delivered MSCs were viable, respectively. The amount of live MSCs delivered in vivo decreased over time, likely due to the inflammatory microenvironment. Similarly, the amount of dead MSCs decreased over time post-implantation, likely due to clearance by phagocytic cells [46,47]. In contrast, in vitro, the amount of live and dead MSCs increased over time, accumulating within the pore spaces of granular hydrogel scaffolds. Observations of clustering may aid in understanding MSC function given previous literature has reported MSCs in large clusters (~30 cells/cluster) compared to small clusters (~5 cells/cluster) has increased global secretion of both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines [16]. In this work, multiphoton microscopy and IHC provided reliable approaches for quantifying the distribution of exogenous MSCs and endogenous macrophages throughout scaffolds, respectively. In future work, the design and formulation of hydrogel scaffolds could be further functionalized with factors to improve MSC survival, resolve inflammation, increase angiogenesis, and further support endogenous cell infiltration into scaffolds to aid bone healing. Additionally, the technique proposed here could be used for a comparative analysis of other biomaterials including bulk hydrogels and decellularized matrices for their efficacy in aiding stem cell survival post-implantation. The evaluation of different biomaterials could help identify specific materials or fabrication techniques to be advantageous, which could be further modified to better protect cells, prolong their survival, and therefore increase their therapeutic efficacy. A better understanding of the in vivo microenvironment and endogenous-exogenous cellular interactions post-implantation can help inform modulations in biomaterial design for bone regeneration applications or regeneration of other tissue types.
