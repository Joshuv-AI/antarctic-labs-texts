Comprehension, viewpoint and precautionary measures against covid-19 among pregnant women attending ante-natal care at a tertiary health facility in Abuja, Nigeria
Authors: Fatima Inusa Anga, Habeebah T. Bakare, Chris O. Ifediora, Halima Inusa Anga, Aminat Suleman-Alabi, Amina Ibrahim Shehu, Aisha Mukhtar Dodo, Shefa’atu Shehu, Karima Abdulaziz, Aishat O AbdulMojeed, Tijani Salami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is a respiratory tract infection caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2). Pregnant women are particularly vulnerable to COVID-19 due to physiological and immunological changes in pregnancy. Understanding their knowledge and preventive practices is essential for guiding maternal-health interventions. This study assessed the knowledge, perceptions, and adoption of preventive measures against COVID-19 among pregnant women attending antenatal care at the Federal Medical Centre (FMC), Jabi, Abuja.MethodA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted from July to September 2021 among pregnant women selected through multistage sampling. Using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire we obtained data on knowledge, perceptions and preventive practices from 120 women participated. Data was analyzed using SPSS version 28 and summarized as descriptive statistics.ResultsAwareness of COVID-19 was high (95.8%) among pregnant women. Most respondents demonstrated adequate knowledge of transmission and prevention, including hand hygiene (99.2%), avoidance of crowds (92.5%), and isolation measures (90.0%). Misconceptions persisted, particularly regarding breastfeeding. Only 35.0% agreed that COVID-19 positive mothers can safely breastfeed, while 42.5% were unsure. Vaccine willingness was moderate (54.2%), reflecting hesitancy despite overall good knowledge.ConclusionWe found high level of awareness and generally positive preventive attitudes toward COVID-19 among pregnant women, but gaps remain in breastfeeding knowledge and vaccine acceptance. Targeted health education and strengthened ANC counselling are needed to address misconceptions and support consistent adoption of preventive practices.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is a respiratory tract infection caused by the novel severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2; formerly called 2019-nCoV) [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) declared the disease a global pandemic on 11th March 2020 after its initial detection in Wuhan City, Hubei Province, China [2]. COVID-19 is transmitted primarily through respiratory droplets and aerosols, making it highly contagious [2]. Most infect individuals typically develop mild to moderate symptoms, such as fever, dry cough, and fatigue, while others present with less common symptoms including sore throat, vomiting, diarrhoea, and conjunctivitis [1]. Approximately 13.8% of infected persons develop severe disease, with about 6.1% requiring respiratory support [1].

The pandemic has alarmingly overwhelmed hospital capacities and healthcare resources globally, particularly in Africa and other regions with limited health infrastructure and resources. Although the development of vaccines and improved public awareness have led to a decrease in the disease severity and mortality, several African countries continue to report an increase in infection, partly due to misinformation, poor understanding of the disease, inconsistent public health practices, and limited implementation of preventive measures [3].

Pregnant women are particularly at high risk of contracting this disease as the immunological and physiological changes that occur during pregnancy increase susceptibility to respiratory pathogens and may worsen the clinical course of viral infections [3]. Therefore, a clear understanding of COVID-19, coupled with positive attitudes and adherence to preventive measures, is essential for safeguarding maternal and fetal health. Proper knowledge of the disease also enables clinicians to provide targeted counselling during antenatal care, clarify misconceptions, and reduce anxiety among pregnant women (Fig. 1).

The WHO recommends several protective measures for pregnant women including adherence to established infection prevention protocols, correct and consistent use of personal protective equipment (PPE), regular hand hygiene, physical distancing, and prompt reporting of symptoms of illness including mental health concerns [3, 5]. Proper use of PPE is particularly critical for preventing both community and nosocomial transmission of COVID-19 [5]. However, adherence to these measures varies widely across countries and is often constrained by resource availability. In Nigeria, Africa’s most populous country with over 200 million people, access to PPE and consistent implementation of guidelines remain challenging [6]. Without improved PPE availability and rational use, public health education, and health-system proactiveness, implementing WHO recommendations may not be fully achievable.

Given these challenges, protecting pregnant women is essential not only for safeguarding maternal and neonatal outcomes but also for maintaining healthcare system resilience during pandemics [7]. Understanding the knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices of pregnant women is therefore crucial for designing effective public health interventions.

This study is a quantitative descriptive cross-sectional survey that examines COVID-19 related knowledge, attitudes, and precautionary measures among pregnant women attending antenatal clinics at the Federal Medical Centre (FMC), Jabi, Abuja.

Statement of the problem

Pregnant women are at a greater risk of contracting COVID-19 due to pregnancy-related immunological and physiological changes. Previous studies have shown that there is an increased risk of adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes, including preterm birth, thrombotic complications and preeclampsia, among pregnant women infected with COVID-19 [8, 9]. These complications place additional strain on already burdened health systems and pose significant risks to both mothers and infants. Consequently, a comprehensive understanding of COVID-19 among pregnant women is vital for reducing transmission and improving health outcomes.

Justification

COVID-19 is a relatively new infectious disease, and its epidemiological and clinical characteristics continue to evolve. Although global research has expanded rapidly, studies examining the knowledge, attitudes, and preventive behaviours of pregnant women, particularly within Nigeria, remain limited. Furthermore, variations in cultural practices, health-seeking behaviours, and public health messaging across different regions may influence how populations perceive and respond to the disease.

In Nigeria, the pandemic placed considerable strain on the healthcare system, including antenatal services. Pregnant women represent a sensitive and high-risk group due to physiological and immunological changes in pregnancy. Understanding how they perceive COVID-19 and the preventive measures recommended during ANC visits is essential for designing effective public-health communication, strengthening risk-reduction strategies and improving the quality of maternal counselling.

This study therefore aims to assess the knowledge, perceptions and adoption of preventive measures against COVID-19 among pregnant women attending antenatal care at a tertiary health facility in Abuja, Nigeria. The findings are intended to support targeted health education and inform strategies that enhance pandemic preparedness within maternal healthcare services.

Aim of the study

This study aims to evaluate the understanding, perspectives, and adoption of preventive measures against COVID-19 among pregnant women receiving antenatal care at Federal Medical Center (FMC) Jabi, Abuja, Nigeria.

Materials and methods

A prospective descriptive study was conducted from 7th July 2021 to 11th September 2021 among pregnant women attending antenatal clinics at FMC Jabi, Abuja. A multistage probability sampling technique was employed to recruit participants. A minimum sample size of 80 was determined based on a similar study by Goudah et al. [7]. However, we enrolled 120 pregnant women who provided informed consent, to improve statistical power and account for possible non-responses. Women at any trimester of pregnancy were eligible to participate. However, trimester data were not collected.

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire designed to assess knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices related to COVID-19. The study sampled physically challenged respondents including those with visual or hearing impairment. A medical assistant who is a specialist in sign language was assigned to administer the study tool to these respondents.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the FMC Jabi Ethical and Health Research Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 28 (IBM, Armonk, New York, United States). Descriptive statistics such as frequencies and percentages were used to summarize variables. The composite knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) scores were analysed and results are presented in tables and charts.

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic profile of the 120 pregnant women attending ANC at FMC Jabi, Abuja, between July and September 2021. Most respondents were between 26 and 35 years (41.7%) and 18–25 years (33.3%). Ethnic representation was relatively balanced among Hausa (29.2%), Igbo (29.2%), and Yoruba (25.0%), with 16.7% belonging to other ethnic groups. The overwhelming majority were married (95.8%), and notably, 90.0% had tertiary education, reflecting the urban and relatively advantaged catchment population of this facility.

Over half of the respondents were employed (58.3%), while 29.2% were self-employed and 12.5% were unemployed. In this study “unemployed” refers to women who are not formally employed outside the home, including full-time housewives. In terms of parity, 71.7% had between one to four children (Para 1–4), while 28.3% had Para ≥ 5.

Stratified analysis of knowledge and preventive practices by sociodemographic characteristics

To further explore contextual factors associated with COVID-19 knowledge and preventive practices, stratified analyses were conducted according to education level, employment status, and parity.

Respondents with tertiary education demonstrated consistently higher awareness and more accurate understanding of COVID-19 transmission and preventive measures compared with those having secondary or primary education. Nearly all tertiary-educated respondents correctly identified respiratory droplets as a major route of transmission and acknowledged the importance of hand hygiene, avoidance of crowds, and isolation measures.

Similarly, employed and self-employed women demonstrated comparatively greater acceptance of preventive practices, including willingness to isolate and comply with public-health recommendations, compared with unemployed respondents. Vaccine acceptance was also relatively higher among women with tertiary education and those who were employed.

Parity-related differences were modest. Women with lower parity (Para 1–4) showed slightly greater acceptance of vaccination and institutional isolation measures compared with women of higher parity (Para ≥ 5), although awareness levels remained generally high across both groups.

Overall, the stratified analysis suggests that educational attainment and employment status may influence COVID-19-related comprehension and precautionary behaviour among pregnant women attending antenatal care.

Perception of covid-19

Table 2 summarizes respondents’ perceptions of COVID-19. A large proportion (94.2%) believed the disease is harmful, and 90.8% correctly identified respiratory droplets as a transmission route. Some misconceptions exist, with 5.8% believing insects could transmit the virus. A strong majority demonstrated correct understanding of prevention measures. Almost all respondents (99.2%) agreed that hand hygiene can prevent the spread of the virus, 92.5% acknowledged the importance of avoiding crowds, and 90% agreed that isolation and treatment are effective in reducing disease spread. However, only 35.0% believed breastfeeding is safe even when the mother is COVID-positive, and 42.5% were unsure, reflecting significant knowledge gaps.

Acceptance of home or institutional isolation measures was relatively high, at 67.5%. Additionally, 54.2% of the participants indicated that they would accept vaccines if they were proven safe during pregnancy. This conditional vaccine acceptance, although moderate, indicates hesitancy despite expressed trust in other prevention measures. More than half (54.2%) also believed the Nigerian government had contributed to reducing the spread of COVID-19, referencing interventions such as lockdowns, provision of facemasks, and national public-health messaging.

Discussion

The study reports a 100% response rate, which strengthens internal validity and suggests strong participant engagement with antenatal health services. Awareness of COVID-19 was exceptionally high (95.8%), consistent with findings from Nigeria, Singapore, Iran and India [10–13]. This high awareness likely reflects urban setting and the robust public health messaging and intensive government-led communication campaigns amid the pandemic.

Although prior studies such as Lee et al. conducted in Singapore identified sociodemographic factors including age above 36 years, ethnicity, frontline employment, and attendance at high-risk clinics as factors influencing attitudes and precautionary behaviours toward COVID-19 [11], our stratified analysis similarly demonstrated that educational attainment and employment status were associated with better comprehension and adoption of preventive measures [13]. Women with tertiary education and those who were employed or self-employed showed comparatively better understanding of transmission routes, preventive practices, and greater acceptance of public-health measures including isolation and vaccination. These findings support existing evidence that higher educational exposure and socioeconomic empowerment may positively influence health literacy and adherence to preventive behaviours during infectious disease outbreaks.

The majority (94.2%) recognized COVID-19 as harmful and most understood its primary transmission routes and preventive measures. Similar findings from Abakaliki (Chidebe et al.) reinforce the role of mass media as a powerful source of health information in Nigeriae [10]. However, persistent misconceptions such as belief in insect transmission demonstrate that even well-educated populations may retain pockets of misinformation. These insights underscore the need for continuous, targeted public health messaging.

Breastfeeding misconception

Only 35% of respondents believed a COVID-positive mother can breastfeed safely, despite WHO guidance confirming that breast milk is not a transmission route [14]. The high uncertainty (42.5%) in this study about a COVID-19 mother breastfeeding mirrors findings from Iran, where Maharlouei et al. reported maternal anxiety and uncertainty about neonatal being affected with COVID-19 through childbirth or breast milk [12]. Misunderstanding breastfeeding safety amid pandemics may contribute to premature cessation, risking malnutrition, loss of immunity benefits, and poorer neonatal outcomes. Such misconceptions should be addressed to protect newborns from the potential risks.

Vaccine acceptance

The moderate vaccine willingness highlights persistent hesitancy, consistent with global patterns among pregnant women. This also aligns with patterns observed in several low- and middle-income settings, where vaccine uptake among pregnant women lagged behind general populations. Fear of fetal harm, misinformation, and limited early pandemic safety data may have contributed to the hesitancy.

A 67.5% acceptance of isolation and the moderate vaccine willingness are encouraging, as they contrast with findings from low-resource settings such as in Nwafor et al., where knowledge was adequate but preventive practices were poor [15]. This contrast is likely due to the differences in infrastructure, health system maturity, and socioeconomic characteristics of both study sites and population. Urban settings like Abuja have easier access to credible information and health services, whereas rural women face multiple barriers including lower literacy, reduced access to ANC, limited health-worker counselling, higher exposure to unverified information etc.

Maternal health disruptions during public health emergencies

Evidence from sub-Saharan Africa highlights that COVID-19 disrupted maternal health services and worsened perinatal outcomes, including stillbirths and preterm births [16–19]. These disruptions demonstrate why pregnant women’s knowledge and practices must be monitored during health crises, as gaps in prevention and care-seeking can translate very quickly into adverse outcomes for mothers and newborns. Declines in ANC attendance, misinformation and service disruptions highlight the vital role of healthcare providers as reliable sources of information [16, 17, 18, 20]. Strengthening communication during ANC visits is therefore essential to closing knowledge gaps and supporting the sustained adoption of preventive behaviours.

Policy and public health implications

Strengthening pandemic readiness within maternal health services requires a coordinated approach that reinforces both the health system and community-level interventions. Health system preparedness must be improved to ensure uninterrupted and safe service delivery during public-health emergencies. Policies that ensure continuity of antenatal services at all levels of care will help mitigate care disruptions that contributed to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes across sub-Saharan Africa during COVID-19.

Vaccine education and deployment strategies must prioritize pregnant women by integrating vaccine counselling into routine ANC visits, providing clear evidence-based guidance, and actively countering misinformation. Combining facility-based counselling with community outreach can substantially improve vaccine acceptance and overall trust in maternal healthcare services. Tailored communication strategies, local-language health campaigns, and community health workers can help reduce these gaps and support more equitable pandemic responses.

These measures will enhance the resilience of maternal health systems, reduce preventable complications, and strengthen Nigeria’s capacity to protect pregnant women during future pandemics or public-health emergencies.

Strength of the study

This study is one of the first studies assessing COVID-19 comprehension, viewpoint and precautionary measures among pregnant women in Nigeria. A key strength of this study is the diverse study population including women across different pregnancy stages (multistage) and physically challenged (visually and hearing-impaired) respondents. The high response rate (100%) and diversity of respondents enhanced equity and inclusivity and enriched the findings. Comprehensive assessment of both positive and negative indicators enabled balanced interpretation, minimized speculative responses and enhanced reliability of the findings.

Limitation of the study

The study was conducted in an urban, socioeconomically advantaged population, limiting generalizability of the findings on COVID-19 awareness and practices to rural parts of Nigeria or other resource-limited settings. The study did not explore the severity of COVID-19 among respondents who may have been infected, nor did it include clinical outcome data. Reliance on self-reported data may have introduced risk of social desirability bias and affected measured knowledge and preventive practices in this study. The stratified analyses conducted in this study were exploratory and limited by the relatively small sample size, particularly within some sociodemographic subgroups. The absence of these variables limits the ability to fully interpret observed differences in perceptions, vaccine acceptance, and precautionary practices across participant groups.

Furthermore, because the study relied on self-reported responses, there is a possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias, which may have resulted in overestimation of positive attitudes and preventive practices. These limitations should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings and generalizing them to broader populations.

Recommendations

This study found high levels of COVID-19 comprehension, viewpoint and precautionary measures among pregnant women. However, broader multi-state research including pregnant women not enrolled in formal ANC and those receiving antenatal care in primary health care facilities, is necessary to obtain nationally generalizable insights.

A qualitative study that deeply explores the low breastfeeding knowledge and moderate vaccine acceptance found in this study is essential to inform the design of appropriate counselling interventions. We recommend complementary studies among healthcare workers to understand provider-perspective and provider-driven barriers or facilitators of COVID-19 knowledge and practices among pregnant women.

Healthcare workers and administrators should strengthen routine ANC counselling on COVID-19, breastfeeding safety and vaccine benefits, incorporating culturally sensitive messaging. National and state governments should invest in community outreach programs and mass-media campaigns that correct misconceptions and reinforce preventive practices. Policymakers should integrate maternal health specific preparedness strategies into national emergency response frameworks to ensure greater certainty towards maternal and neonatal protection during future pandemics.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates high awareness and generally positive perceptions of COVID-19 among pregnant women in Abuja, but important gaps persist in breastfeeding knowledge, vaccine acceptance and certain misconceptions about transmission. These gaps highlight the need for strengthened antenatal counselling and targeted public health interventions, especially in rural and underserved communities.

The findings emphasise the need to build resilient health systems capable of delivering clear and accurate information, sustaining services and supporting vulnerable groups during pandemics. The study highlights the need to sustain preparedness capacities and build trust in vaccines. Scaling the study nationwide will enable development of evidence-based maternal health policies that safeguard mothers and newborns during future public health emergencies.
