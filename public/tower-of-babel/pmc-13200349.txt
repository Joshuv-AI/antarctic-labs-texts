Clinical, genetic and bioinformatic analysis of Saudi families with Joubert syndrome and related disorders
Authors: Riman Alafghani, Deema Aljeaid, Noor Ahmad Shaik, Babajan Banaganapalli, Ramu Elango, Ghayah Khard, Wisam Habhab, Ghada Jadkarim, Dalal Sameer Alshaer, Mahmoud Almutadares, Nuha Mohammad Alrayes, Noha M Issa

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJoubert syndrome and related disorders (JSRD) are clinically and genetically heterogeneous ciliopathies caused by pathogenic variants in over 40 genes, mainly encoding ciliary proteins. However, data on JSRD in the Saudi population remain limited. This study aimed to identify novel and reported JSRD-causing variants in Saudi families and analyze their potential functional impact on protein structure using advanced computational tools.MethodsPatients with genetically or clinically confirmed/suspected JSRD were recruited according to ethical protocols. Clinical data were collected, and exome sequencing was performed, followed by Sanger validation of identified variant. Pathogenicity was assessed using bioinformatics tools, while conservation, expression and RNA structure analyses were conducted to evaluate the potential functional consequences.ResultsHomozygous variants were identified in three consanguineous Saudi families, including a novel stop-gain variant in KIF7 (c.2992 C > T; p.(Gln998Ter)), and novel splice-site variant in CEP104 (c.489 + 1G > A) and a previously reported splice-site variant in TMEM237 (c.869 + 1G > A). All variants were predicted to be pathogenic as per ACMG criteria and located in highly conserved regions, suggesting potential functional impact. RNA analysis suggested possible alterations in folding.ConclusionThis study identified three pathogenic variants in JSRD-related genes in Saudi families, including two novel variants. These findings expand the variant spectrum for JSRD, particularly in the Saudi population. Establishing a comprehensive regional variant database is essential for improving molecular diagnosis and genetic counseling in population with high consanguinity. Finally, these results highlight the need for further functional studies to validate their pathogenicity and elucidate their role in JSRD pathogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40246-026-00953-8.Keywords: Joubert Syndrome, JSRD, Ciliopathy, KIF7, CEP104, TMEM237, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Joubert Syndrome and Related Disorders (JSRD) comprise a clinically and genetically heterogeneous neurodevelopmental ciliopathies first described by Dr. Marie Joubert [15, 23]. The population-based prevalence of JSRD can reach up to 1.7:100,000 with an age range of 0–19 years [15, 23].

JSRD are primarily characterized by hypotonia, developmental delay, intellectual disability, and a unique cerebellar and brainstem abnormality known as the molar tooth sign (MTS) [16, 37]. Additional clinical manifestations may include polydactyly and other skeletal abnormalities, congenital hepatic fibrosis, cystic kidney disease, retinal dystrophy, coloboma, breathing dysregulation, as well as endocrine abnormalities. Many of these symptoms are shared with other ciliopathies [3, 7, 10, 25].

Pathogenic variants in over 40 genes have been implicated in JSRD, the majority encode proteins essential for ciliary structure or function. Most JRSD-associated genes exhibit autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, whereas one (OFD1 gene) is X-linked [7, 37, 41]. Consanguinity is common in the Arab world, including Saudi Arabia; it has been estimated to be more than 50% of Saudi marriages, which increases the prevalence of autosomal recessive disorders [8]. Nevertheless, JSRD cases have been reported in both consanguineous and non-consanguineous marriages [19].

A review of published literature reveals a gap in genetic research on JSRD within the Saudi population. To date, approximately 14 new and previously reported pathogenic variants across nine genes (AHI1, CC2D2A, RPG1PL, CEP290, TMEM237, CPLANE1, TCTN1, CSPP1, and ARMC9) have been identified in the Saudi individuals, leaving the contribution of about 31 genes that cause JSRD unexplored.

Existing studies JSRD are predominantly case reports or series, lacking in-depth analysis of how genetic variants disrupt ciliary protein structure and function. This study aimed to identify the molecular etiology of JSRD in Saudi families, define candidate pathogenic variants, and systematically catalog genetic alterations using exome sequencing (ES). Leveraging the genetic homogeneity of the Saudi population resulting from consanguineous marriage, we hypothesized that novel genes, variants, or both may expand the known JSRD mutational spectrum in this understudied cohort. By integrating ES with advanced computational biology approaches, we characterized the structural and functional consequences of candidate variants on ciliary proteins, thereby elucidating their role in disease pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and recruited participants

This study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of King Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAUH) in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia (Reference No 64 − 23). Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. Participants with a confirmed or suspected JSRD were recruited through the Medical Genetic Clinic at King Abdulaziz University Hospital, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. Detailed clinical data and comprehensive family histories were obtained through parental interviews. Multi-generational pedigrees were constructed by using the CeGaT Pedigree Chart Designer tool, and physical examinations were performed by a clinical geneticist.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using the QIAamp DNA mini kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a Nanodrop Spectrophotometer (DeNovex). The DNA integrity was assessed by using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Exome sequencing and variant filtration

The DNA was purified and enriched for the exonic regions using Agilent’s SureSelect Human All Exon V6 kit. Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencing plate form. Primary analysis software (Real-Time analysis) is used to generate the raw images and base-calling on the Illumina platform. BWA software (Li & Durbin, 2009) was used to align the sequence (FASTQ format) with the human genome reference sequence (GRCh37/hg19). The Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) (McKenna et al., 2010) was used for variant calling. The SnpEff program (Cingolani et al., 2012) was used to estimate and predict the functional effects of the variants. All disease-causing variants documented in HGMD®, ClinVar, or CentoMD®, as well as variations with a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 1% in the gnomAD database, were included. The evaluation process primarily focused on coding exons as well as flanking +/−20 intronic bases. Variant interpretation considered inheritance patterns, family history, and clinical phenotype. Rigorous quality control and validation protocols were implemented. Variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics & Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [29].

Sanger sequencing for variant validation

Sanger sequencing was used to validate identified variants. Primers were designed to amplify target regions. The details of the primers are presented in Table 1. PCR products were purified, and Sanger sequencing was performed using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit. Variants were analyzed using SeqStudio Genetic Analyzer. Variants were identified using the BioEdit Sequence Alignment Editor (Alzohairy., 2011).

Computational analysis

To predict the potential impact of variants identified through ES, several in silico tools were employed as supportive evidence for variant interpretation. The Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) [20], Human Splicing Finder (HSF) [7], and SpliceAI Lookup (SpliceAI) [39] were used to model potential pathogenicity and splicing disruptions. Comparative sequence analysis using Ensembl was performed to assess the evolutionary conservation of variant regions, providing an estimate of biological constraint [7, 20]. Expression patterns of affected genes were evaluated using the Human Protein Atlas [34]. Finally, RNAfold and ViennaRNA were utilized to predict the potential impact of variants on RNA secondary structure [13]. RNA structures were used to assess potential effects of variants on transcript stability, and they do not represent the disease-causing mechanism.

Literature review and case identification

To comprehensively identify previously reported cases of JSRD from Saudi Arabia, we conducted a review of the literature. We utilized databases including PubMed, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect and ResearchGate. Our search strategy employed a combination of keywords such as ‘Joubert syndrome related disorder’, ‘Joubert Syndrome’, ‘Ciliopathies’, ‘Saudi Arabia’, ‘Arab’ and ‘Consanguinity’, combined with genetic terms (‘gene’, ‘variant’, ‘mutation’).

Family A

Proband A (IV.4), is a 5-year-old female born at 40 weeks to a then 31-year-old G3P2 + 1 mother whose two other pregnancies were affected by anencephaly and polydactyly (Fig. 1A). Fetal ultrasound at 22 weeks showed an enlarged cisterna magna and an absent corpus callosum. Birth weight was 3.25 kg, length 53 cm, and head circumference 36 cm. She required 5 months of neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) stay for respiratory distress syndrome, pulmonary hypertension that resolved with sildenafil treatment, and seizures. Clinical features included postaxial polydactyly of hands and feet and significant dysmorphic features. She was also diagnosed with hypothyroidism and started on thyroxine, and growth hormone deficiency, which was treated. A brain MRI showed elongated and thickened superior cerebellar peduncles with MTS configuration, associated with corpus callosum dysgenesis (Fig. 1B). Karyotype was 46, XX. Additional clinical findings and imaging result are in Table 2.

Family B

Proband B (IV-12) is a 5-year-old female born at 39 weeks to a then 31-year-old mother from a consanguineous marriage (Fig. 2A). Birth weight was 2.84 kg, length 51 cm, and head circumference 35 cm. She required 20 days of NICU care due to respiratory distress (including hypoxia and hyperpnea). Bronchoscopy was unremarkable. Brain MRI showed a batwing appearance of the fourth ventricle, prominent thickening and elongation of the superior cerebellar peduncles consistent with MTS, and mild diffuse thinning of the corpus callosum (Fig. 2B). The proband has nystagmus and right ptosis of the eye, as well as truncal hypotonia. Karyotype was 46, XX. Further clinical details are in Table 2.

Family C

Proband C (IV.3) is a 13-year-old female born full term to a then 23-year-old mother from a consanguineous marriage (Fig. 3A). Birth weight was 3 kg. She required 45 days of NICU care for respiratory distress, hypotonia, and seizures. A brain MRI at 2 months of age revealed a classic MTS with horizontal orientation of the superior cerebellar peduncles and a widened batwing configuration of the fourth ventricle with hyperplastic cerebellar vermis (Fig. 3B). Ophthalmic evaluation revealed bilateral optic atrophy and microcornea. Abdominal and renal ultrasound revealed bilateral cysts consistent with nephronophthisis/juvenile medullary cystic disease complex, and early hepatic fibrosis. At 10 months, she displayed failure to thrive and feeding difficulties, necessitating a gastrostomy tube until age 3. Notably, she had multiple hospital admissions with recurrent respiratory infections. Clinical findings and imaging result are summarized in Table 2.

Genetic data

Exome sequencing data was obtained for probands from families A, B, and C. Variants were filtered to include only those with a quality score > 30, a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 in gnomAD, and those located within coding, exon, non-synonymous, or flanking ± 20 bp intronic regions. Homozygous variants specific to the proband were prioritized, and ~ 50 variants were further filtered based on pathogenicity prediction tools. Disease-causing variants were identified by focusing on known JSRD-associated genes.

Homozygous variants in three JSRD-associated genes were identified in the probands of families A, B, and C (Table 3; Figs. 1C, 2C and 3C). In family A, a stop-gain variant, KIF7 c.2992 C > T (p.Gln998*), results in truncation of the KIF7 protein, which is involved in SHH signaling and ciliary function. In family B, a donor splice-site variant, CEP104 c.489 + 1G > A, is predicted to disrupt splicing of the CEP104 gene, which encodes a centrosomal protein essential for cilia elongation and WNT signaling. In family C, a donor splice-site variant, TMEM237 c.869 + 1G > A, is predicted to affect splicing of the TMEM237 gene, which encodes a transmembrane protein involved in ciliogenesis and transition zone function. All three variants are classified as pathogenic according to ACMG criteria and in silico evidence [29] (Table 3).

Sanger sequencing validated the identified variants in the probands and their available parents (Figs. 1D, 2E and 3E). Primers were designed and optimized for PCR amplification, with optimal annealing temperatures were determined to be 60 °C for KIF7, 64 °C for CEP104, and 59 °C for TMEM237. Homozygous variants were confirmed in the probands of all three families: KIF7 c.2992 C > T variant in family A, CEP104 c.489 + 1G > A variant in family B, and TMEM237 c.869 + 1G > A variant in family C.

The parental genotypes, where available, were consistent with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern.

Computational analysis

VEP tool was utilized to predict the potential pathogenicity of the stop-gained KIF7 variant, which was categorized as damaging based on its predicted truncation of the protein product. To assess potential splicing disruptions, HSF and SpliceAI tools were employed to analyze splice site variants in CEP104 and TMEM237. These tools predicted significant alterations in splice site scores as depicted in Figs. 2D and 3D; Table 3.

Sequence alignment of the KIF7, CEP104, and TMEM237 nucleotide sequences demonstrated a high degree of conservation of the identified variants and their flanking regions across primate species as illustrated (Figs. 1E, 2F and 3F). This suggests that these variants may have significant impact on the structure and function of the respective proteins.

Gene expression analysis indicated that KIF7, CEP104, and TMEM237 are expressed in various tissues, with specific patterns in the reproductive system, nervous system, and retina, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 1–3). RNA secondary structure analysis using RNAfold and ViennaRNA suggested that the KIF7 c.2992 C > T and TMEM237 c.869 + 1G > A variants may lead to minor conformational changes, with slight differences in minimum free energy (MFE) compared to their wild-type alleles. In contrast, the CEP104 c.489 + 1G > A variant showed a more substantial predicted change in MFE (−99.48 vs. −102.86 kcal/mol), suggesting a potential impact on RNA secondary structure stability (Figs. 1F, 2G and 3G).

Discussion

Molecular diagnostics have revolutionized the diagnosis and management of JSRD, facilitating accurate genetic counseling, prenatal testing, preimplantation genetic diagnosis, while also improving disease characterization and genotype-phenotype correlation. Although there are currently no approved gene-specific or personalized therapies for JSRD, molecular diagnosis represents a critical prerequisite future therapeutic strategies by enabling the identification of molecular targets and forming phenotype-specific surveillance. Experimental studies in related retinal ciliopathies, including intravitreal antisense oligonucleotide therapy and CRISPR/Cas9-based gene-editing approaches targeting the prevalent CEP290 variant, a gene which is also implicated in JSRD, have demonstrated restoring retinal function in human and animal models, highlighting progress toward mutation-specific interventions [41, 42]. Furthermore, advances in the molecular understanding of ciliopathies may provide a strong rationale for the future of development of gene-based therapies for JSRD [43, 45]. Early diagnosis is critical, as it improves clinical outcomes, reduces unnecessary diagnostic procedures and therapeutic interventions, optimizes healthcare resources, and supports informed family planning [24, 28, 32]. Furthermore, delineating genotype-phenotype correlations may pave the way for tailored therapeutic approaches for JRSD [24, 28, 32]. However, JSRD remains understudied in the Saudi population, with only 14 pathogenic variants across nine genes reported to date (Table 4). These findings highlight the genetic heterogeneity of JSRD in this population, further compounded by consanguinity-driven homozygosity.

Summary of previous JSRD studies in Saudi Arabia

The current study expands the mutational spectrum of JSRD in Saudi Arabia by identifying two novel and one previously reported pathogenic variants in KIF7, CEP104, and TMEM237 genes, respectively, across three consanguineous families, underscoring the utility of ES and computational modeling in elucidating genotype-phenotype correlations.

In family A, we identified a novel stop-gain variant in the KIF7 gene (NM_198525.3): c.2992 C > T; p.(Gln998Ter), predicted to introduce a premature stop codon and classified as pathogenic according to the ACMG guidelines and supported by in silico evidence [29]. Biallelic pathogenic variants in the KIF7 gene (OMIM# 611254) are known to cause Joubert syndrome type 12 and acrocallosal syndrome (OMIM# 200990), both autosomal recessive disorders ciliopathies with overlapping phenotypes. KIF7 (Kinesin Family Member 7) gene encodes a cilia-associated protein that regulates Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway and contributes to microtube acetylation and stabilization [10]. Disruption of KIF7, particularly through truncating variants, impairs ciliary trafficking and signaling, leading to developmental defects. Consistent with human phenotypes, KIF7-deficient murine models exhibit skeletal defects, polydactyly, exencephaly and premature death, all reflecting Hh pathway dysregulation [6, 13, 17]. These findings support the role of KIF7 dysfunction in JSRD pathogenesis.

Four prior studies have reported pathogenic KIF7 variant in JSRD, demonstrating notable genetic and phenotypic variability. Dafinger et al., [10] first identified a homozygous deletion, c.217delG; p.(Ala73Profs*109) variants in an Egyptian family presenting with MTS, agenesis of corpus callosum, intellectual disability, ataxia and polydactyly [10]. Barakeh et al., [6] subsequently reported three individuals with homozygous truncating KIF7 variants displaying variable ciliopathy features; one with typical acrocallosal syndrome c.2272G > T; p.(Glu758Ter), one with JSRD phenotype and classic MTS, c.3331 C > T; p.(Arg1111Ter), and one lacking the typical ciliopathy features c.2896_2897del; p.(Ala966Profs*81) but showing dysgenesis of corpus callosum and intellectual disability [6]. In a larger cohort, Asadollahi et al., [5] identified pathogenic KIF7 variants in five of nine JRSD families, including two Turkish siblings with a homozygous frameshift variant c.1643dup; p.(Arg549Alafs*40), presenting with MTS, corpus callosum dysgenesis and postaxial polydactyly. and a Palestinian family with a stop-gain variant c.2164G > T; p.(Glu722Ter), associated with corpus callosum hypoplasia in the absence of MTS or polydactyly. The remaining families presented with a combination of intellectual disability, developmental delay, hypotonia, dysgenesis of corpus callosum, and dysmorphism with postaxial polydactyly [5]. Niceta et al., [22] further described an individual with concurrent KIF7 c.2675G > A: p.(Arg892His) and KIAA0556 variants who exhibited JSRD phenotype with endocrine abnormalities, resembling our proband’s endocrine and neurodevelopmental features [22]. The clinical overlap with between our proband and previously reported cases underscores the marked phenotypic heterogeneity of KIF7-related ciliopathies (e.g., presence/absence of polydactyly), likely reflecting differences in residual protein function or modifier genes effects [22].

In Family B, we identified a novel splice-site variant in the CEP104 gene (NM_014704.4):c.489 + 1G > A, predicted to disrupt the conserved donor splice site of exon 5. This variant is classified as pathogenic per the ACMG guidelines and supported by in silico evidence [29]. Biallelic pathogenic variants in the CEP104 gene (OMIM# 616690) cause autosomal recessive Joubert syndrome type 25 (OMIM# 616781). CEP104 (centrosomal protein 104-KD) gene encodes a centriolar protein essential for cilium elongation and Hh signaling [12], regulating ciliogenesis through microtubule dynamics [38]. Comparative proteomics in Chlamydomonas demonstrated CEP104 localization to centriolar distal ends prior to ciliogenesis and later to elongating ciliary tips, where it is indispensable for ciliogenesis [30].

Previous studies have reported CEP104 splice-site and truncating variants in JSRD. Srour rt al. [37] reported variants such as c.735 + 2 T > C, c.2572–2 A > G, c.496 C > T; p.(Arg166Ter), and c.1328_1329insT; p.(Tyr444Leufs*3) in individuals exhibiting MTS, oculomotor apraxia, developmental delay and in some cases hypotonia [33]. Similarly, Luo et al., [18] reported a Chinese patient with compound heterozygous variants c.2364 + 1G > A and c.414delC; p.(Asn138Lysfs*11), presenting with MTS, global developmental delay, hypotonia, facial dysmorphism, oculomotor apraxia, and hypotonia [18].

In Family C, the proband was found to have a splice-site variant in TMEM237 gene (NM_001044385.3):c.869 + 1G > A, predicted to disrupt the conserved donor splice-site of exon 9. The variant, classified as pathogenic based on the ACMG guidelines [29], was previously reported in a Saudi patient with JSRD [2]. Biallelic pathogenic variants in TMEM237 (OMIM# 614423) gene cause autosomal recessive Joubert syndrome type 14 (OMIM# 614424). TMEM237 (transmembrane protein 237) gene encodes a tetraspanin protein localized to the ciliary transition zone, where is interacts with ciliopathy-associated proteins (e.g., TMEM216) and regulates Wnt/Planar Cell Polarity signaling and ciliogenesis [14, 36]. Disruption of the TMEM237 interferes with Wnt signaling and ciliogenesis, contributing to renal, hepatic, and ocular defects [14, 36], explaining its multisystem impact.

Huang et al., [14] reported multiple TMEM237 variants, including compound heterozygous c.943 + 1G > T and c.76 C > T; p.(Gln26Ter) in a Spanish patient, and homozygous c.52 C > T; p.(Arg18Ter), c.677 + 1G > T, and c.1066dupC; p.(Gln356Profs*23) in Hutterite, Austrian and Jordanian patients, respectively. Clinical features across these cases included MTS, strabismus, nystagmus, cystic kidney, Dandy-Walker variant, encephalocele, hydrocephalus, posterior fossa abnormality, and cortical visual impairment [14]. The c.869 + 1G > A splice-site variant, identified in family C, was previously reported in a consanguineous Saudi family, causing exon 9 skipping resulting in an in-frame deletion of 64 amino acids in the mature mRNA, as by confirmed by reverse transcription PCR [2]. The reported patient had bilateral increased renal parenchymal echogenicity, delayed psychomotor development, intellectual disability, ataxia, hypotonia, and typical MRI findings [2]. In comparison, our proband shared similar features including the neurodevelopmental involvement and renal disease; however, the clinical presentation was broader and more severe. Notably, our patient exhibited chronic lung disease, cystic dysplastic kidneys/nephronophthisis, early hepatic fibrosis, esotropia, bilateral optic nerve atrophy, and bilateral microcornea, thus expanding the phenotypic spectrum of TMEM237-related JSRD. Finally, Cui et al., [9] reported a Chinese family with JRSD carrying have compound heterozygous variants c.175 C > T; p.(Arg59Ter) and c.553 + 1G > A [9].

The study was limited to three families with JSRD, which restrict the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the absence of data from siblings and extended family members hindered a more comprehensive assessment of familial inheritance patterns. Furthermore, the unavailability of fresh blood samples from Family C prevented definitive confirmation of the variant inheritance pattern. Finally, potential non-genetic contributors, such as environmental influences and genetic modifiers, were not addressed in this study. Future studies involving larger cohorts, combined with functional analyses are warranted to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying these variants and their contribution to JRSD pathogenesis.

Conclusion

This study revealed the genetic basis of JSRD in three consanguineous Saudi families. Exome sequencing identified a novel homozygous stop-gain pathogenic variant in KIF7 gene, a novel homozygous splice-site pathogenic variant in CEP104 gene, and a previously reported homozygous splice-site pathogenic variant in TMEM237 gene. All variants were classified according to ACMG criteria and supported by in silico evidence. Segregation analysis by Sanger sequencing confirmed homozygosity in the probands and heterozygosity in the parents. Computational analyses predicted that these variants may affect the formation and folding pattern of the secondary structure of RNA; however, further functional studies are needed to fully elucidate the biological impact of these variants. These findings expand the spectrum of the JSRD-associated variants, particularly in the Saudi population, and provide valuable insights into the genetic landscape of this disorder. In this context, establishing a comprehensive regional variant database is essential for improving molecular diagnosis and genetic counseling in population with high consanguinity.
