Do process evaluations open up the ‘black box’ of implementation interventions in health care? A scoping review
Authors: Ella L. Bracci, Sarah Collyer, Candice Oster, Elizabeth Lynch, Temi Olasunkanmi-Alimi, Lemma N. Bulto, Gillian Harvey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProcess evaluations are considered an essential component in conducting and reporting complex interventions, such as those studied in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) of implementation interventions, to explain the effect of implementation interventions. Given the growth of RCTs of implementation interventions with embedded process evaluations, it is timely to review the explanatory learnings to date. This scoping review explores process evaluations of RCTs of implementation interventions to examine how studies are conducted and what insights can be offered about how and why implementation interventions achieve (or not) their intended impacts.MethodsThe scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology. MEDLINE, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science and PsycINFO were searched. Articles were screened and data were extracted by two independent reviewers.ResultsOf the 5857 studies screened, 81 process evaluations were included. Two process evaluations reported on the same trial, resulting in a final number of n = 80 independent studies. Half of studies (48%) reported on implementation trials with no demonstrated effect on the primary outcome (null), while n = 32 (40%) reported on trials where the intervention group demonstrated positive changes in the primary outcome (positive). Seven studies (9%) had mixed findings and n = 3 (4%) studies had no reported trial outcomes. When comparing process evaluation findings from positive and null trials, few discernible patterns that clearly explained the difference in outcomes were identified. Education and training was the most common strategy used in implementation interventions, yet one of the most common implementation barriers reported related to knowledge and self-efficacy, which could indicate a misalignment. Availability of resources was the most prominent barrier for both positive and null trials and there was little evidence that implementation interventions were tailored to context despite prominent barriers and enablers at the inner and outer setting level.ConclusionsProcess evaluation studies embedded in RCTs of implementation interventions are recommended as an important method to explain whether and how interventions produce their intended effect. This review suggests a need to further optimise the design and evaluation of implementation interventions, including the conduct and reporting of process evaluations, to continue advancing the science and practice of implementation.Trial registrationProtocol published in Open Science Framework, May 10 2022 (Collyer et al., Process evaluations in randomised trials of implementation interventions in health care: a scoping review protocol. Background

Advances in healthcare are hampered by the lack of application of evidence into routine practice, the so-called evidence-practice gap [1]. The field of implementation science, defined as “the scientific study of methods to promote the systematic uptake of research findings and other evidence-based practices into routine practice” [2] was developed in response to this recognised gap [1–3]. The last 15–20 years has seen an increase in research to develop and test interventions that are designed to increase the uptake of research evidence into practice [4]. Implementation interventions (that is, the interventions designed to enhance the uptake of research evidence) can be discrete, using a singular strategy (e.g., education or printed information) or, more commonly, complex and multifaceted, combining two or more strategies (e.g., training plus audit and feedback). To enhance replicability and create a common language for researchers using implementation interventions, 73 discrete implementation strategies have been identified [5], clustered into 9 themes: evaluative and iterative strategies; providing interactive assistance; adapting and tailoring to context; developing stakeholder interrelationships; training and educating stakeholders; supporting clinicians; engaging consumers; utilising financial strategies; and changing infrastructure [6].

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are the preferred methodology to determine the effectiveness of implementation interventions (hereafter referred to as ‘implementation trials’ [4]. When conducting the RCT, researchers are interested in evaluating the intervention to determine whether it achieved the intended effect in terms of enhancing implementation, changing clinical practice and/or patient or population outcomes. An increasingly common approach is the use of hybrid designs that assess both clinical and implementation effectiveness within the same study, with Type 3 hybrid trials having a primary focus on the effectiveness of the implementation intervention [7, 8]. Eight distinct categories of implementation outcomes have been described; acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, penetration, and sustainability [9].

Within implementation trials, embedded process evaluations are recommended to help explain whether and how an implementation intervention has worked, including examining the quality and fidelity of implementation, mechanisms of change and barriers and enablers of implementation [10, 11]. In line with guidance on the design and evaluation of complex interventions, implementation theories and frameworks can help to plan and conduct the implementation trial and process evaluation, for example, by identifying determinant factors that could influence implementation processes and outcomes, such as characteristics of the intervention and the people involved in implementation, contextual factors, and the way in which the process of implementation was conducted [12]. In the case of implementation interventions, the implementation trial examines whether the implementation intervention was effective, whilst a process evaluation seeks to understand whether the way in which the intervention was implemented (as reported in terms of implementation processes, barriers, enablers, and outcomes) can explain the observed trial outcomes (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Overview of implementation interventions and process evaluations

Despite significant advances in the field of implementation science over the last 15–20 years, a persistent evidence-practice gap in healthcare remains. Studies assessing the appropriateness of healthcare delivery from 1998 [13] through to 2022 [14], across countries including the United States, Canada and Australia [15], consistently report a 30–40 per cent gap between known evidence-based clinical recommendations and what happens in practice, highlighting the importance of applying the findings of implementation research in practice [1, 16]. Process evaluations of implementation trials have the potential to provide important learning about why implementation interventions succeed or fail. In turn, this could help inform the future design and evaluation of implementation studies and optimise the effectiveness and impact of implementation interventions.

Several reviews of process evaluations have been conducted that focus on specific clinical interventions (e.g., for chronic disease [17] and sedentary behaviour [18]), and/or settings (e.g., primary care [19], hospital settings, [20] and low and middle income countries [21]). With a more specific implementation focus, Scott and colleagues [22] conducted a systematic review of the use of process evaluations in knowledge translation research. This review, published in 2019, aimed to synthesise the evidence on knowledge translation process evaluations to examine the ‘state of the science’, and provide future methodological guidance [22]. Studies were not excluded based on the original research design, but the process evaluation had to be reporting on a project that aimed to translate evidence into practice using a defined knowledge translation strategy or intervention to support implementation [22]. The review included 226 studies, the majority of which involved a qualitative process evaluation methodology and post-intervention data collection. Only 38.1% of included studies were informed by theory and the authors concluded that methodological rigour was often lacking in the process evaluation studies they reviewed [22]. The review did not examine whether the reported findings helped to explain the success or failure of the knowledge translation intervention that was the focus of the process evaluation [22].

Other systematic reviews have examined process evaluations within pragmatic RCTs [23] and the use of theory in process evaluations conducted alongside randomised trials of implementation interventions [24]. Common messages from these prior reviews include inconsistent labelling and reporting of process evaluations [18, 20, 23], a need for better and more consistent application of relevant theory [24], greater use of logic models to inform study design and evaluation [18] and a need for better integration of process and outcome data [20]. The review conducted by McIntyre and colleagues commented on the wide variability of outcomes from implementation trials, with limited reporting and poor understanding of the reasons for this variation [24].

The aim of this scoping review is to explore the conduct and findings of process evaluations undertaken alongside RCTs of implementation interventions in health care. Specifically, we were interested to explore what we could learn from published process evaluations about whether, how and why implementation interventions achieved (or not) their intended impacts, a question that previous reviews have not explicitly examined.

Methods

Protocol

The scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews [25] and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [26]. The protocol for this review was registered and published on Open Science Framework [27].

Protocol deviations

There were no deviations from the protocol.

Eligibility criteria

We included all studies that were process evaluations of implementation trials that were underpinned by an evidence-based practice, including cluster, stepped wedge, and hybrid Type 3 RCTs conducted in primary, secondary, tertiary, and community health settings. Quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods process evaluations were all eligible for inclusion. Only studies published in the English language were included, due to limited translation resources. We did not set a restriction on year of publication.

Information sources and search strategy

Comprehensive literature searches were conducted from inception to March 2024, in MEDLINE, Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), PsychINFO, Web of Science, SCOPUS, and ProQuest-Health. The search strategies were prepared and refined in consultation with a research librarian. Search terms combined keywords relating to implementation science and process evaluations, including implementation, theory of change, logic framework, and randomised controlled trial. The full MEDLINE search strategy is included in Additional File 1.

Selection of sources of evidence

All identified citations were collated and uploaded into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, PA, United States) and duplicates removed. The Endnote database was uploaded into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for data screening and extraction. Four reviewers (CO, EL, GH, SC) piloted then refined the screening guide, then each abstract was independently screened by two reviewers (shared between CO, GH, SC, TOA). Conflicts between reviewers at each stage of selection were resolved through discussion.

Methodological quality appraisal

Consistent with guidance on conducting scoping reviews, we did not appraise the methodological quality or risk of bias of included articles [25].

Data extraction, coding, and analysis

A custom data extraction tool was developed, based on the JBI Data Extraction Instrument template [25]. The tool was piloted by three independent reviewers (CO, EL, SC) on six randomly selected papers, then refined to ensure consistency of data extraction. The tool was designed to extract details about health settings (primary, secondary, tertiary, community), implementation interventions and strategies [5, 6], theories and frameworks, barriers and enablers, and reported implementation outcomes [9] (Additional File 2). Data were independently extracted by two reviewers (shared between CO, EL, ELB, GH, LB, SC, TOA). Face-to-face meetings with the authorship team were held on a regular basis to review conflicts.

Defining implementation interventions

We defined implementation interventions firstly by whether they employed one (simple) or multiple (complex) implementation strategies, which in turn were classified according to the nine categories mapped by Waltz and colleagues [6] based on the 73 discrete implementation strategies identified by Powell and colleagues [5].

Classifying trials

To examine the process evaluation and how it explained the trial findings, we looked at the changes in each trial’s nominated primary outcome/s. We classified trials as positive if the intervention group demonstrated significant improvements in the primary outcome compared to the comparator group, or null (trials with no significant difference in outcomes between groups), or negative (in favour of the comparator group). Primary outcomes included patient outcomes and/or clinical practice outcomes. Where there were multiple primary outcomes and the intervention changed some, but not all the primary outcomes, this was categorised as a mixed response.

Defining implementation outcomes

We defined implementation outcomes from process evaluations using the taxonomy developed by Proctor and colleagues [9] consisting of eight outcomes (Fig. 1 and Table 1). However, when reviewing studies, it was apparent that implementation outcomes were often described without using the specific terms in the taxonomy. As such, a level of interpretation was applied during data extraction to map implementation outcomes as described in the process evaluation to the outcome types delineated by the taxonomy. Where there was not a clear fit, outcomes were assigned to the ‘other’ category. Defining barriers and enablers

Data extracted on barriers and enablers to implementation were analysed through the lens of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [12]. CFIR is classified as a determinant framework with five domains (encompassing 39 sub-domains) that influence implementation, namely: Innovation Characteristics, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Characteristics of Individuals, and Process [28]. Two reviewers (CO, ELB) coded the data using the CFIR codebook, which includes a description of each domain and sub-domain and exclusion criteria [29].

Process evaluation findings in relation to trial outcomes

To determine if there were any differences between positive and null trials from a process evaluation perspective, we undertook a sub-analysis of 70 trials. We examined process evaluation findings separately for positive and null trials to explore whether there were common factors or patterns that might be associated with the effectiveness of implementation interventions. Specifically, we examined the following implementation characteristics: number and type of implementation strategies employed within the implementation intervention; types of implementation outcomes studied; delivery of the implementation intervention (who delivered, dose and duration); and reported barriers and enablers of implementation. Trials with multiple primary outcomes, and where the results were mixed (n = 7), or where the main trial outcomes had not been reported (n = 3), were excluded from this sub-analysis.

Data synthesis

Results are descriptively analysed and presented in diagrammatic and/or tabular formats accompanied by a narrative summary addressing the review objective and questions. Data are presented for all the included studies (n = 80 total), followed by the sub-analysis (n = 70 total) of positive (n = 32) and null trials (n = 38).

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

A total of 5857 abstracts were screened, of which 405 articles were eligible for full text review. Key reasons for exclusion of studies at full text review included ‘not an implementation intervention’ (n = 179) and ‘not an RCT’ (n = 95). After full text screening, 81 articles were included in the data extraction (Fig. 2). Implementation trials were conducted in community settings (n = 36, 45%), inpatient acute care (n = 21, 26%), residential aged care (n = 18, 23%), out-patient settings (n = 3, 4%) and within both in and outpatient care (n = 2, 3%).

The interventions targeted a range of participant groups including nursing home staff, General Practitioners and primary health care staff, including non-hospital-based community health care clinicians such as occupational therapists and social workers, hospital-based clinicians and lay health workers.

Implementation interventions and strategies

Most studies (n = 69, 86%) evaluated complex implementation interventions comprising more than one implementation strategy and/or targeting more than one health professional group. Intervention duration (measured in terms of the research personnel interacting with the participants/clinical teams to influence their actions) ranged from less than a day to three years. However, this was difficult to ascertain and confirm in many studies with limited information in both the process evaluation and trial papers.

The most commonly reported implementation strategies were training and education of stakeholders (n = 74, 93%), evaluative and iterative strategies such as audit and feedback (n = 38, 48%), development of stakeholder interrelationships such as identification and preparation of champions (n = 33, 41%) and interactive assistance (n = 30, 38%). The least commonly evaluated implementation strategies were financial (n = 2, 3%), adapting and tailoring to context (n = 6, 8%) and engaging consumers (n = 7, 9%) (Fig. 3, Table 2).Fig. 3Strategies employed within n = 80 implementation interventions

Trial outcomes

Primary outcomes varied markedly between studies. Twenty studies (25%) measured patient outcomes only while 54 studies (68%) reported clinical practice outcomes only. Six studies (8%) measured both patient and practice outcomes. Where clinical practice outcomes were evaluated, typically studies were related to increasing recommended evidence-based actions (such as infection control, medication prescription, screening for conditions or symptoms, referral to services), although some studies focused on de-implementation and measured reductions in practices such as antibiotic prescription, use of physical restraints and unnecessary scans (computed tomography).

Just under half of the process evaluations reported on null implementation trials (n = 38, 48%), while n = 32 (40%) reported on positive trials, and n = 7 (9%) reported mixed results. None of the process evaluations reported negative trial outcomes and three (4%) of the included process evaluations related to implementation trials for which the outcomes had not been published.

Process evaluation characteristics

Process evaluations were conducted using mixed methods (n = 54, 67%), qualitative (n = 15, 19%), and quantitative (n = 11, 14%) approaches. The majority were conducted concurrently (e.g., process data was collected alongside the trial) (n = 57, 71%), while n = 22 (28%) were conducted retrospectively (post-trial) (Table 3 and Additional file 3). Two process evaluations were conducted related to the same study and were therefore considered to report both concurrently and retrospectively (n = 1, 1%). Process evaluation theories and or frameworks

Over half of the included process evaluations (n = 47, 59%) reported the use of a theory or framework to guide the process evaluation, with 9 of the 47 studies employing two or more theories or frameworks within their process evaluation. A mixture of implementation theories/frameworks and classic theories (e.g., those drawn from fields outside of implementation science such as behavioural science) were reported. The most frequently employed theories/frameworks in the included process evaluations were Normalisation Process Theory (NPT) [110] (n = 11, 14%), the framework for design and reporting of process evaluations developed by Grant and colleagues (n = 7, 9%) [111] and the Theory of Planned Behaviour (n = 6, 8%) [112]. Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) [113], the Theoretical Domains Framework [114] and CFIR were included in n = 4 studies (5%) (Table 3).

Reporting of implementation outcomes

More than half (n = 53, 66%) of the included process evaluations reported on acceptability while n = 48 (60%) reported on adoption (Fig. 4, Table 3). Reporting of fidelity was present in n = 41 (51%) while data related to appropriateness, feasibility and ‘other’ were present in n = 36 (45%), n = 20 (25%), and n = 26 (33%) of process evaluations, respectively. Only n = 2 (3%) of studies assessed implementation cost. Examples of ‘other’ implementation outcomes that were described that did not clearly map to Proctor’s taxonomy included adaptation and the four constructs of Normalisation Process Theory (coherence, cognitive participation, collective action, and reflexive monitoring). Number and type of implementation outcomes

The number of implementation outcomes studied (typically 3 or 4 outcomes) were also similar in both positive and null trials. The most commonly evaluated implementation outcomes were acceptability, adoption, appropriateness and fidelity. A higher percentage of positive trials evaluated penetration than null trials (22% compared to 3%, respectively) (Fig. 5c).

Delivery of the implementation intervention

The majority of interventions were delivered by researchers (n = 20/32, 63%, in positive trials; n = 26/38, 68% in null trials). Both positive and null trials employed facilitators, although positive trials reported a greater use of external facilitators and null trials a higher use of internal facilitators (Fig. 5d). Dose of intervention (single versus multiple) tended towards the use of multiple doses of the implementation intervention (n = 17/32, 53% in positive trials; n = 24/38, 65%, in null trials). In relation to duration of the intervention, both positive and null trials demonstrated a large range of duration times: 30 min to 3 years in positive trials and 1 h to 2 years in null trials. The mean duration was 8.3 months for positive trials (median 6 months) and 7.3 months for null trials (median 6 months).

Barriers and enablers to implementation

Most process evaluations reported on barriers and enablers to implementation which we categorised using CFIR (Additional File 4). Only 2 (3%) studies did not report on barriers or enablers [17, 76]. Contextual factors relating to the inner setting were particularly prominent as barriers to implementation in both positive and null trials (identified in 78% and 76% of studies, respectively), as were individual characteristics (53% of positive trials and 61% of null trials) (Table 4). Inner setting features that were most frequently identified as barriers to implementation across all studies were availability of resources, including staff time and schedules, culture, the relative priority of the intervention and compatibility, where the intervention was seen to not fit with workflow, system and processes. In terms of individual characteristics, knowledge and beliefs, and self-efficacy were frequently identified, underscoring the importance of individuals’ beliefs and capability for successful implementation. Discussion

This scoping review set out to examine whether process evaluations conducted alongside RCTs of implementation interventions could provide insights into why implementation interventions succeed or fail. The majority of the 80 included studies were conducted in the United Kingdom and Europe, a similar finding reported in previous reviews of process evaluation studies [18, 20]. This could reflect the influential guidance from the United Kingdom Medical Research Council on undertaking process evaluations as part of complex intervention studies [10] and/or the fact that process evaluation studies are not always labelled as such.

Of the 80 studies included in the current review that presented their primary outcome findings none reported a negative effect, (in favour of the intervention comparator), 39 reported a positive or mixed effect and 38 a null effect. This level of variability is apparent in previous reviews, highlighting the importance of understanding the factors, or combination of factors, that contribute to a favourable (or unfavourable) outcome as critical to progressing the science and practice of implementation [24]. However, although process evaluations are intended to provide an opening into the ‘black box’ of complex interventions, few obvious patterns are evident from this review that could account for the differences in outcomes (Table 5). Both positive and null implementation trials generally used multi-component implementation interventions comprising two or more implementation strategies, with n = 74 (93%) including training and educating stakeholders as the most commonly employed implementation strategy. Contrary to the guidance on conducting process evaluations [10] only n = 47 (59%) trials reported the use of a theory or framework to guide the study. However, this is a higher percentage than the figures of 26% and 38% use of theory reported by McIntyre et al. [24] and Scott et al. [22], respectively. There was also a much higher use of concurrent process evaluation methods than was reported in the prior review by Scott and colleagues (71% compared to 34%) [22].

What then can we take from the review findings that might help us to advance the field of implementation science? On a positive note, there are many well-designed implementation trials with aligned process evaluations that aim to improve the quality of health care across a range of different sectors and settings. It is also encouraging to see the high level of reporting of null trials and that there is no evidence that the implementation interventions evaluated had a negative effect. However, several issues identified in the review have potential implications for the future design, delivery, reporting, and evaluation of implementation interventions.

The first of these relates to the use of education and training within implementation interventions. Despite 93% of the implementation interventions incorporating education and training of stakeholders, two of the most frequently identified implementation barriers and enablers were self-efficacy and knowledge and beliefs, highlighting their influence as implementation determinants. This suggests that the way in which education and training strategies (as one component of an implementation intervention) are designed and delivered may not consistently align with the needs of intended implementers. The high level of variability in terms of who delivered the education and training, how and over what time period also highlights a need to pay closer attention to issues such as dose, delivery, frequency and tailoring to the specific needs of the target audience/s for implementation. Tailoring to context within implementation interventions was reported in only 6 (8%) of the 80 studies, despite well recognised differences in how individuals learn, acquire, and apply new knowledge [115]. Furthermore, contextual factors at the inner and outer setting level were prominent as barriers and enablers of implementation in both positive and null trials.

Alongside the observed variability in what constituted an education and training strategy, there were also considerable differences in the level of detail that was reported about implementation interventions within the process evaluation. This reflects our own and others’ experiences in reviewing implementation interventions in health care [18, 116, 117]. More consistent reporting of key components of implementation strategies and multi-component implementation interventions, for example, by applying the seven elements proposed by Proctor and colleagues [118]—actor, the action, action targets, temporality, dose, implementation outcomes addressed, theoretical justification – would enhance learning within and across implementation research studies.

Similar issues were apparent in the reporting of implementation outcomes, both in relation to the clarity of reporting and the range of implementation outcomes studied. Reflecting the findings of a recently published review [119], acceptability and adoption were the most common implementation outcomes studied, compared to outcomes such as feasibility and cost, both of which are important considerations for potential scale-up. A final point relates more generally to the reporting of process evaluations within implementation trials. When extracting data for this review, we were challenged by the varied terminology that was used, which required interpretation and judgements to be made about the specifics of the implementation intervention, implementation outcomes of interest and the details of delivering the implementation. More consistent reporting, guided by existing recommendations [111] and checklists such as TIDieR and STaRI [120, 121], would help consolidate learning from process evaluations in the future.

Limitations

Despite comprehensive searching it is possible we may not have captured all relevant articles, particularly if trial authors did not explicitly use the term “process evaluation”, an issue highlighted in previous reviews [23]. Nevertheless, our search strategy included terminology that would identify relevant studies that may not have explicitly used this nomenclature. Additionally, there were considerable inconsistencies in the reporting of implementation interventions and outcomes, which required a degree of interpretation in the process of data extraction and analysis. A further limitation of this review is that only the primary outcome of the implementation trial was assessed to categorise the trial outcome as positive, null or negative. However, there were examples where the primary outcome of the trial was null but one of more secondary outcomes were positive and clinically significant (e.g., Mudge et al. [122]). Equally, it highlights the importance of careful selection of primary outcome measures in implementation trials. Further, we did not include a detailed sub-analysis of the mixed and unreported studies in relation to process evaluation outcomes and characteristics.

Due to the nature of scoping reviews, we did not undertake methodological quality or risk of bias. Most of the included studies were conducted in high income countries, which may affect transferability to middle and low-income countries. Only studies published in the English language were included due to limited translation resources, however our search returned only one study in a language other than English.

Conclusions

This scoping review aimed to investigate and examine process evaluations embedded within randomised controlled trials of implementation interventions in health care, driven by a curiosity to explore whether process evaluations really do help open up the ‘black box’ and “provide information to interpret outcome results and aid implementation into practice” [23]. Overall, this review found more similarities than differences between positive and null implementation trials. As such, the review provided limited insights into the ‘black box’. Possible reasons for this finding could be a lack of attention to the design and execution of the implementation intervention itself, particularly the extent to which it is tailored to address identified implementation barriers and enablers. Alternatively, it could highlight the need for more careful and consistent design, conduct and reporting of implementation trials with process evaluations. Most likely, it is a combination of all these factors, highlighting a need to further optimise the planning, conduct and reporting of implementation trials and process evaluations to continue advancing the science and practice of implementation.
