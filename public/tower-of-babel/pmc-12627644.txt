TREM2-mediated regulation of myeloid cells in the tumor microenvironment: new insights and therapeutic prospects
Authors: Yueyu Huang, Wanxia Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) has recently been identified as a key regulator of myeloid cell function within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Accumulating evidence indicates that TREM2-expressing tumor-associated macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells promote immunosuppression, tumor progression, and resistance to immunotherapy. Through modulation of cellular metabolism, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion, TREM2 signaling critically shapes tumor immune dynamics. Owing to this central role, TREM2 has emerged as a promising immunotherapeutic target. Preclinical and clinical studies demonstrate that TREM2 blockade can reprogram the immunosuppressive TME, enhance T-cell infiltration, and augment the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. This review synthesizes current knowledge of the molecular mechanisms underlying TREM2 signaling in myeloid cells, its influence on TME remodeling, and its potential as both a biomarker and therapeutic target in cancer immunotherapy. Finally, we highlight current challenges and future perspectives in harnessing TREM2-directed strategies to counteract tumor immune evasion.

Introduction

Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells (TREMs) are the members of the immunoglobulin superfamily, predominantly expressed on myeloid cells. The family includes TREM1, TREM2, TREM3 (mouse), and several TREM-like receptors (TREML1-4)1. These receptors share conserved immunoglobulin-like extracellular domains and short cytoplasmic tails but differ in ligand specificity, expression patterns, and immunological functions2. TREM1 primarily amplifies pro-inflammatory signaling in neutrophils and monocytes3, whereas TREM2 is expressed in macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), granulocytes, and central nervous system microglia4.

Over the past two decades, TREM2 has been studied extensively in neurodegenerative disorders, including Nasu–Hakola disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and disease5,6. More recently, attention has shifted to its role in oncology. TREM2 expression has been detected in multiple tumor types, including lung cancer, breast cancer, gastric cancer (GC), and colorectal cancers (CRC)7. Functional studies demonstrate that TREM2 knockdown in GC cell lines (SGC-7901 and BGC-823) suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion¹¹, with similar effects observed in glioma, prostate adenocarcinoma, and thyroid cancer8–10. In contrast, TREM2 downregulation in CRC cells (HT29) promotes tumor growth and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo11. These findings underscore the context-dependent nature of TREM2 function, suggesting it may act as either an oncogene or tumor suppressor depending on the malignancies12. Reports of low-level TREM2 expression in tumor cell lines under in vitro conditions exist, but these are often methodologically inconsistent and lack validation in genetically engineered models13,14. The prevailing evidence indicates that TREM2 primarily exerts its biological effects through myeloid-derived cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME)15. Tumor-associated myeloid cells (TAMCs) represent the most abundant immune population in the TME and are characterized by significant heterogeneity and plasticity16. Advances in single-cell technologies have recently uncovered novel TAMC subsets with key roles in tumor progression16,17. Among them, TREM2⁺ myeloid cells have emerged as particularly important and are increasingly recognized as promising therapeutic targets15.

This review synthesizes current insights into TREM2-mediated regulation of myeloid cells in the TME, highlights the molecular and functional diversity of TREM2⁺ subsets, and discusses therapeutic strategies, such as monoclonal antibodies, small molecules, and combination approaches with immune checkpoint blockades (ICBs).

TREM2 biology and signaling pathways

Structure and expression of TREM2

TREM2 is a transmembrane glycoprotein of the immunoglobulin superfamily. It is composed of three main structural domains: an extracellular domain, a single transmembrane region, and a short cytoplasmic tail lacking an intrinsic signaling motif2,18. The extracellular domain contains a V-type immunoglobulin region that mediates ligand binding, followed by a short stalk susceptible to proteolytically cleavage. This cleavage releases soluble TREM2 (sTREM2)18, which is primarily mediated by ADAM10 and ADAM17 metalloproteases, and sTREM2 has been detected in biological fluids such as serum and cerebrospinal fluid19,20.

TREM2 is exclusively expressed in myeloid-derived cells, including macrophages, DCs, neutrophils, and microglia21. Under homeostatic conditions, it plays a key role in regulating innate immune responses, phagocytosis, and tissue remodeling. In the TME, TREM2 is predominantly expressed in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), where it contributes to an immunosuppressive phenotype17. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyses have revealed high TREM2 expression in immunosuppressive TAM subsets across multiple cancer types, including lung, breast, colorectal, and GCs21.

TREM2 ligands and binding partners

TREM2 is a pattern-recognition receptor that interacts with a diverse array of ligands, including phospholipids, lipoproteins, apoptotic cells, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which collectively modulate myeloid cell functions22. The key TREM2 ligands identified so far include lipoproteins and phospholipids: TREM2 binds to lipid-related molecules such as phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylethanolamine, and oxidized low-density lipoproteins23. These interactions are crucial for recognizing apoptotic cells and lipid-rich environments.

Apolipoproteins: (1) ApoE, a lipid transport protein, is a high-affinity ligand for TREM2. The TREM2–ApoE interaction is essential for lipid metabolism and immune homeostasis, particularly in neurodegenerative diseases and cancer24. (2) DAMPs and PAMPs: TREM2 can recognize microbial products and endogenous DAMPs, such as heat shock proteins and extracellular nucleotides, which regulate immune responses22. (3) Cellular debris and apoptotic cells: By binding to apoptotic cells, TREM2 facilitates their clearance through phagocytosis, promoting immune resolution and tissue repair25. In the TME, TREM2-ligand interactions drive myeloid cell differentiation toward an immunosuppressive phenotype, thereby promoting tumor growth and resistance to ICB therapies26.

Downstream signaling pathways

Unlike the classical immune receptors, TREM2 lacks a cytoplasmic signaling motif and relies on adapter proteins for intracellular signal transduction. The key adapter protein, DNAX activation protein 12 (DAP12), contains an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM), which is phosphorylated upon TREM2 activation27. This phosphorylation recruits spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK), initiating a cascade of downstream signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, NF-κB, and ERK, which regulate cell survival, metabolism, and immune functions27–29.

SYK-PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway

Upon ligand binding, DAP12 phosphorylation activates SYK, which leads to the recruitment of PI3K and the subsequent activation of the AKT/mTOR pathway30. This signaling cascade then promotes cell survival, metabolic adaptation, and immune cell polarization. In TME-associated myeloid cells, TREM2-dependent PI3K/AKT activation supports immunosuppressive macrophage differentiation and resistance to pro-inflammatory signaling31.

NF-κB and MAPK pathways

TREM2 activation triggers the NF-κB pathway, which plays a central role in cytokine production and immune modulation. Phosphorylated SYK activates downstream effectors, including MAPKs (ERK, p38), leading to the transcription of genes involved in inflammation and immune regulation32. In tumors, TREM2-dependent NF-κB signaling has been associated with an increased secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, facilitating tumor progression and immune evasion33. Notably, NF-κB activation downstream of TREM2 differs from classical NF-κB activators such as TLRs, which typically induce pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6)34,35. In contrast, TREM2–SYK–NF-κB signaling preferentially promotes the transcription of immunoregulatory cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby reinforcing an immunosuppressive milieu36,37. In addition, SYK activation downstream of TREM2 has been implicated in shaping myeloid cell fate, driving polarization toward tumor-promoting macrophages rather than inflammatory phenotypes27,37. This highlights the unique role of TREM2 signaling in programming myeloid cells to foster immune tolerance and tumor progression.

Crosstalk with metabolic pathways

Emerging evidence indicates that TREM2 acts as a key regulator of cellular metabolism in response to environmental cues. By engaging the PI3K-AKT and mTOR pathways, TREM2 promotes lipid uptake and oxidative phosphorylation, equipping myeloid cells to withstand the nutrient-deprived TME38. This metabolic reprogramming is especially critical in TAMs and MDSCs, where TREM2 signaling sustains immunosuppressive functions and survival39.

sTREM2 and alternative signaling

sTREM2, generated through proteolytic cleavage, exerts distinct biological effects. Although membrane-bound TREM2 primarily mediates intracellular signaling, sTREM2 functions in an autocrine or paracrine manner, which modulates immune responses in a ligand-independent fashion40. Elevated sTREM2 levels in the TME have been associated with increased macrophage survival and tumor progression, indicating its potential role as a biomarker for immune evasion41.

TREM2 in the TME

The TME is composed of a complex network of immune cells, stromal cells, and extracellular matrix components, which collectively influence tumor progression and therapeutic response. Myeloid cells play a crucial role in shaping the immunosuppressive TME, with TREM2 emerging as a key regulator of their function. Recent studies have demonstrated that TREM2+ TAMs, MDSCs, and DCs contribute to immune evasion and tumor growth by suppressing T-cell responses and modulating stromal interactions.

TREM2+ TAMs: immunosuppressive phenotype and function

TAMs have been extensively investigated in the last several decades, as they were the most abundant immune cell population within TME and exhibited high plasticity in different context42,43. To date, the M1/M2 classification of TAMs remains the most accepted. The M1 phenotype is characterized as pro-inflammatory, with the upregulation of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) and co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-1β, IL-12, and IL-18. In contrast, M2 TAMs are immunosuppressive and promote tumor progression. Transforming growth factor-β, IL-10, arginase 1 (ARG1), and CD206 are typical M2 macrophage markers42,44. However, the conventional M1/M2 classification is overly simple. By scRNA-seq and other single-cell technologies, increasing TAM subpopulations, including TREM2+ TAMs, have been identified with novel markers, but not a certain M1 or M2 phenotype45,46.

Characteristics of TREM2+ TAMs

Recently, the TREM2 expression was examined extensively in macrophages from human normal and tumor samples. No TREM2 expression was detected on macrophages in a large majority of the peripheral tissues, except microglia, alveolar macrophages, MITF+ placental macrophages, lipid-associated macrophages, and macrophages in the endometrium14. However, TREM2+ macrophages have been detected in carcinomas from across primary sites, such as lung cancer, breast cancer, gastrointestinal carcinoma, and others, as well as metastatic nests in nodal and distant metastasis, as assessed by immunohistochemistry14,47. Moreover, based on an independent scRNA-seq dataset from multiple cancers, when compared to tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs), the expression of TREM2 increased as per TAM48.

In the tumor tissues from treatment-naive breast cancer patients with different subtypes, TREM2+ macrophages constitute 30–40% of total TAMCs49. To investigate the association of TREM2+ TAM abundance with the clinical outcome of breast cancer patients, Nalio et al. reanalyzed a microarray dataset provided by Finak et al. in a previous study, wherein the authors used laser capture microdissection technology to obtain tumor stromal gene expression profiles from 53 primary luminal ER/PR breast tumors, recording that a high expression of TREM2+ macrophage gene signature (TREM2/SPP1) predicted shorter recurrence-free survival (RFS)47,50. In triple-negative breast cancer patients with paclitaxel and anti-PD-L1 atezolizumab combination treatment, the baseline macrophage frequency in non-responders was higher than that in responders, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis of macrophages indicated an enrichment of TREM2, SPP1, FN1, C3, and LDHA in non-responders51. Altogether, TREM2+ TAM enrichment may predict inferior clinical outcomes in breast cancer.

According to the single-cell analysis of immune cells from tumor lesions, adjacent lung tissue, and the blood of 18 treatment-naive patients with stage I lung adenocarcinoma, a specific enrichment of TREM2+ macrophages was detected in the tumors52. By co-staining CD68 and TREM2, Zhang et al. successfully detected TREM2+ TAMs in tissue microarrays from 292 non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients who received radical resection, and found that the infiltration of TREM2+ TAMs was positively associated with TNM stage and tumor grade26. Intriguingly, the lung adenocarcinoma patients with high TREM2+ TAM accumulation exhibited unique molecular characteristics: harboring relatively lower mutation occurrence, but presenting with significant enrichment of EGFR mutations. Furthermore, it was observed that NSCLC patients with higher TREM2+ TAM infiltration had shorter progression-free survival (PFS) after PD-1 blockade treatment. The association between intratumoral TREM2+ TAM abundance and prognosis was assessed in three independent patient cohorts, and the TREM2+ TAM-high group displayed worse RFS and overall survival (OS)26. Collectively, TREM2+ TAMs may attenuate tumor response to ICBs and act as predictors for poor clinical outcomes in NSCLC. TRM2+ TAMs in different human tumors.

TREM2+ TAMs have been identified across a wide spectrum of human cancers, including both primary and metastatic lesions. Their presence consistently correlates with unfavorable prognosis, as TREM2+ TAM enrichment is linked to poor therapeutic response, notably to TACE and ICT, and to reduced overall survival in cancer patients.

Up to now, it is widely accepted that TREM2⁺ TAMs primarily originate from monocytes. In an earlier study, Molgora et al. observed that TREM2⁺ TAMs ranged from small monocytoid cells to large foamy or multinucleated giant cells14. In contrast, another study reported that TREM2⁺ macrophages were smaller and morphologically similar to monocytes47. Mulder and colleagues also supported a monocytic origin, noting similarities between TREM2⁺ macrophages and the monocyte gene signature47. Recently, Park et al. confirmed the monocytic derivation of TREM2⁺ TAMs and comprehensively investigated their dynamics using the orthotopic KrasG12D/+p53−/−(KP)-GFP model of primary lung adenocarcinoma. By tracking TREM2⁺ cells, they found progressive accumulation within KP-GFP tumors, with most TREM2⁺ cells displaying intracellular tumor-associated GFP signal, suggesting that efferocytosis may be linked to TREM2 expression in monocyte-derived macrophages (mo-macs). Subsequent isolation of mo-macs from tumor-bearing lungs and sorting into GFPlow and GFPhi subsets revealed, through DEG analysis, high expression of the TREM2 program (TREM2, LPL, SYNGR1, APOE) in GFPhi mo-macs61. These findings indicate that efferocytosis of apoptotic tumor cells by mo-macs may induce TREM2 program expression, consistent with previous observations that TREM2 senses phosphatidylserine on dying cells62–67. In a separate study, Zhang et al. applied pseudo-temporal analysis using the Monocle2 algorithm on mononuclear phagocytes from scRNA-seq data of seven NSCLC patients (GSE127465) and found TREM2 expression strongly associated with pseudo-time progression, predominantly driving TAM differentiation26. Additionally, Yao et al. explored the effect of the TME on TREM2 expression in macrophages by preparing conditional medium (CM; 4:1 ratio of medium to 3LL supernatant). They observed that bone marrow-derived macrophages treated with CM produced more TREM2⁺ macrophages than those cultured in normal medium37. Collectively, current evidence supports that TREM2⁺ TAMs derive mainly from monocytes and interact closely with the TME. However, although several TREM2 ligands have been identified, their influence on the dynamic and functional states of TREM2⁺ TAMs, as well as the contribution of other cellular components to TREM2 regulation in macrophages within the TME, remains poorly understood. Thus, further studies are required to elucidate the mechanisms governing the differentiation and function of TREM2⁺ TAMs.

Immunosuppressive function and mechanism of TREM2⁺ TAMs

To further explore the genotype and phenotype of TREM2⁺ TAMs, Zhang et al. reanalyzed scRNA-seq data from seven NSCLC patients (GSE127465). Reclustering mononuclear phagocytes into nine subsets revealed widespread TREM2 expression among TAMs. Based on expression levels, TAMs were divided into TREM2⁺ and TREM2⁻ subsets. DEG analysis showed that TREM2⁺ TAMs expressed both M1- and M2-associated signatures. However, flow cytometry of 49 fresh NSCLC samples revealed higher levels of M2 markers (CD206, IL-10, ARG1), but not the M1 marker CD11b, in TREM2⁺ TAMs compared with TREM2⁻ counterparts26. In another study, Lavin et al. identified a TREM2⁺ TAM signature enriched for genes related to immunosuppression (MARCO, CD163, CD81), lipid metabolism (APOE), and complement activation (C1QB)52. These findings suggest that TREM2⁺ TAMs predominantly display an M2-like phenotype with immunosuppressive activity.

Analysis of scRNA-seq data from tumor samples of five treatment-naive hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients and five patients post-TACE therapy showed that the frequency of TREM2⁺ TAMs, along with other monocyte/macrophage subclusters, markedly increased after TACE60. This was accompanied by a sharp decline in CD8⁺ T-cells. Co-culture assays revealed that TREM2⁺ TAMs significantly impaired CD8⁺ T-cell migration. Mechanistically, Tan et al. found that downregulation of CXCL9 in TREM2⁺ TAMs contributed to suppressed CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration. DEG analysis also revealed elevated LGALS1 (Gal-1) expression in TREM2⁺ TAMs. Consistently, ELISA confirmed reduced CXCL9 and increased Gal-1 secretion from TREM2⁺ TAMs compared with TREM2⁻ TAMs. Importantly, the addition of an anti–Gal-1 antibody enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell migration, while blocking CXCL9 further suppressed it. Moreover, TREM2⁺ TAMs upregulated PD-L1 expression in vascular endothelial (VE) cells via Gal-1, with increased PD-L1⁺ VE cells detected in TACE-treated samples61. These results suggest that TREM2⁺ TAMs can suppress CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration both directly and by modulating VE cells. In NSCLC, however, no direct correlation was observed between TREM2⁺ TAMs and CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration27. Nonetheless, GSEA of a TCGA lung adenocarcinoma cohort revealed enrichment of negative T-cell immunity regulation and exhausted CD8⁺ T-cell signatures in TREM2⁺ TAM-high samples. Moreover, increased expression of co-inhibitory receptors (CTLA4, TIM3) was found on CD8⁺ T-cells from NSCLC samples with high TREM2⁺ TAM accumulation, and co-culture with TREM2⁺ TAMs markedly suppressed CD8⁺ T-cell proliferation and effector functions (CD107a, PRF1, TNF-α)26. Similarly, Mikhail et al. reported correlations between TREM2 expression and T-cell exhaustion signatures in multiple cancers, including breast cancer (BRCA), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), ovarian cancer (OV), and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD)48. Together, these findings establish that TREM2⁺ TAMs suppress CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, expansion, and anti-tumor activity.

Beyond T-cells, NK cells are also targeted by TREM2⁺ TAMs. Park et al. reported enrichment of TREM2⁺ mo-macs in tumor lesions accompanied by a marked reduction in NK cells, based on scRNA-seq data from 35 treatment-naive NSCLC samples. In an OVA-expressing KrasG12D/+p53−/−(KP)-GFP lung adenocarcinoma mouse model, NK-cell abundance was higher in TREM2 knockout (KO) mice than in wild-type (WT) mice, and NK-cell depletion reversed the tumor burden reduction observed in TREM2-KO mice61. TREM2⁺ TAMs expressed high levels of Il18bp, encoding IL-18BP, a known IL-18 antagonist that blocks signaling in NK cells62,63. Co-culture with TREM2-KO mo-macs significantly boosted NK-cell activation, which was abolished when NK cells lacking IL-18 receptors were used61. These findings demonstrate that TREM2⁺ TAMs inhibit NK-cell activation through IL-18 signaling.

In addition to impairing CD8⁺ T- and NK-cell responses, TREM2⁺ TAMs interact with other immune subsets. In NSCLC, specimens with high TREM2⁺ TAM infiltration showed increased frequency and TGF-β expression in FOXP3⁺ Tregs. Cell–cell interaction analysis (iTALK) also revealed interactions between TREM2⁺ TAMs and Tregs via IL1B–IL1R26. CIBERSORT analysis of TCGA-LUAD data further indicated that TREM2⁺ TAMs inversely correlated with naive B-cells and plasma cells. Moreover, positive associations were observed between TREM2⁺ TAMs and immune checkpoint molecules (PDCD1, CD274, CTLA4, LAG3, HAVCR2, TIGIT, ENTPD1) as well as anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TGFB1)26. In summary, TREM2⁺ TAMs orchestrate an immunosuppressive TME through multiple mechanisms, a complexity that warrants further investigation.

TREM2 and MDSCs

MDSCs are immature myeloid cells with potent immunosuppressive functions. Phenotypically resembling monocytes and neutrophils, they are classified into monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) and granulocytic/polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs), both of which promote tumor progression through diverse mechanisms64. In recent years, the central role of TREM2 in myeloid biology has drawn attention to its expression and function in MDSCs.

TREM2 expression in MDSCs

Ford et al. reported that monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) isolated from the spleens of 4T1-bearing mice exhibited higher TREM2 mRNA expression compared with macrophages from B6 mice65. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), patients with elevated frequencies of circulating M-MDSCs displayed higher TREM2 expression. Moreover, increased surface TREM2 levels on M-MDSCs correlated with higher International Prognostic Index scores, bone marrow involvement, and poorer PFS and OS66. In another study, Bancaro et al. identified CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6Clow immunosuppressive neutrophils in Ptenpc−/−prostate cancer-bearing mice, which are also known as PMN-MDSCs67,68. A subset of these neutrophils exhibited features of cellular senescence, confirmed by positive staining for C12FDG (5-dodecanoylaminofluorescein di-β-D-galactopyranoside (C12FDG)), a senescence marker69. Differential transmembrane protein analysis revealed TREM2 as one of the most upregulated receptors on senescent neutrophils. Flow cytometry further confirmed that among tumor-infiltrating myeloid populations, senescent neutrophils represented the predominant TREM2⁺ subset. Accordingly, compared to TREM2−CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6Clow neutrophils, the TREM2+ subset presented a senescence-like phenotype. scRNA-seq of human prostate tumor samples corroborated these findings, showing that most senescent neutrophils expressed TREM2 and that TREM2 levels strongly correlated with the senescent neutrophil transcriptional signature69. Consistently, TREM2 expression was associated with the PMN-MDSC signature previously defined by Veglia et al., which includes multiple validated immunosuppressive neutrophil markers70. This association was confirmed in both primary prostate tumors and castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) cohorts from TCGA. Furthermore, multiplex immunofluorescence of prostate cancer biopsy specimens revealed that the frequency of intratumoral neutrophils with high TREM2 expression increased significantly with disease progression69.

Metabolic and cytokine-mediated regulation of MDSC immunosuppression

In RM-1 prostate cancer mouse models, TREM2mut mice exhibited reduced intratumoral accumulation of senescent neutrophils, increased NK-cell abundance, enhanced CD8+ T-cell activation, and smaller tumors compared with TREM2wt counterparts. Ex vivo assays further demonstrated that tumor-infiltrating TREM2mut neutrophils displayed diminished capacity to suppress T-cell proliferation. Ligand–receptor interaction and in vitro induction analyses identified APOE-TREM2 as a critical signaling axis between Ptenpc−/− tumor cells and senescent neutrophils69. In DLBCL, surface TREM2 expression on MDSCs inversely correlated with circulating CD8+ T-cell counts, while cytoplasmic ARG1 levels in M-MDSCs positively correlated with TREM2 expression66. Similarly, like TAMs, MDSCs can cause L-arginine deprivation and inhibit the proliferation of T-cells by secreting ARG171. Additionally, Wang et al. reported that MDSCs from TREM2-KO mice had significantly lower ARG1 mRNA expression than those from WT mice, and attenuated the ability to suppress CD8+ T-cell proliferation66. In another study, a subpopulation of tumor myeloid cells in mice, with positive ARG1 and TREM2 expression, was termed as regulatory myeloid (Mreg) cells using INs-seq technology to simultaneously record scRNA-seq and intracellular protein activity38. However, Binnewies et al. suggested that the transcriptionally equivalent population in humans corresponds to TAMs and monocytes rather than a distinct MDSC subset48.

Emerging evidence supports a revised view of MDSCs as a dynamic and transitional population closely related to TAMs, neutrophils, and DCs. For example, under hypoxic conditions within tumors, M-MDSCs can differentiate into TAMs, thereby reinforcing immunosuppression in the TME72,73. Future studies are required to delineate the lineage plasticity of MDSCs across tumor contexts and to clarify the role of TREM2 in mediating these cellular transitions.

TREM2+ DCs and their impact on antigen presentation

Despite the lower abundance in TME, DCs are critical antigen-presenting cells and play vital roles in anti-tumor immunity by MHC-mediated antigen-specific signal, the expression of co-stimulatory molecules including CD86, CD80, CD40, OX40L, GITRL, and 4-1BBL, as well as co-inhibitory molecules such as PD-L1, PD-L2, CD31, and V-domain immunoglobulin suppressor of T-cell activation74. Axel et al. first identified TREM2 as a DAP12-associated receptor on DCs. Engagement of the TREM2/DAP12 complex activated PTK and ERK signaling, promoting DC survival and unconventional maturation characterized by elevated CCR7, MHC class II, CD86, and CD40 expression but without cytokine secretion75. In an orthotopic lung cancer mouse model, both the frequency of TREM2+CD11c+ DCs and TREM2 expression on DCs were increased compared with healthy controls38. Bone marrow-derived DCs exposed to conditioned medium from 3LL tumor cells also upregulated TREM2, suggesting tumor-derived factors drive TREM2 induction38. However, these TREM2⁺ DCs exhibited a dysfunctional phenotype: reduced expression of MHC-II, CD80, and CD86; diminished IL-12 but elevated IL-10 secretion; and impaired endocytic activity37,75. Functionally, co-injection of 3LL cells with TREM2− DCs into mice accelerated tumor growth compared with TREM2+ DCs37, while in vitro co-culture assays confirmed stronger T-cell suppression by TREM2+ DCs37. The conflicting results presented across different studies—where TREM2 activation promotes tumor cell survival and partial maturation in one context while inducing immune suppression in another—likely reflect the context-dependent reprogramming of DCs within the complex TME. These findings underscore the urgent need to further elucidate the heterogeneity and plasticity of TREM2+ DC subpopulations across different tumor types to more accurately define their role in immune regulation.

TREM2 and crosstalk with other immune and stromal cells

TREM2+ myeloid cells not only suppress adaptive immunity but also engage in extensive crosstalk with stromal components of the TME, thereby fostering a pro-tumorigenic niche76. Through the secretion of TGF-β and IL-6, TREM2+ TAMs activate cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), driving extracellular matrix remodeling and facilitating tumor invasion77. In parallel, TREM2⁺ TAMs and MDSCs upregulate VEGF, promoting angiogenesis and tumor vascularization78. Beyond these stromal interactions, TREM2 signaling contributes directly to tumor progression by enabling immune evasion. For instance, TREM2+ macrophages promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby enhancing tumor metastasis79. These effects are mediated by multiple signaling cascades, including TGF-β/SMAD, which reinforces immunosuppression and CAF activation; VEGF/VEGFR, which drives angiogenesis; and STAT3/β-catenin, which sustains TAM- and MDSC-dependent immune evasion80.

TREM2, cellular senescence, and immune suppression

Recent evidence suggests that TREM2⁺ myeloid cells, particularly TAMs and MDSCs, can adopt senescent-like phenotypes characterized by cell cycle arrest, upregulation of senescence markers (p16^INK4a, p21), and acquisition of a senescence-associated secretory phenotype69,81. These immunosenescent myeloid populations secrete immunosuppressive cytokines and chemokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β, which inhibit T-cell and NK-cell activity, remodel the TME, and facilitate tumor progression. In prostate tumors, for example, senescent neutrophils expressing high levels of TREM2 correlated with advanced disease and reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration81. Similarly, TREM2+ M-MDSCs in DLBCL patients were associated with higher ARG1 expression and stronger T-cell suppression66.

Mechanistically, TREM2 signaling supports the survival and functional adaptation of senescent myeloid cells, paralleling its established role in disease-associated microglia and lipid-associated macrophages in non-tumor tissues82. The accumulation of TREM2⁺ immunosenescent myeloid cells thus represents a key intersection between cellular senescence and tumor-driven immunosuppression, underscoring TREM2 as a potential therapeutic target to counter immune evasion and restore anti-tumor immunity.

Therapeutic targeting of TREM2 in cancer immunotherapy

TREM2 inhibition as a strategy to reprogram the TME

Over recent years, TREM2 has emerged as a central signaling hub in tumor immunity and a promising target for overcoming resistance to current immunotherapy83. Molgora et al. reported that compared to WT mice, the tumor growth was significantly hampered in TREM2−/− mice in multiple tumor models, including the MCA/1956 sarcoma model, MC38 CRC model, and PyMT mammary tumor model. Moreover, the scRNA-seq analysis of intratumoral immune infiltrates in MCA/1956 tumors showed that tumor-infiltrating macrophages were remodeled in TREM2−/− mice to improve the accumulation and activation of T-cells and NK cells by upregulating their IFNg and PD-1 expression14. To evaluate whether TREM2 deficiency can improve anti-PD-1 efficacy, Martina et al. adopted a suboptimal anti-PD-1 administration by starting anti-PD-1 treatment at day 8 after tumor cell injection, because both MCA/1956 and MC38 are responsive to anti-PD-1 treatment, and it was observed that all TREM2−/− mice rejected tumor growth by increasing intratumoral T-cell infiltration, especially CD4+ T-cells14. These findings suggest that targeting TREM2 seems to be a prospective approach for cancer treatment.

Although most mechanistic insights into TREM2 in the TME have been derived from lung, breast, liver, and prostate cancers, its role in other malignancies remains less defined and, in some cases, even controversial15,84. In gliomas, especially glioblastoma, single-cell transcriptomic studies have identified TREM2-expressing TAMs enriched in perivascular and hypoxic niches, correlating with immunosuppressive signatures85,86. Nevertheless, functional experiments have yielded conflicting outcomes, with some indicating a tumor-promoting role and others exhibiting a minimal impact on tumor progression85. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, TREM2⁺ macrophages accumulate in the desmoplastic regions and are associated with poor prognosis, albeit their contribution to immune evasion versus stromal remodeling remains debatable87,88. In HCC beyond TACE-associated contexts, the TREM2 expression in macrophages has been variably associated with either immunosuppressive phenotypes or tissue-repair programs60,89, highlighting TREM2 signaling complexity in hepatic immunity. Finally, in head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma, bulk and single-cell RNA-seq datasets revealed heterogeneous TREM2 expression among myeloid subsets90; its relationship with T-cell exhaustion and clinical outcome remains to be clarified, particularly within the prognosis-favorable HPV⁺ subgroup91. Collectively, these seminal studies have emphasized that the impact of TREM2⁺ macrophages is context-dependent, influenced by tissue origin, stromal composition, and therapeutic intervention, warranting further tumor-type-specific investigations.

Anti-TREM2 antibodies: strategies and therapeutic potential

In 2006, Turnbull et al. developed an anti-TREM2 monoclonal antibody (mAb178), specific to the mouse TREM2 ectodomain, to map TREM2 distribution across murine tissues92. More recently, Molgora et al. engineered a recombinant form of mAb178 by grafting the heavy-chain variable region onto a mouse IgG2a backbone with a mutated Fc domain, thereby preventing Fc receptor or complement recognition and avoiding antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) or antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis14. In MCA/1956 sarcoma mouse models, recombinant mAb178 alone provided only partial tumor control. However, its combination with suboptimal anti-PD-1 therapy led to complete tumor growth suppression in all treated mice. Mechanistically, recombinant mAb178 reshaped the intratumoral myeloid compartment, notably reducing Ly6ClowMHCII− macrophages and subsets expressing CD206, CD63, and CD9, without affecting the overall macrophage count14. In another study, Mikhail et al. generated two anti-TREM2 mAbs: anti-TREM2-WT (Fc-competent, fucosylated) and anti-TREM2 (Fc effector enhanced, afucosylated). Both exhibited comparable TREM2 binding affinity on the cell surface48. However, the afucosylated anti-TREM2 demonstrated stronger ADCC activity in vitro and significantly suppressed tumor growth in EMT6-bearing mice. Importantly, in the anti-PD-1-resistant CT26 tumor model, neither anti-TREM2 nor anti-PD-1 monotherapy achieved significant tumor inhibition. In contrast, their combined administration produced robust tumor suppression, prolonged tumor-free survival for more than a month after treatment cessation, and generated durable immune memory, as confirmed by resistance to tumor rechallenge in complete responders. In line with Molgora et al., anti-TREM2 primarily modulated the macrophage compartment within tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, significantly reducing TAM frequency while upregulating pro-inflammatory genes and co-stimulatory surface markers, including CD40 and CD86, in the remaining TAMs. Combined anti-PD-1 and anti-TREM2 treatment further enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and activated IFN-γ and TNF-α signaling pathways in tumor tissues48. Recently, Chen et al. developed a bi-specific antibody (BsAb), PD-1-TREM2 single-chain variable fragment (scFv), targeting both PD-1 and TREM293. Compared with PD-1- or TREM2 scFv alone, the PD-1-TREM2 scFv more effectively suppressed tumor growth in MC38-bearing mice. Mechanistically, BsAb treatment enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell accumulation and cytotoxicity while reducing M2 TAMs and MDSC proportions, indicating efficient targeting of TREM2⁺ immunosuppressive myeloid cells and relief of the inhibitory TME. To overcome the short half-life of the BsAb, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)-specific CAR-T-cells engineered to secrete PD-1-TREM2 scFv demonstrated potent anti-tumor efficacy in CEA-expressing MC38 CRC mouse models93.

Clinically, the first-in-human Phase 1a/1b study of the humanized anti-TREM2 antibody PY314 (NCT04691375) has completed enrollment48,94. Preliminary results, presented at AACR 2023, indicated that PY314 monotherapy produced only modest anti-tumor activity (objective response rate <10%) across advanced solid tumors, while combination therapy with pembrolizumab did not meet pre-specified efficacy thresholds, leading to early termination of further development94. Pharmacodynamic analyses confirmed targeted depletion of TREM2⁺ peripheral and TAMs, demonstrating effective biological engagement. Factors likely contributing to limited clinical benefit include: (i) heavily pretreated patients with high tumor burden; (ii) lack of TREM2-high tumor enrichment; and (iii) compensatory immunosuppressive mechanisms. These findings underscore that TREM2 blockade, alone or with PD-1 inhibition, is insufficient in unselected populations and highlight the need for rational combination strategies and biomarker-driven patient selection in future trials.

Small-molecule inhibitors targeting TREM2: mechanisms and clinical prospects

OPA and artesunate (ART) have emerged as promising small molecules for targeting TREM295. ART, a widely used anti-malarial agent, has demonstrated immunomodulatory properties, while OPA, a novel derivative synthesized by tethering ART to platinum(IV), was specifically designed to enhance the inhibition of TREM2 expression and activity95,96. Notably, OPA was the first reported small molecule to directly target TREM295. In a recent study, Wang et al. conducted a large-scale compound screen and identified ART as a potent inhibitor of TREM2 expression in BV-2 murine microglia, the resident macrophages of the central nervous system97. Mechanistically, ART suppressed TREM2 expression at the transcriptional level, though the precise signaling pathways remain to be clarified. Building on this finding, OPA was synthesized and shown to significantly downregulate TREM2 expression in THP-1 macrophages, which exhibit elevated TREM2 expression upon stimulation with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA). Importantly, OPA significantly upregulated CD86, a key M1 macrophage marker, and enhanced type I interferon (IFN-I) production. By reducing TREM2 expression, OPA promoted M1 macrophage polarization and augmented the secretion of anti-tumor cytokines, including IFN-I.

In MC38 murine CRC mouse models, OPA significantly suppressed tumor growth, reduced the frequency of CX3CR1+CD206+ M2 macrophages, and increased the infiltration of immunostimulatory CD83+ DCs, CD8+ T-cells, and NK cells in tumor tissues. Additionally, in the presence of PBMCs, OPA significantly inhibited the viability of HCT116 cells, whereas only a slight reduction in viability was observed in the absence of PBMC co-culture95. These findings suggest that OPA facilitates anti-cancer immunity and may enhance tumor responses to immunotherapy, although further investigations are warranted. In another study, Carmela et al. reported that sulfavant A (SULF A), a sulfolipid with adjuvant activity, exhibited comparable binding capacity to TREM2 on the cell surface and induced an unconventional maturation of human DCs (hDCs), characterized by upregulation of MHC class II, CD86, and CD83, as well as increased migratory activity, but without conventional cytokine production105. When T lymphocytes were co-cultured with SULF A-stimulated hDCs, their proliferation and production of IL-2 and IL-4 were significantly enhanced; however, these effects were not observed in the absence of hDCs, indicating that SULF A primarily acts through DC-mediated T-cell priming and differentiation98. Intriguingly, another study demonstrated that FOXP3 was upregulated in T-cells co-cultured with hDCs and treated with SULF A99. This raises the need for further investigation into the influence of SULF A on T-cell differentiation and the tumor immune microenvironment, as well as its potential as a candidate for anti-cancer therapy. Additionally, the histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors vorinostat and romidepsin were found to significantly reduce the mRNA levels of TREM2, DAP12, and SYK in in vitro-induced senescent myeloid cells. In a CRPC mouse model, combined treatment with romidepsin and enzalutamide significantly reduced the accumulation of senescent immunosuppressive neutrophils and inhibited tumor cell proliferation69. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic promise of small-molecule inhibitors targeting TREM2, and more agents will likely be developed in the future.

TREM2 as a biomarker for patient stratification

TREM2 has emerged as a promising biomarker for patient stratification in cancer therapy due to its role in shaping the TME and influencing immune responses100,101. Its expression on myeloid cells, particularly TAMs and MDSCs, is associated with immune evasion and tumor progression65,102. Monitoring TREM2 levels in tumor biopsies or circulating immune cells may therefore help identify patients most likely to benefit from therapies targeting TREM2 signaling103. Furthermore, TREM2 expression patterns may predict responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and other immunotherapies, as high TREM2 expression often reflects a more immunosuppressive TME26,104. Stratifying patients on this basis could optimize therapeutic strategies, improving outcomes while reducing unnecessary toxicities.

Importantly, TREM2 exists in both membrane-bound (mTREM2) and soluble (sTREM2) forms, which provide complementary diagnostic information4. mTREM2, expressed on TAMs and MDSCs within the TME, can be assessed through tumor biopsies or flow cytometry and serves as a marker of local immunosuppressive activity105. In contrast, sTREM2, generated by proteolytic cleavage of the receptor ectodomain, is detectable in serum or plasma and offers a minimally invasive “liquid biopsy” approach106,107. Elevated sTREM2 levels may reflect systemic myeloid activation and enable dynamic monitoring of immune remodeling during therapy. Integrating mTREM2 and sTREM2 analyses may therefore enhance patient stratification, distinguishing local versus systemic immune states and improving predictive accuracy for response to immunotherapies.

Together, membrane-bound and sTREM2 constitute complementary biomarker modalities. While mTREM2 provides spatial and cellular resolution of immunosuppressive myeloid populations within the TME, sTREM2 allows longitudinal, minimally invasive monitoring of systemic immune changes. Challenges and perspectives

Recent studies indicate that TREM2 signaling exerts distinct, context-dependent effects in myeloid cells. For instance, in HCC, TREM2-deficient macrophages enhance the secretion of chemokines (e.g., CXCL10, CCL5, TNF-α), thereby promoting lymphocyte migration and improving CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, which ultimately inhibits tumor progression108. In contrast, in NSCLC, high infiltration of TREM2⁺ TAMs correlates with impaired responses to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy and poorer clinical outcomes61. Similarly, in CRC and pancreatic adenocarcinoma models, TREM2 blockade enhances antigen presentation, increases co-stimulatory molecule expression (CD80/CD86), and synergizes with checkpoint blockade to suppress tumor growth88,93. These divergent findings likely reflect TAM heterogeneity, tumor-type-specific immune landscapes, and tissue-dependent microenvironmental factors. A more comprehensive understanding of how TREM2 regulates macrophage phenotypes (from pro-inflammatory to immunosuppressive) is essential to refine therapeutic strategies and overcome resistance to cancer immunotherapy.

The mechanisms by which TREM2 mediates immune tolerance remain complex and incompletely understood, necessitating further investigation into its role in tumor immune evasion. Although anti-TREM2 therapies have shown promise in preclinical models, several challenges remain, including improving target specificity and optimizing drug delivery systems14,109. Future efforts should prioritize enhancing the tumor-targeting capacity of anti-TREM2 antibodies and small molecules110,111, while minimizing off-target effects on normal tissues. Importantly, TREM2 also holds potential as a biomarker for personalized therapy. Stratifying patients based on TREM2 expression levels and functional activity could enable tailored treatment approaches, leading to improved efficacy and reduced toxicity. Moving forward, research should focus on precision targeting of TREM2 in combination with the development of safer and more efficient delivery methods to accelerate its translation into cancer immunotherapy.

Conclusion and limitations

Although anti-TREM2 therapies have shown encouraging preclinical results, early clinical trials (e.g., NCT04691375, PY314) were terminated due to limited objective response rates (<10%). These findings suggest that efficacy may be constrained by inadequate patient stratification, tumor-type-specific factors, and compensatory immunosuppressive pathways. This underscores the need for context-specific strategies and rational combination therapies. Future approaches should prioritize the precise selection of TREM2-high patients, integration with ICIs or other therapeutic modalities, and the development of optimized delivery systems to enhance tumor-localized activity while minimizing off-target effects. Moreover, TREM2-expressing cells may serve as promising biomarkers in liquid biopsies, an area warranting further investigation.

Immunotherapy has transformed cancer treatment, with ICBs such as anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, and anti-CTLA4 significantly improving outcomes across multiple cancer types. However, only a subset of patients achieves durable responses. High infiltration of immunosuppressive cells within the TME strongly correlates with ICB resistance. TAMCs, the most abundant immune cells in the TME, comprise diverse populations that promote tumor progression by fostering an immunosuppressive milieu. Increasing evidence indicates that TAMC-targeted strategies can enhance anti-tumor immunity and complement ICBs16.

TREM2, a surface receptor predominantly expressed on distinct TAMC subsets, plays a central role in dampening anti-cancer immunity. Preclinical studies using anti-TREM2 antibodies or small-molecule inhibitors have demonstrated potent immunomodulatory effects (Fig. 2, Table 2)14,48,93,95,98. These strategies hold strong potential to improve anti-tumor immunity and overcome ICB resistance. However, several important questions remain to be addressed in future studies: (1) What are the developmental origins of TREM2⁺ myeloid cells, and from which specific subpopulations of TAMCs do they derive? (2) Given the heterogeneity and plasticity of TAMCs, which factors within the TME regulate TREM2 expression and modulate their functional states? (3) Current knowledge of TREM2 across tumor types is limited; how is TREM2 expressed in tumor cells, and what intrinsic roles does it play in different cancers? (4) Can TREM2-expressing cells be reliably used as biomarkers in liquid biopsies? (5) Do TREM2-targeting interventions enhance the efficacy of other anti-cancer therapies, such as chemotherapy or anti-angiogenic agents?

Collectively, TREM2-expressing immunosuppressive myeloid cells represent promising targets for anti-cancer therapy, and further development of TREM2-directed strategies is needed to enable clinical translation. In addition, it is crucial to clarify the role of TREM2 signaling in different tumor components, including diverse tumor cell populations and myeloid subsets not covered in this review, while also exploring the potential of TREM2-expressing cells for cancer diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment.
