Immunohistochemical characterization of neural stem cells in brains of adult brachycephalic dogs in comparison with canine oligodendroglioma cells
Authors: Hisaki TOKUNO, Kio YOSHIDA, James K CHAMBERS, Kazuyuki UCHIDA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brachycephalic dogs, characterized by unique dome-shaped skulls and limited cerebral cortical volumes, are predisposed to brain tumors, particularly oligodendrogliomas. Canine oligodendrogliomas often arise adjacent to the cerebral ventricles and express several neural stem cell (NSC) markers, including sex-determining region Y-box transcription factor (SOX) 2, suggesting that NSCs are their origin. To investigate why oligodendrogliomas are more prevalent in brachycephalic dogs, the quantity, immunophenotypes, and proliferative activity of NSCs were examined and compared in the brains of adult brachycephalic and non-brachycephalic dogs. Histological examinations revealed clusters of two types of cells, small round cells and medium-sized spindle cells, in the subventricular zone and caudate nucleus in all samples. Immunohistochemically, small round cells were positive for SOX2 and doublecortin, corresponding to type A NSCs (neuroblasts). Medium-sized spindle cells were positive for SOX2, SOX9, and vimentin, corresponding to type B NSCs (multipotent astrocytes). The number of SOX2-positive and proliferating cell nuclear antigen-positive cells in the caudate nucleus was significantly higher in brachycephalic dogs than in non-brachycephalic dogs. We then examined canine oligodendroglioma tissues and found that tumor cells were immunopositive for SOX2, SOX9, and vimentin, similar to the immunophenotype of type B NSCs. These results indicate that brachycephalic adult dogs retain a higher number of NSCs with higher proliferative activity in the brain, which may create an environment prone to oligodendroglioma.

INTRODUCTION

Canine breeds are classified as brachycephalic, dolichocephalic, and mesocephalic based on the cephalic index, which is the ratio of skull width to skull length [4, 15]. Brachycephalic dogs are characterized by a shortened rostrum and dome-shaped skull with a significantly smaller cerebral cortex volume than that of non-brachycephalic dogs [4, 9].

Brachycephalic dogs are predisposed to oligodendrogliomas [9, 27, 34], and approximately 75% of canine oligodendrogliomas occur in brachycephalic breeds, such as French Bulldogs, Boston Terriers, and Boxers [17, 20]. Gliomas mostly occur in adult dogs, with an average age at diagnosis of 9.1 years [20]. Across-breed genome-wide association studies identified several genes associated with glioma susceptibility in brachycephalic dogs. Genetic polymorphisms in the CAMKK2, P2RX7, DENR [35], and DVL2 [2, 26] genes of brachycephalic dogs may be associated with the high incidence of oligodendrogliomas. Moreover, we identified chromosomal gain of platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRA) and PDGFRA mutations as possible drivers of oligodendrogliomas in brachycephalic dogs [39]. However, the reasons why brachycephalic dogs are prone to developing gliomas and how their unique skull and brain morphologies, defined by genetic polymorphisms, contribute to tumorigenesis remain unknown.

Canine oligodendrogliomas often develop adjacent to the cerebral ventricles [17, 29]. We previously reported that canine oligodendroglioma cells and oligodendroglioma-derived cell lines express sex-determining region Y-box transcription factor 2 (SOX2), a neural stem cell (NSC) marker, as well as oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) markers, including oligodendrocyte transcriptional factor-2 (OLIG2), SOX10, and PDGFRA, and neuronal blast markers, such as doublecortin (DCX), βIII-tubulin, and synaptophysin [21, 40]. Although NSCs are primarily involved in brain development, fewer NSCs persist in the adult brain and proliferate to support brain plasticity [14]. In the adult mammalian brain, NSCs are mostly distributed in the subventricular zone (SVZ), caudate nucleus, and hippocampus [11, 13, 36]. Since adult NSCs share similar immunophenotypes and anatomical localizations with oligodendrogliomas, they may be the cell of origin for canine oligodendrogliomas [21, 40].

NSCs in the SVZ are classified into four types: multipotent astrocytes (type B cells), transient amplifying progenitor cells (type C cells), neuroblasts (type A cells), and ependymal cells (type E cells) [7, 13]. Type B cells express SOX2, SOX9, vimentin, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), whereas type A cells express SOX2 and DCX [8, 12, 33]. Type B cells possess a self-renewal capacity and generate transit amplifying progenitors, which ultimately differentiate into type A cells or glial cells upon activation. A recent study on the brains of human patients with gliomas found that a few type B cells in the SVZ harbored the same driver mutations as glioma cells, suggesting that these immature neural cells are the origin of gliomas [25].

We hypothesized that adult brachycephalic dogs, which have a smaller cerebral cortical volume due to a limited cranial length, have a higher number of persistent NSCs with proliferative activity around the cerebral ventricles than non-brachycephalic dogs and, thus, are more prone to developing gliomas than non-brachycephalic dogs. To investigate breed-associated factors in canine oligodendrogliomas, the present study focused on the NSCs of brachycephalic dogs as the origin of oligodendroglioma cells. We compared the quantity, immunophenotypes, and proliferative activity of NSCs in the SVZ and caudate nucleus of brachycephalic and non-brachycephalic dogs. In addition, the expression of NSC markers was immunohistochemically examined in high-grade canine oligodendrogliomas.

Histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Brains were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and cut into coronal sections to produce sections of the SVZ and caudate nucleus, as previously described [13]. Fixed tissues were routinely processed, embedded in paraffin wax, and sectioned at a thickness of 3 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed on sections. The histopathological diagnosis of high-grade oligodendroglioma was made according to a previously described classification [22].

Additional sections were subjected to IHC using primary antibodies specific to DCX (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), GFAP (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), OLIG2 (Merck, Rahway, NJ, USA), proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA, Agilent), PDGFRA (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), SOX2 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), SOX9 (Abcam), and vimentin (Agilent), as listed in Supplementary Table 1. As negative controls, each primary antibody was replaced by an irrelevant antibody. Most adult NSCs are considered to be in the stationary phase, and few NSCs with proliferative activity have been detected using PCNA, a G1/S cell cycle phase marker [14, 23]. Normal canine brain tissue was used as a positive control. Deparaffinized tissue sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol at room temperature for 4 min. After antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with 8% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline at 37°C for 40 min to prevent non-specific reactions and were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Envision horseradish peroxidase-labeled polymer anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG (Agilent) was applied at 37°C for 40 min. 3–3’-Diaminobenzidine was used as a chromogen, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Nuclear immunolabeling was considered to be positive for PCNA, SOX2, SOX9, and OLIG2 and cytoplasmic immunolabeling for DCX, GFAP, and PDGFRA.

The numbers of SOX2- and PCNA-positive NSCs in the SVZ and caudate nucleus were manually counted in 10 high-power fields (2.37 mm2). In cases of high-grade oligodendroglioma (cases 7–10), areas unaffected by the tumor were evaluated for NSCs. The immunolabeling of neoplastic cells in cases 7–10 was manually scored as follows: none, no positive cells; +, 1–30% positive cells; ++, 31–60% positive cells; +++, >60% positive cells. Since the volume of neoplastic tissue specimens was limited, tumor cell scoring was not performed on case 11.

Statistical analysis

The numbers of SOX2- and PCNA-positive cells in the SVZ and caudate nucleus of brachycephalic (n=11) and non-brachycephalic (n=13) dogs were compared, and differences were statistically analyzed using Welch’s t-test. The significance of differences was set at P<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

DISCUSSION

A previous study described NSCs in the SVZ and caudate nucleus, known as striatal neurogenic niches, in the brains of adult dogs [13]. Striatal neurogenic niches are NSC clusters observed in the striatum, including the caudate nuclei, and are involved in continuous adult neurogenesis [11, 13]. In the present study, two types of NSCs in the SVZ and caudate nucleus of adult dogs were further characterized using additional markers, including SOX2 and SOX9. Small round cells expressing SOX2 and DCX correspond to type A cells (neuroblasts), while medium-sized spindle cells expressing SOX2, SOX9, GFAP, and vimentin correspond to type B cells (multipotent astrocytes) [8, 12, 13, 33]. The medium-sized spindle cells in the caudate nucleus were negative for GFAP, a type B cell marker, whereas those in the SVZ were positive for GFAP. However, these NSCs were positive for other type B cell markers, SOX2, SOX9, and vimentin, and negative for the type A cell marker DCX, and were considered appropriate for categorization as type B cells. Type B cells in striatal neurogenic niches are thought to be migrating cells derived from the SVZ [11]; therefore, their functional states may be associated with a lack of GFAP expression in dogs. The results obtained herein showed that striatal neurogenic niches in the adult canine brain comprised type A and B cells, both of which were positive for SOX2.

The present study also demonstrated that the median number of SOX2-positive cells comprising the striatal neurogenic niche in the caudate nucleus was significantly higher in brachycephalic dogs than in non-brachycephalic dogs. SOX2, a member of the SOXB1 family, plays an essential role in regulating stem cell self-renewal and differentiation [3]. Furthermore, the mean number of PCNA-positive cells comprising the striatal neurogenic niche in the caudate nucleus was significantly higher in brachycephalic dogs than in non-brachycephalic dogs. No significant difference was observed in the mean number of SOX2- or PCNA-positive cells in the striatal neurogenic niche of the caudate nucleus between brachycephalic dogs with and without gliomas. These results suggest that the high number and proliferative activity of NSCs in the caudate nucleus of adult brachycephalic dogs are associated with their genetic background and skull morphology rather than with the presence of gliomas.

The genetic background underlying skull morphology may be related to NSC dynamics in brachycephalic dogs. For example, variations in Bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) 3 contribute to the diverse cranial shapes of dogs [32], while BMPs are also involved in the fate and maturation of NSCs during neurulation and throughout adulthood [18]. A frameshift mutation in the WNT pathway gene DVL2 has been implicated in vertebral malformation in brachycephalic dogs [26]. The DVL family is also involved in neurogenesis [16, 26, 42], and DVL2 signaling has been shown to promote self-renewal and tumorigenicity in human gliomas [30]. Therefore, variations in genes involved in cranial morphogenesis may affect the maturation and proliferative activity of NSCs, leading to an adult NSC state prone to the development of gliomas.

Adult NSCs are derived from a slowly dividing subpopulation of embryonic NSCs at the end of development [14]. Brain development may be affected by cranial morphology, as observed in craniosynostosis and microcephaly [24]. In human craniosynostosis, an abnormal skull shape restricts brain expansion during development, resulting in the deformation of brain tissue [19]. Brachycephalic dogs have shortened skulls and smaller gray matter volumes in the olfactory bulb, frontal cortex, marginal gyrus, and cerebellum than non-brachycephalic dogs [4, 31]. Previous studies suggested that brachycephalic dogs are an ideal model for studying skull-brain covariations and development [4, 28]. The present results showing that brachycephalic dogs had a higher number of adult NSCs than non-brachycephalic dogs may reflect the abnormal differentiation or persistence of embryonic NSCs associated with the skull and brain morphologies of dogs, which are decided by the breed’s genetic background.

Canine high-grade oligodendrogliomas were immunolabeled for SOX2, SOX9, and vimentin, similar to the immunophenotype of canine adult NSCs. These molecules are also expressed in human gliomas [1, 37, 41]. In human glioblastoma, approximately 80% of neoplastic cells are positive for SOX2, and SOX2 positivity correlates with tumor grade [5, 10]. SOX2 has also been shown to regulate gene expression and pathways associated with tumor malignancy, such as SOX21, fatty acid-binding protein, IL-8, and the Wnt pathway [3, 5]. Canine oligodendrogliomas share many pathological and molecular features with human glioblastomas, and both are considered to be derived from immature multipotent cells, such as NSCs and OPCs [2, 21, 22]. SOX9 is a transcription factor that is necessary for the maintenance of multipotent NSCs and is involved in the shift from neurogenesis to gliogenesis [6, 33]. Vimentin is expressed in immature neurons and glia, including type B cells and radial glia [8]. Based on these findings and the present results, canine adult type B cells in the SVZ and caudate nucleus, the number of which is higher in brachycephalic dogs, may be the cell of origin of canine oligodendrogliomas.

In conclusion, we herein characterized the immunophenotypes of striatal neurogenic niches in the adult canine brain using NSC markers. The number of SOX2-positive adult NSCs in the brain was higher and their proliferative activity was also higher in brachycephalic dogs, which are predisposed to gliomas, than in non-brachycephalic dogs. Moreover, canine high-grade oligodendrogliomas consistently expressed SOX2. The unique cranial morphology of brachycephalic dogs may be associated with a higher number and higher proliferative activity of adult NSCs, creating an environment prone to the development of oligodendrogliomas.
