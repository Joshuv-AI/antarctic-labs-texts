Audit on knowledge, attitudes and practices on developmental assessment among public health midwives in a sub-urban health area in Sri Lanka
Authors: Aruni Gallage, Peshala Settinayake, Nisansala Karunathilake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAge-appropriate early childhood development is a key determinant of later educational attainment, social functioning, and economic productivity. In Sri Lanka, Public Health Midwives (PHMs) are frontline primary healthcare providers responsible for early childhood care and development (ECCD), including routine developmental assessment (DA). This audit aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices related to DA among PHMs in a sub-urban Medical Officer of Health (MOH) area in Sri Lanka.MethodsThe audit was conducted among PHMs attached to the Mahara MOH area in the Western Province of Sri Lanka. Audit standards were derived from the ECCD Handbook and national developmental standards for Sri Lankan infants and toddlers issued by the Ministry of Health. A structured, self-administered questionnaire assessed knowledge across multiple developmental domains, attitudes towards routine DA, and self-reported DA practices. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise findings.ResultsOf 54 eligible PHMs, 44 participated (response rate: 81.4 %). All participants agreed that DA should be routinely conducted during service encounters. However, none reported being fully confident in performing a comprehensive DA. The mean overall knowledge score was 63.7% (SD = 8.4). Knowledge was lowest for assessing primary reflexes (mean = 49.8%) and cut-off ages for milestone attainment (mean = 32.9%). While attitudes towards DA were generally favourable, reported routine practice and referral for further developmental evaluation were limited.ConclusionsAlthough PHMs demonstrated acceptable overall knowledge and favourable attitudes towards DA, important gaps were identified in critical areas such as knowledge on milestone cut-off ages, primary reflexes, confidence, and routine practice. Strengthening in-service training, supportive supervision, and referral pathways is essential. Background

Early childhood development plays a critical role in shaping an individual’s cognitive capacity, educational achievement, behaviour, and long-term socioeconomic outcomes [1–3]. Brain development begins in early gestation and continues throughout childhood, with the most rapid growth occurring in the first few years of life [3, 4]. During this period, neural connections are strengthened or eliminated through a process known as neuronal pruning, which enables the brain to function more efficiently based on experience and stimulation [5]. Appropriate stimulation and timely identification of developmental delays during early childhood are therefore essential [6].

Developmental assessment (DA) is a structured evaluation of a child’s functioning across key developmental domains, commonly including gross motor, fine motor and vision, hearing and speech, and social-cognitive development [7]. Children achieve developmental milestones within expected age ranges, and failure to attain milestones by defined cut-off ages may indicate developmental delay [8]. Delays may affect one or multiple domains and, if not identified early, can lead to long-term functional impairment and reduced educational readiness [9].

Globally, an estimated 52.9 million children under five years live with developmental disabilities, with the vast majority residing in low- and middle-income countries [10, 11]. The prevalence of global developmental delay is estimated to range between 1% and 3% of birth cohorts [12]. In Sri Lanka, despite favourable maternal and child health indicators, evidence suggests a substantial burden of developmental vulnerability [13]. A community-based study assessing school readiness at five years of age reported that nearly one-fifth of children demonstrated developmental delays affecting preparedness for formal education [14, 15].

Sri Lanka’s public health system is organised around Medical Officer of Health (MOH) areas, which serve as the smallest administrative units for preventive healthcare delivery [16, 17]. Public Health Midwives are the primary frontline healthcare providers within this system, responsible for delivering reproductive, maternal, newborn, child, adolescent, and youth health services [17]. Developmental surveillance and early identification of developmental delays are core responsibilities of PHMs during domiciliary visits and child welfare clinics [18].

Although DA is included in the PHM training curriculum and supported by national ECCD guidelines, evidence suggests that healthcare providers at primary care level often lack sufficient confidence and skills to perform comprehensive assessments and initiate timely referrals [18, 19]. Periodic in-service training is provided; however, the effectiveness of such training in maintaining competencies has not been adequately evaluated.

Within this context, this audit was conducted to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices related to DA among PHMs in a sub-urban MOH area in Sri Lanka, with the aim of identifying gaps to inform quality improvement and capacity-building initiatives.

Methods

Study design and reporting framework

This study was conducted as a clinical audit to assess current practice against established national standards. An internal audit is an independent process with the objective of adding value and improving the operations of an organization. Auditing improves the effectiveness, control, risk management and governance process through a systematic and disciplined approach [20]. On the other hand, a clinical audit refers to a quality improvement process that is necessary for improving patient care and outcomes through a systematic review of care against a set gold standard or explicit criteria known as audit criteria, and the implementation of change. It is an essential part of clinical governance [21]. The findings are reported in accordance with the SQUIRE 2.0 (Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence) guidelines [22].

Study setting and participants

The audit was conducted in the Mahara Medical Officer of Health (MOH) area, a sub-urban health division located in the Western Province of Sri Lanka. The MOH area serves an estimated population of approximately 275,000 and comprises 54 PHM areas. All PHMs attached to the Mahara MOH area were eligible to participate. Data collection was conducted during a routine monthly in-service training programme, which all PHMs are expected to attend as part of continuous professional development activities.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using n=z2pq/i2, where n is the sample size, z2 is the abscissa of the normal curve that cuts off an area at the tails, i is the desired level of precision (0.05), p is the estimated proportion of an expected score for the questionnaire (was taken as 90%, since a high score is expected at from PHMs) and q is 1-p, the calculated sample size for the audit would be 138 [21]. However, the entire PHM population in Mahara MOH area is 54. Therefore, a convenient sample was taken including all the PHMs into the audit.

Audit standards

Audit standards were derived from two national reference documents issued by the Ministry of Health, Sri Lanka:

Early Childhood Care and Development Handbook for Primary Caregivers (2014) [23].

Early Childhood Care and Development Handbook for Primary Caregivers (2014) [23].

Developmental Standards for Sri Lankan Infants and Toddlers (2013) [24].

Developmental Standards for Sri Lankan Infants and Toddlers (2013) [24].

These documents are used in PHM pre-service and in-service training and outline nationally endorsed expectations for DA and surveillance.

Data collection tool and measures

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was developed based directly on the content of the national ECCD reference documents. The questionnaire assessed three domains: knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices related to DA.

Knowledge was assessed using 165 items covering multiple components of DA, including primary reflexes and basic developmental anatomy; gross motor development; fine motor and vision; hearing, speech, and language; social, cognitive, and behavioural development; self-care behaviours; and age-appropriate milestone attainment with corresponding cut-off ages.

Attitudes towards DA were assessed using five statements rated on a five-point Likert scale, focusing on perceived importance, motivation, and confidence in performing DA.

Practices were assessed using two Likert-scale items addressing the frequency of conducting DAs during routine service encounters and referral practices.

The questionnaire was developed in Sinhala, the primary language used by PHMs in training and service delivery, to ensure clarity and accurate comprehension of technical concepts. An English translation is provided as a supplementary file.

Validation and administration

The questionnaire underwent judgemental validation by a panel consisting of a community physician, a community paediatrician, and a public health nursing sister. The audit tool was administered during the in-service programme after explaining the purpose of the audit and obtaining informed verbal consent. Participants completed the questionnaire independently within the allocated time.

Following completion of data collection, a structured educational discussion and capacity-building session was conducted. This session formed part of the quality improvement intervention and did not influence the audit data collected. A community paediatrician conducted a capacity-building program to address specific developmental problems based on a needs assessment. Another audit cycle was planned to be conducted in 3 months to assess the impact of the intervention. This audit cycle was planned to improve the early detection of developmental delays and to strengthen referral pathways.

Scoring and analysis

Each knowledge item was weighted equally and scored as correct or incorrect. Missing or non-responses were scored as zero. Domain-specific and overall knowledge scores were converted into percentages. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, and proportions, were used to summarise findings.

Ethical considerations

Informed verbal consent was obtained from the PHMs before distributing the questionnaire. They were able to clarify any doubts directly. The participants were clearly explained that this was a quality improvement measure of the MOH. The confidentiality of the PHMs was ensured by not collecting personal details. Conducting clinical audits after a needs assessment is highly encouraged by Sri Lankan government and the process is facilitated exempting an audit from ethics approval by the Cabinet Memorandum No 15/0652/616/037 dated 04th March 2015, was approved by the Cabinet of Ministers of Democratic Socialist of Republic of Sri Lanka on 27th May 2015 [25] where it explains that conducting clinical audits is a national priority to improve healthcare quality of Sri Lanka. An audit significantly differs from research from its aspects of quality improvement and new knowledge generation. Therefore, it’s widely discussed that an audit can and should be exempt from ethics approval procedure [26–30].

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The response rate was 84.6% (n = 44). All the PHMs were female, Sinhalese, and Buddhists, and they studied up to GCE Advanced Level in the Science Stream. The mean age was 41.6 years with a standard deviation (SD) of 9.8 years. The mode and median were 40 years. The range and inter-quartile-range were 38 and 10, respectively. The mean work experience was 14.8 years, and the SD was 8.7 years. The work experience followed a bimodal distribution with modes at 10 and 15 years.

The mean score for knowledge on primary reflexes and basic developmental anatomy was 49.88% (SD = 15.36). It was interesting to see that none (n = 44) responded to the statement on handedness/hand preference at the age of one year among the knowledge in the gross motor component. The cumulative mean score for social, behavioural and cognitive domains was 72.5%, followed by fine motor and vision at 70.75%, then the gross motor domain, and hearing and speech at 67.45%. It was noted that the lowest mean scores (32.92%) were obtained for knowledge on cutoff ages.

Attitudes on DA

All the participants (n = 44) responded “yes” to the question on their beliefs regarding the necessity of performing a routine DA at every service encounter. However, for the question on motivation to perform a DA on all children under the age of five years at all service encounters, 50% (n = 22) responded to being “somewhat motivated” and only 39% (n = 17) responded to being “highly motivated”. Meanwhile, 9% (n = 4) were “neutral” and 2% (n = 1) were “usually not motivated”. It was noted that none responded as “not motivated at all”. The majority, 61% (n = 27), were “neutral” when asked about their confidence in performing a full DA and detecting a developmental delay. Meanwhile, 16% (n = 7) were “somewhat confident”, 11% (n = 5) were “highly confident”, 5% (n = 2) were “not confident at all”, and 7% (n = 3) were “usually not confident”.

Practices on DA

Practices on DA were assessed using two questions, and the answers were based on a Likert scale. The majority, 68% (n = 30) claimed that they performed a DA “sometimes” during their routine service encounters over the past three months, while 16% (n = 7) responded that they “usually do not” perform a DA. Additionally, 2% (n = 1) reported that they have never performed a DA over the past three months. Only one PHM (2%) claimed that she has always performed a DA in every service encounter, while 12% (n = 5) claimed that they “usually perform”.

Out of the 44 who PHMs participated in the in-service programme, there were five (12%) referrals to the developmental paediatrician over the past four months, which was confirmed by the quarterly maternal and child health report.

Out of the 54 PHMs, only 44 were able to participate in the audit for three hours. The other ten PHMs had to leave the programme due to unavoidable service needs. Therefore, they were considered non-responders. The missing answers were scored zero when marking.

Discussion

This audit assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices related to DA among Public Health Midwives in a sub-urban Medical Officer of Health area in Sri Lanka, with the objective of identifying gaps to inform quality improvement. The findings demonstrate an important discordance between acceptable overall knowledge and favourable attitudes on one hand, and limited confidence, inconsistent routine practice, and low referral rates on the other.

The overall knowledge score observed in this audit was above average; however, performance varied substantially across developmental domains. Knowledge was comparatively better in social, cognitive, and behavioural development as well as fine motor and vision domains, while markedly lower scores were observed for primary reflexes and cut-off ages for milestone attainment. These latter components are critical for early identification of developmental delay, as failure to recognise abnormal primary reflexes or delayed milestone achievement may result in missed opportunities for early referral and intervention. The particularly low scores related to cut-off ages are of concern, as caregivers frequently seek guidance from PHMs regarding expected developmental milestones, and inaccurate information at this level may delay recognition of red flags.

Despite universal agreement among participants regarding the importance of routine DA, self-reported confidence in performing a comprehensive assessment was low. This gap between perceived importance and confidence is likely to contribute to the observed inconsistency in practice. Since practices were assessed using self-reported measures rather than direct observation of clinical encounters or review of supervision records, the extent of the observed gap between attitudes and practices should be interpreted with caution. Objective assessments such as direct observation, record audits, or referral tracking systems may provide a more accurate estimate of routine DA practices. Although a majority of PHMs reported performing some form of DA intermittently, only a small proportion reported doing so routinely, and referral rates to specialist developmental services were low. These findings suggest that while DA is accepted in principle as part of the PHM role, it may not be fully integrated into routine service delivery due to limitations in skill, confidence, or perceived feasibility within existing workloads.

Findings from this audit are broadly consistent with evidence from other low- and middle-income settings, where healthcare providers demonstrate positive attitudes towards developmental surveillance but limited practical competence and confidence. Studies among early childhood practitioners and primary healthcare workers in South Africa and the Democratic Republic of Congo have similarly reported gaps in applied knowledge and routine practice despite awareness of importance [31, 32]. In contrast, studies from high-income countries indicate higher rates of developmental monitoring, although confidence in identifying specific developmental disorders remains variable [33]. The differences observed across settings highlight the importance of context-specific training and support systems rather than reliance on generic curricular exposure.

A key implication of this audit is the need to strengthen in-service training programmes for PHMs with a focused emphasis on DA as an essential and explicit role expectation. Training should prioritise applied competencies, particularly the assessment of primary reflexes, recognition of delayed milestone attainment using cut-off ages, and decision-making regarding timely referral. Periodic refresher training alone may be insufficient unless accompanied by structured practical components, supportive supervision, and clear referral pathways.

Timely referral for further developmental evaluation and early intervention is particularly important, as evidence indicates that early initiation of intervention substantially improves outcomes in conditions such as speech delay, autism spectrum disorder, and behavioural disorders. PHMs also play a crucial role in communicating developmental findings and recommendations to families. Effective communication is essential to ensure caregiver understanding, acceptance of referrals, and adherence to follow-up, particularly in community settings where misconceptions and stigma may influence health-seeking behaviour.

Bias, limitations and strengths

This audit was conducted in a single MOH area and therefore has limited generalisability to the national context. However, the findings provide valuable insight into potential systemic gaps in DA practices at the primary care level. Similar audits across multiple MOH areas representing diverse geographic and socioeconomic contexts would be beneficial to inform national-level policy and training reforms.

One strength of the audit is the representation of different age groups with a considerably accountable response rate. However, the audit primarily used descriptive statistical analysis to assess performance against predefined standards. Potential determinants such as age, years of experience, and prior training exposure were not examined as predictors of knowledge or practices as it was beyond the service objective. Future analytical studies may explore these factors to better understand variations in DA practices among PHMs.

Attrition bias can be a problem in this audit since some of the audit papers were removed from analysis due to missing data and incomplete records. This can distort the results, especially if the PHMs who did not respond or had missing data were not random. Furthermore, the results relied on self-reported practices measured using a Likert-scale responses rather than direct observations of clinical encounters or supervision records. Self-reported practices may be influenced by social desirability bias, whereby participants tend to overestimate their adherence to recommended practices. Hence, the reported frequency of DA practices may represent an optimistic estimate of the actual practice.

The audit tool comprehensively assessed theoretical knowledge of developmental milestones and related concepts. But it did not directly measure applied competency or clinical reasoning in real-world practice settings. Therefore, future studies incorporating competency-based assessments or direct observation of DA practices would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of PHM performance.

Sampling was done in a routine in-service training session. This allowed access to majority of PHMs in the area. However it may have introduced selection bias if those attending such sessions differ systematically from those who regularly do not attend in terms of engagement of professional development activities.

However, the audit was started with clear and defined criteria. Specifying clear audit standards and criteria improved methodological transparency and ensured that findings could be interpreted against nationally endorsed benchmarks. However, such methodological rigor does not eliminate potential biases associated with self-reported data collection [34]. Standard guidelines were used to ensure a transparent reporting process that included all relevant findings. This helps to reduce reporting bias and fosters trust in the audit’s results.

The audit also did not incorporate the perspectives from caregivers or community members regarding developmental screening at the primary health care level since it is beyond the scope of this audit. Including service-user perspectives in future research may provide valuable insights into barriers and facilitators affecting early identification and referral of children with developmental delays.

Conclusion

This audit identified that Public Health Midwives demonstrated acceptable overall knowledge and favourable attitudes towards DA; however, important gaps were evident in critical areas required for early identification of developmental delay. Knowledge of primary reflexes and milestone cut-off ages was suboptimal, and self-reported confidence and routine practice of comprehensive DA were limited.

Given the central role of PHMs in Sri Lanka’s primary healthcare system, strengthening their capacity to perform DA is essential. In-service training programmes should be reviewed and enhanced to emphasise applied DA skills, timely referral pathways, and effective communication with families. Supportive supervision and periodic re-audits are recommended to ensure sustained improvement in practice and to strengthen early identification and intervention for children with developmental delays.
