Effects of chronic jetlag on high-fat and high-fructose diet induced renal injury in mice
Authors: Lin Xing, Junyan Xie, Ying Shi, Ruoyi Zheng, Tao Zhang, Xuejing Li, Zhijun Zhou, Dongmei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic disruption of the circadian rhythm caused by factors such as jetlag has been associated with increased risks of obesity and chronic kidney disease. Although obesity is a well-known contributor to renal injury, the impact of circadian misalignment on obesity-related kidney disease (ORKD) remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate whether chronic jetlag exacerbates ORKD and to explore its association with the NLRP3/Caspase-1/IL-1β inflammasome signaling pathway. To simulate real-world lifestyle stressors, we established a model wherein mice underwent periodic light–dark cycle alterations (chronic jetlag, JL) combined with a high-fat and high-fructose diet (HFHFD). HFHFD-fed mice exhibited significantly elevated serum lipid levels, whereas chronic jetlag further aggravated body weight gain, glucose intolerance, and urinary protein excretion. Histologically, HFHFD-JL mice exhibited aggravated glomerular hypertrophy, sclerosis, tubular degeneration, and podocyte injury. Chronic jetlag disrupted renal circadian gene rhythms and markedly upregulated NLRP3, Caspase-1, and IL-1β expression, which positively correlated with glomerulosclerosis and fibrosis. In conclusion, chronic jetlag exacerbates renal injuries in obese mice, potentially in association with the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. These findings highlighted circadian disruption as an additional pathophysiological stressor that amplifies obesity-related renal injuries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54538-5.

Introduction

Obesity, a global health crisis with an estimated prevalence of 34% by 20351, often co-exists with diabetes and hypertension, but it has become clear over the past several decades that obesity is an independent cause of chronic kidney disease, termed obesity-related kidney disease (ORKD)2. ORKD is a clinical entity characterized by albuminuria, glomerulomegaly, secondary focal glomerulosclerosis and progressive renal dysfunction, potentially culminating in end-stage kidney disease (ESKD)3–5. Given the ongoing global obesity epidemic, ORKD is of increasing clinical importance. Nonetheless, the mechanisms responsible for the progression of ORKD and its underlying pathogenesis remain insufficiently elucidated.

Environmental influences have been increasingly recognized in the development of chronic kidney disease (CKD)6. One such factor is chronic jetlag, which arises from repeated misalignment between social time and the body’s internal rhythm, commonly experienced by shift workers, frequent flyers, and individuals exposed to irregular light–dark cycles7,8. With the rise of round-the-clock societal demands in modern 24/7 society, chronic jetlag has become an inevitable globalized phenomenon9. Epidemiological studies suggest that shift work is associated with increased risks of CKD and reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate10–12, implying that chronic jetlag may contribute to the development of kidney diseases.

Chronic jetlag serves as a potent disruptor of chronobiology, with adverse health consequences stemming from its disturbance of circadian rhythmicity. The circadian rhythm is orchestrated by an intrinsic timekeeping system that entrains living organisms’ behavioral and physiological processes to the 24-hour solar cycle. In mammals, the circadian system is governed by the central pacemaker located in suprachiasmatic nucleus, which coordinates peripheral oscillators in multiple tissues including the kidney, where local clocks regulate renal blood flow, glomerular filtration, and tubular electrolyte transport13. Experimental studies underscore a pathological link between circadian disruption and CKD progression. For example, repeated light–dark cycle reversal aggravates renal dysfunction, fibrosis, and tissue damage in mice subjected to unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO)14. Clinical observations further support this link, as CKD patients with reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate frequently present with shorter sleep duration, increased sleep fragmentation, and delayed sleep timing15. Collectively, these studies provide mechanistic plausibility that circadian disruption contributes to CKD pathogenesis, and chronic jetlag may theoretically accelerate the progression of ORKD.

In addition, a strong link between circadian disruption and obesity has been well-established. In Spanish adolescents, social jetlag was significantly associated with higher body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, fat percentage, and increased odds of general and abdominal obesity16. Experimental models corroborate this relationship. Animals exposed to chronic jetlag or with mutations in core clock genes develop obesity and adverse metabolic consequences17–19. However, the mechanistic pathways particularly in the context of environmental and lifestyle stressors linking obesity to CKD remain insufficiently elucidated.

The NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome is a cytosolic multiprotein complex composed of NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC), and pro-caspase-120. Upon activation, the NLRP3 inflammasome cleaves pro-caspase-1 into active caspase-1, which subsequently cleaves the precursors of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) into mature inflammatory cytokines21. Recent studies have underscored the significance of NLRP3 inflammasome in the occurrence and development of CKD22,23. In human renal biopsies, increased NLRP3 expression has been observed in various kidney diseases, correlating with worsening renal function24. In animal models of obesity-induced glomerulopathy, heightened expression of NLRP3 inflammasome components was detected in damaged podocytes25. Furthermore, NLRP3 deficiency in mice attenuated tubular injury, inflammation, and fibrosis after UUO, associated with reduced caspase-1 activation and maturation of IL-1β and IL-1826. Small molecule inhibitors targeting NLRP3 have also been shown to ameliorate podocyte injury and renal fibrosis in diabetic nephropathy model27–29. These findings emphasize the pathological role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in ORKD and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target.

Chronic jetlag is often accompanied by unhealthy dietary patterns, particularly the excessive consumption of fats and refined sugars. To model this real-world scenario, we utilized a high-fat, high-fructose diet (HFHFD) to establish a diet-induced obesity model combined with periodic light–dark cycle alterations (chronic jetlag, JL). Mice fed a normal diet (ND) and maintained under a normal light–dark cycle (NC) served as controls. This study aimed to investigate the effects of chronic jetlag-induced circadian disruption on HFHFD-induced renal injury and its association with the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.

Results

Effects of chronic jetlag on body weight and adipose tissue mass

No significant differences in calorie intake and water intake were observed between the ND-NC and ND-JL groups (Fig. 1A and B), but body weight (BW) in the ND-JL group was significantly higher than that in the ND-NC group starting at the 9th week (Fig. 1C). Similarly, in HFHFD-fed mice, the HFHFD-JL group exhibited significantly higher BW than the HFHFD-NC group from the 10th week (Fig. 1C), despite no significant differences in calorie intake (Fig. 1A).

At the end of the study, HFHFD-fed mice showed significantly increased BW, visceral adipose tissue (VAT) mass, VAT/BW ratio, subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) mass, and SAT/BW ratio in comparison with ND-fed mice [all p (diet) < 0.001] (Fig. 1D-H). In ND-fed mice, BW in the ND-JL group was significantly higher than those in the ND-NC group (Fig. 1D). Compared to the HFHFD-NC group, HFHFD-JL group displayed significantly greater BW [p (jetlag) < 0.001] (Fig. 1D), indicating that chronic jetlag exacerbated diet-induced weight gain. VAT mass, SAT mass, VAT/BW and SAT/BW ratio were not significantly different between the HFHFD-JL and HFHFD-NC groups (Fig. 1E-H).

Effects of chronic jetlag on glucose homeostasis and metabolic parameters

The intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) results revealed elevated blood glucose levels at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min in HFHFD-fed mice vs. ND-fed mice (Fig. 2A). In ND-fed mice, ND-JL mice had significantly higher glucose levels at 30 min and 60 min vs. ND-NC mice (Fig. 2A). ND-JL mice also showed significantly increased area under the curve (AUC) of IPGTT than ND-NC mice (Fig. 2B). The AUC of IPGTT in the HFHFD-JL group was higher than those in the ND-NC, ND-JL, and HFHFD-NC groups, with significant main effects of both diet (p < 0.001) and jetlag (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Fasting blood glucose (FBG) and insulin levels were significantly elevated in HFHFD-fed mice compared to ND-fed mice. Chronic jetlag further increased FBG in ND-fed mice and insulin level in HFHFD-fed mice respectively [both p (diet) < 0.001, p (jetlag) < 0.001] (Fig. 2C-D). Homeostasis model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was significantly higher in HFHFD-JL vs. HFHFD-NC, indicating that chronic jetlag aggravates insulin resistance under HFHFD conditions [p (diet × jetlag) < 0.05] (Fig. 2C-E).

Serum total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels in HFHFD-fed mice were significantly higher than those in ND-fed mice [all p (diet) < 0.001], while there was no significant difference in serum triglycerides (TG) level among groups (Figure S2A-D). Besides, serum corticosterone levels revealed no significant differences among groups (Figure S2E). To assess the onset and progression of renal functional injury, we measured 24-hour urinary albumin at the 6th and 15th week. At the 6th week, HFHFD-fed mice already exhibited significantly elevated urinary albumin compared to ND-fed mice [p (diet) < 0.01], while no significant effect of JL was observed at this early stage (Fig. 2H). By the end of the intervention, HFHFD-JL mice had significantly increased kidney weight and 24 h urinary albumin in comparison with ND-NC, ND-JL, and HFHFD-NC mice [both p (diet) < 0.001, p (jetlag) < 0.05] (Fig. 2F and I). Compared to ND-fed mice, HFHFD-fed mice had significantly decreased kidney weight/BW ratio [p (diet) < 0.001] (Fig. 2G). Renal functional indices including serum creatinine (Cr), uric acid (UA) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) did not differ significantly among groups (Figure S2F-H).

Chronic jetlag aggravated renal histopathological changes in HFHFD-fed mice

In the HFHFD-induced obesity model, characteristic features of ORKD were observed. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining showed marked glomerular lesions, including glomerulomegaly and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), predominantly of the perihilar and not otherwise specified variants, with no evidence of tip, cellular, or collapsing types (Fig. 3A and E). Likewise, glomerular volume was significantly increased in HFHFD-fed mice vs. ND-fed mice [p (diet) < 0.01] (Fig. 3B). Semi-quantitative assessment confirmed significantly increased glomerular sclerosis scores in HFHFD-fed mice compared with ND-fed mice [p (diet) < 0.01], with further exacerbation in the HFHFD-JL group [p (jetlag) < 0.05] (Fig. 3H). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed distinct podocyte injuries in HFHFD-fed mice, as evidenced by degradation or fusion of the podocyte foot process (indicated by red arrows) and glomerular basement membrane (GBM) thickening (yellow asterisks) (Fig. 3G). In contrast, podocyte foot processes in ND-fed mice showed tight adhesion to the GBM with uniform distribution.

Concomitantly, pronounced tubular injuries were observed in HFHFD-fed mice, involving tubular atrophy and dilatation, loss of the brush border, and vacuolar degeneration (Fig. 3C). Quantitative analysis showed significant elevations in tubular injury scores in HFHFD-fed mice vs. ND-fed mice (Fig. 3D). These renal tubular lesions were aggravated in HFHFD-JL mice compared to HFHFD-NC mice as indicated by the significant elevated renal tubular injury scores [p (diet) < 0.01, p (jetlag) < 0.05] (Fig. 3C-D). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed extensive renal interstitial fibrosis in HFHFD-fed mice, which was more severe in HFHFD-JL mice vs. HFHFD-ND mice (Fig. 3E). Consistently, densitometric analyses confirmed significantly increased collagen positive area in the HFHFD-JL group compared to the HFHFD-NC group [p (diet) < 0.01, p (jetlag) < 0.01] (Fig. 3F).

Effects of chronic jetlag on renal expression of nephrin, podocin and NGAL proteins

To evaluate renal podocyte and tubular injury, the expression of nephrin, podocin and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) was assessed. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining showed that expression of nephrin was significantly decreased in HFHFD-fed mice compared to ND-fed mice [p (diet) < 0.001] (Fig. 4A-B). Consistently, the expression of podocin was significantly reduced in HFHFD-fed mice relative to ND-fed controls (Fig. 4C-D). In contrast, NGAL expression was significantly elevated in HFHFD-fed mice vs. ND-fed mice, with HFHFD-JL mice displayed even higher expression of NGAL than HFHFD-NC mice (Fig. 4E-F). Main effects of diet (p < 0.001) and jetlag (p < 0.001), as well as diet × jetlag interaction effects (p < 0.01) were observed for NGAL expression. Overall, these data suggested that chronic jetlag exacerbated HFHFD-induced renal tubular injury.

Chronic jetlag disrupted the rhythmicity of core clock genes in kidney

The mRNA expression levels of core circadian clock genes Clock, Bmal1, Cry1, Cry2, Per1, Per2, and Rev-erbα in mouse kidney tissue were detected using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). In the ND-NC group, all genes exhibited rhythmic transcriptional oscillations, indicating renal circadian rhythmicity at the molecular level (Fig. 5A-G). ND-JL mice lost rhythmic expression of Clock, while HFHFD-NC mice exhibited disrupted rhythmicity of Cry1, Cry2. In the HFHFD-JL group, only Bmal1 and Cry1 retained rhythmic oscillations, while the other five genes lost rhythmicity (Fig. 5A-G). BMAL1 protein expression also mirrored the corresponding mRNA rhythmic changes among groups (Figure S3). Notably, Rev-erbα mRNA and REV-ERBα protein expressions were significantly decreased at zeitgeber time (ZT) 2, ZT8 and ZT14 in HFHFD-fed mice compared to ND-fed mice (Fig. 5G, S3). Analysis of circadian parameters revealed significant differences in acrophase, amplitude, and mesor of Rev-erbα among groups, with HFHFD-NC mice displaying a completely reversed acrophase compared to ND-fed mice (Fig. 5H). Compared to ND-NC mice, ND-JL mice exhibited significant elevation in amplitude of Per2 and Rev-erbα, while HFHFD-NC mice displayed increased amplitude of Per2, and decreased amplitude of Rev-erbα (Table 1). In ND-fed mice, ND-JL mice displayed significant elevation in mesor of Per2, Rev-erbα and reduction in mesor of Cry2. Notably, for several genes in the HFHFD-fed groups, circadian parameters could not be determined (indicated as NA in Table 1) due to a complete loss of significant rhythmicity. Among the rhythms that still persisted, HFHFD-NC mice showed increased mesor of Bmal1, Per2, and decreased in mesor of Rev-erbα while HFHFD-JL mice exhibited significant elevation in mesor of Cry1 vs. ND-NC mice (Table 1). These findings indicate that both chronic jetlag and HFHFD independently disrupt circadian parameters, with their combination leading to the most severe loss of circadian rhythmicity in the kidney.

Chronic jetlag changed rhythmic expression of NLRP3 in kidney and promoted NLRP3 inflammasome activation in HFHFD mice

In addition to core clock genes, renal Nlrp3 exhibited robust diurnal variations in all groups except the ND-JL group. Nlrp3 mRNA expression was significantly increased at ZT20 in HFHFD-fed mice than ND-fed mice (Fig. 6A). Mesor analysis showed significantly higher Nlrp3 levels in HFHFD-fed mice vs. ND-NC mice, with no significant differences in amplitude or acrophase (Fig. 5H; Table 1). As shown in Fig. 6B, HFHFD-fed mice demonstrated increased renal mRNA expression of Nlrp3, caspase-1, IL-18, IL-1β vs. ND-fed mice [p (diet) < 0.001]. These proinflammatory genes were further elevated in HFHFD-JL mice relative to HFHFD-NC mice [p (jetlag) < 0.05] (Fig. 6C–E), indicating enhanced NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Correspondingly, cleaved Caspase-1 p20 and IL-1β protein levels were elevated in HFHFD-JL mice [p (diet) < 0.001, p (jetlag) < 0.01] (Fig. 6J–K). In addition, the ratio of cleaved caspase-1 p20 to pro-caspase-1 was significantly increased in the HFHFD-JL group [p (diet) < 0.001, p (jetlag) < 0.01] (Fig. 6K). Immunofluorescence (IF), IHC, and Western blot confirmed increased NLRP3 protein expression in HFHFD-fed mice, with further enhancement in HFHFD-JL mice [p (diet) < 0.001, p (jetlag) < 0.05, p (diet × jetlag) < 0.05] (Fig. 6F-I, L-M).

Discussion

Chronic jetlag extends beyond international travel across time zones and has become a daily reality for millions of individuals due to rotating shift work, late-night social activities, exposure to artificial light at night, and irregular sleep-wake schedules30. With increasing urbanization and around-the-clock societal demands, chronic jetlag-induced circadian misalignment is now pervasive, affecting an estimated 20% of the industrial workforce and 10–15% of the general adult population on a regular basis31,32. Importantly, the kidney possesses an intrinsic circadian clock, renal-specific and systemic clock-gene knockout mice show exacerbation of obesity and renal injury33–36. And various forms of external circadian disruption, such as light-dark inversion, constant light, gradually shifted light-dark cycles, worsen renal fibrosis and inflammation in models such as 5/6 nephrectomy, UUO, high-fat diet, and hypertension14,37–40. However, the specific impact of chronic jetlag on ORKD remains largely unknown. In the present study, we employed a chronic jetlag model to examine effects of circadian misalignment interacts with HFHFD-induced metabolic stress. Our findings demonstrated that chronic jetlag significantly aggravated HFHFD-induced renal injury including increased albuminuria, glomerular hypertrophy and sclerosis, tubular degeneration, and podocyte effacement. These findings indicate that chronic jetlag serves as an additional pathophysiological stressor that amplifies the deleterious effects of metabolic overload. Thus, chronic jetlag emerges as a novel and clinically meaningful risk factor capable of accelerating the development and progression of ORKD.

While glomerular lesions are the traditional hallmark of obesity-related glomerulopathy (ORG)41–43, increasing attention has been given to obesity-induced tubular injury, such as vacuolar degeneration, brush border loss, and Bowman’s capsule dilation44. These changes have prompted a broader term obesity-related kidney disease to encompass both glomerular and tubular injury, despite the absence of definitive pathological or molecular markers for ORKD45. In the current study, we observed multiple pathological features in HFHFD-fed mice, including glomerular hypertrophy, FSGS (predominantly perihilar type), foot process effacement, podocyte depletion, and vacuolar degeneration of tubular epithelial cells. Furthermore, TEM revealed focal glomerular basement membrane thickening in HFHFD-fed mice, resemble with early diabetic nephropathy lesions46. However, mesangial expansion or matrix accumulation, hallmark features of diabetic nephropathy47, were not prominent. These observations suggest that HFHFD-induced obesity triggers both glomerular and tubular injury in a pattern distinct from classical diabetic nephropathy, further supporting the concept of ORKD as a unique clinical and pathological entity.

Compared with the ND-NC mice, ND-JL mice exhibited significant body weight gain and impaired glucose tolerance, indicating the deleterious metabolic effects of chronic jetlag. However, no overt renal injuries were observed in ND-JL mice, which may be explained by the relatively short duration of the experiment. In contrast, in HFHFD-fed mice, chronic jetlag exacerbated HFHFD-induced renal injuries. This finding is consistent with our previous observation that constant light exposure aggravates renal injuries in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed rats37. Together, these results imply that circadian disruption, induced by chronic jetlag or constant light exposure, synergizes with an HFHFD or HFD to aggravate the progression of ORKD.

Systemic metabolic abnormalities such as insulin resistance and obesity are well-established contributors to renal damage5,48,49. In parallel, accumulating evidence indicates that local disruption of renal clock function can directly impair podocyte integrity, tubular sodium handling, hormonal regulation, and inflammatory pathways50–52. In the present study, chronic jet lag led to renal circadian disruption, also exacerbated insulin resistance and body weight gain in HFHFD-fed mice. The current design does not distinguish between mechanisms of renal injury arising from disruption of the renal circadian clock or those arising from systemic metabolic deterioration. Instead, the aggravated renal injury observed in HFHFD-JL mice co-occurred with circadian misalignment and metabolic dysfunction. Further mechanistic studies are needed to clarify the direct causal contribution of these factors.

Corticosterone expression follows a robust circadian rhythm in mice, with low levels during the early light phase and peak levels around dark onset53. Previous study has shown that corticosterone rhythmicity can remain relatively stable under altered photic conditions, with changes being more pronounced at peak than at nadir levels54. In our study, serum corticosterone was measured between 8:00 and 10:00 a.m. (ZT2–ZT4), corresponding to the nadir phase. Therefore, the absence of significant differences among groups may be attributable to the reduced detectability of alterations during this phase. Future studies with time-series sampling are needed to fully characterize the effect of chronic jetlag on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.

The NLRP3 inflammasome has emerged as a critical mediator in the pathogenesis of ORKD55–59 Specifically, NLRP3 activation drives glomerular disease through Gasdermin D cleavage-evoked pyroptosis and caspase-3-mediated apoptosis, directly contributing to glomerular thickening60. Oxidized LDL has been shown to activate NLRP3 in mesangial cells, promoting mesangial expansion61, while intracellular lipid droplets in podocytes can induce NLRP3 activation, resulting in disruption of cytoskeleton structure62. In podocytes, NLRP3 inflammasome activation impairs autophagy and promotes the accumulation of phospholipids in dysfunctional lysosomes, leading to tubular injury63,64. In tubular epithelial cells, activation of NLRP3 exacerbated tubular oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage and malabsorption during overnutrition65. Moreover, NLRP3 downstream cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 not only promote immune cell recruitment but also drive epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition24,66,67. Our study demonstrated increased renal expression of NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β at both mRNA and protein levels in HFHFD-fed mice, with further upregulation in HFHFD-JL mice. Correlation analyses revealed positive associations between tubular injury scores and Nlrp3, Caspase-1, and IL-1β expression. Additionally, glomerular hypertrophy and renal fibrosis severity correlated positively with Nlrp3 and IL-1β levels. These findings reinforce the central role of NLRP3 inflammasome activation in mediating kidney injury in ORKD, particularly under conditions of circadian disruption.

The Nlrp3 gene has been reported to exhibit circadian oscillations in multiple tissues, including the liver, colon, and heart, indicating that its activity may be under circadian control68. In the present study, we observed robust diurnal fluctuations of Nlrp3 mRNA expression in the kidney. Interestingly, the rhythmic expression of Nlrp3 oscillated in antiphase to the core clock gene Rev-erbα, implicating that Nlrp3 might be a transcriptional target of Rev-erbα. Mechanistically, Rev-erbα functions as a transcriptional repressor that mediates the rhythmic expression by binding to the Rev-erbα response element (RRE)69. Indeed, this molecular transcription regulatory mechanism is conserved across pathologies. Wang et al. demonstrated that Rev-erbα binds to an RRE site within the promoter of Nlrp3 and suppresses its transcription in models of colitis and myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury70. Similarly, Reitz et al. and Popov et al. confirmed that Rev-erbα activation attenuates inflammatory responses by suppressing NLRP3 in cardiac and macrophage models, respectively71,72. However, the regulatory landscape appears complex, as highlighted by Hong et al., who found that the inhibitory effect of Rev-erbα on NLRP3 requires the presence of Bmal173. This intricate interplay between core clock components provides a plausible mechanistic foundation for our findings, wherein Rev-erbα might rhythmically repress Nlrp3 transcription, linking circadian regulation to renal inflammatory responses in ORKD.

The therapeutic potential of inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome is increasingly recognized across a spectrum of diseases, including CKD. A range of pharmacological inhibitors have been developed, among which the small-molecule compound MCC950 has shown particular promise in preclinical models74–76. MCC950 effectively reduced podocyte damage in obesity-related glomerulopathy and mitigated renal injury in sepsis77,78. In our study, we found that chronic jetlag aggravated ORKD through activation of renal NLRP3, underscoring the inflammasome as a key mediator in this pathology. An intriguing alternative to direct NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition is the modulation of circadian pathways. Melatonin, a pineal hormone central to circadian regulation, exerts reno-protective and anti-inflammatory effects, including attenuation of NLRP3 activation in models of sepsis and aging79–81. Mechanistically, melatonin modulates the molecular clock component Rev-erbα82, which in turn regulates Nlrp3 transcription. Therefore, by simultaneously correcting circadian misalignment and suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome, melatonin and other chronobiotic agents represent promising therapeutic strategies for ORKD, particularly in the context of lifestyle-induced circadian disruption.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations of this study. Firstly, the findings are observational and demonstrate a strong association between renal injury and activation of the NLRP3/caspase-1/IL-1β pathway, but do not establish direct causality, which will require further studies using genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of NLRP3. Secondly, analyses were performed on whole-kidney tissue, potentially obscuring cell type–specific contributions. Thirdly, only male mice were studied, and sex-specific effects remain to be determined. Despite these limitations, our findings identify chronic jetlag as a clinically relevant modifier of metabolic kidney injury and highlight NLRP3 inflammasome activation as a key correlate of circadian–metabolic interaction.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that chronic jetlag aggravated glomerulomegaly and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, tubular degeneration, and podocyte injury in HFHFD-induced obese mice, which is associated with the activation of NLRP3/Caspase-1/IL-1β pathway. Chronic jetlag, resulting from repeated misalignment between the endogenous circadian clock and environmental light-dark cycles, has become increasingly prevalent in modern society. These findings highlighted the potential of circadian disruption as a novel risk factor that accelerates renal injury under conditions of metabolic stress. Elucidating the mechanisms linking circadian disruption to renal injury could inform novel preventative and therapeutic strategies, particularly for shift work populations exposed to long-term circadian disturbance.

Methods

Experimental animals

The animal experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Use and Care Committee of Central South University (NO. 2020sydw0989). Five-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Slac-Jingda Laboratory Animal Corporation (Hunan, China). Followed by one week’s acclimatization, all mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 12 per group) as follows: (1) ND-NC group: mice were fed on a normal diet (ND, fat 12%, protein 22%, carbohydrate 66%, 3.48 kcal/g) and exposed to a normal lighting condition (NC), (2) ND-JL group: mice were fed on a ND and subjected to a chronic jetlag (JL), (3) HFHFD-NC group: mice were fed on a high-fat diet (60% fat, 20% carbohydrate, 20% protein; 5.24 kcal/g; Research Diets, USA) with 10% g/v high-fructose in drinking water (HFHFD) and exposed to a normal lighting condition, and (4) HFHFD-JL group: mice were fed on an HFHFD and subjected to JL. Mice were housed four per cage and maintained under a controlled environment (22 ± 2 ℃, 40%−50% humidity), with ad libitum access to water and food. Under the normal lighting condition, mice were kept on a standard light/dark cycle (lights on from 6am to 6pm every day). The transition time from dark to light period (6am) was designated as zeitgeber time 0 (ZT0), and the transition time from light to dark period (6pm) was designated as ZT12. Chronic jetlag simulation was induced by dynamically changing the timing of light/dark cycle as previously described83,84. Briefly, mice were maintained under normal lighting condition on each Monday to Wednesday and subjected to an 8-hour phase advance (time for lights off changed from 10am to 10pm) on each Thursday to Sunday (Figure S1). Experiments were performed when the jet lagged mice were in the same light/dark cycle as control mice, and zeitgeber times were synchronized (i.e., ZT0 of jet lag mice corresponded to ZT0 of control mice). Food intake and body weight were recorded weekly during the experiment.

Measurement of urinary albumin

Mice were individually housed in metabolic cages to collect 24 h urine for measurement of urinary albumin at the 6th and 15th week of the experiment. The urinary albumin level was determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Cusabio, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT)

IPGTT was performed at the beginning of the 16th week. Mice were fasted for 8 h and then intraperitoneally injected with 50% glucose (2.0 g/kg body weight). Blood samples were collected from the tail vein at the following time points: T = 0 (before glucose injection) and 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after glucose injection to evaluate glucose concentrations using a glucometer (ACCU-CHEK, Roche Diagnostics, Germany).

Sample collection

After 16 weeks of intervention, mice were fasted overnight to minimize metabolic variability and sacrificed at ZT2, ZT8, ZT14, ZT20 on two consecutive days. To synchronize the circadian phase across all groups, all mice were exposed to a normal light/dark cycle for 24 h before sacrifice. After the mice were anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane (5% induction, 2% maintenance), blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital vein and centrifuged to isolate serum for biochemical analyses. Following blood collection, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation. The weight of the mice at the time of euthanasia ranged from 25.3 g to 50.8 g. The subcutaneous fat, visceral fat (perirenal and epididymal fat) and kidney tissues were immediately isolated and weighed. One portion of the kidneys was preserved for histological and electron microscopy analysis, and the other portion was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ℃ for the following experiments.

Biochemical analyses

Biochemical parameters including FBG, TG, TC, HDL, LDL, Cr, UA, BUN were determined by Beckman Coulter AU5800 automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA).

Measurement of serum insulin and corticosterone

Fasting serum insulin (INS) concentration was determined by ELISA (Cusabio, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was determined using the equation: HOMA-IR = (INS × FBG)/22.585. Serum corticosterone concentration was measured between 8 am (ZT2) and 10 am (ZT4) after overnight fasting by ELISA (Cusabio, Wuhan, China).

Renal histological analysis

Kidneys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections (5 μm) were deparaffinized and stained using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) or a Masson’s Trichrome staining kit. Thereafter, all slides were examined under light microscope. Mean values for glomerular cross-sectional area were estimated by computer-assisted image analysis. Glomerular volume = β/k×(area)3/2 with β = 1.38 and k = 1.186. HE-stained slides were evaluated for tubular injury. Five sections were selected for each group, and 10 randomly selected fields of view were examined from each section. The histological assessment of tubular injury included tubular atrophy and dilatation, loss of the brush border and vacuolar degeneration of proximal tubular epithelium 87. The scoring criteria were as follows: 0, no damage; 1, < 25% damage; 2, 25%–49% damage; 3, 50%–74% damage; and 4, ≥ 75% damage. Glomerular sclerotic lesions were assessed on Masson-stained sections using a semi-quantitative scoring system. For each mouse, 30 glomeruli were randomly selected from the entire renal cortex. Each glomerulus was scored in a blinded fashion according to the percentage of glomerular involvement: 0, no lesion; 1, < 25% sclerotic; 2, 25%–49% sclerotic; 3, 50%–74% sclerotic; and 4, 75%–100% sclerotic88. The glomerular sclerosis index for each mouse was calculated as the average score of the 30 glomeruli. The Masson-stained positive area was quantified at ×200 magnification in 20 random nonoverlapping fields using ImageJ software version 1.53 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) (https://imagej.net/ij/). Two professional pathologists, blinded to the experimental protocol and experimental group assignments, performed these evaluations.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The renal cortex was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h and dehydrated with ethanol gradients. The samples were then infiltrated with acetone-epoxy resin and embedded in epoxy resin for ultrathin sectioning. Finally, ultrathin Sects. (40–50 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate at room temperature and then examined under a transmission electron microscope (HT7700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

Paraffin-embedded sections (5 μm) were deparaffinized according to standard procedures. For antigen retrieval, sections were immersed in 0.01 M sodium citrate solution (pH 6.0) and microwaved for 20 min. Subsequently, sections were incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 15 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Non-specific antibody binding was blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. Then sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit primary antibodies against Nephrin (1:200, GeneTex, USA), Podocin/NPHS2 (1:200, Proteintech, China), NGAL (1:200, abmart, China) and NLRP3 (1:100, Novus Biologicals, USA). After washing with PBST, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Zymed Laboratories, San Francisco, CA) at 37 °C for 30 min. Histochemical reactions were visualized using 3,3-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Thereafter, the stained sections were examined under a light microscope, and protein expression levels were analyzed using ImageJ software version 1.53 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) (https://imagej.net/ij/).

For the detection of NLRP3 expression, frozen tissue blocks were cut into 5-µm sections. After washing with PBST, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton-X 100 (Solarbio, China) for 20 min, blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin, and incubated overnight with NLRP3 antibody (1:50, Novus Biologicals, USA). Subsequently, the tissues were incubated with Cy3–conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) at room temperature, then incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime, China) for 10 min. Finally, the stained samples were imaged by fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

Western blotting

Total proteins from renal cortex were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dartford, UK). Protein concentrations were then determined using a Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dartford, UK). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 10–12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Subsequently, the membranes were blocked in TBST containing 5% skim milk for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: NLRP3 (1:1000, Novus Biologicals, USA), Caspase 1/P20 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), IL-1β (1:500, R&D Systems, USA), BMAL1 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), NR1D1(1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and β-Tubulin (1:5000, Abmart, China). The membranes were washed three times with TBST and incubated with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beverly, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Image acquisition was performed using the chemiluminescent Amersham imager 600 and band densitometry was analyzed using ImageJ software version 1.53 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) (https://imagej.net/ij/).

qRT-PCR

Total RNA of the kidney tissue was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies Co., MA, USA) followed by reverse transcription into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Gene expression was quantified by qRT-PCR using Ultra SYBR Mixture (Low ROX, CWBIO, Beijing, China). The reaction was run on a QuantStudio 6 Flex real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) and analyzed by QuantStudio Software version 1.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (https://www.thermofisher.com/). Data were normalized to GAPDH and quantified by 2−∆∆CT method. Primers purchased from Tsingke Company are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Statistical analyses

All data are presented as mean ± SEM. GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) (https://www.graphpad.com/scientific-software/prism/) were used for statistical analyses. Differences in weekly changes of body weight, water intake, and caloric intake among groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Differences of the final outcome measures among groups were evaluated by two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Cosinor analysis was performed using ClockLab software version 6 (Actimetrics, Wilmette, IL, USA) (http://actimetrics.com/products/clocklab/) to quantify rhythm parameters, including amplitude (reflecting rhythmic variation magnitude) and mesor (the average around which the rhythm oscillates)89. Oscillatory rhythmicity was considered statistically significant if the model fit for the 24-hour cosine curve yielded a p value < 0.05. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
