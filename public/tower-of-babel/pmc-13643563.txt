Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is becoming an increasingly popular choice for aortic valve replacement in patients with severe aortic stenosis. Redo transcatheter aortic valve in transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) poses unique challenges involving unique design and compatibility issues. Additionally, these patients require individualized planning, including staged preparation of the constrained landing zone, precise frame-guided deployment, and multimodality confirmation. We describe a successful and challenging case of redo TAVR in a patient who had 4 prosthetic valves before her procedure.

Case Summary

History of presentation

An 82-year-old woman presented with progressive exertional dyspnea, fatigue, and dependent lower-extremity edema without chest pain or palpitations. On physical examination, she was afebrile, had stage 2 hypertension (143/83 mm Hg), and had a normal heart rate (87 beats/min) with no respiratory distress (respiratory rate 17/min) on room air. She also had a prior sternotomy scar, a grade 3/6 ejection systolic murmur, and 2+ pitting edema.

Past medical history

The patient's medical history included a coronary artery bypass graft (left internal mammary artery to left anterior descending coronary artery and saphenous vein graft to right posterior descending coronary artery), a 29-mm Mosaic surgical mitral valve replacement, and a 21-mm Magna 3,000 surgical aortic valve replacement in 2006. Degeneration of the surgical aortic prosthesis was treated with a 23-mm SAPIEN 3 (Edwards Lifesciences) balloon-expandable transcatheter heart valve in November 2019, with adjunctive valve fracture of the surgical bioprosthesis. Failure of the mitral bioprosthesis was treated with a 26-mm SAPIEN 3 Ultra transcatheter mitral valve-in-valve in February 2022. Additional comorbidities include benign essential hypertension, type 2 diabetes, mixed atherogenic dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease stage IIIb, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and prior bladder cancer with cystectomy.

Differential diagnosis

Differential diagnosis included exacerbation of heart failure secondary to valvular dysfunction (prosthetic stenosis or regurgitation or patient-prosthesis mismatch) vs cardiorenal syndrome.

Investigations

Laboratory investigation revealed a serum creatinine of 1.37 mg/dL (baseline between 1.2 and 1.4 mg/dL) with increased pro–B-type natriuretic peptide at 1,504 pg/mL. Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm with a pre-existing left-bundle branch block. Transthoracic echocardiography showed left ventricular ejection fraction 57%, severe recurrent prosthetic aortic stenosis (peak velocity 3.8 m/s; mean gradient 49 mm Hg), mild bioprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a well-seated transcatheter mitral prosthesis with a mean gradient 8 mm Hg, severe tricuspid regurgitation, and estimated right ventricular systolic pressure 42 mm Hg. Coronary and bypass graft angiography as part of preprocedure work-up demonstrated patent left internal mammary and saphenous vein grafts and patent native left circumflex coronary artery.

The structural heart and cardiac surgery teams judged excessive risks with repeat surgery due to advanced age, prior sternotomy with multivalve surgery and bypass grafting, chronic renal and pulmonary disease, and the presence of both aortic and mitral transcatheter prostheses. A transfemoral redo transcatheter strategy was selected using a 23-mm supra-annular self-expanding transcatheter heart valve (Evolut FX+; Medtronic Inc) to maximize the effective orifice area within the constrained 23-mm balloon-expandable frame in the 21-mm surgical ring. Preprocedural computed tomography facilitated evaluation of frame expansion, coronary ostia height and relation to prior frames, sinotubular junction dimensions, and the anticipated neoskirt (Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5, Video 1).

The procedural objective was to relieve symptomatic obstruction whilst avoiding a third sternotomy and maintaining coronary perfusion in the setting of a small surgical valve, an existing balloon-expandable frame, whilst still preserving the integrity of the transcatheter mitral prosthesis.

Procedural Steps

Access and hemodynamics setup

The right common femoral artery was accessed via micropuncture. The arteriotomy was preclosed using 2 Perclose ProGlide (Abbott Laboratories) suture-mediated closure devices and subsequently upsized to accommodate a 14-F delivery sheath. Secondary arterial access (right radial): A 6-F sheath was placed in the right radial artery for hemodynamic monitoring and secondary access. The right femoral vein was accessed via micropuncture and sequentially upsized to a 7-F venous sheath. A balloon-tipped temporary pacemaker wire was advanced under fluoroscopy and positioned securely within the right ventricular apex.

Valve crossing and valvuloplasty

A 6-F JR-4 diagnostic catheter was advanced to the aortic root. A 0.035″ straight-tipped guidewire was utilized to safely cross the degenerated prosthetic valve complex using steep right anterior oblique 35° and caudal 35° projection, allowing us to visualize segments of the aortic prosthesis not interacting with the mitral prosthesis, thereby “driving past” the mitral prosthesis to preserve its integrity (Videos 2 and 3). Once across, the JR-4 catheter was exchanged over the wire for a 6-F pigtail catheter. The straight wire was then removed and exchanged for a dedicated SavvyWire (Haemonetics Corporation) pacing guidewire. Given the complexity of the TAV-in-TAV-in-surgical aortic valve (SAV) anatomy, a balloon aortic valvuloplasty was performed to facilitate device delivery. This was executed sequentially using a 10-mm Mustang balloon (to visualize under fluoroscopy nondisruption of the mitral prosthesis with a 10-mm balloon before committal to a larger balloon size) and a 20-mm Z-MED (NuMed Inc) balloon under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats/min (Videos 4 and 5).

Valve delivery and deployment

The 23-mm self-expanding transcatheter heart valve was prepped and loaded, and the delivery system was advanced using the 14-F in-line sheath to the ascending aorta. Precise anatomical positioning was achieved by utilizing the fluoroscopic markers of the pre-existing valve frames (the 23-mm balloon-expandable valve seated within the 21-mm surgical bioprosthesis). Alignment was confirmed in both coplanar and steep right anterior oblique 35° and caudal 35° projections. Once optimal positioning was verified, the 23-mm self-expanding valve was deployed under rapid ventricular pacing at 150 beats/min (Video 6).

Postdeployment assessment

Following deployment, intraoperative TTE confirmed that the new bioprosthesis was well-seated and functioning optimally. There was no evidence of significant paravalvular aortic regurgitation. The final invasive hemodynamics demonstrated an excellent result, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 4 mm Hg (Figure 6, Video 7).

Outcome and follow-up

The patient was discharged a day after her procedure with no peri- and postprocedural complications. Her electrocardiogram remained at baseline, with sinus rhythm and a left-bundle branch block; no pacemaker was required. She ambulated without chest pain, dyspnea, or palpitations. Predischarge echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 57%, a well-seated aortic prosthesis, mean gradient of 8 mm Hg, and no regurgitation. Apixaban was resumed at discharge.

Potential Pitfalls

Published valve-in-valve-in-valve experience remains limited, although carefully selected cases and contemporary redo-TAVR registry data support technical feasibility.1,2 Each additional frame reduces available inflow area and changes leaflet and commissural geometry. In a small surgical prosthesis, these constraints increase the risks of underexpansion, high residual gradient, patient-prosthesis mismatch, leaflet dysfunction, and impaired future coronary access.3 A supra-annular self-expanding design was therefore selected to place the functional leaflets above the most constrained portion of the pre-existing valves. Moreover, a TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV with a balloon-expandable transcatheter heart valve with a prior mitral valve-in-valve in situ would not have been technically feasible without disrupting the integrity of the transcatheter mitral valve frame. The fall in mean gradient from 49 to 4 mm Hg invasively and 8 mm Hg on predischarge echocardiography supports this strategy in the present anatomy.

The dominant safety issue in redo TAVR is not simply coronary ostial height. The displaced leaflets of the prior transcatheter valve rise to form a neoskirt, and interaction with the sinotubular junction can sequester the sinus even when the coronary ostia themselves are not directly covered.4,5 Multiplanar computed tomography (CT) assessment of valve-to-coronary and valve-to-sinotubular-junction distances, prior frame expansion, commissural orientation, and predicted neoskirt height is therefore essential. Postprocedure CT adds mechanistic value by documenting expansion and circularity of the new valve, leaflet motion, frame-to-frame interaction, coronary patency, and the realized neoskirt.

Sequential predilatation and use of the 2 prior prosthetic frames as fluoroscopic landmarks allowed controlled deployment in a short, rigid landing zone. The case also underscores what should not be generalized: TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV is an individualized lifetime-management solution, not a default alternative to surgery. Feasibility depends on the identity and true internal diameter of each prosthesis, the coronary and sinotubular anatomy, the mechanism of valve failure, access to coronary protection or leaflet modification strategies, and a realistic plan for future coronary access.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the feasibility of a third aortic prosthesis layer in a patient with recurrent stenosis following SAV replacement and valve-in-valve TAVR. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such case of TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV with prior mitral valve replacement and valve-in-valve transcatheter mitral valve replacement. In a carefully selected patient at prohibitive surgical risk, transfemoral implantation of a supra-annular self-expanding valve within a failed balloon-expandable TAVR housed in a small surgical bioprosthesis achieved excellent early hemodynamics without regurgitation or conduction deterioration. Success depended on CT-based lifetime-valve analysis, staged preparation of the constrained landing zone, precise frame-guided deployment, and multimodality confirmation.6, 7, 8 Longer follow-up is required to establish durability and coronary accessibility after triple-layer aortic valve therapy.
