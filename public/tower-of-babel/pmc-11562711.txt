Influence of Germicidal UV (222 nm) Lamps on Ozone, Ultrafine Particles, and Volatile Organic Compounds in Indoor Office Spaces
Authors: Sara Bjerre Sørensen, Frederik Rask Dalby, Søren Kristian Olsen, Kasper Kristensen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGermicidal ultraviolet lamps with a peak emission at 222 nm (GUV222) are gaining prominence as a safe and effective solution to reduce disease transmission in occupied indoor environments. While previous studies have reported O3 production from GUV222, less is known about their impact on other indoor constituents affecting indoor air quality, especially in real occupied environments. In this study, the effects of GUV222 on the levels of ozone (O3), ultrafine particles (UFPs), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were investigated across multiple offices with varying occupancies. O3 from the GUV222 operation was observed to increase linearly (∼300 μg h–1 m–1) with a UV light path length from 0 to 3 m beyond which it stabilized. When applied in offices, the O3 production models based on continuous measurements revealed O3 production rates of 1040 ± 87 μg h–1. The resulting increases in steady-state concentrations of 5–21 μg m–3 were highly dependent on the number of office occupants. UFP production occurred during both unoccupied and occupied conditions but predominantly in newly renovated offices. Time-resolved measurements with a proton-transfer-reaction time-of-flight mass spectrometer (PTR-TOF-MS) revealed clear alterations in office VOC concentrations. Unsurprisingly, O3 oxidation chemistry was observed, including monoterpene deprivation and 4-oxopentanal (4-OPA) production. But additionally, significant alterations from unidentified mechanisms occurred, causing increased levels of various PTR-TOF-MS signals including C2H5O2+ and C4H9+ hypothesized to arise from photoinduced formation or off-gassing during the GUV222 lamp operation.Keywords: Indoor air quality (IAQ), GUV lamps, far UV–C, photochemistry, office environments

Introduction

The application of germicidal ultraviolet (GUV) lamps has been studied in everyday indoor environments for almost a century.1 As GUV lamps emit light in the shortwave ultraviolet range (UV-C, 200–280 nm), they have the potential to minimize disease transmission through the inactivation of pathogens. Traditionally, filtered mercury lamps with a peak emission of 254 nm (hereafter termed GUV254 lamps) have been used. However, as these lamps can cause damage to mammalian eyes and skin upon direct exposure,2 their usage should be limited to applications inside ventilation ducts, air cleaning technologies, or as upper room installations. This is, however, not considered an issue for shorter-wavelength radiation because of the shorter penetration depth.2 For this reason, and in light of the recent COVID-19 pandemic, alternatives utilizing far UV-C lamps (200–230 nm) have recently gained ground. Specifically, filtered krypton-chloride excimer (KrCl) lamps with a peak emission of 222 nm (hereafter referred to as GUV222), which are also effective in terms of inactivating pathogens,3−6 are considered a promising alternative to the traditional GUV254 lamps in occupied environments. However, UV-C light at wavelengths below 242 nm can contribute to ozone (O3) formation through photodissociation of oxygen (O2).7 Peng et al. showed from model simulations that under poor ventilation conditions, GUV222 lamps could have a greater negative impact on indoor air quality (IAQ) than GUV254 lamps due to O3 production at 222 nm, which does not occur with GUV254 lamps.8 As opposed to previous studies,2,9,10 another study by Peng et al. reported significant O3 formation from GUV222 lamps during operation in both a chamber (736 μg h–1 in 21 m3 Teflon chamber) and an unoccupied office (459 μg h–1, 33 m3).11 The discrepancy, which is approximately an order of magnitude higher compared to previous studies, was hypothesized to originate from the UV light path lengths and surface-to-volume ratios, which is unlike those of real indoor environments for small box experiments. Likewise, Link et al. reported an O3 production rate of 1220 ± 20 μg h–1 in a 31.5 m3 stainless steel chamber.12 While O3 is a pollutant itself, known to cause various health impacts,13 it is also an oxidant. Thus, it can react with both gases and surfaces indoor, potentially forming harmful byproducts such as ultrafine particles (UFPs) or specific volatile organic compounds (VOCs).14,15 In extension hereof, Barber et al. showed that as a consequence of the O3 produced by GUV222 lamps, hydroxyl radicals (•OH) were also formed.16

Although a few studies have reported on the effects of the GUV222 application in real indoor environments, these are focused either on model simulations8 or limited to O3 production.11 Thus, extensive experimental studies assessing the impact on the IAQ are currently lacking. Likewise, the impact of occupancy has also been unexplored. In this study, a GUV222 lamp is applied in real indoor environments during both unoccupied and occupied conditions to uncover its effect on IAQ in terms of O3-, UFP-, and VOC formation and the correlation between UV light path length and O3 production rate.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Design and Measurements

In this study, a filtered GUV222 lamp (Care222, Ushio Inc.) was applied in four similar offices (offices A–D, ∼41 m3, 2.5 × 5.7 × 2.9 m3), which were renovated during spring 2023. A total of 23 experiments were conducted, all using the same GUV222 lamp. A photograph of one of the offices can be seen in Figure S1. The offices were naturally ventilated (HVAC system deactivated) with air change rates (ACRs) of ∼0.2 ± 0.1 h–1. ACRs were calculated by linearization of the CO2 decay rates after injection of excessive amounts of CO2 from a gas cylinder followed by measurements of the CO2 decay rates with sensor nodes (CozIR-Blink, Gas Sensing Solutions in RoomAlyzers and SCD-30, Sensirion in AirNodes) (see the Supporting Information for further elaboration). For all experiments, indoor O3 levels were continuously measured with an O3 monitor (model 202, 2B Technologies), while outdoor O3 concentrations were measured at a measurement station <1 km away (API M400 and 400A). Office temperature and relative humidity were monitored using sensor nodes (RoomAlyzers, IoT Fabrikken and AirNodes, AirLabs). Average office temperatures and relative humidities were 28 ± 3 °C and 38 ± 9% during summer experiments and 23 ± 1 °C and 66 ± 2% during fall experiments.

Out of the 23 experiments, 12 were conducted during the summer of 2024 to evaluate the influence of UV light path length on the production of O3 production. For these experiments, the GUV222 lamp was placed in the middle of the room (with the center of the lamp 1.25 m above the floor and 1.45 m from each side) facing a wall. For each experiment, the distance from the wall faced by the GUV222 lamp was incrementally increased from 0 to 5 m. The experiments were conducted in duplicates in office D, which was unfurnished. An overview of the experiments and the corresponding ACRs, temperatures, and relative humidities can be seen in Table S1.

In addition, 11 experiments were conducted during the summer and fall of 2023 to uncover the impact of GUV222 operation on IAQ under different occupancies. For these experiments, the GUV222 lamp was placed at the far end of the room 2.4 m above the floor pointing slightly downward, giving the light an unobstructed path of 4.4 m (see Figure S1). After each experiment, the offices were as a minimum allowed to stabilize overnight before the next experiment in that specific office. In the experiments with occupants, the occupants entered the office 30 min prior to turning on the GUV222 lamp and stayed 45 min after it was turned off. Particle size distribution (10 nm to 35 μm), number, and mass (particle density = 1.0 g cm–3) concentrations were measured by a mini wide range aerosol spectrometer (MiniWRAS, model 1.371 from Grimm Aerosol Technik, Germany). For selected experiments, the concentration of individual VOCs was continuously measured with a proton-transfer-reaction time-of-flight mass spectrometer (PTR-TOF-MS 4000, IONICON Analytik GmbH, Austria), which was calibrated with a seven-component mixture (methanol, acetone, butanone, phenol, p-cresol, indole, and phenyl-cyclohexanone). Concentrations of all other compounds were calculated using a default proton-transfer reaction rate constant (kPTR) of 2 × 10–9 cm3 s–1, giving an accuracy of around ±50%. Further information on PTR-TOF-MS settings, ion transmission efficiency, and calibration is described by Sørensen et al.17 An overview of the experiments can be seen in Table S2.

Results and Discussion

Effect of UV Light Path Length on Ozone Production Rate

When considering O3 formation from GUV222 lamps, the volume of air illuminated (and hence the UV light path length) is expected to be crucial, as O3 produced by GUV222 lamps depends on photodissociation of O2 in the air. Figure 1 shows the correlation between the loss-corrected O3 production rate from the GUV222 lamp and the UV light path length. O3 production is observed to increase linearly (∼300 μg h–1 m–1) with the UV light path length from 0 to 3 m, with O3 production rates of 248 ± 37, 560 ± 272, and 915 ± 157 μg h–1 at 1, 2, and 3 m, respectively. While the volume of a cone scales with a potency of 3 with distance, irradiance (and fluence rate) perpendicular to a light source scales with a potency of −2 under conditions where nothing is lost to e.g., absorption. Thus, under the assumption that the GUV222 lamp is a point source illuminating a cone-shaped volume of air, a linear increase of O3 with UV light path length would be expected, as long as the attenuation of the UV light caused by the walls, floor, and ceiling is negligible and the light intensity provided by the lamp is sufficient to photolyze O2. After 3 meters, however, the O3 production stabilizes, giving O3 production rates of 965 ± 59 and 994 ± 35 μg h–1 at 4 and 5 m, respectively. This is also observed when looking at the O3 production rate as a function of the volume of air illuminated (see Figure S2), suggesting that at a UV light path length above ∼3 m (perpendicular to the source), the intensity of the GUV222 light is insufficient for O3 production. The derived O3 production rates from the path length experiments are in good agreement with the O3 production rates found in the 11 experiments conducted in offices A–C in the current study and the O3 production rate reported by Peng et al. at a UV light path length of 4.5 m (∼890 μg h–1 when corrected for the reduced ambient pressure in Boulder where the experiments were conducted).11 However, discrepancies were observed between the current study and studies using a UV light path length of ∼3 m or less (Figure 1).11,12,16 The discrepancies might be caused by differences in the volume of air illuminated, the output of the GUV222 lamps (although of the same make), and the reflectivity of the wall materials.18 The O3 production found at a UV path length of 0 m (17 ± 13 μg h–1) confirms that no significant amount of O3 is produced from electrical discharge, as opposed to what has been observed in previous studies.10,11

Effect of GUV222 Operation on Indoor Ozone Levels

Continuous measurements revealed average O3 production rates of 1040 ± 87 μg h–1 during the GUV222 operation in the offices, resulting in elevated O3 concentrations. The production rate agrees well with the expected values from the GUV222 path length experiments (Figure 1). Figure 2 illustrates O3 room concentrations in office B before, during, and after GUV222 operation under various occupancies (see Figure S3 for all measurements). As a result of the O3 production, steady-state O3 concentrations between ∼15 and 23 μg m–3 (8–12 ppb) were observed during the GUV222 operation in the unoccupied offices, corresponding to increases of ∼13–21 μg m–3 (7–11 ppb). As the outdoor concentrations of O3 were stable throughout the day (see Table S2) and penetration of outdoor O3 was minimal (average penetration factor of 0.06 ± 0.03), the increases observed are expected to be caused solely by the GUV222 lamp. The resulting O3 concentrations were considerably lower than outdoor O3 concentrations at the corresponding days (13–34 ppb; see Table S2) but higher than average indoor O3 concentrations of 4–6 ppb as reported in a literature review by Nazaroff and Weschler.19 The results are in agreement with those from previous studies investigating real indoor environments. Measurements by Peng et al. suggested GUV222-induced steady-state O3 concentrations of ∼8–14 ppb (∼16–28 μg m–3) under low ventilation conditions (ACR = 0.62–0.96 h–1) in an ∼33 m3 unoccupied office,11 while model simulations by Peng et al. suggested ∼7.5 ppb (∼15 μg m–3) under low ventilation conditions (ACR = 0.3 h–1) in a 300 m3 room (GUV222 intensity normalized to the required disinfection rate).8 However, the GUV222-induced steady-state O3 concentrations of the current study are significantly lower than steady-state concentrations reported by previous chamber experiments (e.g., >200 ppb by Barber et al.16). These discrepancies are attributed to the small volume of the test chambers, the higher O3 production rates caused by the increased reflectivity of the chamber materials compared to building materials, and the lower O3 decay rates caused by both limited surface deposition and gas-phase reactions. In the current study, O3 decay rates were found to range from 1.13 to 1.54 h–1 (1.3 ± 0.2 h–1) during the GUV222 operation in the three unoccupied offices. In comparison, Peng et al. reported an average O3 deposition rate (O3 decay rate minus ACR) of 0.78 h–1 in an unoccupied office (∼33 m3),11 similar to the results of the current study, but significantly higher than the O3 decay rate reported by Link et al., which was 0.179 ± 0.003 h–1 in a 31.5 m3 stainless steel chamber.12 However, the current study found that the rate of decay of the O3 increased with the number of occupants. While O3 decay rates of 1.8 ± 0.3 h–1 were observed in the presence of one person, this was 3.0 ± 1.5 h–1 in the presence of two persons, thus rendering occupants as an important O3 sink in real indoor environments. The O3 decay rates compare well with those observed by Liu et al. (1.3 h–1) during the investigation of a household with two adult inhabitants.14 The increased O3 decay rates during occupancy are likely caused by reactions between O3 and human bioeffluents, e.g., squalene in human skin oil, as reported in a previous study.20 Consequently, the resulting steady-state concentrations of O3 were lower during occupancy as evident from Figure 2. Effect of GUV222 Operation on Ultrafine Particle Levels

Figure 3 illustrates the room concentrations of UFPs during GUV222 operation. UFP production was observed during both unoccupied and occupied conditions but mainly in the experiments conducted during the summer of 2023 when the offices were newly renovated. This inconsistency in UFP production is hypothesized to originate from variability in VOC room concentrations. Previous studies have hypothesized secondary organic aerosol (SOA) formation as a side effect of GUV222 operation,8,16 as SOA is known to form from O3 oxidation of indoor VOCs, e.g., monoterpenes.21 In the current study, variability in VOC room concentrations might be caused by higher levels of precursors penetrating indoors during summer experiments (#1, #5, #9, and #11) compared to fall (#2–4, #6–8, #10). However, the low ACRs suggest that this would have only a minor influence. Instead, it is speculated that a considerable decline in the material off-gassing experienced during summer shortly after the renovation and until fall might be able to cause the observed inconsistency in UFP production observed. UFP concentrations increased with up to 5.45 × 103 cm–3 h–1 when neglecting the first 1–1.5 h where particle diameters were likely below the 10 nm size cutoff of the MiniWRAS. A pilot experiment, which induced elevated levels of O3 from the operation of the GUV222 lamp, was conducted in office A prior to the experiments included in this paper, likely explaining the lower increase in the UFP concentration in experiment #1 compared to those in experiments #5 and #9. In contrast to model simulations by Peng et al., which predicted an increase of ∼8 μg m–3 (8–16 μg m–3) in indoor SOA concentrations during the operation of a GUV222 lamp under low ventilation conditions (ACR = 0.3 h–1), no increase in particle mass was observed during GUV222 operation, as the particles resided in the UFP size range (see Figure S4). In addition, increasing the number of occupants did not show any clear effect on the number of UFPs formed, as would otherwise have been expected due to the increased amount of precursors from the occupants (e.g., skin lipids).22

Effect of GUV222 Operation on Volatile Organic Compound Levels

VOC measurements by PTR-TOF-MS showed clear indications of the O3 oxidation chemistry. While monoterpene concentrations were increasing in GUV222 off-periods, these were found to either decrease or stabilize when the GUV222 lamp was on, thus suggesting monoterpene consumption during GUV222 operation (Figure S5). Likewise, levels of C5H9O2+ (assumed to be the protonated molecular ion of 4-oxopentanal, hereafter referred to as 4-OPA), which is a dominant secondary oxidation product from the reaction between skin lipids (e.g., squalene) and O3,23 were found to increase during GUV222 operation. This is illustrated in Figure 4, which shows the room concentration of the selected VOCs. The increase of 4-OPA during the GUV222 on-period was significantly larger (p < 0.05) than during background measurements (t < 0) not only in the presence of both one and two persons but also in two out of the three unoccupied offices. This is consistent with results by Liu et al., which showed that the formation of 4-OPA continued several days after vacating the indoor environment due to left-over skin lipids.14 However, alterations in VOC concentrations that are unlikely to originate mainly from O3 oxidation were also observed. As evident from Figure 4, significant (p < 0.05) changes in the PTR-TOF-MS signal of, e.g., C2H5O2+ (assigned to acetic acid) were observed during GUV222 operation compared to background measurements during both unoccupied and occupied conditions. A previous study by Yang et al. reported the generation of acetic acid and propionic acid during radiation of an odorous VOC mixture with UV at 185 and 254 nm. The low-molecular-weight volatile fatty acids generated were speculated to be intermediates of the partial breakdown of heavier-molecular-weight fatty acids.24 In the current study, increases in the ion signals of other compounds are, however, also observed, e.g., C4H9+ (Figure 4). C4H9+ has previously been assigned as a butyl fragment and identified as the most abundant ion of butanol (C4H9OH) during PTR-MS analysis.25,26

Concentrations (ppb) of C5H9O2+ (4-oxopentanal, 4-OPA), C2H5O2+ (acetic acid), and C4H9+ during unoccupied (left) and occupied (right) conditions. The annotated slopes refer to measurements in office A when the GUV222 lamp was on (purple background).

Figure 5 shows a subset of additional experiments carried out in a 65 m3 room subsequent to the office experiments to further investigate the cause of the increasing signal of acetic acid and C4H9+ during GUV222 operation. The room was located in the same building as offices A–D but contained no furniture. It was renovated at approximately the same time, and similar to the offices, the HVAC system was deactivated. To compensate for the larger volume, an additional GUV222 lamp was added to the room. From these experiments, it is evident that no increase in acetic acid concentrations was observed in the experiments with the injection of excessive amounts of O3 produced with an O3 generator (model 1000, Jelight), thus eliminating O3 oxidation as a viable cause (Figure 5b, top). Similarly, O3 oxidation was also deemed unlikely as the main cause of the observed increase in the C4H9+ signal, as only a small increase (0.4 ppb h–1) was observed (Figure 5b, bottom) despite levels of pure O3 being 2–8 fold the steady-state concentrations observed in the offices, thus making it incomparable to the increase observed during GUV222 operation (3.6 ppb h–1; Figure 5a, bottom). In extension hereof, further experiments showed close to equal increases in the levels of acetic acid and C4H9+ both in the absence (Figure 5a) and presence (Figure 5c) of excessive amounts of an •OH scavenger (1 ppm cyclohexane) during GUV222 operation, thereby also ruling out formation due to •OH chemistry. Thus, neither the O3 oxidation chemistry nor •OH chemistry is likely to explain the increases in acetic acid and C4H9+ observed during GUV222 operation. Likewise, these are not likely to originate from the lamp casing either, as no increases were observed when the lamps were placed all the way up against the wall they were facing, thus blocking the light. Instead, the increase is hypothesized to originate from photoinduced formation or surface off-gassing causing enhanced surface emissions from the surfaces exposed to the UV light. Deal et al. suggested UVC-related byproduct formation from photolysis of, e.g., lactic acid resulting in the formation of radicals, CO2, carbon monoxide, acetic acid, and acetaldehyde.27 In a recent study investigating the effect of UV-C (254 nm) on IAQ, Graeffe et al., however, speculated that the energy from the radiation might be absorbed by molecules on the surfaces causing an increase in their thermal energy and hence leading to increased evaporation.28 Large surfaces exposed to direct GUV222 light in the current study include, but are not limited to, walls, flooring, and ceilings (painted plaster walls, vinyl flooring, and acoustic gypsum ceilings).

Overall, the total VOC (m/z 40–250) room signal was found to be affected by the GUV222 lamp operation during unoccupied conditions (see Figure S6). In office C, the GUV222 lamp operation was found to increase the total VOC room signal rate significantly (p < 0.05), with the signal rate changing from a decrease of 10 ppb h–1 prior to the GUV222 lamp operation to a net increase of 1.3 ppb h–1 during the GUV222 lamp operation. In offices A and B, however, the total VOC room signals increased steadily at about 4 ppb h–1 both prior to and during the GUV222 lamp operation when unoccupied. It is speculated that the increase prior to the GUV222 lamp operation is caused by closing the door to the hallway upon initiation of the experiments, thus making the room increasingly airtight. As a result, off-gassing from the room might increase the total VOC signal in the offices prior to the GUV222 lamp operation, thus concealing the effect of the GUV222 lamp. Supporting this, clear decreases in the total VOC signals were observed after turning off the GUV222 lamp in all of the unoccupied offices.

During occupied conditions, however, human emissions were found to dominate the VOC contribution to office air (see Figure S6). While the total VOC room signal during GUV222 operation was increasing at a rate of ∼4 ppb h–1 in office A when unoccupied, this was ∼15 and ∼22 ppb h–1 in the presence of one and two persons, respectively, thus highlighting the contribution from human emanations. Comparing the total VOC room signals prior to-, during-, and post GUV222 lamp operations did not indicate considerable changes caused by the lamp. Looking at the total VOC room signal, changes caused by the GUV222 lamp might therefore be concealed by those caused by human emissions.

Implications

The results showed that in addition to the volume of air illuminated, O3 production rates are highly dependent on the UV light path length. When conducting experiments with GUV lamps, it is therefore important to consider both attenuation of the light (e.g., by walls, floor, ceiling, and furniture) and the reflectivity of nearby surfaces. In extension hereof, the results emphasized the importance of O3 sinks and showed that occupancy is a key factor influencing O3 loss rates and the resulting room concentrations during GUV222 operation. In the current study, the O3 production from the GUV222 lamp increased the steady-state O3 levels measured in the offices, making them exceed the general average indoor O3 concentrations.19 In terms of direct exposure to O3, however, the levels remained below outdoor concentrations because of surface loss and reactions with office VOCs forming byproducts. For this reason, it is important to notice that the changes to the indoor air caused by O3 produced from a GUV22 lamp are not comparable to those induced by outdoor air with a similar amount of O3, as the latter will dilute the indoor air and change the precursor composition.

In terms of byproducts, the present study revealed that while significant production of particles (>10 nm) can occur during GUV222 operation, this finding is not universal. In fact, the majority of experiments in the current study did not identify >10 nm particle production as opposed to what has been hypothesized from model simulations in previous studies. Since VOCs play a key role in SOA formation and growth, VOC room composition is expected to be an essential determinant of UFP production. This is supported by the current study, as considerable UFP (>10 nm) production was only observed in a subset of the experiments conducted when the offices were newly renovated. It is therefore likely that the VOC room concentrations in the non-newly renovated offices were limiting in terms of UFP production and growth, once again highlighting the importance of tests in real indoor environments. However, the production of UFPs below the 10 nm size cutoff of the current study is not unlikely and should be researched further.

Alterations of VOC room concentrations were identified from PTR-TOF-MS measurements of the office air during the GUV222 operation. O3 oxidation chemistry was observed, e.g., depletion of monoterpenes and formation of secondary compounds such as 4-OPA. In addition, the study also revealed significant increases in various PTR-TOF-MS signals including C2H5O2+ and C4H9+. However, the conducted experiments revealed that these were unlikely to be caused by either the O3 or •OH chemistry, thus suggesting alterations caused by yet unexplored mechanisms. Based on the results, it is hypothesized that the UV-C light might cause photoinduced formation and/or surface off-gassing responsible for the increases in the concentrations of certain VOCs. This is in alignment with a hypothesis proposed in a previous study investigating the effect of UV-C (254 nm).28 Although the observed changes are on the order of less than one to a few parts per billion h–1, further investigations on the effect of VOCs are still highly encouraged to elucidate the impact of GUV222 on IAQ.

Conclusively, the results highlight the importance of tests in real indoor environments when assessing the effects of GUV lamps, as the O3 production rates depend on the volume of air illuminated and the UV light path length (and hence potentially also indirectly on the reflectivity of surrounding materials); the O3 decay rates and the resulting O3 levels depend on not only the sinks, e.g., the number of occupants and VOC room composition as discussed in this study, but also the surfaces and ventilation rates; UFP production depends on VOC room composition; and finally alterations in the VOC concentrations depend on the induced O3 levels, existing VOC room composition, and potentially on the surfaces exposed to the emitted UV light. The findings elucidated in the present study emphasize the imperative for additional research exploring both the impact and influence of VOC room composition during GUV operation.
