Parental awareness and practices regarding male circumcision timing, setting, and providers: a multicenter cross-sectional survey from Turkey (n = 637)
Authors: Mevlüt Keleş, Erdal Benli, Abdullah Çirakoğlu, İbrahim Yazici, Nurullah Kadim, Ahmet Anıl Acet, Hasan Durmuş, Mehmet Uğur Yilmaz, Aybegüm Kalyoncu Ayçenk, Numan Baydilli, Nizamettin Kiliç, Mesrur Selçuk Silay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate Turkish parents’ perceptions and attitudes regarding the appropriate age for circumcision, practice conditions, and procedural indications across different regions of Turkey.MethodsThis cross-sectional survey study was conducted between March 2023 and March 2025 across urology and pediatric surgery departments in seven distinct regions of Turkey. A total of 637 literate parents (aged ≥ 18) completed a voluntary 16-question online survey via a URL or QR code. Subgroup analysis was performed for parents whose sons had already undergone circumcision (n = 404). Data regarding demographics, circumcision preferences, and information sources were analyzed using descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests.ResultsThe majority of parents (95.1%) considered circumcision necessary, primarily citing religious (77.7%) and medical (63.8%) reasons. Overall, 76% of participants stated that circumcision should ideally occur outside the psychologically sensitive period (PSP; ages 2–6). Among the 404 parents with already-circumcised sons, 41.1% (n = 166) reported that the procedure was performed during the PSP (ages 2–6). Awareness of the non-PSP increased significantly with higher parental education levels (p < 0.001). Regarding practice, specialists were preferred (pediatric surgeons 54.4%; pediatric urologists 46.3%), and the operating room was identified as the safest setting by 91.7% of respondents. Local anesthesia was the most preferred method (51.6%).ConclusionDespite high awareness regarding specialist qualifications and clinical settings, a significant “knowledge-practice gap” exists concerning the timing of circumcision. Clinicians should proactively counsel parents regarding the psychological risks associated with the PSP, and public health strategies must move beyond general awareness to provide targeted guidance that ensures optimal timing in circumcision.Keywords: Circumcision, Male circumcision, Neonatal circumcision, Parental perception, Psychotrauma, Psychologically sensitive period (PSP), Parental awareness, Pediatric urology, Turkey

Background

Circumcision is one of the most common surgical procedures performed worldwide, predominantly for religious and cultural reasons [1]. While it is a fundamental religious practice in Muslim and Jewish communities, its justification in Western societies often emphasizes prophylactic and therapeutic medical benefits. The medical, psychological, and social implications of circumcision in male children warrant attention [2] .

Perceptions of circumcision vary globally, influencing timing and method. While modern medicine acknowledges several health benefits, the optimal age and technique remain subjects of ongoing debate. The American Urological Association (AUA) highlights that circumcision offers medical benefits, including the prevention of urinary tract infections (UTIs), a reduction in sexually transmitted diseases (STDs), and a decreased risk of penile cancers, with these effects varying by developmental stage [3]. Importantly, circumcision is not always essential; parental decisions are frequently based on cultural and religious factors [3]. Conversely, the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines state that circumcision is indicated only for specific medical conditions and complications, such as paraphimosis or pathological phimosis, balanitis xerotica obliterans, and recurrent UTIs [4]. The EAU further stresses that circumcision should be performed only by qualified physicians under sterile operating room conditions to minimize complications and emotional distress.

There is no consensus on the ideal age for circumcision regarding psychological and developmental aspects. Some argue that performing the procedure during the PSP (ages 2–6) may negatively impact psychosocial development, suggesting that it be avoided during this timeframe [5]. While some studies support neonatal circumcision for faster healing and fewer complications, others suggest that circumcision beyond five years of age may be psychologically more favorable [6].

Circumcision under anesthesia after age six is thought to minimize psychological trauma through anterograde amnesia—often attributed to the use of midazolam for premedication—which prevents the retention of memories related to perioperative pain and distress, thereby reducing anxiety and psychological sequelae [7]. Similarly, existing literature indicates that circumcision performed beyond five years of age better suits a child’s developmental and psychological status, as anesthesia mitigates conscious apprehension [8]. However, the potential risks associated with anesthesia and postoperative recovery must also be considered. A recent study reinforces the link between circumcision age and psychological impact, advocating its postponement until after the age of five due to potential developmental trauma from earlier intervention [9].

The psychological implications of circumcision during the PSP remain a subject of ongoing debate. While classical theories emphasize potential psychological risks during the psychologically sensitive period (PSP; ages 2–6), more recent evidence and systematic reviews suggest that circumcision is not associated with substantiated long-term psychosexual or psychological harm when performed under optimal surgical and anesthetic conditions [2]. Furthermore, studies focusing on the Turkish cohorts have also shown that adult sexual function and psychological well-being are not adversely affected by the timing of circumcision in childhood [10]. Consequently, the emphasis on timing in current clinical practice often serves as a precautionary approach to minimize immediate perioperative distress rather than a confirmed measure for preventing long-term sequelae.

In addition to clinical considerations, non-medical factors—like religious convictions, ancestral traditions, and community norms—substantially influence the perception and social acceptance of circumcision. Perception involves the translation of external stimuli into cognitive constructs and is shaped by the interplay between biological and psychological factors, which significantly influences individual reactions [11]. Parents’ perceptions of circumcision, molded by religious beliefs, cultural norms, and medical information, play a decisive role in driving their health decisions [12].

This study aims to assess Turkish parents’ perceptions regarding the ideal age for circumcision and the optimal procedural conditions. Furthermore, it seeks to identify key parental concerns and perceptions, thereby highlighting the specific issues practitioners should clarify to ensure that parents can make well-informed decisions based on accurate clinical guidance.

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional survey to assess Turkish parents’ awareness and perceptions regarding circumcision, examining its medical, psychological, and cultural dimensions, as well as their primary sources of information.

The study population comprised literate Turkish parents from diverse socio-demographic backgrounds who voluntarily completed the online survey via a URL or QR code. The 16-item questionnaire was developed in accordance with the core principles and indications outlined in the American Urological Association (AUA) and European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines. Expert-driven face and content validity were established through a comprehensive review by a panel of five pediatric urology specialists. The survey primarily assessed descriptive parameters, including demographics, procedural preferences, and information sources (Appendix). It utilized a mixed-format (multiple-choice and open-ended) design, incorporating Likert scales to evaluate perceptions regarding the ideal circumcision age and procedural conditions.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ordu University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision number: 2022/215, Date: 16/09/2022). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data were collected between March 2023 and March 2025 via an online link distributed nationally across seven regions through social media, parenting groups, and schools. To ensure unbiased responses, the survey link was also provided to parents seeking pre-circumcision counseling before they received any medical information from the participating physicians. Physicians at the seven host clinics (co-authors of this study) facilitated data collection by displaying QR codes on informational flyers within their clinics. These codes were shared with parents specifically prior to their clinical briefing to ensure that perceptions were captured before medical counseling was provided. All participants were informed about the study’s objectives and provided electronic informed consent.

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22 (IBM software, Pointe Claire, Quebec, Canada). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and parental perceptions. Group differences were examined using Chi-square (2) tests for categorical variables and independent samples t-tests for continuous variables; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). To achieve 80% statistical power in detecting a small-to-medium effect size (w = 0.15) for Chi-square tests with 3 degrees of freedom at a 0.05 significance level, a minimum of 485 participants was required. The final sample of 637 participants exceeded this requirement, providing robust statistical power (> 90%). Furthermore, effect sizes for the Chi-square tests were evaluated using Cramer’s V, with values interpreted as small (0.10), medium (0.30), and large (0.50). Given the exploratory nature of the survey, which aimed to identify broad categorical trends rather than testing a single confirmatory hypothesis, formal alpha adjustments for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni) were not applied.

Results

A total of 2,300 survey links were distributed online to parents across the seven regions of Turkey, of which 239 yielded responses. Additionally, 400 parents who attended the participating clinics for pre-circumcision counseling completed the survey. After excluding two responses due to incomplete or inconsistent entries, data from a final sample of 637 respondents were included in the analysis.

Mothers aged 30–44 years exhibited the highest OPC response rate at 78.8% (p = 0.018). Among fathers, the 18–29 age group showed the highest adherence to OPC at 86.2% (p = 0.005). Notably, OPC preference rates increased correlation with higher educational levels; for instance, 84.6% of mothers and 83.6% of fathers with a university degree or higher preferred OPC (p < 0.001). Among participants who considered circumcision necessary, the OPC response rate was 74.8%. Interestingly, all participants who deemed circumcision unnecessary indicated a hypothetical preference for OPC if the procedure were to be performed (p = 0.001) (Table 1).

Fathers aged 18–29 and mothers aged 30–44 years demonstrated the highest awareness regarding appropriate circumcision timing. Notably, a discrepancy was observed between parental knowledge and actual practice; despite the prevailing belief that circumcision should be avoided during the PSP, 41.0% of the participants’ children had undergone the procedure within this psychologically sensitive period (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Our study underscores the disparities among international guidelines regarding circumcision. While the AUA recognizes the potential health benefits of the procedure and emphasizes that the ultimate decision should reside with the parents, the EAU maintains a more restrictive stance [3]. Specifically, the EAU does not support routine or prophylactic circumcision for non-medical reasons, advocating that the procedure should be performed primarily when there is a clear medical indication [4]. This distinction is particularly relevant to our study population, where the vast majority of parents (95.1%) perceive the procedure as an essential requirement—largely driven by religious and cultural factors—rather than adhering strictly to the medical framework suggested by European guidelines.

It has been suggested that circumcision performed during the PSP may potentially impair psychosocial development [6]. In contrast, retrospective analyses of adult males have reported no significant differences in the prevalence of erectile dysfunction, premature ejaculation, or depression, regardless of whether the procedure occurred within or outside of this sensitive period [10].

Addressing the absence of a clear international consensus on the necessity of circumcision, our study found that the vast majority of parents (95.1%) considered the procedure necessary, while only 4.9% deemed it unnecessary. This latter finding is particularly noteworthy given the profound religious and cultural entrenchment of circumcision in Turkey. Parental attitudes in this region are deeply intertwined with religious values and regional traditions. Since traditional influences are often more pronounced in the Eastern and Southeastern provinces, our multicenter approach across all seven geographical regions was strategically designed to encompass this sociocultural diversity and identify how these factors modulate parental opinions.

Psychological and timing perspectives

The appropriate age for circumcision remains a subject of significant debate within the medical community. While neonatal circumcision is often favored due to faster wound healing and a lower incidence of complications [6], performing the procedure under anesthesia after age of six may mitigate psychological trauma potentially through pharmacologically induced anterograde amnesia [7]. Conversely, the 2–6 year age range, defining the PSP, remains highly controversial; several studies suggest that circumcision during this developmental stage may trigger castration anxiety and lead to long-term psychological sequelae [5]. On the other hand, an alternative body of research indicates that circumcision performed during the PSP does not necessarily result in significant long-term psychosexual or psychological disparities in adulthood compared to procedures performed at a later age. Specifically, no substantial variations have been reported concerning the prevalence of erectile dysfunction, premature ejaculation, or clinical depression, regardless of whether the surgery took place during this sensitive period or later [10]. The psychological implications of circumcision timing, specifically regarding the PSP (ages 2–6), represent a complex and contentious area in pediatric urology. Some researchers emphasize the potential for acute psychological distress and post-traumatic stress symptoms in children circumcised during this period, particularly when local anesthesia is utilized as the sole method of pain management [13]. However, a more nuanced perspective is emerging from broader literature. Several systematic reviews and long-term follow-up studies suggest that when performed under optimal surgical conditions with appropriate anesthesia, there is no substantiated evidence of permanent psychological harm or negative impact on adult psychosexual health. These findings imply that the quality of the perioperative experience and effective pain management may be more decisive factors than the chronological age at which the procedure is performed. This conflicting evidence suggests that while the PSP remains a period of heightened theoretical risk for immediate perioperative trauma, the long-term clinical sequelae are not yet definitively established. Our study contributes to this discourse by highlighting that even when parents are aware of these theoretical risks, a significant proportion still proceed with the procedure. This trend potentially stems from the absence of clear, consensus-based clinical guidelines that definitively correlate surgical timing to long-term psychological outcomes, leading parents to prioritize other sociocultural or practical factors.

In our study, 76% of parents believed that circumcision should not be performed during the PSP. This widespread perception of potential psychological harm persists despite the conflicting literature. Subgroup analyses revealed that the location of residence—whether urban or rural—had no significant impact on opinions regarding the optimal timing of circumcision (p = 0.117). This lack of disparity may be attributed to the ubiquitous accessibility of medical information through digital platforms and social media. Notably, fathers aged 18–29 and mothers aged 30–44 exhibited the highest awareness concerning the correct timing, suggesting a trend of greater health literacy among younger parental cohorts. Interestingly, having conducted prior research on the procedure did not significantly influence this awareness, implying that general sociocultural knowledge or digital exposure may be more influential than active medical inquiry.

Despite high parental awareness of the appropriate timing for circumcision, 41% of children in our study underwent the procedure during the PSP. The observed discrepancy between theoretical awareness and clinical practice may stem from a demographic bias within the participant cohort. Specifically, younger and more health-literate parents—who correctly identify the non-PSP as ideal—may not have yet proceeded with the procedure for their own children. Consequently, this subset of informed parents may have left the retrospective inquiry, “At what age was your child circumcised?“, unanswered, thereby potentially skewing the results toward parents who had already completed the procedure during the PSP in previous years.

Clinical and organizational preferences

Debates regarding circumcision also focus on the qualifications of the practitioner, the clinical setting, and the procedural methodology. European urology guidelines emphasize that performing circumcision within a sterile operating room reduces infection and complication rates [14]. Furthermore, it is recommended that the procedure be conducted in a well-equipped hospital setting, even when performed under local anesthesia in an office-based environment, to ensure patient safety and immediate access to emergency interventions.

Our results demonstrate that parents predominantly believe physicians should perform circumcision, a finding that strongly aligns with contemporary medical literature. Notably, while the vast majority favored specialized surgeons, a small but significant minority still considers non-physician healthcare personnel (0.5%) or traditional circumcisers (5.8%) acceptable. Although these figures are relatively low, their presence is noteworthy given the established legal and medical frameworks in Turkey. This may reflect the lingering influence of historical traditions or limited access to specialized centers in certain socio-economic strata, highlighting the ongoing transition from traditional practices to standardized medical interventions.

Regarding setting, 91.7% of parents favored an operating room, 13.6% preferred a clinic environment. Notably, 7% surprisingly believed a home setting was appropriate. While health regulations in Turkey strictly mandate that circumcision be performed solely by qualified medical professionals in clinical environments, our findings reveal a small but extant parental preference for home-based procedures. Given the critical safety risks associated with non-clinical settings—including life-threatening hemorrhage, infections, and inadequate analgesia—this 7% preference remains a significant public health concern. This discrepancy does not suggest medically sanctioned practices; rather, it underscores a persistent sociocultural misconception that prioritizes tradition over clinical safety. Consequently, there is a crucial need for targeted health education campaigns designed to inform parents of the potential for severe complications in home settings, ensuring that public perception aligns with modern surgical standards.

The administration of GA, LA, or a combination thereof remains a central consideration in circumcision, with the preferred modality varying significantly across different patient populations. The literature generally advocates for LA in neonates, while GA is typically recommended for older age groups [15]. This preference for GA in older children is largely rooted in the belief that it minimizes perioperative psychological trauma, primarily by facilitating pharmacologically induced anterograde amnesia [7]. Interestingly, one study reported that while circumcision did not generally result in severe post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms, procedures performed under GA were found to have a more negative impact on PTSD outcomes compared to those performed under LA. Furthermore, findings indicated that baseline depression levels served as a significant predictor for subsequent PTSD symptoms, suggesting that the patient’s pre-existing psychological state may be as critical as the anesthetic modality itself [13]. The literature evaluates a wide range of local and regional anesthetic techniques for perioperative pain management during circumcision. Within this context, the caudal block has been demonstrated to be superior to both dorsal penile nerve block and subcutaneous morphine in the effective prevention of procedural pain [16]. These findings suggest that the choice of regional technique is as vital as the anesthetic modality itself in ensuring a trauma-free experience, particularly for older children. In our study, 51.6% of parents preferred LA for circumcision, 31.1% favored GA, and 9.9% preferred a combined approach (LA + GA). Despite literature advocating for LA primarily during neonatal circumcision and GA for older age groups, a predominant portion of our participants still favored LA regardless of the child’s age. Regarding procedural pain management, we maintain that performing circumcision without any form of anesthesia is ethically and clinically unacceptable, notwithstanding the 7.4% of participants who deemed it unnecessary. Furthermore, while LA was the most preferred method among parents (51.6%), we contend that the choice of anesthetic technique should be strictly age-dependent. Specifically, while LA remains suitable for the neonatal period, GA is often preferable for older children to facilitate pharmacologically induced anterograde amnesia and prevent psychological trauma—an approach consistent with current literature emphasizing the mitigation of distress during the PSP.

Traditions, customs, and religious beliefs significantly shape parental perceptions of circumcision. While both Islam and Judaism recommend the procedure, medical necessity is increasingly recognized as a primary justification among modern cohorts [17]. In contemporary society, the internet and social media have emerged as prominent information sources regarding pediatric health, exerting a profound influence on parental perceptions across all demographic groups [18]. Our findings regarding the lack of rural-urban disparity in circumcision timing awareness further support this observation, suggesting that digital platforms act as a universal equalizer in health literacy.

Parents consistently deem information provided by physicians to be the most reliable source of health guidance. A recent study noted that while 89% of parents utilized online resources for health information when their child’s illness, 31% followed this information in lieu of consulting a physician. Notably, parents who maintained high levels of trust in their child’s doctor were more likely to prioritize professional medical advice over conflicting online data, with 91% seeking professional consultation alongside their digital inquiries [19]. In Turkey, religious beliefs often remain a decisive factor in parental decisions regarding circumcision [5]. Consistently, our study found that parents primarily cited a combination of religious and medical justifications, followed by traditional, societal, and psychological factors—findings that strongly align with national data. In contrast, medical benefits are more heavily emphasized in Western societies, where religious influence is less predominant [2]. Despite these cultural nuances, a significant majority of respondents in our study (63.8%) believed that “circumcision should be performed for medical reasons,” demonstrating a convergence of our results with both national trends and global medical perspectives.

A recent Turkish study explored perceptions and decision-making regarding circumcision among parents and medical personnel [20]. While that study addressed similar thematic areas, our larger sample size (n = 637) offers greater generalizability to the broader population. Notably, the aforementioned study uniquely examined the influence of grandparents on circumcision decisions—a relevant factor in patriarchal societies that warrants further consideration. Although their smaller cohort enabled the inclusion of perspectives from pediatricians and general practitioners (areas not encompassed by our research), our nationwide data on parental attitudes provides superior statistical power in assessing contemporary parental trends across Turkey.

The findings of our study align with, yet significantly expand upon, previous research conducted in Turkey. While foundational studies, such as those by Şahin et al. [21] and Corduk et al. [22] highlighted high rates of religious motivation and a strong preference for specialist-led procedures, their conclusions were largely restricted to single-center data or specific urban populations. More recently, Didişen et al. [23] reported on parental knowledge within a more localized context, identifying awareness levels regarding the importance of clinical settings similar to our own.

However, our study provides a broader national perspective by utilizing a multicenter approach across all seven geographical regions of Turkey, involving a significantly larger cohort (n = 637). This multi-regional scope reveals that despite geographical diversity, the preference for specialized care is a consistent national trend. Crucially, the larger sample size enabled a more nuanced analysis of awareness regarding the phallic stage period (PSP; ages 2–6). Unlike prior research that often-assessed general timing, our specific focus on the 2–6 age range identifies a critical knowledge-practice gap: while 76% of parents are theoretically aware of this sensitive period, over 40% still proceed with circumcision during this window. This clear identification of the discrepancy between theoretical awareness and actual practice represents a unique and significant contribution of our study to the national literature.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the sampling method relied exclusively on online distribution, which may have introduced selection bias by inadvertently excluding individuals with limited internet access or lower digital literacy. Second, the use of a self-reported survey instrument is susceptible to social-desirability bias, particularly regarding sensitive inquiries into religious and cultural motivations. Participants may have been inclined to provide responses as socially or culturally appropriate, potentially underrepresenting individual or more secular viewpoints. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of the study may be subject to recall bias concerning the exact timing and procedural details of past circumcisions. Our cohort exhibited a relatively high educational and urban profile, with 51% of participants being university graduates and 96.1% residing in provincial or district centers. While this is higher than the historical national average in Turkey, it aligns closely with recent demographic shifts among younger populations. According to TurkStat’s 2024 National Education Statistics, the proportion of higher education graduates in the 25–34 age group was 44.9%, which is close to the level observed in our sample. Given that most mothers (79.1%) and fathers (72.6%) were under the age of 45, this finding is consistent with the age structure of our cohort, which largely reflects younger parent populations. Furthermore, the utilization of an online survey may have enhanced the representation of younger and more educated individuals, as internet penetration in Turkey correlates markedly with educational attainment. Consequently, while rural and less educated groups may be underrepresented, the sample effectively reflects a substantial and influential segment of the target population. Furthermore, due to its cross-sectional design, the study captures only a snapshot in time, thereby limiting the ability to establish definitive causal relationships between the analyzed variables. Additionally, while the survey questionnaire was not developed using a formal Delphi consensus, the questions were rigorously crafted with direct input from a panel of pediatric urology specialists. This methodological step, which was not explicitly detailed in previous comparative studies, represents a key strength of our approach, ensuring that the inquiries were clinically relevant and specialized. Furthermore, no formal sensitivity or validity testing was conducted for the individual survey items. Additionally, the questionnaire predominantly utilized closed-ended questions with predefined response options. While this structured approach was essential to facilitate standardized data collection and ensure comparability across a large, multicenter sample, it may have introduced a degree of leading bias. Consequently, this format may have restricted participants from expressing unprompted, alternative perspectives or nuanced beliefs that were not captured by the fixed response categories. While our study successfully captured the perceptions of 637 parents, it did not account for the influence of the broader family unit. In many traditional and patriarchal segments of Turkish society, grandparents and family elders often play a decisive role in significant family events, such as circumcision. Their influence on the timing and the selection of the healthcare provider was not directly measured, which may limit the comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted decision-making process within these social contexts.

Future research incorporating multi-generational perspectives would provide a more holistic view of these sociocultural dynamics. Furthermore, achieving a more comprehensive understanding of decision-making necessitates the integration of perspectives from diverse stakeholders, including not only grandparents but also healthcare providers and religious leaders. To build upon the findings of this cross-sectional survey, subsequent research should prioritize longitudinal psychological studies that follow children from the perioperative period through adolescence and into adulthood. Such prospective data are essential to provide definitive, high-quality evidence regarding the long-term impact of circumcision timing on psychosexual health, thereby resolving the persistent ambiguities in the current literature.

Additionally, our identification of a ‘knowledge-practice gap’ suggests that traditional information-sharing methods may be insufficient in the digital age. Future initiatives should explore digital health interventions, such as interactive mobile health applications, expert-led webinars, and targeted social media campaigns. These tools could provide parents with real-time, evidence-based guidance, ensuring that their theoretical awareness of surgical timing and clinical safety translates into informed practice. Evaluating the efficacy of such digital interventions across diverse demographic groups will be a crucial step toward modernizing pediatric public health strategies and bridging the discrepancy between parental intent and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Circumcision, an ancient and globally prevalent surgical procedure, remains a subject of ongoing debate regarding its clinical benefits and potential risks. Parental attitudes toward this procedure are pivotal and are shaped by a complex interplay of various factors. Our study demonstrates that the majority of parents pursue circumcision for a combination of medical and religious reasons. The findings also indicate a high level of parental awareness regarding the optimal age for the procedure, the necessity of specialized practitioner qualifications, and the importance of appropriate clinical environments. Despite these positive trends, a persistent minority of parents still believe that circumcision can be safely performed at home by unqualified individuals. Consequently, we contend that the strategic utilization of mass communication is vital to inform parents about the inherent risks of this standard procedure and to further enhance existing awareness, ultimately ensuring safer surgical practices for all children.
