Convergence Analysis of Natural Power Method and Its Applications to Control This work was supported by JSPS KAKENHI Grant Numbers 23K26126 and 21K12097.
Daiki Tsuzuki, Kentaro Ohki

Abstract
This paper analyzes the discrete-time natural power method, demonstrating its convergence to the dominant r r -dimensional subspace corresponding to the r r eigenvalues with the largest absolute values. This contrasts with the Oja flow, which targets eigenvalues with the largest real parts. We leverage this property to develop methods for model order reduction and low-rank controller synthesis for discrete-time LTI systems, proving preservation of key system properties. We also extend the low-rank control framework to slowly-varying LTV systems, showing its utility for tracking time-varying dominant subspaces.



keywords

Principal component flow, dominant component extraction, model order reduction, linear systems, controller design

1
Introduction

Online principal component algorithms have recently garnered significant attention.
Oja’s component flow (Oja flow), generated by a matrix differential equation introduced by
Oja 1982
;
Oja and Karhunen 1985
, is a prominent example. Its convergence properties have been investigated by
Chen et al. 1998
for positive definite matrices, and by
Kuo et al. 2025
and
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
for general square matrices.
Discrete-time counterparts were subsequently proposed by
Oja 1989
and
Hua et al. 1999
primarily to extract dominant singular subspaces for positive definite matrices.
Notably, Oja’s discrete-time algorithm is the Euler scheme of the continuous-time Oja flow and extracts the same dominant subspace as its continuous counterpart.

Hua et al. 1999
proposed the following principal subspace tracking algorithm, also known as the
natural power method
or
power iteration method
:

\displaystyle U[k+1]=

\displaystyle AU[k](U[k]^{\top}A^{\top}AU[k])^{-1/2},

(1)

where
U[0]
is in the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}(r,n)
and
X^{1/2}
denotes the square root of a positive semidefinite matrix
X
.
Convergence analysis of this algorithm has primarily been investigated for cases where
A
is a symmetric, positive-definite matrix.
For applications involving the principal components of covariance matrices, the positive-semidefiniteness of
A
is a natural requirement.
However, for other applications, such as extracting the left- or right-eigensubspaces of a non-symmetric matrix, positive semidefiniteness is not always appropriate.

This paper investigates the convergence properties of the discrete-time principal component algorithm (
1
) for general square matrices.
We demonstrate that the natural power method extracts the
r
-dominant subspace corresponding to the
r
eigenvalues with the largest
absolute values
.
This result contrasts with the Euler approximation of the Oja flow, which extracts the
r
-dominant subspace corresponding to the
r
eigenvalues with the largest
real parts
(
Kuo et al. 2025
;
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
).
Furthermore, we consider applications to control problems.
Since the dominant subspace extracted by (
1
) corresponds to the dominant mode of discrete-time dynamical systems, the algorithm is naturally applicable to the model order reduction of such systems.
We also attempt a low-rank output feedback synthesis for a linear time-varying system.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In the next section, we briefly summarize existing results.
Our main contributions are presented in Section
3
, where we provide a convergence analysis of the natural power method (
1
) for general square matrices.
Section
4
presents a model order reduction method based on the natural power method and an attempt at low-rank controller synthesis for a linear time-varying system.
We conclude the paper in Section
5
with final remarks.

Notation

The sets of real and complex numbers are denoted by
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
, respectively. The set of
n\times m
real matrices is
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.
I_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix, and
0_{n,m}
denotes the
n\times m
zero matrix. For simplicity, we denote
0_{n}=0_{n,n}
; if the dimension is trivial,
0
is used.

A^{\top}
and
A^{\dagger}
denote the transpose and Hermitian conjugate of a matrix
A
, respectively.
\|x\|
denotes the Euclidean norm of a vector
x
, and
\|A\|_{\rm ind}
denotes the induced norm of a matrix
A
. For a symmetric matrix
A
,
A>0
(
A\geq 0
) indicates positive definiteness (semidefiniteness).
A^{1/2}
denotes the unique positive-semidefinite square root. The eigenvalues of a square matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are ordered such that
|\lambda_{1}[A]|\geq\dots\geq|\lambda_{n}[A]|
. The corresponding (generalized) right eigenvector is
\psi_{i}[A]
, where each vector is normalized. The matrix of eigenvectors is defined as
\Psi[A]:=[\psi_{1}[A],\dots,\psi_{n}[A]]
. The Stiefel manifold is defined as
\mathrm{St}(r,n):=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\mid X^{\top}X=I_{r}\}
.

2
Previous Work

Numerous principal component extraction algorithms exist for both continuous-time and discrete-time settings.
In contrast to continuous-time frameworks, discrete-time algorithms were primarily developed to reduce computational costs.
Notably, the natural power method requires the inverse square root calculation, which may be computationally expensive.

Hua 2004
provides numerical insights into this issue.
This paper focuses primarily on the convergence issues of the natural power method (
1
) rather than its computational cost, as
Hua et al. 1999
noted that this method provides a standard representation for similar algorithms.

Hua et al. 1999
derived the following convergence result for symmetric positive semidefinite matrices.

Proposition 1

(
(
Hua et al. 1999
, Appendix A)
)

Suppose that the following two conditions hold:

1.

A=A^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is positive semidefinite.

2.

The
r
-th eigenvalue is strictly separated from the
(r+1)
-th eigenvalue:
\lambda_{r}[A]>\lambda_{r+1}[A]
.

If the initial condition
U[0]
belongs to the set

\displaystyle U[0]\in\Bigg\{\Psi[A]\begin{bmatrix}K_{r}\\
K_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{St}(r,n)\ \bigg|\ K_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},

\displaystyle K_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-r)\times r},\ \mathrm{det}(K_{r})\neq 0\Bigg\},

then the solution of (
1
) converges exponentially to the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}:=\left\{\Psi[A]\begin{bmatrix}K_{r}\\
0_{n-r,r}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{St}(r,n)\ \bigg|\ K_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\right\}

(2)

with a convergence rate of
\lambda_{r+1}[A]/\lambda_{r}[A]
.

Since the natural power method (
1
) represents a standard form for this class of algorithms [
Hua et al. 1999
;
Attallah and Abed-Meraim 2002
;
Badeau et al. 2005
;
Hua 2004
], Proposition
1
provides an essential convergence benchmark. However, to the best of our knowledge, the convergence of this and related algorithms has not been analyzed for general square matrices.

3
Convergence Analysis for the Natural Power Method

In this section, we analyze the natural power method (
1
) for general square matrices.
We show that the forward invariant set differs from that of the Oja flow, implying that the natural power method extracts a different subspace.

3.1
Convergence Analysis

This section generalizes the convergence results presented in
(
Hua et al. 1999
, Appendix A)
to general square matrices. The solution of the natural power method (
1
) remains on the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}(r,n)
provided that
U[k]^{\top}A^{\top}AU[k]
is non-singular. This condition is always satisfied if
A
is non-singular and
U[0]\in\mathrm{St}(r,n)
. Moreover, the following lemma holds.

Lemma 2

Let
m\geq r
. If the initial condition
U[0]
is in

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}:=\Bigg\{\Psi[A]

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}K_{m,r}\\
K_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{St}(r,n)\ \Bigg|\ K_{m,r}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times r},

\displaystyle K_{\perp}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-m)\times r},\ \mathrm{rank}(K_{m,r})=r\Bigg\}

(3)

and the eigenvalues satisfy
|\lambda_{m}[A]|>|\lambda_{m+1}[A]|
, then
U[k]\in\mathcal{V}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}
and
U[k]^{\top}A^{\top}AU[k]
is non-singular
\forall k\geq 0
.

We use the simplified notation
\mathcal{V}_{r}^{\rm(d)}:=\mathcal{V}_{r,r}^{\rm(d)}
.

{pf}

Let
Z[k]:=U[k]^{\top}A^{\top}AU[k]
,
\Psi:=\Psi[A]
, and
\Lambda:=\mbox{blk-diag}(\Lambda_{m},\ \Lambda_{\perp}):=\Psi^{-1}A\Psi
, where
\Lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
\Lambda_{\perp}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-m)\times(n-m)}
.
At
k=0
, the following holds:

\displaystyle Z[0]=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}K_{m,r}^{\dagger}&K_{\perp}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\Lambda^{\dagger}\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\Lambda\begin{bmatrix}K_{m,r}\\
K_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}K_{m,r}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{m}^{\dagger}&K_{\perp}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\perp}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{m}K_{m,r}\\
\Lambda_{\perp}K_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}[\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi]\left(K_{m}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{m}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{m}K_{m}+K_{\perp}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\perp}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\perp}K_{\perp}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}[\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi]K_{m,r}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{m}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{m}K_{m,r}.

Given the assumption that
\lambda_{m}[A]\neq 0
(i.e.,
\Lambda_{m}
is non-singular) and
\mathrm{rank}(K_{m,r})=r
, it follows that
Z[0]>0
, meaning
Z[0]
is invertible.
Therefore,

\displaystyle U[1]=

\displaystyle AU[0]Z[0]^{-1/2}=\Psi\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{m}K_{m,r}Z[0]^{-1/2}\\
\Lambda_{\perp}K_{\perp}Z[0]^{-1/2}\end{bmatrix}

and
\mathrm{rank}(\Lambda_{m}K_{m,r}Z[0]^{-1/2})=r
since
\Lambda_{m}
and
Z[0]^{-1/2}
are invertible.

Suppose that at time
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle U[k]=

\displaystyle\Psi\begin{bmatrix}K_{m,r}^{(k)}\\
K_{\perp}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{St}(r,n),

where
K_{m,r}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times r}
with
\mathrm{rank}(K_{m,r}^{(k)})=r
and
K_{\perp}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-m)\times r}
.
Note that
K_{\bullet}^{(k)}=\Lambda_{\bullet}^{k}K_{\bullet}Z[0]^{-1/2}\dots Z[k-1]^{-1/2}
for
\bullet=(m,r),\perp
.
By the same argument as for
k=0
,
Z[k]
is invertible. Then,

\displaystyle U[k+1]=

\displaystyle\Psi\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{m}^{k+1}K_{m,r}Z[0]^{-1/2}Z[1]^{-1/2}\dots Z[k]^{-1/2}\\
\Lambda_{\perp}^{k+1}K_{\perp}Z[0]^{-1/2}Z[1]^{-1/2}\dots Z[k]^{-1/2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi\begin{bmatrix}K_{m,r}^{(k+1)}\\
K_{\perp}^{(k+1)}\end{bmatrix}

and therefore,
\mathrm{rank}(K_{m,r}^{(k+1)})=r
.
This implies that
Z[k+1]
is invertible if
Z[k]
is invertible.
By mathematical induction, the statement holds.

From Lemma
2
, we obtain the following convergence result, which parallels
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
.

Theorem 3

Suppose that for a given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, there exists
m\in\{r,\dots,n-1\}
such that
|\lambda_{m}[A]|>|\lambda_{m+1}[A]|
.
If the initial condition
U[0]\in\mathcal{V}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}
is defined in Eq. (
3
), then the solution
U[k]
of (
1
) converges to

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}:=\Bigg\{\Psi[A]\begin{bmatrix}K_{m,r}\\
0_{n-m,r}\end{bmatrix}\in

\displaystyle\mathrm{St}(r,n)\ \Bigg|\ K_{m,r}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times r},

\displaystyle\ \mathrm{rank}(K_{m,r})=r\Bigg\}.

(4)

If
m=r
, we use the simplified notation
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}=\mathcal{U}_{r,r}^{\rm(d)}
.
For any
\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
,

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A\bar{U}_{r}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}K_{r,r}\\
0_{n-r,r}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\Lambda\begin{bmatrix}K_{r,r}\\
0_{n-r,r}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}K_{r,r}\\
0_{n-r,r}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\begin{bmatrix}K_{r,r}\\
0_{n-r,r}\end{bmatrix}K_{r,r}^{-1}\Lambda_{r}K_{r,r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{r,r}^{-1}\Lambda_{r}K_{r,r},

(5)

where
\Lambda:=\mbox{blk-diag}(\Lambda_{r},\Lambda_{\perp})=\Psi^{-1}A\Psi
and
\Psi=\Psi[A]
. This means that
\bar{U}_{r}
preserves the dominant
r
eigenvalues of
A
with the largest absolute values.
Following the argument in
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, §III.C)
, the volume of
\mathcal{V}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}
equals that of
\mathrm{St}(r,n)
.
Therefore, almost all initial matrices converge to the set
\mathcal{U}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}
.

{pf}

From the proof of Lemma
2
,
U[k]\in\mathrm{St}(r,n)
is represented as

\displaystyle U[k]=\Psi[A]\begin{bmatrix}K_{m,r}^{(k)}\\
K_{\perp}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix},\ K_{m,r}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times r},\ K_{\perp}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-m)\times r},

where
K_{m,r}^{(k)}
and
K_{\perp}^{(k)}
are defined as in the proof of Lemma
2
with
K_{m,r}^{(0)}=K_{m,r}
and
K_{\perp}^{(0)}=K_{\perp}
.
Note that for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle K_{\perp}^{(k)}=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\perp}^{k}K_{\perp}(Z[k-1]\dots Z[0])^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\perp}^{k}|\lambda_{m}[A]|^{-k}K_{\perp}(|\lambda_{m}[A]|^{-2k}Z[k-1]\dots Z[0])^{-1/2}

holds whenever
U[0]\in\mathcal{V}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}
.
Since
|\lambda_{m}[A]|>|\lambda_{m+1}[A]|
, for any
\delta\in(0,|\lambda_{m}[A]|-|\lambda_{m+1}[A]|)
, there exists
c=c(\delta)\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\|\Lambda_{\perp}^{k}|\lambda_{m}[A]|^{-k}\|_{\rm ind}\leq

\displaystyle c\left|\frac{\lambda_{m+1}[A]+\delta}{\lambda_{m}[A]}\right|^{k}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}0.

Simultaneously, the term inside the parentheses of the inverse square root,
|\lambda_{m}[A]|^{-2k}Z[k-1]\dots Z[0]
, remains positive or diverges.
Therefore,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|K_{\perp}^{(k)}\|_{\rm ind}=0
.
Since
U[k]\in\mathrm{St}(r,n)
, the convergence of
K_{\perp}^{(k)}
to zero and the dependence of
U[k]
on
K_{\bullet}^{(k)}
,
\bullet=r,\perp
, ensure that
U[k]
converges to
\mathcal{U}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}
.

From Theorem
3
, if the absolute values of consecutive eigenvalues,
|\lambda_{m}[A]|
and
|\lambda_{m+1}[A]|
, are close, the convergence rate may become slow.

Remark 4

The solution
U[k]
of (
1
) generally does not converge to an element of
\mathcal{U}_{m,r}^{\rm(d)}
even if
m=r
under the conditions of Theorem
3
.
We briefly show this fact below.
If
m=r
, any element
\bar{U}_{r}
of
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
can be represented by a non-singular matrix
K_{r}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
as

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{r}=\Psi[A]\begin{bmatrix}K_{r}\\
0_{n-r,r}\end{bmatrix}=\Psi_{r}K_{r},

where
\Psi_{r}:=[\psi_{1}[A],\dots,\psi_{r}[A]]
.
The following holds:

\displaystyle A\bar{U}_{r}=

\displaystyle\Psi_{r}\Lambda_{r}K_{r}=\Psi_{r}K_{r}K_{r}^{-1}\Lambda_{r}K_{r}=\bar{U}_{r}A_{\bar{U}_{r}},

(7)

where
A_{\bar{U}_{r}}:=\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A\bar{U}_{r}
.
If
U[k]=\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
, then

\displaystyle U[k+1]=

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{r}A_{\bar{U}_{r}}(A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top}A_{\bar{U}_{r}})^{-1/2}.

Recall that
A_{\bar{U}_{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is invertible. After
\ell
steps, we have

\displaystyle U[k+\ell]=\bar{U}_{r}\{A_{\bar{U}_{r}}(A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top}A_{\bar{U}_{r}})^{-1/2}\}^{\ell}.

Therefore,
\bar{U}_{r}
is a stationary point if and only if
A_{\bar{U}_{r}}=(A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top}A_{\bar{U}_{r}})^{1/2}
, which implies that
A_{\bar{U}_{r}}=A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top}>0
, which does not hold for general matrices.

On the other hand, the same argument as in
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2024a
, Prop. 2)
yields that any element
\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
is an equilibrium point of the Oja flow, i.e.,

\displaystyle(I_{n}-\bar{U}_{r}\bar{U}_{r}^{\top})A\bar{U}_{r}=0_{n,r}.

(8)

This result is utilized in Section
4
.

Based on the arguments in Remark
4
, one can utilize an algorithm that possesses a stationary point.

Proposition 5

Assume that
A
satisfies
|\lambda_{r}[A]|>|\lambda_{r+1}[A]|
. Then, the sequence generated by the following algorithm converges to an element of
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
if
\hat{U}[0]\in\mathcal{V}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
and
\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A\hat{U}[k]
is non-singular for all
k\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\hat{U}[k+1]=

\displaystyle A\hat{U}[k](\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A^{\top}A\hat{U}[k])^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\times(\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A^{\top}\hat{U}[k]\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A\hat{U}[k])^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\times\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A^{\top}\hat{U}[k].

(9)

{pf}

Note that the difference between (
1
) and (
9
) is that (
9
) includes an additional multiplication
W[k]:=(\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A^{\top}\hat{U}[k]\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A\hat{U}[k])^{-1/2}(\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A^{\top}\hat{U}[k])\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, which is an orthogonal matrix.

Under the assumptions and the arguments in the proof of Lemma
2
, the solution
\hat{U}[k]
of the algorithm (
9
) always satisfies the non-singularity of
\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A^{\top}A\hat{U}[k]
.
Additionally, since
W[k]
is an orthogonal matrix, the same argument as in Theorem
3
implies that
U[k]
of (
9
) converges to
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
.
Thus, the remainder of the proof focuses on stationarity.

Let
\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
. Then there exists a non-singular matrix
K_{r}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
such that
\bar{U}_{r}=\Psi_{r}K_{r}
, where
\Psi_{r}:=[\psi_{1}[A],\dots,\psi_{r}[A]]
.
From Eq. (
7
),
A\bar{U}_{r}=\bar{U}_{r}A_{\bar{U}_{r}}
, where
A_{\bar{U}_{r}}:=\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, resulting in
\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A^{\top}A\bar{U}_{r}=A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top}A_{\bar{U}_{r}}
.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle(\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A^{\top}A\bar{U}_{r})^{-1/2}(\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A^{\top}\bar{U}_{r}\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A\bar{U}_{r})^{-1/2}\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A\bar{U}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top}A_{\bar{U}_{r}})^{-1}A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top}=A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{-1}

holds.
Therefore, if
\hat{U}[0]=\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
, then

\hat{U}[1]=A\bar{U}_{r}A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{-1}=\bar{U}_{r}=\hat{U}[0]
.
This result concludes that the sequence generated by (
9
) converges to an element of
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
.

Numerical experiments indicate that the assumption regarding the non-singularity of
\hat{U}[k]^{\top}A\hat{U}[k]
is not restrictive.
Algorithm (
9
) requires an additional square root inverse operation, increasing computational cost.
Since
U[k]U[k]^{\top}=\hat{U}[k]\hat{U}[k]^{\top}
, the convergence of the natural power method can be confirmed by verifying the convergence of the projection matrix
U[k]U[k]^{\top}
.

3.2
Subspace Dimension Reduction

We expect to efficiently increase or reduce the dimension
r
of the extracted subspace,
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
, once an element
\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
has been computed.
Unlike the Oja flow, as discussed in
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, §III.D)
, the natural power method (
1
) is nonlinear in both
A
and
U[k]
, which makes developing dimension-increasing algorithms challenging. We therefore focus only on reducing the dimension of the extracted subspace.

Let
r\geq 1
and
m\in\{1,\dots,r-1\}
(
r>m
). Assume that the eigenvalues of
A
are strictly separated at the
r
-th and
m
-th indices, such that
|\lambda_{r}[A]|>|\lambda_{r+1}[A]|
and
|\lambda_{m}[A]|>|\lambda_{m+1}[A]|
.
Let
A_{\bar{U}_{r}}:=\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A\bar{U}_{r}
be the projection of
A
onto the
r
-dominant subspace spanned by
\bar{U}_{r}
.
We then propose the following reduced-order natural power method:

\displaystyle u[k+1]=A_{\bar{U}_{r}}u[k](u[k]^{\top}A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top}A_{\bar{U}_{r}}u[k])^{-1/2},

(10)

where
u[0]\in\mathrm{St}(m,r)
is the initial condition for the reduced system, and
\bar{U}_{r}u[0]\in\mathrm{St}(m,n)
is assumed to belong to
\mathcal{V}_{m}^{{\rm(d)}}
.
From the same arguments used in Theorem
3
,
u[k]
converges to the
m
-dominant subspace of the projected matrix
A_{\bar{U}_{r}}
. Consequently, the projected solution
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bar{U}_{r}u[k]
converges to
\mathcal{U}_{m}^{\rm(d)}
.

This reduction method avoids using the original
n\times n
matrix
A
in subsequent iterations and is therefore computationally efficient when
r\ll n
.

4
Applications to Control Problems

Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
proposed a model order reduction (MOR) method based on the Oja flow for continuous-time linear time-invariant (LTI) systems. This section proposes a counterpart for discrete-time linear systems. While related works such as
Hua et al. 2001
considered low-rank filters for static systems and
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2024b
investigated low-rank Kalman filtering for continuous-time state equations with intermittent observations, these approaches did not address the MOR of general discrete-time dynamical systems. We therefore propose low-rank methods for control problems involving discrete-time systems.

4.1
Model Order Reduction for Discrete-Time LTI Systems

Throughout this subsection, we assume
|\lambda_{r}[A]|>|\lambda_{r+1}[A]|
.

Before proceeding to MOR, we present some useful preliminary results. For convenience, we define the projected matrices
A_{Y}:=Y^{\top}AY
,
B_{Y}:=Y^{\top}B
, and
C_{Y}:=CY
for a projection matrix
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.
Equation (
8
) implies that for an orthogonal matrix
Q_{\bar{U}}:=[\bar{U}_{r},\bar{U}_{\perp}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where
\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
and
\bar{U}_{\perp}\in\mathrm{St}(n-r,n)
satisfies
(I_{n}-\bar{U}_{r}\bar{U}_{r}^{\top})=\bar{U}_{\perp}\bar{U}_{\perp}^{\top}
, the system matrix
A
can be simplified via similarity transformation:

\displaystyle Q_{\bar{U}}^{\top}AQ_{\bar{U}}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{\bar{U}_{r}}&\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}A\bar{U}_{\perp}\\
0_{n-r,r}&A_{\bar{U}_{\perp}}\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

Since Eq. (
11
) mirrors the transformation found for the Oja flow in
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, Sec. IV.A)
, similar MOR properties hold for discrete-time LTI systems.
In particular,
A_{\bar{U}_{r}}
preserves
r
-dominant eigenvalues, meaning that the unstable or slow modes of discrete-time linear systems can be extracted by the natural power method.
As in
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, Sec. IV.A)
, we utilize two distinct stable invariant sets:
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}[A]:=\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}
(the right eigensubspace) and
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}[A^{\top}]
(the left eigensubspace). The following lemma characterizes the properties of
\bar{U}_{\perp}
.

Lemma 6

Suppose that
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is non-singular.
Then, the columns of
\bar{U}_{\perp}
spans the minor left eigensubspace of
A
, i.e.,
\bar{U}_{\perp}\in\mathcal{U}_{n-r}^{\rm(d)}[A^{-\top}]
.

{pf}

The eigenvalues of
A^{-\top}
are ordered as the reciprocals of the eigenvalues of
A
, such that
\lambda_{1}[A^{-\top}]=1/\lambda_{n}[A],\dots,\lambda_{n}[A^{-\top}]=1/\lambda_{1}[A]
.
Because
\bar{U}_{\perp}
satisfies
\bar{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\bar{U}_{r}=0_{n-r,r}
,
\bar{U}_{\perp}
has the following form:

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{\perp}=\Psi[A]^{-\dagger}\begin{bmatrix}0_{r,n-r}\\
\tilde{K}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix},\quad\tilde{K}_{\perp}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-r)\times(n-r)}.

On the other hand, using
\Lambda[A]:=\Psi[A]^{-1}A\Psi[A]
,

\displaystyle A^{-\top}=

\displaystyle\Psi[A]^{-\dagger}\Lambda[A]^{-\dagger}\Psi[A]^{\dagger}=\Psi[A^{-\top}]\Lambda[A^{-\top}]\Psi[A^{-\top}]^{-1}

holds.
Notice that since
\Lambda[A]
is in the Jordan form,
\Lambda[A]^{-\dagger}
is in the same block-sized block diagonal matrix.
Then, there exists the same block-sized block diagonal non-singular matrix
J\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and a permutation matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
PJ\Lambda[A]^{-\dagger}J^{-1}P^{\top}=\Lambda[A^{-\top}]
.
Using these matrices
J
and
P
,

\displaystyle\Psi[A]^{-\dagger}J^{-1}P^{\top}\Lambda[A^{-\top}]PJ\Psi[A]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi[A^{-\top}]\Lambda[A^{-\top}]\Psi[A^{-\top}]^{-1}

holds, i.e.,
\Psi[A^{-\top}]^{-1}PJ=\Psi[A]^{-\dagger}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{\perp}=

\displaystyle\Psi[A^{-\top}]^{-1}PJ\begin{bmatrix}0_{r,n-r}\\
\tilde{K}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}=\Psi[A^{-\top}]^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}K_{\perp}\\
0_{r,n-r}\end{bmatrix}

holds for some
K_{\perp}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-r)\times(n-r)}
, i.e.,
\bar{U}_{\perp}\in\mathcal{U}_{n-r}^{\rm(d)}[A^{-\top}]
.

This result confirms that the
\bar{U}_{\perp}
subspace corresponds to the system’s fast subspace, which can be used for singularly perturbed systems. See
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, Sec. IV.C)
for the continuous-time application.

Now, consider the following LTI system:

\displaystyle x[k+1]=Ax[k]+Bu[k],\quad y[k]=Cx[k],

(12)

where
x[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state,
u[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control input, and
y[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the output.
A
,
B
, and
C
are real matrices of appropriate dimensions.

As shown in
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, Props. 21–23)
, similar model order reduction properties hold for the discrete-time linear system (
12
).
Specifically, the projection of the reachability and observability Gramians onto the dominant subspace is preserved.
Note that from Eq. (
7
), the following relations hold for
\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}[A]
and
\bar{V}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}[A^{\top}]
:

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{r}^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(A^{\top})^{i}C^{\top}CA^{i}\right)\bar{U}_{r}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top})^{i}C_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{\top}C_{\bar{U}_{r}}A_{\bar{U}_{r}}^{i},

\displaystyle\bar{V}_{r}^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}A^{i}BB^{\top}(A^{\top})^{i}\right)\bar{V}_{r}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}A_{\bar{V}_{r}}^{i}B_{\bar{V}_{r}}B_{\bar{V}_{r}}^{\top}(A_{\bar{V}_{r}}^{\top})^{i}.

These equations imply that if the system (
12
) is observable and reachable, then the corresponding reduced system is also observable and reachable.

Furthermore,
\bar{V}_{r}^{\top}\bar{U}_{r}
is non-singular
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, Lemma 19)
, and
A_{\bar{U}_{r}}=(\bar{V}_{r}^{\top}\bar{U}_{r})^{-1}A_{\bar{V}_{r}}\bar{V}_{r}^{\top}\bar{U}_{r}
holds, as established in
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, Lemma 20)
for the continuous-time case.
This transformation leads directly to the following result on the transfer function.

Proposition 7

Suppose that the system (
12
) is reachable and observable.
Let
\bar{U}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}[A]
and
\bar{V}_{r}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\rm(d)}[A^{\top}]
.
If the system (
12
) is observable and reachable, then the reduced models with
(A_{\bar{U}_{r}},B_{\bar{V}_{r}(\bar{V}_{r}^{\top}\bar{U}_{r})^{-\top}},C_{\bar{U}_{r}})
and
(A_{\bar{V}_{r}},B_{\bar{V}},C_{\bar{U}_{r}(\bar{V}_{r}^{\top}\bar{U}_{r})^{-1}})
are observable and reachable.
Moreover, these models share the same transfer function:

\displaystyle P_{\rm rd}[z]:=

\displaystyle C_{\bar{U}_{r}}(zI_{r}-A_{\bar{U}_{r}})^{-1}B_{\bar{V}_{r}(\bar{V}_{r}^{\top}\bar{U}_{r})^{-\top}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\bar{U}_{r}(\bar{V}_{r}^{\top}\bar{U}_{r})^{-1}}(zI_{r}-A_{\bar{V}_{r}})^{-1}B_{\bar{V}_{r}}.

The proof is identical to
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, Props. 22–23)
and is therefore omitted.

Error analysis and controller synthesis based on the above model-order reduction method are left for future work.

4.2
Reduced Rank Controller Synthesis for LTV Systems

A low-rank design of observer and state feedback gains for continuous-time LTI systems was proposed in
(
Tsuzuki and Ohki 2025
, §IV.B)
; this approach is readily applicable to discrete-time LTI systems.
In this section, we present an illustrative example that extends low-rank controller synthesis to linear time-varying (LTV) systems.
For continuous-time systems,
Tranninger et al. 2022
demonstrated low-rank filtering for a class of LTV systems under the infinitesimal convergence time condition of a variation of the Oja flow.
As a first step, consider the following slowly varying LTV system:

\displaystyle x[k+1]=A[k]x[k]+Bu[k],\quad y[k]=Cx[k],

(13)

where
A[k]:=Q[k]^{\top}A_{\alpha}Q[k]
,
B:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

,
C:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\end{bmatrix}
,

\displaystyle Q[k]:=

\displaystyle Q_{1}[k]Q_{2}[k],\ Q_{1}[k]:=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\omega_{1}k)&-\sin(\omega_{1}k)&0\\
\sin(\omega_{1}k)&\cos(\omega_{1}k)&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle Q_{2}[k]:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos(\omega_{2}k)&-\sin(\omega_{2}k)\\
0&\sin(\omega_{2}k)&\cos(\omega_{2}k)\end{bmatrix},

with
\omega_{1}=0.01
,
\omega_{2}=0.03
, and
A_{\alpha}
defined as in (
6
).

To stabilize the system (
13
), we consider the following observer-based controller:

\displaystyle\hat{x}[k+1]=

\displaystyle A[k]\hat{x}[k]+Bu[k]+U[k]L[k](y[k]-C\hat{x}[k]),

(14)

\displaystyle u[k]=

\displaystyle F[k]V[k]^{\top}\hat{x},

(15)

where
U[k]\in\mathrm{St}(2,3)
and
V[k]\in\mathrm{St}(2,3)
are the solutions of the natural power method (
1
) for
A[k]
and
A[k]^{\top}
, respectively.

L[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1}
and
F[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 2}
are the observer gain and feedback gain, respectively.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is no comprehensive feedback and observer gain design method for LTV systems.
In this paper, we apply the pole assignment method to obtain the gains instantaneously at each step. Specifically, at each step
k
,
L[k]
and
F[k]
are designed such that
A_{U[k]}-L[k]C_{U[k]}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
A_{V[k]}-B_{V[k]}F[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
have eigenvalues at
0.5
and
0.7
.

5
Conclusion

We investigated the convergence of the discrete-time natural power method, demonstrating its ability to extract the dominant subspace corresponding to the
r
eigenvalues with the largest absolute values—a crucial distinction from the Oja flow. We applied this result to develop methods for MOR and low-rank controller synthesis for discrete-time systems. The MOR scheme was shown to preserve key system properties. Future work will focus on the rigorous analysis of error bounds and broader applications to time-varying control and estimation problems.
