Technology behind augmenting fracture healing
Authors: Devan Mehta, Philipp Leucht
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite bone’s robust regenerative capacity, complications such as delayed union and nonunion affect 5–10% of fractures, with significant clinical and economic burdens. The rising incidence of fractures, particularly in an aging population, highlights the importance of optimizing fracture healing strategies. This review explores current therapies aimed at enhancing bone regeneration, focusing on 2 main categories: local biologic therapies and mechanical therapies. Local biologic treatments, including concentrated bone marrow aspirate, platelet-rich plasma, bone morphogenetic proteins, and platelet-derived growth factor, aim to stimulate osteogenesis at the fracture site. Therapies, such as electrical stimulation, low-intensity pulsed ultrasound, and extracorporeal shockwave therapy, are theorized to modify the mechanical environment to promote healing. Although these therapies show promise, variability in clinical outcomes emphasizes the need for further research to standardize protocols and refine treatment strategies. Overall, advancing our understanding of bone healing mechanisms will continue to drive innovation in fracture management, improving patient outcomes and reducing health care costs.

Introduction

Fracture healing is an intricate biological process that aims to restore biochemical and biomechanical function to an injured bone. In 2019, it was estimated that there were 178 million fractures that occurred globally.1 In the United States, epidemiologists approximate a fracture rate of roughly 4% of the population yearly.2 With the growing elderly population and the increasing prevalence of age-related fractures, this number is projected to increase by at least 10% in the coming decade.2 Notably, 5–10% of these fractures experience delayed or impaired healing, leading to an overall nonunion rate between 2–5%.3,4

These complications are not only distressing for patients and challenging for physicians but they also exert significant pressure on our health care system. Estimating the total financial burden of fractures in the United States is complex. However, just the annual expenses related to treating osteoporotic fragility fractures alone stand at $22 billion, a figure that is anticipated to soar to $95 billion by 2040.5 To further illustrate the economic impact, data from a 2012 US claims database highlighted that the average treatment cost for a long-bone nonunion is more than double to a similar bone that heals expectantly ($102,989 vs. $35,317).6

Hence, optimizing fracture healing serves a dual purpose: It represents the best clinical practice and helps to lower overall cost burden of fracture care. As our understanding of bone regeneration has evolved over the past few decades, the available options to augment fracture healing biology have expanded. This review aims to provide a summary of current therapies designed to optimize fracture repair.

Fracture Healing, Delayed Union, and Nonunion

To understand how available therapies can optimize bone healing, we must understand the biologic processes that occur in fracture healing. It is important to appreciate that if the fracture healing process is successful, new bony regenerate can fully restore biologic and mechanical function without the formation of a fibrous scar.

Bone healing occurs in distinct overlapping phases. First, hours to days after a fracture occurs, is the formation of a hematoma. Blood vessels in the broken bone are disrupted, leading to clot formation which serves as a provisional matrix for the invasion of cells responsible for inflammation and the early stages of healing.7,8 Second is the inflammatory phase which peaks a few days after the fracture occurs. During this phase, inflammatory cells are recruited to the fracture site. These cells remove necrotic tissues and promote angiogenesis and initiate repair by releasing cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor and interleukin-6, which recruit stem cells, chondrocytes, fibroblasts, and osteoblasts.7,8 Third, about 1–2 weeks after injury, is the proliferative phase in which a soft cartilaginous callus bridges the fracture gap. This callus is primarily created by fibroblasts and chondrocytes.8,9 After a few weeks, up to a few months, osteoblasts begin to transform the soft callus into hard bony callus. This hard bone is woven and disorganized but provides strength to stabilize the fracture.7,8 The final phase is the maturation, or remodeling, phase, in which the woven bone is replaced by lamellar bone through the balance of function between osteoblasts and osteoclasts. This can take several months to years.7

Although bone has a robust ability to regenerate, the healing process may fail, causing delayed union or nonunion. There is no standard, universally accepted definition of a delayed union or a nonunion, but there are numerous definitions in the literature. A delayed union can conventionally be defined as a fracture that has not healed after 4 months.10 Alternatively, it can also be defined as a fracture that takes longer than expected to heal, but will heal without the need for additional intervention.11 The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) defines nonunion as a fracture that is at least 9-month-old and has not shown any radiographic evidence of healing for 3 consecutive months.12 Another, more simplified, definition describes it as a fracture that has no capability of healing without further intervention by the treating physician.4 There are various underlying reasons that can contribute to delayed union or the formation of a nonunion. These include, but are certainly not limited to, fracture site, fracture stability, presence of infection, degree of bone loss, degree of soft-tissue injury, patient age and sex, comorbidities such a diabetes and obesity, and social risk factors such as smoking.11,12

Delayed unions and nonunions represent chronic medical conditions that not only negatively affect a patient's overall function but also a patient's psychosocial and economic health. In addition, it poses a significant challenge to the treating physician whose focus must lie on restoring the best possible functional well-being for the patient. Therefore, it is beneficial for treating physicians to understand what technologies are available to prevent nonunions and to treat them if they occur.

Methods of Optimizing Fracture Healing

Autologous bone grafting remains the “gold standard” for augmenting fracture healing because it contains the 3 important properties. It is osteogenic, meaning that it directly provides the cells that produce and maintain bone including skeletal stem cells, osteoblasts, and osteocytes. It is osteoinductive, meaning that it contains certain growth factors that immunomodulatory cytokines that can promote osteogenesis. In addition, autologous bone graft is osteoconductive, meaning that it provides the scaffold to permit bone cell attachment and matrix deposition. The graft's biology and mechanical environment provide a spatial 3-dimensional ingrowth of vasculature, perivascular tissue, and skeletal stem cells.13,14

However, autologous bone grafting, most commonly harvested from the iliac crest, is costly, time consuming, and associated with morbidity including pain, neurovascular injury, hematoma, and fracture.13 Therefore, it is vital for orthopedic surgeons to understand other available technologies that can augment bony healing without the use of autologous bone graft. In this review, we will highlight 2 categories of such available technologies. The first category encompasses local biologic therapies which may be applied directly to the fracture site to enhance the osteogenicity and/or osteoinductivity of the fracture healing environment. The second category encompasses mechanical therapies (Fig. 1). These therapies, known as “bone growth stimulators,” are hypothesized to alter the physical and mechanical environments of a fracture bed, yielding more effective bone healing. Of note, this review will not expand on the numerous available varieties of bone grafts and graft substitutes because this is a topic worthy of separate discussion.

Categorization of technologies for fracture healing augmentation.

Local Biologic Therapies

Concentrated bone marrow aspirate

Bone marrow aspirate is an autologous cell composition containing skeletal stem and progenitor cells (SSPCs) found in bone marrow, which is obtained through needle aspiration, most commonly from the iliac crest. It can also be harvested from the proximal humerus, proximal tibia, or calcaneus, although recent research suggests that aspirate from the iliac crest is the most cell dense and osteogenic.15,16 The SSPCs are osteogenic, self-regenerating, and can produce growth factors to aid in osteoinduction. The potency of the aspirate is increased by centrifugation, which concentrates the number of cells per unit of volume. The resultant product is known as concentrated bone marrow aspirate (cBMA). When compared with unconcentrated bone marrow aspirate, cBMA can have up to a 10-fold increase in the concentration of SSPCs per milliliter.17 Recent research has demonstrated that patient demographics (including age, sex, height, weight, and BMI) are not associated with SSPC concentrations found in cBMA samples taken from the anterior iliac crest.18 However, it is important to note that there are no standard harvesting techniques and numerous processing techniques which certainly can add variability to ultimate cBMA efficacy. There is a paucity of literature comparing differences in preparation and the impact of cBMA effectiveness.19

Although there is no standardized processing technique for cBMA, it is important to have a general understanding of the method (Fig. 2). Once bone marrow aspirate is obtained, it is filtered using a mesh filter and transferred to a conical tube. The isolate then undergoes density gradient centrifugation to separate a buffy coat from red blood cells and acellular fractions. The SSPCs and growth factors are in the buffy coat, which is ultimately extracted as the concentrate.

The harvest, processing, and use of cBMA. This figure illustrates the general method of obtaining and using cBMA. First, bone marrow is aspirated from the desired site. Then, it is transferred to a conical tube and centrifuged. The buffy coat containing all the SSPCs, along with osteogenic growth factors, is isolated and can be injected directly into the desired site or used in conjunction with another type of graft such as cancellous bone chips. cBMA, concentrated bone marrow aspirate; SSPC, skeletal stem and progenitor cells.

cBMA can be applied or injected directly into a fracture site or can be combined with graft or graft substitute to provide structural support. When investigating the impact of cBMA in fracture healing, a systematic review of 9 studies evaluated its use in bone healing.20 Eight of these studies looked at nonunion or delayed union. The studies used cBMA alone or in combination with freeze-dried allograft, bone chips, or demineralized bone matrix with recombinant human BMP-2. All studies reported lower or similar complication rates postoperatively in the cBMA group when compared with control. Bone consolidation and time to bone union was improved in patients receiving cBMA with faster healing rates when even compared with an autograft group. Interestingly, in 1 study analyzed in this review, cBMA sourced from the iliac crest was injected directly into atrophic nonunion sites of the tibia. These interventions ranged from 8–12 months from initial fracture. It was noted that a significantly lower number of progenitor cells were found in cBMA used for fractures that did not reach union. In addition, there was a negative correlation between the time needed to reach union and the concentration of SSPCs.20–22

Although the implementation of cBMA to augment fracture healing is promising, further work is required to optimize the harvesting and preparation technique to maximize efficacy.

Platelet-rich plasma

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous platelet concentration that is suspended in plasma. It is prepared by taking a blood sample from a patient using the standard venipuncture technique. The blood is then centrifuged, separating the blood into 3 distinct layers. The top layer is the plasma, the middle layer (“buffy coat”) contains the white blood cells and the platelets, and the bottom layer contains the red blood cells. The top 2 layers are extracted and placed into a syringe for injection for a desired application.23 It can directly be injected into a fracture site or can be mixed with graft and/or graft substitute. PRP is autologous, safe, and easy to use.21,24 It is important to note that there is no standardization of the harvest technique. Protocols vary widely, yielding varying ratios of leukocytes to platelets in the final preparation, which may alter biologic function.25

Platelets play a vital role in the hematoma, inflammatory, and proliferative stages of fracture healing. In theory, the application of PRP is osteoinductive. Platelets aggregate in the fracture hematoma and subsequently degranulate and release growth factors such as Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), fibroblast growth factor, and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF). These growth factors stimulate the recruitment, proliferation, and differentiation of regenerative cells. Released proteins also promote the fracture healing environment by promoting angiogenesis and functioning as adhesive proteins, clotting factors, fibrinolytic factors, proteases, cytokines, antimicrobial proteins, and membrane glycoproteins.26,27

In recent years, PRP use has gained popularity for the treatment of tendinopathies, knee osteoarthritis, and lateral elbow epicondylitis. However, data in support of its use in fracture healing are sparse. Although there are numerous preclinical in vitro studies that suggest that PRP significantly promotes the proliferation and function of osteoblasts,28 clinical data are mixed. Multiple systematic reviews looking at the benefit of PRP in fracture healing have reached the similar conclusion that PRP may have a clinical role to play in the management of acute and delayed fracture healing; however, results are not yet significant.28–30 This is largely due to the heterogeneity in the study protocols, the techniques of PRP harvest and application, and postoperative evaluation of outcomes. The lack of consistency certainly highlights the need for more standardized randomized control trials investigating the efficacy of PRP in bone healing.

Bone morphogenetic proteins

Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), growth factors which are members of the TGF-β superfamily, are integral in tissue development, repair, and regeneration. To briefly describe their mechanism in bone healing, BMPs are released into the bone matrix by osteoblasts, osteocytes, and endothelial cells. The BMPs then bind to receptors on SSPCs and upregulate osteogenic pathways which in turn lead to osteoblast differentiation and bone formation. Therefore, BMPs are osteoinductive.31

Of the over 20 types of BMPs discovered, BMP-2 and BMP-7 have been studied the most in the setting of fracture healing, because of their commercialization. Previously, both were commercially available as recombinant human BMPs (rhBMP-2 and rhBMP-7) for therapeutic use in bone healing; however, rhBMP-7 has more recently been withdrawn from the market amidst safety and efficacy concerns. rhBMP-2 is currently approved by the FDA for open tibial shaft fractures stabilized with an intramedullary nail within 14 days of injury and for single-level anterior lumbar interbody fusions in patients with degenerative disk disease. However, it is increasingly used for other indications, with reports stating that up to 85% of its use is in fact off-label. There are reports of rhBMP-2 being used for indications such as posterior spinal fusions, segmental bony defects, and nonunions of the femur, tibia, humerus, and forearm.31–34

Recombinant BMP-2 is available as an infused collagen sponge, at a concentration of 1.5-mg/mL, which can be packed into a fracture or fusion site with or without additional bone graft or graft substitute. The size of the collagen sponge and the total amount of rhBMP-2 can be modified based on indication. It is relatively straightforward to use, but it is certainly more expensive than the interventions discussed above, costing almost 30 times more than the materials needed for bone marrow aspiration.35

Studies on the potential of rhBMP-2 in aiding fracture healing are mixed in their outcomes. It has been most notably studied in the acute fracture care setting. In an extensive randomized control trial involving 450 patients with open tibial shaft fractures undergoing irrigation, debridement, and intramedullary unreamed nailing, participants were categorized into either a standard care group or a group receiving standard care alongside a low or high dose of rhBMP-2 infused in a collagen sponge. One year later, the BMP-2 recipients demonstrated notably lower instances of follow-up interventions, an enhanced healing rate, reduced nail fractures, fewer infections, and quicker wound recovery compared with the standard care group.36 However, when another group looked at treating open tibial fractures with rhBMP-2 and reamed nailing, they found no difference in time to union between the standard of care and the rhBMP-2 groups.37

When investigating the role of rhBMP-2 for use in patients requiring secondary procedures because of long-bone nonunion, a meta-analysis from 2022 including 7 studies and 993 patients concluded that the addition of rhBMP-2 revealed statistically significant enhancement of healing for femur, tibia, and humerus nonunited fractures.38 It may be intuitive from such data to believe that nonunion sites simply lack BMP expression. However, this may not be true. Kloen et al.39 performed immunohistochemical localization on tissue from delayed union or nonunion sites and demonstrated even in long-standing nonunion tissue, there is, in fact, ongoing robust BMP signaling. Such research proves that the impact of BMP signaling and modulation are likely more complicated than what we currently understand.

It is important to understand the clinical side effects of using rhBMP-2 to augment fracture healing. Its application is associated with ectopic bone formation, increased local inflammatory reactions, immature osteoclast activation leading to osteolysis and implant loosening, bone cyst formation, wound complications, and infections.40 As with any biologic or implant, the physician must weigh the risks against the benefits of use and be aware of the side effects and their likelihoods before use.

As the orthopedic community continues to research the efficacy, benefits, and safety profile of BMPs, the indications are sure to change with time.

Platelet-derived growth factor

Members of the PDGF family play a significant role in embryonic development and organogenesis, including that of the cranial and axial skeleton. In adults, platelets are a rich source of PDGFs. In the context of fracture healing, PDGFs are mitogenic and chemotactic for SSPCs and can induce their differentiation into fibroblasts and osteoblasts. PDGFs upregulate the expression of VEGF, thereby upregulating the angiogenic response at a fracture site.24,41,42 As a result, PDGF is considered osteoinductive.

There are largely 3 subtypes of PDGF; however, PDGF-BB is considered the universal subtype as it can signal through all PDGF receptor types and has been developed for therapeutic use. Commercially, recombinant PDGF-BB (rhPDGF-BB) is available as combination product with a beta-tricalcium phosphate scaffold.41 This is currently FDA approved for use in ankle and hindfoot fusions. The protein-infused tricalcium phosphate scaffold can be packed into a bony defect, with or without bone graft or bone graft substitute, to promote ankle or hindfoot fusion after appropriate hardware has been placed.35

The evidence for the use of rhPDGF-BB in ankle and hindfoot fusions is favorable. In a prospective randomized control trial of 434 patients undergoing ankle or hindfoot arthrodesis, the investigators assigned patients undergoing ankle and hindfoot fusion to either a group treated with autograft or a group treated with rhPDGF-BB combined with a tricalcium phosphate scaffold. The rhPDGF-BB group had similar fusion rates, less pain, and fewer side effects.43

Although there is a lack of research on rhPDGF-BB in general for fracture healing or nonunion care, the results in ankle and hindfoot fusion are certainly encouraging and clear the path for further research of using rhPDGF-BB in the augmentation of fracture healing.

Mechanical Therapies

Electrical stimulation

Electrical stimulation therapy is based on the idea that different electric potentials can cause bone to either form or resorb. Mechanical stress applied to bone results in the generation of electrical potentials. When there is compression on bone, an electronegative potential is created, which promotes the formation of new bone. When there is a tension force on bone, an electropositive potential is created and bone is resorbed.44,45 Electrical stimulation therapy is done through an external device in which electrodes are placed over the skin or cast/brace at the level of the fracture (Fig. 3). A low-level electric field is emitted from the device creating an electronegative potential which promotes bone healing and consolidation. This is a relatively safe and noninvasive method of augmenting fracture healing. There is no standardized protocol available for the use of electric stimulation, with recommendations ranging from 20 minutes to several hours daily for weeks to months.46 There is a lack of sound evidence available whether increasing the time or intensity of stimulation impacts bone healing.

Electrical stimulation therapy. Electrical stimulation therapy is administered using an external device with electrodes positioned on the skin or over a cast/brace at the fracture site. The device emits a low-level electric field, generating an electronegative potential that supports bone healing and consolidation.

A meta-analysis performed in 2016 included 15 randomized sham-controlled trials to establish the efficacy of electrical stimulation therapy for bone healing. Four trials included patients undergoing spinal fusion, 5 trials included the treatment of delayed or nonunions, and 1 trial monitored patient healing after surgical osteotomy. After analysis of all available data, the authors found a significant improvement in pain in the electric stimulation groups when compared with the sham groups. They also found a significant difference in radiographic nonunion rates by 35% at 12 months after intervention.47 Other meta-analyses have not been able to reach such a conclusion; thus, the efficacy of electrical stimulation in the role of bone healing remains controversial.48 However, electrical stimulation remains a safe and noninvasive tool available to treat acute fractures, delayed unions, and nonunions.

Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound

Another category within mechanical therapies is known as low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS). LIPUS therapy uses pulsed ultrasound waves that generate micromechanical stress at the fracture site. This micromechanical stress then stimulates cellular responses that are vital in fracture healing such as the upregulation of TGF-β and BMPs. This upregulation promotes osteoblast proliferation and bone matrix production. The mechanical stress is also thought to promote angiogenesis at the fracture site and curb excessive inflammation that may inhibit the progression of fracture healing.49–51

LIPUS is a noninvasive therapy that can easily be done by a patient in the comfort of their own home. An ultrasound probe is placed externally over the fracture site and pulsed ultrasound waves are administered. On average, this is done for 20 minutes a day for an average of 5 months.52

Like electrical stimulation, the evidence favoring LIPUS being effective in the promotion of bone healing is conflicting. Earlier reports suggested good efficacy. In 2004, a randomized control study assigned patients undergoing treatment for complex tibial fractures to either a LIPUS group or a sham group. The authors found that the LIPUS-treated group showed statistically significant better healing in multiple outcome measures including time to resolution of pain and full weight bearing in addition to radiographic evidence of union.53

However, a more recent meta-analysis was not as optimistic. This study analyzed data from a total of 26 randomized controlled trials. Seven trials enrolled patients with operatively managed acute fractures, 6 trials enrolled patients with nonoperatively managed acute fractures, 2 trials enrolled patients with stress fractures, 3 trials enrolled patients with nonunions, and 8 trials enrolled patients with osteotomies (5 of which were distraction osteogenesis). After analysis, the authors concluded that LIPUS fails to accelerate return to work, return to full weight bearing, pain reduction. Their data showed that LIPUS did not reduce the need for subsequent operation and failed to accelerate radiographic healing. They do however say that most of these data are applicable only to acute fracture treatment and that there is ultimately no trustworthy evidence available for nonunion treatment or pediatric fractures.54,55 In addition, a large multicenter randomized trial, the TRUST trial, was conducted between 2008 and 2012 investigating whether LIPUS could improve fracture healing in patients with tibial fractures treated with intramedullary nailing. The trial was ultimately terminated because of futility because there was no benefit over placebo demonstrated in interim analysis.56

Regardless of its ultimate efficacy, LIPUS, like electrical stimulation therapy, is safe and noninvasive and may be considered in the treatment of fractures.

Extracorporeal shockwave treatment

Extracorporeal shockwave treatment is another mechanical therapy that is theorized to aid in fracture healing. Historically, a German urologist noted that there were cortical reactions in iliac bone in patients following lithotripsic procedures to break up kidney stones. It was initially theorized that the ultrasonic shockwaves caused microfractures that induced a fracture healing response. However, it is now believed that the shockwaves induce compressive, tensile, and shear forces within tissues and can change the membrane potentials of cells involved in osteogenesis, predisposing these cells to induce bone healing pathways.57 This is called mechanotransduction. In the literature, much like electrical stimulation or LIPUS, there is no standardized protocol to deliver this therapy, and it is largely at the discretion of the treating surgeon. This may be done in the operating room under fluoroscopic guidance or even in the outpatient setting.58

The evidence for shockwave treatment demonstrates mixed results. In 1 prospective randomized trial of 59 acute long-bone fractures, either shockwave therapy or no therapy was provided at the fracture site at the end of surgery. At a follow-up of 6 months, there was a high union rate in the shockwave group than the control group (63% vs. 23%).59

In 2009, a systematic review of 11 studies in which extracorporeal shockwave therapy was used to treat patients with acute fractures, delayed union or nonunions was done. This found that randomized control trials show no effect of shockwave therapy as compared with control for bone healing. It did, however, find that observational studies demonstrated a high union rate in when shockwave was used as adjuvant treatment. However, in the observational studies included, there was no standardization, and the data were admittedly heterogeneous.58 Another systematic review from 2023 concluded that the efficacy of shockwave therapy for fracture healing is “uncertain” as there is no evidence to prove that it is beneficial.60

Conclusion

Successful fracture healing relies on a complex interplay of biologic processes and a conducive mechanical environment. When an issue arises in this relationship, delayed union or nonunion can occur, causing morbidity to patients and a challenge to treating physicians. Currently, we have local biologic therapies that certainly increase the osteogenicity and osteoinductivity of a fracture site and therapies that physically stimulate the fracture healing environment. As our understanding of the science of bone healing improves, the available therapies for augmenting and optimizing fracture healing will continue to evolve and advance.
