High dimensional Mean Test for Temporal Dependent Data
Yuchen Hu, Xiaoyi Wang, Long Feng

Abstract
This paper proposes a novel test method for high-dimensional mean testing regard for the temporal dependent data. Comparison to existing methods, we establish the asymptotic normality of the test statistic without relying on restrictive assumptions, such as Gaussian distribution or M-dependence. Importantly, our theoretical framework holds potential for extension to other high-dimensional problems involving temporal dependent data. Additionally, our method offers significantly reduced computational complexity, making it more practical for large-scale applications. Simulation studies further demonstrate the computational advantages and performance improvements of our test.



Keywords
: Asymptotic normality; high dimensional data; mean testing; temporal dependent

1
Introduction

High-dimensional dependent data have gained increasing prominence in various disciplines, including finance, biomedical sciences, and geological studies. Such data typically exhibit both temporal dependence and correlations among different components, leading to intricate and multifaceted dependence structures. The complexity frequently renders traditional statistical approaches insufficient for effectively analyzing these data. This growing complexity has spurred the development of statistically valid inference procedures designed to address the challenges posed by high-dimensional dependent data. Among these challenges, the high-dimensional mean test is of particular importance.

In this paper, we focus on testing the mean vector of high-dimensional dependent data. Much of the existing literature has predominantly concentrated on methods developed for independent and identically distributed data, with the challenges posed by dependent data receiving comparatively less attention. Broadly speaking, these approaches can be categorized into five types, for a detailed review, see
Huang et al. 2022
. The first category includes modifications of the traditional Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test, as explored by
Chen et al. 2011
;
Li et al. 2020
and others. Since the inversion of the covariance matrix becomes increasingly challenging in high-dimensional settings, alternative strategies have been developed. For example,
Bai and Saranadasa 1996
and
Chen and Qin 2010
examined the
L_{2}
norm to measure the difference between mean vectors, leveraging largedimensional random matrix theory and U-statistic theory, respectively. Numerous other tests based on the
L_{2}
norm have been proposed, including those by
Chen et al. 2019
;
Feng et al. 2016
;
Gregory et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2016
;
Srivastava and Du 2008
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Zhong et al. 2013
. These tests are generally effective when signals are dense and relatively small but tend to lose power when dealing with sparse signals. To address the limitations of
L_{2}
norm-based methods in detecting sparse and strong signals,
L_{\infty}
norm-based methods have been proposed by
Cai et al. 2014
;
Chang et al. 2017
;
Xue and Yao 2020
. These methods provide higher power in scenarios where the alternative hypothesis involves sparse signals. Furthermore, adaptive methods that combine the strengths of both
L_{2}
norm-based and
L_{\infty}
norm-based approaches have been introduced by
He et al. 2021
and
Xu et al. 2016
. They proposed a relatively robust testing method by combining different
L_{q}
norms, which is adaptable to alternative hypotheses with unknown levels of signal sparsity. Finally, the fifth category of methods involves random projections, as studied by
Srivastava et al. 2016
;
Thulin 2014
and others.

In contrast to the extensive research on independent data, relatively few studies have focused on testing high-dimensional means in temporally dependent data. Motivated by
Bai and Saranadasa 1996
;
Ayyala et al. 2017
considers a M-dependent strictly stationary Gaussian process, and examined the
L_{2}
norm of the sample average and investigated its asymptotic normality. In addition,
Zhang et al. 2025
proposed a band-excluded U-statistic by removing the inner products between pairs of observations that are sufficiently distant from each other in the construction of the
L_{2}
norm-based test statistic. They also established the asymptotic normality of this test statistic with
\beta
-mixing dependence. However, their method incurs a substantial computational cost, primarily due to the use of U-statistic-based estimators for estimating the asymptotic variance of the test statistic.

In this paper, we proposed a test statistic by removing all the inner products of the observations from the
L_{2}
norm of the sample average. Compared to the method of
Ayyala et al. 2017
, our approach can enhance higher power, as previously demonstrated by
Chen and Qin 2010
under the independent setting. Furthermore, we demonstrate the asymptotic normality of the test statistic without relying on the stringent assumptions of Gaussian distribution or
M
-dependent structure among the observations. In our investigation of the theoretical properties, we assume that the data exhibit a linear dependence structure across observations. The primary contribution of this paper lies in the technical proof methodology. Importantly, our theoretical framework holds potential for extension to other high-dimensional problems involving temporal dependent data. Additionally, the computational burden of our method is significantly lower than that of
Zhang et al. 2025
, as will be illustrated in the simulation studies in Section 3. Through this work, we aim to contribute to the understanding of mean testing in high-dimensional dependent data, offering a powerful and efficient approach that is better suited to handling the dependencies among components and observations.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we introduce the test statistic, and its asymptotic distribution under the null hypothesis. We conduct extensive simulation studies in Section 3. An empirical application is provided in Section 4. Section 5 contains some discussions. All technical details are included in the Appendix.

2
Our Methods

2.1
Test statistic

We consider a
p
-dimensional stationary time series
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=(X_{t,1},\dots,X_{t,p})^{\top}\}_{t=1}^{n}
with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and auto-covariance structure given by
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h}=\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{X}_{t+h})
for
h=-(n-1),\dots,(n-1)
. We are interested in testing

\displaystyle H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\mu}\neq\boldsymbol{0}.

(1)

In this paper, we propose the following test statistic

\displaystyle T_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(n\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\bigg),

(2)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
.
The problem of testing high-dimensional location parameters has been extensively studied for independent data, with notable contributions including
Bai and Saranadasa 1996
,
Srivastava 2009
,
Chen and Qin 2010
, et al. Recently, the literature has been extended to the high-dimensional settings, with works such as
Ayyala et al. 2017
and
Zhang et al. 2025
proposing test statistics that estimate the
L_{2}
-type distance
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}
to quantify the discrepancy between the null and alternative hypotheses. Below, we provide a detailed mutual comparison of our proposed method with those of
Ayyala et al. 2017
and
Zhang et al. 2025
.

Ayyala et al. 2017
were among the first to address this problem in the context of high-dimensional dependent data. They assumed the data follows the high-dimensional
M
-dependent Gaussian stationary processes and proposed a test statistic based on the squared Euclidean norm of the sample mean vector, namely,
T_{\tiny{\mbox{APR}}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}
. To address bias induced by the autocovariance structure, they constructed an unbiased estimator for
\mathbb{E}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n})
and developed a ratio-consistent estimator for the asymptotic variance of the test statistic. Furthermore, they established the asymptotic normality under the null hypothesis as both sample size
n
and the dimension
p
tend to infinity, by assuming
p=O(n)
,
M=O(n^{1/8})
, and
\operatorname{tr}\{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{a}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{b}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{c}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{d}\}=o\{\operatorname{tr}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}^{2})\}
for any
a,b,c,d=0,\dots,M
, where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
denotes the covariance matrix of
\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}
multiplied by
n
. A key limitation, however, is that their asymptotic theory relies heavily on the Gaussian process assumption. By relaxing the
M
-dependent Gaussian assumption,
Zhang et al. 2025
introduced a band-excluded U-statistic (BEU) to estimate the
L_{2}
norm of the population mean vector. Their test statistic is
T_{\tiny{\mbox{ZCQ}}}={(n-b)(n-b+1)}^{-1}\sum_{|t_{1}-t_{2}|\geq b}\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{2}}
, where
b
is an exclusion bandwidth parameter. By systematically excluding cross-products of data vectors at temporally close points, their approach mitigates bias caused by long-range temporal dependencies. They studied the asymptotic theory of the BEU-statistic under a linear innovation model with
\beta
-mixing conditions. They considered two regimes of decay: exponential, which allows
p
to grow exponentially with
n
, and polynomial, which requires
p=O(n^{\tau})
for some
\tau>0
. To estimate the asymptotic variance of the test statistic under the null hypothesis, they applied a similar band-exclusion technique and employed a kernel-smoothing technique with a quadratic spectral kernel, though this involves the substantial computational cost and nuisance bandwidth parameter.

Relative to
Ayyala et al. 2017
, we propose a test statistic by removing the terms of diagonal cross-product terms to enhance the power performance, which is inspired by
Chen and Qin 2010
. We adapt this insight to the dependent data framework. More fundamentally, our method diverges by relaxing the restrictive
M
-dependent Gaussian assumption to a more general linear process framework.
Compared to the method of
Zhang et al. 2025
, the key difference lies in the estimation of the asymptotic variance. We leverage a sample-splitting technique for the variance estimation of the asymptotic variance of the test statistic under a linear process framework. This fundamental difference circumvents the need for selecting the nuisance kernel smoothing parameter required in
Zhang et al. 2025
. Furthermore, by departing from the U-statistic formulation, our method achieves a significant reduction in computational burden while maintaining theoretical consistency, rendering it more practical for large-scale applications.

2.2
Main results

Define
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
, the covariance matrix of
\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}
multiplied by the sample size,

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}+2\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}\Big(1-\frac{h}{n}\Big)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h},

and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}+2\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h}
.
For investigating the asymptotic normality of
T_{n}
, we assume the following assumptions in the analysis.

Assumption 1.

For
t=1,\dots,n
,
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}
, where
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}=(Y_{t,1},\dots,Y_{t,p})^{\top}
with
Y_{t,j}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}Z_{t-k,j}
, and
\{Z_{t,j}\}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with
\mathbb{E}[Z_{t,j}]=0
,
\mathbb{E}[Z_{t,j}^{2}]=1
and
\mathbb{E}[Z_{t,j}^{4}]=\mu_{4}<\infty
.

Assumption 2.

There exist two positive constants
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{2}\leq C_{0}
and
\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})/p\geq C_{1}
. Here
\|\cdot\|_{2}
stands for the spectral norm of a matrix.

Assumption 3.

(i)
b_{n}=o(n^{-5})
, i.e.
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}n^{5}b_{n}=0
; (ii)
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}=s\neq 0
; (iii)
p=O(n)
and
p\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Assumption 1 assume the linear process model of
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
, which is widely used in time series analysis (
Brockwell and Davis 2013
;
Zhang et al. 2018
). This linear process model cover the well known MA(
q
) model and AR(1) model. Under the Assumption 1, we have
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h}=a_{h}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, where
a_{h}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}b_{k+h}
. Assumption 2 implies that the correlation between the variables cannot be excessively large. Assumptions 3-(i) and 3-(ii) ensure that the temporal correlation becomes negligible as
n
increases. Assumption 3-(iii) allows the dimension
p
to increase to infinity as
n
approaches infinity, which is a significant departure from the assumption that
n
approaches infinity before
p
does in
Breitung and Das 2005
.

Based on simple calculations, we have

\mathbb{E}(T_{n})=\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}(1-\frac{h}{n})\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h})
under
H_{0}
. Let
M=\lceil\min(n,p)^{1/8}\rceil
, we replace
\mathbb{E}(T_{n})
by

\displaystyle\mu_{n}=\sum_{h=1}^{M}\Big(1-\frac{h}{n}\Big)\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h}).

(3)

Due to the multiple occurrences of fourth moments, the variance of
T_{n}
has a complicated structure. Define

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2}).

(4)

To address this issue, we use
\sigma^{2}_{n}
to approximate
\operatorname{Var}(T_{n})
under
H_{0}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Under Assumptions 1–4 and
H_{0}
, if
M=\lceil\min(n,p)^{1/8}\rceil
, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle(T_{n}-\mu_{n})/\sqrt{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\xrightarrow{\ \textup{d}\ }N(0,1),

(5)

where
\mu_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}
is defined in (
3
) and (
4
), respectively.

In order to construct the test statistic that can be used for the hypothesis test (
1
), we need to estimate
\mu_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}
. We consider estimating
\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h})
with

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{h})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n-h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t+h}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}),

and naturally propose the plug in estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{h=1}^{M}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{n-h}\frac{n-h}{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t+h}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n})

(6)

for
\mu_{n}
.
We next find a ratio consistent estimator of
\sigma_{n}^{2}
.
Ayyala et al. 2017
introduced an unbiased estimator of
\sigma_{n}^{2}
. Their approach involves constructing a specific set of subscripts, ensuring that only pairwise correlations exist among the four samples in each component of the estimator. However, the consistency of their method is demonstrated under
M
-dependent Gaussian assumption. Building on this,
Zhang et al. 2018
propose a new estimator which is suitable for some special correlation structure. Motivated by
Zhang et al. 2018
, we select the datapair
(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{X}_{s})
that are sufficiently separated in time. Specifically, we require
|s-t|\geq\lfloor n/2\rfloor\gg M
, which make sure
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{s}
are approximately independent under the assumed dependence structure. This allows us to use two non-overlapping segments of the series to estimate
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h_{2}}
independently. Thus, we propose

\displaystyle S_{h_{1},h_{2}}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{[n/2]-h_{2}}\displaystyle\sum_{s=t+[n/2]}^{n-h_{2}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n})(\boldsymbol{X}_{t+h_{1}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s+h_{2}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n})}{(n-h_{2}/2-\frac{3}{2}[n/2]+1/2)([n/2]-h_{2})}.

(7)

to estimate
\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h_{1}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h_{2}})
.
It follows that

\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(S_{0,0}+2\sum_{h_{1}=1}^{M}S_{h_{1},0}+2\sum_{h_{2}=1}^{M}S_{0,h_{2}}+4\sum_{h_{1}=1}^{M}\sum_{h_{2}=1}^{M}S_{h_{1},h_{2}}\bigg).

(8)

Theorem 2.2
.

Under Assumptions 1–4 and
H_{0}
, if
M=\lceil\min(n,p)^{1/8}\rceil
, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle(T_{n}-\hat{\mu}_{n})/\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}\xrightarrow{\ \textup{d}\ }N(0,1).

(9)

According to Theorem
2.2
, we reject the null hypothesis when
(T_{n}-\hat{\mu}_{n})/\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}}>z_{\alpha}
, where
z_{\alpha}
is the upper
\alpha
-quantile of the standard normal distribution
N(0,1)
for a level
\alpha
test.

3
Simulation Study

In this section, we conduct a comparative analysis between our proposed method, referred to as
T_{n}
, and two established test approaches: the method developed by
Srivastava 2009
for independent data, denoted as
T_{\tiny\mbox{SRI}}
, and the approach introduced by
Zhang et al. 2025
for
\beta
-mixing dependent data, denoted as
T_{\tiny\mbox{ZCQ}}
. We exclude the test proposed by
Ayyala et al. 2017
from this comparison due to the Gaussian
M
-dependent assumption, as well as its substantial computational demands.

We consider the following generating model

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\sum\limits_{h=0}^{q}\boldsymbol{A}_{h}\boldsymbol{Z}_{t-h},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{A}_{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{A}_{q}
are
p\times p
symmetric matrices which determine the autocovariance structure of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
, and

Z_{t,j}
are independently and identically distributed from
N(0,1)
and
Gamma(4,2)-2
. Besides that, we set
\boldsymbol{A}_{0}=\boldsymbol{I}_{p}
, and for
h=1,2
,

\boldsymbol{A}_{h,i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{\phi_{1}}{h}&|i-j|=0,\\
\dfrac{\phi_{1}}{h|i-j|^{2}}&1\leq|i-j|\leq pw,\\
0&|i-j|>pw,\end{array}\right.\quad\quad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&|i-j|=0,\\
\dfrac{\phi_{2}}{|i-j|^{2}}&1\leq|i-j|\leq pw,\\
0&|i-j|>pw,\end{array}\right.

where
(w,\phi_{1},\phi_{2})=(0.5,0.3,0.2)
. Here,
w
represents the sparsity and
(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})
control the strength of dependence between the variables. For
h>2
, we simply consider
\boldsymbol{A}_{h,i,j}=e^{-2h}
.
In this paper, we choose
q=0,2,n-1
in model (
10
) to mimic the independent and dependent cases. To apply our proposed method effectively, we recommend taking
M=\lceil\min(n,p)^{1/8}\rceil
. For the comparison, we followed the recommendation outlined in
Zhang et al. 2025
and take the lag window of their method as
\lfloor n/10\rfloor
. We set the nominal significance level
\alpha=0.05
, and all the simulation results are based on 1000 replications.

Table
1
summarizes the empirical sizes of the three tests under the null hypothesis. When
q=0
, indicating temporal independent, all testing procedures efficiently control the empirical type I error across a wide range of sample sizes and dimensions. However, under dependency setting, that is
q=2
and
q=n-1
, the
T_{\tiny\mbox{SRI}}
test exhibits notable deviation from the nominal level, which is anticipated given their inability to account for the dependent structure. Compared to our method, the
T_{\tiny\mbox{ZCQ}}
test shows a slight deviation from the nominal significance level in empirical type I error evaluation. Overall, our proposed test
T_{n}
demonstrates superior robustness in maintaining the nominal size across different dependency scenarios.

Real Data Analysis

In this section, we apply our proposed method to a pricing problem in finance, specifically exploring whether the weekly excess return of any asset is zero on average. Denote
X_{tj}=R_{tj}-\mbox{rf}_{t}
by the excess return of the
j
th asset at time
t
for
t=1,\dots,n
and
j=1,\dots,p
, where
R_{tj}
is the return on asset
j
during period
t
and
\mbox{rf}_{t}
is the risk-free return rate of all asset during period
t
. We study the zero-factor pricing model

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\quad t=1,\dots,n,

where
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=(X_{t1},\dots,X_{tp})^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{p})^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=(\xi_{t1},\dots,\xi_{tp})^{\top}
is the zero-mean error vector. We consider the null hypothesis test
H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{0}
, which means that, the return rate of any asset
R_{tj}
is equal to the risk-free return rate
\mbox{rf}_{t}
on average.
We analyzed the weekly return rates of stocks in the S&P 500 index from January 14, 2005, to November 24, 2023. The weekly data were calculated using the stock prices on Fridays. After deleting missing values, we obtained a dataset with
p=393
securities across
n=888
observations.

First, we examine the presence of time series dependence in the observed sequences of our dataset. To achieve this, we apply the Box-Pierce test, a traditional method for detecting autocorrelation. The p-value histogram from the Box-Pierce test for the U.S. datasets is shown in Figure
1
–(a). We observe a significant number of p-values below 0.05, indicating that some observed sequences may exhibit autocorrelation. Furthermore, we employ the max-type high-dimensional white noise test procedure, as described by
Feng et al. 2022
, to assess whether the excess return are white noise. This test produces a p-value of zero, strongly suggesting time dependence among the sequences. Consequently, test procedures that assume independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random vectors may not perform well and could yield inaccurate results.

We employed a rolling window procedure with a window length of
m=260
weeks (approximately 5 years) to evaluate the performance of our proposed
T_{n}
test and
T_{\mbox{\tiny{ZCQ}}}
test. Figure
1
–(b) displays the p-values for each test procedure over time, with the black horizontal line representing the 0.05 significance level.
In the first nine years and the last eight years, both
T_{n}
test and
T_{\mbox{\tiny{ZCQ}}}
test failed to reject the null hypothesis, suggesting that the weekly excess returns of any asset may be zero on average during these periods. However, during the ﬁve-year period in the middle, both the
T_{n}
test and
T_{\mbox{\tiny{ZCQ}}}
test frequently reject the null hypothesis, indicating that the weekly excess returns of any asset were not zero on average in the interval. It is noteworthy that our proposed test demonstrated a stronger rejection of the null hypothesis compared to the
T_{\mbox{\tiny{ZCQ}}}
test, indicating higher sensitivity and potentially greater accuracy in detecting deviations from zero weekly excess returns.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduce a novel mean test method for high-dimensional data, accommodating both cross-sectional and time series dependencies. The asymptotic normality of the proposed test statistic is established as both
p
and
n
approach infinity concurrently. The tests presented in this paper follow a sum-type procedure, exhibiting robust performance under dense alternatives when the time series of a majority of variables are depart from zero. An intriguing challenge is to develop a max-type test procedure that performs well under sparse alternatives, where the time series of a handful of variables are depart from zero.

Moreover, several studies, including
Feng et al. 2024
and
Chen et al. 2024
, have explored the asymptotic independence between sum-type and max-type test procedures. Therefore, it would be fascinating to investigate the asymptotic independence between these two types of procedures in the context of high-dimensional unit root testing.

Important Lemmas

We first establish the following lemmas which will be frequently used in our subsequent proofs.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{W}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a_{t}\boldsymbol{Z}_{t}
, where
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\left|a_{t}\right|<\infty
, there exists a positive constant
\tau_{1}\geq 3
such that for all
p\times p
positive semi-definite matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{W})^{2}\right]\leq\tau_{1}\left[\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{W})\right]^{2}.

Proof

Let
[B]_{ij}
denotes the entry in row
i
, column
j
of
\boldsymbol{B}
. Since
\boldsymbol{B}
is positive semi-definite matrix,
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}
, where
\boldsymbol{D}
is an orthogonal matrix and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{p})
,
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}\geq 0
. Let
\boldsymbol{Z}_{t}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{t}
, we can calculate their second and fourth moments

\mathbb{E}[{Z^{\prime}_{t,j}}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{p}[D]_{ij}Z_{t,i}\right]^{2}=1,

\mathbb{E}[{Z^{\prime}_{t,j}}^{4}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{p}[D]_{ij}Z_{t,i}\right]^{4}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}[D]_{ij}^{4}(\mu_{4}-3)+3\leq\mu_{4}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{W})\right]=\sum_{r=1}^{p}[B]_{r,r}\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}Z_{i,r}\bigg)^{2}\right]=\sum_{r=1}^{p}[B]_{r,r}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{B}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{W})^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{4}\leq p}[B]_{r_{1},r_{2}}[B]_{r_{3},r_{4}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{4}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}Z_{i,r_{j}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{p}[B]_{rr}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}Z_{i,r}\right]^{4}

\displaystyle+\sum_{r\neq s}\left(2[B]_{r,s}^{2}+[B]_{r,r}[B]_{s,s}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}Z_{i,r}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}Z_{i,s}\bigg)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{p}[B]_{r,r}^{2}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}^{4}\mu_{4}+\sum_{i\neq j}3a_{i}^{2}a_{j}^{2}\bigg)+\sum_{r\neq s}\left(2[B]_{r,s}^{2}+[B]_{r,r}[B]_{s,s}\right)\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{B})^{2}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}^{4}\mu_{4}+\sum_{i\neq j}3a_{i}^{2}a_{j}^{2}\bigg)+\left(2\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{B}^{2})+\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{B})^{2}\right)\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\mu_{4}+6)\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\bigg)^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{B})^{2}=(\mu_{4}+6)\left[\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{W})\right]^{2}.

This completes the proof by taking
\tau_{1}=\mu_{4}+6
.

\blacksquare

Lemma B.2
.

Under Assumptions 1–3, for all
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}

\tau_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\mu_{4}+1)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}^{2}b_{k+h}^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})+\sum_{k,l=0,k\neq l}^{\infty}(b_{k}b_{l+h}b_{l}b_{k+h}+b_{k}^{2}b_{l+h}^{2})\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg\{(\mu_{4}+1)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}^{2}b_{k+h}^{2}+\left(\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}b_{k}b_{k+h}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}b_{l+h}^{2}\right)\bigg\}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\mu_{4}+3)\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}^{2}\bigg)^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}).

It completes the proof with
\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2}\right)=\bigg(\sum\limits_{h_{1}=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}b_{k+h_{1}}\bigg)^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})
and
b_{n}=o(n^{-5})
.

\blacksquare

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}\leq[n/2]-s\\
i_{1}+[n/2]\leq j_{1}\leq n-s\end{subarray}}\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{2}\leq[n/2]-s\\
i_{2}+[n/2]\leq j_{2}\leq n-s\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j_{1}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i_{1}+r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i_{2}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j_{2}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i_{2}+r}^{\top})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(n\operatorname{tr}^{3}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+n^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})+\operatorname{tr}^{3}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(np^{3/2}+n^{2}p^{1/2})\operatorname{tr}^{3/2}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}).

Since
\mathbb{E}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n})=O(n^{-1}p^{1/2}\operatorname{tr}^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}))
, These implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{M}\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq[n/2]-s\\
i+[n/2]\leq j\leq n-s\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i+r}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}}{(n-s/2-\frac{3}{2}[n/2]+1/2)([n/2]-s)}=O_{p}(n^{-4}pM^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{2}))=o_{p}(\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})).

Similarly, we can verify

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{M}\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq[n/2]-s\\
i+[n/2]\leq j\leq n-s\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}}{(n-s/2-\frac{3}{2}[n/2]+1/2)([n/2]-s)}=O_{p}((n^{-3/2}p^{1/2}+n^{-2}p)M\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}))=o_{p}(\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{M}\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq[n/2]-s\\
i+[n/2]\leq j\leq n-s\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}}{(n-s/2-\frac{3}{2}[n/2]+1/2)([n/2]-s)}=O_{p}(n^{-2}pM^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}))=o_{p}(\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{M}\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq[n/2]-s\\
i+[n/2]\leq j\leq n-s\end{subarray}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{n}}{(n-s/2-\frac{3}{2}[n/2]+1/2)([n/2]-s)}=O_{p}(n^{-2}pM^{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}))=o_{p}(\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})).

\blacksquare
