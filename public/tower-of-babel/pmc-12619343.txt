Precision medicine exploration of postpartum rectus abdominis muscle separation: from basic research to clinical practice
Authors: MengMeng Huang, Zhihong Huang, Hanzhang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostpartum diastasis recti abdominis (PDRA), characterized by pathological separation of the rectus abdominis muscles, affects 30%-60% of women, with many cases persisting beyond 6 months postpartum and having significant impacts on musculoskeletal function and quality of life. This review synthesizes recent advancements across molecular pathogenesis, precision diagnostics, and therapeutic innovations. Key findings reveal that PDRA pathophysiology involves pregnancy-induced hormonal fluctuations, extracellular matrix remodeling, and mechanical overloading, necessitating individualized management. Artificial intelligence (AI)-enhanced ultrasonography achieves superior diagnostic accuracy (Dice score: 85.93%, r = 0.944 vs. manual measurements) over traditional methods, while minimally invasive techniques including laparoscopic “Slim-Mesh” repair demonstrate reduced recurrence rates (< 10%) and enhanced recovery compared to open surgery. Biomaterial integration (such as synthetic and bioabsorbable meshes) promotes anatomical reconstruction. Intelligent rehabilitation systems combining surface electromyography and wearable sensors improve inter-rectus distance (IRD) reduction through real-time biomechanical feedback. Critical challenges persist regarding the lack of standardized diagnostic criteria, long-term outcome validation of emerging technologies (> 5-year follow-up gaps), and optimization of AI-driven predictive models. We propose a potential precision medicine framework that integrates dynamic imaging, molecular profiling, and patient-specific interventions to optimize functional recovery and address heterogeneity in PDRA management.Keywords: Postpartum diastasis recti abdominis, Precision medicine, Minimally invasive surgery, Artificial intelligence, Bioabsorbable mesh

Introduction

Postpartum diastasis recti abdominis (PDRA) is a common clinical condition defined by pathological separation of the rectus abdominis muscles along the linea alba, frequently seen during the perinatal period [1]. This condition manifests as weakened abdominal wall integrity, leading to biomechanical abnormalities in trunk stability and functional deficiencies in daily activities [2]. Clinical investigations suggest that PDRA progression correlates with gestational age, with a 33% incidence at 21 weeks gestation that increases to almost 80% prevalence in the umbilical/upper abdominal region during late pregnancy. Postpartum follow-up data show persistent separation above 2.0 cm in 30%−60% of cases at 6 months postpartum, with 20% developing chronic symptoms affecting musculoskeletal function [3, 4].

Current therapeutic approaches for PDRA encompass two principal modalities: non-invasive rehabilitation and surgical correction. Conventional interventions including neuromuscular electrical stimulation, abdominal bracing, and targeted core stabilization exercises demonstrate variable efficacy, particularly in cases with separation distances exceeding 3 cm [5]. In recent years, surgical interventions have emerged as a pivotal approach for addressing PDRA, driven by advancements in minimally invasive techniques that overcome the limitations of conventional therapeutic approaches in prevention or cure. Nevertheless, current treatment paradigms confront substantial challenges, including the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria and therapeutic guidelines, as well as inadequate development of tailored intervention strategies to address heterogeneous clinical presentations [6].

The underlying physiological underpinnings of PDRA include complex biological processes as well as multivariate interactions. A number of studies show that gestational hormonal changes, decreased tissue elasticity of abdominal wall, and increased intra-abdominal pressure are collectively responsible for the pathophysiology of PDRA [7]. Recent molecular and cellular discoveries have clarified potential molecular targets, enabling the creation of customized preventive and treatment plans through precise therapeutic approaches [8].

The diagnosis and treatment of PDRA remains a topic of significant clinical interest, yet considerable variation exists in diagnostic criteria, the surgical indications, and the rehabilitation regimens that currently in use exist [9]. The lack of uniform long-term follow-up data and validated assessment methods seriously impedes the refinement of evidence-based treatment plans because of the problems that have to be resolved [10].

Recent studies highlight the possibility of data-driven analytics, precision individualized rehabilitation training, and artificial intelligence (AI) for advancing diagnostic and therapeutic methods for PDRA [11]. These approaches are grounded in precision medicine, which involves to tailoring medical decisions, treatments, and interventions to individual patients based on their age, biomarker, phenotypic, or psychosocial characteristics. Nonetheless significant gaps remain in current studies, including the development of robust, individualized prediction models, comprehensive frameworks for long-term predictive assessment, and the optimization of minimally invasive interventions [12].

This review aims to provide a thorough synthesis of current developments in PDRA management, with a special focus on its pathophysiological changes and individualized clinical diagnosis and treatment plans. We propose a novel potential precision medicine framework that integrates molecular profiling, AI-enhanced diagnostics, minimally invasive surgery, and intelligent rehabilitation. We critically examine the limitations of contemporary therapeutic approaches by reviewing research findings from the last five years. The analysis provides evidence-based insights and practical suggestions to help optimize clinical decision-making and direct future research in this developing area.

Methods

Data sources and searches

This narrative review aims to synthesize current evidence regarding PDRA through a comprehensive literature search across major databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search string was ((Postpartum diastasis recti abdominis) OR (PDRA) OR (diastasis recti abdominis)) AND ((Basic Research) OR (Personalized Medicine) OR (Individualized Medicine) OR (Clinical) OR (Surgery) OR (Exercise)) for articles published between Jan 1, 2020 and May 1, 2025. The investigation focuses on identifying novel and sophisticated developments in the field of postpartum abdominal muscle separation and reviewing current research findings. The initial search yielded 234 publications. Primary focus is placed on interdisciplinary and multivariate creative research that advances individual diagnostic and therapeutic technologies while explaining the pathophysiology of the diseases. The literature screening process involved three sequential steps: (1) initial screening of titles and abstracts to exclude clearly irrelevant studies based on predefined criteria (non-postpartum populations, non-diastasis conditions, or non-precision medicine approaches); (2) full-text review of potentially eligible articles to assess study eligibility and extract data on study design, population, interventions, outcomes, and precision medicine components, and (3) manual supplementation through backward/forward citation tracking to identify additional relevant studies that might have been missed in database searches, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the literature while maintaining methodological rigor.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The types of studies eligible for inclusion were only original articles (including retrospective, prospective, randomized clinical trials, Meta analysis and systematic review). We applied the PCC framework to define scope and prespecify inclusion/exclusion criteria. Population: Inclusion: Postpartum women with diastasis recti abdominis (PDRA) at any time after delivery, confirmed by clinical examination or imaging (e.g., ultrasound, CT or MRI). Mixed samples were eligible if postpartum PDRA data were separable. Exclusion: Pregnant populations without postpartum data; non-postpartum populations (men, nulliparous women) unless postpartum subgroup data were extractable; pediatric or adolescent samples. Concept༚Inclusion: Studies on PDRA epidemiology and pathophysiology; medical measurement (e.g., ultrasound-based inter-recti distance, elastography); conservative management (exercise therapy, physiotherapy, bracing or taping); surgical management (minimally invasive or open); and studies linking advanced technologies to individualized or precision care (e.g., imaging-based stratification, biomarkers, risk models, machine-learning decision support). Exclusion: Abdominal wall or aesthetic studies without confirmed PDRA; pure engineering or algorithm papers lacking clinical PDRA outcomes; hernia-only populations without PDRA. Context༚Inclusion: Clinical (inpatient or outpatient), rehabilitation, or community settings, any country or region. Study designs and evidence types: Inclusion: Randomized controlled trials, nonrandomized intervention studies, cohort and case–control studies, cross-sectional studies, qualitative and mixed-methods studies, implementation studies. High-quality systematic reviews and meta-analyses were used to contextualize findings. Case series were eligible when methods and outcomes were adequately reported. Exclusion: Single-patient case reports, editorials, letters, viewpoints, protocols, and conference abstracts with insufficient data. Time window and language: Records published from Jan 1, 2020 to May 1, 2025; written in English.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data including bibliographic details; country; design; sample size; participant characteristics; PDRA definitions and measurement protocols (e.g., ultrasound landmarks, IRD cut-points); intervention/exposure details (exercise protocols, physiotherapy modalities, bracing or taping; surgical techniques including minimally invasive vs. open); comparators; outcomes (inter-recti distance, function, pain and symptoms, health-related quality of life, adverse events and recurrence); follow-up duration; and precision-medicine elements (e.g., stratification variables, biomarkers, imaging phenotypes, risk models). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

While this review integrates data from diverse sources, it should be acknowledged that inherent limitations—including potential publication bias and heterogeneity in study designs—may influence the generalizability of our conclusions. As a narrative review it may not sufficiently distinguish the strength of evidence across studies. We hope this review can be used to make further clinical decisions and suggest future research directions to improve patient-specific care protocols.

Results

Molecular and cellular insights into pathological mechanisms

Diastasis Recti Abdominis (DRA) arises from intricate anatomical and biological remodeling processes, particularly involving structural alterations of the rectus abdominis muscles and their associated fasciae [2, 13].Classical anatomical observations indicate that the rectus abdominis muscles are typically interconnected via the robust linea alba. However, in DRA cases, the linea alba undergoes structural thinning and widening, accompanied by compromised integrity and tension, leading to irreversible separation of the rectus muscles [7, 14]. Beyond anatomical changes, DRA progression involves multifaceted molecular regulation, with hormonal fluctuations, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and biomechanical adaptations serving as critical determinants [15].

During pregnancy, synergistic actions of estrogen and progesterone induce metabolic reorganization of collagen and elastin in skeletal muscle, significantly diminishing the mechanical strength and elasticity of myofascial tissues [16]. Estrogen-mediated regulation of muscular architecture operates through multilayered biological pathways [17]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that 17-β estradiol modulates skeletal muscle growth and remodeling via the PI3K-Akt signaling cascade, a mechanism potentially central to PDRA pathogenesis [18]. Concurrently, G protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER) plays a pivotal role in cellular stress responses [19], suggesting its involvement in hormone-mediated biomechanical adaptations. Notably, interindividual variability in hormonal sensitivity—manifested through differential expression of sex hormone receptors and signaling efficiency [20]—may explain the heterogeneous incidence of PDRA among gravid populations.

At the cellular level, PDRA is defined by significant ECM remodeling, driven by integrin signaling pathways, intercellular junctional proteins, and inflammatory mediators [21]. Mechanotransduction and stress redistribution mechanisms are important biomechanical underpinnings of fascial disruption, as sustained mechanical loading during gestation induces progressive ECM reorganization [22]. Hormonally induced collagen and elastin metabolic changes further increase tissue susceptibility to biomechanical stresses, establishing a synergistic interplay between endocrine and mechanical variables in DRA etiology [23].

Importantly, PDRA involves multifaceted, fascial restructuring that includes changed collagen fiber orientation, packing density, and ECM composition in addition to muscular diastasis. The response of the organism to gestational biomechanical needs is shown by this adaptive remodeling. It is important to further clarify the molecular and cellular pathways is critical for the development of focused therapeutic approaches.

Diagnosis and individualized assessment

Accurate diagnosis and comprehensive evaluation of Diastasis Recti Abdominis (DRA) are important for developing effective therapeutic strategies. Conventional diagnostic approaches primarily rely on ultrasound imaging, which can be limited by operator-dependent subjectivity and measurement inaccuracies. The combination of AI and multidimensional assessment technology offers new opportunities for improving diagnostic precision and enabling individualized evaluations. This section reviews current PDRA diagnostic criteria and methods, compares the advantages and limitations of conventional versus emerging imaging techniques, and proposes a framework for multimodal assessment protocols.

Imaging and AI-Driven diagnostics

Diagnosis of PDRA predominantly hinges on inter-rectus distance (IRD) quantification. Conventional approaches including palpation and caliper measurements, operate with a straightforward method but have a considerable degree of subjectivity and variability that highly dependent on the examiner [24, 25]. Due to its high spatial resolution and noninvasive nature, ultrasound imaging has become the gold standard [26], allowing accurate IRD measurements to assess the degree of separation at umbilical, supraumbilical, and infraumbilical levels [13]. The rapid advancement of AI is revolutionizing conventional imaging diagnostics. While ultrasound remains indispensable for real-time, noninvasive evaluation of rectus abdominis separation, its analytical limitations—particularly operator variability in measurements—are now being addressed through technological innovations.

In a study by Wang et al. [24]., 675 ultrasound images of the rectus abdominis were collected from 94 postpartum women and divided into training (448 images), validation (86 images), and test (141 images) sets. Three segmentation models—U-Net, UNet++, and Res-UNet—were evaluated for their performance in segmenting the rectus abdominis and measuring the inter-rectus distance. The models were evaluated using the Dice score, which measures the degree of overlap between two sets of data, with a higher value indicating better segmentation accuracy. The Res-UNet model outperformed the other two, achieving the highest Dice score of 85.93% ± 0.26%. The mean difference between the inter-rectus distances measured using Res-UNet-generated segmentation masks and those manually measured by experienced sonographers was only 3.44 ± 0.16 mm. Moreover, the two sets of measurements were highly correlated (r = 0.944) with no systematic difference observed. Deep learning-based automated segmentation techniques (e.g., Res-UNet models) achieve precise muscle boundary delineation and IRD quantification, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy and efficiency [24]. Furthermore, three-dimensional ultrasound and dynamic imaging modalities provide comprehensive evaluations of abdominal wall architecture, including real-time functional assessments of tissue elasticity [29], and are proving particularly advantageous for complex cases involving concomitant hernias or structural defects. In contrast, traditional imaging modalities show limited efficacy in such complex scenarios. AI-enhanced ultrasound analytics enable automated tissue recognition and quantitative measurements, generating objective data to inform surgical planning [30].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) are high-resolution modalities that provide static anatomical data. Meanwhile, AI algorithms leverage these datasets to extract richer diagnostic information [31]. For example, deep learning models can automatically identify changes in the structure of the muscles, assess fascial integrity, and perform three-dimensional reconstructions from abdominal CT and MRI scans [32].These developments not only increase diagnostic precision but also streamline preoperative planning through thorough anatomy mapping.

Concurrently, AI enables personalized risk prediction by combining clinical parameters, imaging biomarkers, and patient-specific data to build predictive models [33]. Although such models are still in development for PDRA, they have the potential for prognostic stratification and therapeutic outcome forecasting.

PDRA diagnoses is being transformed by the AI-driven integration of computational analytics with imaging data, enabling precision medicine approaches for individualized risk assessment. The importance of AI-assisted systems in routine practice will be further solidified by continuing algorithmic development and clinical validation.

Functional and multidimensional assessment

A comprehensive assessment of rectus abdominis performance after PDRA is essential for developing individualized clinical management strategies. A detailed investigation requires the development of a specific evaluation system based on four main dimensions: musculoskeletal biomechanical testing, kinematic analysis, the use of multidimensional functional evaluation metrics, and the standardization of quantitative approaches.

Musculoskeletal biomechanical testing remains pivotal in functional assessment. Recent advancements in ultrasonography have enabled quantitative evaluation of muscle mechanics, particularly in measuring thickness, cross-sectional area, and elasticity [34]. For instance, ultrasound-derived measurements of the quadriceps femoris demonstrate high reproducibility and reliability [35]. Integration with elastography further enhances biomechanical characterization by quantifying tissue stiffness and elastic modulus, offering theoretical foundations for postpartum diastasis recti assessment.

Kinematic analysis introduces dynamic perspectives through three-dimensional motion capture systems and surface electromyography (sEMG), enabling real-time monitoring of activation patterns and neuromuscular coordination during movement [36]. AI-enhanced algorithms can generate important kinematic parameters such as contraction velocity, motion trajectories, and joint angle changes, which were previously validated in upper limb rehabilitation and are now being automated [37]. Such approaches significantly enhance the functional quantification of the rectus abdominis, improving diagnostic accuracy for movement-related impairments.

A multidimensional evaluation system should combine strength, endurance, coordination, and biomechanical features [38], which is especially critical for postpartum recovery. The synergistic use of ultrasonography, kinetic analytics, and AI technologies facilitates the development of composite indices that fully comprehensively depict muscle function, enabling evidence-based clinical judgments and guiding effective intervention strategies.

The ability of cross-sectional data to compare results depends on the standardization of quantitative procedures used. Multicenter studies highlight that that current limitations in ultrasonography and kinematic measurement protocols, such as pressure artefacts and variable probe positioning, necessitate standardized guidelines [39, 40,41]. AI-powered automation tools show potential to mitigate operator-dependent biases, enhancing methodological consistency.

Future research should focus on developing integrated biomechanical-kinematic protocols and multidimensional assessment systems. These advancements are crucial for improving the clinical applicability and reliability of rectus abdominis evaluation, ultimately optimizing therapeutic interventions and enabling early management of functional deficits.

Technological innovations in surgical management

The surgical treatment of PDRA has evolved from conventional approaches toward minimally invasive techniques. This section provides a comprehensive scholarly overview of PDRA surgical therapies by assessing technical developments in minimally invasive procedures and the comparative advantages of different mesh placement approaches.

Advancements in minimally invasive techniques: comparative efficacy of surgical modalities

For mild cases of PDRA, spontaneous recovery remains possible. Surgical intervention is recommended for patients who fail physiotherapy 6–12 months postpartum, aligning with optimal postpartum rehabilitation timelines.

Traditional open procedures, such as abdominoplasty, historically served as the gold standard for PDRA correction. Key techniques include midline linea alba plication to reduce IRD. While open surgery effectively improves abdominal contour [42, 43], it carries significant drawbacks: prolonged recovery (average hospitalization: 3–5 days), higher complication rates (e.g., wound infection, hematoma, and recurrence rates up to 20%–30%) [43], and elevated risks of recurrence in obese populations due to excessive tissue tension and linea alba weakening [44, 45]. Additionally, open surgery imposes stringent demands on the patient’s overall health status and is contraindicated for individuals with multiple comorbidities.

Laparoscopic techniques have emerged as a mainstream alternative, leveraging minimally invasive advantages. Core methods include the reinforcement of the linea alba and mesh repair (e.g., “Slim-Mesh” technique). Laparoscopic methods use small incisions to minimize tissue damage, allowing for direct management of muscle separation and enhanced integrity of the linea alba through mesh or suturing [44, 46]. According to a clinical study on obese patients, the use of the Slim-Mesh technique shortened the average surgical time to 60 min, achieved an IRD reduction rate exceeding 80% at 6 months postoperatively, and resulted in no serious complications such as chronic pain or intestinal adhesions [44]. Concurrent hernia repair during laparoscopic procedures further reduces recurrence rates (< 10%) in cases with umbilical or linea alba hernias [5, 45].However, widespread adoption faces obstacles due to technical complexity, the need for sophisticated surgical expertise and equipment, and higher material costs.

A robot-assisted surgery integrates laparoscopic minimally invasiveness with enhanced three-dimensional visualization and precision. Preliminary research suggests benefits such as less intraoperative bleeding and a greater degree of suture accuracy in complex cases (e.g., extensive linea alba laxity or recurring cases) [5, 47]. Nevertheless, prohibitive costs and steep learning curves hinder its clinical adoption, while long-term efficacy data remain insufficient to establish superiority over conventional laparoscopy [47].

In summary, open surgery prioritizes aesthetic outcomes at the expense of invasiveness, whereas laparoscopy excels in mild-to-moderate cases with rapid recovery. Robotic techniques, though promising, require further validation. Future research should focus on refining minimally invasive protocols and exploring hybrid strategies to optimize therapeutic efficacy and safety.

Impact of mesh placement strategies on postoperative recovery in PDRA repair

Mesh reinforcement techniques have become a core component of minimally invasive PDRA surgery due to their low recurrence rates and benefits for functional restoration [44]. The choice of mesh placement strategy critically influences surgical outcomes, recurrence risk, and complication rates. The three main approaches used in PDRA are: preperitoneal, intraperitoneal, and superficial fascial placements.

Preperitoneal placement: minimizing visceral adhesion risks

The preperitoneal approach positions mesh between the rectus posterior sheath and peritoneum, reinforcing the weakened linea alba without breaching the peritoneal cavity. This technique demonstrates reduced intestinal adhesion risks (< 5% seroma rates) and preserves peritoneal integrity [44, 48], making it adaptable to laparoscopic procedures. Studies report mean operative times of 45 min using “Slim-Mesh” technology with favorable biomechanical support attributed to the posterior sheath’s longitudinal tensile properties [44, 48]. However, restricted retro rectus space necessitates precise dissection, with suboptimal fixation increasing displacement risks in obese populations [49].

Intraperitoneal placement: High-Strength repair for complex cases

Intraperitoneal mesh application via laparoscopy provides uniform tension distribution and enhanced mechanical strength, particularly for DRA complicated by umbilical hernias. Adhesion-resistant composites (e.g., polypropylene-polyvinylidene fluoride) minimize visceral contact while leveraging intra-abdominal pressure for mesh adherence [49, 50]. Retrospective data indicate lower recurrence rates (3.8% vs. 12.1%) and postoperative pain scores (VAS: 2.1 vs. 5.3) compared to open surgery [50]. Caution is warranted regarding intestinal injury risks (1.5%) and the need for long-term monitoring for erosion and pain.

Superficial fascial placement: expedited repair with limited evidence

Anterior rectus sheath mesh fixation offers technical simplicity for mild-to-moderate PDRA, avoiding interference with deep neurovascular structures. While accelerating abdominal contour restoration, this method exhibits higher recurrence (10%–15%) due to inadequate deep fascial reinforcement and tissue elasticity compatibility issues [44, 47, 49]. In addition, the direct contact between the mesh and the subcutaneous tissue may increase the risk of seroma and infection (the incidence rate is about 8% −12%), especially in obese or diabetic patients necessitating strict screening of indications [44].

Optimal mesh positioning requires multidimensional evaluation of anatomical complexity, comorbidities, and functional-aesthetic goals. Preperitoneal and intraperitoneal methods prioritize biomechanical stability at the cost of operative complexity, whereas superficial placement suits less severe cases despite higher recurrence potential. Future research should refine material-placement synergies and validate indications through randomized trials.

Biomaterials in tissue reconstruction

PDRA tissue reconstruction has seen significant progress in recent years, driven by innovation in biomaterials. The introduction of biomaterials not only compensates for the limitations of traditional suturing techniques, but also provides better anatomical repair and functional recovery solutions for complex cases. Biomaterials in PDRA repair are mainly divided into the following three categories: (1) Synthetic Meshes: Non absorbable materials such as polypropylene and polyester are widely used for abdominal wall reconstruction due to their high mechanical strength and long-term stability. Research has shown that polyester meshes have successfully repaired cases in 73 patients with complex DRA by strengthening the rectus abdominis sheath and linea alba, with no postoperative recurrence. This type of material can form stable scar tissue and enhance regional tissue strength by inducing local tissue fibrosis: however it may carry the risk of chronic foreign body rejection reactions [45, 50]. (2) Bioabsorbable: Materials such as collagen matrix and polylactic acid (PLA) promote the regeneration and repair of autologous tissues by providing temporary mechanical support. Its advantage lies in reducing long-term complications, but they require a balance between the degradation rate and tissue regeneration cycle to avoid premature degradation that results in insufficient self-repaired tissue strength [51]. (3) Composite patch: Combining synthetic materials and biological coatings aims to optimize tissue compatibility and reduce the risk of recurrence. This approach can mitigate some drawbacks of purely synthetic or absorbable meshes, potentially shorten surgical time, and reduce postoperative complications [51].

Biomaterials restore dynamic abdominal stability by reinforcing deficient musculofascial layers. Clinical evidence demonstrates synthetic meshes reduce recurrence rates and promote scaffold-guided fibrogenesis by sharing intra-abdominal pressure load. Kai he et al. that the intraperitoneal onlay mesh (IPOM) technique lowered hernia-DRA recurrence from 15% to < 5% [51]. Redi U et al. showed that component separation techniques with mesh integration achieves near-physiological abdominal compliance at 1-year follow-up. However, long-term data (>5 years) on material degradation and mechanical fatigue remain lacking, necessitating extended outcome studies.

Synergistic integration of minimally invasive techniques, mesh placement optimization, and biomaterial advancements continues to expand PDRA surgical solutions. Standardized protocols and longitudinal outcome assessments remain critical research priorities.

Rehabilitation and functional reconstruction: personalized approaches

Precision rehabilitation interventions

PDRA has a major effect on postpartum women’s overall quality of life and functional recovery. The main focus of traditional rehabilitation methods is on static assessments and muscular training, which frequently lack customization and robust feedback evaluation. Advances in intelligent technologies are shifting rehabilitation methods toward individualized and intelligent paradigms. This section outlines the design of conventional versus intelligent rehabilitation protocols and the scientific principles supporting individualized functional restoration.

Design of conventional and intelligent rehabilitation protocols

Current conventional PDRA rehabilitation methods include: (1) Core Muscle Training: Techniques such as diaphragmatic breathing, plank exercises, and pelvic lifts aim to enhance abdominal core strength and stability, reducing IRD and improving abdominal wall functionality [52]. (2) Functional Movement Training: Exercises like bridge poses, side-lying leg lifts, and cat-cow stretches target the restoration of muscular coordination and functional capacity [53]. (3) Adjunctive Physiotherapy: Manual therapy, massage, and electrical stimulation promote muscle recovery and circulation [53], facilitating tissue regeneration. Although traditional rehabilitation methods can improve PDRA to some extent, they have the following shortcomings: (1) Absence of Real-Time Feedback: During independent training, patients find it difficult to determine movement accuracy, which can lead to poor outcomes or secondary muscle damage [54]. (2) Limited Personalization: Standardized protocols inadequately address individual variability in patient conditions. (3) Reduced Compliance: Monotonous regimens may diminish patient motivation, undermining long-term compliance [27].

In response to the shortcomings of traditional training methods, intelligent rehabilitation training programs have been proposed that achieve precise evaluation and dynamic intervention through technical means. The following are several specific intelligent rehabilitation methods: (1) Surface Electromyography (sEMG)-Based Monitoring: Continuous sEMG measurements analyze abdominal muscle activation patterns and training quality, enabling real-time adjustments [55]. (2) Dynamic Ultrasound Imaging with Deep Learning: Real-time ultrasonography capturesdynamic changes in the rectus abdominis muscle, while deep learning models automate IRD quantification and training efficacy evaluation, mitigating overstretching risks and secondary injury [24, 56]. (3) Intelligent Rehabilitation Devices: Smart belts and flexible sensors monitor muscle contraction intensity and kinematic trajectories, providing feedback via mobile applications. These tools facilitate home-based training and data-driven protocol optimization [25]. Research confirms that these intelligent approaches enhance compliance and outcomes, with patient satisfaction surpassing conventional methods [5].

Scientific principles of individualized functional restoration

The success of individualized functional reconstruction hinges on developing rehabilitation protocols tailored to anatomical features, functional capacity, and quality-of-life demands. The formulation of personalized protocols adheres to core scientific principles encompassing: 1. Anatomical evaluation: quantitative assessment of IRD and abdominal wall thickness via ultrasound or MRI to establish baseline structural integrity [13, 26]. 2. Dynamic functional profiling: Combining dynamic ultrasound and electromyography analysis to evaluate dynamic function and coordination of the abdominal wall muscles and to determine training priorities [24, 55, 56]. 3. Progressive training: Training intensity and complexity should be gradually increased based on the patient’s recovery progress to avoid secondary injuries caused by overtraining [52]. 4. Multidimensional outcome metrics: Attention should be paid not only to anatomical restoration but also to functional reconstruction of rectus abdominis and improvement in postpartum quality of life, such as enhanced core muscle stability and reduced discomfort from PDRA-related lower back pain [52]. Implementation of these principles enhances intervention specificity and functional recovery efficacy while satisfying patient-specific needs.

Conventional rehabilitation techniques can be beneficial in the treatment of PDRA; however, they have disadvantages including low compliance and a lack of individualization. The integration of wearable sensor technology, real-time dynamic imaging, and surface electromyography (sEMG) in intelligent systems has addressed these drawbacks through biofeedback and precision monitoring mechanisms. Future research should focus on technical adaptation for diverse patient groups, longitudinal outcome validation, and clinical translation of intelligent rehabilitation systems.

Long-term follow-up and management

Long-term monitoring and management are crucial for ensuring functional recovery and improving quality of life in PDRA patients. Establishing a thorough and dynamic follow-up evaluation system facilitates monitoring of rehabilitation effectiveness and provides scientific evidence for long-term, individualized therapeutic interventions. This section outlines three main components: multidimensional prognostic indicators, personalized rehabilitation tracking methodologies, and intelligent analysis of longitudinal follow-up data.

Multidimensional prognostic assessment metrics

Long-term PDRA monitoring requires an integrated examination of anatomical, functional, and quality-of-life parameters to comprehensively assess the recovery trajectory. Key indicators include: (1) Anatomical Parameters: Ultrasonography or dynamic imaging are used to quantitatively measure IRD changes during rehabilitation, objectively reflecting the anatomical restoration of the rectus abdominis [13, 26]. (2) Functional Metrics: (1) Core Muscle Function: sEMG is used to assess the activation patterns and neuromuscular coordination of the pelvic and abdominal floor muscles, serving as biomarkers for functional recovery. (2) Dynamic Stability: Motion capture systems and dynamic balance assessments quantify abdominal wall stability during activities of daily living [38]. The recovery of rectus abdominis muscle strength can also be evaluated through two indicators: maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) and duration voluntary contraction (DVC) [57]0.3. Quality-of-Life Indicators: (1) Pain/Discomfort༚Standardized pain scales (e.g., Visual Analog Scale) quantify lumbopelvic discomfort severity, correlating with functional restoration outcomes. (2) Psychosocial Function:Validated questionnaires (e.g., SF-36) can systematically evaluate psychological well-being, social engagement, and global quality of life, providing insights into psychosocial rehabilitation [58, 59]. The integration of these multidimensional metrics enables comprehensive surveillance of rehabilitation status, thereby informing evidence-based long-term management strategies.

Personalized rehabilitation monitoring protocol

Individualized rehabilitation monitoring constitutes the core of longitudinal follow-up, designed to establish dynamically adjusted protocols based on real-time clinical evaluations and provide continuous feedback on postpartum rectus abdominis functional recovery. Current evidence underscores the necessity of implementing short-term (1–3 months post-intervention), medium-term (6 months post-intervention), and long-term (≥ 12 months post-intervention) monitoring phases for PDRA patients [59]. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated significant IRD reduction in intervention groups compared to controls at 8 weeks, justifying short-term monitoring initiation within 1–3 months post-treatment [60]. Kim et al. revealed that while PDRA prevalence decreases from 80% at 35 gestational weeks to 39% at 6 months postpartum, residual risks persist, necessitating medium-term surveillance to evaluate disease progression [61]. Longitudinal monitoring proves critical given heterogeneous diagnostic criteria across different regions, with studies indicating persistent disparities in lumbar pain severity, quality of life metrics, and abdominal muscle strength between PDRA and non-PDRA cohorts over 3–20 year intervals [2, 58]. Therefore, long-term tracking should be conducted at 12 months or longer after intervention to evaluate the impact of PDRA on the long-term health of postpartum patients.

The multimodal monitoring framework comprises: (1) Periodic imaging assessments: Tracking IRD variations and abdominal wall thickness through ultrasonography or MRI-based anatomical assessments [13, 26]. (2) Functional capacity testing: Integrated electromyography and dynamic postural stability analyses to measure the functional restoration of the core muscles. (3) Telemonitoring systems: Wearable sensors for real-time motion pattern tracking combined with AI-powered mobile applications for personalized biofeedback and exercise recommendations [2, 58, 61]. (4) Patient-reported outcomes: Systematic capture of patients’ subjective feelings—including pain, mobility, and quality of life—through standardized questionnaires or mobile application [58]. This adaptive monitoring paradigm ensures the optimization of evidence-based rehabilitation plan and guarantees maximum therapeutic efficacy throughout the recovery continuum.

Intelligent analysis of follow-up data

The integration of AI and big data analytics has transformed long-term management strategies for PDRA by enabling sophisticated processing of multidimensional follow-up data. Recent studies indicate that machine learning algorithms can synthesize imaging characteristics, functional metrics, and quality-of-life evaluations to generate individualized predictive models. These models demonstrate increased precision in monitoring IRD variations [24], thereby enabling accurate assessment of rehabilitation progress and recurrence risks. While wearable devices and mobile applications permit real-time monitoring of kinematic patterns and physiological parameters during unsupervised therapeutic exercises, current implementations have limitations in providing individualized remedial feedback [62]. This technical gap highlights the necessity for AI-enhanced systems capable of detecting biomechanical abnormalities during rehabilitation activities and initiating rapid corrective interventions.

The establishment of comprehensive, dynamic, and individualized long-term follow-up frameworks represents a critical advancement in PDRA management. Multidimensional prognostic evaluation systems, when combined with AI-powered data analytics, enable continuous optimization of rehabilitation strategies while generating evidence-based insights for therapeutic decision-making. Future research should prioritize the standardization of AI-assisted follow-up protocols and explore their potential for delivering personalized rehabilitation pathways, ultimately bridging the gap between technological innovation and clinical implementation in PDRA management.

Discussion

The goal of this review is to synthesize the most recent developments in PDRA research, systematically addressing the pathophysiological mechanisms of PDRA, diagnostic advancements, surgical interventions, and rehabilitation approaches. We clarify critical discoveries, technological developments, and emerging challenges in this area by conducting a thorough examination of global studies.

Current progress and key advancements

The pathogenesis of PDRA includes complicated interactions among anatomical changes, hormonal dysregulation, and biomechanical stresses. Notably, G protein-coupled estrogen receptors modulate cellular stress responses [22], while pregnancy induced hormonal fluctuations, particularly of 17-β estradiol activate PI3K-Akt signaling pathways to disrupt tissue remodeling [15, 20]. Concurrently, sustained mechanical loading stimulates extracellular matrix degradation, aggravating myofascial separation [21]. These molecular insights increase our knowledge on PDRA progression and highlight possible therapeutic targets.

AI has transformed PDRA assessment by integrating with cutting-edge imaging modalities. Compared to manual palpation or caliper-based methods, Deep learning architectures (e.g. Res-UNet) achieve superior accuracy in IRD measurement [27, 29, 33]. Comprehensive assessment of the integrity of the abdominal wall is made possible by three-dimensional ultrasonography and dynamic imaging, which also facilitates treatment planning and personalized risk stratification.

In terms of surgical methods, the development of minimally invasive surgery and biological mesh materials has provided PDRA patients with safer and more effective treatment alternatives. Laparoscopic and robot assisted surgery significantly reduce surgical trauma, enhancing recovery speed and quality of life [5, 44, 46, 47], which is particularly important for postpartum women.

Rehabilitation paradigms are now focusing on intelligent, data-driven management. Traditional methods have drawbacks related to adherence and personalization [27, 54]. Emerging solutions that combine surface electromyography, dynamic ultrasound imaging, and AI-powered devices to establish real-time feedback systems are expected to improve rehabilitation precision and patients ‘compliance [5, 25, 58, 59, 62].

We propose a novel precision medicine framework (Fig. 2.) that integrates molecular profiling, AI-enhanced diagnostics, minimally invasive surgery, and intelligent rehabilitation, which is designed to promote individualized and precise clinical management of PDRA. In this figure, we presents an evidence-based individualized pathway for PDRA, including diagnostic evaluation, tiered interventions, efficacy evaluation, personalized rehabilitation monitoring and long‑term follow‑up. It applies to women screened at 6–12 weeks postpartum or at any postpartum stage when women with clinical symptomatic, such as abdominal bulging, core weakness, lumbopelvic pain and pelvic floor symptoms. When patient confirmed with PDRA, we use standardized physical examination plus AI-enhanced ultrasound to measure IRD and linea alba tension at supra‑, peri‑, and infra‑umbilical levels at rest and during gentle contraction. Meantime, Multidimensional assessment of rectus muscle function combined with sEMG, synergy analysis, dynamic ultrasound, and Musculoskeletal biomechanical testing is also necessary. According to analysis results of ultrasound and rectus muscle function, the severity of PDRA is stratified by IRD with Mild, Moderate and Sever. Patients with Mild or moderate PDRA and no major complications receive conventional or intelligent rehabilitation, which depends on local economic and medical capabilities. Reassessment at 8–12 weeks uses objective metrics (IRD/linea alba tension, core endurance/motor control) and quality-of-life indicators to progress or optimize rehabilitation exercise process. Sever PDRA patients, failure of over 6 months of rehabilitation therapy, recurrent hernia or patients with aesthetic requirements turn to Surgical protocol. Long‑term follow up adopts staged follow‑up at short-term (1–3 months post-intervention), medium-term (6 months post-intervention), and long-term (≥ 12 months post-intervention), monitoring anatomical restoration, rectus abdominis function, quality-of-life evaluations, adverse events, and recurrence. Multidimensional prognostic evaluation systems combined with AI-powered data analytics supports effectiveness evaluation, enable continuous optimization of rehabilitation strategies while generating evidence-based insights for therapeutic decision-making.

A novel Personalized diagnosis and treatment Framework of PDRA

Methodological limitations and technical challenges

Despite these advances, critical gaps persist. Pathological studies mainly focus on molecular cascades, neglecting gene-environment interactions, patient characteristics and epigenetic factors [23]. There is still a significant gap in understanding how the underlying mechanisms of PDRA can effectively guide clinical practice.

Diagnostics lack standardized protocols across studies, complicating clinical translation and ethical governance [47, 48]. Ultrasound acquisition and reporting remain insufficiently standardized, with variability in probe frequency, patient posture, respiratory phase, anatomical landmarks (supra-, peri-, or infra-umbilical), examination condition (rest vs. curl-up), and repeatability. This heterogeneity, compounded with significant operator dependence, introduces substantial and often uncontrolled inter-study variability. Many AI studies rely on single-center, small, or convenience datasets with limited expert-consensus labeling and scarce external validation, raising concerns about data leakage and diagnostic result deviation. Reporting often emphasizes overlap metrics (e.g., Dice) or correlations while neglecting decision-curve analysis and cost-effectiveness to demonstrate utility [27, 30]. Meanwhile, the application of AI in healthcare faces ethical and safety challenges and remains largely confined to small-scale trials.

Surgical treatment, while an effective intervention for PDRA, remains challenging due to an insufficient consensus on procedure selection and mesh placement strategies for different PDRA severities. Comparisons among open, laparoscopic, and robotic techniques are frequently retrospective or nonrandomized research, with selection bias and learning-curve effects. Cost-effectiveness, complication spectra such as chronic pain, foreign body reactions, infection and seroma, and reoperation rates are seldom stratified, limiting causal inference across different mesh placement strategy (preperitoneal, intraperitoneal, superficial). Long-term efficacy data from multicenter comparative trials are urgently needed [47].

In rehabilitation and follow-up management, the long-term tracking of functional recovery in PDRA patients is hampered because the high cost and technical complexity of intelligent devices limit their widespread adoption in primary healthcare institutions and hinder the development of accessible, individualized rehabilitation guidance [5, 25].

The costs and resource concentration of advanced imaging, robotic surgery, and intelligent rehabilitation risk widening inequities across regions and medical levels. AI has raised concerns in privacy, informed consent, data sharing, bias and discrimination, explainability, and accountability. These limitations highlight critical directions and challenges for future research.

Conclusion

The diagnosis and treatment of PDRA is shifting from a traditional rehabilitation model to a multidisciplinary and intelligent precision medicine paradigm. Multimodal diagnostic systems, minimally invasive surgical innovations, and intelligent rehabilitation platforms collectively enhance therapeutic outcomes. However, persistent issues—including diagnostic standardization, ethical AI deployment, surgical guideline optimization, and healthcare equity—demand urgent resolution. Future research must prioritize interdisciplinary collaboration, robust clinical validation, and affordable technological solutions to achieve personalized PDRA care.
