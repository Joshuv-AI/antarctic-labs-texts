Introduction

The rapid ageing of the global population, with those aged 65 and over expected to reach 16% by 2050 [1], is driving a surge in chronic noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), which now account for 74% of global deaths [2]. Simultaneously, the number of people living with severe disabilities has increased to 1.3 billion [3], amplifying demand for health care services. Traditional hospitals, comprising 58.5% of the global hospital services market and valued at $2.05 trillion in 2023 [4, 5], face resource constraints that limit their expansion [6]. Hospital-at-home services provide comprehensive care, including specialist services, for patients preferring treatment in a home setting supported by family [7, 8]. To address these challenges, more governments and health care providers are increasingly adopting hospital-at-home models to improve access and control costs [9].

China’s ageing population, which has been influenced by family planning, urbanization, and smaller family sizes, has strained traditional family-based elderly care [10, 11]. The government is responding by building community-based health services, including hospital-at-home programs [12], although adoption of these services remains limited. Historically, as early as 1979, the China Ministry of Health proposed the establishment of outpatient clinics and family hospital beds to increase public access to medical care [13]. In December 1984, regulations emphasized the integration of Chinese and Western medicine [14]. Over the past decade, local governments have recognized the demand for these services in economically developed areas such as Beijing [15], Shanghai [16], Shenzhen [17], and Guangzhou [18]. In 2023, Shenzhen, one of the fastest-growing regions, established 21,885 hospital-at-home service beds, serving a population of 565,217 elderly individuals aged 65 and above, with an average of 0.039 hospital-at-home service beds per elderly person [19]. The limited supply of services nationwide falls short of meeting the needs of the broader population, and hospital-at-home service accessibility remains constrained [20].

The expansion in the number and distribution of institutions offering home-based hospital bed services may be linked to various factors, including the growing demand for at-home health services driven by population ageing [21], the increased prevalence of chronic diseases [22], and shifts in family care structures. The availability and accessibility of HaH services may be influenced by patient demand and positive experiences, which increase their willingness to choose HaH services [23]. Moreover, whereas the risks associated with providing HaH can deter institutions from expanding these services [24], cost-effective strategies such as Canada’s innovative care for frail elderly individuals can encourage adoption [25]. However, policy factors remain a critical influence [26]. Most studies have focused on micro-clinical effects [27], with limited macro-service policy evaluations [28].

Shanghai’s high population density and significant ageing demographic make it an ideal setting for evaluating the effectiveness of hospital-at-home policy in China. This study uses multistage interrupted time series analysis to evaluate the differential impacts of two policies on health care accessibility in Shanghai, designating community health service centres as the experimental group and hospitals as the control group.

Methods

Study design

This cohort study uses an interrupted time series design to examine the impact of two local policy implementations on the provision of hospital-at-home services in Shanghai. The policies in question are the “Specifications for Hospital-at-Home Services (DB31/T487—2010)” implemented on October 1, 2010 [29], and the “Shanghai Hospital-at-Home Services Regulation” implemented on January 10, 2020 [30]. All data for this study were sourced from the Shanghai Statistical Yearbooks (2007–2023) published by the Shanghai Bureau of Statistics (https://tjj.sh.gov.cn/tjnj/index.html). We collected relevant data at yearly intervals to assess the provision of hospital-at-home services by hospitals and primary health care institutions in Shanghai. This study report adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

Study settings

This study examines Shanghai, China’s economic hub, with a GDP of 4.72 trillion yuan in 2023 [31], as a focal point for the development of hospital-at-home services. As one of the most urbanized and ageing regions, with 28.20% of its 24.76 million residents aged 65 or older by 2022 [31], Shanghai offers a crucial testing ground for elder care initiatives [32]. The first policy, issued in 2010 by the Shanghai Municipal Health Bureau, introduced the “Specifications for Hospital-at-Home Services” (DB31/T487—2010), which defined service scope, institutional qualifications, service items, equipment requirements, and processes for establishing and managing hospital-at-home services [29]. In 2019, the Shanghai Municipal Health Commission implemented a second policy, the “Shanghai Hospital-at-Home Services Regulation,” which provided a detailed list of services and reinforced the qualifications required for institutions to offer these services [30].

This study examines the impact of these two policies on the number of service providers and the volume of services. Between 2007–2022, the number of institutions offering home-based hospital bed services increased from 161 to 250. However, the number of hospitals providing these services decreased from 27 to 3, whereas the number of community health service centres/stations increased from 134 to 247. Over the same period, the number of annual patient visits decreased from 1,038,331 to 506,317. To assess the differential impact of these policies, this study designates community health service centres/stations as the experimental group and hospitals as the control group, focusing on changes in institutional capabilities, home-based medical services, and hospital-at-home beds across different types of institutions.

Outcome indicators

Our primary outcomes encompass three dimensions—institutional capacity, home-based medical services, and hospital-at-home beds—as reflected through seven indicators (y1–y8) to capture trends in the supply of hospital-at-home services, including the number of institutions, medical visits, and bed availability in Shanghai from early 2007 to the end of 2022 (see the Supplementary Material, sTable 1 for details). In our analysis, we employed Cronbach’s alpha to assess internal consistency and found an interitem correlation of 0.924, indicating a high level of reliability among the items.

Specifically, institutional service capacity is measured by the number of institutions providing hospital-at-home services (units) and the number of institutions providing hospital-at-home services per 10,000 population (units/10,000 people). The supply of regional home-based medical services is assessed by the total number of home-based medical service visits (visits) and the number of home-based medical service visits per 10,000 population (visits/10,000 people). Results

Descriptive analysis

The variations in the eight indicators of the hospital-at-home services provided by hospitals in Shanghai from 2007–2022 are shown in sTable 2 of the Supplementary Material. Following the implementation of Policy 1 (Specifications for Hospital-at-Home Services) [29] from early 2007 to the end of 2010, before the implementation of Policy 2 (Shanghai Hospital-at-Home Services Regulation) [30] in Shanghai, a notable shift in the provision of hospital-at-home services was observed. The supply of hospital-at-home services in Shanghai before and after the reforms

Single-policy growth rate (SPGR): Measures the percentage change in home health care supply following the implementation of the first policy compared to the period immediately before that policy. The number of institutions providing HaH services in Shanghai increased significantly following policy interventions. From 2007–2010, 149.80 institutions offered HaH services, which rose to 216.30 after the first policy implementation and further increased to 256.30 after the second policy intervention.

Conversely, the number of hospitals providing HaH services decreased from 16.50 before policy implementation to 6.44 after the first policy was implemented and further to 3.33 after the second policy was implemented (2020–2022). In terms of total home-based medical service visits, both community health service centres/stations and hospitals experienced a decline. The number of visits decreased from 1,000,295.00 before policy implementation (with 87,438.00 visits in the control group) to 1,046,063.00 after the first policy was implemented (control group: 34,805.11 visits) and then decreased to 629,301.30 after the second policy was implemented (control group: 4,052.67 visits). A similar trend was observed for the number of active HaH beds at year end, which decreased significantly from 21,177.00 beds (control group: 1,363.75) before policy implementation to 20,873.67 after the first policy was implemented (control group: 670.56) and further to 18,726.00 beds after the second policy was implemented (control group: 306.33). The total number of HaH beds established during the year also declined, from 40,223.25 beds (control group: 2,083.50) before policy implementation to 50,095.78 after the first policy was implemented (control group: 1,266.89) and finally to 58,294.67 beds after the second policy was implemented (control group: 695.00).

Discussion

HaH aims to alleviate the overburdened inpatient services in hospitals, reduce the medical burden on chronic disease patients and elderly individuals, and improve health care accessibility [9]. Two key policies were implemented at the end of 2010 and early 2020. This study is the first to use multistage interrupted time series analysis with a control group to assess the impact of Shanghai’s policies on the number of institutions (hospitals and community health service centres/stations) providing HaH services and the volume of services delivered. Shift from hospitals to community health centres: policy-driven institutions’ growth

The number of institutions providing hospital-at-home services in community health service centres/stations initially experienced a significant positive shift following the first policy (β1 + β3 + β5 + β7 = 8.20), with a similar pattern observed in the number of institutions per 10,000 population. ITS analysis also revealed that the implementation of two policies led to a significant increase in the number of community health centres offering hospital-at-home services in Shanghai, contributing to overall growth in the total number of service providers. Ageing in Shanghai is driving increased demand for home care services [39], with providers offering better bedside manner and skills than hospital staff do, leading to a preference for home-based care among seniors [40]. Following the 2010 policy, Shanghai emphasized that community health centre staff should enter homes to meet residents’ medical needs [29]. Moreover, the policy has broadened the scope of services, leading more residents in Shanghai to opt for convenient and accessible home health services as an alternative to traditional inpatient care, thus promoting the development of service institutions [41].

Additionally, the policy has enhanced the performance evaluation mechanisms for family doctor teams and the institutions affiliated with community health centres, directly incentivizing the expansion of services [40]. This trend is also profitable for community health centres by increasing the number of service providers. Other policies, including China’s tiered diagnosis and treatment policy with medical insurance reimbursements that steer patients towards primary care and the gradual improvement of the urban primary health care system, have contributed to the increase in the number of community health service centres (stations) in Shanghai, thereby increasing the total number of service-providing institutions [42].

Initial growth followed by decline: home-based medical service visits

The study focuses on changes in community health service provision, with limited impact observed on hospital services over time. ITS analysis revealed a significant decline in the total number of home-based medical service visits to community health service centres/stations following the first policy implementation (β1 + β3 + β5 + β7 = − 90,214.350). After the second policy was implemented, hospital visits per 10,000 population declined slowly (β9 = 1.271). First, the inherent risks associated with home medical care have led to stringent qualification criteria for institutions, resulting in the closure of illegal facilities and those with inadequate infrastructure. Second, restrictions on the qualifications and team composition of family doctors and nurses exacerbate the shortage of health care professionals in communities, as many prefer to work in tertiary hospitals [43]. Additionally, the high-risk nature of home services and challenges in ensuring safety complicate their implementation. The lack of medical insurance support for home care also drives low-income families to choose inpatient services instead [44]. The 2019–2021 COVID-19 pandemic and related prevention measures may have limited family doctors’ ability to conduct home-based medical visits, which could be a limitation of this study. Finally, transportation limitations further impede the development of home nursing services in rural areas [45].

Moreover, hospitals may be less impacted by policy interventions, leading to more stable numbers of institutions providing hospital-at-home bed services than in the experimental group, where fluctuations are less pronounced. However, there are currently only 0.101 organizations per 10,000 population offering these services in Shanghai, indicating that accessibility remains limited. The initial growth in home-based medical visits (Fig. 3), followed by a decline, indicates that while policies contributed to the increase, external factors and policy limitations likely caused the subsequent decrease, preventing sustained growth.

Surge in home hospital bed numbers followed by a decline in home medical services

Research has confirmed that the total number of hospital-at-home beds established annually has increased following the implementation of these two policies. In the southern regions of Israel, the establishment of hospital-at-home beds has been associated with reduced hospitalization costs and improved treatment outcomes, further driving the increase in bed numbers. Home hospital services focus on bedside care for patients needing long-term medical attention. Home-based medical services, on the other hand, offer comprehensive care, including nursing, rehabilitation, disease management, and health education, delivered by specialized teams such as nurses and therapists. requiring advanced technology and personnel support [47, 48]. The increase in home hospital beds, alongside a decline in home medical services, may be due to several factors: Home medical services carry greater risks [49], especially for chronic and elderly patients, leading families to prefer inpatient care due to stricter safety regulations. Additionally, home medical services have higher qualification requirements for providers [50], whereas home hospital beds focus on chronic or disabled patients with lower staffing requirements. Home medical services offer limited technical support and a narrow range of services [51], prompting patients to opt for insurance-covered hospitals’ inpatient care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the observed changes in the eight reference indicators may not be entirely attributable to regional policy impacts, as other factors, such as rising demand for home health services due to demographic shifts, evolving consumer preferences, and the COVID-19 pandemic, may also play a role. Second, by focusing on two specific policy time points, the analysis may have overlooked the interactive effects of concurrent reforms, including adjustments to the hierarchical medical system, health care insurance reforms, and the expansion of family doctor contracting services.

Third, this study focuses on the impact of regional policies in Shanghai on the availability of home-based hospital services, excluding service quality, safety, and patient satisfaction. Finally, while the dataset encompasses all institutions in Shanghai, a highly developed region, the findings may not be fully generalizable to less-developed or remote areas of China, where the implementation of home-based hospital services has been slower. Caution is advised in applying these results more broadly.

Conclusion

Two key policies, introduced in 2010 and 2020, may have influenced the development of hospital-at-home (HaH) services in Shanghai. The study suggests that these policies have played a role in initially increasing the capacity of community health centres to offer HaH services. However, it also highlights other factors, such as sociodemographic changes, the impact of COVID-19, economic development, and broader health care trends, which have influenced the overall growth and quality of these services. Policies may play a role in the decline in home-based medical visits and the increase in hospital-at-home beds, but other contributors include challenges in sustaining growth and the need to address service quality and patient satisfaction. Factors contributing to this decline include stringent qualification requirements, workforce shortages, and challenges in ensuring safety. In summary, while policies have shown some success in promoting HaH services, there is a need for further development to address these complexities and ensure sustainable growth in home-based health care services.
