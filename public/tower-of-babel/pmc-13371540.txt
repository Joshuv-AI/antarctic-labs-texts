Asymptomatic COVID-19 among university students during the low-risk transmission period
Authors: Dragana Plavša, Maja Stošić, Verica Jovanović, Vladan Šaponjić, Marija Milić, Edita Grego, Ljiljana Pavlović, Tatjana Gazibara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBased on the transmission indicators in 2023 and level of herd immunity, it is expected that there are still asymptomatic cases of COVID-19 in the population. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the prevalence and factors associated with asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection during the low-intensity transmission period.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from February 20 to April 30, 2023 among students at the University of Belgrade. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire. Students who visited the Student Health Center for health checks were invited to participate. Two nasopharyngeal swabs were collected, which were tested on-site using the SARS-CoV-2 rapid antigen test and RT-PCR in the laboratory.ResultsOut of 1,307 university students enrolled in the study, 28 tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 on RT-PCR, resulting in a prevalence of asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 cases of 2.1%. Based on the adjusted Firth’s logistic regression model, independent factors associated with developing asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection were: being male (odds ratio [OR] = 0.26, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.12–0.55), living with people older than 65 years (OR = 1.90, 95% CI 1.03–3.16), bedtime until 10 pm (OR = 0.36, 95% CI 0.15–0.96), attending celebrations/parties (OR = 2.32, 95% CI 1.06–5.40), and having contact with a person who had a respiratory infection in the last 14 days (OR = 8.09, 95% CI 1.96–26.02).ConclusionThe prevalence of asymptomatic infection in the university student population is around 2% during the low-risk transmission period. Despite the low prevalence of COVID-19, surveillance of COVID-19 should still continue in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13651-6.Keywords: Students, Asymptomatic, SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19, Infection, Predictors

Introduction

People who have asymptomatic coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) represent unrecognized viral reservoirs. They have a similar viral load to people who have clinical symptoms [1, 2] and are, therefore, perpetuating the viral spread in the susceptible population. Mathematical models suggested that in the period 2020-end of 2022, the prevalence of asymptomatic COVID-19 cases rose from 31% to 44% among the unvaccinated and up to 49% among the vaccinated individuals [3]. However, in spring of 2023, the transmission of COVID-19 gradually decreased [4] and preventive measures were relaxed. Nevertheless, the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) did not entirely disappear. In fact, evidence from a university student population suggested that in 2023 there were as few as 0.5% of asymptomatic cases [5].

Young adults, such as university students, are more often exposed to person-to-person contact because of social activities (e.g. using public transportation, libraries, close contact in dormitories, food courts, and taking part in social events), which favor transmission via respiratory droplets. Because of this, dynamics of COVID-19 spread among students could provide compelling information about the anticipated prevalence of COVID-19 cases in the general population. Bearing in mind the indicators of low viral transmission in 2023 as well as a high level of herd immunity arising from vaccination or past infections [6], we expected that the rate of asymptomatic cases of COVID-19 in the population remained low. Continuing surveillance of COVID-19 in March 2026 suggests that the worldwide seroprevalence is around 3% [7].

While surveillance of COVID-19 in the Republic of Serbia is ongoing and COVID-19 remains a notifiable communicable disease to this day, only people who present with clinical symptoms may be tested. As a result, there has been a lack of data on asymptomatic cases in the low transmission period. These pieces of information could provide additional cues about potential risk factors for asymptomatic COVID-19. The purpose of this study was to: (1) evaluate the prevalence of asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection among university students and (2) identify factors associated with having asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection in the low-intensity transmission period. This study will, therefore, improve our understanding how SARS-CoV-2 keeps circulating during the low-level transmission period.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted among the students of the University of Belgrade. The University of Belgrade with around 100,000 students is the largest university in Serbia. Students are mandated to have biannual health checks at the Students’ Heath Center, an institution which provides primary and secondary health care. A health check was a condition for completing the academic year.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Public Health of Serbia (approval no. 1014/1). The study was conducted in line with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants signed informed consent prior to enrolment.

Sample and procedure

The inclusion criteria were: attending study program at the Belgrade University, age range 18 to 30 years and signed informed consent for participation. The exclusion criteria were: age above 30 years, having acute respiratory illness in the past 14 days before the date of testing and refusal to participate.

The sample size calculation was based on the size of the university student population (around 100,000), anticipated prevalence of asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection (5%) [8], confidence interval of 95% and margin of error of 1.25%. To calculate the sample size we used online calculator https://www.calculator.net/sample-size-calculator.html. The minimum sample size was 1,155 students.

Before enrolment, students were informed in detail about the study aims and principles and were asked to sign informed consent for participation in the study.

Data collection

Data were collected from February 20, 2023, to April 30, 2023 from students who came for a regular health check to the Student Health Center. Students provided data in paper-based form, and their nasopharyngeal swabs were collected just before they filled in the questionnaire in the office of a primary health care physician. Two nasopharyngeal swabs were taken simultaneously. One nasopharyngeal swab was tested using the SARS-CoV-2 rapid antigen test directly on site, and the second with RT-PCR test in the laboratory. All data were securely stored in a password-guarded file to maintain confidentiality. The status of the RT-PCR test was observed as the study outcome.

Students also filled in a pen-and-paper questionnaire (the questionnaire is provided in the Appendix). The questionnaire was developed by the authors for the purpose of this research to better understand factors associated with having asymptomatic COVID-19 infection. The first component of the questionnaire examined socio-demographic characteristics: sex, age, height, weight, type of study program, year of study, type of residence (student dormitory/rented apartment/family apartment/other), the number of people in the household, as well as those over 65 years old living in the household. Body weight and height were measured by the physician and retrieved from the medical record. Based on these data, the body mass index (BMI) was calculated.

The second questionnaire component referred to COVID-19 vaccination, whether they were vaccinated and time since receiving the last dose.

The third component of the questionnaire covered information about health-related behaviors. Presence of chronic illneses was tested by providing a list of most common chronic diseases among young adult population (asthma, diabetes, cancer, heart-related diseases excluding hypertension, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, immunodeficiency [including organ transplants and HIV], neurological diseases [including cerebro-vascular disease], rheumatic diseases, anemia or other blood disorders, obesity, or other chronic diseases or conditions). Students were asked to encircle all conditions that applied to them.

History of respiratory infections in the past five years was also explored: frequency of infections (less than once a year/once a year/twice a year/three or more times a year/never); severity of symptoms (mild/moderate/severe); approach to usual treatment of respiratory infections (nothing/natural remedies and herbal beverages/intake of vitamins and other supplements/using symptomatic therapy/taking antibiotics).

Further, students were asked about current smoking (yes vs. no) and the number of cigarettes smoked per day. Smoking status was also classified as: nonsmoker, former smoker, occasional smoker or smoker. Alcohol consumption was assessed with the question, ‘Do you drink alcoholic beverages, and if so, how often?’ The response options were: never/once a month/two to four times a month/ two to three times a week/ and four or more times a week. Other health-related behaviors assessed included typical bedtime during the working week, sleep duration, and sleep quality (very poor/poor/average/good/very good), as well as the use of supplements to boost immunity (such as vitamins, minerals, herbal supplements, etc.).

The fourth component of the questionnaire explored history of COVID-19 and COVID-19 vaccination. Participants were asked whether they had ever had confirmed COVID-19 and the number of COVID-19 infections, as well as when they had their most recent SARS-CoV-2 infection (less than a month ago/three months ago/six months ago/six months to a year ago/more than a year ago/never). Also, students were asked whether they were vaccinated and how many COVID-19 vaccine doses they received. Also, vaccine manufacturer of the last received vaccine dose was recorded (Pfizer/AstraZeneca/Moderna/Sputnik V/Sinopharm/Johnson & Johnson/do not know).

The last, fifth component of the questionnaire referred to potential exposure to SARS-CoV-2. Information about close contact with COVID-19 cases and/or individuals with respiratory infections in the last 14 days was included. Participants were asked how often in the last 14 days they have used public transport, dined in the student cafeteria, gone to cafes/clubs, visited shopping centers, attended celebrations/parties with large groups, and participated in lectures or exercises at the faculty with other people. Responses were rated as: every day/four to five times a week/two to three times a week/never. The responses were merged to binary answers never = no vs. all other responses = yes.

Data analysis

Data were entered to a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet before being analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA). Firth’s regression models were run in JASP, version 0.18.3.0.

Sensitivity of antigen test relative to RT-PCR was calculated as the proportion of participants who were positive on antigen testing out of all positive participants on RT-PCR. Specificity of antigen test relative to RT-PCR was calculated as the proportion of participants who were negative on antigen testing out of all negative participants on RT-PCR. Table with the exact calculation of sensitivity and specificity is available in the Supplementary material.

Description of the study sample included frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with corresponding interquartile ranges for continuous variables, because they were not normally distributed. Differences between categorical variables with 2 categories when there were more than 5 observations per cell were tested using the Chi square test. Differences between categorical variables with 2 categories when there were ≤ 5 observations per cell were tested using the Fisher’s exact test. Differences between categorical variables with 3 or more categories were tested using the Chi square test. Differences in continuous variables were tested using the Mann-Whitney test, because all continuous variables were not normally distributed, as per the Kolmogorov Smirnov test.

Bearing in mind that the outcome of this study – COVID-19 positive PCR – was a rare event, the Firth’s logistic regression model was applied to evaluate factors associated with asymptomatic COVID-19.

The outcome variable was binary – having asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection (yes/no). All the independent variables (predictors) were tested in a univariate Firth’s logistic regression model. The following variables were assessed: sex, age, BMI (as a continuous variable), field of study (health science vs. other), household size (i.e. total number of people living in the household), number of people older than 65 years in the household, living in dormitory (yes/no), having chronic illnesses (yes/no), number of COVID-19 vaccines received, type of COVID-vaccine manufacturer (Pfizer vs. other), time since last vaccination against COVID-19, frequency of having respiratory infections in the past 5 years, usual symptom intensity of respiratory infections (mild vs. moderate/severe), use of antibiotics when having respiratory infections (yes/no), length of smoking, number of cigarettes smoked per day, alcohol intake, supplements intake (yes/no), average length of sleep during working week, sleep quality, bedtime (before 10 pm vs. after 10 pm), having exams in the past 2 weeks, number of confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infections in the past, time since last SARS-CoV-2 infections, use of public transportation (yes/no), use of student libraries (yes/no), going to clubs/cafes (yes/no), use of students’ canteen for meals (yes/no), attending lectures at school (yes/no), visiting shopping malls (yes/no), attending celebrations/parties (yes/no) and having contact with a person who had confirmed respiratory infection (yes/no).

All variables univariately associated with having asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection entered the final multivariate Firth’s logistic regression model model. The probability level of p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Prevalence of asymptomatic infection

A total of 1,307 university students were enrolled in the study. Out of 1,307 students, 28 were tested SARS-CoV-2 positive on RT-PCR, which makes the prevalence of asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection 2.1%. Of all RT-PCR SARS-CoV-2 positive samples, 5 were positive on rapid antigen testing, suggesting that rapid antigen testing had a sensitivity of 17.9% (95% confidence interval [CI] 6.1%-36.9%) and specificity was 100% (95%CI 99.7% − 100.0%).

Description of the study sample

Characteristics of the study sample according to asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection status are presented in Tables 1, 2 and 3. The majority of study participants were females (73.8%). The median age of the participants was 20 years (interquartile range 19–21). Almost three quarters of students (72.5%) were classified as having normal weight. Most participants studied health sciences (60.1%). One in five students lived in the students’ dormitory (19.7%). Most participants shared their place of residence with 2 other people, while few of them lived with people older than 65 years. Only 5.1% had a confirmed chronic illness (Table 1).

Since the beginning of the COVID-19 outbreak 47.1% of students had at least one confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection. Among them, the majority (70.9%) had their last confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection more than one year prior to this study. Most participants who were asymptomatic (60.7%) received at least one vaccine dose. On the other hand, vaccine coverage among students who were COVID-19 negative was 37.3%. Of asymptomatic students, most received 2 (47.0%) or 3 doses (41.2%). Out of the vaccinated COVID-19 negative students, the majority received 2 vaccine doses (60.8%). Pfizer was the most frequently received vaccine among students (70.6% among asymptomatic students and 69.9% among COVID-19 negative students). In the last five years 87.9% of students reported having had symptomatic respiratory infection (56.7% rarely and 31.2% frequently). Most of them (83.0%) were experiencing mild symptoms of respiratory infection (Table 1).

About two-thirds of students (65.9%) consumed alcohol. Most of them (90.5%) go to sleep after 10 pm. Also, 91% reported average to very good sleep quality. In the last 14 days 0.5% had contact with a person who had a confirmed COVID-19 and 2.1% had contact with an individual with a respiratory infection (Table 2).

Over the last 14 days prior to study participation, the majority of students used public transportation (95%) and attended lectures (93.6%). Around one-half of participants (53.6%) were visiting shopping malls, but less than one-half attended celebrations/parties (48.7%), used the student canteen (43.1%) and student libraries (38.4%) (Table 3).

As per the Chi square test, asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection was significantly more frequent among males, students who shared their place of residence with people older than 65 years, those who went to sleep until 10 pm, those who attended celebrations/parties and had close contact with a person who had respiratory infection in past 14 days (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Disscussion

This study found that, at the time of low-level transmission, the prevalence of asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 cases in the Belgrade university student population was of 2.1%. Being male, living in a household with people older than 65 years, bedtime before 10 pm, attending parties and contact with people who had a clinically evident respiratory infection were likely contributors to presence of an asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection. Our results suggest that the frequency of asymptomatic COVID-19 was very low among university students, but some specific factors predisposed the presence of the infection in this population group.

In this study, we observed that COVID-19 vaccination status and receiving Pfizer vaccine were univariately associated with asymptomatic COVID-19. However, this association disappeared when other univariately associated characteristics of the study sample were included in the Firth's logistic regression model. This means that no significant independent association was detected relative to the asymptomatic COVID-19 in this study sample. The effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in prevention of severe clinical forms has been well documented, with Pfizer having the highest efficacy rate for both symptomatic and asymptomatic infection [9]. However, the antibody titer after vaccination wanes over time [10], which increases the risk of re-infection. Nevertheless, vaccination has been observed to decrease the viral load in infected individuals [11], which may explain their lower likelihood to transmit SARS-CoV-2 because of faster viral clearance [12]. Although we expected that COVID-19 vaccination may predispose asymptomatic infection, it should be kept in mind that we did not measure antibody titer or performed other laboratory tests to include cellular immunity. Finally, individual immune status, regardless of vaccination could play a role in the onset of asymptomatic form of COVID-19 [13]. As a result, the association between vaccination and asymptomatic COVID-19 warrants further investigation.

In this study males were more likely to have asymptomatic COVID-19. Empirical evidence suggests that testosterone affects neutrophils and monocytes circulation, probably through the differentiation of a multipotent hematopoietic progenitor in the myeloid lineage [14]. Women, on the other hand, exhibit stronger innate and adaptive immune responses than men, which makes them less prone to microbial infections and more efficient at fighting viruses [15, 16]. Experiments on animal models highlighted that the composition of gut microbiota in males may give rise to bacterial invasion [17]. Therefore, the differences observed in our study regarding the response to SARS-CoV-2 in men and women may possibly be attributed to a higher susceptibility of men toward infections [18, 19].

Previous studies focused on household transmission of the SARS CoV-2 infection from younger to older household members [20, 21], because of the large number of social contacts among students as well as increased susceptibility of the elderly to infections due to lower immune response, particularly a decline in cell-mediated immunity [22]. Infections that show the greatest increase among older people are viral respiratory infections, including SARS CoV-2, due to the functional changes and diminishing functional reserve along with age. In addition, lifestyle-associated factors such as nutrition and physical activity, which are reduced as people get older, are known as important factors in the development of the SARS CoV-2 infection [23]. Аs cross-sectional studies do not allow the direction of the association to be precisely determined, reverse causality may also be a potential explanation. However, it is necessary to confirm these findings in further research.

Earlier bedtime has been associated with good sleep health and overall good health status. Several previous studies suggested that later bedtime and shorter sleep duration can predispose people to developing acute infectious illness [24, 25]. Additionally, a significant association has been found between poor sleep quality and an increased risk of COVID-19 infection, as well as worse clinical outcomes [26]. Sleep is vital for immune cell development, cytokine production, and antibody response, which are essential in fighting infections [27]. Disruptions in sleep can lead to immune cell and cytokine dysfunction, making individuals more susceptible to viral infections, including SARS-CoV-2 [28]. Previous data showed that an extra hour of sleep can reduce the risk of COVID-19 infection by up to 12% [29]. Thus, sleep disorders increase the risk of respiratory infections, including COVID-19 [30]. Also, people with asymptomatic COVID-19 may sleep for longer hours than those who have symptoms of COVID-19 [31].

This study found that participation in celebrations/parties was associated with having asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection. Consistent with previous literature data [32, 33] social events and gatherings are one of the key risk factors for spreading of respiratory infections including COVID-19 because of close proximity and high density of people in closed spaces which allows droplet spread and inhalation of viral particles. Additionally, possible reasons for this include poor ventilation, air temperature, and humidity [34–37] In contrast to indoor spaces with poor ventilation, outdoor environments provide supplemental fresh air that can dilute airborne droplet concentrations and reduce the risk of virus transmission. In terms of humidity, a dry indoor environment with low relative humidity prolongs the suspension time of droplets in the air, significantly increasing the likelihood of virus transmission through airborne particles.

Having had contact with a person who has signs and symptoms of respiratory infection in the past 14 days is a typical situation in which droplet spread enables the transmission of microbes causing respiratory infections. Students and people who work outside their homes when having mild clinical symptoms of respiratory disease continue their daily activities and social engagements as usual for various reasons – not missing out on school or work or not missing out on activities. In Serbia, when taking on a sick leave, people also have a wage cut and when having mild conditions, many people are reluctant to take sick leave.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. This study was conducted on a representative sample of students of the Belgrade University. However, because it is the largest urban area of the country, these results may not be applicable to the entire student population of Serbia. This study primarily relied on self-reports, which may introduce potential bias due to recall errors and subjective interpretations. Furthermore, the questionnaire did not include information on past influenza vaccines. Although we used PCR to identify asymptomatic participants, their antibody titer was not evaluated. Thus a more nuanced analysis was limited. Cross-sectional study design does not allow for causality inference or to determine the exact direction of the association. Although we used questionnaire to maximize data collection about potential exposures, it is unlikely that all possible exposures were captured, so unobserved confounding could be a limitation to this study.

In conclusion, this study suggested that the prevalence of asymptomatic infection in the university student population is around 2% during the low-risk transmission period. Presence of asymptomatic infection can be explained by several contributors. These include being male, living with older people, sleep time before 10 pm, attending parties, and having contact with people who has clinically evident respiratory infections. Socio-demographics, living arrangement, lifestyle and social contacts may be essential in developing asymptomatic COVID-19. Preventive measures to reduce COVID-19 among young people should remind the population about risk of infection through contact, while also highlighting the relevance of vaccination. Despite the low prevalence of COVID-19, surveillance of COVID-19 should still continue in the future.
