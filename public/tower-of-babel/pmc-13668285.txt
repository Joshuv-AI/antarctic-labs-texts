Menopause Education in Obstetrics and Gynecology Residency Training: A Scoping Review
Authors: Haggar Zoe Aspitz Leibou, Adriane Burgess, Josselyn Thornton, Melissa Noel, Carie Burke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obstetrics and gynecology residents' knowledge of and comfort with menopause is limited despite the Council on Resident Education in Obstetrics and Gynecology objectives of menopause training in residency.

Menopause affects more than 1 billion women worldwide, yet the medical community has not consistently met the complex physical, psychological, and social needs associated with the menopause transition.1 Despite increased attention to menopause in recent years, many patients continue to experience challenges in accessing appropriate care because of ongoing misconceptions among health care practitioners and limited understanding of the full range of menopausal symptoms and evidence-based treatment options.2 In North America, 36–50% of women report suffering from menopausal symptoms.3 One study found that over 20% of women suffer from their symptoms for over a year before they are recognized as menopausal symptoms by their health care practitioners.4 Furthermore, barriers to appropriate and adequate treatment persist even after women's symptoms are identified as menopausal.5

The lack of structured menopause education during residency training remains a significant barrier to providing comprehensive menopause care. A recent study of obstetrics and gynecology residency programs found that only 31.3% reported having a formal menopause curriculum.6 In a study by Steiner et al,7 fourth-year obstetrics and gynecology residents performed significantly worse on climacteric and menopause-related questions than on the overall Council on Resident Education in Obstetrics and Gynecology (CREOG) in-training examination, achieving a mean score of 42% correct compared with 63% correct overall. The CREOG develops educational objectives for residency programs. In the latest edition of educational objectives, there are clear learning objectives related to perimenopause and menopause, including but not limited to the ability to counsel and advise patients about menopause symptoms and the pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic treatment options for their symptoms.8 The high number of women reporting unmet menopause care needs, combined with the lack of formal menopause curricula in residency programs, suggests that current training may not adequately prepare physicians to meet the needs of this population.

Given the identified gaps in both menopause care and residency education, a scoping review was conducted to examine and map the existing literature on menopause education in obstetrics and gynecology residency programs in the United States. A scoping review was selected to capture the breadth of available evidence, to identify the types of educational interventions currently in use, and to highlight persistent gaps in residents' training, knowledge of menopause, and comfort in managing menopausal patients. This approach allowed us to evaluate the existing literature on menopause-related education within obstetrics and gynecology residency programs, to assess the effectiveness of implemented educational tools, and to determine where additional curricular development is needed.

SOURCES

This scoping review was conducted between 2025 and 2026 with established scoping review methodology to systematically map the extent and nature of the literature available on obstetrics and gynecology residents' knowledge about menopause and how comfortable are they in managing menopausal patients. In addition, this review was conducted to describe what interventions have been implemented to incorporate menopause-specific education into obstetrics and gynecology residency curricula and to identify where gaps remain. The methodology for this scoping review was informed by the framework proposed by Arksey and O'Malley and further refined by the Joanna Briggs Institute.9 This review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses–Scoping Reviews) guidelines.10

The scoping review team included content and information science experts, specifically a medical librarian, two obstetrics and gynecology residents, a nurse scientist with expertise in women's health and health professions education who also serves as an administrator for obstetrics and gynecology residency research, and an attending obstetrician–gynecologist who is a Menopause Society Certified Practitioner and the Administrator for the Residency Obstetrics and Gynecology Menopause Clinic. All team members were invested in strengthening menopause education within obstetrics and gynecology residency curricula.

STUDY SELECTION

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in September 2025 by a medical librarian across three electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar (Fig. 1). The authors deemed that ClinicalTrials.gov was not applicable to this topic. To maximize sensitivity, no date or language restrictions were applied during the initial search; eligibility criteria were applied during screening. Eligibility criteria included articles published in English within the past 20 years (2005–2025) that included obstetrics and gynecology residents as a study population. We chose this time period because of the limited amount of available research identified on this topic when initially limiting the search. A protocol was not registered for this scoping review; however, details of the search strategy can be found in Appendix 1 (available online at https://links.lww.com/AOG/F25).

The search was structured to capture all articles that addressed residents' menopause-related knowledge, comfort, or preparedness in caring for menopausal patients; knowledge of menopause treatment or educational interventions; and associated improvement in knowledge or confidence. Studies not involving obstetrics and gynecology residents, conducted outside the United States, or published more than 20 years ago were excluded. Articles were not limited by the study design; however, we excluded editorials.

To ensure agreement, two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of a total of 63 unique articles to ensure alignment with inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved by the two reviewers discussing and rereviewing the abstract against inclusion criteria. Included articles were critically appraised and placed into an evidence table to support discussion and analysis. Data points collected were the article title, authors, and year of publication, as well as the main research question, study design, population and sample size, results, and main implications and conclusions of the study (Table 1).

Themes:

1) Lack of knowledge of menopause symptoms and treatment among obstetrics and gynecology residents.

2) Lack of comfort managing menopause patients by obstetrics and gynecology residents.

3) Limited menopause education and training in obstetrics and gynecology residency.

4) Improvement after both interactive and noninteractive menopause education interventions in obstetrics and gynecology residency programs.

Three reviewers met to discuss and synthesize the results of each article, and themes were identified. To inform our analysis, consultation with a faculty member with an obstetrics and gynecology residency in South Central Pennsylvania was undertaken to gain a better understanding of their perceptions of the gaps that exist in menopause education within obstetrics and gynecology residency.

RESULTS

Our initial search yielded 63 results after duplicates were removed. Of these, 49 records were excluded because they did not meet the defined inclusion criteria. In the second stage, full articles were reviewed, and 5 additional articles were excluded for not meeting criteria. No additional articles that met criteria were identified in the second stage of the search when we reviewed citations of the included articles. Nine articles were included in the final review (Table 1). The final articles included cross-sectional studies (n=4), prospective quasi-experimental studies (n=3), and prospective randomized cross-over studies (n=2).

Four main themes were noted in review of the articles that met inclusion criteria; these themes were lack of knowledge of menopause symptoms and treatment among obstetrics and gynecology residents (n=4), lack of comfort managing menopause patients by obstetrics and gynecology residents (n=4), limited menopause education and training in obstetrics and gynecology residency (n=3), and improvement in management of menopause after both interactive and noninteractive menopause education interventions in obstetrics and gynecology residency programs (n=5).

Four articles in the review reported that residents had low levels of knowledge about menopause.11–14 Christianson et al,11,14 Kling et al,12 and Hubka et al13 all showed that residents reported and demonstrated limited knowledge of menopausal symptoms and their management. Findings consistently demonstrated that residents had limited knowledge of the full range of menopausal symptoms, including but not limited to joint pains, as well as pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic treatment options. In addition, all four of the studies reported deficiencies in residents' knowledge of menopause-related hormonal changes and the physiologic implications of menopause. Although gaps in knowledge were identified, two of the authors found that increased training on menopause competencies was associated with increased knowledge in all areas of menopause knowledge, including treatment with hormone and nonhormone therapy for menopausal symptoms.11,14

In addition to lacking the knowledge of menopause symptomology and treatment, Christianson et al,14 Kling et al,12 Shinnick et al,15 and Hubka et al13 also found that obstetrics and gynecology residents reported low levels of comfort in providing menopause care.12–15 Three of the studies found that the majority of residents reported feeling only somewhat prepared to manage women experiencing menopause.12,14,15 One study found that more than one-third of respondents reported they were not at all prepared to care for menopausal patients.12 All four studies reported significant improvement in resident's comfort in managing menopause symptoms and treatment of menopause after receiving menopause-specific education.12–15

Three studies included in the review, by Christianson et al,11 Kling et al,12 and Allen et al,6 all showed that residents received very limited menopause-specific training during residency.6,11,12 Residents reported in two of the three studies that their residency program offered no more than one lecture on menopause during their entire 4 years, and even fewer residents reported that their program offered any didactic sessions on management of menopause.6,12 Christianson et al11 evaluated resident's preferred training modality for menopause education, and found that the majority of residents preferred supervised menopause clinics. However, in evaluation of the type of menopause training residents receive, Christianson et al11 found that only 16.25% of obstetrics and gynecology residents participated in a menopause clinic, and Allen et al6 found that only 29.3% of obstetrics and gynecology residents had dedicated time assigned to a menopause clinic, only a small increase in the percentage of residents involved in clinics over the span of 10 years.

To address gaps in curriculum in obstetrics and gynecology residency training around menopause, several studies evaluated obstetrics and gynecology resident improvement in menopause management and knowledge after the use of educational tools and initiatives. The educational tools used varied, and some were interactive, including modalities such as podcasts combined with case-based learning reviews and menopause clinics.16,17 One study used a clinical rotation that included a weekly menopause clinic in which residents oversaw the care and treatment of menopausal patients along with a menopause specialist.16 Another study implemented a menopause curriculum that used a combination of podcasts and a case-based learning curriculum on menopause.17 Both studies showed that residents had significant improvement in their knowledge after menopause-specific training interventions.16,17 In addition, Vesco et al17 reported a significant dose–response effect, with greater adherence to the podcast curriculum and case-based learning sessions associated with greater improvements in menopause knowledge (P<.001). Residents who completed all six podcast episodes and attended the case-based learning sessions improved their knowledge scores from 61% to 85%, whereas those who only partially engaged with the podcasts and did not attend the sessions demonstrated minimal improvement (60–63%).17

Other educational initiatives used were noninteractive, including lectures and learning modules.14,15,18 One study evaluated the use of a 2-year curriculum of lectures,14 whereas the other two studies examined menopause-specific learning modules.15,18 Christianson et al,14 Shinnick et al,15 and Kalluru et al18 all found significant improvement in residents' knowledge of menopause after these educational initiatives.

DISCUSSION

Our findings highlight a lack of knowledge of menopausal physiology and symptomology among obstetrics and gynecology residents.11–15 Similarly, obstetrics and gynecology residents report a lack of comfort in managing the menopausal patient.11–15 Hubka et al13 suggested that deficits in menopause competencies persist beyond residency training and found that practicing clinicians report that their current capabilities in caring for patients with vasomotor symptoms are less than desired. In 2023, Allen et al6 reported that program directors agreed that menopausal education was lacking and rated the mean effectiveness in preparing obstetrics and gynecology residents to care for this patient population beyond residency as 3.83 on a scale of 10.

This gap in preparedness persists despite the inclusion of menopause pathophysiology and management as core CREOG Educational Objectives since their initial publication in 1976.19 Since then, CREOG has continued to emphasize perimenopause and menopause care, further mapping menopause pathophysiology and management as core objectives in the 11th edition and onward.20 Although menopause education has been an expected component of obstetrics and gynecology residency training since 1976, only nine studies were identified that evaluated resident knowledge, confidence, or educational experiences related to menopause care. At the same time, a growing population of women is becoming increasingly informed and empowered regarding menopause care while many obstetrics and gynecology residents may not be receiving sufficient education and training to confidently provide evidence-based management. This disconnect has the potential to undermine patient trust in the women's health care system and to widen gaps in menopause care delivery. Ultimately, improved educational interventions are needed to better equip obstetrics and gynecology residents to care for the menopausal patient.13–18

There is limited research exploring the utility and efficacy of various educational interventions that can be used to achieve menopause competency among obstetrics and gynecology residents.6,11,12 Only five of the nine articles we identified in our search explored educational interventions. These interventions included podcasts, case-based learning activities, small group laboratories, supervised clinics, web-based training modules, and traditional lectures.11,14–18 As expected, there was an increase in resident knowledge and comfort of menopause pathophysiology and management after intervention in each of the five studies that explored educational strategies.11,14–18 Of these articles, only the work of Shinnick et al11,14 compared different educational formats by tailoring their PowerPoint presentation according to one of three learning styles—typical, visual, and interactive formats—to assess influence on immediate and 3-month postexposure knowledge and comfort. The other articles examined did not include a comparison of educational interventions. Therefore, our ability to draw conclusions about the efficacy of one learning modality over the other is limited.

Further examination of these strategies suggests that when given a choice, the majority of residents favored learning opportunities provided by a menopause-specific supervised clinic.11 Unfortunately, few health care organizations offer a specialty clinic tailored to menopause, and fewer yet provide opportunities for resident engagement.6,11,16 When resident involvement in menopause-specific training was prioritized, it was associated with improved resident performance on menopause knowledge assessments.16 For those residency programs that can do so, development of a menopausal clinic may be a welcomed and effective intervention to improve knowledge of menopause.11,16 However, incorporating rotations within a menopause clinic into obstetrics and gynecology residency requires substantial resources and planning that may limit its applicability across residency programs.16

Alternative interventions to improve knowledge and comfort with menopause include requiring residents to complete standardized online training modules, which have the advantage of being easily incorporated, disseminated, and self-paced.15,18 Although a more traditional educational approach, these modules were associated with statistically significant improved knowledge scores on assessment of topics such as menopause basics and hormone therapies.15,18 More innovative strategies included incorporation of a podcast format or case-based learning activities.11,17 Case-based learning activities provided the advantage of group engagement and were associated with a dose-dependent improvement in comfort and knowledge surrounding menopause treatment.17 For program directors looking to incorporate menopause education in a meaningful way, case-based learning activities may be an effective method that capitalizes on the advantages of problem-solving as a team and exploring real-life applications of menopause pathophysiology in the management of the symptomatic patient.

An additional challenge involves sustaining menopause-related knowledge and clinical competency over time so that concepts learned during residency are retained and effectively translated into independent clinical practice. Shinnick et al15 failed to demonstrate consistent long-term gains in knowledge after the implementation of menopause-related learning modules; a return to self-assessed knowledge neared baseline at the 3-month follow-up. Therefore, it is imperative that program directors and faculty consider how menopause competencies are incorporated throughout residency education and not provided as a single intervention. The educational interventions developed and implemented by Christianson et al11 were unique among the studies reviewed in that the authors incorporated lectures and small group laboratories with case studies across a 2-year curriculum. Not only did residents demonstrate improvements in knowledge assessment and comfort, but most residents felt that the 2-year timeline was ideal. The authors did not explore whether residents retained the knowledge gained beyond the postcurriculum assessment. Further research is needed to compare the efficacy of various educational strategies and to evaluate their ability to support long-term retention of menopause competencies. A longitudinal curriculum that builds on menopause competencies learned earlier in residency may provide opportunities for repetition and improve retention. Obstetrics and gynecology trainees must address numerous obstetric and women's health knowledge and hands-on competencies during their limited time in residency. Therefore, future research is needed to design the ideal educational strategy that addresses key menopause competencies and promotes clinician comfort and preparedness for caring for patients in menopause.

An important consideration is that the relative lack of menopause education in obstetrics and gynecology residency programs may not reflect a lack of recognition of its importance but rather the increasing demands placed on residency curricula. Over time, programs have been required to incorporate additional educational content, including wellness, quality improvement, patient safety, health equity, and other emerging competencies, while continuing to meet procedural and surgical volume requirements and adhere to duty-hour restrictions. As a result, residency programs face competing educational priorities within a fixed training period. Efforts to expand menopause education must therefore be considered within the broader context of curriculum design and may require innovative educational strategies, restructuring of existing curricula, competency-based training pathways, or reconsideration of the duration and organization of residency training.

This review has several limitations. A scoping review methodology was selected because the available literature on menopause education in obstetrics and gynecology residency training is limited and heterogeneous. Only nine relevant studies were identified, precluding quantitative synthesis and limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions. Most studies were conducted at single institutions, included small sample sizes, and relied on nonvalidated survey instruments, limiting generalizability and increasing the potential for bias. In addition, outcomes were based primarily on self-reported measures of knowledge and confidence, which may not accurately reflect residents' actual competence in menopause care. However, persistent reports from women describing gaps in menopause care suggest that deficiencies in training may exist among all cohorts of obstetrics and gynecology residents. To capture all relevant literature, a broad time frame was used; however, the oldest study was published in 2012 and the most recent in 2025, limiting the time frame to only 14 years. Finally, the review was restricted to U.S. residency programs because training requirements vary internationally, limiting the applicability of findings to other countries.

There is a dearth of literature exploring obstetrics and gynecology residents' knowledge of and comfort with providing menopause-specific care despite CREOG objectives clearly outlining menopause as a foundational competency in obstetrics and gynecology residency education.8 This lack of research supports what midlife women report encountering in practice, that obstetrics and gynecology clinicians are not prepared to identify and treat their unique health care needs. This necessitates a call to action among obstetrics and gynecology program directors and faculty to design, implement, and evaluate innovative educational strategies to ensure that CREOG (2023) objectives on menopause care are thoughtfully infused into residency training to ensure resident comfort in caring for women in perimenopause and menopause.
