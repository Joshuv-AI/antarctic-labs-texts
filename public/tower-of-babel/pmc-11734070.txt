Large Free-Floating Left Atrial Ball Thrombus With Intermittent Mitral Valve Occlusion
Authors: Amitoj Singh, Usama Nasir, Sherif B. Labib
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
An 83-year-old man with known history of atrial fibrillation presented for preoperative evaluation for elective left nephrectomy for cancer. Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a large, free-floating, left atrial mass. Further profiling with transesophageal echocardiogram showed a free-floating mass intermittently obstructing the mitral valve.

An 83-year-old man with history of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation on warfarin presented for preoperative evaluation for elective left nephrectomy for papillary urothelial carcinoma. The patient had been intermittently struggling with hematuria and was inconsistent with warfarin use. He underwent a transthoracic echocardiogram that showed a dilated left atrium (LA), a large echodense mass (3 × 4 cm), normal left ventricular systolic function, and no valvular disease. The patient underwent a transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) to further characterize the mass that revealed a mobile free-floating mass in the LA without any attachment to the septum or atrial wall (Figure 1A). The mass was noted to intermittently obstruct the mitral valve (Figures 1B and 1C) but because of its dimensions was unable to fully cross the mitral orifice. Free-floating ball thrombi are extremely rare and usually occur in the context of mitral stenosis.1 The echocardiographic findings of a free-floating mass without a stalk or attachment point along with the presence of spontaneous echocardiographic contrast in the clinical context of atrial fibrillation and hypercoagulable state from malignancy favored a ball thrombus as the likely etiology of the mass. The patient did not have syncope, which was attributed to the small number of cardiac cycles when the mass became fully occlusive,2 in part related to the protective “pinball flipper effect” of the mitral leaflets that prevented entry to the left ventricle by ejecting the mass back to the LA.

Various management options were considered. There was concern that anticoagulation or lysis may alter the size of the mass enough to clear the mitral orifice but not the left ventricular outflow tract, which is smaller in cross-sectional area and does not have the protective mobility effect of the mitral leaflets, leading to a potentially life-threatening complication.3 Transcatheter options using electrocautery and/or aspiration techniques are emerging treatment modalities for masses within the cardiac chambers. However, the size of the LA mass would be expected to pose challenges, especially if removing it through a trans-septal approach.

Our patient underwent an emergent surgery on the same day as the TEE. Intraoperative evaluation confirmed the diagnosis of a freely mobile LA thrombus. Exploration of the atrium did not demonstrate any endocardial or jet lesions. A small clot was noted within the appendage, presumably the origin of the subsequent free-floating LA mass, and the appendage was oversewn. The thrombotic etiology of the mass was confirmed histologically. The patient was treated with warfarin postoperatively and had an event-free course. He underwent elective nephrectomy 5 weeks later.
