Acupoint Catgut Embedding Ameliorates Ulcerative Colitis in Association with Modulation of cGAS-STING Signaling and M1-Like/M2-Like Macrophage Markers
Authors: Dan Long, Siqing Chen, Fan Yang, Xudong Tang, Chenhan Mao, Ying Zhu, Lian Yu, Yin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOriginating from traditional acupuncture, acupoint catgut embedding (ACE) represents an integrated stimulatory therapy effective for numerous disorders. This study aims to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the protective effects of ACE against 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced ulcerative colitis (UC) in rats, focusing on cGAS-STING signaling and macrophage-associated inflammatory responses.MethodsRats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8) in Experiment 1 (control, model, SASP, and ACE) and Experiment 2 (control, model, ACE, and ACE + 5,6-dimethylxanthenone-4-acetic acid [DMXAA]). Integrative untargeted metabolomics and RNA sequencing were employed to explore underlying mechanisms. The expression of M1-like/M2-like macrophage markers, cGAS-STING pathway components, and epithelial barrier proteins was investigated using RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence. Macrophage marker profiles were characterized by flow cytometry, and cytokine secretion was measured by ELISA.ResultsACE significantly ameliorated the DAI, colon shortening, and histological scores in UC rats. Metabolomics and RNA sequencing revealed that ACE regulated arginine metabolism and the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway. ACE treatment was associated with decreased expression of M1-like macrophage markers and increased expression of M2-like macrophage markers, accompanied by altered inflammatory cytokine profiles in colonic tissues. Furthermore, ACE markedly suppressed the cGAS-STING pathway, as evidenced by decreased mRNA expression and protein phosphorylation of downstream molecules. Administration of the STING agonist DMXAA partially reversed ACE-associated changes in macrophage marker expression and intestinal mucosal integrity.ConclusionACE ameliorated TNBS-induced colitis, which was associated with reduced activation of cGAS-STING signaling and altered macrophage-associated inflammatory marker expression. This study provides a potential adjunctive therapeutic strategy for UC.Keywords: ulcerative colitis, acupoint catgut embedding, macrophage, cGAS-STING signaling pathway, intestinal mucosal barrier

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC), a major subtype of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), is characterized by relapsing intestinal inflammation and persistent mucosal injury, resulting in substantial clinical and socioeconomic burdens.1,2 The pathogenesis of UC involves a complex interplay among immune dysregulation, intestinal barrier dysfunction, genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, and gut microbiota alterations. Immune dysregulation and mucosal barrier disruption reciprocally reinforce each other, perpetuating inflammation and contributing to UC relapse and progression. Current therapies, including aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and biologics, have significantly improved disease control. However, limited response rates, treatment-related adverse effects, high costs, and frequent relapse remain major clinical challenges. Therefore, the development of safe and effective complementary therapeutic strategies for UC remains an important clinical priority.

Intestinal macrophages are predominantly located in the lamina propria of the intestinal mucosa and play essential roles in maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis, regulating inflammatory responses, and facilitating tissue repair.3 Growing evidence has demonstrated that macrophage polarization is dysregulated in UC, characterized by an imbalance between pro-inflammatory M1 and tissue-repairing M2 macrophage phenotypes. During UC progression, excessive activation of M1 macrophages promotes the production of inflammatory mediators and contributes to epithelial damage, whereas M2 macrophages facilitate inflammation resolution and mucosal repair.4,5 Therefore, restoring macrophage polarization balance represents a potential therapeutic strategy for UC.6–8

Increasing evidence indicates that the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway, a major cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, plays a critical role in innate immune regulation and inflammatory responses.9 Recent studies have demonstrated that the cGAS-STING pathway is aberrantly activated in UC and contributes to intestinal inflammation through the excessive production of type I interferons (IFNs) and pro-inflammatory cytokines, ultimately leading to impaired mucosal barrier integrity.10 Notably, excessive cGAS-STING signaling may promote macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype,11,12 suggesting that this pathway may represent a key molecular link between innate immune activation and macrophage-mediated intestinal injury. Given the central role of cGAS-STING signaling in linking innate immune activation with macrophage dysfunction, modulation of this pathway may represent a potential strategy for restoring intestinal immune homeostasis.

Acupoint catgut embedding (ACE), an acupuncture-derived therapy, provides prolonged and mild mechanical stimulation through implantation of biodegradable catgut into specific acupoints and has been increasingly applied for chronic inflammatory disorders.13 In UC, accumulating evidence indicates that ACE can alleviate clinical symptoms and reduce pathological damage.14 Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying its therapeutic effects remain poorly understood. Growing evidence suggests that acupuncture-derived mechanical stimulation can engage neuroimmune circuits to orchestrate systemic inflammatory responses, thereby influencing immune cell phenotypes beyond the site of stimulation. Previous studies have suggested that acupuncture-related interventions, particularly electroacupuncture, may alleviate experimental colitis through the activation of the vagal cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway and regulation of macrophage polarization.15,16 However, despite increasing evidence supporting the immunomodulatory effects of acupuncture-derived therapies, no previous studies have directly investigated whether acupuncture or ACE regulates the cGAS-STING pathway in intestinal inflammation. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the therapeutic effects of ACE in 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced UC and explore its underlying mechanisms, including improvement of intestinal mucosal barrier integrity, attenuation of inflammatory responses, and alterations in cGAS-STING signaling and macrophage-associated inflammatory marker expression. These findings may provide new insights into the immunological mechanisms of ACE and support its potential as a complementary therapeutic strategy for UC management.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

Six- to eight-week-old male specific pathogen‑free (SPF) Sprague‑Dawley (SD) rats weighing 180–220 g were used in this study. The animals (License No. SCXK 2021-0002) were purchased from Slack Jingda Animal Co., Ltd. (Changsha, China). All rats were acclimatized to the laboratory environment for 7 days prior to the experiment. They had free access to standard chow and water and were housed under standard conditions: ambient temperature 20–25 °C, relative humidity 40–60%, and a 12‑hour light/dark cycle. The animal experiment protocol was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 202503302). All animal procedures followed the NIH Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE guidelines.

UC Induction and Intervention

Based on previously described methods,17,18 the rat UC model was established via the intrarectal instillation of TNBS (P2297, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After fasting for 24 hours with free access to water, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane. A lubricated medical-grade polyurethane catheter was inserted transanally to a depth of approximately 8 cm, and a mixture of 5% TNBS and 50% ethanol was administered at a dose of 100 mg/kg TNBS. The anal orifice was subsequently occluded, and the rats were kept in a Trendelenburg position for 3 min to facilitate full retention of the colonic instillate. After recovery from anesthesia, rats were allowed free access to food and water.

In Experiment 1, a total of 32 rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n=8 per group) using a computer-generated random number sequence: control, model, sulfasalazine (SASP), and ACE. The sample size was selected based on previous studies using similar experimental colitis models and our preliminary experimental experience. Rats in the SASP group were intragastrically administered SASP suspension (H31020557; Lot: 09240806, Shanghai Xinyitianping Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at a daily dose of 300 mg/kg. Rats in the control, model, and ACE groups received an equal volume of normal saline via gavage. Catgut embedding treatment was performed in the ACE group on days 1 and 8 after modeling at four acupoints, including Zusanli (ST36), Tianshu (ST25), Dachangshu (BL25), and Shangjuxu (ST37). ST36 is approximately 3 mm inferior to the fibular head on the posterolateral side of the hind knee joint. ST25 is located 40 mm below the xiphisternal junction and 5 mm lateral to the abdominal anterior midline. BL25 lies 5 mm lateral to the inferior border of the spinous process of the fourth lumbar vertebra. ST37 is positioned approximately 5 mm below ST36 on the lateral hind limb. For the ACE procedure, all rats were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation. The skin over each acupoint was shaved and disinfected with iodophor. A 5 mm sterile catgut segment was loaded into a sterile No. 7 embedding needle. After stretching the local skin taut, the operator rapidly inserted the needle subcutaneously. Following the arrival of the deqi sensation, the needle core was advanced while the outer needle tube was slowly withdrawn, allowing the catgut to be embedded within the subcutaneous and muscular layers. Rats in the control, model, and SASP groups underwent identical isoflurane anesthesia and fixation duration at corresponding time points, but did not receive ACE intervention.

In Experiment 2, rescue experiments were conducted using the STING agonist 5,6-dimethylxanthenone-4-acetic acid (DMXAA; HY-10964, MCE, USA) in combination with ACE to evaluate whether STING activation influences the ACE-associated protective effects and changes in macrophage inflammatory marker expression.19 A total of 32 rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8 each) using a computer-generated random number sequence: control, model, ACE, and ACE + DMXAA groups. ACE treatment was administered to rats in the ACE and ACE + DMXAA groups as detailed in Experiment 1. Rats in the control and model groups received identical isoflurane anesthesia and restraint procedures at matched time points and durations. Rats in the DMXAA + ACE group were given daily intraperitoneal injections of DMXAA (7 mg/kg) for 14 consecutive days. The dose of DMXAA was selected based on previous studies demonstrating effective STING activation by DMXAA in experimental models,20,21 with consideration of interspecies dose conversion. During the treatment period, no mortality or abnormal behavioral changes were observed.

On day 15 of the experiment, rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (P3761, Sigma, USA) at a dose of 30 mg/kg. After anesthesia, blood, colon tissues, and spleen were collected for subsequent analyses. Subsequently, the rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation performed by trained personnel. Death was confirmed by the complete absence of respiratory movement and lack of response to toe pinch.

Assessment of Disease Activity Index (DAI)

Following UC model establishment, rat body weight and stool characteristics (loose and bloody stools) were monitored daily across all groups. The Disease Activity Index (DAI) was calculated using the formula:22 DAI = (body weight change score + bloody stool score + loose stool score)/3. The detailed scoring criteria are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

Colon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. The sections were prepared at a thickness of 5 μm. After dewaxing and rehydration, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Subsequently, the sections were dehydrated with graded ethanol and cleared with xylene, and observed under a light microscope for image collection. Histopathological scoring was assessed by investigators who were unaware of the group allocation, referring to previously documented criteria23 (Supplementary Table 2).

Alcian Blue-Periodic Acid-Schiff (AB-PAS) Staining

The mucus-secreting ability of the colon was evaluated using an Alcian Blue and Periodic Acid-Schiff (AB-PAS) Staining Kit (G1285, Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Colon tissues were cut into approximately 1 mm3 pieces, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. After dehydration in graded ethanol, tissues were embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged using an HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

Reverse Transcription-Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from colon tissues using RNA isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). According to the manufacturer’s protocol, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (R223-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudioTM 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) was employed as an endogenous reference gene. The relative gene expression levels were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primer pairs were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and the corresponding primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Western Blotting

Total proteins were extracted from rat colon tissues using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (CW2333s, CWBio, Beijing, China) supplemented with 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The tissue lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants were harvested. Protein concentrations were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (E-BC-K318-M, Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Equal quantities of protein from each sample were mixed with protein loading buffer and denatured by boiling at 95°C for 10 min. Subsequently, according to the molecular weights of target proteins, equivalent protein samples were separated via 7.5% or 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and electrotransferred onto 0.45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). Following a 5% non-fat milk blocking step, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, with all primary antibodies diluted following the manufacturer’s instructions. Comprehensive details regarding all antibodies utilised in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 4. Following three successive washes with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (SA00001-2, 1:10,000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (BL520B, BioSharp, Hefei, China) and scanned using a ChemiDoc XRS+ chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) according to predefined criteria and standardized procedures, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference protein for normalization.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Concentrations of interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and interferon-beta (IFN-β) in colonic tissues were determined with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Cat. Nos. E-EL-R0012, E-EL-R0015, E-EL-R2856, E-EL-R0016, E-EL-R0545, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the supplier’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Colonic paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and rinsed with ultrapure water. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer under high-pressure heating conditions. The sections were blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, including CD68 (AB303565, 1:500, Abcam, UK), CD86 (BM4121, 1:200, Boster, Wuhan, China), CD206 (A02285-2, 1:400, Boster, Wuhan, China), Zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1; 21,773-1-AP, 1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Occludin (ET1701-76, 1:500, HuaAn Biotechnology, Hangzhou, China), and Claudin-1 (13,050-1-AP, 1:200, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After thorough washing, the sections were incubated with Cy3-conjugated (BA1032, 1:100, Boster, Wuhan, China) or FITC-conjugated (BA1101, 1:100, Boster, Wuhan, China) goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37°C in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) at room temperature. CD68/CD86 and CD68/CD206 co-localization were used to evaluate M1-like and M2-like macrophage-associated phenotype in colonic tissues, respectively. Fluorescence images were acquired under identical acquisition parameters, and the percentage of colocalized area between two fluorescence channels was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Briefly, the overlapping fluorescence area between two channels was measured and normalized to the total positive fluorescence area. Five randomly selected fields from each section were analyzed, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Metabolomics Analysis

As previously described,24 serum samples from the control, model, and ACE groups were subjected to untargeted metabolomics analysis using an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Metabolites with a variable importance in projection (VIP) value > 1 and a P-value < 0.05 were defined as differential metabolites.

Transcriptomics Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from colon samples using TRIzol reagent, and its concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Sequencing libraries were constructed from 1 μg of total RNA using the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, mRNA was enriched with oligo(dT) magnetic beads, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into double-stranded cDNA using random hexamer primers. The cDNA was then subjected to end-repair, phosphorylation, and adapter ligation. Size selection of cDNA fragments (300–400 bp) was performed using magnetic beads, followed by PCR amplification for 10–15 cycles to enrich the library. After quantification with a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, the library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform using the NovaSeq Reagent Kit in paired‑end 150 bp (PE150) mode. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 package based on raw read counts. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method, and adjusted P-values (P-adjust) were used for statistical significance evaluation. Genes with |log2 fold change (FC)| ≥ 1 and P-adjust < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Flow Cytometry

To evaluate systemic macrophage-associated immune alterations following ACE treatment, fresh rat spleens were collected for flow cytometric analysis. Fresh rat spleens were mechanically dissociated using a frosted glass slide, filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer, and erythrocytes were lysed with ACK lysis buffer for 5 min, followed by two washes with PBS. Cells were stained with Zombie AquaTM fixable viability dye for 15 min and blocked with 3% BSA. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 1 μL each of the following antibodies at 4°C for 30 min in the dark: APC/FireTM 750 anti-rat CD45 (202221, BioLegend, USA), PE/Cyanine7 anti-rat CD11b/c (201817, BioLegend), APC anti-rat CD86 (200315, BioLegend), and RB545 anti-rat CD163 (756427, BioLegend). After fixation and permeabilization, intracellular staining was performed using FITC anti-rat CD68 antibody (MA5-28262, Invitrogen, USA) at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. Data were acquired using a BD LSRFortessaTM flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10.8.1, BD Biosciences, USA). The gating strategy was as follows: debris were excluded based on FSC-A vs SSC-A, doublets were excluded by FSC-A vs FSC-H, and viable cells were identified as Zombie AquaTM-negative. Subsequently, CD45+ leukocytes, CD11b/c+ myeloid cells, and CD68+ macrophages were sequentially gated. M1-like macrophage-associated cells were identified as CD68+CD86+ cells, whereas M2-like macrophage-associated cells were identified as CD68+CD163+ cells. CD163 was selected as the M2-like marker for flow cytometric analysis because a validated anti-rat CD206 antibody suitable for flow cytometry was not commercially available at the time of the study. The percentages of CD68+CD86+ and CD68+CD163+ cells were calculated within the CD68+ macrophage population.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.6.0, San Diego, California, USA). For longitudinal data, including body weight changes and DAI scores measured at multiple time points, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to evaluate the effects of treatment and time. For comparisons among multiple groups at a single time point, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ACE Alleviates TNBS-Induced Colitis in Rats

TNBS-induced rats exhibited typical UC-like symptoms, including lethargy, hypoactivity, diarrhea, and hematochezia. Compared with the control group, TNBS-induced UC rats exhibited a marked reduction in body weight, elevated DAI scores, diminished colon length, and an increased spleen index (Figure 1A–E) (P < 0.001). Compared with the TNBS model group, ACE treatment markedly relieved diarrhea and hematochezia, mitigated body weight loss, decreased the DAI, restored colon length, and reduced the spleen index (P < 0.01). H&E staining of colonic tissues revealed severe pathological damage in the UC group, characterized by extensive mucosal ulceration, crypt disruption, goblet cell loss, and robust inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 1F and G). ACE treatment significantly lowered colon histological scores (P < 0.001). Compared with the control group, the TNBS group displayed elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and reduced anti-inflammatory IL-10 (P < 0.001) (Figure 1H). Relative to the TNBS group, ACE intervention lowered the expression of these pro-inflammatory cytokines and increased IL-10 levels (P < 0.05).

ACE Improves Intestinal Barrier Integrity in TNBS-Induced UC Rats

AB‑PAS staining revealed abundant goblet cells and mucus secretion in the colonic epithelium of control rats (Figure 2A). By contrast, the model group exhibited a marked reduction in the positive staining area, whereas both ACE and SASP administration significantly enhanced mucus secretion compared with the model group (P<0.001) (Figure 2B). Western blotting analysis further verified the protective effects of ACE on the intestinal epithelial barrier. As shown in Figure 2C–F, the protein levels of ZO‑1, Occludin, and Claudin‑1 were markedly downregulated in the model group relative to the control group (P<0.01). However, treatment with ACE or SASP substantially increased the abundance of these tight junction proteins (TJPs) (P<0.01).

ACE Modulates M1-Like/M2-Like Macrophage Marker Expression in Colonic Tissue

Macrophages play pivotal roles in regulating immune responses in UC and can display heterogeneous inflammatory phenotypes, including M1-like and M2-like marker expression patterns. M1 macrophages, characterized by CD86 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), mainly initiate inflammatory cascades and aggravate colonic tissue injury, while M2 macrophages, marked by CD163, CD206, and arginase 1 (Arg1), exert anti-inflammatory functions and facilitate mucosal repair.25,26 Western blotting showed that TNBS challenge significantly upregulated iNOS and downregulated Arg1 expression (P<0.001) (Figure 3A–C). ACE intervention effectively reversed these alterations, with markedly reduced iNOS and elevated Arg1 protein levels (P<0.01). Immunofluorescence double staining showed that the colocalization area of CD68⁺ and CD86⁺ was significantly increased in the TNBS group compared with the control group (P < 0.001), whereas the colocalization area of CD68⁺ and CD206⁺ was significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Figure 3D and E).

ACE Alters Serum Metabolic Profiles in TNBS-Induced Colitis Rats

Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS‑DA) analyses showed clear separation among the groups (Figure 4A–D). The OPLS‑DA model parameters were as follows: for the comparison between the control and model groups, R2Y = 0.997 and Q2 = 0.682; for the comparison between the model and ACE groups, R2Y = 0.994 and Q2 = 0.527. Both models had R2Y values close to 1 and Q2 values greater than 0.5, indicating excellent goodness‑of‑fit and good predictive ability, with overall stability and reliability. The volcano plots revealed that TNBS treatment upregulated 61 metabolites and downregulated 157 metabolites (Figure 4E), whereas ACE intervention upregulated 33 and downregulated 136 metabolites (Figure 4F). After ACE treatment, metabolites such as nicotinamide, L‑arginine, 3‑pyridineacetic acid, and 21‑deoxycortisol were elevated compared with the model group, while L‑glutamine, alanylglycine, L‑glutamate, and taurolithocholic acid were decreased. Among them, 11 metabolites were upregulated in the model group and reversed by ACE, while four metabolites were downregulated in the model group and upregulated by ACE. The shared differential metabolites between groups included 3‑(5‑methoxy‑1H‑indol‑3‑yl)propanoic acid, S‑sulfoglutathione, 21‑deoxycortisol, and lithocholic acid‑3‑O‑glucuronide. KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated that the differential metabolites between the normal and model groups were mainly enriched in biosynthesis of cofactors, ABC transporters, biosynthesis of amino acids, aminoacyl‑tRNA biosynthesis, pyrimidine metabolism, and glutathione metabolism (Figure 4G). The differential metabolites between the model and ACE groups were predominantly enriched in arginine and proline metabolism, ABC transporters, arginine biosynthesis, pyrimidine metabolism, and glutathione metabolism (Figure 4H). The ACE-associated increase in Arg1 expression and reduction in iNOS expression were consistent with alterations in arginine metabolism. These findings suggest that ACE treatment is associated with changes in macrophage-associated inflammatory marker expression in experimental colitis.

Transcriptomic Analysis Reveals Potential Regulatory Effects of ACE

The PCA score plot showed significant separation among the three groups (Figure 5A). Compared with the control group, the UC group exhibited 3024 DEGs, including 1899 upregulated and 1125 downregulated genes (Figure 5B). As shown in Figure 5C, following ACE intervention, a total of 3493 DEGs were identified, comprising 1257 upregulated and 2236 downregulated genes. Venn diagram analysis revealed a substantial overlap of the identified DEGs, with 1890 DEGs shared between the two comparison sets (Figure 5D). There were 1345 genes that were upregulated in the model group but significantly downregulated after ACE intervention, including Sting1, Irf5, Cxcl2, Cd80, Nos2, Il1β, Il1α, and Tnf. In contrast, 543 genes that were downregulated in the model group were significantly upregulated following ACE treatment, including Shh, Gli1, Acss2, and Cldn15. Notably, two shared DEGs, namely Carbonic anhydrase 13 (Car13) and the predicted gene ENSRNOG00000076685 (no assigned gene symbol), were upregulated in the TNBS model compared with control and were further upregulated in the ACE group compared with the TNBS group.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying ACE treatment of UC, we performed KEGG pathway analysis. The 3024 DEGs between the model and control groups were considerably enriched in immune regulation pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, NF‑κB, and NOD‑like receptor (NLR) (Figure 5E). The 3493 DEGs between the ACE and model groups were prominent in analogous immune-related pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, NF‑κB, NLR, and cytosolic DNA‑sensing pathways, indicating that ACE influenced the colonic inflammatory microenvironment (Figure 5F). The RT-qPCR validation results demonstrated that ACE significantly reduced the mRNA expression level of Sting in the colonic tissues of UC rats (Supplementary Figure 1).

Therapeutic Effects of ACE are Associated with Reduced cGAS-STING Signaling Activation

Transcriptomic analysis suggested that ACE significantly affected the cGAS‑STING pathway, a central cytosolic DNA‑sensing axis, in the colonic tissue of UC model rats. Western blotting and immunofluorescence results indicated that ACE inhibited the M1 macrophage markers iNOS and CD86 while upregulating the M2 markers Arg1 and CD206. Given that the cGAS‑STING pathway serves as a critical signaling axis mediating pro‑inflammatory activation of macrophages,27 we hypothesized that the protective effects of ACE against intestinal inflammation and tissue damage in UC may be associated with reduced cGAS-STING signaling activation and altered macrophage-associated inflammatory marker expression. To verify this hypothesis, we carried out rescue experiments with DMXAA, a STING agonist. After TNBS induction, model rats were treated with ACE with or without concurrent administration of DMXAA.

As shown in Figure 6A–F, the protein expression of cGAS and the ratios of phosphorylated to total STING, TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), and p65 were elevated in the colonic tissue of model rats (P < 0.01). ACE treatment significantly suppressed cGAS protein expression and reduced the phosphorylation levels of STING, TBK1, IRF3, and p65 (P < 0.01). Compared with the ACE group, the ACE + DMXAA group increased activation levels of these signaling molecules (P < 0.05). RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that cGAS and STING mRNA expression levels were significantly increased in the model group compared with the control group, whereas ACE treatment reduced their elevated expression levels (P < 0.001) (Figure 6G and H). However, co‑administration of DMXAA partially reversed this inhibitory effect, leading to a significant re‑elevation of cGAS and STING mRNA levels (P < 0.01). Furthermore, RT-qPCR and ELISA assays showed that IFN-β expression exhibited similar patterns (P < 0.05) (Figure 6I and J). Collectively, these results indicated that ACE treatment was associated with attenuation of cGAS-STING signaling activation in TNBS-induced colitis.

ACE-Associated Changes in Macrophage Marker Expression are Related to cGAS-STING Signaling

To further investigate the involvement of cGAS-STING signaling in ACE-associated changes in macrophage marker expression, we performed flow cytometry, Western blotting, RT-qPCR, and immunofluorescence analyses. As shown in Figure 8A and B, the proportion of CD68⁺CD86⁺ M1-like macrophages was significantly increased, whereas that of CD68⁺CD163⁺ M2-like macrophages was markedly decreased in the model group compared with the control group (P < 0.001). ACE treatment was associated with a decreased proportion of CD68⁺CD86⁺ cells and an increased proportion of CD68⁺CD163⁺ macrophages (P < 0.001). Compared with the ACE group, the ACE + DMXAA group exhibited a significantly increased proportion of CD68⁺CD86⁺ macrophages and a decreased proportion of CD68⁺CD163⁺ macrophages (P < 0.01). Consistently, iNOS mRNA and protein expression levels were increased, whereas Arg1 expression levels were decreased in the ACE + DMXAA group compared with the ACE group (P < 0.05) (Figure 8C–G). Similarly, CD68⁺CD86⁺ colocalization signals were increased, whereas CD68⁺CD206⁺ colocalization signals were reduced in the ACE + DMXAA group compared with the ACE group (P < 0.05) (Figure 9A–D). Collectively, these findings suggested that cGAS-STING signaling may be involved in ACE-associated changes in M1-like/M2-like macrophage marker expression.

As shown in Figure 10A, epithelial cells in the model group exhibited moderate swelling, with markedly shortened and partially lost microvilli, swollen mitochondria with matrix lysis, and disrupted or shortened cristae structures. Discussion

Using a TNBS-induced colitis rat model, we demonstrated the protective effects of ACE against experimental colitis. Several disease-associated parameters were improved following ACE treatment, including DAI score, colon length, spleen index, histopathological score, goblet cell number, and tight junction protein expression. Furthermore, ACE treatment was associated with changes in M1-like/M2-like macrophage marker expression, characterized by reduced M1-associated markers and increased M2-associated markers. We also observed that ACE was associated with alterations in arginine metabolism and the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, accompanied by reduced cGAS-STING signaling activation. To further investigate the involvement of the cGAS-STING signaling axis, DMXAA, a pharmacological STING agonist, was employed in rescue experiments. DMXAA partially attenuated ACE-associated changes in macrophage marker expression and intestinal barrier improvement, suggesting that attenuation of cGAS-STING signaling activation may contribute to the protective effects associated with ACE treatment in experimental colitis (Figure 11).

Brown upward- and downward-pointing arrows indicate upregulation and downregulation, respectively, under UC conditions, whereas orange upward-pointing arrows indicate increases following ACE treatment.

Colonic specimens obtained from active UC patients display markedly increased macrophage infiltration accompanied by enhanced macrophage activation.28 Macrophages are often broadly classified into the classically activated pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and the alternatively activated anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. M1-like macrophages are characterized by increased expression of markers such as CD16/32, CD80, and CD86, increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, and elevated iNOS expression. In contrast, M2-like macrophages exhibit higher expression of CD163, CD206, and Arg-1, and are associated with anti-inflammatory responses mediated by factors such as IL-4, IL-10, and TGF- β.29 In the present study, macrophage-associated changes were evaluated using complementary approaches. Flow cytometric analysis of splenic macrophages was performed to assess systemic macrophage-associated immune alterations following ACE treatment, whereas immunofluorescence staining of colonic tissues was used to evaluate local macrophage-associated marker expression within the intestinal inflammatory microenvironment. It should be noted that splenic macrophage populations may not fully represent macrophages residing in the inflamed colonic lamina propria; therefore, splenic flow cytometry findings should be interpreted as reflecting systemic immune alterations rather than direct changes in local intestinal macrophage populations.

Our results showed that ACE treatment was associated with reduced expression of M1-like macrophage-associated markers, as indicated by decreased CD68⁺CD86⁺ co-localization signals in colonic tissues, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, and downregulated iNOS expression. Meanwhile, ACE treatment was associated with increased expression of M2-like macrophage-associated markers, reflected by enhanced CD68⁺CD206⁺ co-localization signals in colonic tissues, increased IL-10 levels, and elevated Arg-1 expression. Together, these findings suggest that ACE is associated with coordinated alterations in both systemic and local macrophage-associated inflammatory profiles, although further studies using colon-derived immune cell profiling are needed to directly characterize macrophage responses within the intestinal microenvironment.

As a key cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, the cGAS-STING pathway plays an important role in regulating innate immune responses and intestinal inflammation. Elevated levels of extracellular DNA have been detected in the serum and intestinal tissues of patients with UC and in experimental models of colitis.30,31 Upon recognition of cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), cGAS catalyzes the production of the second messenger 2′3′-cGAMP. Binding of 2′3′-cGAMP to endoplasmic reticulum-resident STING induces STING activation and translocation to the Golgi apparatus, where STING recruits and activates TBK1. Activated TBK1 promotes IRF3 phosphorylation, dimerization, and nuclear translocation.32 In parallel, TBK1 activates the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, leading to IκBα degradation and subsequent NF-κB nuclear translocation. IRF3 and NF-κB cooperatively regulate the expression of IFN-β and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, thereby amplifying inflammatory responses. Clinical studies have demonstrated aberrant activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in the intestinal mucosa of patients with active UC.33 Furthermore, human colonic organoid studies have shown that combined IFN-β and TNF-α stimulation induces cGAS-STING pathway-related gene expression and promotes inflammatory injury in intestinal epithelial cells. In addition, cGAS-STING activation has been reported to promote pro-inflammatory macrophage responses, partly through chemokine signaling such as CCL2.34 In chronic inflammatory conditions such as UC, impaired intestinal barrier integrity may facilitate the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) from damaged epithelial cells. Accumulation of cytosolic mtDNA may contribute to sustained cGAS-STING activation, which is associated with enhanced inflammatory responses and intestinal tissue injury.27,35 ACE treatment was associated with reduced activation of the cGAS-STING signaling cascade and decreased expression of downstream inflammatory mediators. ACE treatment was associated with reduced expression and activation of these pathway-related proteins. Moreover, DMXAA administration partially reversed the ACE-associated suppression of downstream cGAS-STING pathway activation. Importantly, DMXAA co-administration altered ACE-associated changes in M1-like/M2-like macrophage marker expression in colonic tissues. Collectively, these findings suggest that modulation of the cGAS-STING signaling axis may contribute to the immunomodulatory effects of ACE on macrophage-associated inflammatory responses.

Maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity relies on epithelial tight junctions, an intact mucus layer, and the homeostatic regulation of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs). Excessive accumulation of M1-like macrophages in the intestinal lamina propria of patients with IBD has been associated with altered tight junction protein expression and increased IECs apoptosis, thereby contributing to impaired intestinal barrier integrity.25 In contrast, M2-like macrophages are generally associated with resolution of inflammation and maintenance of intestinal homeostasis.36 Therefore, maintaining balanced macrophage-associated immune responses and intestinal barrier integrity represents an important therapeutic goal in UC. We further investigated whether modulation of cGAS-STING signaling is associated with ACE-induced improvement of intestinal barrier-related changes. Ultrastructural analysis revealed improved intestinal epithelial integrity following ACE treatment, characterized by preserved tight junction structures and mitochondrial morphology. In addition, ACE treatment increased goblet cell numbers and enhanced the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1, in TNBS-induced colitis rats. These findings suggest that ACE-associated improvement of experimental colitis is accompanied by reduced cGAS-STING signaling activation, altered M1-like/M2-like macrophage marker expression, and improved intestinal barrier integrity.

Although the precise molecular link between ACE and cGAS-STING signaling remains to be fully elucidated, accumulating evidence from acupuncture-related interventions provides a plausible neuroimmune framework explaining how peripheral stimulation may influence innate immune responses in distant organs.37 Unlike pharmacological agents that directly target intracellular signaling molecules, acupuncture-based therapies exert biological effects through integrated regulation involving neural, endocrine, and immune networks. Previous studies have demonstrated that peripheral acupuncture stimulation can activate somatosensory afferent pathways and subsequently modulate autonomic nervous system activity, particularly the vagal cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. For instance, electroacupuncture has been shown to enhance vagal efferent activity and suppress systemic inflammatory responses through α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR)-dependent mechanisms, thereby reducing inflammatory cytokine production in experimental colitis models.15 Furthermore, recent evidence indicates that EA alleviates intestinal inflammation by promoting α7nAChR-mediated M2 macrophage polarization, restoring epithelial barrier integrity, and remodeling gut microbial homeostasis.16 These findings suggest that acupuncture-related therapies can translate peripheral mechanical stimulation into immune regulation through neuroimmune communication.

Importantly, excessive activation of innate immune DNA-sensing pathways, including the cGAS-STING axis, is closely associated with cellular stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and accumulation of endogenous DNA fragments in inflammatory disorders.38 Therefore, acupuncture-related interventions may indirectly restrain aberrant activation of the cGAS-STING signaling axis by improving inflammatory homeostasis, reducing oxidative stress, and regulating macrophage functional phenotypes.39 Consistent with this concept, our findings demonstrate that ACE treatment was associated with reduced activation of the cGAS-STING signaling axis and altered M1-like and M2-like macrophage-associated marker expression in experimental colitis. Importantly, our results do not establish STING as the direct molecular target of ACE; instead, ACE may act upstream of STING by modulating inflammatory stress, mitochondrial integrity, or endogenous DNA sensing processes.

Although our study provides meaningful insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although ACE treatment was associated with altered M1-like/M2-like macrophage marker expression and improved intestinal barrier integrity, whether macrophage-associated changes directly contribute to barrier repair remains unclear. The current study did not include macrophage-specific functional approaches, such as macrophage depletion, adoptive transfer, or epithelial functional assays using macrophage-derived factors. Second, a DMXAA-only group was not included in the present study; therefore, the possibility that DMXAA itself may influence inflammatory responses or intestinal injury cannot be completely excluded. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as indicating that STING activation can partially counteract ACE-associated protective effects rather than demonstrating that ACE directly acts through STING signaling. Third, although ACE is a clinically applied acupuncture-derived intervention, sham ACE or non-acupoint embedding groups were not included, and therefore the contribution of non-specific effects associated with local mechanical stimulation or catgut implantation procedures cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, some molecular analyses were performed using a limited number of biological replicates. Although the sample size was comparable to previous studies using similar experimental colitis models, a prospective power calculation was not performed, which represents a limitation of the present study. Future studies with larger sample sizes and formal power analyses are warranted to further validate these findings. Fifth, only male rats were included in the present study. Although UC does not exhibit a strong sex bias in clinical settings, sex-related differences in immune responses and disease susceptibility may exist; therefore, studies including female animals are needed to determine whether the effects of ACE are consistent across sexes. Finally, this study was conducted using only the TNBS-induced colitis model. Although TNBS is a widely used experimental colitis model that reproduces several features of intestinal inflammation, validation using additional models, such as dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis or other complementary models, is necessary to further establish the generalizability of the findings. Future studies incorporating these approaches are warranted to further clarify the mechanisms and translational relevance of ACE in experimental colitis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the protective effects of ACE in a rat model of UC. ACE treatment was associated with reduced activation of cGAS-STING signaling and altered M1-like/M2-like macrophage marker expression, accompanied by decreased intestinal inflammation and improved mucosal barrier integrity. These findings provide new insights into the potential immunomodulatory effects of ACE in UC and highlight the cGAS-STING pathway as a potential target for further investigation in acupuncture-based interventions.
