Development a practical method for calculation of the block volume and block surface in a fractured rock mass
Authors: Alireza Shahbazi, Ali Saeidi, Alain Rouleau, Romain Chesnaux
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces a practical method for calculating the volume and surface area of blocks formed by the intersection of three persistent joint sets in a fractured rock mass. The proposed method considers the geometrical characteristics of these joint sets, demonstrating the relationship between block volume, surface area, true spacings of joint sets, and the angle between edge vectors and the normal to joint sets. The determination of required angles is facilitated through stereographic projections of joint sets using known spacing values. Validation is performed using 3DEC version 7.0 software and field data from a quarries mine, while response surface methodology (RSM) illustrates the parameter effects. Additionally, the analysis of errors from previous models identifies the reliability range of each method. The stereographic projection is further employed to analytically determine volumetric fracture intensity (P32) by dividing block surface by block volume.Keywords: Block volume, Block surface, 3DEC, Rock mass, RSMSubject terms: Structural geology, Hydrogeology, Civil engineering

The mechanical behavior of a rock mass is influenced by various geomechanical characteristics, encompassing the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), inter-block shear strength (Jr/Ja), block size (Vb), and joint openings (Jo). Among these parameters, block geometry holds particular significance, playing a crucial role in the stability of both underground and aboveground structures6–8. In this context, several analytical models for the calculation of block volume have been developed1–4,6,7,9, as they are listed in Table 1. Nevertheless, block volumes calculated by these methods often deviate from real field data. For instance9, observed substantial differences between the calculated block volume (Vb) using analytical methods and the actual values derived from the average spacing of discontinuities (Sa), real spacing (S), and the number of volumetric joints (Jv).

In this study, building upon the established vectoral model for calculating block volume10, we have developed an analytical model. This model leverages the stereographic projection of joint sets for the purpose of block volume computation. The model’s accuracy is verified through numerical simulation utilizing 3DEC version 7 software11. Despite the noticeable discrepancy observed between the results obtained from this method (or numerical simulation) and those of all the methods listed in Table 1, an analytical equation has been formulated for block surface calculation. This equation is based on the previously developed vectoral model12–14 and has been validated through numerical simulation. It’s important to emphasize that currently, there is no existing analytical model for block surface calculation, which limits the ability to compare this method with other established models. The stereographic method for calculating both block volume and surface has been elucidated. Lastly, employing the response surface methodology (RSM), the study delves into the influence of affecting parameters on the error associated with each method listed in Table 1.

Given that precise calculation of block volume plays a crucial role in accurately estimating the mechanical and hydrological characteristics of rock masses, the findings of this study are poised to significantly influence both past and future work in these domains. Several geomechanical characteristics of rock masses were previously defined based on block volume2,7. Considering this investigation, a thorough revision of these characteristics is needed, as almost all previously developed equations for block volume calculation exhibit a limitation. Moreover, the formulation of block surface area has hitherto remained unexplored, rendering this article the inaugural investigation to introduce an analytical method for block surface calculation. As a consequential development, the analytical computation of volumetric fracture intensity (P32) becomes feasible through the integration of equations governing block volume and surface. Collectively, engineers, practitioners, and professionals engaged in the domains of rock mechanics and geology stand to benefit from a heightened level of reliability and accuracy in estimating block volume, courtesy of the equations introduced in this study. Despite certain assumptions underpinning the model’s development, such as the presumption of three persistent joint sets within a rock mass, opportunities for enhancing model comprehensiveness exist. The inclusion of additional influential parameters, such as joint persistence and the number of joint sets, holds promise for refining the model further.

Theoretical background

Based on the existing equations for calculation of the block volume that is provided in Table 1 and in spite of widespread application of Eq. 1 for this purpose15, a significant discrepancy already exists between the results of this model and field data. Accordingly, new models are developed in section 3 for calculation of the volume and surface of the block that is created by three persistent joint sets by knowing dip, dip direction and spacing of joints and using the stereographic projection method. Therefore, signification parameters that affect the volume and surface of the block are discussed in section 4.2. In addition, the output of the models is validated using both by the results of 3DEC version 7 software in section 3.1.1 and field data in section 3.1.2.

The mechanical characteristics of a rock mass mostly depend on the properties of the rock matrix and the fracture networks7,16. Fracture networks consist of joint sets, i.e., a family of parallel, evenly spaced discontinuities that can be specified by orientation (dip, dip direction), spacing, etc. Figure 1 shows the most significant parameters of a fractured rock mass.

The structural characteristics of a rock mass.

Basically, blocks are produced due to the existence of a system of discontinuities in rock mass, that its size and shape are defined by geometrical characteristics of joint sets, such as spacing, orientation, joint set number and persistence. In this article, the analytical method is used for determination of the block volume and surface and for this purpose, the ideal conditions of the discontinuities are considered. In this regard, it is assumed that joint sets are persistent and the values of spacing and orientation are fixed in a joint set. On this basis, it is assumed that the volume and surface area of the block could be determined by knowing the dip, dip direction and spacing of joint sets, and the persistence is not included for development of this model. In addition, if the rock mass contains less than three joint sets, the random joints are defining the block volume and for more than three joint sets, the block volume could be determined by considering a rock mass with three joint sets, instead15. On this basis, it is assumed that the rock mass comprises of three joint sets. It is a reasonable assumption since most of the models that are listed in Table 1 were developed based on this hypothesis. Therefore, the parameters that have been considered for development of the models are the spacing and orientation of joint sets.

Discussion

Stereographic illustration of the angles

As it is demonstrated in section 3, Eq. 9 and Eq. 10 could be used for calculation of the volume and surface of the block, respectively. However, determination of some parameters in these equations might be obscured. In this section, the method for specifying these parameters is shown by using the stereographic projection. For this purpose, the volume and surface of the blocks for a few examples of rock mass are calculated to better clarifying the issue.

In Eq. 9 and Eq. 10, the true spacings of the joint sets are assumed to be known. However, the angles θ1, θ2 and θ3 and γAB, γAC and γBC should be specified. For this purpose, Fig. 5 shows the stereographic projection of the poles and planes of three joint sets as 1, 2 and 3. According to Fig. 2, the edge vectors A, B and C are the lines of intersection between joint sets 2&3, 1&3 and 1&2, respectively. Therefore, the crossline of the planes of each pair of joint sets are the points that show the edge vectors. Accordingly, γAB, γAC and γBC are the angles between each pair of edge vector and could simply measured by the defining the angle between each pair of points A, B and C in Fig. 5. In addition, θ1, θ2 and θ3 are the angles between normal to joint set and its relevant edge vector, as it is illustrated in Fig. 2. For example, θ1 is the angle between edge vector A and normal to joint set 1 (NJ1) as it is illustrated by the angle between P1 and A in Fig. 2. θ2 and θ3 could be measured by the same method. Therefor, all the required angles in Eq. 9 and Eq. 10 could be measured by the stereographic projection of joint sets.

Stereographic projection of three joint sets and the method for measuring angles θ1, θ2 and θ3 and γA/B, γA/C and γB/C. P1, P2 and P3 are the poles of joint sets 1, 2 and 3, respectively.

To provide further clarity on the stereographic projection method, Fig. 5 has been designed to illustrate the spatial relationships between the joint sets, edge vectors, and the required angles. Each step in determining the parameters is based on the projection of the poles and planes of the joint sets. First, the intersection lines of joint sets 1&2, 1&3, and 2&3 are plotted as edge vectors A, B, and C, respectively. These vectors correspond to the crosslines of the planes of joint sets, which appear as points on the stereographic projection. Once the edge vectors are defined, the angles γAB, γAC, and γBC are calculated by measuring the angular distances between points A, B, and C on the stereographic projection. Similarly, the angles θ1, θ2, and θ3 are determined by identifying the angular relationship between each edge vector and the normal to the corresponding joint set. For instance, as shown in Fig. 2, θ1 is measured as the angle between edge vector A and the pole of joint set 1 (NJ1), depicted as the angle between P1 and A on the stereographic projection.

By combining these visual aids with a detailed explanation, the stereographic projection method provides a systematic way to identify and measure all required angles. This approach ensures that the relationships between the geometric parameters are accurately represented, facilitating the calculation of the block’s volume and surface area with precision.

Effect of parameters on block volume and surface

Following the discussion that is provided on section 3 for selection of the parameters that have effect on block volume and surface area, apart from the persistence that is not considered in this study, the spacing and orientation of the joint sets are defined to be the effective parameters that have impact on block characteristics. In this section and by using the response surface methodology (RSM), the effect of joint spacing (Si: Joint Spacing of joint set i) and the angle between them (γi: angle between joint set j and k) on the volume and surface area of the block that are calculated by Eq. 9 and Eq. 10, are evaluated. For this purpose, it is assumed that the Si and γi for three joint sets are identical and by changing the values of the parameters, the variation of block volume and surface is evaluated. In this regard, the central composite design (CCD) has been applied for identifying the required experiments by using the Design-Expert® software. In the application of the Central Composite Design (CCD) using Design-Expert software, second-order response surface models were developed to represent the relationships between input variables (Si and γi) and the block characteristics (volume and surface area). These models define spatially continuous surfaces that illustrate how changes in joint spacing and orientation affect the response variables. The surfaces constructed are typically quadratic in nature, capturing both linear and interaction effects, and are used to identify optimal or critical regions within the design space. In addition, for the range of variation of parameters, it is assumed that Si varies between 0.4 and 4 m and γi changes between 10 and 90 degrees. The equation of Latham is mostly overestimating the volume of the block. However, in γ lower than around 25°, this method underestimates the block volume. In addition, the error of this method is very sensitive to γ, as this parameter is not included in development of this model and hence, in a constant value of spacing, a large value of error produces by variation of γ.

As a conclusion to section 4.3, application of the previous methods for block volume calculation is associated with error. Since the role of angles between joint sets is not properly considered in the equations, this parameter is responsible for most of the errors associated with calculation of the block volume, while the spacing (or multiplication of spacings), is the common parameter in most of the equations and in this regard, it has no contribution in the amount of produced error. Regarding the accuracy of the models, Palmström equations give more reliable results by getting close to the orthogonal joint sets and by deviation from perpendicularity, its accuracy reduces.

Conclusion

This article introduces analytical methods designed for the precise calculation of both volume and surface area for rock blocks resulting from the intersection of three persistent joint sets, along with the determination of volumetric fracture intensity (P32). To establish a robust model, the assumption is made that the rock mass comprises three persistent joint sets, with the effective parameters identified as the true spacings and orientation of these joint sets. It is evident that the subsequent refinement of Eq. 9 and Eq. 10 will encompass the influence of additional parameters on block volume and surface, such as joint persistence and the number of joint sets. This evolving model seeks to capture a more comprehensive understanding of the rock mass characteristics and enhance the accuracy of volume and surface area calculations.

The block volume and surface area could be stereographically calculated using Eq. 9 and Eq. 10. The equation for calculation of the surface area of the block (Eq. 10) is developed for the first time through this article. Furthermore, through the development of the block volume model, it became evident that the angles utilized in previous studies for block volume calculations, such as the angle between joint sets, were inaccurately selected. The present study rectifies this by presenting the correct angles relevant to these calculations. However, it is noteworthy that the impact of true spacing on block volume, as addressed by previous models, aligns closely with the findings of the current study.

The block volume and surface area calculation by Eq. 9 and Eq. 10 represents the most reliable results among all previously developed models. This claim is validated by using the numerical simulation with 3DEC software and field data of a quarries mine, since the results of Eq. 9 and Eq. 10 are fully compatible with numerical and field data results. An often-overlooked consideration in the numerical calculation of block volume is the representativeness of the numerical model. The average block volume computed by small models may not accurately reflect the block volume of the entire rock mass. To address this concern, it is essential that the dimensions of the numerical model be sufficiently large to encompass a substantial portion of intact blocks, as exemplified in Fig. 3. This ensures a more reliable and representative estimation of the overall block volume within the rock mass.

In evaluating the influence of joint set spacing and orientation on block volume and surface, it is discerned that these factors exert both independent and interactive effects on block characteristics. Notably, the impact of spacing becomes more pronounced at smaller values of the angle between joint sets, while conversely, the effect of the orientation angle (γ) becomes more prominent at larger values. Moreover, the study reveals that the primary source of error in previous models for block volume calculation stems from the improper consideration of the angle between joint sets (γ), instead of the angle between the edge vector and the normal to the joint set (θ). Consequently, the error in previous models tends to escalate particularly when deviating from the perpendicular intersection of joint sets.

The volumetric fracture intensity (P32) is another useful parameter that is widely used in geomechanical and hydrogeological investigations of rock mass. In this article, an equation is developed for this purpose by Eq. 11 and using the stereonet. As Eq. 9 and Eq. 10 were already validated by numerical simulation, this parameter is automatically validated.
