Bacillus subtilis as cell factory for enhanced production of the biopolymer precursor pyridine-2,6-dicarboxylic acid
Authors: Taichi Chen, Haris Uzunovic, Stanley Brul, Jeroen Hugenholtz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPyridine-2,6-dicarboxylic acid (DPA) is a valuable dicarboxylic acid that has the potential to serve as a precursor for bio-sustainable and bio-degradable materials and self-healing polymers. It also plays a crucial role in the heat resistance of Bacillus subtilis spores. However, extracting DPA from spores is resource-intensive and technically complex, limiting its industrial application. To overcome these challenges, this study aims to engineer B. subtilis as a microbial cell factory for the direct production of free and soluble DPA.ResultsThis study first demonstrated that blocking sporulation reduced DPA production due to the repression of dipicolinate synthase expression. To enhance extracellular DPA production, dipicolinate synthase expression was fine-tuned, increasing the extracellular DPA titer to 330 ± 10 mg/l in strain BSDYvyDVF. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that spore coat assembly genes modulate DPA production. By disrupting a spore coat assembly activator in strain BSDYvyDVF-gerE, sporulation was successfully inhibited, significantly boosting the DPA yield to 944 ± 3 mg/l. Further optimization of fermentation conditions was performed using an orthogonal design. The highest DPA titer of 1250 mg/l was achieved and validated through fed-batch fermentation in a 1.5-l bioreactor.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the potential of engineered B. subtilis BSDYvyDVF-gerE as an efficient cell factory for sustainable DPA biosynthesis. In addition, it identifies key challenges in DPA production, including synthesis efficiency (regulation of key enzyme expression) and transport (intracellular-to-extracellular export), and proposes corresponding solutions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02859-x.Keywords: Biopolymer, Dicarboxylic acid, Dipicolinic acid, Spore coat assembly, Fermentation optimization

Introduction

With functional properties such as lightweight, robust, and cheap, a rapidly growing number of plastic products (polymers) have become part of the human society since the 1950s [1]. Till now, polymers have played a crucial role in multiple fields, including the packaging industry [2–4], household good [5], building construction [6], sports safety gear [7], and medical applications [8]. However, the strive for durability and robustness of the polymers hinder their complete absorption and degradation [9, 10] in the natural environment. Currently, approximately seven million tons of plastic waste are generated annually, with 50% comprising single-use plastics used for packaging; yet less than 10% of this plastic is re-used or recycled [2, 11–13]. The primary methods for plastic waste management currently encompass physical and chemical recycling, landfilling, incineration [14, 15], and biodegradation [16]. These various approaches of plastic waste management would be significantly improved by development and use of more biodegradable polymers.

Dicarboxylic acids, including long-chain and cyclic types, are important constituents of biodegradable materials since each molecule contains two reactive groups making it suitable for polymerization reactions [17, 18]. Among the different types of dicarboxylic acids produced in nature, pyridine-2,6-dicarboxylic acid (dipicolinic acid, DPA) stands out with a heterocyclic aromatic structure combined with polymerization potential leading to biomaterials with both flexible and robust properties. The biopolymer products derived from DPA are not only versatile but also exhibit enhanced thermal stability and chemical resistance [19], distinguishing them from other biopolymer precursors such as lactic acid [20], adipic acid [21] and terephthalic acid [22]. For example, a novel polyester synthesized by polymerizing DPA has been tested as a sustainable and reliable material for food packaging [23]. Despite the potential of DPA as a bio-based monomer, its production currently relies predominantly on chemical processing of coal tar [24]. Therefore, the microbial synthesis of DPA could significantly advance its future applications in the bio-based economy.

DPA is a ubiquitous molecule occupying approximately 25% of the spore core’s dry weight in spores of the Bacillus and Clostridium species [25]. In spores, DPA chelates with calcium and plays an important role in the heat resistance of spores and initiation of germination. Although the DPA content in Clostridium and Bacillus spores are similar, the use of Clostridium species in industrial applications is often hindered by challenges such as anaerobic growth requirements. Bacillus species, as aerobes, can be grown to much higher cell densities, potentially leading to much higher DPA-production. Additionally, clear genetic background, pathway information, simple nutrient requirements and various genetic engineering tools for B. subtilis make it a more economically viable option for large-scale production (Fig. 1). It has been reported that different B. subtilis strains showed varying ability to synthesizing DPA. Among the most intriguing industrial-related strains, PS832 showed 126 mg of DPA per gram of spores, which is 68% higher than the most widely used model strain B. subtilis 168, yet DPA is undetectable in the fermentation medium [26]. It implies that as a potential microbial cell factory, PS832, despite its significant DPA synthesis capacity, largely sequesters DPA within the spores.

In this study, B. subtilis PS832 was developed as a cell factory to efficiently produce and secrete DPA. First, specific stages of the sporulation process were inhibited by knocking out key genes to enhance the DPA production in vegetative cells or medium, such as the initiation of sporulation (spo0A-null), septum formation (spoIIE-null), and cortex formation (spoIVB-null). Subsequently, genetic engineering was employed to relieve the inhibition of DPA synthase expression. Transcriptomic analysis of the high-yield DPA strain identified that a perturbed assembly of the spore coat was crucial for DPA overproduction. Finally, the cultivation conditions of the high-yield strain were optimized, and its industrial production potential was validated through fed-batch fermentation in a 1.5-l bioreactor.

Results

Disrupting sporulation inhibited DPA synthesis

To investigate the effects of inhibiting spore formation on DPA synthesis and transport, three spore-deficient mutant strains: BSD0A, BSDIIE, and BSDIVB were developed by knocking out spo0A, spoIIE and spoIVB, respectively. After 72 h of cultivation, the wild-type B. subtilis PS832 exhibited an 83% sporulation rate, whereas all three mutants didn’t produce any spores (Fig. 2A). Upon the disruption of spore synthesis, the three mutant strains displayed a similar growth pattern: they grew faster than PS832 during the first 12 h, but their growth was suppressed by over 53% at 24 h (Fig. 2B). Specifically, the earlier the stage at which spore formation was blocked, the more pronounced the growth inhibition (BSD0A (Δspo0A) > BSDIIE (ΔspoIIE) > BSDIVB (ΔspoIVB)).

Surprisingly, blocking spore formation severely suppressed DPA production in both supernatant and pellets. By the 6th hour of the exponential growth phase, the OD600 of BSD0A was 34% higher than that of PS832 (Fig. 2B). However, DPA levels in the supernatant and pellet of BSD0A were 0.0 ± 0.0 and 0.2 ± 0.0 mg/l, respectively, in contrast to 0.9 ± 0.1 mg/l in both fractions for PS832 (Fig. 2C). After 72 h, the DPA concentration in the PS832 pellet reached 10.2 ± 0.9 mg/l, attributed to the presence of spores. In comparison, the BSD0A, BSDIIE and BSDIVB showed only 1.2 ± 0.0 mg/l of DPA in their pellets, suggesting the DPA synthesis was repressed in the spore-deficient mutants. The relative transcriptional strength of the crucial enzyme - dipicolinate synthase (SpoVF) for DPA synthesis, was reduced by 75% in all three mutant strains compared to the PS832 strain (Supplementary Fig. 1). It suggests that directly inhibiting spore formation in PS832 interfered with the normal expression of dipicolinate synthase, resulting in decreased DPA production.

Deregulating transcriptional control of dipicolinate synthase improved DPA production

To determine the effect of overexpressing the dipicolinate synthase which is regulated by the late mother cell sporulation regulator sigma factor SigK during the vegetative stage on growth and DPA production, gene spoVF was first integrated into the pDG148 vector and was expressed by the Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG)-inducible promoter Pspac. Then the integrated plasmid pDGVF was introduced to a spoVF-null mutant, resulting in BSPDF which lacks the spoVF gene in the genome but harbors a plasmid that allows for the inducible expression of spoVF (Fig. 3A). The expression of spoVF was induced at the 3rd and 12th hours of fermentation using two different concentrations of IPTG, to investigate the impact of induction timing and intensity on spoVF expression. As a result, the transcription level of spoVF at the 24th hours, as well as the growth and DPA production of the BSDPF strain, showed no big difference between the induction of 0.5 mM and 1 mM of IPTG. On the other hand, the timing of induction significantly affects both the growth of the strain and DPA production. Inducing spoVF expression at the 3rd hour of fermentation resulted in approximately 61% of growth inhibition during the first 24 h (Fig. 3B). In contrast, induction at the 12th hour didn’t affect growth, which remained comparable to that of the wild type and the non-induced mutant strain. After 72 h of fermentation, the total DPA produced in the fermentation broth and cells of the mutant strain was 3.2 mg/l for the uninduced condition, 194.1 mg/l for induction at the 3rd hour, and 149.0 mg/l for induction at the 12th hour (with 1 mM of IPTG) (Fig. 3C). It implies that although early expression of spoVF may impact growth due to the metabolic burden, it can significantly improve the production of DPA.

Then, the native promoter of gene spoVF in the PS832 was replaced with a constitutive promoter PyvyD from PS832, resulting in the mutant BSDYvyDVF (Fig. 3A). Compared to PS832, the BSDYvyDVF strain exhibited 43% growth inhibition after 24 h of fermentation (Fig. 3B). However, the BSDYvyDVF cells continued to grow after 48 h, whereas for PS832 an OD600 decrease of 6 was observed after 48 h (Fig. 2B). Regarding DPA production, the DPA concentration in the BSDYvyDVF fermentation supernatant consistently surpassed that of both the PS832 and BSDPF. After 72 h, the DPA concentrations in the BSDYvyDVF fermentation supernatant and pellets reached 330.0 ± 10.0 mg/l and 3.0 ± 0.0 mg/l, respectively (Fig. 3C). Meanwhile, no DPA was detected in the fermentation supernatant of PS832, likely due to the stringent regulation of intracellular DPA synthesis and transport under normal conditions. In general, the spoVF-overexpressed BSDYvyDVF showed potential as a DPA microbial production chassis.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed spore coat assembly genes to be modulators of DPA overproduction

In the BSDYvyD strain, further strategies such as enhancing precursor supply (L-aspartate supplementation) and knocking out competing pathways (acetoin biosynthesis) were tested (Supplementary Fig. 2). However, none of these approaches produced a positive effect on DPA yield comparable to that achieved by spoVF overexpression. To elucidate the metabolic impacts of enhanced SpoVF expression, a comparative analysis was conducted between the mutant strain BSDVegVF and the wild-type strain PS832. BSDVegVF employs the stronger Pveg promoter instead of the PyvyD promoter, to drive SpoVF expression, which has led to increased DPA production. Therefore, BSDVegVF was subjected to further study of the influence of increased SpoVF expression on DPA overproduction. Transcriptomic analysis of the DPA overproducer BSDVegVF (PS832; spoVF :: Pveg-spoVF) in comparison with the wild type PS832 was performed at the 6th hours and the 24th hours as described in the material and methods. By the 6th and 24th hours, the transcriptional strength of genes in the lysine synthesis pathway was significantly elevated. Notably, the expression level of spoVF increased by over 100-fold, while the asd-dapG-dapA gene cluster exhibited at least a 16-fold increase in transcriptional activity (Fig. 4A). It implies that the overproduction of DPA necessitates the proper overexpression of genes within the lysine synthesis pathway.

In addition to the upregulated genes, many of the severely downregulated genes were associated with spore coat assembly. For example, after 24 h, the expression level of the cotX and cotV were repressed by 41-fold and 45-fold, respectively (Fig. 4B). It may be correlated with the substantial secretion of DPA into the environment by BSDYvyDVF, as DPA is typically tightly regulated and transported into the spore under normal conditions in PS832. Furthermore, the Gene Ontology (GO) cellular component enrichment analysis was performed. It showed that the pathways involved from forespore formation to spore maturation and spore coat assembly were significantly inhibited (Fig. 4C and D). Given the downregulated genes and the enrichment analysis, we proposed that incorrect formation of spore coat may contribute to the overproduction of DPA.

Disturbing spore coat assembly enhanced intracellular and extracellular DPA concentration

Nine overlapping genes were observed at 6–24 h when the top 20 down-regulated genes in the DPA overproducer was listed out (Fig. 5A). Then, six mutants containing proper gene knockouts were developed to determine the effect of these genes on the DPA production. After 72 h of shake flasks fermentation, all of the six mutants produced higher amount of DPA than the BSDYvyDVF (Table 1). What’s more, most of these genes were related to the spore coat assembly and activated by the GerE, GerR or SpoIIID, which are responsible for the correct assembly of spore coat. Hence, three single gene knockout mutants were first developed to investigate the effect of activators on the DPA production and sporulation process. It turned out that all mutants showed strongly inhibited sporulation but only BSDYvyDVF-gerE (BSDYvyDVF, ΔgerE) showed further improvement of DPA production (Fig. 5B). The deletion of GerE and GerR resulted in 944 ± 3 mg/l and 798 ± 18 mg/l of DPA production, respectively. While deleting the GerE and GerR genes together in BSDYvyDVF, the concentration of DPA in the medium became significantly lower (210 ± 34 mg/l). Meanwhile, the deletion of SpoIIID resulted in no DPA in the medium (Fig. 5B). To achieve higher extracellular DPA production, the GerE knockout strain BSDYvyDVF-gerE was used for further investigation.

With the high yield of DPA in the supernatant, only 3.2 mg/l of DPA was detected in the pellet of BSDYvyDVF-gerE. It seems that the higher yield in the supernatant of BSDYvyDVF-gerE was because of better capacity of secretion of DPA. To determine the DPA concentration per cell, the sporulation rate of BSDYvyDVF-gerE was first determined at 6th hour and 72nd hour. Serious inhibition on the sporulation at both timepoints (no spores) was observed, suggesting that all DPA production detected in the pellets was stored in the vegetative cells. Then, the DPA concentration was calculated into per cell of BSDYvyDVF and BSDYvyDVF-gerE as described in the material and methods. After 6 h fermentation, the intracellular and extracellular concentrations of DPA of BSDYvyDVF-gerE were 2.8 ± 0.3 g/l and 23.7 ± 0.2 mg/l, respectively, representing increases of 33% and 19% compared to BSDYvyDVF. It indicated that the knockout of GerE may lead to improper assembly of the spore coat, which could enhance DPA accumulation within the vegetative cells and result in higher secretion of DPA into the culture medium.

Optimizing fermentation parameters and scaling up in a 1.5-l bioreactor

To determine the best condition to increase the DPA production of BSDYvyDVF-gerE, a set of three levels orthogonal experiments was designed to optimize the fermentation parameters, including glucose concentration, yeast extract concentration shaking speed and inoculation level (Table 2). The final DPA titer of the strain BSDYvyDVF-gerE cultured in shake flasks was used as the evaluation index. The ratio of the F-distribution (F-value) that represents the influence and importance of a control factor was determined for each factor (higher F-value, greater influence). Depending on the analysis of the F-distribution (F-value), the impact intensity of four factors on the fermentation production of DPA was glucose > shaking speed > yeast extract > > inoculation level. The optimal fermentation medium for the production of DPA consisted of the highest carbon source (40 g/l of glucose) and nitrogen source (7.5 g/l of yeast extract) while being incubated at 37 °C and shaken at 180 rpm (Fig. 6A). When BSDYvyDVF-gerE was fermented in the optimized conditions, the final DPA titer reached 972.2 ± 5.0 mg/l. This was the highest titer which was observed throughout the experiments.

To further assess the DPA production capacity of BSDYvyDVF-gerE, a fed-batch fermentation was performed in a 1.5-l bioreactor using the optimized medium. During the fermentation process, dissolved oxygen (DO) was maintained at approximately 40% by controlling the agitation speed and airflow rate (Fig. 6B). The glucose concentration was maintained at about 20 g/l through pulse feeding. At the 5th hour of fermentation, we observed 64 mg/l of DPA production in the supernatant. DPA accumulated significantly between the 12th and 42nd hours, with production showing little fluctuation during the initial and final stages of fermentation. After 48 h, the extracellular DPA production peaked at 1250 mg/l and the specific production rate of DPA reached 10.4 mg/gDCW/h.

Discussion

In this study, B. subtilis PS832, a strain that does not secrete DPA into the medium was selected and the expression levels of the dipicolinate synthase in PS832 was fine-tuned to realize high DPA production. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that the attenuated natural spore maturation positively influenced the extracellular DPA production. As a result, a mutant strain, BSDYvyDVF-gerE was developed. BSDYvyDVF-gerE is spore-deficient and exhibits high DPA yield in both shake flasks and a 1.5-l bioreactor. The DPA titer and specific production rate of DPA was 1250 mg/l and 10.4 mg/gDCW/h, respectively. Although the DPA titer achieved in BSDYvyDVF-gerE is lower than the 1.5 g/l reported for Corynebacterium glutamicum [29] and 4.7 g/l in Escherichia coli [30], BSDYvyDVF-gerE exhibits comparable or superior productivity. Additionally, in contrast to other studies, BSDYvyDVF-gerE was subjected to only two rounds of genetic modification, laying the foundation for further optimization to the generally required tens of grams per liter for a suitable biproduction process.

In wild-type B. subtilis, DPA is synthesized during the late stage of sporulation and is transported from the mother cell into the immature spore via enzyme-mediated transport. Therefore, three genes essential for spore formation were initially knocked out to investigate whether DPA could accumulate extracellularly in the absence of spore incorporation, thereby reducing the complexity of downstream separation. Spo0A is essential for initiating sporulation [31], spoIIE for asymmetric septation [32], and spoIVB for cortex formation [33]. Each deletion was designed to disrupt a distinct checkpoint in the sporulation process, with the evidence that progressively later-stage disruptions would affect fewer genes and thus cause smaller perturbations to overall cellular metabolism. The results showed that, although blocking sporulation at any of these stages led to low DPA concentrations in vegetative cells, it also reduced the transcription level of spoVF, ultimately resulting in decreased DPA synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, by either plasmid-based or genome-integrated constitutive overexpression of spoVF, substantial DPA was synthesized and its subsequent transport to the extracellular environment. It indicates that spoVF expression is a key limiting factor in DPA biosynthesis and that its upregulation can effectively enhance both production and secretion.

To investigate the efficient DPA secretion associated with SpoVF overexpression, DPA levels in spores and vegetative cells were quantified. As extracellular DPA concentration increased, a gradual decrease in the DPA content was observed within spores of the mutant strains: PS832 (126 mg/gspore) >BSDYvyDVF (99 mg/gspore) >BSDYvyDVF-gerE (no spores). Transcriptome analysis revealed that the expression of spore coat genes was markedly reduced in high-DPA-yielding strains. Specifically, the gene encoding the crust cotV was downregulated by a factor of 45, the outer coat protein cotB was downregulated by a factor of 27, and the inner coat protein cotF was downregulated by a factor of 13 (Supplementary Table 3). It has been reported that the disruption of some spore crust assembly genes such as cotVWX and cgeAB could trigger worse crust structure [34, 35] and thinner spore polysaccharide [36]. In this study, it may contribute to the intracellular accumulation of DPA due to the inappropriate transportation of DPA. In support, there was no DPA found in the BSDYvyDVF-spoIIID medium which suggests the competition of DPA between transport to the spore and secretion in the environment as the spore DPA-transporter SpoVV was overexpressed in BSDYvyDVF-spoIIID [37]. Consequently, investigating the expression of spore coat formation proteins and the mechanisms involved in DPA transport into the spores could provide opportunities to further increase the extracellular yield of DPA.

At the sixth hour of fermentation, the DPA concentrations in BSDYvyDVF-gerE cells and the environment reached 2.8 g/l and 23.7 mg/l, respectively. By calculating the Gibbs free energy required for DPA transport across various intracellular and extracellular concentration gradients, it turns out that for extracellular/intracellular DPA concentration ratios ranging from 0.001 to 1000, the free energy required to transport 1 mol of DPA ranges from − 17 kJ to 17 kJ. This is within the range of spontaneous reactions (-30 to 30 kJ), suggesting that the accumulation of extracellular DPA is likely to progress by diffusion without the need for specific transport proteins. To further enhance DPA transport efficiency, developing mutant strains with dicarboxylate transport proteins that can specifically bind and transport DPA over the plasma membrane may be explored.

Conclusions

In this study, a recombinant B. subtilis strain, BSDYvyDVF-gerE was constructed, which demonstrates a robust capacity for industrial-scale DPA production. The metabolic pathway responsible for its overproduction was proposed (Table 3): The overexpression of dipicolinate synthase SpoVF leads to intracellular DPA accumulation while simultaneously reducing DPA’s incorporation into spores by downregulating key spore coat formation genes. The accumulated DPA is then transported into the environment via passive diffusion. Therefore, modifying DPA membrane permeability by expression of specific (facilitated) transporter proteins might further enhance DPA secretion.

Strains used in this study

The lysogeny broth (LB) medium was used for strain propagation and consisted of 5 g/l Yeast extract, 10 g/l Tryptone, and 5 g/l NaCl. The fermentation medium M9YE was adapted from general M9 salt medium and was composed of 40 g/l glucose, 5 g/l yeast extract, 6.78 g/l Na2HPO4, 3 g/l KH2PO4, 1 g/l NH4Cl, 0.5 g/l NaCl, 0.25 g/l MgSO4∙7H2O, 0.02 g/l CaCl2·2H2O, 0.01 g/l FeCl3·6H2O, 10 ml/l of 100 ×Trace elements solution. 100 × Trace elements solution consists of 0.17 g/l ZnCl2, 0.1 g/l MnSO4·4H2O, 0.06 g/l CoCl2·6H2O, 0.06 g/l Na2MoO4·2H2O, 0.043 g/l CuCl2·H2O. For washing of the strains, a 10 × Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) solution was prepared with 35.81 g/l Na2HPO4.12H2O, 2.45 g/l KH2PO4, 80.07 g/l NaCl, 2.01 g/l KCl. All media were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min.

Construction of B. subtilis mutant strains

The primers and DNA templates used for gene amplification are listed in Supplementary Table 1. For gene knock out experiments, the homogenous arms of target-gene were amplified from B. subtilis PS832 and the erythromycin resistance gene was amplified from BKE02310 by a high-yield DNA polymerase instruction (2 × Phanta Flash Master Mix DNA polymerase, Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For in situ replacement of promoters, the sequence of PyvyD promoter was added right after erythromycin resistance gene and before target gene using specific primers. Then three fragments (upstream homogenous arm - erythromycin resistance gene - downstream homogenous arms) were integrated by overlapping polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and purified using a DNA purification kit (MSB® Spin PCRapace, Invitek, Germany). The purified DNA was then transformed into B. subtilis cells by the method of Anagnostopoulos & Spizizen, resulting in the mutants of B. subtilis strains [27]. The concentrations of the fragments for overlapping PCR and transformation were set as 150 ng/µl and 1500 ng/ml. Colony PCR was then performed with specific primers for verification and sequencing.

Shake flask fermentation and fed-batch fermentation

Cultures of strains were taken from glycerol stocks stored at -80 °C and were streaked on LB agar petri dishes which were then placed in a 37 °C incubator overnight. For each strain, one colony was picked and transferred into a 50 ml Erlenmeyer flask loaded with 10 ml of LB medium. After 6 h of incubation, the cultures were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 2 min, and the resulting pellets were washed three times with 1 × PBS. Finally, the pellets were resuspended in 1 × PBS to obtain OD600 of. Then flasks containing 50 ml of M9YE medium (supplemented with kanamycin at 100 µg/ml for plasmid-bearing strains ) were inoculated with 500 µl of the resuspended culture, resulting in the initial OD600 is 0.1 for each flask. The inoculated flasks were placed in a shaker at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 72 h.

Fed-batch fermentation was conducted with a 1.5-l of Multifors-2 parallel bioreactor system (Infors, Switzerland). The initial medium consisted of 600 mL of M9YE medium containing 7.5 g/l of yeast extract, with a starting pH of 7. The initial agitation speed was set at 600 rpm, and the aeration rate was 0.6 ml/min. For each reactor, we prepared 200 ml of 500 g/l glucose solution for feeding and 200 ml of 28% ammonia solution for pH adjustment. The seed culture steps were the same as those described for the shake flask fermentation experiments, except that the LB volume was 80 ml in a 250 ml flask, and the initial OD600 in the fermenter was set to 0.3.

Total RNA extraction and quantitative Real-time (qRT) PCR analysis

One ml samples were drawn from shake-flask cultures at the 6th and 24th hour of fermentation, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10 min. After freezing the samples were kept at -80 °C until RNA purification. Total RNA was purified by using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, German) according to the manual. The quantity and purity of RNA was measured by absorbance measurements at 260 and 280 nm using a Nanodrop DS-11 spectrophotometer (DeNovix, US). Subsequently, cDNA, which was obtained by reverse transcription of total RNA using a PrimeScript TM RT-PCR Kit (Bio-RAD, Japan), was used as the template for qPCRs. qPCR was performed in a 96-well plate with a total reaction volume of approximately 20 µl using SYBR Premix Ex Taq TM (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s specifications. The reactions were conducted with the Applied Biosystems 7300 real time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). The hbsU gene was used as the internal standard [28]. The primers used for qRT-PCR was shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Transcriptomic analysis

In BSDVegVF, SpoVF is expressed under the stronger Pveg promoter, instead of the PyvyD promoter, leading to higher DPA production. However, issues with DPA production stability arose during subsequent strategic layering, and thus BSDVegVF was solely used to investigate the effects of enhanced SpoVF gene expression on DPA yield. Cells of PS832 and BSDVegVF cells were harvested at 6 h and 24 h for total RNA extraction described above. The total RNA sequencing was performed by the RNA Biology & Applied Bioinformatics Group of the University of Amsterdam. Data sets consisted of about 16 M clean reads per sample with a single-end 75 bp read length. The raw data were uploaded to the website Galaxy (https://usegalaxy.eu/) for further analysis. First, the sequencing quality was checked by the FastQC function in Galaxy. Then the adaptor (TruSeq2, single-ended, SLIDEWINDOW: 4:20) and bad reads (MINLEN: 36) were cut using the Trimmomatic function. Afterwards, the RNA-seq data were assembled and analyzed by comparing it with the translational region of annotated DNA sequence data of reference B. subtilis 168 (GenBank accession number: GCA_000009045.1) by Bowtie2 and Featurecounts functions. Lastly, DESeq2 function in Galaxy was used for determining differentially expressed features in two strains at two timepoints. Log2(fold change) of 1 was used as a threshold, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was set at 0.05. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure was applied to adjust the p-values obtained from the significance testing of gene expression differences. To investigate the biological variation between samples, PCA analysis was performed for feature counts results. The sample-to-sample distances, dispersion estimates and histogram of p-values were performed as well to ensure data quality and the appropriateness of the statistical models used. The GO enrichment was performed on ShinyGO0.80 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) with an FDR cutoff of 0.5.

Central carbon metabolites and DPA measurement

One ml of fermentation culture was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter and diluted 10-fold with deionized water prior to high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis. The pellet was resuspended in 1 ml phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to an OD600 of 2, followed by incubation at 100 °C for 1 h to ensure complete release of DPA from cells and spores. The suspension was subsequently centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was filtered (0.22 μm) and diluted 10-fold before HPLC measurement. The standards of DPA, lactate, acetate, acetoin and glucose were prepared with a set of concentration within 0–50 mM. As described in our previous study, all standards and samples were measured by HPLC using a Shimadzu instrument (LC-20AT, Prominence, Shimadzu) equipped with an Ion exclusion Rezex ROA-Organic Acid H+(8%) column (300 × 7.8 mm column, Phenomenex), along with a guard column (Phenomenex). Aqueous H2SO4 (5 mM) was used as mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min at 55 °C. A cell temperature of 40 °C was set for refractive index detector (RID 20 A, Shimadzu).

Calculation of DPA content per cell

Colony-forming unit (CFU) counts were performed to determine the amount of DPA produced per cell. All measurements were conducted in triplicate. At the same time intervals as the fermentation experiments, 1 ml samples were collected and their OD600 measured. Each sample was adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0 with PBS and divided into two 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tubes. One tube was plated directly on LB agar to enumerate total viable vegetative cells. The second tube was heat-treated at 72 °C for 20 min to inactivate vegetative cells and allow subsequent germination of spores, and then plated on LB agar. All plates were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h, The difference between CFU counts from the untreated and heat-treated samples allowed quantification of vegetative cells and sporulation rates, ensuring that the measurement of DPA from vegetative cells was not affected by spores. Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD). Two biological replicates were measured for biomass and DPA measurement. Three replicates were taken for qRT-PCR experiments and RNA-seq sampling. Statistical analyses like Ordinary One-way ANOVA (Tukey) were carried out using Prism 10 GraphPad software (significance level at 0.05).
