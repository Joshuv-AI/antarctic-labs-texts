Paediatric Primary Intracranial Tumours: A 14-year Clinicopathologic Study in North-Central Nigeria
Authors: Philip Ojile Akpa, Barka Vandi Kwaghe, Innocent Emmanuel, Christiana Nwanneka Ibeanu, James Eveshojaime Ben, Jacob Anzaku Emmanuel, Jeneral Dumura Alfin, Danaan Joseph Shilong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Paediatric intracranial tumours are the commonest solid neoplasms affecting children with increasing incidence worldwide. They are second only to leukaemia in incidence and have the highest rate of mortality among childhood cancers. The aim of this study is to determine the age and sex distribution, histopathological subtypes, WHO tumour grades and neuroanatomical sites of occurrence of paediatric primary intracranial tumours diagnosed histologically in a tertiary health centre in north-central Nigeria.Methodology:This was a retrospective descriptive study of primary intracranial tumours histologically diagnosed in children (0-17 years) over a 14-year period at the Jos University Teaching Hospital, Jos, Plateau State, Nigeria. Data on age, sex, tumour site, histological diagnosis, tumour grade and clinical presentation were retrieved and analysed using descriptive statistics.Results:Patient ages ranged from 6 weeks to 17 years with a mean age of 8.99 ± 5.26 years. Peak diagnosis occurred in children 0-4 years (30.8%) with overall female predominance (M: F=1:1.26). The most common tumour was pilocytic astrocytoma (23.1%), followed by medulloblastoma (15.4%). A predominance of low-grade (73.1%) over high-grade tumours (26.9%) was observed, and tumours were equally distributed between the supratentorial and infratentorial compartments. Headache (67.3%) was the most common presenting symptom, followed by impaired vision (30.8%) and gait disturbance (26.9%).Conclusion:The clinicopathologic pattern of primary intracranial tumours observed in our study is broadly consistent with regional and global reports. An improvement in diagnostic capacity and collaborative research in this field is necessary for improving outcomes.

Introduction

Primary intracranial tumours are the most common solid neoplasms in the paediatric age group and are second only to leukaemia in childhood cancer incidence. [1,2] They represent a significant cause of morbidity and are the class of cancers with the highest rate of mortality in childhood. [1,3] The incidence of childhood brain tumours is increasing worldwide, with the social burden highest in low-income countries, which are least equipped to deal with the burden. [4-7] Nigeria is poorly equipped in both manpower and facilities geared towards the management of paediatric brain tumours, with an estimated 2000 childhood deaths attributed to this disease in Nigeria in 2016. [4,8] Treatment is challenging despite advances in diagnostic imaging, surgical techniques and adjuvant therapies. [9-11] The diverse biological behaviour and critical location of these tumours within the developing brain contribute to the considerable morbidity and mortality associated with them. [11]

Primary intracranial tumours in children differ significantly from those in adults in terms of epidemiology, histological subtypes, topographical distribution and biological behaviour. [11-13] Brain tumours exhibit a bimodal age distribution, with the first peak occurring in children and the second peak in older adults, usually from the 5th to 7th decades. [11] Within the paediatric age group, the types of lesions diagnosed in early and late childhood also vary. [15,16] The sex distribution of paediatric intracranial tumours varies from study to study; overall, a male predominance is commonly reported. [17-23] While a male bias is clearly established for tumours such as ependymoma and medulloblastoma, a sex predilection is however not seen in other common tumours such as pilocytic astrocytoma and craniopharyngioma. [15,21]

The World Health Organisation (WHO) classification of tumours of the central nervous system has undergone significant revisions over the years, incorporating molecular and genetic markers that refine diagnostic accuracy and prognostic stratification. [16,24] Histomorphological assessment, however, remains a cornerstone in diagnosis despite these advancements, especially in resource-poor settings where advanced diagnostics are scarce or non-existent. [25] A wide array of histopathological entities is encompassed under paediatric intracranial tumours, and accurate elucidation of the various tumours is necessary for both treatment and prognostication. The most common tumours include pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma, ependymoma, and craniopharyngioma. [16,26] The tumours are graded from I to IV based on biologic behaviour and morphology; however, this characterization does not clearly determine outcome, as both high- and low-grade tumours cause fatal raised intracranial pressure and neurologic sequelae. [16,27] Available literature reveals that low-grade tumours (grades I and II) outnumber high-grade tumours (grades III and IV); the most common low-grade tumour diagnosed in children is pilocytic astrocytoma, while the most common high-grade tumour is medulloblastoma. [26,28]

Supratentorial tumours significantly outnumber infratentorial tumours in adults, while paediatric brain tumours show either a clear-cut infratentorial predominance or occasional slight supratentorial bias. [27,29,30] The ratio of supratentorial to infratentorial tumours may vary from early to late childhood. [16,17] Medulloblastoma, pilocytic astrocytoma and ependymoma are frequently infratentorial while craniopharyngiomas and diffuse gliomas commonly localize to the supratentorial region. [15,26,31]

The clinical manifestations of intracranial tumours are widely variable and depend on their anatomical localization, size and rate of growth. [5,20] The nonspecific nature of these symptoms in children, in addition to lack of facilities in low-income settings, often leads to diagnostic delay. [17,19,27,31] A high clinical suspicion followed by neuroimaging and histopathological confirmation is therefore necessary for a prompt diagnosis and a favourable outcome.

This study aims to determine the age and sex distribution, histopathological subtypes, WHO tumour grades and neuroanatomical distribution of primary intracranial tumours of children.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective descriptive study of paediatric primary intracranial tumours diagnosed histologically at the Jos University Teaching Hospital, Jos, Plateau State, Nigeria.

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Departments of Neurosurgery and Anatomic Pathology and Forensic Medicine of the Jos University Teaching Hospital, which serves as a referral centre for the Sub-region.

Study population

The study included all paediatric patients (0-17 years) with histologically confirmed primary intracranial tumours diagnosed between January 2012 and December 2025. The Anatomic pathology laboratory maintains an archive of histology slides, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks and histopathological reports.

Sample Size and Sampling Method

Total population sampling was employed as eligible cases of paediatric primary intracranial tumours diagnosed during the fourteen-year period were included using a census sampling technique.

Inclusion criteria

Paediatric patients (17 years and below at the time of diagnosis), histologically confirmed primary intracranial tumours, availability of complete clinical records, histopathological records and histologic slides/formalin fixed paraffin embedded tissue blocks.

Exclusion criteria

Metastatic brain tumours, cases with missing slides who also have inadequate or poorly preserved FFPE tissue blocks, cases with incomplete clinical or demographic information, and non-neoplastic lesions.

Data collection

a. Identification and retrieval of cases

Cases were identified using the pathology department report archives, the hospital's electronic database and manual records. Demographic data such as age and sex, along with clinical presentation and site of tumour were collected. Archived slides (and FFPE tissue blocks in cases of missing slides) for each case were also retrieved.

b. Histopathological review

Archived Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained slides and freshly cut slides from FFPE blocks where necessary were reviewed by at least two qualified pathologists involved in the study to confirm diagnosis. Immunohistochemistry was applied as an adjunct to histology for diagnostic confirmation in some selected cases such as equivocal cases of medulloblastoma and ependymoma. Tumour typing and grading were performed according to the histopathological tumour types recognised in the 2021 edition of WHO classification of tumours of the central nervous system. [24]

Data analysis

Data obtained were entered into SPSS and analysed using descriptive statistics. Descriptive statistics determined frequencies, percentages, means and medians. The distribution of tumour types, age, sex, site of tumour, WHO tumour grades and clinical presentation were presented in tables and charts.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Jos University Teaching Hospital health research ethics committee

dated 15th of June 2026 with reference number NHREC/JUTH/05/10/25. Informed consent was waived in this study in accordance with institutional guidelines as this is a retrospective study using anonymized information that did not have patient identifiers. Patient confidentiality was therefore maintained.

Results

Fifty-three (53) cases of primary intracranial tumour were diagnosed within the 14 years covered by the study, with 52 meeting the inclusion criteria and one (1) excluded for incomplete data. An average of 3.7 cases were diagnosed per year. Patient ages ranged from 6 weeks to 17 years, with a mean age of 8.99 ± 5.26 years (10.26 ± 5.09 years for males and 7.99 ± 5.26 years for females). Children between 0-4 years (30.8%) constituted the largest age group affected (Table 1).

Discussion

Brain tumours account for approximately 21.0% of all childhood malignancies, with the highest incidence in the United States, where it is the commonest cause of paediatric cancer death. [32,33] They differ from their adult counterpart clinically, histologically and neuroanatomically. [34,35] In this study, patient ages ranged from 6 weeks to 17 years with a mean age of 8.99 ± 5.26 years. The mean age correlates with the mean age of 9 years reported by Ndubuisi et al in Enugu, Nigeria. [31] It is higher than the mean age of 8 years reported by Raiz et al in Pakistan and slightly below the mean age of 9.95 years reported by Gandhi et al in India. [19,33] These mean ages, however, fall within a narrow age bracket. The patients aged 0-4 years constituted the largest age group affected in this study, a finding that correlates with the report by Ogun et al in Ibadan, Nigeria and Suresh et al in southern India. [18,20] Our age group of peak occurrences is, however, lower than the peaks reported by several authors in Nigeria and elsewhere, whereby peaks occurred in children older than 4 years. [12,13,19,33,36] Pilocytic astrocytoma (mean age at diagnosis of 7.1 ± 4.80 years) and ependymoma (mean age at diagnosis 6.6 ± 5.91 years) were the commonest tumours diagnosed in patients ≤ 4 years in this study.

A female predominance (male to female ratio of 1:1.26) was observed in this study, which aligns with the observations of Uwaezuoke et al in Enugu, Olasode et al in Ibadan and Govindan et al in India. [27,37,38] The female bias contrasts with the more common finding of a male predominance documented by Uche et al, Udoh et al and Ogun et al in Nigeria and several international studies. [8,12,17-20,36] This study revealed a marked female predominance for pilocytic astrocytoma (M: F=1:5) and a slight female predominance for medulloblastoma (M:F=1:1.7). These observations are a deviation from established literature whereby medulloblastoma occurs more frequently in males while Pilocytic astrocytoma shows no gender bias. [13,15] The female gender bias observed in our study may reflect regional variability of paediatric intracranial tumours. However, the modest sample size with attendant limited sampling variation may be responsible for this outcome and may therefore not be a reliable reflection of the true sex distribution.

Astrocytic tumours were the most common primary intracranial tumours diagnosed in our study, accounting for 42.3%. Pilocytic astrocytomas accounted for 54.5% of astrocytic tumours and 23.1% of all tumours in this study. The predominance of astrocytic tumours is documented by other Nigerian studies, including reports from Benin, Ibadan and Enugu. [17,18,31] Reports from Pakistan, Egypt, India and Great Britain also showed a predominance of astrocytic tumours. [12,19,33,39] Pilocytic astrocytomas are the commonest tumours diagnosed in children worldwide; they are often well circumscribed, occurring in the posterior cranial fossa and can usually be surgically removed with a favourable prognosis. [26,40] Medulloblastoma (15.4%) and ependymoma (13.5%) were the second and third most common histopathologic tumour types in this study, consistent with global trends identifying these tumours as major posterior fossa tumours in children. [26] Medulloblastomas are the commonest malignant brain tumour in children; they account for a majority of childhood embryonal tumours and over two-thirds occur in children under the age of 10 years. [26] Ependymomas are the third most common tumour of the brain in children after pilocytic astrocytoma and medulloblastoma; they are assigned grades II and III and generally have a less favourable prognosis than pilocytic astrocytoma. [26]

Low-grade tumours (WHO grade I and II) constituted 73.1% of cases, and the remaining 26.9% were high-grade tumours (grades III and IV). This predominance of low-grade over high-grade tumours aligns with

the observations of Uwaezuoke et al in Enugu, Nigeria. [27] The predominance of low-grade over high-grade tumours is consistent with most paediatric central nervous system tumour series globally. [8,12,18,21,27,31] The predominance of low-grade over high-grade tumours was observed across all age groups, in both sexes and within the supratentorial and infratentorial compartments.

In this study, supratentorial and infratentorial tumours occurred in equal proportions, aligning with the report by Udoh et al in Benin, Nigeria. [17] Our finding contrasts with the prevalent paediatric series within Nigeria and internationally, which traditionally report infratentorial predominance. [18,19,27,31,33] There was marked infratentorial compartment predominance in children 4 years and below, with a supratentorial bias recorded between 5-9 years and 15-17 years. This pattern of distribution observed in our study differs from the observations of El-Gaidi in Egypt, whereby supratentorial tumours predominated in children ≤ 4 years and infratentorial tumours predominated in children above 4 years. [12] While male children had a predominance of supratentorial tumours, an infratentorial bias was observed in females. In this study, infratentorial tumours correlated strongly with pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma and ependymoma, while supratentorial tumours correlated with craniopharyngiomas, meningiomas, diffuse fibrillary astrocytomas and glioblastomas, consistent with neuroanatomical-histologic associations. [15,41]

With increased size of a tumour in the unyielding cranium, the increased intracranial pressure can result in headache, vomiting, and altered mental state. [7,17,19] Focal neurological deficits, ataxia, cranial nerve palsies, seizures, motor weakness and endocrinopathies can also occur based on the site of the tumour. [17,27] Headache was the most common presenting symptom in this study, occurring in over two-thirds of patients (67.3%). This was followed by impaired vision (30.8%), gait disturbance (26.9%), vomiting (26.9%), seizures (21.1%) and motor weakness (21.1%). These findings agree with both Nigerian and international studies identifying features of raised intracranial pressure and focal neurological deficits as dominant presenting complaints. [17,19,27,33] Visual impairment correlated with sella region tumours, notably craniopharyngiomas, reflecting optic pathway involvement. Seizures were more frequent in supratentorial lesions, especially hemispheric astrocytomas, while gait disturbance was predominantly associated with infratentorial tumours, reinforcing well-established clinico-anatomical relationships. [15,27,41] Slowly growing tumours in infants and young children may manifest as macrocephaly and developmental delay. [17] Increased occipitofrontal circumference was confined to younger children and was strongly associated with supratentorial and ventricular tumours, particularly low-grade gliomas and the single case of choroid plexus papilloma.

Limitations included the single-centre tertiary referral setting, retrospective design, modest sample size, and incomplete archival data, limiting generalizability and statistical reliability. The absence of molecular diagnostic facilities also restricted tumour classification and prevented comprehensive characterization according to contemporary international standards.

Conclusion

The study findings broadly align with regional and global literature, identifying pilocytic astrocytoma and medulloblastoma as common tumours, with the cerebellum frequently affected and headache the predominant symptom. Larger multicentre prospective and population-based studies, alongside improved diagnostic capacity, treatment facilities, and specialist workforce, are recommended to enhance outcomes.
