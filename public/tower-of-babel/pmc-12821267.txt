Prostate cancer risk-associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms impact the conformational dynamics of prostate-specific antigen
Authors: Srilakshmi Srinivasan, Brooke K Hayes, Mauricio G S Costa, Blake T Riley, Emily Wilson, Emilia M Marijanovic, Itamar Kass, Hannu Koistinen, David E Hoke, Judith Clements, Ashley M Buckle, Jyotsna Batra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTwo non-synonymous single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) rs61752561 (D95N substitution) and rs17632542 (I163T substitution) in the KLK3 gene encoding prostate-specific antigen (PSA), a chymotrypsin-like serine protease, are associated with prostate cancer risk and have been shown to reduce the activity of PSA. However, the structural impact of these SNPs on PSA, which may underlie the observed risk associations and functional alterations, has not been fully explored.ResultsComputational modelling predicted that the variants D95N and I163T do not cause drastic structural changes in PSA. However, molecular dynamics simulations suggested that while the two prominent loops of wild-type PSA remain tethered to their initial conformations over 500 ns of simulation, they are disrupted in both variants, leading to increased loop dynamics. Frustration analysis, normal mode analysis (NMA) and perturbation response scanning identified dynamic links between mutation sites and increased loop dynamics that trigger long-range conformational changes, disrupting the active site and potentially hindering catalytic activity. Thermal denaturation stability assays using recombinant protein show the impact of D95N and I163T substitution on the protein stability.ConclusionsThese data show that KLK3 SNPs disrupt dynamic communication of the key loops required for proteolytic activity of PSA, which may explain the association of these SNPs with prostate cancer risk and/or progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02458-7.Keywords: Prostate cancer , Prostate-specific antigen, Molecular dynamic simulations, Single-nucleotide polymorphism, Protein stability, Kallikrein-related peptidase, Conformational dynamics

Background

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) also known as kallikrein-related peptidase 3 (KLK3) is a serine protease belonging to the family of 15 kallikrein-related peptidases (KLKs) with highly conserved structures around the active site and 40–80% amino acid sequence similarity within the family [1–3] (Fig. 1). The PSA test, which measures serum PSA protein levels, is commonly used for detection and monitoring of prostate cancer [4], which is the most common cancer in men in over 110 countries, accounting for 15% of all cancers [5]. However, PSA levels may also rise due to prostate inflammation, advanced age, and benign prostatic hyperplasia [6, 7]. Therefore, the use and interpretation of PSA as a screening tool has been widely debated. Furthermore, it also tends to detect less significant cancers, leading to unnecessary treatment that results in reduced quality of life, or conversely fail to identify instances of high-risk disease [8, 9]. While the accuracy of the PSA test to identify clinically significant prostate cancers can be increased by analysing different forms of PSA, such as the ratio of free and total PSA, or a kallikrein panel, there is a critical need for more specific and sensitive diagnostic/prognostic indicators of prostate cancer [10].

Genome-wide association studies have identified two single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) rs61752561 (leading to amino acid substitution from Asp95Asn/D95N) and rs17632542 (leading to amino acid substitution from Ile163Thr/I163T) in the PSA encoding KLK3 gene, that are associated with a reduced risk of prostate cancer (odds ratio (OR) = 0.89, 95% CI 0.86–0.93, P = 2.33 × 10−8 for rs61752561 SNP and OR = 0.70, 95% CI 0.67–0.73, P = 9.61 × 10−69 for 17,632,542 SNP) in a sample set of 49,941 cases and 32,001 controls [11–13]. These two SNPs were also associated with reduced serum PSA levels, which makes them interesting from a diagnostic perspective [11, 12, 14–20]. Furthermore, these SNPs were identified as the most significant protein Quantitative Trait Loci (pQTL) variants among the 447 variants associated with plasma PSA levels [21]. To date, functional studies have shown that these coding SNPs reduce the stability and/or glycosylation of the PSA variants, as well as decrease proteolytic activity towards substrates involved in tumour invasion and metastasis [12, 13]. These changes were associated with lowered PSA-mediated proliferation and migration of prostate cancer cells [12, 13]. The rs61752561 SNP was shown to increase the dynamics of both the kallikrein- and the 148-loops which are known to be important for enzyme activity [12]. The rs17632542 SNP, although associated with reduced prostate cancer risk, is also associated with increased risk for aggressive disease and poor survival [13]. These observations provide a functional basis for the risk observed at the genetic level. However, no studies to date have fully characterized the structural consequences of the two non-synonymous KLK3 SNPs.

Current structural data for PSA derives mainly from complexes with antibodies [22], with the only un-complexed structure being horse PSA [23]. PSA, like all serine proteases, is composed of three α-helical segments and two six-stranded anti-parallel β-barrels, with a catalytic triad consisting of D102 and H57 on one side of the active site, and S195 on the other (human chymotrypsin numbering) (Fig. 1). Towards the N-terminus from D102, PSA has an 11-residue insertion, known as the 99-loop or the kallikrein loop (since human chymotrypsin does not have such a loop, these residues are numbered as 95) [24]. In addition to PSA, this insertion is only present in KLK-1 and −2, while other members of the KLK family have shorter insertions in this region [3]. Molecular models of PSA based on structural alignment to other proteases combined with molecular dynamics simulations, have suggested that the active site of PSA can be either exposed or covered by the kallikrein loop in an “open” or a “closed” conformation, respectively [25]. However, crystal structure studies of human PSA in complex with monoclonal antibodies [22, 26] have not been able to unequivocally support this hypothesis in the absence of an uncomplexed PSA structure.

In this work, we used a fluorescence thermal denaturation stability assay and molecular dynamics simulations to investigate how the aforementioned SNPs in the KLK3 gene encoding PSA influence the protein conformational dynamics that may affect its biochemical activity. We propose that two non-synonymous SNPs in KLK3 gene, encoding the PSA variants D95N and I163T, disrupt the active site structure and hinder catalytic activity by perturbing conformational dynamics, thereby modulating PSA function within the prostate.

Results

Perturbation response scanning suggests mutation sites are effective propagators of intra-molecular dynamic information

To understand if the single amino acid changes, corresponding to the rs61752561 and rs17632542 SNPs in the KLK3 gene, impact the energetic connectivity between the mutation sites and the rest of the enzyme, we performed perturbation response scanning. For all PSA residues, the normalized effectiveness was above 0.7, indicating that the global architecture/topology favors efficient intramolecular communication between distant sites (Fig. 2). The effectiveness score at position 95 was 0.9 and that of residue 163 was 0.85, indicating that both SNPs (D95N and I163T) have the potential to efficiently propagate dynamic communication between distant loops in PSA. Interestingly, the 148 and kallikrein loops (Fig. 2) were enriched in sensors (flexible residues more sensitive to external perturbations), particularly the kallikrein loop with a peak sensitivity of 1.0.

SNPs alter dynamics of key PSA loops assisting substrate access to the binding pocket

Next, we evaluated whether these amino acid changes affect the PSA conformational variability by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of wild-type (Wt), D95N and I163T PSA. We first assessed the stability of each system during the simulations using conventional structural analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The time evolution of the average RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation) from the respective initial structures reveals similar average values, with slightly larger deviations for both variants (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A). As expected, the 70 s loop (residues 70–80), kallikrein loop (comprising 11 residues) and 148 loop exhibited substantial mobility (Fig. 3A). Inspection of the RMSF (Root Mean Square Fluctuations) profiles revealed similar behaviour for all systems (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B) with increased flexibility at the kallikrein loop for both PSA variants (Fig. 3C–F). On the other hand, the dynamics of loop 70/80 were similar in all systems (Fig. 3C–F). To assess the impact of mutations on intrinsic motions of PSA, each conformational state sampled during the MD simulations was projected onto Wt PSA normal modes that describe 148 and kallikrein loop displacements (modes 2 and 4) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). The free energy profiles along these coordinates revealed that while the Wt PSA simulation explored two adjacent free energy wells, the D95N variant was more stable and exhibited a unique population (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B). The I163T variant also explored two low-energy populations, but with an increased range of displacements along mode 4, which is related to the Kallikrein loop opening.

Effect of amino acid substitutions on the network of frustrated contacts and intramolecular communication

To determine how favorable contacts and energetic conflicts are spread in the global structure of PSA, we conducted a local frustration analysis. First, we calculated the mutational index to assess the effect of sequence perturbations on energetic frustrations. The Wt PSA central core is minimally frustrated, as is common among cores of globular proteins [27]. We also identified a network of highly frustrated interactions linking the 148 loop, the kallikrein loop, the residue D95N and the active site (Fig. 4). Moreover, we also conducted a configurational frustration analysis, where the effects of both structural and sequence perturbations are evaluated on the network of contacts. Compared to the mutational index, where only sequence perturbations are applied, the density of highly frustrated contacts is decreased, but still energetic conflicts are observed around the SNPs and at the 148 and kallikrein loops. This result indicates that conformational flexibility has a stabilizing role on PSA without disrupting the local conflicts that were likely selected by evolution. Indeed, these frustrated regions are often related to functional requirements, such as allostery, as observed in other kallikrein-related peptidases [28, 29].

To gain further insights into allostery and communication, we calculated the dynamical cross-correlation coefficients from MD trajectories and mapped them to the structure. For Wt PSA, we observed a significant anti-correlation between the residues near the top of the kallikrein loop, R95g (Chymotrypsin numbering—see Methods), D97 and Q174, and the 148 loop (Fig. 3B). In both variants D95N and I163T, this inter-loop anti-correlation was significantly reduced or not present at all, indicating a general stabilization that potentially leads to a preference for more compact conformations. Intra-loop anti-correlations were only observed in the I163T variant between the base and the top of the kallikrein loop, indicating an extended conformation that might propagate dynamics through different routes than those observed for the Wt PSA (Fig. 3C–F).

D95N substitution allows increased flexibility in the kallikrein loop, limiting its ability to mediate substrate access to the binding pocket

In the Wt PSA simulation, residue D95 forms polar contacts with the positively charged kallikrein loop residue R95g, with the highly polar guanidinium end of R95g at most 8 Å from the negatively charged aspartate D95 over the course of 500 ns (Fig. 3A). The balance between these polar contacts, and the contact network between R95g, D97 and Q174 facilitating a tight turn at the top of the kallikrein loop, is one factor mediating the dynamics of this loop. In simulations of the D95N variant, the kallikrein loop was displaced from its position in Wt PSA, being pulled outward (Fig. 3C). This is likely due to the mutation from a negatively charged aspartate to neutral asparagine at position 95, disrupting the strong polar interaction with R95g.

To test if the mutations affect the ability of PSA to form complexes with endogenous protease inhibitors in serum, recombinant PSA variants were incubated with female serum and assayed for free and total PSA. The I163T PSA exhibited a higher free/total PSA ratio compared to the Wt and D95N PSA, indicating a reduced ability to form complexes with protease inhibitors (Additional File 2: Table S1).

KLK3 SNPs impact protein thermostability

Having shown that the two SNPS impact the PSA structural dynamics and ability to complex with protease inhibitors, we investigated the influence of the two KLK3 SNPs on the stability of the PSA variants they encode, recombinant PSA variants [Wt, D95N and I163T] were expressed and purified from Pichia pastoris. Differential scanning fluorimetry analysis showed that both PSA variants, D95N and I163T, indeed affect thermal stability. The D95N variant showed increased stability (Tm = 69.7 ± 0.5 °C), while the I163T had reduced stability (Tm = 64.2 ± 0.4 °C) compared to the Wt PSA (Tm = 68.8 ± 0.4 °C) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, we report the molecular dynamic simulation analysis and thermal stabilities of Wt and D95N and I163T PSA variants encoded by prostate cancer risk associated SNPs of the KLK3 gene. Together, these data show that the single amino acid changes, as a result of these polymorphisms, disrupt the protein dynamics, and the key loops that determine protein stability and catalytic activity of PSA, which may explain the association of these SNPs with prostate cancer.

Fluorescence monitored thermal stability analysis revealed that amino acid change I163T lowered protein stability, while the D95N substitution increased the stability of PSA. The D95N substitution creates a novel N-glycosylation site [12], which may explain improved stability. Moreover, the solvent-exposed location of the SNP substitutions in flexible loops is distant from the active site, and their relatively conservative nature (neither D → N nor I → T are predicted to disrupt interactions crucial to the integrity or function of the protein) suggest that amino acid substitution may instead indirectly affect the active site structure and/or accessibility of substrates by disrupting dynamic communication networks.

Perturbation response analysis was undertaken to determine the sensitivity and the effectiveness of each amino acid in Wt PSA. Through perturbation response scanning, we observed that the active site residues have among the lowest sensitivities of all PSA residues and are thus unlikely to display altered dynamics due to any effects resulting from the SNP mutations. However, the 140 s loop and especially kallikrein loop are highly sensitive to perturbations at other sites in the protein. Perturbations at effector sites, such as the SNP substitutions, are thus likely to have an impact on distant loop movement. The decrease in protein stability is likely due to these changes in loop movement, as opposed to a direct effect on the active site.

To further investigate loop movement, we performed molecular dynamics simulations of Wt PSA and variants D95N and I163T. Wt PSA MD simulations for the 70 s loop, the kallikrein loop and the 148 loop were consistent with expected conformational flexibility in PSA [24, 30, 31]. In the simulations of PSA variants (D95N and I163T), dynamics in loop 70/80 were similar to those in Wt PSA; however, both the kallikrein and 148-loops displayed greater conformational flexibility. While some of these observations can be explained by short-range changes in electrostatic interactions, deeper analyses are required to understand the connections between these SNP substitutions and movement at distant sites. The increased amplitude, and the subsequent loss of anti-correlation of the kallikrein-loop and 148-loop movement in variant D95N suggests that their decoupled movements are in some way responsible for reducing the stability of the PSA. The increased flexibility probably resulted from the D95N amino acid substitution, which in turn disrupts the contact network at the top turn of the kallikrein loop, allowing some outward movement away from the active site.

Although the polarity change of the D95N substitution gives insight into the changes in kallikrein loop dynamics, it is not in itself an explanation for the dramatic changes in dynamics at the 148 loop, which is more than 20 Å away from the amino acid substitution. In the simulation of Wt PSA, although the 148 loop is one of the most mobile regions, it remains in an upwards or open conformation over 500 ns (Fig. 3A). Most intriguingly, in simulations of the D95N variant we observed the same loop drift downwards into a closed conformation (Fig. 3C). Localized energy changes upon sequence variations throughout the protein and the analyses from dynamic trajectories and intrinsic loop motions were studied by mutational and configurational frustration analysis, respectively and by local frustration analysis, respectively. Both of these suggested that a network of frustrated residues passing through the active site, by which D95N and the 148 loop are connected, might be dictating the inter-loop dynamics that is disrupted upon mutations (Figs. 3 and 4). The more scattered configurational frustrated network reveals that structural variations alleviate several energetic conflicts, which reinforces that stabilization of the 148 and kallikrein loops is crucial for PSA activity (Fig. 4).

While the kallikrein loop is highly mobile in all three simulated systems, the most dramatic structural variations were found upon I163T substitution that exhibited displacements from a closed, into an open, and then back to closed conformation over the course of 500 ns of simulation. The kallikrein loop is involved in mediating substrate access to the active site [24] and here, the dynamic equilibrium between an open and closed conformation is disrupted for the I163T variant compared to Wt PSA. The intra-KLK-loop dynamical connections seen in the Wt PSA are thus disrupted in the I163T variant, explaining in part the dynamic shift of the kallikrein loop observed on SNP substitution. The substituted threonine is more buried into the protein than the Wt isoleucine, potentially triggering a redistribution of the hydrogen bonding network within PSA, explaining the effects of the SNP substitution on distal regions. In our simulations of the I163T PSA variant, we observed that the most mobile regions of the protein are the kallikrein loop and the 148-loop (Fig. 3E). The results of the frustration analysis outlined above confirm that the substituted residue 163 is also connected to the 148 loop-active site-kallikrein loop frustration network. Our previous studies have shown that, compared to Wt PSA, D95N and I163T PSA variants may exhibit, in addition to reduced potency to cleave peptide and protein substrates, differences in their substrate specificity with selected protein substrates [12, 13]. Furthermore, the D95N and I163T recombinant protein isoforms exhibited differences in their complexing ability with serum inhibitors with a higher free-to-total PSA (f/t PSA) ratio observed for the I163T variant. PSA variants were incubated with female serum and assayed for free and total PSA. The I163T PSA exhibited a higher free/total PSA ratio compared to the Wt and D95N PSA, indicating reduced ability to form complexes with protease inhibitors. D95N exhibited a lower f/t PSA ratio compared to Wt PSA. This suggests differences in the complexing ability of these two PSA variants with the major complexing ligands in serum, such as Apha-2-macroglobulin (A2M) and α−1-antichymotrypsin (ACT) which may impact the clinically measured f/t PSA ratio for prostate cancer diagnosis [12, 32]. These changes as shown by the frustration network analysis may affect protein stability and the way substrate hydrolysis occurs by changing substrate binding and recognition, thus, leading to change in activity.

Conclusions

Our analysis reveals that, relative to Wt PSA, the key loops assisting substrate access to the binding pocket and active site are displaced in PSA variants associated with a reduced risk of prostate cancer. Overall, the SNP substitutions prompt greater intramolecular communication allowing substitutions to effect dynamics at distant sites and protein stability, ultimately disrupting the structure of the active site and hindering catalytic activity. We propose that the two coding KLK3 SNPs disrupt dynamic communication required for normal protein function that may alter their protein stability and complexing ability with protease inhibitors, thereby modulating their role in prostate cancer development and/or progression.

Methods

Atomic coordinates and modelling

Atomic coordinates for PSA were obtained from the PDB entry 2ZCH. Structural representations were produced using PyMOL version 2.0.0. Molecular models of the PSA variants D95N (SNP rs61752561) [33] and I163T (SNP rs17632542) [15] were produced by PyMOL mutagenesis, choosing the sterically least-hindered rotamer for each mutant residue side-chain. Chymotrypsin numbering is used, reflecting KLK3 insertions after a sequence alignment with chymotrypsin. For example, we refer to the residue R95g—the inserted residues at residue 95 are given the letters a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, j, and k.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Wt PSA and SNP variants were protonated according to states appropriate for pH 7.0 as determined by PROPKA [34, 35] and placed in a rectangular box with a border of at least 10 Å. The box was explicitly solvated TIP3P water [36] and Na + and Cl − ions counter-ions added to neutralize total charge. All systems were parametrized using the AMBER ff14SB all-atom force field [37, 38]. After an energy minimization stage consisting of at least 10,000 steps, an equilibration protocol was performed, in which harmonic positional restraints of 10 kcal Å2 mol−1 were applied to the protein backbone atoms. The temperature was incrementally increased while keeping volume constant from 0 to 300 K over the course of 0.5 ns, with Langevin temperature coupling relaxation times of 0.5 ps. After the target temperature was reached, pressure was equilibrated to 1 atm over a further 0.5 ps using the Berendsen algorithm [39]. Following this, production MD simulation runs were performed in the NPT ensemble using periodic boundary conditions and a time step of 2 fs. Temperature was maintained at 300 K using the Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 2 ps, and electrostatic interactions computed using an 8 Å cutoff radius and the Particle Mesh Ewald method [40]. Pressure was maintained at a constant 1 atm using Berendsen pressure bath coupling with a time coefficient of 0.1 ps [39]. Each simulated system was repeated three times from the same starting structure but with different starting velocities, with each run extending for 0.5 µs using AMBER 18 [41].

Local frustration analysis

Local frustration analysis was conducted with the Frustratometer2 Web server [42]. Energetic frustration is obtained by comparing the native state interactions with the generated decoy states. For the calculation of the mutational index, decoys are generated by mutating the amino acids 5 Å around a given interaction pair; for the configurational index, additional variations in the atomic positions are also applied. Each interaction is defined as highly or minimally frustrated by comparing frustration energy values with the distribution of energies obtained for the set of generated decoy states as described previously [43]. Briefly, if the frustration score for the native state is lower than − 1, the interaction is assigned as highly frustrated. Scores higher than 0.78 are related to minimally frustrated interactions. Intermediate values between these two thresholds relate to neutral interactions.

Dynamical cross-correlation matrix calculations

Global backbone alignment was utilized to remove rotational and translational degrees of freedom on MD trajectories. Cross correlation coefficients between pairs of residues were measured using the R program with the bio3D package [44].

Normal mode analysis and perturbation response scanning

Normal mode analysis (NMA) of the Wt PSA structure was carried out using ProDy v.2.0 [45] using the Anisotropic Network Model (ANM) [46]. The overall potential consists of the sum of harmonic potentials between interacting nodes placed at C-alpha atoms. All nodes within a certain cutoff distance (15 Å) were assumed to interact. All other parameters were left as default. This calculation yielded 3N-6 modes of motion (where N corresponds to the number of C-alpha atoms) corresponding to Wt PSA intrinsic motions. They are ranked according to their frequencies, which relate to the energetic cost of displacements along the corresponding vectors. Low-frequency modes describe the softest motions [47]. Even though our calculations provided access to the whole vibrational spectrum, we focused our analysis on those that mostly contributed to displacements on the Kallikrein and 148 loops.

Perturbation response scanning (PRS) was conducted with ProDy using the same ANM model above. In PRS systematic perturbations are applied on the network and the displacement of all residues is calculated in response to external forces. This method separates residues in two groups: sensors and effectors [48, 49]. This analysis was performed considering the 20 slowest (frequency) nonzero ANM modes, as previously described. These modes describe the largest amplitude and most energetically favorable collective changes in the structure.

Recombinant PSA production

6XHis/FLAG/EK (6XHis + RTGDYKDDDDK, in which DDDDK is an enterokinase cleavage sequence)-mature KLK3 (PSA) coding sequence was cloned into the pPIC9K vector. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed to generate D95N (Asp95Asn), I163T (Ile163Thr) and S195A (Ser195Ala) mutations [12]. The PSA-sequence was sub-cloned into a pPIC9K vector, followed by expression in Pichia pastoris GS115 cells as described previously [51, 52]. Secreted recombinant proteins were purified from the cell-free conditioned media by cation exchange chromatography using a Resource S column (GE Life Sciences) as described previously [12]. The purified proteins were treated with enterokinase (EK) (New England Biolabs) at 12,000:1 ratio of PSA: EK (w/w) and incubated at 23 °C for 18 h. EK-treated PSA isoforms were further purified by cation exchange chromatography as described previously [12]. The efficiency of the EK processing was assessed by Western blot analysis using an anti-FLAG antibody.

Protein thermal stability assay

Protein thermal stability was assessed by differential scanning fluorimetry assay. Recombinant PSA protein (2 µg) was incubated with fluorescent dye SYPRO Orange (Invitrogen, Australia) and the thermal unfolding of the PSA protein was monitored by measuring the fluorescence when the protein was incubated with SYPRO Orange and heated in stepwise increments of 0.5 °C per min from 25 to 99 °C. The excitation and emission wavelength for SYPRO Orange were set to 580 ± 10 and 623 ± 14 nm, respectively. Fluorescence was measured after every minute for each 0.5 °C increase. The results were analysed the Protein Thermal Shift™ Software (Invitrogen). The melting point of the PSA and control proteins relative to the no protein control wells was calculated using the Protein Thermal shift™ software (Invitrogen).
