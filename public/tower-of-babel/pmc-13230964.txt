Biophysical and sociodemographic determinants of green infrastructure (GI) spatial distribution in Gdańsk, Poland
Authors: Khansa Gulshad, Michał Szydłowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrbanization and climate change are intensifying environmental challenges in cities, necessitating innovative approaches to manage stormwater, mitigate urban heat, and enhance ecosystem services. Green Infrastructure (GI) has emerged as an effective solution to combat these challenges, yet research on GI spatial distribution is limited, and it remains unclear what factors drive or constrain their placement in cities. This study investigates the spatial distribution of GI in Gdańsk, Poland, employing a grid-based econometric framework to understand its relationship with biophysical and sociodemographic factors. GI density exhibits strong spatial clustering, with high density areas found to be in the western and eastern regions occupying state forests and low-density regions are found in northern, central urban areas, and parts of southern regions. Regression models, including ordinary least squares, spatial lag/error, and geographically weighted regression, indicated that GI distribution is constrained by urbanization indicator (impervious surface), while heat exposure (LST), and air quality variables (NO2, O3) act as spatial stress indicators that co-occur with areas of low GI with geographically varying effects. The findings align with the Gdańsk Greenery Policy (2023) but also highlight additional areas where GI provision can be expanded to improve climate adaptation, environmental equity and urban livability. The framework demonstrates how spatial data can support municipal decision-making and underscores the value of integrating environmental and socio-economic indicators into GI planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47276-1.Keywords: Urbanization, Climate change, Green infrastructure, Spatial statistics, Regression modelingSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies, Geography, Geography

Introduction

Urbanization has led to a fast increase in impervious areas, leading to an increase in compact soil and a decrease in infiltration capacity1–4. Simultaneously, the uncertainties of climate change further exacerbated the frequency and intensity of precipitation events2,5, such as in Europe, between 1980 and 2016, extreme weather events caused 436 billion euros in economic losses6. Additionally, by 2050, population density in risk-prone coastal areas is projected to rise by 25%, hence exposing more people to the rising impacts of climate change7,8. The combined effect of urbanization and climate change leads to adverse environmental challenges associated with urban sustainability, including flooding and urban heat islands9,10.

The traditional gray infrastructure proves insufficient to manage urban water problems due to their demand for intensive resources and environmental impacts. In response, many nature-based solutions (NBS) have been proposed in various countries to mitigate urban water problems10. They are referred to as Low Impact Development (LID) and Green Infrastructure (GI), defined as a “strategically planned network of natural and semi-natural areas with other environmental features designed and managed to deliver a wide range of ecosystem services”11. Unlike conventional grey infrastructure, GI uses green elements to control stormwater runoff2,3. Environmental benefits, along with the cost-effectiveness of implementation3, have made GI an increasingly common design element in urban planning initiatives2.

Notably, many GI projects have been experimental and opportunistic, often shaped by funding availability, land ownership, or regulatory compliance12 rather than systematic spatial optimization. While early GI implementation in many cities was strongly motivated by stormwater management and flood mitigation requirements2, a substantial body of literature has since demonstrated GI’s multifunctional role in urban cooling13, air quality improvement14, biodiversity support15, and human well-being16, and these benefits are increasingly used in spatial prioritization frameworks. However, despite this growing recognition of GI’s multi-benefit potential, empirical studies of GI spatial distribution frequently examine individual services in isolation or rely on broad greenness indicators, with limited attention to how multiple biophysical constraints and socio-demographic conditions jointly shape where GI is implemented. As a result, it remains insufficiently understood how planning priorities, regulatory drivers, and local urban conditions interact to produce the observed spatial patterns of GI in cities.

Effective GI planning is complex and should be based on the driving mechanisms reflecting physical, geographic, socioeconomic, and planning policy factors/governance context17. Several studies (Table 1) find GI is sensitive to physical and geographic characteristics, including soil type, elevation, slope, rainfall patterns18,19, runoff, geology20, and other hydrological factors2, presumably because these factors affect GI patterns and functions. Among socio-economic factors, Xu et al. 21and Liu et al.17 found that places with more capital, technology, and information impact GI development and accessibility. This highlights that even if demographic or physical conditions favor GI, sufficient resources and supportive policies are crucial for implementing projects.

Socioeconomic transformations that began in the 1980s and accelerated with the post-socialist transition in the early 1990s have profoundly reshaped land use patterns in Central and East-Central European region22. The introduction of a market-based agricultural economy, combined with the influx of Western European subsidized food products made traditional farming less profitable, causing widespread decline in agricultural activity and expansion of uncultivated land23. Similar dynamics have been observed in Poland9,24,25, Hungary24,26, Austria27, Romania28, Slovakia and the Czech Republic29, and more broadly across Central Eastern Europe (CEE)27,30.

After Poland’s accession to European Union, international and national frameworks-such as the EU Green Infrastructure Strategy and the Common Agriculture Policy (CAP), Green Surge project and Poland’s Strategic Adaptation Plan for Sectors and Areas Sensitive to Climate Change (SPA 2030), have been pivotal in shaping land use changes, and have sought to reintegrate ecological and climate considerations into land use policy23,31,32. At city-level, Gdańsk Greenery Policy (GPZ, 2023) and participatory planning efforts aim to explicitly address climate resilience, ecological connectivity, and equitable GI access33. Thus, the current spatial distribution of GI reflects both the path-dependent legacies of post-socialist transition and the new multi-level governance layers introduced by EU and climate-driven policies.

Despite these efforts, GI development in Gdańsk often occurs opportunistically, driven by available funding, regulatory mandates (like mandatory integration of retention features in new developments based on Gdańsk Small Retention Guide/Gdański Poradnik Małej Retencji), as well as local geomorphological constraints and varying levels of public support34. While this approach has led to innovative projects and community engagement, it risks overlooking areas where GI could mitigate urban heat, air pollution, or improve access to greenery. This highlights the importance of analyzing the spatial patterns of GI on the ground to identify gaps in implementation and ensure equitable distribution across the city.

A growing body of literature focuses on the spatial distribution of GI and its relationship with environmental and socio-demographic factors. Table 1 summarizes few representative studies on intra-urban GI distribution, that explicitly model the spatial distribution of existing GI or green space as a dependent variable in relation to explanatory environmental or socio-demographic factors. Studies were selected based on (i) explicit spatial modelling of GI distribution, (ii) quantitative regression or spatial statistical analysis, and (iii) focus on intra-urban scale.

Most studies reviewed (Table 1) emphasize either socio-economic or demographic factors determinants because these approaches dominate the GI distribution literature, particularly in North American and European contexts19,35,36, or biophysical determinants (slope, elevation etc.), but seldom within a unified spatial modelling framework. In contrast, biophysical variables are more extensively treated in separate research streams focused on ecosystem service provision37, climate adaptation site suitability6, or hydrological modelling38, but less frequently integrated with social equity assessments in spatial distribution studies. Moreover, fewer studies explicitly distinguish between drivers and constraints of GI placement within a unified modelling framework. By presenting this socio-economic-heavy literature base, we highlight the gap our study addresses: by jointly examining both biophysical and socio-demographic factors and distinguishing between constraints on GI implementation and need indicators. Here, biophysical variables include (DEM, slope, NDVI), built environment (imperviousness, industrial proximity, stormwater network proximity, health and education proximity), socio-demographic (population density, elderly population) and environmental stressors (LST, NO2, O3, PM10, PM2.5), climate projection (RCP 4.5 and 8.5), and flood hazard, which were not widely analyzed in earlier GI determinant studies.

Furthermore, unlike previous studies summarized in Table 1, which focuses on aggregated geographic units such as census block group (CBG) or census tract, this research considered a grid-scale approach enabling finer resolutions and detailed patterns in GI density. The study uniquely examines Gdańsk, Poland, a city that has not yet been extensively explored in the GI discourse, making it a valuable case study for understanding GI dynamics in Central-Eastern Europe. Study area

Gdańsk, the largest city in northern Poland, lies on the southern coast of the Baltic Sea, covering an area of 262 km2 (Fig. 1, (created using ArcGIS Pro 3.5.11)). Gdańsk topography is divided into the lower terrace with almost 40 km of coastline, and an upper terrace with forest complexes like Tri-City Landscape Park separated by fragmented plateau edge. Gdańsk’s spatial development follows a strip layout between the Bay of Gdańsk and green areas with a polycentric structure (Śródmieście, Wrzeszcz, Oliwa)43.

Study area map: GI map of Gdańsk showing different types of green areas (created using ArcGIS Pro 3.5.1).

While not classified as a high flood-risk zone, Gdańsk is vulnerable to extreme rainfall events due to its terrain. Between 2010 and 2017, the State Fire Service Headquarters recorded 635 rainfall interventions44,45, underscoring the relevance of GI-based flood mitigation.

Green infrastructure is a key component of the city’s spatial planning. Ogólnomiejski System Terenów Aktywnych Biologicznie/City System of Biologically Active Areas (OSTAB) includes various forms of greenery, water reservoirs, rivers, streams and adjacent areas, selected agricultural areas, allotment gardens. OSTAB ensures continuity and provides connections with valuable natural areas outside the city boundaries43. In Gdańsk, the detailed GI classes shown in Fig. 1 were aggregated into four broader policy-based (Gdańsk Greenery Policy) GI categories for descriptive reporting: forest and semi-forest greenery (46%), waterside greenery (9%), recreational greenery (7%), and other green areas (4%)33,43 (Supplementary Information Fig. S1, Table S1) Agriculture (33%) was excluded from the GI density analysis, focusing on urban greenery and other sustainable GI. However, these proportions describe the overall composition of GI within the city and were not used as separate components in statistical modeling.

Datasets and methodology

The methodology adopted in this study is shown in Fig. 2. It encompasses five steps: (i) Input data: input data from multiple sources was downloaded, (ii) Data preprocessing: all data was standardized to 1 km grid, then it was normalized, and multicollinearity was calculated using correlation and VIF (iii) Spatial analysis: spatial autocorrelation was calculated using Moran I analysis, (iv) Regression models: a sequence of global and spatial regression models were implemented.

Data preprocessing

In the second stage of methodology, a regular 1 km grid was generated in ArcGIS Pro 3.5.1 covering the administrative extent of Gdańsk. This grid resolution was selected as a compromise between data availability and statistical requirements. Several predictors (air pollutants, climate projections) are only available at 500 m-5 km resolution, so using cells finer than 1 km would not introduce additional independent information but would increase spatial autocorrelation. At the same time, the 1 km grid provides many more analysis units (N = 342 cells) than district-level aggregation (N = 35 districts), improving statistical power for multivariate and spatial regression. Administrative districts as units were not considered because they vary greatly in size and land-cover composition, which would exacerbate the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP). While small GI features such as pocket parks are partially generalized at this scale, the 1 km grid preserves the dominant contrasts between forest complexes, industrial corridors, and dense urban districts that are relevant for identifying city-wide spatial disparities that inform strategic planning priorities.

For regression analysis, all green infrastructure classes (forests, wetlands, parks, cemeteries, allotments, etc.,)33,48,51 were aggregated into a composite GI variable representing total green infrastructure per 1 km grid cell to enable robust statistical modeling. This structural aggregation aligns with prior spatial econometric studies of GI distribution (e.g.,in Baltimore, Maryland and Portland, Oregon; in Phoenix, Atlanta, and Portland; Washington D.C2,19,41), where the objective is to model spatial patterns of overall GI provision rather than differentiate ecosystem service performance by type. Hence, all GI polygons, regardless of size, were intersected with the 1 km grid. All GI polygons were merged into a single composite layer and GI density per grid was calculated as the proportion of area of GI features within a grid by the area of the grid2,19:

Consequently, small GI elements contribute proportionally to grid-level density values. While very small or isolated green patches may have limited influence on this strategic scale, the approach ensures that cumulative GI provision is fully represented across the city. The resulting GI density (continuous 0–1) within each grid served as the dependent variable for all spatial analyses.

All factors (e.g., LST, impervious surfaces, NO2 emissions, precipitation) were standardized to a 1 km ×1 km grid cells using ArcGIS Pro 3.5.1, served as independent factors and exported for further processing. All statistical and spatial modeling was performed in Google Colab (Python 3.11). Factor maps were created using Python 3.112 matplotlib library.

Independent variables were normalized using z-score method. Multicollinearity was assessed using Pearson correlation and variance inflation factors (VIF) to eliminate noise and redundancy. The refined variables were analyzed using multiple regression models.

Multiple regression models

In the fourth stage of methodology, to model the relationship between GI density and explanatory factors, a sequence of global and spatial regression models was implemented in Python (Google Colab). The modelling pipeline consisted of: Ordinary least squares (OLS), weighted least squares (WLS), generalized least squares (GLS), spatial lag model (SLM), spatial error model (SEM), and geographically weighted regression (GWR) models. All models used the 1 km grid GI density as the dependent variable.

OLS served as a baseline global model, assuming no spatial dependence in residuals56. However, Moran’s I test on residuals revealed spatial autocorrelation, necessitating spatial regression. OLS was estimated using PySAL. Residual diagnostics indicated violation of OLS assumptions. Significant heteroscedasticity motivated WLS and GLS estimation using spreg.WLS and spreg.GLS. Spatial dependence was subsequently diagnosed using Lagrange Multiplier tests, which indicated that both SLM and SEM were suitable. GI priority areas

To enhance the practical relevance of the grid-based analysis, we derived a priority map (detailed method in supplementary file Sect. 1. Derivation GI priority map). For this purpose, GI density map and environmental stress indicators (LST, impervious surface, NO₂, flood occurrence) maps were selected. Priority cells were identified where GI density values were below the 25th percentile and were classified as “low GI supply”. Environmental stress indicators above the 75th percentile were classified as “high stress”. To avoid subjective weighting and ensure comparability across variables with different units and distributions, a percentile-based thresholding approach was applied. Cells exhibiting low GI density and at least two concurrent stress indicators were classified as high-priority areas. Processing was done using Python 3.11 in Google Colab while resulting map was created using ArcGIS Pro 3.5.1.

Results

Spatial distribution of biophysical, socio-demographic factors

LST

The highest mean temperatures, ranging from 22.62 °C to 23.46 °C (Supplementary Fig. S2 online) are primarily concentrated in the central and northern parts (Przymorze Małe, Wrzeszcz Dolny, Letnica, and Młodeo Miasto) of the city which also have been identified as priority areas in Gdańsk Greenery Policy with temperature being one of the key factors influencing this designation33. These regions are likely to experience higher average temperatures due to dense urbanization, impervious surfaces, and lower levels of GI.

DEM

The mean elevation ranges from below sea level (-0.41) (eastern and southeastern regions) to 144.66 (western and northwestern parts) (Supplementary Fig. S2). The plateau of the Gdańsk moraine hills (western part) is less urbanized, covered with state forests, and with gentler slopes3,45,62. Urban planning should protect and enhance GI on moraine hills (upper terrace) and focus on integrating GI into the low-lying urban areas (lower terrace) for flood management.

Flood

Figure S2 in supplementary shows flood distribution, with color intensity indicating the number of flood events. Wrzeszcz Górny, Wrzeszcz Dolny, Osowa, Śródmieście, Oliwa, and Orunia districts had the most interventions44,63 during rainfall events. Densely built and flat regions near the edge zone and the lower terrace receive runoff from the upper terrace during heavy rainfall like Historic Center of Gdańsk49. The eastern part of Gdańsk and the upper terrace show low flood occurrences due to high GI density and higher elevations62.

Population density

Population densities, ranging from 0 to 574.38 person per square kilometer (Supplementary Fig. S2) Highest densities occur in Chełm (south) and Zaspa (north), with the lowest in Stogi Portowe and Lasy Oliwskie. Between 2008 and 2021, population growth in south and western urban districts reflected ongoing urban development9,10,64, leading to more demand for infrastructure and services and low GI density.

Pollutants

Supplementary figure S2 shows NO2 and O3 concentrations, key pollutants from industrial activities and vehicles. NO2 concentrations are highest in north-central-eastern region (Stogi and Nowy Port districts) while O3 concentrations are highest in the northern regions. Due to complex atmospheric chemistry, O3 concentrations vary spatially and are not always directly proportional to NO2 levels. These maps highlight the environmental pressures on GI due to industrial proximity, NO2 emissions, and ozone levels. This can guide targeted interventions in high-stress areas. The maps for the rest of the factors are available in the supplementary information file (Fig. S3, S4).

Spatial patterns of GI

GI density varies spatially across the city, as illustrated by GI density map (Fig. 3), showing the raw density values across a 1 km grid scale. Central urbanized zones (Przymorze Małe, Wrzeszcz Dolny, Letnica, Wzgórze Mickiewicza, Letnica, Zaspa Młyniec and Rozstaje) show the lowest greenery.

Areas with non-significant local Moran’s I (p > 0.05) means no strong clustering was detected in those locations. These areas show negative local Moran’s I values between − 1.04 and 0.15 (Supplementary Fig. S5).

HL clusters (high GI densities adjacent to low GI densities) represent isolated green spaces in center and southern areas, such as small urban parks or cemeteries: Oliwskie Łąki/Oliwa Meadows in the north of Letnica and south of Nowy Port, Park Imienia Ronalda Reagana in Przymorze Wielkie, Cmentarz Łostowicki/cemetery in the Ujeścisko-Łostowice and Siedlce districts. LH areas (low densities surrounded by high GI densities) are sparse appearing in the northwest of Gdańsk in Matarnia, around Tricity Landscape Park, beside Lasy Otomińskie forest in the southwest, and along the eastern coastal belt of Wyspa Sobieszewska. Their scarcity suggests that low-GI areas transition gradually to high-GI areas through non-significant regions. These non-significant areas represent mixed land-use mosaics where neither greenery nor built surfaces dominate. In Gdańsk, these correspond to edge zones between forest complexes (e.g., Tricity Landscape Park, coastal forests) and compact residential districts such as Wrzeszcz-Oliwa and the mid-terrace corridor. Together local Moran’s I result and the global Moran’s I give a comprehensive view of GI distribution in Gdańsk, revealing both the general trend and local deviations from this trend.

Relationship of factors with GI

The correlation analysis between factors and GI density revealed several significant negative relationships (Fig. 5b). PM10 (-0.50), PM2.5 (-0.53), NO₂ (-0.42), LST (-0.60), impervious surface (-0.52), and population density (-0.36) showed moderate to strong negative correlations with GI density. To determine whether population concentration itself constrains GI density or whether population serves as a proxy for built-up urban form., partial correlation analysis was conducted between population density, impervious surface, and GI density. After controlling the impervious surface, the correlation between population density and GI density became weak and statistically non-significant from r = -0.36 to r = -0.06 with p = 0.26, an 83% reduction. In contrast, impervious surface only modestly reduced impervious-GI correlation from r = -0.52 to r = -0.39, (a 25% reduction) with p < 0.001 when controlling the population density.

Among positive correlations, none emerged as a strong driver of GI. Weak positive correlation included rainwater collectors (0.28), O3 (0.16), NDVI (0.25), distance from educational institutes (0.21), distance from industrial areas (0.28), and DEM (0.13). Relationship between factors

To assess collinearity among predictors and ensure regression model interpretability, pairwise correlations were computed between all factors (Fig. 5a). Several strong associations indicated redundancy within groups of variables. PM2.5 and PM10 showed a high correlation of 0.98. Future precipitation scenarios were also correlated (0.73), DEM and future precipitation (2071–2100 under RCP 4.5) were highly correlated (0.74). Impervious areas and population density showed a moderate correlation of 0.63. Based on these correlations, PM2.5, PM10 and the precipitation scenario for 2021–2050 (RCP 4.5) were identified as potentially redundant predictors.

These were further verified by multicollinearity results (Supplementary table S1). PM10 (VIF = 96.93), PM2.5 (VIF = 81.72), future precipitation 2021–2050 under RCP 4.5 (VIF = 10) exceeded multicollinearity threshold and therefore were removed from the analysis to avoid issues in estimating regression coefficients. After removing, all remaining factors had VIF below 10, indicating negligible collinearity and suitability for regression analysis.

Spatial heterogeneity in GI drivers (GWR)

The GWR model provides a detailed spatial analysis of the relationship between predictors and GI density, revealing significant local variations. Although GWR provided an R2 of 0.84 and an adjusted R2 of 0.78, explaining 84% of the GI density variation, this improvement is expected due to its local fitting nature. The reduction in AIC to 72.94 and residual sum of squares from 47.53 (global model) to 14.81, indicates improved model fit (Table 4) than other models as supported by other studies18,21. However, GWR results are interpreted primarily as evidence of spatial non-stationarity rather than as a definitive replacement for global models.

The GWR highlights spatial heterogeneity in the effects of predictors. For instance, impervious areas showed negative coefficient with a mean of -0.210, and LST with mean effect of -0.165. Conversely, O3 exhibits both positive and negative effects depending on the region, with a mean of 0.161 and a range from − 0.20 to 0.71.

Discussion

Urban green infrastructure is a keyway to implement nature-based solutions (NBS), while providing measurable benefits. However, GI distribution across cities is often shaped by topography, historical development, and current planning priorities. Understanding these patterns and what drives them is essential for evidence-based urban greening strategies that target areas of greatest need. Therefore, this study examines how topography, urban form, environmental stressors, and sociodemographic factors relate to GI density in Gdańsk, Poland.

The spatial patterns observed in Gdańsk indicate that GI distribution is not random but strongly shaped by physiographic and urban morphological structures. Forested upland areas on the moraine hills and coastal belts exhibited the highest GI density, as confirmed by Moran’s I clustering analysis, while the central, northern, and southern urbanized districts formed persistent low-density clusters. This is confirmed by temporal LULC analysis (1985–2024) in Gdańsk which showed that forests remained stable at the periphery, while interior districts underwent densification and industrial conversion, reinforcing the spatial GI patterns identified by the LISA analysis9. These patterns reflect interactions between terrain (upper terrace vs. lower terrace), hydrological pathways, and historical land conversion3,45,62.

The upper terrace acts as a GI reservoir, with high permeability, large forest complexes, and low flood occurrence while low lying areas between terraces are flood prone and have less GI due to dense development and runoff from higher elevations64. This shows how natural landforms influence current GI distribution and create unequal access to ecosystem services such as cooling, runoff mitigation, and recreation. In southeastern and eastern Gdańsk, lowland polders (agricultural areas of Żuławy Gdańskie and Wyspa sobieszewska) serve agriculture, control water-level regulation and manage short, intense rainfalls without significant adverse impacts62. However, their future role in GI planning must be evaluated, especially as urban expansion and climate change may increase runoff from adjacent built-up areas.

Previous district scale studies in Gdańsk revealed similar results: Oliwa (west) and Wyspa Sobieszewska (east) districts have the largest nature-based solutions. While Rudniki, Olszynka, Orunia-Św. Wojciech-Lipce districts in the east, Wzgórze Mickiewicza in the center and Nowy Port in the north exhibited low GI density10,65. Recent development trend in Gdańsk further suggest that northern side (Wrzeszcz, Oliwa, Zaspa, and Przymorze), and the south-western band (Śródmieście, Chełm, Gdańsk-South, and Piecki-Migowo)10,64 could benefit from further urban planning interventions to address the GI disparities.

While land use and topography explained broad GI patterns, correlation analysis clarified the underlying drivers. Population density correlates negatively with GI but also correlates strongly with impervious surface suggesting it reflect development intensity rather than exerting a direct effect on GI. To test this, partial correlations were examined by controlling for impervious surface. When impervious cover is held constant statistically, the population-GI correlation weakens, while impervious-GI remains strongly negative. This suggests that built surfaces and pavement are the primary mechanism limiting GI, while population density reflects the degree of urbanization. Similar relationships have been documented in cities with diverse urban morphologies, including Bradford, UK.35, Baltimore, Washington DC, USA2,41, Melbourne, Australia39, Sheikhupura40, Pakistan, indicating that the relationship is not unique to Gdańsk.

Not all associations are unidirectional, the negative associations between GI density and environmental stressors (LST, NO2, PM10, PM2.5) require careful interpretation because the underlying relationships are bidirectional. Previous studies found that GI plays a significant role in mitigating urban heat islands through shading and evapotranspiration at different scales of analysis66. GI also improves air quality via deposition and filtration12,67, so higher GI can lead to lower LST and pollutant concentrations. Our cross-sectional regression models therefore capture existing spatial configurations rather than one-way causal effects. In this study, we interpret LST and air pollutants as environmental stress indicators that co-occur with low GI, rather than as factors that directly constrain GI implementation.

Apart from strong negative correlations, several weak positive correlations need additional interpretation. Rainwater collectors show positive correlation with GI density because they are located along ravines and valley bottoms draining upland forests towards lower terrace rivers. They often are adjacent to forest complexes, parks and other vegetated corridors. In contrast, low lying port and industrial zones are dominated by closed underground drainage and offer little room for vegetated retention49,62,64. The positive association therefore captures the co-location of GI with hydrological infrastructure in priority zones. NDVI showed modest positive associations with GI density, indicating its role as general indicator of vegetation cover.

DEM is weakly positive due to the location of upland forests in the western part of Gdańsk. Although, bivariate correlation shows positive correlation between GI density and distance from industrial areas reflecting Gdańsk’s spatial structure rather than a direct causal effect. However, the spatial lag and spatial error models produce negative coefficients for this variable. This difference arises because simple correlation reflects the overall spatial pattern of the city, where GI appears adjacent to industrial zones as buffers and wetlands on the low terrace, and far from them as large forest complexes on the upland moraine terraces62,64 with a low GI residential belt in between. However, once other variables such as impervious surface, LST, NDVI, DEM, and spatial dependence are included in the regression models, the coefficient represents the independent effect of industrial proximity while holding these other factors constant. As a result, the regression models capture a more localized relationship than the simple correlation68.

Correlation analysis doesn’t account for spatial dependence; therefore, the regression models were used to capture spatial dependence in GI analysis. The OLS and WLS/GLS models provided baseline associations but failed to capture spatial autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity, indicating that GI density is not spatially independent. Spatial Lag models suggest that GI density in a given location is influenced by GI in neighboring cells confirming that urban form variables such as impervious cover and LST generate both local and spillover impacts, consistent with the idea that urban heat, sealed surfaces, and development patterns form continuous spatial fields rather than discrete patches consistent with First Law of Geography68. The Spatial Error model showed lower explanatory power, indicating that spatial dependence in GI distribution arises from substantive spatial processes rather than unobserved spatial noise. Across all models, population density, impervious area and LST showed negative effects on GI, while NO2 and O3 showed slight positive and negative impact (SLM and SEM estimate a single global average coefficient across the entire study area, which is negative, while the GWR model allows coefficients to vary spatially, ) reflecting spatial heterogeneity. This indicates that the negative global coefficient represents an average effect across heterogeneous local processes, where strong positive relationships in eastern districts are offset by negative or weak effects elsewhere. The divergence between global and local NO2 and O3 coefficients indicates spatial non-stationarity, while the overall city-wide effect is negative, eastern districts exhibit significant positive local relationships, demonstrating that global averages mask localized environmental dynamics which is better explained by GWR model.

GWR analysis revealed substantial spatial heterogeneity in how different factors affect GI density across Gdańsk. Impervious surfaces and LST showed consistently negative coefficients across the city, but with stronger effects in dense central and northern districts with urban fabric, limited soil exposure, and pronounced urban heat. In east, weaker relationship of impervious area correspond to less urbanization and more green areas as identified in the Gdańsk Greenery Policy (GPZ, 2023) as ecologically valuable green corridors33. Southern districts (e.g., Chełm, Ujeścisko-Łostowice) showed similar weaker relationship due to ongoing development where private gardens and fragmented green patches buffer the negative effect of new impervious surfaces, producing a weaker local relationship33. Population growth in south and western urban districts (2008 and 2021) confirmed ongoing urban development9,10,64, leading to infrastructure demand while limiting GI density. Similar trends are observed in different cities of world like Melbourne39, Pest County, Hungary, Małopolskie Province, Poland23,24, Łódź and Prague25 and Western, Central, Eastern European cities69.

Previous studies on UHI in Gdańsk confirms the impact of urban structure and land use on temperature variations such as central urban areas (Wrzeszcz Dolny) contributing to elevated temperatures70. Moreover, similar negative relationships between LST and urban greenery have been observed in Polish cities34, both eastern and western European countries69, reinforcing the critical role of GI in mitigating urban heat. In this study, the spatially varying LST coefficients should be interpreted as reflecting GI need. Higher LST values occur where GI is absent or sparse due to biophysical cooling processes, producing a negative association through reverse causality. Thus, LST acts as a heat exposure indicator, identifying districts where cooling services provided by GI are most needed but currently insufficient.

O3 showed bidirectional effects, reflecting the complex interaction between vegetation and air pollution. In northeastern districts (Stogi, Krakowiec-Górki Zachodnie, Rudniki) with forests and coastal green spaces, positive coefficients suggest GI interacts with ozone through natural processes. Plants release biogenic volatile organic compounds (BVOCs) such as isoprene which participate in ozone formation under sunny conditions71. At the same time, GI can help remove pollutants. Similarly, industrial and port districts (Nowy Port, Stogi, and Port of Gdańsk) exhibit complex pollutant interactions. These areas have high emissions from traffic and industry. Although coastal forests are present but as historically documented in Gdańsk72,73, contamination and industrial emissions can reduce the functional efficiency of vegetation10.

A previous study in Gdańsk showed ozone formation is linked to NOx and VOC concentrations under high solar radiation74. In our case, the spatial co-occurrence of industrial zones, high NO2, elevated O3 and GI presence in industrial and coastal districts aligns with the known ozone formation process involving NOx emissions, anthropogenic VOCs and biogenic VOCs. Monitoring data from ARMAG (2022)75 confirm recurrent exceedances of permissible NO2 levels in port-adjacent districts (Nowy Port and Stogi), further supporting the local influence of industrial and traffic emissions. The positive GWR coefficients in these areas suggest that GI tends to be located where pollutant loads are highest, likely because these areas require environmental mitigation. These patterns emphasize the importance of considering atmospheric processes, industrial morphology and pollutant transport when interpreting spatial drivers of GI density and highlight the need for localized environmental interventions.

The spatial patterns, correlations, and modeling results documented above have direct implications for urban planning policy, particularly in the context of climate adaptation and nature-based solutions frameworks.

Green infrastructure represents a key operational strategy within the broader nature-based solutions umbrella, which emphasizes working with natural processes to address challenges including climate change, disaster risk reduction, and human well-being12,16,41. The spatial analysis presented here demonstrates how GI provision can be systematically assessed and targeted to maximize climate adaptation benefits. By identifying areas where environmental stressors (heat, pollution, flood risk) coincide with GI deficits, the analysis supports prioritization and strategic allocation of resources at the municipal level. This will be helpful to achieve policy targets such as the EU Green Infrastructure Strategy11, EU Biodiversity Strategy76, the implementation of the Nature Restoration Law77 and the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 11 and 13)78, which call for integrated spatial planning that embeds nature-based solutions into urban development trajectories.

Locally, Gdańsk Water Policy/Gdańska Polityka Wodna (GPW, 2021), and the GPZ (2023) focus on developing waterside areas, flood protection and increasing the greenery33 such as in Przymorze Małe, Wrzeszcz Dolny, Młodeo Miasto and Letnica33,64. This study aligns with these priorities exhibiting high land surface temperatures, low GI density, and higher impervious surfaces, reinforcing the need for targeted interventions in line with GPZ objectives. However, due to the 1 km grid scale analysis, this study suggested some more areas like central districts of Wzgórze Mickiewicza, Zaspa Młyniec, and Rozstaje, suggesting that these regions have yet to benefit from GPZ’s prioritization fully (Fig. 8). These areas represent spatial mismatches between GI provision and environmental need and may serve as candidate zones under municipal policies. However, the grid scale used in this study serves as a standardized analytical frame for diagnosing spatial patterns in GI provision and environmental stress across Gdańsk. These insights can support municipal-level strategic planning and other policy instruments. While the study identifies priority areas for GI, the selection of specific GI types requires site-specific planning, ecological assessment, and design-level analysis, which is beyond the scope of this city-wide spatial assessment.

While the GPZ considers key environmental stressors, this study indicates that planners may benefit from integrating additional bio-physical and socio-demographic factors, such as population density, proximity to industrial areas, LST and air quality. From a normative planning perspective, the strong negative correlations indicate spatial mismatch between need and supply. For instance, industrial and port-adjacent districts (e.g., Nowy Port, Stogi) experience high pollutant concentrations but relatively sparse GI, while high-density residential districts (e.g., Chełm, Zaspa) exhibit limited public green space per capita. Continuous geospatial monitoring could support equitable distribution of GI and targeted climate adaptation.

Comparable patterns have been documented in other Central and Eastern European cities (e.g., Bucharest, Kraków, Budapest, Warsaw), where suburbanization, and industrial restructuring influenced green space distribution24,28. However, our results do not establish causality regarding governance transitions. Disentangling natural constraints (topography, hydrology) from institutional choices (zoning, privatization policy) requires temporal analysis tracking how GI patterns changed before, during, and after the transition period. Nonetheless, the clustering of low-GI districts and negative coefficients of impervious cover and LST reflect similar pressures identified in Poznań, Dubrovnik and Ljubljana30,79–81, where fragmented governance, transport priorities and limited funding mechanisms complicate GI implementation. To address these issues, Gdańsk could learn from Western European approaches (e.g., Netherlands green roof project82 to mitigate urban flood risk and work towards a more sustainable and equitable urban environment. However, direct policy transfer requires careful adaptation to local institutional capacities, funding mechanisms, and public acceptance. Successful GI implementation depends not only on technical suitability but also on governance structures, stakeholder engagement, and long-term maintenance commitments, factors that vary substantially across urban contexts.

Several limitations and considerations exist in the current study. For example, there were limitations to data acquisition in Gdańsk, particularly the unavailability of fine-scale income and education data that would allow a more comprehensive GI equity assessment. In this study, the elderly population was used as a proxy socio-demographic vulnerability indicator, given evidence that older residents are more sensitive to heat stress and more dependent on accessible nearby green space. However, other vulnerable groups (e.g., low-income households or children) could not be evaluated due to data constraints. GI density represents a structural indicator of green space presence and availability, not a functional indicator of ecosystem service provision. Aggregating heterogeneous GI types into a single density measure introduces functional uncertainty, because different GI forms vary in cooling capacity, pollutant removal, hydrological regulation, accessibility, and planning constraints. Therefore, spatial associations identified in this study should be interpreted as reflecting patterns of overall green space availability, rather than equivalent ecological performance.

There is also uncertainty associated with climate data (future precipitation) used for the analysis. This study used existing flood inventory data to create a flood map to evaluate the current conditions for the GI assessment. However, for future research aimed at optimizing GI site selection, using a flood susceptibility map, generated using climate data together with other factors in our previous research, will be more effective. This approach can help ensure better optimization and more resilient GI planning.

In the future, it would be beneficial to differentiate between two types of green spaces: undeveloped green spaces, like intact forests or natural areas, and intentionally designed green spaces, like parks or landscaped areas. This will provide a clearer picture of the distribution and role of natural versus human-made green areas.

Spatial scales for examining GI are also important. Previously conducted studies examined these relationships aggregated up to the census tract scale19,39,41 or city scale21. This study considered a finer scale when exploring the distribution of GI. However, for future research, it is essential to compare different scales of analysis (district scale, census tract, sewer-shed scale) as some relationships only appear at specific scales while others remain consistent.

Conclusion

Nature-based solutions play a critical role in making cities more resilient, equitable, and climate-adaptive. This study advances their implementation by revealing how biophysical and socio-demographic factors shape GI distribution in Gdańsk. Using statistical modeling, the study moves beyond descriptive assessments to systematically distinguish between factors that constrain GI development and indicators that show need for GI implementation.

By jointly considering environmental stressors, urban form, population characteristics, the framework offers new insights into how urbanization pressures, and policy interventions shape present-day GI patterns. Furthermore, the framework highlights opportunities for targeted GI expansion to be done in low-density/high-stress zones, retrofitting impervious areas with greenery to cool microclimates, integrating air-purifying GI in industrial zones.

Together, this research provides a reproducible pathway for diagnosing gaps in GI provision, supporting more equitable spatial distribution, and aligning local planning with broader climate and sustainability goals (SDG 11 and SDG 13).
