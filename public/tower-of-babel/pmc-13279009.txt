Chimeric biohybrid nanovesicles induce immunogenic cell death for targeted and immune-potentiated glioblastoma therapy
Authors: Xiaojing Zhu, Yutong Xing, Wenrui Jin, Haoran Geng, Fan Li, Yeying Li, Hongtao Zhang, Chengjun Wu, Chen Chen, Zhenyong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) is shielded by both the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Here, we develop a chimeric biohybrid nanovesicle (BEV-RVG29-PTX) that integrates viral tropism, bacterial vesiculation, and chemotherapeutic cytotoxicity into a single genetically programmable platform. Genetic fusion of rabies virus glycoprotein 29 (RVG29) to the AIDA1 autotransporter translocator domain enables robust, autonomous surface expression on bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) without the need for chemical conjugation. The BEV-RVG29-PTX drives receptor-dependent BBB transcytosis and achieves efficient glioma accumulation. Encapsulated paclitaxel (PTX), otherwise restricted by BBB impermeability, is effectively delivered to intracranial tumors and induces reactive oxygen species-driven immunogenic cell death. Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells immune-activation experiments further confirmed an approximately 2-fold increase in CD80/CD86 activation. Synergizing with the pathogen-mimetic characteristics of BEVs, these signals also elicit an approximately 2-fold increase in intratumoral CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, overcome immune exclusion, and achieve durable tumor control with extended survival in orthotopic GBM models. Accordingly, this virus-bacteria-drug biohybrid strategy enables targeted brain delivery while simultaneously amplifying antitumor immunity, offering a promising and translatable approach for GBM treatment.Keywords: Bacterial extracellular vesicles, Blood-brain barrier, Chimeric protein peptide, Glioblastoma, Immunogenic cell death, Paclitaxel

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most aggressive tumor of the central nervous system in adults [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Although maximum surgical resection is carried out, the survival times of patients are generally less than 15 months, and the survival rates are less than 10% [5]. Three are prominent characteristics that hinder this tumor treatment: the first is that the tumor grows diffusely and the boundary is blurred; the second reseason is that the tumor has strong heterogeneity and is resistant to chemotherapy drugs; the third is that the tumor microenvironment (TME) is immunosuppressive, and a variety of cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β are secreted [6]. Coupled with the impermeable nature of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), these characteristics drive GBM toward an immune-cold microenvironment with minimal responsiveness to current immunotherapies.

In the process of cancer treatment, immunogenic cell death (ICD) is a new concept, which can enhance the antitumor immunity of patients [7]. Transforming cytotoxic stress into a self-amplifying antitumor immune response [8]. Tumor cells secrete adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and use calreticulin (CRT) to send signals to antigen-presenting cells (APCs). These are all damage-related molecular patterns (DAMPs), which can promote the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs) cells and enhance the antigen cross-presentation ability, which is equivalent to the tumor vaccine [9]. Anthracyclines and other ICD inducers can enhance the effect of immune checkpoint blockade [10,11], but the ICD induced by temozolomide (TMZ) is relatively weak, and most ICD inducers are excluded by the BBB.

In the process of solving the above problems, nanotechnology has a unique advantage [12]. Among a series of nanosystems, bacteria can secrete nanoscale lipid bilayer vesicles—bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) during growth, and these vesicles are a programmable delivery platform [13,14]. It can activate Toll-like receptor signaling via pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), promote the maturation of DCs and release cytokines, and enhance the adaptive immunity induced by ICD [15,16]. However, there are many problems in the clinical translation of BEVs, such as the risk of cytokine storm and the difficulty of maintaining the consistency of cargo loading [17]. Additionally, the permeability of BBB and the ability of tumor tropism are relatively weak, which restricts the use of BEVs.

In order to bypass these restrictions, we adopted a bioengineering strategy to modify BEVs to achieve brain targeting. This strategy is based on molecular design. First, rabies virus protein 29 (RVG29) is a neurotropic peptide, which is taken from rabies virus glycoprotein [18]. It has strong infectivity, through binding to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, and mediates targeting of endothelial cells [[19], [20], [21]]. Second, RVG29 is fused with the translocator domain of AIDA1, which is a bacterial autotransporter, so that it can be stably presented on the outer membrane of BEV without chemical conjugation. The virus-bacteria chimeric interface can make the system programmable, which can deliver drugs to the brain and achieve transcytosis.

Paclitaxel (PTX) is a kind of hydrophobic chemotherapy drug, which is subject to physical and chemical constraints in the process of passing through the BBB [22,23]. Although it can play an important role in ICD, its immunogenicity is relatively low. When encapsulated in BEVs, PTX can be released in the tumor and accumulate in the brain. The above analysis shows that BEV-RVG29-PTX can pass through the BBB and accumulate in glioma. PTX triggers ATP and HMGB1 release, which can play an important role in CRT. The existence of DAMPs can stimulate DCs, reprogram the TME, and have a strong anti-tumor effect (Scheme 1). In this study, we found that the chimeric BEV has a strong programmability, which can activate the immune system, penetrate the BBB, and achieve enhanced ICD.

Materials and methods

Materials

BCA protein assay kit, 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (Dil) dye, and Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Paclitaxel was obtained from MCE (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Oregon, USA). Escherichia coli (E. coli) BL21 (DE3) cells were obtained from Shanghai Shifeng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Chloramphenicol and indocyanine green (ICG) were obtained from Adamas (Shanghai, China). Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium was purchased from Corning (New York, USA).

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China). All the animals were housed in specific pathogen-free rooms (temperature: 22–25 °C; relative humidity: 50%–60%) under a 12-h light/dark cycle. All operations were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica (Approval No. SYXK-2020-0042). Tumor size was routinely assessed in all treatment studies to ensure compliance with the permitted tumor burden limits.

Plasmid construction

For the construction and surface display of the RVG29 peptide, we used the pAIDA1 plasmid (Plasmid, #79180) purchased from Addgene and performed a minor adjustment. In short, the N-terminal His6-tag was removed, and the RVG29 signal peptide sequence (YTIWMPENPRPGTPCDIFTNSRGKRASNGC) was inserted between the Kpn I and Sal I restriction sites [24]. All plasmids used in this study were synthesized by Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Preparation and characterization of engineered BEV-RVG29

To prepare BEVs expressing RVG29, a plasmid encoding RVG29 was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3). The engineered E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain expressing RVG29 was grown in freshly prepared Luria–Bertani (LB) medium (1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 1% NaCl; pH 7.0) supplemented with chloramphenicol (100 µg/mL). The culture was incubated on a shaking platform (37 °C, 200 rpm) until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (0.1 mM) was then added to induce RVG29 expression, followed by further incubation at 37 °C for 3 h. BEVs were collected by two rounds of centrifugation (4000 rpm, 4 °C, 15 min) and filtered through a 0.22 µm polyethersulfone filter (Corning, USA). The filtrate was then concentrated using a 100,000 MWCO centrifugal filter (Biofil, Guangzhou, China) (5000 rpm, 10 min) and further purified by ultracentrifugation at 28,300 rpm for 2 h at 4 °C (Sorvall WX 100, Thermo Scientific). Finally, the concentration of the engineered BEV-RVG29 was determined using a BCA protein assay kit, and the prepared BEVs were stored in saline (0.90%, w/v NaCl) at −80 °C until use.

BEVs were characterized using a Zetasizer Advance (Malvern Panalytical, UK) to measure the hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential via dynamic light scattering (DLS). The morphology of the BEVs was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM-1400, JEOL, Japan).

In vitro BBB model

An in vitro BBB model was established according to a previously reported method [25,26]. Briefly, bEnd.3 cells were seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell plate at a density of 20,000 cells per well. After the transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) reached 150 Ω·cm², PBS, free ICG, BEV-ICG (10 µg/ml), and BEV-RVG29-ICG (10 µg/ml) were added to the upper chamber and incubated for 2 h. The fluorescence intensities in the upper and lower chambers were measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, USA) at excitation/emission wavelengths of 785 nm/825 nm. Preparation of BEV-RVG29-PTX

BEV-RVG29-PTX nanoparticles were prepared by encapsulating PTX into BEV-RVG29. In brief, BEV-RVG29 and PTX were mixed in NaCl at mass ratios of 1:1 and 1:2. The mixtures were coincubated on a vortex shaker at 37 °C and 750 rpm for 4 h. The free drug was then separated by ultrafiltration centrifugation. The absorbance of the samples was measured at a wavelength of 230 nm using a UV spectrophotometer. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading efficiency (LE) of PTX was calculated as the following equations: EE (%) = (Total mass of PTX used for preparation − Mass of free PTX in the supernatant)/(Total mass of PTX used for preparation) × 100%; LE (%) = (Total mass of PTX used for preparation − Mass of free PTX in the supernatant)/(Total mass of BEV-RVG29s) × 100%.

Orthotopic GBM model

To establish an orthotopic GBM mouse model, GL261-Luc cells were digested and resuspended in PBS. Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus, and each skull was exposed after anesthesia with isoflurane. A total of 50,000 cells were injected using a microsyringe at the coordinates (1, 2, −3) mm relative to the bregma. To verify successful model establishment, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and administered an intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin potassium salt (200 µl, 15 mg/ml). Bioluminescence images of the brain were acquired 10 min post-injection using a Newton 7.0 FT-500 imaging system (IVIS; Vilber Lourmat, France).

In vivo antitumor efficacy

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of BEV-RVG29-PTX, glioma-bearing mice were randomized into four groups (n = 5 per group) and administered BEV-RVG29-PTX (PTX-equivalent dose of 1 mg/kg), BEV-RVG29, paclitaxel (1 mg/kg), or saline on Day 1, 3 and 5 (with the implantation day designated as Day-14). The researchers were blinded to the group allocations during the assessment of the therapeutic outcomes to ensure unbiased evaluation. The body weight of each animal was measured every 2 d. At different time points during the treatment period, tumor fluorescence signals in the mouse brains were detected using a small animal in vivo imaging system after intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin potassium salt (3 mg per mouse). The fluorescence intensity of the tumor tissue was quantified using Kuant software. The body weights of the mice in each group were also monitored. At a certain time point, the mice were euthanized, and the tumor tissue was collected for immunohistochemical staining. By observing the survival time and body weight of the mice after treatment, the therapeutic effect of the drug was evaluated. TUNEL test was used to confirm whether the drug could induce apoptosis. After the mice were euthanized, the brain tissue was frozen and sectioned. The sections were stained with DAPI and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analyses

In this study, biological replicates and specific statistical tests are described in the figure legends. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using two-tailed t tests, whereas ALT levels were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test due to non-normal distribution (Supplementary information). Survival time data were analyzed using the log-rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software v.9.5 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results and discussion

Biological design and mechanistic framework of the chimeric biohybrid nanovesicles

To achieve BBB permeability, we constructed a BEV-based platform. Central to this design is the RVG29-AIDA1-CT chimeric construct, in which the neuron-tropic RVG29 peptide, a 29-amino acid fragment derived from the rabies virus glycoprotein, is fused to the C-terminal translocator domain of the autotransporter AIDA1 [24]. The AIDA1 translocator is an outer membrane anchor, which can direct the chimeric protein to the vesicle surface and play a role in the BEV biogenesis process, and can anchor the RVG29 without chemical modification (Fig. 1A).

The RVG29 moiety retains its native affinity for acetylcholine receptors on neuronal and endothelial cells [27], enabling receptor-mediated BBB transcytosis and glioma targeting, while the AIDA1-CT anchor ensures robust vesicle surface expression. This virus-bacterium fusion could not only eliminate the pathogenicity of the virus, but also play the role of targeting, and form a new fused peptide between different species. This virus and bacteria vesiculation laid the foundation for the preparation of virus-bacteria-drug hybrid vesicles.

TEM was used to observe the morphology of the wildtype and chimeric BEVs, and we found that the two types of BEVs were spherical with a diameter of 10–30 nm (Fig. 1B–1E). DLS showed that the hydrodynamic diameters of the two types of BEVs were 85.6 ± 2.1 nm and 88.6 ± 0.3 nm, respectively, with PDIs of 0.28 ± 0.02 and 0.32 ± 0.06, and zeta potentials of −21.3 ± 1.0 mV and −20.4 ± 2.3 mV (Fig. 1F; Table S1). Analysis of the ζ-potential and hydrodynamic size by DLS indicated that RVG29 incorporation had minimal effects on the surface charge and particle size. Western blotting confirmed the expression and incorporation of the RVG29-AIDA1-CT fusion protein on E. coli and its derived vesicles (Fig. 1G). The surface localization of RVG29 were confirmed by immunogold labeling and flow cytometry (Fig. 1H–1I). Quantitative analysis of immunogold labeling revealed that approximately 55% of vesicles in the BEV-RVG29 group were RVG29-positive (Fig. S1). The 98% engineered BEVs were RVG29 positive by flow cytometry test (Fig. 1I). SDS–PAGE analysis revealed that the overall protein composition of BEV-RVG29 remained similar to that of BEV-WT (Fig. 1J). Collectively, the above data show that the chimeric vesicles can be successfully constructed, which have membrane out surface display with structural stability.

RVG29-AIDA1-CT functionalization enhances the BBB traversal and glioma tropism

In order to analyze the function of RVG29-AIDA1-C signal peptide, we first assessed its transcytosis efficiency through an in vitro BBB model. The results showed that the translocation efficiency of BEV-RVG29 was 2.5 times that of BEV-WT (Fig. 2A and 2B). This transcytosis was nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR)-dependent, as evidenced by the significant inhibition of transport and uptake upon methyllycaconitine (MLA) blockade (Fig. S2B and S2C). The BBB barrier integrity was maintained during the antagonist treatment, indicated by the stable TEER value (Fig. S2A). In vivo fluorescence imaging also found that BEV-RVG29 could rapidly accumulate in the brain, and the intracranial signal intensity was significantly enhanced 2 h after injection (Fig. 2C‒2F). The vesicles were detected in the liver and kidneys, but the retention rate of BEV-RVG29 in the off-target organs was relatively low (Fig. S3). Flow cytometry showed that, compared with BEV-WT, BEV-RVG29 could be internalized by glioma cells, but the uptake of the two vesicles by microglia was not significantly different (Fig. 2G). Confocal microscopy of brain cryosection images confirmed extensive parenchymal penetration and preferential localization of BEV-RVG29 within glioma lesions 24-h after treatment (Fig. 2H).

PTX loading confers cytotoxic and apoptotic potency to BEV-RVG29

The cytotoxicity and biochemical properties of BEV-RVG29-PTX nanovesicles were systematically analyzed. TEM observation of BEV-RVG29-PTX revealed that the vesicles were spherical and uniform (Fig. 3A). Through DLS measurement, it was found that the hydrodynamic diameter of BEV-RVG29-PTX was 93.1 ± 2.2 nm (PDI = 0.29 ± 0.03), and the average zeta potential was -23 ± 1.5 mV, indicating good colloidal stability in the aqueous medium (Fig. 3B; Table S1). UV–vis spectrophotometry results revealed the encapsulation efficiency of PTX was 60% and the corresponding loading efficacy was 40% after optimization (Fig. S4A). Additionally, BEV-RVG29-PTX exhibited a pH-responsive release kinetic compared with physiological pH 7.4 (Fig. S4B). Through the CCK-8 assay, it was found that the proliferation of human U87 glioma cells and mouse GL261 cells was inhibited by PTX, and the cell viability decreased significantly in a concentration dependent way (Fig. 3C and 3D). The antiproliferative effect of BEV-RVG29-PTX was similar to the free PTX at 1 µM (Fig. S5). In order to further analyze the cytotoxic mechanism, the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in the two cell lines was detected; and the release of LDH increased in a concentration dependent way, which was consistent with the damage to the cell membrane (Fig. 3E and 3F). Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining was used for flow cytometry analysis to determine the proportion of early and late apoptotic cells after BEV-RVG29-PTX exposure (Fig. 3G and 3H) and further confirmed the strong apoptosis induction by the BEV-RVG29-PTX.

It should be noted that under specific conditions, PTX itself can induce ICD [15]. Considering its outstanding performance in promoting apoptosis, we analyzed whether BEV-RVG29-PTX could promote the occurrence of ICD and contribute to tumor immunity.

Triggering of ICD and DC activation by BEV-RVG29-PTX

To investigate whether BEV-RVG29-PTX-mediated cytotoxicity involves ICD, we assessed three classic molecular events in U87 glioblastoma cells. Through confocal microscopy, it was found that CRT was fully exposed after BEV-RVG29-PTX treatment (Fig. 4A), indicating the generation of an early prophagocytic signal that was clearly distinguishable from the diffuse, chaotic CRT leakage observed during passive necrotic death (Fig. S6). At the same time, HMGB1 protein was released into the extracellular environment, and its level increased significantly after BEV-RVG29-PTX treatment (Fig. 4B), which was another signal during the process of ICD. BEV-RVG29-PTX elevated ATP levels in the extracellular environment (Fig. S7), confirming the cell death induced by immunogenicity [28].

In order to evaluate the impact of DAMPs on immune stimulation, the supernatant of tumor cells after treatment with different preparations was collected and co-cultured with BMDCs. Flow cytometry revealed a pronounced increase in the percentage of CD80⁺CD86⁺ mature BMDCs (64.4%) after exposure to BEV-RVG29-PTX-treated supernatant, compared with the percentages in the control (31.1%) and BEV-RVG29 groups (42.9%) (Fig. 4C and 4D). Consistently, ELISA analysis of cytokine secretion revealed significant upregulations of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 (Fig. 4E–4G), indicating a Th1-polarized, proinflammatory environment conducive to T-cell activation.

Through above investigations, it was demonstrated that BEV-RVG29-PTX could transform chemotherapy to immunotherapy. By integrating paclitaxel-induced ICD with BEV-RVG29-induced innate immune activation, it bridged tumor cell death with DC activation, thereby enhancing antitumor responses.

Role of ROS-driven mitochondrial collapse in ICD

In order to clarify the mechanism of BEV-RVG29-PTX-induced ICD, ROS levels and mitochondrial integrity were analyzed. After BEV-RVG29-PTX treatment, the concentrations of mitochondrial and cytoplasmic ROS increased significantly in the GL261 cells (Fig. 5A and 5B). The results indicated that mitochondrial function was significantly impaired after 24 h of treatment. Confocal microscopy revealed marked disruption of the mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 5C). Subsequent analysis via flow cytometry (Fig. 5D) and confocal microscopy (Fig. 5E) confirmed a significant increase in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, which is consistent with the observation of increased MitoSOX red fluorescence intensity. The results further showed that the accumulation of ROS in cells was related to the concentration of BEV-RVG29-PTX, and CRT was exposed on the cell surface (Fig. S8A–S8C). There was a significant positive correlation between CRT expression and the ROS level (R2 = 0.9497, P = 0.0255; Fig. S8D), indicating that the ICD response was triggered by ROS generation.

From the above results, it was seen that PTX loaded in BEVs led to mitochondrial dysfunction. The mitochondrial collapse and subsequent destruction triggered the release of DAMPs, which served as the basis for ICD induction and stimulated the immune system to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy (Fig. 5F).

Therapeutic efficacy in orthotopic GBM models

We next assessed the therapeutic efficacy of BEV-RVG29-PTX in an orthotopic GL261 GBM model. Fifteen days after tumor implantation, the tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5) and treated on Day 1, 3 and 5 post-grouping. PBS, BEV-RVG29, BEV-RVG29-PTX (1 mg/kg) and free PTX (1 mg/kg) were administered intravenously to the mice three times at regular intervals (Fig. 6A). Bioluminescence imaging revealed marked tumor growth inhibition following systemic delivery of BEV-RVG29-PTX, compared with the levels of inhibition following delivery of PBS, BEV-RVG29, or free PTX (Fig. 6B and 6C). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that compared with the control group, BEV-RVG29-PTX treatment significantly prolonged survival. The median survival duration increased from 21 d in the control group to 29 d in the BEV-RVG29-PTX-treated group (log-rank test, P = 0.0021). Furthermore, compared with the control group, the risk of death in the BEV-RVG29-PTX treatment group was significantly reduced, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.049 (95% confidence interval: 0.007–0.332) (Fig. 6D).

Histopathological examination (H&E staining) showed that the BEV-RVG29-PTX treatment group yielded the most favorable tumor response (Fig. 6E). The weights of the mice were closely monitored, indicated that there was no significant systemic toxicity (Fig. 6F). Serum biochemical analysis showed that the levels of ALT and AST in all experimental groups were within the normal range (Fig. S9A). H&E staining of the five major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) showed that the tissue structures were intact, without edema, congestion, necrosis and other lesions (Fig. S9B). Immunohistochemistry in the BEV-RVG29-PTX group showed that the number of Ki-67 positive cells was significantly reduced, and the TUNEL staining result showed an increase, which indicated that the tumor cells underwent apoptosis (Fig. 6G).

From the above data, it was demonstrated that this platform played an important role in tumor treatment, not only achieving BBB penetration, but also inhibiting glioma growth and prolonging the survival time of mice.

Immune microenvironment remodeling by chimeric BEV-RVG29-PTX nanotherapy

To investigate the synergistic immunomodulatory effect of BEV-RVG29-PTX in vivo, comprehensive flow cytometry was performed on immune cells from both tumor sites and peripheral organs. The number of DCs in the tumor tissue increased significantly, and the expression levels of CD80 and CD86 were significantly upregulated, which indicated that the antigen presentation capability was enhanced and the DC maturation was improved (Fig. 7A and 7B). After treatment with BEV-RVG29-PTX, the infiltration of CD3⁺ T cells in the TME increased significantly, and CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes accounted for a high proportion, indicating that effector T cells were enriched in the tumor site (Fig. 7C–7F). Notably, splenic analysis (Fig. 7C–7J) further revealed the systemic immune response. Macrophages exhibited a marked polarization toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype (F4/80⁺CD86⁺), accompanied by a concurrent reduction in tumor-associated M2 macrophages (F4/80⁺CD206⁺). (Fig. 7G–7J). The M1/M2 ratio was used to describe the polarization of macrophages, which was 4.0 in the BEV-RVG29-PTX group (Fig. S10). It could be seen that the use of BEV-RVG29-PTX changed the tumor immune microenvironment and enhanced anti-tumor activity.

Overall, these findings demonstrated that BEV-RVG29-PTX served as a nanotherapeutic modality capable of promoting DC maturation, inducing tumor cell death, enhancing T cell infiltration, and reprogramming macrophages. Given its relatively high safety profile, this platform holds great promise.

In this study, we have developed a drug platform BEV-RVG29-PTX, which can target the brain and induce immunogenic effects. Surface-RVG29 anchored via AIDA1-CT promotes glioma accumulation, while the encapsulated PTX induces ICD. This combination acts as an adjuvant in brain tumor therapy by modulating the tumor immune microenvironment, enabling T cells to exert cytotoxic effects and thereby achieving tumor suppression.

In the development of nanomedicine, the biggest challenge is to overcome the BBB and immune evasion. To achieve this goal, this study constructs a BEV platform with characteristics of immune regulation and targeted brain delivery; this virus-bacteria-drug design is the key to its function. The virus-bacterium-drug design is central to its functionality. At the molecular targeting level, RVG29, displayed on the vesicle surface via an AIDA1-CT-based outer membrane anchoring strategy [24], enables receptor-mediated BBB transcytosis and glioma tropism. This strategy effectively addresses the long-standing limitations of native BEVs, namely, their insufficient brain specificity and limited tumor-targeting ability. At the therapeutic level, encapsulated PTX induces ROS-driven mitochondrial collapse, triggering canonical ICD characterized by CRT exposure, ATP secretion, and HMGB1 release [29,30]. The DAMPs not only cooperate with the PAMPs of BEVs, but also promote the maturation of DCs and enhance the sensitivity of cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Additionally owing to its physicochemical properties, PTX alone poorly penetrates the BBB; although it can induce ICD, the magnitude of immune activation is limited, similar to that of other conventional chemotherapeutics [[31], [32], [33]]. In this study, a bioengineering strategy was used to enhance the immunogenicity of PTX. Flow cytometry analysis found that after the treatment with PTX, the proportion of F4/80⁺CD206⁺ M2-type macrophages decreased, indicating that the treatment could effectively inhibit the polarization of M2-type macrophages, which was consistent with previous research results [34]. Importantly, compared with PTX monotherapy or BEV-RVG29 alone, BEV-RVG29-PTX resulted in a more pronounced reduction in the number of M2 macrophages, highlighting the synergistic effect of targeted delivery and the intrinsic immune-stimulating properties of BEVs. Coupled with efficient BBB penetration, these data demonstrate that BEV-RVG29-PTX both optimizes drug delivery and drives the reprogramming of the immunosuppressive TME, providing a mechanistic basis for its superior therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, intratumoral CD8⁺ cytotoxic T-cell infiltration roughly doubled, demonstrating a robust enhancement of adaptive antitumor immunity.

From a mechanistic point of view, the chimeric strategy can transform the traditional chemotherapy into an immunotherapy. Moreover, the interaction between AIDA1 and RVG29 can enable BEV-RVG29-PTX to cross the BBB and accurately reach the glioma site, and the cellular stress caused by PTX can activate the immune response. Our findings indicate that this strategy can change the microenvironment of GBM and promote the formation of a self-amplifying anti-tumor immune circuit. It should be noted that the nanocarrier can not only play the role of transporting drugs but also play the role of an immunomodulator.

Although the BEV-RVG29-PTX system demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in an orthotopic glioma model, extending the median survival of tumor-bearing mice from 21 to 29 d, several limitations must be addressed to enable clinical translation. First, the production of BEVs on a large scale under GMP conditions is a major challenge. In the process of industrialization, we need to optimize the purification and fermentation processes to ensure the consistency of drug loading efficiency and the size of the vesicles [35]. Second, the immunogenicity of bacterial components is a double-edged sword. It can play the role of a natural adjuvant, promote the maturation of BMDCs, and trigger ICD through the interaction of ROS and paclitaxel, but it can also cause adverse immune reactions in the human body. Repeated administration can lead to cytokine release syndrome, so comprehensive biosafety evaluations in non-human primate models are essential prior to clinical application. Third, although the GL261 model can reflect the characteristics of the tumor immune microenvironment, GBM patients have spatiotemporal heterogeneity [36]. The therapeutic efficacy of this system depends on the binding of RVG29 to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), yet nAChR expression levels vary substantially among patients and across tumor subclones. Therefore, the single-target strategy cannot completely eliminate all GBM variants, which may lead to tumor escape [37]. In order to eliminate the above obstacles, we should make full use of the advantages of BEV-RVG29-PTX, and develop personalized multi-target strategies in the next stage of research, especially by integrating immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies) to enhance antitumor immunity and prevent tumor recurrence [38].

Conclusions

BEV-RVG29-PTX integrates several functions into a single-vesicle nanoplatform, which shows outstanding performance in immune activation, BBB penetration, and cytotoxicity. The modular and programmable architecture of this platform has good translational potential, and can provide a general strategy for the treatment of solid tumors, overcoming not only the physical barrier, but also the immunological barrier. This method is not only suitable for the treatment of glioblastoma, but also can become the basis for the development of new nanomedicines, facilitating trans-barrier delivery and molecular targeting in the therapeutic process.
