The presence of a Segond fracture in ACL-injured patients is associated with increased internal tibial rotation on preoperative MRIs
Authors: Zhi-yu Zhang, Hong-de Wang, Si-yao Wang, Pakezhati Maimaitijiang, Cheng Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSegond fracture is considered a component of the anterolateral complex (ALC) injury, yet the underlying cause and clinical outcomes of this bony avulsion remain subjects of debate. Additionally, MRI measurements of altered tibiofemoral position in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL)-injured patients with a Segond fracture have not been reported. The purpose of this study is to measure the rotational tibiofemoral position on MRI in ACL-injured patients with a Segond fracture.MethodsA total of 44 patients with a primary ACL injury and a concomitant Segond fracture were included in the Segond fracture group, with the time from injury to MRI within 3 months. Avulsion was confirmed via preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans. The control group comprised 44 matched patients with primary ACL injury and an MRI-determined ALC injury but without a Segond fracture, also with the time from injury to MRI within 3 months. The MRI-determined ALC injury included injuries to the anterolateral ligament, Kaplan fibers, and anterolateral joint capsule, as identified based on previous studies. Sex, age, and BMI were matched between the two groups using propensity score matching (PSM). Arthroscopic findings, concomitant collateral ligament injuries, and preoperative MRI measurements were compared between the two groups.ResultsThe Segond fracture group demonstrated a higher frequency of concomitant patellar and femoral trochlear injuries (p = 0.0110) and lateral collateral ligament injuries (p = 0.0121) compared to the control group. Additionally, significantly increased internal rotational tibial subluxation (IRTS) (p = 0.0095) and axial internal tibial rotation (ITRa) (p = 0.0306) were observed in the Segond fracture group. A strong positive correlation was found between IRTS and ITRa (rp = 0.8201), indicating that these two tibial rotation measurement methods were correlated. No significant differences were observed in the measurements of anteroposterior tibiofemoral position and posterior tibial slope (PTS) between the two groups.ConclusionThe presence of a Segond fracture was associated with significantly increased internal tibial rotation measured on MRIs, suggesting that this bony avulsion may represent a more severe form of ALC injury in ACL-injured patients and should be managed with caution.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament, Segond fracture, Anterolateral complex, Internal rotational tibial subluxation, Internal rotation, Tibiofemoral position, MRI

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is one of the most common sports injuries, potentially resulting in reduced knee function [10, 17, 32] and contributing to the progression of meniscal injuries and osteoarthritis [4, 11, 12]. Surgical reconstruction of the ACL and postoperative rehabilitation have been extensively discussed in recent literature [2, 15, 16, 18]. Additionally, increasing attention has also been directed toward the preoperative identification of patients at high risk of ACL reconstruction failure [14]. Researchers have observed that a concomitant bony avulsion at the lateral tibial plateau occurs in some cases of ACL injury, which has drawn considerable interest [21].

In 1879, French surgeon Paul Segond first described this bony avulsion of the proximolateral tibial plateau caused by internal tibial rotational force applied to cadaveric knees [19], which later became known as the Segond fracture. Recent studies have reported that the prevalence of Segond fractures in patients with primary ACL injuries is 7–9%, as confirmed by radiographs and computed tomography (CT) scans [21, 27]. However, the cause and clinical outcome of this bony avulsion remain subjects of considerable debate.

The consensus group meeting acknowledged that the anterolateral ligament (ALL), deep iliotibial band (ITB) and biceps aponeurosis attach in the region of the Segond fracture [3]. Additionally, some researchers consider the Segond fracture to be part of the anterolateral complex (ALC) injury [20]. The ALC of the knee consists of the distal ITB with its Kaplan fiber, the ALL, and the anterolateral capsule of the knee joint, contributing to internal rotational knee stability and restricting pivot-shift phenomenon as a secondary stabilizer to ACL [3, 7, 12, 24, 25]. While some studies have reported that MRI-determined ALC injury is associated with a higher risk of failure and worse functional scores after isolated ACLR [3, 22, 23], other studies have found that the presence of a Segond fracture does not affect knee stability in patients with ACL tears, nor does it result in significant differences in postoperative clinical scores or knee stability [27]. Moreover, the presence of a Segond fracture has been reported to not be associated with a higher risk of ACLR failure [21].

The MRI measurements of altered tibiofemoral position have been introduced to aid in the diagnosis, management of surgical indications, and outcome evaluation of ACL injuries and concomitant ALC injuries [13, 29, 31]. Increased anterior tibial subluxation (ATS) has been identified on sagittal MRIs in patients with ACL injury compared to those with intact ACL [29]. Furthermore, increased internal rotational tibial subluxation (IRTS) has been demonstrated to be associated with MRI-determined ALC injuries in patients with ACL injury [31]. However, MRI measurements specifically for ACL-injured patients with a Segond fracture have not yet been reported. The aim of the present study is to compare preoperative MRI measurements between ACL-injured patients with a Segond fracture (Segond fracture group) and those with a concomitant MRI-determined ALC injury but without a Segond fracture (control group). The MRI measurements performed in this study include anteroposterior and rotational tibiofemoral position [9, 29, 31], as well as posterior tibial slope [6], as introduced in previous studies. Since the presence of a Segond fracture may require greater force to cause a bony avulsion of the ALC, we hypothesized that the Segond fracture group would demonstrate increased internal tibial rotation on preoperative MRIs compared to the control group.

Methods

Patients

The flow diagram of patient inclusion is shown in Fig. 1. A systematic search was performed using the term “Segond” or “anterolateral” across clinical records of 1,287 inpatients who underwent ACLR by a medical group of attending surgeons from May 2017 to May 2022 at our institute. A total of 348 patients were diagnosed with concomitant ALC injury on preoperative MRI scans based on methods from previous studies [1, 5, 26, 28], which included identifying injuries to the anterolateral ligament, Kaplan fibers, and anterolateral joint capsule (Fig. 2). A total of 44 patients meeting the following criteria were included: (1) a primary ACL injury with concomitant Segond fracture; (2) available preoperative anteroposterior radiograph, CT scans and MRI scans; (3) time from injury to MRI was within 3 months; (4) no concomitant posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injury or other fractures; and (5) no history of patellar dislocation or arthroscopic surgery. The avulsion was reconfirmed on the anteroposterior radiograph and the coronal view of the CT scan (Fig. 3). Patients diagnosed with concomitant ALC injury but without Segond fracture on preoperative CT scans were selected using the same inclusion criteria. A propensity score matching (PSM) process was then performed to balance sex, age and body mass index (BMI), resulting in 44 matched patients being selected as the control group.

MRI measurements

MRI scans were performed using GE Discovery MR750 3.0 T System (PrizMed Imaging, Unit A Willowick, OH, USA) according to the standardized institutional protocol. Slice thicknesses were 3 mm for the sagittal T1-weighted images (T1WIs) and fat-saturated proton density-weighted images (FS-PDWIs). Patients were required to remain in a supine position with the knee fully extended in a neutral position without hyperextension. All MRI measurements were performed using Adobe Illustrator CC 2021 (Adobe Inc., USA), and the data are presented with one decimal place. The methods for MRI measurements were based on a recent study that clearly illustrated the approach with detailed Figures [32].

The anteroposterior tibiofemoral position was evaluated on sagittal T1WIs, the anterior tibial subluxation (ATS) in the lateral (LATS) and medial (MATS) compartments was measured according to previous studies, using the longitudinal tibial axis as the reference line [29, 31]. Global ATS (GATS) was calculated as the average of LATS and MATS.

The evaluation of the rotational tibiofemoral position included measuring internal rotational tibial subluxation (IRTS) and axial internal tibial rotation (ITRa). IRTS was measured on sagittal T1WIs, calculated as the difference between LATS and MATS (LATS minus MATS) [29]. ITRa was measured on axial FS-PDWIs using methods described in previous studies, defined as the angle between the line tangent to the posterior border of the femur and the tibial plateau [9, 31]. It should be noted that a negative ITRa value indicates external tibial rotation.

Posterior tibial slope (PTS) in the lateral (LPTS) and medial (MPTS) compartments was measured on sagittal T1WIs based on the protocol described by Hudek et al. [6]. Global PTS (GPTS) was calculated as the average of LPTS and MPTS, while PTSL−M was determined as the difference between LPTS and MPTS (LPTS minus MPTS).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R for Mac OS X GUI version 4.0.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were described as means (standard deviation) if they followed a normal distribution confirmed by the Shapiro‒Wilk test, or as medians [interquartile range] if they did not. Discrete variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Equality of variances was confirmed by the Bartlett test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using the R package “MatchIt” (method = “nearest”, ratio = 1) to balance sex, age and BMI between the Segond fracture group and the control group.

The unpaired t-test and Mann–Whitney test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) were used to compare differences in continuous variables between the two groups. The chi-squared test, chi-squared test with Yates’ continuity correction, and Fisher’s exact test were employed to identify differences in discrete variables. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the correlation between IRTS and ITRa. The two variables followed a bivariate normal distribution, confirmed by the Shapiro‒Wilk multivariate normality test. The Pearson correlation coefficient and its 95% confidence interval were calculated.

Intra- and interobserver reliability of the MRI-determined ALC injury and MRI measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient in accordance with the established guideline [8]. Three researchers were blinded to patient information and performed the observations for the interobserver reliability test. The first researcher repeated the observations for the intraobserver reliability test. The first set of observations from the first researcher was used for data analysis. The results demonstrated good to excellent reliability.

Power analysis was performed with an acceptable alpha error of 0.05 using the “pwr.t.test” function (n = 44, sig.level = 0.05, type = “two.sample”, alternative = “two.sided”) in the R package “pwr”. The effect size was estimated by calculating Cohen’s d using the mean IRTS values from the two groups and the pooled standard deviation. The analysis indicated a power of 83.8%.

Results

A total of 44 patients were included in the Segond fracture group, consisting of 35 male and 9 female patients, with a mean age of 32.4 years and a median time from injury to MRI of 12 days. The control group included 44 matched patients, consisting of 32 males and 12 females, with a mean age of 29.5 years and a median time from injury to MRI of 42 days (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were identified between the two groups in terms of sex (p = 0.4531), age (p = 0.1772), BMI (p = 0.6918), and laterality (p = 0.1990) (Table 1). Although the control group had a significantly longer time from injury to MRI (p = 0.0002) (Table 1), all patients in both groups were included only if the MRI scan was performed within 90 days of injury. Additionally, the Segond fracture group had a significantly higher incidence of concomitant articular cartilage injuries at the patella and femoral trochlea (p = 0.0110) and LCL injuries (p = 0.0121) (Table 2).

Discussion

The most important finding of the present study was that a significantly increased IRTS and ITRa was observed in acute ACL-injured patients with a Segond fracture compared to those with concomitant MRI-determined ALC injury without a Segond fracture. The results suggest that the Segond fracture may represent a more severe ALC injury, leading to increased internal tibial rotation as measured on MRIs.

The Segond fracture can be observed around the anterolateral knee following an ACL rupture [3]. While the specific structure primarily responsible for the Segond fracture remains unclear, some researchers consider it a type of ALC injury [5, 20]. However, the reported clinical outcomes for the presence of a Segond fracture do not align with our expectations. Yoon et al. found that the presence of a Segond fracture did not affect knee stability in patients with ACL tears, nor did it result in significant differences in postoperative clinical scores or knee stability [27]. Additionally, Slagstad et al. reported that the presence of a Segond fracture was not associated with a higher risk of ACLR failure [21]. In this study, we found that the presence of a Segond fracture was associated with a significantly increased internal tibial rotation as measured on preoperative MRIs. This finding suggests that the Segond fracture may result in a worse tibiofemoral position on MRIs, supporting a possible association between Segond fractures and ALC injury. We assume that no difference in clinical outcomes for Segond fractures may be due to the potential for this bony avulsion to self-heal over an extended time period. In this study, we included only acute patients with a time from injury to MRI within 3 months. This could be a key reason why we were able to identify differences in tibiofemoral position as measured on MRIs. Future studies should focus on the healing rate of Segond fractures when evaluating their clinical outcomes. Investigating the potential association between preoperative IRTS or ITRa and the healing rate of Segond fractures could enhance our understanding of this phenomenon.

The MRI measurements of the rotational tibiofemoral position (IRTS and ITRa) have been introduced and garnered attention [9, 29, 31]. Zhang et al. [31] reported that increased internal tibial rotation can be observed on preoperative MRIs in patients with concomitant ALC injury compared to those with isolated ACL injury, suggesting that these MRI measurements could serve as an indicator for ALC injury and contribute to clinical management (Fig. 5). In this study, we found a strong positive correlation between IRTS (measured on sagittal images) and ITRa (measured on axial images), providing internal validation for the reliability of our measurement methods.

The MRI measurements can be influenced by numerous factors. Previous studies have confirmed that concomitant meniscal injuries and a long injury-to-MRI time can result in an increase in ATS [13, 29, 33], and a strong positive correlation between PTS and ATS has also been reported [30]. In this study, we controlled for sex, age, and BMI between the two groups. Although the time from injury to MRI was significantly different between the groups (Table 1), we included only acute ACL-injured patients within 3 months to control for this variable. No differences were identified in concomitant meniscal injuries, ATS, and PTS between the two groups (Table 1), indicating that the influence of these potential confounders was minimal.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, this study lacks data on the pivot-shift maneuver, so no direct evidence was provided to support the association between the MRI measurements and rotational knee instability. The cross-sectional study design and limited sample size are notable limitations. Sampling bias due to inadequate records is a major source of bias in this study. Furthermore, the small sample size in this study makes multivariable analysis inappropriate. The bias in MRI measurements is attributed to the slice thickness (3 mm) of the MRI scan, the height of the tibia visible in the images, and the knee flexion angle at the time of imaging. The ALC injury without Segond fracture group had a longer time from injury to MRI (Table 1), and the influence of this variable was not controlled in this study, which may or may not be significant.

Conclusion

The presence of a Segond fracture was associated with significantly increased internal tibial rotation measured on MRIs, suggesting that this bony avulsion may represent a more severe form of ALC injury in ACL-injured patients and should be managed with caution.
