Not Your Typical Hyperammonemia: Non-hepatic Severe Hyperammonemia in an Adult
Authors: Michele Barnhill, Reena Yaman, Krystal Renszel, Sadeer Alzubaidi, Hugo Vargas, David Chascsa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHyperammonemia in adults is most often due to cirrhosis. Ammonia is metabolized through the urea cycle. With liver disease, this pathway is altered, and urea is unable to be formed, creating a buildup of ammonia with numerous side effects, including encephalopathy. A less common presentation is hyperammonemia in the absence of liver disease. We present a rare case of non-cirrhotic hyperammonemia due to a splenogonadal shunt.

INTRODUCTION

Hyperammonemia in adults is most often due to cirrhosis. Ammonia is predominantly produced in the gastrointestinal tract from breakdown of nitrogenous compounds.1 Ammonia is then absorbed by the portal system and transferred to the liver where it is converted to urea through the urea cycle so that it may be excreted by the kidneys.1 Liver disease alters this pathway and urea is unable to be formed, creating a buildup of ammonia which can cause encephalopathy.2 Hyperammonemia in the absence of liver disease is a less common presentation, but is an important clinical diagnosis to be aware of because patients can be mistakenly diagnosed with psychiatric disorders or misdiagnosed with cirrhosis. We present a rare case of hyperammonemia due to a splenogonadal shunt in the absence of cirrhosis.

CASE REPORT

A 73-year-old woman with history of end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and congestive heart failure was referred to hepatology for evaluation of encephalopathy to be worked up for liver disease. She had been evaluated at a local hospital and started on lactulose and rifaximin. Per review of outside hospital documentation, the working diagnosis was chronic liver disease due to alcohol or primary biliary cholangitis in the setting of elevated alkaline phosphatase. However, on further evaluation, there did not appear to be a significant history of alcohol abuse, and anti-mitochondrial antibody was negative.

Extensive laboratory work demonstrated normal liver function tests (total bilirubin 0.4 U/L, aspartate transaminase 19 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 10 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 105, and international normalized ratio 0.9) and no cause of chronic liver disease. Imaging was without findings suggestive of cirrhosis. Transient elastography was consistent with fibrosis stage 1 and steatosis grade 3. She was lost to follow-up for 3 years. In the interim, she was denied candidacy for kidney transplantation on the basis of poor functional status due to significant cognitive dysfunction affecting the ability to manage activities of daily living.

Subsequently, the patient was hospitalized after a ground-level fall complicated by multiple fractures and small subdural hematomas that did not require surgical intervention. During this admission, she continued to have evidence of grade II hepatic encephalopathy despite lactulose and rifaximin administration. Head imaging was negative for both acute cerebrovascular accident and progression of known subdural hematomas. Her ammonia level was found to be elevated to 346 mcmol/L (normal <51 mcmol/L) and had reached a peak of 528 mcmol/L. Cross-sectional imaging again did not show any evidence of cirrhosis and no signs of ascites. She was transferred to our institution for further care. Follow-up abdominal imaging demonstrated a patent moderate-to-large portosystemic shunt from the inferior mesenteric vein to the right gonadal vein (Figure 1). The internal mesenteric vein measured 11 mm in diameter and the main portal vein measured 7 mm in diameter, resulting in the majority of portal flow drainage through the shunt. Transjugular liver biopsy demonstrated a wedged portal pressure of 9 mm Hg and a portosystemic gradient of 4 mm Hg, with liver biopsy demonstrating no evidence of advanced fibrosis. Interventional radiology performed a plug-assisted retrograde transvenous occlusion. Subsequently, the day after plug-assisted retrograde transvenous occlusion, her ammonia level normalized with complete resolution of her encephalopathy. Follow-up genetic testing to evaluate for urea cycle disorder was negative.

DISCUSSION

In evaluating non-hepatic hyperammonemia, rarer etiologies must be considered after liver disease has been fully excluded. Broadly, these can be divided into increased production of ammonia and decreased clearance of ammonia.1 Increased production includes iatrogenic causes, such as medication-induced and hyperalimentation in addition to infection-mediated, oncologic (multiple myeloma), and muscular (high-intensity exercise or seizure activity).1 Decreased clearance includes metabolic disorders (urea cycle), renal dysfunction or anatomic (portosystemic shunting).1

In cirrhosis, there is increased angiogenesis, which affects portal hypertension and can lead to formation of portosystemic shunts. It is less common to see portosystemic shunt formation in those without cirrhosis because there is no portal hypertension, which is what makes our case unique.3 Potential causes include congenital formation, downstream vascular clot burden, abdominal trauma, or prior intra-abdominal surgery.4 Watanabe et al classified 5 different types of portosystemic shunts in non-cirrhotic patients, the most common of which is extrahepatic—which was present in our patient. Typically, these shunts will originate from the left gastric vein, splenic vein, or superior mesenteric vein with connection to the renal vein.4 In the United States, there are scattered case reports of hyperammonemia due to splenorenal shunts. Internationally, a Japanese study evaluated non-cirrhotic hyperammonemia due to portosystemic shunts. A total of 47 cases were found, with 23 classified as extrahepatic with variable clinical presentations. These portosystemic shunts can result in significant encephalopathy as in our case but can also be completely asymptomatic.3 Interventions for splenorenal shunts include embolization, surgical closure, or plug-assisted closure, the latter of which was performed in our case.3

Non-cirrhotic hepatic encephalopathy is an important entity to keep within a medical differential, particularly in elderly patients because they can often be misdiagnosed with an underlying psychiatric or neurocognitive disorder or falsely labeled with cirrhosis.4 As noted previously, our patient was declined kidney transplantation on the premise of poor functional status—of which her underlying encephalopathy was most certainly playing a role.
