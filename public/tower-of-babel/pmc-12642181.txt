Small versus large bore chest tube in traumatic hemothorax, hemopneumothorax, and pneumothorax: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with trial sequential analysis
Authors: Stefano Granieri, Stefano Piero Bernardo Cioffi, Alessandro Asaro, Michele Altomare, Andrea Spota, Francesco Virdis, Roberto Bini, Shailvi Gupta, Kimberly Davis, Stefania Cimbanassi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal tube size for managing traumatic hemothorax, pneumothorax, or hemopneumothorax remains debated. While large-bore chest tubes (LCTs—≥ 28 Ch) are traditionally favored, emerging evidence suggests that small-caliber tubes (SCTs—≤ 14 Ch), such as pigtail catheters and small straight tubes, may offer similar efficacy with fewer complications. This study aimed to evaluate the comparative effectiveness and safety of SCTs versus LCTs from Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) in adult trauma patients and to assess the conclusiveness of the current evidence using trial sequential analysis (TSA).MethodsThe study was conducted according to the Cochrane recommendations, searching the PubMed, Scopus, and EMBASE datasets up to 25th March 2025 without language restrictions (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251023165). The primary outcome was treatment failure; secondary outcomes included insertion-related complications, duration of drainage, and length of hospital stay. Random effects models based on restricted maximum likelihood and Hartung-Knapp correction were developed. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to detect sources of heterogeneity. The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. TSA was used to evaluate the risk of random error and to determine whether the required information size (RIS) had been reached.ResultsFour RCTs (n = 676 patients) were included. Pooled analysis showed no significant difference in failure rates between SCTs and LCTs (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.66–1.35, I2 = 0%). No significant differences were observed in complication rates or hospital stay. Duration of tube placement was significantly shorter in the SCT group (MD − 0.49 days, p = 0.02). TSA indicated that the cumulative evidence was underpowered, achieving only 22% of the RIS (3110 patients). The Z-curve did not cross thresholds for benefit, harm, or futility.ConclusionSCTs appear to be as effective and safe as LCTs for selected trauma patients with uncomplicated thoracic injuries. However, due to limited sample size and heterogeneity across trials, current evidence is inconclusive. Larger, high-quality RCTs are warranted to confirm these findings and guide clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13017-025-00655-x.

Introduction

Thoracic trauma accounts for a substantial proportion of trauma-related hospitalizations and deaths worldwide, with hemothorax (HTX), pneumothorax (PNX), and hemopneumothorax (HPTX) being among the most frequent clinical consequences. While many of these injuries can be managed non-operatively, appropriate and timely drainage remains a cornerstone in preventing complications such as retained hemohoraces, empyema, and respiratory deterioration. Traditionally, large-bore chest tubes (LCTs), ranging from 28 to 40 Ch, have been the standard of care for draining hemothoraces after trauma. This approach assumes that larger tube diameters ensure more effective evacuation of blood or air from the pleural space, thereby minimizing the risk of retained HTX and associated morbidity [1, 2]. Moreover, larger tubes are traditionally believed to enhance drainage performance, not only due to increased luminal diameter but also because of larger fenestrations, which may facilitate evacuation of more viscous fluids such as clotted blood.

However, the last two decades have witnessed growing interest in the use of small-bore chest tubes (SCTs), including percutaneous pigtail catheters and smaller straight thoracostomy tubes, as potentially equivalent—and possibly superior—alternatives to LCTs in selected trauma patients. The advantages of smaller catheters include easier bedside placement using the Seldinger technique, less procedural trauma, reduced insertion-site pain, improved patient comfort, and lower rates of wound complications [3–6]. These features are particularly appealing in patients with isolated thoracic injuries who are hemodynamically stable and not in need of emergent decompression.

Despite these theoretical benefits, the clinical effectiveness of SCTs remains debated. While several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared SCTs to LCTs in various trauma populations, methodological inconsistencies, limited sample sizes, and diverse outcome measures have hindered the development of a clear consensus. Questions remain regarding whether smaller catheters are truly equivalent in preventing treatment failure, defined as retained HTX or PNX requiring re-intervention, and whether their use carries a comparable safety profile.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to synthesize the current evidence from RCTs comparing small and large-bore chest tubes in adult patients with traumatic HTX, HPTX, or PNX. By incorporating a trial sequential analysis (TSA), we further assess the robustness and conclusiveness of the cumulative evidence, accounting for the risks of random error and insufficient statistical power that often compromise meta-analytic findings based on few and small trials.

Methods

Search strategy

The systematic review protocol was registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251023165). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) and Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR II) guidelines [7, 8] were followed.

The PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus databases were screened without time restrictions up to 25th March 2025. The research also included all the MeSH Terms. The full search queries are available in the supplementary materials.

Articles without free full text available were searched through the University of Milan digital library, the “Alberto Malliani” library, or direct contact with the authors. A hand-search of references of included studies and previous reviews on the topic was also performed to include additional relevant studies according to our selection criteria. Two investigators (SG, SPBC) carried out the literature search independently.

A specific population (P), intervention (I), comparator (C), outcome (O), and study design (S) (PICOS) framework was specified to define study eligibility, as recommended. Systematic review process and data extraction

The Rayyan web application was used to identify and remove duplicates among identified records [9]. Overall, 1644 articles were originally identified. After excluding duplicates, two independent reviewers (SG, SPBC) screened titles and abstracts of 1039 records. Investigators were blinded to each other’s decisions. Disagreement was solved through discussion.

Five articles were assessed for eligibility, and 4 were eventually included in data synthesis. The study by Baumann et al. was excluded due to the overlap of the years of enrollment with the study by Kulvatunyou et al.. The flowchart depicting the overall review process according to PRISMA is reported in supplementary materials.

Data were extracted independently by two authors (AA, SG). Information about study details, participant demographics and baseline characteristics, and outcomes of interest was gathered in a computerized spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel 2016; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond; WA). In case of disagreement, two further investigators (AS, MA) helped resolve it through discussion.

Assessment of the methodological quality (Risk of Bias) and certainty of evidence

The risk of bias was assessed for individual studies according to the ROB-2 tool provided by the Cochrane Collaboration [10] (SPBC). Data were collected according to the methodology proposed by Higgins in a computerized spreadsheet. Bar and traffic light plots were created to display the results of the risk of bias assessment graphically.

One investigator (SG) assessed the certainty of the evidence (CoE) according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, using the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool (GDT) [11]. As recommended, the overall CoE was determined by using the lowest grade for any of the outcomes deemed critical. A “Summary of findings” table was produced to condense the certainty of evidence assessment.

Endpoints

The primary outcome was failure rate, defined as incompletely drained or retained HTX requiring a second intervention (second chest tube insertion, thrombolysis, interventional-radiology guided drainage, video-assisted thoracoscopy surgery (VATS), or open thoracotomy). The secondary outcomes were ICU length of stay (ICU-LOS), hospital LOS, initial and total drainage output, tube days, and insertion-related complications.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome measure was expressed Risk Ratio (RR) and 95% Confidence Intervals (95%CI). Secondary outcome measures were reported as MD or Risk Ratio (RR) with 95% CI as appropriate. Means were retrieved from each manuscript. Whenever not overtly reported, they were computed from medians, ranges, interquartile ranges (IQR), and sample sizes according to Wan’s method [12]. Meta-analyses of means and binary outcomes were developed.

Common and random effects models based on the generic inverse variance method with a restricted maximum-likelihood estimator (REML) and Hartung-Knapp variance correction were developed. Between-studies heterogeneity was explored using I2 statistic, but since it heavily depends on the precision of the included studies, τ2 and 95% prediction intervals were estimated as well. In the presence of low heterogeneity, a common effects was preferred over a random effects model. The presence of outliers was explored, and influential cases were detected through the leave-one-out method or graphical display of study heterogeneity (GOSH) analysis. Forest plots were developed to display the results graphically. Since fewer than 10 studies were included in the present meta-analysis, no publication bias assessment was conducted.

A trial sequential analysis (TSA) was conducted to calculate the required information size (RIS—the sample size that should be obtained to consider the results “not at risk” for type I and II errors) and to assess the robustness and sufficiency of the cumulative evidence. The RIS was calculated considering a control event rate (pc) of 30%, a relative risk reduction (RRR) of 15%, a type I error at 5%, and type II at 20%. The Z-curve was designed by adding each study sequentially. The conventional, monitoring limits, and futility boundaries were drawn. The conventional edge indicates when the P value is equal to 0.05. A type I error may occur if the Z-curve crosses the monitoring boundary before reaching the required information size (RIS). These boundaries indicate thresholds beyond which false positives can be excluded. If the Z-curve remains within both conventional and monitoring boundaries without reaching the RIS, a type II error is possible. However, once the RIS is met without a significant effect, type II error can be ruled out, and further studies are unlikely to alter the conclusion.

Statistical analysis was conducted with R statistical software (The Comprehensive R Archive Network—CRAN, ver. 4.0.0 × 64) [13], using “meta”, “metafor”, “robvis”, “dmetar”, and “RTSA” packages [14–18].

Results

Descriptive analysis of included studies

Overall, 4 studies [3–6] were included in the data synthesis for a total of 676 patients. Three studies were conducted in the USA and one in China over a period of 18 years (from 2003 to 2021). Three RCTs included only patients sustaining traumatic HTX and HPTX [4–6], one trial included only patients suffering from traumatic PNX [3]. The proportion of male and female patients was 53.1% and 46.9%, respectively, and 92.3% of patients sustained blunt trauma. Only one RCT (Kulvatunyou et al. 2021) explicitly reported the number of surgical reinterventions. The remaining studies defined failure as the need for any additional intervention without distinguishing between procedural types.

All studies reported data regarding the failure rate. No differences were detected between the SCT and LCT groups (RR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.66–1.35; p = 0.76). Secondary endpoints

Only the study by Kulvatunyou et al. (2021) reported information on the ICU length of stay and initial drainage output, and only the studies by Yi et al. and Kulvatunyou et al. (2021) reported data on total drainage output. No meta-analysis was carried out for these outcomes.

All studies reported data regarding insertion-related complications. No differences were detected between the SCT and LCT groups (RR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.49–1.36; p = 0.44). No heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 0%; τ2 = 0; PI: 0.45–1.48; Q-test p = 0.84). No outliers or influential cases were detected.

Three studies [3, 4, 6] reported data on hospital length of stay. No differences were detected between the two groups (MD: 1.01; 95% CI: − 0.3–2.33; p = 0.13). Low heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 9.4%; τ2 = 0.168; PI: − 9.35–11.43; Q-test p = 0.33). No outliers or influential cases were detected.

All studies reported data regarding tube days. Patients treated with SCT had a significantly shorter duration of tube placement compared to those treated with LCT (MD: − 0.49; 95% CI: − 0.91 to − 0.08; p = 0.02). No heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 0%; τ2 = 0; PI: − 0.96 to − 0.25; Q-test p = 0.85). No outliers or influential cases were detected.

TSA

The cumulative meta-analysis showed a pooled relative risk (RR) of 0.95 with a 95% TSA-adjusted CI of 0.41 to 2.20 and a naïve p-value of 0.76. The heterogeneity was negligible (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0.00, D2 = 0.0%), with a non-significant heterogeneity p-value of 0.61, indicating consistency across included trials.

Despite the sequential inclusion of data, the cumulative Z-curve remained close to the null line and did not cross either the alpha-spending boundary or the beta-spending boundary. This suggests that the current body of evidence is insufficient to confirm a statistically significant benefit or harm of the intervention. Importantly, the cumulative Z-curve also did not cross the futility boundary, indicating that the absence of effect cannot be conclusively ruled out either.

The required information size (RIS) was 3110 participants, while only 22% of the information size (676 events) has been accrued so far.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of the evidence across all evaluated outcomes (failure rate, insertion-related complications, hospital length of stay, and tube duration) was rated as moderate. The main factor contributing to the downgrading of evidence certainty is imprecision, as reflected in wide confidence intervals that include both potential benefit and harm. Additionally, the duration of drainage outcome was influenced by a study with a higher risk of bias, further affecting the reliability of the pooled estimate. The GRADE summary of findings is reported in the supplementary materials.

Discussion

Recent advances in the management of traumatic HTX have challenged the traditional preference for large-bore chest tubes. Although historically favored due to concerns over tube obstruction and higher evacuation capacity, growing evidence supports using small-caliber, image-guided chest drainages as a non-inferior alternative. Preclinical studies, including swine models [19], and multiple clinical investigations have demonstrated that small-bore chest tubes (typically 8–16 Ch) can achieve comparable drainage efficacy to large-bore counterparts (28–40 Ch), while offering advantages such as reduced procedural pain, lower complication rates, and improved patient comfort.

Our findings are consistent with these pieces of evidence in terms of both drainage efficacy and complication rates in patients with thoracic trauma. Specifically, there were no significant differences in failure rates, insertion-related complications, or hospital length of stay between the two groups. Notably, the only outcome that favored the use of SCTs was a significantly shorter duration of tube placement, which may reflect earlier mobilization, less discomfort, a lower severity of injury, or reduced drainage-related complications.

Nevertheless, these results must be interpreted with caution due to several limitations of the primary studies included in the analysis. Across the four RCTs evaluated, important methodological weaknesses emerged that impact the certainty and generalizability of the findings. Most studies were single-center trials with relatively small sample sizes, thereby limiting statistical power and increasing the likelihood of both type I and type II errors [20–22]. In the trial performed by Yi and colleagues, despite the large volume of patients enrolled, power analysis and sample size calculation were not performed. In two RCTs [4, 6] the sample size estimated from power analysis was not reached, with the trial's recruitment interrupted prematurely after interim analysis proved that the non-inferiority assumption was met.

The inclusion criteria were often narrow, focusing on patients with simple, uncomplicated thoracic injuries. Patients presenting with hemodynamic instability, massive HTX, or bilateral chest trauma were frequently excluded, which restricts the applicability of these findings to the broader trauma population. This limits the ability to determine whether SCTs are effective in draining larger collections adequately or whether further interventions such as video-assisted evacuation of retained hemothoraces might be necessary.

In addition, heterogeneity in device selection and insertion protocols may have introduced performance bias. The trials variously employed 5 Ch central venous catheters, 14 Ch pigtail catheters, 14 Ch straight Thal tubes, and 28–60 Ch standard chest tubes, with placement via either Seldinger or open cut-down techniques. Such differences complicate cross-study comparisons, especially on outcomes such as pain, drainage efficiency, and complication rates. Several studies relied on subjective measures such as pain scales or “insertion perception” scores, which are inherently prone to bias [6, 21].

The TSA further highlights the inconclusiveness of the current evidence base. Despite consistent point estimates suggesting equivalence between SCTs and LCTs, the accrued sample size reached only 22% of the RIS, estimated at 3110 patients. The cumulative Z-curve did not cross any of the pre-specified boundaries for benefit, harm, or futility, indicating that the observed effects could be due to random error. This finding underscores the risk of overinterpreting results derived from underpowered analyses and emphasizes the urgent need for additional high-quality, adequately powered RCTs [23].

In interpreting the results, it is also important to recognize that the clinical impact of drainage modality may vary based on injury type and severity. For example, while small-bore drains appear effective in managing uncomplicated pneumothorax or low-volume HTX, their role in high-volume, actively bleeding injuries remains unproven. Likewise, the presence of clotted HTX may necessitate larger drainage calibers or surgical intervention, rendering SCTs suboptimal in such scenarios.

The methodological and statistical rigor of the present meta-analysis represents one of its main strengths. Previous meta-analyses have been limited by the inclusion of heterogeneous study designs, poor differentiation between traumatic and iatrogenic etiologies, or pooled analyses that combine randomized trials with retrospective or observational studies, reducing the reliability of effect estimates [24–26]. In contrast, the current study exclusively includes randomized controlled trials, adheres strictly to PRISMA and AMSTAR II guidelines, and applies advanced meta-analytic techniques to enhance the robustness of findings.

In addition to the above-mentioned concerns, the present meta-analysis has several limitations. First, the total number of eligible RCTs was limited to four, encompassing 676 patients. While this represents a meaningful dataset, it remains statistically underpowered to draw firm conclusions. Additionally, clinical and procedural heterogeneity was present across studies, with differences in the type of small-bore devices used, insertion techniques, and institutional protocols for tube management. Moreover, one included RCT focused on isolated traumatic pneumothorax. While the endpoint was conceptually aligned, this introduces further clinical heterogeneity. For this reason, we preemptively performed subgroup analysis separating HTX/HPTX from PTX. As can be seen from the forest plot reported in Fig. 1, the pooled results were not affected by the exclusion of the study by Kulvatunyou et al. (2014).

The risk of bias assessment revealed methodological concerns in several domains, most notably in the randomization process and outcome measurement. Moreover, outcome reporting across studies was inconsistent. Several endpoints, such as initial drainage output, total volume of HTX evacuated, and ICU length of stay, were not uniformly reported and thus could not be pooled. Moreover, given the 18-year time span across which the included studies were conducted (2003–2021), it is important to acknowledge that paradigms in chest trauma management have likely evolved during this period. Advances in imaging, interventional techniques, pain management strategies, and institutional protocols may have influenced outcomes, particularly regarding indications for tube thoracostomy and definitions of treatment success. This temporal heterogeneity may limit the comparability of trials and should be considered when interpreting our results. Finally, an important clinical consideration that could not be adequately addressed in our analysis is whether SCTs are equally effective in managing large-volume hemothoraces (i.e. those exceeding 500 cc of initial output). Unfortunately, only one included study reported this outcome, and data were insufficient for subgroup analysis. This represents a significant limitation in assessing the appropriateness of SCTs in high-volume bleeding scenarios.

Taken together, these factors underscore the need for high-quality, adequately powered multicenter RCTs to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of small versus large-bore chest tubes in broader trauma populations. While it is generally accepted that SCTs are effective in the management of uncomplicated, low-volume hemothoraces and pneumothoraces, it is unclear whether these tubes are effective in more complex, severely injured patients. In this context, in designing future RCTs, addressing all the methodological gaps identified in our study, objective inclusion and exclusion criteria should be considered, aiming for the external validity and reproducibility of the results. For instance, the volume of HTX/PTX should be systematically assessed and measured with CT scan, the chest tube placement, timing, and its clinical management should follow institutional protocols. Volume of evacuated HTX and any needed additional procedures should be included in the dataset. The definitions of failure rate, insertion-related complications, along with pain assessment and control, need to be standardized.

The current evidence does not yet justify a widespread shift in clinical practice towards the use of SCTs, which can be suggested in selected trauma patients, particularly those who are hemodynamically stable and present with uncomplicated thoracic injuries. Further research is warranted to determine patient subgroups who may benefit most from smaller caliber drainage systems, to standardize insertion protocols, and to evaluate long-term outcomes such as chronic pain, tube-site infections, and quality of life.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that small-bore chest drains are not inferior to large-bore chest tubes in terms of safety and efficacy for the management of traumatic hemothorax, hemopneumothorax, and pneumothorax in selected, hemodynamically stable patients. While the use of smaller drains may offer practical advantages, such as reduced insertion-related discomfort and shorter drainage duration, the current body of evidence is limited by moderate certainty due to imprecision, small sample sizes, and methodological concerns. Importantly, trial sequential analysis underscores that the accrued data remain insufficient to draw definitive conclusions. Future large-scale, multicenter randomized trials are needed to strengthen the evidence base, identify optimal patient subgroups, and inform clinical guidelines regarding chest drainage strategies in trauma care.
