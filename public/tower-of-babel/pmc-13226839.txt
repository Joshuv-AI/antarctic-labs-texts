Bone tunnel repair of traumatic lumbar hernias without mesh: A multidisciplinary case series and literature review
Authors: Sharmila Raju, Kendall Vignaroli, Aldin Malkoc, Elizabeth Tadevosyan, Anthony Tran, Mehnaaz Mohammed, Bradley Carlson, Lance Lamore, Joseph Elsissy, Brandon Woodward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lumbar hernias are rare, comprising less than 1.5% of all hernias, with traumatic lumbar hernias (TLH) representing an even rarer subset. Due to their rarity, there is limited consensus on optimal surgical repair techniques. Traditional TLH repairs typically involve primary closure or mesh reinforcement using open, laparoscopic, or hybrid intra-abdominal approaches. We present three cases of TLH managed with a novel bone tunnel technique, approximating the external oblique musculature to the iliac crest under intra-peritoneal visualization, without the use of mesh. To our knowledge, these are the first reported cases of mesh-free iliac crest fixation in TLH repair.

Introduction

Lumbar hernias are exceptionally rare, comprising less than 1.5% of all hernias, and result from protrusion of intra-abdominal or extraperitoneal contents through defects in the posterolateral abdominal wall [1]. This region is bounded by the 12th rib superiorly, iliac crest inferiorly, erector spinae posteriorly, and the posterior border of the external oblique anteriorly. Herniation occurs at areas of weakness, specifically the superior lumbar triangle (Grynfeltt-Lesshaft) or the inferior lumbar triangle (Petit's). While congenital and spontaneous lumbar hernias are documented, traumatic lumbar hernias (TLH) are exceedingly rare and usually follow high-energy blunt or penetrating trauma. Common mechanisms include motor vehicle collisions and direct impact injuries, often accompanied by musculoskeletal disruption or visceral injury. The rarity of TLH and lack of standardized repair guidelines underscore the need for further case characterization to inform optimal surgical management [1].

Diagnosis can be challenging due to nonspecific symptoms such as localized swelling, pain, or a palpable bulge-often masked by more severe trauma. However, computed tomography (CT), now routine in trauma evaluation, enables precise identification of muscular disruption and defect extent. A recent systematic review highlights the frequent association of TLH with intra-abdominal injuries, emphasizing the need for a multidisciplinary approach [2]. Timing of repair – immediate versus delayed – depends on patient stability and presence of bowel injury [1].

Surgical repair has evolved from traditional open techniques using local flaps to minimally invasive approaches with synthetic or biosynthetic mesh. Laparoscopic and robotic-assisted repairs offer reduced postoperative pain and shorter recovery, while hybrid techniques combining laparoscopic visualization with open tension-free mesh placement have shown lower recurrence rates [2]. Still, contaminated fields or extensive trauma pose limitations to these methods. Durable repair hinges on tension-free technique and appropriate material choice [1]. Open repair remains preferred in emergent cases, with primary suture or mesh use guided by tissue integrity and defect size.

This case series presents three TLH cases managed at our institution, highlighting diagnostic and operative challenges- particularly in the presence of skeletal injuries requiring orthopedic collaboration. Through case analysis and literature review, we aim to enhance understanding and inform multidisciplinary strategies for managing this rare condition.

Case presentation

Case one

A 34-year-old female (BMI 33.38) with a history of amphetamine use, appendectomy, and cesarean section was airlifted to a level-one trauma center following a head-on motor vehicle collision at 55 mph. She was a restrained passenger and experienced loss of consciousness. On arrival, she had left femoral head and wrist deformities. Computed Tomography (CT) scan revealed a sternal fracture, a large right lumbar hernia, retroperitoneal hematoma along the right gonadal vessels, and hemoperitoneum (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen demonstrating a large right-sided traumatic lumbar hernia with protrusion of intra-abdominal contents through the posterolateral abdominal wall (Patient 1).Fig. 1

Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen demonstrating a large right-sided traumatic lumbar hernia with protrusion of intra-abdominal contents through the posterolateral abdominal wall (Patient 1).

She underwent emergent exploratory laparotomy, which revealed a large right lumbar hernia lateral to the iliac vessels with complete avulsion of the external oblique muscle and devascularized bowel and mesentery herniating through the defect. A 22 cm segment of jejunum was resected, followed by primary anastomosis. Mesh repair was deferred due to contamination risk. Orthopedic surgery was consulted intraoperatively for repair of the avulsed external oblique. A lateral incision exposed the defect, and the muscle was re-approximated to the iliac crest using Tycron sutures passed through bone tunnels. Intra-peritoneal access confirmed successful hernia repair and bowel protection. An intra-abdominal drain was placed, and fascial closure was completed.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful. She was discharged on postoperative day 7. At outpatient follow-up on day 29, the flank incision was well-healed. The drain had minimal purulent output, and she was treated with a week of oral antibiotics.

Case two

Case three

A 58-year-old male (BMI 46.96) with a history of right total knee arthroplasty presented to a level-one trauma center after a 50 mph head-on motor vehicle collision with loss of consciousness. Imaging revealed a 2 mm left parasagittal acute subdural hematoma, multiple rib fractures, right periprosthetic femur and calcaneus fractures, left open tibial plateau and fibular fractures, and a T7–T8 distraction injury. CT also showed bilateral lumbar hernias, with the left containing descending colon (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen demonstrating a large left-sided traumatic lumbar hernia containing descending colon (Patient 3).Fig. 4

On hospital day 11, the patient underwent exploratory laparotomy with trauma and orthopedic surgery. A left lateral incision exposed avulsed musculature. With trauma surgery protecting the bowel, the peritoneum and abdominal wall were mobilized and reattached to the iliac crest using Tycron sutures through bone tunnels. Abdominal wall closure was confirmed by palpation. A Hemovac drain was placed, fascia closed with Vicryl, skin with staples, and a Prevena wound vac applied.

His hospital course included multiple orthopedic procedures and was complicated by acute blood loss, electrolyte imbalances, and a suspected UTI, all of which resolved. He was discharged to inpatient rehab on day 25.

Discussion

Traumatic lumbar hernias (TLHs) are exceptionally rare, and as such, there is limited literature regarding their recommended surgical approach. This case series presents three cases of TLH, all of which were repaired through re-approximation of the external oblique musculature to the iliac crest, utilizing concomitant intra-peritoneal visualization, and without the use of mesh.

The majority of TLH repairs in the literature are performed via an intra-peritoneal approach. Laparotomy is typically favored for emergent repairs, while laparoscopy is often employed for delayed repairs in more stable patients [2]. However, some reports describe the use of bone fixation repairs with mesh, either through an open approach or laparoscopically [3], [4], [5]. In a multi-center U.S. study conducted from 2012 to 2018, 10.8% of all traumatic abdominal wall hernias were classified as lumbar hernias. Of the 19 lumbar hernias included, only 6 (31.6%) were repaired using bone tunnel fixation [6]. It is unclear whether mesh reinforcement was used in any of these cases.

Another recent multi-center study found that 15.8% of lumbar hernias were associated with concomitant bowel injury [7]. Many surgeons believe it is unsafe to place mesh in the presence of acute bowel injury. Therefore, our technique of bony tunnel repair without mesh may be particularly beneficial in cases of lumbar hernias with concomitant bowel injury, as demonstrated in one of our three cases. To our knowledge, these are the first documented instances of TLH repair using bone tunnel fixation without mesh. Furthermore, these cases are the first documented examples of TLH repair with bone tunnel fixation assisted by intra-peritoneal visualization.

While this technique is particularly useful in the setting of bowel injury, it would be valuable to document additional cases with longer follow-up periods to assess the rate of recurrence without mesh. Although the literature suggests similar rates of hernia recurrence with and without bone tunnel fixation, there are no studies specifically comparing hernia recurrence rates in bone tunnel fixations with and without mesh [6].

A limitation of this study is the lack of consistent, standardized outpatient follow-up across all patients, as not all patients returned for post-discharge evaluation. Consequently, long-term durability and recurrence rates cannot be definitively assessed. Notably, no patients re-presented to our institution with clinically apparent lumbar hernia recurrence over a two-year follow-up period.

Conclusion

Traumatic lumbar hernias (TLHs) are rare and present unique challenges in both diagnosis and management. This case series demonstrates a novel approach to TLH repair using bone tunnel fixation without mesh, particularly in the presence of concomitant bowel injury. The technique, assisted by intra-peritoneal visualization, has proven successful in our cases and offers a promising alternative to traditional mesh repair. Further long-term follow-up studies are needed to evaluate the recurrence rates and outcomes of this approach, but it presents a viable option for TLH repair, especially in complex trauma cases.
