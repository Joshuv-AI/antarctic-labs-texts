Default mode network resting-state functional connectivity and event centrality in adult acute trauma survivors
Authors: Amy Z. Wang, Carissa W. Tomas, Sarah Stevens, Sadie E. Larsen, Terri A. deRoon-Cassini, Christine L. Larson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research has identified functional connectivity abnormalities in the default mode network (DMN), a functional brain network theorized to be involved in self-referential processes, among trauma survivors. Event centrality (EC) is an important cognitive component of posttrauma stress disorder (PTSD) development that heavily involves self-referential processing and autobiographical memory. However, the impact of EC in a neuroimaging context remains underexplored. This study investigated the relationships between DMN resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) and event centrality and the mediating effects of adverse psychological outcomes on DMN rsFC and event centrality among acute traumatic injury survivors. Two hundred and fifteen adult participants were recruited from a Level I trauma center after sustaining a traumatic injury, followed by resting-state fMRI scanned at 2–4 weeks post-injury and self-report questionnaires collected at 2–4 weeks and 3-, 6-, and 12 months post-injury. Event centrality, at 3- and 12-month, was significantly associated with measures of adverse psychological outcomes, and EC at 12-month was significantly associated with the rsFC of two nodes of the DMN: the lateral parietal cortex and hippocampus. In addition, specific adverse psychological outcomes significantly mediated this relationship between rsFC and event centrality. This novel neuroimaging finding suggests potential mechanisms that contribute to increased psychological distress and greater event centrality in traumatic injury recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51392-3.

Introduction

Trauma is among the leading causes of death and disability in the United States, and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is one of the most prevalent long-term psychological outcomes of traumatic injuries (e.g., car accidents, assault/altercations, gunshot wound)1,2. In addition, many traumatic injury survivors may not meet clinical criteria for PTSD but experience posttraumatic stress symptoms3–5. Studies estimate 10–21% of single incident traumatic injury survivors will have chronic PTSD posttrauma6,7, and once chronic, PTSD is more difficult to ameliorate with treatment. Moreover, the single strongest predictor of poor quality of life among traumatic injury survivors is not the severity of the injury but the presence of PTSD8.

Event centrality and trauma-related outcomes

Event centrality has been highlighted as an important cognitive attribution of a traumatic experience. This manifests as the extent to which a traumatic memory forms a central component of personal identity and whether that memory becomes a turning point and/or reference point in identity development9. Research has suggested that as a traumatic injury becomes more central to one’s identity, individuals are more likely to experience posttraumatic stress symptoms10–12. However, very few works have explored event centrality longitudinally following trauma exposure. One study reported that, in contrast to most studies proposing event centrality as a predictor of PTSD severity, PTSD symptom severity at earlier timepoints predicted event centrality over time13. Their findings suggested that development of PTSD symptoms following a traumatic event might lead to perceiving the traumatic experience as more central to one’s identity in the long term. More investigations are needed to understand the longitudinal relationship between centrality and PTSD symptoms.

In addition to PTSD symptoms, studies have identified other psychological outcomes associated with event centrality in response to trauma exposure, particularly symptoms of distress-related disorders such as depression and anxiety10,11,14,15. Depression and anxiety symptoms are both highly comorbid with PTSD development and are significantly associated with event centrality11. Nonetheless, few studies have examined the longitudinal relationship between event centrality and trauma-related psychological outcomes among traumatic injury survivors13, and no study to date has investigated the role of neural mechanisms in this key cognitive attribution of a traumatic event over time.

Empirical findings on resting-state functional connectivity posttrauma

Neuroimaging studies have demonstrated changes in neural circuits associated with PTSD development16–19. Growing attention has shifted toward resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC; i.e., how brain regions interact with each other at rest) of PTSD-related brain structures with each other and the rest of the brain. One functional network active while the brain is “at-rest,” not engaging in external stimuli, is the default mode network (DMN;20). One way in which the DMN is theorized to be involved is internally focused processes, such as self-referential processing, autobiographical memory retrieval, and future envisioning21. Trauma-related neuroimaging research has shown that DMN functional connectivity is associated with posttrauma psychopathology; however, findings are mixed among different populations, symptomatology, timing of studies, and other factors22–29. Studies have revealed decreased DMN connectivity in veterans who experienced combat trauma and civilians with PTSD22,23 and positive associations between DMN connectivity and PTSD symptoms24,25. In addition, individuals with greater posttrauma anhedonia presented lower DMN rsFC26, suggesting a relationship between the DMN and other comorbid adverse posttrauma psychological outcomes. Given the complex evidence on the role of the DMN in trauma recovery, it may be worth considering whether the DMN is also involved in event centrality formation.

Multiple cognitive dimensions of autobiographical memory, including vividness of memory, emotional intensity of memory, and physical reaction to memory11, have been associated with event centrality, indicating the potential role of autobiographical memory processing in event centrality formation. Although subnetworks and regions of the DMN may support many other processes, given its role in self-reference, autobiographical memory, and future envisioning, which are closely aligned with dimensions of event centrality, this network could serve as a critical neurobiological mechanism in the formation of event centrality and its associated psychological distress, including posttraumatic stress, depression, and anxiety symptoms. In addition, studies have reported that trauma-exposed individuals with a PTSD diagnosis or more severe symptoms reported more vivid recollection of events, drawing more connections between trauma memory and current events, greater emotional intensity, and more negative trauma memory valence30–32. This finding is consistent with Stevens et al.’s13 finding that more severe PTSD symptoms prospectively predicted event centrality. Another study discovered that depression symptoms were related to less salient and coherent trauma memories30. These findings suggest that the severity of PTSD symptoms and other adverse psychological outcomes following trauma interferes with autobiographical memory, trauma memory processing, and personal identities related to the trauma. Thus, existing literature establishes a relationship between abnormal DMN rsFC and adverse posttrauma psychological outcomes, suggesting that memory disturbances following more severe posttrauma distress could lead to greater event centrality. Taken together, these findings suggest that increased psychological distress would mediate the relationship between abnormal DMN rsFC and event centrality (due to its relationship with trauma memory processing, not measured here).

Current study

The current study aimed to examine the association between DMN resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) and event centrality among acute traumatic injury survivors, with a focus on post-injury adverse psychological outcomes (PTSD symptoms, depression, anxiety, and stress) as potential mechanisms. We examined rsFC both within the DMN and between central nodes of the DMN and the rest of the brain. We hypothesized that greater event centrality, at 3- and 12-month post-injury, would be associated with greater posttrauma stress symptoms, depression, anxiety, and stress, at 6-month post-injury. With respect to rsFC, we predicted that individuals with greater within-network and whole-brain rsFC of the DMN at 2–4 weeks post-injury would show greater event centrality at 3- and 12-month post-injury. Moreover, we anticipated that individuals’ post-injury adverse psychological outcomes would mediate the relationship between DMN rsFC and event centrality.

Methods and materials

Participants

Two hundred and fifteen adult participants were recruited from the Emergency Department of a Level I Trauma Center in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, for a larger study, the Imaging Study on Trauma and Resilience [iSTAR, R01MH106574;7,13,33–35], which investigated the risk and resilience factors for post-injury outcomes. Participants were included in the study if they were treated in the emergency department following a traumatic injury, 18–60 years old, fluent in English, met MRI safety requirements, had a Glasgow Coma Scale score greater than 13, and could schedule a research visit within 30 days of injury. Participants were excluded from the study if they had a spinal cord injury with neurological deficits, a moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment or severe traumatic brain injury, history of manic or psychotic symptoms, severe vision or hearing problems, or were admitted due to self-inflicted injuries, pregnant or planning to become pregnant, currently taking antipsychotic medication.

Procedure

Potential participants were screened for eligibility through the emergency department daily intake. Eligible participants were approached by study staff with research information and enrolled if interested. Baseline visits were completed two-to-four weeks post-injury, including resting-state functional MRI scans and a battery of self-report measures and biospecimens. Follow-up visits occurred at 3-, 6-, and 12-month post-injury with self-report measures (Fig. 1). Participants provided written informed consent before baseline assessment and were compensated for their time at each study visit. The larger study’s procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Medical College of Wisconsin (PRO#24344). Measures

Centrality of Event Scale-7

Event centrality was assessed with the 7-item Centrality of Event Scale (CES;36) at 3- and 12-month post-injury. This scale measures whether an individual considers the traumatic event to be an anchor point for their identity and meaning and a turning point in their life. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 “totally disagree” to 5 “totally agree”. Score ranges from 7 to 35, with a higher score indicating greater event centrality. The CES demonstrates high reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from .93 to .95 across time points in the parent study.

PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5)

PTSD symptoms were evaluated with the 20-item PCL-537 at 6-month post-injury. The PCL-5 corresponds to the DSM-5 symptom criteria for PTSD, assessing how much an individual is bothered by each symptom. Items were rated on a scale from 0 “Not at all” to 4 “Extremely.” PTSD symptom severity was evaluated with the total symptom severity score, with a higher score indicating greater PTSD symptoms. A provisional diagnosis of PTSD is determined by counting the “moderately” or higher rated symptoms according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th ed; DSM-5;38) PTSD diagnostic criteria. The PCL-5 demonstrated strong reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .94 (53).

Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21)

In addition to PTSD symptoms, depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms were assessed with the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21;39) at 6-month post-injury, as measures of adverse psychological outcomes. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 “did not apply to me at all” to 4 “applied to me very much, or most of the time.” The DASS-21 consists of three individual subscales of depression, anxiety, and stress, with reliability values of .91, .81, and .89, respectively39. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity for each subscale. A score of 14 and higher is considered moderate for depression, 10 and higher is considered moderate for anxiety, and 19 and higher is considered moderate for stress.

Resting-state fMRI imaging acquisition and preprocessing

Participants underwent an 8-min resting-state scan within one month of their injury, with their eyes open and fixed on a white crosshair on the screen. Neuroimaging scans were conducted via a 3.0 Tesla short-bore General Electric Signa Excite system with a 32-channel head coil. High resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired in a sagittal orientation (repetition time = 8.2 ms; echo time = 3.2 ms; field of view = 24 cm; flip angle = 12; voxel size = 0.9375 × 1.071 × 1). An 8-min resting-state scan was completed (240 volumes; field of view = 22.4 mm; matrix = 64 × 64; slice thickness = 3.5 mm; 41 sagittal slices; repetition time = 2000 ms; echo time = 25 ms; flip angle = 77). Images were preprocessed in the CONN toolbox (v. 20; RRID: SCR_009550) with SPM (v. 12) and MATLAB (v. 2019b, Mathworks) using the default pipeline, including magnetic field stabilization (first three volumes discarded), motion correction with a 6-parameter linear transformation, slice-time correction, spatial realignment to the first image, normalization to Montral Neurological Institute (MNI) space, resampling to 2 × 2 × 2 mm voxels, and smoothing with a 4-mm kernel. Volumes with more than 0.3 mm of framewise displacement were excluded from analysis. During first-level denoising, nuisance covariates, including head motion parameters, white matter signals, and cerebrospinal fluid signals, were accounted for with the anatomical component-based noise correction procedure (aCompCor) and scrubbing (ART-based). The threshold for excluding scans from analyses was 20% or more of the volume scrubbed.

Data analysis

Bivariate correlations

Questionnaires with up to 20% missing items were mean imputed for those with 7 or more items. Bonferroni-corrected correlation analyses were conducted to investigate the relationships between 3- and 12-month event centrality and each indicator of the 6-month adverse psychological outcome, including PTSD symptoms, anxiety, depression, and stress.

Resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) analyses

First, average DMN within-network rsFC was obtained with ROI-to-ROI analysis across 149 ROIs identified as the DMN according to Schaefer parcellation (700 areas, 7 networks, ROI-to-ROI matrix size: 149 × 149)40. Separate linear regressions were conducted with acquired within-network DMN average rsFC values predicting 3- and 12-month event centrality, accounting for age, sex, and income. Next, whole-brain seed‒to-voxel analyses were conducted with preestablished central nodes of the default mode network in the CONN toolbox as seed regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex, lateral parietal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus, and parahippocampal gyrus. Separate group-level general linear models were conducted to investigate the relationships between event centrality, at 3- and 12-month post-injury, and whole-brain seed-based connectivity, with age, sex, and income as covariates. A cluster-size family-wise error (FWE) corrected p-value of .05 indicated statistical significance, with a voxel threshold of p < .001 (uncorrected), two-sided.

Mediation analyses

Statistically significant whole-brain seed-based rsFC clusters’ connectivity values were extracted as predictors of the mediation models, predicting 12-month event centrality, with each 6-month psychological outcome as a separate mediator (PTSD symptoms, anxiety, depression, and stress), accounting for age, sex, and income. In order to examine whether psychological outcomes mediated the relationship between early post-trauma rsFC and event centrality, only symptoms collected at 6-months post-injury were included in the study to follow temporal sequence. Bivariate correlations

Greater event centrality at 3-month post-injury was significantly correlated with worse outcomes at 6-month post-injury, including greater PTSD symptoms (r = .49, p < .001), depression symptoms (r = .35, p < .001), anxiety symptoms (r = .46, p < .001), and stress (r = .36, p < .001). Similarly, greater event centrality at 12-month post-injury was significantly correlated with worse outcomes at 6-month post-injury, including greater PTSD symptoms (r = .45, p < .001), depression symptoms (r = .40, p < .001), anxiety symptoms (r = .44, p < .001), and stress (r = .38, p < .001). Functional connectivity

DMN within network rsFC

Contrary to our hypothesis, accounting for age, sex, and income, average DMN within-network rsFC at baseline was not significantly associated with event centrality at 3- or 12-month post-injury.

DMN seed-to-voxel whole-brain rsFC

Among the identified DMN seed regions, 3-month event centrality significantly related to baseline connectivity with two seeds, the lateral parietal cortex and right parahippocampal gyrus, after adjustment for age, sex, and income. Greater event centrality was associated with less rsFC of the lateral parietal cortex with a significant cluster in the lingual gyrus [n = 147; peak cluster coordinates (x, y, z): + 10, − 60, − 06; size = 90, p-FWE = .03]. In addition, greater event centrality was associated with less rsFC of the right parahippocampal gyrus with a cluster of the cuneal cortex, lateral occipital cortex, and intracalcarine cortex [n = 147; peak cluster coordinates (x, y, z): − 06, − 78, + 24; size = 346, p-FWE < .001]. There was no significant main effect of 3-month event centrality on connectivity with any other DMN seed regions.

Among the identified DMN seed regions, 12-month event centrality significantly related to baseline connectivity with two seeds, the lateral parietal cortex and hippocampus, after adjustment for age, sex, and income. Greater event centrality was associated with greater rsFC of the hippocampus with two significant clusters, including portions of the putamen and pallidum [n = 109; first peak cluster coordinates (x, y, z): − 20, + 00, + 02; size = 107, p-FWE = .01; second peak cluster coordinates (x, y, z): + 24, − 02, − 02; size = 98, p-FWE = .02; Fig. 4]. In addition, greater event centrality was associated with less rsFC of the lateral parietal cortex (LP) with clusters of the lateral occipital cortex (LOC), intracalcarine cortex (ICC), lingual gyrus (LG) and occipital pole [OP; n = 109; first peak cluster coordinates (x, y, z): − 22, − 78, + 34; size = 142, p-FWE = .002; second peak cluster coordinates (x, y, z): + 10, − 86, + 04; size = 92, p-FWE = .02; Fig. 5]. Our primary aim is to understand the relationship between the DMN and event centrality. Given the established relationship between event centrality and PTSD symptoms, we explored what relationship remained after accounting for PTSD symptoms reported 2–4 weeks post-injury. With the hippocampus as a seed, one significant cluster remained, including portions of the putamen and pallidum [peak cluster coordinates (x, y, z): + 24, − 02, 02; size = 87, p-FWE = .03]. The lateral parietal cortex associated clusters were no longer significant after accounting for baseline PTSD symptoms.

Mediating effects of adverse psychological outcomes

DMN within network rsFC

Taking psychological outcomes at 6-month post-injury as mediators, anxiety significantly mediated the relationship between DMN within-network rsFC at 2–4 weeks and event centrality at 12-month post-injury [accounting for age, sex, and income, effect = 14.69, 95% CI (2.13, 28.81)]. Depression, stress, or PTSD symptoms were not significant mediators.

DMN seed-to-voxel whole-brain rsFC

We evaluated psychological outcomes at 6-month post-injury (PTSD, depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms) as mediators of the relationships between baseline rsFC of each of the significant clusters mentioned above and event centrality at 12-month, accounting for age, sex, and income.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the associations between DMN resting-state functional connectivity and event centrality and post-injury psychological outcomes among acute traumatic injury survivors. In support of our hypothesis, greater event centrality at 3- and 12-month post-injury was correlated with greater PTSD, depression, anxiety symptoms, and stress at 6-month post-injury. Contrary to our hypotheses, DMN within-network rsFC was not associated with event centrality. However, anxiety symptoms mediated the relationship between DMN within-network rsFC and later event centrality. With whole-brain rsFC, the hippocampus showed greater rsFC with the putamen and pallidum in individuals with greater 12-month event centrality. This main effect was mediated by 6-month PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms. In addition, the lateral parietal cortex showed lower rsFC with the lateral occipital cortex, intracalcarine cortex, lingual gyrus and occipital pole in individuals with greater 12-month event centrality. PTSD symptoms at 6-month post-injury mediated the relationship between baseline rsFC and event centrality at 12-month post-injury.

Many studies have established the close association between event centrality and PTSD symptoms. When an individual uses the traumatic event as a reference for evaluating current and future experiences and sees the trauma as a turning point of their life, they are more likely to develop fearful and avoidant responses to that event, leading to the development of PTSD symptoms9. In addition to PTSD symptoms, event centrality is related to other psychological outcomes, such as depression and anxiety14,42. Our results are consistent in showing that individuals who considered the traumatic event more central to their identity had greater PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms and stress over multiple time points across the first year of recovery following traumatic injury.

In neuroimaging literature, the hippocampus is a critical region involved in memory disturbances in PTSD43,44. As a central node of the DMN, the hippocampus exhibits lower functional connectivity with other regions of the DMN and across the brain among individuals with PTSD45,46, which has been suggested to contribute to PTSD symptoms such as intrusive memories. In contrast, we found that higher hippocampal FC with the putamen and pallidum at rest was correlated with event centrality. In addition to the trauma literature, studies suggest that better episodic memory encoding and retrieval are associated with greater activity in the putamen and the hippocampus47. This cooperative relationship could explain the contrary result of greater connectivity between the hippocampus, the traumatic/autobiographical memory center, and regions involved in episodic memory processing—the putamen and pallidum—which suggests that how trauma memory is encoded, perceived, or retrieved soon following trauma might be a critical neutral pathway leading to greater event centrality during recovery. Higher hippocampal FC aligns with the conceptualization of event centrality as enhanced accessibility of traumatic memory, potentially due to stronger encoding.

In addition, we found that PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms at 6-month significantly mediated this main effect of increased connectivity between the hippocampus and pallidum/putamen and increased event centrality. In addition to episodic memory processing, the roles of these regions in the reward circuitry have been highlighted in trauma research. In individuals with PTSD, compared with those in controls, the putamen, along with other striatal reward processing network regions (e.g., the nucleus accumbens), has been shown to have lower activation in response to reward cues48,49. Our findings are consistent with some studies that suggest that disrupted functional connectivity between the DMN and reward regions plays a role in the development or maintenance of anhedonia symptoms posttrauma26. This significant mediation suggests another potential pathway in which individuals with disturbances in reward processing following a traumatic event may be more vulnerable to nonremitting PTSD symptoms, along with depression and anxiety symptoms, making them more likely to view the traumatic event as more central to their identities, leading to increased psychological distress over time. Along with the proposed episodic memory pathway, our findings invite future research to investigate the neural mechanisms that contribute to the development of nonremitting psychological distress and long-term cognitive attribution of traumatic injury in recovery.

In addition, results showed significant lower functional connectivity between the lateral parietal cortex of the DMN and the lateral occipital cortex, intracalcarine cortex, lingual gyrus and occipital pole in individuals with greater 12-month event centrality, indicating the role of visual processing regions in cognitive attribution of the traumatic injury. In clinical populations, the visual cortex has shown greater functional activation during self-referential processes among women with PTSD and decreased functional connectivity with greater childhood maltreatment and more severe depression50–52. In addition, visual cortical regions show decreased functional connectivity with the hippocampus during suppression of unwanted memory53. Overall, the literature strongly suggests that visual processing regions work closely with other brain circuits (e.g., threat circuits) in trauma recovery54. Our findings partially align with this evidence observing visual processing regions as critical brain networks implicated in trauma recovery and that even at rest, these brain regions are related to self-referential processes, potentially through mechanisms related to trauma memory processing. Although some of these works touched on self-referential processing, to our knowledge, no other research has investigated how these regions may be involved in event centrality.

In addition, we found that PTSD symptoms at 6-month post-injury significantly mediated the relationship between rsFC of the lateral parietal cortex and visual processing regions and event centrality at 12-month post-injury, which may shed some light on a potential mechanism of this main effect. Event centrality is the cognitive interpretation of a traumatic event and reflects how an individual appraises this event. In the cognitive reappraisal literature, much evidence has suggested that the occipital lobe plays a crucial role in reappraisal of negative (vs positive) stimuli55–57 and that arousal from stimuli impacts activation in visual processing brain regions and the amygdala58. More specifically, individuals with PTSD found negative stimuli to be more unpleasant and arousing and showed increased correlation with visual processing region activation59. These findings of reappraisal consistently highlight the relationships between visual processing regions and the negative cognitive attributions of presented stimuli. Therefore, it is not surprising to see our finding that individuals with atypical rsFC between a DMN region and visual processing regions were more at risk for development of nonremitting PTSD symptoms, reinforcing the negative cognitive attributions over months of recovery.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the neural mechanisms related to long-term event centrality following traumatic injury, especially at rest. Our imaging findings identified relationships between DMN regions and event centrality and suggested potential neural mechanisms leading to greater event centrality, such as episodic memory processing, cognitive reappraisal, and reward processing. Results on the mediating role of adverse psychological outcomes on these main effects are consistent with previous findings suggesting that the development of PTSD symptoms may lead individuals to interpret their traumatic events as more central to their identity13, and our imaging findings suggest potential neural risk factors for this connection. In addition, neuroimaging data was collected acutely (within a month) after trauma, which allowed for prospective correlation of later event centrality and captured this important window of opportunity for the prevention of PTSD development. Although this study is observational, the longitudinal design allows for reasonable speculation of potential causal relationships between neutral mechanisms, the development of adverse psychological outcomes, and the cognitive attribution of trauma.

This study has several limitations. First, our sample was relatively homogenous in terms of the types of injury experienced, with a majority of participants suffering injury from a motor vehicle accident. This poses a limitation to the generalizability of our findings, as research has shown differences in recovery among types of trauma60,61. Violin plots illustrating differences across different mechanisms of injury are included in the supplement. In addition, most participants did not meet the criteria for PTSD at the follow-up time points, which may also limit the generalizability of the findings to individuals with a PTSD diagnosis. Next, recent research has suggested that functional connectivity is more reliable as scan time increases62. The parent project of the current study began data collection in 2015 when 8-min resting-state scan was consistent with the standards at that time. Moreover, with a clinical population of acutely post-injury participants, the burden of long MRI scan sessions was taken into consideration of study designs. We acknowledge that a briefer scan time poses limitations to the reliability of functional connectivity. While resting-state functional scans are typically used in neuroimaging studies to capture activity in the DMN63,64, they may not necessarily always capture self-referential processes. Although participants were not engaging in any voluntary tasks during the scan, they may not have been engaging in self-generated thoughts related to themselves or the trauma injury. In addition, subnetworks and individual regions of the DMN may support other processes, such as processing external stimuli, integrating information, and task switching65. Our discussion of findings is limited to DMN’s function most relevant to event centrality. Finally, the study only collected our data of interest at certain timepoints, e.g., event centrality was only collected at 3- and 12-month following injury. The limited number of timepoints available restricted our ability to fully explore or replicate the longitudinal relationships between event centrality and PTSD symptoms suggested by previous literature.

This study highlights the importance of understanding the neural mechanisms related to event centrality following traumatic injury. A consistent relationship between event centrality and adverse psychological outcomes has been previously identified; however, biomarkers contributing to this connection have not been investigated. Our findings show that differences in whole-brain functional connectivity of certain DMN nodes are associated with differences in the cognitive attribution of how central a traumatic event is to one’s identity, potentially through the development of nonremitting adverse psychological outcomes. This points to a need to better understand the longitudinal relationships among self-referential processing, cognitive interpretations, and psychological outcomes during trauma recovery. Future research should explore other brain networks’ roles to more fully understand the neural mechanisms that interact with cognitive constructs in symptom development and recovery.
