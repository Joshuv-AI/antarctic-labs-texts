The airway mycobiome in chronic respiratory diseases: current advances and future frontiers
Authors: Wenliang Zhu, Feng Li, Dingnan Lin, Lixian Cai, Chunmei Dai, Haiyue Liu, Yihua Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe airway mycobiome is an important component of the respiratory microbiome and of clinical relevance, but has been largely underappreciated. Recent studies have characterized fungal community composition in the airways and elucidated its associations with the pathogenesis and progression of chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs), including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis (CF). A systematic understanding of the composition and dynamic changes of fungal microbiota in the airways, as well as their currently known interactions with the host in CRDs, may provide comprehensive insights into the pathological mechanisms of CRDs and improve diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. In this review, we synthesize current evidence on the role of the respiratory mycobiome in CRDs, focusing on mycobiome dysbiosis during disease pathogenesis, host-fungal interactions, and associated immune modulation. Additionally, we explore future research directions, key challenges, and potential therapeutic strategies targeting the fungal microbiota. This review redefines the contribution of fungi to the pathophysiology of CRDs based on current evidence and insights.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07441-y.Keywords: Microbiome, Chronic respiratory diseases, Mycobiome, Fungi

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) represent a heterogeneous spectrum of pathological conditions primarily defined by persistent impairment of respiratory function. It mainly includes Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, cystic fibrosis (CF) and bronchiectasis [1]. COPD, asthma, and bronchiectasis all present with chronic airway obstruction accompanied by cough and sputum production. Their etiological profiles, however, differ: asthma is often linked to a genetic predisposition to atopy, COPD is primarily associated with cigarette smoking, and bronchiectasis is frequently related to recurrent infections and underlying immunodeficiency. Asthma and COPD also diverge in their inflammatory patterns, immune mechanisms, and the reversibility of airflow limitation, although some patients exhibit overlapping clinical features of both disorders [2].

With the advancement of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, the airway mycobiome has gained increasing recognition. Currently, the primary sequencing approaches used to characterize the airway mycobiome include metagenomic sequencing and internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequencing, particularly targeting regions such as ITS1 and ITS2. Compared with healthy individuals, patients with CRDs harbour a markedly altered respiratory microbiota, which is closely associated with disease onset, progression, and exacerbation. It has been confirmed that there is a complex fungal ecosystem in the airway, besides, the bacteria-fungi interactions play an crucial but underappreciated role in the occurrence and development of diseases [3, 4]. Compared with earlier studies that focused solely on bacterial communities, integrated analyses of both bacterial and fungal components in the airways offer valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying disease onset and progression, and are essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. Alterations in the airway mycobiome have also been linked to clinical outcomes in CRDs. The host immune responses triggered by these fungi, along with their interactions with other microorganisms such as bacteria, may significantly influence lung function and disease progression [5].

In healthy individuals, the predominant phyla of the respiratory mycobiome include Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, with common genera such as Candida, Saccharomyces, Penicillium, Cladosporium, and Fusarium [5–8]. In contrast, the lung mycobiome of patients with respiratory diseases is primarily dominated by Candida albicans, Aspergillus, and Penicillium [9]. The composition of the respiratory mycobiome structure has been reported in different CRDs, but the pathological mechanism of the respiratory mycobiome on disease progression remains unclear [10]. This review focuses on recent advances in the airway mycobiome, the mechanisms by which the mycobiome interacts with host immunity, and the mycobiome’s impact on chronic respiratory disease progression.

Search strategy

This review followed the PRISMA guidelines. The primary search for article screening was conducted using PubMed (n = 243), Web of Science (n = 229) and Scopus (n = 308). The main medical subject headings and keywords included mycobiome, fungi, cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, COPD, and asthma. Using the PubMed database as an example, we present the detailed search strategy in Supplementary Table S1.

The literature screening was conducted independently by two researchers. After a rigorous selection process based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, studies published within the past 20 years were considered, and a total of 87 articles were included in this review. Composition of the airway mycobiome in chronic respiratory diseases

Recent studies have reported the airway fungal composition in CRDs. ITS sequencing showed that Candida, Pichia and Fusarium were common oral microbiota [11], and Fusarium, Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Penicillium brevicompactum have been identified as potential constituents of the human pulmonary mycobiome [12]. Although fungi are considerably less abundant than bacteria in the airways, healthy pulmonary mycobiota are typically dominated by Candida and other yeasts. Notably, fungal dysbiosis has been consistently associated with disease severity in chronic respiratory disease, including COPD, asthma, bronchiectasis, and CF [13]. Moreover, Fungal diversity is reduced in most CRDs and is associated with poor lung function [14].

The respiratory mycobiome in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease

The prevalence of COPD is increasing worldwide, and it will become the third leading cause of death by 2030 [15]. Studies have suggested that the microbial dysbiosis and microbiota-host immune response of airway microbiota in COPD patients may be involved in the occurrence and development of COPD [16]. Bacteria, viruses, fungi and other related respiratory infections are important causes of acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD) [17]. A multicenter study investigating the airway mycobiome in patients with COPD found that fungal community composition was similar across different Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) ABCD categories and lung function severity grades.

However, fungal diversity was higher in COPD patients compared to healthy individuals. In addition to an increased relative abundance of fungal genera commonly found in healthy airways, several unique genera were identified in COPD patients, including Alternaria, Aspergillus, Cladosporium, Cryptococcus, Mycosphaerella, Penicillium, Trametes, and Wickerhamomyces [18]. At the phylum level, the proportion of Basidiomycota in COPD patients may be higher than that in healthy controls, but Ascomycota is still the main phylum in general [19]. A study analyzing nasal lavage fluid from 20 patients with moderate-to-severe COPD revealed that the most abundant fungal taxa were Malassezia, Candida, Cladosporium, and Aspergillus [20]. The airways of COPD patients are in a state of microecological disorder, and patients with COPD are observed to have an obvious increase in the α-diversity of fungal communities [18]. The most clinically significant pathogenic fungi in the airways of COPD patients were Candida albicans and Aspergillus fumigatus [21, 22] (Fig. 2). High-throughput sequencing analysis revealed a severe disruption of the airway microbial community structure in patients with AECOPD. The relative abundance of pathogenic fungi, particularly Blastomyces, Candida, and Aspergillus was markedly increased. Most patients showed an overrepresentation of one or more pathogenic fungal species [23, 24].

Dominant fungal genera in the airways of patients with chronic respiratory diseases. The respiratory mycobiome in healthy individuals is predominantly composed of fungi from the genera Candida, Saccharomyces, Penicillium, Cladosporium, Fusarium, and other fungal taxa. In patients with COPD, the dominant genera include Candida and Aspergillus, along with other genera such as Alternaria, Cladosporium, Cryptococcus, Coccidioides, Penicillium, Phoma, Wickerhamomyces, Malassezia, and Blastomyces. In asthma, the airway mycobiome is primarily composed of Aspergillus, Candida, and Schizophyllum, as well as various other genera such as Mucor, Malassezia, Fusarium, Myceliophthora, Wallemia, and Alternaria. In bronchiectasis, the respiratory fungal community includes Candida and Aspergillus, along with other genera such as Penicillium, Cryptococcus, Mucor, Saccharomyces, Clavispora, and Curvularia. In patients with CF, the airway mycobiome is predominantly represented by Candida and Aspergillus, along with other fungi such as Apotrichum, Trichosporon, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Malassezia, Exophiala, and Scedosporium

The respiratory mycobiome in asthma

Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic respiratory disease characterized by allergic airway inflammation, remodeling, and airway hyperresponsiveness [25]. The mycobiome of asthma patients differs significantly from that of healthy individuals. Fungal colonization, particularly by filamentous fungi such as Aspergillus fumigatus, is more prevalent in the lower respiratory tract of asthma patients. This colonization may cause airway surface mycosis, thereby exacerbating asthma symptoms and triggering allergic reactions [26]. The fungal burden in healthy individuals remains low, with Malassezia predominating the mycobiota [27]. However, significant increases in the abundance of Ascomycota (particularly Aspergillus and Candida) and Basidiomycota (notably Schizophyllum) were observed in the airways of asthma patients [28] (Fig. 2). The airway mycobiome in steroid-free asthmatics is dominated by Mucor plumbeus and Malassezia restricta. In contrast, Eremothecium sinecaudum and Systemostrema alba were predominant in healthy controls [29]. Nevertheless, patients treated with inhaled glucocorticoids (ICS) exhibited a higher abundance of Fusarium and Mortierella, and a lower frequency of Wardomyces, Alternaria, and Aspergillus, in comparison with untreated asthma patients [5].

The respiratory mycobiome in bronchiectasis

Bronchiectasis is a chronic disease that is irreversible and progressive, and which is characterised by heterogeneity. A key component of this disease is microbial colonization and infection, and the change from health to disease. The airway mycobiome in bronchiectasis patients is dominated by Ascomycota, with a high prevalence of genera such as Aspergillus, Candida, Penicillium, and Cryptococcus [13]. Among the most commonly isolated species are Candida albicans and Aspergillus fumigatus [30]. Compared to healthy controls, bronchiectasis patients show significant genus-level differences, particularly in Aspergillus, Cryptococcus, and Myxospora. ITS sequencing showed that Candida was the dominant species, followed by Aspergillus, Saccharomyces, Clavispora and Cryptococcus (Fig. 2). The stable stage group showed higher levels of Candida and Aspergillus, and in the acute exacerbation stage, the abundance of Cryptococcus and Curvularia increased significantly [13].

A study examining airway fungal communities in adults with CF and non-CF bronchiectasis founded that non-CF bronchiectasis patients exhibited greater fungal diversity but lower overall abundance compared to those with CF. Candida albicans was one of the most frequently detected species, accounting for 21.8% of isolates [30]. Cuthbertson et al. reported that, based on NGS, Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus dermatitidis, and Aspergillus aculeatus were detected in 96.9%, 28.1%, and 21.9% of non-CF bronchiectasis patients, respectively. In contrast, conventional culture methods failed to detect any filamentous fungi. This result also demonstrates the limited role of culture in the diagnosis of bronchiectasis fungal infections [30]. However, culture-independent methods, particularly NGS, can detect fungi that are missed by traditional culture techniques. These methods enable the characterization of microbial communities in complex ecosystems without the need for cultivation [2].

The respiratory mycobiome in cystic fibrosis

CF is a genetic disease marked by persistent microbial colonization and chronic inflammation of the respiratory tract, which progressively impairs lung function and causes structural lung damage [31]. Despite the considerable attention that the bacterial microbiome has received from researchers, the mycobiome remains comparatively understudied. Nevertheless, the role of the mycobiome in the pathophysiology of CF has attracted increasing attention. It was found that common fungi in the airways of CF patients include Aspergillus spp., such as Aspergillus fumigatus and Aspergillus niger, and Candida spp., such as Candida dublinensis [32] (Fig. 2). Colonization of the airways by these fungi may be associated with chronic inflammation and disease progression. Notably, although Aspergillus was highly prevalent in culture studies, its relative abundance was low in sequencing-based studies. Other fungi such as Apotrichum, Trichosporon, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Malassezia, Exophiala and Scedosporium showed a high abundance distribution in a few samples [7, 30]. In a study on the respiratory microbiome of adult CF patients, it was found that the airway fungal community structure of patients in the acute exacerbation stage was significantly different from that in the stable stage (p = 0.01). Candida dublinensis accounted for a higher proportion in the exacerbation stage, while Candida albicans dominated in the stable stage [14].

Respiratory mycobiome and the progression of chronic respiratory diseases

Emerging evidence reveals that diverse patterns of dysbiosis within the lung microbiome are intricately linked to the onset and progression of various chronic respiratory diseases [33]. To effectively prevent and treat these conditions, it is crucial to identify the key fungal species that drive their development and pathogenesis. A plethora of studies have corroborated the notion that lung dysbiosis leads to disruption of the balance between fungal colonization and disease [33]. Furthermore, airway fungal diversity is reduced in most chronic respiratory diseases, a finding that is associated with poorer lung function [13]. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease

The diversity and relative abundance of the lung microbiota in patients with COPD differ substantially from those in healthy individuals, and this difference is known to increase further during exacerbations [68, 69]. There is growing evidence that dysbiosis of the lung microbiota is involved in this disease. Previous studies have also confirmed that fungal infections are associated with COPD severity and worse clinical outcomes [39, 42].

The respiratory mycobiome of COPD patients showed a unique pattern associated with exacerbation frequency. Studies have shown that patients with COPD who experience frequent exacerbations (more than three times per year) exhibit marked alterations in the pulmonary mycobiome, along with intensified fungal-fungal interactions, compared to those with infrequent exacerbations. Based on the previous finding, lung mycobiota α-diversity was inversely correlated with both mortality and disease severity in critically ill AECOPD patients, and was significantly lower during acute exacerbations compared to the stable phase of COPD [18, 54, 55]. Certain fungal genera, such as Aspergillus, Curvularia, and Penicillium, have been identified as indicators of high-risk fungal communities, which are strongly linked to greater susceptibility to exacerbations and higher mortality risk [18]. In stable COPD, an increase in fungal α-diversity is associated not only with poorer lung function but also with greater symptom severity [53]. Moreover, specific genera such as Candida, Penicillium, and Malassezia are overrepresented in COPD airways, indicating a dysregulated state that may exacerbate inflammation and disease progression [18, 20, 38].

Among pathogenic fungi, the most clinically significant species are Aspergillus fumigatus and Candida albicans [70, 71]. Notably, colonization of the airways by Aspergillus has been linked to more frequent acute exacerbations of COPD [34, 35]. Besides, Aspergillus is the most extensively studied fungal pathogen in COPD. Sensitization to Aspergillus has been linked to increased symptoms, accelerated lung function decline, disease progression, and the presence of bronchiectasis [39–41].

Frequent use of antibiotics and corticosteroids in COPD patients predisposes them to persistent fungal infections [72]. Emerging evidence indicates that the composition of the airway mycobiome is closely associated with eosinophil levels and lung function impairment. Patients with eosinophilic COPD experience more frequent non-infectious acute exacerbations. Their airways exhibit a significantly increased relative abundance of Aspergillus and Bjerkandera. Furthermore, supporting the link between fungal colonization and eosinophilic inflammation, Alvarez Baumgartner et al. found that a higher blood eosinophil level explained 14.4% of the variance (p = 0.004) in the nasal mycobiome structure, with Malassezia becoming the overwhelmingly dominant genus (relative abundance > 70%) in this patient group [20, 73]. Moreover, the fungal microbiota in these patients demonstrates significantly reduced species richness and diversity [43]. Tiew et al. conducted a comparative analysis of two groups of patients with COPD. One group exhibited a yeast-dominant airway mycobiome and a higher symptom burden, as assessed by the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score. The other group was characterized by a predominance of Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Curvularia, and was associated with a higher frequency of acute exacerbations and increased mortality. Notably, patients in the latter group demonstrated significantly elevated levels of specific Immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies against these three fungal genera [18]. In addition, serum IgE levels in COPD patients are positively correlated with clinical indicators such as dyspnea and reduced lung function, suggesting a potential role of fungal sensitization in adverse outcomes [42].

Pneumocystis is another important opportunistic fungal pathogen in COPD. Pneumocystis has a high isolation rate in this population, which is related to the severity of the disease and the degree of airflow limitation. In COPD patients co-infected with Pneumocystis, CXCR3 expression on Th1 lymphocytes is upregulated, suggesting that this fungal pathogen may play a role in disease progression [36, 37]. Further study on nasal fungal group also found that COPD patients with severe Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 Second (FEV1) had a significant change in nasal fungal composition, and the abundance of Malassezia was higher [20]. In summary, the imbalance of fungal community, specific fungal sensitization and opportunistic fungal infection are all involved in the amplification of inflammation and disease progression of COPD.

Asthma

Fungal exposure is strongly associated with the occurrence, progression, severity and acute exacerbation of asthma. Among these factors, fungal sensitization has been identified as an independent risk factor for poor pulmonary function prognosis [61]. Emerging evidence indicates that asthmatic airways exhibit altered fungal diversity, with specific genera showing phenotype-dependent abundance patterns. This is exemplified by the significantly lower fungal diversity observed in patients with eosinophilic asthma (EA) compared to those with non-eosinophilic asthma (NEA) [8, 43]. Evidence suggests that the relative abundance of Aspergillus, Cladosporium, Alternaria, Psathyrella and Bjerkandera adusta was significantly increased in patients with EA [43]. It is worth noting that Aspergillus and Bjerkandera adusta may exacerbate T helper type 2 (Th2) inflammatory responses by inducing eosinophil infiltration and secretion of Th2-type cytokines such as Interleukin-4 (IL-4), Interleukin-5 (IL-5), and Interleukin-13 (IL-13) [44, 74, 75]. In contrast, patients with NEA are dominated by Rhodotorula and Papiliotrema flavescens, which may be associated with neutrophil infiltration and activation of non-Th2-type immune pathways [28].

Clinical evidence suggested that increased abundance of certain fungal genera, including Candida and Rhodotorula, is positively associated with asthma severity. Additionally, Schizophyllum may contribute to disease progression by disrupting host immune regulation, thereby promoting airway inflammation and increasing the frequency of acute exacerbations [28]. More notably, the reduced airway fungal diversity observed in asthmatic patients, together with an overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens such as Aspergillus, may accelerate airway remodeling. Clinically, this lower fungal richness is associated with poorer lung function and worse asthma control [5, 56, 59]. Moreover, Aspergillus sensitization has been identified as a key predictor of frequent exacerbations of severe asthma [61]. Research has established an association between inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use and modifications to the fungal microbiome diversity and structure in the asthmatic airway [5, 57]. In patients with severe asthma, glucocorticoid therapy is strongly associated with an increased airway burden of filamentous fungi, particularly Aspergillus. This suggests that fungal colonization may contribute to the pathogenesis of steroid-resistant asthma [60]. Longitudinal analysis showed that Alternaria abundance was positively correlated with ICS dose, and negatively correlated with body mass index (BMI). The abundance of Aspergillus was positively correlated with both ICS dose and duration of disease [5]. Early-life exposure to fungi, particularly Malassezia, has been associated with a significantly increased risk of developing asthma in children sensitized to allergens [27, 45].

Children with fungal sensitization usually exhibit elevated serum total IgE levels and are frequently co-sensitized to other aeroallergens such as dust mites and pet dander. These patients typically require high-dose ICS treatment, and with studies showing that fungal-sensitized asthmatics have an earlier disease onset and a longer disease duration, fungal sensitization may serve as a potential biomarker for severe asthma [58, 76]. The above evidence suggests that the respiratory mycobiome may be used as a biomarker for asthma classification or prognosis, which is helpful for therapeutic target screening.

Bronchiectasis

Bronchiectasis patients with fungal infection tend to present with more impaired pulmonary function, elevated fungus-specific IgE levels, and a higher frequency of disease exacerbations compared to those without fungal involvement [62]. Patients with bronchiectasis often have impaired immune defense, which may promote fungal colonization and infection. Studies have found that long-term use of antibiotics is significantly related to the detection rate of Candida albicans. In contrast, the colonization of Aspergillus fumigatus is closely related to factors such as advanced age, severity of pulmonary disease, long-term use of antibiotics, and increased sputum purulency [13].

The colonization of airways by Aspergillus and Candida is closely associated with acute exacerbations of bronchiectasis, especially in patients with poor response to antibiotics. Longitudinal studies have shown that patients with bronchiectasis often exhibit persistent Candida colonization during the stable phase, whereas Aspergillus colonization tends to peak during inflammatory exacerbation [29]. The CAMEB cohort study further found that fungal sensitization, especially sensitization status to Aspergillus, was a potential marker of bronchiectasis disease severity [13]. In a study of bronchiectasis patients in Southeast Asia, the presence of Aspergillus in the airways was significantly associated with elevated systemic chitotriosidase (CHIT1) activity. Notably, CHIT1 activity was not only a strong predictor of disease exacerbation but also closely correlated with the presence of at least one Aspergillus species and frequent exacerbations [64]. These findings suggest that the mycobiome may serve as potential biomarkers for acute exacerbations of bronchiectasis and poor prognosis [77].

In addition, the characteristics of the mycobiome in patients with bronchiectasis are closely related to the host immune response. An Aspergillus-dominated airway mycobiome is associated with a proinflammatory immune phenotype, such as Th2 inflammation. This immune response is linked to impaired lung function, accelerated disease progression, and an increased frequency of acute exacerbations [13, 41]. Additional studies have demonstrated that an increased abundance of yeast is linked to enhanced Th2 and T helper type 17 (Th17) immune responses, characterized by elevated levels of IL-4 and interleukin-17 (IL-17), and is also associated with eosinophilic bronchiectasis [47]. Different clinical phenotypes can also influence the composition of the fungal community. For example, Aspergillus enrichment is more commonly observed in patients with bronchiectasis–chronic obstructive pulmonary disease overlap (BCO). Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) has a higher prevalence in BCO patients and is closely associated with severe bronchiectasis and frequent exacerbations [63].

Fungal-bacterial co-infections are commonly observed in patients with bronchiectasis, with Aspergillus fumigatus frequently co-detected alongside Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and non-tuberculous mycobacteria. Such co-infections may reduce airway microbiome diversity, as indicated by decrease Shannon and Simpson indices, suggesting ecological imbalance. This imbalance may aggravate airway inflammation and structural destruction through a synergistic mechanism [78, 79]. In contrast, fungi such as Cladosporium, Bipolaris, and Wickerhamomyces, are less frequently detected in patients with bronchiectasis and may be associated with a more favorable prognosis. However, when co-existing with Aspergillus species, they may enhance immune sensitization responses and contribute to disease exacerbation [13, 30].

Cystic fibrosis

The airway microbiota in patients with CF changes dynamically during disease progression. In the early stage, bacterial colonization predominated, with Staphylococcus aureus being the dominant species [80]. However, in the late stage of the disease, fungi such as Aspergillus fumigatus and Scedosporium gradually dominate the flora, and their increased abundance is significantly linked with decreased lung function and a higher risk of acute exacerbation [7]. The diversity of the airway mycobiome in CF patients is negatively correlated with the severity of the disease, and fungal diversity gradually decreases during the progression of the disease and the deterioration of lung function [7, 65, 66].

Fungal dysregulation is common in patients with CF and is typically characterized by the overgrowth of pathogenic fungi such as Candida and Aspergillus. A prospective observational study showed that Candida albicans colonization was strongly associated with increased exacerbation frequency, decreased FEV₁, and co-colonization with Aspergillus species [48]. It is suggested that C. albicans plays an important role in disease progression through interactions with other members of the microbiome, although the specific mechanism is still unclear.

Moreover, a prospective cross-sectional study found that Candida albicans was the predominant colonizer (accounting for 51.6%) during the stable phase of CF, whereas Candida dubliniensis was more frequently detected during acute exacerbations. The abundance ratio of Candida dubliniensis to Candida albicans was significantly associated with disease exacerbation (p < 0.001), suggesting that the dynamic succession of Candida species may contribute to the onset and progression of the disease [14].

Persistent Aspergillus fumigatus infection has been identified as an independent risk factor for hospitalization in patients with CF [67]. In a cohort of 206 adolescents and adults with CF, O’Dea et al. found that the presence of Aspergillus fumigatus in respiratory cultures was associated with lower FEV₁% predicted and a higher rate of severe pulmonary exacerbations [49]. Sensitization to Aspergillus fumigatus may have an adverse effect on the disease course of CF patients by increasing airway inflammation and accelerating the decline of lung function [50]. The prevalence of ABPA in CF patients is estimated to range from 1% to 25% [67]. In addition, Scedosporium species are associated with pneumonia and invasive fungal infections, with an increased risk particularly observed in immunocompromised patients with CF [51].

Weiser et al. further found that the abundance of Aspergillus, Exophiala, and Candida parapsilosis increased with age, while the overall diversity of fungal groups decreased. This may be related to changes in chronic infection status and immune regulatory mechanisms [52]. Longitudinal studies have also shown that the structure of the fungal community in CF patients can change significantly during antibiotic treatment, but the core fungal community represented by Aspergillus and Candida shows strong stability [81].

Mechanisms of mycobiome-mediated pathogenesis in the airways

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease

In contrast to bacteria, which directly influence the lung environment and host immune responses, the pulmonary mycobiome is believed to function as a co-factor in modulating host immunity and inflammation, potentially contributing to declining lung function and disease progression [82, 83]. Due to the impairment of mucociliary clearance, patients with COPD are exposed to fungi widely present in the environment for a long time, which can easily lead to continuous colonization of fungi in the airway and induce allergic reactions. The enhanced immune and inflammatory responses triggered by fungal exposure may help to explain the marked heterogeneity in symptoms and the frequent exacerbations observed in some COPD patients [18, 41].

From an immunological perspective, cell wall components of fungal pathogens (such as Aspergillus), including β-glucans and mannans can trigger innate immune responses through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and dendritic cell-associated C-type lectin-1 (Dectin-1). This recognition activates innate immune signaling pathways, such as the Syk–CARD9 axis, driving Th17-type responses and leading to excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-8, IL-17, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), as well as the recruitment of neutrophils [84]. Notably, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are increased in COPD airways and have been linked to airflow limitation and exacerbation frequency in human studies [85]. This persistent inflammatory response further aggravates airway obstruction and alveolar structure destruction, which is the typical pathological feature of COPD. Experimental models also support these observations: in mice challenged with Aspergillus fumigatus, Dectin-1 deficiency impairs the IL-17/IL-23 axis and alters antifungal immunity, while chronic fungal exposure through Dectin-1 signaling can promote airway immunopathology [84, 86]. In addition, fungi can also secrete a variety of metabolites, such as mycotoxins and proteases, that damage the airway epithelium and disrupt the mucosal barrier. For example, proteases derived from Aspergillus degrade tight junction proteins, enhancing pathogen invasion and maintaining persistent inflammation [87] (Fig. 3).

The respiratory mycobiome-host immune crosstalk in chronic airway disease. Fungal components such as β-glucans and chitin are recognized by PRRs on airway epithelial cells and dendritic cells, including dectin-1 and TLR2/4. This recognition activates downstream signaling pathways——such as NF-κB, Syk/CARD9, IL-17, and MAPK——leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines by epithelial and immune cells. These mediators recruit innate immune cells and modulate adaptive immune responses, ultimately contributing to AHR and mucus hypersecretion, which exacerbate allergic airway inflammation. In addition, fungal allergens can induce IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reactions and promote eosinophilic inflammation through signaling pathways such as IL-5. Fungal-derived metabolites, including mycotoxins and proteases, may further damage the airway epithelium and disrupt the mucosal barrier. Collectively, these mechanisms illustrate the complex interactions between host immunity and fungi in airway diseases such as asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis, and CF

The airway mycobiome also affects the structure and function of lung bacteria through a variety of mechanisms, which are manifested as synergistic or antagonistic microbial interactions [88, 89]. Fungi can selectively promote or inhibit the growth of specific bacterial groups, regulate the expression of virulence factors, affect the morphological development of bacteria, and serve as a substrate source for biofilm formation. Meanwhile, bacteria are also capable of modulating fungal growth dynamics, pathogenicity, morphological differentiation, and adhesion capacity [2]. Fungi and bacteria can cooperate to form mixed biofilms, thereby improving their tolerance to dehydration, antimicrobial agents and host immune response, leading to the formation and spread of multi-drug resistant strains [90, 91].

Moreover, the pulmonary Aspergillus community exhibits complex and dynamic interactions with the bacterial microbiota in the airways of COPD patients. For instance, co-colonization of Aspergillus with bacterial pathogens like Haemophilus or Pseudomonas aeruginosa can synergistically worsen local inflammation and tissue damage, highlighting the complexity of polymicrobial infections during acute exacerbations of COPD [9] (Fig. 4).

Fungal–bacterial interactions in the respiratory microbiome. Fungi can selectively promote or inhibit the proliferation of specific bacterial taxa, modulate the production of bacterial virulence factors, and influence bacterial morphogenesis. Fungal colonization may also affect the bacterial ecological niche through the secretion of metabolites. Conversely, bacteria can regulate fungal growth dynamics, pathogenic potential, morphological transitions, and surface adhesion. Certain bacterial metabolites including phenazines, rhamnolipids, and siderophores have been shown to inhibit fungal growth. Fungi and bacteria can form polymicrobial biofilms, which provide protection against desiccation, antimicrobial agents, and immune clearance. Co-colonization may synergistically aggravate airway inflammation and tissue damage, facilitate the development of multidrug-resistant and transmissible microbial strains, and jointly drive neutrophilic inflammation. Furthermore, fungal–bacterial interactions can amplify pro-inflammatory cytokine and suppress regulatory T cell function, contributing to persistent inflammation, increased antibiotic resistance, and chronic infection in the airways

Asthma

The respiratory mycobiome is involved in the pathogenesis of asthma through multiple pathways. First, some fungi can act as allergens, directly inducing immune responses and causing airway inflammation [92]. In addition, interactions between fungi and other microorganisms can disturb airway microbiome homeostasis, thereby indirectly facilitating the onset and progression of asthma [5].

At the immunological level, cell wall components of fungi such as Candida albicans and Aspergillus fumigatus, particularly β-glucans and chitin, are recognized by pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), including Dectin-1 and Toll-like receptors 2 and 4 (TLR2/4), on dendritic and epithelial cells. This recognition activates the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and recruiting neutrophils and eosinophils. The ensuing cellular influx results in airway hyper-responsiveness (AHR) and mucus hypersecretion, thereby aggravating allergic airway inflammation [93, 94].

Fungal colonization also leads to an imbalance in T cell differentiation: Candida and Aspergillus stimulate dendritic cells to produce IL-4/IL-13, driving IgE production and eosinophilia [95, 96]. Rhodotorula and Malassezia activate the Dectin-1/Syk-CARD9-NF-κB signaling axis, promoting robust secretion of IL-17A and IL-22 by Th17 cells. This leads to the recruitment of neutrophils, which release elastase and reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing airway tissue damage. Additionally, these processes activate the AP-1 pathway, suppressing glucocorticoid receptor (GR) function, thereby contributing to steroid resistance and the development of treatment-refractory asthma. Serine proteases secreted by Aspergillus can degrade epithelial tight junction proteins (occludin, claudin-5), destroy the mucosal barrier and impair the mucociliary clearance function [96, 97] (Fig. 3).

The synergism or antagonism between fungi and bacteria also plays a key role in the pathological process of asthma. The coexistence of Aspergillus and Streptococcus can synergistically amplify the inflammatory response [28]. The symbiosis of Candida and Streptococcus enhances IL-17-mediated neutrophil chemotaxis by activating the NF-κB pathway and is one of the features of severe asthma [94, 98]. Community network analysis showed that there was specific coupling between fungal and bacterial modules in asthma patients. For example, Cladosporium showed a negative correlation with certain bacterial taxa, suggesting that fungi may influence disease phenotypes by modulating bacterial community composition [28]. In neutrophilic asthma, fungi co-colonize with Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, etc., which may synergistically drive neutrophilic inflammation (Fig. 4) [98]. The above findings reveal the multiple roles of fungi in the immune regulation of asthma and highlight the complexity of synergism among fungi and bacteria.

Bronchiectasis

Fungal sensitization, most commonly reflected by IgE reactivity to Aspergillus spp., is strongly associated with an immunoallergy phenotype in bronchiectasis. This phenotype is characterised by elevated serum specific IgE, intensified eosinophilic inflammation and an increased incidence of ABPA. Notably, fungal sensitization is particularly common in patients with BCO, and is inversely associated with decreased diversity of the airway mycobiome [63, 99].

The airway mycobiome is involved in the pathogenesis of bronchiectasis by regulating the host immune response. For example, Aspergillus fumigatus colonization can stimulate bronchial epithelial cells to release IL-33 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), initiating Th2-type immune responses characterized by elevated IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 production. This cascade promotes eosinophil recruitment and activation, a hallmark feature of the eosinophilic bronchiectasis endotype [29, 100]. In addition, Aspergillus fumigatus has the ability to resist phagocytosis by macrophages and neutrophils, showing an immune escape mechanism [101]. Candida albicans, by engaging the Dectin-1 pathway, drives IL-17 production and neutrophilic infiltration [102].

Fungal pathogens also amplify Th17 responses by upregulating IL-17 and IL-23, driving neutrophil recruitment and NETs formation–key mechanisms in airway tissue damage and persistent inflammation in bronchiectasis [29]. C. albicans further promotes mucus hypersecretion via IL-8 and MUC5AC up-regulation [47], whereas Aspergillus-derived proteases degrade epithelial tight junctions and accelerate airway remodelling [103] (Fig. 3).

In addition, intricate fungi-bacterial interactions exist in the airways of patients with bronchiectasis. For instance, Pseudomonas aeruginosa colonization inhibits fungal growth (antagonism), while Aspergillus species enhance bacterial biofilm formation (synergy). This cross-kingdom effect, revealed by interactome analyses, is linked to a higher risk of acute exacerbations. Notably, co-colonization of Aspergillus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa amplifies local inflammation, up-regulates the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-8, and inhibit regulatory T cell function, thereby establishing a chronic inflammation. This persistent inflammatory state contributes to antibiotic resistance development and infection chronicity–hallmarks of advanced bronchiectasis [99, 104] (Fig. 4).

In addition, nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infection, which is also common in patients with bronchiectasis, can increase host susceptibility to Aspergillus colonization, thereby further promoting disease progression. Fungi not only aggravate host immune damage by secreting proteases and antigens, but may also cooperate with NTM to promote airway inflammation and aggravate the disease [41, 101].

Cystic fibrosis

CF associated fungi directly damage airway tissues and induce inflammatory responses through multiple mechanisms. Aspergillus fumigatus secretes proteases and toxins that directly injure airway epithelial cells, and its conidia can evade intracellular killing by alveolar macrophages, triggering robust inflammatory responses [29]. Candida can enhance tissue invasion ability through yeast-to-hyphal morphological switching, which is regulated by a variety of signaling pathways, and the hyphal form is an important virulence factor [6].

Complex fungal-bacterial interactions are also present in CF airways. For example, Candida albicans and Pseudomonas aeruginosa may interact through mixed biofilm formation and nutrient competition, promoting chronic infections and enhancing antibiotic resistance [66]. In addition, sequencing-based studies have revealed synergistic and antagonistic interactions between fungi and bacteria. Notably, potential cooperative relationships have been reported between Aspergillus and Streptococcus spp [7, 105]. Pseudomonas aeruginosa can inhibit fungal growth through molecules such as phenazines, rhamnolipids, and siderophores [106]. Furthermore, anaerobic bacteria such as Streptococcus and Prevotella generate short-chain fatty acids that may favor the growth of opportunistic pathogens like P. aeruginosa. Conversely, fungal hyphal structures may shield bacteria from antibiotic activity [107] (Fig. 4).

Beyond direct damage and sheltering effects, fungal molecules potently modulate host immunity in CF. A. fumigatus cell-wall components and secreted proteases activate epithelial alarmins and type-2 pathways in some contexts, and also stimulate Th17/IL-17–mediated neutrophilic responses; IL-17 and NETs formation contribute to neutrophil-dominant inflammation and the abundant extracellular DNA characteristic of CF sputum [108, 109] (Fig. 3). Moreover, galactosaminogalactan of Aspergillus fumigatus can inhibit neutrophil activity and promote the escape of the fungus from immune clearance [110]. Collectively, these interconnected mechanisms, including direct epithelial damage, the morphogenetic virulence of Candida, cross-kingdom biofilm interactions, and immune modulation via the Dectin-1/IL-17 axis and NETosis, help explain why fungal colonization in CF contributes to mucus stasis, persistent infection, and accelerated lung function decline.

Cross-disease immunological mechanisms of fungal–host interactions

Across chronic respiratory diseases, fungal–host interactions converge on common immune pathways that shape disease progression. In COPD, fungal β-glucans activate Dectin-1/Syk/CARD9 signaling, amplifying IL-17–mediated neutrophilic inflammation, while excessive NETosis contributes to airflow limitation and frequent exacerbations [84, 85]. In asthma, fungal proteases disrupt epithelial barriers and induce alarmins, with Th17/IL-17 responses particularly enriched in severe or fungus-sensitized phenotypes, whereas evidence for NETosis is less consistent [96, 97]. In bronchiectasis, chronic fungal colonization sustains Dectin-1–IL-17 activation and drives persistent neutrophilic inflammation; NETs in sputum correlate with disease severity and exacerbation risk [29, 102]. In CF, impaired clearance allows persistent fungal growth, accompanied by elevated IL-17A levels and abundant NET-derived extracellular DNA that increases mucus viscosity and promotes infection persistence [108, 109]. Collectively, these shared mechanisms highlight conserved immunopathological pathways by which airway fungi exacerbate inflammation and tissue injury across diverse chronic respiratory diseases.

Future directions and challenges

Integrated multi-omics approaches

The airway microbiome comprises bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other microorganisms that engage in complex interkingdom interactions. To date, most investigations of chronic respiratory diseases have focused on a single microbial kingdom. Future studies should adopt integrated, multi-omics platforms that simultaneously profile bacterial, viral, and fungal communities, quantify their crosstalk, and determine how these microbial networks shape clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses. As discussed above, antagonistic or synergistic interactions between fungi and bacteria are common across chronic respiratory disorders and appear to influence disease progression. However, their overall patterns and underlying mechanisms in chronic airway diseases remain poorly understood [111].

Functional mechanism studies

Whole-genome shotgun metagenomic sequencing offers a powerful approach to elucidate the functional roles of fungi in chronic airway diseases. Future research should focus on characterizing fungal metabolites, immunomodulatory mechanisms, and interactions with host cells to clarify how fungi contribute to disease onset and progression. This includes investigating how fungi regulate airway inflammation and tissue remodeling by modulating host immune responses, how fungal cell wall components and metabolites activate or suppress immune cells, and how host immunity, in turn, affects fungal colonization and growth. Additionally, studies should explore fungal contributions to antibiotic resistance and allergenicity. Although current sequencing technologies have limitations in detecting viral and fungal genomes, metagenomic approaches provide valuable functional insights that help uncover the pathogenic roles of fungi in airway diseases [112].

Research on disease overlap syndromes

Future studies should explore the role of the mycobiome in chronic airway disease overlap syndromes. For instance, enrichment of Aspergillus species has been associated with BCO, and the prevalence of ABPA is notably higher in BCO patients. ABPA in this context has been linked to more severe bronchiectasis and increased frequency of disease exacerbations [63]. Investigating the involvement of the mycobiome in these overlap syndromes may provide deeper insights into the shared pathophysiological mechanisms of chronic airway diseases and inform more effective therapeutic strategies.

Geographical variation studies

Given the influence of geographical factors on the airway mycobiome [41], future research should focus on comparing the fungal communities in patients with chronic airway diseases across different regions. Such studies should also examine how these variations are shaped by environmental exposures, genetic backgrounds, and lifestyle factors. Understanding these region-specific patterns may help inform the development of personalized prevention and treatment strategies.

Clinical application research

Future studies should explore the potential clinical applications of mycobiome profiling in chronic airway diseases. This includes its use as a biomarker for disease diagnosis, monitoring, and prognosis. Evidence suggests that alterations in the airway mycobiome are associated with disease severity and exacerbation frequency. However, the reliability and validity of fungal markers in clinical settings require further validation. Additionally, therapeutic strategies targeting the airway mycobiome such as modulating fungal communities to alleviate symptoms show potential and merit further investigation.

Strengthening longitudinal and large-cohort studies

Most investigations of the airway mycobiome in chronic respiratory diseases have been cross-sectional, making it difficult to establish causal links between fungal community dynamics and disease onset or progression. Future research should therefore prioritize longitudinal designs that follow patients from early disease stages through subsequent phases, enabling characterization of temporal shifts in the airway mycobiome. In parallel, large-sample cohort studies are needed to minimize the influence of individual variability and environmental confounders, thereby enhancing the reliability and generalizability of findings. Although microbiome research began decades ago, the lack of integrative, longitudinal perspectives has limited our understanding of how microbial communities shape disease trajectories, underscoring the critical importance of both longitudinal and large-cohort approaches.

Challenges

Research on the airway mycobiome in chronic respiratory diseases faces several significant challenges that hinder progress and clinical translation. First, technical limitations in sequencing methodologies such as the absence of standardized primers and incomplete fungal reference databases impede accurate species-level identification. Moreover, inconsistencies in sample processing and sequencing protocols introduce technical variability, which may bias results. In addition, most existing studies adopt an observational design, which limits the ability to infer causal relationships between alterations in the mycobiome and disease progression. Compounding these issues, sample collection is often susceptible to contamination from oral microbiota, and differences in processing techniques can further compromise the accuracy of fungal detection.

Patient-related factors, including genetic background, lifestyle, and comorbidities, also contribute to substantial inter-individual variability, adding complexity and potential confounding to data interpretation. Furthermore, many studies are restricted to specific disease types or particular disease stages, lacking a longitudinal perspective and comprehensive comparisons across the spectrum of chronic respiratory conditions. Finally, the intricate interactions between fungi and other microbial communities, such as bacteria and viruses, remain poorly understood, highlighting the need for more integrative, cross-kingdom studies to clarify the mycobiome’s role in disease pathogenesis.

Airway mycobiome regulation: a novel target for intervention in chronic respiratory diseases

Increasing evidence has shown that changes in the structure and function of the airway mycobiome play a key role in the occurrence, development and prognosis of chronic respiratory diseases. Some studies have shown that concurrent use of antibiotics or hormones has no significant impact on the structural composition of airway fungal communities in patients with COPD [18, 22]. Longitudinal analyses performed during exacerbations and after treatment with antibiotics and glucocorticoids did not show any significant changes in the airway mycobiome [18]. This suggests that the fungal community may be more resilient to conventional therapies than the bacterial microbiome. Despite the symptomatic relief provided by conventional therapies, chronic respiratory diseases remain challenging to manage due to their recurrent exacerbations and progressive deterioration. Therefore, there is a pressing need to identify novel therapeutic targets and intervention strategies (Table 3).

Regulatory interventions for airway mycobiome in chronic respiratory diseases

Antifungal agents

Conventional antifungal therapy has been shown to reduce fungal burden and improve prognosis in patients with CF [113]. In clinical settings, azole antifungals such as voriconazole are commonly used for Aspergillus infections, while echinocandins are often administered during exacerbations to manage Candida infections [30, 131]. Both voriconazole and itraconazole can reduce fungal antigen exposure and decrease inflammatory responses [114], although their use requires careful consideration of potential antifungal resistance.

Targeted elimination of pathogenic fungi, for example, using echinocandins against Candida species may further enhance respiratory immune defenses [115]. Fungal–bacterial interactions, such as the coexistence of Candida and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, may compromise the efficacy of antibiotic therapy, suggesting that combined antifungal and antibacterial treatments could improve clinical outcomes [116]. For instance, the concomitant use of amphotericin B and antibiotics has shown promise in enhancing therapeutic efficacy during acute exacerbations of COPD [34].

In addition, mucolytic agents that disrupt fungal biofilms such as N-acetylcysteine may enhance drug penetration and thereby potentiate antimicrobial effectiveness [117].

Immunomodulatory interventions

Targeted immunomodulators hold promising therapeutic potential in controlling airway fibrosis induced by fungal infections [118]. Strategies aimed at the Dectin-1/CARD9 signaling pathway have shown effectiveness in attenuating excessive fungal-induced inflammatory responses. Currently, small-molecule inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies targeting the Dectin-1/Syk/CARD9 axis are under investigation and have demonstrated promising immunoregulatory properties [119].

Restoring the balance between Th17 and Treg cells particularly through interventions targeting the tryptophan–kynurenine metabolic pathway may serve as an effective approach to alleviate fungus-associated inflammation [120]. In patients with fungal sensitization, therapies targeting IgE (omalizumab), IL-5 (mepolizumab), or specific fungal antigens can mitigate symptoms and suppress Th2-driven inflammation by interrupting fungus-induced allergic pathways [41, 121, 122]. Strengthening host defense mechanisms is also essential; for instance, supplementation with vitamin D or interferon-γ may boost macrophage phagocytic activity and improve control over fungal colonization [123].

Diet

Dietary interventions-particularly fiber-rich diets-may help stabilize airway fungal populations and inhibit fungal overgrowth by restoring gut–lung axis homeostasis [124, 125]. In order to rebuild healthy fungal community structure and restore microecological balance, some studies have explored using probiotics, such as Lactobacillus, to regulate fungal composition [126].

Notably, certain yeasts such as Pichia have shown potential in antagonizing pathogenic fungi and may serve as probiotic candidates [46]. Preclinical studies have shown that this yeast can modulate intestinal immune responses and indirectly reduce the secretion of Th2 cytokines (e.g., IL4, IL5, IL13), thereby influencing airway inflammation. It may also alleviate allergic airway inflammation by enhancing the function of regulatory T cells (Treg) [114].

Modulation of environmental exposure

Given the marked geographical differences in fungal community composition, future strategies could involve adjusting patients’ living environments such as improving humidity control and ventilation to reduce exposure to region-specific dominant fungi like Cladosporium [127, 128]. Studies have also shown that early exposure to green natural environment is associated with a reduced risk of asthma attack [129].

Moreover, the distinct differences between outdoor airborne fungi (e.g., Mycosphaerella tassiana) and the airway mycobiome suggest that transient colonization by inhaled fungal spores may contribute to the respiratory disease pathogenesis. Therefore, minimizing environmental fungal exposure (e.g., via air filtration) or inoculating specific commensal fungi to reestablish a stable microbial community may help maintain homeostasis in airway fungal ecology and improve disease management [130].

Sampling strategies and biomass quantification

The lung microbiome presents unique methodological challenges, particularly with respect to sampling. Direct acquisition of lung tissue is rarely feasible outside of surgical contexts such as transplantation or tumor resection. Therefore, a range of invasive and non-invasive procedures are employed as proxies for characterizing the pulmonary environment.

The chosen sampling strategy plays a major role in shaping the interpretation of airway mycobiome data. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) is generally regarded as the most reliable representation of the lower respiratory tract. Nonetheless, Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) often yields extremely low fungal biomass, which reduces detection sensitivity and increases vulnerability to contamination. For instance, studies report that many BAL samples, particularly from culture-negative or healthy individuals, fail to generate sufficient ITS amplicons owing to limited fungal DNA input [132]. By contrast, induced sputum (IS) offers a less invasive and repeatable option but is more prone to contamination from the upper airway. Elevated Candida signals detected in IS and oral wash samples, compared with BALF, underscore the influence of anatomical site and the risk of misattributing oral taxa to the lower lung environment [19]. In children with CF, direct comparison of BALF and IS samples revealed broadly overlapping fungal communities, yet significantly higher Candida albicans abundance in IS, illustrating how sampling depth can shape apparent mycobiome composition [52].

Another key methodological factor is the quantification of fungal biomass. High-throughput sequencing generally reports relative abundance, which does not necessarily reflect true fungal load. In low-biomass samples, sequencing data may be distorted by stochastic amplification effects or by the predominance of host DNA, resulting in biased estimates of fungal diversity. For example, one study using qPCR to measure fungal copy numbers in airway samples identified marked inter-sample variability. Although sequencing suggested negligible Aspergillus fumigatus, absolute quantification revealed a biologically relevant burden (≈15,900 copies/µL), highlighting the risk of underestimation when relying solely on relative metrics [14]. Thus, integrating qPCR-based quantification, flow cytometry, or culture counts alongside sequencing enhances data interpretation, especially in distinguishing colonization from contamination or true infection.

Despite these methodological caveats, several primary studies have reported that IS and BAL samples yield broadly concordant trends when assessing pathogenic fungi and their associations with disease severity. For example, in studies of children with CF, the study demonstrated that fungal community composition including key genera such as Aspergillus and Candida was largely similar in BAL and IS samples, supporting the clinical relevance of IS sampling [52]. Similarly, in non-CF bronchiectasis, persistent isolation of Aspergillus spp. and Candida albicans from sputum was significantly associated with worse lung function, more frequent exacerbations, and purulent sputum, indicating that sputum-derived metrics can consistently reflect disease severity across sampling approaches [133]. In COPD, there is growing evidence that Aspergillus colonization detected in sputum or BALF correlates with poorer clinical outcomes, including accelerated lung function decline, higher exacerbation rates, and increased short-term mortality, reinforcing that sputum sampling can similarly capture clinically relevant mycobiome–severity associations in this population [134, 135].

Conclusions

Research on the respiratory mycobiome in chronic airway diseases holds great potential, and faces significant challenges in technology, methodology and research depth. Overcoming these limitations will require technological advances, interdisciplinary collaboration, and deeper mechanistic studies to advance the field and develop new strategies for prevention and treatment.

The airway microbiome plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of chronic respiratory diseases. Although research over the past decade has expanded our understanding of microbial contributions, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms across different disease contexts remain elusive. Currently, respiratory microbiome research is still in its early stage and requires further exploration using advanced biological models.

Most existing studies have focused on the functional impact of individual microbial (bacterial, viral, or fungal) kingdom, while integrative analyses of multi-kingdom interactions remain limited. Future research should prioritize functionally relevant microbial species or communities to enable precise diagnosis and targeted therapies for patients with chronic respiratory diseases.

The clinical significance of respiratory mycobiome research is particularly evident in the identification of fungal biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Unraveling these mechanisms could yield substantial benefits for both patients and public health.
