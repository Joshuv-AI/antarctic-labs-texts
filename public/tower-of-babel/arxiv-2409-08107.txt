WhisperNER: Unified Open Named Entity and Speech Recognition Thanks: * Equal contribution.
Gil Ayache * , Menachem Pirchi * , Aviv Navon, Aviv Shamsian, Gill Hetz, Joseph Keshet

Abstract
Integrating named entity recognition (NER) with automatic speech recognition (ASR) can significantly enhance transcription accuracy and informativeness. In this paper, we introduce WhisperNER, a novel model that allows joint speech transcription and entity recognition. WhisperNER supports open-type NER, enabling recognition of diverse and evolving entities at inference. Building on recent advancements in open NER research, we augment a large synthetic dataset with synthetic speech samples. This allows us to train WhisperNER on a large number of examples with diverse NER tags. During training, the model is prompted with NER labels and optimized to output the transcribed utterance along with the corresponding tagged entities. To evaluate WhisperNER, we generate synthetic speech for commonly used NER benchmarks and annotate existing ASR datasets with open NER tags. Our experiments demonstrate that WhisperNER outperforms natural baselines on both out-of-domain open type NER and supervised finetuning.



I
Introduction

Recent advancements in both speech processing and natural language processing (NLP) have dramatically expanded the capabilities of automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems. These improvements, driven by large-scale models like Whisper
[
1

, have significantly enhanced transcription accuracy. Meanwhile, the emergence of powerful NLP models, particularly large language models (LLMs), has led to major breakthroughs in tasks like language understanding, text generation, and named entity recognition (NER).

Traditionally, speech-driven applications have relied on pipeline architectures, where ASR systems transcribe speech into text, which is then processed by NLP models for tasks such as sentiment analysis, question answering, and NER. While this approach has proven effective across a range of applications, it suffers from error accumulation — where transcription errors in the ASR stage propagate through the pipeline, reducing the performance of downstream NLP tasks. This challenge is particularly evident in complex tasks requiring high accuracy, such as NER.

A growing area of interest is the integration of speech models into a variety of NLP tasks beyond transcription,
allowing models to process spoken language directly and eliminate intermediate stages
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. For instance, spoken language understanding models have been developed to perform tasks like intent detection and NER
[
6
,
2
,
5
]
directly from speech, bypassing the need for separate ASR components. These end-to-end models have shown promising results by jointly optimizing for both speech and language understanding.

II
Our Method

In this section we describe our approach, WhisperNER, which extends the Whisper
[
1
]
ASR model to allow for joint open NER and speech recognition. The model is designed to output a tagged transcription sequence given an audio input, effectively performing both tasks simultaneously.

II-A

Model Architecture and Training

Let
\mathbf{x}
denote the input audio features, which are fed into the encoder. The encoder produces a sequence of hidden states
\mathbf{h}=\text{Encoder}(\mathbf{x})
, which are then used to condition the generation process.

In our approach, we further condition the decoding process on a set of entity tags
\mathbf{t}=[t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}]
(where each
t_{i}
represents a specific entity type such as
person
,
location
, or
food
). The decoder then generates the output sequence
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}]
, which includes both the transcribed text and the corresponding entity tags, see Figure
1
.

Formally, the decoder generates each token
y_{t}
based on the previous tokens and the encoder’s hidden states,

y_{t}=\text{Decoder}(y_{1:t-1},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{t}).

The model is trained to minimize the standard cross-entropy loss between the predicted output sequence
\mathbf{y}
and the ground truth sequence
\mathbf{y}^{*}
, which includes both the correct transcription and the correct entity tags:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}^{*})=-\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log P(y_{t}=y_{t}^{*}\mid y_{1:t-1},\mathbf{h},\mathbf{t}).

To mark the entities in the output sequence, we explore two approaches. The second approach uses the uses the BIO tagging scheme, similar to the approach used in
[
11
]
, i.e.,
...walks(O) in(O) the(O) national(B-location) park(I-location)
. The second marks entities with start and end markers as follow
<
t_{i}
>
y_{i_{1}}...y_{i_{n}}
<
t_{i}
>>
, for example,
<location>national park<location>>
(see Figure
1
). While the former may induce greater supervision and improve generalization
[
11
]
, this approach increases the length of the generated sequence, hence it is potentially less compute and time efficient.

Our prompting based training procedure, allows WhisperNER to handle both traditional (i.e., predefined set of entities) and open-type NER tasks. By exposing the model to diverse entity types during training, we ensure that it can generalize well to new and unseen entities during inference, making it highly adaptable to a wide range of applications.

II-B

Negative Sampling and Entity Type Dropout

In our experiments, we find that including negative samples, i.e., prompting the model with entity labels not presented in the speech utterance, to significantly enhance the model’s generalization performance, in line with findings in previous literature on open-type NER
[
8
]
.
We explored various negative sampling approaches and chose to use the complete entity set from a negative example, randomly selected from the training set, which proved most effective.

Additionally, we applied random entity type dropout during training, as inspired by Sainz et al.
[
10
]
. This involves randomly eliminating a subset of entity types from the prompt and output sequence, compelling the model to focus on the remaining defined entity types. We found this approach to reduce entity hallucination risks during inference – where the model output entities not presented in the input prompt. Also, we incorporate random shuffling of entities in the input prompt to further promote generalization across diverse input sequences. For a detailed analysis of these methodologies and their impacts, see Section
III-D
.

Experiments

In this section, we evaluate WhisperNER against naturall, pipeline based baselines, using NER and open type NER speech datasets.

Datasets.

Baselines.
We evaluate our WhisperNER againt natural two-stage baselines, in which we initially employ a Whisper large-v2 model for speech-to-text transcription, followed by a NER model. Evaluation metrics.
We assess the performance of our system using two evaluation metrics: Word Error Rate (WER), which quantifies the accuracy of the automatic speech recognition transcription, and F1 Score, which evaluates the effectiveness of named entity recognition. For a NER span prediction to be deemed correct, both the transcription must be accurate and the assigned NER label must match the true entity. This dual requirement ensures that our evaluation reflects both the precision of the speech recognition and the correctness of the entity identification. For the baseline methods, we compute the WER metric on the ASR’s output.

III-D

Ablation

To better understand the impact of negative sampling strategies and entity type dropout on model performance, we conducted an ablation study. We analyzed varying the proportion of negative samples, the method for negative sampling, and the inclusion of random entity type dropout during training. For efficiency and consistency, all models were trained for 25K steps using the augmented NuNER dataset
[
9
]
and evaluated on the MIT NER benchmarks.

Table
IV
shows that using full entity sets from negative examples, “random sample”, consistently outperforms random negative sampling of NER types, likely due to the contextual consistency of the selected examples. We also tested a “hard negative” approach, where negative examples are chosen based on semantic similarity to positive examples. While this method performed comparably, it introduced additional overhead in querying the dataset, hence we opt for random sampling from the training set for efficiency.

Figure
3
demonstrates that using two negative samples (approximately
66\%
negative entity types) provides the best F1 and WER results. Figure
2
highlights the effectiveness of random entity type dropout, which helps reducing entity tags hallucinations during inference. Based on these findings, we train WhisperNER with entity type dropout and negative sampling based on the entity set from two negative examples.

IV
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose WhisperNER, a novel approach for joint open type named entity recognition and automatic speech recognition. Our model is designed to generalize to novel entities at inference, effectively handling a diverse range of entity types. We demonstrate that WhisperNER not only outperforms traditional pipeline-based baselines in terms of entity recognition accuracy but also achieves this with minimal to no increase in transcription errors. This work marks a step forward in the integration of speech and language processing, significantly improving both the effectiveness and precision of speech-driven applications.
