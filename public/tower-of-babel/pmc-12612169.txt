Xiaoyao San against depression via glia-mediated neuroinflammation using network pharmacology and experimental validation
Authors: Xin-Yi Liu, Yi-Zhi Yan, An-Jun Jiang, Wan-Ying He, Ying-Yan Fang, Chao-Hui Li, Man-Fei Deng, Si-Jie Tan, Peng Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Xiaoyao San (XYS) has been proven effective in treating depression, but its underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. This study aims to explore the key phytochemicals and mechanisms of the anti-depressant effects of XYS. We focus on the 16 blood compounds of XYS identified through the UPLC-MS/MS method. We also validated the results of the network pharmacology analysis using an lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced depression mouse model and the BV2 microglial cell line. In silico prediction, atractylenolide I, ferulic acid, kaempferol, quercetin, and vanillic acid exhibit good oral bioavailability, safety, and ADMET properties. The results of GSEA and GO enrichment analysis showed that neuroinflammation is the key mechanism of the anti-depressant effect of XYS. Kaempferol, quercetin, atractylenolide II, z-ligustilide, paeoniflorin, saikosaponin A, atractylenolide III and glycyrrhizic acid (GA) were identified as the main phytochemicals targeting neuroinflammation. XYS gavage treatment significantly ameliorated LPS-induced depressive-like behaviors in mice by reducing neuroinflammation (microglial activation) in the prefrontal cortex. The reduction of LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels by GA was also validated in BV2 cells, and its mechanism is associated with JNK pathway. The phytochemicals of XYS exhibit favorable safety and pharmacological parameters, with targeting neuroinflammation being the key mechanism underlying its anti-depressant effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23296-1.

Introduction

Depression, also known as major depressive disorder (MDD), characterized by significant and persistent feelings of sadness along with cognitive impairments, has emerged as a major contributor to the rising global suicide rate1. In China, depression is increasingly characterized by concealment and an earlier onset, leading to long-term negative impacts on families and society at large. Therefore, it is crucial to prevent and control both the occurrence and progression of depression2. The pathogenesis of depression remains inconclusive, primarily involving neurotransmitters, neuroimmune inflammatory responses, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and neuroplasticity3. The first-line treatment drugs for depression include selective 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin/norepinephrine (NE) reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and NE/specific 5-HT anti-depressants4. SSRIs include paroxetine, sertraline, and escitalopram, while SNRIs includeduloxetine and venlafaxine, among others. However, the overall efficacy rate of SSRIs is less than 60%5, and with the widespread and long-term use of these medications, some patients do not respond to SSRIs6. Anti-depressant medications are ineffective for approximately 40% of patients with depression7. Currently, clinical anti-depressant medications exhibit several drawbacks, including a slow onset of action, significant adverse effects, high relapse rates, and a propensity for the development of drug resistance8,9. The treatment of depression through traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has a long-standing history and offers unique advantages10. It is imperative to analyze the key phytochemicals and potential mechanisms of TCM compounds in treating depression, as this holds significant clinical importance.

TCM compounds designed to soothing the liver and relieving depression hold therapeutic potential for treating depression11. Xiaoyao San (XYS) originates from the Taiping Huimin Heji Jufang of the Song Dynasty and is one of the classic prescriptions for soothing the liver and relieving depression. It consists of six Chinese herbs, namely Radix Bupleuri, Radix AngelicaeSinensis, Radix Paeoniae Alba, Poria, Rhizoma Atractylodis Macrocephalae and Radix Glycyrrhizae Preparata. Among them, Radix Bupleuri serves as the monarch drug of XYS, whereas Radix AngelicaeSinensis and Radix Paeoniae Alba function as the minister drugs. Substances with both medicinal and edible properties are deeply rooted in TCM cultures and exert significant health benefits12. The four TCM herbs in XYS are substances that serve both medicinal and edible substances, named Radix AngelicaeSinensis, Poria, Rhizoma Atractylodis Macrocephalae and Radix Glycyrrhizae Preparata. This suggests that XYS possesses good safety and potential for drug development. A recent systematic review and network meta-analysis indicated that XYS ranks third among TCM compounds in terms of reducing scores on the Hamilton Depression Scale, while also exhibiting the lowest incidence of adverse reactions13. Clinically, XYS has been used long-term for the treatment of depression accompanied by liver qi stagnation and spleen deficiency syndrome, demonstrating excellent efficacy and safety14. XYS has shown potent anti-depressant effects in various rodent models of depression, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS)15, chronic restraint stress (CRS)16, chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS)17,18, and chronic immobilization stress19,20. However, further investigation is needed to elucidate the pharmacodynamic basis, safety, and specific molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-depressant properties of XYS.

Network pharmacology has facilitated the transition of TCM from an empirical medicine to an evidence-based approach, thereby enhancing our understanding of its therapeutic effects21,22. Microglia, the primary resident immune cells in the brain, mediate neuroinflammation that is closely associated with the onset of depression23. The substantial production of pro-inflammatory factors following microglia activation ultimately results in neuronal degeneration and necrosis. LPS is the most commonly used potent activator of microglia both in vitro and in vivo. The LPS-induced neuroinflammation model in mice has been reported and confirmed24,25. Regardless of whether LPS is administered peripherally or intracerebrally, it can induce depressive-like behaviors in experimental animals26–28. Unlike previous studies, we focus on the neuroinflammatory mechanisms of XYS in anti-depressant effects, identifying the phytochemicals in XYS that regulate neuroinflammation. We established a mouse model of depression by intraperitoneal injection of LPS, and an in vitro cell model of neuroinflammation was created by treating BV2 cells with LPS. This study integrates network pharmacology, gene expression profiles of patients with depression, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and both in vivo and in vitro experiments to explore the phytochemicals and potential mechanisms of XYS in targeting neuroinflammation. This study provides new insights into the anti-depressant mechanism of XYS and offers a series of lead compounds targeting neuroinflammation for the development of anti-depressant drugs.

Results

The main phytochemicals of XYS and their safety evaluation

This study focuses on 16 representative phytochemicals in XYS (Table 1)29,30. We identified 16 XYS phytochemicals listed in Table 1 using a non-targeted metabolomics of TCM. The 16 main phytochemicals found in XYS interact with a total of 227 targets, which have been identified through reference mining, database mining, and structure-based prediction. XYS phytochemicals target multiple human organ systems, including the cardiovascular and nervous systems (Fig. 1A). For the cell type-specific enrichment analysis, the potential targets of XYS are primarily localized to macrophage, basal cell, epithelial cell, endothelial cell and glial cell (Fig. 1B). We employed ADMETlab 3.031 to calculate the physicochemical properties pertinent to the Lipinski, Pfizer, and GSK rules. Among these phytochemicals, atractylenolide III, ferulic acid, kaempferol, quercetin and vanillic acid fully comply with the aforementioned criteria, indicating that these components possess favorable oral bioavailability, safety, and absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profile (Fig. 1C). According to the rule that acute toxicity greater than level 3 and fewer than 3 positive toxicity endpoints indicate non-toxic32, all phytochemicals in XYS except atractylenolide II are non-toxic (Fig. 1D). Atractylenolide I has the highest safety, with an LD50 of 5105 mg/kg, followed by isoliquiritin and paeoniflorin (LD50 = 4000 mg/kg). To evaluate the safety of the XYS, Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining was performed on the liver after 2 weeks of XYS treatment (1342.9 mg/kg/day). The results showed no significant pathological changes in the liver, indicating that XYS has no hepatotoxicity and good biosafety (Fig. 1E). Moreover, we also tested the levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in the serum, and the results showed that there was no significant difference between XYS and CON group (Fig. 1F, G).

XYS phytochemicals exert anti-depressant effects by targeting neuroinflammation

The MDD dataset GSE98793 comprises gene expression data derived from whole blood samples collected from donors diagnosed with MDD and healthy controls. By utilizing gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), we identified that the signaling pathways related to neuroinflammation are closely associated with depression. GSEA indicated that the genes identified in GSE98793 were significantly enriched in the inflammatory response (Normalized enrichment score, NES = 2.07, p < 0.001), innate immune response (NES = 1.83, p < 0.001), and cytokine production (NES = 1.69, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A-C). We further identified the Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes (BPs) regulated by XYS phytochemicals, among which multiple signaling pathways related to inflammatory responses were significantly enriched (Fig. 2D). Among these BPs, cellular response to cytokine stimulus (GO:0071345), response to lipopolysaccharide (GO:0032496), regulation of inflammatory response (GO:0050727), and inflammatory response (GO:0006954) contained the highest number of enriched targets. Consequently, we propose that targeting neuroinflammation is one of the key signaling pathway in XYS treatment for depression.

To validate that XYS phytochemicals are potent modulators of neuroinflammation, we analyzed the core targets within the XYS target profile. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was generated using Cytoscape software, where the 221 nodes represent targets and the 3759 edges represent all pairwise connections that with a combined score exceeding 0.40 (Fig. 2E). Herein, the top 20 targets by degree value ranking were selected as core targets, named TP53, AKT1, TNF, IL6, BCL2, IL1B, ESR1, HSP90AA1, CASP3, MYC, JUN, PTGS2, PPARG, NFKB1 (corresponding to the phytochemical paeoniflorin), MTOR, CYCS, CCND1, GSK3B, IL10 and HSPA4 (Fig. 2F). In the BPs of XYS regulating apoptotic signaling pathway, inflammatory response, cytokine production, and innate immune response, there are 14, 12, 11, and 10 core targets, respectively (Fig. 2G). MAPK8 and MAPK14 are also implicated in neuroinflammation, with kaempferol and paeoniflorin identified as the corresponding phytochemicals. This data underscores the critical role of XYS in modulating neuroinflammation. Among the XYS phytochemicals, kaempferol, quercetin, atractylenolide II, z-ligustilide, paeoniflorin, saikosaponin A, atractylenolide III, glycyrrhizic acid (GA), and liquiritin possess a greater number of anti-neuroinflammatory targets, suggesting their potential as neuroinflammatory modulators (Fig. 2H).

Molecular docking to detect the direct interaction between the phytochemicals of XYS and its core targets

The 12 phytochemicals of XYS correspond to 20 core targets, with a total of 44 molecular docking interactions between receptors and ligands (Fig. 3A). The 6 phytochemicals found in XYS demonstrate strong binding affinities (score ≤ −7 kcal/mol) to 7 core targets, resulting in a total of 10 molecular dockings. Furthermore, 10 phytochemicals show moderate binding affinities (score ≤ −4 kcal/mol) to 13 core targets, accounting for a cumulative total of 24 molecular dockings. GA has the best binding affinity with PTGS2 (encodes Cox2) and TNF (TNF-α), with docking scores of −10.09 and − 7.05 kcal/mol, respectively. Furthermore, saikosaponin A and liquiritin have the strong binding affinity with PTGS2, with a docking scores of −8.95 and − 8.55 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 3A). The 2D plots of the protein-ligand interactions were derived through LigPlot. GA forms 6 hydrogen bonds with amino acid residues Asn382, Hsd207 and Thr206 in the PTGS2 binding pocket (Fig. 3B). Albiflorin formed potential interactions with residues Asp274 and Asp292 of AKT1 through 2 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 3C). Figure 3D-I selectively demonstrates the interactions formed between the phytochemicals of XYS and the amino acid residues of the active pockets of the corresponding core targets.

We conducted MD simulations to further investigate the stability of the binding model of the ligand-protein complexes. The GA-PTGS2 complex remained stable throughout the 100 ns simulation, with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) ranging from 0.2 to 0.3 nm (Fig. 3J). We analyzed the number of hydrogen bonds throughout the MD simulation, and the GA-PTGS2 complex typically forms 1 to 5 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 3K). MD simulation demonstrated that the resulting GA-TNF-α complex retains stable structure for 100 ns, with the RMSD of 0.3 nm (Fig. 3L). The formation of numerous hydrogen bonds in MD simulations corroborates the findings from molecular docking and suggests the establishment of stable complexes during the 100 ns timescale simulation (Fig. 3M).

Glial cells are activated in the mouse brain following intracerebral LPS challenge

The depression model induced by peripheral or central administration of LPS is a commonly used animal model for studying depression26–28. The GSE122815 dataset consists of 6 hemisphere brain samples obtained from 18 mice after intrastriatal injection of LPS or PBS for 4 days. The GSE122815 shared 477 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the LPS and PBS groups with threshold was set |LogFC| > 1 and adjust p values < 0.05 (Fig. 4A). Among the DEGs, Saa3, Ccl5, C3, Ms4a7, Lyz2, Lcn2, Cybb, Lyz1, Cd52, and Ch25h were identified as the top 10 up-regulated DEGs, whereas Myh3, Ret, and Otx2 were classified as down-regulated DEGs (Fig. 4B). The activation of microglia and astrocytes represents a hallmark of neuroinflammation, characterized by alterations in the number, size, and morphology of these cells33. We conducted cell type enrichment analysis of DEGs using the Single Cell Expression module of the Alzdata database34. The results showed that 27 up-regulated DEGs were primarily localized in astrocytes, and 128 up-regulated DEGs were mainly localized in microglia (Fig. 4C). Among these DEGs, Chi3l1, Fxyd1, Gfap, Phyhd1, Slc14a1 and Tep1 holds a potential to serve as a specific marker for the astrocytes, while Aif1 (encoding Iba1), C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, C3, Ccl2, Cd68, Ch25h, Cx3cr1, Fpr1, Hcls1, Igsf6, Il1b and Trem2 can be used as specific markers for microglia. In the PPI network constructed from 477 DEGs (414 nodes, 5628 edges), the top 20 DEGs ranked by degree value were Tnf, Il1b, Ptprc, Tlr2, Fcgr3, Tyrobp, Itgam, Ccl2, Cd68, Cxcl10, Csf1r, Adgre1, Icam1, Ccl5, Itgb2, Ctss, Cd44, Fcgr2b, Syk, and Fcer1g were identified as hub genes (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, all of the aforementioned 20 DEGs were involved in neuroinflammation, and their BPs were significantly enriched in inflammatory response (GO:0006954), cytokine production (GO:0001816), glial cell activation (GO:0061900) and neuroinflammatory response (GO:0150076). We further conducted GO biological process enrichment analysis to understand the functions of the 477 DEGs. As shown in Fig. 4E, GO BPs related to neuroinflammation were significantly enriched, such as innate immune response (GO:0045087), inflammatory response (GO:0006954), microglial cell activation (GO:0001774) and astrocyte activation (GO:0048143). In the PPI network of microglial cell activation, the hub genes Csf1r, Itgam, Tnf, Tyrobp and Tlr2 were involved (Fig. 4F). This suggests that intracerebral LPS injection significantly activates glial cells, thereby mediating neuroinflammation and depressive-like behaviors.

Among the 477 DEGs treated with LPS in the brain, 17 genes are shared targets with XYS, such as Ptprc, Hck, Cd14, Icam1, Cdk1, Pik3cg, Socs3, Casp8, Xdh, Il1b (Fig. 4G). The aforementioned 17 targets were significantly enriched in inflammatory response (GO:0006954) and response to tumor necrosis factor (GO:0034612). We further screened the DEGs directly interacting with the 17 common targets in Fig. 4G based on the PPI network (Fig. 4H). The 17 shared targets formed a PPI network with DEGs, comprising 284 nodes and 4633 edges, indicating that the 17 shared targets can directly interact with 267 DEGs. Moreover, XYS exerts its anti-depressant effects by directly acting on four hub genes, named Tnf, Il1b, Ptprc and Icam1. The above results indicate that XYS ameliorates LPS-induced depressive-like behaviors by directly or indirectly acting on multiple DEGs.

XYS ameliorated depressive-like behaviors in the LPS-challenged mice

We established an animal model of depression by intraperitoneal injection of LPS35,36 (Fig. 5A). After 2 weeks of XYS treatment (1342.9 mg/kg/day), the mice were subjected to a series of depression-like behavioral tests. Our results indicated that LPS administration significantly reduced the total distance traveled in the open field test (OFT), and this impairment was not alleviated by XYS treatment (Fig. 5B, C). The anxiety-like behavior was assessed in the elevated plus maze (EPM) and marble burying test (MBT) test. The ratio of time spent in the open arms (open arms time/closed arms time) in the LPS group was significantly lower compared to both the CON and XYS groups in EPM, whereas treatment with XYS led to a significant increase (Fig. 5D, E). In the MBT, oral administration of XYS resulted in a significant reduction in the number of buried marbles (11.45 ± 1.11) compared to LPS-treated mice (16.4 ± 0.45) (Fig. 5F). LPS exposure significantly increased the immobility time in the forced swimming test (FST) (63.4 ± 5.44 s), while XYS treatment significantly reduced the immobility time (41.91 ± 6.96 s) and improved behavioral despair in LPS-treated mice (Fig. 5G). On day 19, XYS treatment for 2 weeks significantly increased the proportion of LPS-induced sucrose consumption decline and ameliorated anhedonia in LPS mice (Fig. 5H).

Discussion

This study investigated the anti-depressant effects of XYS and its phytochemicals using network pharmacology, molecular docking, MD simulations, an LPS-induced depressive mouse model, and the BV2 microglial cell line. The results demonstrated that the primary mechanism of action for the anti-depressant effects of XYS targets neuroinflammation. In the phytochemicals of XYS, kaempferol, quercetin, atractylenolide II, z-ligustilide, paeoniflorin, saikosaponin A, atractylenolide III, GA, and liquiritin were identified as the main phytochemicals targeting neuroinflammation. This study enhances the understanding of the pharmacological basis and mechanism of action of XYS in the treatment of depression, while also providing a theoretical foundation for the clinical application of XYS and the development of anti-depressant drugs.

The rapidly developing field of network pharmacology is facing a series of problems and challenges46. The common pitfalls of network pharmacology were discussed in our previous studies11,22,47. A distinctive feature of this study is the implementation of multi-dimensional validation. We employed molecular docking to validate the optimal binding mode of direct interactions between XYS phytochemicals and their corresponding core targets, and further confirmed the binding stability through MD simulations. In addition, we also validated the anti-depressant effects of XYS at the animal level and the protective effects of GA against LPS-induced inflammatory responses in BV2 cells, along with its underlying mechanisms. This study focuses on the 16 blood compounds of XYS identified in plasma through the UPLC-MS/MS method29,30, rather than those predicted from public databases such as TCMSP. These 16 phytochemicals are considered to represent the pharmacokinetic characteristics of XYS, as determined through serum pharmacochemistry research and literature review. However, other phytochemicals in XYS with low bioavailability or undetectable levels may also play a role in the anti-depressant effects of XYS. This remains to be further studied in subsequent research. To advance the modernization of TCM, we have also conducted a systematic review of the pharmacological parameters and safety of the phytochemicals of XYS. The preclinical research of XYS has issues in the extraction methodology. Different researchers have adopted varying extraction times, frequencies, and solvents used, which affects both the composition and content of the XYS extracts. One of the advantages of this article is that XYS consists of commercially available Chinese herbal medicine prescription granules. Chinese herbal medicine prescription granules, processed using modern techniques, ensure the stability of medicinal ingredients and therapeutic efficacy.

There are various modeling methods for animal models of depression, such as stress-induced modeling, drug-induced modeling, and surgical modeling. Neuroinflammation is closely associated with the pathogenesis of depression, and anti-inflammatory drugs may be an effective anti-depressant therapy33,48. Clinically, the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α have been utilized as biomarkers for depression49,50. Microglial activation is one of the characteristics of neuroinflammation. Microglia are significantly activated in the brains of depressive suicide victims51. Activated microglia can release pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL1β, IL6, and TNF-α) and damage neurons. Knockout of IL1β in the hippocampus alleviated LPS-induced depressive-like behaviors in mice52. In the expression profiling microarray of MDD patients (GSE98793 dataset), GSEA also revealed significant enrichment of multiple neuroinflammation-related signaling pathways, such as inflammatory response, innate immune response, and cytokine production (Fig. 2A-C). Therefore, we opted to construct a depression mouse model using the effective inflammatory response activator LPS35,36,53,54, rather than employing methods such as CRS or CUMS. The peripheral or intracerebral administration of LPS has been used to induce neuroinflammation in animals, and both peripheral and central administration of LPS can induce depressive-like behaviors in animals26–28. In the mouse brain following intracerebral LPS challenge, microglia and astrocytes were significantly activated and notably enriched in various BPs of neuroinflammation (Fig. 4). Our results also demonstrated that peripheral administration of LPS significantly activated microglia in the PFC, while XYS treatment alleviated the activation of microglia and LPS-induced depressive-like behaviors. As shown in the results of Fig. 5, the behavioral outcomes of EPM, MBT, FST, and SPT support our conclusion. The reduction of LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels by GA was also validated in BV2 cells.

The neuronal loss in specific brain regions is one of the critical pathological bases of depression. The PFC plays a significant role in neuropsychiatric processes such as depression and anxiety disorders. The PFC has become one of the most severely affected regions in MDD, and as the patient’s symptoms gradually worsen, the PFC undergoes atrophy55. Moreover, postmortem studies of depressed patients have revealed reductions in neuronal volume, synaptic atrophy, and a decrease in the number of synapses in the PFC56. In the PFC and hippocampus of animals exposed to chronic stress, neuronal atrophy and loss were observed, which correlated with a reduction in the volume of these brain regions57. A study has shown that after drug-induced impairment of the morphology and function of pyramidal neurons in the PFC, mice exhibited depression- and anxiety-like behaviors58. Recent multiple studies have shown that alleviating neuroinflammation in the PFC improves depressive-like behaviors in mice25,59,60. The knockdown of colony stimulating factor 1 in PFC prevents CUMS-induced depressive-like behaviors, with the mechanism involving the interaction between microglia and neurons61. The activation of microglia and astrocytes in the PFC mediates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, ultimately resulting in neuronal loss and facilitating the onset of depression62. This study focuses on the neuroinflammation in the central functional brain region PFC to explore the key phytochemicals of XYS for its anti-depressant effects. Similar to our results, XYS treatment was effective in controlling microglial activation and pro-inflammatory responses in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and hippocampus, thereby ameliorating obesity-associated anxiety and depressive-like behaviors63.

This study identified a series of potential neuroinflammatory modulators, including kaempferol, quercetin, atractylenolide II, z-ligustilide, paeoniflorin, saikosaponin A, atractylenolide III and GA. GA is the active triterpenoid saponin component found in Radix Glycyrrhizae Preparata, a substance that serves both medicinal and edible substances. Molecular docking, MD simulations, and in vitro experiments all indicate that GA interacts with multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL6, 1L1β and Cox2. In CUMS-induced depressive mice, GA ameliorates depressive-like behaviors by inhibiting HMGB1 through modulation of the kynurenine pathway64. Another study indicates that GA treatment ameliorates CRS-induced anxiety-like behaviors through GLT1 and Per1/2-dependent pathways65. Preclinical studies have shown that GA possesses safety, economical, and anti-inflammatory properties, making it a potential adjunctive treatment for depression. Clinical studies have shown that the reduction of TNF-α mediates the association between GA treatment and clinical improvement, and it is beneficial for symptom improvement only in patients with high inflammation (baseline CRP > 3 mg/L)66. A recent study has demonstrated that GA mitigates neuroinflammatory damage caused by necrotizing enterocolitis by inhibiting the HMGB1/TLR4 pathway, protecting the integrity of the blood-brain barrier and reducing pyroptosis in BV2 cells41. The relationship between TCM compounds and their individual phytochemicals is not a simple superposition or substitution, but rather a synergistic or complementary system formed based on the complexity of disease mechanisms, the multi-target characteristics of drug actions, and clinical needs. Subsequent research needs to validate these through rigorous controlled experiments.

Intracerebral injection of LPS induces depressive-like behaviors accompanied by activation of the JNK pathway and increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, while inhibition of JNK alleviates depressive-like behaviors and reduces the levels of TNF-α and IL1β67. The pathological responses characterized by reduced social exploratory behavior and increased immobility time, induced by the central injection of TNF-α in mice, can be effectively mitigated through prior lateral ventricular injection of the JNK inhibitor DJNKI-168. This suggests that the JNK pathway may be a potential target for the treatment of inflammation-related depression. A study has shown that inhibiting the JNK pathway reduces the inflammatory cascade in LPS-activated BV2 cells45. This study also found that the anti-inflammatory effect of GA is associated with the JNK pathway. However, the anti-depressant effects of other phytochemicals in XYS that have neuroinflammatory regulatory properties require further investigation.

Conclusions

Neuroinflammation is one of the key mechanisms through which XYS exerts its anti-depressant effects. In XYS, a variety of phytochemicals serve as potential neuroinflammatory modulators, including kaempferol, quercetin, atractylenolide II, z-ligustilide, paeoniflorin, saikosaponin A, atractylenolide III, GA, and liquiritin. The phytochemicals of XYS primarily regulate the core targets TP53, AKT1, TNF, IL6, BCL2, IL1B, ESR1, HSP90AA1, CASP3, MYC, JUN, PTGS2, PPARG, NFKB1, MTOR, CYCS, CCND1, GSK3B and IL10, modulating neuroinflammation to mediate the treatment of depression. The findings of this study hold significant implications for the clinical application of XYS and the development of anti-depressant drugs targeting neuroinflammation.

Materials and methods

Drugs and antibodies

XYS was obtained from the Affiliated Hospital of Gansu University of Chinese Medicine, and the Chinese herbal medicine prescription granules it contains were acquired from Guangdong Yifang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. LPS (≥ 500000 EU/mg, # BS904-10 mg) was from Biosharp (Beijing, China). Glycyrrhizic acid (purity = 99.84%, CAS #: 1405-86-3) was procured from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China) and dissolved in DMSO. Toluidine blue (CAS #: 6586-04-5) was purchased from Macklin (Macklin, China). The primary antibodies specific for Iba1 (# 10904-1-AP), β-actin (# 66009-1-lg), cyclooxygenase 2 (Cox2, # 27308-1-AP), IL-1β (# 26048-1-AP), IL6 (# 26404-1-AP) and TNF-α (# 29652-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). JNK (# AF6318) and p-JNK (Thr183 + Tyr185, # AF3318) were purchased from Affinity Biosciences (Jiangsu, China). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-rabbit/mouse (# A21020/A21010) IgG was used as secondary antibody and purchased from Abbkine (Wuhan, China).

Collect the main phytochemicals of XYS and their potential targets

This study focuses on the 16 blood compounds of XYS in plasma identified through the UPLC-MS/MS method29,30. Each compound’s SMILES was downloaded from the PubChem database. The potential targets of XYS phytochemicals were collected from the HERB database (http://herb.ac.cn/)69 and the Swisstargetprediction server (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/)70. It should be noted that XYS phytochemicals are preferentially retrieved from the HERB database, while those phytochemicals such as atractylenolide I, atractylenolide II, atractylenolide III and Dl-3-n-butylphthalide that cannot be retrieved from the HERB database are predicted through the Swisstargetprediction server. We further utilized WebCSEA (https://bioinfo.uth.edu/webcsea/)71 to classify the potential targets of XYS by organ systems and cell types.

Evaluate the Pharmacological and toxicological parameters of the phytochemicals in XYS

Compounds that comply with Lipinski rule exhibit better oral bioavailability, those that adhere to Pfizer rule are generally non-toxic, and compounds conforming to GSK rule may possess more favorable ADMET profile. The aforementioned three rules of XYS phytochemicals were evaluated using the ADMETlab 3.0 platform (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/)31. We calculated the toxicological parameters of XYS phytochemicals using the Protox II webserver (https://tox-new.charite.de/protox_II/)72. According to the toxicity rules11,32, if the acute toxicity is greater than grade 3 and the number of positive toxicity endpoints is fewer than 3, it indicates non-toxicity.

GSEA

Human brain tissue samples are extremely scarce and are generally obtained from post-mortem autopsy samples and invasive biopsy samples. Compared to brain tissue samples, the collection of peripheral blood is a minimally invasive procedure with no significant health risks to the patient. The GSE98793 dataset73, comprising whole blood samples from 64 healthy controls and 128 samples from individuals with MDD, was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Depression is not merely a central nervous system disorder, but a systemic disease characterized by the dysregulation of the central-peripheral system interaction. The changes in the peripheral blood transcriptome are not isolated events, but rather exhibit clear molecular associations with central pathological mechanisms. GSEA was performed to identify MDD-related gene enrichment using the ClusterProfiler R package (version 3.12.0), with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. For the GSEA results, both the NES and p value were reported.

Analysis of gene expression profiles in the mouse brain following intracerebral LPS challenge

The gene expression dataset GSE12281574 (specie: Mus musculus) was downloaded from the GEO database for analysis. These data were obtained from the GPL11180 platform (Affymetrix HT MG-430 PM Array Plate), which includes hemisphere brain samples after 4 days of intrastriatal injection of LPS or PBS (n = 3, three biological replicate samples were combined to form a single sample). DEGs were identified using the BioConductor Limma package with cut-offs of adjusted p-value < 0.05 and fold change > 2. We further analyzed the brain cell localization of DEGs using the “Single Cell Expression” module of the AlzData database (http://www.alzdata.org/)34, including neurons, astrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells, and oligodendrocytes. We have paid special attention to the DEGs specifically localized in astrocytes and microglia. We further utilized Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html) to identify the common genes between XYS and DEGs.

GO enrichment analysis for biological process (BP)

GO enrichment analysis for BP was obtained using the ClusterProfiler R package75, with a corrected p < 0.05 as the threshold for significance. The ratio of the number of genes to the total number of genes annotated in this term is referred to as the rich factor. We have primarily focused on the BPs related to neuroinflammation or apoptotic signaling pathway. Bubble plot presentation of BP enrichment analysis was generated using a web-based tool (www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

Construction of PPI network and screening of core targets

The PPI networks of XYS targets were built using STRING version 12.0 (https://cn.string-db.org/) with a confidence score threshold of 0.4, and visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.3. The analysis was limited to the organism Homo sapiens. The PPI network comprises nodes, which represent a target protein, and edges, which represent protein-protein interactions. The thickness of the edges represents the combined score. Degree represents the number of other nodes directly connected to a node. A higher degree value indicates greater significance of the node within the network. The Cytoscape plugin, Network Analysis plugin, was employed to determine the degree values22. In this study, the top 20 target proteins were selected and identified as core targets based on their degree rankings. We further utilized Venny 2.1 to identify the core targets involved in multiple neuroinflammatory BPs.

Molecular Docking simulations and MD simulations

The molecular docking procedure LeDock (http://www.lephar.com/software.htm)76 was used for the automated molecular docking simulations. Briefly, the three-dimensional structures of target proteins were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB database, http://www.rcsb.org/)77, while the three-dimensional SDF format files of XYS phytochemicals were retrieved from the PubChem database. The LePro tool was utilized to process receptor files, including hydrogenation and the acquisition of the binding pocket. The docking score (in kcal/mol) was calculated using the default scoring function. The interaction residues and hydrogen bond lengths between the ligand and receptor were visualized using LigPlot (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/software/LigPlus/)78. Generally speaking, docking scores < −7 kcal/mol have strong binding affinity, and − 4 < docking scores ≤ −7 kcal/mol have moderate interactions79,80. Ultimately, the optimal docking conformation, characterized by the lowest docking score, was selected for subsequent MD simulation analysis. The MD simulations were carried out with the GROMACS 2024.1 package. The ligand parameters were obtained from the General Amber Force Field (GAFF2), and the charges were derived using the AM1-BCC method. AMBER ff14sb_OL15 protein force field together with the TIP3P water model was used. The production MD simulations were run at 300 K for 100 ns with a time step of 2 fs under constant volume and temperature. The stability of the complex structure was evaluated using the RMSD.

Animals and treatments

The 8-week-old male C57/BL/6J mice (22–24 g, SPF grade, SCXK(Su)2020-0009, n = 43) were purchased from Huachuang Sino Pharmaceutical Technology Company (Jiangsu, China), and the animal certification number was B202407030457. All mice were housed and maintained in the Medicine Animal Center of University of South China (the use license number is SYXK(Xiang)2020-0002), and the experimental procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of University of South China (No. 2023-36).

The XYS, which is derived from the Chinese herbal medicine prescription granules, were freshly dissolved in boiling water and stirred thoroughly before application. The recommended dosage of XYS for adult human in clinical applications is 147.57 mg/kg/day. Based on the mouse equivalent dose being 9.1 times that of humans81,82, the gavage dose of XYS is 1342.9 mg/kg/day. Mice were first randomly divided into two groups, with the XYS group (n = 23, 1342.9 mg/kg/day) receiving XYS via oral gavage and the CON group (n = 20) receiving an equivalent volume of water, for a duration of two weeks. The LPS-induced mouse model is a typical inflammatory animal model of depression53. During the last 3 days of the gavage treatment, LPS (0.83 mg/kg/day)35,36,54 or an equivalent volume of normal saline was intraperitoneally injected while continuing the gavage. Starting from day 12, the mice were divided into four groups: CON group (n = 10), XYS group (n = 12), LPS group (n = 10), and XYS + LPS group (n = 11). Twenty-four hours after the last LPS injection, all mice underwent depression-related behavioral tests. Detailed experimental procedures provided in Fig. 5A. Mice were sacrificed to collect brain and liver samples for further analysis. Isoflurane was used for mouse anesthesia, and Pentobarbital was used for mouse euthanasia.

Behavioral tests

OFT

All mice were individually tested in an open-field apparatus consisting of a white square base (45 × 45 cm) with white walls (45 cm in height). A single mouse was carefully placed in the center of the chamber, and all spontaneous activities were recorded for 5 min using a video-computerized tracking system. Immediately following each experiment, the boxes were sanitized with 75% alcohol. The total distance traveled in the OFT was used as an index of locomotor activity and depression/anxiety-like behavior.

EPM

The EPM experiment was conducted using a plus-maze apparatus that comprised two open arms, two closed arms, and a central platform. Both the open and closed arms measured 50 × 10 cm, with the closed arms featuring baffles on either side. Mice were placed individually in the central area, 50 cm above the ground, and their movements were recorded for 5 min. Following each experiment, the enclosures were promptly cleaned using 75% alcohol. Locomotion was monitored and tracked through an automated system. The durations spent in the closed arms, open arms, and central platform were documented.

MBT

We assessed anxiety-related behaviors in mice through MBT. To conduct the test, prepare a cage with dimensions of 40 × 25 × 20 cm, which should be lined with 5 cm of corn cob bedding. Arrange 20 glass marbles, each with a diameter of 2.5 cm, in a “4 × 5” configuration within the cage. Introduce a single mouse into the cage and record the number of marbles buried by the mouse within a 10 min observation period. A marble is identified buried when at least two-thirds of its surface is covered by the corn cob bedding.

FST

We used the immobility time in the FST to assess the despair behavior of the animals. The mice were placed individually in a Plexiglas cylinder (50 cm in height × 30 cm in diameter) filled with 30 cm water (25 °C). Immobility was defined as no movement at all or only minor movements necessary to keep the nose above the water versus mobility, which was defined by swimming and struggling behaviors. Each test session lasted for 6 min, with the final 4 min scored for immobility.

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

As a classic behavioral experiment for assessing anhedonia, SPT comprises two phases: training and testing. During the training phase, without altering the normal diet of the mice, an additional 1.5% sucrose solution was provided at irregular intervals 2–3 days before the SPT. In the testing phase, the mice were deprived of water and food for 24 h, and then both sugar water and normal drinking water were offered, but no food was provided. The consumption of sugar water over 24 h was recorded. Sucrose preference was calculated using the following formula: sucrose preference (%) = sucrose consumption/(water consumption + sucrose consumption) × 100%.

Measurement of liver function

Serum was obtained from the blood samples by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min and stored at 80 °C. Serum AST (# E-BC-K236-M) and ALT (# E-BC-K235-M) levels were tested by using corresponding assay kits from Elabscience Biotech (Wuhan, China). The detection was performed strictly according to the kit instructions.

HE staining

We employed HE staining to assess histopathological damage in liver tissues. The liver tissues from both the control group and the XYS group were excised post-surgery and subsequently rinsed with pre-cooled PBS. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China) for 24 h, routine paraffin embedding and section were performed. Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit (Biosharp, China) was utilized for HE staining. All morphological images in this study were taken using an Olympus optical microscope (Olympus BX60, Tokyo, Japan).

IHC staining and Nissl staining

Microglia in the PFC were visualized using Iba1 (# 10904-1-AP, 1:200) IHC staining. The IHC staining procedure was conducted in accordance with our previous studies83,84. The IHC universal type two-step detection kit (PV-9000) and diaminobenzidine (DAB) coloration (ZLI-9018) were obtained from Zhongshan Jinqiao (Beijing, China). Nissl staining of a 30 μm coronal section with toluidine blue solution (0.1% toluidine blue in 0.1% sodium carbonate, pH = 11.1) was used. All images were captured by an Olympus optical microscope (Olympus BX60, Tokyo, Japan). Microglia in a healthy brain are in a relatively round body, longer, thinner and resting state (resting cells). In the states initially activated after brain injury, they rapidly proliferate, cell bodies enlarge and processes retract or become thicker. Therefore, this study selected the perimeter and area of the cell bodies to reflect the activation of microglia85. The analysis of the perimeter and area of microglial cell bodies, along with the quantification of Nissl-stained positive neurons in the PFC, was conducted using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/).

Western blotting

The protein concentrations of the PFC tissues or cell samples were measured using a bicinchoninic acid kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Western blotting was conducted as previously described83,84. The PVDF membranes (0.45 μm) were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Signals were visualized with an enhanced chemiluminescence imaging system (SCG-W2000, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). ImageJ software was used to analyze the results of the Western blotting.

Cell culture and CCK8 assay

The immortalized mouse BV2 microglial cell line, exhibiting morphological and molecular characteristics similar to primary microglial, was purchased from the Boster Biological Technology (product number: XBS0421-0017, Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin at 37 ℃ a humidified incubator under a 95%/5% (v/v) mixture of air and CO2. We established an in vitro cell model of neuroinflammation by treating BV2 cells with LPS (1 µg/ml) for 24 h42,43.

Cell viability was assessed indirectly using a CCK-8 assay. BV2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a suitable density. GA is a phytochemical found in Radix Glycyrrhizae Praeparata, classified by the FDA as Generally Recognized As Safe as a food additive. Various concentrations of GA (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 µM) were applied to treat cells for 24 h. After 24 h exposure, the medium was replaced with 100 µL of fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent (# C0038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 60 min at 37 °C. Optical densities of CCK8 were measured by a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA) at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as the means ± standard error of mean (SEM). The differences between groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA analysis and Tukey’s multiple comparison using SPSS 19.0 statistical software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Data visualization was performed using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). The significance level was defined as p < 0.05.
