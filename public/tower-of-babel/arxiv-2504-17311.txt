FLUKE : A Linguistically-Driven and Task-Agnostic Framework for Robustness Evaluation
Yulia Otmakhova, Hung Thinh Truong, Rahmad Mahendra, Zenan Zhai, Rongxin Zhu Daniel Beck Jey Han Lau

Abstract
We present FLUKE ( f ramework for l ing u istically-driven and tas k -agnostic robustness e valuation), a task-agnostic framework for assessing model robustness through systematic minimal variations of test data. FLUKE introduces controlled variations across linguistic levels — from orthography to dialect and style varieties — and leverages large language models (LLMs) with human validation to generate modifications. We demonstrate FLUKE ’s utility by evaluating both fine-tuned models and LLMs across four diverse NLP tasks, and reveal that (1) the impact of linguistic variations is highly task-dependent, with some tests being critical for certain tasks but irrelevant for others; (2) while LLMs have better overall robustness compared to fine-tuned models, they still exhibit significant brittleness to certain linguistic variations; (3) all models show substantial vulnerability to negation modifications across most tasks. These findings highlight the importance of systematic robustness testing for understanding model behaviors.

•

We demonstrate the utility of

•

Related Work

Task-based Benchmarking

Much of previous work in evaluating models is centered around collating
datasets that are representative of a range of typical NLP
applications
(
Wang et al. 2018
;
Song et al. 2023
)
. The datasets
in these benchmarks usually follow the typical paradigm
of having an in-domain test set, coming from the same distribution as
the training data. While this is useful in many experimental
settings (such as model comparison), it is restricted to
reporting in-domain performance.
The importance of evaluation in out-of-domain scenarios is well
known. Many shared tasks in NLP include test sets that are unrelated
to the training data
(
Pei et al. 2023
;
Ousidhoum et al. 2024
,
inter alia)
. The development of so-called “challenge sets”,
initially explored in machine translation
(
Goyal et al. 2022
)
but now
extended to LLMs
(
Zhong et al. 2024
)
also falls into this space. However, the range of domain differences is
massive, which explains why it is hard to create
“general”, collated benchmarks for out-of-domain scenarios.

Linguistic Competence Probing

Another way to assess
models is through linguistic competence. A common approach is to use

probing classifiers
, tuned on datasets
tailored to specific competences, such as morphology or syntax

(
Blevins et al. 2023
;
Mahowald et al. 2024
)
.
Holmes
(
Waldis et al. 2024
)
attempt to unify multiple datasets created to test
linguistic competences. While probing for linguistic competence can provide insights about the model’s understanding of specific
linguistic phenomena, it is important to note that model competence does not necessarily translate to task performance.

Robustness Evaluation

One line of work
explores the idea of
minimal contrastive
pairs

(
Warstadt et al. 2020
;
Kann et al. 2019
;
Marvin & Linzen 2018
;
Warstadt et al. 2019
;
Sennrich 2017
;
Linzen et al. 2016
)
,
to evaluate a model’s robustness to a specific
phenomena in the context of a specific task (e.g. subject-verb agreement in syntax

(
Warstadt et al. 2020
)
or novel words in machine translation

(
Sennrich 2017
)
).
Another line focuses on creating
adversarial examples
to understand model failures
(
Eger & Benz 2020
;
Keller et al. 2021
;
Tan et al. 2020
;
Formento et al. 2023
)
.
These studies use model loss or gradient to learn how to create perturbations of test data that would break the model.

Ribeiro et al. 2020
introduce the Checklist framework to test linguistic capabilities, but: (1) it is not fully
task-agnostic, as it requires developers to design their own
suite of tests (which, as we show below, may not fully reveal model brittleness); and (2) it has three different types of tests — minimum
functionality, directional and invariance tests — and the first one
requires developers to construct instances from scratch which can be
difficult to scale. To address these limitations, we propose a comprehensive suite of
linguistic modifications together with large language model prompts
that are ready to be applied to any tasks.

3.1
Linguistic tests

We design linguistic tests for all levels of language structure, starting from the low-level orthography
7
7

7

This is a proxy for the phonology level in linguistics, as we are dealing with written text only.
tests and ending with dialect and style modifications.

Orthography

Here we test the ability of models to deal with slight, surface-level changes. In particular,
spelling
modifications include
addition
or
omission
of a letter, as well as
swapping
two letters.
Capitalization
tests imply modifying the register to
lower case
,
Upper case
,
sPoNgEcAsE
(alternating caps) or
ALL CAPS
. Lastly, for
punctuation
modifications, we either
add
or
change
a punctuation mark, or
remove the space
between two words, gluing them together.

Morphology

We test if words that are more complex morphologically are harder for models. In
derivation
modification, we change a non-derived word (a word without any suffixes or prefixes)
into a derived word. For
compound
modifications, we replace a word which has only one root with a word that has several stems.

Syntax

Syntax modifications test if a model can adjust to changing the sentence structure. In particular, we change a verb in
active form to passive voice
(the subject and object are flipped accordingly to preserve the meaning). For
grammatical role
modifications, we leave the verb intact, but swap two entities. For
conjunctions
modification we change a word into multiple words combined with a coordinating conjunction
and
.

Semantics

We devise tests that involve different degrees of meaning change. First, in
concept
modifications, we replace words with
synonyms
, with
hypernyms
or
hyponyms
(semantically close words that have a broader or narrower meaning), with artificially created, non-existing
nonce words
8
8

8

We use a list of nonce words from
Cremers 2022
.
, and with
idioms
. We also use
negation
to test for the change of meaning that goes beyond individual words. Apart from
verbal
negation (adding
not
to the verb), we consider less straightforward types of negation:
approximate
, where the meaning is only partially negated, and
absolute
, which explicitly excludes non-negative interpretation, as well as
lexical
negation (affixal negation and antonymy)
(
Pullum & Huddleston 2002
)
. Finally, we look at
double
negation, i.e. linguistically valid structures which contain two negation elements and typically negate only some aspect of meaning
(
Truong et al. 2022
)
.

Discourse

Here, we look at sensitivity to manipulating
discourse markers
: we
add
a discourse marker within a sentence, explicitly connecting ideas,
change
a discourse marker, which sometimes leads to a different interpretation, or
delete
a discourse marker, making the sentence more difficult to interpret.
We also test the sensitivity to adding markers of
appraisal
– sentiment-bearing words, phrases, or clauses.

Language Varieties

In these tests the changes are more extensive and span across all linguistic aspects. Specifically, we change the
style
to highly
casual
English, and rewrite the sentence in a particular
dialect:
Singaporean creole English (
Singlish
), which
has distinct and systematic linguistic differences from American or British English varieties used in the original benchmarks.

3.2
Bias tests

In addition to linguistic tests, we include tests for data artifacts that PLMs and LLMs are known to exploit. We test if changing words to their old-fashioned variants affects the performance (
temporal bias
). When testing for
geographical bias

(
Faisal & Anastasopoulos 2023
;
Godey et al. 2024
)
, we use a list of regions that are likely to be underrepresented in training corpora.
9
9

9

Africa, Middle East, Southeast, East and Central Asia, Oceania, Latin America, Eastern Europe
Then we change proper nouns to names and locations specific to one of such regions, and replace common nouns with relevant cultural entities. Finally, in
length bias
tests we either
shorten
or
lengthen
a sentence, while preserving its meaning.

4.1
Test set creation and validation

We apply
FLUKE
to modify instances for four tasks: dialogue contradiction, coreference resolution, named entity recognition (NER) (
Sections

4.3
,
4.4
and
4.5
), and sentiment analysis (which we discuss in
Section

A.4
due to length limitations). Instances are randomly selected from the
test partition
of the task’s dataset, and the modifications are created by prompting GPT-4o with instructions. We first create and evaluate a general prompt template for each linguistic test (and subtests), and then further revise it for each task (see
Section

A.1
for an example).
10
10

10

Prompt templates for each task and their tests are in:
https://github.com/joey234/fluke/data_generation/

Most of the modifications described in
Section

3
were applied
minimally
, i.e. only once per sample.

The generated modifications may be incorrect. For example, a resulting sentence may not have the required modification, or be incoherent. Moreover, the ground-truth label for the task may change after modifications (e.g. after applying negation the sentiment can change from positive to negative). To address this, we introduce two validation experiments: the first one to check if a modification targets the intended linguistic test and produces a sensible output, and the second to re-classify the modified instances.
We ran this validation on Prolific
11
11

11

https://www.prolific.com/
, with thorough quality control and four annotators per sample (see
Section

A.5
). We produce around 100 modified instances for each test in each task.
12
12

12

The full dataset:
https://github.com/joey234/fluke/data/modified_data/

4.2
Tested models and evaluation

We experiment with three smaller PLMs (BERT, GPT-2, and T5) and three LLMs (GPT-4o, Llama 3.1 and Claude 3.5).
The pretrained models are fine-tuned on the training set with an additional task-specific classification layer on top (
Section

A.3
); while the LLMs are prompted with instructions (zero-shot) to solve the tasks (
Table

3
).

To ensure a fair comparison of performance between the original and modified set, for each linguistic test we evaluate only the subset of the original samples that corresponds to the modified instances. To evaluate performance, we compute the relative difference between the original set vs. the modified set, weighted by the (log) performance on the original set, to put more emphasis on the change in high-performing models. This comes from the intuition that a drop in high-performing models (e.g. 90%
\rightarrow
80%) shows a more serious effect than a drop in low-performing models (e.g. 50%
\rightarrow
40%). Formally:

Weighted\Delta=(B-A)\times\frac{log(A)}{log(100),}

(1)

where
A
and
B
is the performance of a model on the original and the modified set respectively,
1/log(100)
is added to scale the output to 0-100 range for interpretability. A negative value means the model performs worse on the modified set, and a large difference means the model is not robust to a specific variation. Note that the performance metric depends on the task and follows the method adopted for the original dataset: we use accuracy for sentiment analysis, dialogue contradiction and coreference resolution; and entity-level mean F1
(
Nakayama 2018
)
for NER. But since we report the gain or drop in performance rather than absolute values, the results are comparable between tasks.

Use Case: Dialogue Contradiction Detection

We use the DECODE dataset
(
Nie et al. 2021
)
to evaluate dialogue understanding capabilities. Each sample is a multi-turn dialogue, where the final turn may be consistent with (0) or contradict (1) the preceding context.

As
Table

2
shows, LLMs are overall more robust to modifications than PLMs; however, T5 is a clear exception and performs similarly to LLMs. In particular, BERT and GPT2 demonstrate

) significant in Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Colour indicates the magnitude of change (red
=
performance drop; green
=
performance gain.

Some of the results are more sporadic, with GPT2 failing to recognize a consistent continuation after replacing a word with a related
concept
, GPT4o struggling with
derived
words and Llama not detecting
appraisal
marker inconsistent with the context. Interestingly, the performance of BERT improves after
discourse markers
(and thus relations between parts of text) are explicitly spelled out, which shows its poor ability to infer them.

4.4
Use Case: Coreference Resolution

We chose the KnowRef dataset
(
Emami et al. 2019
)
, which consists of pronoun disambiguation problems where each sample has the Winograd Schema Challenge
(
Levesque et al. 2012
)
format, consisting of a sentence, the pronoun we want to disambiguate, two candidates, and the index (0 or 1) of the correct candidate as label.

Surprisingly, LLMs do not outperform PLMs on this task, showing drops in performance across two tests (see
Table

2
):
negation
test in GPT-4o and Llama, and
dialect
test for Claude and Llama (for example, in
Ken try to keng siam the boss cos he finding people who late lor
, they fail to detect that
he
refers to the boss due to not understanding the dialectal
keng siam
, “sneak past”). Interestingly, Llama does better after changing
capitalization
, but this is due to cases where the correct entity happened to be be capitalized (as in
The
Humans
were afraid of the robots because they were weak
), which shows that it tends to rely on superficial cues to determine the antecedent.
Changes in PLMs performance are more sporadic, with BERT sensitive to change in
style
, T5 showing a minor drop on the
concept
test, and GPT2 making mistakes after addition of an
appraisal
word or phrase.

4.5
Use Case: Named Entity Recognition

In contrast to the previous three tasks, which are instance-level classification problems, named-entity recognition (NER) is a token-level classification problem.
We chose Few-NERD
(
Ding et al. 2021
)
,
a NER dataset of Wikipedia sentences, using its 8 first-level entity categories, i.e.,
Person, Location, Organization, Art, Building, Product, Event, Misc
.

The most significant drops were due to
Orthography
modifications. In particular, almost all models lack robustness to
capitalization
, heavily relying on capital letters as a cue for named entity presence or type. For example,
animals
is misunderstood as an entity after changing its register in
The Cnidaria are a group of ANIMALS…
, while
Amrinder
fails to get recognized as a name when it is lower-cased (
Pune lost… after amrinder came on
). This shows that named entities are identified relying on superficial cues rather than on the relations between them and the context. Similarly, all models often fail to identify a named entity after it is glued together (
punctuation
test), as in
DivineStyler
instead of
Divine Styler
.
Both PLMs and LLMs are brittle to seemingly innocuous changes of context. In particular, BERT, Claude and GPT4o demonstrate
temporal bias
, while T5 is affected by change in
style
. Similarly, T5 and Claude have performance drops when an
appraisal
marker such as
miraculously
is added to the context. Interestingly, both PLMs and LLMs struggle with dialectal modifications, misinterpreting words and phrases that are unusual for British and American English as named entities, for example
lah
as ORGANIZATION, or
say a bit only
as ART.

Two surprising observations from this task is that negation modifications do not lead to significant performance drops, and that the performance of GPT4o improves after some modifications. We discuss them in more detail in
Sections

5.3
and
5.4
below.

5
Discussion

5.1
Model brittleness is task-specific

We call our framework task-agnostic; this, however, does not mean that all tests are meaningful for all the tasks. As the results above show, the tests that actually “fired”, or showed substantial drops or increases, were different for each task. Even test such as
negation
which is known to be difficult for models
(
Ravichander et al. 2022
;
Truong et al. 2022
)
has almost no effect on the NER task.
On the other hand, seemingly “simple” tests (
capitalization
and
punctuation
) that have a negligible effect on most of the tasks led to some dramatic drops on the NER task, even for LLMs.
Thus, our framework is task-agnostic because it can be applied to reveal lack of robustness on a new task without having a preconceived (and potentially wrong) notion of what tests are meaningful for it.

5.2
LLMs are not always more robust than PLMs

Overall, PLMs see a larger drop in performance compared to LLMs, suggesting they are less robust to modifications (see the bottom
Avg
line in
Table

8
in
Section

A.6
). This is expected, as modifications are likely to change the data distribution and break the identical distribution assumption that the fine-tuning process is based on. That said, LLMs are very much still affected by these modifications, and even have a worse performance in some tests than PLMs (e.g.
negation
and
dialect
in Coreference resolution,
temporal bias
in NER).

Averaged across all tasks and tests, Claude (among LLMs) and T5 (among PLMs) are most robust (bottom line (
Average
) in
Table

8
). Among tests,
negation
stands out as most difficult for both PLMs and LLMs (the right-most column
Average
in
Table

8
), followed by
style
and
dialect
modifications, as well as seemingly minimal changes in
capitalization
and
grammar
.

5.3
Negation is difficult, but its type matters

Aside from NER, all other tasks (and especially sequence classification) are affected by negation (see
Table

9
in
Section

A.6
).
In particular, in the sentiment analysis task models tend to incorrectly predict sentences with
double negation
(
it’s not a … work without considerable force and truth
) as negative, and ignore the polarity switching effects of
approximate
and
absolute
negation adverbs such as “rarely”, “never” etc. For dialogue contradiction, we observe consistent drops in performance for
all negation types
, including
lexical negation
, which does not have the same impact on other tasks. This is not surprising as a slight change in meaning can make the whole dialog incoherent.
In coreference resolution, however, negation has less impact. Errors tend to occur when
verbal negation
is added to samples that require commonsense knowledge to resolve. For example, in
Rice beat Texas, even though they were the best team
,
it
refers to
Texas
, but in the negated text
Rice did not beat Texas, even though they were the best team
,
it
then refers to
Rice
. Negation does not affect NER at all since the span-level concepts stay the same despite the change of overall meaning.

5.4
Creating tests with LLMs works, but should be used with caution

Though it is a common practice to use LLMs to generate data, it can be questioned how well they can perform the task and if the created modifications are not “too easy”, especially when testing the same model that was used to modify samples. To answer the first question, we examine how many of the modifications performed by GPT4o were considered to be correct by the majority of human annotators. We find that for 10 out of 14 tests over 70% of generated modifications were found correct (=the necessary modification was applied) and fluent (=the modified text was still coherent) (see column
Retain rate
in
Table

6
in
Section

A.5
). Among the tests that GPT4o struggled to create (with a retain rate of 50 to 63%) were
derivation
,
compounds
,
grammatical roles
, and
discourse markers
, which all require more linguistic knowledge. When applying our framework, these tests probably would require human checks; however, we believe the other modifications (especially those that reached over 80% retain rate) can be applied with minimal human supervision.

Despite being generated, the modifications were able to reveal performance instabilities in other models, including those with a comparable performance such as Claude, which shows their usability for testing other models. Some caution, however, should be applied when using them to test the same model: while the results for GPT4o for most of the tasks were in line with those of other models, for the NER, GPT4o shows a clearly different trend, its performance often improving significantly after modifications. This can be explained by the fact that when the context the entities appear in — but not the entities themselves — was modified by GPT4o, it became easier for the model to detect the entities as something “alien” to the context. This effect, however, does not occur when the model needs to consider the whole modified sample rather than single out its parts, as in the other three tasks.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we present
FLUKE
, a task-agnostic, linguistically-driven framework that introduces minimal modifications to existing datasets to create a benchmark for assessing robustness. We automate these
modifications by prompting GPT-4o, followed by crowdsourced validation to ensure data quality. To showcase the generalizability of this framework, we apply it to create modified instances across four tasks, ranging from classification to structured prediction, and evaluate the performance of both smaller fine-tuned models and large language models. Our findings reveal that: (1) the impact of modifications is highly task-dependent, with some tests being critical for certain tasks but irrelevant for others; (2) while LLMs demonstrate better overall robustness compared to PLMs, they still exhibit significant brittleness to certain linguistic variations; and (3) all models show substantial vulnerability to negation modifications across most tasks, but the affected subtype of negation differs.
We hope that
FLUKE
provides an alternative approach for evaluating models, and that model developers would consider integrating these results
into model cards
(
Mitchell et al. 2019
)
when releasing models.

Ethics statement

The proposed
FLUKE
framework involves the use of GPT-4o to synthesize data automatically, and human annotation for quality control.

Potential biases of generated data

Previous works have pointed out potential biases in data generated by LLMs, stemming from the lack of its diversity
(
Ding et al. 2024
)
. In our work, such biases are most likely to occur in the geographical bias modification. To ensure a diverse representation, we compile a list of underrepresented regions as discussed in
Section

3.2
, and explicitly specify a region from this list in the prompt to generate samples across the variety of underrepresented geographical locations.

Human annotation

The human validation experiments has been approved by the ethics board (application ID: anonymised) and compensated at the level above standard. Please refer to
Section

A.5
for the details of the annotation process and how we compensate annotators.

A.5
Annotation process and instructions

We perform a two-staged validation of modified samples using a crowdsource annotation platform Prolific
13
13

13

https://www.prolific.com/
. To ensure high quality of annotations, we use a high per hour rate of 12 euro (overall cost: 7233 euro), choose only annotators from English-speaking countries (except for the
Dialect
test where we only used annotators from Singapore), and with a minimum success rate of 90%, as well as perform quality checks as described below.

During Stage 1, the annotators received instructions that described an intended modification (
Voice, Negation, Style
etc., see
Section

3
) and were asked if the sample they see is modified correctly according to these instructions (see Figure
1
for an example of annotation interface).
For quality control, we include four control questions, which were randomly sampled from a different modification.
We remove annotators who fail to answer more than one of such questions (i.e. selecting “Correct” twice), or select one option too frequently (i.e. selecting “Incorrect” for all questions). Each of the samples is annotated by four annotators, and receives at least two annotations after the quality checks.
We filter our samples which were not deemed to be “Correct” by the majority (over 50%) of annotators.
In case of tie, we select the answer of the annotator with better performance on the control questions.
The inter-annotator agreement in terms of Krippendorf’s
\alpha

(
Krippendorff
)
, averaged majority-class agreement (percentage of samples where the majority of annotators chose the same label), and the retain rate (percentage of samples that pass phase 1 annotation) are shown in Table
6
. We choose Krippendorf’s
\alpha
as an agreement metrics to accommodate for the difference in number of annotations per sample. We note, however, that it is not directly applicable here as it assumes that all annotations are provided by a fixed, ordered set of observers and factors in the consistency of their decisions, i.e. measures the annotators behaviour agreement rather than agreement of labels. Thus, in our case the Majority rate agreement reveals more about the patterns of annotation agreement, and achieves substantially high levels.

During Stage 2, the annotators were given instructions for one of the four tasks (
Sentiment Analysis, Dialogue Contradiction Detection, Coreference Resolution, Named Entity Recognition
, see
Sections

A.4
,
4.3
,
4.4
and
4.5
) and asked to assign a label specific for that task (for example, judge a modified sample as having
positive
or
negative
sentiment for the
Sentiment Analysis
task, see Figure
2
).
For quality checks, we used unmodified samples from the training data of the task (i.e. samples for which we knew the label); and apply the same filtering criteria in Stage 1. The final label was also selected based on the majority vote. We compared the resulting labels for modified samples with the labels for the original data to check if any inconsistencies were due to the genuine label change resulting from the modification, rather than to annotators disagreement. The inter-annotator agreement results (measured similarly to Stage 1) are presented in Table
