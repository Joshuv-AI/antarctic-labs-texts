Prevalence of malaria and diagnostic performance of malaria rapid diagnostic tests compared to microscopy among febrile patients at Pawe General Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Fikadu Legesse, Nega Berhane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major public health problem in Ethiopia, particularly in endemic areas such as Pawe district. Accurate and timely diagnosis is essential for effective case management and control. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of malaria, evaluate the diagnostic performance of malaria rapid diagnostic tests (mRDTs) against light microscopy, and assess associations between socio-demographic factors and malaria infection among febrile patients at Pawe General Hospital.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from November to December 2022 among 422 febrile patients suspected of malaria. Socio-demographic data were collected using a structured questionnaire. Capillary blood samples were examined by Giemsa-stained thick and thin smears (microscopy) and Bioline™ Malaria Ag Pf/Pv mRDTs. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26; logistic regression assessed associations between socio-demographic variables and malaria infection. Diagnostic performance indices of mRDT (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value) and Cohen’s kappa were calculated using microscopy as the reference standard.ResultsThe overall prevalence of malaria by microscopy was 27.0% (114/422). Plasmodium falciparum accounted for 84.2%, P. vivax for 7.9%, and mixed infections for 7.9%. In the multivariable analysis, male participants were nearly twice as likely to be infected with malaria compared to females (AOR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.16–3.04; p = 0.011). Participants from households with five or more family members had over two times higher odds of malaria infection than those from smaller families (AOR = 2.19; 95% CI: 1.36–3.54; p = 0.001). The mRDT showed 99.1% sensitivity, 99.3% specificity, 98.3% positive predictive value, and 99.7% negative predictive value, with almost perfect agreement with microscopy (κ = 0.98).ConclusionMalaria remains a significant health concern in the study area. The Bioline™ mRDT demonstrated diagnostic accuracy comparable to microscopy and can be reliably used for routine malaria diagnosis in resource-limited settings. Male sex and large family size were identified as significant socio-demographic risk factors for malaria infection. Background

Malaria remains a major global public health problem, with an estimated 263 million cases and 619,000 deaths reported worldwide in 2023, predominantly in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. The disease is caused primarily by Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax, transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. Effective malaria control relies on early diagnosis, prompt treatment, vector control interventions such as insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS), and robust surveillance systems. Despite these efforts, malaria continues to impose a substantial health and economic burden, especially in endemic regions with favorable ecological and climatic conditions for vector proliferation [2, 3].

In Ethiopia, malaria is a leading cause of outpatient visits and hospital admissions, particularly in endemic low-altitude areas such as the north-western and western regions, including Benishangul-Gumuz [4, 5]. Transmission is seasonal and unstable, typically peaking after the main rainy season (June–September), with inter-annual and spatial variation driven by altitude, rainfall and temperature patterns as well as human movement [6–8]. Both Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax co-exist in Ethiopia, with P. falciparum responsible for the majority of clinical cases and malaria-related deaths in most settings [7]. Over the past two decades, Ethiopia has scaled up core malaria control interventions LLINs/ITNs, IRS, larval source management, early diagnosis and treatment, and strengthened surveillance — though implementation challenges and recent vector and ecological changes remain important constraints [9]. In Benishangul-Gumuz region, vector control interventions are regularly conducted in collaboration with the President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI) and the Federal Ministry of Health, targeting high-risk populations such as migrant agricultural workers, children under five, and pregnant women [10]. Despite these efforts, persistent transmission in some areas — notably parts of Benishangul-Gumuz — is sustained by ecological suitability for vectors and population movements, producing focal high burden that requires targeted interventions [10, 11].

Several risk factors increase individual susceptibility to malaria infection in Ethiopia. Male sex, larger family size, outdoor sleeping, houses with open eaves, and lack of ITN) uses have been reported as significant contributors to higher malaria prevalence [12–14]. Understanding these risk factors is essential for designing targeted interventions and reducing malaria incidence. Climatic factors such as high rainfall, irrigation practices, and warmer temperatures create favorable conditions for mosquito breeding and parasite development. In Benishangul-Gumuz Region, persistent malaria transmission has been associated with suitable ecological conditions, widespread irrigation schemes, and the presence of efficient vectors such as Anopheles arabiensis [15, 16]. Despite ongoing control efforts, recurrent outbreaks and seasonal surges continue to occur in the region.

Comparable studies in northwestern Ethiopia have reported varying malaria prevalence across districts, reflecting local differences in transmission dynamics. For instance, prevalence rates have been reported from Metema [17], Abobo [18], and Assosa [19]. These findings underscore that malaria remains a major public health concern in lowland, high-transmission areas of the country. However, evidence from Pawe district—an area characterized by similar ecological features, irrigation development, and vector presence—remains scarce. Updated, facility-based data on malaria prevalence and diagnostic performance are particularly limited in this setting.

Accurate and timely diagnosis is essential for malaria case management and surveillance. Light microscopy remains the gold standard, allowing parasite detection and species identification [20, 21]. However, it requires skilled personnel, quality reagents, and functional laboratory infrastructure, which may be limited in resource-constrained settings. Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) provide a practical alternative because they are quick, easy to use, and suitable for peripheral health facilities. Nevertheless, their diagnostic accuracy may vary with parasite density, species type, storage conditions, and the occurrence of histidine-rich protein 2/3 (hrp2/hrp3) gene deletions affecting P. falciparum detection (22–24).

Therefore, this study aimed to determine malaria prevalence and assess the diagnostic performance of malaria rapid diagnostic tests (mRDTs) compared with light microscopy among febrile patients attending Pawe General Hospital. To the best of our knowledge, no recent study has systematically evaluated malaria prevalence and diagnostic accuracy of mRDTs in Pawe district. The findings of this study will generate context-specific evidence to inform local malaria control and elimination efforts in Benishangul-Gumuz Region, where updated epidemiological and diagnostic data are scarce.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted at Pawe General Hospital, located in Pawe district, Metekel Zone, Benishangul-Gumuz Region, Northwest Ethiopia. Pawe district is located 565 km to the Northwest of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. Geographically the study area is located at an altitude of 1050 m above sea level and situated at latitude of 11°09′N and longitude of 36°03′E. Pawe district has an average annual rain fall of 1555.1 mm and maximum temperature of 32∘C with mean monthly values ranging in 27–37∘C [25]. According to the 2019 national population projection, Pawe district has an estimated total population of 98,821, of which 46,494 were males and 52,327 were females [26]. The district has 20 villages (Kebeles), of which 3 are urban and the remaining 17 are rural, with an estimated 15,967 households. The district covers an area of 64,300 hectares. The area is malarious, and the major and minor malaria transmission peak seasons are from September to December and April to May, respectively, which coincide with the major harvesting and planting seasons. The highest rainfall occurs from July to August [25]. Pawe district has three functioning health centers and only one hospital (Pawe General Hospital) that caters to the patients of the district and neighboring districts. The hospital serves a malaria-endemic population and provides both inpatient and outpatient services.

Study design and period

A hospital based cross-sectional study was employed to assess the prevalence of malaria and evaluate the diagnostic performance of mRDT in comparison to microscopy among malaria suspected febrile patients who visited Pawe General Hospital. The study was conducted from November to December 2022, which corresponds to the post-rainy season when malaria transmission typically peaks in Ethiopia. Resource constraints limited the study to this period, and we acknowledge this as a limitation.

Study population

All febrile patients suspected of malaria and referred for malaria testing at Pawe General Hospital during the study period were eligible for inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Participants were eligible if they had a history of fever within the 24 h preceding their visit. Adults aged 18 years and above, as well as parents or guardians of children under 18 years who volunteered to participate, were included for the questionnaire survey. For malaria prevalence and diagnostic performance evaluation, febrile patients suspected of malaria who provided informed consent or assent and had not received anti-malarial therapy within the preceding four weeks were enrolled. This four-week window was chosen to minimize the risk of detecting residual HRP2 antigen from previous infections, ensuring that RDT and microscopy results reflected current malaria infections. Other malaria-compatible symptoms, such as headache, joint pain, back pain, chills, and fatigue, were recorded as supporting clinical information but were not required for inclusion.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals who were unable to communicate or who had a mental disability that limited their ability to participate effectively were excluded from the study.

Sample size for malaria parasite prevalence testing and performance evaluation of RDT kit was calculated using the single population proportion formula [27]:

Where, n - sample size, z - Confidence level at 95% (standard value of 1.96), p - Estimated prevalence of malaria infection in the study area, d - Margin of error at 5% (standard value of 0.05). As the prevalence of malaria infections in the area where this study was conducted has not been specifically known, the probability of 0.5(50%) was used for the determination of the sample size. Thus, n = z2 * P (1-p)/d2 = (1.96)2* (0.5)(1-0.5)/(0.05)2 = 384.

The calculated sample size for this study was 384. Considering the non-response rate would be 10% (0.1), (0.1) (384) = 38. Therefore, the study participants that were involved in the study should be: 384 + 38 = 422.

Thus, a total of 422 patients with fever (≥ 37.5 °C) and other supporting symptoms, including headache, back, and joint pain, were included in the study. A systematic random sampling technique using sampling frame was used to recruit the study participants. For the purpose, during data collection, the prior seven days average number of febrile cases were taken then multiplied by the data collection period to determine the average total number of febrile cases. Then, the total value was divided by the sample size of the study participants to determine Kth values and 1 up to K element were selected by lottery method. Finally, every Kth participant was included from the sampling frame until the required sample size was obtained.

Data collection and processing

Prior to data collection process, a two days training was given for eight data collectors about objective of the study, data collection process, data collection tools and ethical issues. Patients who had been visiting Pawe General Hospital during the study period were first clinically diagnosed to rule out history of fever according to diagnosis protocol by trained health professionals (Clinical nurses/health officers/medical doctors) of the hospital [28].Those participants eligible to be included were selected consecutively based on a systematic interval. Before demographic data and blood sample collection, the study subjects were informed about objectives and procedures of the study by data collectors. Those volunteer individuals to participate in the study were invited and their consent/assent was obtained.

Questionnaire data collection

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire developed specifically for this study. Trained data collectors conducted face-to-face interviews in a private setting. To maintain confidentiality, unique identifiers rather than names were recorded, and all data were stored securely with access limited to the research team. The questionnaire focused on basic socio-demographic variables, including age, gender, educational status, family size, occupation, and household monthly income. For malaria-suspected patients younger than 18 years, socio-demographic information such as occupation, educational level, and income was obtained from parents or guardians. The instrument was pretested on a small sample prior to the main study to ensure clarity, comprehension, and consistency in data collection.

Blood sample collection

After the interview was completed regarding demographic characteristics of the study participants, blood sample collection was performed according to Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) developed by WHO (2009) [29]. Accordingly, blood samples were collected aseptically by finger prick using disposable sterilized lancet from each study participants by two laboratory technicians of the hospital who took the training. The blood samples were used for malaria examination using RDT and to prepare thick and thin blood smear for malaria parasites microscopic examination.

Microscopic examinations

After cleaning the finger with cotton moistened with alcohol, finger prick blood samples were collected from clinically malaria suspected patients. The blood samples were used for microscopy to diagnose malaria. For microscopic examination, thick and thin blood smears were prepared on the same slide side by side and were labeled with a code. After the slides had been air dried in a horizontal position for about 30 s, the thin blood smears were fixed with absolute methanol for about 30 s. Then, both the thin and thick blood smears were stained with 10% Giemsa solution for 10 min following the procedure described elsewhere [30]. Then, blood slides were read under oil immersion objectives (high power magnification objective (100 xs) by two experienced laboratory technologists blinded to RDT results. Each screened 100 microscopic fields before declaring a slide negative. The thick blood smears were used for the detection of parasite positivity, while the thin smears were used for Plasmodium species identification. Plasmodium species were identified based on standard morphological features observed on Giemsa-stained blood films, including the shape of the ring stage, the size of infected red blood cells, the appearance of gametocytes, and the presence of stippling (Schüffner’s dots), in accordance with WHO guidelines. All positive and 5% of randomly selected negative slides were blindly re-examined by senior laboratory technologist of the Hospital to check if discordant results exist. The results were recorded qualitatively (Negative, Positive, Mixed) on malaria laboratory report result format. Microscopy was performed solely for parasite detection and species identification; parasite density was not quantified.

Malaria rapid diagnostic test procedure

The malaria rapid diagnostic test was performed using the Bioline™ Malaria Ag Pf/Pv kit (Abbott Diagnostics Korea, Inc., Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each test device was labeled with the respective sample code, and 5 µl of the patient’s blood specimen was dispensed into the sample well. Subsequently, four drops of lysis buffer (assay diluent) were added to the buffer well to lyse the cells, release the target antigens, and facilitate antibody-antigen recognition. The test results were read after 15 min and before 30 min. The results were interpreted as follows: the appearance of two bands—one in the control area and another in the Pv area—indicated a positive result for P. vivax; bands in the control and Pf areas indicated an infection due to P. falciparum; bands in the control, Pv, and Pf areas signified a mixed infection; a single band in the control area indicated a negative result; and if the control line did not appear, the result was considered invalid.

Data entry and analysis

Data were first entered into Microsoft Excel for cleaning and then exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, and means) were used to summarize participant characteristics and malaria prevalence. Associations between malaria positivity and socio-demographic variables were initially assessed using chi-square tests. Variables with p < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were further included in a multivariable logistic regression model to identify factors independently associated with malaria infection. Results of logistic regression are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For the comparison of diagnostic methods, malaria rapid diagnostic tests (mRDTs) were evaluated against light microscopy as the reference standard. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and overall diagnostic accuracy of mRDTs were calculated. Agreement between the two methods was assessed using the Kappa statistic with 95% confidence intervals [31]. All results were presented using text, tables, and figures.

Quality control

To maintain the quality of the data, SOP was used and followed in each step of the test procedures and structured questionnaires. Before the actual data collection period, questionnaire data quality control was assessed by conducting pre-test and the necessary correction and structuring of the questionnaires was made. Training was given for the team before data collection. Before data entry, the returned questionnaires were checked for completeness and corrective measures were taken. Expiry date of reagents, materials and mRDT were checked daily before data collection takes place. The quality of Giemsa staining was assessed by using positive and negative control blood films. RDT kits were stored at recommended temperatures. All positive and 5% of randomly selected negative slides were blindly re-examined by senior laboratory technologists of the hospital who were experienced in malaria diagnosis. There was no discordant result between the first and the second examiners.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 422 malaria-suspected patients participated in the study. Of these, 205 (48.6%) were males and 217 (51.4%) were females. The age of participants ranged from 1 to 70 years, with a median of 24.19 years (SD = 15.3). The majority, 305 (72.3%), were aged 15 years and above, while 47 (11.1%) were children under 5 years of age and 70 (16.6%) were adolescents aged 14–15 years. For participants younger than 18 years, socio-demographic information such as occupation, educational level, and income was obtained from their parents or guardians.

Regarding occupation, nearly half of respondents, 213 (50.5%), were farmers, followed by government employees (16.8%) and housewives (19.4%). About one-third, 145 (34.4%), were unable to read and write, while 103 (24.4%) had completed primary education. Most respondents, 190 (45%), reported a monthly household income of 10–30 United States dollars (USD). The average family size per household was 4.73 (range: 1–13), and more than half, 222 (52.6%), lived in households with five or fewer members (Table 1).

From a total of 422 individuals, the overall prevalence of malaria was 27% (114 of 422) and 27.3% (115 of 422), by microscopy and RDT, respectively. All 422 participants were assessed for malaria-related clinical manifestations.

Socio-demographic factors associated with malaria infection

In the bivariate analysis, six socio-demographic variables were evaluated, of which four—age, sex, educational status, and family size—met the p < 0.25 criterion and were retained for multivariable analysis.

After adjusting for potential confounders, sex and family size remained significantly associated with malaria infection. Male participants were nearly twice as likely to be infected with malaria compared to females (AOR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.16–3.04; p = 0.011). Participants from households with five or more family members had over two times higher odds of malaria infection than those from smaller families (AOR = 2.19; 95% CI: 1.36–3.54; p = 0.001). Although older participants (≥ 15 years) had higher odds of malaria infection compared to children under 5 years (AOR = 2.23; 95% CI: 0.89–5.59), this association was not statistically significant (p = 0.088). Educational status did not show a significant association with malaria infection after adjustment for other variables (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, malaria prevalence among patients attending Pawe General Hospital was notably higher than national averages, reflecting persistent heterogeneity in transmission risk across Ethiopia [32–35]. Despite nationwide progress through interventions such as ITNs, IRS, expanded diagnostic services, and community health programs, local hot-spots remain, particularly in the Benishangul-Gumuz region [5, 36]. Seasonal factors, local ecological conditions, and gaps in intervention coverage likely contributed to the elevated prevalence observed. Moreover, the facility-based design targeting febrile patients inherently yields higher prevalence compared with community-based surveys [37, 38].

The observed prevalence rate of 27% aligns with findings from other parts of Ethiopia and the WHO African Region, where prevalence ranged from 25% to 28.1% [39–42]. However, the current study’s prevalence was lower than rates documented in Kersa Woreda, Jimma (43.8%) [43], Halaba (82.8%) [44], and Nigeria (39.5%) [45]. Conversely, the prevalence observed here was significantly higher than that reported in Mozambique (15.7%) [46], Bangladesh (2.2%) [47], and several other locations in Ethiopia, where rates ranged between 0.93% and 11.45% [48, 49]. The possible reasons for these differences could include variations in study design, geographical location (rainfall, temperature, and altitude), sample size, study period (due to the seasonality of malaria in Ethiopia), and the malaria control measures implemented in each study area.

In the present study, Plasmodium falciparum was the predominant malaria species, consistent with national and regional reports from Oromia [50]. In contrast, studies conducted in Southern Ethiopia (Halaba Special District) [51], Southwest Ethiopia [52], Adama City, Oromia [53], and Northwest Ethiopia (University of Gondar Specialized Referral Hospital) [54] documented a higher prevalence of P. vivax, indicating regional variation in species distribution. Such variation may reflect ecological and climatic differences, vector species composition, and the variable effectiveness of control interventions across regions. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of malaria epidemiology in Ethiopia and emphasize the importance of continuous surveillance to inform effective treatment and control strategies.

Males in this study were nearly twice as likely to be infected with malaria compared to females (AOR ≈ 1.9). This study is consistent with previous studies in Ethiopia and elsewhere, where males have been reported to experience a significantly higher malaria burden than females, although the magnitude of association varied across settings [1, 12, 14, 17, 34, 55]. The elevated risk among males is largely attributed to gender-specific occupational and behavioral factors that increase exposure to malaria vectors. In the present study area, males frequently participate in outdoor activities such as agricultural work, livestock herding, seasonal labor migration, and evening social gatherings, which coincide with peak mosquito biting hours. A study conducted in the Dembia district of Northwest Ethiopia also found that outdoor activity was significantly associated with malaria infection [56]. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating gender-sensitive approaches into malaria prevention strategies, including targeted health education and promotion of insecticide-treated net (ITN) use, particularly among men engaged in outdoor nighttime activities.

Participants from households with five or more members had more than twice the odds of malaria infection compared to those from smaller households (AOR ≈ 2.2). This finding is consistent with previous studies in Ethiopia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Rwanda, which reported that individuals living in larger households had significantly higher odds of malaria infection [57–60]. The increased risk among larger families is likely related to limited access to insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), resulting in fewer nets per person and shared sleeping spaces that heighten human–mosquito contact. Overcrowding may also reduce the effectiveness of indoor residual spraying and facilitate easier entry of mosquitoes, particularly when housing conditions are poor [61, 62]. However, a study from Mozambique reported no significant relationship between family size and malaria prevalence [63], possibly due to differences in study settings and ITN distribution coverage. These findings emphasize the importance of ensuring adequate ITN coverage and vector control measures tailored to household size to effectively reduce malaria transmission risk.

While male sex and household size were significant predictors of malaria infection, age and educational level were not independently associated in this study. Participants aged 15 years and older had higher odds of infection compared with children under five (AOR ≈ 2.23), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.088). Age-related malaria patterns in Ethiopia vary: some studies report higher burdens among adolescents and working-age adults, while others find children under five remain most vulnerable [64, 65]. Similarly, educational level showed no independent association after adjustment, although it can indirectly influence malaria risk through knowledge, prevention practices, and care-seeking behavior, often mediated by household resources, ITN access, and local transmission intensity [66, 67]. Overall, gender and household size emerged as the main socio-demographic determinants of malaria infection in this study, whereas age and educational attainment were less important.

This study demonstrated that the Bioline™ Malaria Ag Pf/Pv rapid diagnostic test (RDT) exhibited high diagnostic accuracy compared with light microscopy. The agreement between the two diagnostic methods was nearly perfect, with only three discordant results (two false positives and one false negative), indicating that RDT findings were consistent with microscopy outcomes in this setting. The few discordant results may be related to low parasite density undetectable by microscopy, highlighting the complementary roles of microscopy and RDTs in malaria diagnosis. This confirms the reliability of RDTs for malaria case management, particularly in health facilities with limited laboratory capacity. Importantly, these findings align with the World Health Organization’s performance benchmarks for malaria diagnostic tools [68] and validate their use under routine operational conditions. Although RDTs performed comparably with microscopy, continued implementation should be supported by periodic quality assurance and training to sustain diagnostic accuracy.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and other malaria-endemic regions of Africa, which have also reported strong diagnostic performance of Pf/Pv (or Pf/Pan) RDTs. For example, Tadesse et al. [69] reported high sensitivity and specificity of SD Bioline RDTs in southern Ethiopia, while Zeleke et al. [70] observed similar reliability in northwestern Ethiopia. Studies from Madagascar [71] and Uganda [72] likewise reported high diagnostic performance, though some variability was noted, particularly in cases involving low parasite densities or non-falciparum species. Variability in RDT performance reported elsewhere has been attributed to low parasite density, mixed infections, or operational factors such as storage conditions and user proficiency [73, 74].

Minimal discordance in this study suggests that the Bioline™ RDT provides reliable results under field conditions, and issues such as HRP2/HRP3 gene deletions were not evident, supporting its continued use alongside microscopy as a complementary diagnostic tool. While RDTs offer operational advantages—such as rapid results, ease of use, and minimal infrastructure requirements—microscopy remains essential for reference laboratories and quality control, particularly for species differentiation and parasitemia quantification. The few false positives and false negatives observed further underscore the importance of ongoing training, supervision, and performance monitoring to maintain high diagnostic standards.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a well-defined hospital-based sample of febrile patients and the use of both light microscopy and a standardized RDT, allowing direct comparison of diagnostic performance. Additionally, the inclusion of key demographic factors such as age, sex, educational status, and family size provides valuable insights into malaria risk patterns in the study area. However, there are important limitations. First, while microscopy was used as the reference standard, its sensitivity declines at low parasite densities, and the absence of molecular confirmation methods such as PCR or qPCR may have led to underestimation of true malaria prevalence and influenced RDT diagnostic accuracy. Second, data were collected only during November–December, the peak transmission season, which may limit generalizability to other times of the year. Third, the study was conducted at a single hospital, potentially limiting the representativeness of the findings for other districts or regions. Fourth, emerging HRP2 gene deletions in P. falciparum could reduce RDT sensitivity, but these deletions were not specifically assessed. Fifth, the questionnaire captured demographic characteristics but did not include other household or environmental factors, such as house type, presence of eaves, ITN use, or prior malaria history, which are known to influence malaria risk. Despite these limitations, the study provides important evidence on malaria prevalence and the performance of RDTs in a malaria-endemic district of Northwest Ethiopia.

Conclusions

This study revealed a high prevalence of malaria (27%) in the study area, with P. falciparum as the predominant species. The findings underscore the continued need for sustained and targeted public health interventions to reduce the local malaria burden. Moreover, the Bioline™ Malaria Ag Pf/Pv RDT demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy compared with microscopy, supporting its use in malaria case management in resource-limited settings. While the results should be interpreted in light of the study’s limitations—including the absence of household and environmental factors such as house type, presence of eaves, ITN use, and prior malaria history—they provide important evidence to guide malaria control strategies in Northwest Ethiopia. Future studies incorporating molecular methods, broader geographic coverage, and detailed household and environmental variables are recommended to provide a more comprehensive understanding of malaria risk factors.
