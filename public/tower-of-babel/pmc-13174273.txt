Shoebill: an interpretable AlphaFold2-informed predictor of protein crystallization propensity using XGBoost
Authors: Kuan-Ju Liao, Yuh-Ju Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractX-ray crystallography is a central technique for high-resolution protein structure determination; however, the production of diffraction-quality crystals remains a major bottleneck due to high experimental cost and low success rates. Computational prediction of protein crystallization propensity offers a promising strategy to prioritize targets and reduce unnecessary experimental screening. While numerous computational predictors have been proposed, many rely primarily on sequence-derived features or provide limited interpretability, thereby offering little practical guidance for experimental design. Here, we present Shoebill, an interpretable protein crystallization propensity predictor that integrates AlphaFold2 (AF2)-derived structural descriptors with an XGBoost framework to assess whether a protein—assuming successful expression and purification—is likely to form diffraction-quality crystals. Shoebill leverages a comprehensive feature set extracted directly from AF2-predicted structures, capturing complementary structural information beyond sequence alone, including structural disorder, AF2 confidence metrics, molecular geometry, and surface physicochemical properties. On our primary independent benchmark, Shoebill outperforms existing nondeep-learning crystallization propensity predictors while maintaining balanced sensitivity and specificity, improving the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve from 0.700 to 0.804 and more than doubling the Matthews correlation coefficient from 0.123 to 0.297 relative to DCFCrystal, the best-performing nondeep-learning method. Although the deep learning-based predictor SADeepcry achieves higher overall predictive accuracy, Shoebill provides feature-level explanations for individual predictions through SHAP analysis, highlighting biologically meaningful features associated with crystallization propensity that may help guide rational strategies for construct optimization. The Shoebill source code and a user-friendly web server are publicly available at https://github.com/KJ-Liao/Shoebill.Keywords: X-ray crystallography, protein crystallization, crystallization propensity, AlphaFold2, XGBoost

Introduction

Accurate three-dimensional (3D) protein structures are indispensable for elucidating molecular mechanisms [1] and enabling structure-based drug design [2–4]. Among the prevalent structure-determination techniques, including X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and cryo-EM, X-ray crystallography has long been the dominant method [5, 6], contributing >85% of all structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [7].

The protein crystallization workflow comprises three sequential stages [8, 9]: (i) expression of target protein, (ii) purification of soluble material, and (iii) production of diffraction-quality crystals. Advances in expression systems [10] and purification strategies [11] have markedly improved the success of the first two steps. In parallel, robotic screening platforms [12, 13] and computational tools [14–16] have reduced protein consumption and facilitated more systematic exploration of crystallization conditions. Nevertheless, X-ray crystallography remains costly and inefficient due to the persistent difficulty of the final stage—obtaining diffraction-quality crystals [9, 17, 18]. Indeed, large-scale studies consistently report that <20% of purified proteins ultimately yield crystals suitable for X-ray structure determination [19, 20]. Thus, improving the ability to predict whether a target protein is crystallizable remains a long-standing and unresolved challenge.

Over the past two decades, numerous computational approaches have been developed to estimate crystallization propensity using statistical and machine-learning methods based on sequence-derived features. These methods can be broadly categorized into single-stage and multi-stage predictors. Single-stage approaches, such as TargetCrys [21], BCrystal [22], and CLPred [23], output a binary evaluation of overall crystallizability. Although useful, these methods do not indicate which specific step of the crystallization pipeline may fail, limiting their utility for guiding experiments. To tackle these issues, multi-stage predictors have been proposed to explicitly assess the likelihood of success at each crystallization stage. Representative examples include PPCpred [8] and fDETECT [24], which model individual stages using sequence-derived physicochemical features, as well as DCFCrystal [25] and the recent deep learning-based SADeepcry [26], which employ more complex feature representations and architectures to capture global sequence patterns.

Despite these advances, several critical challenges persist. First, most existing predictors rely heavily on simple sequence-derived features, such as amino acid composition, physicochemical properties, or predicted disorder content [8, 21–26]. While informative, these features are often indirect proxies for structural behavior, leading to modest accuracy in predicting crystallization outcomes. Second, deep learning-based models generally achieve better predictive performance but at the cost of transparency [27, 28], offering limited mechanistic insight into the molecular determinants influencing crystallization [26]. When such models classify a protein as “hard-to-crystallize,” they rarely suggest concrete strategies for protein engineering or construct optimization. Third, many currently available predictors were trained on datasets compiled more than a decade ago [8, 21–26], which may contain outdated, incorrect, or inconsistently annotated TargetTrack records. Updated, high-quality datasets are therefore essential for developing robust and generalizable crystallization predictors.

Recent breakthroughs in protein structure prediction, particularly AlphaFold2 (AF2) [29], AlphaFold3 (AF3) [30], RoseTTAFold [31], and ESM-2 [32], now enable highly accurate 3D models to be generated directly from amino acid sequences. These advances create an opportunity to move beyond sequence-only descriptors and incorporate more biologically meaningful structure-informed descriptors inferred from predicted models into crystallization prediction [19, 33], including surface electrostatics, solvent accessibility, conformational flexibility, and disorder-related metrics. Such descriptors provide a more direct connection to the physicochemical properties related to crystal packing and lattice formation [19, 33], thereby offering the potential to enhance both predictive accuracy and interpretability.

Because expression and purification of the target protein have benefited substantially from technological developments [10, 11], this study focuses on the remaining major bottleneck in X-ray crystallography: the production of diffraction-quality crystals. Here, we introduce Shoebill (https://github.com/KJ-Liao/Shoebill), an interpretable crystallization propensity predictor based on XGBoost framework [34]. Shoebill specifically evaluates whether a protein—assuming successful expression and purification—tends to form diffraction-quality crystals by leveraging a comprehensive feature set derived from both protein sequence and AF2-predicted structures. Benchmarking results show that Shoebill outperforms several existing nondeep-learning predictors while maintaining balanced and reliable performance. More importantly, by exploiting SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) algorithm [35], Shoebill offers feature-level explanations for individual predictions and suggests potential strategies for improving crystallization success.

Materials and methods

Benchmark datasets

Dataset curation

TargetTrack is a structural genomics target registration database that provides detailed experimental annotations for proteins deposited by more than 40 structural genomics centers worldwide [36]. Its final release in 2017, containing 333 006 proteins and 961 521 experimental trials (comprising both X-ray and NMR experiments), was used in this study.

Each protein entry may have multiple trials, each representing a distinct experimental procedure. We found that some previous studies curated datasets using target-level statuses and sequences instead of trial-level annotations [25, 26, 37, 38]. However, trial constructs are frequently modified, e.g. by affinity tags, mutations, or truncations, meaning that the target sequence does not always match the sequence actually used for crystallization (Table S1). Given that most existing predictors derive features directly from protein sequences, using mismatched target sequences raises concerns about annotation integrity.

Additionally, several earlier studies adopted the classification definitions summarized in Table S2 [25, 26, 37, 38], but annotations such as “crystallized” or “diffraction” merely indicate that crystallization or diffraction experiments were performed and had not progressed to the next stage by the last update. These annotations do not necessarily represent final outcomes, as trials may still have been ongoing or may have halted due to resource limitations, project termination, or other practical considerations. Accordingly, a protein should not be classified as a crystallization failure unless explicit evidence is provided in <stopStatus>, <targetStopDetails>, or <trialStopDetails> [8, 24]. Moreover, trials labeled “in PDB” do not guarantee crystallization success, as some entries correspond to cases where crystallization was attempted but the final structure was solved by NMR. Furthermore, trial deposition and update dates can occasionally be incorrectly ordered; therefore, we used the update date of the most recent <status> entry in <statusHistoryList> to determine trial completion dates.

Training set and independent test set

For training and test set construction, the curated HC and CR proteins were merged, and sequences longer than 1000 amino acids were excluded. The remaining samples were randomly stratified into a training set (TRAIN_3000) and a test set (TEST_749) using an 8:2 split. Shoebill was trained on TRAIN_3000 using five-fold cross-validation, while TEST_749 was held out exclusively for performance evaluation.

To further assess generalization, we incorporated an additional independent dataset, CF_DS, curated by Zhu et al. [25], which has been widely used for benchmarking crystallization propensity predictors [26, 37, 38]. However, several issues required correction:

AlphaFold2

High-quality predicted 3D structures were generated using AF2. Protein sequences from the curated datasets were submitted to AF2 running on the Academia Sinica Grid Computing Centre (Taipei, Taiwan), equipped with NVIDIA A100 GPUs and AMD EPYC 7302 CPUs (16 cores, 3 GHz). For each sequence, AF2 produced five ranked models together with predicted aligned error (PAE) matrices. All predicted models and PAE files were retained for subsequent feature extraction.

Architecture of Shoebill

Feature engineering

An overview of the Shoebill framework is shown in Fig. 1. Shoebill integrates conventional sequence-derived features, including amino acid composition, molecular weight, isoelectric point (pI), and AAindex-based physicochemical encodings, together with a broad set of structural descriptors computed from top-ranked AF2 models. These features were designed to capture complementary information spanning primary sequence properties and higher-order structural organization relevant to protein crystallization [19, 33].

Overview of the Shoebill framework. Shoebill predicts protein crystallization propensity by integrating sequence-based descriptors with a diverse set of structure-based features extracted from AF2 models. Sequence-derived features include global physicochemical properties such as molecular weight, amino acid composition, isoelectric point, and AAindex-based encodings. Structure-derived features capture complementary aspects of protein architecture, including surface physicochemical properties, global structural metrics, secondary-structure content, intrinsic disorder, AF2 model confidence and uncertainty, shape descriptors, and statistical energy estimates. Features are extracted from the top five AF2 predictions and combined with sequence-derived properties to generate 830 initial descriptors. Feature selection using mutual information, XGBoost importance, and correlation-aware pruning reduces this to 36 biologically meaningful features, which are then integrated by an XGBoost classifier to produce the final Shoebill prediction. Proteins with predicted scores ≥0.420 are classified as crystallizable, whereas those with scores <0.420 are classified as hard-to-crystallize.

During feature extraction, we observed a pronounced distributional bias in His-tag usage between CR and HC samples. Specifically, most HC entries with definite <stopStatus> remarks originated from the New York SGX Research Center for Structural Genomics (NYSGXRC), which routinely employed His-tag constructs, whereas CR cases deposited by other groups were often reported without tags. Similar artifacts have been noted previously in Thumuluri et al. [40], suggesting that predictors may inadvertently learn tag-associated signatures rather than genuine crystallization-related factors. To prevent such confounding, AF2 predictions were generated using full-length sequences (including tags to preserve any folding influences), but tags were removed prior to feature calculation.

Feature selection

To mitigate redundancy and potential noise in the initial 830-feature set, Shoebill employed a two-stage feature selection strategy combining mutual information (MI) [50], XGBoost-based feature importance [34], and correlation-driven pruning.

In the first stage, MI values were computed for each feature on TRAIN_3000 using three random seeds and averaged to reduce estimator variance. In parallel, XGBoost models were trained under five-fold cross-validation with the same three seeds using a conservative baseline configuration (n_estimators = 1000, learning_rate = 0.03, max_depth = 4, min_child_weight = 4, reg_lambda = 1.0, subsample = 0.7, colsample_bytree = 0.7). Early stopping was applied in all folds to prevent overfitting, and class imbalance was handled using scale_pos_weight = Nn/Np, where Nn and Np denote the numbers of HC and CR proteins, respectively. Feature importances from XGBoost were then normalized and averaged across folds and seeds to obtain ensemble importance scores.

The second stage sought a compact, nonredundant subset of informative features. We intersected the top MI- and XGBoost-ranked features at cumulative contribution thresholds of 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and 90%. For each threshold, the intersected features were grouped based on pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients (r), using a distance metric of 1 − |r|, followed by average-linkage hierarchical clustering with cutoffs of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3. Within each cluster, only the feature with the highest XGBoost-based importance was retained. This procedure yielded 15 candidate feature subsets (five cumulative thresholds × three clustering cutoffs), which were carried forward for hyperparameter optimization.

Shoebill training

Shoebill was implemented using an optimized XGBoost classifier [34], which iteratively fits decision-tree learners to the residuals of previous models to progressively improve predictive performance. The choice of XGBoost as the core classifier was justified through systematic benchmarking against other widely used machine learning algorithms, including support vector machine, random forest, and logistic regression (Supplementary Text S1).

Model training was conducted on the 15 feature subsets generated in the feature selection pipeline using five-fold stratified cross-validation on TRAIN_3000, with performance evaluated separately on internal training and validation partitions. Early stopping and class-imbalance correction were applied throughout. Because XGBoost exposes a large hyperparameter space that renders exhaustive grid search computationally prohibitive, we adopted a staged optimization strategy. Starting from a conservative baseline configuration, hyperparameters were sequentially refined by tuning model complexity, regularization strength, learning rate, and sampling parameters. At each stage, candidate models were filtered using strict performance and generalization criteria, ensuring that only stable and well-performing configurations advanced. Full details of the staged optimization procedure, including parameter ranges and filtering rules, are provided in Supplementary Text S2.

The final Shoebill model employed feature set 8 (36 features in total, defined by the intersection of the top 70% MI- and XGBoost-ranked features with a correlation-clustering cutoff of 0.3) with the optimal configuration: n_estimators = 35, learning_rate = 0.05, max_depth = 3, min_child_weight = 12, gamma = 1, reg_lambda = 5, reg_alpha = 1, subsample = 0.6, and colsample_bytree = 0.6. This model was retrained on the full TRAIN_3000 dataset and subsequently evaluated on TEST_749 and TEST_283 to assess generalization.

Evaluation metrics

Model performance was assessed using four standard evaluation metrics: sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), accuracy (ACC), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). All metrics were calculated from the numbers of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN), as defined below:

Because SEN, SPE, ACC, and MCC depend on the classification threshold, careful threshold selection is essential for fair comparison among predictors. For PPCpred, DCFCrystal, and SADeepcry, the recommended default cutoffs were used. For fDETECT—which does not specify a default cutoff—and for Shoebill, optimal thresholds were determined by grid search (0.01–1.00 in increments of 0.01), selecting the value that maximized MCC on TRAIN_3000 (fDETECT = 0.520; Shoebill = 0.420). In addition, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was reported as a threshold-independent measure of overall performance. For Shoebill, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to quantify statistical uncertainty in SEN, SPE, and ACC using Wilson intervals, and in MCC and AUC using bootstrap resampling.

Results

Performance comparison

Shoebill was benchmarked on two independent datasets, TEST_749 and TEST_283, against four widely used crystallization predictors: PPCpred, fDETECT, DCFCrystal, and the deep learning-based SADeepcry. Predictions for PPCpred, fDETECT, and DCFCrystal were obtained via their respective web servers, whereas SADeepcry was run locally using its publicly released implementation with minor corrections to ensure execution. Model performance was assessed using SEN, SPE, ACC, MCC, and AUC.

As summarized in Tables 3, S5 and S6, Shoebill showed minimal overfitting and reliable generalization, with highly consistent AUC values between internal training (0.834) and validation (0.808) sets, as well as between the full training set and the primary benchmark TEST_749 (AUCTRAIN_3000 = 0.844 versus AUCTEST_749 = 0.804). Performance on TEST_749

TEST_749 serves as the main benchmark curated using the same stringent criteria applied to TRAIN_3000. As depicted in Table 3, SADeepcry achieved the greatest overall performance (ACC = 0.913, SEN = 0.923, SPE = 0.912, MCC = 0.602, AUC = 0.971), reflecting the strength of deep neural architectures trained on extensive sequence embeddings. Among nondeep-learning predictors, Shoebill performed best across nearly all metrics (Fig. 2A and Table 3).

ROC analysis of protein crystallization propensity predictors. ROC curves comparing Shoebill with existing predictors on two independent test sets, TEST_749 (A) and TEST_283 (B). The AUC is indicated for each method.

Shoebill reached an AUC of 0.804 (95% CI: 0.742–0.861), exceeding those of DCFCrystal (0.700), PPCpred (0.603), and fDETECT (0.540) by 10.4%, 20.1%, and 26.4%, respectively. Consistently, Shoebill attained the highest MCC among nondeep-learning methods (0.297, 95% CI: 0.215–0.377), more than doubling DCFCrystal (0.123) and substantially surpassing PPCpred (0.095) and fDETECT (0.061). These improvements underscore Shoebill’s ability to distinguish crystallizable from noncrystallizable proteins, even under the severe class imbalance.

Shoebill further demonstrated the most balanced SEN-SPE trade-off among all models excluding SADeepcry. Its SPE (0.673, 95% CI: 0.538–0.785), SEN (0.812, 95% CI: 0.781–0.839), and ACC (0.802, 95% CI: 0.772–0.829) were all higher than those of the next-best predictor, DCFCrystal, by 25.0%, 3.0%, and 4.5%, respectively. In contrast, PPCpred and fDETECT showed extreme class skewness (SPE > 0.95; SEN < 0.10), indicating a strong bias toward majority-class prediction and therefore limited practical utility.

Collectively, Shoebill emerges as the leading alternative to deep learning-based crystallization predictors, achieving competitive performance (AUC > 0.80) while using only 36 biologically meaningful features—far fewer than the >9000 features employed by SADeepcry. This compact design enables accurate predictions while also offering a framework for analyzing structural features underlying crystallization propensity.

Performance on TEST_283

Evaluation on TEST_283 revealed similar trends (Fig. 2B and Table 4). SADeepcry again outperformed all others overall (ACC = 0.890, SEN = 0.929, SPE = 0.536, MCC = 0.433, AUC = 0.831). Shoebill and DCFCrystal formed the next tier, with comparable AUC values around 0.7 (95% CI: 0.597–0.804). Specifically, Shoebill exhibited higher SPE (0.536, 95% CI: 0.358–0.705) but slightly lower SEN (0.816, 95% CI: 0.764–0.858) and MCC (0.254, 95% CI: 0.114–0.389) than DCFCrystal. Notably, Shoebill reached the same level of SPE as SADeepcry despite employing a much simpler architecture.

In accord with results on TEST_749, PPCpred and fDETECT again exhibited very low SEN (~0.10) alongside superficially high ACC (>0.85) and SPE (>0.90), leading to low MCC values (0.073 and 0.097) and modest AUCs (0.673 and 0.633). Remarkably, the MCC values of PPCpred and fDETECT fell below the lower bounds of Shoebill’s 95% CIs on TEST_283 (0.114–0.389), whereas DCFCrystal, exhibiting comparable performance, remained within Shoebill’s uncertainty range. Overall, aside from SADeepcry, Shoebill steadily remained the most balanced and reliable model across both test sets.

Understanding the reduced performance on TEST_283

It is noteworthy that most predictors, including SADeepcry, exhibited reduced performance on TEST_283 relative to TEST_749. This decline is likely attributable to two factors. First, TEST_283 is highly imbalanced, containing only 28 negative (HC) samples, making evaluation metrics extremely sensitive to a small number of challenging cases; even minor misclassifications can lead to large fluctuations in MCC, AUC, and SPE. Second, negative labels in TEST_283 are less reliable because they were inferred solely from trial statuses rather than explicit <stopStatus> annotations, introducing potential label noise that adversely affects all predictors. Consequently, the diminished performance on TEST_283 likely reflects dataset ambiguity but not reduced generalization ability of Shoebill or other predictors. Taken together, although absolute performance values differ between TEST_749 and TEST_283, the relative ranking of predictors remains consistent, with Shoebill exhibiting most robust performance among nondeep-learning crystallization predictors.

Ablation analysis of feature category contributions

To quantify the contribution of different feature categories to model performance, we performed a leave-one-category-out ablation analysis on the final set of 36 selected features. Each ablation variant was retrained using the same hyperparameters as the full Shoebill model under five-fold cross-validation on TRAIN_3000, and performance was evaluated on TEST_749.

As shown in Fig. S2 and Table 5, among the structure-derived feature groups, removal of the ‘Disorder’ category resulted in a noticeable decrease in performance (MCC = 0.253; AUC = 0.767), highlighting the importance of incorporating AF2-derived model confidence and structural uncertainty into the predictive framework. In contrast, removing other individual feature categories led to relatively modest changes in performance. This observation is consistent with the presence of partial correlations among the selected features, which allow compensatory effects when a single category is removed (Fig. 3C).

Global feature interpretation using XGBoost importance

A key advantage of XGBoost over neural network-based models is its ability to provide intrinsic estimates of feature importance. In Shoebill, global importance was quantified using the gain metric, which measures the total improvement in tree split attributable to each feature. Features that never contributed to any split received a gain value of zero, effectively pruning uninformative descriptors automatically during training.

To ensure robustness, gain values for the final Shoebill model were averaged across ten-fold cross-validation and three random seeds (0, 42, and 324). Feature rankings were highly consistent, with pairwise Spearman correlations exceeding 0.9 (rnd0 versus rnd42 = 0.952; rnd0 versus rnd324 = 0.945; rnd42 versus rnd324 = 0.948). This strong concordance confirmed that Shoebill prioritizes the same subset of descriptors, independent of stochastic variation during training. The cumulative gain curve plateaued after approximately the top 20 features, which together accounted for >95% of the total model importance (Fig. 3A). These principal contributors are summarized in Fig. 3B, with complete gain values listed in Table S7.

Because gain scores can be influenced by feature correlations, we further clustered all 36 Shoebill features using hierarchical clustering on pairwise Pearson distances (1 − |r|) (Fig. 3C; feature definitions and computational procedures are provided in Supplementary File 2). With a cluster-distance cutoff of 0.5, 16 coherent groups were identified (Table S8). Aggregated importance revealed three dominant macro-classes explaining roughly 90% of total importance. The largest class (cluster 1: 69.2%) consisted primarily of descriptors related to amino acid composition and structural disorder within low-confidence AF2 regions. The second class (cluster 2: 12.1%) reflected global and surface amino acid composition, particularly charged residues such as lysine and glutamate. The third class (cluster 3: 8.9%) was mainly composed of 3D Zernike moments describing overall molecular shape. Together, these findings suggested the central roles of structural disorder, AF2 confidence, and surface entropy in governing crystallization propensity [19, 33, 51].

While gain-based importance effectively identifies influential features, it does not indicate whether higher feature values promote or hinder crystallization. To obtain directional and sample-level feature explanations, we next applied SHAP analysis to quantify both the magnitude and direction of each feature’s contribution [35].

SHAP analysis of individual feature contributions

To clarify how individual features affect crystallization outcomes, SHAP analysis was applied to the trained Shoebill model. SHAP decomposes each prediction into additive feature contributions, enabling both global directional interpretation and sample-specific explanations.

Figure 4A ranks the top 15 features by their mean absolute SHAP values (|SHAP|), which quantify the overall influence of each feature on model output regardless of direction. Features with larger |SHAP| values exert stronger effects on crystallization predictions. Figure 4B provides directional insight, where red denotes higher raw feature values and blue indicates lower values. Positive SHAP values shift predictions toward CR class, whereas negative values drive predictions toward HC class.

Seven features with mean |SHAP| > 0.05 were identified as the dominant contributors of Shoebill’s predictions (features 508, 624, 558, 575, 201, 580, and 8; see Supplementary File 2 for feature details), displaying strong agreement with the top-ranked features highlighted by XGBoost gain analysis. Figure 4C further illustrates the distribution of raw feature values for these seven dominant features in crystallizable (CR) versus hard-to-crystallize (HC) samples. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided Welch’s t-tests. Collectively, these effects can be grouped into four categories.

(1) Structural disorder and conformational flexibility

(1) Structural disorder and conformational flexibility

The proportions of serine (feature 508) and glycine (feature 558) within low-confidence AF2 regions exhibited the strongest negative associations with crystallizability. Both residues promote backbone flexibility and are commonly enriched in disordered or solvent-exposed segments that rarely participate in stable lattice contacts [52–54]. Beyond residue composition, the similar flexibility trend is captured by PAE-related metrics. PAE provides an informative measure of inter-domain uncertainty [47], with elevated values often reflecting conformational flexibility or ambiguous inter-domain arrangements that impede the formation of well-ordered crystal lattices [9, 55, 56]. In line with this interpretation, both the fraction of flexible regions quantified using residue-level PAE smoothed over a five-residue window (feature 624) and the number of contiguous loop segments inferred from residues with PAE > 5 Å (feature 580) were negatively correlated with crystallization propensity. Together, these results suggest practical construct-engineering strategies, such as shortening serine/glycine-enriched loops and stabilizing flexible regions with cofactors, ligands, or binding partners.

(2) AF2 confidence and model reliability

(2) AF2 confidence and model reliability

Among AF2-derived confidence metrics, the first quartile of pLDDT values (feature 575) ranked as a strong positive contributor to crystallizability. pLDDT has been commonly used as a proxy for local structural order and model reliability, with higher values indicating more confidently predicted and structurally stable regions [45–47]. The positive association between pLDDT lower-bound values and crystallization success therefore aligns well with established crystallographic intuition [9, 19, 56]. Accordingly, construct optimization—such as removal of low-confidence regions or redesign of poorly predicted segments using tools like ProteinMPNN [57]—may improve both AF2 confidence and crystallization outcomes.

(3) Molecular geometry

(3) Molecular geometry

Protein long-axis length (feature 201) emerged as another key characteristic. Proteins with more elongated geometries exhibited reduced crystallization propensity, likely due to inefficient packing or increased conformational heterogeneity. This observation aligns with long-standing experimental experience in protein crystallography, where elongated proteins, particularly long fibrous molecules [58], are often more difficult to pack into well-ordered crystal lattices. Designing more compact constructs or segmenting large proteins into independently folded domains may therefore help mitigate these issues.

(4) Charged residues and surface entropy

(4) Charged residues and surface entropy

Overall glutamate content (feature 8) also showed a clear negative association with crystallization success. This trend is consistent with the surface-entropy-reduction (SER) hypothesis, which implicates charged, high-entropy residues such as glutamate, lysine, and glutamine in disrupting formation of ordered lattice contacts [51, 59]. SHAP analysis captures this effect, demonstrating that elevated surface entropy shifts predictions toward the HC class. Substitution of glutamate and other charged residues with lower-entropy alternatives therefore represents a rational strategy for enhancing crystallization probability.

Overall, SHAP analysis reveals that Shoebill captures biologically meaningful feature contributions among structural disorder, AF2 confidence, molecular geometry, and surface entropy—factors previously implicated in protein crystallization. Importantly, these insights offer potential guidance for rational construct modification, including reducing disorder and long flexible loops, improving AF2 confidence through sequence redesign, segmenting large proteins into compact domains, and lowering surface entropy via targeted mutagenesis.

Case study: human ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme CDC34

To illustrate how Shoebill’s SHAP-based explanations inform rational construct design, we examined a representative hard-to-crystallize protein from the testing set: the human ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme CDC34 (UniProt ID: P49427).

Figure 5A compares the AF2-predicted full-length model (cyan; 236 amino acids) with the experimentally determined crystal structure (green; PDB ID: 2OB4, corresponding to residues 7–184) [60]. The structured core regions of the two models are highly similar (RMSD = 0.479 Å); however, the AF2-predicted full-length model contains an extended disordered segment at the C-terminus that is absent from 2OB4. Correspondingly, both PAE and pLDDT profiles indicate that the terminal ~44 residues exhibit substantial positional uncertainty and low local confidence (Fig. 5B). When evaluated using Shoebill, the full-length AF2 model received a crystallization propensity score of 0.288, well below the classification threshold, thus classifying CDC34 as hard-to-crystallize. SHAP analysis further revealed that this prediction is primarily driven by strongly negative contributions from features associated with structural disorder, loop flexibility, and elongated molecular geometry, consistent with the patterns described in the previous section (Fig. 5E).

(A) Structural comparison of the AF2-predicted full-length CDC34 model and the experimentally determined crystal structure (PDB ID: 2OB4). (B) PAE matrix and per-residue pLDDT profile of the AF2-predicted full-length model. (C) Structural superposition of the AF2-predicted C-terminally truncated CDC34 model and the crystal structure. (F) SHAP waterfall plot illustrating feature-wise contributions to the Shoebill prediction for the truncated model.

Because the low-confidence region is confined to the protein C-terminus, C-terminal truncation naturally emerges as a straightforward strategy for enhancing crystallizability. We therefore generated a new AF2 model matching the experimentally crystallized construct by removing the disordered C-terminal segment. As shown in Fig. 5C, the truncated model (blue) adopts a compact fold and closely overlaps the crystal structure (RMSD = 0.484 Å). The updated PAE and pLDDT profiles show uniformly increased confidence and markedly reduced structural uncertainty (Fig. 5D). Importantly, SHAP contributions for key disorder- and shape-related features—including feature 580, feature 624, and feature 201—reverse from negative to positive (Fig. 5F), consistent with the stabilization and compaction of the truncated construct. Accordingly, Shoebill’s predicted crystallization probability increases from 0.288 for the full-length protein to 0.659 for the truncated construct, in agreement with its successful crystallization.

In conclusion, this case study demonstrates that Shoebill not only predicts crystallization propensity but also provides SHAP-derived insights that may facilitate construct optimization, potentially increasing the likelihood of obtaining diffraction-quality crystals.

Discussion

In this study, we present Shoebill, an interpretable XGBoost-based crystallization propensity predictor that combines conventional sequence-derived features with AF2-derived structural descriptors. Shoebill consistently outperforms most existing methods, including PPCpred, fDETECT, and DCFCrystal, and approaches the performance of the deep learning-based model SADeepcry, which benefits from extensive sequence embeddings and a more complex architecture.

A major contribution of this work is the incorporation of a comprehensive set of structure-informed features, encompassing AF2 confidence metrics, disorder-related measures, geometric shape descriptors, and surface physicochemical characteristics, which have been largely underrepresented in earlier sequence-based methods. SHAP analysis provides feature-level explanations linking these structural properties to crystallization propensity. These insights suggest practical directions for construct modification, such as reducing disordered regions, optimizing low-confidence segments using tools like ProteinMPNN, splitting large proteins into smaller and more robust domains through construct engineering, and decreasing surface entropy via targeted mutagenesis. While these strategies align with long-standing empirical principles in structural biology [9, 19, 33, 56], Shoebill provides a quantitative and model-supported assessment at residue and feature resolution.

Another important component of this study is the rigorous re-curation of crystallization outcomes in TargetTrack. By enforcing strict criteria based on <stopStatus> annotations, we generated high-quality training and benchmark datasets and demonstrated Shoebill’s robustness across two independent test sets. The curated datasets, together with the newly engineered library of 830 sequence- and structure-derived features, constitute a valuable resource for future methodological development.

Despite its strengths, Shoebill has limitations. Its reliance on AF2-predicted structures restricts applicability to protein classes where AF2 performance is known to be suboptimal, such as membrane proteins, highly intrinsically disordered proteins, or multi-domain complexes [61, 62]. Moreover, as a gradient-boosting model, its predictive capacity is inherently constrained relative to deep learning architectures. We therefore position Shoebill not as a competitor to deep learning-based predictors, but as a complementary tool that prioritizes feature explanations, transparency, and biologically meaningful structural insights. While deep learning models may excel at maximizing predictive performance, Shoebill enables users to identify which AF2-derived structural properties most influence crystallization propensity and how they may be targeted for rational construct optimization. Future work may explore hybrid strategies that integrate Shoebill’s interpretability with the expressive power of deep architectures, or leverage Shoebill-derived insights to constrain and guide generative protein design pipelines.

Additionally, while SHAP analysis provides useful post hoc feature explanations and biologically plausible insights, these interpretations remain computational. The CDC34 case study serves as a proof-of-concept example, illustrating how SHAP-derived insights from Shoebill can highlight structural features that may hinder crystallization. However, further validation across diverse protein families will be necessary to fully assess Shoebill’s utility in guiding construct design. Furthermore, Shoebill does not yet include a dedicated module for systematically evaluating the impact of specific amino acid substitutions on crystallization likelihood. Existing tools such as Crysalis [63] and the SERp server [64] have demonstrated the value of computational scoring for individual mutations in improving crystallization outcomes. Future work may therefore explore extending Shoebill toward mutation design by coupling the predictor with sequence redesign approaches.
