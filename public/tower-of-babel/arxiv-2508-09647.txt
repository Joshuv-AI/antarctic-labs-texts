AN ALGORITHM FOR THE PRODUCT-SUM EQUALITY
Hlib Husarov

Abstract




Received: 13 August 2025

Abstract

We propose a recursive algorithm for identifying all finite sequences of positive integers whose product equals their sum. Our method uses solutions of strictly shorter length that are iteratively extended in pursuit of a valid solution. The algorithm is efficient, with a time complexity similar to the quick sort algorithm.

Introduction

This paper puts forward a recursive algorithm to identify all positive integer sequences
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
that satisfy the Diophantine equation

\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}

(1.1)

and gives a precise asymptotic result concerning its time-complexity.

While this problem has been studied with the help of computer programs—especially in the search for a number
n
with at most one solution---the underlying algorithms are rarely disclosed, and concrete implementations appear to be unavailable in the public domain.

The Algorithm

Throughout this paper, we assume without loss of generality that the integer sequences
(a_{i})_{i}
are non-increasing, i.e.,
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\dots\geq a_{n}
, whether or not they satisfy (
1.1
).

Let
n\geq 2
be a positive integer. The following recursive algorithm searches for solutions to (
1.1
). It maintains a candidate solution
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})
, where
i\geq 1
is the current index. Define the partial sum and partial product of the candidate solution as

s=\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{j},\quad p=\prod_{j=1}^{i}a_{j},

Due to Theorem
4
, if
a
can be extended to a solution of (
1.1
) of length
n
, than it must satisfy the feasibility condition

p\leq s+n-i,\quad i\leq n;

(2.1)

Equality holds, whenever the solution is found (in which case
a_{i+1}=\dots a_{n}=1
).

The recursive function takes the triple
(p,s,i)
that satisfies (
2.1
) and extends the candidate solution
a
by adding a new element
a_{i+1}
(we typically start at
p=1
(empty product),
s=0
(empty sum) and
i=0
, which also satisfies (
2.1
). )

The algorithm proceeds as follows:

1.

If
i\geq 1
, set the maximum allowed value for
a_{i+1}
as
m:=a_{i}
; otherwise, set
m:=n
.

2.

For each candidate extension
a^{\prime}=2,3,\dots,m
:

(a)

Extend the partial solution by setting
a_{i+1}:=a^{\prime}
.

(b)

Update the sum and product:

s^{\prime}:=s+a^{\prime},\quad p^{\prime}:=p\times a^{\prime}.

(c)

Check the feasibility condition for the extended partial solution:

•

If
p^{\prime}>s^{\prime}+n-(i+1)
, prune this branch and backtrack.

•

If
p^{\prime}=s^{\prime}+n-(i+1)
, record the solution

(a_{1},\dots,a_{i},a_{i+1},\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{n-i-1\text{ times}}),

then prune this branch and backtrack.

•

Otherwise, recursively call the function with updated parameters
(p^{\prime},s^{\prime},i+1)
to continue extending the solution.

3.

After all candidate values
a^{\prime}
have been tested, return from the current recursive call.

In the following three subsections, we develop statements that reveal the efficiency of the algorithm.

2.1
Completeness

The algorithm does not consider every integer sequence of length
n
; instead, it restricts sequences by imposing
a_{i}\leq n
. Moreover, if at any point the product
p
satisfies
p\geq s+n-i
, the algorithm stops extending the sequence
(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})
by adding numbers greater than one. These restrictions do not exclude any solutions:

Theorem 1
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Starting from the initial values
(s=0,p=1,i=0)
, the algorithm finds all solutions to equation (
1.1
). The last solution identified by the algorithm is the basic solution (
1.2
).

Proof.

To prove the first part, it suffices to show that any candidate solutions omitted by the algorithm do not satisfy the equation.

First, Lemma
4
guarantees that any valid solution must satisfy
a_{i}\leq n
, which justifies restricting the maximum value of
a_{i}
in the loop to
m
.

Second, by Theorem
4
, if
p>s+n-i
, no solution
a
to (
1.1
) exists with initial terms
a_{1},\dots,a_{i}
. If
p=s+n-i
, then
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i},1,\dots,1)
is a solution. In both cases, extending the sequence further with terms
a_{i+1}>1
is unnecessary due to Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
, explaining why the corresponding branches terminate in the Algorithm.

For the base case
i=0
, we have
m=n
, so the for-loop runs from
a=2
to
a=n
. In particular, when
a_{1}=n
, we get
s=p=n
, which satisfies
p<s+(n-1)
. The recursive call proceeds, immediately assigning
a_{2}=2
and thereby finding the basic solution, then returning to index
i=1
, and since
a_{1}=n=m
, the loop concludes here.
∎

2.2
Relation to Nyblom’s Algorithm

Note that
[
4
]
recursively generates all sets
S_{r}(n)
for
2\leq r\leq m+1
, where
m\leq\log_{2}(n)+1
, and
S_{r}(n)
denotes the set of all solutions to (
1.1
) with exactly
r
coefficients satisfying
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\dots\geq a_{r}>1
.
Even in the case
r=2
, the construction of
S_{r}(n)
in
[
4
]
involves determining the prime factorization of
n-1
, for which no efficient (that is, of polynomial-time) algorithm is known. Although one could sidestep this issue in the special case
r=2
by selecting a larger integer
N>n
such that
N-1
is prime, this workaround becomes impractical for
r\neq 2
due to the significantly more complex divisibility conditions involved.

In contrast to
[
4
]
, our algorithm performs no explicit divisibility checks. However, like the method in
[
4
]
, it generates all solutions for
n^{\prime}<n
, but does so in a significantly more efficient manner. This claim is proved next (In the following, we adopt a simplified notation to describe the recursive step of the algorithm):

Theorem 2
.

Let
n\geq 3
. The algorithm efficiently generates all solutions of

\prod_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}a_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}a_{j}

(2.2)

for any
n^{\prime}<n
:

1.

Whenever
p^{\prime}<s^{\prime}+(n-i^{\prime})
, then
\widetilde{a}=(a_{1},a_{i},a^{\prime},1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{n^{\prime}}
with
n^{\prime}:=p^{\prime}-s^{\prime}+i^{\prime}
solves
(
2.2
), and
i\leq n^{\prime}<n
.

2.

The algorithm produces all positive solutions of (
2.2
) for any
n^{\prime}<n
.

3.

The algorithm is efficient in that no solution is produced twice.

Proof.

The first statement is just a reformulation of Lemma
1
below. Proof of Part
3
: Since the algorithm does not produce a finite sequence twice, and since the same sequence for which (
2.3
) holds cannot satisfy
equality in (
2.2
) for two different
n^{\prime}
, we have a one-to-one map between those sequences that satisfy (
2.3
) for
n
and the solutions of (
2.2
) for
n^{\prime}<n
.
∎

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i})
with
i\leq n
and
a_{i}>1
solves

\prod_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}<\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}+(n-i),

(2.3)

then
n^{\prime}:=\prod_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}+i
satisfies
i\leq n^{\prime}<n
and
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i},1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{n^{\prime}}
solves (
2.2
).)

Conversely, if for some
n^{\prime}<n
,
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i},1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{n^{\prime}}
solves (
2.2
), then
a
satisfies (
2.3
).

Proof.

Note that by definition of
n^{\prime}
,
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{i},1,\dots,1)
satisfies (

), and
n^{\prime}<n
, due to strict inequality in (

). Furthermore, since
i=n^{\prime}-(\prod_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{j})
, and since products of integers strictly greater than one exceed their sum, we have
i\leq n^{\prime}
.

The second part is trivial, (
2.2
) implies that
\prod_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}+(n^{\prime}-i)<\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{j}+(n-i)

as
n^{\prime}<n

∎

2.3
Efficiency

Finally, we establish the time-complexity of our algorithm. To have a simple measure of time-complexity, we count how many products

(p,s,i)
are checked in the algorithm, whether they satisfy
p<s+n-i
. We call this number,
the number of steps
\Theta(n)
.

Before we state the precise result, we give a rough estimate. By
[
1
, Boundedness proposition 1]
(see also
[
7
, Lemma 6.3]
), any combination
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
satisfying the inequality also fulfills the condition
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq 2n
. Therefore, the runs of our algorithm satisfy the same asymptotic upper bound, as the number of factorizations
h(l)
of an integer
l\geq 1
, where order is irrelevant and each factor is strictly greater than 1, satisfies the asymptotic estimate:

\sum_{l\leq 2n}h(l)=O\left(2n\cdot\frac{e^{2\sqrt{\log(2n)}}}{(\log(2n))^{3/4}}\right)

(2.4)

(cf.
[
5
, Eq (4.51)]
.)

However, we have a more precise result, whose proof does not require an application of Ecker’s Boundedness proposition and also gives asymptotics for a lower bound:
4
4

4

In the theorem, we use
x(n)=O(a(n))
in the following way: There exists constants
0<b<B
such that
b\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x(n)/a(n)\leq B
.
It rather counts the number of
all
solutions of (
2.2
) for any
n^{\prime}\leq n
:

Theorem 3
.

The number of steps
\Theta(n)
required to solve (
1.1
) is proportional to the number of solutions of (
2.2
), for any
n^{\prime}\leq n
, and thus satisfies the asymptotics (from above and below)

\Theta(n)=O\left(\frac{ne^{2\sqrt{\log(n)}}}{\log^{3/4}(n)}\right).

(2.5)

Proof.

We begin by counting all steps in the algorithm where
p^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime}+n-(i+1)
for the extended sequence
a^{\prime}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i},a_{i+1})
. When equality holds, we obtain a solution to (
1.1
). In the case of strict inequality, Lemma
1
implies that the sequence
a^{\prime}
forms a solution for some
i+1\leq n^{\prime}<n
. Moreover, by Theorem
2
, part
3
, such a solution for any
n^{\prime}
does not repeat. Consequently, according to
[
7
]
, the number of algorithmic steps satisfying

p^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime}+n-(i+1)
follows the asymptotic behavior described in (
2.5
).

The algorithm halts recursion at the first index
i
where
p^{\prime}>s^{\prime}+n-(i+1)
. Since the number of such runs is at most twice the number of non-repeating solutions of length
n^{\prime}\leq n
, the overall time complexity likewise satisfies the asymptotic bound in (
2.5
).
∎

If
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is a solution, then by equation (
6a
) in Section
1
, we have
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq 2n
. This bound does not explicitly appear in our algorithm, which raises the question of whether adding an extra pruning condition—namely, terminating a branch when
\prod>2n
—would improve efficiency. However, this turns out not to be the case: with or without the additional condition, the same number of branches are pruned.

To see this, suppose the recursive function is called with arguments
(s,p,i)
. Then

p=a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{i}<a_{1}+\dots+a_{i}+(n-i)=s+(n-i).

By
[
7
, Lemma 6 (iii)]
, if
p^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime}+n-(i+1)
, then the new product satisfies
p^{\prime}=pa_{i+1}\leq 2n
as well. (This bound for product-sum inequalities generalizes the result in (
6b
) due to Ecker that holds for product-sum equalities.) In other words, any branch not pruned by the original algorithm would also not be pruned by the additional condition, rendering the modification ineffective.

However, the number of function calls does not directly correspond to computation time. From a practical standpoint, checking the condition
\prod_{k=1}^{i}a_{k}>2n
is computationally slightly cheaper than evaluating
\prod_{k=1}^{i}a_{k}>\sum_{k=1}^{i}a_{k}+n-i
. Nonetheless, since the simpler condition is violated in only a small fraction of branches, the overall reduction in runtime is minimal. In fact, numerical experiments indicate that for
n\approx 10^{4}
, the additional pruning accounts for roughly 10% of cases, while for
n\approx 10^{6}
, the impact drops to about 5% and continues to decrease as
n
grows.

Fundamental Inequalities

In this section, we present some fundamental statements concerning integer sequences whose product equals their sum.
5
5

5

The problem of this paper is old and elementary, hence similar statements may be found in the literature, though not formulated in the same manner.

Theorem 4
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Assume that the sequence
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
with
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n}
satisfies equation (
1.1
). Then, for any
1\leq k\leq n
,

\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+n-k.

(3.1)

Let
k_{\star}
be the greatest integer
k
for which
a_{k}>1
. Then (
3.1
) holds as equality for
k\geq k_{\star}
,
and as strict inequality for
k<k_{\star}
.

Proof.

n\geq 2
implies that
k_{\star}\geq 2
(that is, any solution has at least two integer factors that are strictly greater than one). The first statement can be easily verified for
n=2
, where the only solution of (
1.1
) is
a=(2,2)
. Therefore, we assume
n\geq 3
for the rest of the statement.

By Definition of
k_{\star}
,
a_{k\star+1}=a_{k_{\star}+2}=\dots=a_{n}=1
. Therefore, (
3.1
) holds for
k\geq k_{\star}
with equality. Next we show that for
k<k_{\star}
, the inequality is strict. For
k=1
this is obviously true.

We proceed by induction, however by decreasing the index
k
(and thus,
k=1
is not the basis): To establish that the statement holds for the inductive basis
k=k_{\star}-1
, we compute

a_{k_{\star}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{k_{\star}}a_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\star}}a_{i}+n-k_{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+a_{k_{\star}}+(n-k)-1

(3.2)

and thus division by
a_{k_{\star}}
we get
6
6

6

Note that
s_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}>1
and
a_{k_{\star}}\geq 2
, hence also
s_{k}(a_{k_{\star}}-1)>a_{k_{\star}}-1
, whence
s_{k}+a_{k_{\star}}-1<s_{k}a_{k_{\star}}
and substituting this inequality into (
3.2
) yields (
3.3
).

\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}<\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+(n-k).

(3.3)

To make the inductive step, assume (
3.3
) holds for some
1<k<k_{\star}
. Then

a_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}<\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+(n-k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}+a_{k}+(n-k),

and division by
a_{k}
gives

\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}<\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}+1+(n-k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}+(n-(k-1)).

∎

The following two auxilliary statements, also rely on the fundamental inequality of Theorem
4
. The following statement in particular implies that by modifying the
1
’s in a solution of (
1.1
) no further solutions can be obtained.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
n>2
and
k<n
. If
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k},1,\dots,1)
satisfies
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+(n-k)
, then the extended sequence

a^{\prime}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k},a_{k+1}^{\prime},1,1,\dots,1)

with
a_{k+1}^{\prime}>1
satisfies
\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}a_{i}^{\prime}>\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}a_{i}^{\prime}+(n-k-1)
and thus does not solve (
1.1
).

Proof.

Note that
a_{1}<a_{1}+n-1
, hence the assumed inequality
\leq
implies that
k\geq 2
. Since
a_{k+1}^{\prime}\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}a_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=a_{k+1}^{\prime}\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\geq 2\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\geq 2\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+2(n-k)

\displaystyle>\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+a_{k}+2(n-k)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}a_{i}^{\prime}+(n-(k-1))

where the strict inequality holds because
k\geq 2
, and the last inequality holds, because
k<n
and
a_{k+1}^{\prime}\leq a_{k}
. Thus
a^{\prime}
does not satisfy the inequality (
3.1
). Hence
a^{\prime}
does not solve (
1.1
).
∎

A simpler modification is increasing solutions (in the sense of lexicographic order), without altering the length
k_{\star}
of the non-trivial part of the solution; also this modification does not yield any solution:

Lemma 3
.

Suppose
n>2
and
k<n
. If
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k},1,\dots,1)
satisfies
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+(n-k)
, then the modified sequence

a^{\prime}=(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{k}^{\prime},1,\dots,1)

with
a_{j}^{\prime}\geq a_{j}
for any
1\leq j\leq k
such that
a^{\prime}\neq a
, satisfies
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{\prime}>\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{\prime}+(n-k)
. In particular,
a^{\prime}
does not solve (
1.1
).

Proof.

By an inductive argument, it suffices to show that an increase of one
a_{j}
,
1\leq j<k
to
b=(a_{1},\dots,a_{j-1},(a_{j}+1),a_{j+1},\dots,a_{k})
(or, if
j=k
to
b=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1},(a_{k}+1)
) yields a strict inequality. First, since
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+(n-k)
, at least one
a_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
(
i\neq j
) must be strictly larger than
1
, and thus

\frac{1}{a_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=\prod_{i\neq j}a_{i}>1
. Hence,

\prod_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+\frac{1}{a_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}>\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+(n-k)+1=\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}+(n-k).

Thus
a^{\prime}
does not satisfy the inequality (
3.1
). Therefore,
a^{\prime}
cannot be a solution of (
1.1
).
∎

The third, important fact limits the range for the solutions’ summands
a_{i}
, (cf. the
[
1
, Boundedness Proposition 1 and 2]
). Here is an independent proof that uses Theorem
4
.

Lemma 4
.

Any solution
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
of (
1.1
) satisfies
a_{i}\leq n
. Any solution, except the basic one (
a_{1}=n,a_{2}=2
) satisfies
a_{i}<n
(and hence
a_{1}<n
).

Proof.

Since
a_{i}
is a non-increasing sequence, it suffices to show that
a_{1}\leq n
. Suppose, for a contradiction, that
a_{1}>n
, say
a_{1}=n+r
for some
r\geq 1
. By Theorem
4
, we have for
k=2
the inequality

(n+r)a_{2}\leq n+r+a_{2}+(n-2)

implying

0\leq n(a_{2}-2)\leq-(a_{2}-1)(r-1)-1<0,

an impossibility, whence indeed
a_{i}\leq n
.

Clearly, the basic solution satisfies
a_{1}=n
. Assume now
a_{1}=n
and
k_{\star}=2
, then
na_{2}=n+a_{2}+n-2
, whence
a_{2}=2
, which means,
a
is the basic solution.

Assume now
k_{\star}\geq 3
, and for some
i\geq 1
,
a_{i}=n
. Then
a_{1}=n
. If
a_{2}=2
, then the solution
a
would be obtained from the basic solution by appending an integer
a_{3}\geq 2
(and
k_{\star}-3
further integers greater than one), which is in contradiction with Lemma
2
. Thus
a_{2}>2
and by Theorem
4
, we get the strict inequality for
k=2
, that is,

na_{2}<n+a_{2}+n-2

which implies
n(a_{2}-2)<a_{2}-2
, hence
n<1
. Thus
a_{1}<n
, as claimed.
∎

The final statement – that is however not used in verifing the validity of the algorithm – demonstrates that solutions of (
1.1
) have very few coefficients strictly larger than one.

Lemma 5
.

Let
n\geq 3
and let
a
satisfy the inequality

\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}.

(3.4)

For
2\leq m\leq n
, let
k_{\star,m}
be the greatest integer
k
for which
a_{k}=m
. Then

k_{\star,m}<\log_{m}(n)+1.

(3.5)

Remark 1
.

This statement pertains to the efficiency of our algorithm. For
m=2
,
k_{\star,2}
denotes the exact number of coefficients
a_{i}
in solutions
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
of (
1.1
) that are strictly greater than 1. Since the algorithm terminates as soon as the product exceeds the sum, it never explores sequences containing more than
\lceil k_{\star,2}\rceil+1
such elements.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

First, we claim that for
n\geq 3
,
k_{\star,2}<n
(and thus
k_{\star,m}<n
for any
m>1
). In fact, this can be directly checked to hold for
3\leq n\leq 4
. Furthermore, for
n\geq 5
, we know by induction that
2^{n}>n^{2}
. Assume, for a contradiction,
k_{\star,2}=n
. Then
2^{n}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq n^{2}
, which is impossible.

Thus we have to prove that if the product of
n\geq 3
natural numbers
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n}\geq 1
satisfies (
3.4
) and precisely
1\leq k<n
of them are greater than or equal to
m
:

\displaystyle a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{k}\geq m

(3.6)

\displaystyle m>a_{k+1}\geq a_{k+2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n}

(3.7)

Then:

k\leq\left\lceil\log_{m}n\right\rceil.

Proof: Since
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{k}
, we have:

a_{1}\ a_{k}^{k-1}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}

(3.8)

From (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), we have:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}<a_{1}\ k+m\ (n-k).

(3.9)

Substituting this into (
3.8
) we get:

a_{1}\ a_{k}^{k-1}<a_{1}\ k+m\ (n-k).

Dividing both sides by
a_{1}
, we get:

a_{k}^{k-1}<k+\frac{m}{a_{1}}\ (n-k)\leq n,

the last inequality holding due to
a_{1}\geq m
. Thus,

k-1<\log_{a_{k}}n

From (
3.6
), we know that
a_{k}\geq m
, thus
k<\log_{m}n+1
.
∎
