What’s my phase again? Computing the vacuum-to-vacuum amplitude of quadratic bosonic evolution
Nicolás Quesada

Abstract
Quadratic bosonic Hamiltonians and their associated unitary transformations form a fundamental class of operations in quantum optics, modeling key processes such as squeezing, displacement, and beam-splitting. Their Heisenberg-picture dynamics simplifies to linear (or possibly affine) transformations on quadrature operators, enabling efficient analysis and decomposition into optical gate sets using matrix operations. However, this formalism discards a phase, which, while often neglected, is essential for a complete unitary characterization. We present efficient methods to recover this phase directly from the vacuum-to-vacuum amplitude of the unitary, using calculations that scale polynomially with the number of modes and avoid Fock space manipulations. We reduce to integration the general problem, including the case of time-dependent Hamiltonians, and provide analytical results for key cases including when the Hamiltonian is positive definite, passive, active, or single-mode.



I
Introduction

Quadratic bosonic Hamiltonians and their associated Gaussian unitaries are among the most widely studied operations in quantum optics
Serafini 2023
. They describe fundamental processes such as mode squeezing and displacement, as well as beam splitting, phase and controlled-phase operations in the continuous-variable model of quantum computation
Lloyd and Braunstein 1999
and certain discrete-variable quantum error correction encodings
Gottesman et al. 2001
. These transformations can be efficiently analyzed in the Heisenberg picture, where their action on the quadrature operators reduces to linear (or possibly affine) transformations implemented via symplectic matrices (and possibly displacement vectors).

This symplectic formalism enables the decomposition of such transformations into optical gate sets—e.g., beam splitters, squeezers, and displacements—through purely finite-dimensional matrix operations
Braunstein 2005
;
Reck et al. 1994
;
Clements et al. 2016
;
de Guise et al. 2018
;
Kumar and Dhand 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
López Pastor et al. 2021
;
Bell and Walmsley 2021
;
Arends et al. 2024
;
Yasir and van
Loock 2025
;
Pereira et al. 2025
;
Saygin et al. 2020
;
Idel and Wolf 2015
. However, a key limitation of this approach is the loss of phase information: two unitaries that act identically on quadratures may still differ by a physically meaningful phase. One can use Schur’s lemma(cf. Appendices A and B of Ballentine
Ballentine 2014
) to formalize this observation by stating that such unitaries differ by at most a phase when their action is indistinguishable on an irreducible set of operators, such as the bosonic quadratures.

This phase, although often neglected, plays an essential role in several contexts: it appears in superpositions of Gaussian states
Yao et al. 2024
;
Marshall and Anand 2023
;
Bourassa et al. 2021
;
Tara et al. 1993
and in controlled-unitary operations relevant to relativistic quantum information
Sachs et al. 2017
;
Funai 2025
, and it is also important for the development of quantum information technologies
Didier et al. 2015
;
Zhu et al. 2013
. Furthermore, accurate phase accounting is necessary when Gaussian unitaries are combined into larger computations or used as components in approximating non-Gaussian states and processes
Yao et al. 2024
;
Motamedi et al. 2025
;
Dias and König 2024
.

We should mention that related results have been very recently derived by Guaita et al.
Guaita et al. 2024
using tools from representation theory for time-independent (fermionic and bosonic) Hamiltonians. Similarly, Funai
Funai 2025
has addressed related problems in the context of quantum fields for a set of restricted Hamiltonians in quantum field theory. Our results address the problem using linear algebra tools and generalize to time-dependent problems.

In this work, we present efficient methods to recover the phase of quadratic unitaries by computing their vacuum-to-vacuum amplitude. These methods scale polynomially with the number of modes and entirely avoid manipulations in the infinite-dimensional Fock space. We provide analytical results for special cases
—when the Hamiltonian is positive definite, passive, active, or single-mode— and
show how the general multimode problem can be reduced to integration.

The manuscript is structured as follows. Sec.
II
formalizes the problem and sets up the notation. Sec.
III
presents an analytical method based on the Williamson decomposition for the case of positive-definite Hamiltonian matrices. The general case is addressed in Sec.
IV
, where we show how the vacuum amplitude can be obtained via a reduction to linear differential equations. Analytical results are provided for key limiting cases, and concluding remarks, including a discussion of time-dependent problems, follow in Sec.
V
.

II
Notation and Problem statement

We setup some basic notation before stating the problem. The vector of quadrature operators of an
M
-mode system is
Serafini 2023

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{r}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{x}}\oplus\hat{\bm{p}}=(\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{M},\hat{p}_{1},\ldots,\hat{p}_{M})^{T}.

(1)

These operators satisfy the canonical commutation relations
[\hat{r}_{j},\hat{r}_{k}]=i\hbar\Omega_{jk}
where
\bm{\Omega}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&\ \mathbb{I}_{M}\\
-\mathbb{I}_{M}&\ 0\end{smallmatrix}\right),

is the so-called symplectic matrix, and
\mathbb{I}_{M}
is the
M
-dimensional identity matrix.
It is also useful to introduce annihilation and creation (collectively ladder) operators for mode
j

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hbar}}(\hat{x}_{j}+i\hat{p}_{j}),\quad\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hbar}}(\hat{x}_{j}-i\hat{p}_{j}).

(2)

Finally, the multimode vacuum state
\ket{\bm{0}}=\ket{0}_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\ket{0}_{M}
as the state that satisfies
\hat{a}_{j}\ket{\bm{0}}=0\ \forall j
. Note that we use boldface for vectors and matrices and use hats for operators in Fock space.

The question that concerns this manuscript is motivated by the following problem: given a quadratic Hamiltonian in quadrature operators
Serafini 2023
, specified by an even-dimensional symmetric matrix
\bm{H}
(the case where the Hamiltonian has linear terms in the quadrature operators is analyzed in Appendix
A
),

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\tfrac{1}{2\hbar}\hat{\bm{r}}^{T}\bm{H}\hat{\bm{r}}=\tfrac{1}{2\hbar}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{l=1}^{M}H_{kl}\hat{r}_{k}\hat{r}_{l},

(3)

how can one factor its associated unitary operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}=\exp(-i\hat{\mathcal{H}}t)=\exp\left(-\tfrac{i}{2\hbar}\hat{\bm{r}}^{T}\bm{H}\hat{\bm{r}}t\right)

(4)

into simple one- and two-mode gates, such as squeezing and beamsplitter operations?

Significant progress on this problem can be obtained by looking at the action of the operator in Eq. (
4
) in the Heisenberg picture. Defining

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{r}}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{U}}\hat{\bm{r}}\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\bm{r}}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{\Omega}\bm{H}\hat{\bm{r}}(t)\longrightarrow\hat{\bm{r}}(t)=\underbrace{\exp(\bm{\Omega}\bm{H}t)}_{\bm{S}}\bm{r}.

(6)

It is straightforward to verify that the matrix
\bm{S}
is symplectic, satisfying
\bm{S}\bm{\Omega}\bm{S}^{T}=\bm{\Omega}
.

One may invoke Schur’s lemma (cf. Appendices A and B of Ballantine
Ballentine 2014
) to argue that any sequence of unitary operations
\hat{\mathcal{V}}
that achieves

\hat{\mathcal{V}}\bm{r}\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\dagger}=\bm{S}\bm{r}
must be equal to
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
up to a phase. Typically, this sequence is obtained by combining passive operations, which do not change the total photon number, and active operations in the form of squeezing
1
1

1

The precise way to obtain this sequence using the Bloch-Messiah decomposition of the symplectic matrix associated with the unitary transformation
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
is discussed in Sec. 4.1 of Ref.
Kalajdzievski and Quesada 2021
. A self contained discussion of how to obtain the Bloch-Messiah decomposition of a symplectic matrix can be found in Ref.
Houde et al. 2024
.
. The method just described is successful at finding a suitable sequence of gates to decompose the action of a quadratic Hamiltonian but is of course unable to account for the phase in the argument just presented. This motivates the main problem addressed by this manuscript, how to obtain this phase which cannot be accounted for once one considers evolution in the Heisenberg picture Eq. (
5
).

To give an example of when this phase is non-trivial consider the single-mode quadratic-phase gate

\displaystyle\hat{P}(t)=\exp(it\hat{x}^{2}/2\hbar),

(7)

from which we read
\bm{H}=-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and find

\displaystyle\bm{S}

\displaystyle=\exp(\bm{\Omega}\bm{H}t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
t&1\end{pmatrix}

(8)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{r}&0\\
0&e^{-r}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\tilde{\theta}&-\sin\tilde{\theta}\\
\sin\tilde{\theta}&\cos\tilde{\theta}\end{pmatrix},

(9)

where the parameters above are related to
t
as follows
\sinh r=t/2,\ \theta=\atan 2(e^{r},1),\ \tilde{\theta}=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta
(cf. Eq. 41 of Ref.
Kalajdzievski and Quesada 2021
).
The Bloch-Messiah decomposition above gives the following sequence in Fock space

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\right)\exp(\tfrac{r}{2}(\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}-\hat{a}^{2}))\exp\left(i\tilde{\theta}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\right).

(10)

It is easy to see that the equation above does not correctly capture the phase of the gate as

\displaystyle\braket{0|\hat{\mathcal{V}}|0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cosh r}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{1+\tfrac{t^{2}}{4}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

(11)

while for the actual gate one can use that
\braket{0|x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{\pi\hbar}}\exp(-x^{2}/(2\hbar))
and that the (single-mode) identity operator can be resolved in terms of eigenkets of the position operator
\mathbb{1}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\ket{x}\bra{x}
to find

\displaystyle\braket{0|\hat{P}(t)|0}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\braket{0|P(t)|x}\braket{x|0}

(12)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\braket{0|x}\exp\left(i\frac{t}{2\hbar}x^{2}\right)\braket{x|0}

(13)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\exp\left(i\frac{t}{2\hbar}x^{2}\right)|\braket{x|0}|^{2}

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-i\tfrac{t}{2}}}\in\mathbb{C}.

(15)

We see that the phase we are after is also the phase associated with the vacuum-to-vacuum amplitude of the unitary in Eq. (
4
), and we can write
\sqrt[4]{1+\tfrac{t^{2}}{4}}/\sqrt{1-i\tfrac{t}{2}}
as the correction to the decomposition in Eq. (
10
).

In the rest of the manuscript we show how the amplitude and phase

\displaystyle\alpha=\braket{\bm{0}|\exp\left(-\tfrac{i}{2\hbar}\hat{\bm{r}}\bm{H}\hat{\bm{r}}t\right)|\bm{0}},\ e^{i\varphi}=\alpha/|\alpha|,

(16)

can be obtained.
Note that the absolute value of this amplitude, the vacuum-to-vacuum
probability
, is easy to find. To see this simply note

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}=\bra{\bm{0}}\hat{\mathcal{U}}\ket{\bm{0}}\bra{\bm{0}}\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}\ket{\bm{0}}.

(17)

This can be interpreted as the fidelity between two pure Gaussian states, namely
\hat{\mathcal{U}}\ket{\bm{0}}\bra{\bm{0}}\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}
and
\ket{\bm{0}}\bra{\bm{0}}
, for which we can use phase-space methods to obtain the probability
Brask 2021

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\left[\frac{1}{2}(\bm{S}\bm{S}^{T}+\mathbb{I}_{2M})\right]}},

(18)

which is also proportional to the value of the Husimi Q-function of the Gaussian state
\hat{\mathcal{U}}\ket{\bm{0}}\bra{\bm{0}}\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}
with Husimi covariance matrix
\frac{1}{2}(\bm{S}\bm{S}^{T}+\mathbb{I}_{2M})
evaluated at the origin.

Finally, note that with the tools we develop below one can also calculate directly the vacuum-to-vacuum amplitude of any Gaussian state, which is obtained from the vacuum by application of a Gaussian unitary
\ket{\psi}=\hat{\mathcal{Y}}\ket{\bm{0}}
since we can always do

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}\hat{\mathcal{U}}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\bra{\bm{0}}\hat{\mathcal{Y}}^{\dagger}\exp\left(-\tfrac{i}{2\hbar}\hat{\bm{r}}^{T}\bm{H}\hat{\bm{r}}t\right)\hat{\mathcal{Y}}\ket{\bm{0}}

(19)

\displaystyle=\bra{\bm{0}}\exp\left(-\tfrac{i}{2\hbar}\hat{\mathcal{Y}}^{\dagger}\hat{\bm{r}}^{T}\bm{H}\hat{\bm{r}}\hat{\mathcal{Y}}t\right)\ket{\bm{0}}

(20)

\displaystyle=\bra{\bm{0}}\exp\left(-\tfrac{i}{2\hbar}\hat{\bm{r}}^{T}\underbrace{\bm{M}^{T}\bm{H}\bm{M}}_{\equiv\bm{H^{\prime}}}\hat{\bm{r}}t\right)\ket{\bm{0}}

(21)

where we used that
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}^{\dagger}\hat{\bm{r}}\hat{\mathcal{Y}}=\bm{M}\hat{\bm{r}}
with
\bm{M}
a symplectic matrix. Thus the calculation of the amplitude for an arbitrary Gaussian state reduces to the one of the vacuum-to-vacuum amplitude of a different Hamiltonian.

IV
General Hamiltonians

To express the solution for an arbitrary Hamiltonian we would like to express

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}=

\displaystyle:\hat{\mathcal{H}}:=

Final remarks

We have analyzed the vacuum-to-vacuum amplitude associated with unitaries generated by quadratic bosonic Hamiltonians. For Hamiltonians with positive-definite matrices, we derived a closed-form expression for this amplitude by leveraging recent results on the photon-number characteristic function of Gaussian states
