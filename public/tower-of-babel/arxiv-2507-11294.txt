Markov approximation for controlled Hawkes Jump-Diffusions with general kernels
Mahmoud Khabou, Mehdi Talbi

Abstract
We present a Markov approximation for jump-diffusions whose jump part consists in a Hawkes process with intensity driven by a general (possibly non-monotone) kernel. Under minimal integrability conditions, the kernel can be approximated by a linear combination of exponential functions. This implies that Hawkes jump-diffusions can be approximated with Markov jump-diffusions. We illustrate the usefulness of this approximation by applying it to a class of stochastic control problems.



Keywords.
Jump-diffusion stochastic differential equations, Hawkes process, Stochastic control, Markov approximation.

This approximation is particularly useful in stochastic control, as it allows to characterize the (approximated) value function with as the solution of a partial differential equation.

Preliminary results

Throughout the paper, we denote by

\hat{\Omega}^{\Pi}=\Big\{\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(t_{i},\theta_{i},y_{i})},0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n},(\theta_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}},n\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{+\infty\}\Big\}.

Let

We denote by

the Poisson measure under which the counting process

The third component

If the points hold a constant non-zero mark (say

(1.2)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=(x_{0}^{1},\cdots,x^{d}_{0})

The functional coefficients

Naturally, due the this positive feedback loop in the positive kernel case, there is a risk of “explosion” if a mutual cross-excitation or a self-excitation has a high intensity or is too persistent in time. To avoid such a phenomenon, we introduce the stability assumption
1.2

Furthermore,

The jump rate

Continuity with respect to the kernel

3
The Markov approximation of Hawkes jump-diffusions

Due to the presence of the convolution in the intensity’s definition in Equation (
1.2
), the stochastic process
X
-as well as
(X,\lambda)
- is not a Markov process. This means that the standard techniques used for stochastic control no longer apply.

However, we can show that if the kernel belongs to a certain family of
\mathbb{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})\cap\mathbb{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
functions, one can retrieve the Markov property , albeit at the cost of adding auxiliary processes.

A function
\phi
is called a sum of exponentials if there exists a positive integer
n
, a set of coefficients
(\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and of exponents
(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n}
such that

\phi(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}e^{-\beta_{k}t},\quad\text{for all }t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(3.1)

Without loss of generality, we assume that
0<\beta_{1}<\cdots<\beta_{n}
.

We now take
(X,\lambda)
to be a solution of (
1.2
), with
\phi
defined by (
3.1
). In this case, the intensity takes the form

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\infty}(t,X_{t-})+\psi\left(\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\phi(t-s)b(y)\nu(s,X_{s-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\infty}(t,X_{t-})+\psi\left(\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}e^{-\beta_{k}(t-s)}b(y)\nu(s,X_{s-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\infty}(t,X_{t-})+\psi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\xi^{k}_{t}\right),

where the auxiliary processes
(\xi^{j}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
are defined by

\xi^{j}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-\beta_{j}(t-s)}b(y)\nu(s,X_{s-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y).

(3.2)

Thanks to the flow property of the exponential function, we have that

e^{\beta_{j}t}\xi^{j}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}e^{\beta_{j}s}b(y)\nu(s,X_{s-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y),

which by differentiation yields

{\operatorname{d}}\xi^{j}_{t}+\beta_{j}\xi^{j}_{t}{\operatorname{d}}t=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}b(y)\nu(t,X_{t-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{t}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}t,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y).

Therefore, the augmented process
(X,\xi^{1},\cdots,\xi^{n})
solves the first order SDE

\begin{cases}{\operatorname{d}}X_{t}=&\mu(t,X_{t}){\operatorname{d}}t+\sigma(t,X_{t}){\operatorname{d}}W_{t}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}y\gamma(t,X_{t-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{\infty}(t,X_{t-})+\psi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\xi^{k}_{t}\right)}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}t,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)\\
{\operatorname{d}}\xi^{1}_{t}=&-\beta_{1}\xi_{t}^{1}{\operatorname{d}}t+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}b(y)\nu(t,X_{t-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{\infty}(t,X_{t-})+\psi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\xi_{t}^{k}\right)}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}t,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)\\
\vdots\\
{\operatorname{d}}\xi^{n}_{t}=&-\beta_{n}\xi_{t}^{n}{\operatorname{d}}t+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}b(y)\nu(t,X_{t-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{\infty}(t,X_{t-})+\psi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\xi_{t}^{k}\right)}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}t,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)\\
\end{cases}.

(3.3)

Such a process is therefore a Markov process whose infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{A}
defined for any smooth function
g:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}g(t,x,\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n}):=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}+\mu(t,x)\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}\xi_{k}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\xi_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t,x)\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial x^{2}}

\displaystyle+\left(\lambda_{\infty}(t,x)+\psi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\xi_{k}\right)\right)\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}g\left(t,x+\gamma(t,x)y,\xi_{1}+\nu(t,x)b(y),\cdots,\xi_{n}+\nu(t,x)b(y)\right)

\displaystyle-g(t,x,\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n})m({\operatorname{d}}y)\bigg).

For such dynamics, it is then possible to write the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman for the augmented process
(X,\xi^{1},\cdots,\xi^{n})
and one can use the classical techniques for stochastic control on jump-diffusion equations.

We now show that such processes are universal approximators of jump-diffusion processes driven by Hawkes processes. Similarly to observation 2.9 in
[
27
]

[
27
]
, this is based on the following two facts:

•

Sums of exponentials are universal approximators of integrable functions, and not only of completely monotonous ones.

•

Hawkes jump-diffusions are continuous with respect to their kernel.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
are in force. Given a time horizon
T>0
, let
(X,\lambda)
be a solution of the SDE (
1.2
) on
[0,T]
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and a
n+1
-dimensional Markov process
(\tilde{X},\tilde{\xi}^{1},\cdots,\tilde{\xi}^{n})
that follows the dynamics (
3.3
) such that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\left|X_{s}-\tilde{X}_{s}\right|\right]\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

According to Theorem
2.4
, there exist positive
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for any solution of (
1.2
) with the same parameters and a different kernel
\tilde{\phi}
satisfying Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
we have that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\left|X_{s}-\tilde{X}_{s}\right|\right]\leq C_{1}\|\phi-\tilde{\phi}\|_{1}e^{C_{2}T}.

According to the corollary of Theorem 1 in
[
39
]
or Theorem 1 in
[
6
]
, for any
\beta>0
the set of finite linear combinations of exponentials
\left\{\left(e^{-\beta kt}\right)_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
is dense in
\mathbb{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
for any
p\geq 1
. Thus, we can find a function

\tilde{\phi}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}e^{-\beta kt}

such that

\|\phi-\tilde{\phi}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\varepsilon e^{-C_{2}T}}{C_{1}},

hence the result.
∎

In practice, the approximation of a given kernel
\phi\in\mathbb{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
can be approached in the following way:

•

While in theory the linear combinations of
\left\{\left(e^{-\beta kt}\right)_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
is dense in
\mathbb{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
for any fixed choice
\beta>0
, this quantity be seen as a decay parameter that determines the length of the time span after which the effect of a past event becomes negligible. This means that a more appropriate choice of
\beta
will lead to a better approximation with fewer exponentials.

•

Once
\beta>0
is fixed, we choose the number
n\geq 1
of approximating exponentials. Naturally a higher number of terms has the potential to better approximate the kernel
\phi
, but comes at the price of increased complexity. For instance, the cost of simulation of the dynamics (
3.3
) is of order
\mathcal{O}(nT)
. This is still cheaper than the
\mathcal{O}(T^{2})
needed to simulate the original SDE (
1.2
).

•

We then optimise the convex function

J(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}e^{-\beta kt}-\phi(t)\right|{\operatorname{d}}t.

This can be done either:

1.

Directly numerically, for instance using the method
scipy.optimize.minimize
coupled with the preferred quadrature method in Python.

2.

By optimising the function

I(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}e^{-\beta kt}-\phi(t)\right)^{2}{\operatorname{d}}t

which has advantage of being differentiable. In fact, the equation
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}I(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\boldsymbol{0}
has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}
which can be determined by inverting the system

M_{H}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}=\boldsymbol{v}

where
\boldsymbol{v}=\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\beta jt}\phi(t){\operatorname{d}}t\right)_{j=1,\cdots,n}
and
M_{H}=\left(\frac{1}{\beta(i+j)}\right)_{i,j=1,\cdots,n}
is a modified Hilbert matrix.

We then use the fact that
J(\boldsymbol{\eta})\leq I(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{1/2}
(an immediate application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality) to obtain an approximation in
\mathbb{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
and
\mathbb{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
.

•

If the desired precision is not obtained,
n
should be increased and a new set of coefficients
\boldsymbol{\eta}
should be computed. We point out that the exponential functions are not orthogonal in
\mathbb{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
.

•

We do not optimise over
\beta>0
because of the lack of convexity. This means that if we optimise on
\beta
, the numerical methods are no longer guaranteed to converge to the global minimiser.

Remark 3.2
.

It is also possible to prove that for any
\beta>0
, the span of
\left\{x^{k}e^{-\beta x}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
is also dense in
\mathbb{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})\cap\mathbb{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, cf.
[
6
]
for instance. This family also has the Markov property, due to the fact that

satisfies the first order ODE

\frac{{\operatorname{d}}}{{\operatorname{d}}x}f_{k}(x)=kf_{k-1}(x)-\beta f_{k}(x).

The curious reader can also adapt the approach introduced by Beylkin and Monzón in
[
16
]
to exponential sums with real coefficients and exponents. This method might have the advantage of providing a more accurate approximation as it also optimises on the decay parameters
(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 1}
.

To illustrate the Markov approximation discussed above, we consider the following modified Hawkes-Ornstein-Uhlenbeck SDE

(3.5)

where

,

,

,

,

and

.
The kernel is chosen to be

which is not monotonous (

not completely monotonous) and exhibits both excitation and inhibition as can be seen in Figure

and

which gurantees that the exponential functions become negligible for

(we assume that any quantity below

is negligible). We then approximate

using the methodology explained above, yielding

and

Given a Brownian motion

4
Application to stochastic control of Hawkes jump-diffusions

4.1
The control problem

In this paragraph, we denote by
\mathbb{F}:=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
the filtration generated by
(W,\Pi)
.
Let
A
be a Polish space.
Given
\alpha:[0,T]\times\Omega\longrightarrow A
, we then consider the controlled version of the dynamics (
1.2
):

\begin{cases}X_{t}&=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(s,X_{s},\alpha_{s}){\operatorname{d}}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s},\alpha_{s}){\operatorname{d}}W_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}y\gamma(s,X_{s-},\alpha_{s-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)\\
\lambda_{t}&=\lambda_{\infty}(t,X_{t-})+\psi\left(\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\phi(t-s)b(y)\nu(s,X_{s-},\alpha_{s-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)\right),\end{cases}

(4.1)

where we omit to write the dependence of
(X,\lambda)
in
\alpha
for notational simplicity. We denote by
\mathcal{A}
the set of
\mathbb{F}
-adapted processes taking their values in
A
such that, for all
\varphi\in\{\mu,\sigma,\gamma,\nu\}
and all control
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, the mapping
(t,x,\omega)\mapsto\varphi(t,x,\alpha_{t}(\omega))
satisfies Assumptions
1.1
-
1.3
. In particular, for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, the equation (
4.1
) admits a unique solution,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., and we have:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X_{T}|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}\quad\mbox{for all}\ p\geq 1,\quad\mbox{with}\ C_{p}\ \mbox{independent of}\ \alpha.

(4.2)

Now, given
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the optimal control problem:

V_{0}:=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t},\alpha_{t}){\operatorname{d}}t+g(X_{T})\Big]

(4.3)

As said in the Introduction, due to the possibly nonexponential kernel in the dynamics of the intensity
\lambda
, (
4.3
) is a Volterra-type control problem, and therefore cannot be solved through the standard dynamic programming techniques. This motivates us to introduce a Markov approximation of
(X,\lambda)
.

4.2
The Markov approximation

Let
\phi^{n}(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}e^{-\beta_{k}t}
be such that
\lVert\phi^{n}-\phi\rVert_{1}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
. We denote by
(X^{n},\lambda^{n})
the controlled Hawkes jump-diffusion (
4.1
) driven by the kernel
\phi^{n}
instead of
\phi
. We consider the control problem:

V_{0}^{n}:=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t}^{n},\alpha_{t}){\operatorname{d}}t+g(X_{T}^{n})\right].

(4.4)

As seen in Section
3
, introducing the processes
(\xi^{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
defined in (
3.2
), we have

\lambda_{t}^{n}=\lambda_{\infty}(t,X_{t-})+\psi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\xi_{t}^{k}\right),

and the dynamics
(X^{n},\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{n})
is actually a Markov jump-diffusion, which allows us to analyze the control problem (
4.4
) using the dynamic programming approach. In particular, under mild regularity assumptions, it is standard to verify that
V_{0}^{n}=v(0,X_{0},0,\dots,0)
, where
v:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a viscosity solution to the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation:

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}v(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})-\mathcal{H}[v](t,x,\boldsymbol{\xi})=0,\quad v|_{t=T}=g,

where:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}[v](t,x,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\sup_{a\in A}\Big\{

\displaystyle\mu(t,x,a)\partial_{x}v(t,x,\boldsymbol{\xi})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t,x,a)\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x,\boldsymbol{\xi})-(\boldsymbol{\beta\xi})\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}v(t,x,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle+\big(\lambda_{\infty}(t,x)+\psi(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\big)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\big(v(t,x+\gamma(t,x,a)y,\boldsymbol{\xi}+\nu(t,x,a)b(y)\boldsymbol{1})-v(t,x,\boldsymbol{\xi})\big)m({\operatorname{d}}y)\Big\},

with the notations
\boldsymbol{y}:=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{1}=(1,\dots,1)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We refer to
[
43
]

[
43
]
for more information on the control of Markov jump-diffusion processes.

We now state the main result of this paragraph:

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
f
and
g
are continuous with polynomial growth in
x
of order
m\geq 1
, uniformly in
(t,a)
. Then we have:

V_{0}^{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}V_{0}.

In particular, if
f
is Lipschitz-continuous in
x
, uniformly in the other variables, and
g
is Lipschitz-continuous, we have:

|V_{0}^{n}-V_{0}|\leq\lVert\phi^{n}-\phi\rVert_{1}C_{1}e^{C_{2}T}

for some nonnegative constants
C_{1},C_{2}
.

Proof.

The proof can be found in Section
5.4
.
∎

4.3
Example: portfolio optimization in markets with contagion

We revisit the study of
[
4
]

[
4
]
, who analyzed the problem of optimization a portfolio driven by a Hawkes jump-diffusion process. In their framework, the intensity of the Hawkes process is driven by an exponential kernel, which induces a Markov dynamics. We presently discuss the case of a general nonexponential kernel.

General utility.

Consider a risky asset
S
driven by the following dynamics:

\frac{{\operatorname{d}}S_{t}}{S_{t-}}=\mu{\operatorname{d}}t+\sigma{\operatorname{d}}W_{t}+\gamma{\operatorname{d}}N_{t},

where
{\operatorname{d}}N_{t}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\theta\leq\lambda_{t}\}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}t,{\operatorname{d}}\theta)
and the intensity
\lambda
satisfies:

\lambda_{t}=\lambda_{0}+\int_{0}^{t-}\phi(t-s){\operatorname{d}}N_{s},

(4.5)

where
W
is a standard Brownian motion,
\Pi
an independent Poisson measure and
\phi\in\mathbb{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. An investor holds a proportion
\omega_{t}\in[0,1]
of his capital in the risky asset
S
at time
t
. The remaining proportion
1-\omega_{t}
is invested in a risk-free asset, which evolves according to a constant interest rate
r
. At time
t
, the investor also consumes an amount
c_{t}X_{t}{\operatorname{d}}t
of his capital. His wealth is then represented by the dynamics:

\frac{{\operatorname{d}}X_{t}}{X_{t-}}=(r+(\mu-r)\omega_{t}-c_{t}){\operatorname{d}}t+\sigma\omega_{t}{\operatorname{d}}W_{t}+\gamma\omega_{t}{\operatorname{d}}N_{t},\quad X_{0}>0.

(4.6)

We denote by
\mathcal{A}
the set of
\mathbb{F}
-predictable processes
(c,\omega)
such that
c_{t}>0
and
\omega_{t}\in[0,1]
a.s. for all
t\in[0,T]
. The investor wants to choose his consumption
c
and his investment strategy
\omega
in order to solve:

V_{0}:=\sup_{(c,\omega)\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}U(c_{t}X_{t}){\operatorname{d}}t\Big],

(4.7)

with
\rho>0
and
U:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
some utility function. Since
\phi
is a general nonexponential kernel, the above control problem is path-dependent, and therefore dynamic programming cannot be applied directly. According to Theorem
3.1
, we then introduce a sequence of kernels
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
such that:

\lVert\phi^{n}-\phi\rVert_{1}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\phi^{n}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}e^{-\beta_{k}t}.

Denote by
X^{n}
the solution of (
4.6
) corresponding to the kernel
\phi^{n}
, and introduce the processes

\xi_{t}^{i}=\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}e^{-\beta_{i}(t-s)}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}^{n}\}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta),\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

with
\lambda_{t}^{n}
solution to (
4.5
) with kernel
\phi^{n}
. Then, the system
(X^{n},\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{n})
satisfies the Markovian jump-diffusion dynamics:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{{\operatorname{d}}X_{t}^{n}}{X_{t-}^{n}}=(r+(\mu-r)\omega_{t}-c_{t}){\operatorname{d}}t+\sigma\omega_{t}{\operatorname{d}}W_{t}+\gamma\omega_{t}{\operatorname{d}}N_{t}^{n},\quad X_{0}^{n}=X_{0},\\
{\operatorname{d}}\xi_{t}^{i}=-\beta_{i}\xi_{t}^{i}{\operatorname{d}}t+{\operatorname{d}}N_{t}^{n},\quad\xi_{0}^{i}=0,\quad 1\leq i\leq n,\end{array}\right.

(4.8)

with
N^{n}
a Poisson process of intensity
\lambda_{t}^{n}=\lambda_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\xi_{t}^{k}
. The corresponding investor’s problem writes:

V_{0}^{n}:=\sup_{(c,\omega)\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}U(c_{t}X_{t}^{n}){\operatorname{d}}t\Big].

(4.9)

Since
(X^{n},\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{n})
is Markov, we formally have
V_{0}=v(X_{0}^{n},0,\dots,0)
, with
v:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
solution to the dynamic programming equation:

-\mathcal{H}[v](x,\boldsymbol{\xi})=0,

(4.10)

where:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}[v](x,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\sup_{c\geq 0,\omega\in[0,1]}\Big\{

\displaystyle(r+(\mu-r)\omega-c)x\partial_{x}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\xi})\cdot\nabla_{\xi}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})-\rho v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle+U(cx)+(\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\big[v(x+\gamma\omega x,\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{1})-v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})\big]\Big\}.

Log utility.

We now set
U:=\log
. We first observe that
V_{0}<\infty
. Indeed, using the fact that

X_{t}=X_{0}\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{t}[r+(\mu-r)\omega_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(\omega_{s})^{2}-c_{s})]{\operatorname{d}}s+\sigma\omega_{s}{\operatorname{d}}W_{s}+\gamma\omega_{s}{\operatorname{d}}N_{s}\Big),\quad\mbox{$\mathbb{P}$-a.s.,}

we see that:

\displaystyle V_{0}\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{c\geq 0}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\big(\log(c_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}c_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s\big){\operatorname{d}}t\Big]+\sup_{\omega\in[0,1]}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\big(r+(\mu-r)\omega_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(\omega_{s})^{2}\big){\operatorname{d}}s+\gamma\omega_{s}{\operatorname{d}}N_{s}\Big){\operatorname{d}}t\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{c\geq 0}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}c_{t}e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s}{\operatorname{d}}t\Big]+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(C_{1}t+C_{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s\right]\right){\operatorname{d}}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{c\geq 0}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[1-\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho e^{-\rho t}e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s}{\operatorname{d}}t\Big]+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}C_{3}t{\operatorname{d}}t<\infty,

for some constants
\{C_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq 3}
, where we used the integration by parts formula and Lemma
A.2
.
As in
[
4
]
, we look for a solution to (
4.10
) under the form:

v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})=f(\boldsymbol{\xi})+K\log(x),

where
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
K\in{\mathbb{R}}
have to be determined. Compute:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{K}{x},\quad\partial_{xx}^{2}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi}))=-\frac{K}{x^{2}},\quad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}f(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

\displaystyle v(x+\gamma\omega x,\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{1})-v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})=K\log(1+\gamma\omega)+f(\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{1})-f(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

and plug it in (
4.10
):

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in[0,1]}\Big\{(\mu-r)\omega x\partial_{x}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})+(\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\big[v(x+\gamma\omega x,\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{1})-v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})\big]\Big\}

\displaystyle+\sup_{c\geq 0}\Big\{-cx\partial_{x}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})+U(cx)\Big\}+rx\partial_{x}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})-\rho v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\xi})\cdot\nabla_{\xi}v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\psi(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})+(\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})(f(\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{1})-f(\boldsymbol{\xi}))+\sup_{c\geq 0}\Big\{-cK+U(cx)\Big\}+rK-\rho v(x,\boldsymbol{\xi})-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\xi})\cdot\nabla_{\xi}f(\boldsymbol{\xi})=0,

with

\displaystyle\psi(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}):=K\sup_{\omega\in[0,1]}\Big\{(\mu-r)\omega-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\omega^{2}+(\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\log(1+\gamma\omega)\Big\}.

Note that he maximum in
c
is reached in
c=1/K
, and that all the terms in
x
cancel in (
4.10
) if and only if
K=\frac{1}{\rho}
. Then we obtain:

-\rho f(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\boldsymbol{\beta\xi}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}f(\boldsymbol{\xi})+(\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})(f(\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{1})-f(\boldsymbol{\xi}))=1-\frac{r}{\rho}-\log(\rho)-\psi(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Observe now that the left hand side of the above equation corresponds to the infinitesimal generator of the
n
-dimensional process
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=(\xi_{t}^{1},\dots,\xi_{t}^{n})
defined by the dynamics (
4.8
). Therefore, by Feynman-Kac formula, we have the probabilistic representation:

f(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{1}{\rho}\Big(1-\frac{r}{\rho}-\log(\rho)\Big)-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\psi(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\xi}\Big]{\operatorname{d}}t,

and therefore:

\displaystyle V_{0}^{n}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho}\Big(\log(X_{0}^{n})+1-\frac{r}{\rho}-\log(\rho)\Big)-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\psi(\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})\Big]{\operatorname{d}}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho}\Big(\log(X_{0})+1-\frac{r}{\rho}-\log(\rho)\Big)-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\psi(\lambda_{t}^{n}-\lambda_{0})\Big]{\operatorname{d}}t

By Theorem
3.1
, we have:

V_{0}^{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{\rho}\Big(\log(X_{0})+1-\frac{r}{\rho}-\log(\rho)\Big)-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\psi(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{0})\Big]{\operatorname{d}}t

The last result to prove is that
V_{0}^{n}
indeed converges to
V_{0}
as
n\to\infty
.

Convergence of the value function.

Theorem
4.1
can not be applied directly as the control problems (
4.7
) and (
4.9
) are formulated in infinite horizon. However, we have, for all
T>0
:

\displaystyle V_{0}\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{(c,\omega)\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}U(c_{t}X_{t}){\operatorname{d}}t\Big]+e^{-\rho T}\sup_{(c,\omega)\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-\rho(t-T)}U(c_{t}X_{t}){\operatorname{d}}t\Big],

We see that both suprema are finite by the same arguments implying the finiteness of
V_{0}
. Therefore, all
\varepsilon>0
, we may find
T_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

V_{0}\leq V_{0}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon,

(4.11)

where

V_{0}^{\varepsilon}:=\sup_{(c,\omega)\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T_{\varepsilon}}e^{-\rho t}U(c_{t}X_{t}){\operatorname{d}}t\Big].

(4.12)

As for
V^{n}
, we know there exist an optimal control
(c^{n},\omega^{n})
such that
c^{n}_{t}=\rho
. Therefore, denoting
\hat{X}
the optimally controlled dynamics:

\displaystyle V_{0}^{n}\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}U(\rho\hat{X}_{t}^{n}){\operatorname{d}}t\Big]+e^{-\rho T}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-\rho(t-T)}U(\rho\hat{X}_{t}^{n}){\operatorname{d}}t\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{(c,\omega)\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}U(c_{t}X_{t}^{n}){\operatorname{d}}t\Big]+e^{-\rho T}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-\rho(t-T)}U(\rho\hat{X}_{t}^{n}){\operatorname{d}}t\Big]

By Theorem
3.1
, for any control
(c,\omega)
,
X^{n}
converges to
X
in
\mathbb{L}^{1}
. In particular, this implies that the family
\{\hat{X}_{t}^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is uniformly integrable for all
t\geq 0
. Since
U
has linear growth, we may conclude that there exists
T_{\varepsilon}
(independent of
n
) such that:

V_{0}^{n}\leq V_{0}^{n,\varepsilon}+\varepsilon,

(4.13)

where

V_{0}^{n,\varepsilon}:=\sup_{(c,\omega)\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{T_{\varepsilon}}e^{-\rho t}U(c_{t}X_{t}^{n}){\operatorname{d}}t\right].

(4.14)

Now, combining (
4.11
) and (
4.13
) with the natural inequalities
V_{0}^{\varepsilon}\leq V_{0}
and
V_{0}^{n,\varepsilon}\leq V_{0}^{n}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle|V_{0}^{n}-V_{0}|\leq|V_{0}^{\varepsilon,n}-V_{0}^{\varepsilon}|+|V_{0}^{\varepsilon}-V_{0}|+|V_{0}^{\varepsilon,n}-V_{0}^{n}|\leq|V_{0}^{\varepsilon,n}-V_{0}^{\varepsilon}|+\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}.

Now, since the control problems (
4.12
) and (
4.14
) have a finite horizon, by Theorem
4.1
we have
|V_{0}^{\varepsilon,n}-V_{0}^{\varepsilon}|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then, for
n
sufficiently large, we have
|V_{0}^{n}-V_{0}|\leq\varepsilon
.

Conclusion.

We have
V_{0}^{n}\to V_{0}
as
n\to\infty
, and therefore:

V_{0}=\frac{1}{\rho}\Big(\log(X_{0})+1-\frac{r}{\rho}-\log(\rho)\Big)-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\psi(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{0})\Big]{\operatorname{d}}t,

with optimal consumption policy
C^{*}_{t}=\rho
and optimal investment policy satisfying:

\omega_{t}^{*}\in\frac{1}{\rho}\underset{\omega\in[0,1]}{\mathrm{argmax}}\Big\{(\mu-r)\omega-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\omega^{2}+(\lambda_{t})\log(1+\gamma\omega)\Big\}.

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}+\psi(0)+L\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\left|\phi(t-s)b(y)\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\right|\left(\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)-{\operatorname{d}}s{\operatorname{d}}\theta m({\operatorname{d}}y)\right)+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\left|\phi(t-s)\right|{\mathbb{E}}[b(Y)]\lambda_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{t}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\left|\phi(t-s)\right|{\mathbb{E}}[b(Y)]\lambda_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s

where
M_{t}=\lambda+\psi(0)+L\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\left|\phi(t-s)b(y)\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\right|\left(\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)-{\operatorname{d}}s{\operatorname{d}}\theta m({\operatorname{d}}y)\right)
. We then apply Lemma
A.1
and proceed just like the proof of Lemma 6.4 in
[
25
]
.
Note that since we assumed that
\phi
is bounded and
\mathbb{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, it is automatically in
\mathbb{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})

Lemma A.3
.

Let
p>2
and assume that Assumptions
1.3
and
1.1
hold. Assume furthermore that
b(Y)
has a finite
p-
th moment. Then there exists a positive constant
C_{p}
such that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lambda_{t}^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}e^{C_{p}T^{p}}.

.

Proof.

In this proof we omit to explicitly write the localisation argument as it has already been used in the proof of Proposition
1.4
.
Using the Lipschitz growth of
\psi
and the fact that
\lambda_{\infty}
is bounded from above (by some constant
\bar{\lambda}
) we have that

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}\leq

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}+\psi(0)+L\left|\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\phi(t-s)b(y)\nu(s,X_{s-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(1+\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\left|b(y)\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(1+\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\left|b(y)\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\left(\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)-{\operatorname{d}}s{\operatorname{d}}\theta m({\operatorname{d}}y)\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s\right),

because
\nu
and
\phi
are bounded from above. Therefore we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lambda_{t}^{p}\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\left(1+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\left|b(y)\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\left(\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)-{\operatorname{d}}s{\operatorname{d}}\theta m({\operatorname{d}}y)\right)\right)^{p}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s\right)^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\left(1+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t-}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}}\left|b(y)\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\theta\leq\lambda_{s}}\left(\Pi({\operatorname{d}}s,{\operatorname{d}}\theta,{\operatorname{d}}y)-{\operatorname{d}}s{\operatorname{d}}\theta m({\operatorname{d}}y)\right)\right)^{p}+T^{p-1}\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\lambda_{s}^{p}{\operatorname{d}}t\right).

Using the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality for jump martingales (
cf.
Theorem 4.4.23 in
[
7
]
) and Hölder’s inequality we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lambda_{t}^{p}\right]\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\left(1+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\lambda_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s\right)^{p/2}\right]+T^{p-1}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\lambda_{s}^{p}\right]{\operatorname{d}}t\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\left(2+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\lambda_{s}{\operatorname{d}}s\right)^{p}\right]+T^{p-1}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\lambda_{s}^{p}\right]{\operatorname{d}}t\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}\left(1+T^{p-1}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\lambda_{s}^{p}\right]{\operatorname{d}}t\right).

Using Grönwall’s inequality, we have that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lambda_{t}^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}e^{C_{p}T^{p}}.

∎
