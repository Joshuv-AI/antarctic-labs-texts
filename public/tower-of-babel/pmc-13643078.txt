Heat-related illnesses in the Republic of Korea Armed Forces: epidemiology, comparative analysis, and preventive strategies
Authors: Sunju Kim, Kun Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat-related illnesses (HRIs) represent an increasingly critical threat to the health and operational readiness of the Republic of Korea (ROK) Armed Forces. Rising global temperatures, combined with Korea’s humid summers and intensive military training, have increased both the incidence and severity of HRIs among soldiers. This paper provides a comprehensive review of HRIs in the ROK military, beginning with an overview of their pathophysiology and classification, ranging from heat cramps to exertional heat stroke—the most severe and life-threatening form. We then examine recent epidemiological trends, which reveal over 1,200 documented cases between 2018 and 2023, with new recruits disproportionately affected. A comparative analysis across service branches indicates that Army personnel, who engage in high-intensity outdoor training, bear the greatest burden, while Navy and Air Force members face distinct occupational exposures in engine rooms and on airfields, respectively. The risk in the ROK military is striking in international comparison: odds ratios suggest that soldiers face more than 27 times the risk of HRIs compared with the civilian population, exceeding reported burdens in allied militaries such as the United States and Japan. Lessons from other nations, particularly the use of real-time surveillance, wearable technologies, and structured acclimatization programs, offer valuable models for ROK. Preventive strategies must therefore extend beyond medical management to include leadership accountability, environmental engineering, digital monitoring, and a cultural shift that promotes reporting. In an era of accelerating climate change, HRIs must be reframed not only as medical conditions but also as preventable threats to combat readiness, soldier welfare, and national security.Keywords: Heat stroke, Republic of Korea, Military personnel

INTRODUCTION

Heat-related illnesses (HRIs) are no longer rare complications of summer training; rather, they represent a systemic challenge to the resilience of military forces worldwide. In the Republic of Korea (ROK), this threat is magnified by rising summer temperatures, high humidity, and the rigorous demands placed on young conscripts, many of whom are not acclimatized to prolonged physical exertion in hot conditions [1,2].

Reports from the ROK Armed Forces Medical Command show a steady rise in military HRIs, with cases concentrated in July and August, particularly during forced marches, shooting exercises, and prolonged outdoor drills. The Ministry of National Defense of ROK noted that more than 300 soldiers experienced HRI in 2023 alone, and several cases required intensive care [3].

The situation of the ROK is not unique. Militaries worldwide grapple with similar challenges; however, the ROK Armed Forces face a disproportionate burden, with odds ratios suggesting that the risk of HRI is more than 27 times higher than in the civilian population (Fig. 1). Understanding this burden requires examining the physiological basis of heat illness, the epidemiology within Korea, and lessons that can be drawn from international counterparts [4,5].

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND CLASSIFICATION OF HRIs

The human body maintains a delicate balance between heat production and heat dissipation. During military exertion (marching in full combat gear, standing prolonged guard duty, or working in confined ship engineering rooms), this balance can be disrupted. Sweat evaporation, the body’s primary cooling mechanism, is often impaired by humidity, heavy clothing, and restricted airflow [6].

When thermoregulation fails, a predictable spectrum of illnesses can develop, including heat cramps (painful spasms due to electrolyte loss), heat syncope (collapse due to vasodilation and dehydration), heat exhaustion (fatigue, tachycardia, nausea, and collapse, typically with a core temperature <40 °C), and heat stroke (the most severe form, characterized by a core temperature ≥40 °C and altered mental status; it can rapidly progress to multiorgan failure if untreated). In the military, exertional heat stroke predominates. Unlike classic heat stroke in older adults, exertional heat stroke may occur even when sweating continues, making recognition more difficult. At the cellular level, excessive heat denatures proteins, disrupts cell membranes, and triggers systemic inflammatory cascades like those seen in sepsis [7]. The results include endothelial damage, coagulopathy, rhabdomyolysis, and potentially death (Table 1).

EPIDEMIOLOGY IN THE ROK MILITARY

The ROK Armed Forces Medical Command reports that between 2018 and 2023, more than 1,200 cases of HRI were treated across all branches, corresponding to an estimated incidence of approximately 35 cases per 100,000 personnel per year, with the Army accounting for nearly two-thirds of cases. In 2023 alone, 318 cases were officially documented, with most occurring among new recruits during their first summer of training [8].

Geographically, cases cluster in inland provinces such as Gangwon-do and Chungcheong-do, where daytime training often coincides with extreme heat and humidity [2]. Seasonality is pronounced: most cases occur in July and August, although May and September are not exempt (Fig. 2).

Despite improved awareness, underreporting persists due to a cultural reluctance to admit weakness during training. Fatalities, while rare, still occur, underscoring the gap between written policy and field implementation.

COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS ACROSS SERVICE BRANCHES

Distinct patterns of HRIs emerge when cases are analyzed by service branch, reflecting differences in training environments, occupational exposures, and operational demands.

Army

Army personnel account for approximately 65% of all reported HRI cases, with an incidence exceeding 40 cases per 100,000 soldiers annually. High-intensity outdoor training, prolonged marches, and the use of heavy personal protective equipment are key contributors. The imbalance across branches underscores the need for branch-specific preventive protocols. A “one-size-fits-all” model is insufficient (Table 2).

Navy

Navy personnel experience HRIs primarily in enclosed environments such as engine rooms and shipboard facilities. Although the overall incidence is lower than in the Army (approximately 20 cases per 100,000), radiant heat exposure and limited ventilation pose unique risks.

Air Force

Air Force personnel have the lowest incidence (approximately 15 cases per 100,000), yet prolonged sun exposure on flight lines and at guard posts remains a significant hazard. Smaller population size and different training profiles partially account for the lower absolute numbers.

INTERNATIONAL COMPARISON AND LESSONS

Internationally, the burden of HRIs varies across military systems. In the US Armed Forces, incidence rates range from 15 to 20 cases per 100,000 personnel annually, supported by robust monitoring and prevention infrastructure [9]. Japan reports lower absolute numbers but emphasizes civilian-military integration in heat-risk communication [10]. Data from China are sparse, but published research highlights progress in wearable technology and thermally adaptive gear for soldiers [11]. Taiwan has demonstrated the value of long-term surveillance and early adoption of wearable physiological monitoring [12].

Compared with these nations, the ROK military exhibits a higher incidence rate, suggesting gaps in acclimatization protocols, command-level accountability, and real-time environmental monitoring. These comparisons highlight opportunities to adopt best practices tailored to ROK’s climatic and cultural context (Table 3).

CONCLUSIONS

HRIs are more than seasonal inconveniences; they are predictable and preventable threats to the health, morale, and combat readiness of the ROK Armed Forces. The evidence is unequivocal: ROK has one of the highest military-to-civilian odds ratios for heat illness worldwide [1,14]. Nevertheless, international comparisons suggest that progress is possible.

By combining command responsibility, medical vigilance, and technological innovation, the ROK can not only reduce heat casualties but also set a new standard for military health resilience in Asia. The fight against HRIs is not only about keeping soldiers alive in summer—it is about preparing a force capable of sustaining operations under the evolving climate realities of the 21st century.
