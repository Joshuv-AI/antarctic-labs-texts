Molecular Mechanisms Linking Eosinophils to Lung Function Impairment in Respiratory Diseases
Authors: Meiqi Li, Anhao Zheng, Yi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEosinophils are key immunopathological cells in respiratory diseases and contribute to airway inflammation, tissue remodeling, and disease heterogeneity. Although eosinophils have traditionally been regarded as biomarkers of type 2 inflammation, increasing evidence indicates that they also function as active mediators of lung function impairment in selected respiratory diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, and eosinophilic pneumonia. These disorders were selected because they represent distinct yet complementary models of eosinophil-associated airway and parenchymal injury, allowing comparison of shared and disease-specific mechanisms. Mechanistically, eosinophils impair lung function through the coordinated release of cytotoxic granule proteins, reactive oxygen species, lipid mediators, and cytokines, while interacting with epithelial, immune, and structural cells to amplify inflammation, airway remodeling, and tissue injury. These advances have improved understanding of eosinophil biology and facilitated the development of clinically relevant biomarkers and targeted therapies. Blood and sputum eosinophil counts remain the most widely used biomarkers, whereas emerging biomarkers, including eosinophil-derived neurotoxin, may further improve disease stratification and treatment monitoring. Biologics targeting interleukin-5 and its receptor have demonstrated therapeutic benefit in selected eosinophilic respiratory diseases, although treatment responses differ among disease phenotypes. This review summarizes current evidence regarding the molecular mechanisms linking eosinophils to lung function impairment, discusses the evolving role of eosinophils as both biomarkers and pathogenic drivers, and highlights the biomarker and therapeutic implications for precision management of respiratory diseases.

Introduction

Eosinophils have emerged as important immunopathological contributors to respiratory diseases, actively participating in airway inflammation, tissue injury, and structural remodeling in disorders such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and eosinophilic pneumonia (EP).1 Although traditionally regarded as biomarkers of type 2 inflammation, accumulating evidence indicates that eosinophils actively participate in disease pathogenesis through the release of cytotoxic granule proteins, cytokines, lipid mediators, and reactive oxygen species (ROS), as well as through interactions with epithelial, immune, and structural cells. These mechanisms can directly contribute to airway dysfunction and progressive lung function impairment rather than simply reflecting underlying inflammation.1 Similar eosinophil-associated type 2 inflammatory pathways have also been implicated in local allergic rhinitis, supporting the concept that localized eosinophilic inflammation may contribute to respiratory mucosal dysfunction even in the absence of systemic atopy.2

Increasing attention has focused on the relationship between eosinophils and lung function across respiratory diseases. Elevated blood eosinophil counts have been associated with accelerated decline in forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), increased exacerbation risk, and poorer clinical outcomes in COPD.3 Similar associations have been reported in asthma, where eosinophilic inflammation correlates with airway hyperresponsiveness, airflow limitation, and reduced FEV1/FVC.1,4 However, these relationships vary among diseases. In asthma, eosinophils are well-established pathogenic effectors, whereas in COPD they may function as both inflammatory mediators and clinically useful biomarkers of corticosteroid responsiveness. In EP, eosinophils directly mediate alveolar injury and impaired gas exchange. These disease-specific differences highlight the need to distinguish the biological functions of eosinophils beyond simple eosinophil quantification.

The present review focuses on COPD, asthma, and EP because these diseases represent the major clinical settings in which eosinophils are closely associated with impaired lung function, while also exhibiting distinct pathological mechanisms involving the airways, lung parenchyma, or both. Comparing these disorders provides an opportunity to distinguish common molecular pathways from disease-specific mechanisms and to better understand how eosinophils contribute to respiratory dysfunction across different clinical contexts.

To provide a focused and clinically relevant overview, we reviewed recent studies published in peer-reviewed journals, emphasizing evidence from the past five years while incorporating landmark studies when necessary to explain established mechanisms or pivotal clinical trials. Priority was given to studies investigating molecular mechanisms, lung function outcomes, clinically applicable biomarkers, and targeted therapies involving eosinophils.

Although previous reviews have summarized the roles of eosinophils in individual respiratory diseases, few have systematically integrated the molecular pathways linking eosinophilic inflammation with lung function impairment across different respiratory disorders or discussed whether eosinophils primarily function as biomarkers, pathogenic drivers, or both in different disease settings. Furthermore, recent advances in eosinophil-targeted biologics and emerging molecular therapies have substantially expanded therapeutic opportunities, making a mechanism-oriented synthesis clinically relevant. Therefore, this review focuses on the molecular basis of eosinophil-mediated lung function decline, summarizes current biomarkers and targeted therapies, and discusses their implications for precision management of respiratory diseases.

Biological Characteristics and Immunological Functions of Eosinophils

Eosinophils are most prominent during inflammatory responses and act as key effectors in the development of both allergic and non-allergic asthma.5 In the respiratory tract, changes in eosinophil density are directly linked to various medical conditions. In patients with asthma and allergic rhinitis, eosinophil accumulation in the airways contributes to specific inflammatory patterns that lead to damage of epithelial structures.6 Research shows that eosinophils organize into specific spatial patterns that help protect the airway epithelial surface from airborne infectious agents (Figure 1A). Eosinophils express a variety of surface receptors that enable them to recognize and respond to cytokines, particularly interleukin (IL)-5 and IL-13. These signaling pathways promote eosinophil development and survival while also amplifying the inflammatory response, contributing to tissue damage in affected areas. Eosinophils play a critical role in host defense against parasitic infections (Figure 1B). While eosinophils perform their traditional effector functions, they also influence immune responses by interacting with T cells and other immune cells, either activating or suppressing immune activity depending on the context (Figure 1B). In allergic diseases, eosinophil accumulation and activation are directly associated with the severity of airway inflammation, contributing to worsening asthma symptoms.7 Taken together, these cells release various mediators that not only amplify local inflammation but also increase tissue sensitivity to external stimuli, potentially exacerbating underlying medical conditions (Figure 1B).

There are two main types of eosinophils: inflammatory eosinophils (iEos) and resident eosinophils (rEos), which differ in both function and tissue distribution (Figure 1C). iEos are primarily involved in acute inflammatory responses, such as those triggered by allergic reactions and infections. These immune cells are recruited and activated by type 2 cytokines, particularly IL-5 and IL-13, and express high levels of the CC chemokine receptor 3.8 Research has shown that elevated iEos levels are associated with more severe asthma symptoms and a reduction in FEV1.9 In contrast, rEos function as part of a regulatory system that helps maintain immune homeostasis and protect tissues from damage. iEos are pro-inflammatory cells that produce and release cytokines such as IL-5, IL-13, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF)-α, which amplify local immune responses and contribute to structural changes in airway tissues.10 In asthma patients, elevated iEos levels are associated with increased airway hyperresponsiveness and bronchoconstriction, contributing to symptom severity.11 In contrast, rEos play a dual role by maintaining pulmonary homeostasis and preventing excessive immune activation.12 However, under conditions of prolonged allergic inflammation, rEos may shift toward a more pro-inflammatory phenotype, potentially altering the course of disease progression.13

Mechanisms of Eosinophil-Mediated Lung Function Impairment

Release of Inflammatory Mediators and Airway Remodeling

Eosinophils are key effector cells in airway inflammation and remodeling, primarily through the release of cytokines, lipid mediators, and cytotoxic granule proteins (Figure 2A). IL-5 is the central survival and activation factor for eosinophils. After binding to IL-5 receptor alpha on eosinophils, IL-5 activates downstream JAK/STAT, PI3K–Akt, and MAPK signaling pathways, thereby promoting eosinophil differentiation, survival, tissue migration, priming, and degranulation.14,15 Persistent activation of the IL-5 pathway maintains airway eosinophilia and amplifies type 2 inflammation, which contributes to airway hyperresponsiveness, recurrent exacerbations, and progressive airflow limitation. Airway remodeling refers to persistent structural changes, including epithelial injury, goblet cell hyperplasia, mucus hypersecretion, airway smooth muscle hypertrophy, basement membrane thickening, subepithelial fibrosis, and extracellular matrix deposition, all of which can reduce airway caliber and impair respiratory function16 (Figure 2A). Eosinophils contribute to these remodeling processes by releasing cytotoxic granule proteins such as major basic protein (MBP), eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), eosinophil peroxidase (EPO), and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin (EDN). These mediators directly damage airway epithelial cells, disrupt epithelial barrier integrity, and impair epithelial repair.14,17 In addition, eosinophil-derived IL-24 has recently been shown to compromise epithelial integrity and aggravate airway remodeling, suggesting that eosinophils can drive epithelial dysfunction through both granule protein-dependent and cytokine-dependent mechanisms.17

IL-13 is another major mediator linking eosinophilic inflammation to lung function impairment. Through IL-4Rα/IL-13Rα1 and downstream STAT6 signaling, IL-13 promotes goblet cell differentiation, MUC5AC expression, mucus plugging, airway smooth muscle hypercontractility, and extracellular matrix deposition.6 These changes increase airway resistance and contribute to reductions in FEV1 and FEV1/FVC. Eosinophils may also promote profibrotic remodeling by enhancing transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)-associated pathways, which activate fibroblasts and increase collagen deposition, leading to subepithelial fibrosis and more fixed airflow obstruction.18

Eosinophils also participate in epithelial–immune feedback loops that sustain airway remodeling. Injured airway epithelial cells release alarmins such as IL-25, IL-33, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), which activate type 2 immune responses and promote further eosinophil recruitment and survival.19 In turn, activated eosinophils further injure epithelial cells and stimulate airway structural cells, forming a self-perpetuating cycle of epithelial damage, type 2 inflammation, mucus overproduction, and airway wall remodeling. These mechanisms explain why eosinophilic inflammation is closely associated with impaired lung function and why structural airway changes may persist even after partial control of inflammation.

Oxidative Stress and Tissue Damage

Oxidative stress is an important downstream mechanism through which eosinophil activation contributes to airway and lung tissue injury (Figure 2B). Redox imbalance is common in chronic airway diseases such as asthma and COPD and can act both as a trigger and an amplifier of inflammation.20,21 Activated eosinophils generate ROS and release cytotoxic granule proteins, including MBP, EPO, ECP, and EDN.22 These mediators damage airway epithelial cells, disrupt epithelial barrier integrity, impair mucociliary clearance, and promote epithelial apoptosis or necrosis, ultimately contributing to tissue injury and reduced lung function.

EPO is particularly important in eosinophil-associated oxidative injury. In the presence of hydrogen peroxide and halides, EPO catalyzes the formation of reactive oxidants that can modify proteins, lipids, and extracellular matrix components. This process increases epithelial permeability, promotes local inflammatory signaling, and may aggravate airway remodeling. In parallel, ROS activate redox-sensitive pathways such as nuclear factor kappa-B and activator protein-1, leading to increased expression of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, adhesion molecules, and matrix-remodeling enzymes21 (Figure 2B). Evidence from experimental lung injury models further supports this mechanism, showing that eosinophil recruitment is associated with increased oxidative stress and epithelial damage.23

Eosinophil granule proteins further intensify tissue damage. MBP can destabilize epithelial cell membranes and enhance airway hyperresponsiveness, whereas ECP and EDN may contribute to epithelial stress, immune activation, and impaired tissue repair.24 These effects create a feed-forward cycle: epithelial injury promotes the release of epithelial-derived alarmins, which further recruit and activate eosinophils, while activated eosinophils release more ROS and cytotoxic proteins. This cycle helps explain why persistent eosinophilic inflammation can lead to chronic epithelial dysfunction, airway remodeling, and progressive airflow limitation.

Recent studies have also highlighted eosinophil extracellular trap formation as an additional mechanism of tissue injury. Under strong inflammatory stimulation, eosinophils can release extracellular DNA traps containing histones and granule proteins such as MBP and EPO. These eosinophil extracellular traps may increase mucus viscosity, impair mucociliary clearance, and maintain local inflammation.25 In eosinophil-rich tissues, extracellular trap formation is also associated with Charcot–Leyden crystal formation. Charcot–Leyden crystals have been shown to activate macrophages and the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby promoting IL-1β release.24,25 Collectively, these mechanisms indicate that eosinophil-associated oxidative stress is not only a cause of acute epithelial injury but also a driver of persistent inflammation, airway remodeling, and lung function impairment.

Interactions with Other Immune Cells

Eosinophils are increasingly recognized as active immunoregulatory cells rather than terminal effector cells in allergic inflammation. They interact with immune and structural cells, including airway epithelial cells, mast cells, T cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, and airway smooth muscle cells, thereby shaping the intensity and persistence of airway inflammation.26,27

A key upstream mechanism is the epithelial–type 2 immune axis (Figure 2C). When airway epithelial cells are injured by allergens, viruses, pollutants, or mechanical stress, they release epithelial-derived alarmins, including IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP. These cytokines activate group 2 innate lymphoid cells and Th2 cells, which then produce IL-5 and IL-13 to promote eosinophil recruitment, survival, and activation.19 Activated eosinophils further damage epithelial cells through granule proteins and cytokines, forming a positive feedback loop that sustains type 2 inflammation, mucus production, airway hyperresponsiveness, and remodeling.6

Eosinophil–mast cell interactions also contribute to bronchoconstriction and symptom exacerbation. Mast cells release histamine (Figure 2C), prostaglandin D2, and cysteinyl leukotrienes, which promote eosinophil recruitment and activation.27 In turn, eosinophil-derived MBP can stimulate mast cell degranulation and enhance mediator release. This reciprocal eosinophil–mast cell interaction provides a mechanistic explanation for acute airflow narrowing and worsening symptoms during allergic airway inflammation.28

Eosinophil–T cell interactions further amplify airway inflammation. Eosinophils can release IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and other mediators that support Th2 polarization and type 2 immune responses. They may also promote T-cell activation through direct cell-to-cell contact.29 In addition, eosinophil-derived IL-4 has been shown to protect CD8⁺ T cells from apoptosis and support memory T-cell generation, suggesting that eosinophils may have context-dependent immunoregulatory functions.30 In chronic eosinophilic airway disease, however, persistent eosinophil–T cell crosstalk can reinforce type 2 inflammation and contribute to recurrent exacerbations.

Interactions with structural cells are also important for lung function impairment. Airway basal epithelial cells can recruit eosinophils by expressing chemokines such as C-C motif chemokine ligand 11 and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 5.6,19 Eosinophils can then act on fibroblasts and airway smooth muscle cells by releasing transforming growth factor-beta, vascular endothelial growth factor, platelet-derived growth factor, and granule proteins. These mediators promote extracellular matrix deposition, airway smooth muscle contraction, and airway wall thickening.27,31 Therefore, eosinophil-mediated cell–cell communication links inflammation with structural remodeling and persistent airflow limitation.

Clinical Relevance of Eosinophils and Lung Function

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease

Eosinophil counts are significantly elevated in a subset of patients with COPD and serve as clinically useful markers of airway inflammation.32 Approximately 20% to 40% of patients with COPD show increased eosinophil levels in blood or sputum, which are associated with reduced FEV1, increased airway resistance, and more extensive emphysematous damage.33 Clinical data from Chinese patients further demonstrate that higher eosinophil counts correlate with worse lung function, especially lower FEV1 and greater emphysema severity, suggesting a potential role for eosinophils in irreversible airway and parenchymal injury.34

In COPD, eosinophilic inflammation may represent a type 2 inflammatory phenotype superimposed on smoking- or pollutant-related airway damage (Figure 3A). Blood eosinophils also help identify patients who are more likely to benefit from inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-containing therapy. Patients with higher eosinophil counts, particularly those with frequent exacerbations, generally show greater exacerbation reduction after ICS treatment, whereas patients with low eosinophil counts may derive less benefit and face a higher risk of ICS-related adverse effects such as pneumonia.35,36 A blood eosinophil count of approximately 300 cells/μL or higher is commonly used as a threshold indicating a greater likelihood of benefit from greater corticosteroid responsiveness with an appropriate exacerbation history.37 In hospitalized acute exacerbation-COPD populations, eosinophil-predominant inflammation is associated with better improvement in FEV1 and symptoms after corticosteroid therapy.38 Accordingly, eosinophils in COPD are not only inflammatory biomarkers but also indicators of disease activity, exacerbation risk, and corticosteroid responsiveness.

Collectively, these findings indicate that eosinophilic inflammation in COPD is not merely a biomarker but an active driver of airway injury and a clinically useful indicator for corticosteroid responsiveness and disease progression.

Asthma

In eosinophilic asthma, eosinophils are active drivers of airway dysfunction rather than simple markers of inflammation. Elevated blood eosinophil levels are associated with poor disease control, increased exacerbation risk, airway hyperresponsiveness, and reduced FEV1.39 Persistent eosinophilic inflammation promotes mucus plugging, airway remodeling, and fixed airflow obstruction, thereby worsening lung function and quality of life.

The disease-specific mechanism begins with epithelial activation. Allergens, viruses, and environmental irritants stimulate airway epithelial cells to release IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP, which activate type 2 immune responses and sustain eosinophil recruitment and survival19 (Figure 3B). Activated eosinophils release MBP, ECP, EPO, and EDN, which injure airway epithelial cells, impair epithelial repair, and amplify local inflammation.6 MBP further disrupts airway neural regulation by inhibiting M2 muscarinic receptor function on parasympathetic nerves, increasing acetylcholine release and promoting vagally mediated bronchoconstriction and airway hyperresponsiveness.40

Lipid mediators and type 2 cytokines further connect eosinophilic inflammation with airflow limitation. Cysteinyl leukotrienes, including leukotriene C4, D4, and E4, induce bronchoconstriction, increase mucus secretion and vascular permeability, and amplify type 2 inflammation.41 Eosinophils also interact with mast cells: mast cell-derived prostaglandin D2 and cysteinyl leukotrienes promote eosinophil recruitment and activation, whereas eosinophil-derived MBP can enhance mast cell degranulation. This reciprocal interaction aggravates acute airflow narrowing and symptom exacerbation. In parallel, IL-13 signals through IL-4Rα/IL-13Rα1 and STAT6 to promote goblet cell hyperplasia, MUC5AC expression, mucus hypersecretion, airway smooth muscle hypercontractility, and airway remodeling.42 Together, epithelial injury, neural dysregulation, leukotriene-driven bronchoconstriction, mucus plugging, and remodeling increase airway resistance and contribute to persistent reductions in FEV1 and FEV1/FVC.

Sputum cytology remains the most direct method for assessing eosinophilic airway inflammation. In addition, eosinophil-related proteins and microRNAs in circulating exosomes may improve asthma phenotyping and treatment monitoring. Specific exosomal microRNAs, including hsa-miR-25-3p and hsa-miR-16-5p, have been identified in asthma patients and may help assess disease severity.43 Exosomal EPO, MBP, and ECP have also been linked to lung function decline, disease severity, and clinical outcomes, supporting their potential value in longitudinal disease assessment.

These findings indicate that eosinophils simultaneously promote airway inflammation, neural dysregulation, mucus hypersecretion, and remodeling, making them central mediators of persistent airflow limitation.

Eosinophilic Pneumonia

EP is characterized by eosinophil accumulation in the lung parenchyma and alveolar spaces. These eosinophils become activated and undergo degranulation, releasing cytotoxic granule proteins and inflammatory mediators that cause alveolar epithelial injury, interstitial inflammation, impaired gas exchange, and symptoms such as dyspnea, cough, and fever.44,45 EP includes acute eosinophilic pneumonia (AEP) and chronic eosinophilic pneumonia (CEP). AEP typically presents with rapid respiratory failure and diffuse pulmonary infiltrates, whereas CEP usually follows a more subacute course with recurrent symptoms and peripheral blood eosinophilia.44

The disease-specific mechanism in EP is centered on eosinophil-mediated alveolar and interstitial injury (Figure 3C). Eosinophils recruited into the alveolar compartment release MBP, ECP, EPO, and ROS, which damage alveolar epithelial and capillary endothelial barriers.6,45 Barrier disruption increases alveolar-capillary permeability, causing interstitial edema, alveolar filling, and impaired oxygen diffusion. These changes reduce lung compliance and produce restrictive ventilatory impairment, while airway involvement and mucus obstruction may contribute to airflow limitation in some patients with CEP. In addition, activated eosinophils can form eosinophil extracellular traps (EETs), which consist of a DNA scaffold decorated with cytotoxic granule proteins. These structures further exacerbate alveolar epithelial injury, amplify local inflammatory responses, and delay the resolution of inflammation.46 This mechanism explains why EP can present with hypoxemia, reduced gas exchange, abnormal pulmonary function tests, and, in some cases, persistent respiratory impairment despite treatment.44,47

Radiographic imaging commonly shows bilateral, multifocal, irregular opacities, and pulmonary function tests often reveal a restrictive pattern, although obstructive or normal physiology may also occur depending on disease severity and timing of assessment.44,47 A bronchoalveolar lavage eosinophil fraction greater than 25% provides important diagnostic support.47 Because eosinophilic inflammation is the dominant pathological process, corticosteroids usually produce rapid clinical and radiographic improvement.44 However, CEP may relapse during dose reduction and often requires prolonged corticosteroid therapy. In relapsing or steroid-dependent eosinophilic lung diseases, biologics targeting IL-5 or IL-5 receptor signaling, such as benralizumab, have been explored in selected cases.48 These findings support the concept that eosinophils are both diagnostic markers and active mediators of lung parenchymal injury and respiratory dysfunction in EP.

Taken together, these mechanisms explain why eosinophilic inflammation directly contributes to impaired gas exchange and lung function deterioration in EP.

Mepolizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds circulating IL-5 and prevents its interaction with IL-5 receptor alpha (IL-5Rα) on eosinophils. By reducing IL-5-mediated eosinophil survival and activation, mepolizumab decreases blood and airway eosinophil counts and reduces eosinophilic inflammation. A large-scale analysis involving approximately 7600 participants showed that anti-IL-5 therapies, including mepolizumab, reduce exacerbation frequency in patients with severe eosinophilic asthma.49 Although improvements in lung function are generally modest, with FEV1 increases of approximately 0.08–0.15 L, these changes are statistically significant and are accompanied by improvements in symptoms and quality of life.49,51

Benralizumab has a different mechanism of action. It binds directly to IL-5Rα on eosinophils and basophils. Because its Fc region is afucosylated, benralizumab strongly engages FcγRIIIa on natural killer cells, triggering antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) and rapid eosinophil apoptosis.15,56 This mechanism leads to near-complete depletion of circulating eosinophils and marked reduction of airway eosinophilic inflammation. In severe eosinophilic asthma, benralizumab has shown clinically meaningful benefits, including reduced exacerbation frequency, improved asthma control, lower OCS requirement, and improvement in lung function in selected patients with elevated eosinophil counts.52–54

However, the efficacy of eosinophil-targeted biologics differs across diseases. In COPD, benralizumab did not significantly reduce the annualized rate of COPD exacerbations compared with placebo in the Phase III GALATHEA and TERRANOVA trials, despite enrolling patients with elevated blood eosinophil counts and frequent exacerbations.57 This negative result suggests that eosinophilic inflammation in COPD is more heterogeneous than in asthma and that blood eosinophil counts alone may be insufficient to identify patients who benefit from IL-5Rα blockade. Subsequent analyses have suggested that specific COPD subgroups may still benefit, but these findings remain exploratory and require further validation.58

Collectively, anti-IL-5 and anti-IL-5Rα biologics support a more personalized treatment paradigm for eosinophilic respiratory diseases by reducing eosinophil-mediated inflammation, improving symptom control, and lowering exacerbation risk in appropriately selected patients.50 Nevertheless, biologic therapy should be integrated into a broader management plan that includes optimized inhaled therapy, monitoring of lung function, assessment of comorbidities, and prevention of future exacerbations.59 Longitudinal lung function tracking remains important for evaluating treatment response, guiding therapeutic adjustment, and improving long-term disease control.59

Emerging Molecular Targets

A recent study suggests that the oxoeicosanoid (OXE) receptor pathway, activated by 5-oxo-6,8,11,14-eicosatetraenoic acid (5-oxo-ETE), may serve as a promising target for the treatment of allergic diseases.60 The antiparasitic drug miltefosine, originally developed for the treatment of leishmaniasis, is now being investigated for its immunomodulatory properties.55 Miltefosine has been shown to inhibit eosinophil proliferation by blocking Akt protein activation and disrupting intracellular calcium levels. These actions impair eosinophil chemotaxis and degranulation and reduce eosinophilic airway inflammation in murine models of allergic airway disease. This is accompanied by improvements in lung function parameters, suggesting its potential for treating eosinophil-associated airway diseases55 (Table 1). Overall, these findings suggest that miltefosine may have therapeutic potential in eosinophil-associated airway disease by attenuating eosinophilic inflammation and improving lung function in preclinical models. However, the available evidence is currently limited to experimental studies, and clinical efficacy and long-term safety in humans remain to be established. Therefore, miltefosine should be regarded as a promising experimental strategy rather than an established therapeutic option.

Future Perspectives

Genetic studies have found specific mutations and patterns that trigger inflammation caused by eosinophils. Proteomic analyses further clarify the functional molecules released by eosinophils under different disease conditions. For example, levels of IL-5 and IL-13 are significantly elevated in asthma patients, correlating with increased disease severity and exacerbation frequency. Metabolomic studies provide additional insight into how eosinophil metabolic activity is closely linked to changes in the airway microbiome. In COPD, eosinophil metabolic reprogramming has been associated with alterations in microbial communities, suggesting that these shifts may modulate host immunity and influence disease outcomes.61 Multi-omics integration has revealed that microbial composition correlates with lung function outcomes. For instance, the presence of Streptococcus species in the gut microbiota has been associated with reduced FEV1, while Lachnospiraceae species are linked to lower FVC and percent-predicted FEV1 values.62 These findings support the concept that eosinophils comprise multiple biologically distinct subtypes, each with specific roles in respiratory disease, underscoring the need for targeted therapies tailored to individual patient profiles.

In addition to reflecting disease activity, eosinophil counts and functional phenotypes may help identify patients who are more likely to benefit from biologic therapies.63 Approximately 40% of patients with COPD exhibit eosinophilic inflammation, and this subgroup may derive greater benefit from targeted treatment strategies. Among emerging biomarkers, EDN has attracted particular interest because of its stability and detectability in blood, urine, and BAL fluid.64 EDN has been associated with persistent asthma, nocturnal symptoms, and acute exacerbations, and may also participate in antiviral host defense, further supporting its potential clinical utility.65

In clinical practice, integrating blood eosinophil counts with fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), allergen-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE), and lung function indices may improve biologic selection, treatment monitoring, and prediction of future exacerbation risk.66 At the same time, elevated eosinophil levels have been associated with specific clinical phenotypes of COPD, accelerated lung function decline, and emphysematous features on chest CT in some COPD populations, particularly in studies using a threshold of ≧300 cells/μL.34,67 Collectively, these findings suggest that blood eosinophil assessment may help identify high-risk COPD phenotypes, facilitate disease stratification, and support longitudinal monitoring of treatment response.

Although blood eosinophil counts are currently the most widely used biomarker in clinical practice, they have several limitations.32 Eosinophil levels may fluctuate over time and can be influenced by corticosteroid treatment, infections, smoking status, and comorbidities. Furthermore, blood eosinophil counts do not always accurately reflect airway eosinophilic inflammation or predict treatment response across different respiratory diseases. These observations support that combining eosinophil counts with biomarkers such as FeNO, EDN, sputum eosinophils, and clinical characteristics may improve patient stratification and therapeutic decision-making.

However, broader clinical implementation of eosinophil-related biomarkers will require standardized protocols for blood and sputum assessment, including sample processing, measurement procedures, and interpretation criteria. Such standardization may improve diagnostic accuracy, result reproducibility, and the reliability of treatment evaluation and clinical decision-making.68 On the one hand, future longitudinal studies integrating lung function trajectories, immunophenotyping, and long-term follow-up are needed to clarify the mechanisms by which eosinophils influence lung function decline, identify optimal intervention windows, and support individualized treatment strategies. On the other hand, future studies should determine whether eosinophil functional phenotypes rather than eosinophil counts better predict lung function decline and therapeutic responses. In addition, advances in artificial intelligence may enable the development of risk prediction models that combine immunological, physiological, and environmental exposure data to support real-time assessment and more precise management of respiratory diseases. Although multi-omics approaches have considerably expanded our understanding of eosinophil biology, most findings remain exploratory and require prospective validation before routine clinical implementation.

Conclusions

Eosinophils are receiving increased attention due to their central role in the development of respiratory diseases and their direct impact on lung function. Monitoring eosinophil counts alongside pulmonary function tests provides critical insights into disease activity, treatment effectiveness, and the risk of acute exacerbations. Advancing new treatment approaches depends on deepening our understanding of how eosinophils drive inflammation and how this process contributes to lung function decline. Ongoing research is likely to establish eosinophils as a key therapeutic target further, opening new avenues for treatment in patients with chronic airway diseases. Nevertheless, the biological and clinical significance of eosinophils differs across respiratory diseases. In asthma and EP, eosinophils act predominantly as pathogenic effector cells, whereas in COPD they also serve as biomarkers for disease stratification and corticosteroid responsiveness. Mechanistically, eosinophils impair lung function through granule protein-mediated cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, mucus hypersecretion, neural dysregulation, airway remodeling, and alveolar-capillary barrier injury. Future studies should further define disease-specific eosinophil phenotypes to improve precision medicine.
