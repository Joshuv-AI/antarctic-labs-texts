Reduced cough severity and improved confidence in self-management following completion of a Virtual Physiotherapy Group Intervention for Chronic Cough: ViP-Cough
Authors: Charles C. Reilly, Ellen Shiner, Sarah V. Floyd, Inge Vermeulen, Cameron Ross, Peter Cho, Caroline J. Jolley, Surinder S. Birring
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionProlonged waiting times significantly limited access to our respiratory physiotherapy service for cough control therapy. In response, we developed a virtual physiotherapy group intervention for individuals with chronic cough (ViP-Cough) to improve accessibility. This article describes the uptake and potential benefits of this novel approach.MethodsA service evaluation was carried out utilising a retrospective observational cohort study design involving patients with chronic cough referred to a single-centre respiratory physiotherapy service. All referrals were assessed by a specialist physiotherapist and offered access to ViP-Cough, a single-session virtual group intervention comprising: 1) education and lifestyle advice; 2) cough control strategies; and 3) breathing pattern retraining and vocal hygiene. Patients completed outcome measures at baseline and 4 weeks post-intervention, including numerical rating scales (NRS) for cough severity, frequency and impact on daily activities (0–10; higher scores indicating worse outcomes), and confidence in self-management (0–10; higher scores indicating better outcomes). Descriptive statistics and paired samples t-tests were used for analysis.ResultsOf the 194 patients referred (median age 61 years; 79% female), 70% reported cough duration >5 years. Of those screened, 155 (80%) opted in; 116 attended, and 106 completed all assessments. Significant improvements were observed in cough severity (mean difference (95% CI) −1.26 (−0.82– −1.70), p<0.001), frequency (−1.19 (−0.79– −1.60), p<0.001), impact on daily life (−1.16 (−0.23– −1.61), p<0.001) and confidence in self-management (+2.16 (1.64–2.68), p<0.001).ConclusionViP-Cough is a promising, scalable and low-cost approach to delivering nonpharmacological cough therapy. These preliminary findings support further evaluation in a randomised controlled trial.

Introduction

Chronic cough, defined as a cough persisting for >8 weeks, affects ∼10% of the general adult population [1, 2]. Research consistently highlights its negative impact on individuals, inclusive of social, physical and psychological outcomes [1, 3, 4] The current perspective among researchers and clinicians specialising in chronic cough is that it represents a hypersensitivity disorder, characterised by vagal nerve neuropathy and central nervous system dysfunction [2, 5]. Recent clinical guidelines increasingly align with the paradigm shift that chronic cough is a distinct disease rather than merely a symptom of an underlying condition [2, 3, 6].

Nonpharmacological approaches to self-management of chronic cough include education, laryngeal hygiene and hydration, cough control techniques, breathing exercises and psychoeducational counselling [7–12]. Nonpharmacological interventions for chronic cough have been consistently shown to enhance quality of life and reduce cough frequency [7, 9–11]. Clinical guidelines recommend these treatments [2, 6, 13]; however, access in clinical practice is often restricted due to shortages of specialist staff (such as speech and language therapists and physiotherapists), limited funding and capacity constraints. As these interventions require significant time and resources, traditionally these interventions have been provided on an individualised basis, typically through face-to-face sessions over four to six treatment appointments [7, 9–11].

Following the COVID-19 pandemic, prolonged waiting times significantly limited access to our respiratory physiotherapy service for cough control therapy. In response, we developed a virtual physiotherapy group intervention for individuals with chronic cough (ViP-Cough) to improve accessibility. The aim of this study is to describe both the uptake and potential benefits of this novel approach.

Methods

This project was conducted as a service evaluation utilising a retrospective observational cohort study design involving patients with chronic cough who were referred to respiratory physiotherapy for cough control therapy at a single centre (King's College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust) between October 2022 and February 2025.

Respiratory physiotherapy outpatient referral criteria

Consecutive patients with chronic cough from a specialist cough clinic were assessed by the responsible clinicians and referred for specialist physiotherapy input for cough control therapy, in accordance with current guidelines [2, 13]. Patients who were either newly referred or already in the waiting list for cough control therapy were screened and offered the ViP-Cough intervention. Patients that opted for ViP-Cough were booked in to the next available session.

Individuals referred who were non-English speaking and those without access to the necessary technology, due to the virtual, group-based nature of the ViP-Cough intervention, were unable to access ViP-Cough, and such patients were offered face-to-face physiotherapy support.

Physiotherapy assessment

A standardised physiotherapy assessment was conducted as a one-on-one video appointment (Microsoft teams) for all ViP-Cough patients (see supplementary material S1 for full details).

The ViP-Cough intervention

The ViP-Cough intervention consisted of a single group therapy session which provided patients with education and evidence-based nonpharmacological approaches to self-management of chronic cough [7–11] (figure 1).

The ViP-Cough intervention was delivered in real time by highly specialised respiratory physiotherapists (E. Shiner, C. Ross, I. Vermeulen, S. Floyd). The ViP-Cough group therapy sessions were conducted virtually on the Microsoft Teams platform, with a maximum of 12 patients per session. The ViP-Cough group therapy session lasted an hour and included a standardised educational PowerPoint Presentation, followed by a question and answers session. The ViP-Cough intervention was standardised in both the educational content and cough control techniques, which were reinforced using written resources.

The goals of the single ViP-Cough group session were both to support cough management and to reduce cough hypersensitivity. The ViP-Cough group session included three components: 1) education and lifestyle advice; 2) cough control therapy; and 3) breathing pattern retraining and vocal hygiene (detailed description provided in table 1).

Patient empowerment within the ViP-Cough intervention was facilitated through a combination of clinician-led education during the online session, provision of written resources (e-mailed as a PDF version of the presentation) and follow-up telephone consultation. The ViP-Cough intervention development and delivery was informed by the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [14], targeting domains such as knowledge, skills, beliefs about capabilities and behavioural regulation. Detailed description of embedded behaviour change techniques (BCTs) is provided in table 1.

After the session patients were posted and/or e-mailed written resources to act as reinforcement and cues to support them with the self-management advice provided. The written materials consisted of the session's PowerPoint presentation converted into a PDF, which included all educational content, techniques and lifestyle advice discussed during the session.

4 weeks later, patients were followed up by a physiotherapist via a telephone call, which provided the opportunity to review compliance to advice provided in the session, discuss issues they had with techniques, collect patient-reported outcomes, plan ongoing care and to gain feedback regarding the session. While compliance with the recommended techniques was discussed during follow-up, no formal compliance data were collected or analysed.

Patients were asked to rate the perceived helpfulness of the ViP-Cough intervention using a numerical rating scale (NRS) ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater perceived benefit. In most cases, the same clinician conducted the assessment, delivered the therapy and completed the follow-up.

Patient-reported outcome measures

Patients completed outcome measures at baseline and 4 weeks following the group therapy session. These included: 1) an NRS for cough severity (range 0–10; higher scores indicating worse outcomes); 2) NRS for cough frequency (range 0–10; higher scores indicating worse outcomes); 3) NRS for cough impact on daily activity (range 0–10; higher scores indicating worse outcomes); and 4) NRS for confidence in cough self-management (range 0–10; higher scores indicating better outcomes), all based on a 7-day recall period [2, 16, 17]. Initial assessments were done either by telephone or online video call (Microsoft Teams).

Data analysis

Simple descriptive statistics were used to summarise referrals, uptake of ViP-Cough and patient characteristics. Pre–post intervention outcomes were analysed using paired samples t-test. Assumptions for the paired t-test were considered, and normality of the difference scores was assumed based on the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), given the sample size of 106 participants. The CLT suggests that for moderately large samples (typically ≥30), the sampling distribution of the mean difference approximates normality even when the underlying data are not normally distributed, thereby supporting the use of parametric testing in this study [18]. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d to quantify the magnitude of change between pre- and post-intervention measures and are reported alongside p-values to aid interpretation of clinical relevance. All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Ethical approval

As all patients were receiving routine care provided by King's College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust (KCH), this project was classified as a service evaluation and quality improvement initiative. Accordingly, NHS Research Ethics Committee (REC) approval was not required. Local institutional approvals were obtained prior to commencing the evaluation. The ViP-Cough intervention represented standard care at KCH, introduced as part of a quality improvement initiative. No comparator or control group was included. Crucially, the project did not meet the criteria for research as defined by the UK Policy Framework for Health and Social Care Research. This classification was confirmed using the Health Research Authority (HRA) decision tool, Is my study research? (https://hra-decisiontools.org.uk/research/).

Results

Of these, 70% reported experiencing cough symptoms for >5 years. Of those screened, 155 (80%) opted for ViP-Cough, 11 declined as their preference was for individualised treatment, six were limited by information technology (IT) issues and two language barriers, 17 did not attend their referral screening appointment and three declined all physiotherapy input (table 2).

Of the 155 patients that opted into ViP-Cough and were subsequently booked into the ViP Cough group intervention, 116 (75%) attended. Among those that attended, 106 patients completed all assessment outcome measures and were included in the final analyses.

Patients referred for cough control therapy who opted for the ViP-Cough intervention had characteristics representative of the overall population (table 2). Barriers related to IT (n=6), including lack of equipment, internet access and digital literacy deterred some individuals from being able to choose the ViP-Cough option (table 2). These IT-related barriers were primarily among older individuals living in areas of high deprivation (table 2). Language appeared to be a less significant barrier (n=2) to the uptake of ViP-Cough (table 2). A subset of patients declined physiotherapy input altogether, regardless of the delivery method (table 2).

Additional characteristics of patients who opted for ViP-Cough are summarised in table 3. The majority (72.3%) of patients had a chronic refractory cough, and over 70% of patients reported a cough duration of >5 years. 68.4% of patients reported that their cough was constant, occurring throughout the day and every hour (table 3). Patients indicated high scores (mean±sd) on NRS scales cough severity (7.18±1.96), cough frequency (6.66±1.90) and impact on activities of daily life (6.22±2.73) (table 3). Overall, 57.4% of patients reported no symptoms of sinus disease or of gastro-oesophageal reflux. Among those with sinus symptoms, 31.6% indicated their symptoms were well managed. 33.5% of patients with gastro-oesophageal reflux reported effective symptom management (table 3). Mouth breathing, or a combination of mouth and nasal breathing, was self-reported by 43.8% of patients (table 3). 27.7% of patients referred with chronic cough had a coexisting respiratory diagnosis documented by their clinician, the most prevalent being asthma (table 3).

Patients who completed the ViP-Cough intervention (n=106) demonstrated statistically significant improvements across all measured domains (figure 2). Cough severity, assessed using the NRS, decreased from a mean±sd pre-intervention score of 6.84±1.93 to a post-intervention score of 5.58±2.25. The mean difference was −1.26 (95% CI −0.82 to −1.70), with a moderate effect size (Cohen's d 0.55; p<0.001). NRS cough frequency also showed a significant reduction, with mean±sd scores declining from 6.44±1.88 pre-intervention to 5.25±2.25 post-intervention. The mean difference was −1.19 (95% CI −0.79 to −1.60), corresponding to a moderate effect size (Cohen's d 0.57; p<0.001). NRS cough impact on activities of daily living improved significantly, with mean±sd scores decreasing from 5.78±2.67 to 4.62±2.97. The mean difference was −1.16 (95% CI −0.23 to −1.61), with a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen's d 0.48; p<0.001). NRS confidence in cough self-management increased following the intervention. Mean±sd scores increased from 3.85±2.34 to 6.01±2.16, resulting in a mean difference of 2.16 (95% CI 1.64 to 2.68) and a large effect size (Cohen's d 0.80; p<0.001) (figure 2 and table 4). Exploratory subgroup analysis demonstrated that these improvements were maintained regardless of whether patients with chronic refractory cough had coexisting respiratory disease or not (table 5). The mean±sd NRS score for perceived usefulness of ViP-Cough was 7.6±2.3.

Discussion

Key findings

We adopted a pragmatic, problem-solving approach to the development and implementation of ViP-Cough, utilising existing clinical staff resources and infrastructure to deliver cough respiratory physiotherapy in a single-session online group format. The primary objective of ViP-Cough was not to replace individualised face-to-face treatments, but to enhance access to education and evidence-based self-management interventions for chronic cough and reduce waiting times. The high uptake rate among those offered ViP-Cough indicates strong acceptability. ViP-Cough represents an example of frugal innovation [19], addressing the challenges posed by limited clinical service capacity and workforce shortages in meeting the increasing demand for cost-effective cough-specific therapy within healthcare services [13, 14, 19, 20].

Patients who completed the ViP-Cough intervention (n=106) demonstrated statistically significant improvements across all measured outcomes. The mean reductions in cough severity (−1.26), frequency (−1.19) and impact on daily life (−1.16), all measured on a 0–10 NRS, exceed the commonly accepted minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold of ≥1.0 point for chronic numeric rating (0–10) symptom scales, e.g. chronic breathlessness [21, 22] and chronic pain [23, 24]. These changes corresponded to moderate effect sizes: cough severity (Cohen's d 0.55), cough frequency (0.57) and impact on daily life (0.48), indicating likely clinically meaningful improvement for many patients. However, we acknowledge that the lower bounds of the 95% confidence intervals for these outcomes (−0.82, −0.79 and −0.23, respectively) fall below the MCID threshold, indicating that while many patients likely experienced clinically meaningful improvements, the effect may not have been uniform across the entire cohort. Although limited by sample size, exploratory subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent improvements in patients both with and without coexisting respiratory disease.

Confidence in cough self-management improved by +2.16 points (95% CI 1.64 to 2.68, p<0.001), with a large effect size (0.80). Although a formal MCID for this measure has not yet been established in chronic cough populations, the magnitude of change is above the 1-point threshold used in related respiratory symptom scales [21–24], therefore suggesting a clinically meaningful enhancement in self-efficacy. This is particularly relevant given the behavioural nature of the ViP-Cough intervention, where patient confidence and engagement are critical to sustained symptom control.

Potential mechanisms underpinning observed improvements

ViP-Cough provided participants with evidence-based information about chronic cough, alongside practical strategies for symptom monitoring, trigger reduction and improved cough control through breathing techniques and behavioural modification [25]. Emerging evidence including functional magnetic resonance imaging data and behavioural treatment outcomes suggests central neural processing plays a key role in chronic refractory cough and treatment outcomes [26]. We hypothesise that the improvements seen in NRS cough scores may also have been mediated by enhanced self-efficacy [25]. However, it is possible ViP-Cough also impacts peripheral mechanisms in chronic cough. Notably, ∼65% of patients with refractory cough improved with Gefapixant, a P2X3 antagonist that acts peripherally and does not cross the blood–brain barrier [27]. Therefore, both peripheral and central abnormalities are likely to contribute to chronic refractory cough, with their relative importance varying between individuals. Future work should aim to better delineate these mechanisms to optimise personalised treatment strategies.

Notably, mouth and mixed (mouth–nose) breathing were common in our cohort, highlighting the need to assess dysfunctional breathing in chronic cough [28]. The inclusion of the Breathing Pattern Assessment Tool (BPAT) [29] in both routine cough assessment and future clinical trials may provide valuable insights and enhance the evaluation of treatment outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

The novelty of our approach is delivering evidence-based self-management interventions [2, 7–9, 11, 13] traditionally provided over time and in person, as a single online group session. To our knowledge this is the first time that this approach has been used in chronic cough. The clinical decision making underpinning the offer of ViP-Cough as a single group treatment session was informed by a randomised controlled trial [30], which found no added benefit, including cost-effectiveness, of three versus one breathing training session for breathlessness self-management.

As this was a retrospective service evaluation, without a control group, we acknowledge that observed pre–post treatment effects may reflect placebo effect, symptom fluctuation regression to the mean or other confounding factors [31, 32]. Furthermore, whilst most chronic cough therapy models have been led by speech and language therapists (SLTs), the ViP-Cough intervention was developed and delivered by specialist respiratory physiotherapists, reflecting the available workforce within our clinical service, which does not include SLT input. We are unable to directly compare professional differences in delivering nonpharmacological self-management interventions, and so we cannot comment on whether one model is more favourable. Therefore, our data should be considered preliminary hypothesis-generating to inform subsequent randomised controlled trials of ViP-Cough.

It is important to acknowledge that some patients who were scheduled for a ViP-Cough appointment did not attend. A notable limitation of this service improvement project is the absence of data to explore the reasons behind these “did not attend” (DNA) instances. However, the impact of DNAs, which contribute to clinical inefficiencies and incur significant costs to healthcare systems [33], may be somewhat mitigated in group-based intervention formats such as ViP-Cough.

Patients were not specifically asked whether they intended to pursue face-to-face treatment after ViP-Cough. Although follow-up plans were discussed during the final session, we acknowledge that some individuals may have chosen not to pursue further treatment for reasons unrelated to treatment satisfaction such as travel constraints, scheduling difficulties or assumptions about the similarity of treatment content, and this represents a limitation of the current evaluation.

The absence of validated and objective outcome measures is a limitation of this service evaluation. Conducted within routine clinical practice, the study prioritised pragmatic, patient-reported outcomes that were feasible to collect across a large cohort. The NRS used for assessing cough severity, frequency, impact and self-management confidence are commonly employed in clinical settings and supported by emerging validation studies [17]; however, they do not replace validated instruments such as the Leicester Cough Questionnaire or objective cough frequency monitoring. Future clinical trials must incorporate validated and objective outcome measures to strengthen the evidence base for the ViP-Cough intervention. A potential source of bias in this study is that, in most cases, the same clinician conducted the assessment, delivered the intervention and completed the follow-up. This lack of blinding may have influenced both the delivery and evaluation of the intervention and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Implications for service development and future research

Given the positive findings from our service evaluation, ViP-Cough will continue to be offered as part of our respiratory physiotherapy outpatient clinical service. We must however ensure that the digital transformation of health services and the development of digital interventions do not further exacerbate health inequalities by widening the digital divide between those who can and cannot access digital healthcare [34]. Our findings emphasise the importance of patient choice in how individuals engage with and receive healthcare. For some, group therapy and virtual treatment options may not be appropriate or preferential. This insight is crucial for clinical service planning, as it underlines the need to ensure that clinical service capacity can meet the demand for varying models of care delivery such as group therapy, one-to-one virtual appointments and one-to-one face-to-face consultations.

Conclusion

ViP-Cough offers a promising approach for delivering evidence-based non-pharmacological cough control therapy at scale, and lower cost. These preliminary positive findings warrant further testing in a randomised controlled trial.
