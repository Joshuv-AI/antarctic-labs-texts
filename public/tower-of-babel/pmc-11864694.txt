NLRP4 drives olaparib resistance in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Mingming Xiao, Xianjun Yu, Si Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOlaparib has been approved as a treatment for metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma in patients with BRCA1 (BRCA1 DNA repair associated) or BRCA2 mutations. However, a large portion of pancreatic cancer patients either exhibit inherent resistance or develop resistance over time. Understanding the molecular mechanisms that drive this resistance is crucial to develop more effective targeted therapies. In this study, we found that NLRP4 (NLR family pyrin domain containing 4) upregulation is associated with increased resistance to olaparib in pancreatic cancer. In addition, NLRP4 plays a role in both the DNA damage response (DDR) and autophagy. Specifically, NLRP4 enhances DNA repair capacity and leads to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and autophagy upon olaparib treatment. Notably, NLRP4-generated mitochondrial ROS promote autophagy without directly impacting DNA damage. Inhibition of either mitochondrial ROS production with MitoQ or autophagy with chloroquine (CQ) could sensitize pancreatic cancer cells to olaparib. These findings emphasize NLRP4’s role in promoting both autophagy and DNA repair in response to olaparib, suggesting that patients with low NLRP4 expression might respond more favorably to olaparib treatment.KEYWORDS: Olaparib, NLRP4, DNA damage response, autophagy, ROS

Olaparib, a PARP (poly ADP-ribose polymerase) inhibitor, has emerged as a promising therapeutic option for pancreatic cancer patients harboring BRCA1 (BRCA1 DNA repair associated) or BRCA2 mutations, thanks to its ability to exploit defective DNA repair mechanisms in cancer cells. However, one of the major hurdles is the widespread resistance, either intrinsic or acquired, that limits olaparib’s efficacy. Addressing the molecular basis of this resistance is essential for enhancing olaparib therapeutic potential. Our study summarized here sheds light on how NLRP4 (Nod Like Receptor family pyrin domain containing 4) contributes to olaparib resistance, which we found is primarily through its involvement in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, autophagy, and DNA damage response (DDR) pathways in pancreatic cancer cells [1].

One of the central mechanisms behind olaparib resistance is the restoration of DNA repair pathways, allowing cancer cells to bypass the need for homologous recombination repair (HRR). Our recent study focused on the role of NLRP4, a member of the NLR (Nod Like Receptor) family, in promoting olaparib resistance [1]. We revealed that olaparib treatment induces ROS generation, which in turn triggers autophagy, particularly through NLRP4’s modulation of intracellular ROS levels. Specifically, our study showd that NLRP4 upregulates the expression of NADPH oxidase subunit NOXO1 (NADPH oxidase organizer 1), a key source of ROS, through the inhibition of nuclear localization of SIRT7 (Sirtuin 7), a class III histone deacetylase that belongs to the sirtuin family. This cascade leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and amplified ROS production, contributing to cellular adaptation and survival under olaparib treatment.

Autophagy is a well-established process that helps cells manage oxidative stress and protein degradation. We found that NLRP4-induced ROS play a crucial role in driving autophagy in pancreatic cancer cells. This ROS-autophagy axis offers a protective effect to cancer cells by mitigating the damage caused by excessive ROS and sustaining mitochondrial function, thereby limiting olaparib’s efficacy. Interestingly, our study highlights that NLRP4-induced ROS do not contribute directly to DNA damage, which is a critical finding because it indicates that NLRP4’s contribution to olaparib resistance is primarily through enhanced DNA repair and autophagy, but not through ROS-induced DNA damage.

Reducing mitochondrial ROS generation using MitoQ, a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, or blocking autophagy with the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ), sensitized pancreatic cancer cells to olaparib, demonstrating that targeting these processes can overcome resistance. This opens a new therapeutic avenue in which combining olaparib with autophagy inhibitors or ROS modulators could enhance the response to treatment in pancreatic cancer patients, even in cases in which BRCA mutations are absent.

While ROS are typically linked to DNA damage, this study suggests a more nuanced role for ROS in modulating DDR pathways indirectly through autophagy. RNA-seq data revealed that NLRP4 upregulates critical DDR genes such as BRCA1 and RAD51 (DNA Repair Protein RAD51 homolog 1), both of which are crucial for the homologous recombination repair of DNA breaks. We propose that the upregulation of these genes, much like NLRP4’s effect on NOXO1, might be driven by epigenetic regulation through histone acetylation. These insights suggest that NLRP4 may enhance DNA repair capabilities via mechanisms independent of direct ROS-induced DNA damage.

The findings of our study have significant clinical implications. Most notably, the targeting of autophagy and mitochondrial ROS offers a potential strategy to sensitize BRCA wild-type pancreatic cancer cells to olaparib. This is particularly important because while BRCA-mutated patients are the primary beneficiaries of PARP inhibitors like olaparib, a large proportion of pancreatic cancer patients do not harbor these mutations. By understanding the role of NLRP4 and the interplay between autophagy, ROS, and DNA repair, this study suggests broader applicability for olaparib, potentially extending its use beyond BRCA-mutated tumors. Nonetheless, further research is needed to fully unravel the complexities of NLRP4’s role in DNA repair and its interaction with autophagy. It remains unclear whether additional autophagy molecular players are involved in mediating olaparib resistance, or whether NLRP4 directly regulates the expression of DDR genes through mechanisms other than epigenetic modification. Moreover, the potential therapeutic use of MitoQ and CQ as adjuncts to olaparib therapy in clinical settings warrants further exploration in preclinical and clinical trials.

As research in this area continues, it is likely that the combination of PARP inhibitors with autophagy modulators could become a cornerstone of therapy for pancreatic cancer patients facing olaparib resistance.
