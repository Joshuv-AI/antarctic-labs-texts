Network nonlocality with continuous variables: probing nonbilocality through pseudospin measurements
Sudip Chakrabarty, Amit Kundu, A. S. Majumdar

Abstract
Quantum networks offer a compelling platform for probing nonlocal correlations beyond the Bell scenario. While network nonlocality has been well studied in discrete-variable (DV) systems, its exploration in continuous-variable (CV) systems remains limited. In this work, we study the bilocal scenario, the simplest quantum network, in a CV framework using pseudospin measurements. We show that two-mode squeezed vacuum (TMSV) states violate the bilocality inequality for any nonzero squeezing, with the violation increasing monotonically and reaching the maximum in the infinite squeezing limit. The robustness of this violation is explored in the presence of local thermal noise. Next, we demonstrate nonbilocality in two key non-Gaussian states, viz. , the entangled coherent state and the CV Werner state. Finally, we investigate the enhancement of nonbilocal correlations through photon subtraction applied to the TMSV state. We show that a coherent superposition of single-photon subtractions across modes enables maximal bilocality violation even at zero squeezing, demonstrating non-Gaussianity as a strong resource in the low-squeezing regime. Our findings underscore the potential of CV systems for realizing network-based quantum correlations.

Quantum networks have emerged as a foundational setting for exploring nonlocal correlations that go beyond those captured by standard Bell-type experiments

In this work, our motivation is to investigate quantum nonlocality in a bilocal network involving CV states. To this end, we demonstrate nonbilocality in various examples, including pure and noisy Gaussian states, as well as pure non-Gaussian states. In order to probe nonbilocal features in these infinite-dimensional systems, we employ pseudospin measurements. We show that the TMSV state violates the bilocality inequality for any nonzero squeezing, with the violation growing monotonically and reaching the maximum in the infinite-squeezing limit. We study the robustness of this violation under local thermal noise.

Beyond the ideal and noisy TMSV states, we examine the effect of single photon subtraction on enhancing nonbilocality. Specifically, we consider three scenarios:

(i)

Asymmetric subtraction
(a single photon is subtracted from only one mode),

(ii)

Symmetric subtraction
(a single photon is subtracted from each mode), and

(iii)
a
Coherent superposition of subtractions across both modes

The coherent superposition stands out in the small squeezing regime, it outperforms the other schemes and achieves the maximum at zero squeezing—a valuable feature given experimental constraints on squeezing. We further explore the nonbilocality of other non-Gaussian CV states, including entangled coherent state and CV Werner state, both of which exhibit significant violations, underscoring the potential of CV resources in quantum networks.

II
Preliminaries

This section outlines the essential background for our study. We first recall the bilocal network, which forms the foundational structure for our investigation. This is followed by a discussion of pseudospin observables and their relevant properties, which play a central role in our analysis of nonbilocality in CV states.

II.1
Bilocal versus nonbilocal correlations

In this subsection, we outline the structure of the bilocal network (see Fig.
1
). This network involves three spatially separated observers: Alice, Bob, and Charlie. The central party, Bob, shares an entangled state with Alice, distributed by a source
S_{1}
, and another entangled state with Charlie, distributed by a second independent source
S_{2}
.

Figure 1
:
Bilocal network scenario

The defining feature of the bilocal scenario is the independence of the two sources. In a classical hidden variable framework, this implies that the variables associated with
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, denoted by
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, are statistically independent. Any correlation that respects the bilocal structure must admit a probability decomposition of the form:

\displaystyle p(A,B,C

\displaystyle|x,y,z)=\int\!\!\int d\lambda_{1}d\lambda_{2}q_{1}(\lambda_{1})q_{2}(\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle\times p(A|x,\lambda_{1})p(B|y,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})p(C|z,\lambda_{2}),

(1)

where
x
,
y
, and
z
denote the measurement settings chosen by Alice, Bob, and Charlie, respectively, and
A
,
B
, and
C
are their corresponding outcomes.

In the quantum realization, Bob receives two particles, one from each entangled pair, and performs a joint measurement—typically a Bell state measurement (BSM)—on them. Alice and Charlie each receive one particle from their respective pairs and perform local measurements with binary inputs
x,z\in\{0,1\}
and outputs
A_{x},C_{z}\in\{\pm 1\}
. Bob’s measurement yields two binary outcomes, denoted as
B_{0},B_{1}\in\{\pm 1\}
.

Taking into account the bilocal decomposition, it can be shown that any bilocal hidden variable model must satisfy the following inequality
Gisin et al. 2017
:

S_{\mathrm{biloc}}=\sqrt{|I|}+\sqrt{|J|}\leq 2,

(2)

where the correlators
I
and
J
are defined as

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\langle(A_{0}+A_{1})B_{0}(C_{0}+C_{1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sum_{x,z=0}^{1}A_{x}B_{0}C_{z}\rangle,

(3)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\langle(A_{0}-A_{1})B_{1}(C_{0}-C_{1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sum_{x,z=0}^{1}(-1)^{x+z}A_{x}B_{1}C_{z}\rangle.

(4)

Here,
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the expectation value taken over many experimental runs.

Violation of the inequality in Eq. (
2
) provides evidence of non-bilocal correlations—that is, correlations that cannot be explained by any bilocal hidden variable model. This offers a refined notion of nonclassicality, going beyond standard Bell nonlocality by incorporating assumptions about source independence.

II.2
Pseudospin measurements

Pseudospin measurements were introduced to study Bell nonlocality with the TMSV state
Chen et al. 2002
. Later generalization have enabled to capture nonlocal correlations with odd photon number difference between the two modes
Zhang et al. 2011
. For a single-mode light field, the generalized pseudospin operators are defined in the Fock basis
\{\ket{n}\}
as follows:

\displaystyle s_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\ket{2n+q}\bra{2n+q+1}+\ket{2n+q+1}\bra{2n+q}\right),

\displaystyle s_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\ket{2n+q}\bra{2n+q+1}-\ket{2n+q+1}\bra{2n+q}\right),

\displaystyle s_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\ket{2n+q+1}\bra{2n+q+1}-\ket{2n+q}\bra{2n+q}\right),

(5)

where
q
is an integer. These operators satisfy the standard
\mathfrak{SU}(2)
algebra, making them formally analogous to Pauli matrices
\bm{\sigma}^{j}
, and are thus interpreted as effective spin-
1/2
operators acting in the photon-number parity space.

The operator
s_{z}
corresponds to the negative of the parity operator
P=(-1)^{N}
, where
N
is the photon number operator, i.e.,
s_{z}=-P
. The raising and lowering operators
s_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(s_{x}\pm is_{y})
serve as "parity-flip" operators. In terms of annihilation and creation operators, one can express
s_{-}
as:

s_{-}=\frac{I+(-1)^{N}}{2\sqrt{N+1}}\hat{a},

The commutation relations among the pseudospin operators are:

\left[s_{z},s_{\pm}\right]=\pm 2s_{\pm},\quad\left[s_{+},s_{-}\right]=s_{z},

(6)

which mirror those of the standard spin-
1/2
algebra. Hence, the vector operator
\vec{\textbf{s}}=(s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})
behaves analogously to the spin operator
\vec{\bm{\sigma}}
, and can be viewed as generating rotations in the "parity spin" space of photons.
In particular, rotations in this space can be described by the unitary operator

\displaystyle U({\tau},\bf{\hat{n}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-i\frac{\tau}{2}\,{\bf\hat{n}}\cdot\vec{\textbf{s}}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right)-i\left({\bf\hat{n}}\cdot\vec{\textbf{s}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right),

where
\tau
is a rotation angle and
{\bf\hat{n}}
is an arbitrary unit vector. This structure resembles that of
SU(2)
rotations in conventional spin systems, allowing a direct mapping between operations on CV states and qubit-based formalisms.

III
Bilocal correlations in CV systems

In this section, we establish the mathematical framework necessary to study bilocal correlations in CV quantum networks using pseudospin measurements. As explained in the previous section, the use of pseudospin operators allows for an effective connection between the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space of CV systems and the well-known two-dimensional formalism of spin-1/2 systems. This analogy facilitates the construction of Bell-type measurements and bilocal expressions suited for CV states. We begin by defining parity-based Bell like states and outlining partial Bell measurements, followed by the formulation of bilocal correlations and the maximization strategy.

III.1
Partial Bell measurements

Following Ref.
Chen and Zhang 2002
, we consider the parity eigenstates associated with mode
j
of the light field, defined as

\left|+\right\rangle_{j}\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\cal A}_{n}^{(j)}\left|2n\right\rangle_{j},\quad\left|-\right\rangle_{j}\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\cal A}_{n}^{(j)}\left|2n+1\right\rangle_{j},

(8)

where the coefficients
{\cal A}_{n}^{(j)}
satisfy the normalization condition
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|{\cal A}_{n}^{(j)}|^{2}=1
. These parity-defined superpositions of Fock states form a qubit-like basis within an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space and can be viewed as analogues of the spin-up and spin-down states in the standard qubit formalism.

The action of the pseudospin operators on these states is given by:

s_{jz}\ket{\pm}_{j}=\pm\ket{\pm}_{j},\;s_{j\pm}\ket{\pm}_{j}=0,\;s_{j\pm}\ket{\mp}_{j}=\ket{\pm}_{j}.

(9)

Thus,
\ket{\pm}_{j}
are eigenstates of
s_{jz}
with eigenvalues
\pm 1
, and
s_{j\pm}
serve as raising and lowering operators in the parity space—mirroring the behavior of spin ladder operators in two-level systems.

Using the rotation operator defined in Eq. (
7
), one can construct eigenstates of arbitrary spin projections
{\bf\hat{n}}_{j}\cdot\vec{\textbf{s}}_{j}
for each mode. With this structure in place, one can define analogues of the Bell states for CV systems:

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi^{+}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{+}_{1}\ket{+}_{2}+\ket{-}_{1}\ket{-}_{2}\right),

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi^{-}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{+}_{1}\ket{+}_{2}-\ket{-}_{1}\ket{-}_{2}\right),

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^{+}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{+}_{1}\ket{-}_{2}+\ket{-}_{1}\ket{+}_{2}\right),

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^{-}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{+}_{1}\ket{-}_{2}-\ket{-}_{1}\ket{+}_{2}\right).

(10)

These entangled states are defined entirely within an infinite-dimensional Fock space but retain the algebraic structure of qubit Bell states due to the pseudospin formalism.

We now define the partial Bell measurements performed by Bob using projective measurements in the pseudospin basis. Specifically, the measurement operators are:

\displaystyle B_{0}

\displaystyle=(+1)\times(\ket{\Phi^{+}}\bra{\Phi^{+}}+\ket{\Phi^{-}}\bra{\Phi^{-}})

\displaystyle+(-1)\times(\ket{\Psi^{+}}\bra{\Psi^{+}}+\ket{\Psi^{-}}\bra{\Psi^{-}})

\displaystyle=s_{z}\otimes s_{z},

(11)

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=(+1)\times(\ket{\Phi^{+}}\bra{\Phi^{+}}+\ket{\Psi^{+}}\bra{\Psi^{+}})

\displaystyle+(-1)\times(\ket{\Phi^{-}}\bra{\Phi^{-}}+\ket{\Psi^{-}}\bra{\Psi^{-}})

\displaystyle=s_{x}\otimes s_{x}.

(12)

Importantly,
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
are separable operators composed of local pseudospin measurements. While they distinguish between certain pairs of Bell states, they do not implement a full Bell-state measurement, but rather a partial Bell measurement, as described in Ref.
Branciard et al. 2012
.

III.2
Bilocal correlations with pseudospin observables

For two-qubit systems, bilocal inequalities based on Pauli operators offer necessary and sufficient conditions for detecting nonbilocality
Gisin et al. 2017
. This is due to the fact that Pauli operators span the full operator space. In contrast, pseudospin operators in CV systems form only a subspace of all dichotomic observables. Hence, the pseudospin-based bilocal inequality used here gives a sufficient but not necessary condition for nonbilocality. Obtaining a necessary condition would require optimization over all dichotomic observables in CV systems—a task that is practically infeasible as the basis for constructing any dichotomic observable is also infinite. Nevertheless, pseudospin observables have proven to be effective and one of the best options in various studies—achieving Tsirelson’s bound for EPR states

Banaszek and Wódkiewicz 1998

Dorantes and Lucio M 2009

This formalism sets the stage for the analysis presented in the next sections, where we investigate the bilocality violation for TMSV and
several non-Gaussian states.

IV
Maximal bilocality violation with Two-Mode Squeezed Vacuum

Gaussian states play a central role in the analysis of CV quantum systems
Adesso et al. 2014
. Within this class, the most general form of pure states is given by displaced squeezed states
Braunstein and van Loock 2005
. However, for the purpose of investigating quantum correlations, it is well known that the displacement operation does not affect a state’s nonlocal features. Therefore, without any loss of generality, we restrict our analysis to TMSV, excluding displacements. This state is obtained by squeezing the two-mode vacuum state (
\ket{0,0}

\mathcal{S}(\zeta)=\exp(\zeta\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger} \hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger} - {\zeta}^{*} \hat{a}_1 \hat{a}_2)

\begin{split}|\zeta\rangle=\mathcal{S}(\zeta)\ket{0,0}=\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\sum_{n-0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\ket{n,n}\end{split}

(18)

\lambda=\tanh{r}\in[0,1]

Resistance to thermal noise

The discussions so far have assumed an idealized setting, where the TMSV states are unaffected by any form of environmental disturbance. However, in realistic experimental conditions, the presence of noise is almost unavoidable. Now we take into account an example of such imperfection by introducing local thermal noise into the system. We investigate how this noise influences the strength of bilocality violation, showing that it generally diminishes the violation and can, in certain regimes, even eliminate it altogether.

Consider the noisy TMSV state,

\displaystyle\rho_{*}=p\ket{\zeta}\bra{\zeta}+(1-p)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\ket{n}\bra{n}\otimes\sum_{m=0}\eta_{m}\ket{m}\bra{m}\right),

(25)

where
0\leq p\leq 1
, and
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\eta_{m}=1
. The local thermal noise parameters are given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-\beta_{1}})e^{-\beta_{1}n},

\displaystyle\eta_{m}

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-\beta_{2}})e^{-\beta_{2}m}

(26)

Here,
\beta_{i}=\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{i}}
, representing the inverse temperature of the thermal noise associated with
i
-th mode,
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant, and the magnitude of the associated energies are assumed to be
1
.

Now we consider the bilocality scenario, where
\rho_{AB}=\rho_{BC}=\rho_{*}
, with all states having same squeezing value
r
, and all pseudospin observables with
q_{i}=0
. Using Eq. (
14
) and Eq. (
15
), we get,

\displaystyle\langle A_{x}B_{0}C_{z}\rangle_{\rho_{*}\otimes\rho_{*}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{1}^{2}\cos(\theta_1)\cos(\theta_2)

(27)

\displaystyle\langle A_{x}B_{1}C_{z}\rangle_{\rho_{*}\otimes\rho_{*}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{2}^{2}\sin(\theta_1)\sin(\theta_2)

(28)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left[p+(1-p)\tanh\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}\tanh\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{2}

\displaystyle=pK(r)

(29)

with
K(r)=\text{tanh}(2r)
. Using Eq. (
3
), and Eq. (
4
), we can calculate the values of
I
,
J
and
S
,

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{*}\otimes\rho_{*}}

\displaystyle=4\cos(\theta_1)\cos(\theta_2)\mathcal{N}_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle J_{\rho_{*}\otimes\rho_{*}}

\displaystyle=4\sin(\theta_1)\sin(\theta_2)\mathcal{N}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle S_{\rho_{*}\otimes\rho_{*}}

\displaystyle=2\left[\mathcal{N}_{1}\sqrt{\cos(\theta_1)\cos(\theta_2)}+\mathcal{N}_{2}\sqrt{\sin(\theta_1)\sin(\theta_2)}\right]

(30)

The optimal measurement settings, which maximize the value of
S_{\rho_{*}\otimes\rho_{*}}
are given by,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=\theta=\arctan\left(\frac{\mathcal{N}_{2}}{\mathcal{N}_{1}}\right),

(31)

such that the maximal bilocality quantity
S^{\max}_{\rho_{*},\rho_{*}}
reduces to,

\displaystyle S^{\max}_{\rho_{*},\rho_{*}}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{1}^{2}+\mathcal{N}_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{p^{2}K^{2}+\left[p+(1-p)\tanh\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}\tanh\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\right]^{2}}

(32)

Thus, violation of the bilocality inequality occurs when,

\displaystyle p>\frac{-\mathcal{E}+\sqrt{\mathcal{E}^{2}-4\mathcal{DF}}}{2\mathcal{D}}

(33)

where,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=K^{2}+(1-\epsilon)^{2},\;\mathcal{E}=2\epsilon(1-\epsilon),\;\mathcal{F}=\epsilon^{2}-1,

with
\epsilon=\tanh\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}\tanh\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}.

Figure 3
:

is plotted as a function of

and

, for fixed inverse temperature parameters

. The red contour line corresponds to
S^{\max}_{\rho_{*},\rho_{*}}=2
, separating the bilocal (
S^{\max}_{\rho_{*},\rho_{*}}\leq 2
) and nonbilocal (

To visualize the effect of local thermal noise on the bilocality violation, we analyze the behavior of the maximal bilocality expression
S^{\max}_{\rho_{*},\rho_{*}}
across different state and noise parameters. In Fig.
3
, we consider a fixed thermal environment with
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=1
, and study how
S^{\max}_{\rho_{*},\rho_{*}}
varies with the noise probability
p
and the entanglement parameter
K=\tanh(2r)
. The plot clearly identifies the critical boundary separating the region where the bilocality inequality is violated from where it is satisfied. This demonstrates how both the degree of squeezing and the amount of noise jointly determine the bilocal behavior of the system. Fig.

presents the temperature dependence of the bilocality expression for several choices of
(p,K)
, with
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\beta
. The plot captures the transition from a nonbilocal to a bilocal regime for certain states, as the inverse temperature
\beta
decreases, i.e., as thermal noise increases. This behavior reflects the fragility of nonclassical features under thermal noise and the increasing robustness of the bilocality violation as the temperature is lowered. The comparison across different curves illustrates how higher entanglement and lower noise fractions allow the system to tolerate greater thermal disturbances while still exhibiting nonbilocality. Together, these plots provide a clear and quantitative picture of how thermal noise affects nonbilocal correlations in noisy CV networks.

V
Violation of bilocality for Non-Gaussian states

Despite their broad applicability, Gaussian states have a key limitation: measurement statistics involving only Gaussian operations can always be efficiently simulated classically
Bartlett et al. 2002
. This underscores the importance of non-Gaussian states, which are crucial for achieving universal CV quantum computation
Lloyd and Braunstein 1999
. In this section, we explore nonbilocality using two prominent non-Gaussian states—entangled coherent state (ECS) and CV Werner state.

V.1
Entangled coherent state (ECS)

The entangled coherent state (ECS)
Sanders 1992
is an important non-Gaussian CV entangled state. Such states, also labeled as "cat" states are regarded to be of foundational as well as practical relevance
Nandi et al. 2024
;
Hoshi et al. 2025
. Several applications in quantum information processing have been studied utilizing ECS
van Enk and Hirota 2001
;
De Fabritiis et al. 2023
. Here we consider the asymmetric ECS (
|{\rm ECS}\rangle
), which can be defined as
Jeong et al. 2003
,

\displaystyle|{\rm ECS}\rangle_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}(|\alpha\rangle|-\alpha\rangle-|-\alpha\rangle|\alpha\rangle),

(34)

\displaystyle\text{where,}\hskip 5.69054pt|\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle,

(35)

where
{\cal N}
is a normalization factor given by,

{\cal{N}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-e^{-4\alpha^{2}})}}

(36)

and
|\alpha\rangle
is a coherent state with
\alpha\neq 0
, we have considered
\alpha
to be real for simplicity.

We consider the bilocal network scenario where the states shared between Alice and Bob
(\ket{\psi_{AB}})
, and the state shared between Bob and Charlie
(\ket{\psi_{BC}}
both are two entangled coherent states,
\ket{\rm{ECS}}_{\alpha}
and
\ket{\rm{ECS}}_{\beta}
respectively. Using Eq. (
14
) and Eq. (
15
), we get,

\displaystyle\langle A_{x}B_{0}C_{z}\rangle_{|{\rm{ECS}}\rangle_{\alpha}\otimes|{\rm{ECS}}\rangle_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta_1)\cos(\theta_2)

(37)

\displaystyle\langle A_{x}B_{1}C_{z}\rangle_{|{\rm{ECS}}\rangle_{\alpha}\otimes|{\rm{ECS}}\rangle_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=Q^{2}(\alpha,\beta)\sin(\theta_1)\sin(\theta_2)

(38)

where,

\displaystyle Q^{2}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\sum\limits_{n,m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{4n+1}}{\sqrt{(2n)!(2n+1)!}}\frac{\beta^{4m+1}}{\sqrt{(2m)!(2m+1)!}}\right)^{2}}{\cosh(\alpha^2)\sinh(\alpha^2)\cosh(\beta^2)\sinh(\beta^2)}

(39)

Using Eq. (
3
), and Eq. (
4
), we can calculate the values of
I
,
J
and
S
,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=4\cos(\theta_1)\cos(\theta_2)

\displaystyle J_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=4Q(\alpha,\beta)\sin(\theta_1)\sin(\theta_2)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=2\left[\sqrt{\cos(\theta_1)\cos(\theta_2)}+Q(\alpha,\beta)\sqrt{\sin(\theta_1)\sin(\theta_2)}\right]

(40)

The optimal measurement settings, which maximize the value of
S_{\zeta}
, also the violation of the bilocality inequality considered in Eq. (
2
), are given by,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=\theta=\arctan(Q)

(41)

\displaystyle\text{Hence,}\hskip 5.69054pt\cos

\displaystyle\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+Q^{2}}}\hskip 5.69054pt\text{and}\hskip 5.69054pt\sin{\theta}=\frac{Q}{\sqrt{1+Q^{2}}}

such that the maximal bilocality quantity
S^{\max}
reduces to,

\displaystyle S^{\max}_{\alpha,\beta}=2\sqrt{1+Q^{2}}

(42)

Thus ECS always violate the bilocality inequality Eq. (
2
) as,
0<Q(\alpha,\beta)<1
, and the degree of violation is completely specified by magnitude of
Q(\alpha,\beta)
.
Note that,
Q(\alpha,\beta)
approaches
1
when
\alpha,\beta\rightarrow 0
(but
\alpha,\beta\neq 0
) giving maximal violation of the bilocality inequality (see Fig.
5
), and when
\alpha,\beta\rightarrow\infty
.

when
\alpha\rightarrow 0
.

V.2
CV Werner state

In the DV regime, the Werner state
Werner 1989
is a convex mixture of a maximally entangled state and the maximally mixed state:

\rho_{\text{Werner}}=p\ket{\Psi_{d}}\bra{\Psi_{d}}+\frac{1-p}{d^{2}}I_{1}\otimes I_{2},\;\;0\leq p\leq 1,

(43)

where
\ket{\Psi_{d}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\ket{i}_{1}\ket{i}_{1}
. Extending this concept to CV systems is nontrivial due to the absence of a proper maximally mixed state in infinite dimensions. An operational analogue can be defined by mimicking the DV structure. Since the reduced states of a TMSV are thermal states, a natural CV analogue involves mixing the TMSV with a product of two identical thermal states
Mišta et al. 2002
,

\rho_{T}=(1-\lambda_{2}^{2})^{2}\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{2}^{2(m+n)}\ket{m}\bra{m}\otimes\ket{n}\bra{n},

(44)

where
\lambda_{2}=\tanh s
and
\langle n\rangle_{T}=\sinh^{2}s
.

Thus, the CV Werner state takes the form

\rho_{W}=p\,\rho_{\text{TMSV}}+(1-p)\,\rho_{T},\quad 0\leq p\leq 1,

(45)

where
\rho_{\text{TMSV}}=\ket{\zeta}\bra{\zeta}
, and
\ket{\zeta}
is the TMSV state (
18
). This defines a class of states parametrized by
(p,r,s)
. For simplicity, we take
r=s
, reducing to two parameters
(p,r)
. In the limit
r\to\infty
, the CV Werner state approaches a mixture of a maximally entangled EPR state and a maximally mixed state, closely resembling the DV case.

We now consider the bilocal network scenario where the states shared between Alice and Bob
(\ket{\psi_{AB}})
, and the state shared between Bob and Charlie
(\ket{\psi_{BC}}
both are CV Werner states with parameters
(p_{1},r)
and
(p_{2},r)
respectively. Using Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
), we get,

\displaystyle\langle A_{x}B_{0}C_{z}\rangle_{\rho_{W_{1}}\otimes\rho_{W_{2}}}

\displaystyle=T_{I}\cos(\theta_1)\cos(\theta_2)

(46)

\displaystyle\langle A_{x}B_{1}C_{z}\rangle_{\rho_{W_{1}}\otimes\rho_{W_{2}}}

\displaystyle=T_{J}\sin(\theta_1)\sin(\theta_2)

(47)

where

\displaystyle T_{I}

\displaystyle=p_{1}p_{2}+(1-K^{2})(p_{1}-2p_{1}p_{2}+p_{2})

\displaystyle+(1-p_{1})(1-p_{2})(1-K^{2})^{2}\;\;,\;\;\;

\displaystyle T_{J}

\displaystyle=p_{1}p_{2}K^{2}\;,

(48)

with
K=\tanh{2r}
. Using Eq. (
3
), and Eq. (
4
), we can calculate the values of
I
,
J
and
S
,

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{W_{1}}\otimes\rho_{W_{2}}}

\displaystyle=4T_{I}\cos(\theta_1)\cos(\theta_2)

\displaystyle J_{\rho_{W_{1}}\otimes\rho_{W_{2}}}

\displaystyle=4T_{J}\sin(\theta_1)\sin(\theta_2)

\displaystyle S_{\rho_{W_{1}}\otimes\rho_{W_{2}}}

\displaystyle=2\left[\sqrt{T_{I}\cos(\theta_1)\cos(\theta_2)}+\sqrt{T_{J}\sin(\theta_1)\sin(\theta_2)}\right]

(49)

The optimal measurement settings which maximize the violation of the bilocality inequality considered in Eq. (
2
), are given by,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=\theta=\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{T_{J}}{T_{I}}}\;\;\right)

(50)

such that the maximal bilocality quantity
S^{\max}
reduces to,

\displaystyle S^{\max}_{{\rho_{W_{1}},\;\rho_{W_{2}}}}=2\sqrt{T_{I}+T_{J}}

(52)

We analyze the bilocality violation in the following two scenarios:

Case 1:
When both Werner-type resource states have equal noise parameters, i.e.,
p_{1}=p_{2}=p
. We obtain,

\displaystyle S^{\max}_{\rho_{W_{1}},\;\rho_{W_{2}}}

\displaystyle(p_{1}=p_{2}=p)

\displaystyle=2\times\{

\displaystyle p^{2}(1+K^{2})+2p(1-p)(1-K^{2})

\displaystyle+(1-p)^{2}(1-K^{2})^{2}\}^{\frac{1}{2}}

(53)

Violation of the bilocality inequality occurs when,

\displaystyle p>\frac{-E+\sqrt{E^{2}-4D(F-1)}}{2D}

where,
D=3K^{2}-1+(1-K^{2})^{2},\quad E=2K^{2}(1-K^{2}),\quad F=(1-K^{2})^{2}-1
(see Fig.
6
).

In particular, for the extremal case
K=1
, this condition reduces to
p>\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, matching the known threshold for nonbilocality in the two-qubit Werner state. On the other hand, when
p=1
, the expression reduces to that for the ideal TMSV state, recovering maximal nonbilocality.

Case 2:
Now we set,
p_{1}=1
and
p_{2}=p
, we find

\displaystyle S^{\max}_{\rho_{W_{1}},\;\rho_{W_{2}}}(p_{1}=1,\;p_{2}=p)=2\sqrt{1+(2p-1)K^{2}}.

(54)

Here, the bilocality inequality is violated whenever
p>\frac{1}{2}
, for any non-zero squeezing (i.e.,
K>0
) (see Fig.
7
).

when
p>\frac{1}{2}
.

VI
Maximization of the bilocality expression using Correlation matrices

In order to explore further examples of non-Gaussian states, we now adopt
the following procedure. To determine the maximal violation of the bilocality inequality, we follow an optimization method originally developed in Ref.
Gisin et al. 2017
for two-qubit states. This method can be adapted to the bilocal network scenario, where an intermediate party (Bob) performs a Bell-state measurement (BSM), and the outer parties (Alice and Charlie) carry out local measurements along optimized directions.

Let the measurement settings for Alice and Charlie be denoted by unit vectors
(\bm{\vec{a},\vec{a}^{\prime}})
and
(\bm{\vec{c},\vec{c}^{\prime}})
, respectively. Bob performs a BSM in an appropriately chosen basis. The bilocality expression
S_{\text{biloc}}
, defined in Eq. (
2
), involves expectation values over the joint state
\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{BC}
.
I
and
J
within this expression can be rewritten in terms of the local pseudospin observables as:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left[(\bm{\vec{a}+\vec{a}^{\prime}})\cdot\bm{\vec{s}}\otimes s_{z}\otimes s_{z}\otimes(\bm{\vec{c}+\vec{c}^{\prime}})\cdot\bm{\vec{s}}\,\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{BC}\right]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left[(\bm{\vec{a}+\vec{a}^{\prime}})\cdot\bm{\vec{s}}\otimes s_{z}\,\rho_{AB}\right]\cdot\text{Tr}\left[s_{z}\otimes(\bm{\vec{c}+\vec{c}^{\prime}})\cdot\bm{\vec{s}}\,\rho_{BC}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(a_{i}+a_{i}^{\prime})\,t^{AB}_{i3}\cdot\sum_{k}t^{BC}_{3k}\,(c_{k}+c_{k}^{\prime})

(55)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left[(\bm{\vec{a}-\vec{a}^{\prime}})\cdot\bm{\vec{s}}\otimes s_{x}\otimes s_{x}\otimes(\bm{\vec{c}-\vec{c}^{\prime}})\cdot\bm{\vec{s}}\,\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{BC}\right]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left[(\bm{\vec{a}-\vec{a}^{\prime}})\cdot\bm{\vec{s}}\otimes s_{x}\,\rho_{AB}\right]\cdot\text{Tr}\left[s_{x}\otimes(\bm{\vec{c}-\vec{c}^{\prime}})\cdot\bm{\vec{s}}\,\rho_{BC}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(a_{i}-a_{i}^{\prime})\,t^{AB}_{i1}\cdot\sum_{k}t^{BC}_{1k}\,(c_{k}-c_{k}^{\prime})

(56)

Here,
t^{AB}
and
t^{BC}
are the correlation matrices associated with the bipartite states
\rho_{AB}
and
\rho_{BC}
, respectively, with matrix elements

t^{AB}_{ij}=\text{Tr}[s_{i}\otimes s_{j}\,\rho_{AB}],\quad t^{BC}_{ij}=\text{Tr}[s_{i}\otimes s_{j}\,\rho_{BC}],

with
\bm{\vec{s}}=(s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})=(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
. To extract the optimal measurement directions, we invoke the polar decomposition of the correlation matrices. Each matrix
t^{AB}
can be decomposed as:

t^{AB}=U^{AB}R^{AB}

where
U^{AB}
is a unitary matrix, and
R^{AB}=\sqrt{(t^{AB})^{\dagger}t^{AB}}
is a positive semidefinite matrix. The non-negative eigenvalues of
R^{AB}
are denoted as
\nu_{1}\geq\nu_{2}\geq\nu_{3}\geq 0
. Similarly, for the other state
\rho_{BC}
, the correlation matrix satisfies
t^{BC}=U^{BC}R^{BC}
, with eigenvalues of
R_{BC}
,
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\mu_{3}\geq 0
. The eigenvectors corresponding to the dominant eigenvalues of these matrices define the optimal measurement planes. Bob’s BSM basis is oriented such that the principal axes align with these eigenvectors.

To maximize the bilocality parameter
S_{\text{biloc}}
, we confine Alice’s and Charlie’s measurement vectors to the two-dimensional subspaces spanned by the top two eigenvectors of
R^{AB}
and
R^{BC}
, respectively. This simplification allows the use of the following parameterization:

\displaystyle\bm{\vec{a}}

\displaystyle=(\sin\theta_{1},0,\cos\theta_{1}),

\displaystyle\bm{\vec{a}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(\sin\theta_{1}^{\prime},0,\cos\theta_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\bm{\vec{c}}

\displaystyle=(\sin\theta_{2},0,\cos\theta_{2}),

\displaystyle\bm{\vec{c}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(\sin\theta_{2}^{\prime},0,\cos\theta_{2}^{\prime})

The optimal angles are obtained by maximizing the bilocality expression with respect to the measurement parameters. Setting the partial derivatives of
S_{\text{biloc}}
with respect to all angles to zero yields the following optimality conditions:

\theta_{1}^{\prime}=-\theta_{1},\quad\theta_{2}^{\prime}=-\theta_{2}

and the angle
\theta_{1}
(and similarly
\theta_{2}
) satisfies:

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{1}=\cos\theta_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{1}\mu_{1}}{\nu_{1}\mu_{1}+\nu_{2}\mu_{2}}}

(57)

Substituting these into the bilocality expression, the maximal violation is found to be:

\displaystyle S_{\text{biloc}}^{\max}=2\sqrt{\nu_{1}\mu_{1}+\nu_{2}\mu_{2}}

(58)

This leads to the following condition for violation of the bilocality inequality (Eq.
2
):

\nu_{1}\mu_{1}+\nu_{2}\mu_{2}>1

An interesting limiting case emerges when the two bipartite states are identical, i.e.,
\rho_{AB}=\rho_{BC}
. In this case, the condition above simplifies to the well-known Horodecki criterion for the violation of the CHSH Bell inequality
Horodecki et al. 1995
.

VII
Enhanced bilocality Violation in Single Photon-Subtracted Squeezed States

Violation of bilocality inequality with TMSV has been studied in Sec.
IV
. TMSV can be de-Gaussified through local photon addition or subtraction on individual modes. It has been shown that such non-Gaussian operations lead to a monotonic enhancement of entanglement
Navarrete-Benlloch et al. 2012
. From an experimental perspective, photon subtraction is generally more feasible than photon addition, as the latter typically requires an auxiliary photon pumping apparatus
Kim 2008
. Although both operations can yield enhanced nonlocality in theory, photon subtraction is often the method of choice in practical implementations. In what follows, we investigate how single-photon subtraction influences the extent of violation of bilocality inequality.

We consider three types of photon-subtracted states:

1.

Asymmetric: a single photon is subtracted from one mode.

2.

Symmetric: a single photon is subtracted from both modes.

3.

Coherent superposition of photon subtractions across modes.

In each case, we analyze two network configurations:

1.

A hybrid scenario where Alice-Bob share the photon-subtracted state, while Bob-Charlie share a TMSV state.

2.

A symmetric scenario where both shared states are photon-subtracted and identical.

We assume the squeezing parameter
r
to be the same for all involved states. The corresponding maximization procedures follow the prescription detailed in Sec.
VI
.

VII.1
Asymmetric Photon Subtraction: Single-Mode Operation

We now explore the impact of asymmetric photon subtraction on the maximal violation of the bilocality inequality. Specifically, we consider the case where a single photon is subtracted from one mode of a TMSV state.

Applying the annihilation operator
(\mathbb{I}\otimes\hat{b})
on the second mode of a TMSV yields the unnormalized state

\displaystyle\ket{\xi_{1}}

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\sqrt{n}\ket{n,n-1},

(59)

where
\lambda=\tanh r
and
\hat{b}
is the annihilation operator for the second mode. Upon normalization, this becomes

\displaystyle\ket{\xi_{1}}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda^{2})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1,n}.

(60)

For the state
\ket{\xi_{1}}
the optimal pseudospin measurement settings correspond to the parameters
q=1
for the first mode and
q=0
for the second mode.

Configuration A1:
In the first scenario, Alice and Bob share the photon-subtracted state
\ket{\xi_{1}}
, while Bob and Charlie share the standard TMSV state
\ket{\zeta}
. That is,

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}=\ket{\xi_{1}}\bra{\xi_{1}},\qquad\rho_{BC}=\ket{\zeta}\bra{\zeta}.

The two largest eigenvalues of the correlation matrices
R^{AB}
and
R^{BC}
(see Sec.
VI
), in this case are found to be

\displaystyle\nu_{1}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=1,

(61)

\displaystyle\nu_{2}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=2\lambda(1-\lambda^{2})^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{4n}\sqrt{(2n+1)(2n+2)},

(62)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

(63)

\displaystyle\mu_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}=K(r).

(64)

Thus, the maximal value of the bilocality expression becomes

\displaystyle S^{\max}_{\xi_{1},\zeta}=2\sqrt{\nu_{1}^{(a)}\mu_{1}+\nu_{2}^{(a)}\mu_{2}}.

(65)

Configuration A2:
In the second configuration, both shared states are identical and given by the photon-subtracted state
\ket{\xi_{1}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}=\rho_{BC}=\ket{\xi_{1}}\bra{\xi_{1}}.

In this case, the maximal value of the bilocality expression is,

\displaystyle S^{\max}_{\xi_{1},\xi_{1}}=2\sqrt{(\nu_{1}^{(a)})^{2}+(\nu_{2}^{(a)})^{2}}.

(66)

Our results for bilocality violation versus the squeezing parameter for both the above cases are presented in Fig.
8
. Note that the configuration A2 leads to more bilocality violation compared to the configuration A1.

VII.2
Symmetric Photon Subtraction: Dual-Mode Operation

We now turn our attention to the impact of symmetric photon subtraction on the maximal violation of the bilocality inequality. Specifically, we investigate the case where a single photon is subtracted from both modes of a TMSV.

Applying the annihilation operators
(\hat{a}\otimes\hat{b})
on the TMSV yields the normalized two-mode state

\displaystyle\ket{\xi_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-\lambda^{2}}{\lambda\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}\right)\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}n\ket{n-1,n-1}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda^{2})\sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}(n+1)\ket{n,n},

(67)

where
\lambda=\tanh r
, and
\hat{a}
,
\hat{b}
denote annihilation operators for the first and second mode, respectively.

For the state
\ket{\xi_{2}}

VII.3
Coherent Superposition of Photon Subtractions across Modes

Let us now consider an explicit example of a non-Gaussian state that is obtained with coherent superposition of two asymmetric single photon subtracted states. This photon subtraction operation can be written as,

\mathcal{G}=a\otimes I+(-1)^{k}I\otimes b

(72)

where
k\in\{0,1\}
. It is also assumed that the specific mode from which the photon is subtracted remains unknown. The state is represented as
Chowdhury et al. 2014
;
Mallick et al. 2025
,

\begin{split}\ket{\xi_{3}}=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sinh{r}}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\sqrt{n}[\ket{n-1,n}\\
&+(-1)^{k}\ket{n,n-1}]\\
=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1-\lambda^{2})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\sqrt{n+1}[\ket{n,n+1}\\
&+(-1)^{k}\ket{n+1,n}]\end{split}

(73)

with
\lambda=\tanh{r},\hskip 2.84544ptr>0

k=0

It is known that continuous-variable (CV) entangled states of the form

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}_{n}\ket{n,n+\bar{n}}

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}_{n}\ket{n+\bar{n},n}

do not exhibit a violation of the Bell-CHSH inequality when
\bar{n}
is an odd integer and the pseudospin measurement parameters
q_{i}
are chosen to be zero

. However, we demonstrate that this limitation does not hold when coherent superpositions of such states are considered. In particular, to reveal the nonbilocal nature of the state
\ket{\xi_{3}}
, we evaluate the bilocality expression using pseudospin observables with
q_{i}=0
.

Configuration C1:
In this configuration, the entangled state shared between Alice and Bob is
\ket{\xi_{3}}
, while Bob and Charlie share the original TMSV state
\ket{\zeta}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}=\ket{\xi_{3}}\bra{\xi_{3}},\qquad\rho_{BC}=\ket{\zeta}\bra{\zeta}.

are given by

\displaystyle\nu_{1}^{(c)}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\nu_{2}^{(c)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\lambda^{4}}{(1+\lambda^{2})^{2}}.

The resulting maximal bilocality expression becomes

\displaystyle S^{\max}_{\xi_{3},\zeta}=2\sqrt{\nu_{1}^{(s)}\mu_{1}+\nu_{2}^{(s)}\mu_{2}}.

(76)

Configuration C2:
Here, both shared states are taken to be
\ket{\xi_{2}}
, making this a fully symmetric setting:

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}=\rho_{BC}=\ket{\xi_{3}}\bra{\xi_{3}}.

\displaystyle S^{\max}_{\xi_{3},\xi_{3}}=2\sqrt{(\nu_{1}^{(c)})^{2}+(\nu_{2}^{(c)})^{2}}.

(77)

It may be noted from Fig.

that all photon subtracted cases yield higher bilocality violation compared to the TMSV in the low squeezing regime. An intriguing aspect of the state
\ket{\xi_{3}}
is that its bilocality expression attains the maximum,

\lambda=0

\ket{\xi_{3}}
is not a maximally entangled state in the full infinite-dimensional Hilbert space when

\ket{\xi_{3}}
effectively resides in a two-dimensional subspace. Within this subspace, it is indeed a maximally entangled state, as it takes the form:

\displaystyle\ket{\xi_{3}(r=0)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{01}+\ket{10}),

(78)

which is structurally identical to a Bell state.

The enhancement in the maximal violation of the bilocality inequality due to photon subtraction can be quantified using the measure

\displaystyle{\Delta}=\frac{S^{\max}_{\psi_{AB},\phi_{BC}}-S^{\max}_{\zeta,\zeta}}{S^{\max}_{\zeta,\zeta}}.

VIII
Conclusions

Quantum networks provide a powerful setting for investigating nonlocal correlations that go beyond the conventional Bell framework. Among these, the bilocal scenario serves as a minimal yet foundational model, directly linked to entanglement-swapping protocols
Żukowski et al. 1993
, which play a key role in the construction of quantum repeaters
Azuma et al. 2023
. These networks facilitate the distribution of entanglement over long distances without requiring direct interaction between distant nodes, making them highly suitable for practical quantum architectures. While the study of nonlocal correlations in DV quantum networks has seen substantial progress
Tavakoli et al. 2022
, CV networks have received very less attention. This is despite the notable advantages offered by CV systems—including near-deterministic state preparation, experimental accessibility, and their compatibility with long-distance communication
Braunstein and van Loock 2005
. In this context, our work takes a critical step toward understanding and leveraging nonlocal correlations in CV-based quantum networks.

In this work, we have investigated quantum nonlocality in the bilocal network configuration with CV systems, using pseudospin measurements. We have shown that TMSV states violate the bilocality inequality for any nonzero squeezing, with the degree of violation increasing monotonically with squeezing, and reaching the quantum maximum in the infinite-squeezing limit. We have also shown how the nonbilocal behavior of the TMSV state can get quantitatively
degraded in the presence of local thermal noise. Going beyond Gaussian states, we have demonstrated nonbilocality for two classes of important non-Gaussian states,
viz.
, the entangled coherent state and the CV Werner state. Moreover, we have examined the effect of photon subtraction on bilocality violation. It follows from our analysis that a coherent superposition of single-photon subtraction across modes leads to maximal violation even in the absence of squeezing. This result highlights that non-Gaussianity by itself can give rise to strong nonlocal correlations, making it a valuable resource for quantum protocols, especially when squeezing is minimal.

Our findings contribute significantly to the understanding of network nonlocality in infinite-dimensional systems and open up several promising avenues for future research. These include the study of more complex quantum network configurations using CV resources, and systematic investigations of the role of non-Gaussian operations in enhancing network nonlocality. A further interesting direction would be the extension of our analysis to measurement schemes involving genuine continuous outcomes, which might offer deeper insights into the structure of nonlocal correlations in the CV regime, and further bridge the gap between theory and experimental implementation.
