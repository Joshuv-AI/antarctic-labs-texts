Associations Between Baseline Severity and Treatment Effects in Digital Interventions for Eating Disorders: A Meta‐Regression
Authors: Claudia Liu, Cleo Anderson, Mariel Messer, Jake Linardon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveWhile it is assumed that digital interventions are more suitable for milder eating disorder presentations, the evidence supporting this assumption remains unclear. We conducted meta‐regressions testing whether baseline severity at the study level was associated with treatment effects from digital interventions for eating disorders.MethodDatabases were searched to identify RCTs of digital interventions compared to controls in individuals with diagnostic, subthreshold, or self‐reported eating disorders. Baseline severity was operationalised as global levels of eating disorder psychopathology and objective binge eating. We conducted bivariable and multivariable meta‐regressions to test whether baseline severity was associated with treatment versus control effect sizes and dropout.ResultsThirty‐one trials were included. Bivariable meta‐regressions showed that higher average objective binge eating was associated with greater treatment effect sizes and lower dropout rates. These associations remained significant in most sensitivity analyses, but not in the multivariable meta‐regressions. Average global eating disorder psychopathology levels at baseline were not associated with treatment effects in bivariable meta‐regressions, but was significantly and positively associated with effects in the multivariable model that adjusted for other characteristics.ConclusionHigher baseline severity may not limit the effectiveness or acceptability of digital interventions and in some contexts be associated with larger effects.Trial Registration https://osf.io/j9chm/overview Keywords: digital interventions, eating disorders, meta‐analysis, randomised controlled trials

Introduction

Interventions delivered via modern technologies—including the internet, smartphones, or chatbots—are increasingly recognised as effective and scalable approaches for addressing eating disorder symptoms (Torous et al. 2025). Digital interventions offer several advantages that align well with the needs of individuals with eating disorders, including low cost, flexible delivery formats, and the ability to be completed privately with no or minimal professional support (Graham et al. 2023; Kazdin et al. 2017). A considerable body of evidence indicates that digital interventions are well‐tolerated and produce clinically meaningful reductions in core symptoms relative to controls (Linardon et al. 2020), with some research also showing that certain programs may achieve outcomes that are comparable to traditional in‐person treatments (de Zwaan et al. 2017; van Beers et al. 2025).

However, determining which individuals should be recommended or prescribed a digital intervention programme remains an open question, as the available evidence base offers limited and inconsistent guidance on reliable outcome predictors (McClure et al. 2024). There is frequently an assumption that digital treatment delivery formats are most appropriate for individuals with milder levels of symptom severity (Mohr et al. 2021). This assumption is often grounded in the belief that milder presentations are sufficiently responsive to self‐directed psychological techniques, while more severe cases necessitate ongoing monitoring and structured input from trained professionals or multidisciplinary teams from the outset. Indeed, clinical staging paradigms in psychiatry (Fusar‐Poli et al. 2017)—which are now increasingly recognised in eating disorders (Hyam et al. 2023)—conceptualise mental illness as progressing through successive stages of increasing severity, persistence, and functional impairment, with more intensive intervention reserved for later and more severe stages. This implies that individuals presenting with more severe psychopathology may be viewed as less suitable candidates for digital interventions and, as a result, are often directed towards more intensive treatments from the beginning.

The rapid growth of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) of digital interventions for eating disorders provides a timely opportunity to aggregate these data and examine whether study‐level indicators of baseline symptom severity are associated with clinically relevant treatment outcomes. While the influence of baseline severity on outcomes from digital interventions has been examined in individual studies (McClure et al. 2024), findings have been mixed and difficult to interpret due to methodological limitations common to single trials, including modest sample sizes, inconsistent reporting practices, and limited statistical power for moderator analyses. One way to overcome these limitations is to pool data across multiple trials and test whether study‐level differences in average baseline severity scores predict variation in treatment effects using meta‐regression techniques (Cuijpers et al. 2026). Although this approach provides an initial, indirect test of severity effects, it plays a necessary role in determining whether more resource‐intensive and methodologically sophisticated approaches (e.g., individual participant data meta analyses) are warranted to examine moderation effects at the individual level.

The current study draws on data from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) of digital interventions for eating disorders to examine associations between study‐level indicators of baseline symptom severity and clinically meaningful outcomes. Specifically, we use meta‐regression to test whether baseline severity is associated with treatment effect sizes and with dropout rates across trials. We only focus on effect sizes at post‐test given the limited number of trials reporting follow‐up outcomes. Therefore, any associations detected should not be generalised to longer follow‐up time points.

Method

Study Identification and Selection

The current study represents pre‐registered secondary analyses using data collected from a larger meta‐analytic review on the efficacy of digital interventions for eating disorders (Anderson et al., forthcoming). In this larger, PRISMA‐compliant meta‐analysis, the Medline, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and Scopus databases were searched (October 2025) by combining a series of keywords that are synonymous with eating disorders, digital interventions, and randomised controlled trials. The full search string can be seen in the Supporting Information S1.

For this study, we included RCTs that evaluated the effects of a digital intervention for eating disorders against a control condition (e.g., waitlist, usual care), and which reported relevant baseline severity measures (see outcome section below for further detail). Samples could comprise participants selected for elevated eating disorder symptoms, defined by a threshold or subthreshold diagnosis on a structured interview, scores above validated cut‐offs on standardised measures, or endorsement of eating disorder behaviours at baseline. A digital intervention was defined as one delivered via a computer, mobile phone, or tablet, using platforms such as the Internet, downloadable software, CD‐ROMs, smartphone applications, or chatbots. Guided professional or peer support was permitted. Trials in which the digital intervention was explicitly designed as an adjunct to another experimental treatment (e.g., a digital programme delivered alongside a face‐to‐face psychotherapy as part of the study protocol) were excluded. However, we did not exclude studies in which participants may have accessed additional treatments outside the trial, as this is typically not under experimental control in digital health research. Published and unpublished studies were eligible.

Records from all databases were combined and duplicates removed. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, with potentially eligible studies assessed in full text. Study screening and selection were independently undertaken by two reviewers.

Quality Assessment and Data Extraction

Study quality was assessed using four domains of the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment, and incomplete outcome data (Higgins et al. 2019). Each domain was rated as low, high, or unclear risk. Data were also extracted on participant characteristics (pre‐selection criteria, demographic composition), intervention and control conditions (orientation, guidance, programme name, delivery format), baseline severity indicators, outcome measures, and information required for effect size computation. Extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers, with minor disagreements resolved by consensus.

Outcomes

For this study, analyses focused on two primary outcomes that are routinely reported in trials of digital eating disorder interventions and represent primary indicators of treatment response. The first was global eating disorder psychopathology, operationalised through either the interview or self‐report version of the Eating Disorder Examination (Fairburn and Beglin 1994). The second was the frequency of objective binge eating episodes over the past month, either assessed through self‐report or interview‐based methods. Because trials of digital interventions typically use self‐report measures rather than interviewer‐administered assessments, we prioritised self‐report instruments to maximise consistency in outcome operationalisation. Sensitivity analyses restricted to self‐report measures were also conducted to examine whether findings varied by assessment method, given that symptom ratings obtained via self‐report and interviewer‐administered measures do not show perfect concordance (Berg et al. 2011). These same outcomes were also used to index baseline symptom severity in moderator analyses, defined as the sample mean at baseline for the respective outcome. We additionally examined associations between baseline severity and dropout in treatment arms, with dropout defined as the proportion of participants randomised to the digital intervention who did not complete the post‐intervention assessment (Liu et al. 2025).

Meta‐Analysis

Meta‐analyses were performed using Comprehensive Meta‐Analysis Version 4.0 (Borenstein et al. 2009). The effect size was calculated by dividing the difference between the group means at post‐test by the pooled standard deviation. Hedges' g was reported to correct for small sample bias (Hedges and Olkin 1985). To calculate a pooled effect size, each study's effect size was weighted by its inverse variance (Borenstein et al. 2009). In trials that compared two or more interventions to one control group (or vice versa), the sample size of the shared group was divided to prevent double counting, per recommendations (Higgins and Green 2011). For dropout, event rates were calculated for each study as proportion of participants allocated to the treatment arm who failed to complete the post‐test assessment. Meta‐analyses were conducted on the logit scale using inverse‐variance weighting under random effects models. Pooled estimates and 95% confidence intervals were back‐transformed to the proportion scale and expressed as a percentage (Borenstein et al. 2009). The 95% prediction interval (PI) was also reported to indicate the range within which the true effect of a future study is expected to fall. Heterogeneity was assessed through the I 2 statistic (Higgins and Thompson 2002). Random effects models were used for all analyses.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we pooled effects while excluding outliers, using the ‘non‐overlapping confidence intervals’ approach (Harrer et al. 2021). Second, we pooled effects while removing trials that received a higher risk of bias rating, defined as meeting between 0–2 of the quality criteria. Third, we pooled effects while only including trials that sampled participants with a clinical diagnosis as established through diagnostic interviews. Fourth, we pooled effects while excluding the small number of trials that used an interview‐based method for assessing the indicators of baseline symptom severity.

The association between baseline severity and effect size was examined using meta‐regression analyses. This association was also estimated within each of the sensitivity analyses. Meta‐regressions were estimated using weighted least squares, with inverse‐variance weights derived from the random‐effects model (Borenstein et al. 2009). For dropout analyses, meta‐regressions were conducted on logit‐transformed event rates using the same inverse‐variance weighted random‐effects framework. We first conducted a bivariable meta‐regression with baseline severity entered as the sole predictor. We then performed a multivariable meta‐regression that included baseline severity alongside other key study characteristics as predictors.

Results

Study Characteristics

Of the 36 trials included in the larger meta‐analysis, 31 met full inclusion criteria for this study (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). Supporting Information S1: Table S1 presents the characteristics of the included trials. Fifteen trials sampled participants with a confirmed clinical eating disorder, where six trials each targeted binge‐eating disorder and bulimia nervosa, and three trials targeted transdiagnostic presentations. The remaining trials targeted either subthreshold presentations or pre‐selected participants based on self‐reported endorsement of eating disorder behaviours (e.g., > 1 binge episode a week) at baseline or scoring above a clinical cut‐off on a validated rating scale (e.g., global score > 2.30 on the EDE‐Q). Thirty intervention conditions were based on CBT, 25 offered professional guidance (15 were unguided), and 37 interventions were delivered primarily via computers or the web (3 treatment conditions were delivered via apps). Waitlists were the most common control groups (k = 28). In terms of baseline severity, all trials reported the global score on the EDE‐Q (k = 28) or the EDE (k = 3), while 16 reported objective binge eating, with 14 of these relying on participant self‐report. Seventeen trials met all four risk of bias criteria, six met three, five met two, and three only met one of the criteria (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

Main Analyses

Global Eating Disorder Psychopathology

The pooled effect size of the 40 comparisons between digital interventions and controls for global eating disorder psychopathology at post‐test was g = 0.49 (95% CI = 0.37, 0.60), with a 95% PI of −0.11 to 1.09 and I 2 of 71%. The outcomes of the main analyses and sensitivity analyses are presented in Table 1. Bivariable meta‐regression analyses showed no significant association between baseline symptom severity and treatment effect sizes in the overall sample or across most sensitivity analyses. One exception was observed when restricting the models to clinically diagnosed samples, where higher baseline EDE(Q) global scores were significantly associated with larger effect sizes (b = 1.05, p = 0.001). In the multivariable meta‐regression that adjusted for various trial characteristics (Table 2), higher baseline severity was significantly, positively and uniquely associated with effect sizes (b = 0.22, p = 0.042).

Objective Binge Eating

The pooled effect size of the 20 comparisons for objective binge eating frequency at post‐test was g = 0.42 (95% CI = 0.27, 0.57), with a 95% PI of −0.20 to 1.05 (I 2 = 74%). Sensitivity analyses are provided in Table 1. Bivariable meta‐regression showed a significant association between baseline objective binge eating severity and treatment effect sizes in the overall analysis (b = 0.05, p = 0.040; see Figure 1, left panel). This association remained significant in sensitivity analyses that adjusted for outliers and excluded the one trial that used an interview assessment. Bivariable meta‐regression analyses also indicated a significant positive association between baseline eating disorder psychopathology and treatment effect sizes for objective binge eating (b = 0.44, p = 0.003). In the multivariable meta‐regression, baseline binge eating severity was not significantly associated with effect sizes (Table 2).

Bivariable meta‐regressions on the association between baseline objective binge eating and the post‐test effect size (left panel) and the dropout rate for the digital treatment arm (right panel).

Dropout

The pooled dropout estimate from 37 treatment conditions was 29.4% (95% CI = 24.6, 34.8), with a 95% PI of 0.09–0.62 and high heterogeneity. Table 3 presents the main and sensitivity analyses for dropout. Bivariable meta‐regression revealed a significant relationship between baseline objective binge eating frequency (but not global eating disorder psychopathology) and the logit event rate (b = −0.05, p = 0.034), indicating that higher baseline severity was associated with lower dropout (see the bubble plot in Figure 1, right panel). This association remained significant in sensitivity analyses that adjusted for outliers, included only clinically diagnosed samples, and limited models to one treatment arm per trial. Baseline severity indicators were not significantly associated with dropout rates in the multivariable meta‐regressions (Table 4).

Discussion

We examined whether variation in baseline severity at the study level was associated with treatment effects in digital intervention trials for eating disorders. We found no evidence that higher baseline severity was associated with poorer treatment outcomes, contrary to common assumptions that low‐intensity digital mental health interventions may be less effective for individuals with more severe symptoms (Mohr et al. 2021). Instead, bivariable meta‐regressions indicated the opposite pattern, with higher average baseline objective binge eating frequency associated with larger treatment effects and lower dropout rates overall and across several sensitivity analyses. However, these associations were no longer significant in multivariable models that adjusted for other trial characteristics. In contrast, average baseline levels of global eating disorder psychopathology were not significantly associated with outcomes in bivariable analyses but emerged as a significant, positive correlate of treatment effect sizes in the multivariable model. Overall, these findings provide preliminary evidence that digital interventions may be suitable across a range of severity levels.

There are a number of possible reasons why effects are larger among samples with more severe symptoms at baseline. One reason is that samples with higher baseline severity have more room for improvement, as average scores are further from the measure's minimum threshold. This avoids the floor effect that can occur in milder samples, where the potential for measurable improvement is restricted by the lower bound of the assessment tool. Another reason could be that samples with more severe symptoms may experience a greater sense of urgency and motivation to seek relief. This could promote more active engagement with the digital interventions, which has been linked to larger treatment effects (Linardon et al. 2025). The observed association between higher symptom severity indicators and lower dropout rates provides tentative support for this interpretation. A third possibility is that digital delivery formats may better align with the needs or preferences of individuals with more severe symptoms (Linardon et al. 2021). Greater symptom severity is typically accompanied by heightened barriers to traditional care (e.g., worsened stigma, higher costs, etc.), so largely self‐directed digital tools may present a more accessible and acceptable entry point, promoting stronger uptake and therapeutic engagement.

Broader clinical and research implications emerge from the present findings. Clinically, these results suggest that digital treatment delivery formats may be suitable across the broad range of symptom severity, including among those with more severe psychopathology. Critically, the existing evidence base supports the safety of digital health tools for eating disorders, with minimal evidence of iatrogenic effects, especially when implemented with appropriate clinical oversight (Linardon et al. 2024). This suggests that clinicians, service planners, or policy makers could reasonably advocate the use of empirically validated digital interventions as part of a stepped or blended care approach, either to extend access where specialist services are limited or to augment existing treatment pathways. From a research perspective, the signals suggesting potential prognostic relevance of baseline severity highlight the need for more rigorous approaches that aggregate trial data at the individual participant level to more precisely test whether and how baseline severity influences therapeutic outcomes. Such methods better enable direct modelling of individual‐level heterogeneity and avoid limitations inherent in study‐level meta‐regressions.

These findings should be interpreted within the context of their limitations. First, there was considerable heterogeneity across many of the analyses, indicating that factors beyond baseline severity contribute meaningfully to variability in outcomes. This was somewhat supported in the multivariable meta‐regression models, highlighting the multifactorial nature of treatment response to digital mental health interventions. Second, all observed associations were correlational in nature and should not be interpreted as evidence of causality. Accordingly, these findings must be interpreted cautiously, as unmeasured confounding and study‐level aggregation may influence these relationships. Third, given the limited number of available trials, it was difficult to stratify analyses based on specific diagnostic subtypes. While our results provide evidence for the potential importance of baseline severity in a broad sense, findings cannot be readily generalised to distinct eating disorder subtypes.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that higher baseline severity may not limit the effectiveness or acceptability of digital mental health interventions for eating disorders and may, in certain contexts, be associated with larger treatment effects. These findings challenge common assumptions that lower intensity treatments may only be suitable for milder presentations. They also highlight the need for more granular, individual‐level analyses to clarify for whom and under what set of circumstances digital interventions are most beneficial.
