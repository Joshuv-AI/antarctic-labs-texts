Structure of heavy quarkonia under strong magnetic field
Ahmad Jafar Arifi, Kei Suzuki

Abstract
We investigate the structural modifications of heavy quarkonia in the presence of strong magnetic fields using a constituent quark model. By incorporating the effects of spin mixing and quark Landau levels, we employ a nonrelativistic Hamiltonian that captures the essential features of quark dynamics in a magnetic field. The two-body Schrödinger equation is solved using the cylindrical Gaussian expansion method, which respects the cylindrical symmetry induced by a magnetic field. We extract the corresponding light-front wave function (LFWF) densities and analyze their transverse and longitudinal structures, revealing characteristic features such as transverse momentum broadening. While the longitudinal structure is only slightly modified within the nonrelativistic Hamiltonian, we discuss some corrections that can significantly affect its longitudinal structure. Furthermore, we discuss the structure modifications of excited states and find notable changes in the LFWF densities, and state reshuffling near avoided crossings. These results demonstrate the sensitivity of hadron structure to external magnetic fields and help bridge our understanding to relativistic approaches.

Formalism

In this section, we construct the effective Hamiltonian
and introduce the CGEM to solve the Scrödinger with the cylindrical symmetry constraint.
The LFWF is then introduced and some related quantities are discussed.

II.1
Quark model in a magnetic field

In this study, we investigate quarkonia in the nonrelativistic quark model.
In the presence of an external magnetic field, the two-body Hamiltonian composed of a quark and an antiquark can be expressed as
Machado et al. 2013
;
Alford and Strickland 2013

H_{q\bar{q}}=\sum_{i=q,\bar{q}}\left[m_{i}+\frac{(\bm{p}_{i}-q_{i}\bm{A})^{2}}{2m_{i}}-\bm{\mu}_{i}\cdot\bm{B}\right]+V_{q\bar{q}}(r).

(1)

Here,
m_{i}
and
\bm{p}_{i}
denote the mass and momentum of quark or antiquark labeled by
i=q,\bar{q}
, respectively.
The magnetic field is contained in the Hamiltonian through the vector potential
\bm{A}
, which alters the kinetic-energy term of the single particle.
We adopt the symmetric gauge,
\bm{A}(\bm{r}_{i})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{B}\times\bm{r}_{i}
, and include the coupling to the quark magnetic moment
\bm{\mu}_{i}
.

For the effective quark-antiquark potential
V_{q\bar{q}}
, we adopt the following form
Eichten et al. 1975

\displaystyle V_{q\bar{q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C+\sigma r-\frac{A}{r}+\alpha(\bm{S}_{q}\cdot\bm{S}_{\bar{q}})\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda r^{2}},\quad

(2)

where
r=\sqrt{z^{2}+r_{\perp}^{2}}
,
C
is a constant shift, and the magnitudes of the confining,
color Coulomb, and spin-spin potentials are characterized by the parameters,
\sigma
,
A
, and
\alpha
, respectively.
We smear the spin-spin interaction as a Gaussian function with a parameter
\Lambda

Barnes et al. 2005
, for simplicity.
The term
\Braket{\bm{S}_{q}\cdot\bm{S}_{\bar{q}}}
yields the values of
1/4
and
-3/4

for the vector (spin-triplet) and pseudoscalar (spin-singlet) mesons, respectively.

In a magnetic field, the kinetic momentum
\bm{p}_{i}
is not conserved, while one can introduce the so-called pseudo-momentum
Johnson and Lippmann 1949
.
The two-body pseudo-momentum is defined as
Gor’kov and Dzyaloshinskii 1968
;
Carter 1969
;
Avron et al. 1978

\displaystyle\bm{K}=\sum_{i=q,\bar{q}}\left[\bm{p}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}q_{i}\bm{B}\crossproduct\bm{r}_{i}\right],

(3)

which is a conserved quantity.
By using the pseudo-momentum, the total wave function can be factorized as

\displaystyle\Phi(\bm{R},\bm{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[i\bm{P}\cdot\bm{R}\right]\psi(\bm{r})

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[i(\bm{K}-\frac{q}{2}\bm{B}\crossproduct\bm{r})\cdot\bm{R}\right]\psi(\bm{r}),

(5)

where
\bm{R}
and
\bm{r}
are the center-of-mass and relative coordinate, respectively.

\bm{P}
is the center-of-mass momentum.

Here we focus on the magnetic field
B
along the
z
direction and meson with
\bm{K}=0
.
We also focus on heavy quarkonia with neutral charge,
q_{1}=-q_{2}=q
.
In this case, the two-body Hamiltonian is reduced to the Hamiltonian for the relative motion
Alford and Strickland 2013
;
Andreichikov et al. 2013a
;
Bonati et al. 2015
;
Suzuki and Yoshida 2016
;
Yoshida and Suzuki 2016

\displaystyle H=-\frac{\bm{\nabla}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{q^{2}B^{2}}{8\mu}r_{\perp}^{2}-\sum_{i=q,\bar{q}}\bm{\mu}_{i}\cdot\bm{B}+V(r).\quad

(6)

where
\mu=m_{q}/2
is the reduced quark mass.
The above Hamiltonian maintains the rotational symmetry on the transverse
x
-
y
plane and the reflection symmetry along the
z
-axis.
The second term proportional to
B^{2}r_{\perp}^{2}
leads to the so-called quark Landau levels.

We discuss the contribution of the effects induced by
\bm{\mu}_{i}\cdot\bm{B}

Machado et al. 2013
;
Alford and Strickland 2013
, where
\bm{\mu}_{i}={gq_{i}\bm{S}_{i}}/{2m_{i}}
is the quark magnetic moment with the Lande
g
-factor,
g=2
.
Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle-(\bm{\mu}_{1}+\bm{\mu}_{2})\cdot\bm{B}=-\frac{gq}{4}\left(\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{1}}{m_{1}}-\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{2}}{m_{2}}\right)\cdot\bm{B}

(7)

where we used
\bm{S}_{i}=\bm{\sigma}_{i}/2
.
Then, the eigenstates with the different spin quantum numbers, mix with each other

\displaystyle\bra{10}-(\bm{\mu}_{1}+\bm{\mu}_{2})\cdot\bm{B}\ket{00}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{gqB}{4\mu},

(8)

\displaystyle\bra{00}-(\bm{\mu}_{1}+\bm{\mu}_{2})\cdot\bm{B}\ket{10}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{gqB}{4\mu},

(9)

where the spin eigenstates are labeled by
\ket{SS_z}
with
S=0,1
and
S_{z}=0,\pm 1
.
Thus, only the longitudinal component
\ket{10}
of
S=1
can mix with the
S=0
state whereas the transverse components
\ket{1\pm 1}
do not mix with other components.
Taking into account the off-diagonal interaction, we should solve a coupled-channel Schrödinger equation.
We note that in the case of quarkonia, all the diagonal matrix elements, such as
\bra{00}-(\bm{\mu}_{1}+\bm{\mu}_{2})\cdot\bm{B}\ket{00}
, are vanishing: there is no hadronic Zeeman shift.

II.2
Cylindrical Gaussian Expansion Method

To diagonalize the Hamiltonian (
6
), we use a cylindrical Gaussian expansion method
Suzuki and Yoshida 2016
;
Yoshida and Suzuki 2016
, which is a generalization of the Gaussian expansion method
Kamimura 1988
;
Hiyama et al. 2003
.
In this method, we expand the wave function in terms of a set of Gaussian basis functions,
\phi^{CG}_{n}
, with a different Gaussian range parameter as

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{n_{\mathrm{max}}}c_{n}\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{CG}},

(10)

where
c_{n}
represents the expansion coefficient.
The Gaussian basis function in position space is given by

\displaystyle\phi^{\mathrm{CG}}_{n}(z,\bm{r}_{\perp})=N_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}{r}_{\perp}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{n}z^{2}},

(11)

and the basis function in the momentum space, obtained through the Fourier transformation

\displaystyle\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{CG}}(p_{z},\bm{p}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential r_{\perp}r_{\perp}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential z\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\phi

(12)

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{e}^{-ip_{z}z}\mathrm{e}^{-i\bm{r}_{\perp}\cdot\bm{p}_{\perp}}\phi^{\mathrm{CG}}_{n}(z,\bm{r}_{\perp}),

is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{CG}}(p_{z},\bm{p}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-{p}_{\perp}^{2}/4\beta_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-{p}_{z}^{2}/4\gamma_{n}}.

(13)

The normalization factors of the basis function,
N_{n}
and
\tilde{N}_{n}
, are determined by
\innerproduct{\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{CG}}}{\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{CG}}}=1
.

In the GEM, two sets of parameters must be determined: the expansion coefficients
c_{n}
and the Gaussian range parameters
(\beta_{n},\gamma_{n})
.
The coefficients
c_{n}
are obtained by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem,

\bm{H}_{q\bar{q}}\,\bm{c}=M_{q\bar{q}}\,\bm{S}\,\bm{c},

(14)

where the Hamiltonian matrix elements are given by
H_{q\bar{q},nm}=\bra{\phi_n^\mathrm{CG}}\hat{H}\ket{\phi_m^\mathrm{CG}}
, and the elements of the overlap matrix are
S_{nm}=\Braket{\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{CG}}|\phi_{m}^{\mathrm{CG}}}
.
The Gaussian range parameters
\beta_{n}
and
\gamma_{n}
are determined by adopting a geometric progression
Kamimura 1988
;
Hiyama et al. 2003

\displaystyle\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{\perp n}^{2}},r_{\perp n}=r_{\perp 1}b^{n-1},b=\left(\frac{r_{\perp\mathrm{max}}}{r_{\perp 1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{max}}-1}},

(15)

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z_{n}^{2}},z_{n}=z_{1}c^{n-1},c=\left(\frac{z_{\mathrm{max}}}{z_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{max}}-1}},

(16)

which reduces them to only four parameters (
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{n_{\mathrm{max}}}
) and (
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{n_{\mathrm{max}}}
) to be optimized.
We note that the basis functions are non-orthogonal
S_{nm}\neq\delta_{nm}
and the states are normalized as

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi}=\sum_{n,m}c_{n}^{*}S_{nm}c_{m}=1.

(17)

Since the spin singlet
\ket{00}
and longitudinal component
\ket{10}
of
the spin triplet mix with each other in the magnetic field,
the wave function should take into account the coupled channel.
The basis function is given as a linear combination of spin-0 and spin-1 components

\displaystyle\Psi(r_{\perp},z)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}^{S=0}\Phi_{n}^{S=0}+\sum_{n=N+1}^{2N}c_{n}^{S=1}\Phi_{n}^{S=1}\quad

(18)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}^{S=0}(r_{\perp},z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{n}^{P}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}^{P}r_{\perp}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{n}^{P}z^{2}},

(19)

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}^{S=1}(r_{\perp},z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{n}^{V}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}^{V}r_{\perp}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{n}^{V}z^{2}}.

(20)

In this case, we have four range parameters

\displaystyle(\beta_{n}^{P},\gamma_{n}^{P}),\qquad(\beta_{n}^{V},\gamma_{n}^{V}).

(21)

Because of the geometric progression applied, in total we have eight parameters to optimize.

II.3
Light-front wave functions

After diagonalizing the Hamiltonian (
6
), the resulting wave functions are transformed into LFWFs
Cardarelli et al. 1995
;
Choi et al. 2015
;
Arifi et al. 2022
;
Ridwan et al. 2025
;
Arifi et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2025
.
These LFWFs are expressed in terms of internal variables that are invariant under Lorentz boosts in the longitudinal direction, defined as

\displaystyle x_{i}=\frac{p_{i}^{+}}{P^{+}},\qquad\bm{k}_{\perp i}=\bm{p}_{\perp i}-x_{i}\bm{P}_{\perp},

(22)

where
P^{\mu}=(P^{+},P^{-},\bm{P}_{\perp})
is the meson’s total four-momentum and
p_{i}^{\mu}
denotes the four-momentum of the
i
-th constituent quark. Here, we denote on the quark’s longitudinal momentum fraction
x\equiv x_{q}
and transverse momentum
\bm{k}_{\perp}\equiv\bm{k}_{\perp q}
.

To obtain the LFWFs, we first perform a change of variables from the momentum-space coordinates
(k_{z},\bm{k}_{\perp})
to
(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})
.
The longitudinal momentum component
k_{z}
can be expressed as

k_{z}=\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)M_{0}+\frac{m_{\bar{q}}^{2}-m_{q}^{2}}{2M_{0}},

(23)

where the invariant meson mass
M_{0}
is given by

M_{0}^{2}=\frac{\bm{k}_{\perp}^{2}+m_{q}^{2}}{x}+\frac{\bm{k}_{\perp}^{2}+m_{\bar{q}}^{2}}{1-x}.

(24)

The radial part of the LFWF is defined as

\Phi(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\sqrt{2(2\pi)^{3}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\partial k_{z}}{\partial x}}\,\psi(\bm{k}),

(25)

where
\psi(\bm{k})
is the wave function obtained from the Hamiltonian diagonalization. The Jacobian factor associated with the variable transformation is

\frac{\partial k_{z}}{\partial x}=\frac{M_{0}}{4x(1-x)}\left[1-\frac{(m_{q}^{2}-m_{\bar{q}}^{2})^{2}}{M_{0}^{4}}\right].

(26)

The spin and orbital angular momentum part
\mathcal{R}^{JJ_{z}}_{\lambda_{q}\lambda_{\bar{q}}}
of the meson LFWF is obtained through the interaction-independent Melosh transformation
Melosh 1974
, which relates the instant-form spin states to the light-front helicity basis.
Here
J
and
J_{z}
denote the total spin and its projection along the quantization axis, respectively, while
\lambda_{q}
and
\lambda_{\bar{q}}
are the light-front helicities of the quark and antiquark.
This procedure assumes that the meson is described in terms of a non-interacting quark–antiquark basis,
in line with the Bakamjian–Thomas construction
Bakamjian and Thomas 1953
;
Arifi et al. 2023a
;
Arifi et al. 2023b
;
Arifi et al. 2025
.
This procedure connects the spin-orbital structure of the ordinary rest-frame wave function, which carries definite quantum numbers
J^{PC}
, to its light-front representation in a consistent way
Bakamjian and Thomas 1953
;
Choi et al. 2015
.
The effect of the external magnetic field is first included in the rest-frame wave function, by diagonalizing the Hamiltonian (
6
).
We assume that these modifications are then carried over to the LFWFs via the Melosh transformation.
Since our goal is to understand how the LFWFs are modified in the nonrelativistic quark model, this approach captures the dominant effects of the magnetic field such as spin-mixing and quark Landau levels.

For spin-orbital angular momentum part, it is convenient to work with the covariant form as
Jaus 1991
;
Choi and Ji 1999

\mathcal{R}^{JJ_{z}}_{\lambda_{q}\lambda_{\bar{q}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\tilde{M}_{0}}\bar{u}_{\lambda_{q}}(p_{q})\,\Gamma_{\rm M}\,v_{\lambda_{\bar{q}}}(p_{\bar{q}}),

(27)

where
\tilde{M}_{0}\equiv\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}-(m_{q}-m_{\bar{q}})^{2}}
, and
u(p_{q})
and
v(p_{\bar{q}})
are the light-front Dirac spinors for the quark and antiquark, respectively, with the effect of the Melosh tranformation implicitly included through the spinor structure
Ji et al. 1992
.
The vertex function
\Gamma_{\rm M}
depends on the meson type, with

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm P}=\gamma_{5},\qquad\Gamma_{\rm V}=-\not{\epsilon}(J_{z})+\frac{\epsilon\cdot(p_{q}-p_{\bar{q}})}{M_{0}+m_{q}+m_{\bar{q}}},

(28)

where
\Gamma_{\rm P}
and
\Gamma_{\rm V}
correspond to pseudoscalar and vector mesons, respectively.
The polarization vectors
\epsilon^{\mu}(J_{z})=(\epsilon^{+},\epsilon^{-},\bm{\epsilon}_{\perp})
are defined by

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\mu}(\pm 1)

\displaystyle=\left(0,\frac{2\bm{\epsilon}_{\perp}(\pm)\cdot\bm{P}_{\perp}}{P^{+}},\bm{\epsilon}_{\perp}(\pm)\right),

(29)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\mu}(0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{P^{+}}{M_{0}},\frac{-M_{0}^{2}+\bm{P}_{\perp}^{2}}{M_{0}P^{+}},\frac{\bm{P}_{\perp}}{M_{0}}\right),

(30)

with the transverse polarization vectors
\bm{\epsilon}_{\perp}(\pm 1)=(1,\pm i)/\sqrt{2}
.
Lastly, the spin-orbit wave function
\mathcal{R}^{JJ_{z}}_{\lambda_{q}\lambda{\bar{q}}}
satisfies the normalization condition

\sum_{\lambda_{q},\lambda_{\bar{q}}}\mathcal{R}^{JJ_{z}\dagger}_{\lambda_{q}\lambda_{\bar{q}}}\mathcal{R}^{J^{\prime}J_{z}^{\prime}}_{\lambda_{q}\lambda_{\bar{q}}}=\delta_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J_{z}J_{z}^{\prime}}.

(31)

The LFWF of heavy quarkonia in momentum space can be expressed as

\varPsi^{JJ_{z}}_{\lambda_{q}\lambda_{\bar{q}}}(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\Phi(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})\,\mathcal{R}^{JJ_{z}}_{\lambda_{q}\lambda_{\bar{q}}}(x,\bm{k}_{\perp}),

(32)

where
\Phi(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})
represents the radial component of the wave function, and
\mathcal{R}^{JJ_{z}}_{\lambda_{q}\lambda_{\bar{q}}}(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})
accounts for the spin and orbital angular momentum structure.
The LFWF density is defined by summing over the quark and antiquark helicities,
Wen et al. 2025

\rho(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\lambda_{q},\lambda_{\bar{q}}}\left|\varPsi^{JJ_{z}}_{\lambda_{q}\lambda_{\bar{q}}}(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})\right|^{2}.

(33)

The wave function normalization condition is given by

\int\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{k}_{\perp}\,\rho(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})=1.

(34)

We can also project the LFWF density to its longitudinal and transverse components, defined as

\displaystyle\int\differential xf(x)=\int\differential k_{\perp}f(k_{\perp})=1.

(35)

It is worth noting that, in the vacuum, the longitudinal distribution
f(x)
corresponds to the PDFs and the
\rho(x,\bm{k}_{\perp})
corresponds to the transverse momentum dependent distribution (TMD)
Lorcé et al. 2015
;
Choi and Ji 2024
.

As we will see later that the LFWFs become deformed under strong magnetic fields, it is useful to introduce the anisotropy parameter defined by

\epsilon_{\mathrm{LF}}=\frac{\langle k_{\perp}^{2}\rangle}{2\langle k_{z}^{2}\rangle}-1,

(36)

where
\langle k_{\perp}^{2}\rangle=\langle k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}\rangle
. The factor of 2 in the denominator ensures a consistent comparison between the two-dimensional transverse and one-dimensional longitudinal momentum components.
The longitudinal momentum operator
k_{z}
is defined in Eq. (
23
), and the expectation values
\langle k_{\perp}^{2}\rangle
and
\langle k_{z}^{2}\rangle
are determined by the Gaussian range parameters
\gamma
and
\beta
of the wave function.
For a spherical Gaussian basis, the relation
\langle k_{\perp}^{2}\rangle=2\langle k_{z}^{2}\rangle
holds exactly, resulting in
\epsilon_{\mathrm{LF}}=0
and reflecting spherical symmetry. However, this relation no longer holds when using a cylindrical Gaussian basis, indicating anisotropic deformation of the LFWFs.

II.4
Numerical Setup

For the parameters of the constituent quark model, in this work, we adopt those used in Ref.
Yoshida and Suzuki 2016
, where some parameters were determined from lattice QCD simulations of charmonia
Kawanai and Sasaki 2012
;
Kawanai and Sasaki 2015
, as listed in Table
1
.
These parameters well reproduce the experimental masses
Navas et al. 2024
of heavy quarkonia in vacuum, as shown in Table
2

III
Results and Discussion

In this section, we investigate how the structures of charmonia and bottomonia are modified in the presence of strong magnetic fields.
For each quarkonium, we first present the magnetic-field-induced mass shifts, recalculated using a denser set of data points than in previous studies
Yoshida and Suzuki 2016
.
In this work, we specifically focus on examining the corresponding LFWF densities.
We also discuss how the transverse and longitudinal momentum distributions change and quantify the deformation using momentum expectation values and an anisotropy parameter.
Finally, we discuss a possible relativistic correction, which plays an important role in modifying the longitudinal structure of the LFWFs, found using the light-front Hamiltonian approach
Wen et al. 2025
.

III.2
Bottomonia

Following our detailed investigation of charmonium states, we now turn to the bottomonium sector.
The bottom quark have a heavier mass and a smaller electric charge than the case of the charm quark. These features render bottomonia more nonrelativistic and less sensitive to external magnetic fields compared to charmonia. Nonetheless, they provide an important benchmark to test the universality of magnetic-field effects across heavy quarkonia.

III.3
Relativistic correction

So far, we have demonstrated the deformation of the LFWFs under magnetic field using the nonrelativistic Hamiltonian model as input.
One of the dominant effects observed is transverse momentum broadening.
However, within this setup, the longitudinal distribution remains largely unmodified for the ground states as seen in Figs.
6
and
11
.

The recent result based on the light-front Hamiltonian approach
Wen et al. 2025
has shown that the PDFs can also be significantly altered.
However, in the nonrelativistic Hamiltonian (
6
), since the magnetic-field-dependent potential (i.e., the Landau level term) is proportional to
r_{\perp}^{2}
, this term leads to the transverse momentum broadening but does not directly contribute to the longitudinal momentum distribution.
In this sense, while our numerical results are reasonable, it might be important to discuss possible scenarios of the PDF modifications within our model.

In this work, we phenomenologically introduce a
k_{z}
-dependent Landau level term as a relativistic correction (i.e., a higher-order term in the
1/m
expansion):

V^{\pm}_{L}=\frac{q^{2}B^{2}}{8\mu}r_{\perp}^{2}\left(1\pm\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}\right).

(37)

This term is not exactly derived from the two-body Hamiltonian, but a possible origin from a one-body Hamiltonian is explained in Appendix
A
.
Although the sign of this correction is suggested to be negative in Appendix
A
, we treat the sign as a free parameter to explore its impact on the PDFs.
We then diagonalize the Hamiltonian with the correction term (
37
) and focus on only the
J/\psi_{T}
as a simple example where the pseudoscalar-vector meson mixing is absent.
We note that the correction may be valid only for values of
\sqrt{qB}
smaller than the quark mass because of the
\sqrt{qB}/m
expansion.

IV
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we have investigated the structural modifications of heavy quarkonia, charmonia and bottomonia, under strong magnetic fields in the constituent quark model.
By solving the two-body Schrödinger equation with the cylindrical Gaussian expansion method
Suzuki and Yoshida 2016
;
Yoshida and Suzuki 2016
,
we obtained precise wave functions that respect the cylindrical symmetry imposed by the magnetic field.
Here, these wave functions were then transformed into LFWFs to reveal their momentum-space structure.

Our results show that the presence of a strong magnetic field leads to a clear transverse momentum broadening of the LFWFs, driven by the magnetic-field–induced potential in the nonrelativistic Hamiltonian
Suzuki and Yoshida 2016
;
Yoshida and Suzuki 2016
. In contrast, the longitudinal momentum fraction distribution for the ground states remains largely unchanged within this framework. For excited states, however, we find characteristic shape transitions and nodal restructuring near avoided crossings, which result in significant modifications to the longitudinal momentum distributions.
Notably, these avoided crossings play a key role in reorganizing the level structure and internal dynamics of quarkonia under external magnetic fields.
To explore the effect beyond this framework, we have introduced a phenomenological relativistic correction to the Landau level term that significantly modifies the longitudinal structure.
Furthermore, we also expect that bottomonia behave more nonrelativistically and are less sensitive to external magnetic fields compared to charmonia.
It is worth noting that the anisotropy parameter may be useful in discussing the structural modifications.
This can be compared with relativistic approaches to further explore the role of relativistic effects.

These findings provide a qualitative understanding of how quarkonia structure is modified by magnetic fields within a nonrelativistic Hamiltonian framework
Suzuki and Yoshida 2016
;
Yoshida and Suzuki 2016
and help bridge this understanding with results obtained from the light-front Hamiltonian approach
Wen et al. 2025
. The insights gained here can guide future lattice QCD studies aimed at quantifying magnetic-field effects on hadron structure.
Further extensions of this work could include anisotropy of the confining or Coulomb potential observed in lattice QCD simulations
Bonati et al. 2014
;
Bonati et al. 2016
;
D’Elia et al. 2021
and explicit time-dependent magnetic fields
Guo et al. 2015
;
Suzuki and Lee 2017
;
Dutta and Mazumder 2018
;
Hoelck and Wolschin 2017
;
Bagchi et al. 2023
;
Iwasaki et al. 2021b
to better approximate the conditions of heavy-ion collisions.
Additional effects and phenomena related to quarkonia in strong magnetic fields also remain to be explored for future studies.

Appendix A
Relativistic corrections

In the main text, we have investigated the impact of a relativistic correction (
37
).
In this Appendix, we explain the origin of this term.

From the Dirac equation in magnetic fields, we can derive the single-particle energy
Rabi 1928
;
Johnson and Lippmann 1949
[also see, e.g., Eq. (83) of Ref.
Hattori et al. 2023
]

\epsilon_{n}=\pm\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+2n|qB|+m^{2}},

(38)

where the transverse momentum
p_{\perp}
is quantized, and
n=0,1,2,\cdots
is the index of Landau levels.
Now, we consider a nonrelativistic expansion of the single-particle energy as
2
2

2

This expansion holds in the condition of
\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+2n|qB|}\ll m
.
If
n|qB|
is large enough, this expansion cannot be used, but it is valid as long as we focus on the lower Landau levels and weak magnetic fields.

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=m\sqrt{1+\frac{p_{z}^{2}+2n|qB|}{m^{2}}}

(39)

\displaystyle=m+\frac{p_{z}^{2}+2n|qB|}{2m}-\frac{(p_{z}^{2}+2n|qB|)^{2}}{8m^{3}}+\dots

(40)

In the last form, the first and second terms are consistent with the mass, the kinetic energy, and the nonrelativistic Landau levels with the cyclotron frequency
|qB|/m
, which are used in the usual nonrelativistic framework.
On the other hand, the third term is the higher-order corrections proportional to
1/m^{3}
, which
includes the cross term between
p_{z}^{2}
and
2n|qB|
.
Thus, the momentum
p_{z}
and the Landau level effect can be correlated as a higher-order relativistic correction.
The energy shift from this term is

\displaystyle\Delta\epsilon_{n}=-\frac{4n|qB|p_{z}^{2}}{8m^{3}}=-\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{2m^{2}}\times\frac{n|qB|}{m}.

(41)

The factor of
-p_{z}^{2}/2m^{2}
may be regarded as a correction to the original energy shift of Landau levels.

However, the rigorous derivation of the two-body potential from such a single-particle energy is technically difficult.
Because of that, the study toward such a direction is limited so far.
Under such a situation, to reproduce the narrowing of PDF (found in Ref.
Wen et al. 2025
), we have phenomenologically introduced the relativistic correction (
37
).
Note that we can also consider the corrections proportional to
p_{z}^{4}/m^{3}
or
|qB|^{2}/m^{3}
, but they are not relevant for our purpose.

Appendix B
Potential matrix elements

While the potential matrix elements have been shown in Ref.
Yoshida and Suzuki 2016
,
here we present the analytical expressions for the matrix elements relevant to the Landau-level potential and its relativistic correction.
Using the cylindrical Gaussian basis
\phi_{n}^{CG}
, the matrix element, relevant for the Landau term, is given by

\displaystyle\Braket{r_{\perp}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{\phi_n^{CG}}{r_\perp^2}{\phi_m^{CG}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{n}N_{m}\pi^{3/2}}{\beta_{nm}^{2}\sqrt{\gamma_{nm}}},

(42)

where
\beta_{nm}=\beta_{n}+\beta_{m},\gamma_{nm}=\gamma_{n}+\gamma_{m}
, and
the normalization factor

\displaystyle N_{n}=\frac{2^{3/4}\gamma_{n}^{1/4}\sqrt{\beta_{n}}}{\pi^{3/4}}.

(43)

The correction term (
37
), proportional to
r_{\perp}^{2}k_{z}^{2}
, can be computed as

\displaystyle\Braket{r_{\perp}^{2}k_{z}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\matrixelement{\phi_n^{CG}}{r_\perp^2 \frac{\partial^2}{\partial z^2}}{\phi_m^{CG}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma_{m}\gamma_{n}}{\gamma_{nm}}\left[\frac{N_{n}N_{m}\pi^{3/2}}{\beta_{nm}^{2}\sqrt{\gamma_{nm}}}\right],

(44)

where
k_{z}^{2}
corresponds to
-\partial^{2}/\partial z^{2}
in coordinate space.
