A specific basal forebrain–medial prefrontal cholinergic pathway improves attentional control in male mice
Authors: Giulia R Fois, Maria-Carmen Medrano, Rémi Proville, Stephanie Caille, Karine Guillem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcetylcholine (ACh) release in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) is a major contributor to the balance between attention and inhibitory control, which is crucial for the execution of adaptive goal-directed behaviors. Yet, our understanding of the functional heterogeneity within the mPFC, particularly how distinct cholinergic afferences and circuits regulate these processes, remains limited. Here, we used in vivo fiber photometry, neuronal tracing, and chemogenetics manipulations to demonstrate the role of the prelimbic sub-region of the mPFC (PrL) and its ascending cholinergic projections in a cued-Fixed Consecutive Number task (FCNcue task) in male mice. We found that following a transient activation at the initiation of the behavioral response (cue detection), persistent inhibition of PrL neuronal activity, measured by fiber photometry, may be necessary to maintain engagement in the task and completion of the chain of required responses (i.e., optimal responses). Moreover, we found that the PrL receives dense ACh projections almost exclusively from the most anterior-medial part of the basal forebrain (BF) comprising the horizontal and ventral parts of the diagonal band of Broca (HDB and VDB) and the substantia innominata (SI) nuclei. Finally, chemogenetic activation of this ACh pathway inhibited PrL activity and enhanced behavioral performance of the mice by increasing the percentage of optimal responses. Overall, this study provides insights into the spatial and temporal dynamics of cholinergic signaling to the PrL and its causal role on attentional control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51042-8.Keywords: Prefrontal cortex, Basal forebrain, Acetylcholine, AttentionSubject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

The ability to detect a cue, to maintain focus (i.e., attention) and to suppress behavior until conditions are appropriate (i.e., inhibitory control) is fundamental for adaptive behavior across species. These cognitive factors determine the success of goal-directed behavior in both humans and animals1,2. Deficits in attention and inhibitory control are hallmark symptoms in a wide range of psychiatric disorders and neurodegenerative diseases ranging from schizophrenia, compulsivity, and addiction disorders as well as cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s disease3.

The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) is a central hub for attentional processes such as sustained attention, conflict/inhibition control in both humans and animals4–7, and neuronal activity in the mPFC has been critically involved in various attentional tasks8–11. In particular, the prelimbic sub-region of the mPFC (PrL) has been particularly implicated in sustaining attentional states, controlling response accuracy to sensory cues, and maintaining attention over consecutive trials and inhibitory control12–15. The neuromodulation of attentional control has long been associated with acetylcholine (ACh) release in the mPFC, including the PrL. Indeed, while the cortical ACh activity has been associated with arousal, attention, learning and memory16, disruption of cortical ACh transmission has been implicated in attentional deficits associated with the aforementioned disorders while sparing other cognitive functions17–20. The main ACh innervation of the mPFC originates in the basal forebrain (BF), a brain area composed of several cholinergic nuclei21–23. Classically, these BF-mPFC cholinergic projections have been viewed as diffuse and thought to produce global and uniform effects in the mPFC24,25. However, growing anatomical evidence suggests that BF cortically projecting neurons are rather complex with an heterogeneous spatial and segregated distribution23,26–30. Consequently, it raises the critical question of how specific ACh projections from the BF to the mPFC engage targeted regulation of attentional processes.

Therefore, in the current work, we used a combination of in vivo fiber photometry, neuroanatomical tracing and pathway-specific chemogenetics manipulations to study the contribution of the PrL and its ascending cholinergic projections in a cued-Fixed Consecutive Number task (FCNcue task) in mice, a task that measures both attention and inhibitory control and that is sensitive to the pro-cognitive effects of nicotine31. Our photometry results demonstrate that persistent inhibition of PrL neuronal activity is essential to maintain engagement in the task and good attentional performance in mice. It is likely to involve a specific anterior BF ACh innervation of the PrL since we demonstrate a causal link whereby chemogenetic activation of this pathway inhibits PrL activity and sharpens attentional performance.

Results

PrL neuronal activity decreases during goal-directed attention

We first assessed the role of the PrL in attention and inhibitory control, by measuring changes in neuronal activity using in vivo fiber photometry in mice performing an attentional FCNcue task31. Briefly, mice were first trained in the FCNcue task to carry out a chain of sequential actions into 2 different nose-poke holes (NP) (i.e., 6 consecutive nose-pokes on to the FCN-NP followed by 1 nose-poke on the RWD-NP) to receive a reward (see Materials and Methods for details; Fig. 1a and Supplemental Fig. 1). Mice were next injected with a GCaMP6m-expressing virus targeting mainly the pyramidal neurons (AAV9-CamKII-GCaMP6m) into right PrL and implanted with an optical fiber above the injection site (n = 5 mice; see Materials and Methods for details; Fig. 1b). Then they were re-trained in the FCNcue task. Mice had approximately 35 training sessions in total before being recorded using in vivo fiber photometry. In the recording sessions, performance of the mice showed an average optimal response rate (percentage of completed chains of 6 visits in the FCN-NP that results in a reward delivery) of 52.09 ± 7.03% and a premature response rate (percentage of chains of 1 visit in the FCN-NP immediately followed by a visit in the RWD-NP) of 11.21 ± 2.17% (Fig. 1c). The total trial durations are 45.96 ± 1.19 s and 14.97 ± 0.91 s for optimal and premature trials respectively. The average interval between successive FCN-NP during a successful chain was 7.66 ± 0.27 s. Chain breaks at intermediate chain lengths (e.g., FCN 2–5) are negligible in these well-trained mice, occurring in less than 10% of the trials (Supplemental Fig. 2). We then quantified fluorescence in the PrL for optimal and premature trials during different behavioral events (see Materials and Methods for details). The 1st nose-poke on the FCN-NP induced a rapid and robust increase in calcium fluorescence (ΔF/F) followed by a slower decrease in both optimal and premature trials (peak latency: 0.66 ± 0.12 s and 0.75 ± 0.11 s for optimal and premature trials, respectively; Fig. 1d). Moreover, there was no difference in the amplitude of the changes observed between optimal and premature trials, indicating similar neuronal response between the two trials at the initiation of the behavioral response on the FCN-NP (peak amplitude: 0.83 ± 0.14 and 0.86 ± 0.13, for optimal and premature trials respectively; Wilcoxon rank-sum = 67, NS and AUC: − 0.13 ± 1.06 and − 0.13 ± 1.16, for optimal and premature trials respectively; Wilcoxon rank-sum = 66, NS). In contrast, the two calcium signal responses strongly differed when mice responded on the RWD-NP. Though PrL neuronal activity was still inhibited in optimal trials, it increased above baseline and peaked around the time of the nose-poke in the RWD-NP in premature trials (0.66 ± 0.11 s; Fig. 1e). Such an increase in PrL neuronal activity was also observed in optimal trials but appeared significantly latter, 1.06 ± 0.13 s after the nose-poke (Wilcoxon rank-sum = 9; p < 0.01), a time corresponding to when mice moved towards the licker to receive their reward (reward latency: 1.43 ± 0.13 s). PrL neuronal activity during optimal trials was also strongly inhibited thereafter during reward consumption (from 1.06 to 3 s after the RWD-NP, Fig. 1e) while it remained elevated in premature trials that lead to no reward (AUC: − 5.74 ± 1.91 and 2.39 ± 1.11, for optimal and premature trials respectively; Wilcoxon rank-sum = 16, p < 0.01). Then, PrL neuronal activity during optimal trials returned to baseline level (from 17.5 to 20 s after the RWD-NP, Fig. 1e), a trial transition period where no task-relevant stimuli were present and no operant responses were required. Finally, the activity of PrL pyramidal neurons gradually decreased across the 6 successive FCN-NP responses necessary to the completion of an optimal trial (Fig. 1f). Moreover, the duration of the optimal FCN-NP responses (i.e., referred as to the elapsed time between the 1th and the last 6th FCN-NP nose-poke) was negatively correlated with the amplitude of the PrL inhibition (Pearson correlation: r = − 0.55; p < 0.05) (Supplemental Fig. 3), and slower chains of responses were associated with stronger PrL inhibition. Together, these results suggest that, following a transient activation, sustained inhibition of PrL neuronal activity may be a necessary condition for the successful execution of the task sequence and the prevention of premature response.

A cholinergic pathway from the basal forebrain to the PrL drives attentional performance

As cholinergic transmission has been implicated in the regulation of attentional control, we first sought to identify whether specific ACh nuclei targeted the PrL. We injected the cholera toxin subunit B (CTb) retrograde tracer in the PrL and mapped the afferent projections from the BF nuclei (Fig. 2a). Retrogradely labeled neurons projecting to this part of the mPFC were mostly found in ACh neurons originating from the more anterior-medial part of the BF comprising the horizontal diagonal band of Broca (HDB, 56%), the vertical DB (VDB, 34%) and the substantia innominata (SI, 6%) subnuclei (refereed as to SI/DB region thereafter), while almost no labelling was observed in the more posterior and lateral part of the BF such as in the lateral limb of the DB (LDB, 4%, * p < 0.05), or the nucleus basalis of Meynert (B, 0%, *** p < 0.001) (Fig. 2b-c).

Then, we assessed whether modifications of the neuronal activity of this SI/DB region during the FCNcue task could influence the cognitive performances of mice. We performed CNO-induced chemogenetic manipulations in sham mice (n = 11) and ChAT-Cre mice injected with either and an excitatory (AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry, eDREADD, n = 13) or an inhibitory DREADD (AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry, iDREADDs, n = 11) in the SI/DB region (Fig. 3a). CNO was injected intraperitoneally 30 min before testing. Epifluorescent analysis showed that mCherry colocalized with a neuronal marker of ACh (hM3D-mCherry+/ ChAT+: 77.2 ± 4.4% and hM4D-mCherry+/ ChAT+: 72.9 ± 3.1%), indicating efficient transfection of chemogenetic viruses in ACh neurons. A two-way ANOVA with repeated measures revealed a significant interaction between group and treatment for optimal response (F(2,32) = 4.30, p = 0.02; Fig. 3b). In eDREADD mice, CNO significantly increased the percentage of optimal responses (Saline: 31.51 ± 4.62 vs CNO: 44.07 ± 7.09, p = 0.01), while it had no effect in sham animals (Saline 27.99 ± 4.77 vs CNO: 26.41 ± 5.62, p = 0.98) or iDREADD mice (Saline 38.82 ± 6.33 vs CNO: 36.24 ± 9.59, p = 0.91). In contrast, CNO administration had no effect on premature responses (group X treatment interaction: F(2,32) = 1.53, p = 0.23; NS; Fig. 3c) across all groups (SHAM saline: 36.19 ± 6.47 vs CNO: 40.19 ± 7.13 ; eDREADDS Saline: 35.55 ± 5.00 vs CNO: 30.80 ± 5.96 ; iDREADDS Saline: 27.42 ± 5.29 vs CNO: 30.78 ± 6.47). This CNO-induced increase in attentional performance in eDREADD mice was remarkably specific since it was not associated with other behavioral changes, including response rate (F(2,32) = 0.68, p = 0.51), the total number of chains (F(2,32) = 0.32, p = 0.73), latency to retrieve the reward (F(2, 32) = 1.50, p = 0.24), or locomotor activity (F(2,32) = 0.65, p = 0.53) (Supplemental Table 11. Efficiency of the CNO treatment to activate ACh neurons was further demonstrated as CNO-treated mice had significantly more cFos+ ACh neurons that co-localized with eDREADD-mCherry compared to saline-treated sham mice (F(2,37) = 217.5; p < 0.001; Fig. 3d). However, and possibly due to a floor effect, the CNO treatment did not decrease in the percentage of cFos+ ACh neurons in the iDREADD group. Importantly, we also found that activation of the SI/DB cholinergic pathway after CNO strongly decreased PrL neuronal activity (F(2,24) = 10.47 ; p < 0.001; Fig. 3e), while inhibition of this pathway did not change cFos expression in the PrL compared to sham.

Finally, we employed a double viral approach to selectively activate SI/DB ACh inputs projecting to the PrL. Specifically, we injected bilaterally a retro-AAV virus expressing Cre recombinase (AAVrg-Cre) in the PrL and an AAV8-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry in the SI/DB region in WT mice (WT-DREADDs; n = 11; Fig. 4a). A sham control group of WT mice (Sham; n = 10) had the same surgery but no viruses were injected. Infusions of AAVrg-Cre in the PrL led to expression of CRE in the SI/DB, as hM3d(Gq)-mCherry expression was restricted to this area (Fig. 4b), indicating that AAVrg-Cre specifically infected SI/DB to PrL projection neurons and expressed CRE, inducing hM3d(Gq)-mCherry expression in the SI/DB only. As in the previous experiment, CNO administration (30 min before testing, i.p.) significantly increased the percentage of optimal responses selectively in WT-DREADDs mice (group X treatment interaction: F(1, 19) = 4.30, p = 0.05) improving from 35.93 6.53 after saline injection to 50.71 8.40 after CNO injection (saline vs CNO WT-DREADDS p = 0.0461; Fig. 4c). There were no significant changes in sham WT-mice optimal response (saline: 46.49 8.31 vs CNO 43.26 6.04). CNO injection seemed to influence premature responses in opposite direction in sham and WT-DREADDS mice (group X treatment interaction: F(1,19) = 7.09, p = 0.01; Fig. 4d), however, there was no significant alteration in premature response in sham (Saline 20.17 5.12 vs CNO 29.66 ± 6.36, Sidak NS) and in WT-DREADDs mice (Saline 30.15 ± 6.80 vs CNO 22.53 ± 5.09; Sidak NS). CNO treatment decreased locomotor activity selectively in WT-DREADDs mice (group X treatment interaction: F(1,19) = 4.47, p = 0.05; Saline vs CNO p = 0.01; Supplemental Table 2), but had no effect on other behavioral parameters (response rate: F(1,19) = 0.28, p = 0.60; total number of chains: F(1,19) = 0.01, p = 0.95; reward latency : F(1,19) = 0.03, p = 0.87; Supplemental Table 2). Finally, in order to pharmacologically confirm the implication of the cholinergic neurotransmission in the behavioral performances, we tested the effect of nicotine peripheral administration (0.15 mg/kg, see Supplemental Fig. 4). It increased optimal response (saline: 46.55 ± 5.05; nicotine 56.51 ± 4.62; t-test p = 0.03) while decreasing premature actions (saline: 20.63 ± 3.04; nicotine 14.62 ± 2.32; t-test p = 0.01).

Discussion

Accumulating anatomical evidence revealed a complex topographic organization of BF cholinergic neurons consisting of segregated and overlapping pools of projection neurons following a rostro-caudal axe26–30,32,33. Specifically, rostral cholinergic neurons in the BF located mainly in the horizontal and ventral parts of the diagonal band of Broca (HDB/VDB) tend to innervate the rostral and ventral regions of the mPFC, while caudal part of the BF in the substantia innominata (SI) and the nucleus basalis of Meynert (B) tend to target the dorsal and caudal regions of the mPFC. This rostro-caudal mapping distribution of BF neurons was gradual, showing a shift of highest density of innervation from ventral to dorsal associated with anteroposterior positioning in the mPFC23,28. Consistent with this, we found that the PrL-projecting cholinergic neurons were located in the HDB and VDB subnuclei. However, in our case, the location of the BF neurons was restricted to the antero-medial part of the BF as we did not observe any retro-labelling in the lateral limb of the DB or the more caudal nucleus basalis B. It has been suggested that the extent of overlap of BF-projecting neurons between two cortical regions depends on the degree of connectivity between these regions26. Whether these PrL-projecting cholinergic neurons also project to other cortical areas remains to be investigated. Our result add to growing evidence of an anatomical segregated organization of the BF-mPFC cholinergic projections, and further suggest that specific BF nuclei may transmit information to distinct medial prefrontal areas and thus mediate distinct cognitive-behavioral processes.

We also report that PrL neuronal activity is altered during distinct period of the attentional FCNcue task. First, PrL neuronal activity increased rapidly around the 1st nose-poke on the FCN-NP at the initiation of the behavioral response. While we cannot definitively rule out a component of motor preparation, the fact that the signal continues to rise after the animal has initiated the nose-poke suggests that the PrL activity primarily reflects cue-detection and task-related processing rather than motor preparation or locomotion. Moreover, this increase was observed whether mice correctly completed the chain of responses (e.g., optimal trials) or ended it up prematurely (e.g., premature trials), indicating that this neuronal response was not predictive of the behavioral output but rather represents a general cue-induced action initiation-related activity. Thereafter, PrL neuronal activity persistently decreased when mice correctly completed the chain of responses, whereas a failure to maintain this inhibitory state is strongly associated with the cessation of the FCN-NP chain and premature response, suggesting that sustained PrL suppression may be necessary for successful completion of the required sequence of FCN-NP responses. Interestingly, PrL neuronal activity gradually decreased during completion the chain of responses. Such a ramping activity has been previously recorded in the PFC across tasks and species34–37, and has been suggested to critically regulate the temporal control of action, an effect that may ease the mice to complete an accurate behavioral chain of responses.

Importantly, the fast increase in PrL neuronal activity was observed for the 1st NP but not during completion of the rest of the chain of responses, a period during which PrL activity was instead durably inhibited. This finding suggests that changes in PrL neuronal activity mediate shifts from a state of perceptual attention to cue-evoked activation and generation of a behavioral response. Such a shift from monitoring to cued response has been previously observed with ACh transients in the PFC38. Whether these biphasic changes in PrL neuronal activity (i.e., initial fast increase and the slow progressive decrease) are due to changes in ACh levels remain to be investigated. However, these changes in PrL neuronal activity were opposite in direction, suggesting that different mechanisms and/or distinct sets of neuronal ensembles might be implicated. It is important to note that, despite the non-specificity of CaMKII promotor to pyramidal cells, the recorded photometry signal would overwhelmingly reflect their activity as they constitute the vast majority of the neurons in the PrL. Moreover, because cholinergic BF neurons projecting to the mPFC co-released others neurotransmitters, such as GABA and glutamate39–42, we cannot exclude that the alteration in PrL neuronal activity observed in our fiber photometry experiment might be mediated by a complex interplay of ACh and non-ACh inputs to the PrL.

The FCNcue task is a complex cognitive task which includes both constructs of inhibition and working memory as well43,44. Here, the animal has to maintain items actively in memory while it has to deal with other stimuli, such as reward-related nose-poke (i.e., RWD-NP) and licker, which interfere with the goal-directed chain of actions essential to an optimal response. Interestingly, we found that the PrL activity decreased when the animal performs a chain of actions that will be successful. Such a deactivation of mPFC neuronal activity has been previously observed in working memory tasks and supposed to prevent reorienting to distracting events45. Thus, the decrease in PrL activity when mice complete the chain of responses could be related to the tradeoff between behavioral inhibition and working memory which is necessary to sustain attention and optimal response.

Good attentional performance also causally depends on cholinergic neuromodulation and controlled release of BF-ACh in the mPFC46–50. Two distinct modes of ACh signaling in the mPFC have been reported, a fast or phasic signaling and a relatively slower or tonic signaling27,51. Importantly, evidence suggest that these two components of ACh activity mediate separate yet interacting aspects of cue detection and attentional control, with the phasic component supporting the activation of cue-associated task, while the tonic one stabilizes task-set and contributes to the general readiness for cortical input processing, therefore potentially also influencing the efficacy of the detection process48,52. Consistent with this, global or pathway-specific chemogenetic activation of SI/DB-ACh neurons that project to the PrL improved behavioral performances, possibly by increasing the control of the cue over responding. While the different temporal resolutions of the methods used (e.g., chemogenetics and cFos measurements versus fiber photometry recordings) preclude a direct one-to-one comparison, together they suggest that the ACh inputs to the PrL could represent a neuromodulatory tonic state that allows for the precise, phasic activity required for successful sequence execution. However, chemogenetic inhibition of BF-ACh neurons did not impair FCN performances. This lack of effect could result from an insufficient efficacy of iDREADDs to inhibit of ACh activity, as suggested by the lack of changes in cFos expression in downstream regions. Alternatively, and consistent with other works, this negative result may also suggest that inhibition of BF cholinergic neurons alone is not sufficient and that BF inhibition should actually encompass both cholinergic and non-cholinergic BF neurons to be deleterious for attentional control53–55.

ACh modulation of the mPFC is highly relevant for the modulation of cognitive functions13,50,56. Here we confirm that peripheral administration of nicotine, an ACh nicotinic receptor agonist, enhances cognitive performances31,57,58, which has been shown to depend mainly on mPFC activity modulation27,57,59. Despite the absence of local pharmacological approaches in our study, which hinders further demonstration of the specific contribution of ACh transmission in the control of PrL activity and behavior during our cognitive task, there is evidence in the literature to support this hypothesis (for review, see60). Interestingly, recent studies have indicated associations between changes in ACh levels and alterations of mPFC activity, as well as behavioral alterations in associative learning tasks33,61. Although the double viral strategy improves the anatomical specificity of ACh input targeting compared to non-conditional chemogenetic manipulation, it does not directly demonstrate that the observed behavioral enhancement depends exclusively on ACh-induced PrL inhibition. Future experiments including local pharmacological interventions, selective PrL inactivation, and in vivo ACh recordings will undoubtedly yield valuable insights regarding the specific contribution of cholinergic transmission to PrL activity and behavioral performance in the FCNcue task.

The effects of ACh are not homogeneous across the mPFC but layer-specific16,62,63, an effect consistent with the differential expression of ACh receptors across cortical layers59. However, the major influence of the cholinergic input to the cortex is supposed to be predominantly inhibitory through the innervation of GABAergic interneurons42,64. Consistent with this, we found that chemogenetic activation of the SI/DB-ACh neurons inhibited cFos neural activity in the PrL. Though the mechanism remains to be determined, this ACh stimulation-induced inhibition of PrL neuronal activity could be achieved through contacts upon specific interneurons expressing distinct nicotinic42,65,66, and/or muscarinic receptors67–69. Additional research is thus needed to better characterize how ACh modulates the local PrL and attentional behavior, and to selectively target these micro-circuits in future work.

Overall, our study highlights the role of a BF-PrL ACh pathway in attentional performances and, more importantly, provides new evidence favoring the hypothesis that target-dependent cholinergic inputs control distinct cognitive and attentional processes.

Material and methods

Animals

A total of 41 adult male ChAT–cre (ChAT-IRES-Cre Knock-In mice, The Jackson Laboratory, stock number: 006410) and 37 adult male wild type (WT, C57BL/6 J Charles River, France) mice were used (25–30 g at the beginning of experiments, University of Bordeaux, France). Mice were housed in groups of 10 maximum and were maintained in a light- (reverse light–dark cycle), humidity- (60 ± 20%) and temperature-controlled vivarium (21 ± 2 °C), with water and food available ad libitum. All behavioral testing occurred during the dark phase of the light–dark cycle. Experimental transgenic animals were heterozygous and backcrossed on a C57BL/6 J background70.

Ethics statement

All experiments were carried out in accordance with the local ethics committee of the University of Bordeaux and the European Committee Council Directive. All experiments were approved by local ethics committee of the University of Bordeaux and the European Committee Council Directive (approval number 2016012716035720). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used.

Stereotaxic surgeries

Stereotaxic surgeries for viral injections and tracer were performed under 1.0–1.5% isoflurane anesthesia (in 50% air/50% O2; 1 L/min). After animal placement in the stereotaxic frame (Kopf), a scalp incision was made, flat-skull position was verified and then a hole was drilled in the skull overlaying the PFC and/or SI/DB regions, and the dura was reflected. The surgery was completed by suturing the skin over the craniotomy, and rats were kept under post-operative monitoring for 5 days. Viruses were subdivided into aliquots and stored at − 80 °C until use.

Virus injection and fiber implantation for photometry

An adeno-associated virus of serotype 9 (AAV9) carrying the GCaMP6m gene, under the control of the CaMKIIɑ promoter (AAV9-CamKII-GCaMP6m; 1.5 × 1013; SignaGen Laboratories) to target mainly glutamatergic neurons was delivered unilaterally into the right prelimbic part of the mPFC (PrL: AP + 2.1 mm/bregma, ML + 0.9 mm/bregma, DV − 2.1 mm from brain surface with an angle of 10°)71 in WT mice (n = 5). A total of 0.75 μl/hemisphere was injected at a rate of 0.1 μl/min using a Hamilton syringe mounted in an infusion pump after which the injector will be left in place for an additional 10 min to allow virus diffusion. A monofiber optic cannula (1.25 mm diameter, 400 µm core, 0.5 NA; RWD Life Science) was implanted in the mPFC slightly above the injection site (~ 250 µm) above the injection site and secured to the skull with screws and opaque dental cement (SuperBond C&B + graphite powder). Four weeks were allowed between virus injection and photometry recordings.

Retrograde tracer injections

The retrograde tracer, 60 nL of 0.5% cholera toxin subunit B (CTb ; Cholera toxin B subunit > 95% (SDS-PAGE), lyophilized powder, Sigma Aldrich C9903), was microinfused by pressure into the right PrL. Glass micropipettes (20–30 µm tip diameter) were back-filled with CTb and lowered stereotaxically into the PrL (AP + 2.1 mm/bregma, ML + 0.4 mm/midline, DV − 2.0 mm from brain surface)71. WT mice (n = 5) were kept for 10 days before being transcardially perfused with saline solution 0.9% and then fixed by paraformaldehyde 4% (PFA 4%).

Virus injection for chemogenetic manipulations

The AAV8-hSyn-DIO-hM4D (Gi)-mCherry virus (6,7 × 1012; UNC Vector Core, NC, USA) or AAV8-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry virus (2,1 × 1013; UNC Vector Core, NC, USA) was bilaterally injected into the SI/DB region in male ChAT-Cre mice (n = 30) at the following coordinates: AP − 0.1 mm/bregma, ML ± 1.5 mm/midline, DV − 5.3 mm from skull. A total of 0.3 µl/hemisphere was injected. A sham control group of ChAT-Cre mice had the same surgery but no virus was injected (Sham; n = 11). Moreover, we used a double viral strategy in WT mice (n = 14) to selectively activate ACh neurons that project to the mPFC and injected a retro-AAV virus expressing the Cre-recombinase (AAVrg.hSyn.eGFP-Cre-WPRE-SV40; Addgene, MA, USA; 2.5 × 1013) into the PrL and then an AAV8-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry virus (2.1 × 1013; UNC Vector Core, NC, USA) into the SI/DB region, bilaterally. A sham control group of WT mice had the same surgery but no viruses were injected (Sham; n = 10)72,73. For retrograde AAV virus expressing the CRE-recombinase, a total of 0.5 µl/hemisphere was injected. All viruses were injected at a rate of 0.1 µl/min using a Hamilton syringe mounted in an infusion pump after which the injector will be left in place for an additional 10 min to allow virus diffusion. More than 4 weeks were allowed between virus injection and clozapine N-oxide (CNO) administration.

Cued-Fixed Consecutive Number (FCNcue)

FCNcue boxes

The FCNcue procedure was conducted in 12 identical sound-attenuated and ventilated operant chambers (24 × 16 × 21 cm; Imetronic, France) located in a dimly lit room with a background white noise. Each operant chamber was equipped with two nose-poke holes (NP), one on the right wall and one on the left wall, and cue lights situated above each NP (Fig. 1a), and equipped with infrared activity detectors. The floor consisted of stainless-steel bars separated by 0.4 cm. A liquid dipper (licker) was situated on the left wall, next to the NP. 75 µl of diluted sweet condensed milk (1:5) solution was delivered in the licker as a reward. The operant chambers were interfaced with a PC computer for data acquisition and storage. The reward delivery, presentation of visual stimuli, and recording of behavioral data were controlled and recorded by POLY software (Imetronic, France).

FCNcue task

The FCNcue schedule measured the ability of mice to carry out a chain of sequential acts (NP) to obtain a reward (Supplemental Fig. 1). The task required a fixed minimum number of responses (1 for FCN1, 2 for FCN2 and so on to 6 for FCN6) on the NP on the right (from now on, FCN-NP) followed by a response on the second NP, on the left (reward NP, RWD-NP), resulting in one reward delivery (1 optimal chain = 1 reward). The completion of the required number of FCN-NP was signaled by the switching off of the cue light above the FCN-NP. Then, the RWD-NP became active, which was indicated by the switching on of the cue light above it. After nose-poking in the RWD-NP, the cue light turned off and the reward was distributed in the licker. Once all the reward was consumed (45 licks required), the cue light above FCN-NP turned on indicating the beginning of a new chain. Premature response on the RWD-NP prior to the completion of the chain of visits to the FCN-NP, reflected poor inhibitory control and this reset the chain without reward distribution. High rate of optimal chains reflected good selective attention to the cue light.

FCNcue training and test

Before starting the training in the operant cages, mice were handled and the habituation phase consisted in three sessions during three consecutive days. The first day, mice were habituated to empty operant cages (no NP, cue lights or licker) for 30 min. The second and third day, the FCN-NP was installed into the box with its cue light above turned on. Each visit to the FCN-NP resulted in the delivery of the reward. Then, mice underwent FCNcue training during which both, FCN-NP and RWD-NP, were continuously available throughout the session. First, mice had to nose-poke once in the FCN-NP and then once in the RWD-NP to obtain sweet condensed milk (FCN1). After 3 sessions, mice were moved to the next level, FCN2 according to the same criterion. Then to FCN3. Mice underwent surgery after the third session in the level FCN3. After 5 days of recovery, animals came back to FCN3 training. After three consecutive stable sessions in FCN3, they moved to FCN4. Finally, mice reached FCN6. After three consecutive sessions of stable performance in the FCN6, corresponding to a plateau in the responding level, mice were injected with saline (subcutaneous, s.c.) for two consecutive sessions as a control. In the following sessions, mice were injected with CNO 2 mg/kg (intraperitoneally, i.p.). All, training and test, sessions lasted for 1 h and animals underwent one session per day. Finally, at the end of the last experiment, mice were tested with nicotine 0.15 mg/kg injected immediately before the session starts.

Behavioral measurements in FCNcue task

All behavioral data showed in this study were calculated in the FCN6, corresponding to the test phase of the task. The mean percentage of optimal responses [(number of chains of 6 FCN-NP/ (total number of chains of NP) × 100] was an index of attentional performance. Therefore, a high percentage of optimal responses denoted good attention, whereas a low percentage of optimal responses denoted poor attention. The mean percentage of premature responses [(number of chains of 1 FCN-NP) / (total number of chains of NP) × 100] was considered as an index of poor inhibitory control, i.e. high percentage of premature response reflected impulsivity, as previously demonstrated31. Response rate, calculated as the total number of nose-pokes per minute [(FCN-NP + RWD-NP) / 60] and the total number of chains of NP, were used as an index of motivation. We recorded the general activity (total counts of individual beam breaks) as well. The mean latency to collect the reward was considered a measurement of reward seeking and the sensitivity to the reward.

In vivo fiber photometry

Recordings

Photometry recordings were collected using a dual color multichannel photometry system (RWD Life Science) and a low-autofluorescence fiber-optic patch cord (400 μm core, 0.5 NA; RWD Life Science). Two excitation LEDs (470 nm and 410 nm) were bandpass filtered and used to excite calcium-dependent and -independent fluorescence from GCaMP6. Laser output for each excitation wavelength was set that laser intensity at the cable tip was 20–30 μW. Laser was passed through the patch cord for 30 min prior to daily photometry recordings to minimize autofluorescence of the cable during recordings. Recordings occurred using interleaved pulses of 410 and 470 nm LEDs. Fluorescence data were collected at 20 frames per second using RWD software, and behavioral events were aligned to photometry fluorescence using TTL timestamp outputs from Imetronic software (Imetronic, France). Mice were habituated to the optic cable setup in the operant chamber for 3 days prior to photometry recordings.

Processing and analysis

Data were processed and analyzed using custom-written Python scripts. Traces were visualized. The isosbestic control trace was fitted to fluorescent signal traces at 470 nm using a least squares polynomial fit of degree 1. The change in fluorescence ΔF/F0 was calculated by subtracting the fitted isosbestic signal from the fluorescent signal and then dividing by the fitted isosbestic signal (calculated as (F-F0)/ F0). Fluorescence was quantified for optimal and premature trials responses during the following behavioral events: 1) the 1st nose-poke on the FCN-NP (i.e., 1st FCN-NP) which correspond to the initiation of a chain of responses, 2) the nose-poke in the RWD-NP (i.e., RWD-NP) which correspond either to the end of an optimal chain for optimal trials, or to interruption of the responding prior to the completion of the chain for premature trials, and 3) during the 6 successive FCN-NP responses necessary to the completion of an optimal trial. Changes in fluorescence around behavioral events were Z-scored by using the baseline period, 2 s before the event, during which mean and standard deviation were computed to then normalize the entire signal snippet. Individually Z-scored snippets were then averaged for each animal. Figures show the average + /− SEM for the entire experimental group. The post-event peak was detected using the find_peaks function from Scipy, its prominence was used as a measure of its amplitude and its latency was also quantified as the delay between the event onset and the peak time. The area under the curve (AUC) was computed using the simps function from Scipy, applied in a time window of 4 s after event onset. Measurements taken on individual PETH were compared using a Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05).

To account for the variability in the duration of six successive FCN-NP responses across trials, we normalized the time axis for each response, aligning them on a common temporal scale. Specifically, we rescaled the time between successive FCN-NP events to fit a normalized interval between 0 and 1 for each event within a trial. Photometry signals were then resampled using interpolation at 500 evenly spaced points along this normalized time axis. This approach allowed for the comparison and averaging of ΔF/F₀ time courses across trials, while preserving the temporal dynamics of the signal relative to the behavioral events. A baseline normalization (z-scoring) was optionally applied using the pre-event window to standardize signal amplitudes across trials.

Immunohistochemistry for viral expression and histological verification

Mice were perfused transcardially with PFA 4% in PBS 0.1 M. Brains were post-fixed overnight in 4% PFA and then immunohistochemistry was performed along the rostro-caudal axis of the brain serially cut on a vibratome (50 µm-thick). Briefly, 50 µm free floating sections were first put in a blocking solution with Normal Donkey Serum 5% in PBS-Triton 0.3% (PBST) during 90 min and then incubated overnight with primary polyclonal goat anti-CTb (1:5,000; List Biological Laboratories 7032A10), rabbit anti-VAChT (1:500; Sigma Aldrich 099M4867V). For fluorescence microscopy, after washing sections were incubated with a donkey anti-goat A555 secondary antibody (labelling of Ctb, 1:1000, Invitrogen A21432) and a donkey anti-rabbit A488 secondary antibody (labelling of cholinergic neurons, 1:1000, Invitrogen A21206). Similar procedures were followed to label cFos-, mCherry-, GFP- and cholinergic-positive neurons, using primary rabbit-anti-cFos (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology 2250S; incubation during 2 night at 4 °C), rat-anti-RFP (1:1000; Chromoteck 90228002AB12), chicken-anti-GFP (1:1000, Abcam GR3361051-2) and goat-anti-ChAT (1:500, Millipore 3067431) respectively. The day after, sections were washed with PBST and revealed with donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa 488 (1 :1000, Invitrogen A21206), donkey anti-rat A555 (1 :1000, Invitrogen A48270), donkey anti-chicken A488 (1 :500, Jackson Immuno Research 703–546-155) and donkey anti-goat conjugated to A555 (1 :1000, Invitrogen A21432) for 90 min at RT. Finally, brain slices were washed and then mounted in Vectashield medium (Vector Laboratories) and cover slipped. Immediate-early gene cFos were used to quantify the effect of CNO administration in chemogenetic experiments, as a marker for neuronal activation. Images of fiber implantation sites, viral expression, and injection sites were taken using an epifluorescent microscope (Olympus) and were overlaid on the coronal atlas of Paxinos. Cell counting was done manually by an experimenter blind to the conditions using Image J.

Drugs

Clozapine N-oxide (CNO, Enzo Life Sciences) was dissolved in 0.9% saline to obtain a final concentration of 2 mg/kg based on previous research72. Saline or CNO was injected intraperitoneally 30 min before behavior. Nicotine hydrogen tartrate (Sigma-Aldrich, France) was dissolved in saline and pH adjusted. Nicotine was injected at 0.15 mg/kg expressed in nicotine base. All solutions were injected at a volume of 5 ml/kg.

Data analysis

Normality of the distribution was first tested using a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Behavioral data were subjected to two-way ANOVAs with repeated measures (1 between factor group, 1 within factor CNO), followed by “Sidak” Saline vs CNO (within comparison) post hoc tests where relevant. Percentage of cFos + cells were analyzed using one-way ANOVAs with a group between factor (saline, eDREADD and iDREADD), followed by “Sidak” post hoc test where relevant. The percentage of retrogradely labeled neurons was quantified across the different BF nuclei and compared using a Part-of-Whole statistical analysis. Statistics were performed on data using GraphPad Prism version 9.2.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla California USA). Comparisons were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.
