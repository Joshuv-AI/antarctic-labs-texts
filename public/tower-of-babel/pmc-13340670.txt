Blunt abdominal trauma revealing a giant splenic artery aneurysm in a cirrhotic patient: a case report
Authors: Setareh Mahmoodi, Delaram Mousavi, Koosha Sami, Yasaman Tavakoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Giant visceral artery aneurysms are exceedingly rare and carry a high risk of rupture. Patients with liver cirrhosis, portal hypertension, or connective tissue disorders are at an increased risk. Timely recognition and surgical intervention are critical to prevent life-threatening complications.Case presentation:A 66-year-old male with hepatitis B-related cirrhosis presented after blunt abdominal trauma. Imaging revealed a well-defined, contrast-enhanced hyperdense lesion in the mid-splenic artery, consistent with a giant aneurysm, along with a small hypodense liver lesion. Due to the aneurysm’s size and rupture risk, surgical resection of the splenic artery aneurysm (SAA) with splenectomy was performed via midline laparotomy, along with a liver wedge biopsy. Pathology confirmed a true SAA with wall calcification and atherosclerosis, an unremarkable spleen, reactive lymph nodes, and mild cirrhosis without malignancy. Postoperatively, the patient recovered well with an improved platelet count, stable liver function, and continued hepatitis B management.Clinical discussion:Giant SAAs may be asymptomatic or detected incidentally, yet they pose a significant rupture risk, particularly in cirrhotic patients. Surgical resection remains the definitive treatment, reducing the risk of catastrophic hemorrhage and improving clinical outcomes.Conclusion:Prompt diagnosis and early surgical management of giant SAAs are essential, especially in high-risk patients, to prevent rupture and achieve favorable postoperative recovery.

Introduction

Visceral artery aneurysms are rare but clinically significant due to the risk of rupture, hemorrhagic shock, and death. They are classified as arterial dilations that exceed 50% of the normal diameter. Complications include rupture with intra-/retroperitoneal hemorrhage, fistula development, and compression of neighboring organs[1]. Among abdominal aneurysms, infrarenal aortic aneurysms are the most common, followed by iliac artery aneurysms. Splenic artery aneurysm (SAA) is the most frequent visceral artery aneurysm[1]. SAA is a rare condition with an estimated prevalence of approximately 0.7%[2].

True aneurysms account for 60% of SAA cases and primarily affect women, with a 4:1 female-to-male ratio, which is frequently connected with pregnancy-related findings[3]. However, emergency department presentations show male predominance, particularly in middle-aged men with liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension[4]. Common risk factors include pancreatitis, atherosclerosis, and hypertension[5]. Most SAAs are diagnosed at a mean size of approximately 3 cm, with the majority measuring ≤2 cm at detection[6]. While typical SAAs demonstrate slow growth rates (0.04–0.17 mm/year) and often remain stable over years of observation, aneurysms exceeding 5–10 cm represent a distinct clinical entity with different pathophysiology[6,9,10]. Growth of SAAs ≤2 cm is rare and typically develops very slowly, suggesting that unusually large aneurysms may be attributable to a different, yet unexplained, pathophysiology. Data on the natural history of SAAs larger than 5 or 10 cm are lacking because interventional therapy has been proposed for nearly all such patients[6].

Giant SAAs, defined as aneurysms ≥5 cm (or ≥10 cm by some definitions), are extremely uncommon clinical entities that differ dramatically from conventional SAAs in presentation, demographics, and management[1,11–13]. Giant aneurysms present unique challenges due to their significant rupture risk, with a lifetime rupture risk of 28% for giant aneurysms[14]. Epigastric or left upper quadrant pain with pulsatile masses on examination is a common clinical presentation[11,14,15]. Giant SAAs differ from smaller aneurysms in terms of gender predominance, vascular localization, and clinical presentation patterns[11]. Surgical intervention is the primary treatment, with choices including aneurysm excision with splenectomy or distal pancreatectomy, depending on the location and patient characteristics[14,15]. Despite the availability of less invasive procedures, open surgery remains the preferred approach for these large aneurysms[14].

Cirrhotic patients represent a high-risk population for SAAs, with a reported prevalence of up to 15% in the setting of portal hypertension, significantly higher than in the general population[6]. Moreover, these patients tend to develop larger aneurysms at younger ages, suggesting an accelerated pathophysiological process related to portal hypertension and altered splenic hemodynamics[16–18]. The incidental detection of a giant SAA during blunt abdominal trauma introduces a unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, particularly in distinguishing pre-existing true aneurysms from trauma-related vascular injuries[8,19]. Given the rarity of this clinical intersection and the limited data on optimal management, reporting such cases provides valuable insight into decision-making in complex, high-risk scenarios[6,13].

In this study, we present the case of a 66-year-old male with chronic liver disease who was incidentally found to have a giant SAA following blunt abdominal trauma. Due to the aneurysm’s size and associated risks, he underwent a successful open surgical resection with splenectomy. This paper has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[20].

Case presentation

A 66-year-old male presented to the emergency department following blunt abdominal trauma. On admission, his vital signs were stable (blood pressure: 126/85 mmHg; pulse rate: 95 beats/min). The primary survey revealed no abnormalities. The patient reported a history of vague, intermittent abdominal pain localized to the left upper quadrant and epigastric region, persisting for more than 1 year.

He was a non-smoker, non-alcoholic, and non-diabetic. His past medical history was notable for liver cirrhosis for 30 years, secondary to chronic hepatitis B infection, for which he had been receiving tenofovir for the past 3 months.

On physical examination, there were no remarkable findings. The abdomen was soft, without tenderness, palpable mass, or organomegaly. No stigmata of acute abdominal injury were identified. Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma (FAST), performed by a radiologist, showed no free fluid or evidence of visceral rupture.

Given the persistence of abdominal pain, a contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scan was performed. Imaging demonstrated a hyperdense, homogeneous, well-defined, round mass-like lesion measuring 72 × 68 × 76 mm in the mid-portion of the splenic artery, highly suggestive of an SAA.

Subsequent CT angiography confirmed the diagnosis, revealing a large SAA (72 × 68 × 63 mm) with mild wall calcification but without intraluminal thrombosis. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated a well-defined, hyperdense aneurysmal lesion arising from the mid-portion of the splenic artery, measuring approximately 72 × 63 mm in the axial view (Fig. 1a and b) and 63 × 66 mm in the coronal plane (Fig. 1c). Additionally, a 16 mm hypodense lesion in segment VII of the liver with a nonspecific radiologic appearance was identified. Laboratory investigations showed moderate thrombocytopenia (platelet count: 85 000/µL), while the coagulation profile was within normal limits aspartate aminotransferase (AST): 40, alanine aminotransferase (ALT): 29, alkaline phosphatase (ALP): 157, bilirubin total: 0.8, and bilirubin direct: 0.2). Although the management of asymptomatic SAAs is usually conservative, intervention is indicated in symptomatic cases or when the diameter exceeds 2 cm due to the high risk of rupture. Available options include minimally invasive endovascular interventions with angiographic techniques and surgical procedures that may be performed either open or minimally invasive (laparoscopic or robotic). In cases of giant aneurysms, however, open surgical approaches are generally preferred to avoid life-threatening complications.

In our case, the aneurysm was not entirely asymptomatic, although its diagnosis was made incidentally. Considering the aneurysm’s size and the patient’s background of cirrhosis with thrombocytopenia, open surgical intervention was deemed appropriate.

Under general anesthesia, a midline laparotomy was performed with the patient in the supine position, extending from the xiphoid to 2 cm below the umbilicus. The lesser sac was opened from the antrum to the gastric fundus, where a giant 7 × 7 cm aneurysm was identified on the body of the pancreas (Fig. 2). The left gastric artery, adherent to the aneurysm, was isolated along its course to the celiac trunk. The splenic artery was then double-ligated proximal to the aneurysm, just distal to its origin from the celiac trunk. The splenic vein was similarly double-ligated near the pancreatic tail, proximal to the entry point of the inferior mesenteric vein. Splenectomy with en bloc resection of the aneurysm was subsequently performed (Fig. 3). A wedge biopsy of the hepatic lesion was also obtained. Hemostasis was secured, and the abdomen was closed in a standard layered fashion. Histopathological examination confirmed a true SAA measuring 7 cm with a calcified wall, indicating a chronic process. The liver biopsy revealed a hepatic adenoma with surrounding cirrhotic tissue, consistent with the patient’s known history of hepatitis B-related cirrhosis.

The patient was transferred to the surgical ward after the procedure. He experienced an immediate postoperative hemoglobin drop of 2 units, which improved spontaneously the following day. Platelet count increased to 135 000/µL on the second postoperative day. His postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged on postoperative day 3, as he remained hemodynamically stable, afebrile, with no significant drop in hemoglobin levels and a progressive increase in platelet count, meeting low-risk criteria for early discharge. The patient received post-splenectomy vaccination with the 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) at the time of hospitalization. This was followed by the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) 1 month later. A plan was also made to administer the meningococcal vaccine 2 months after discharge, along with annual influenza vaccination. Liver function tests remained stable, with no deterioration in the severity of cirrhosis, both in the immediate postoperative period and at the 6-month follow-up. Antiviral therapy with tenofovir was continued (Table 1). Discussion

Compared with the cases summarized in Table 2, the present case shares several features with previously reported giant SSAs, while also demonstrating distinctive aspects. Similar to a subset of reported cases, our patient presented with vague abdominal pain and incidental detection of the aneurysm after trauma; however, many cases in the literature presented more dramatically with rupture, hemorrhagic shock, or gastrointestinal bleeding, necessitating emergent intervention. In terms of management, open surgical repair remains the most frequently utilized approach for giant SAAs, particularly in cases with large size or complex anatomy. While endovascular and hybrid approaches have been increasingly applied in selected patients with favorable anatomy, their use in giant aneurysms remains limited. Notably, our case differs from most reports by the coexistence of long-standing cirrhosis and thrombocytopenia, as well as the context of blunt abdominal trauma leading to diagnosis, which is rarely described. The etiology of SAAs is multifactorial and includes both systemic and local factors. Portal hypertension and cirrhosis also play a major role by elevating splenic artery pressure and, in some cases, causing extrahepatic portal vein obstruction[1,37]. In our case, the patient had a long history of hepatitis B-related cirrhosis and moderate thrombocytopenia, both of which are known risk factors for SAA development. The association between cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and SAA formation is well established. The prevalence of SAAs in patients with portal hypertension has been reported to be as high as 15%, significantly exceeding that of the general population[6]. This increased risk is primarily attributed to a hyperdynamic circulatory state and increased splenic arterial inflow, leading to progressive arterial wall stress and dilation[17]. In addition, structural vascular alterations and impaired connective tissue integrity in cirrhotic patients may further predispose them to aneurysm formation and enlargement. Notably, portal hypertension has been identified as an independent risk factor for aneurysm growth in multivariate analyses, highlighting its central role in disease progression[16,38].

Clinical presentation of SAAs and splenic artery pseudoaneurysms (SAPs) is variable, with many cases – particularly large lesions – detected incidentally on imaging; notably, up to 17.07% of giant aneurysms remain asymptomatic[1]. When symptoms occur, the most common manifestation is abdominal pain, typically in the left upper quadrant or epigastric region, sometimes associated with a palpable or pulsatile mass[39,40]. In more advanced cases, complications such as compression-related symptoms or rupture may develop, the latter potentially leading to life-threatening hemorrhage or gastrointestinal bleeding[1,39,41].

In our patient, the clinical picture was atypical. Although he had a giant true SAA measuring 7 cm, the lesion was discovered incidentally after blunt abdominal trauma. The only preceding symptom was vague and chronic abdominal pain in the left upper quadrant and epigastric region for about 1 year, without a palpable mass or specific gastrointestinal complaints. Similar to our case, however, several reports describe incidentally detected giant aneurysms that remained silent until complications arose or imaging was performed for unrelated reasons. This emphasizes that, although the majority of giant SAAs become symptomatic, a subset can remain relatively silent, especially in patients with cirrhosis, where vague abdominal pain may easily be attributed to underlying liver disease.

In order to explain why giant SAAs can remain largely asymptomatic, it is important to note that most SAAs are asymptomatic and found incidentally. The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) notes that unusually large aneurysms may be attributable to a different, yet unexplained pathophysiology, as the growth of SAAs ≤2 cm is rare and typically very slow[6]. The slow expansion likely allows gradual adaptation of surrounding structures. In a systematic review of 92 giant SAAs (>5 cm), 73% were symptomatic at presentation – most commonly with abdominal pain – and 34% presented with rupture, carrying an overall mortality rate of 12.5%[13]. This patient’s vague chronic pain is consistent with the most common presentation of giant SAAs.

Contemporary management of SAAs has evolved significantly, with minimally invasive approaches such as laparoscopic and robotic surgery emerging as safe and effective alternatives to open repair in selected patients[42–45]. These techniques have demonstrated favorable perioperative outcomes, including low conversion rates, minimal blood loss, reduced postoperative morbidity, and shorter hospital stays compared to open surgery[43–46]. In particular, robotic-assisted surgery offers enhanced three-dimensional visualization and improved instrument dexterity, facilitating precise dissection and even vascular reconstruction, which may allow spleen-preserving procedures and avoidance of splenectomy-related immunologic complications[42,47,48]. Despite these advantages, minimally invasive approaches are not universally applicable. Giant aneurysms, typically defined as >5 cm, complex anatomical relationships (especially proximity to the pancreatic body or splenic hilum), and ruptured or hemodynamically unstable cases, remain challenging and are often better managed with open surgical techniques[8,13,49]. Furthermore, endovascular approaches are generally preferred as first-line therapy when anatomically feasible, although they may be associated with higher reintervention rates compared to surgical repair[8,50]. Therefore, the choice of treatment should be individualized based on aneurysm size, location, patient comorbidities, and institutional expertise. In the present case, the large size of the aneurysm and its close anatomical relationship with adjacent structures made open surgery a more reliable and definitive treatment option. The aneurysm was classified as giant, and the patient had underlying thrombocytopenia and multiple comorbidities, increasing the overall procedural risk. In addition, significant intraoperative adhesions were anticipated, further limiting the feasibility of minimally invasive approaches. Under these conditions, less invasive strategies such as endovascular or laparoscopic interventions were considered suboptimal, as potential complications might have been difficult to control or manage effectively, making open surgery the safest and most definitive approach.

The management of SAAs and SAPs focuses on minimizing rupture risk. Guidelines recommend treating non-ruptured SAPs and SAAs in women of childbearing age, symptomatic patients, aneurysms >3 cm, or those showing growth, while small aneurysms (<3 cm) in patients with significant comorbidities may be observed[51]. Endovascular therapy is generally preferred for elective cases, providing shorter hospital stays and favorable immediate outcomes through coils, stents, plugs, or embolic agents, though complications such as arteriovenous fistulas, pseudoaneurysms, hematomas, infections, and splenic issues require careful radiological follow-up[1], as seen in studies by Leow[26], Connors et al[29], and Shetty et al[34]. Open surgery or hybrid approaches are usually reserved for giant, complex, or ruptured aneurysms, or when endovascular resources are limited. Common procedures include splenectomy with distal pancreatectomy, aneurysmectomy, artery ligation, and arterial reconstruction, with associated risks such as hemorrhage, infection, organ injury, pancreatic fistula, and incisional hernia[33], as reported by Zain et al[27], Mulpuri et al[31], and Shabunin et al[25]. Hybrid strategies combining endovascular and open techniques have also been described to minimize rupture risk[1], like in the study by Salimi et al[33]. Although current guidelines, including those from the Society for Vascular Surgery, recommend intervention for SAAs ≥2–3 cm or in the presence of symptoms, the evidence specifically addressing giant SAAs (>5–7 cm) remains limited[8]. Most available data are derived from case reports and small case series, with a lack of high-quality comparative studies or randomized trials. A systematic review by Hamid et al demonstrated that giant SAAs frequently present with symptoms or rupture and are most commonly managed with open surgical approaches, particularly aneurysmectomy with splenectomy[13]. However, these findings are based on heterogeneous and low-level evidence, limiting the generalizability of recommendations. Therefore, in the absence of robust evidence, management decisions for giant SAAs must be individualized, taking into account aneurysm size, anatomical complexity, patient comorbidities, and institutional expertise.

Conclusion

Giant SAAs, though rare, carry a high risk of rupture, especially in patients with underlying risk factors such as cirrhosis or portal hypertension. This case underscores the importance of timely recognition and definitive surgical management, even in asymptomatic patients, to prevent potentially life-threatening complications. Early intervention, tailored to aneurysm size and patient comorbidities, can lead to favorable postoperative outcomes and improved overall prognosis.
