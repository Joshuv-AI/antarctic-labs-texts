A Modular Lean 4 Framework for Confluence and Strong Normalization of Lambda Calculi with Products and Sums
Arthur F. Ramos, Anjolina G. de Oliveira, Ruy J. G. B. de Queiroz, Tiago M. L. de Veras

Abstract
We present Metatheory , a comprehensive library for programming language foundations in Lean 4, featuring a modular framework for proving confluence of abstract rewriting systems. The library implements three classical proof techniques—the diamond property via parallel reduction, Newman’s lemma for terminating systems, and the Hindley-Rosen lemma for unions of relations—within a single generic framework that is instantiated across six case studies: untyped lambda calculus, combinatory logic, simple term rewriting, string rewriting, simply typed lambda calculus (STLC), and STLC extended with products and sums. All theorems are fully mechanized with zero axioms or sorry placeholders. The de Bruijn substitution infrastructure, often axiomatized in similar developments, is completely proved, including the notoriously tedious substitution composition lemma. We demonstrate strong normalization via logical relations for both STLC and its extension with products ( A × B A\times B ) and sums ( A + B A+B ), with the latter requiring a careful treatment of the case elimination form in our Lean 4 formalization. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive confluence and normalization framework for Lean 4.



1
Introduction

Confluence is a fundamental property of rewriting systems, guaranteeing that the
order of reductions does not affect the final result. The Church-Rosser theorem
for lambda calculus
[
6
]
established that
\beta
-reduction is
confluent, ensuring that every term has at most one normal form. This property
is essential for programming language semantics: it guarantees that evaluation
order does not change program meaning and that type systems are coherent.

Beyond confluence,
strong normalization
ensures that all reduction
sequences terminate—a property that holds for well-typed terms in the simply
typed lambda calculus. Together, confluence and normalization provide the
foundation for reasoning about type-theoretic languages: they ensure that
type checking is decidable and that types serve as meaningful specifications.

Despite decades of study, formalizing these results remains challenging.
The standard confluence techniques—parallel reduction
[
15
]
,
Newman’s lemma
[
10
]
, decreasing diagrams
[
12
]
—have
distinct proof patterns and preconditions.
Strong normalization requires logical relations
[
14
]
, involving
subtle reasoning about term structure and reduction.

Moreover, the underlying infrastructure for lambda calculus with de Bruijn
indices
[
5
]
requires numerous technical lemmas about shifting
and substitution. These lemmas are notoriously tedious to prove and are often
axiomatized
[
1
]
or avoided through named representations or
locally nameless encodings. We prove all such lemmas completely.

Generic Framework

Our framework provides three meta-theorems for proving confluence.

3.1
Diamond Property Implies Confluence

Theorem 3.1
(
confluent_of_diamond
)

\mathit{Diamond}(r)\implies\mathit{Confluent}(r)

The proof proceeds by induction on
a\rightarrow^{*}b
, using the diamond property
to “strip” one step at a time via a helper lemma

.

3.2
Newman’s Lemma

For terminating systems, local confluence suffices:

Theorem 3.2
(Newman’s Lemma)

Termination and local confluence imply confluence.

The proof uses well-founded induction on the termination order.

3.3
Hindley-Rosen Lemma

When combining confluent relations that commute, their union is confluent
[
8
]
:

Theorem 3.3
(Hindley-Rosen)

If
r
and
s
are confluent and commute, then
r\cup s
is confluent.

5.1
Typing and Subject Reduction

Typing contexts
\Gamma

\Gamma(n)=A

\dfrac{\Gamma(n)=A}{\Gamma\vdash\mathtt{var}(n):A}\;{\scriptstyle\mathsf{Var}}\quad\dfrac{A::\Gamma\vdash M:B}{\Gamma\vdash\lambda M:A\to B}\;{\scriptstyle\mathsf{Lam}}\quad\dfrac{\Gamma\vdash M:A\to B\;\;\Gamma\vdash N:A}{\Gamma\vdash M\,N:B}\;{\scriptstyle\mathsf{App}}

(

\Gamma\vdash M:A\land M\rightarrow_{\beta}N\implies\Gamma\vdash N:A

Candidate Properties.

Reducibility satisfies three key properties
that Girard
[
7
]
calls the “candidat de réductibilité” conditions:

CR1

\mathit{Reducible}(A,M)\implies\mathit{SN}(M)

Reducible terms are strongly normalizing.

CR2

\mathit{Reducible}(A,M)\land M\rightarrow N\implies\mathit{Reducible}(A,N)

Reducibility is closed under reduction.

CR3

\mathit{Neutral}(M)\land(\forall N.\,M\rightarrow N\implies\mathit{Reducible}(A,N))\implies\mathit{Reducible}(A,M)

A neutral term is reducible if all its reducts are reducible.

Here,
\mathit{Neutral}(M)
means

is not a redex—i.e., not of the form

(\lambda M^{\prime})\,N

are neutral.

Fundamental Lemma.

The main lemma states that well-typed terms
are reducible under any reducible substitution:

Theorem 5.2
(

If

and

Theorem 5.3
(

)

\Gamma\vdash M:A\implies\mathit{SN}(M)

Proof

Apply the fundamental lemma with the identity substitution (variables are
reducible by CR3), then extract SN by CR1.

Related Work

CoLoR.

The Coq library on rewriting and termination
[
4
]

is the most comprehensive formalization of term rewriting in a proof assistant.
It includes termination orderings, polynomial interpretations, and dependency
pairs. Our work differs in language (Lean 4), scope (we focus on confluence
techniques plus strong normalization rather than termination), and the inclusion
of complete de Bruijn proofs without axiomatization.

Isabelle Formalizations.

Nipkow
[
11
]
formalized multiple
Church-Rosser proofs in Isabelle/HOL, comparing parallel reduction, residuals,
and complete developments. Our parallel reduction approach follows similar lines.
The Nominal Isabelle framework provides elegant binder handling but requires
specialized infrastructure. We demonstrate that de Bruijn indices, while
requiring more lemmas, can be completely formalized.

POPLmark Challenge.

The POPLmark challenge
[
2
]

benchmarked different approaches to binding in mechanized metatheory. Solutions
ranged from named representations to de Bruijn indices to locally nameless
encodings. Aydemir et al.
[
1
]
popularized the locally nameless
approach. Many POPLmark solutions axiomatized substitution lemmas; we prove all
lemmas completely, demonstrating that full formalization is tractable.

Agda Formalizations.

Various Agda developments formalize lambda
calculus with de Bruijn indices, including strong normalization proofs for
STLC. Our work differs in being a unified framework for multiple techniques
and systems, culminating in the products-and-sums extension.

Software Foundations.

The PLF volume of Software Foundations
[
13
]

includes strong normalization for STLC in Coq using logical relations.
Our development extends to products and sums, which are not covered there,
and demonstrates the additional complexity this introduces.

9
Conclusion

We presented
Metatheory
, a modular confluence and normalization framework
for Lean 4 featuring three proof techniques across six case studies, culminating
in strong normalization for STLC extended with products and sums. Our fully
mechanized development (10,367 LOC, 497 theorems, 0 axioms) demonstrates that
de Bruijn infrastructure can be completely proved and that Lean 4 is viable for
programming language metatheory.

Appendix 0.D
Reduction Rules for STLCext

Computational Rules.

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Beta})

\displaystyle(\lambda M)\,N\rightarrow M[N]

\displaystyle(\mathsf{FstPair})

\displaystyle\mathtt{fst}\,(M,N)\rightarrow M

\displaystyle(\mathsf{SndPair})

\displaystyle\mathtt{snd}\,(M,N)\rightarrow N

\displaystyle(\mathsf{CaseInl})

\displaystyle\mathtt{case}\,(\mathtt{inl}\,V)\,N_{1}\,N_{2}\rightarrow N_{1}[V]

\displaystyle(\mathsf{CaseInr})

\displaystyle\mathtt{case}\,(\mathtt{inr}\,V)\,N_{1}\,N_{2}\rightarrow N_{2}[V]

Congruence Rules.

\displaystyle(\mathsf{AppL})

\displaystyle M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\implies M\,N\rightarrow M^{\prime}\,N

\displaystyle(\mathsf{AppR})

\displaystyle N\rightarrow N^{\prime}\implies M\,N\rightarrow M\,N^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Lam})

\displaystyle M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\implies\lambda M\rightarrow\lambda M^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{PairL})

\displaystyle M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\implies(M,N)\rightarrow(M^{\prime},N)

\displaystyle(\mathsf{PairR})

\displaystyle N\rightarrow N^{\prime}\implies(M,N)\rightarrow(M,N^{\prime})

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Fst})

\displaystyle M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\implies\mathtt{fst}\,M\rightarrow\mathtt{fst}\,M^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Snd})

\displaystyle M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\implies\mathtt{snd}\,M\rightarrow\mathtt{snd}\,M^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Inl})

\displaystyle M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\implies\mathtt{inl}\,M\rightarrow\mathtt{inl}\,M^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{Inr})

\displaystyle M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\implies\mathtt{inr}\,M\rightarrow\mathtt{inr}\,M^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{CaseM})

\displaystyle M\rightarrow M^{\prime}\implies\mathtt{case}\,M\,N_{1}\,N_{2}\rightarrow\mathtt{case}\,M^{\prime}\,N_{1}\,N_{2}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{CaseN_{1}})

\displaystyle N_{1}\rightarrow N_{1}^{\prime}\implies\mathtt{case}\,M\,N_{1}\,N_{2}\rightarrow\mathtt{case}\,M\,N_{1}^{\prime}\,N_{2}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{CaseN_{2}})

\displaystyle N_{2}\rightarrow N_{2}^{\prime}\implies\mathtt{case}\,M\,N_{1}\,N_{2}\rightarrow\mathtt{case}\,M\,N_{1}\,N_{2}^{\prime}
