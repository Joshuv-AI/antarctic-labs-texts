Biomechanical features and fracture characteristics of mandible based on digital image correlation technique
Authors: Shu-Guang Zhao, Wen-Bin Jiang, Xin-Yi Liang, Yi-Fan Zhang, Jian-Zhi Zhang, Run-Qing Li, Shuai Qin, Yuan-Yuan Quan, Kai Ding, Yan-Bao Li, Zhi-Qiang Li, Wei Chen, Chun-Yan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeCurrent understanding of mandibular biomechanics remains limited due to methodological constraints in characterizing full-field mechanical responses during trauma. This study employs Digital Image Correlation (DIC) Technique to quantify strain distributions and fracture thresholds during condylar axial loading, addressing gaps in correlating biomechanical loading patterns with clinical fracture.Materials and methodsThis study is a preliminary qualitative study. Six human mandibles (59.1 ± 6.2 years) without osseous pathology (CBCT-verified) underwent axial loading (0–600 N, 5 N/s) along the condylar axis using a Bose Electroforce 3520-AT system. Full-field strain and displacement were monitored via DIC. Destructive testing proceeded until mandible fracture, recording fracture thresholds and patterns.ResultsUnder 600 N axial loading to the mental region, peak strain localized at the condylar head (1261 ± 388 μm/m, P < 0.001). The condylar head exhibited a statistically significant reduction in coronal axis displacement (0.055 ± 0.033 mm, P < 0.05). Compressive strains dominated anterior structures (the condylar head, anterior condylar neck, anterior mandibular ramus, and mandibular body), while tensile strains concentrated posteriorly (in the posterior condylar neck, condylar base, posterior mandibular ramus, and mandibular angle). Fracture initiation occurred at critical thresholds of 1895 ± 393 N load and 3.1 ± 0.6 mm displacement, with 66.7% (4/6) of specimens exhibiting concomitant fractures in the mental regions, mental foramen, and molar regions.ConclusionAxial loading to the mentum-condylar induces maximum strain concentration at the condylar head, mandibular fossa constrains condylar displacement. The head of the mandibular condyle is most prone to fracture and is also prone to be accompanied by fractures in the mentum, mental foramen area, and molar region.

Introduction

The mandible is the largest and strongest bone in the human face. It has a horseshoe-shaped structure and is positioned prominently in the lower third of the face, playing a crucial role in mastication and facial aesthetics [1]. Biomechanical factors predispose the mandible to traumatic fractures, comprising 29% of craniofacial injuries. This pattern predominantly affects males aged 18–35 years due to load-bearing anatomical traits and high-impact mechanical exposure [2, 3]. The condylar neck, mandibular angle, mental foramen, and mandibular symphysis represent key mandibular fracture sites. Condylar fractures (25%−45% prevalence) predominantly arise from indirect mechanisms: impacts to the mental region or body induce stress concentration in the biomechanically vulnerable condylar region due to its slender morphology and critical role in force distribution, often resulting in displaced fractures distal to the impact site [4, 5]. Inadequate condylar fracture management predisposes to complications including chronic pain, malocclusion, hemifacial asymmetry, and temporomandibular ankylosis due to aberrant biomechanical loading patterns [6].

Understanding fracture etiopathogenesis facilitates preventive strategies and guides targeted therapeutic interventions. Contemporary biomechanical investigations of mandibular fractures employ three primary methodologies: advanced imaging modalities [7], computational modeling [8–10], and strain gauge quantification [11], each addressing distinct mechanical parameter analyses. Finite element analysis (FEA), predominantly employed in osseous biomechanics assessment, necessitates experimental validation through empirical biomechanical data. Current finite element modeling remains constrained by computational limitations in replicating cortico-cancellous architecture. In contrast, ex vivo mandibular specimen testing provides direct quantification of anisotropic strain distribution and fracture resistance thresholds, enabling precise characterization of mandibular biomechanical behavior under physiological loading conditions [12–14]. While contemporary strain gauge techniques provide localized mandibular strain measurements, they lack capacity to characterize macroscopic load distribution patterns essential for holistic biomechanical assessment [15]. Current methodological constraints impede comprehensive elucidation of mandibular mechanical characterization and fracture etiopathogenesis, resulting in incomplete clinical understanding of these biomechanical processes. Digital Image Correlation (DIC) is a non-contact optical metrology technique that provides full-field displacement measurements through automated image analysis. Characterized by minimal optical configuration requirements, broad applicability across material systems, and high resistance to environmental interference, this method has become a standard modality for mechanical characterization in multidisciplinary research [16, 17]. DIC overcomes limitations of conventional biomechanical methods: it supersedes strain gauges’ single-point measurement constraints and specimen surface damage risks, while addressing FEA shortcomings including simplification-induced inaccuracies and real-time monitoring deficiencies. Its full-field strain mapping provides experimental data with enhanced biomechanical fidelity compared to conventional strain analysis techniques [18], improving clinical predictability. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of DIC in mandibular biomechanics research. Quantified biomechanical parameters obtained enable both dynamic fracture progression analysis to enhance diagnostic accuracy and evidence-based development of patient-specific therapeutic strategies with improved prognostic validity.

The mental region represents the predominant impact locus in mandibular trauma, particularly during falls and pugilistic injuries. Biomechanical analyses demonstrate that axial loading along the condylar trajectory induces maximal von Mises stress concentration at the condylar neck, correlating with high fracture susceptibility [19, 20]. This experimental protocol therefore applies controlled dynamic loading along this anatomical axis to simulate clinical impact scenarios. Through DIC, the real-time strain propagation patterns and fracture initiation thresholds were quantified. The correlation between biomechanical loading patterns of mandibles and clinical fractures was studied qualitatively.

Materials and methods

Materials and equipment

This study is a preliminary qualitative study. Six cryopreserved human mandibles (donor age 59.1 ± 6.2 years, range 53–65) were donated from Hebei Medical University Department of Anatomy. Following CBCT screening (NewTom VGi), specimens demonstrating skeletal pathologies (neoplasms, severe osteoporosis, or developmental deformities were excluded. Specimen fixation was achieved using polyvinyl siloxane dental impression material. Biomechanical testing was conducted using an Electroforce 3520-AT testing system (Bose, USA) for dynamic mechanical loading synchronized with an ARAMIS 3D system (GOM GmbH, Germany) for 3D strain mapping. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval Number: S2021-019-1).

Specimen preparation

Specimens underwent sequential preparation: surgical removal of extraosseous soft tissues, surface abrasion with 200-grit and 600-grit silicon carbide paper, and ethanol-acetone cleansing. For optical tracking, surfaces received white primer coating followed by black speckle application (0.2 mm diameter, DL944017, Ningbo Deli Tool Co., Ltd.) to generate high-contrast patterns for ARAMIS 3D deformation analysis (Fig. 1).

Load setting

The experiment simulated mental region trauma conditions predisposing to mandibular fracture. A 600 N vertical load (representing physiological occlusal forces [21]) was applied along the condylar axis (mandibular symphysis to bicondylar midpoint) to assess elastic strain distribution. Destructive testing employed progressive loading (0 N Start) until structural failure, establishing fracture thresholds through synchronized mechanical optical monitoring.

Fixation of the specimen

Specimens were inverted with the mental region secured in an upper fixture using polymerizing resin, maintaining bicondylar horizontal alignment and vertical condylar axis orientation. A 4.5-mm condylar head embedment in resin-coated lower plates simulated temporal bone mandibular fossa anatomy [22], followed by 15-minute ambient curing to achieve biomechanically valid fixation (Fig. 2). Following established acetabular modeling techniques [23], this study employed autopolymerizing resin to replicate temporomandibular joint fossa morphology.

Experimental process

Vertical compression experiments

A standardized loading protocol was implemented: specimens underwent 200 N preload for 120 s to mitigate viscoelastic effects. Subsequently, vertical loading from 0 to 600 N was applied at 5 N/s under displacement control, maintaining 600 N for 120 s to simulate physiological loading duration. ARAMIS 3D continuously recorded full-field strain (Fig. 3) and displacement (Fig. 5) data in a unified anatomical coordinate system (X = sagittal, Y = vertical, Z = coronal axes). Nine 10 × 10 mm regions of interests (ROIs) were digitally mapped to quantify Z-axis biomechanical responses (Fig. 7): 1. Condylar head; 2.Condylar neck; 3. Condylar base; 4. Mandibular foramen; 5. Coronoid process; 6. Mandibular angle; 7. Ramus anterior zone; 8. Molar region; 9. Mental foramen. Mean principal strain magnitudes (µm/m) and Z-axis displacement values (µm/m) across ROIs were computationally derived using ARAMIS 3D, with quantitative results tabulated for comparative analysis (Tables 1 and 2).

Sabotage experiments

Displacement-controlled vertical compression was applied at 2 mm/min with synchronized acquisition of axial force and gross displacement. The testing system automatically terminated loading upon detecting mandibular fracture. Full-field strain and displacement vectors were captured using ARAMIS 3D, as shown in Figs. 4 and 6. Post-test fracture morphology was documented.

Statistical analysis

The IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 was used to analyze data. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed via one-way ANOVA with Tukey comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests. P < 0.05 was considered that the difference was statistically significant.

Results

Strain distribution characteristics of the mandible surface

Under 600 N vertical loading, strain analysis demonstrated significant concentrations in the condylar head (1261.2 ± 387.9 μm/m), condylar neck (508.5 ± 188.6 μm/m), condylar base (567.2 ± 111.5 μm/m), mandibular foramen (345.2 ± 136.8 μm/m), and mental foramen (336.3 ± 152.4 μm/m) (Fig. 3A; Table 1). Comparatively reduced strain magnitudes were observed in the coronoid process 47.1 [IQR 25.6–100.8.6.8] µm/m), mandibular angle (167.8 ± 73.6 μm/m), ramus anterior (97.0 ± 47.7 μm/m), and molar region (129.5 ± 87.0 μm/m), reflecting site-specific biomechanical responses to compressive loading. The peak strain localized at the condylar head (1261 ± 388 μm/m, P < 0.001). Strain vector analysis revealed predominantly horizontal orientation (compressive strain) in four anatomical regions: the condylar head, anterior condylar neck, anterior mandibular ramus, and mandibular body. Y-axis-aligned tensile strains predominated in the posterior condylar neck, condylar base, posterior mandibular ramus, and mandibular angle (Fig. 3B, C, D). Progressive loading (0–600 N) demonstrated nonlinear strain accumulation, the strain value of the upper part of the mandible increased obviously, with the condylar head exhibiting the strongest load-strain correlation (Fig. 7B). Destructive testing validated vertical compression strain localization patterns while revealing amplified strain gradients at fracture initiation sites (Fig. 4).

Characteristics of mandibular displacement distribution

Under 600 N vertical loading, mandibular displacement analysis revealed whole filed translation along the loading axis (Fig. 5A). While X/Y-axis displacement fields demonstrated homogeneous distribution patterns, Z-axis displacement analysis demonstrated region-specific variations (Fig. 5B, C and D; Table 2), ranging from 0.007 ± 0.034 mm (mental foramen region) to 0.110 (0.105, 0.128) mm (condylar base). The condylar base exhibited significantly greater displacement than other regions (P<0.01), followed by the mandibular foramen (0.108 ± 0.023 mm) and condylar neck (0.102 ± 0.020 mm). Minimal displacement occurred in the mental region (0.03 ± 0.03 mm) and coronoid process (0.101 ± 0.035 mm, P<0.05), reflecting differential load transmission pathways during vertical compression. Progressive loading (0–600 N) induced spatially graded Z-axis displacements across mandibular regions (Fig. 7D), forming a bimodal distribution pattern. Displacement magnitudes ascended from the mental foramen region (0.007 ± 0.034 mm) to peak at the condylar base (0.110 (0.105, 0.128) mm), then descended toward the condylar head (0.055 ± 0.033 mm). This displacement gradient reflects the mandible’s differential load-bearing capacity, with maximal deformation occurring at the biomechanically vulnerable condylar base during vertical compression. Fracture initiation analysis revealed triaxial displacement field stratification, with Z-axis gradients demonstrating the most pronounced mechanical discontinuity. Features of mandibular fractures

Fracture analysis revealed universal condylar involvement (6/6 specimens), with fracture distribution as follows: condylar head 83.3% (5/6), condylar neck 66.7% (4/6), and bilateral condyles 83.3% (5/6). Concomitant fractures occurred in 66.7% (4/6) of cases, primarily involving the mental region, mandibular foramen, and molar region. Critical fracture thresholds averaged 3.1 ± 0.6 mm displacement with 1895.2 ± 392.5 N load. Progressive stiffness loss was observed, yielding 875.9 ± 249.9 N at 1 mm displacement and 1418.7 ± 256.0 N at 2 mm (Table 3).

Discussion

This study pioneers the application of Digital Image Correlation (DIC) in a mento-condylar axial loading model, revealing critical biomechanical mechanisms of mandibular trauma. The condylar head exhibited peak von Mises stress concentration, with anterior-posterior strain divergence correlating to U-shaped morphology. Displacement gradients demonstrated maximal ramus separation at the condylar base versus constrained condylar head displacement due to mandibular fossa biomechanical coupling. Fracture initiation occurred at 1895 ± 393 N load with 3.1 ± 0.6 mm displacement, predominantly involving the condylar head (83.3%) and exhibiting 66.7% concomitant fractures in the mental region, mental foramen, and molar region.

Current mandibular biomechanical research faces three key limitations: (1) Overreliance on finite element analysis with compromised accuracy due to isotropic material assumptions and oversimplified boundary conditions, failing to capture bone tissue heterogeneity [24]; (2) Epidemiological inconsistencies in fracture incidence rates stemming from regional disparities in data collection and classification systems [25]; (3) Technical constraints of resistive strain gauges, including limited range, stress field interference, and difficulty in dynamically characterizing the fracture process [26]. These limitations impede precision treatment optimization. DIC overcomes conventional constraints via non-contact full-field strain analysis.

This study pioneers DIC in mandibular trauma modeling, quantifying fracture thresholds (1,895 ± 393 N) and strain redistribution during stress, enabling biomechanically-informed surgical strategies. Our findings corroborated finite element analyses [19, 20] in demonstrating significant strain localization at the condylar head (1261 ± 388 μm/m). Crucially, novel strain elevations were identified in the mandibular foramen (345 ± 137 μm/m) and mental foramina regions (336 ± 152 μm/m) - a phenomenon diverging from prior observations. This divergence is attributed to the fenestrated anatomical architecture of these neurovascular transit zones, where cortical discontinuity creates biomechanical stress risers during compressive loading. Strain vector mapping revealed compressive strains along the biomechanical axis (condylar head→mandibular symphysis) versus tensile strains at posterior regions (mandibular angle/condylar base), correlating with mandibular U-shaped geometry. This biomechanical dichotomy reflects differential moment arm effects during axial loading, consistent with Wolff’s law adaptation. DIC enables unprecedented full-field vector mapping, overcoming prior methodological constraints in capturing comprehensive biomechanical signatures across mandibular substructures. The Z-axis displacement gradient (coronal plane) peaked at the condylar base and diminished toward the condylar head, constrained by temporal bone mandibular fossa morphology (Fig. 7D). This validates mandibular ramus separation mechanics in the previous study [27, 28], demonstrating the capacity of DIC to map stress-shielding effects in craniofacial biomechanics. This study pioneers the quantification of site-specific displacement gradients in mandibular substructures, providing a new mechanical basis for understanding traumatic temporomandibular joint injuries.

Destructive testing demonstrated universal condylar fracture occurrence (6/6 specimens), with fracture distribution showing anatomical specificity: condylar head (83.3%, 5/6) > neck (66.7%, 4/6) > base (0%). This fracture hierarchy directly correlated with pre-failure strain concentration patterns (1261 ± 388 μm/m at head vs. 567 ± 112 μm/m at base). Notably, these findings diverged from epidemiological data by Kozakiewicz et al. [29] reporting condylar base fracture predominance (54% vs. 34% head/16% neck), suggesting biomechanical constraints from articular eminence morphology may protect the condylar base during experimental axial loading. The measured fracture threshold (1,895.2 ± 392.5 N) significantly diverges from prior reports: Nahum et al. [30] (1,890-4,120 N) and Unnewehr et al. [31] (2,500-3,100 N). This discrepancy likely stems from methodological variances in load application. While clinical fractures typically involve multidirectional vectors, our condylar axial loading protocol (terminated at condylar head failure) maximized mandibular stress concentration coefficients [19, 20]. This axis-aligned loading preferentially channels stresses to the condylar head, explaining its high fracture incidence (83.3%) versus clinical basilar predominance (54% [29]). Condylar fractures frequently present with concurrent fractures (66.7%) in other regions, indicating mechanical load transfer cascades at critical mandibular loads. When impact vectors align with the mentum-condylar axis, particularly in boxers, clinicians can leverage these findings for trauma assessment. Boxers sustaining mandibular fractures likely experience impact forces ≥ 1,895.2 ± 392.5 N with mandibular displacements ≥ 3.1 ± 0.6 mm. During CT evaluation of chin trauma, clinicians should meticulously assess condylar head integrity, focusing on bone mineral density alterations, morphological deviations, and early microfracture signs to prevent diagnostic oversight. Beyond condylar evaluation, thorough examination for concomitant fractures in the mentum, mental foramen, and molar regions is imperative. This study provides clinicians with pathological insights into fracture and enables data-driven optimization of osteosynthesis strategies based on individualized biomechanical profiling. These findings advocate for optimized facial protection design through enhanced impact energy management along the mentocondylar axis. For high-risk activities (e.g., boxing, motorcycling), incorporating energy-absorbing materials into chin guards or facial interfaces may attenuate stress transmission to the condylar region, thereby reducing fracture risks.

In future research, the combination of the electrical resistance strain measurement method and DIC can be used to study more detailed issues. DIC is suitable for the overall analysis of surfaces with large curvature, while the strain gauge method still has advantages in local fine measurement (such as the lingual area of the mandibular symphysis). In addition, DIC and the finite element method can also be used in combination, serving as tools for mutual verification. The combined application of multiple methods can enable the clinic to have a more thorough understanding of the biomechanical properties of the mandible and the occurrence and development process of fractures.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the sample size was small (n = 6) and all samples were from elderly individuals, which, while sufficient for an initial qualitative investigation, may limit the generalizability of the findings and preclude meaningful statistical analysis of biomechanical features distribution patterns. Second, the patterns of clinical mandibular fractures were relatively complex. However, this experiment only established an experimental model of trauma to the mental region of the mandible with a relatively fixed load direction, and did not investigate trauma to other regions of the mandible. Finally, the load of the experimental model was increased uniformly, which also had a certain difference from the instantaneous impact force in real mandibular fractures. Future studies utilizing larger, prospectively collected cohorts with well-documented donor characteristics are warranted to confirm and expand upon these initial observations, and should further optimize the experimental model to enhance clinical relevance.

Conclusion

Axial loading along the condylar axis induces maximal strain concentration in the condylar head (1261 ± 388 μm/m). The U-shaped morphology creates biomechanical heterogeneity, manifesting as compressive strains along the biomechanical axis versus tensile strains at the posterior regions. Articular constraints limit condylar head displacement (0.55 ± 0.33 mm vs. 1.83 ± 0.31 mm at base), while condylar head fracture initiation occurs at 1895 ± 393 N with 3.1 ± 0.6 mm displacement, and it is prone to be accompanied by fractures in the mentum, mental foramen area, and molar regions.
