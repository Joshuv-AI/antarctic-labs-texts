Projected gradient methods for nonconvex and stochastic smooth optimization: new complexities and auto-conditioned stepsizes
Guanghui Lan, Tianjiao Li, Yangyang Xu

Abstract
We present a novel class of projected gradient (PG) methods for minimizing a smooth but not necessarily convex function over a convex compact set. We first provide a novel analysis of the constant-stepsize PG method, achieving the best-known iteration complexity for finding an approximate stationary point of the problem. We then develop an “auto-conditioned” projected gradient (AC-PG) variant that achieves the same iteration complexity without requiring the input of the Lipschitz constant of the gradient or any line search procedure. The key idea is to estimate the Lipschitz constant using first-order information gathered from the previous iterations, and to show that the error caused by underestimating the Lipschitz constant can be properly controlled. We then generalize the PG methods to the stochastic setting, by proposing a stochastic projected gradient (SPG) method and a variance-reduced stochastic gradient (VR-SPG) method, achieving new complexity bounds in different oracle settings. We also present auto-conditioned stepsize policies for both stochastic PG methods and establish comparable convergence guarantees.

Introduction

Nonconvex optimization has recently found broad applications in machine learning
Mairal et al. (2009)
;
Mason et al. (1999)
;
Shi et al. (2014)
;
Liu et al. (2015)
, reinforcement learning
Puterman (2014)
;
Sutton and Barto (2018)
, and simulation optimization
Andradóttir (1998)
;
Fu (2002)
. In this paper, we consider the following general nonlinear, possibly nonconvex, optimization problem

\displaystyle f^{*}:=\min_{x\in X}f(x),

(1.1)

where
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex compact set with diameter
D_{X}:=\max_{x,y\in X}\|x-y\|
, and
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable but possibly nonconvex function. Let
x^{*}
denote one minimizer of (
1.1
) and assume
f
is smooth with Lipschitz continuous gradient, i.e., for some
L\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|,\quad\forall x,y\in X.

(1.2)

It indicates that
L
is the upper curvature bound such that

\displaystyle f(x)-f(y)-\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\leq\tfrac{L}{2}\|x-y\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in X.

(1.3)

Additionally, we assume that
f
is weakly convex with a lower curvature parameter
l\in[0,L]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle f(x)-f(y)-\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\geq-\tfrac{l}{2}\|x-y\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in X.

(1.4)

It is easy to see that
f
is a convex function
on
X
when
l=0
. Incorporating the lower curvature parameter
l
enables a unified treatment for both convex and nonconvex problems. This unified approach is particularly valuable for solving real-world nonlinear programming problems. For instance, many general nonconvex objective functions exhibit local convexity, and a unified treatment allows the algorithm to leverage this local convexity structure.
Additionally
, in black-box settings where the convexity of the objective function is typically unknown, a unified approach helps address such structural ambiguity effectively.
Moreover, many smooth optimization problems arising from machine learning and data science applications can have much larger upper curvature than lower curvature, such as the nonconvex quadratic program,
\min_{x}~\tfrac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax+c^{T}x
, where
\lambda_{\max}(A)>-\lambda_{\min}(A)\geq 0
. Another prominent example is the minimization of a smooth convex loss with a weakly convex regularizer, such as smoothly clipped absolute deviation (SCAD) and minimax concave penalty (MCP). In such settings, leveraging the lower-curvature information allows us to design more efficient optimization algorithms.

First-order methods are widely used to tackle nonconvex optimization problems.
In particular, it is well-known that the gradient descent method requires at most
\mathcal{O}\big(L(f(x_{0})-f^{*})/\epsilon^{2}\big)
gradient evaluations to achieve an
\epsilon
-stationary point such that
\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq\epsilon
, for unconstrained problems (see, e.g.,
Nesterov (2004)
). This bound is tight for gradient descent, as supported by Cartis et al.
Cartis et al. (2010)
. In the stochastic setting with access to an unbiased stochastic first-order oracle, Ghadimi and Lan
Ghadimi and Lan (2013)
proposed a stochastic gradient method, achieving
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{4})
sample complexity for outputting an
\epsilon
-stationary solution where
{\mathbb{E}}[\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}
.
Moreover, Ghadimi et al.
Ghadimi et al. (2016a)
analyzed the projected gradient descent method and its stochastic variants for constrained composite problems and established the same complexity bounds as in
Ghadimi and Lan (2013)
, for achieving an
\epsilon
-approximate stationary point in terms of the projected gradient mapping (see Subsection
1.2
).
Further studies on stochastic subgradient methods for nonsmooth nonconvex problems can be found in
Davis and Drusvyatskiy (2019)
. In general, the
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{4})
sample complexity is optimal for smooth nonconvex stochastic problems, as supported by
Arjevani et al. (2023)
. However, with an additional assumption on the Lipschitz continuity of the stochastic gradient, the sample complexity can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{3})
through variance-reduction techniques, see, e.g.,
Lan (2020)
;
Fang et al. (2018)
;
Cutkosky and Orabona (2019)
;
Wang et al. (2019)
;
Tran-Dinh et al. (2022)
;
Xu and Xu (2023)
;
Mancino-Ball et al. (2023)
.

Despite these advances, all the above-mentioned analyses of (projected) gradient descent and its stochastic variants do not leverage the lower curvature information
l
, thus failing to provide a unified treatment of convex and nonconvex problems.
Notably, Ghadimi and Lan
Ghadimi and Lan (2016)
proposed an accelerated gradient method (generalized from Nesterov’s accelerated gradient descent
Nesterov (1983)
) and established a unified gradient complexity of

\mathcal{O}\big((\tfrac{LD_{X}}{\epsilon})^{\frac{2}{3}}+\tfrac{LlD_{X}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\big)

(1.5)

in the deterministic setting; see further developments for accelerated gradient methods with unified complexity bounds in
Liang and Monteiro (2021)
;
Liang and Monteiro (2023)
.
On the other hand, proximal-point methods
Rockafellar (1976)
have also been applied to tackle nonconvex optimization problems
Hare and Sagastizábal (2009)
;
Davis and Grimmer (2019)
;
Lan et al. (2023)
, where the best-known complexity for smooth nonconvex problems is given by

\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{\tfrac{L\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|}{\epsilon}}+\tfrac{\sqrt{Ll}(f(x_{0})-f(x^{*}))}{\epsilon^{2}}\big),

(1.6)

as shown in
Lan et al. (2023)
.
Observe that, while the first term in (
1.6
) improves upon the corresponding term in (
1.5
)
especially in terms of its dependence on
\epsilon
, the second term in (
1.6
) is not necessarily better than its counterpart in (
1.5
) when the feasible set is bounded. This is because the second term can only be bounded by
\sqrt{lL^{3}}D_{X}^{2}/\epsilon^{2}
, as indicated in (
1.3
).
Compared to the aforementioned accelerated gradient methods or proximal-point type methods, the “vanilla” gradient descent method and its stochastic variants are more broadly applicable in modern large-scale optimization tasks, such as training
large-scale machine learning models
Lan (2020)
;
Bottou et al. (2018)
, due to their simple algorithmic structure and low memory requirements. However, it remains unclear whether gradient descent and its stochastic variants can achieve the unified complexities that incorporate the lower curvature information.

Another common challenge in implementing the first-order methods lies in the choice of the stepsizes. In particular, we usually need to estimate the upper curvature
L
to define the stepsizes, but
L
can be unavailable or difficult to compute in many practical scenarios. For example, for a quadratic function
f(x):=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax
, it can be time-consuming to estimate
\|A\|
when
A
is a large-scale dense matrix. Additionally, even when
L
is available, the stepsizes that rely on this global parameter can be too conservative, leading to slow convergence of the algorithms. Backtracking or line search procedures
Armijo (1966)
are commonly used in tackling this issue.
However, line search subroutines usually increase the per-iteration computational cost. Moreover, performing line search or backtracking in the stochastic settings can be particularly challenging, due to the additional “bias” of the stochastic gradient estimators created by the search stopping condition. To alleviate these limitations, there is a growing interest in the development of adaptive first-order methods that do not require line search procedures, especially for convex optimization (see, e.g.,
Orabona and Pál (2016)
;
Carmon and Hinder (2022)
;
Ivgi et al. (2023)
;
Malitsky and Mishchenko (2019)
;
Latafat et al. (2023)
;
Li and Lan (2025)
;
Zhou et al. (2024)
;
Lan and Li (2024)
). Notably, the recent work
Li and Lan (2025)
proposed an “auto-conditioned” fast gradient method (AC-FGM), achieving the uniformly optimal rate for smooth, weakly smooth, and nonsmooth convex optimization problems. The term “auto-conditioned” refers to the property that the method can automatically adapt to the problem parameters, such as the upper curvature
L
, without the employment of line search procedures. A key motivating question for our study is whether it is feasible to develop such “auto-conditioned” methods for nonconvex optimization.

Projected gradient methods for nonconvex optimization

In this section, we consider the deterministic nonconvex optimization where we have access to exact first-order information. In Subsection
2.1
, we present a novel analysis of the
constant-stepsize
projected gradient (PG) method that achieves the best-known convergence rate for finding an approximate stationary point, unifying convex and nonconvex optimization. In Subsection
2.2

2.1
A novel analysis of the projected gradient (PG) method

We consider the simplest projected gradient (PG) method in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Projected gradient (PG) method

Input
: initial point
x_{0}\in X
.

for

t=1,\cdots,k

do

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}\right\}.

(2.1)

end

for

Here, the update (
2.1
) can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle x_{t}=\mathcal{P}_{X}\left(x_{t-1}-\gamma_{t}^{-1}\nabla f(x_{t-1})\right),

where
\mathcal{P}_{X}
defines the Euclidean projection onto the feasible region
X
, and
\gamma_{t}^{-1}
can be viewed as the stepsize. For simplicity, we let
g_{X,t}
denote the projected gradient at step
t
, i.e.,

\displaystyle g_{X,t}\equiv P_{X}(x_{t},\nabla f(x_{t}),\gamma_{t+1}):=\gamma_{t+1}(x_{t}-x_{t+1}).

(2.2)

It is well-known that the (projected) gradient descent method can find an
\epsilon
-stationary solution of problem (
1.1
) s.t.
\|g_{X,k}\|\leq\epsilon
within at most
k=\mathcal{O}\left([L(f(x_{0})-f^{*})]/\epsilon^{2}\right)
iterations. However, this complexity bound does not take advantage of the weak-convexity property (
1.4
). In the following theorem, we present a novel analysis for PG and obtain a new complexity bound which reflects both upper curvature
L
and lower curvature
l
.

Theorem 2.1

Let
\{x_{t}\}
be generated by Algorithm
1

with an initial search point
x_{0}
and stepsize

\gamma_{t}=\gamma\geq L,\forall\,t
.
Let
\mathcal{E}_{X}(x_{0}):=\{x\in X|f(x)\leq f(x_{0})\}
and
D_{X,x_{0}}:=\max_{x,y\in\mathcal{E}_{X}(x_{0})}\|x-y\|
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq t\leq k-1}\|g_{X,t}\|^{2}\leq\left(\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\right)^{-1}\left(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}}+\tfrac{lk}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D_{X,x_{0}}^{2}}\right).

(2.3)

Specifically, if
\gamma_{t}=L,\forall\,t
, then

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq t\leq k-1}\|g_{X,t}\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{2L^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}}}{k(k-1)}+\tfrac{2Ll{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D_{X,x_{0}}^{2}}}{k-1}.

(2.4)

Proof

By the optimality condition of (
2.1
), we have the following three-point inequality, i.e., for any
x\in X
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

Adding
f(x_{t-1})
on both sides of the above inequality and rearranging the terms, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x_{t-1}\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x-x_{t-1}\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

(2.5)

Then using the smoothness condition (
1.3
) on the LHS of the above inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{t})+(\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x-x_{t-1}\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

(2.6)

Taking
x=x_{t-1}
in the above inequality provides us with

\displaystyle(\gamma_{t}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\leq f(x_{t-1})-f(x_{t}).

(2.7)

Clearly, this inequality indicates that the function value is non-increasing over iterations as we choose
\gamma_{t}\geq L/2
.
Therefore, we have
\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},x_{t}\in\mathcal{E}_{X}(x_{0})
, and consequently,
\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq D_{X,x_{0}}^{2}
.

Setting
x=x^{*}
in Ineq. (
2.6
) and using the weakly convex condition
f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x^{*}-x_{t-1}\rangle\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f(x^{*})}+l\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{t})+(\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq f(x^{*})+\tfrac{l}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}.

(2.8)

By taking a telescope sum of the above inequality from
t=1
to
k
, and invoking
\gamma_{t}=\gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{l}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\gamma}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}}+\tfrac{lk}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D_{X,x_{0}}^{2}}.

(2.9)

We can further lower bound the LHS of the above inequality by

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x_{k})+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[\textstyle\sum_{j=t}^{k-1}\big(f(x_{j})-f(x_{j+1})\big)+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\geq}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[\textstyle\sum_{j=t}^{k-1}(\gamma-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{j}-x_{j+1}\|^{2}+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big((t-\tfrac{1}{2})\gamma-\tfrac{t}{2}L\big)\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\|g_{X,t-1}\|^{2},

(2.10)

where
(i)
follows from (
2.7
).
By combining Ineqs. (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq t\leq k-1}\|g_{X,t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\right)^{-1}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\|g_{X,t-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\right)^{-1}\left(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}}+\tfrac{lk}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D_{X,x_{0}}^{2}}\right),

which completes the proof.

In view of Theorem
2.1
, to find an
\epsilon
-stationary solution such that
\|g_{X,t}\|\leq\epsilon

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{L{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}}}{\epsilon}+\tfrac{Ll{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D_{X,x_{0}}^{2}}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(2.11)

This complexity bound unifies both convex and nonconvex optimization
for the constant-stepsize PG method
. Specifically, if we set

. Such dependence on the initialization

enables the algorithm to benefit from the restarting scheme when the problem exhibits local growth or error-bound structures.
On a separate note, when
l>0
, the best-known complexity for nonconvex optimization achieved by
Lan et al. (2023)
is
\mathcal{O}(\tfrac{\sqrt{Ll}(f(x_{0})-f^{*})}{\epsilon^{2}})\leq\mathcal{O}(\tfrac{\sqrt{l}L^{3/2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D_{X,x_{0}}^{2}}}{\epsilon^{2}})
, which does not necessarily outperform the leading term
\mathcal{O}(\tfrac{Ll{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D_{X,x_{0}}^{2}}}{\epsilon^{2}})
in (
2.11
).

Remark 1

The guarantees in Theorem
2.1
and all the remaining sections of this paper naturally extend to the problem of minimizing a composite function

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}:=\min_{x\in X}\{\Psi(x):=f(x)+h(x)\},

(2.12)

where

is a “prox-friendly” closed convex function such that
the
“proximal-mapping” subproblem

\displaystyle x^{+}=\arg\min_{u\in X}\left\{\langle g,u\rangle+h(x)+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x-u\|^{2}\right\}

can be efficiently solved. Specifically, we define
P_{X}(x,g,\gamma):=\gamma(x-x^{+})
and slightly modify step (
2.1
) to

\displaystyle x_{t}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x\rangle+h(x)+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}\right\}.

Then by utilizing the convexity of

, we can show that Theorem
2.1
also holds for the composite problem (
2.12
), with the composite projected gradient
P_{X}(x,\nabla f(x),\gamma)
serving as the termination criterion.
Here, we require
\bar{X}:=\{x\in X|h(x)<+\infty\}
to be a closed convex set, and
\mathcal{E}_{\bar{X}}(x_{0}):=\{x\in\bar{X}|\Psi(x)\leq\Psi(x_{0})\}
to be bounded.

The detailed derivation can be found in Appendix
A.1
.
Moreover, all the following results in this paper can be generalized similarly,
but these extensions require the feasible set of the composite problem
\bar{X}
to be a compact set. How to extend the unified complexity bounds, e.g., Theorem
2.1
, to the general composite setting with an arbitrary closed convex feasible set like
\mathbb{R}^{n}
remains open.

Remark 2

The unified analysis in Theorem
2.1
can also be generalized to the setting where
f
can be potentially weakly convex, convex, or strongly convex, i.e., for

,

\displaystyle f(x)-f(y)-\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\geq-\tfrac{l}{2}\|x-y\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in X.

(2.13)

In Appendix
A.2
, we provide unified convergence guarantees for PG (Algorithm
1
) in terms of the norm of the projected gradient mapping, which covers the strongly convex setting (

). However, when using the auto-conditioned stepsizes, our unified guarantees presented in the following sections do not extend straightforwardly to the strongly convex setting.

2.2
Auto-conditioned projected gradient (AC-PG) method

In this subsection, we consider a parameter-free variant of the PG method, which does not require any prior knowledge of the upper curvature
L
or lower curvature
l
. Meanwhile, the algorithm is also free of any line search or backtracking procedures.

Algorithm 2

Auto-conditioned projected gradient (AC-PG) method

Input
: initial point
x_{0}\in X
and
L_{0}
such that
and
0<L_{0}\leq L
.

for

do

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}\right\},

(2.14)

\displaystyle\text{where }\gamma_{t}=\hat{L}_{t-1}:=\max\{L_{0},...,L_{t-1}\},

(2.15)

\displaystyle L_{t}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2(f(x_{t})-f(x_{t-1})-\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x_{t-1}\rangle)}{\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}}.

(2.16)

end

In Algorithm

L_{0}
can be determined by simply choosing two points in
X
and computing a local smoothness factor using (

2.16

whenever the search point

becomes available, and the new stepsize will be determined purely by the past local smoothness estimations.
Here, computing the local smoothness estimator

3
3

3

Similar arguments also apply in the stochastic setting.
. Therefore, our scheme does not significantly increase either the per-iteration cost or the total amount of elementary computations. On the other hand, recall that we adopt the convention

It is important to note that by only using past local Lipschitz estimators, we may underestimate the local Lipschitz constant, especially when this constant increases sharply at the current search point. Consequently, the AC-PG method cannot always ensure a decrease in the function value. However, it is worth highlighting that the function value only increases in a limited number of iterations, and the total magnitude of these increases can be appropriately bounded, as they form a summable sequence.
To this end, we partition the iterations
\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{k}\}

Specifically, the iterates

2.16

By construction, the following properties hold:

\displaystyle L_{1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle>\tfrac{3}{2}\hat{L}_{s_{i-1}}^{(i-1)},~\text{for }i\geq 2

Theorem 2.2

By the optimality condition of (

\displaystyle f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x_{t-1}\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}

By using the definition of

\displaystyle f(x_{t})+(\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}-\tfrac{L_{t}}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x-x_{t-1}\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

Taking

\displaystyle(\gamma_{t}-\tfrac{L_{t}}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\leq f(x_{t-1})-f(x_{t}).

Clearly, when

On the other hand, setting

\displaystyle f(x_{t})+(\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}-\tfrac{L_{t}}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}

By taking a telescope sum of the above inequality from

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})+(\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}-\tfrac{L_{t}}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}(\tfrac{\gamma_{t+1}}{2}-\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2})\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{l_{t-1}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\tfrac{\gamma_{k}}{2}D_{X}^{2}+\tfrac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\max\{0,l_{t}\}}{2}D_{X}^{2}\overset{(ii)}{=}\tfrac{\hat{L}_{k-1}}{2}D_{X}^{2}+\tfrac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\max\{0,l_{t}\}}{2}D_{X}^{2},

Theorem
2.2
enables us to establish the complexity bounds for the AC-PG algorithm (Algorithm
2
). Recall that
m

Remark 3

Algorithm
2
employs a non-increasing stepsize policy, which may be conservative for certain problems. However, the algorithm can be readily adapted to enhance convergence efficiency. For example, incorporating line search steps or periodically resetting

3
Stochastic projected gradient methods for nonconvex stochastic optimization

In this section, we consider the stochastic setting where only stochastic first-order information about
f
is available.
In particular, we assume access to a stochastic oracle that provides the stochastic gradient
{\cal G}(x,\xi)
at the query point
x\in X
, where
\xi\in\Xi
denotes a random variable with probability distribution supported on the set
\Xi
.
The
\xi
’s generated by the stochastic oracle are assumed to be independent and identically distributed. We further impose the following two assumptions:

Assumption 1
(Unbiasness)

For any
x\in X
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[{\cal G}(x,\xi)]=\nabla f(x).

(3.1)

Assumption 2
(Bounded variance)

There exists
\sigma^{2}>0
such that for any
x\in X
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\|{\cal G}(x,\xi)-\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}.

(3.2)

We consider the mini-batch approach, which is widely used in practice. Specifically, at iteration
t
, we define

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{t}(x)=\tfrac{1}{b_{t}}\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{b_{t}}{\cal G}(x,\xi^{t}_{j}),\

where
\{\xi^{t}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{b_{t}}
are mutually independent random variables. Then, we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{G}_{t}(x)]=\nabla f(x)
.
Furthermore, it follows from Assumption
2
that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|\bar{G}_{t}(x)-\nabla f(x)\|^{2}]\leq\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{b_{t}}.

In analogy to Section
2
, we first present a novel analysis of the stochastic projected gradient (SPG) method in Subsection
3.1
and then introduce an auto-conditioned variant of SPG with adaptive stepsizes in Subsection
3.2
. Moreover, in Subsection
3.3
, we extend the expectation guarantees of Subsection
3.2
to high-probability guarantees by employing a two-phase approach.

3.1
Stochastic projected gradient (SPG) method

In this subsection, we consider the following stochastic variant of the PG method (Algorithm

) equipped with mini-batches.

For convenience, we define the projected stochastic gradient at iterate

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{X,t}:=P_{X}(x_{t},\hat{G}_{t+1}(x_{t}),\gamma_{t+1})=\gamma_{t+1}(x_{t}-x_{t+1}),

The convergence guarantees of SPG are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

Assume Assumptions
1

2

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x_{R(k)}=x_{t-1})=\left(\textstyle\sum_{j=2}^{k}W(j)\right)^{-1}\cdot W(t),

(3.7)

where

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|g_{X,R(k)}\|^{2}\right]\leq\left(\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}(\tfrac{3t-2}{8\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{4\gamma^{2}})\right)^{-1}\left[(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}+\tfrac{L}{4}+\tfrac{lk}{2})D_{X}^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}(\tfrac{7t-2}{4\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}})\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{b_{t}}+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma b_{1}}\right].

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|g_{X,R(k)}\|^{2}\right]\leq\tfrac{40L^{2}D_{X}^{2}}{k(k-1)}+\tfrac{24LlD_{X}^{2}}{k-1}.

(3.9)

Proof

First, the optimality condition of (

\displaystyle\langle\hat{G}_{t}(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

on both sides of the above inequality and rearranging the terms yield

\displaystyle f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x_{t-1}\rangle+\langle\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t-1}-x\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

By further using Young’s inequality
\langle\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x_{t-1}\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{4}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\geq-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma_{t}}
, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x_{t-1}\rangle+\langle\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1}),x_{t-1}-x\rangle-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma_{t}}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{4}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t-1}-x\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

Next, we apply the smoothness condition of
f
(Ineq. (
1.3
)) on the LHS of the above inequality, and obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{t})+(\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{4}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}+\langle\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1}),x_{t-1}-x\rangle-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma_{t}}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t-1}-x\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

(3.10)

Taking
x=x_{t-1}
in the above inequality and rearranging the terms, then we have

\displaystyle f(x_{t-1})-f(x_{t})\geq(\tfrac{3\gamma_{t}}{4}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma_{t}}.

(3.11)

Setting
x=x^{*}
in Ineq. (
3.10
), applying the weakly convex condition
f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t-1}-x^{*}\rangle\leq f(x^{*})+\tfrac{l}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}
, and rearranging the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})+(\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{4}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{l}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\langle\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1}),x^{*}-x_{t-1}\rangle.

(3.12)

Then, by taking the telescope sum of the above inequality for
t=1,...,k
, and using
\gamma_{t}=\gamma
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})+(\tfrac{\gamma}{4}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{l}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\langle\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1}),x^{*}-x_{t-1}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{lk}{2}D_{X}^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}D_{X}^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\langle\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1}),x^{*}-x_{t-1}\rangle.

(3.13)

Next, we further lower bound the LHS of the above inequality, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})+(\tfrac{\gamma}{4}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x_{k})+(\tfrac{\gamma}{4}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[\textstyle\sum_{j=t}^{k-1}[f(x_{j})-f(x_{j+1})]+(\tfrac{\gamma}{4}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\geq}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[\textstyle\sum_{j=t}^{k-1}[(\tfrac{3\gamma}{4}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{j}-x_{j+1}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{j+1}(x_{j})\|^{2}}{\gamma}]+(\tfrac{\gamma}{4}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[(\tfrac{(3t-2)\gamma}{4}-\tfrac{tL}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}-\tfrac{t\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\geq}\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}\left[(\tfrac{3t-2}{4\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}})\|\hat{g}_{X,t-1}\|^{2}\right]-\tfrac{L}{4}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|^{2}-\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{t\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma}

\displaystyle\overset{(iii)}{\geq}\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}\left[(\tfrac{3t-2}{8\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{4\gamma^{2}})\|g_{X,t-1}\|^{2}-(\tfrac{3t-2}{4\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}})\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}\right]-\tfrac{L}{4}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|^{2}-\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{t\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}}{\gamma}.

(3.14)

Here, step (i) follows from Ineq. (
3.11
), step (ii) uses the definition of
\hat{g}_{X,t}
and
\gamma>L
, and step (iii) is due to Young’s inequality and the fact that
\|g_{X,t-1}-\hat{g}_{X,t-1}\|\leq\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|
(see Proposition 6.1 of
Lan (2020)
). By combining Ineqs. (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), and rearranging the terms, we have

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}\left[(\tfrac{3t-2}{8\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{4\gamma^{2}})\|g_{X,t-1}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}+\tfrac{L}{4}+\tfrac{lk}{2})D_{X}^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}(\tfrac{7t-2}{4\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}})\|\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1})\|^{2}+\tfrac{\|\hat{\delta}_{1}(x_{0})\|^{2}}{\gamma}+\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\langle\hat{\delta}_{t}(x_{t-1}),x^{*}-x_{t-1}\rangle.

Taking the expectation on both sides of the above inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\tfrac{3t-2}{8\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{4\gamma^{2}})\|g_{X,t-1}\|^{2}\right]\leq(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}+\tfrac{L}{4}+\tfrac{lk}{2})D_{X}^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}(\tfrac{7t-2}{4\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}})\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{b_{t}}+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma b_{1}}.

Then, due to the random output rule (
3.7
), we have
\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\tfrac{3t-2}{8\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{4\gamma^{2}})\|g_{X,t-1}\|^{2}\right]=\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{k}(\tfrac{3t-2}{8\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{4\gamma^{2}}){\mathbb{E}}[\|g_{X,R(k)}\|^{2}]
, and thus complete the proof.

A few remarks about Theorem
3.1
are in place.
Firstly, Theorem
3.1
assumes the knowledge of the problem parameter
L
to properly choose stepsizes. Meanwhile, to achieve the unified convergence rate in (
3.9
), we also require the knowledge of
l
,
D_{X}^{2}
, and
\sigma^{2}
to choose the proper batch sizes.
Secondly, to find an
\epsilon
-approximate stationary point
x_{R}\in X
such that
{\mathbb{E}}\|g_{X,R}\|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}
, the number of SPG iterations is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{LD_{X}}{\epsilon}+\tfrac{LlD_{X}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right),

which matches the iteration complexity (
2.11
) of the PG method in the deterministic setting. Thirdly, considering the batch size in each iteration, the total number of oracle calls
\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}b_{t}
(or the sample complexity) can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{LD_{X}}{\epsilon}+\tfrac{LlD_{X}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{LD_{X}\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon^{3}}+\tfrac{LlD_{X}^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon^{4}}\right).

(3.15)

To the best of our knowledge, this complexity bound is new for the SPG method in the literature.

3.2
Auto-conditioned stochastic projected gradient (AC-SPG) method

In this subsection, we develop a parameter-free variant of the SPG method. To achieve this goal, we require a stronger assumption on the stochastic oracle such that it can output both the stochastic function value
{\cal F}(x,\xi)
and the stochastic gradient
{\cal G}(x,\xi)=\nabla{\cal F}(x,\xi)
, where
{\cal F}(x,\xi)
is smooth uniformly for all
\xi
.

Assumption 3
(Smoothness of the stochastic function)

Assume
{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[{\cal F}(x,\xi)]=f(x)
,
{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[{\cal G}(x,\xi)]=\nabla f(x)
, and for each
\xi\in\Xi
, there exists an
{\cal L}(\xi)\geq 0
such that for any
x,y\in X
,
\xi\in\Xi
,

\displaystyle\|{\cal G}(x,\xi)-{\cal G}(y,\xi)\|\leq{\cal L}(\xi)\|x-y\|,

(3.16)

and

\displaystyle{\cal F}(x,\xi)-{\cal F}(y,\xi)-\langle{\cal G}(y,\xi),x-y\rangle\leq\tfrac{{\cal L}(\xi)}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

(3.17)

Here,
{\cal L}(\xi)\leq\hat{\cal L}
for any
\xi\in\Xi
almost surely, and
{\mathbb{E}}[{\cal L}(\xi)^{2}]\leq\bar{\cal L}^{2}
.

For notational simplicity, for any
x,y\in X
, we define

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{x,y}(\xi)

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{2({\cal F}(x,\xi)-{\cal F}(y,\xi)-\langle\nabla{\cal F}(y,\xi),x-y\rangle)}{\|x-y\|^{2}},~\text{and}~L_{x,y}:=\tfrac{2(f(x)-f(y)-\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle)}{\|x-y\|^{2}}.

(3.18)

Therefore, we have
{\cal L}_{x,y}(\xi)\leq{\cal L}(\xi)\leq\hat{\cal L}
and
L_{x,y}\leq L
.

Before formally presenting the auto-conditioned variant of the SPG method, we first propose two different choices to estimate the local Lipschitz constant at the local search points
x_{t}
and
x_{t-1}
using the stochastic oracle.

•

Estimator I
: Collect
b_{t}^{\prime}
i.i.d. observations from the stochastic oracle, denoted by
\bar{\xi}_{1}^{t},...,\bar{\xi}_{b_{t}^{\prime}}^{t}
, and compute

\displaystyle\bar{\cal L}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{b_{t}^{\prime}}\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{b_{t}^{\prime}}{\cal L}_{x_{t},x_{t-1}}(\bar{\xi}_{j}^{t}),~~\text{for $t\geq 1$}.

(3.19)

•

Estimator II
: Set
\bar{\cal L}_{0}
such that
0<\bar{\cal L}_{0}\leq\hat{\cal L}
. Collect
b_{t}^{\prime}
i.i.d. observations from the stochastic oracle, denoted by
\bar{\xi}_{1}^{t},...,\bar{\xi}_{b_{t}^{\prime}}^{t}
, and compute

\displaystyle\bar{\cal L}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{\bar{\cal L}_{0},\max_{j=1,...,b_{t}^{\prime}}{\cal L}_{x_{t},x_{t-1}}(\bar{\xi}_{j}^{t})\right\},~~\text{for $t\geq 1$}.

(3.20)

Clearly, Estimator I is an averaged estimator constructed with i.i.d. observations; thus, it is also an unbiased estimator of

\displaystyle L_{t}:=\tfrac{2(f(x_{t})-f(x_{t-1})-\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x_{t-1}\rangle)}{\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}}

(3.21)

under Assumption
3
.
The next lemma characterizes the concentration of
\bar{\cal L}_{t}
in (
3.19
) around
L_{t}
.

Lemma 1

Suppose Assumption
3
holds,
and let
L_{t}
and
\bar{\cal L}_{t}
be defined in (
3.21
) and (
3.19
), respectively.

We have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{\cal L}_{t}]={\mathbb{E}}[L_{t}],\quad{\mathbb{E}}[(\bar{\cal L}_{t}-L_{t})^{2}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\tfrac{\bar{\cal L}^{2}}{b_{t}^{\prime}}],

(3.22)

and with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\bar{\cal L}_{t}\leq L_{t}+\tfrac{2(\hat{\cal L}+L)\log(1/\delta)}{3b_{t}^{\prime}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{2\bar{\cal L}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{b_{t}^{\prime}}}.

(3.23)

Proof

For convenience, we define

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{t}(\bar{\xi}_{j}^{t}):=\tfrac{2({\cal F}_{t}(x_{t},\bar{\xi}^{t}_{j})-{\cal F}_{t}(x_{t-1},\bar{\xi}^{t}_{j})-\langle{\cal G}_{t}(x_{t-1},\bar{\xi}^{t}_{j}),x_{t}-x_{t-1}\rangle)}{\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}}.

(3.24)

Then by the definition of
\bar{\cal L}_{t}
in (
3.19
), we have
\bar{\cal L}_{t}=\tfrac{1}{b_{t}^{\prime}}\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{b_{t}^{\prime}}{\cal L}_{t}(\bar{\xi}_{j}^{t})
. By Assumption
3
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{\cal L}_{t}]={\mathbb{E}}[L_{t}].

(3.25)

By further utilizing Assumption
3
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(\bar{\cal L}_{t}-L_{t})^{2}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[(\tfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{b_{t}^{\prime}}{\cal L}_{t}(\bar{\xi}_{j}^{t})}{b_{t}^{\prime}}-L_{t})^{2}]={\mathbb{E}}[\tfrac{1}{b_{t}^{\prime}}({\cal L}_{t}(\bar{\xi}_{1}^{t})-L_{t})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\tfrac{1}{b_{t}^{\prime}}({\cal L}_{t}(\bar{\xi}_{1}^{t}))^{2}]\leq\tfrac{\bar{\cal L}^{2}}{b_{t}^{\prime}}.

(3.26)

Then by using Bernstein’s inequality and
|\bar{\cal L}_{t}-L_{t}|\leq\hat{\cal L}+L
almost surely, we have that for any
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\cal L}_{t}-L_{t}\geq\tfrac{(\hat{\cal L}+L)\lambda}{3b_{t}^{\prime}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{2\bar{\cal L}^{2}\lambda}{b_{t}^{\prime}}}\right)\leq\exp(-\lambda),

(3.27)

which indicates (
3.23
).

On the other hand, compared to Estimator I, Estimator II takes the maximum over
\bar{\cal L}_{0}
and the estimators generated from i.i.d. observations, making it more conservative. However, we find Estimator II critical to attain a unified complexity bound for the convex and nonconvex settings similar to (
3.15
). To achieve this goal, we first prove the following lemma, which characterizes the probability that Estimator II significantly underestimates the deterministic local Lipschitz constant.

Lemma 2

Let
\bar{\cal L}_{0}
satisfy
0<\bar{\cal L}_{0}\leq\hat{\cal L}
, and define
p(x,y):=\mathbb{P}\left(\max\{\bar{\cal L}_{0},{\cal L}_{x,y}(\xi)\}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}L_{x,y}\right)
for
x,y\in X
. We have

\displaystyle\beta:=\max_{x,y\in X}p(x,y)<1.

(3.28)

As a consequence, for Estimator II in (
3.20
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bar{\cal L}_{t}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}L_{t})\leq\beta^{b_{t}^{\prime}}.

(3.29)

Proof

For notational simplicity, we define
g(x,y,\xi):=\max\{\bar{\cal L}_{0},{\cal L}_{x,y}(\xi)\}-\tfrac{1}{2}L_{x,y}
. If
L_{x,y}>0
, we have

\displaystyle p(x,y)=\mathbb{P}(g(x,y,\xi)\leq 0)\leq\mathbb{P}({\cal L}_{x,y}(\xi)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}L_{x,y})\overset{(i)}{\leq}\mathbb{P}({\cal L}_{x,y}(\xi)<L_{x,y})\overset{(ii)}{<}1,

where step (i) follows from
L_{x,y}>0
and step (ii) follows from
{\mathbb{E}}[{\cal L}_{x,y}(\xi)]=L_{x,y}
. If
L_{x,y}\leq 0
, since
\bar{\cal L}_{0}>0
, we have
p(x,y)\leq\mathbb{P}(\bar{\cal L}_{0}\leq 0)=0
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle p(x,y)<1,~~\forall(x,y)\in X\times X.

(3.30)

On the other hand, due to the continuity of
f(x)
,
\nabla f(x)
,
{\cal F}(x,\xi)
, and
{\cal G}(x,\xi)
regarding
x
(for any
\xi
), it is not hard to see that, for any
\xi
,
(x,y)\rightarrow g(x,y,\xi)
is a continuous mapping on
X\times X
. Next, we show that
p(x,y)={\mathbb{E}}[\mathbb{I}\{g(x,y,\xi)\leq 0\}]
is upper semicontinuous. Let
(x_{n},y_{n})\rightarrow(x,y)
in
X\times X
. Then for any
\xi
, the continuity of
g
indicates
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g(x_{n},y_{n},\xi)=g(x,y,\xi)
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{I}\{g(x_{n},y_{n},\xi)\leq 0\}\leq\mathbb{I}\{g(x,y,\xi)\leq 0\}.

(3.31)

This inequality holds since: if
g(x,y,\xi)>0
, then for sufficiently large
n
, we have
g(x_{n},y_{n},\xi)>0
, so
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{I}\{g(x_{n},y_{n},\xi)\leq 0\}=0=\mathbb{I}\{g(x,y,\xi)\leq 0\}
; if
g(x,y,\xi)\leq 0
,
\mathbb{I}\{g(x,y,\xi)\leq 0\}=1
and the inequality is trivial. Then we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}p(x_{n},y_{n})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbb{I}\{g(x_{n},y_{n},\xi)\leq 0\}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{I}\{g(x_{n},y_{n},\xi)\leq 0\}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbb{I}\{g(x,y,\xi)\leq 0\}]=p(x,y),

where step (i) follows from Fatou’s lemma, and step (ii) follows from Ineq. (
3.31
). Therefore,
p(x,y)
is an upper semicontinuous function on
X\times X
. Invoking that
X\times X
is compact and
p(x,y)<1,~\forall(x,y)\in X\times X
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\beta:=\max_{x,y\in X}p(x,y)<1.

Finally, we show Ineq. (
3.29
). Specifically, if
\bar{\cal L}_{0}>\tfrac{L_{t}}{2}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\bar{\cal L}_{t}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}L_{t})=0
. If
\bar{\cal L}_{0}\leq\tfrac{L_{t}}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bar{\cal L}_{t}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}L_{t})=\prod_{j=1}^{b_{t}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left({\cal L}_{x_{t},x_{t-1}}(\bar{\xi}_{j}^{t})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}L_{t}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{b_{t}^{\prime}}p(x_{t},x_{t-1})\leq\beta^{b_{t}^{\prime}},

(3.32)

and we complete the proof of the lemma.

Through Lemma
2
, we show that Estimator II is robust in the sense that the probability of significantly underestimating the local Lipschitz constant
L_{t}
decays geometrically as the batch size
b_{t}^{\prime}
increases. Moreover, in many stochastic optimization problems, such as generalized linear regression,
1-\beta
is typically a constant bounded away from zero, so that we would not need a very large
b_{t}^{\prime}
to control the probability in (
3.29
).

Auto-conditioned stochastic projected gradient (AC-SPG) method

x_{0}\in X
,
\bar{\cal L}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{b_{t}}\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{b_{t}}{\cal G}(x,\xi_{j}^{t}).

The AC-SPG method
is presented in Algorithm
4
. In particular,

\{\xi_{1}^{t},...,\xi_{b_{t}}^{t}\}

), while the second batch is employed to

\bar{\cal L}_{t}
of the local smoothness constant.
For

t\in\mathbb{Z}
,
we define the filtration

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\sigma(\xi_{1}^{1},...,\xi_{b_{1}}^{1},\bar{\xi}_{1}^{1},...,\bar{\xi}_{b_{1}^{\prime}}^{1},......,\xi_{1}^{t},...,\xi_{b_{t}}^{t},\bar{\xi}_{t}^{t},...,\bar{\xi}_{b_{t}^{\prime}}^{t}),

Then clearly,

is
{\cal F}_{t}
-measurable, and
\gamma_{t}
is
{\cal F}_{t-1}
-measurable. Meanwhile, the batch size

{\cal F}_{t-1}
-measurable.

Similar to the deterministic setting, we can divide the iterates

,

\displaystyle\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{k}\}=\big\{\underbrace{x_{0}^{(1)},x_{1}^{(1)},...,x_{s_{1}}^{(1)}}_{\text{segment 1}},\underbrace{x_{1}^{(2)},...,x_{s_{2}}^{(2)}}_{\text{segment 2}},...,\underbrace{x_{1}^{(m)},...,x_{s_{m}}^{(m)}}_{\text{segment $m$}}\big\},

3.3
A two-phase auto-conditioned stochastic gradient method with high probability guarantees

In this subsection, we analyze the complexities of the AC-SPG algorithm in achieving an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-stationary solution, defined as
\mathbb{P}\left(\|g_{X,R}\|\leq\epsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta
.
In the previous subsection, we established the convergence guarantees for AC-SPG in expectation. These guarantees can be generalized to probability bounds using Markov’s inequality, resulting in polynomial dependences on
\delta
, e.g.,
\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon^{2}\delta}+\tfrac{1}{\epsilon^{4}\delta^{2}}\right)
. Notably, in the convex setting, this dependence on
\delta
can be improved to logarithmic factors under light-tail assumptions. However, in the nonconvex setting, it is generally unclear how to determine the output iterate in a single loop algorithm to achieve high-probability guarantees.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose a two-phase AC-SPG algorithm (2-AC-SPG), inspired by
Ghadimi and Lan (2013)
;
Ghadimi et al. (2016a)
. The algorithm consists of two phases: an optimization phase and a post-optimization phase.
In the optimization phase, we execute
{\cal R}
independent AC-SPG trials, generating a candidate solution for each trial. In the post-optimization phase, we select and output the solution with the smallest projected stochastic gradient (evaluated using new samples) among the
{\cal R}
candidate solutions.
Notably, the 2-AC-SPG algorithm offers flexibility: we can pause the optimization phase to perform the post-optimization phase and then resume the optimization phase, provided we retain the necessary information for each AC-SPG trial in memory. Furthermore, the optimization phase can be implemented efficiently in parallel computing environments to enhance scalability.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{r\in[{\cal R}]}\|g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{2Q_{k}}{M_{k}}\right)=\prod_{r=1}^{{\cal R}}\mathbb{P}(\|g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{2Q_{k}}{M_{k}})\leq\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{\cal R}.

(3.71)

Now let us bound
\max_{r\in[{\cal R}]}\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}
. For convenience, we denote
\delta_{\cal K}(x):=\bar{G}_{\cal K}(x)-\nabla f(x)
where
\bar{G}_{k}(x)
is defined in Algorithm
5
. Then by the property of the projection, we have
\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|\leq\|\delta_{\cal K}(\bar{x}_{r})\|
, thus

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\leq{\mathbb{E}}\|\delta_{\cal K}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}.

Then by Markov’s inequality, we have for any
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{\lambda\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)\leq\tfrac{{\mathbb{E}}[\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}]{\cal K}}{\lambda\sigma^{2}}\leq\tfrac{1}{\lambda}.

Using the uniform probability bound provides us with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{r\in[{\cal R}]}\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{\lambda\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)\leq\tfrac{{\cal R}}{\lambda}.

(3.72)

By combining (
3.70
), (
3.71
), and (
3.72
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|g_{X}(\bar{x})\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{8Q_{k}}{M_{k}}+\tfrac{6\lambda\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{r\in[{\cal R}]}\|g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{2Q_{k}}{M_{k}},\max_{r\in[{\cal R}]}\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{\lambda\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-(\tfrac{1}{2})^{{\cal R}}-\tfrac{{\cal R}}{\lambda},

(3.73)

which completes the proof.

Utilizing the convergence guarantees of 2-AC-SPG established in Theorem
3.3
, we now derive the iteration and sample complexity bounds for obtaining an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-stationary solution, i.e., a solution satisfying
\mathbb{P}\left(\|g_{X,R}\|\leq\epsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta
.

For simplicity of presentation, we focus on the case when
using Estimator II (
3.20
) with
b_{t}^{\prime}\geq 2\log_{1/\beta}(k+1)
and choosing
\alpha
in the order of
\mathcal{O}(\min\{\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon D_{X}},\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{lD_{X}^{2}}\})
,

and the complexity bounds for Estimator I (
3.19
) can be obtained in a similar manner.
By Theorem
3.3
,
at most
\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}D_{X}}{\epsilon}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}lD_{X}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\log\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}}\right)
iterations are required in each AC-SPG trial to ensure the term
\tfrac{8Q_{k}}{M_{k}}
to converge to the
\epsilon^{2}
-level. Consequently, the total iteration complexity is given by

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}D_{X}\log\delta^{-1}}{\epsilon}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}lD_{X}^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\log\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}}\cdot\log\delta^{-1}\right)}.

Next, we analyze the total sample complexity, which includes the sample observations used in both the optimization and post-optimization phases. In the post optimization phase, at most
\mathcal{O}(\tfrac{\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\delta\epsilon^{2}})
samples are required to reduce the term
\tfrac{12\lceil\log_{2}(2/{\delta})\rceil\sigma^{2}}{\delta{\cal K}}
to the
\epsilon^{2}
-level. For the optimization phase, the total number of samples used in each trial of AC-SPG is given by
\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}(b_{t}+b_{t}^{\prime})
, which can be upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left(b_{t}^{\prime}+\max\big\{1,\lceil(\tfrac{11I(t-1)}{8}+\tfrac{7}{8})\tfrac{\alpha}{\gamma_{t}}\rceil\big\}\right)}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\tfrac{k}{1-\beta}+\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{m}\tfrac{s_{i-1}\alpha}{\gamma_{t}}+\alpha M_{k})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\tfrac{k}{1-\beta}+\tfrac{k\alpha}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}}+\tfrac{\alpha Q_{k}}{\epsilon^{2}}).

(3.74)

As a result,
by setting
\alpha
in the order of
\mathcal{O}(\min\{\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\epsilon D_{X}},\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{lD_{X}^{2}}\})
,
the total sample complexity of 2-AC-SPG,
after hiding certain additive factors related to
\log\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
, can be bounded by

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}D_{X}\log\delta^{-1}}{(1-\beta)\epsilon}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}lD_{X}^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{(1-\beta)\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}D_{X}\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\epsilon^{3}}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}lD_{X}^{2}\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\epsilon^{4}}+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\delta\epsilon^{2}}\right).}

Clearly, this sample complexity improves
the dominating term by a factor of
\hat{\cal L}/\bar{\cal L}_{0}
compared with the expectation bound in (
3.65
).

Nevertheless, the sample complexity above still exhibits polynomial dependence on
\delta
. To reduce this dependence to a logarithmic factor, we introduce the light-tail assumption:

Assumption 4
(Light-tail assumption)

For any
x\in X
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\left[\exp\left(\tfrac{\|{\cal G}(x,\xi)-\nabla f(x)\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]\leq\exp(1).

(3.75)

Under this assumption, we introduce the following result (Lemma 4 in
Ghadimi et al. (2016a)
).

Lemma 3

Under Assumption
4
, for any
\lambda\geq 0
and any
{\cal K}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\delta_{\cal K}(x)\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{2(1+\lambda)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)\leq\exp(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{3}),

(3.76)

where
\delta_{\cal K}(x):=\bar{G}_{\cal K}(x)-\nabla f(x)
, with
\bar{G}_{k}(x)
defined in Algorithm
5
.

The following proposition describes the improved convergence results.

Proposition 1

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
4
hold and let
\{\bar{x}\}
be generated by Algorithm
5
. With probability at least
1-(\tfrac{1}{2})^{{\cal R}}-{\cal R}\exp(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{3})
, we have

\displaystyle\|g_{X}(\bar{x})\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{8Q_{k}}{M_{k}}+\tfrac{12(1+\lambda)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}},

(3.77)

where
M_{k}
and
Q_{k}
are defined in Theorem
3.3
.
Consequently, by setting
{\cal R}=\lceil\log_{2}(\tfrac{2}{\delta})\rceil
and
\lambda=\sqrt{3\log\tfrac{(2\lceil\log_{2}(\frac{2}{\delta})\rceil}{\delta}}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\|g_{X}(\bar{x})\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{8Q_{k}}{M_{k}}+12\left(1+\sqrt{3\log\tfrac{2\lceil\log_{2}(\frac{2}{\delta})\rceil}{\delta}}\right)^{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}.

(3.78)

Proof

First, note that Ineqs. (
3.70
) and (
3.71
) still hold. Meanwhile, by the property of projection and Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{2(1+\lambda)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\|\delta_{\cal K}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{2(1+\lambda)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)\leq\exp(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{3}).

Using the uniform probability bound, we then derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{r\in[{\cal R}]}\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{2(1+\lambda)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)\leq{\cal R}\exp(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{3}).

(3.79)

Combining the inequality above with (
3.70
) and (
3.71
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|g_{X}(\bar{x})\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{8Q_{k}}{M_{k}}+\tfrac{12(1+\lambda)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{r\in[{\cal R}]}\|g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{2Q_{k}}{M_{k}},\max_{r\in[{\cal R}]}\|\bar{g}_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})-g_{X}(\bar{x}_{r})\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{2(1+\lambda)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{{\cal K}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-(\tfrac{1}{2})^{{\cal R}}-{\cal R}\exp(-\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{3}).

(3.80)

This completes the proof.

In light of Proposition
1
, the polynomial dependence on
\delta
in Theorem
3.3
is improved to a logarithmic dependence. Specifically, in the post-optimization phase, ignoring log-log factors, at most
\mathcal{O}(\tfrac{\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\epsilon^{2}})
samples are needed to reduce the second term on the right-hand side of Ineq. (
3.78
) to the
\epsilon^{2}
-level. Consequently, 2-AC-SPG achieves an improved sample complexity of

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}D_{X}\log\delta^{-1}}{(1-\beta)\epsilon}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}lD_{X}^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{(1-\beta)\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}D_{X}\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\epsilon^{3}}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}lD_{X}^{2}\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\epsilon^{4}}+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}\log\delta^{-1}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

4
Variance reduction methods for nonconvex stochastic optimization

This section leverages variance reduction techniques to further speed up the convergence rate of the projected gradient method under Assumption
3
. In Subsection
4.1
, we introduce a variance-reduced stochastic projected gradient (VR-SPG) method and present a new complexity result that unifies both convex and nonconvex cases for the first time in the literature. In Subsection
4.2
, we propose an “auto-conditioned” step-size policy for VR-SPG and provide improved convergence guarantees compared to the AC-SPG method discussed in Subsection
3.2
.

4.1
Variance-reduced stochastic projected gradient method

In Algorithm
6
, we present a variance-reduced stochastic projected gradient (VR-SPG) method. VR-SPG computes a batched gradient estimator
\tilde{G}_{t}
, using a sample size
N
at every
T
iterations. During the intermediate iterations, the gradient estimator
\tilde{G}_{t}
will be updated in a recursive manner based on
\tilde{G}_{t-1}
, using a mini-batch of size
b_{t}
.

Algorithm 6

Variance-reduced stochastic projected gradient (VR-SPG) method

Input
: initial point
x_{0}\in X
,
step size parameter
\{\gamma_{t}\}
, batch size parameters
\{b_{t}\}
and
N
, epoch length parameter
T
,
and epoch index
s=0
.

for

t=1,\cdots,k

do

if

\mathrm{mod}(t,T)=1

then

Set
s\leftarrow s+1
.

Generate i.i.d. observations
\xi^{t}_{1},...,\xi^{t}_{N}

and compute

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{t}=\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\cal G}(x_{t-1},\xi^{t}_{i}).

(4.1)

else

Generate i.i.d. observations
\xi_{1}^{t},...,\xi_{b_{t}}^{t}
and compute

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{t}=\tfrac{1}{b_{t}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{b_{t}}[{\cal G}(x_{t-1},\xi_{i}^{t})-{\cal G}(x_{t-2},\xi_{i}^{t})]+\tilde{G}_{t-1}.

(4.2)

end

if

Compute

\displaystyle x_{t}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{\langle\tilde{G}_{t},x\rangle+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}\right\}.

(4.3)

end

for

We define the projected stochastic gradient at the iterate
x_{t}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{X,t}:=P_{X}(x_{t},\tilde{G}_{t+1},\gamma_{t+1})=\gamma_{t+1}(x_{t}-x_{t+1}),

(4.4)

and recall the corresponding projected gradient defined in (
3.6
).
In the following discussion, we use two equivalent indexing conventions interchangeably:

\displaystyle x_{t}=x_{s,u},\quad\text{where $t=(s-1)T+u$.}

(4.5)

Here
s
denotes the epoch index as defined in Algorithm

u
represents the index within the epoch. Additionally, We have
x_{s,0}=x_{s-1,T}

\tilde{G}_{t}

Lemma 4

If Assumptions
1
-
3
hold, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\tilde{\delta}_{s,u}\|^{2}\leq\textstyle\sum_{j=2}^{u}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tfrac{\bar{\cal L}^{2}}{b_{s,j}}\|x_{s,j-1}-x_{s,j-2}\|^{2}\right]+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{N},

where
\bar{\cal L}
is defined in Assumption
3
.

Proof

When
u=1
the result follows directly. For
u\geq 2
, we can bound

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\tilde{\delta}_{s,u}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\|\tfrac{1}{b_{s,u}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{b_{s,u}}[{\cal G}(x_{s,u-1},\xi_{i}^{s,u})-{\cal G}(x_{s,u-2},\xi_{i}^{s,u})]-\nabla f(x_{s,u-1})+\nabla f(x_{s,u-2})+\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\|\tfrac{1}{b_{s,u}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{b_{s,u}}[{\cal G}(x_{s,u-1},\xi_{i}^{s,u})-{\cal G}(x_{s,u-2},\xi_{i}^{s,u})]-\nabla f(x_{s,u-1})+\nabla f(x_{s,u-2})\|^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\|\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\tfrac{1}{b^{2}_{s,u}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{b_{s,u}}\|{\cal G}(x_{s,u-1},\xi_{i}^{s,u})-{\cal G}(x_{s,u-2},\xi_{i}^{s,u})-\nabla f(x_{s,u-1})+\nabla f(x_{s,u-2})\|^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\|\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tfrac{1}{b^{2}_{s,u}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{b_{s,u}}\|{\cal G}(x_{s,u-1},\xi_{i}^{s,u})-{\cal G}(x_{s,u-2},\xi_{i}^{s,u})\|^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\|\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tfrac{1}{b^{2}_{s,u}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{b_{s,u}}{\cal L}^{2}(\xi_{i}^{s,u})\|x_{s,u-1}-x_{s,u-2}\|^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\|\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tfrac{\bar{\cal L}^{2}}{b_{s,u}}\|x_{s,u-1}-x_{s,u-2}\|^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\|\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1}\|^{2},

where the last two inequalities follow from Assumption
3
. By utilizing the above inequality recursively, we obtain the desired result.

Lemma 5

If Assumptions
1
-
2
hold, for any
u\geq 1
and any sequence of positive real numbers
\{\beta_{s,j}\}_{j=1}^{u-1}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\tilde{\delta}_{s,u},x^{*}-x_{s,u-1}\rangle]\leq\textstyle\sum_{j=2}^{u}{\mathbb{E}}[\tfrac{1}{2\beta_{s,j-1}}\|x_{s,j-1}-x_{s,j-2}\|^{2}]+\textstyle\sum_{j=2}^{u}{\mathbb{E}}[\tfrac{\beta_{s,j-1}}{2}\|\tilde{\delta}_{s,j-1}\|^{2}].

Proof

It is not difficult to see that when
u=1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\tilde{\delta}_{s,1},x^{*}-x_{s,0}\rangle]=0.

(4.6)

For
u\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\tilde{\delta}_{s,u},x^{*}-x_{s,u-1}\rangle]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\langle\nabla f(x_{s,u-1})-\nabla f(x_{s,u-2})+\tilde{G}_{s,u-1}-\nabla f(x_{s,u-1}),x^{*}-x_{s,u-1}\rangle]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\langle\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1},x_{s,u-2}-x_{s,u-1}\rangle]+{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1},x^{*}-x_{s,u-2}\rangle]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}{\mathbb{E}}[\tfrac{1}{2\beta_{s,u-1}}\|x_{s,u-1}-x_{s,u-2}\|^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}[\tfrac{\beta_{s,u-1}}{2}\|\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1}\|^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\tilde{\delta}_{s,u-1},x^{*}-x_{s,u-2}\rangle],

where step (i) follows from Young’s inequality.
By applying the above relationship recursively and using the fact (
4.6
), we obtain the desired result.

We are now ready to establish the main convergence results of VR-SPG.

Theorem 4.1

Suppose that Assumptions
1
-
3

\{x_{t}\}

6

k\geq T

\gamma_{t}=\gamma\geq 4\bar{\cal L}

\displaystyle b_{s,u}:=\begin{cases}\tfrac{T^{2}}{u-1},&\text{for $s=1$}\\
\tfrac{13T}{2},&\text{for $s\geq 2$}.\end{cases}

(4.7)

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tfrac{(t-1)\gamma-tL}{2}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}+\tfrac{t}{8\gamma}\|g_{X,t-1}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\tfrac{3}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\sum_{u=1}^{U}\left(\textstyle\sum_{j=2}^{u}\tfrac{\tilde{\cal L}^{2}_{s,j-1}}{\gamma_{s,j-1}b_{s,j}}\|x_{s,j-1}-x_{s,j-2}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma_{s,1}N}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{3}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\sum_{u=1}^{U-1}\tfrac{(U-u)\tilde{\cal L}^{2}_{s,u}}{\gamma_{s,u}b_{s,u+1}}\|x_{s,u}-x_{s,u-1}\|^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tfrac{3U\sigma^{2}}{2\gamma_{s,1}N}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}\tfrac{3}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\sum_{u=1}^{U-1}\tfrac{\tilde{\cal L}^{2}_{s,u}}{\gamma_{s,u}}\|x_{s,u}-x_{s,u-1}\|^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tfrac{3U\sigma^{2}}{2\gamma_{s,1}N}\right],

where step (i) follows from Lemma
6
, and step (ii) uses from
b_{s,u}=T
. Summing up over all epochs yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{3\|\tilde{\delta}_{t}\|^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{3\tilde{\cal L}_{t}^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\right]+\tfrac{3k\sigma^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}N}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{3\tilde{\cal L}_{t}^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\right]+\tfrac{3k\sigma^{2}}{8\bar{\cal L}_{0}N},

where the last step follows from
\gamma_{t}\geq\gamma_{1}=4\bar{\cal L}_{0}
.
Let us define an indicator function
{\cal I}
such that

\displaystyle{\cal I}(t):=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $\hat{\cal L}_{t-1}\geq\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\tilde{\cal L}_{t}$}\\
1,&\text{if $\hat{\cal L}_{t-1}<\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\tilde{\cal L}_{t}$}.\end{cases}

Note that
\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}{\cal I}(t)\leq\lfloor\log_{\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}}\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}}\rfloor+1
. Using
\gamma_{t}=4\hat{\cal L}_{t-1}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{3\|\tilde{\delta}_{t}\|^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sum_{1\leq t\leq k,{\cal I}(t)=0}\tfrac{2\hat{\cal L}_{t-1}^{2}}{\gamma_{t}}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\bigg]+{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sum_{1\leq t\leq k,{\cal I}(t)=1}\tfrac{3\tilde{\cal L}_{t}^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\bigg]+\tfrac{3k\sigma^{2}}{8\bar{\cal L}_{0}N}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sum_{1\leq t\leq k,{\cal I}(t)=0}\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{8}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\bigg]+{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sum_{1\leq t\leq k,{\cal I}(t)=1}\tfrac{3\tilde{\cal L}_{t}\hat{\cal L}}{8\bar{\cal L}_{0}}D_{X}^{2}\bigg]+\tfrac{3k\sigma^{2}}{8\bar{\cal L}_{0}N}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sum_{1\leq t\leq k,{\cal I}(t)=0}\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{8}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\bigg]+\tfrac{3\hat{\cal L}^{2}D_{X}^{2}}{(8-4\sqrt{3})\bar{\cal L}_{0}}+\tfrac{3k\sigma^{2}}{8\bar{\cal L}_{0}N}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sum_{1\leq t\leq k,{\cal I}(t)=0}\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{4}\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\bigg]+\tfrac{3\hat{\cal L}^{2}D_{X}^{2}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}}+\tfrac{3k\sigma^{2}}{8\bar{\cal L}_{0}N},

(4.39)

where the step (i) follows from that for two consecutive
{\cal I}(t)=1
points, denoted as
t_{1}<t_{2}
, we have
\tilde{\cal L}_{t_{2}}>\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}{\cal L}_{t_{1}}
. Finally, by combining Ineqs. (
4.38
) and (
4.39
), and invoking the output rule (
4.31
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{1}{4\gamma_{t}}\|g_{X,R(k)}\|^{2}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{1}{4\gamma_{t}}\|g_{X,t-1}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{0})-f(x^{*})+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}D_{X}^{2}}{2}+\tfrac{3\hat{\cal L}^{2}D_{X}^{2}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}}+\tfrac{3k\sigma^{2}}{8\bar{\cal L}_{0}N}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{0})-f(x^{*})+\tfrac{7\hat{\cal L}^{2}D_{X}^{2}}{2\bar{\cal L}_{0}}+\tfrac{3k\sigma^{2}}{8\bar{\cal L}_{0}N},

(4.40)

which completes the proof.

We now add a few remarks about the complexity bounds of AC-VR-SPG. For simplicity, let us assume
\nabla f(x^{*})=0
, so that
f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})=f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})-\langle\nabla f(x^{*}),x_{t}-x^{*}\rangle\leq\tfrac{LD_{X}^{2}}{2}
. In order to find an
\epsilon
-stationary point, we need to set
N
on the order of
\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}\sigma^{2}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}\epsilon^{2}}
. This requires estimating the quantity
\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}\sigma^{2}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}}
, meaning the method is not fully parameter-free. However, in practical scenarios, we could obtain rough empirical estimations of
\sigma^{2}
and
\hat{\cal L}
by running AC-VR-SPG for a few iterations. Additionally, we set
T=\sqrt{N}
. The total number of iterations required by AC-VR-SPG to find an
\epsilon
-approximate solution is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}^{3}D_{X}^{2}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}\epsilon^{2}}\right).

Using the batch size parameters
N
and
b_{t}
defined in Theorem
4.2
, the corresponding sample complexity is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}^{3}D_{X}^{2}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{\hat{\cal L}^{3.5}\sigma D_{X}^{2}}{\bar{\cal L}_{0}^{1.5}\epsilon^{3}}\right).

It should be noted that, due to the adaptive selection of stepsizes, the resulting complexity bounds do not depend explicitly on
l
.
Nevertheless, in terms of the dependence on
\epsilon
, this complexity still improves the sample complexity of AC-SPG by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
. Moreover, the same two-stage procedure outlined in Section
3.3
can be applied to AC-VR-SPG to obtain high probability guarantees. For the sake of brevity, we omit these details to avoid redundancy.

6
Concluding remarks

In summary, we present a novel class of projected gradient (PG) methods for nonconvex optimization over a convex compact set.
We first provide a novel analysis of the
constant-stepsize
PG method, which achieves the best-known iteration complexity, unifying convex and nonconvex optimization. Then we propose the parameter-free and line search-free AC-PG method, which attains the same iteration complexity up to an additive constant. Next, we generalize the PG methods to the stochastic setting by introducing a stochastic projected gradient (SPG) method with new unified complexity bounds. We also provide auto-conditioned stepsize policies for SPG to deal with unknown Lipschitz parameters. Finally, we propose the variance-reduced stochastic gradient (VR-SPG) method under the assumption of Lipschitz continuity of the stochastic gradient. VR-SPG achieves the best-known sample complexity, which is nearly order-optimal in both convex and nonconvex settings. We also develop the auto-conditioned variant of VR-SPG. Our theoretical guarantees are corroborated by numerical experiments.

Appendix

A.1
Extension to the composite optimization setting

In this appendix, we extend our unified analysis for PG, i.e., Theorem
2.1
, to the composite optimization setting. Specifically, we consider

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}:=\min_{x\in X}\{\Psi(x):=f(x)+h(x)\},

(A.1)

where
f
is a smooth function as defined in the main parts of the paper and
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a “prox-friendly” closed convex function such that the “proximal-mapping” subproblem

\displaystyle x^{+}=\arg\min_{u\in X}\left\{\langle g,u\rangle+h(x)+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x-u\|^{2}\right\}

admits a closed-form solution or is easily computable by some efficient computational procedure. We assume
\bar{X}:=\{x\in X|h(x)<+\infty\}
is a closed convex set. Additionally, we assume
x_{0}\in\bar{X}
and
D_{\bar{X},x_{0}}:=\max_{x,y\in\mathcal{E}_{\bar{X}}(x_{0})}\|x-y\|<\infty
, where
\mathcal{E}_{\bar{X}}(x_{0}):=\{x\in\bar{X}|\Psi(x)\leq\Psi(x_{0})\}
with a slight abuse of notation.
To iteratively solve problem (
A.1
), we modify step (
2.1
) in Algorithm
1
to

\displaystyle x_{t}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x\rangle+h(x)+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}\right\}.

(A.2)

We define
P_{\bar{X}}(x,g,\gamma):=\gamma(x-x^{+})
and
g_{\bar{X},t}\equiv P_{\bar{X}}(x_{t},\nabla f(x_{t}),\gamma_{t+1}):=\gamma_{t+1}(x_{t}-x_{t+1})
. The convergence guarantees for this proximal version of the PG method are characterized in the following theorem.

Theorem A.1

Let
\{x_{t}\}
be generated by the proximal gradient method with updates (
A.2
). If the stepsizes satisfy
\gamma_{t}=\gamma\geq L,\forall\,t
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq t\leq k-1}\|g_{\bar{X},t}\|^{2}\leq\left(\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\right)^{-1}\left(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\tfrac{lk}{2}D^{2}_{\bar{X},x_{0}}\right).

(A.3)

Specifically, if
\gamma_{t}=L,\forall\,t
, then

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq t\leq k-1}\|g_{\bar{X},t}\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{2L^{2}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}}{k(k-1)}+\tfrac{2LlD^{2}_{\bar{X},x_{0}}}{k-1}.

(A.4)

Proof

By the optimality condition of (
A.2
), we have the following three-point inequality (e.g., Lemma 3.5 in
Lan (2020)
): for any
x\in X
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x\rangle+h(x_{t})+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}\leq h(x)+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

Adding
f(x_{t-1})
on both sides of the above inequality and rearranging the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x_{t}-x_{t-1}\rangle+h(x_{t})+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x-x_{t-1}\rangle+h(x)+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

(A.5)

Then, by applying the upper curvature condition (
1.3
) on the LHS of the above inequality and invoking the definition
\Psi(x)=f(x)+h(x)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Psi(x_{t})+(\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x-x_{t-1}\rangle-f(x)+\Psi(x)+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x\|^{2}.

(A.6)

Setting
x=x_{t-1}
in the above inequality generates

\displaystyle(\gamma_{t}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|^{2}\leq\Psi(x_{t-1})-\Psi(x_{t}).

(A.7)

Clearly, this inequality indicates that the function value
\Psi
is non-increasing over iterations as we choose
\gamma_{t}\geq L/2
. Thus we have
\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},x_{t}\in\mathcal{E}_{\bar{X}}(x_{0})
, and consequently,
\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq D_{\bar{X},x_{0}}^{2}
. Meanwhile, setting
x=x^{*}
in Ineq. (
A.6
) and using the weakly convex condition
f(x_{t-1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{t-1}),x^{*}-x_{t-1}\rangle-f(x^{*})\leq l\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}/2
yields

\displaystyle\Psi(x_{t})+(\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq\Psi(x^{*})+\tfrac{l}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}.

(A.8)

By taking a telescope sum of the above inequality from
t=1
to
k
, and applying
\gamma_{t}=\gamma
, we obtain

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[\Psi(x_{t})-\Psi(x^{*})+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tfrac{l}{2}\|x_{t-1}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\tfrac{lk}{2}D^{2}_{\bar{X},x_{0}}.

(A.9)

On the other hand, we can lower bound the LHS of the above inequality by

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[\Psi(x_{t})-\Psi(x^{*})+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[\Psi(x_{t})-\Psi(x_{k})+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[\textstyle\sum_{j=t}^{k-1}\big(\Psi(x_{j})-\Psi(x_{j+1})\big)+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\geq}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left[\textstyle\sum_{j=t}^{k-1}(\gamma-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{j}-x_{j+1}\|^{2}+(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac{L}{2})\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big((t-\tfrac{1}{2})\gamma-\tfrac{t}{2}L\big)\|x_{t-1}-x_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\|g_{\bar{X},t-1}\|^{2},

(A.10)

where step
(i)
follows from (
A.7
).
By combining Ineqs. (
A.9
) and (
A.10
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq t\leq k-1}\|g_{X,t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\right)^{-1}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\|g_{\bar{X},t-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big(\tfrac{2t-1}{2\gamma}-\tfrac{tL}{2\gamma^{2}}\big)\right)^{-1}\left(\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\tfrac{lk}{2}D^{2}_{\bar{X},x_{0}}\right),

which completes the proof.

It should be noted that, if
\bar{X}:=\{x\in X|h(x)<+\infty\}
is a convex compact set, all other results proposed in this paper can be generalized to the composite optimization setting in a similar manner.
