Introduction

A fundamental setting for mathematics in numerous fields is
the Lebesgue space
L^{p}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty

(with important special cases
p=1,2
, or
\infty
).
However, some problems require the examination of
non-homogeneous functionals which do not neatly slot into any
Lebesgue space. One example from statistical physics is
the entropy
E
of a probability density function
f
given by

E(f)=-\int f(\xi)\log f(\xi)\,d\xi,

which is not well-defined for all
f\in L^{1}
.
To solve this problem, one turns to the spaces
introduced by W. Orlicz in
[
10
]
, which
we now call Orlicz spaces. These are denoted by
L^{\Phi}
,
where
\Phi
is a Young function, a certain type of convex function.
The special case
\Phi(t)=t^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, gives

L^{\Phi}=L^{p}
. By
[
6
]
, one can avoid
the continuity problem for
E
above by replacing
the classical statistical framework
\langle L^{\infty},L^{1}\rangle
with
\langle L^{\Psi},L^{\Phi}\rangle
, where

\Phi(t)=t\log(1+t)
and
\Psi(t)=\cosh t-1
.

Since the Orlicz space
L^{\Phi}
is completely determined by
the Young function
\Phi
, it is clear that
the analysis of Young functions and their properties is essential.
This analysis is mainly done via certain indices,
which provide an estimate of the growth rate of
a Young function
\Phi
. We consider quantities such as
the limits at the origin and infinity of

\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}
and
\frac{\ln\Phi(t)}{\ln t}
.
Such indices, and many more,
appear in
[
7
,
8
]
.

Much of the analysis of this paper revolves around
the so-called Lebesgue exponents
p_{\Phi}
and
q_{\Phi}
,
which appeared in
[
12
]
, and have subsequently been analyzed in, e.g.,
[
3
]
. We will mainly focus on
the case that
p_{\Phi}<\infty
,
which is equivalent to

\Phi
fulfilling the (global)
\Delta_{2}
-condition
(see
Remark

2.4
).

In this paper, we examine the Lebesgue exponents closely and
determine their most glaring limitations.
Namely, we show how equivalent Young functions can be constructed
with widely differing Lebesgue exponents.
We also present some alternative methods of obtaining
growth estimates of Young functions, and by extension,
obtain inclusion properties for Orlicz spaces.
Furthermore, we investigate mixed Orlicz spaces,
which are useful in interpolation theory.
Particularly, we investigate the
trace type Orlicz spaces
L^{\Phi,\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
,
where the same Young function
\Phi
is used for
the two
d
-dimensional variables, but separately,
as opposed to
L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d}).

In the case of Orlicz modulation spaces,
as was shown in
[
13
, Corollary 2.5]
,
we have
M^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=M^{\Phi,\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
meaning that
\|V_{\varphi}\cdot\|_{L^{\Phi}}

is equivalent to

\|V_{\varphi}\cdot\|_{L^{\Phi,\Phi}}
,
where
V_{\varphi}
denotes the short-time Fourier transform
with window function
\varphi
.
This equivalence does not hold in general.

Inclusions between the spaces
L^{\Phi,\Phi}
and
L^{\Phi}

are shown to be linked to inequalities between
the quantities

\rho_{\Phi}(f)=\int\Phi(|f(x)|)\,dx

and
\Phi(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}})
.
We find that for
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
,

L^{\Phi,\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi}

if and only if

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\Phi(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}})

for some constant
C>0
, which in turn is equivalent to

\Phi
being submultiplicative. The reversed inclusion is equivalent to the reverse inequality holding, which in turn is equivalent to
\Phi
being supermultiplicative. Evidently,
L^{\Phi}=L^{\Phi,\Phi}
therefore holds if and only if

C^{-1}\Phi(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}})\leq\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\Phi(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}})

for some constant
C>0
, which by
[
4
]
is also equivalent to
L^{\Phi}=L^{p}
for some
p\geq 1
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

1
, we introduce necessary notations and definitions. The aforementioned Lebesgue exponents are
examined in
Section

2
.
Their limitations are described in
Section

2.1
,
where it is found that if
p_{\Phi}<p<\infty
and
1<q<q_{\Phi}
,
then there exists an equivalent Young function
\Psi

with
p<p_{\Psi}<\infty
and
1<q_{\Psi}<q
(
Proposition

2.12
).
In
Section

2.2
and
Section

2.3
,
we rectify these problems in different ways by focusing on shared properties for the equivalence class and ultimately obtain better growth estimates for
\Phi
(
Proposition

2.20
and
Theorem

2.24
).

The relation between spaces
L^{\Phi}
and
L^{\Phi,\Phi}

is explored in
Section

3
. Here,
Section

3.1

is focused on the Young function modular

\rho_{\Phi}(f)=\int\Phi(|f(x)|)\,dx
, and

Section

3.2
contains sufficient and necessary conditions
on
\Phi
,
\Phi_{1}
, and
\Phi_{2}
for inclusions between
L^{\Phi}
and

to hold. In particular, such results are stated in the case that

(

and

).

1.
Preliminaries

By
A\subseteq B
, we mean that
A
is a (not necessarily proper) subset of
B
, and we will write
A\subsetneq B
to specify that
A
is a proper subset of
B
. We will use
d
exclusively to denote dimension, and we will deal only with functions whose domains are

\mathbb{R}_{+}=\{\,t\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,t\geq 0\,\},

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, or
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, let
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=L^{p}
denote the usual Lebesgue space with norm

\|f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\equiv\|f\|_{L^{p}}\equiv\|f\|_{p}\equiv\begin{cases}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(x)|^{p},dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&p<\infty,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle{\esssup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}|f(x)|,&p=\infty,\end{cases}

where
{f}:{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
is a Lebesgue measurable function. The norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}}
imposes certain decay conditions on the functions in
L^{p}
which depend on the parameter
p
. Using different such conditions with respect to different variables, we arrive at the definition of mixed norm Lebesgue spaces, which we recall below.

Definition 1.1
.

The
mixed Lebesgue space

L^{p,q}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\right)
consists of all Lebesgue measurable functions
{f}:{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\|f\|_{L^{p,q}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})}\equiv\|f\|_{L^{p,q}}\equiv\|f\|_{p,q}\equiv\|f_{2}\|_{q}

is finite, where

f_{2}(y)=\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{p}.

Note that if
p=q
in the definition above, then
L^{p,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})=L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
.

We recall the following definition of Young function.

Definition 1.2
.

A function
{\Phi}:{[0,\infty)}\rightarrow{[0,\infty]}
is called a
Young function
if

(i)

\Phi
is convex,

(ii)

\Phi(0)=0
,

(iii)

\Phi(t)<\infty
for some
t>0
,

(iv)

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(t)=+\infty
.

A Young function does not have to be continuous by this definition. In fact, we do not exclude
\Phi
with infinite function values, hence the function

\Phi(t)=\begin{cases}0&0\leq t<1,\\
t-1&1\leq t<2,\\
\infty&t\geq 2\end{cases}

is a Young function. However, the only possible discontinuities for a Young function
\Psi
appear at points
t_{0}>0
such that
\Psi(t)=\infty
for all
t>t_{0}
, since otherwise
\Psi
would not be convex. Evidently, this means that
\Psi
can have at most one jump.

The reason we require
(iii)
in the definition above is so that each Young function has a well-defined complementary Young function, given by

\Psi(t)=\sup_{s\geq 0}\left(st-\Phi(s)\right).

Using the notations of
[
3
, Section 1]
, we recall the definition of Lebesgue exponent as follows.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\Phi
be a Young function and let
\Omega=\{\,t>0\,:\,0<\Phi(t)<\infty\,\}
. Then the
Lebesgue exponents

p_{\Phi}
and
q_{\Phi}
are given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle p_{\Phi}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{cases}{\displaystyle{\sup_{t\in\Omega}\left(\frac{t\Phi_{+}^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}\right)}},&\Omega=(0,\infty),\\[4.30554pt]
\infty,&\Omega\neq(0,\infty),\end{cases}

and

(1.2)

\displaystyle q_{\Phi}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{cases}{\displaystyle{\inf_{t\in\Omega}\left(\frac{t\Phi_{+}^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}\right)}},&\Omega\neq\emptyset,\\
\infty,&\Omega=\emptyset.\end{cases}

Since, for any
a>0
,
\Phi^{\prime}(a)
is the slope of the tangent to
\Phi(t)
in the point
t=a
(or the slope of the right tangent if
\Phi
is not differentiable in
a
), and
\Phi(a)/a
is the slope of the line segment between the origin and
(a,\Phi(a))
, it is clear that
a\Phi^{\prime}(a)/\Phi(a)\geq 1
for all
a>0
by the convexity of
\Phi
. Hence, for all Lebesgue exponents we must have
1\leq q_{\Phi}\leq p_{\Phi}\leq\infty
. An important special case is when
q_{\Phi}=p_{\Phi}=p
, as then
\Phi(t)=Ct^{p}
for some constant
C>0
, and
L^{\Phi}=L^{p}
.

The Lebesgue exponents may be found in
[
12
]
, which is their first appearance according to
[
7
]
. These indices and similar ones of note for the study of Orlicz spaces can be found in
[
7
, p. 20]
.

We will often require that a Young function
\Phi
fulfills
p_{\Phi}<\infty
. Since this is equivalent to
\Phi
fulfilling the
\Delta_{2}
-condition (see
Remark

2.4
), we write
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
whenever
\Phi
is a Young function with
p_{\Phi}<\infty
. If
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
then
\Phi
fulfills
0<\Phi(t)<\infty
for all
t>0
, but this condition is not sufficient to guarantee that
p_{\Phi}<\infty
. For example, if
\Phi(t)=e^{t}-1
, then
\Phi
is a Young function with
0<\Phi(t)<\infty
for all
t>0
, but
p_{\Phi}=\infty
. We will also consider the “nice” Young functions of
[
11
]
, the so-called
N functions
. Recall that a Young function
\Phi
is an N function if

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}=\infty.

Remark 1.4
.

Young functions are not necessarily differentiable, but being convex, they are still semi-differentiable. Since the definition above, as well as all computations and results involving derivatives of Young functions in this paper, are the same whether one uses the left derivative or the right derivative, we will simply perform these calculations with the right derivative, and let
\Phi^{\prime}\equiv\Phi^{\prime}_{+}
unless otherwise stated.

Remark 1.5
.

As the Lebesgue exponents will be central to our discussions in Section
2
, we will mention some basic properties, (cf.
[
3
]
). Observe that if
p_{\Phi}<\infty
, there are constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

C_{1}t^{p_{\Phi}}\leq\Phi(t)\leq C_{2}t^{q_{\Phi}}

for
t\leq 1
, and

C_{1}t^{q_{\Phi}}\leq\Phi(t)\leq C_{2}t^{p_{\Phi}}

for
t\geq 1
. It follows that if
q_{\Phi}=p_{\Phi}
, then

\Phi(t)=Ct^{p_{\Phi}}

for some constant
C>0
. Moreover, if

\Phi_{1}(t)=\begin{cases}C_{1}t^{p_{\Phi}}&t\leq 1\\
C_{1}t^{q_{\Phi}}&t>1,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{2}(t)=\begin{cases}C_{2}t^{q_{\Phi}}&t\leq 1\\
C_{2}t^{p_{\Phi}}&t>1,\end{cases}

then
L^{\Phi_{1}}=L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}}
,
L^{\Phi_{2}}=L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}}
,
and there exist constants
C_{3},C_{4}>0
such that

\Phi_{1}(t)\leq C_{3}\Phi(t)\leq C_{4}\Phi_{2}(t),\quad t\geq 0,

hence

(1.3)

L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}}\subseteq L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}}.

To simplify notations, we will let

\rho_{\Phi}(f)=\|\Phi(|f|)\|_{L^{1}}
, and we will refer to
\rho_{\Phi}
as
the
\Phi
modular
.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
\Phi
be a Young function.
The
Orlicz space

L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
consists
of all Lebesgue measurable functions

{f}:{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\equiv\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}}\equiv\|f\|_{\Phi}\equiv\inf\left\{\,\lambda>0\,:\,\rho_{\Phi}\Big(\frac{f}{\lambda}\Big)\leqslant 1\,\right\}

is finite.

In
Section

2
, we will need to discuss Young functions which are equivalent in the sense that they generate the same Orlicz space. For this reason, we recall the following definition.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
f
and
g
be non-negative functions defined on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then
f
and
g
are said the be
equivalent
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

C^{-1}g(t)\leq f(t)\leq Cg(t),\quad t\geq 0.

In particular, this defines an equivalence relation that partitions the set of Young functions into equivalence classes.
Note that if two Young functions
\Phi
and
\Psi
are equivalent, then so too are their norms, and therefore
L^{\Phi}=L^{\Psi}
. Hence an equivalence class of Young functions, which we will denote
[\Phi]
, is the set of all Young functions
\Psi
for which
L^{\Psi}=L^{\Phi}
.

Of particular note in
Section

3
are the mixed Orlicz spaces, whose definition we recall as follows.

Definition 1.8
.

Let
\Phi,\Psi
be Young functions. The
mixed Orlicz space

L^{\Phi,\Psi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
consists of all Lebesgue measurable functions
{f}:{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\|f\|_{L^{\Phi,\Psi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})}\equiv\|f\|_{L^{\Phi,\Psi}}\equiv\|f\|_{\Phi,\Psi}\equiv\big\|f_{2}\big\|_{L^{\Psi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

is finite, where

f_{2}(y)=\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}.

In this paper, we will focus on the case that
\Phi=\Psi
in the definition above, and refer to these as
trace type Orlicz spaces
. For
\Phi(t)=t^{p}
, it is clear that
L^{\Phi,\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})=L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
.

Lastly, we will recall the following definition.

Definition 1.9
.

A function
{\Phi}:{[0,\infty)}\rightarrow{[0,\infty]}
is said to be
submultiplicative
, if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\Phi(ab)\leq C\Phi(a)\Phi(b),\quad a,b>0.

Similarly,
\Phi
is said to be
supermultiplicative
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\Phi(ab)\geq C\Phi(a)\Phi(b),\quad a,b>0.

Example 1.10
.

(i)

If
\Phi(t)=t+t^{2}
, then
\Phi
is a submultiplicative Young function, since

\Phi(ab)=ab+(ab)^{2}\leq ab+ab^{2}+a^{2}b+(ab)^{2}=\Phi(a)\Phi(b)

for every
a,b\geq 0
.

(ii)

If
\Phi(t)=\begin{cases}t^{3}&t<1,\\
t&t\geq 1,\end{cases}

then
\Phi
is a supermultiplicative Young function.

(iii)

For any
p\geq 1
and
C>0
,
\Phi(t)=Ct^{p}
is a Young function which is both sub- and supermultiplicative. In fact, in this case
\Phi(ab)=\frac{1}{C}\Phi(a)\Phi(b)
for every
ab\geq 0
. By
[
11
, Sec. 2.3, Prop. 12]
, up to equivalence,
\Phi(t)=t^{p}
,
1<p<\infty
are the only N functions which are both sub- and supermultiplicative.

2.
Orlicz spaces and Lebesgue exponents

2.1.
Lebesgue exponents

We begin by stating some observations about the properties of Young functions and some connections to their Lebesgue exponents
p_{\Phi}
and
q_{\Phi}
. The following is a consequence of
[
7
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
,
c\in[0,1]
, and
C\in[1,\infty)
. Then

(2.1)

c^{p_{\Phi}}\Phi(t)\leq\Phi(ct)\leq c^{q_{\Phi}}\Phi(t)

and

(2.2)

C^{q_{\Phi}}\Phi(t)\leq\Phi(Ct)\leq C^{p_{\Phi}}\Phi(t).

Proof.

For any
t>0
, by definition,

q_{\Phi}\leq\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}\leq p_{\Phi},

or equivalently (since
\Phi(t)>0
),

t\Phi^{\prime}(t)-p_{\Phi}\Phi(t)\leq 0\leq t\Phi^{\prime}(t)-q_{\Phi}\Phi(t).

This implies that

\frac{t^{p_{\Phi}}\Phi(t)-p_{\Phi}t^{p_{\Phi}-1}\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{t^{2p_{\Phi}}}\leq 0\leq\frac{t^{q_{\Phi}}\Phi(t)-q_{\Phi}t^{q_{\Phi}-1}\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{t^{2q_{\Phi}}},

meaning

\left(\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{p_{\Phi}}}\right)^{\prime}\leq 0\leq\left(\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{q_{\Phi}}}\right)^{\prime}.

Hence, there exists an increasing function
f(t)
and a decreasing function
g(t)
such that

\Phi(t)=t^{q_{\Phi}}f(t)=t^{p_{\Phi}}g(t).

If
c\in[0,1]
, then

\Phi(ct)=(ct)^{q_{\Phi}}f(ct)\leq c^{q_{\Phi}}t^{q_{\Phi}}f(t)=c^{q_{\Phi}}\Phi(t)

and, using the other equality,

\Phi(ct)=(ct)^{p_{\Phi}}g(ct)\geq c^{p_{\Phi}}\Phi(t)

giving (
2.1
). The two inequalities of (
2.2
) are obtained by arguing analogously.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The assumption that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
in
Proposition

2.1
is not necessary for the second inequality of (
2.1
) or the first inequality of (
2.2
) to hold; since
\Phi
is a Young function, if
\Phi(s_{1})=0
and
\Phi(s_{2})=\infty
, then
s_{1}<s_{2}
,
\Phi(t)=0
for all
t\leq s_{1}
, and
\Phi(t)=\infty
for all
t\geq s_{2}
. Repeating the arguments of the proof for
t\in(s_{1},s_{2})
yields the results, since the aforementioned inequalities trivially hold for
t\in(0,s_{1}]\cup[s_{2},\infty)
.

Remark 2.3
.

Evidently, by the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{\Phi}
, we must have

\rho_{\Phi}\left(\frac{f}{\|f\|_{\Phi}}\right)\leq 1,\quad f\in L^{\Phi},

but if
p_{\Phi}<\infty
, then if
f\in L^{\Phi}
and
\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}}\neq 0
, we must have

\rho_{\Phi}\left(\frac{f}{\|f\|_{\Phi}}\right)=1.

To see why, suppose that

\rho_{\Phi}\left(\frac{f}{\|f\|_{\Phi}}\right)=\alpha<1.

Let
\alpha^{1/p_{\Phi}}<c<1
, and let
\lambda=c\|f\|_{\Phi}
. Then, by
Proposition

2.1
,

\rho_{\Phi}\left(\frac{f}{\lambda}\right)=\rho_{\Phi}\left(\frac{f}{c\|f\|_{\Phi}}\right)\leq\frac{\alpha}{c^{p_{\Phi}}}<1.

But then
\lambda<\|f\|_{\Phi}
and
\rho_{\Phi}(f/\lambda)<1
, which contradicts the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{\Phi}
.

Remark 2.4
.

A Young function is said to fulfill the
\Delta_{2}
-condition

[
2
, p. 6]
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\Phi(2t)\leq C\Phi(t),\quad t>0.

The fact that
p_{\Phi}<\infty
implies
\Phi
fulfills the
\Delta_{2}
-condition
is an easy consequence of the second inequality in (
2.2
). The converse is also easily shown since

\Phi^{\prime}(t)\leq\frac{\Phi(2t)-\Phi(t)}{t}\leq C\frac{\Phi(t)}{t},

whereby
\Phi
fulfilling the
\Delta_{2}
-condition for some constant
C>0
implies that
\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}\leq C
for every
t>0
, hence
p_{\Phi}<\infty
. In the remainder of this paper, we write
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
to indicate that
\Phi
is a Young function fulfilling the
\Delta_{2}
-condition.

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
. Then

\Phi(1)t^{p_{\Phi}}\leq\Phi(t)\leq\Phi(1)t^{q_{\Phi}},\quad t\leq 1

and

\Phi(1)t^{q_{\Phi}}\leq\Phi(t)\leq\Phi(1)t^{p_{\Phi}},\quad t\geq 1.

Proof.

Apply (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) with
t=1
.
∎

We can extend this to the following.

Corollary 2.6
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
and let
\Psi(t)=\frac{\Phi(t)}{\Phi(1)}
. Then the following holds true.

(i)

L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=L^{\Psi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
;

(ii)

p_{\Phi}=p_{\Psi}
and
q_{\Phi}=q_{\Psi}
;

(iii)

t^{p_{\Psi}}\leq\Psi(t)\leq t^{q_{\Psi}},\qquad t\leq 1

and

t^{q_{\Psi}}\leq\Psi(t)\leq t^{p_{\Psi}},\qquad t>1;

(iv)

q_{\Psi}t^{p_{\Psi}-1}\leq\Psi^{\prime}(t)\leq p_{\Psi}t^{q_{\Psi}-1},\qquad t\leq 1,

and

q_{\Psi}t^{q_{\Psi}-1}\leq\Psi^{\prime}(t)\leq p_{\Psi}t^{p_{\Psi}-1},\qquad t>1;

(v)

the inverse
\Psi^{-1}
exists and fulfills

t^{1/q_{\Psi}}\leq\Psi^{-1}(t)\leq t^{1/p_{\Psi}},\qquad t\leq 1,

and

t^{1/p_{\Psi}}\leq\Psi^{-1}(t)\leq t^{1/q_{\Psi}},\qquad t\geq 1.

Proof.

Statements
(i)
-
(iii)
follow directly from definitions and
Corollary

2.5
.

For statement
(iv)
, we have

q_{\Psi}\Psi(t)\leq t\Psi^{\prime}(t)\leq p_{\Phi}\Psi(t)

and hence the statement follows by
(iii)
.
Finally,
(v)
follows from
(iii)
by noting that

t^{q_{\Phi}}\leq\Phi(t)
implies
\Phi^{-1}(t^{q_{\Phi}})\leq t
, so that, with
s=t^{q_{\Phi}}
,
\Phi^{-1}(s)\leq s^{1/q_{\Phi}}
. Since
s\leq 1
whenever
t\leq 1
, the first inequality in
(v)
holds. The others follow by analogous arguments.
∎

We now present some examples of Young functions
\Phi
with different Lebesgue exponents

and
p_{\Phi}
. The values of these Lebesgue exponents in
(i)
and
(ii)
follow by very simple calculations. The calculations for
(v)
and

Of course, we can simply add another finite segment where the function has the form

Proposition 2.11

Let

\Phi

Let

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{r_{1}-r_{2}},

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{r_{1}-r_{2}}\beta^{r_{2}-p},

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r_{1}},

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle d_{3}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{r_{1}-r_{2}}\beta^{r_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{r_{2}}-\frac{1}{p}\right)+d_{2}.

It is clear that, for fixed

\displaystyle=r_{1}d_{1}=r_{1}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r_{1}}\right),

\displaystyle l_{\alpha}

\displaystyle q_{\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{p,\inf_{1<t\leq\alpha}\left(\frac{r_{1}}{1+kt^{-r_{1}}}\right),\inf_{\alpha<t\leq\beta}\left(\frac{r_{2}}{1+l_{\alpha}t^{-r_{2}}}\right),\inf_{t\geq\beta}\left(\frac{p}{1+m_{\alpha,\beta}t^{-p}}\right)\right\}.

\displaystyle=\min\left\{p,\frac{r_{1}}{1+k\alpha^{-r_{1}}},\frac{r_{2}}{1+l_{\alpha}\alpha^{-r_{2}}}\right\}

2.2.
Equivalent Young functions

The preceding proposition poses a difficult problem when attempting to analyze Orlicz spaces based on the Lebesgue exponents of the corresponding Young functions. In an attempt to avoid this problem, we will consider equivalence classes of Young functions and define the Lebesgue exponents
q_{\Phi}
and
p_{\Phi}
on these classes as a whole. This provides an opportunity to improve certain results, although there are limitations to this approach as well.

We note that if
\Phi
and
\Psi
are equivalent Young functions, then their (semi) derivatives are also equivalent, although they may not be Young functions.

Proposition 2.13
.

Suppose
\Phi,\Psi
are equivalent Young functions and
p_{\Phi},p_{\Psi}<\infty
. Then there exists a constant
C_{1}\geq 1
such that

C_{1}^{-1}\Psi^{\prime}\leq\Phi^{\prime}\leq C_{1}\Psi^{\prime}.

Proof.

Let
C\geq 1
be a constant fulfilling

C^{-1}\Psi\leq\Phi\leq C\Psi.

Since

q_{\Phi}\leq\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}\leq p_{\Phi},\quad q_{\Psi}\leq\frac{t\Psi^{\prime}(t)}{\Psi(t)}\leq p_{\Psi},\quad t>0,

we obtain

\Phi^{\prime}(t)\leq p_{\Phi}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}\leq Cp_{\Phi}\frac{\Psi(t)}{t}\leq\frac{Cp_{\Phi}}{q_{\Psi}}\Psi^{\prime}(t),

and similarly

\Psi^{\prime}(t)\leq p_{\Psi}\frac{\Psi(t)}{t}\leq Cp_{\Psi}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}\leq\frac{Cp_{\Psi}}{q_{\Phi}}\Phi^{\prime}(t),

hence the desired inequality is fulfilled with

C_{1}=\max\left\{\frac{Cp_{\Phi}}{q_{\Psi}},\frac{Cp_{\Psi}}{q_{\Phi}}\right\}.\qed

As
Proposition

2.12
shows,
\Phi
and
\Psi
can be equivalent even if
p_{\Phi}\neq p_{\Psi}
,
q_{\Phi}\neq q_{\Psi}
. Conversely,
p_{\Phi}=p_{\Psi}
,
q_{\Phi}=q_{\Psi}
does not imply that the Young functions are equivalent. This can be seen by considering, for example,

\Phi(t)=t^{2}\ln(1+t)\quad\text{and }\quad\Psi(t)=t^{2}+t^{3}.

Then
p_{\Phi}=p_{\Psi}=3
and
q_{\Phi}=q_{\Psi}=2
, but
\Phi
and
\Psi
are not equivalent (since, for instance, with
d=1
and
f(x)=(1+x^{2})^{-1/4}
,
\|f\|_{\Phi}<\infty
, but
\|f\|_{\Psi}=\infty
.)

Since equivalent Young functions have equivalent growth rates, we define Lebesgue exponents for equivalent classes as follows.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
\Phi
be a Young function and
[\Phi]
its equivalence class. We define

p_{[\Phi]}=\inf\left\{\,p_{\Psi}\,:\,\Psi\in[\Phi]\,\right\}

and

q_{[\Phi]}=\sup\left\{\,q_{\Psi}\,:\,\Psi\in[\Phi]\,\right\}.

The following proposition follows immediately from
[
3
, Corollary 2.8]
.

Proposition 2.15
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is a Young function. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

p_{[\Phi]}<\infty

(q_{[\Phi]}>1)
;

(ii)

p_{\Psi}<\infty

(q_{\Psi}>1)
for every
\Psi\in[\Phi]
;

(iii)

p_{\Psi}<\infty

(q_{\Psi}>1)
for some
\Psi\in[\Phi]
.

This means that if
p_{\Phi}=\infty
and
q_{\Phi}=1
, then the same is true for all
\Psi
in the equivalence class
[\Phi]
, and further
p_{[\Phi]}=\infty
and
q_{[\Phi]}=1
. However, this does not mean that any
\Phi_{1}
,
\Phi_{2}
with
p_{\Phi_{j}}=\infty
,
q_{\Phi_{j}}=1
,
j=1,2
are equivalent. One counterexample is given by

\Phi_{1}(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t=0,\\
e^{-1/t},&0<t<1/2,\\
e^{-2}(4t-1),&t\geq 1/2,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Phi_{2}(t)=e^{t}-1.

We now provide some examples of operations which preserve equivalence classes of Young functions. The details are left for the reader. For more information on the second of the following three examples (in the case that the Young functions involved are not equivalent), as well as several other examples, see
[
8
, p. 98-99]
.

Example 2.16
.

Suppose that
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\Psi
are Young functions and that
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in[\Psi]
.

(i)

If
\Phi=\alpha\Phi_{1}+\beta\Phi_{2}
,
\alpha,\beta>0
, then
\Phi\in[\Psi]
,

p_{\Phi}\leq\min\left\{\frac{C}{C+1}(p_{\Phi_{1}}+p_{\Phi_{2}}),\max\{p_{\Phi_{1}},p_{\Phi_{2}}\}\right\}

and

q_{\Phi}\geq\max\left\{\frac{1}{1+C}(q_{\Phi_{1}}+q_{\Phi_{2}}),\min\{q_{\Phi_{1}},q_{\Phi_{2}}\}\right\}

for some constant
C\geq 1
.

(ii)

If
\Phi=\max\{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\}
, then
\Phi\in[\Psi]
,

p_{\Phi}\leq\max\{p_{\Phi_{1}},p_{\Phi_{2}}\},\quad q_{\Phi}\geq\min\{q_{\Phi_{1}},q_{\Phi_{2}}\}

(iii)

If

\Phi=\sup\left\{\,\Phi_{3}\text{ convex}\,:\,\Phi_{3}\leq\min\{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\}\,\right\},

then
\Phi
is a Young function and
\Phi\in[\Psi]
.

It will become clear that
p_{[\Phi]}
and
q_{[\Phi]}
above are closely related to how the function

g_{\Phi}(t)=\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}

behaves at infinity and at the origin. The quantities
\limsup_{t\rightarrow\omega}g_{\Phi}(t)
and
\liminf_{t\rightarrow\omega}g_{\Phi}(t)
, where
\omega=0
or
\omega=\infty
, appear in the literature (cf. e.g.
[
8
, Theorem 11.11]
or
[
11
, Corollary 4]
). In the case that the limit of
g_{\Phi}(t)
exists as
t
approaches infinity or the origin, one can say the following.

Lemma 2.17
.

Suppose that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are equivalent Young functions.

(i)

If

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}=p_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t\Psi^{\prime}(t)}{\Psi(t)}=p_{2},

then
p_{1}=p_{2}
.

(ii)

If

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}=q_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{t\Psi^{\prime}(t)}{\Psi(t)}=q_{2},

then
q_{1}=q_{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p_{1}<p_{2}
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
K_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

-\varepsilon<\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}-p_{1}<\varepsilon,\qquad t>K_{\varepsilon},

which implies

\left(t^{-(p_{1}+\varepsilon)}\Phi(t)\right)^{\prime}<0,\qquad\left(t^{-(p_{1}-\varepsilon)}\Phi(t)\right)^{\prime}>0

hence

\Phi(t)=t^{p_{1}+\varepsilon}f(t)=t^{p_{2}-\varepsilon}g(t),

where
f(t)=t^{-(p_{1}+\varepsilon)}\Phi(t)
is a decreasing function, and
g(t)=t^{-(p_{1}-\varepsilon)}\Phi(t)
is an increasing function.
Hence

A_{\varepsilon}t^{p_{1}-\varepsilon}\leq\Phi(t)\leq B_{\varepsilon}t^{p_{1}+\varepsilon},\quad t>K_{\varepsilon},

where
A_{\varepsilon}=g(K_{\varepsilon})
and
B_{\varepsilon}=f(K_{\varepsilon})
. By a similar argument, there exist
C_{\varepsilon},D_{\varepsilon},L_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

C_{\varepsilon}t^{p_{2}-\varepsilon}\leq\Psi(t)\leq D_{\varepsilon}t^{p_{2}+\varepsilon},\quad t>L_{\varepsilon}.

By picking
\varepsilon<\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{2}
, we get for
t>\max\{K_{\varepsilon},L_{\varepsilon}\}

t^{p_{1}+\varepsilon}<t^{p_{2}-\varepsilon}\leq C_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\Psi(t)\leq CC_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\Phi(t)\leq\frac{CB_{\varepsilon}}{C_{\varepsilon}}t^{p_{1}+\varepsilon}.

This implies that, for
C_{1}=\frac{CB_{\varepsilon}}{C_{\varepsilon}}
and
t>\max\{K_{\varepsilon},L_{\varepsilon}\}
,

C_{1}^{-1}t^{p_{1}+\varepsilon}\leq t^{p_{2}-\varepsilon}\leq C_{1}t^{p_{1}+\varepsilon}

which is a contradiction. Hence
(i)
holds, and
(ii)
follows by analogous computations.
∎

As before, let
g_{\Phi}(t)=\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}
. If
g_{\Phi}(t)
approaches
p_{0}
and
p_{\infty}
as
t
approaches the origin and infinity, respectively, then it is clear that

q_{\Phi}\leq p_{0},p_{\infty}\leq p_{\Phi}.

We can, however, relate these quantities more closely.

Proposition 2.18
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is a Young function,

p_{0}=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)},\quad\text{and}\quad p_{\infty}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}.

Let
p=\max\{p_{0},p_{\infty}\}
and
q=\min\{p_{0},p_{\infty}\}
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a Young function
\Psi\in[\Phi]
with

p\leq p_{\Psi}<p+\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad q-\varepsilon<q_{\Psi}\leq q.

Proof.

Suppose
\varepsilon>0
. Let
g_{\Phi}(t)=\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}
.
Suppose
q\leq r\leq p
and for
n>1
let

\Psi_{n}(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{\Phi(t)}{\Phi^{\prime}(1/n)},&t\leq 1/n,\\[5.0pt]
\frac{at^{r}}{r}+b,&1/n<t\leq n,\\[5.0pt]
\frac{c\Phi(t)}{\Phi^{\prime}(n)}+d,&t>n,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=n^{r-1},

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{g_{\Phi}(1/n)}-\frac{1}{r}\right),

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=n^{2r-2},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=n^{2r-1}\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{g_{\Phi}(n)}\right)+n^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{g_{\Phi}(1/n)}-\frac{1}{r}\right).

We have

\Psi^{\prime}_{n}(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi^{\prime}(1/n)},&t\leq 1/n,\\[5.0pt]
at^{r-1},&1/n<t\leq n,\\[5.0pt]
\frac{c\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi^{\prime}(n)},&t>n,\end{cases}

hence

\frac{t\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(t)}{\Psi_{n}(t)}=\begin{cases}g_{\Phi}(t),&t\leq 1/n,\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{r}{1+k_{n}t^{-r}},&1/n<t\leq n,\\[10.0pt]
\dfrac{g_{\Phi}(t)}{1+l_{n}\cdot(\Phi(t))^{-1}},&t>n,\end{cases}

where

k_{n}=\frac{rb}{a}=n^{-r}\left(\frac{r}{g_{\Phi}(1/n)}-1\right)

and

l_{n}=\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(n)d}{c}=\Phi(n)\left[\left(\frac{g_{\Phi}(n)}{r}-1\right)+n^{-2r}\left(\frac{g_{\Phi}(n)}{g_{\Phi}(1/n)}-\frac{g_{\Phi}(n)}{r}\right)\right].

Since

\frac{t\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(t)}{\Psi_{n}(t)}=g_{\Phi}(t),\qquad t<1/n,

it is clear that
p_{\Psi_{n}}\geq p_{1}\geq q_{\Psi_{n}}
, and since

\frac{g_{\Phi}(t)}{1+l_{n}\cdot(\Phi(t))^{-1}}\rightarrow p_{2},\qquad t\rightarrow\infty,

we must have
p_{\Psi_{n}}\geq p_{2}\geq q_{\Psi_{n}}
as well. Hence
p_{\Psi_{n}}\geq p
and
q_{\Psi_{n}}\leq q
.

Let
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon
be chosen such that
\frac{p_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}{p_{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}>1-\frac{\varepsilon}{q}
and
\frac{p_{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}{p_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}<1+\frac{\varepsilon}{p}
(it is sufficient that
\varepsilon_{1}<\frac{p_{2}\varepsilon}{2p+\varepsilon}
). Then, there exists
N_{1}>0
such that

\left|n^{-2r}\left(\frac{g_{\Phi}(n)}{g_{\Phi}(1/n)}-\frac{g_{\Phi}(n)}{r}\right)\right|<n^{-2r}\left(\frac{p_{\Phi}}{q_{\Phi}}-\frac{q_{\Phi}}{r}\right)<\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2r},\qquad n>N_{1}

and

p_{2}-\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2}<g_{\Phi}(t)<p_{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2},\qquad t>N_{1}.

Hence, if
l_{n}>0
, then for
t>n>N_{1}
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{g_{\Phi}(t)}{1+l_{n}\cdot(\Phi(t))^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq g_{\Phi}(t)<p+\varepsilon,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\dfrac{g_{\Phi}(t)}{1+l_{n}\cdot(\Phi(t))^{-1}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{g_{\Phi}(t)}{1+l_{n}\cdot(\Phi(n))^{-1}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{rg_{\Phi}(t)}{p_{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}\geq\frac{r(p_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})}{p_{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}>q-\varepsilon.

If instead
l_{n}<0
, then

\displaystyle\dfrac{g_{\Phi}(t)}{1+l_{n}\cdot(\Phi(t))^{-1}}

\displaystyle\geq g_{\Phi}(t)>q-\varepsilon,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\dfrac{g_{\Phi}(t)}{1+l_{n}\cdot(\Phi(t))^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{g_{\Phi}(t)}{1+l_{n}\cdot(\Phi(n))^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{rg_{\Phi}(t)}{p_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}\leq\frac{r(p_{2}+\varepsilon_{1})}{p_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}<p+\varepsilon.

Choose
N_{2}>0
such that

p_{1}-\varepsilon<g_{\Phi}(1/n)<p_{1}+\varepsilon,\qquad n>N_{2}.

If
k_{n}>0
then

g_{\Phi}(1/n)\leq\frac{r}{1+k_{n}t^{-r}}<r,\qquad 1/n<t\leq n,

and if
k_{n}<0
then

r<\frac{r}{1+k_{n}t^{-r}}\leq g_{\Phi}(1/n)\qquad 1/n<t\leq n.

Since
q-\varepsilon<r<p+\varepsilon
, we have

q-\varepsilon<\frac{r}{1+k_{n}t^{-r}}<p+\varepsilon,\qquad 1/n<t\leq n,

and, lastly, we note that

q-\varepsilon<g_{\Phi}(t)<p+\varepsilon,\qquad t\leq 1/N_{2}.

Let
n>\max\{N_{1},N_{2}\}
. Then the arguments above now show that

\displaystyle p_{\Psi_{n}}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\sup_{t\leq 1/n}g_{\Phi}(t),\sup_{1/n<t\leq n}\left(\dfrac{r}{1+k_{n}t^{-r}}\right),\sup_{t>n}\left(\dfrac{g_{\Phi}(t)}{1+l_{n}\cdot(\Phi(t))^{-1}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle<p+\varepsilon,

and similarly,
q_{\Psi_{n}}>q-\varepsilon
. This completes the proof.
∎

With these results in mind, we can directly connect the limits of
\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}
as
t
approaches the origin or infinity to the equivalence class Lebesgue exponents as follows.

Corollary 2.19
.

Let
\Phi
be a Young function with

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}=p_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}=p_{\infty}.

Then

\displaystyle p_{[\Phi]}=\max\{p_{0},p_{\infty}\}\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad q_{[\Phi]}=\min\{p_{0},p_{\infty}\}.

Proof.

Let
p=\max\{p_{0},p_{\infty}\}
,
q=\min\{p_{0},p_{\infty}\}
.
It is evident that for any
\Psi\in[\Phi]
,

p_{\Psi}\geq p,\quad q_{\Psi}\leq q,

hence
p_{[\Phi]}\geq p
and
q_{[\Phi]}\leq q
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, let
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
be chosen so that
p\leq p_{\Psi}<p+\varepsilon
and
q\geq q_{\Psi}>q-\varepsilon
(as is possible by
Proposition

2.18
). Then

p_{[\Phi]}=\inf\left\{\,p_{\Psi}\,:\,\Psi\in[\Phi]\,\right\}\leq\inf_{\varepsilon>0}p_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}=p

and

q_{[\Phi]}=\sup\left\{\,q_{\Psi}\,:\,\Psi\in[\Phi]\,\right\}\geq\sup_{\varepsilon>0}q_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}=q,

giving the result.
∎

Using Lebesgue exponents for equivalence classes, we can improve (
1.3
) in the case that the limit of
\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}
exists as
t
approaches zero or infinity as follows.

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
\Phi
be a Young function and
[\Phi]
its equivalence class. Suppose that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}

exist. If
1<q_{[\Phi]}\leq p_{[\Phi]}<\infty
, then

L^{p}\cap L^{q}\subseteq L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{p}+L^{q}

for all
p\in(p_{[\Phi]},\infty)
and
q\in(1,q_{[\Phi]})
.

Proof.

For any
\Psi\in[\Phi]
,

L^{p_{\Psi}}\cap L^{q_{\Psi}}\subseteq L^{\Psi}\subseteq L^{p_{\Psi}}+L^{q_{\Psi}}.

Since
L^{\Psi}=L^{\Phi}
, the result now follows from
Lemma

2.17
,
Proposition

2.18
, and
Corollary

2.19
.
∎

Unfortunately, we can not guarantee that there exists a Young function
\Psi
in the equivalence class of Young functions
[\Phi]
such that
p_{\Psi}=p_{[\Phi]}
and
q_{\Psi}=q_{[\Phi]}
, hence we can not guarantee that
L^{p_{[\Phi]}}\cap L^{q_{[\Phi]}}\subseteq L^{\Psi}
, nor that
L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{p_{[\Phi]}}+L^{q_{[\Phi]}}
. In fact, the following provides a counter example.

Example 2.21
.

Let
\Phi(t)=t^{2}\ln(2+t)
. Then

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}=2

hence
p_{[\Phi]}=q_{[\Phi]}=2
and so

L^{2+\varepsilon}\cap L^{2-\varepsilon}\subseteq L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{2+\varepsilon}+L^{2-\varepsilon}

for all
\varepsilon>0
by Proposition
2.20
. In fact, since
q_{\Phi}=2
we must have

L^{2+\varepsilon}\cap L^{2}\subseteq L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{2+\varepsilon}+L^{2}

for every
\varepsilon>0
,
but
L^{\Phi}\neq L^{2}
, and there is no
\Psi\in[\Phi]
with
p_{\Psi}=p_{[\Phi]}=2
.

2.3.
A different approach

So far, our attempts to analyze Orlicz spaces revolve around comparing the behaviors of Young functions (and in particular,
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
) to that of
t^{p}
for
1\leq p<\infty
. One approach is to make this connection more literal and simply let the exponent
p
depend on
t
. Somewhat naively, we write
\Phi(t)=\Phi(1)t^{r_{\Phi}(t)}
, for some function
{r_{\Phi}}:{[0,\infty)}\rightarrow{[-\infty,\infty]}
. Then, by normalizing
\Phi(t)
we obtain
\tilde{\Phi}(t)=t^{r_{\Phi}(t)}
for
\tilde{\Phi}(t)=\frac{\Phi(t)}{\Phi(1)}
, and therefore

r_{\Phi}(t)=\frac{\ln\tilde{\Phi}(t)}{\ln t}.

For analysis of indices related to this quantity, see
[
7
]
. Much in the same way as
g_{\Phi}(t)=\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}
,
this is a function which enables comparisons with Lebesgue spaces. If
\Phi(t)=t^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, then

g_{\Phi}(t)=r_{\Phi}(t)=p.

If
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
, then by
Corollary

2.6
(iii)
, we must have

q_{\Phi}\leq r_{\Phi}(t)\leq p_{\Phi},\quad t>0.

Therefore, if we let
a_{\Phi}=\inf_{t>0}r_{\Phi}(t)
and
b_{\Phi}=\sup_{t>0}r_{\Phi}(t)
, then
q_{\Phi}\leq a_{\Phi}\leq b_{\Phi}\leq p_{\Phi}
, and by definition of
r_{\Phi}
, we must have

\displaystyle\Phi(1)t^{a_{\Phi}}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(t)\leq\Phi(1)t^{b_{\Phi}},\qquad t\geq 1,

\displaystyle\Phi(1)t^{b_{\Phi}}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(t)\leq\Phi(1)t^{a_{\Phi}},\qquad t\leq 1,

which is (possibly) an improvement of
Corollary

2.6
(iii)
.

Observe also that if we replace
\tilde{\Phi}
with
c\tilde{\Phi}
for any constant
c>0
in the expression for
r_{\Phi}(t)
above, then

\frac{\ln c\tilde{\Phi}(t)}{\ln t}=\frac{c}{\ln t}+r_{\Phi}(t).

On one hand,
\frac{c}{\ln t}
is undefined for
t=1
. On the other hand, the term
c/\ln t
vanishes as
t
approaches the origin or infinity, hence it does not matter if
\Phi
is normalized or not in the expression for
r_{\Phi}
when considering only those limits.

In
Section

2.2
, we are not able to guarantee that the limit of
\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}
as
t
approaches zero or infinity is always well-defined. Looking instead at
r_{\Phi}(t)
, we can say the following.

Proposition 2.22
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
. Then the following is true.

(i)

The limits

r_{0}=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}r_{\Phi}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad r_{\infty}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}r_{\Phi}(t)

exist.

(ii)

The limits
r_{0}
and
r_{\infty}
fulfill

q_{\Phi}\leq\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}g_{\Phi}(t)\leq r_{0}\leq\limsup_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}g_{\Phi}(t)\leq p_{\Phi}

and

q_{\Phi}\leq\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}g_{\Phi}(t)\leq r_{\infty}\leq\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}g_{\Phi}(t)\leq p_{\Phi},

where
g_{\Phi}(t)=\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}
.

(iii)

If the limits

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}g_{\Phi}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}g_{\Phi}(t)

also exist, then they equal
r_{0}
and
r_{\infty}
, respectively.

(iv)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
K_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

t^{r_{0}+\varepsilon}<\Phi(t)<t^{r_{0}-\varepsilon},\quad t<\frac{1}{K_{\varepsilon}}

and

t^{r_{\infty}-\varepsilon}<\Phi(t)<t^{r_{\infty}+\varepsilon},\quad t>K_{\varepsilon}.

Proof.

It is evident that
(ii)
implies
(iii)
. We will show
(i)
in the case that
t
tends to infinity, as the case that
t
tends to zero follows by analogous argument. The parts
(ii)
and
(iv)
will be proven along the way.

Let

p=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}\quad\text{and}\quad q=\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}.

Since
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
, we have
q\leq p<\infty
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
L_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

q-\varepsilon<\frac{t\Phi^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}<p+\varepsilon,\quad t>L_{\varepsilon}.

Performing the same kinds of manipulations as in the proofs of
Proposition

2.1
and
Lemma

2.17
gives

A_{\varepsilon}t^{q-\varepsilon}\leq\Phi(t)\leq B_{\varepsilon}t^{p+\varepsilon},\quad t>L_{\varepsilon}

where
A_{\varepsilon}=g(L_{\varepsilon})
for some increasing function
g
and
B_{\varepsilon}=f(L_{\varepsilon})
for some decreasing function
f
.
This implies that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{p_{1}}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t\Phi(t)}{t^{p_{2}}}=\infty

whenever
p_{1}>p+\varepsilon
and
p_{2}<q-\varepsilon
, and since this holds for any
\varepsilon>0
, the statement is true for all
p_{1}>p
and
p_{2}<q
. Since
\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{\alpha}}
is a decreasing, continuous function with respect to
\alpha
, there exists

r=\inf\left\{\,\alpha\in[q,p]\,:\,\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{\alpha}}=0\,\right\}=\sup\left\{\,\alpha\in[q,p]\,:\,\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{\alpha}}=\infty\,\right\}.

Now,
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{r}}
may be
0,\infty
, or may not exist at all, but for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{r+\varepsilon}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{r-\varepsilon}}=\infty.

Hence, for every
j=1,2,3\dots
there exists
M_{j,\varepsilon}>0
and
N_{j,\varepsilon}
such that

\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{r+\varepsilon}}<\frac{1}{j},\quad t>M_{j,\varepsilon}

and

\frac{\Phi(t)}{t^{r-\varepsilon}}>j,\quad t>N_{j,\varepsilon}.

By letting
K_{\varepsilon}=\max\{M_{1,\varepsilon},N_{1,\varepsilon}\}
,
we obtain

(2.3)

t^{r-\varepsilon}<\Phi(t)<t^{r+\varepsilon},\quad t>K_{\varepsilon}.

Taking logarithms, we now obtain

r-\varepsilon<\frac{\ln\Phi(t)}{\ln t}<r+\varepsilon

for all
t>\max\{K_{\varepsilon},1\}
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we have proven
(i)
when
t
approaches infinity by the discussion in the beginning of this section. Proceeding in the same way for the other case gives
(i)
. This also proves
(ii)
by construction of
r
, and (
2.3
) shows
(iv)
.
∎

As is shown above, for
\Phi,\Psi\in\Delta_{2}
, the limits of
\frac{\ln\Phi(t)}{\ln t}
and
\frac{\ln\Psi(t)}{\ln t}
as
t
approaches zero or infinity are well-defined. If
\Phi
and
\Psi
are equivalent, then

\frac{\ln\Phi(t)}{\ln t}\leq\frac{\ln C\Psi(t)}{\ln t}=\frac{\ln C}{\ln t}+\frac{\ln\Psi(t)}{\ln t},

and similarly

\frac{\ln\Psi(t)}{\ln t}\leq\frac{\ln C}{\ln t}+\frac{\ln\Phi(t)}{\ln t}.

Taking limits as
t
approaches zero or infinity will lead to the
\frac{\ln C}{\ln t}
term vanishing, hence we obtain the following.

Proposition 2.23
.

Suppose that
\Phi,\Psi\in\Delta_{2}
are equivalent. Then

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}r_{\Phi}(t)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}r_{\Psi}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}r_{\Phi}(t)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}r_{\Psi}(t).

We can now prove the following analogue to
Proposition

2.20
.

Theorem 2.24
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
. Let

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}r_{\Phi}(t),

\displaystyle r_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}r_{\Phi}(t),

and suppose that
r_{0},r_{\infty}>1
.
Then

L^{p}\cap L^{q}\subseteq L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{p}+L^{q}

for every

p>\max\left\{r_{0},r_{\infty}\right\},\quad\text{and}\qquad 1<q<\min\left\{r_{0},r_{\infty}\right\}.

Proof.

Since
p_{\Phi}<\infty
by assumption,
r_{0},r_{\infty}<\infty
by
Proposition

2.22
(ii)
. Let

p=\max\{r_{0},r_{\infty}\},\qquad\text{and}\qquad q=\min\{r_{0},r_{\infty}\}.

With
Proposition

2.23
in mind, by the exact same construction as in the proof of
Proposition

2.18
(now with
r\in[q,p]
with the
p
and
q
defined above), for every
q-1>\varepsilon>0
, there exists an equivalent Young function
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
with
p\leq p_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}<p+\varepsilon
and
q-\varepsilon<q_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}\leq q
.
Since

L^{p_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}}\cap L^{q_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}}\subseteq L^{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}\subseteq L^{p_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}}+L^{q_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}}

and
L^{\Phi}=L^{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}
, the result now follows.
∎

The benefit of
Theorem

2.24
as opposed to
Proposition

2.20
is that we do not need to assume that the limits
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}r_{\Phi}(t)
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}r_{\Phi}(t)
exist; they always exist by
Proposition

2.22
(i)
, assuming
\Phi
fulfills the
\Delta_{2}
-condition.

3.
Trace type Orlicz spaces

For mixed Orlicz spaces, we have similar inclusions to those of
Remark

1.5
,
Proposition

2.20
, and
Theorem

2.24
. To express these, we will need some (admittedly cumbersome) notation.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{1}
,
\mathcal{B}_{2}
be Banach spaces. Then, formally, the space
(\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2})
consists of all
{f}:{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\|f_{2}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}<\infty,

where
f_{2}(y)=\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}
.

With this notation,
L^{\Phi,\Psi}=(L^{\Phi},L^{\Psi})
, and we observe that

(L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Psi}}\cap L^{q_{\Psi}})\subseteq L^{\Phi,\Psi}\subseteq(L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Psi}}+L^{q_{\Psi}}).

If we let
\Phi=\Psi
, we obtain the spaces that we here refer to as trace type Orlicz spaces, and the inclusions above become

(L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}})\subseteq L^{\Phi,\Phi}\subseteq(L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}}).

For the usual Lebesgue spaces,
(L^{p},L^{p})=L^{p}
and the purpose of this section is to investigate the relationship between
L^{\Phi,\Phi}
and
L^{\Phi}
. Generally, it is not the case that
(L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}})=L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}}
nor that
(L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}})=L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}}
, but

(L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}})\subseteq L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}}\subseteq L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}}\subseteq(L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}}).

To show that neither
L^{\Phi,\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi}
nor
L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi,\Phi}
in general, we will provide examples which utilize the fact that if
p_{\Phi}\neq q_{\Phi}
, then

L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}}\subsetneq(L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Phi}}+L^{q_{\Phi}}),

and similarly

(L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}},L^{p_{\Phi}}\cap L^{q_{\Phi}})\subsetneq L^{p_{\Psi}}\cap L^{q_{\Psi}}.

The first of these examples is as follows.

Example 3.1
.

Let
d=2
and let
\Phi(t)=t+t^{2}
. Then
L^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=L^{1}\cap L^{2}
and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Phi}
is equivalent to

\|f\|_{\Phi}=\|f\|_{L^{1}}+\|f\|_{L^{2}}.

On the other hand, the norm for the space
L^{\Phi,\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
then becomes

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\Phi,\Phi}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\int|f(x,y)|dx+\left(\int|f(x,y)|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\right)dy

\displaystyle+\left(\int\left(\int|f(x,y)|dx+\left(\int|f(x,y)|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}dy\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{L^{1,1}}+\|f\|_{L^{2,1}}+\|\big(\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{1}}+\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{2}}\big)\|_{L^{2}}.

It is evident that
\|f\|_{\Phi}\leq\|f\|_{\Phi,\Phi}
for every
f\in L^{\Phi,\Phi}
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n=2}^{\infty}
be a sequence of functions with

f_{n}(x,y)=e^{-nx^{2}+2\sqrt{n-1}xy-y^{2}},\qquad n=2,3,4,\dots.

Then, since

-nx^{2}+2\sqrt{n-1}xy-y^{2}=-n\left(x-\frac{\sqrt{n-1}}{n}y\right)^{2}-\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{n-1}}{n}\right)^{2}y^{2},

performing appropriate variable substitutions yields

\|f_{n}\|_{\Phi}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{n-\sqrt{n-1}^{2}}}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt[4]{n-\sqrt{n-1}^{2}}}=\pi+\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}},\qquad n=2,3,4,\dots.

On the other hand, similar calculations yield

\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2,1}}=\frac{\sqrt[4]{\pi}}{\sqrt[4]{2n}}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{n-1}{n}}}=C\sqrt[4]{n},

where
C=\frac{\pi^{3/4}}{2^{1/4}}
.

Hence, if we let

g(x,y)=\sum_{n=2}\frac{1}{n^{5/4}}f_{n}(x,y),

then

\|g\|_{\Phi}\leq\left(\pi+\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right)\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{5/4}}<\infty,

but

\|g\|_{\Phi,\Phi}\geq\|g\|_{L^{2,1}}=C\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}=\infty.

In other words,
g\in L^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
but
g\notin L^{\Phi,\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

The example above shows that there exist Young functions
\Phi
such that
L^{\Phi}\neq L^{\Phi,\Phi}
, and specifically
L^{\Phi,\Phi}\subsetneq L^{\Phi}
. One can perform similar arguments to show that more generally, if
\Phi(t)=t^{q}+t^{p}
with
q<p<\infty
, then
L^{\Phi,\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})\subsetneq L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

By duality arguments, we can take this example and transform it into an example where the opposite proper set inclusion holds.

Example 3.2
.

Pick
\Phi(t)=t^{2}+t^{3}
so that, by slightly altering the construction in
Example

3.1
, we have
L^{\Phi,\Phi}\subsetneq L^{\Phi}
. Since
p_{\Phi}=3<\infty
, there exists a Young function
\Psi
so that
L^{\Psi}=(L^{\Phi})^{\prime}
, the dual of
L^{\Phi}
(cf.
[
1
, Theorem 8.19]
). Moreover
L^{\Psi,\Psi}=(L^{\Phi,\Phi})^{\prime}
(see also
[
11
, Sec. VII]
).
Since
(L^{q}\cap L^{p})^{\prime}=L^{q^{\prime}}+L^{p^{\prime}}
, where
q^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime}
fulfill

\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1,\quad\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1,

and
L^{\Phi}=L^{2}\cap L^{3}
, we must have
L^{\Psi}=L^{2}+L^{3/2}
. One choice of
\Psi
is therefore given by

\Psi(t)=\begin{cases}t^{2},&t\leq 1\\
2t^{3/2}-1,&t>1\end{cases}

Since
L^{\Phi,\Phi}\subsetneq L^{\Phi}
, it therefore follows by duality that
L^{\Psi}\subsetneq L^{\Psi,\Psi}
.

3.1.
The Young function modular

To compare the spaces
L^{\Phi}
and
L^{\Phi,\Phi}
in general, we will need to compare the norms
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\Phi}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\Phi,\Phi}}
.
When possible, it is typically convenient to work with
\rho_{\Phi}(f)=\|\Phi(|f|)\|_{1}
in place of
\|f\|_{\Phi}
(in the same way that working with
\||f|^{p}\|_{1}=\|f\|_{p}^{p}
sometimes leads to simpler calculations than using
\|f\|_{p}
), despite the fact that
\rho_{\Phi}(f)
is not generally a norm. The following proposition relates the two quantities
\|f\|_{\Phi}
and
\rho_{\Phi}(f)
to each other.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
. If
f
is a Lebesgue measurable function, then

\|f\|_{\Phi}<\infty\iff\rho_{\Phi}(f)<\infty,

and in this case, one of the three following statements is true:

(i)

1<\|f\|_{\Phi}\leq\rho_{\Phi}(f);

(ii)

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq\|f\|_{\Phi}<1;

(iii)

\|f\|_{\Phi}=\rho_{\Phi}(f)=1.

The first statement is a well-known result and is a direct consequence of, for instance,
[
1
, p. 268, 8.9]
.
Parts which can be inferred by the other statements, such as
\rho_{\Phi}(f)>1
if and only if
\|f\|_{\Phi}>1
are also commonly known (cf.
[
5
, p. 45]
). In an effort to be self-contained, we prove all parts of the Proposition here.

Proof.

To abbreviate further the notations for this proof, let
\alpha=\|f\|_{\Phi}
and
\beta=\rho_{\Phi}(f)
.

Since
\alpha=0
if and only if
f=0
if and only if (under the given assumptions on
\Phi
)
\beta=0
, we perform the calculations of the proof under the assumption that
f\neq 0
, so that
\alpha,\beta>0
.

If
\alpha<\infty
then
\|\tilde{f}\|_{\Phi}=1
for
\tilde{f}=f/\alpha
. This implies
\rho_{\Phi}(\tilde{f})\leq 1
, hence by
Proposition

2.1
,

\beta=\rho_{\Phi}(\alpha\tilde{f})\leq\alpha^{p}\rho_{\Phi}(\tilde{f})\leq\alpha^{p}<\infty,

where
p=p_{\Phi}
if
\alpha\geq 1
and
p=q_{\Phi}
otherwise.

If
\beta<\infty
then, using the convexity of
\Phi
, for any
c\geq 1
,

\rho_{\Phi}(f/c)\leq c^{-1}\beta.

Since
c^{-1}\beta\rightarrow 0
as
c\rightarrow\infty
, there exists
c_{0}\geq 1
such that
c^{-1}\beta\leq 1
for all
c\geq c_{0}
. Then, by definition of
\alpha
, we must have
\alpha\leq c_{0}
, hence
\alpha<\infty.

To show that the three listed cases are the only possible outcomes, we begin by showing that
\alpha>1
if and only if
\beta>1
, and that in this case
1<\alpha\leq\beta
.

First suppose
\alpha>1
. Then, since
\Phi
is convex and
1/\alpha<1
,
\Phi(|f|/\alpha)\leq 1/\alpha\Phi(|f|)
. By definition of
\alpha
,

\rho_{\Phi}(f/\alpha)\leq 1,

but under the given assumptions on
\Phi
(see
Remark

2.3
), we must have

\rho_{\Phi}(f/\alpha)=1,

since otherwise there would exist
\lambda<\alpha
with

\rho_{\Phi}(f/\lambda)\leq 1.

Therefore, using the convexity of
\Phi
,

1=\rho_{\Phi}(f/\alpha)\leq\frac{\beta}{\alpha},

giving
1<\alpha\leq\beta
.

On the other hand, if
\beta>1
, then we must have
\alpha>1
, since otherwise

\rho_{\Phi}(f/\alpha)\geq\beta>1

(
\Phi
is increasing), but using the convexity of
\Phi
again,

\rho_{\Phi}(f/\beta)\leq\frac{\beta}{\beta}=1,

hence
1<\alpha\leq\beta
by definition of
\alpha
.

Now, we show that
\alpha<1
if and only if
\beta<1
and that in this case
\beta\leq\alpha<1
. (This will complete the proof, as the only other possible outcome will then be
(iii)
.)
Suppose
\alpha<1
. Then
1/\alpha>1
, and since
\Phi
must be strictly increasing under the assumptions above,

1=\rho_{\Phi}(f/\alpha)>\beta.

On the other hand, by
Proposition

2.1
yet again (noting that
q_{\Phi}\geq 1
),

\rho_{\Phi}(f/\beta)\geq\beta^{1-q_{\Phi}}\geq 1=\rho_{\Phi}(|f|/\alpha),

so that
\beta\leq\alpha<1
.

Lastly, suppose
\beta<1
. Then, repeating the last argument gives
\beta\leq\alpha
in the exact same way. Since
\beta<1
, we are not in case
(i)
and therefore, at least,
\alpha\leq 1
. But
\alpha=1
must imply
\beta=1
by
Remark

2.3
again, hence the proof is complete.
∎

Using
Proposition

2.1
and arguing similarly to the proof above, we can make the following observation, which more closely mirrors the fact that
\|f\|_{p}^{p}=\||f|^{p}\|_{1}
for the usual Lebesgue spaces, with
p<\infty
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
and
f\in L^{\Phi}
. Then

\|f\|_{\Phi}^{p_{\Phi}}\leq\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq\|f\|_{\Phi}^{q_{\Phi}}<1

if
\|f\|_{\Phi}<1
, and

1<\|f\|_{\Phi}^{q_{\Phi}}\leq\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq\|f\|_{\Phi}^{p_{\Phi}}

if
\|f\|_{\Phi}>1
.

One might wonder if in fact
\Phi(\|f\|_{\Phi})=\rho_{\Phi}(f)
, as is the case when
\Phi(t)=t^{p}
,
p\geq 1
. There are many counterexamples (see
Remark

3.16
), and the following is one of them.

Example 3.5
.

Let
\Phi(t)=t^{2}+t^{3}
and
f(x)=(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}
. Then

\rho_{\Phi}(f)=1+\frac{\pi}{2}\approx 2.571

but

\|f\|_{\Phi}=\sqrt[3]{\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\pi^{3}}{6^{3}}}}+\sqrt[3]{\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\pi^{3}}{6^{3}}}}\approx 1.496,

giving
\Phi(\|f\|_{\Phi})\approx\Phi(1.496)\approx 5.586
.

On the other hand,
Corollary

3.4
tells us that

\|f\|_{\Phi}^{2}\leq 1+\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\|f\|_{\Phi}^{3}
. Since
\|f\|_{\Phi}^{2}\approx 2.238
and
\|f\|_{\Phi}^{3}\approx 3.348
, this provides a good estimate of
\rho_{\Phi}(f)
.

Of course, we are more likely to want to provide upper and lower bounds for
\|f\|_{\Phi}
in terms of
\rho_{\Phi}(f)
, as this is generally a simpler quantity to compute. These bounds can easily be extracted from
Corollary

3.4
as follows.

Corollary 3.6
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
and that
f
is a Lebesgue measurable function with
\|f\|_{\Phi}<\infty
. Then

\rho_{\Phi}(f)^{1/q_{\Phi}}\leq\|f\|_{\Phi}\leq\rho_{\Phi}(f)^{1/p_{\Phi}}\leq 1

if
\rho_{\Phi}(f)<1
, and

1\leq\rho_{\Phi}(f)^{1/p_{\Phi}}\leq\|f\|_{\Phi}\leq\rho_{\Phi}(f)^{1/q_{\Phi}}

if
\rho_{\Phi}(f)>1
.

Applying
Corollary

3.6
to
Example

3.5
, we can see that

\left(1+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{1/3}\leq\|f\|_{\Phi}\leq\left(1+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{1/2},

and since
(1+\pi/2)^{1/3}\approx 1.370
,
(1+\pi/2)^{1/2}\approx 1.603
, we have arrived at a rough approximation for
\|f\|_{\Phi}
.

In the following proposition, we highlight the difference between
L^{\Phi}
and
L^{\Phi,\Phi}
in terms of the Young function modular
\rho_{\Phi}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
, let
{f}:{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be a function, and let

G_{f}(y)=\Phi\big(\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad H_{f}(y)=\|\Phi(f(\cdot,y))\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}.

Then

\|f\|_{L^{\Phi,\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})}<\infty\iff\|G_{f}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}<\infty,

and

\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})}<\infty\iff\|H_{f}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}<\infty.

Proof.

By
Proposition

3.3
,

\|f\|_{\Phi,\Phi}=\big\|\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{\Phi}\big\|_{\Phi}<\infty\iff\rho_{\Phi}\big(\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{\Phi}\big)=\|G_{f}\|_{L^{1}}<\infty.

Similarly,

\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}}<\infty\iff\rho_{\Phi}(f)=\|H_{f}\|_{L^{1}}<\infty.

∎

3.2.
Sub- and supermultiplicative Young functions

It is natural to consider sub- and supermultiplicative Young functions
[
11
, pp. 28]
, as such properties greatly simplify calculations with
\|\cdot\|_{\Phi}
. With respect to such considerations, we have the following.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
.

(i)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that
\Phi(ab)\leq C\Phi(a)\Phi(b)
for all
a,b\geq 0
if and only if

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\Phi\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\right),\quad f\in L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

(ii)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that
\Phi(ab)\geq C\Phi(a)\Phi(b)
for all
a,b\geq 0
, if and only if

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\geq C\Phi\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\right),\quad f\in L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\Phi(ab)\leq C\Phi(a)\Phi(b)

for every
a,b\geq 0
.
Since the desired result trivially holds whenever
\|f\|_{\Phi}=0
, we assume that
\|f\|_{\Phi}\neq 0
.
Then, with
a=\|f\|_{\Phi}
and
b=\frac{|f(x)|}{\|f\|_{\Phi}}
,

\Phi(|f(x)|)\leq C\Phi\left(\|f\|_{\Phi}\right)\Phi\left(\frac{|f(x)|}{\|f\|_{\Phi}}\right),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

By the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{\Phi}
,

\int\Phi\left(\frac{|f(x)|}{\|f\|_{\Phi}}\right)dx\leq 1.

Hence

\int\Phi(|f(x)|)\,dx\leq C\Phi(\|f\|_{\Phi})\cdot 1,

meaning

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\Phi(\|f\|_{\Phi}),\quad f\in L^{\Phi}.

This proves the first implication of
(i)
. To prove the same implication for
(ii)
, follow the same steps, but use the fact (
Remark

2.3
) that

\int\Phi\left(\frac{|f|}{\|f\|_{\Phi}}\right)dx=1.

To prove the other implication of
(i)
, suppose that
\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\Phi\left(\|f\|_{\Phi}\right)
for every
f\in L^{\Phi}
, but that
\Phi(ab)>C\Phi(a)\Phi(b)
for some
a,b>0
. Let

g(x)=\begin{cases}ab&x\in U,\\
0&x\notin U,\end{cases}

where
U
is a ball such that
\|g\|_{\Phi}=a
. (Since
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
, this is fulfilled if
|U|=1/\Phi(b)
.)

Then, for every
x\in U
,

\Phi(|g(x)|)>C\Phi(\|g\|_{\Phi})\Phi\left(\frac{|g(x)|}{\|g\|_{\Phi}}\right).

Integrating both sides gives (since
g=0
outside of
U
)

\int\Phi(|g(x)|)\,dx>C\Phi(\|g\|_{\Phi})\int\Phi\left(\frac{|g(x)|}{\|g\|_{\Phi}}\right)dx.

Since
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
(
Remark

2.3
again), we have

\int\Phi\left(\frac{|g(x)|}{\|g\|_{\Phi}}\right)dx=1

hence, we obtain

\rho_{\Phi}(g)>C\Phi(\|g\|_{\Phi}),

which is a contradiction. This proves
(i)
. The second implication of
(ii)
follows analogously.
∎

To state the results in this section with more generality, we will consider the following two conditions.
Suppose that
\Phi,\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
are Young functions.
Then the conditions are as follows.

(C1)

There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\Phi(C_{1}ab)\leq\Phi_{1}(a)\Phi_{2}(b)

for all
a,b\geq 0
.

(C2)

There exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\Phi(C_{2}ab)\geq\Phi_{1}(a)\Phi_{2}(b)

for all
a,b\geq 0
.

Remark 3.9
.

If we set
\Phi=\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}
then
(C1)
and
(C2)
become

(C1
′
)

There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\Phi(C_{1}ab)\leq\Phi(a)\Phi(b)

for all
a,b\geq 0
.

(C2
′
)

There exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\Phi(C_{2}ab)\geq\Phi(a)\Phi(b)

for all
a,b\geq 0
.

If
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
, then
(C1′)
is equivalent to submultiplicativity of
\Phi
and
(C2′)
is equivalent to supermultiplicativity.

We note the following reformulation of Conditions
(C1)
and
(C2)
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Suppose that
\Phi
,
\Phi_{1}
, and
\Phi_{2}
are bijective Young functions.

(i)

Condition
(C1)
holds if and only if

C_{1}\Phi_{1}^{-1}(a)\Phi_{2}^{-1}(b)\leq\Phi^{-1}(ab)

for all
a,b\geq 0
.

(ii)

Condition
(C2)
holds if and only if

\Phi^{-1}(ab)\leq C_{2}\Phi_{1}^{-1}(a)\Phi_{2}^{-1}(b)

for all
a,b\geq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(C1)
holds. For any
a,b\geq 0
let
x=\Phi_{1}^{-1}(a)
and
y=\Phi_{2}^{-1}(b)
. Since

\Phi(C_{1}xy)\leq\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{2}(y),

we thus have

\Phi(C_{1}\Phi_{1}^{-1}(a)\Phi_{2}^{-1}(b))\leq ab.

Since
\Phi
is bijective, this inequality implies that

C_{1}\Phi_{1}^{-1}(a)\Phi_{2}^{-1}(b)\leq\Phi^{-1}(ab),

which shows one implication of
(i)
.

Now suppose that

C_{1}\Phi_{1}^{-1}(a)\Phi_{2}^{-1}(b)\leq\Phi^{-1}(ab).

for all
a,b\geq 0
. For any
a,b\geq 0
, let
x=\Phi_{1}(a)
and
y=\Phi_{2}(b)
. Then, in particular,

C_{1}\Phi_{1}^{-1}(x)\Phi_{2}^{-1}(y)\leq\Phi^{-1}(xy).

Since
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are bijective, we have

C_{1}ab\leq\Phi^{-1}(\Phi_{1}(a)\Phi_{2}(b)).

Since
\Phi
is bijective,

\Phi(C_{1}ab)\leq\Phi_{1}(a)\Phi_{2}(b),

which gives the other implication of
(i)
. The proof of
(ii)
follows analogously.
∎

The following lemma follows by straight-forward computations.

Lemma 3.11
.

Suppose that
\Phi
,
\Phi_{1}
, and
\Phi_{2}
are bijective Young functions. Let
A,B
be measurable subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
|A|>0
and
|B|>0
and let
f_{A,B}
be the indicator function for the set
A\times B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
. Then

\|f_{A,B}\|_{L^{\Phi}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})}=\frac{1}{\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{|A|\cdot|B|}\right)},

\|f_{A,B}(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\Phi_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{|A|}\right)}&y\in B\\
0&y\notin B,\end{cases}

and

\|f_{A,B}\|_{L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})}=\frac{1}{\Phi_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{|A|}\right)\Phi_{2}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{|B|}\right)}.

With this lemma in mind, we obtain the following proposition, which we prove here in an effort to be self-contained.

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose that
\Phi,\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in\Delta_{2}
. Then the following holds.

(i)

We have
L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}\subseteq L^{\Phi}
if and only if
(C1)
holds.

(ii)

We have
L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}
if and only if
(C2)
holds.

(iii)

We have
L^{\Phi}=L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}
if and only if
\Phi=\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}
and
L^{\Phi}=L^{p}
for some
p\geq 1
.

Proof.

Results
(i)
and
(ii)
can be found in
[
9
, p. 295]
. Moreover,
(iii)
is due to
[
4
, p. 288-289]
. Here, we prove
(i)
using a similar method to that of the latter proof.

If
L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}\subseteq L^{\Phi}
, then there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}},\quad f\in L^{\Phi}.

Let
f_{A,B}
be as in
Lemma

3.11
. By that same lemma,

\frac{1}{\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{|A|\cdot|B|}\right)}\leq\frac{C}{\Phi_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{|A|}\right)\Phi_{2}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{|B|}\right)}.

For any
a,b>0
, choose
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
|A|=\frac{1}{a}
and
|B|=\frac{1}{b}
. Then

\Phi_{1}^{-1}(a)\Phi_{2}^{-1}(b)\leq C\Phi^{-1}(ab).

Since the inequality trivially holds whenever
a=0
or
b=0
, this is equivalent to
(C1)
, by
Lemma

3.10
.

Suppose instead that
(C1)
holds. Then for any
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, by letting
a=\frac{|f(x,y)|}{\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}}
and
b=\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}
we obtain

\Phi(|f(x,y)|)\leq C_{1}\Phi_{1}\left(\frac{|f(x,y)|}{\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}}\right)\Phi_{2}(\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}),\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Integrating with respect to
x
and using the definition of
\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}
, we obtain

\int\Phi(|f(x,y)|)\,dx\leq C\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}.

Integrating with respect to
y
therefore gives

(3.1)

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\rho_{\Phi_{2}}(\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}).

By
Proposition

3.7
,
\rho_{\Phi}(f)<\infty
if and only if
f\in L^{\Phi}
(since
p_{\Phi}<\infty
), and by slightly generalizing the statement of that same proposition,
\rho_{\Phi_{2}}(\|f(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}})<\infty
if and only if
f\in L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}
(since
p_{\Phi_{2}}<\infty
). Hence (
3.1
) shows that
f\in L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}
implies
f\in L^{\Phi}
. This proves
(i)
. To prove
(ii)
, use analogous arguments with reverse inequalities, and utilize
Remark

2.3
.
∎

Setting
\Phi=\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}
, we now obtain the following.

Corollary 3.13
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
. Then the following holds.

(i)

L^{\Phi,\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi}
if and only if
\Phi
is submultiplicative.

(ii)

L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi,\Phi}
if and only if
\Phi
is supermultiplicative.

(iii)

L^{\Phi}=L^{\Phi,\Phi}
if and only if
L^{\Phi}=L^{p}
for some
p\geq 1
.

Remark 3.14
.

Comparing
Corollary

3.13
(i)
and
(ii)
to
(iii)
of the same corollary, we see that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
is both sub- and supermultiplicative if and only if
\Phi
is equivalent to
t^{p}
for some
p\geq 1
.

Combining
Corollary

3.13
with
Proposition

3.8
, we immediately obtain the following.

Corollary 3.15
.

Suppose that
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
. Then the following holds:

(i)

L^{\Phi,\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi}
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\Phi\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}}\right),\quad f\in L^{\Phi};

(ii)

L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi,\Phi}
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\geq C\Phi\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}}\right),\quad f\in L^{\Phi};

(iii)

L^{\Phi}=L^{\Phi,\Phi}
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

C^{-1}\Phi\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}}\right)\leq\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\Phi\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}}\right),\quad f\in L^{\Phi},

if and only if
\Phi
is equivalent to
t^{p}
for some
p\geq 1
.

Remark 3.16
.

Evidently, if
\Phi(t)=t^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, then

\Phi(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}})=\int\Phi(|f(x)|)\,dx.

On the other hand, if
\Phi(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}})=\int\Phi(|f(x)|)\,dx
then
\|f\|_{L^{\Phi}}<\infty
if and only if
\rho_{\Phi}(f)<\infty
hence
\Phi\in\Delta_{2}
(cf.
[
1
, Section 8.9]
). In particular,
\Phi
is bijective. Hence, for any
a,b>0
, with

f(x)=\begin{cases}ab&x\in A\\
0&x\notin A,\end{cases}

where
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
fulfills
|A|=\frac{1}{\Phi(b)}
, arguing as in
Lemma

3.11
we obtain

\Phi(a)=\frac{\Phi(ab)}{\Phi(b)},

which implies that
\Phi(t)=t^{p}
for
p=\Phi^{\prime}(1)
.

Of course, we can generalize
Corollary

3.15
analogously to
Proposition

3.12
, by generalizing
Proposition

3.8
. We state this result without proof, as it follows by analogous arguments.

Theorem 3.17
.

Suppose that
\Phi,\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in\Delta_{2}
. Then the following holds:

L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}\subseteq L^{\Phi}
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\Phi_{2}\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}\right),\quad f\in L^{\Phi};

L^{\Phi}\subseteq L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\rho_{\Phi}(f)\geq C\Phi_{2}\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}\right),\quad f\in L^{\Phi};

L^{\Phi}=L^{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}}
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

C^{-1}\Phi_{2}\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}\right)\leq\rho_{\Phi}(f)\leq C\Phi_{2}\left(\|f\|_{L^{\Phi_{1}}}\right),\quad f\in L^{\Phi},

if and only if
\Phi=\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}
is equivalent to
t^{p}
for some
p\geq 1
.
