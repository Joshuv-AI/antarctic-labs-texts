A theory of strange metals
Simone Fratini

Abstract
I present a theory of transport and optical response in quantum materials that is based on the assumption that the motion of the charge carriers obeys the correspondence principle, in the sense that the quantum-mechanically averaged carrier diffusion in time is analogous to the classical case. In bad metals, where wave-like coherence is lost at each hop between neighboring atoms, this leads to strange T T -linear resistivities with apparently Planckian scattering rates as well as both the stretched Drude and displaced Drude peaks that are commonly observed in optical absorption experiments. The present framework might also indicate a solution to the problem of universal dielectric relaxation in insulators.



Introduction.—

Strange metals are characterized by
T
-linear resistivities, apparently Planckian scattering rates
\hbar/\tau\simeq k_{B}T
, and stretched Drude peaks. The latter are defined by a decay of the optical conductivity with frequency that is slower than the
1/\omega^{2}
expected from simple exponential relaxation of the momentum, exhibiting instead
fractional exponents
van der Marel et al. 2003
;
Michon et al. 2023
. In some cases such stretched Drude peaks become displaced Drude peaks, i.e. the peak conductivity is shifted to finite frequencies. In those cases, the peak frequency generally increases with temperature
Delacrétaz et al. 2017
;
Fratini and Ciuchi 2021
. I derive here an analytical theory that consistently explains all five observations, and discuss some of its implications.

Theory.—

Our starting point is the Kubo formula for the optical conductivity
Kubo 1957
;
Ryogo Kubo 1991
, in particular its expression in terms of the carriers’ quantum diffusion that appears in Refs.
Lindner and Auerbach 2010
;
Ciuchi et al. 2011
.
The quantum diffusion of electrons in a given spatial direction
can be measured via their quantum-mechanical spread
\Delta X^{2}(t)\equiv\langle[\hat{X}(t)-\hat{X}(0)]^{2}\rangle
, where
\hat{X}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{i}(t)

is the total position operator of
N
electrons and
\langle\cdots\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta H}(\ldots)]/Z
denotes the quantum thermodynamic average.

The Kubo formula for the real part of the optical conductivity can be rewritten exactly as:

Re\ \sigma(\omega)=-\frac{e^{2}\omega^{2}}{\nu}\frac{\tanh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)}{\hbar\omega}Re\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\Delta X^{2}(t)dt

(1)

with
\beta=1/k_{B}T
,
e
the electric charge and
\nu
the volume.
The prefactor in Eq. (
1
), will be crucial for the discussion that follows.
It entails the non-commutation between position and momentum, and
it is therefore an explicit manifestation of the uncertainty principle arising when
\hbar\neq 0
.
In the Kubo formula it originates from the fact that quantum mechanical response function is defined in terms of the current (velocity) commutator, while the real-space trajectories entering in
\Delta X^{2}(t)
are defined through the anti-commutator
Kubo 1957
;
Ryogo Kubo 1991
;
Lindner and Auerbach 2010
;
Ciuchi et al. 2011
.

Eq. (
1
) can be inverted to give

\Delta X^{2}(t)=-\frac{2\hbar\nu}{\pi e^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos\omega t)\frac{Re\ \sigma(\omega)}{\omega\tanh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)}d\omega.

(2)

Classical diffusion.—

The Drude theory for the d.c. and optical conductivity:

\sigma(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{1+(\omega\tau)^{2}}\;\;\;;\;\;\;\sigma_{0}=\frac{ne^{2}\tau}{m}.

(3)

is inherently classical, which is apparent from the fact that
\hbar
does not appear explicitly.

The motion of classical independent current carriers in the time domain can be derived by taking explicitly the classical limit
\hbar=0
in Eq. (
2
). Performing the integral starting from Eq. (
3
) leads to the behavior illustrated in Fig.
1
(a) (black line), featuring
two characteristic regimes. The first is ballistic motion
\Delta X^{2}(t)/N=\langle v_{x}^{2}\rangle t^{2}
at times shorter than the scattering time
\tau
, i.e. when scattering events have not happened yet. The second regime is diffusion at longer times,
\Delta X^{2}(t)/N=2\langle v_{x}^{2}\rangle\tau t
, with the diffusion constant given by
D=(d\Delta X^{2}/dt)/2N=\langle v_{x}^{2}\rangle\tau
. In the case of independent classical carriers,
\langle v_{x}^{2}\rangle=k_{B}T/m
from the equipartition principle.
The Lorentzian shape in Eq. (
3
), with its characteristic decay
1/\omega^{2}
at high frequency, directly follows from the assumption that the momentum relaxation is exponential in time
Dressel and Grüner 2002
.

Quantum Drude formula and apparent Planckian scattering.—

We now want to generalize Eq. (
3
) to quantum carriers.
To this aim, we cannot apply the correspondence principle between classical and quantum mechanics to the optical conductivity itself, because this quantity involves the commutator of the current correlations
Kubo 1957
;
Ryogo Kubo 1991
;
Mahan 2000
, that has no classical equivalent. We can apply it instead to the anti-commutator, or, equivalently, to the particle trajectories in real space, that do have a classical analog. In other words, we will assume that the many-body quantum-mechanical spread
\Delta X^{2}(t)
in the quantum case
is analogous to the one derived above for classical carriers, i.e. it is first ballistic then diffusive.
Our question is then:

as Eq. (
1
) knows explicitly about
\hbar
, what are the consequences of the uncertainty principle
on the response of quantum carriers to electric fields?

Applying Eq. (
1
) to the
\Delta X^{2}(t)
obtained previously yields the following quantum Drude formula:

Re\ \sigma^{(Q)}(\omega)=\frac{\tanh(\hbar\omega/2k_{B}T)}{\hbar\omega/2k_{B}T}\frac{\sigma_{0}^{(Q)}}{1+(\omega\tau)^{2}},

(4)

which is the main result of this work, with
\sigma_{0}^{(Q)}=ne^{2}D/k_{B}T
.

Let us apply this framework to bad metals
Hussey et al. 2004
. These are systems where the scattering rate is saturated to the maximum value allowed on the lattice, implying that wavelike coherence is lost at each hop. This corresponds to the Mott-Ioffe-Regel (MIR) condition
\ell=a
, where
\ell=v\tau
is the mean-free-path and
a
the nearest-neighbor distance. Setting
v=2\mathcal{J}a/\hbar
describing the ballistic motion of quantum electrons on a lattice with transfer integral
\mathcal{J}
,
this yields a scattering rate
\tau_{MIR}=\hbar/2\mathcal{J}
, corresponding to a classical diffusion constant
D=2\mathcal{J}a^{2}/\hbar
. Using
m=\hbar^{2}/(2\mathcal{J}a^{2})
on the lattice, this is equivalent to the limiting diffusivity
D=\hbar/m

Sommer et al. 2011
;
Bardon et al. 2014
;
Fratini et al. 2025
.

From Eq. (
4
) above, the d.c. conductivity is
\sigma_{0}^{(Q)}=(ne^{2}/m)\ \hbar/k_{B}T
.
Comparing this with the classical result
\sigma_{0}=ne^{2}\tau/m
, we see that as far as the d.c. conductivity
(and only the d.c. conductivity)
is concerned, evaluating the response of quantum diffusive carriers in a bad metal amounts to replacing the actual scattering time
\tau_{MIR}
in the classical Drude formula with the Planckian time

\tau^{(Q)}=\frac{\hbar}{k_{B}T}.

(5)

This means that if we interpret transport experiments in terms of the classical Drude formula, we will extract an apparently Planckian scattering time
\tau^{(Q)}
instead of the actual, temperature-independent
\tau_{MIR}
fixed by the lattice.
This apparently larger scattering time suggests that thanks to quantum uncertainty, the carriers diffuse faster than what their average trajectories would imply.

Heuristically, the conductivity
\sigma^{(Q)}
is equivalent to that of classical particles of velocity
v=\sqrt{k_{B}T/m}
moving by a thermal de Broglie length
\lambda=\hbar/\sqrt{mk_{B}T}
at each scattering event, instead of just
\ell
: the apparent time for this process would be
\lambda/v=\tau^{(Q)}
.

The corresponding resistivity can be rewritten in lattice units as

\rho=\bar{\rho}\ \frac{k_{B}T}{2\mathcal{J}},

(6)

where
\bar{\rho}\equiv 1/\bar{\sigma}=(\hbar/e^{2})d/x
on a layered two-dimensional lattice with interlayer distance
d
and concentration
x
per unit cell.
The resistivity Eq. (
6
) is much lower than the one predicted by the classical Drude model,
\rho_{MIR}=\bar{\rho}
, see Fig.
1
(b).

Note that the present theory is agnostic about the nature of the Planckian carriers: it works for electronic carriers as well as for heavier emergent excitations such as Trugman loops
Trugman 1988
;
Grusdt et al. 2018
or other fractionalized excitations
Coleman and Andrei 1989
;
Senthil et al. 2003
.
Numerically, Eq. (
6
) has been shown to hold quantitatively in the t-J model in the paramagnetic regime
T\gtrsim J
(see Ref.
Fratini et al. 2025
and Fig.
1
(b)), where the loss of quasiparticle coherence has been ascribed to the presence of strong spin fluctuations
Fratini et al. 2025
;
Campbell et al. 2024
.
Eq. (
4
) might also find application to the problem of ”strange Fermi liquids”, systems exhibiting

\rho\propto T^{2}
that cannot be explained by Umklapp scattering
Pal et al. 2012
;
Maslov
;
Lin et al. 2015
.
The present framework can rationalize this behavior using a non-saturated
1/\tau_{ph}\propto T
from electron-phonon scattering, leading to
1/\tau^{(Q)}=(k_{B}T/2\mathcal{J})\ 1/\tau_{ph}\propto T^{2}

Planckian scale in the frequency domain.—

Regardless of the d.c. value, Eq. (
1
) highlights the emergence of the Planckian
time
\tau^{(Q)}=\hbar/k_{B}T
controlling — and possibly modifying — the frequency-dependent response of the charge carriers.
The shape of the quantum response Eq. (
4
) is indistinguishable from the classical one at all frequencies
\omega\lesssim 1/\tau^{(Q)}
.
This means that in systems where the scattering rate is lower than
k_{B}T/\hbar
, the entire spectrum can be safely described via the classical Drude formula. Reassurignly, this is the case in good metals, where
1/\tau\propto T^{2}\ll T
.

In bad metals, instead, the optical absorption becomes explicitly dependent on
\hbar
. For
\omega\gtrsim 1/\tau^{(Q)}
:

\sigma^{(Q)}(\omega)\simeq\frac{2k_{B}T}{\hbar\omega}\frac{\sigma_{0}^{(Q)}}{1+(\omega\tau_{MIR})^{2}}=\frac{4\mathcal{J}}{\hbar\omega}\frac{\bar{\sigma}}{1+(\hbar\omega/2\mathcal{J})^{2}}.

(7)

This form implies an anomalous frequency dependence
\sigma\sim 1/\omega
for
1/\tau^{(Q)}\lesssim\omega\lesssim 1/\tau_{MIR}
,
and it is temperature independent.
Unlike in good metals, here the classical Drude formula does not apply.

Fig.
1
(c) shows the optical conductivity Eq. (
4
) together with its imaginary part and absolute value obtained via Kramers-Kronig transformation, at a temperature
k_{B}T/\mathcal{J}=0.02
(for the cuprates, this corresponds to
T\simeq 100K
).
The power-law decay of the real part is
1/\omega
as demonstrated above, while it is
1/\omega^{0.65}
for the absolute value, matching the one measured in Ref.
van der Marel et al. 2003
.

The inset of Fig.
1
(c) shows
Re\ \sigma
at different temperatures. As anticipated from Eq. (
7
), the response is temperature independent and equal to
4\mathcal{J}\bar{\sigma}/\hbar\omega
at large frequencies (dashed line).
The peak width is set by the Planckian rate
1/\tau^{(Q)}
, that acts as an infrared cutoff (arrows), and it is therefore much narrower than
1/\tau_{MIR}
that would be expected from classical Drude theory. This makes the d.c. conductivity higher than the classical value (the resistivity, lower), and temperature dependent, as shown in Fig.
1
(b): what would classically be a bad metal, with resistivities of the order of the MIR value
\rho_{MIR}=\bar{\rho}
, is converted into a strange metal by quantum uncertainty.
Quantum effects enhancing the conductivity (vertex corrections) are still sizable at temperatures as high as
T=\mathcal{J}/k_{B}
, as shown in Fig.
1
(d), in agreement with numerical results on correlated models

Vučičević et al. 2019
;
Fratini et al. 2025
.

We finally report in Fig.
1
(e) the corresponding reflectivity spectra. Despite a sizable d.c. conductivity, the reflectivity of the quantum Drude model exhibits a steep drop and the absence of a clear plasma edge, i.e. it maintains a bad metallic character as observed in experiments
Quijada et al. 1999
(strange metals are dark, not shiny).

We now consider the possibility that the carrier motion is subdiffusive in a given time window, before normal diffusion is established at long times. This idea of ”transient subdiffusion” is similar to the one underlying transient localization theory,
that has been shown to give rise to displaced Drude peaks
Ciuchi et al. 2011
;
Fratini et al. 2016
;
Rammal et al. 2024
. The latter originate from the fact that for subdiffusive motion the conductivity strictly vanishes when
\omega\to 0
. Because the conductivity necessarily decays to zero when
\omega\to\infty
, subdiffusion implies the existence of a maximum at finite frequency.
If the subdiffusive behavior stops at a time
\tau_{in}
, a finite
d.c. conductivity
\simeq\sigma(\omega=1/\tau_{in})
is restored
(see Refs.
Ciuchi et al. 2011
;
Fratini et al. 2016
;
Rammal et al. 2024
for details).

In practice we take
\Delta X^{2}(t)\simeq\frac{Nv^{2}t^{2}}{1+(t/2\tau)^{2-\alpha}}

with
\alpha<1
, illustrated in Fig.
1
(a) (red).

2
2

2

For
\alpha=1
this formula reproduces the time dependence obtained numerically from the classical Drude formula everywhere within
15\%
, and it recovers the asymptotic limits exactly.

We insert it in Eq. (
1
), and proceed with the same steps described in the preceding section.
The high frequency behavior is now
Re\ \sigma\sim 1/\omega^{\alpha}
instead of
1/\omega
(the decay of
Abs\ \sigma
is also slower than in the diffusive case). The resistivity is still linear in temperature, but the slope is larger than in the diffusive case, as shown in Fig.
2
(a), and depends logarithmically on the extent of the subdiffusive regime.
More importantly, Fig.
2
(b) shows that
Re\ \sigma
exhibits a displaced Drude peak as anticipated, whose position shifts with temperature
Delacrétaz et al. 2017
;
Fratini and Ciuchi 2021
as
\omega_{DDP}\propto T
at low
T
(arrows).

We add that
Im\ \sigma
becomes negative at low frequencies, Fig.
2
(c), and

Re\ \epsilon
, that is always negative in a normal metal, becomes positive at low frequencies, resembling that of an insulator, Fig.(
2
)(d). The latter observation suggests that the subdiffusive paths form local, polarizable objects of finite size during the entire time window where subdiffusion lasts. The collective charge excitations formed when these objects interact through long-ranged Coulomb forces could be at the origin of the broad dispersionless signal seen in RIXS and M-EELS experiments
Arpaia et al. 2019
;
Mitrano et al. 2018
. Such ”electronic phonons” might also contribute to the pairing mechanism
Kohn and Luttinger 1965
;
Abrikosov 1978
.

Extended Drude analysis.—

Optical conductivity spectra are customarily analyzed using the so called extended Drude model, introducing a frequency-dependent scattering rate
\gamma(\omega)
and effective mass
m^{*}(\omega)
as

\sigma(\omega)=(\omega_{p}^{2}/4\pi)/[\gamma(\omega)-i\ \omega\ m^{*}(\omega)/m]
.
Experimentally it is found that the optical scattering rate
\gamma(\omega)
is roughly linear in frequency, with an offset that increases with temperature
Michon et al. 2023
. The experimental results at different temperatures show scaling behavior when plotted in units of
k_{B}T/\hbar
, which is taken as further evidence of Planckian behavior. The effective mass
m^{*}(\omega)/m
is generally a decreasing function of
\omega
at low temperatures, and it can become nonmonotonic at high temperatures
Michon et al. 2023
.
Figs.
3
(a,b) demonstrate that the quantum Drude formula Eq. (
4
) is able to rationalize these observations, including the Planckian scaling properties.

Figs.
3
(c,d) show the same quantities obtained from the subdiffusive model. In this case,

\gamma(\omega)
shows a logarithmic upturn at low frequency, that becomes prominent at high temperature
Michon et al. 2023
. The optical scattering rate
is larger than in the diffusive case, and scaling is partially lost.
Finally,
m^{*}(\omega)/m
is now non-monotonic, exhibiting a maximum whose position scales with
1/\tau^{(Q)}
.

Link to universal relaxation.—

We have recently shown that the optical conductivity in the t-J
Fratini et al. 2025
and Hubbard model
Fratini
differs from the classical Drude shape, being instead better described by the
stretched Drude
form
Re\ \sigma(\omega)=\sigma_{0}/(1+(\omega\tau)^{\nu})
with
\nu<2
.
Alternatively, the function
\sigma(\omega)=\sigma_{0}/(1-i\omega\tau)^{c}
with
c<1

is numerically similar
Hill 1978
, with the practical advantage of giving immediate access to the full complex function.
These phenomenological formulas, called respectively the Cole-Cole and Cole-Davidson functions, have been very successful in
describing the dielectric relaxation processes in insulators and liquids
Hill 1978
, yet
they have never been justified on microscopic grounds.

It turns out that these functions also
provide excellent fits to the experimental optical conductivity spectra of correlated materials, using only a very limited set of parameters (only one stretching exponent needs to be added to the usual Drude fits)
Dressel and Fratini
. In Ref.
Fratini et al. 2025

we suggested that
the stretched Drude form could actually be an identifying feature of strange metals.
In fact Eq. (
4
) proves this statement, and at the same time it provides a
theoretical basis for the popular phenomenological functions:
as shown in Fig.
1
(c), the Cole-Davidson form (dotted lines) is completely indistinguishable from the quantum Drude formula derived in this work (the stretching parameter in the shown example is
c=0.76
).

We know that a Lorentzian function in frequency is equivalent to an exponential decay in time. This is the essence of the Drude model Eq. (
3
) in the classical limit. Having demonstrated how the Drude formula is modified in the quantum regime, we can now ask ourselves to which type of relaxation this corresponds.

Let us take the Fourier transform of Eq. (
4
),
f(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(\omega t)\ \sigma^{(Q)}(\omega)\ d\omega
, which is plotted in Fig.
4
. When
k_{B}T\ll 1/\tau
we can use Eq. (
7
) and obtain, at times
t\lesssim 2\tau^{(Q)}
:

f(t)\simeq\frac{4\mathcal{J}\bar{\sigma}}{\hbar}\left[-\gamma_{EM}-\log(0.88\ t/\tau^{(Q)})\right]

(8)

with
\gamma_{EM}
the Euler-Mascheroni constant.
As shown in Fig.
4
this function is numerically identical to the Kohlrausch–Williams–Watts (KWW) stretched exponential,

f(t)=A\ e^{-(t/t_{0})^{b}}
, in a wide range of
t
.
Similar to the Cole-Cole and Cole-Davidson formulas in the frequency domain, the KWW function is empirical
Phillips 1996
;
Lukichev 2019
. The quantum Drude formula Eq. (
4
) or, for dielectrics, an equivalent quantum Debye formula, may provide a physical justification to this widely observed behavior. The physical origin of the
\log
is the
Planckian time cutoff embedded in the uncertainty principle.
