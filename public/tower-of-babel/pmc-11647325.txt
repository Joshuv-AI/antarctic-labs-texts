Assessment of a colposcopic scoring system (Swedescore) to reduce the number of cervical biopsies: a protocol for a clinical multicentre non-randomised intervention study in Denmark
Authors: Ane-Kersti Skaarup Knudsen, Tina Hovgaard Randrup, Ulrik Schiøler Kesmodel, Berit Booth, Line Gustafson, Ameli Tropé, Björn Strander, Pekka Nieminen, Anne Hammer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionColposcopy is a standard procedure for evaluating cervical abnormalities and collecting cervical biopsies. The procedure is associated with intra- and inter-observer variation. A colposcopic scoring system, Swedescore, has been designed to standardise and facilitate colposcopy training. Swedescore has performed well in a routine clinical setting when used by expert colposcopists to find or exclude high-grade lesions. Danish clinical guidelines for colposcopy differ from other countries, as they recommend collecting four cervical biopsies in all women regardless of risk factors or colposcopy findings. Swedescore has never been examined to assess the reduction of cervical biopsies in a real-world clinical setting. This study aims to investigate whether the implementation of Swedescore can optimise the diagnostic work-up for whom the collection of biopsies can be safely omitted or reduced.Methods and analysisThe design is a clinical multicentre non-randomised intervention study in Denmark. According to a power calculation, we will need to include 586 women referred for colposcopy. Colposcopy with Swedescore will be compared with conventional colposcopy with no Swedescore. Cervical biopsies will be divided into two separate vials (target and random biopsies). The primary outcome will be normal or cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 1 detected in cervical biopsies. χ2 and logistic regression will be used to compare estimates between arms.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been submitted to the Ethical Committee in Central Denmark region and is not notifiable to the Committee (j.no.: 1-10-72-124-22). Introduction

In recent years, increasing attention has been given to reduce the number of unnecessary tests, procedures and treatments within the healthcare system. Unnecessary procedures should be avoided or minimised to reduce patient harm and lower the healthcare system’s burden, including costs. In many developed countries, new diagnostic procedures are often implemented without knowing the potential impact on patients, clinicians and the load for the overall healthcare system. Thus, it is essential to evaluate the performance of new diagnostic procedures within a real clinical setting before implementation.

Colposcopy is the gold standard for evaluating cervical abnormalities, and it is used to guide the collection of cervical biopsies. Cervical precursor lesions are categorised based on the severity of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), grade 1, mild (CIN1); grade 2, moderate (CIN2); and grade 3, severe (CIN3). Swedescore is a validated colposcopic scoring tool, which previously has shown to perform well in a routine clinical setting when used by expert colposcopists to find high-grade lesions classified as CIN2+.1 2 It has never been assessed whether Swedescore can be used to determine when biopsies can safely be omitted or reduced in a routine clinical setting corresponding to normal or CIN 1 in cervical biopsies (<CIN1). This research opportunity is unique given that the current Danish clinical guidelines recommend the collection of four cervical biopsies, even when no abnormalities are visualised by colposcopy, and regardless of the woman’s risk profile.3 4 The American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology, the European Federation For Colposcopy,5 6 and other Nordic countries such as Norway, Sweden and Finland7,9 do not routinely recommend the collection of random cervical biopsies.

Screening

Cervical cancer is caused by human papillomavirus (HPV), the most common sexually transmitted infection.10 Screening aims to identify women at increased risk of the disease who need to be referred for colposcopy. Danish women aged 23 to 64 years are invited to regular cervical cancer screening every three to 5 years (table 1). In the past decade, primary HPV-based screening has progressively been replacing cytology-based screening in many developed countries as several studies have demonstrated that HPV-based screening has a higher sensitivity in detecting CIN2+ (90% vs 60%).11,13 However, HPV-based screening has a lower specificity for CIN2+ than cytology, particular in populations with a high HPV prevalence.14 Also, a previous study has shown that risk of CIN3+ varies tremendously by cytology and HPV status. For example, the immediate risk of CIN3+ is 4.4% in HPV-positive women with atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASCUS), while it is 25% in HPV-negative women with high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) and 49% in HPV-positive women with HSIL.15 In Denmark, the free of charge national screening programme is currently transitioning from cytology to HPV-based screening in women aged 30 to 59 years and is expected to be implemented for all women aged 30 years and older within a few years (table 1). This transition will presumably cause a noticeable workload of HPV-screen positive women who will be referred for colposcopy.16

Vaccination

Besides cervical cancer screening, cervical cancer and precancer can be prevented through prophylactic HPV vaccination.17 HPV vaccination is free of charge in Denmark and was implemented in the childhood immunisation programme for girls in 2009 and for boys in 2019. In Denmark, the proportion of children completing the HPV vaccination programme is 75% for girls born in 1993–2010.18 Thus, the initial vaccination cohorts are currently part of the cervical cancer screening programme in Denmark. Catch-up programmes were implemented in 2008 and 2012 targeting girls born in 1993–1995 and 1985–1992, respectively.19 However, the cohorts in the catch-up programmes have a higher risk of high-grade lesions or worse, compared with girls who are HPV vaccinated as recommended before sexual debut.20 Furthermore, the Danish clinical guidelines for colposcopy do not distinguish between vaccinated or unvaccinated subjects. Hence, the rethinking of the Danish clinical guideline and the improvement of diagnostic work-up with colposcopy is critical in years to come.

Colposcopy

The main goals of colposcopy are to inspect the cervix, define the transformation zone (TZ) type, identify the squamocolumnar junction (SCJ) and abnormal areas that should be subjected to biopsy collection, and make a colposcopic diagnosis. As the subsequent clinical management relies on this, colposcopy remains an important part of cervical cancer prevention. Colposcopy is associated with a high intra and inter-observer variation,21 22 and the sensitivity for CIN2+ is suboptimal in most studies at about 55–57%,23,25 while one study reported a sensitivity of 90%.26 A previous review on diagnostic accuracy in performance of colposcopy describes a wide range of reported sensitivity and specificity for CIN2+ in the studies and various forms of bias to consider. The colposcopic impression provides an initial assessment, but taking cervical biopsies is necessary to confirm the histopathological diagnosis.27 Studies have shown that the collection of multiple biopsies during colposcopy increases the detection of CIN2+.28,31 Therefore, to reduce the risk of underdiagnosis, Danish clinical guidelines recommend the collection of four biopsies, even when no abnormalities are detected, and regardless of the women’s cervical screening history, HPV vaccination status or other risk factors.4 As mentioned, a growing proportion of women in screening will have received the HPV vaccine, which reduces the likelihood of CIN3+. Similarly, women who are HPV positive with ASCUS have a very low immediate risk of CIN2+. If all women continue to have multiple biopsies collected, regardless of colposcopic findings and other risk factors, women will be at increased risk of overtreatment and unnecessary harm. This is important because collecting multiple biopsies in all women, regardless of visible lesions, may cause an increased risk of side effects (vaginal discharge, bleeding, pain, pre- and postcolposcopy anxiety).32 Second, it may be more time-consuming and expensive due to pathology overload.33,35

Colposcopic scoring systems

Two colposcopic scoring systems have been developed to improve the detection of cervical precancer: the Reid index36,38 and the Swedescore.1 The Swedescore (table 2) is the more modern and updated scoring system and was designed to standardise and systematise the examination and facilitate colposcopy training. The Swedescore was developed in 2005 to predict CIN2+ and is endorsed by the International Agency for Research on Cancer39 and the International Federation of Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology.40 The Swedescore can be used when the SCJ is visible, that is, TZ type 1 or type 2. The Swedescore is not applicable for TZ 3, since SCJ is non-visible, which causes an incomplete or unsatisfactory colposcopy. In the original clinical study, sensitivity and specificity were high, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.87. The Swedescore entails the use of acetic acid and Lugol’s iodine, while conventional colposcopy usually entails the use of acetic acid alone. Thus, in both cases, the colposcopic impression relies heavily on the observer’s ability to recognise patterns. Most studies have mainly included women referred due to abnormal cervical cytology, as screening historically has been cytology-based.1 Thus, we do not know whether Swedescore performs equally well in an HPV-based screening cohort or in an HPV vaccinated cohort. Moreover, it remains unclear whether Swedescore can be used in the diagnostic work-up to identify women where the collection of biopsies can be safely reduced or omitted. This is particularly important to explore in a country like Denmark, where there is no systematised training or certification of colposcopy.

Objectives

This study will investigate if Swedescore can be used in a routine clinical setting to determine when collecting of biopsies is not needed because the risk of CIN2+ is low. The potential patient benefit and desirable reduction in histopathological overload are of great interest from a national and international perspective.

Hypotheses

We hypothesise that cervical biopsies can be reduced or omitted when using Swedescore, compared with conventional colposcopy method without using Swedescore. Thus, we hypothesise that, compared with women in the reference arm, women in the Swedescore arm will be less likely to have disease detected in the random biopsies that are more severe than that observed in targeted biopsies. The primary outcomes will be histopathological diagnosis with normal or CIN 1 in cervical biopsies (< CIN1).

Methods and analysis

Study design and population

We will conduct a clinical multicentre non-randomised intervention study in Denmark. We will include women referred for colposcopy due to an abnormal cervical screening test (cytology and/or high-risk HPV (hrHPV)). Women will be eligible for inclusion if they have a TZ type 1 or type 2 according to the 2011 International Federation of Cervical Pathology and Colposcopy nomenclature.41 Women will be excluded if they have a TZ type 3, atrophic cervical mucosa (impossible to use Lugol’s iodine), allergy towards Lugol’s iodine, are pregnant, had a previous hysterectomy or due to methodological errors (ie, no colposcopy performed, biopsies not collected in two separate vials, cervical biopsies are insufficient or not available in the Pathology Databank) (figure 1). Women will be recruited by colposcopic personnel (doctors and nurses) from five public colposcopy clinics and two private gynaecology clinics in the Central Denmark Region and Southern Denmark Region (table 3). All healthcare costs for the referred patients to public and private colposcopy clinics are free of charge in Denmark, and women can choose if they want to be referred to a public or private clinic. All sites follow the same Danish clinical guideline, and there is no patient fee or difference in patient rights at the included sites. Prior to recruitment, all clinicians taking part in enrolment at inclusion sites will receive standard education about HPV, the Danish cervical cancer screening programme, Danish clinical cervical cancer and precancer guidelines, and basic interpretation of colposcopy findings. Colposcopic evaluation will be performed using one of two methods: No Swedescore method (reference clinics) and Swedescore method (intervention clinics). In addition, the intervention clinics will receive extended education about the Swedescore and use of Lugol’s iodine for pattern recognition. We aim at including both private and public colposcopy clinics in each arm to ensure representativeness in the study population. As the number of referrals differ significantly between intended colposcopy clinics, sites will be allocated to Swedescore or No Swedescore taking this into account. All intended sites have agreed to participate. Reference clinics will perform conventional colposcopy as follows: colposcopy begins with cleaning of the cervix followed by application of freshly mixed acetic acid, while the cervix is inspected for a total of at least 2 min. Findings will be documented, including the visualisation of the TZ, presence/absence of acetowhitening, capillary patterns and mosaic/punctuation. Also, a clinical colposcopic impression (negative/normal, low grade, high grade, cancer) will be recorded. Intervention clinics will use the Swedescore method, which besides the conventional colposcopy includes the application of Lugol’s iodine and the collection of the score (0–2) for five variables and the total score (0–10) for Swedescore (table 2). In all clinics, enrolled women will have four cervical biopsies collected complying with Danish clinical guidelines. Cervical biopsies will be taken with a biopsy forceps and then placed in two separate vials containing formalin and marked with the number 1 or 2. The clinicians will be responsible for which vials contain which biopsies, and that the correct vial number follows the pathology request in the Pathology Databank. The clinicians decide how many biopsies (1, 2 or 3) are sent in each vial (the total number is 4 according to current Danish clinical guidelines). Each inclusion site will have a non-blinded research vial guideline, which is known by the principal investigators, and blinded for the collaborating pathology department. Biopsies will be collected from all abnormal or areas suspected of cervical precancer (targeted biopsies). Subsequently, the other biopsies will be collected from areas that are classified as normal (random biopsies). Targeted and random biopsies will be sent for histopathological examination in separate vials. In the case only random biopsies (normal colposcopy) or targeted biopsies (cervix with a large lesion or many lesions) are collected, biopsies will be sent in two separate vials as well to determine if the number of biopsies can be reduced without compromising diagnostic accuracy (figure 2). If the histologic diagnosis of the random biopsies is less severe than that in targeted biopsies, but only in the Swedescore arm and not the reference arm, the number of random biopsies can be reduced or safely omitted. This may result in a need to update the current Danish clinical guideline.

Cytology and human papillomavirus (HPV) testing

Cervical cytology will be reported according to the Bethesda 2014 grading system: ‘low-grade’ will include ASCUS and low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL). ‘High-grade’ will include HSIL, atypical squamous cells cannot exclude high-grade lesion (ASC-H), atypical glandular cells (AGC) and adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS). HPV type will be categorised as negative, HPV 16, HPV 18 or hrHPV non-16/18. HPV testing will be performed using Cobas 4800 DNA test (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland).

Pathology

Cervical biopsies will be sent for histopathological examination at three Danish Pathology Departments (Viborg, Randers, Vejle) with extensive experience in gynaecological pathology. Samples will be processed in the pathology laboratory along with other routine samples. Each vial with biopsies will get a separate histopathological diagnosis and be reported using the CIN grading as follows: normal (including inflammation and reactive changes), CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, AIS or cancer, which also will be according to the current WHO classifications.42 When there is not enough tissue or other deficiencies present, the biopsy will be deemed not representative or inadequate. Grading of cervical biopsies will be performed by the local pathologists who are blinded from all clinical characteristics except the age of the woman, result of the current screening test, screening history and previous histopathological diagnosis. The pathologists have no knowledge of which vials contain target or random biopsies.

Data collection

On all women enrolled, we will state the given colposcopic method (No Swedescore vs Swedescore), and from medical records, we will collect baseline characteristics such as age, parity, screening history, HPV vaccination status, previous cervical disease including conisation, menopause status, gynaecological symptoms, body mass index and smoking status. These baseline characteristics will be self-reported information from the visit when the women are referred to colposcopy, except results on previous and current screening history, cervical biopsies and/or conisation, which will be retrieved from the Danish Pathology Databank. From medical records, we will collect information on type of TZ, colposcopic findings (acetowhitening, vessel patterns, and mosaic and/or punctuation), the colposcopic impression (normal, low grade, high grade or cancer) and number of cervical biopsies (target and random) collected. For intervention clinics, the total score of Swedescore will be obtained. The histological diagnosis will be collected for the two separate vials in the Pathology Databank.

Missing data from baseline characteristics will be reported, and if relevant, a statistical imputation method will be used. All data will be entered and stored in REDCap, a secure web application for building and managing online surveys and databases.43 44 Data management and statistical analyses will be performed in STATA (V.18; StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Statistical analysis

Primary outcome will be <CIN1 detected in the random cervical biopsies. We will report the number and percentage of women with <CIN1 in random biopsies among women undergoing colposcopy with Swedescore compared with the percentage in those undergoing conventional colposcopy without Swedescore, including 95% CIs. Same analysis will be presented for target biopsies between the two groups. Results will be reported overall and stratified by age (groups in years: 23–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50+), screening result (ASCUS/LSIL with/without HPV vs HSIL, ASC-H, AGC, AIS with/without HPV) and HPV vaccination status (vaccinated vs not vaccinated), to assess potential differences across subgroups. For the Swedescore method, the total score (1–10) will be presented with the given histopathological diagnosis in percentages (< CIN1) for both target and random biopsies. Crude comparison of the outcome across the two groups will be made using χ2 test. Using logistic regression analysis, we will adjust for potential confounders, such as age, screening result and HPV vaccination status.

Sample size

Based on a power calculation, including review of previous studies and clinical experience, we anticipate an overall detection rate of <CIN1 at 70% for colposcopy without the Swedescore method. To detect an effect size of minimal clinical significance of 10% in the proportion of histologically verified <CIN1 between intervention and reference clinics (70% vs 80%), we estimate a sample size of n=586 (293 in each group of reference and intervention clinics), with a statistical power (β) of 80% and a significance level (α) 0.05. As we assume 40% will not be eligible, we need to recruit a minimum of 938 women. The sample size stated on ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05870787 originates from an overall study for comparison of the Swedescore method (intervention clinics) to the conventional method without Swedescore (reference clinics) in detecting CIN2+, where the estimated enrolment will include 1066 women. Hence, this population will be used for the statistical analysis of this study for the detection of <CIN1 in cervical biopsies with n=586.

Timeline

Enrolment started 1 May 2023, at one inclusion site, and as from January 2024, four additional sites are enrolling participants. We expect enrolment to be finished at inclusion sites by 1 July 2025. If needed, the recruitment period can be extended. Hence, recruitment will expectedly take place in the period of 1 May 2023 to 1 July 2025, and if the sample size is reached before the end date, the recruitment will finish.

Dissemination

Results from this project may generate important knowledge on the value of the Swedescore in clinical practice when used by clinicians with various degrees of colposcopic training. Findings may potentially result in a revision of the Danish clinical guidelines recommending not always taking four biopsies. With this peer-reviewed protocol article, the study is made public. The study results will be presented at national and international academic meetings. The study results will be submitted for publication in a relevant peer-reviewed journal. Finally, press releases will be made available to the public.

Discussion

This is the first clinical intervention study to explore whether Swedescore can be used in a routine clinical setting to assess when it is safe to omit or reduce the number of cervical biopsies. This aspect is critical to examine for relevant and evidence-based diagnostic work-up of women with an abnormal cervical cancer screening test. Collection of cervical biopsies is associated with pain, bleeding, discharge, and pre- and postcolposcopy anxiety.32 From a public health point of view, it is important to reduce the number of unnecessary tests, procedures and treatments in the healthcare system. New procedures may be cost-effective, have lack of clinical evidence, be time-consuming and have no benefit for the involved patients. Also, in daily practice, diagnostic procedures are often introduced and implemented without knowing much about their potential impact. This is concerning because adopting new diagnostic procedures may put an additional burden on healthcare professionals and the patients. Furthermore, it may turn out that the new diagnostic procedure does not improve patient care. Therefore, it is critical that performance of new diagnostic procedures, such as the Swedescore, is properly investigated before implementing it in clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this study are the unique possibility to include patients in a real clinical setting prospectively, where a new diagnostic colposcopic tool can be explored before considering implementation. This is especially relevant since, in Denmark, collection of four biopsies is still recommended in the Danish clinical guidelines based on results from previous studies showing a correlation between the number of biopsies and CIN2+ detection rate.24 25 30 31 Nevertheless, the referred population of women with an abnormal screening test is changing, particularly with the current transition to HPV based screening, and more women will have received the HPV vaccine in the years to come. Hence, this study is not only relevant in Denmark but internationally.

Another strength is that the pathologists are blinded. Also, by separating the cervical biopsies in two vials, we get independent histopathological diagnosis for both target and random biopsies. This multicentre study will be conducted in Denmark where screening, treatment and follow-up for cervical precancer are free of charge for all included participants. We find it as a strength that inclusion sites represent both private and public colposcopy clinics in each arm.

A limitation is the doctors’ cross-over, since Denmark is a relatively small country and staff shifting over the study period is impossible to prevent. However, these challenges reflect the real-life clinical setting. Thus, information bias can be a limitation due to doctors’ cross-overs and due to research sharing at regional/national meetings or conferences. Another limitation of the study can be delayed inclusion of patients or missing standard education for new doctors at the inclusion sites. Hence, a local investigator has been assigned to each site to prevent enrolment interruptions. Furthermore, the risk of detection bias is present, since clinicians are not blinded when performing the colposcopy and cervical biopsies are collected, however, this simply reflects colposcopic management and clinical reality.

In clinical research, randomised study designs are often preferred because confounding is limited. We considered conducting a stepped wedge randomisation design. However, it is necessary to explore whether Swedescore can be used to reduce cervical biopsies in a real-life clinical setting before implementation. Second, the clusters having agreed to participate are expectedly relatively homogeneous, and hence parallel studies tend to deliver better statistical performance than a stepped wedge randomisation designed studies.46 Thus, the strength of using parallel non-randomised cluster is preferable in this clinical multicentre study with two arms.

Perspectives

Many healthcare systems are facing crises of excess demand, increased number of visits related to negligible conditions/diseases and escalating medicine costs. Recent evidence suggests that it is a result of overdiagnosis, overtreatment and overall low-value care. This study will provide important knowledge regarding whether a diagnostic tool in routine clinical practice can lower the number of cervical biopsies, without missing disease, especially in benefit for the patient, but also a reduction in the current workload for pathologists. Thus, it is of major clinical importance to assess a colposcopic diagnostic tool such as Swedescore before implementation. Findings from this study may be useful for many colposcopy clinics in several countries. Thus, study results may cause a change in national guidelines and potentially reduce the number of unnecessary procedures, which is of great value for the patient, the colposcopy clinics, the pathology departments, and the healthcare system.
