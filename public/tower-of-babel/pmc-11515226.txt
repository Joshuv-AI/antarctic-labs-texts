Interobserver variability of clinical target volume delineation in patients undergoing breast-conserving surgery without surgical clips: a pilot study on preoperative magnetic resonance simulation
Authors: Shuning Jiao, Yiqing Wang, Jiabin Ma, Jing Shen, Xi-Qian Zhang, Bing Zhou, Xiansong Sun, Haoran Xu, Xia Liu, Ke Hu, Fuquan Zhang, Xiaorong Hou, Jie Qiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn patients undergoing breast-conserving therapy without surgical clip implantation, the accuracy of tumor bed identification and the consistency of clinical target volume (CTV) delineation under computed tomography (CT) simulation remain suboptimal. This study aimed to investigate the feasibility of implementing preoperative magnetic resonance (MR) simulation on delineations by assessing interobserver variability (IOV).MethodsPreoperative MR and postoperative CT simulations were performed in patients who underwent breast-conserving surgery with no surgical clips implanted. Custom immobilization pads were used to ensure the same supine position. Three radiation oncologists independently delineated the CTV of tumor bed on the images acquired from MR and CT simulation registration and CT simulation alone. Cavity visualization score (CVS) was assigned to each patient based on the clarity of the tumor bed on CT simulation images. IOV was indicated by generalized conformity index (CIgen), denoted as CIgen−CT and CIgen−MR/CT, and the distance between the centroid of mass (dCOM), denoted as dCOMCT and dCOMMR/CT. The variation of IOV in different CVS subgroups was analyzed.ResultsA total of 10 patients were enrolled in this study. The median and interquartile range (IQR) of maximum pathological diameter of the tumors in all patients were 1.55 (0.80–1.92) cm. No statistical significance was found between the volumes of CTVs on CT simulation and on MR/CT simulation registration images (p = 0.387). CIgen−MR/CT was significantly larger than CIgen−CT (p = 0.005). dCOMMR/CT was significantly smaller than dCOMCT (p = 0.037). The median and IQR of CVS in all patients were 2.34 (2.00–3.08). The difference of CIgen between CIgen−MR/CT and CIgen−CT was larger in the low CVS group (p = 0.016). The difference of dCOM showed a decreasing trend when CVS was lower, although it did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.095).ConclusionsFor patients who underwent breast-conserving surgery without surgical clip implantation, the use of preoperative MR simulation in delineating the CTV of tumor bed decreased the IOV among observers. The consistency of tumor bed identification was improved especially in cases where the margins of tumor bed were challenging to visualize on CT simulation images. The study findings offer potential benefits in reducing local recurrence and minimizing tissue irritation in the surrounding areas. Background

Adjuvant radiotherapy following breast-conserving surgery is recommended due to its significant impact, reducing the risk of local recurrence from 26 to 7% and improving 15-year survival rates by 5.4% in patients with early-stage breast cancer [1]. Since local recurrences mainly occur in proximity to the primary tumor site, a tumor bed boost following standard whole breast radiation therapy (WBRT) could further reduce the risk, as evidenced by a hazard ratio of 0.65 from a 20-year follow-up randomized clinical trial [2]. Therefore, WBRT followed by a tumor bed boost is recommended as a first-line treatment for patients at high risk of local recurrence [3]. Accelerated partial breast irradiation (APBI) is considered an alternative approach, as it can achieve equivalent local control to WBRT in low-risk patients, while reducing the irradiated volume and mitigating adverse events [4, 5]. For both approaches, ensuring accurate target volume delineation becomes fundamental in order to minimize the risk for local recurrence and prevent excessive radiation which could lead to a decrease in quality of life.

The current identification of the tumor bed relies on structural interpretation from CT simulation images, supported by a combination of preoperative imaging data, the surgical scar, surgical clips, and the presence of seroma when available. Computed tomography (CT) simulation is the most commonly used imaging modality in clinical practice; nevertheless, it is limited by low soft tissue contrast and ionizing radiation [6]. While the current guidelines recommend the use of surgical clips as auxiliary positioning markers in breast-conserving surgery, the position of the surgical clips may change under certain circumstances, leading to inaccurate target volumes of the tumor bed [7, 8]. Due to cultural differences and economic considerations, many patients are reluctant about foreign body implantations. Therefore, some institutions, including ours, have not implemented the use of surgical clips in breast-conserving surgeries [9, 10]. Some radiation oncologists refer to the seroma that develops after surgery to delineate the target volume, but studies have shown that the seroma varies greatly over time, with the seroma volume decreasing by up to 70% during the course of radiotherapy, which may lead to excessive irradiation to adjacent tissues [11, 12]. At the same time, the formation of seroma is subject to institutional procedures, such as some hospitals perform closed cavity surgeries using full-thickness closure, which leads to reduced seroma formation and obscures the actual boundaries of the tumor bed [13, 14]. Consequently, accurate delineation of the target volume remains a subject that requires further study, particularly for patients without the assistance of surgical clips or seromas. While comparison with pathology is considered the gold standard for assessing accuracy, it is rarely achievable. Reducing interobserver variability (IOV) and reaching agreement among experienced radiation oncologists for consistent target volume delineation are crucial, as the target volume determined by consensus is presumed to be closest to the ground truth [15]. Furthermore, these factors establish the basis for standardized and reproducible treatment plans, ensuring uniform quality of cancer care across institutions and patient cohorts. They also improve the precision of measurements, thereby enhancing the credibility and applicability of research findings in radiation therapy.

Magnetic resonance (MR) simulation offers several advantages over CT simulation, particularly in terms of enhancing soft tissue visualization and the ability to generate multiple sequences that assist in the localization of tumor beds. Numerous observational studies have confirmed the superiority of MR over CT simulation for tumor bed visualization in breast cancer patients [10, 16, 17]. Our previous work has explored the advantages of postoperative MR simulation over standard CT simulation for target volume delineation of the tumor bed in patients undergoing closed cavity breast-conserving surgery without surgical clip implantation. Compared with CT, postoperative MR simulation has shown improvements in tumor bed visualization, lowering the target volume, and reducing IOV [16]. Building upon these findings, the concept of co-registering preoperative MR simulation has been proposed, as it could offer additional valuable information for target volume delineation by providing the tumor location in situ and characterizing normal tissues in adjacency, such as the breast glandular tissue, meanwhile mitigating potential interference resulting from post-surgical seroma. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the advantages of incorporating preoperative MR simulation with standard CT simulation in target volume delineation for patients undergoing breast-conserving therapy without surgical clips. We also compared the IOV of patients categorized into high and low cavity visualization score (CVS) subgroups to explore factors influencing the IOV variation and to identify those who would benefit most from the application of preoperative MR simulation.

Methods

Patient selection

Patients who had undergone preoperative physical and radiological assessments and were clinically diagnosed with breast cancer were prospectively enrolled. Preoperative radiological assessments included breast ultrasound, mammography, MRI and CT. Patients who met the following criteria were included: female; aged ≥ 18 years; diagnosed with unilateral primary breast cancer by preoperative evaluation; not previously diagnosed with other malignant tumors; planned to receive breast-conserving surgery without surgical clip implantation and postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy; not contraindicated for MR imaging, such as having metal implants or a history of claustrophobia. Patients who met the following criteria were excluded: not clinically diagnosed with T1−2N0−1M0; received neoadjuvant treatment; failed to receive breast-conserving surgery; received oncoplastic surgery; not pathologically diagnosed as primary breast cancer; pregnant or lactating. All eligible patients underwent preoperative MR simulation, breast-conserving surgery and postoperative CT simulation.

Simulation and image acquisition

Preoperative MR simulation was performed within one week prior to the surgery. Immobilization pads were custom-made for each patient using Styrofoam, a thermoplastic material. Patients took a supine position on the pad with both arms elevated and abducted, just as the position planned for postoperative radiotherapy (Fig. 1). Patients’ upper body area including the head, neck, shoulders, arms, back, and lumbar region was provided with a stable and comfortable support to minimize the position shifts between MR and CT simulations. Radiofrequency coils and their support were adjusted to a suitable position to minimize the deformation of breasts. MR simulation images were acquired with a 3.0-T, 70-cm bore simulator (Ingenia Elition X, Philips, Holland). The scan range extended from below the chin to 5 cm below the breast fold. The following MR sequences were included for their specific benefits in breast imaging: T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), T2-weighted spectral attenuated inversion recovery (T2W-SPAIR), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). T1WI is excellent for illustrating breast anatomy, while T2WI is valuable for assessing lesion size, margins, and tumor invasion into surrounding tissues. T2W-SPAIR suppresses the background signal from fat tissues, hence is particularly useful in patients with a high percentage of fat in the breasts. By assessing the motion of water molecules, DWI is sensitive to microstructural changes and tissue cellularity, aiding in assessing tumor aggressiveness in breast cancer [18]. The 2.5 mm scanning slice thickness was selected based on the American College of Radiology (ACR) consensus recommendations, adjusted according to the machine acceptance testing, and validated through clinical experience to achieve optimal lesion-normal tissue contrast. The specific parameters for each MRI sequence are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. For each patient, the frame of reference was the same in all sequences to ensure consistency in subsequent registration.

Body positioning during the simulation. (A) anterior body coils; (B) flexible coils; (C) immobilization pad; (D) coil support

When performing CT simulation, patients used the same immobilization pad and took the same posture to maintain the physical position as consistent as possible. Lead wires were placed along the surgical scars to indicate the incision sites. CT simulation images were acquired with an 80-cm bore CT simulator (Revolution ES, GE Healthcare, USA) with a 5-mm slice thickness and an in-plane pixel value of 0.98 × 0.98 mm2. The scan range was the same as that for MR simulation. Both MR and CT simulation images underwent quality control by at least one radiotherapy technologist and one radiation oncologist before being approved for use, then imported to the Varian Eclipse treatment planning system (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, USA).

MR simulation images were reconstructed to 5-mm slice thickness before registering to CT simulation images in the planning system. Automatic rigid registration was first performed based on body structures such as the nipple, breast border, and bones. Manual adjustment was allowed if the fusion consistency was not satisfactory. All MR sequences were accessible and reviewed during registration to achieve optimal breast gland alignment. The registration process was completed when all observers confirmed that the images could be accepted.

Target volume delineation

Three experienced radiation oncologists served as observers in this study and independently delineated the CTV of tumor bed. A consensus meeting was held by all observers and an expert in breast radiotherapy to develop written delineation standards. The principle of delineating the CTV of tumor bed on CT was based on guidelines [19]. The medial margin of CTV was restricted by the chest wall or pectoral muscles, and the lateral margin was restricted by 5 mm below the skin surface. The identification of tumor bed on CT and MR registration images relied on the location of the primary lesion, the adjacent relationship with surrounding glandular tissue, and possible postoperative changes such as seroma. The CTV was ultimately delineated on the CT simulation image. Lead wire marks, preoperative physical and radiological examination results, surgical records and pathological reports were provided as references when contouring. For each patient, each observer first assigned a cavity visualization score to the CT simulation image based on the following criteria [20]: 1, cavity not visualized; 2, cavity visualized but margins indistinct; 3, cavity visualized with some distinct margins; 4, cavity visualized with all but one margin distinct; and 5, all cavity margins clearly defined. Then, the observers delineated the CTV of tumor bed on the CT simulation images. After at least two weeks, the observers delineated the CTV of tumor bed on the CT and MR registration images. Each observer was blinded to his or her previous delineation on CT and the delineations of other observers.

Data processing and statistical analysis

The mean CVS was calculated for each patient. The volumes of the CTV of tumor bed were marked as CTVCT and CTVMR/CT. The location of centroid of mass (COM) of the CTV was recorded in three-dimensional coordinates. The IOV generally refers to the degree of difference in the identification or measurement made by different observers when assessing the same phenomenon, in the scenario of this study, the target volume [21]. In the present study, the IOV was measured by the generalized conformity index (CIgen) and the distance between the centroid of mass (dCOM). CIgen was defined as the sum of overlapping volumes of all possible pairs divided by the sum of unions of all possible pairs [22]. A larger CIgen indicated a smaller IOV. The equation is shown as:

with Ai representing each CTV. For each patient, CIgen calculated using target volumes delineated on CT simulation images alone was referred to as CIgen−CT, while CIgen obtained based on delineations on MR and CT registration images was denoted as CIgen−MR/CT. dCOM was calculated by the geometric distance between the coordinates of COMs. The mean values of dCOM were marked as dCOMCT and dCOMMR/CT for each patient. A smaller dCOM indicated a smaller IOV. When the value of dCOM was 0, the COMs were at the same location, indicating the best consistency.

Shapiro–Wilk test was used to examine whether the variables conformed to normal distributions. Variables that did not conform normality were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test, a nonparametric test used to compare differences between related paired samples, was applied to compare the volume of CTV, CIgen and dCOM between delineations on CT and on MR/CT simulation registration images. The Spearman’s correlation analysis, a nonparametric test used to assess the strength and direction of the association between two variables, was performed to explore the relationship between the IOV parameters and CVS. The Mann–Whitney–U test, a nonparametric test used to compare differences between two independent groups, was performed to assess the variations of CIgen and dCOM between different CVS subgroups. Two-tailed p values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and Graphpad Prism 9.5 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 10 patients were enrolled in this study between May and August 2023 and met the eligibility criteria. All patients underwent breast-conserving surgery without surgical clips implanted. Pathology revealed that all patients had confirmed negative margins, and they were scheduled to receive adjuvant radiotherapy. All patients underwent MR simulation with a median interval of 4 days (range: 1–7 days) prior to surgery. Six patients underwent CT simulation with a median interval of 45 days (range: 43–49 days) after the surgery. The remaining four patients, who received postoperative chemotherapy, underwent CT simulation with a median interval of 150 days (range: 140–159 days) after the surgery. Comparison of the IOV

The median and interquartile range (IQR) of CTVCT were 12.40 (8.20–14.80) cm3. The median and IQR of CTVMR/CT were 10.75 (8.68–13.38) cm3. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed no statistical significance between the volumes of CTV delineated on different modalities (Z=-0.865, p = 0.387).

The median and IQR of CIgen−CT were calculated to be 0.47 (0.43–0.51), while those of CIgen−MR/CT were 0.57 (0.54–0.63). CIgen−MR/CT was significantly larger than CIgen−CT (Z=-2.803, p = 0.005), which suggested a higher degree of overlap in the delineation of CTVs. The median and IQR of dCOMCT were calculated to be 0.56 (0.39–0.69) cm, while those of dCOMMR/CT were 0.34 (0.31–0.40) cm. dCOMMR/CT was significantly smaller than dCOMCT (Z=-2.090, p = 0.037), which suggested greater similarities in identifying the locations of the tumor beds. Thus, the IOV of delineations on CT simulation images were larger than that on MR/CT registration images. The use of preoperative MR simulation greatly increased the consistency of the CTV delineations of tumor bed in the breast. Two patients’ preoperative MR simulation images of various sequences, postoperative CT simulation image, and delineations of the CTVs are shown in Fig. 2 as examples to illustrate the difference in IOV. Variation of IOV between different CVS subgroups

The median and IQR of CVS for all patients were 2.34 (2.00–3.08). The average CVS calculated for most patients from the 3 observers was 2.0, as shown in Fig. 3. No correlation was found between CIgen−CT and CVS (p = 0.272) or between CIgen−MR/CT and CVS (p = 0.283). The correlation between dCOM and CVS was not significant (p = 0.225 for dCOMCT; p = 0.805 for dCOMMR/CT).

CIgen (A) and dCOM (B) as a function of CVS for CT and MR/CT delineations of CTV. Patient numbers are specified for each modality

To explore whether CVS had an impact on the variation of IOV, the patients were divided into two subgroups based on the median CVS, 2.34. As shown in Fig. 4, although the difference of dCOM (dCOM–diff) between dCOMMR/CT and dCOMCT showed a decreasing trend between the CVS ≤ 2.34 group and the CVS > 2.34 group, it was not statistically significant (Z=-1.776, p = 0.095) (Fig. 4B). However, the difference of CIgen (CIgen–diff) between CIgen−MR/CT and CIgen−CT was significantly larger in the CVS ≤ 2.34 group than in the CVS > 2.34 group (Z=-2.402, p = 0.016) (Fig. 4A). This may suggest that for patients whose tumor bed is less clear on CT simulation images, the application of preoperative MR simulation would result in greater benefits regarding delineation consistency.

Discussion

In this study, the role of preoperative MR simulation in patients undergoing breast-conserving surgery was assessed by comparing the IOV of delineations using co-registered MR and CT simulation images versus CT simulation alone. Among the ten patients enrolled in the study, no surgical clips were implanted, and seromas were rarely seen on the postoperative CT simulation images due to closed cavity surgery. Preoperative MR simulation was found to improve the consistency among observers, as indicated by the reduction of IOV in the CTV delineations of tumor bed. In particular, the preoperative MR simulation images resulted in a better visualization of the locations of the primary lesions and their relationships with adjacent breast tissues, notably glandular tissues. This advantage holds significant potential for more accurate contouring, especially for tumor beds with unclear margins and a low CVS. In addition, both MR and CT simulations were acquired in a supine position, with the use of custom-made pads to maintain consistent body positioning across both simulations and subsequent irradiation treatments.

Currently, breast-conserving therapy remains the primary treatment option for early-stage breast cancer. Large cohort studies have consistently demonstrated improved overall survival among patients undergoing breast-conserving therapy in comparison with mastectomy, with a relative risk of 0.64 (95% confidence interval 0.55–0.74) [23]. Such survival benefits underscore the importance of radiotherapy and emphasize the significance of accurately identifying tumor beds for effective treatment and local control. Despite guidelines recommending surgical clip placement, a significant number of patients opt against clip implantation due to cultural, economic, and institutional factors. Nevertheless, there is an absence of standardized practices on tumor bed identification for this subset of population. The visualization of the tumor bed on postoperative CT simulation is often inadequate when surgical clips are omitted, and it becomes even more challenging when closed cavity surgeries are utilized, as postoperative fluids rarely accumulate within the tumor cavity due to full-thickness closure. As demonstrated by Dzhugashvili et al. [24], many of these cases received low CVS scores of 1 to 2 from both experts and trainee oncologists, indicating common inaccuracies in tumor bed identification for these patients. Moreover, other researchers noted that in patients where surgical clips were used, the contouring still relies on the oncologists’ experiences [11]. These inconsistencies can either compromise target volume accuracy or result in excessive irradiation to normal tissues, leading to an increased risk of local recurrence, radiation dermatitis, and tissue fibrosis. Therefore, there is a pressing need to find effective and clinically feasible methods to determine the CTV and achieve precise radiation delivery for early-stage breast cancer patients.

Preoperative MR images offer the highest sensitivity in breast cancer detection among current clinical imaging modalities, enabling the discrimination between benign and malignant tumors, and the detection of multiple tumors that are often missed by ultrasonography [25, 26]. Moreover, previous studies have indicated that compared to ultrasonography and mammography, MRI provides unique advantages for high-risk breast cancer patients, especially those with invasive lobular cancer or premenopausal status [27, 28]. In our study, we acquired T1WI, T2WI, T2W-SPAIR and DWI sequences for each patient. The rationale for choosing these four sequences was tied to the reported advantages of each sequence in visualizing tumor morphology. Unlike several previous studies focusing on the role of MR simulation [29, 30], we did not compare the differences in the delineations between individual MR sequences; instead, all MR sequences, along with each patient’s various medical records were available to the observers during contouring. In this way, the collective advantages of MR sequences were assessed simultaneously, which is believed to be more clinically relevant.

In our study, the application of preoperative MR simulation resulted in an absolute increase of 0.10 in the median CIgen, representing a relative increase of 21.3% compared to delineations on CT simulation images alone. The dCOM also exhibited a significant decrease with the addition of MR registration, with the median dCOM decreasing from 0.56 to 0.34 cm. dCOMMR/CT demonstrated a smaller IQR, which may suggest a more concentrated and robust distribution. Our findings align with den Hartogh et al. [31] and Jiang et al. [30] who studied the impact of contrast‑enhanced MR imaging (CE-MRI) in patients with surgical clips. Den Hartogh et al. [31] showed that additional preoperative supine CE-MRI improved the CI from 0.57 to 0.59 and decreased dCOM from 5.11 to 4.56 mm compared to CT alone. In the study conducted by Jiang et al. [30], preoperative CE-MR simulation in the prone position also significantly increased CI. In contrast to their study design, we maintained the therapeutic position in both simulation imaging sessions and omitted the use of contrast dye, thereby reducing positional variation and expanding the number of eligible patients for clinical practice.

Our study found no significant difference in the volumes of CTV respectively generated from CT simulation and MR/CT simulation registration. However, previous studies have shown heterogeneous results regarding the impact of MR images on CTV. Dong et al. [32] observed no difference in CTV between CT and MR sequences, except for dyn-eTHRIVE. Jiang et al. [30] reported a universal reduction in CTV across all preoperative MR sequences compared to CT simulation. Conversely, den Hartogh et al. [31] reported an increase in the tumor bed volume with the addition of preoperative MR imaging. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in patient positioning and the impact of immobilization techniques. In addition, scanning sequences and imaging qualities may influence the results, as partial volume effect may affect the target volume interpretation, especially at low resolutions. Den Hartogh et al. utilized a 1.5 T MRI for dynamic T1WI Dixon sequence with a 1.2 × 1.21 × 2.4 mm3 voxel size. The detailed parameters of MR images were not mentioned in the study by Jiang et al. Dong et al. used a 3.0 T MR scanner and acquired sequences with in-plane resolutions similar to ours: T1WI with a pixel size of 1.0 × 1.25 mm2, T2WI with 0.75 × 1.0 mm2, T2-SPAIR with 1.0 × 1.25 mm2, and DWI with 2.8 × 2.8 mm2, all with a slice thickness of 4 mm. Dyn-eTHRIVE had a pixel size of 1.0 × 1.0 mm2 and a slice thickness of 1 mm, and was reported to generate a smaller CTV volume compared to CT simulations. The investigators of the previous research also explained that their delineation approach involved expanding the boundary of the delineation replicated from CT images towards the direction of the primary tumor observed on preoperative MR images, without narrowing the boundaries in other directions [31]. However, in our delineation process, there was a minimum two-week interval between the two target volume delineations. Our observers determined the CTV of tumor bed through the relationship between tumor cavity with adjacent glands, rather than solely enclosing the position of the primary tumor on the registered image. In general, our findings suggested that adding preoperative MR images might not necessarily lead to a reduction in target volume, as we prioritized avoiding the potential outcome of local recurrence caused by under inclusion of the target volume, over the perceived adverse effects associated with excessive inclusion of normal breast tissues. Regarding planning target volume (PTV), our experience indicates that a 5-mm CTV-to-PTV margin is generally sufficient in patients without surgical clips implantation. Large prospective cohort studies could help determine whether the PTV margin should be further increased or decreased using margin recipe formulas [33]. In patients without surgical clips, a number of verification modalities can be implemented. In addition to the Cone-beam Computed Tomography, the Fan-beam Computed Tomography and ultrasound can help localize the tumor bed. For patients treated with MR linear accelerator (MR-Linac), MR verification images can provide enhanced soft tissue visualization.

Most patients in this study were assigned with a low CVS. To investigate the factor influencing IOV and identify the optimal population who can benefit from preoperative MR simulation, we categorized our patients into two subgroups based on the median CVS score. Several previous studies have shown a positive correlation between CVS and CI [17, 26]. Our results showed that neither CIgen−CT nor CIgen−MR/CT exhibited a significant correlation with CVS. However, the increase in CIgen was significantly higher in the low CVS subgroup upon additional MR simulation (p = 0.016), consistent with findings by den Hartogh et al. [31], where patients with CVS ≤ 3 demonstrated greater CI improvement. In our study, although the change in the dCOM was not statistically significant between the low and high CVS subgroups (p = 0.095), a strong decreasing trend was observed in the low CVS subgroup. Taken together, our subgroup analysis suggested that preoperative MR simulation might particularly benefit patients whose tumor bed is difficult to visualize. A larger sample size may be needed to further explore these findings, given the high prevalence of patients with low CVS in current clinical practice.

Our results collectively suggested that preoperative MR simulation could enhance the consistency of CTV delineation, potentially improving the accuracy of tumor bed contouring, thereby contributing to increased local control and reducing side effects. Accurate identification of the tumor bed is crucial not only for patients undergoing WBRT with tumor bed boost, but also for patients planning on APBI and those who may receive neoadjuvant radiotherapy in the future. While current radiotherapy planning primarily relies on CT simulation in clinical practice, the utilization of MRI-guided radiotherapy (MRgRT) using an MR-Linac continues to expand. Therefore, incorporating preoperative MR simulation at the same therapeutic position may offer multiple benefits for future treatments. Furthermore, our results suggest that additional preoperative MR simulation may be particularly beneficial for specific patient groups. Future research should explore factors associated with low CVS, particularly those measurable preoperatively. These factors could include tumor features (such as size, shape, and location) and individual patient characteristics (such as age, breast size, and breast tissue density). Identifying these factors will help select patients who would benefit most from preoperative MR simulation.

It should be noted that the accuracy of target volume delineation can only be verified by pathology, which is practically infeasible in the clinical setting; expert consensus and minimized IOV among multiple observers are generally used as surrogate endpoints [15, 21]. The result of implementing preoperative MR simulation to reduce IOV does not necessarily ensure an improvement in accuracy, as changes in the basis for delineation may introduce negative or positive systematic errors. In addition, due to the lengthy scan time required for each sequence, preoperative MR simulations might not be compatible with the utilization of deep inspiration breath hold (DIBH) in breast radiotherapy. Potential solutions include registering preoperative MR simulations to the free-breathing phase of CT simulation images or attempting deformable image registration to correlate with the DIBH phase. Future research could explore the feasibility of predicting cardiac dose using preoperative MR simulation. If the predicted dose is low or DIBH offers minimal benefit, DIBH may be deemed unnecessary.

We acknowledge several limitations within our current study. First, the sample size was limited, with only 10 patients included in the study. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. To enhance reliability and generalizability of the current findings, future studies may need to adopt a multicenter framework to recruit a larger number of observers and patients. Another limitation is the use of rigid registration for aligning CT with preoperative MR simulation images. Although the body position was maintained as consistently as possible, given the non-rigid nature of breast morphology, the performances of registration may not be entirely satisfactory. Another limitation of this study is the potential impact of geometric uncertainties on the results, including the CIgen and dCOM. These uncertainties arise from the registration process between CT and MR images, as well as from inherent inaccuracies in MR images, especially those from DWI. Such issues can lead to misalignments and affect the precision of contour delineations. In the future, artificial intelligence holds promise in extracting radiomic features from two consecutive simulation images, facilitating the automatic segmentation, and delineating target volumes with greater precision and efficiency. Furthermore, investigating the potential of preoperative MR simulation in delineating organs at risk is of particular interest, as it could help mitigate collateral damage during cancer treatment. Further research is expected to discover more applications and development prospects of this technique.

Conclusions

In breast-conserving patients lacking surgical clips, preoperative MR simulation enhanced the consistency in delineating the CTV of tumor bed. Patients who are treated with closed cavity surgery and possess tumor beds with unclear margins may benefit most from these procedures. It is essential to note the limitation of the small sample size in the current study; therefore, future studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate our findings.
