Clinical and epidemiological characteristics of dengue with warning signs among hospitalized children in Vietnam: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yen Nguyen Thi Ngoc, Phong Nguyen Doan, Trung Nguyen Quang, Lien Tran Thi, Linh Dang Viet, Nhung Pham Thi, Linh Dang Phuong, Chuc Dang Van
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDengue is a major vector-borne viral infection and a frequent cause of pediatric hospitalization in endemic settings. This study described the clinical and laboratory characteristics of hospitalized children with laboratory-confirmed dengue in northern Vietnam and examined factors associated with dengue with warning signs (DWS).MethodsThis hospital-based cross-sectional study included retrospective and prospective components. Medical records were retrospectively reviewed for eligible children aged 0–16 years admitted from 1 September to 29 December 2022, whereas eligible children in the same age group were prospectively enrolled from 30 December 2022 to 31 August 2023. DWS was classified according to the World Health Organization 2009 criteria and the Vietnamese Ministry of Health guideline. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with DWS.ResultsAmong 319 children, 126 (39.5%) met the criteria for DWS. In the multivariable analysis, age >10 years (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.17, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.28–3.66; p = 0.004), urban residence (aOR 2.88, 95% CI 1.27–6.51; p = 0.011), platelet count ≤100 × 109/L (aOR 3.34, 95% CI 1.86–5.97; p < 0.001), and AST ≥100 U/L (aOR 2.83, 95% CI 1.58–5.05; p < 0.001) were independently associated with DWS. Among children with DWS, the most frequently observed warning signs were abdominal pain or tenderness (64.3%), persistent vomiting (31.7%), and mucosal bleeding (25.4%).ConclusionsDWS was common among hospitalized children with dengue at a tertiary children's hospital in northern Vietnam. Older age, urban residence, thrombocytopenia, and elevated aspartate aminotransferase may help identify children who require closer monitoring during hospitalization.

Introduction

Dengue is one of the most important mosquito-borne viral infections in tropical and subtropical regions and remains a major cause of acute febrile illness and pediatric hospitalization. It is caused by dengue virus, a flavivirus transmitted primarily by Aedes mosquitoes, particularly Aedes aegypti. The clinical spectrum is broad, ranging from self-limited febrile illness to dengue with warning signs (DWS) and severe dengue, which may involve plasma leakage, severe bleeding, organ impairment, shock, or death.1,2

The global burden of dengue has increased substantially over recent decades. A modelling study estimated approximately 390 million dengue infections annually, of which about 96 million are clinically apparent. 3 The World Health Organization also identifies dengue as a continuing global public health concern, with billions of people living in areas at risk of infection. 4 Despite progress in vector control, diagnostics, and vaccine development, there is still no specific antiviral treatment for dengue; therefore, early recognition, close monitoring, appropriate fluid management, and timely identification of warning signs remain central to clinical care.2,4

Vietnam is a dengue-endemic country where transmission varies seasonally and geographically. Although the burden has traditionally been greater in the central and southern regions, dengue activity has also expanded and become increasingly complex in northern Vietnam. 5 The large dengue outbreak in Vietnam in 2022 further highlighted the continuing threat of dengue, with virological studies showing the involvement of local dengue virus lineages and a newly introduced DENV-2 lineage. 6 Recent Vietnamese data have also emphasized the clinical severity of dengue during this period, particularly among hospitalized patients. 7 However, evidence focusing specifically on DWS among hospitalized children in northern Vietnam remains limited. Identifying clinical and laboratory factors associated with DWS is important because affected children require closer monitoring and timely intervention to prevent progression to severe dengue.

This study aimed to describe the clinical, epidemiological, and laboratory characteristics of hospitalized children with laboratory-confirmed dengue at a tertiary children's hospital in northern Vietnam and to identify factors associated with DWS.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Hai Phong Children's Hospital, a tertiary pediatric referral hospital in Hai Phong, northern Vietnam. The study included retrospective and prospective components. Medical records were retrospectively reviewed for eligible children admitted from 1 September to 29 December 2022. From 30 December 2022 to 31 August 2023, eligible children were prospectively enrolled, and their clinical and laboratory data were collected during hospitalization using a standardized data collection form.

Study population

Consecutive sampling was used in both study components. During the retrospective component, potentially eligible children were identified from hospital medical records. During the prospective component, potentially eligible children were screened and enrolled during hospitalization. All hospitalized children aged 0–16 years with laboratory-confirmed dengue who met the predefined eligibility criteria during the study period were consecutively included. Children were classified into the DWS and dengue without warning signs (non-DWS) groups for comparative analyses.

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because all eligible children identified during the retrospective component and enrolled during the prospective component were included. The final study size was therefore determined by the number of eligible participants available during the study period. A total of 319 children were included, of whom 126 had DWS and 193 had non-DWS.

Diagnostic criteria and case classification

For study purposes, dengue diagnosis and classification were retrospectively determined from the medical records according to the Vietnamese Ministry of Health guideline for the diagnosis and treatment of dengue hemorrhagic fever, issued under Decision No. 2760/QĐ-BYT dated 4 July 2023, and the World Health Organization 2009 dengue guideline.2,8 For the retrospective component, these criteria were applied to the clinical and laboratory information documented in the medical records. For the prospective component, the same criteria were applied using information recorded during hospitalization.

For study classification, laboratory confirmation of dengue was based on the testing algorithm specified in the Vietnamese Ministry of Health guideline. 8 Non-structural protein 1 antigen (NS1) testing was performed during days 1–7 of illness and was prioritized during the first 5 days. From day 5 onward, dengue immunoglobulin M (IgM) testing was performed in clinically suspected cases when the NS1 result was negative. Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was recognized as an acceptable confirmatory method during the febrile phase; however, no participant in the present study was classified solely on the basis of an RT-PCR result. An isolated positive IgM result was considered supportive of acute dengue only when obtained from day 5 of illness onward in a child with a clinically compatible acute illness. 8

Laboratory-confirmed dengue was defined as a clinically compatible acute dengue illness with at least one positive diagnostic test, including NS1 antigen, dengue IgM interpreted according to the day of illness, or RT-PCR.2,8

DWS was defined as dengue with at least one of the following features: abdominal pain or tenderness, persistent vomiting, clinical fluid accumulation, mucosal bleeding, lethargy or restlessness, liver enlargement >2 cm, or an increase in hematocrit concurrent with a rapid decrease in platelet count.2,8 During the retrospective component, warning signs were identified from the medical records. During the prospective component, warning signs were recorded during hospitalization using the same predefined definitions. In both components, warning signs were interpreted in relation to the documented day of illness, particularly during the critical phase, which typically occurs between days 3 and 7 after illness onset.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Children were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 0–16 years, admitted to Hai Phong Children's Hospital during the study period, and had laboratory-confirmed dengue.

Children were excluded if they had documented co-infection with another bacterial or viral pathogen, an underlying hematologic or chronic liver disease that could affect the platelet count or liver enzyme levels, or missing key clinical or laboratory data required for the analyses.

Data sources and measurements

During the retrospective component, data were extracted from medical records using a standardized data collection form. During the prospective component, data were collected from clinical assessments, laboratory results, and medical records generated during hospitalization using the same standardized form. The collected variables included demographic characteristics, clinical signs and symptoms, dengue warning signs, diagnostic test results, and laboratory findings.

The primary outcome was DWS status. Variables evaluated as potential factors associated with DWS included sex, age, place of residence, leukocyte count, platelet count, hematocrit, and AST level.

Laboratory parameters included in the analyses were obtained within the first 24 h after hospital admission and included white blood cell count, hematocrit, platelet count, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, and fibrinogen. The same variable definitions, data sources, and measurement periods were applied to the DWS and non-DWS groups.

Dengue virus infection was identified from laboratory test results documented during routine clinical care, including the OnSite Dengue Ag Rapid Test and/or the OnSite Dengue IgG/IgM Combo Rapid Test (CTK Biotech, USA).

To reduce selection bias, all eligible children were consecutively included in both study components. To reduce information and classification bias, the same predefined diagnostic criteria, variable definitions, standardized data collection form, and measurement periods were applied throughout the study. Laboratory parameters were restricted to those obtained within the first 24 h after hospital admission. Nevertheless, incomplete documentation during the retrospective component and residual measurement or classification errors during routine clinical care could not be completely excluded.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Age was categorized as ≤5, 6–10, and >10 years for descriptive comparisons and dichotomized as >10 versus ≤10 years for logistic regression analysis. Laboratory variables were categorized as leukocyte count <5 versus ≥5 × 109/L, platelet count ≤100 versus >100 × 109/L, hematocrit ≥45% versus <45%, and AST ≥100 versus <100 U/L. The age categories were selected to represent preschool-aged, school-aged, and older pediatric groups. The laboratory cutoffs were selected based on clinically relevant thresholds used in dengue assessment and previous literature.

Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables, as appropriate. Univariable logistic regression analysis was used to estimate crude odds ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals for factors associated with DWS. Variables with p < 0.05 in the univariable analyses were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were reported.

Participants with missing key clinical or laboratory information were excluded according to the predefined eligibility criteria. The analyses were therefore conducted using complete cases, and missing values were not imputed. No weighting was applied because all eligible participants were consecutively included in both study components. No subgroup, interaction, or sensitivity analyses were performed. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hai Phong University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Hai Phong, Vietnam (Approval No. 2909/QĐ-YDHP, dated 30 December 2022). The protocol covered both the retrospective review of de-identified medical records for eligible children admitted from 1 September to 29 December 2022 and the prospective enrollment of eligible children from 30 December 2022 to 31 August 2023.

The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee for the retrospective component because only de-identified medical record data were used. For the prospective component, written informed consent was obtained from the parent or legal guardian of each participating child before enrollment. Prospective enrollment commenced only after ethical approval had been granted.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All patient data were de-identified before analysis, and no information that could directly or indirectly identify individual participants is presented in this manuscript. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. 9

Results

Clinical and epidemiological characteristics of the study population

All 319 eligible children had complete data for the variables included in the analyses. Of these, 126 (39.5%) had DWS, whereas 193 (60.5%) had non-DWS. Sex distribution did not differ significantly between the DWS and non-DWS groups (p = 0.095). In contrast, age distribution differed significantly between the two groups (p < 0.001). Children aged >10 years accounted for a larger proportion of the DWS group than of the non-DWS group (59.5% vs. 34.7%), whereas children aged ≤5 years accounted for a smaller proportion of the DWS group (6.3% vs. 21.8%). Urban residence was more common in the DWS group than in the non-DWS group (91.3% vs. 83.4%; p = 0.045). The distribution of confirmatory diagnostic tests did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.096) (Table 1).

Clinical warning signs and selected severity-related findings

Among children with DWS, abdominal pain or tenderness was the most common warning sign, occurring in 81 children (64.3%). Persistent vomiting was reported in 40 children (31.7%), mucosal bleeding in 32 children (25.4%), hepatomegaly in 9 children (7.1%), and lethargy or restlessness in 2 children (1.6%). Clinical fluid accumulation was uncommon, with ascites recorded in 1 child (0.8%) and no cases of pleural effusion. Oliguria was recorded in 16 children (12.7%). AST ≥100 U/L was observed in 51 children (40.5%). (Table 2)

Laboratory findings among children with DWS

Laboratory findings obtained within the first 24 h after hospital admission showed that decreased white blood cell counts were present in 101 children with DWS (80.2%), whereas increased white blood cell counts were observed in 2 children (1.6%). Increased hematocrit was recorded in 52 children (41.3%), and decreased platelet counts were observed in 56 children (44.4%). AST and ALT levels were elevated in 51 (40.5%) and 45 (35.7%) children, respectively. Reduced prothrombin activity was recorded in 19 children (15.1%), prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time in 16 children (12.7%), and decreased fibrinogen concentration in 29 children (23.0%) (Table 3).

Factors associated with DWS

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with DWS among children with laboratory-confirmed dengue. In the univariable analyses, age >10 years, urban residence, leukocyte count <5 × 109/L, platelet count ≤100 × 109/L, hematocrit ≥45%, and AST ≥100 U/L were significantly associated with DWS. Sex was not significantly associated with DWS (p = 0.095).

In the multivariable analysis, age >10 years, urban residence, platelet count ≤100 × 109/L, and AST ≥100 U/L remained associated with DWS after adjustment. Children aged >10 years had higher odds of DWS than those aged ≤10 years (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.17, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.28–3.66; p = 0.004). Urban residence was associated with higher odds of DWS than rural residence (aOR 2.88, 95% CI 1.27–6.51; p = 0.011). Children with platelet counts ≤100 × 109/L had higher odds of DWS than those with platelet counts >100 × 109/L (aOR 3.34, 95% CI 1.86–5.97; p < 0.001). Similarly, AST ≥100 U/L was associated with higher odds of DWS than AST <100 U/L (aOR 2.83, 95% CI 1.58–5.05; p < 0.001). Leukocyte count <5 × 109/L and hematocrit ≥45% were not statistically significant after adjustment (Table 4).

Discussion

Epidemiological characteristics of the study population

In this hospital-based study, 126 of 319 children with laboratory-confirmed dengue met the criteria for DWS, accounting for 39.5% of the study population. Thus, DWS accounted for a substantial proportion of hospitalized pediatric dengue cases in our setting. However, because the study included only hospitalized children, this estimate should be interpreted as the proportion of DWS among hospitalized dengue cases rather than as a population-level prevalence. Hospital-based studies in other endemic settings have also documented substantial burdens of dengue with warning signs or severe dengue, although direct comparisons are limited by differences in age distribution, case definitions, admission criteria, and referral patterns.10,11

Boys predominated among children with DWS in our cohort. Nevertheless, male sex was not significantly associated with DWS in the univariable analysis. Therefore, the observed male predominance should be interpreted as a descriptive characteristic of the hospitalized DWS group rather than evidence of higher odds of DWS among boys. Evidence regarding sex is context dependent: a systematic review and meta-analysis found female sex to be associated with dengue shock syndrome, whereas a large Vietnamese cohort observed a male predominance among children with uncomplicated dengue and suggested that healthcare-seeking behavior might partly contribute to this pattern.12,13

Most children with DWS were school-aged or older, with the largest proportion occurring among those aged >10 years. Age-related patterns vary across studies and depend on the clinical outcome examined. The risk of symptomatic dengue increased with age in a Vietnamese analysis, and severe dengue was more frequent among children aged 7–12 years in a Brazilian pediatric cohort, whereas a meta-analysis found younger age to be associated with dengue shock syndrome.13–16 These differences may reflect variation in outcome definitions, population characteristics, transmission patterns, and healthcare settings. Most children with DWS also lived in urban areas. This finding should be interpreted in the context of the hospital catchment area, population distribution, healthcare-seeking behavior, referral patterns, and access to tertiary pediatric care.

The monthly distribution of hospitalized dengue cases showed a clear seasonal pattern. The total number of dengue cases increased from September 2022 and peaked in November, whereas DWS cases peaked in October. Both total dengue and DWS cases subsequently declined and remained low during the first eight months of 2023, with only minor month-to-month fluctuations. This pattern is consistent with Vietnamese studies demonstrating seasonal dengue transmission and associations with temperature and rainfall.17–19 In northern Vietnam, recognizing this seasonal pattern may help hospitals anticipate increases in dengue admissions and strengthen triage, laboratory monitoring, clinical surveillance, and fluid-management resources during periods of higher transmission.

Clinical warning signs and severity-related findings

The clinical profile of children with DWS in our cohort was characterized mainly by gastrointestinal and bleeding manifestations. Abdominal pain or tenderness was the most frequent warning sign, followed by persistent vomiting and mucosal bleeding. These findings are consistent with the WHO 2009 dengue classification, in which these manifestations are recognized as warning signs requiring closer clinical monitoring. 2 A hospital-based cross-sectional study conducted during the 2023 dengue outbreak in Bangladesh similarly reported that gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly vomiting, were more frequent among patients with severe dengue than among those with non-severe dengue. 10

Bleeding manifestations should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously because they form part of the criteria used to classify dengue severity and should therefore be regarded primarily as clinical markers of disease activity rather than independent causal determinants. Their pathophysiology is multifactorial and may involve thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, endothelial dysfunction, and disturbances of coagulation and fibrinolysis.20,21

Hepatomegaly and lethargy or restlessness were uncommon in this cohort. However, the absence of clinically detectable hepatomegaly does not exclude hepatic involvement, which may be identified more sensitively through biochemical abnormalities. Clinical fluid accumulation was also uncommon. Its low frequency should not reduce clinical vigilance because plasma leakage may evolve dynamically during the critical phase and may not be evident at the time of initial assessment. 2

Overall, these findings support the systematic assessment of warning signs and other severity-related manifestations in hospitalized children with dengue. Differences in their frequency across studies may reflect variation in study populations, disease severity at presentation, timing of hospital admission, circulating dengue virus serotypes, case definitions, and local admission practices.10,22,23

Laboratory findings

The laboratory profile of children with DWS included leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, increased hematocrit, aminotransferase elevation, and selected coagulation abnormalities. These findings are consistent with the recognized laboratory features of dengue. The WHO 2009 classification includes leukopenia among the findings supporting a diagnosis of probable dengue and identifies an increase in hematocrit concurrent with a rapid decrease in platelet count as a warning sign of possible plasma leakage. 2

The interpretation of leukocyte count, platelet count, and hematocrit should take into account the day of illness, hydration status, and individual baseline values. Because laboratory parameters in this study were assessed only within the first 24 h after hospital admission, their subsequent dynamic changes during hospitalization could not be evaluated. This is particularly relevant in dengue, in which hematological parameters may change rapidly during progression from the febrile to the critical phase.

Aminotransferase abnormalities are common in dengue and may become more pronounced with increasing disease severity.24–26 Biochemical evidence of hepatic involvement may therefore be present even in the absence of clinically detectable hepatomegaly. Because AST is found in both hepatic and extrahepatic tissues, particularly skeletal muscle, elevated AST levels may not exclusively reflect hepatic injury. 24 The association between AST elevation and DWS is discussed further in the following section.

Coagulation abnormalities were less prominent than leukopenia and aminotransferase elevation in this cohort. Nevertheless, previous evidence indicates that dengue may be accompanied by abnormalities in coagulation tests, reduced fibrinogen concentrations, and activation of fibrinolytic pathways, particularly in patients with more severe disease.21,27

Factors associated with DWS

The adjusted analysis identified several demographic and laboratory factors associated with DWS. Because this was a cross-sectional observational study with retrospective and prospective components, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Moreover, the cross-sectional design does not establish whether the identified characteristics preceded the development of warning signs.

Age >10 years was associated with higher odds of DWS than age ≤10 years. Previous studies have demonstrated age-related differences in the clinical presentation and severity of dengue, although the direction and magnitude of these associations vary across populations and clinical outcomes.13–16,28 Older children and adolescents may have different exposure patterns and immune histories from younger children. Greater mobility, school attendance, outdoor activities, and previous dengue infection are possible explanations. However, dengue serotype, primary versus secondary infection, and individual exposure history were not assessed in this study; therefore, these explanations remain hypotheses rather than findings directly supported by our data.

Urban residence also remained associated with DWS after adjustment. This association may partly reflect environmental conditions favorable to dengue transmission, because Aedes aegypti is well adapted to densely populated domestic and peri-domestic settings and breeds readily in artificial water containers.17–19,29 Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. In a hospital-based study, urban residence may also reflect the hospital catchment area, population distribution, healthcare access, referral patterns, and differences in the likelihood of hospital admission or laboratory testing. Urban residence should therefore be regarded as a contextual characteristic associated with DWS in this study population rather than as a direct biological determinant.

Among the laboratory variables, platelet count ≤100 × 109/L remained associated with DWS after adjustment. Thrombocytopenia in dengue may result from several mechanisms, including bone marrow suppression, immune-mediated platelet destruction, increased peripheral consumption, platelet dysfunction, and interactions among inflammatory, endothelial, coagulation, and fibrinolytic pathways.2,20 Although thrombocytopenia alone does not define severe dengue, a platelet count ≤100 × 109/L in a hospitalized child should prompt careful reassessment for warning signs, bleeding manifestations, and evidence of plasma leakage.

AST ≥100 U/L was also associated with DWS after adjustment. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that aminotransferase abnormalities, particularly AST elevation, may be more pronounced in patients with more severe clinical manifestations of dengue.24–26,30 Because AST is present in hepatic and extrahepatic tissues, including skeletal muscle, its elevation may reflect hepatic involvement as well as injury to other tissues. 24 Therefore, AST ≥100 U/L should be interpreted together with the clinical presentation and other laboratory findings rather than as an isolated predictor of clinical deterioration.

Leukocyte count <5 × 109/L and HCT ≥45% were associated with DWS in the univariable analyses but were no longer statistically significant after adjustment. This attenuation suggests that their crude associations may have been partly explained by correlations with other demographic or laboratory variables included in the multivariable model.

This study provides setting-specific evidence on the clinical, epidemiological, and laboratory characteristics of hospitalized children with dengue and the factors associated with DWS in Hai Phong, Vietnam. These findings may support the earlier recognition and closer monitoring of hospitalized children with characteristics associated with DWS, although validation in larger, multicenter studies is warranted.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary children's hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to children with milder disease managed in outpatient settings and to other geographic populations. Second, differences in data collection between the retrospective and prospective components may have introduced information or classification bias, particularly for clinical manifestations and the timing of symptom onset. Third, laboratory parameters were assessed only within the first 24 h after admission; therefore, their subsequent dynamic changes could not be evaluated. Fourth, data on dengue serotype, viral load, and primary versus secondary infection were unavailable. Some diagnostic misclassification among cases confirmed solely by IgM positivity also cannot be excluded because IgM antibodies may persist after recent infection. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; thus, the identified factors should be interpreted as associations rather than causal risk factors or validated predictors.

Conclusions

DWS accounted for a substantial proportion of hospitalized children with laboratory-confirmed dengue at a tertiary children's hospital in northern Vietnam. Age >10 years, urban residence, platelet count ≤100 × 109/L, and AST ≥100 U/L remained associated with DWS after adjustment. These findings provide setting-specific evidence that may help clinicians recognize hospitalized children with characteristics associated with DWS and prioritize closer clinical and laboratory monitoring. However, the identified associations should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger, multicenter prospective studies.
