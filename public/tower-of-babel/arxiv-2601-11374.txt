Reward Modeling for Scientific Writing Evaluation
Furkan Şahinuç, Subhabrata Dutta, Iryna Gurevych

Abstract
Scientific writing is an expert-domain task that demands deep domain knowledge, task-specific requirements and reasoning capabilities that leverage the domain knowledge to satisfy the task specifications. While scientific text generation has been widely studied, its evaluation remains a challenging and open problem. It is critical to develop models that can be reliably deployed for evaluating diverse open-ended scientific writing tasks while adhering to their distinct requirements. However, existing LLM-based judges and reward models are primarily optimized for general-purpose benchmarks with fixed scoring rubrics and evaluation criteria. Consequently, they often fail to reason over sparse knowledge of scientific domains when interpreting task-dependent and multi-faceted criteria. Moreover, fine-tuning for each individual task is costly and impractical for low-resource settings. To bridge these gaps, we propose cost-efficient, open-source reward models tailored for scientific writing evaluation. We introduce a two-stage training framework that initially optimizes scientific evaluation preferences and then refines reasoning capabilities. Our multi-aspect evaluation design and joint training across diverse tasks enable fine-grained assessment and robustness to dynamic criteria and scoring rubrics. Experimental analysis shows that our training regime strongly improves LLM-based scientific writing evaluation. Our models generalize effectively across tasks and to previously unseen scientific writing evaluation settings, allowing a single trained evaluator to be reused without task-specific retraining. Introduction

Due to the strong text generation capabilities of large language models (LLMs), their application to scientific text generation, such as related work generation, review generation, and revising papers, has gained increasing attention recently
23
;
25
;
1
. However, without proper evaluation, it is difficult to assess the accuracy and reliability of the generated texts. Therefore, the limitations in evaluation can block the entire development pipeline. Since scientific writing tasks have diverse and task-specific requirements, developing appropriate evaluation frameworks is a challenging problem. Training evaluators for each individual task is costly and, in some cases, infeasible due to limited data availability.

LLM-as-a-judge approaches
28
;
47
are the most widely adopted evaluation paradigms in scientific writing tasks. However, they often fail to reason over the given domain knowledge and task-specific preferences (see Figure
1
). This motivates the need for mechanisms that allow models to reason over and remain grounded in explicit evaluation guidelines (aka,
constitution
) at inference time. Inference-time adaptability is a major challenge for existing approaches like Constitutional AI
4
, which internalize fixed constitution during training and therefore cannot be readily applied to a diverse set of evaluation tasks. This rigidity is problematic while evaluating scientific text generations, as evaluation guidelines can be different, even contradictory, across different aspects, tasks, or domains.

To improve the reasoning capabilities of LLM-based judges, training reward reasoning models has recently gained popularity
3
;
14
;
6
;
7
;
41
. However, existing reward models are primarily optimized for improving performance on community-standard benchmarks, such as mathematical reasoning, instruction-following, and human-preference modeling for coding, helpfulness, and safety
22
;
31
;
11
. Therefore, they fall short of capturing the nuanced requirements of scientific writing evaluation. In addition, the vast majority of reward models encode task preferences in a pairwise manner, which prevents independent assessment of text quality based on explicit task-specific criteria. Another drawback of such models is that they are optimized for fixed scoring rubrics and criteria. Scientific tasks have unique characteristics; each requires domain-specific expertise and evaluation dynamics that differ from those of open-ended creative writing tasks
5
. Even for the same scientific artifact, it is possible to evaluate from multiple aspects requiring different criteria and rubrics. However, current reward models experience performance degradation when they are applied to other tasks with evaluation rubrics that differ from those seen during training
46
. On the other hand,
35
offers insights on multiple-aspect evaluation for expert-domain tasks. However, their approach is limited to a single task, and their most accurate pipeline relies on proprietary LLMs, which restricts scalability and hinders generalization across diverse scientific writing tasks.

In this work, we adapt reward model training strategies to enhance LLMs’ capabilities regarding
what to evaluate
and
how to evaluate
scientific writing tasks. Concretely, we design reward models that are conditioned on an explicit evaluation constitution—a structured description of criteria and label space—present during both training and inference. Furthermore, we introduce a two-stage optimization process in which models not only learn to follow the constitution but also reflexively reinterpret it to correct and stabilize their own reasoning. This process involves a joint optimization of in-context preference-following and reasoning abilities, which
21
identifies as a missing piece in modern LMs trained using RL.

2
Related Work

2.1
Scientific Writing Evaluation

Utilizing LLMs as direct evaluators is one of the most intuitive approaches for scientific writing evaluation due to their flexibility in being prompted with various task settings without additional training overhead
28
;
47
. However, prior work has shown that vanilla LLM-as-a-judge setups are prone to systematic biases and failures in domain-grounded reasoning
24
;
39
;
12
. Moreover, the relative scarcity of scientific writing evaluation datasets also prevents LLMs from becoming more familiar with these tasks in the training phase.

To address these limitations,
18
focus on evaluating scientific text revision. They highlight that LLM-as-a-judge methods struggle to grasp task-specific evaluation aspects in the absence of a gold reference. In a complementary direction,
33
classify the peer-reviews according to common mistakes of reviewers referred to as
lazy-thinking
patterns. A key limitation of their classification scheme is that, although some review sentences are suitable for multiple error categories, the dataset imposes a one-to-one label assignment. Similarly,
34
introduce a direct-evaluation benchmark that measures the utility of scientific reviews across the aspects of actionability, grounding, verifiability, and helpfulness.

Addressing a different task,
35
propose a fine-grained evaluation framework for related work generation. Instead of producing a single overall score, they perform aspect based evaluation. Although their evaluation achieves strong alignment with human experts, their implementation is limited to the related work generation task.

2.2
Evaluation-Tuned Models

Reward modeling has a significant impact on the success of post-training with reinforcement learning. Although verifiable rewards are computationally efficient and demonstrate strong performance in math and coding tasks
42
, many complex tasks are not suitable for directly verifiable reward signals, such as writing quality or helpfulness. These limitations and the recent success of reasoning-centric models on complex tasks
9
have motivated the development of reward reasoning models
3
;
14
;
6
;
7
;
41
. The primary objective of such models is to generate more reliable and accurate rewards in non-verifiable tasks by leveraging intermediate thinking steps. Along with reasoning features, prior works also explore improving long-context comprehension
40
, integrating external documents
30
, and bridging pointwise and pairwise scoring paradigms in reward modeling settings
43
;
17
. The main drawback of these models is that they are optimized for standard reward benchmarks, which do not involve any scientific writing tasks. Therefore, they struggle to adapt to the varying scientific writing criteria and scoring.

Although there are attempts to automatically generate task-specific evaluation criteria alongside the evaluation itself
27
;
29
;
26
, the generated criteria are mostly surface-level and do not match the scientific writing evaluation requirements which are highly specific, diverse, and involve domain expertise.

Besides reward models, evaluation-tuned LLM-as-a-judge models also exist. In general, these models are not specialized for a particular task but are designed as general-purpose judge models
28
;
19
;
2
;
10
;
37
. This feature provides flexibility, allowing them to be employed in a diverse set of tasks. However, since they are not specialized for expert-domain tasks like scientific writing, they experience performance drops. In contrast, our work directly focuses on the evaluation of scientific writing and generalizes across different tasks with unique criteria and evaluation schemas.

3
Methodology

3.1
Dataset

To improve the generalization capabilities of our reward models, we include various scientific writing tasks that can be evaluated from multiple aspects using different scoring rubrics. These tasks focus on related work and review generation.
35
provide a fine-grained set of aspects and principles for evaluating LLM-generated related work sections. From these aspects, we include coherence (whether citation sentences are consistent with the cited papers), positioning type (whether related works position themselves within existing literature, given a specific type), and positioning consistency (how well this positioning is maintained across different paragraphs) in our dataset. These aspects are subject to binary evaluation. To increase data diversity, we expand their dataset (originally containing NLP papers) by incorporating papers from other top-tier machine learning venues. We follow their preprocessing methodology to construct individual data instances.

Additionally, we incorporate the RevUtil dataset
34
. The tasks in this dataset are evaluated on a 1–5 scale, with higher scores reflecting better quality. The number of instances and the corresponding scoring rubrics for each aspect of the training tasks are given in Appendix
A
. In addition, example data instances can be found in evaluation prompts in Appendix
C
.

3.2
Reward Model Training

Our training scheme consists of two distinct sequential stages: (1) teaching model to evaluate a candidate scientific text by reasoning upon an in-context constitution and (2) intensifying the reasoning acquired at stage 1 by teaching model self-verification based on the constitution. In both stages, we employ the GRPO algorithm due to its effectiveness in preference optimization and reasoning enhancement
36
. We formulate our task as follows: Given a prompt
x=(q,c,e)
, consisting of a task query
q
, criteria (or constitution) with a scoring rubric
c
and example evaluations for each possible score
e
, generate an evaluation output
M(x)=y=(j,s)
that consists of a reasoning (or justification) component
j
along with a final score (or verdict)
s
.

To facilitate trivial extraction of rewards from the model outputs across different tasks and scoring rubrics, we use formatted outputs where the reasoning and scoring parts are encapsulated between
<reasoning>

</reasoning>
and
<score>

</score>
tags, respectively. The reasoning part helps to improve evaluation performance, as shown in previous works
20
;
43
;
30
. We calculate our rewards based on compliance with the formatting and correctness of the generated score. In particular, let
y
be the output, and let
m(y)
be the score assigned by the model within
<score>

</score>
in
y
, or
m(y)=\varnothing
if no valid tag is present.
Let
s^{*}
be the gold score and let
S
be the set of possible scores in the corresponding scoring rubric. The reward function for the first training stage is defined as:

r(y,s^{*},S)=\begin{cases}-0.5&\text{if }m(y)=\varnothing\\
0&\text{if }m(y)\notin\mathbb{R}\\
0.25&\text{if }m(y)\notin S\\
0.5&\text{if }m(y)\in S\text{ \& }m(y)\neq s^{*}\\
1.5&\text{if }m(y)\in S\text{ \& }m(y)=s^{*}\\
\end{cases}

This reward strategy helps identify different error types, including formatting and semantic errors. Our initial experiments showed that models may attempt to exploit the reward function
38
by generating responses that only focus on the scoring criteria. We also observed occasional instability during training, where models would fail to finish their outputs and continue generating until the maximum length was reached. We introduce a length penalty function to mitigate training instability and reward hacking.

f(L,T)=\begin{cases}\frac{(kT-L)^{2}}{kT^{\,2}},&L<kT,\\[10.00002pt]
\frac{(L-(1-k)T)^{2}}{(T-(1-k)T)^{\,2}},&L>(1-k)T,\\[10.00002pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Here,
L
and
T
stand for the number of generated tokens and the maximum token length, respectively. The parameter
k\in(0,0.5]
controls how strict the penalty will be. When the generated output is too short or too long—i.e., closer to the boundary regions, the penalty quadratically increases with the distance from the acceptable range. If the length is within this range, no penalty is applied. The final reward is calculated by

R(x)=r(M(x),s^{*},S)-f(L,T).

To further enhance the reasoning capabilities of our model, we introduce a second reinforcement learning stage, where the model is incentivized to update its evaluation by reflecting on the constitution. We first obtain the scores and reasoning from the model on the data instances. Next, we remove the scoring part and append the reasoning part with a reflection prompt that encourages the model to revisit the query and criteria before outputting its final score. The adjusted reward function for the second stage training is as follows:

r_{p}(s_{i},s_{f},s^{*})=\begin{cases}0.5&\text{if }s_{i}=s^{*}\text{ \& }s_{f}=s^{*}\\
1.0&\text{if }s_{i}\neq s^{*}\text{ \& }s_{f}=s^{*}\\
-0.5&\text{if }s_{i}\neq s^{*}\text{ \& }s_{f}\neq s^{*}\\
-1.0&\text{if }s_{i}=s^{*}\text{ \& }s_{f}\neq s^{*}\end{cases}

s_{i}
and
s_{f}
denote the initial and final scores, respectively. This rewarding strategy depends not only on the correctness of the final decision but also on the model’s prior performance. If the model corrects itself, it receives the highest reward; when it backslides, it is harshly penalized. Consistent behaviors yield a moderate reward or penalty based on correctness. In other words, we encourage the model to revisit the criteria and its earlier reasoning either to fix the incorrect trajectory or to maintain the correct one. We refer to the model trained only in the first stage as
SciRM
, and the model trained using the two-stage procedure as
SciRM-Ref
.

4.1
Unseen Aspect Experiments

To evaluate whether our training scheme provides an improvement over the baselines on unseen aspects of the same evaluation task, we conduct an experiment that excludes selected evaluation aspects from the training data. Specifically, we remove the
Actionability
and
Grounding
aspects from the RevUtil dataset and test whether models trained on the remaining ones can still improve performance on unseen evaluation aspects. We refer to the resulting model as
SciRM-Masked
.

4.2
Unseen Task Experiments

To assess the generalization capabilities of our reward model, we test it on two additional tasks that are not included in the training data: comparison of novelty assessments and evaluation of scientific revision quality. For the first task, we utilize the novelty-evaluation dataset from
1
, which contains summaries of both human and LLM-generated novelty assessments. Human summaries are accompanied by human-annotated binary labels indicating the novelty of the papers. However, LLM-generated assessment summaries do not include final novelty labels. To address this, we use GPT-5.1
32
to infer the novelty labels from the LLM-generated summaries. Our evaluation objective is to assess whether LLM-generated summaries align with human summaries in terms of their novelty verdicts (i.e.,
novel
,
not novel
). We treat equality of the novelty labels as the ground-truth indicator of alignment between summary pairs. In the final evaluation dataset, there are 76 assessment pairs with 56 aligned pairs and 20 non-aligned pairs.

For the second task, we use the scientific revision dataset from
18
, which includes paragraphs from scientific papers and corresponding LLM-generated revisions for a given instruction. Each candidate revision is annotated along two dimensions: whether it addresses the given instruction (i.e.,
relatedness
) and whether it correctly applies the instruction to produce an improved version of the original text (i.e.,
correctness
). For each aspect, we have 3,092 data instances, resulting in a total of 6,184 data points.

Error Analysis.
We analyze model errors to identify salient error patterns. For example, in novelty alignment evaluation, models sometimes ignore previously mentioned important contributions in summaries when they encounter statements such as
"not a groundbreaking fundamental new contribution"
. However, a paper can still be novel along other dimensions even if the contributions are not groundbreaking for the respective field
7
7

7

ARR Reviewer Guidelines
. In addition,
34
report that some instances do not have full annotator agreement in RevUtil dataset. While evaluating such instances, models can confuse two adjacent scores. These error cases highlight both the inherent difficulty of scientific writing evaluation, which can lead to disagreement even among experts, and the importance of clear, fine-grained task-specific evaluation criteria.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we address the problem of evaluating scientific writing tasks. Owing to their unique characteristics, both the generation and evaluation of scientific writing require greater attention than common, well-defined NLP tasks. Moreover, this uniqueness often results in limited annotated data, making it impractical to build specialized models for individual tasks. To address this challenge, we propose reward models that enhance reasoning capabilities and capture task dependencies through explicit evaluation criteria and scoring rubrics. Our models outperform strong baselines in both seen and unseen task settings, highlighting their reasoning and generalization capabilities and underscoring their practicality for real-world scientific workflows. This design enables a single trained evaluator to generalize across tasks, substantially reducing the need for task-specific retraining.

We believe that this work takes a step toward more reliable, interpretable, and reusable evaluation frameworks for scientific writing. A promising direction for future work is to improve scientific text generation across diverse tasks using reinforcement learning, where rewards are provided by our models. By producing fine-grained, aspect-level feedback on scientific text quality, our models can guide generators along multiple evaluation dimensions rather than optimizing a single objective. Finally, the cost efficiency of our open-source models further enhances their practicality compared to proprietary LLM-based evaluators.

Limitations

Due to the computational requirements of GRPO, we restrict our experiments to 7B-scale models. We hypothesize that larger models could better capture task-specific evaluation nuances, generalize more effectively, and exhibit more accurate reasoning. Nevertheless, our training scheme provides an opportunity to develop small-sized evaluation models that can outperform or remain competitive with proprietary models. Another limitation is the scarcity of evaluation datasets for scientific writing. This results in most of the scoring rubrics being binary. With the introduction of more fine-grained scientific writing evaluation datasets, a more comprehensive analysis can be conducted as future work.
