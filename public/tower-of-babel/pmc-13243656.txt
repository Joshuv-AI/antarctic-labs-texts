Experimental study on dynamic mechanical properties of frozen unsaturated silty clay
Authors: Tingcong Wan, Xilei Yao, Haibo Wang, Mengxiang Wang, Hao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To study the influence of the change in soil strength of frozen silty clay on the support design during the foundation pit construction process, a water-rich silt-clay layer railway tunnel foundation pit in Jinan, Shandong Province was taken as the background. Dynamic impact tests on frozen silty clay were conducted using a separated Hopkinson pressure rod test device to analyze the effects of different impact pressures, different moisture contents and different temperatures on frozen silty clay. The test results show that: (1) The dynamic stress-strain curve of frozen silty clay can be divided into three stages: a elastic stage, b plastic stage, and c failure stage. In the plastic stage, a significant strain rate effect is exhibited. (2) As the moisture content continuously increases, the strain continuously decreases. The peak strength first increases and then decreases. At a moisture content of 26% (natural moisture content), the peak strength reaches its maximum. (3) The fracture morphology of the specimen increases with the increase of impact air pressure, moisture content and temperature. The research results can provide experimental basis for winter tunnel construction.

Introduction

So far, China ranks third in the world in terms of seasonal frozen soil volume. Cold-region projects represented by the Sichuan-Xizang Railway and the Qinghai-Xizang Expressway have been fully launched1,2. The open-cut tunnel project for the new section of the Jinan connection line of the Tianjin-Weifang High-Speed Railway in water-rich and weak strata is located in Jinan, Shandong Province. Due to the low-temperature environment in the northern region, the extreme temperature in winter has reached -22.5℃. Under the influence of the natural environment, the soil in the open-cut tunnel foundation pit will freeze, thus forming permafrost. The maximum depth of permafrost has reached 44 cm. The low-temperature environment has increased the complexity and unpredictability of the project during the construction process3,Guo et al. 2025,4,5,6,(Wang et al. 2024) Therefore, conducting performance research on frozen soil is an important guarantee for the safety of engineering construction under negative temperature conditions.

Many experts and scholars have conducted experimental research on the physical and mechanical properties of low-temperature frozen soil and achieved rich results7–10. Wu et al explored the physical and mechanical properties of frozen soil in the freezing project of the connection passage through experiments, and evaluated the safety of the frozen wall based on the test results, providing an experimental basis for the safety and reliability of the connection passage construction11. Chen et al conducted research on the uniaxial compression mechanical properties and creep performance of frozen clay in a certain mine in northern Anhui Province, and established creep function equations at different freezing temperatures12. Lu et al conducted compressive and shear tests to study the mechanical properties of frozen silty clay with different moisture contents13. They found that the cohesion, internal friction Angle and compressive strength of the specimens first increased and then decreased with the increase of moisture content. There exists an optimal moisture content for frozen silty clay. Wang et al conducted laboratory experiments to study the triaxial mechanical properties and freeze-thaw deformation laws of artificially frozen silky clay14,15. The research results are of great significance for guiding engineering applications. Ma et al analyzed the strength and failure characteristics of frozen silty clay under different small principal stresses and low-temperature conditions using a true triaxial instrument16. Therefore, moisture content and temperature have a significant impact on the physical and mechanical properties of frozen soil.

During the excavation of foundation pits in winter, frozen soil in tunnel foundation pits is often subjected to dynamic loads such as mechanical excavation and mechanical transportation17–20. Its mechanical properties are different from those under static loads. Li et al found that the dynamic peak intensity of permafrost continuously weakened with the increase in the number of freeze-thaw cycles and the decrease in temperature, and damage occurred when the intensity reached the limit21,22. Zhu et al proposed a dynamic constitutive model of permafrost in a multi-axis state based on the different forms exhibited by permafrost under confining pressure and its strengthening characteristics23. Kang et al conducted a comparative analysis of separated Hopkinson compression tests and numerical simulations on Dandong frozen clay at different temperatures, and concluded that a decrease in temperature would cause the specimens to tend towards tensile shear failure24. Qiao analyzed the influence of different initial moisture contents on the strength of frozen soil by conducting impact tests on three unsaturated frozen soil samples with different initial moisture contents25. Zhang et al analyzed the dynamic elastic modulus of unsaturated silt at different saturation levels through temperature-controlled dynamic triaxial experiments, with different moisture contents as variable conditions26. Li et al conducted impact compression tests on frozen soil under freeze-thaw cycles and found that an increase in the number of freeze-thaw cycles would reduce the peak stress of frozen soil, while an increase in strain rate and a decrease in temperature would increase the peak stress of the specimens21,22. Based on the test results, they established a damage viscoelastic constitutive model of the Z-W-T equation. Cai investigated the dynamic mechanical properties of frozen soil under confining pressure and found that the presence of confining pressure would reduce the viscosity of frozen clay and enhance the dynamic compressive strength of the material27.

At present, a large number of permafrost impact tests are conducted under the conditions of saturated permafrost. However, the structure of frozen soil is inherently complex, being a heterogeneous and anisotropic complex composed of soil particles, ice particles, liquid water and gas28. To further explore the impact mechanical properties of frozen soil with different moisture contents, this paper takes the high-speed railway open-cut tunnel project in the water-rich and weak stratum of the Jinan connection line section of the Tianjin-Weifang High-Speed Railway as the engineering background. The Hopkinson compression bar (SHPB) device is used to conduct dynamic compression tests on the silty clay in the foundation pit at different temperatures, moisture contents and impact pressures. To analyze the influence of different conditions on the stress-strain and failure mode of frozen silty clay, and to provide a reference for the winter construction of foundation pits in Shandong region.

Specimen preparation

The test was conducted using reshaped soil. The soil samples were crushed and an appropriate amount of soil samples were placed in a drying oven and dried at 105℃ for 24 hours. The soil samples were further crushed and passed through a 2mm sieve. According to the soil conditions at the construction site, three types of silvery clay with moisture contents of 21%, 26% and 31% were prepared. Groundwater from the nearby construction site was added to the dry soil in several portions as required. The three unsaturated soil samples with different moisture contents were placed in moisture-retaining dishes and left to stand for more than 48 hours. Take an appropriate amount of soil sample, prepare it with an acrylic mold with a diameter of 50mm and a height of 30mm, smooth it out, wrap it with cling film and place it in a -25℃ freezer for 3 hours for rapid solidification. Then, quickly remove the mold and continue to freeze it at -10℃, -15℃ and -20℃ for more than 48 hours respectively. The test process is shown in Fig. 2. After the specimen preparation was completed, the tests were carried out using the SHPB device as shown in Fig. 3. Test principle

The experiment achieves the control effect of air pressure through a pressure controller. The bullet is ejected by high-pressure nitrogen gas and strikes the incident rod, generating stress waves. Meanwhile, the resistance strain gauge adhered to the incident rod collects the incident electrical signal. Part of the incident stress wave passes through the test block and is transmitted into the transmission rod. The semiconductor strain gauge attached to the transmission rod records the transmitted electrical signal. Part of the stress wave is destroyed and absorbed by the test piece, and the remaining part is reflected back into the incident rod and recorded by the strain gauge in the form of reflected waves29. When conducting experimental tests, the following basic assumptions must be met:One-dimensional stress wave assumption: After a bullet strikes the incident rod, a compression wave is generated. It is assumed that the propagation of the compression wave is entirely along the longitudinal surface of the rod and always in a plane, and there is no dissipation phenomenon during the propagation.Uniformity assumption: Throughout the entire propagation process, the stress propagates completely uniformly on both the incident rod and the transmitted rod.

For the assumption of one-dimensional stress waves, it can be achieved by using an incident rod with a variable cross-section, and ensuring that the cross-sections of the incident rod, specimen, and transmission rod are in full contact can be integrated through vaseline. The uniformity assumption requires that the incident rod be long enough to prevent the incident stress from generating reflected stress upon contact with the test block. The reflected stress and the incident stress do not overlap, thereby ensuring the uniformity of stress propagation. Under these assumptions, the entire experiment was carried out in the following ways: The bullet is fired with high-pressure nitrogen gas through a pressure control device at a speed of VsI. The bullet collides with the incident rod to generate an incident stress wave σI that propagates through the incident rod. It also collides with the test block and the transmission rod. The test block absorbs part of the incident stress, part of the transmitted stress wave σT, and part of the reflected stress wave σR. The specific propagation form is shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4The propagation law of stress waves between the member and the specimen.

The propagation law of stress waves between the member and the specimen.

At the contact interface between the specimen and the compression rod, evenly apply a layer of low-temperature vaseline (about 0.1 mm thick). On the one hand, it fills the tiny gaps at the interface and reduces the difference in interface wave impedance. On the other hand, it prevents the end face of the specimen from adhering to the compression rod due to low temperature during the test. Signal acquisition is carried out on the transmission rod using semiconductor strain gauges. The model of the semiconductor strain gauge is HU-101B-120, with a resistance value of 120(±0.05) Ω and a sensitivity coefficient of 110(±5)%. Before the test, the incident wave, reflected wave and transmitted wave signals were collected through an oscilloscope to verify the matching effect. Designed in response to the low-temperature and fragile characteristics of frozen specimens, the above measures not only ensure the integrity of the specimens but also achieve smooth transmission of stress waves through layered matching, which can effectively improve the accuracy of dynamic stress-strain data in SHPB tests. Strain rate effect

The effects of different influencing factors on the peak stress of frozen silty clay at different strain rates are shown in Fig. 7, and the dynamic compressive stress-strain curve of frozen silty clay is shown in Fig. 8.Fig. 7Influencing factors of peak stress in frozen silty clay.Fig. 8Dynamic compressive stress-strain curve of frozen silty clay.

Influencing factors of peak stress in frozen silty clay.

Dynamic compressive stress-strain curve of frozen silty clay.

As can be seen from Fig. 7, under the same temperature and moisture content conditions, frozen silty clay has a distinct strain rate effect, that is, as the strain rate continuously increases, the peak stress of the frozen soil gradually increases. As can be seen from Fig. 8, under the same temperature conditions, the dynamic compressive peak strength of frozen silt and frozen silty clay continuously increases with the increase of strain rate. With the continuous increase of strain rate, the peak strain shows the same effect. As the peak strain keeps increasing, the stress-strain curve of frozen soil shows a significant strain rate effect.

The stress-strain curves of frozen silty clay at different temperatures all have a distinct plateau stage. That is, the dynamic stress-strain curve of frozen soil can be divided into three stages: (a) Elastic stage - As the specimen is a mixture of soil and ice water after freezing, it will exhibit the type of elastic stage in the early stage of the impact compression process. Moreover, as the strain rate increases, during the impact process, due to the larger strain rate, the front end of the soil is more prone to damage, resulting in a greater strain on the front end of the soil, which will cause the sample to enter the plastic stage more quickly. (b) Plastic stage (platform stage) - At this stage, the sample undergoes irreversible damage, with both the soil sample and the ice-water mixture being destroyed simultaneously, resulting in the highest strength. However, as the strain continues to increase, the sample still retains a certain load-bearing capacity, and the stress is basically maintained within the peak stress range. (c) Failure stage - As the strain further increases, the ice-water mixture no longer has the load-bearing capacity, and the soil cementation sharply decreases, resulting in a sharp drop in stress. However, when the strain rate is relatively large, the deformation of the sample will be greater, making the sample failure more severe, and the strain will continuously increase with the increase of the strain rate.

The variation law of moisture content

The stress influence law of moisture content was analyzed. Under the same strain rate, the variation law of moisture content of frozen soil at different temperatures was analyzed, as shown in Fig. 9.Fig. 9The law of the influence of moisture content in frozen silty clay on stress.

The law of the influence of moisture content in frozen silty clay on stress.

As can be seen from Fig. 9, the peak stress of frozen silt and frozen silty clay shows a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with the variation of moisture content. At -10℃, -15℃ and -20℃, the peak stress variation trend of frozen silty clay during the process of moisture content ranging from 21% to 31% is an increasing trend first and then a decreasing one. The analysis of the variation law of the peak stress of frozen silty clay, which first increases and then decreases with water content, is because when the moisture content of silty clay is 26%, it is already in a state without voids at the natural moisture content. Therefore, at this time, the low-temperature frozen test block reaches the ice-soil equilibrium, causing the stress intensity to reach the maximum.

When the moisture content is relatively low, that is, 21%, there are still voids inside the frozen sample, so there will be a compression process. During the transition from the elastic deformation stage to the plastic deformation stage of the frozen soil sample, the ice solid in the soil mass is the first to be destroyed. During the plastic deformation stage, the strength of the frozen soil sample with a moisture content of 21% will be lower than that of the frozen soil sample with a moisture content of 26% without voids. As the moisture content increases, when it reaches 31%, after the voids in the sample are filled, further increasing the moisture content will lead to a decrease in the soil content. An increase in water content, after low-temperature freezing, is manifested as an increase in the ice body content and a decrease in the soil content. In terms of strength, the strength of the ice body is lower than that of the soil, so the peak stress shows a pattern of stress reduction.

Temperature effect

The stress-strain curves of frozen soil at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 10, and the relationship between the dynamic peak stress and temperature of three specimens with different moisture contents is shown in Fig. 11.Fig. 10Stress-strain curves of frozen soil under different temperature influences.Fig. 11The relationship between dynamic peak intensity and temperature.

Stress-strain curves of frozen soil under different temperature influences.

The relationship between dynamic peak intensity and temperature.

As can be seen from Fig. 10, the stress of frozen silty clay gradually increases with the decrease of temperature, showing a temperature effect. The influence of temperature on strain is relatively significant. It can be clearly seen from the Fig. that the variation trend of stress with strain is obvious. From the start of the test until the strain reaches 1%, that is, in the elastic stage, since the frozen silty clay has not yet been damaged, the stress at -10℃ to -20℃ decreases, and the corresponding strain is also relatively low. At the failure stage, at -20℃, the deformation of the sample increases, and the failure tends to be more like that of rock-type materials. The peak stress also increases accordingly. This is also because the lower temperature leads to better bonding effect of frozen soil, thereby increasing hardness. The final strain shows a distinct temperature effect, that is, as the temperature decreases, the final strain gradually decreases. This is mainly due to the increase in ice content in the test block, and ice is a brittle material. Its deformation effect is definitely not as small as that of the water-soil mixture, so it is manifested as a decrease in final strain.

As can be seen from Fig. 11, under different moisture contents, there are certain differences in the fitting curves of peak intensity and temperature. When the moisture content is 26%, the peak strength and temperature fitting curve fits well under three impact pressures, with the coefficient (R2) above 0.95. The fitting effect is not so good when the moisture content is 31%. This might be because the frozen silty clay test block contains a relatively large amount of ice at this time, thus showing different force characteristics. Due to different dynamic peak intensities, there will be slight deviations when fitting the peak intensity and temperature, resulting in a lower fitting coefficient. However, the temperature effect of the specimen is not affected.

After conducting impact tests on the specimens, the failed specimens with a temperature of -10℃ and a moisture content of 26% were taken for analysis. The failure patterns of frozen silvery clay under different impact pressures are shown in Fig. 12. It can be seen that under the impact pressure of 0.3 MPa, the failure of the specimen is just in a fragmented state. With the increase of the impact pressure, the number of broken pieces becomes more and more, and the size of the pieces becomes smaller and smaller. The failure mode analysis of test blocks with an impact pressure of 0.3 MPa and a moisture content of 26% at different temperatures is shown in Fig. 13. It is clearly observed that as the temperature drops, the fracture state of the specimens becomes more pronounced. At -10℃, they break into two pieces, and at -15℃, they also break into two pieces, but with more small pieces. At -20℃, they break into four pieces, accompanied by small pieces. During the process of temperature decrease, the ice content of the specimens increases significantly. As the water molecules in the soil transform into ice molecules, the failure of the specimens tends to be more brittle, and thus the degree of failure is more severe. Under the basic conditions of a temperature of -20 degrees Celsius and an impact pressure of 0.3 MPa, the failure modes with different moisture contents are shown in Fig. 14. As the moisture content keeps increasing, the damage to the specimens becomes more severe. This is because when the moisture content rises, water molecules turn into ice under negative temperature conditions. With the increase of ice content, the deformation ability of the specimens deteriorates, making them more prone to damage. Therefore, when the moisture content increases, the damage to the specimens becomes more serious.

Conclusions

I.The dynamic compressive peak strength of frozen silty clay continuously increases with the increase of strain rate, showing a significant strain rate effect. The dynamic stress-strain curve can be divided into three stages: a, the elastic stage; b, the plastic stage (plateau stage); and c, the failure stage. In the plastic stage, a significant strain rate effect is exhibited.II.Under the same temperature and impact pressure, the peak dynamic strength of frozen silky clay first increases and then decreases with the increase of moisture content. At a moisture content of 26% (natural moisture content), the peak strength reaches its maximum.III.The dynamic peak strength of frozen silty clay shows a significant temperature effect, that is, as the temperature continuously decreases, the peak strength of frozen clay keeps expanding. The relationship between peak intensity and temperature shows a standard linear relationship.IV.The fracture morphology of the specimen continuously increases with the increase of impact air pressure, moisture content and temperature.V.For projects such as roadbeds and tunnels in cold regions that bear dynamic loads like train vibrations, the moisture content of frozen soil should be controlled within the optimal range of 26%. Optimize the structural load-bearing parameters in combination with temperature, evaluate the impact of strain rate effects, avoid disturbing the frozen soil framework, and ensure the long-term stability of the project.
