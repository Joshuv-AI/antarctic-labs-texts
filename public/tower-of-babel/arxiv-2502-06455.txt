Analysis of a finite element method for the Stokes–Poisson–Boltzmann equations
Abeer F. AlSohaim, Ricardo Ruiz-Baier, Segundo Villa-Fuentes

Abstract
We define a finite element method for the coupling of Stokes and nonlinear Poisson–Boltzmann equations. The novelty in the formulation is that the coupling from the electric potential to the drag in the momentum balance is rewritten as a weighted advection term. Using Banach’s contraction principle, the Babuška–Brezzi theory, and the Minty–Browder theorem, we show that the governing equations have a unique weak solution. We also show that the discrete problem is well-posed, establish Céa estimates, and derive convergence rates. We exemplify the properties of the proposed scheme via some numerical experiments showcasing convergence and applicability in the study of electro-osmotic flows in micro-channels. 2020 MSC codes: 65N30 (primary); 35J60, 76D07 (secondary). Keywords: Stokes–Poisson–Boltzmann equations; Fixed-point analysis; Finite element discretisation; Error estimates.

Introduction

Scope.

Electrically charged flows are of utmost importance in chemistry applications that concern, for example, the design of nanopores for biomedical devices and the modelling of water purification systems. One of the simplest processes to transport fluid without mechanical stimulation is electro-osmosis. The mobility of the fluid due to thin double layers that attract and repel ions in an electrolyte, can be described by the Stokes equations with a forcing term that depends on the charge of the electrolyte and an externally applied electric field. In a simplified scenario, the flow itself or its pressure gradients are not dominant enough to influence the distribution of the double layer electrostatic potential, and so the electric charge density can be simply related to the potential using (even simplified versions of) the Poisson–Boltzmann equation.

Regarding unique solvability and finite element (FE) discretisation for the Poisson–Boltzmann equation we refer to the work
[
6
]
(see also, e.g.,
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
) that use, for example, a splitting between regular and singular contribution expansion and apply convex minimisation arguments, and show an
L^{\infty}
bound for the solution using a cutoff-function approach. Here we take the regularised counterpart of this equation, which maintains the same type of nonlinearity (a hyperbolic sine) but does not include the distributional Dirac forcing terms. In addition, here we also include an advection term in the regularised potential equation, and following
[
9
]
we restrict the functional space of the double layer potential to a bounded set (in turn, permitting us to have a bounded nonlinear operator).

The Stokes–Poisson–Boltzmann equations.

Let us consider a Lipschitz bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n=2,3
with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
split into two parts where different types of boundary conditions will be considered, and denote by
\boldsymbol{n}
the outward unit normal vector on the boundary. The domain is filled with an incompressible fluid subjected to pressure gradients and electric charges in an electrolyte. The set of equations that govern the stationary regime are written in terms of fluid velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
, pressure
p
, and electrostatic double layer potential
\psi
, and read as follows

\displaystyle-\mu\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}-\varepsilon\Delta\psi\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\kappa(\psi)+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\psi-\varepsilon\Delta\psi

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\psi

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{D},

(1.1d)

\displaystyle(\mu\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}-p\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\varepsilon\nabla\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{N}.

(1.1e)

Here
\mathbf{I}
is the identity tensor in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\mu
is the fluid viscosity,
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
is a vector of external body forces,
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is an externally applied electric field (typically along the longitudinal direction, and assumed uniformly bounded by
\bar{E}>0
),
\varepsilon
is the electric permittivity of the electrolyte,
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is an external source/sink of potential, and

\kappa(\psi)=k_{0}\sinh(k_{1}\psi)

is the charge of the electrolyte as a nonlinear function of potential, where
k_{0}>0
depends on the valence, the electron charge, and the bulk ion concentration, and
k_{1}>0
depends additionally on the Boltzmann constant and the reference absolute temperature.
Following
[
9
, Lem. 2.1]
we can assume that

•

the potential is uniformly bounded between the values
\alpha\leq 0\leq\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

In addition, we assume
\kappa(0)=0
, and that there exists
\bar{K},\underline{K}>0
such that

•

|\kappa(s_{1})-\kappa(s_{2})|\leq\bar{K}|s_{1}-s_{2}|
for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in[\alpha,\beta]
,

•

|\kappa(s_{1})-\kappa(s_{2})|\geq\underline{K}|s_{1}-s_{2}|
for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in[\alpha,\beta]
.

Homogeneity of (
1.1d
)-(
1.1e
) is only assumed to simplify the exposition, however the results remain valid for more general assumptions.

Existence and uniqueness of weak solution

Weak formulation.

Consider
the following test and trial functional spaces for velocity, double layer potential, and pressure, respectively

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega):\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \text{on}\ \Gamma_{D}\},\hskip 20.00003pt\Phi_{0}:=\{\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega):\varphi=0\ \text{on}\ \Gamma_{D}\},

\displaystyle\Phi:=\{\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega):\varphi=0\ \text{on}\ \Gamma_{D}\ \text{and}\ \alpha\leq\varphi\leq\beta\},\hskip 20.00003pt\mathrm{Q}:=L^{2}(\Omega).

Then, multiplying (
1.1a
)-(
1.1c
) by suitable test functions, integrating by parts, and employing the boundary conditions, we are left with the following weak formulation: find
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\psi)\in\mathbf{V}\times\mathrm{Q}\times\Phi
such that

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+A^{\psi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)

\displaystyle=F^{\psi}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V},

(2.1a)

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{u},q)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall q\in\mathrm{Q},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle(\kappa(\psi),\varphi)+c(\boldsymbol{u};\psi,\varphi)+d(\psi,\varphi)

\displaystyle=G(\varphi)

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in\Phi_{0},

(2.1c)

where the following bilinear and trilinear forms are used

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\mu\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}:\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v},\hskip 10.00002ptb(\boldsymbol{v},q):=-\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{v}\,q,

\displaystyle c(\boldsymbol{v};\psi,\varphi):=\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\psi)\varphi,\hskip 10.00002ptd(\psi,\varphi):=\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\varphi,

as well as (for a fixed
\hat{\psi}
) the following
linear
functionals and bilinear form

F^{\hat{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{v}):=\!\int_{\Omega}\{\boldsymbol{f}+[g-\kappa(\hat{\psi})]\mathbf{E}\}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},\ G(\varphi):=\!\int_{\Omega}g\,\varphi,\ A^{\hat{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\!\int_{\Omega}[\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\hat{\psi}](\mathbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}).

Note that the specific forms of
F^{\psi}(\bullet)
and
A^{\psi}(\bullet,\bullet)
do not have the electric permittivity
\varepsilon
, since the weak formulation we propose comes from rewriting the last source term in the right-hand side of (
1.1a
) using the potential equation (
1.1c
). This has the advantage that we do not need to integrate by parts the term containing the Laplacian of the potential in the momentum equation, and it gives us a more convenient structure for the fixed-point analysis below. The bilinear and trilinear forms above are uniformly bounded, that is, for all
\boldsymbol{u}
,
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}
,
q\in\mathrm{Q}
,
\psi
,
\hat{\psi}\in\Phi
and
\varphi\in\Phi_{0}
, the following inequalities hold

\displaystyle|a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})|\leq\mu\,\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega},\hskip 10.00002pt|b(\boldsymbol{v},q)|\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}\|q\|_{0,\Omega},

\displaystyle|d(\psi,\varphi)|\leq\varepsilon\|\psi\|_{1,\Omega}\|\varphi\|_{1,\Omega},\hskip 10.00002pt|{c}(\boldsymbol{v};\psi,\varphi)|\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}\,\|\psi\|_{1,\Omega}\,\|\varphi\|_{1,\Omega},

\displaystyle|A^{\hat{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})|\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\bar{E}\|\hat{\psi}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega},

where it suffices to apply Hölder’s inequality and the estimate
\|\phi\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}\|\phi\|_{1,\Omega}
,
valid for all
\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
(cf.
[
13
, Th. 1.3.3]
). So are the linear functionals in
\mathbf{V}^{\prime}
(under the assumption that
\hat{\psi}
is in a bounded set), and
\Phi^{\prime}

|F^{\hat{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{v})|\leq\bigl[\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}+\bar{E}\|g\|_{0,\Omega}+\bar{K}\bar{E}\|\hat{\psi}\|_{1,\Omega}\bigr]\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega},\hskip 10.00002pt|G(\varphi)|\leq\|g\|_{0,\Omega}\|\varphi\|_{1,\Omega}.

Using the Poincaré inequality
\|\phi\|_{1,\Omega}\leq C_{\mathrm{p}}|\phi|_{1,\Omega}
, with
C_{\mathrm{p}}>0
and valid for all
\phi\in\{\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega):\varphi=0\ \text{on}\ \Gamma_{D}\}
, it is not difficult to see that the bilinear forms
a(\bullet,\bullet)
and
d(\bullet,\bullet)
are coercive in
\mathbf{V}
and
\Phi
, respectively,

|a(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})|\geq{\mu}[C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}]^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt|d(\varphi,\varphi)|\geq\varepsilon[{C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}]^{-1}\|\varphi\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\varphi\in\Phi_{0}.

Furthermore,
b(\bullet,\bullet)
satisfies the following inf-sup condition (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
)

\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{v},q)}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}}\geq\beta\,\|q\|_{0,\Omega}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall q\in\mathrm{Q}.

Well-posedness analysis.

We will employ a fixed-point argument in combination with the Babuška–Brezzi theory
[
4
]
and the Minty–Browder theorem
[
7
]
. The analysis closely follows the approach in, e.g.,
[
2
,
5
]
for Boussinesq-type of problems where one separates the incompressible flow equations from the nonlinear transport equation and then connects them back using Banach’s fixed-point theorem.
First, consider the following set

\mathrm{Z}:=\bigl\{\hat{\psi}\in\Phi:\hskip 10.00002pt\|\hat{\psi}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq{\mu}[{2C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\bar{E}}]^{-1}\bigr\},

(2.2)

and, for a fixed
\hat{\psi}\in\mathrm{Z}
, the problem of finding
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathbf{V}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

\begin{array}[]{clc}a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+A^{\hat{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)&=F^{\hat{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V},\\
b(\boldsymbol{u},q)&=0&\forall q\in\mathrm{Q}.\end{array}

(2.3)

Owing to the unique solvability of the Stokes equations with mixed boundary conditions
[
8
]
(in turn derived from the following properties of
[a+A^{\hat{\psi}}](\bullet,\bullet)

\displaystyle|a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+A^{\hat{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(\mu+{\mu}[{2C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}]^{-1}\bigr)\,\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega},

\displaystyle|a(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})+A^{\hat{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})|

\displaystyle\geq{\mu}[{2C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}]^{-1}\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2},

(2.4)

which arise from the properties of
a(\bullet,\bullet)
and
A^{\hat{\psi}}(\bullet,\bullet)
, the definition of
\mathrm{Z}
, and the continuity and stability of
b(\bullet,\bullet)
), we conclude that the operator

\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{flow}}:\Phi\to\mathbf{V}\times\mathrm{Q},\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\psi}\mapsto\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{flow}}(\hat{\psi})=(\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{flow}}_{1}(\hat{\psi}),\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{flow}}_{2}(\hat{\psi})):=(\boldsymbol{u},p),

is well-defined. Moreover, the continuous dependence on data provided by the Babuška–Brezzi theory gives, in particular, that

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq 2C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\mu^{-1}\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}+\bar{E}\|g\|_{0,\Omega}+\bar{K}\bar{E}\|\hat{\psi}\|_{1,\Omega}\bigr).

(2.5)

On the other hand, associated with the velocity, we define the following set

\mathbf{W}:=\bigl\{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{V}:\hskip 10.00002pt\|\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq{\varepsilon}[{2C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}}]^{-1}\bigr\},

(2.6)

and consider, for a fixed
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{W}
, the problem of finding
\psi\in\Phi
such that

(\kappa(\psi),\varphi)+c(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}};\psi,\varphi)+d(\psi,\varphi)=G(\varphi)\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\varphi\in\Phi_{0}.

(2.7)

Using Hölder and Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities we proceed to verify that the operator associated with this problem is bounded

|(\kappa(\psi),\varphi)+c(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}};\psi,\varphi)+d(\psi,\varphi)|\leq(\bar{K}+\varepsilon+C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1,\Omega})\,\|\psi\|_{1,\Omega}\|\varphi\|_{1,\Omega}.

Next we can use the coercivity of
d(\bullet,\bullet)
, the definition of
\mathbf{W}
, and the monotonicity of
\kappa(\bullet)
, to obtain the strong monotonicity of the solution operator

\displaystyle(\kappa(\psi_{1})-\kappa(\psi_{2}),\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})+c(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}};\psi_{1}-\psi_{2},\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})+d(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2},\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\geq{\varepsilon}[{2C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}]^{-1}\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|^{2}_{1,\Omega}\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\Phi.

(2.8)

Then, the Minty–Browder theorem gives that

\exists!\,\psi\in\Phi
so the map

\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{elec}}:\mathbf{V}\to\Phi,\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\mapsto\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{elec}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}})=\psi,

is well-defined. In addition, using (
2.8
), we have that the solution satisfies

\|\psi\|_{1,\Omega}\leq{2C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}{\varepsilon}^{-1}\|g\|_{0,\Omega}.

(2.9)

Then, we introduce an operator equivalent to the solution map of (
2.1
):

\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{W}\to\mathbf{W},\hskip 20.00003pt{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}\mapsto\mathbf{T}({\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}):=\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{flow}}_{1}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{elec}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}})).

(2.10)

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and

satisfy

4C_{\mathrm{p}}^{4}C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}[\mu\varepsilon]^{-1}\bigl(1+\bar{E}+2\bar{K}\bar{E}C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}{\varepsilon}^{-1}\bigr)(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|g\|_{0,\Omega})\leq 1.

Then, the operator

is well-defined and
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{W})\subseteq\mathbf{W}
.

Proof.

Given
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{W}
, (

) implies

. Combining this with (

) gives

. Using that

and

are well-defined, it follows that

is well-defined. Moreover, from (

) and (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{flow}}_{1}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{elec}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}))\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\mu^{-1}\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}+\bar{E}\|g\|_{0,\Omega}+\bar{K}\bar{E}{2C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}{\varepsilon}^{-1}\|g\|_{0,\Omega}\bigr).

Combining this with the small data assumption (
2.11
), we conclude that
\mathbf{T}(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}})\in\mathbf{W}
, thereby completing the proof.

3
Finite element discretisation

Let us denote
\mathcal{T}_{h}
a shape-regular simplicial mesh of
\Omega
with mesh-size

. Given

,

\chi_{p}:=\hat{q}_{h}-p_{h}
,
\xi_{\psi}:=\psi-\hat{\varphi}_{h}
and

\chi_{\psi}:=\hat{\varphi}_{h}-\psi_{h}
.
Proceeding as in Theorem
1
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|+\|\chi_{p}\|+\|\chi_{\psi}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\bar{K}\bar{E}C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}C_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\|\psi_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}+\varepsilon\|\xi_{\psi}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\ +C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\|\psi\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}+(1+\mu)\|\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\xi_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\ +C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\xi_{\psi}\|_{1,\Omega}+C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\bar{E}\|\psi_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\xi_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1,\Omega}.

Then, from
(
2.13
) and (
3.2
), assumption (
3.3
), and the fact that
(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},\hat{q}_{h},\hat{\varphi}_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\Phi_{h}
is arbitrary, the desired result is obtained.
∎

Finally, the following result is a direct consequence of Theorem
3
and standard interpolation properties for Taylor–Hood FEs
[
8
]
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{u},p,\psi),(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\psi_{h})
be the continuous and discrete solutions assuming
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbf{V}\cap\mathbf{H}^{s+1}(\Omega)
,
p\in H^{s}(\Omega)
, and
\psi\in\Phi\cap H^{s+1}(\Omega)
, for some
s\in(1/2,k+1]
. Then, there exists
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\psi-\psi_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq C\,h^{s}\{\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1+s,\Omega}+|p|_{s,\Omega}+\|\psi\|_{1+s,\Omega}\}.

Finally, we present a simulation of electrically charged flow in a nanopore sensor with obstacles. The geometry of size 12 nm
\times
16 nm and flow configuration are adapted from
[
12
, Section 4.5]
(although that work focuses on Poisson–Nernst–Planck/Stokes equations). An ionic current is generated by a difference of potential on the top and bottom of the nanopore
\psi_{\max}=2
,
\psi_{\min}=0
. Also, a parabolic inflow velocity
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}=(0,-0.1\tanh(40(6-x)^{2}))^{\tt t}
is imposed on the top boundary and outflow boundary conditions are considered on the bottom. On the outer left and right segments, we impose a slip condition
(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0)
and on the remainder of the boundary we impose no-slip conditions for the velocity
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}
and zero-flux for the potential. For this example, we incorporate again the advective term in the potential equation as well as the convective nonlinearity in the momentum balance, and consider the following parameter values (all in the nm scale)

\mu=0.1\,\mathrm{Pa}\cdot\mathrm{s},\hskip 10.00002pt\varepsilon=0.075,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{E}=(0.1,-0.1)^{\tt t},\hskip 10.00002ptk_{0}=10^{-3}.

The aim of the example is simply to simulate the electric patterns and corresponding flow associated with an applied field pointing not straight down, but with a slight angle to break symmetry. In Figure
4.3
we show the velocity line integral contours, the pressure drop and the potential distribution. Thanks to a comparable drag force used herein, we can see a similar recirculation as in the aforementioned reference.
