A subset of SLC2A1 missense variants interfere with GLUT1 trafficking in human brain endothelial cells
Authors: Sara Lazzari, Agnese Giovannetti, Francesco Petrizzelli, Veronica De Paolis, Francesca Matarazzo, Alvaro H. Crevenna, Tommaso Biagini, Tommaso Mazza, Chiara Parisi, Viviana Caputo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1), encoded by the SLC2A1 gene, mediates basal glucose uptake and is essential for brain energy homeostasis. Genetic variants in SLC2A1 cause GLUT1 Deficiency Syndrome (GLUT1 DS), a rare neurometabolic disorder characterized by impaired glucose transport across the blood–brain barrier and encompassing a wide and expanding phenotypic spectrum. Despite the fact that a high proportion of reported pathogenic SLC2A1 variants are missense, their effects on GLUT1 cellular biology remain incompletely understood, and the relative contribution of altered transport, protein stability, and intracellular trafficking has not been fully defined.MethodsEight SLC2A1 missense variants were selected and mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis of GLUT1-EGFP encoding plasmid. Subcellular localization and trafficking were investigated by using confocal microscopy and quantitative colocalization analysis in human brain endothelial (hCMEC/D3) and HeLa cells, also in response to changes in extracellular glucose availability; protein content in response to autophagy perturbation was evaluated by western blotting; protein degradation rate was assessed through cycloheximide chase assays.ResultsWe found that four of the selected variants were associated with altered GLUT1 distribution and responsiveness to extracellular glucose availability, promoting intracellular retention, with most of them showing marked association to lysosome-related compartments (N34S, R126L and Q283R).ConclusionsOur study identifies variant-associated differences in GLUT1 intracellular distribution in a subset of pathogenic SLC2A1 missense variants, with increased lysosomal targeting representing a recurrent cellular fate. These findings provide insights into variant-associated differences in GLUT1 cellular behaviour and highlight intracellular trafficking analysis as a complementary approach to transport-based functional assays for investigating GLUT1 biology. Further studies integrating cellular phenotypes with patient-derived models, biochemical and clinical data will be required to determine how the observed trafficking alterations relate to disease biology and clinical presentation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10020-026-01584-4.

Background

GLucose Transporter Type 1 (GLUT1), encoded by the solute carrier family 2 member 1 (SLC2A1, MIM *138,140) gene, is a facilitated glucose transporter that ensures basal glucose uptake in multiple tissues and is essential for maintaining systemic energy homeostasis. It is involved in basal glucose uptake in erythrocytes and in the endothelial cells of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), where it plays an essential role in glucose supply to the brain (Scheepers et al. 2004). GLUT1 plasma membrane association is dynamically regulated by trafficking mechanisms responsive to cellular energy status. Once internalized, cytoplasmic GLUT1 can be routed through endosomal compartments, where it may undergo recycling to the plasma membrane or trafficking toward late endosomal/lysosomal pathways, depending on cellular context and metabolic cues (Wu et al. 2013; Simonetti et al. 2022).

Over the years, SLC2A1 sequence variants have been associated with different disorders, most notably GLUT1 Deficiency Syndrome (GLUT1 DS, MIM #606,777 and #612,126), a rare genetic neurometabolic disorder caused by impaired glucose transport into the brain, characterized by epilepsy, developmental delay, and movement disorders (Seidner et al. 1998; De Vivo et al. 1991). To date, the clinical presentation of SLC2A1 variants has progressively expanded beyond the classic GLUT1 DS phenotype into a highly heterogeneous spectrum characterized by diverse neurological manifestations, variable severity, and differing ages of onset (Wang et al. 2025). Accordingly, several distinct neurological disorders and isolated clinical features are currently considered within the expanding spectrum of SLC2A1-related conditions (Wang et al. 2025). Traditionally, GLUT1 DS diagnosis relies on a combination of the occurrence of clinical manifestations, biochemical findings, and molecular confirmation (Wang et al. 2025). The main biochemical marker is hypoglycorrhachia, which, documented by lumbar puncture, indicates the reduced glucose ratio between the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood. Early diagnosis is pivotal, as the ketogenic diet, that represents the standard treatment, should be initiated early for optimal outcomes (Wang et al. 2025).

Approximately 90% of GLUT1 DS patients carry SLC2A1 mutations, with haploinsufficiency being considered the main molecular mechanism of pathogenesis (Wang et al. 2025). Most patients represent sporadic cases carrying pathogenic variants that arise de novo, although autosomal dominant familial cases and, more rarely, recessive familial forms have been described (Klepper et al. 2009; Rotstein et al. 2010). According to currently standardized guidelines for the interpretation of DNA sequence variants (ACMG/AMP criteria, (Richards et al. 2015)), de novo occurrence provides strong evidence supporting pathogenicity, and many disease-causing variants can be classified as pathogenic and likely pathogenic on the basis of genetic evidence. Nevertheless, complementary clinical, segregation and, especially, functional data remain important in many cases, particularly when variant interpretation is uncertain or inherited variants are identified (Wang et al. 2025). While recent advances in the functional characterization of SLC2A1 variants have improved our understanding of their molecular consequences, many of them remain insufficiently characterized (Klepper 2025). This is particularly true for missense variants, which account for approximately 39% of the SLC2A1 mutations reported in major clinical databases (Human Gene Mutation Database, HGMD (Stenson et al. 2020), and ClinVar (Landrum et al. 2014)) and contribute substantially to the broad phenotypic spectrum of SLC2A1-related disorders (Wang et al. 2025). However, despite the prevalence and clinical relevance of missense variants, genotype–phenotype correlations remain elusive (Wang et al. 2005; Brockmann 2009).

To date, to investigate the functional impact of SLC2A1 variants, different in silico and in vitro approaches have been employed. In silico approaches suggest that variants may destabilize GLUT1 structure or alter protein interactions (Deng et al. 2014; Park 2015; Galochkina et al. 2019; Raja and Kinne 2020). On the other hand, in vitro functional assays have been developed to analyze the effect of different types of variants, through the evaluation of the kinetics of glucose transport, primarily in patients’ erythrocytes (Klepper et al. 1999) and Xenopus oocyte system (Wang et al. 2003; Zaman et al. 2018), and, more recently, through surface quantification assays (METAglut1 (Mochel et al. 2023)). However, while transport assays provide essential information on catalytic activity, they do not capture potential alterations in intracellular routing and spatial distribution that may influence the effective availability of the transporter at the cell surface.

Here, we comparatively examine eight SLC2A1 missense variants associated with diverse clinical phenotypes and distributed across different structural domains of GLUT1, with the aim of characterizing their subcellular distribution patterns, including their response to changes in extracellular glucose availability. Rather than directly assessing transport kinetics, our study focuses on trafficking-based phenotyping to determine whether distinct variants display differential intracellular routing and compartment association.

The panel includes variants located in distinct structural regions of GLUT1, with different levels of clinical and functional evidence, comprising previously reported pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants (N34S, R126L, R153C, R223P, R223W, Q283R, P485L) and one variant (I404V), classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in ClinVar included as a comparator, in order to assess its intracellular behaviour under the same experimental conditions. Two substitutions affecting the same residue (R223P and R223W) were included to assess whether alternative amino acid changes at a single position result in divergent trafficking behaviours. Functional studies were primarily conducted in hCMEC/D3 cells, an established in vitro model of the human BBB that is particularly relevant to the pathophysiology of GLUT1 deficiency (Weksler et al. 2013). Key findings were subsequently validated in HeLa cells, a widely used cellular system for investigating GLUT1 biology (Qualls-Histed et al. 2023; Yan et al. 2018), to assess the reproducibility of the observed phenotypes across distinct cellular backgrounds.

By identifying variant-specific redistribution patterns, this approach provides complementary information to conventional transport-based assays and expands the cellular characterization of SLC2A1 missense variants.

Materials and methods

SLC2A1 cloning in pEGFP-N1 vector and mutagenesis

To investigate the functional impact of selected GLUT1 missense variants, specific constructs were generated. The human SLC2A1 gene sequence (NM_006516.4) coding for GLUT1 protein (NP_006507.2) was amplified through PCR from hTERT-RPE1 cells cDNA using the following primers:SLC2A1 Forward 5’- GAGCAGGAGACCAAACGACGGG -3’SLC2A1 Reverse 5’- CACTTGGGAATCAGCCCCCAG-3’

SLC2A1 Forward 5’- GAGCAGGAGACCAAACGACGGG -3’

SLC2A1 Forward 5’- GAGCAGGAGACCAAACGACGGG -3’

SLC2A1 Reverse 5’- CACTTGGGAATCAGCCCCCAG-3’

SLC2A1 Reverse 5’- CACTTGGGAATCAGCCCCCAG-3’

Fragment was cloned into the Strataclone pSC-A-amp/kan vector and transformed using Strataclone Solo-Pack Competent Cells (Agilent Technologies). Plasmid DNA was isolated using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (QIAGEN) and subjected to Sanger sequencing using the BigDye Terminator Sequencing Kit v3.1 (Applied Biosystems). Then, the fragment corresponding to the SLC2A1 coding sequence was subcloned from pSC-A-amp/kan vector to the eukaryotic expression vector pEGFP-N1, in-frame with the EGFP coding sequence at the C-terminal end, using the HindIII (NEB) and SacII (NEB) restriction enzymes. The DNA construct was transformed into DH5α competent cells, isolated using Plasmid Maxi Kit (QIAGEN), and subjected to Sanger sequencing.

Selected GLUT1 missense variants were introduced in pEGFP-N1-GLUT1 vector by site-directed mutagenesis, using the Phusion Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit (ThermoScientific) with 5’-phosphorylated primers, one of which carries the selected variant. To increase mutagenesis efficiency, Dam-methylated parental plasmid DNA was digested by adding FastDigest DpnI enzyme to the PCR reaction mixture. The DNA construct was transformed into DH5α competent cells, isolated using the Plasmid Maxi Kit (QIAGEN), and Sanger sequenced. All the selected GLUT1 missense variants and the relative primer pairs used for mutagenesis experiments are listed in the Supplementary Table S1.

Cell cultures

The following cell lines were used for immunofluorescence and/or Western blot experiments. Human Cerebral Microvascular Endothelial Cell Line, hCMEC/D3, was purchased from Cedarlane (cat. #CLU512) and cultured in Endothelial Basal Medium (EBM-2) (Lonza, cat. #190,860) supplemented with 10% v/v heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Life Technologies, cat. #10,270–106), 0.1% v/v chemically defined lipid concentrate (Life Technologies, cat. #11,905,031), 5 μg/ml ascorbic acid (Sigma, cat. #A4544), 1 ng/ml human basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (Sigma, cat. #F0291), 10 mM HEPES (Life Technologies, cat. #15,630–080), 1.4 μM hydrocortisone (Sigma, cat. #H0135) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Life Technologies, cat. #15,140–122). hCMEC/D3 cells were grown on Petri dishes previously coated with a thin layer of collagen I (Collagen Type I, rat tail; Sigma, cat. #08–115), at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

HeLa cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco) supplemented with 10% v/v heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, at 37 °C and 5% CO2, on uncoated Petri dishes.

For experiments under conditions of glucose deprivation or defined glucose concentrations, Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium without glucose (Gibco) was used and supplemented, when required, with D-(+)-Glucose solution (Sigma, #G8644).

Transfection and immunofluorescence experiments

To study the subcellular localization and trafficking behaviour of the selected GLUT1 missense variants, immunofluorescence experiments were performed. hCMEC/D3 cells were seeded at 180,000 cells/well and HeLa cells at 150,000 cells/well in 6-well plates onto 12-mm cover glasses. After 24 h, cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen) with 250 ng of each plasmid in a 2:1 ratio: pEGFP-N1, pEGFP-N1-GLUT1-Wild-Type (WT)/-N34S/-R126L/-R153C/-R223P/-R223W/-Q283R/-I404V/-P485L. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 5 h, then Opti-MEM was replaced by hCMEC/D3 or HeLa complete medium, and cells were returned to the incubator for 48 h to perform all the experiments under standard culture conditions.

To investigate the impact of extracellular glucose availability on GLUT1 trafficking to lysosomes, 24 h after transfection, HeLa cells were subjected to glucose deprivation for 24 h. After glucose deprivation, cells were fixed (t0) or shifted toward different glucose concentrations, i.e. 5 mM, 15 mM, and 25 mM, for 24 h.

To investigate the involvement of the autophagic process, 24 h after transfection, hCMEC/D3 cells were treated with chloroquine (CQ) (Sigma, #C6628) at a concentration of 100 μM for 2 h or maintained under standard conditions.

For immunofluorescence analysis, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min at room temperature, cells were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) for 30 min. Then, selected primary antibodies were added in 1% BSA buffer and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. To study the subcellular localization of GLUT1 WT and mutant proteins, different primary antibodies were selected to mark specific cellular organelles and structures: Calreticulin Polyclonal antibody (proteintech, cat. #27,298–1-AP) to detect endoplasmic reticulum, Golgin-97 (D8P2K) Rabbit mAb (Cell signaling, cat. #13,192) for Golgi apparatus, RAB5 (D-11) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat. #sc-46692) for early endosomes, RAB7 Polyclonal Antibody (invitrogen, cat. #PA5-52,369) for late endosomes, LAMP1 antibody [H4A3] (abcam, cat. #ab25630) for lysosomes, Monoclonal Pan Cadherin antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #C1821) for plasma membrane and LC3 (Sigma, cat. #L7543) for autophagosomes. After several washes with 0.2% BSA in PBS, the appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies were added (Alexa Fluor® 647 AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), Jackson ImmunoResearch, cod. #711–605-152; Rhodamine (TRITC) AffiniPure® Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat. #711–025–152; Rhodamine (TRITC) AffiniPure® Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L), Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat. #715–025–150) followed by incubation at room temperature for 1 h under gentle agitation. After further several washes with 0.2% BSA in PBS, nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma). Coverslips were extensively rinsed and mounted onto microscope slides using Fluoromount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Sigma).

For LysoTracker staining, cells were incubated with LysoTracker Deep Red (ThermoScientific) diluted in pre-warmed culture medium to a final concentration of 50 nM. Following incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde.

Images acquisition and colocalization quantification

3D confocal images were sequentially acquired by Olympus’ PLAPON 60X OSC2 super-corrected objective confocal apparatus. Sequential 0.5-μm-thick z-stacked sections were imaged through the entire cell profile using a 60 × objective lens and were used to create maximum intensity projections (MIPs) and processed with Fiji (National Institute of Health). Alternatively, images were acquired with a Leica Thunder equipped with a Cicero Spinning Disk Confocal (Crest Optics), a sCMOS K8 camera (Leica) and a solid state laser engine (89-Noth), using a 60 × oil-immersion objective 1.4NA. All images were acquired with the same laser intensity before the analysis in Fiji. Colocalization of two fluorophores that are aligned with no cross-talk was quantified using Pearson's correlation coefficient (PCC) within the desired areas of the images. Imaging data from the experiments were analyzed using the Coloc 2 plugin of the Fiji distribution of ImageJ software (http://imagej.net/Coloc_2). Individual areas were selected as regions of interest (ROIs) before applying a mask and running the plugin. After running the plugin, Pearson’s R value (no threshold) reported in the ImageJ Log window was recorded for each cell. Values shown represent mean ± SEM (30 cells). Statistical significance was determined by an unpaired Student’s t-test (p < 0.01).

Western blot analysis

hCMEC/D3 cells were seeded at 180,000 cells/well in 6-well plates and transfected as described above. To assess whether perturbation of autophagy could affect the steady-state levels of WT and mutant GLUT1 proteins, Western blot analysis was performed to quantify GLUT1-EGFP protein in control condition (CTRL) and after CQ administration. For this purpose, after 24 h of transfection, chloroquine was administered at a concentration of 100 μM for 2 h, then proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer and 100X protease inhibitors (SERVA, cat. #39,102.01) for 30 min on ice and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The degradation rate of WT and mutant GLUT1 proteins was evaluated using cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were incubated with CHX (50 μg/mL; Sigma, cat. #C-7698), and protein extracts were harvested at 0, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h after treatment as described above. Proteins were resolved using SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (GVS North America, cat. #1,215,471), blocked with 5% nonfat dried milk powder (ITW reagents, cat. #A0830) in TBS tween 0,1% for 1 h, and incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies (Purified anti-GFP Epitope Tag Antibody, Biolegend, cat. #902,601; Anti-β-Actin − Peroxidase antibody, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #A3854) followed by incubation with secondary antibodies (Peroxidase AffiniPure® Rabbit Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L), Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat. #315–035-003) for 1 h RT. Immunocomplexes were detected by chemiluminescence (WesternBright Sirius, Advansta, cat. #K-12043-D10) and analyzed by densitometry using ImageJ. All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

For CQ experiments, statistical significance was determined by paired Student’s t-test (p < 0.05). For CHX experiments, values were expressed relative to time 0. Protein decay over time was analyzed using linear regression after logarithmic transformation of normalized values. Half-life values were calculated from the slope of the regression line using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software). Statistical significance was determined by unpaired Student’s t-test (p < 0.05).

Results

Subcellular localization of WT and Mutant GLUT1-EGFP proteins in hCMEC/D3 cells

To investigate variant-associated differences in GLUT1 subcellular localization and trafficking, we selected eight missense variants using criteria aimed at generating a mechanistically diverse comparison set. The panel included variants reported in the literature and/or clinical databases (HGMD, ClinVar) in association with the SLC2A1-related phenotype spectrum (N34S, R126L, R153C, R223P, R223W, Q283R, and P485L), together with one missense variant (I404V) currently reported as VUS in ClinVar and lacking previous functional evidence. Variants were chosen to span distinct topological/structural regions of GLUT1 (Supplementary Fig. S1) and to capture heterogeneity in prior clinical and experimental support. R223P and R223W were both included to assess whether different substitutions at the same residue could be associated with similar or divergent trafficking phenotypes. The selected variants should therefore be regarded as a targeted pilot panel rather than as a representative sampling of all SLC2A1 missense variants. Details of the selected variants are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

We firstly analyzed plasma membrane association of WT and selected GLUT1-EGFP mutants in hCMEC/D3 cells with an antibody that specifically recognizes all the cadherin family members. Qualitative confocal microscopy showed that GLUT1 WT was predominantly distributed on the plasma membrane, as expected, comparable to the R153C, R223P, R223W and I404V mutants. Conversely, N34S, Q283R, R126L and P485L mutants showed reduced colocalization with the plasma membrane marker and a concomitant increase in intracellular signal intensity (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S2A). Subcellular localization of WT and mutant GLUT1-EGFP proteins in HeLa cells

To validate our findings in a different cellular context, we performed immunofluorescence experiments in HeLa cells. We analyzed GLUT1 WT and the mutants N34S, Q283R, and P485L, which had shown significant differences in plasma membrane, early endosomes and lysosomes’ localization in hCMEC/D3 cells. The R153C mutant was also included, as its subcellular distribution was comparable to that of the WT.

Confocal microscopy images revealed evident intracellular retention for the N34S, Q283R, and P485L mutants, and a predominant membrane localization for the R153C mutant, similar to the WT, consistent with what was observed in hCMEC/D3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S9A).

Quantitative colocalization analysis of GLUT1-EGFP and cadherins signals resulted in a statistically significant reduction for the Q283R and P485L mutants compared with the WT (mean PCC = 0.71), albeit with markedly different values (mean PCC: Q283R = 0.58; P485L = 0.27), consistent with the trend observed in hCMEC/D3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S9B).

Similarly, in HeLa cells, intracellular accumulations of the N34S and Q283R GLUT1 mutants overlapped almost completely with LAMP1-positive signals. Quantitative colocalization analysis between GLUT1-EGFP and the lysosomal marker LAMP1 confirmed a significant increased colocalization for the N34S (mean PCC = 0.71) and Q283R (mean PCC = 0.59) mutants compared with the WT (mean PCC = 0.43), whereas the P485L mutant showed a slightly higher PCC value that did not reach statistical significance (mean PCC = 0.54; p = 0.03) (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Colocalization analysis of GLUT1-EGFP and the early endosomes marker RAB5 revealed significantly greater overlap in all tested mutants compared with the WT (Supplementary Fig. S11A). Quantitative analysis showed low mean PCC for the WT (mean PCC = 0.14), whereas all mutants resulted in significantly higher levels of colocalization, albeit to varying extents. Among them, the P485L mutant displayed the highest increase (mean PCC = 0.58), consistent with its behaviour in hCMEC/D3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S11B).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated selected GLUT1 missense variants to assess their subcellular localization and trafficking in response to changes in metabolic conditions.

These variants localize in different structural domains of the GLUT1 protein (Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Fig. S1), including cytoplasmic (R153C, R223P, R223W, P485L), extracellular (N34S), and transmembrane regions (R126L, Q283R, I404V). The eight missense variants analyzed show different levels of functional and clinical evidence in databases and in the literature (Supplementary Table S2) (Rotstein et al. 2010; Sánchez-Lijarcio et al. 2022; Tayebi et al. 2023; Mauri et al. 2022; Slaughter et al. 2009). Seven variants are classified as disease-causing mutation (DM) in HGMD and six of these are concurrently classified as pathogenic in ClinVar (N34S, Q283R, R153C, R126L, R223W and P485L). R223P is classified as a risk factor in ClinVar, while I404V is currently reported as a VUS in ClinVar and is absent from HGMD.

The number of patients reported in the literature for these variants varies considerably, ranging from single cases to approximately 20–30 patients, while I404V has not been reported in published clinical cases. The analyzed variants have been reported in the literature and in clinical databases in association with both classic GLUT1 DS and the broader SLC2A1-related phenotypic spectrum, including a range of neurological manifestations such as epilepsy, neurodevelopmental disorders, and movement abnormalities, with highly variable clinical severity and age of onset (Supplementary Table S2). Hypoglycorrhachia has been documented for the following variants: N34S, R153C, R126L, R223W, and P485L (Rotstein et al. 2010; Sánchez-Lijarcio et al. 2022; Tayebi et al. 2023; Mauri et al. 2022; Slaughter et al. 2009).

Notably, several of the analyzed variants receive low or ambiguous pathogenicity scores from AlphaMissense (Cheng et al. 2023), despite being classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in clinical databases and/or supported by experimental functional evidence (Supplementary Table S2). This discrepancy highlights that in silico prediction tools may not fully detect the complete spectrum of variant-specific effects on protein biology, including impacts on subcellular localization and trafficking, which are not directly captured by sequence-based prediction scores and may provide complementary information on variant-associated cellular effects.

Functional evidence available from the literature for the analyzed SLC2A1 variants are predominantly based on glucose uptake assays performed in different experimental systems, including patients’ erythrocytes, Xenopus oocytes and mammalian cell lines (Supplementary Table S2). In particular, N34S, R126L, R153C, R223P, R223W, and P485L, have consistently shown reduced glucose transport activity, although the extent of impairment varies among variants and across experimental systems (Supplementary Table S2). Moreover, preliminary data have linked the R126L variant to reduced plasma membrane expression (Wong et al. 2007; Pascual et al. 2008), while P485L has been shown to exhibit altered localization to intracellular compartments (Meyer et al. 2018; Lacoste et al. 2024). By contrast, Q283R and I404V lack experimental functional characterization.

Immunofluorescence analyses were performed in hCMEC/D3 cells, a human brain microvascular endothelial model that recapitulates key properties of the BBB (Weksler et al. 2013), representing a physiologically relevant context for GLUT1-related disorders. A summary of the molecular and cellular phenotypes identified in this study for all analyzed variants is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

GLUT1 WT was predominantly localized at the plasma membrane, with only a few small intracellular vesicles detected in early and late endosomes, consistent with constitutive trafficking through secretory and endosomal compartments reported for GLUT1 (Wu et al. 2013; Simonetti et al. 2022; Qualls-Histed et al. 2023). A similar distribution was observed for the R153C, R223P, R223W, and I404V mutants with no major alterations across endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi, early or late endosomes, or lysosomes.

For R153C, R223W, and R223P, which have been previously associated with reductions in glucose uptake (Wang et al. 2005; Zaman et al. 2018; Pascual et al. 2008; Suls et al. 2009), no evident redistribution toward intracellular compartments was observed under the experimental conditions used. I404V exhibited a WT-like intracellular distribution profile, with no evidence of enrichment in lysosomal or endosomal compartments.

In contrast, N34S, R126L, Q283R, and P485L showed reduced plasma membrane signal and prominent intracellular localization. Among them, N34S, R126L and Q283R had significantly increased colocalization with the lysosomal marker LAMP1, with N34S and Q283R also exhibiting strong association with LC3-positive compartments.

In details, R126L, previously shown to reduce membrane expression and glucose uptake in Xenopus oocytes and mammalian cells (Wong et al. 2007; Pascual et al. 2008; Klepper et al. 1999), and N34S, previously associated with variable clinical phenotypes (CM052363, HGMD 2025.4 (Stenson et al. 2020)) and reduced glucose uptake in erythrocytes (Wang et al. 2005; Mochel et al. 2023; Tayebi et al. 2023; Klepper et al. 2005), displayed increased association with LAMP1-positive compartments, suggesting redistribution toward intracellular compartments that may influence functional availability. Q283R, for which no previous in vitro functional data were available, exhibited one of the most pronounced lysosomal-associated phenotypes, providing, to our knowledge, the first experimental evidence of a distinct intracellular redistribution phenotype associated with this variant. P485L displayed a distinct pattern characterized by strong colocalization with Golgi and early endosomal markers and relatively limited association with lysosomes, further underscoring the molecular heterogeneity of SLC2A1 missense variants. This phenotype is consistent with previously reported intracellular mislocalization and enhanced interaction with components of the endocytic machinery (Meyer et al. 2018; Lacoste et al. 2024). These findings suggest that defective intracellular routing represents an additional cellular consequence of specific missense variants that may coexist with structural effects suggested by in vitro and in silico studies, indicating that multiple, non-mutually exclusive mechanisms can contribute to their cellular phenotype.

We replicated immunofluorescence analyses in HeLa cells, which are widely used in functional studies of GLUT1 (Qualls-Histed et al. 2023; Yan et al. 2018), for GLUT1 WT and for those mutants that showed significant differences in hCMEC/D3 cells. Results in HeLa cells largely confirmed trafficking alterations observed in brain endothelial cells, suggesting that the observed phenotypes are not restricted to a single cellular model, supporting the reproducibility of the observed variant-associated distribution patterns across different cellular backgrounds.

Recent reviews have emphasized that GLUT1 trafficking is dynamically integrated with endolysosomal and autophagy-related pathways that respond to cellular metabolic state (Petrosino and Grumati 2026). Within this framework, the preferential association of selected mutants with LAMP1- and LC3-positive compartments observed in our study supports the view that intracellular distribution of GLUT1 represents a metabolically responsive process rather than a purely degradative endpoint.

Consistent with this concept, our analysis of glucose-dependent redistribution revealed that GLUT1 WT exhibited increased association with LAMP1-positive compartments following glucose supplementation, indicating glucose-dependent modulation of intracellular compartment association. While R153C retained a similar dynamic profile, N34S, Q283R, and P485L displayed substantial LAMP1 association even under glucose deprivation, suggesting altered responsiveness of intracellular distribution to extracellular glucose availability. The atypical behavior of Q283R at high glucose concentration further indicates that specific missense variants may perturb the coupling between metabolic cues and GLUT1 intracellular routing.

In addition, N34S and Q283R showed a strong association with autolysosome LC3/LAMP1-positive structures, pointing to their aberrant gathering in the autophagic compartments, that was partially reduced upon chloroquine treatment. A defect in the autophagic machinery was then evidenced by analysis of GLUT1-EGFP expression. In fact, while WT and R153C proteins accumulate in response to chloroquine treatment, we did not observe any variation for N34S and Q283R, probably indicating an engulfment of autophagic machinery impeding physiological protein degradation. P485L, which showed limited lysosomal association, was likewise unaffected by chloroquine, consistent with a trafficking alteration occurring upstream of increased LAMP1 association.

Additional insight into the relationship between intracellular trafficking and protein homeostasis was provided by cycloheximide chase experiments. While R153C and P485L mutant proteins displayed degradation kinetics comparable to those of WT GLUT1, N34S and Q283R exhibited significantly decreased degradation rates and prolonged half-life. These findings are particularly noteworthy when considered in the context of the specific trafficking phenotypes observed for these variants. Indeed, the increased half-life of these mutant proteins supports the hypothesis that, although extensively directed to lysosome- and autophagy-related compartments, they are not effectively cleared once delivered. Their prolonged half-life therefore appears consistent with the accumulation of mutant GLUT1 within these structures, indicating that intracellular targeting and degradation may become uncoupled for these variants. Although the molecular bases remain to be elucidated, our data support a model in which some GLUT1 missense variants interfere with protein trafficking favouring lysosome-associated compartments targeting and this phenomenon is accompanied by inefficient protein degradation, resulting in their abnormal persistence within the lysosomal-autophagic network.

Moreover, although our data are highly consistent with a scenario whereby specific GLUT1 mutants are mistrafficked through the endo-lysosomal and autophagic systems, we cannot completely exclude a contribution of chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) to the intracellular fate of these proteins. CMA is a selective lysosomal degradation pathway mediated by HSC70 recognition of KFERQ-like motifs and interaction with the LAMP2A receptor at the lysosomal membrane (Li et al. 2011). In this context, conformational changes or altered accessibility of targeting motifs caused by missense variants could potentially influence recognition by CMA and contribute to the differential processing and degradation of mutant GLUT1 proteins. Even if none of the analyzed variants seems to interfere with KFERQ-like motifs in our preliminary inquiries, the prediction and interpretation of these motifs remain challenging, thus requiring a more in-depth analysis to draw definitive conclusions. Future studies will further dissect the precise molecular mechanisms driving these altered trafficking phenotypes and to define the relative contribution of CMA and vesicular trafficking pathways to mutant protein homeostasis.

Overall, our findings suggest that missense variants in SLC2A1 do not converge on a single cellular phenotype but instead differentially affect GLUT1 intracellular distribution patterns and their responsiveness to extracellular glucose conditions. These alterations may represent an additional layer of variability among GLUT1 missense variants.

Taken together, these results contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms through which SLC2A1 variants could impair GLUT1 function. Future studies using patient-derived models will be required to determine whether reproducible functional subclasses can be identified and whether they correlate with patient-level biochemical and clinical features.

In this context, trafficking-based cellular phenotyping assays may complement conventional transport measurements by providing additional information on the biological consequences of specific variants. Indeed, functional assays based solely on erythrocytes, which lack intracellular membrane systems, may fail to detect variants associated with altered intracellular routing activity. Some limitations should be acknowledged. All experiments were performed using overexpression systems with EGFP-tagged constructs. Nevertheless, consistent phenotypes across two independent cell models support the robustness of the observed alterations.

Moreover, only a subset of reported missense variants was examined. The analyzed variants were selected to provide a structurally and clinically heterogeneous pilot panel, rather than a representative sampling of the full SLC2A1 missense landscape.

Nevertheless, to our knowledge, this is the first study analyzing subcellular localization of such a number of SLC2A1 missense variants in parallel, under identical experimental conditions, using a BBB cell model that is directly relevant to the pathophysiology of GLUT1 deficiency.

However, the integration of these cellular findings with biochemical and clinical data remains challenging because of the rarity of GLUT1 DS and by the uneven availability of detailed information for individual variants. Addressing these limitations will be necessary to establish the clinical relevance of the observed cellular phenotypes.

Conclusions

Our study identifies variant-specific intracellular redistribution patterns, differences in protein turnover and altered responsiveness to extracellular glucose availability, highlighting the cellular heterogeneity of SLC2A1 missense variants. These findings expand our understanding of GLUT1 biology and provide a framework for future studies investigating the relationship between GLUT1 variant-associated cellular phenotypes and disease pathogenesis.
