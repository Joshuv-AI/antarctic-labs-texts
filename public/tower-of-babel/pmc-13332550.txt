Embedding Palliative Care Clinicians in ALS Teams Improves ALS Clinicians' Confidence in Their Patient Management and Satisfaction With Palliative Care
Authors: Astrid Grouls, Yaowaree L Leavell, Ambereen K Mehta, Gayle Kojimoto, David L O'Riordan, Samuel Maiser, Benzi M Kluger, Steven Z Pantilat, Kara E Bischoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsSpecialty palliative care (SPC) can improve symptoms and well‐being for people living with ALS. Few studies capture how ALS clinicians utilize or are impacted by SPC. We sought to understand how the presence of SPC clinicians on ALS teams impacts ALS clinicians' referrals to and satisfaction with SPC.MethodsWe conducted a survey of interdisciplinary ALS clinicians to understand their confidence in their team's care, SPC referral patterns, and satisfaction with SPC. We compared responses from ALS clinicians with and without SPC in their team.Results141 ALS clinicians completed the survey, having primarily neurology, nursing, and therapy backgrounds. ALS clinicians who had SPC embedded in their team reported more confidence in their team's ability to address pain (63.3% v 37.4%, p < 0.001), dyspnea (88.8% v 70.8%, p = 0.05), and end‐of‐life needs (71.4% v 52.1%, p = 0.005). They reported fewer SPC referral barriers and referred to SPC for different reasons. ALS clinicians with SPC on their team were more satisfied with SPC's ability to help with motor symptoms (92.7% v 71.4%, p = 0.02), dyspnea (98.3% v 86.7%, p = 0.04), and care coordination (90.0% v 73.5%, p = 0.04).DiscussionThe presence of SPC embedded within multidisciplinary ALS teams is associated with ALS clinicians feeling more confident in their team's ability to address certain care needs, more satisfied with the care provided by SPC, and experiencing fewer barriers to involving SPC. Additional research should explore how embedding SPC on ALS teams impacts patient outcomes.Keywords: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, neuromuscular disease, specialty palliative care

Introduction

While multidisciplinary ALS clinics are the evidence‐based standard of care, given the number of profound needs of people living with ALS (pALS) even robust multidisciplinary ALS teams can struggle to meet all of the needs of pALS and their care partners through primary palliative care (PC) [1, 2, 3]. Specialty palliative care (SPC) clinicians can assist ALS clinicians and patients in addressing a wide range of symptoms and complex care needs, to improve quality of life in the face of the illness [4, 5, 6, 7]. ALS teams have adopted multiple models for partnering with SPC, which range from embedding neuropalliative physicians within the ALS clinic to referring to external SPC clinics [8, 9]. However, there are few studies capturing how ALS clinicians across the U.S. collaborate with SPC clinicians and are impacted [1].

Here, we sought to assess whether having SPC clinicians embedded on ALS teams is associated with ALS clinicians' confidence in their team's care, referral patterns to SPC, or satisfaction with the SPC care provided.

Methods

Approval for this study was obtained from the University of California, San Francisco Institutional Review Board (#23‐40639).

Survey Development & Distribution

This is a subgroup analysis of a previously published dataset [1]. Detailed methods of survey development and distribution throughout the U.S. have been published previously [1]. In brief, a multisite, multidisciplinary research team (five neurologists, five SPC physicians, three members of non‐profit ALS advocacy organizations, two survey researchers, one research coordinator, and a care partner representative) developed a survey for multidisciplinary ALS clinicians. The survey was reviewed and refined by an ALS Patient and Family Advisory Council. Multidisciplinary ALS clinicians were defined as physicians, nurses, therapists, chaplains, and social workers, but not SPC clinicians, who work on an ALS team. Survey completion was voluntary. Informed consent was obtained prior to survey completion.

Analysis

We compared survey responses from ALS clinicians who reported having a SPC clinician embedded in their ALS team to those of ALS clinicians who did not have a SPC clinician in their team. Frequencies were calculated for categorical variables. Bivariate associations between categorical variables were assessed using chi‐squared tests (χ2). A two‐sided alpha level of < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. All analyses were performed using SPSS for Mac, version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

Results

Respondent and Practice Location Characteristics

One‐hundred and forty‐one multidisciplinary ALS clinicians from 72 institutions in 30 states completed a survey. Most respondents were therapists (physical, occupational, and speech‐language), neurologists, and nurses, female, and White. They had been providing ALS care for approximately 9 years. Nearly two‐thirds (n = 91) of respondents reported having a specialty‐trained PC clinician on their ALS team (Table S1).

Discussion

We found that having SPC clinicians embedded in multidisciplinary ALS teams was associated with increased ALS clinician confidence in their team's care, more proactive reasons for referral to SPC (e.g., to improve quality of life rather than to address refractory pain), decreased barriers to accessing SPC, and improved satisfaction with the care provided by SPC clinicians. This is consistent with prior research outside of neurology demonstrating that availability of SPC changes referral practices and referral frequency correlates with satisfaction with SPC services [10, 11].

One contributor to these differences in confidence, referral patterns, and satisfaction may have been bidirectional influences between ALS clinicians and SPC clinicians when SPC clinicians are embedded in ALS teams. For example, in the case of dyspnea management, while ALS clinicians reported overall lower satisfaction with SPC clinicians' dyspnea management compared to other aspects of their care, the presence of an embedded SPC clinician on the ALS team was associated with better satisfaction with SPC clinicians' dyspnea management as well as increased ALS clinician confidence in their team's care in this area of practice. ALS and SPC clinicians may share knowledge and skills in a bidirectional manner leading to these improved outcomes, rendering collaborative, transdisciplinary care that is better than the sum of its parts. Improvement in SPC also likely results from SPC clinicians gaining more experience with ALS as a disease state, which is a recognized benefit of specialty‐aligned SPC [12]. Furthermore, the subtle increase in satisfaction ALS clinicians report of SPC management with a breadth of needs, including symptom and non‐symptom needs speaks to the role SPC may have in promoting team‐based care within the ALS team.

The higher proportion of referrals to SPC for ACP, communication, and to improve quality of life suggest a shift to more proactive PC consults in teams with embedded SPC. The presence and availability of SPC clinicians on the ALS team may prompt ALS clinicians to consider how SPC clinicians can benefit patients and families in advance of crises, through screening for needs before they become severe, promoting healthy coping, and promoting ACP, which are demonstrated advantages of proactive PC [13, 14, 15].

Given the barriers to accessing SPC for this population, it is meaningful—if unsurprising—that fewer ALS clinicians with SPC clinicians on their teams reported that SPC was not available or accessible to their patients. However, unfortunately, even with SPC integration into ALS clinics, some ALS clinicians still worried that patients may find involvement of SPC to be upsetting or burdensome. ALS clinicians should be reassured that people with ALS and their caregivers are generally receptive to PC, though some report that it should be better distinguished from hospice care and some felt a change in the name to “supportive care” would be helpful [2]. Despite this, current barriers to routine embedding of SPC in ALS clinics include a known SPC workforce shortage and stigma about SPC; there has been substantial progress on both of these fronts and we expect it to continue [16, 17]. Importantly, as palliative care providers can bill for their care to cover the cost of their salaries, adding a PC provider to a multidisciplinary ALS clinic does not typically add institutional costs for the clinic, unlike adding registered nurses or other clinicians who cannot bill for their services.

Some limitations to this study were acknowledged and discussed in the original publication of this survey [1]. Briefly, concerns include potential bias towards multidisciplinary ALS clinics and those interested in SPC, predominantly White or Asian and female respondents, and focus on clinicians' perceptions rather than on patient outcomes. Additionally, while the survey asked respondents to comment on their confidence in their ALS team's ability to address various issues, it is possible that some of the interdisciplinary respondents did not have a clear sense of their team's ability to address certain needs that were outside their scope of practice. It is also possible that ALS clinicians who have a SPC clinician embedded on their team are biased in favor of palliative care; a subset of respondents may have even advocated for the addition of SPC to the team. Further, the designation of having a SPC clinician “on the ALS care team” does not define precisely how the SPC clinician was involved in the care of people with ALS so there is likely heterogeneity in how teams were structured within each group being compared. Finally, we were not able to compare the impact of having a SPC clinician embedded on the ALS team and having a SPC team that is separate from but frequently collaborates with the ALS team.

Conclusion

Integration of SPC clinicians into ALS teams is associated with increased ALS clinicians' confidence in aspects of their team's care, more proactive SPC referrals, reduced barriers to accessing SPC, and improved ALS clinician satisfaction with the SPC provided. These findings reinforce previous findings about the benefits of SPC for pALS and add information about how this care can be best delivered. Future research is needed to characterize whether and how patient and care partner outcomes are impacted.
