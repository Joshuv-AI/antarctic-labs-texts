1.
Introduction and main results

We investigate the gradient regularity of weak solutions to widely degenerate parabolic equations of the type

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u-\divv\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,t,Du)=f\qquad\text{in\penalty\ $\Omega_{T}$}.

Here,
\Omega
denotes a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\geq 2
). The datum
f
is assumed to be of class
L^{n+2+\sigma}(\Omega)
for some parameter
\sigma>0
. For every
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
, the function
\mathcal{F}\colon\Omega_{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfies certain regularity and structural conditions; however, these conditions only apply to gradient values that lie outside some fixed bounded and convex subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the property that
0\in\inn E
, while
\mathcal{F}
entirely vanishes within this set. We refrain from stating the precise conditions imposed on
\mathcal{F}
for the moment and rather refer the reader to Section
1.2
for a detailed explanation. Outside the closure of the set
E
we assume
\mathcal{F}
to be non-degenerate with respect to the
\xi
variable, with an ellipticity constant that depends on the distance to
E
. The notable feature of equation (
1.1
) is its extensive degeneracy, which places it in the category of
widely degenerate equations
. This situation poses challenges, as one can generally expect less regularity for weak solutions to (
1.1
) in comparison to equations that degenerate at only a single point, such as the well-known
p
-Laplacian operator when
1<p<\infty
and
p\neq 2
. We recall that if
p=2
, the
p
-Laplacian simplifies to the Laplacian, which is not only linear but also elliptic across the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, it cannot be expected that weak solutions will satisfy more than Lipschitz regularity in general. To exemplify this point, we consider a prototype example of widely degenerate equations, which serves as a fundamental reference. This example can be expressed as follows:

(1.2)

\partial_{t}u-\divv\left(a(x,t)\frac{(|Du|-1)^{p-1}_{+}Du}{|Du|}\right)=f\quad\text{in }\Omega_{T},

where
p>1
represents an arbitrary growth exponent, and
x\mapsto a(x,t)\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
for any
t\in(0,T)
are coefficients that satisfy Lipschitz continuity with respect to the spatial variable
x
. Additionally, these coefficients satisfy the condition
0<C^{-1}\leq a(x,t)\leq C
for every
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
and some positive constant
C>0
. For equation (
1.2
) the set of degeneracy
E
is given by the closed unit ball, i.e.
E=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\xi|\leq 1\}\eqqcolon\overline{B}_{1}(0)
. It can be quickly verified that any time-independent
1
-Lipschitz function satisfies the homogeneous variant of equation (
1.2
) weakly. Hence, in general no more than Lipschitz regularity of weak solutions to (
1.1
) is to be expected.

1.1.
Literature overview

In a few scenarios, certain higher regularity has been shown for the composition of a suitable function that vanishes within the set of degeneracy with the gradient
Du
. For example, in
[
2
]
it has been shown that the following higher Sobolev regularity

(1.3)

\frac{(|Du|-1)_{+}}{|Du|}Du\in L^{2}_{\loc}\big(0,T;W^{1,2}_{\loc}(\Omega)\big)

holds true, provided the datum
f
itself also satisfies higher spatial differentiability. In case the datum does not exhibit any weak differentiability and is for example only locally square integrable in
\Omega_{T}
, one can still infer the very same higher regularity as provided in the statement of (
1.3
) for a different nonlinear function vanishing within any set that is slightly larger than the set of degeneracy, cf.
[
22
]
. A similar state of affairs pertains when investigating any continuity regularity of the gradient
Du
. In the stationary setting, it has first been shown by Santambrogio and Vespri
[
29
]
in the scalar case that the composition
\mathcal{K}(Du)
is continuous in
\Omega
for any continuous function
\mathcal{K}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that vanishes inside the closed unit ball, provided that
n=2
. This regularity result has been generalized by Colombo and Figalli in
[
11
]
to the case where
n\geq 2
and the set of degeneracy is given by some bounded convex set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, not necessarily being the closed unit ball, with the property that the origin belongs to the interior of
E
, i.e.
0\in\mbox{Int$(E)$}
. We observe that these assumptions regarding the set of degeneracy
E
by Colombo and Figalli align with those outlined in this manuscript. Indeed, the results of Santambrogio and Vespri and Colombo and Figalli hold true in a very general setting, where they considered locally Lipschitz continuous solutions to equations of the type

(1.4)

\divv\nabla\mathcal{F}(Du)=f\qquad\text{in\penalty\ $\Omega$}

for a convex and non-negative function
\mathcal{F}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{E},\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
satisfying the respective stationary ellipticity estimate (
1.7
). Recently, it has been derived by the author in
[
30
]
that the very same regularity results from
[
11
,
29
]
remain in fact true in case the function
\mathcal{F}
additionally exhibits a dependence on the spatial variable
x
, provided
\nabla\mathcal{F}=\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,\xi)
is locally Lipschitz continuous with respect to
x
for any value
\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\xi|_{E}>1\}
. In the vectorial case, this regularity result has also been obtained for the model equation in
[
3
]
by Bögelein, Duzaar, Giova, and Passarelli di Napoli. Mons
[
27
]
established the same regularity result for vector valued solutions within a broader class of highly degenerate equations, where the vector field included in the diffusion term of the system is assumed to satisfy standard
p
-growth conditions in the region where
|\xi|>1
. Furthermore, Grimaldi
[
24
]
considered a more general structural framework, where the usual Euclidean norm appearing in the prototype equation (
1.2
) is replaced by a norm induced by a bounded, symmetric, and coercive bilinear form defined on the space
\mathbb{R}^{Nn}
, and recovered the regularity result.

In the parabolic setting, it has recently been shown by the very same authors in
[
4
]
that the above regularity result continues to hold true for the model equation, even if the vectorial case is considered. To our knowledge, a respective result has not been derived in the general setting of
[
11
,
29
,
30
]
, which is our aim in this very article.

1.2.
Structure conditions

We now state the precise regularity assumptions made on the function
\mathcal{F}
. As before, let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote a fixed bounded and convex set with
0\in\inn E
. By
|\cdot|_{E}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we denote the
Minkowski functional
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is defined as

(1.5)

\displaystyle|\xi|_{E}\coloneqq\inf\{t>0:\xi\in tE\},\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
tE\coloneqq\{tx:x\in E\}
. This expedient mapping has already been considered in comparable works
[
11
,
30
]
and will also turn out to be crucial in the course of this article. We refer the reader for the moment to Section
2.2
, where a more detailed explanation on convex sets and the Minkowski functional
|\cdot|_{E}
is provided. We assume

\mathcal{F}\colon\Omega_{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

to be a non-negative function that is subject to the following conditions

(1.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)=0&\mbox{for any\penalty\ $(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}$,\penalty\ $\xi\in E$},\\
\xi\mapsto\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)\penalty\ \mbox{is convex}&\mbox{for any\penalty\ $(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}$},\\
\xi\mapsto\mathcal{F}(x,\xi)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{E})&\mbox{for any\penalty\ $x\in\Omega$},\\
|\partial_{x_{i}}\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)|\leq C(L)&\mbox{for any\penalty\ $(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}$,\penalty\ $|\xi|_{E}\leq L$}\end{cases}

for any
i=1,\ldots,n
and any
L\geq 1
, where
C=C(L)
denotes a positive Lipschitz constant that depends on
L\geq 1
. According to conditions (
1.6
), we assume that
\mathcal{F}
admits convexity with respect to the gradient variable
\xi
and entirely vanishes whenever
\xi\in E
. Moreover, the partial mapping
\xi\mapsto\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)
is assumed to be continuous outside the closure of the set
E
for any
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
. In particular, the
C^{2}
-regularity may break down at the boundary
\partial E
. On the other hand,
\nabla\mathcal{F}
is assumed to be locally Lipschitz continuous with respect to
x
for any
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
. With the notation
\nabla
and
\nabla^{2}
, we consistently refer to derivatives with respect to the gradient variable
\xi
, whereas
\partial_{x}
respectively
\partial_{t}
represent the derivative with respect to the spatial variable
x
and the time variable
t
respectively.

Finally, the following ellipticity condition for
\mathcal{F}
is assumed, which states that
\mathcal{F}
is elliptic for any value of
\xi
that lies outside the degeneracy set
E
, i.e. for any
\delta>0
there exist constants
0<\lambda(\delta)\leq\Lambda(\delta)
, such that there holds

(1.7)

\qquad\lambda(\delta)|\eta|^{2}\leq\langle\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)\eta,\eta\rangle\leq\Lambda(\delta)|\eta|^{2}\qquad\text{for any\penalty\ $1+\delta\leq|\xi|_{E}\leq\delta^{-1}$}

for all
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
. The ellipticity constant
\lambda(\delta)
vanishes in the limit
\delta\downarrow 0
, leading to a lack of ellipticity in general. Moreover, the upper bound for the Hessian is only local, with
\Lambda(\delta)
possibly becoming unbounded when
\delta\downarrow 0
.

Let us now provide an example of a function
\mathcal{F}
that actually satisfies the structural conditions (
1.6
) and (
1.7
). In a similar fashion to
[
30
]
, consider the model example

\mathcal{F}_{p}(x,t,\xi)=\frac{a(x,t)}{p}(|\xi|-1)^{p}_{+},

where
x\mapsto a(x,t)\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{>0})
denote Lipschitz coefficients with
|\partial_{x}a(x,t)|\leq A
for all
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
that satisfy
0<C_{1}\leq a(x,t)\leq C_{2}<\infty
for any
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
. We note that in this particular setting, the set of degeneracy of
\mathcal{F}_{p}
is given by the closed unit ball. Then, it is easy to check that the Minkowski functional
|\cdot|_{E}
reduces to the standard Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and we have
|\xi|_{E}=|\xi|
for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By following the very same approach taken in
[
30
]
, we infer that both structure conditions (
1.6
) as well as (
1.7
) are satisfied. In particular, we note that the eigenvalues of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{p}
become unbounded for large
|\xi|
in the case where
p>2
, while in the sub-quadratic case
1<p<2
the largest eigenvalue of
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{p}
blows up when approaching the closed unit ball from outside. Only in the quadratic case where
p=2
, the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{p}
are bounded universally by
\Lambda
. For example, when
p\geq 2
, there holds the quantitative estimate

(1.8)

C_{1}\frac{(|\xi|-1)^{p-1}_{+}}{|\xi|}|\eta|^{2}\leq\langle\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{p}(x,t,\xi)\eta,\eta\rangle\leq C_{2}(p-1)(|\xi|-1)^{p-2}_{+}|\eta|^{2}

for a.e.
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
and
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For an arbitrary parameter
0<\delta<1
, consider
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
1+\delta\leq|\xi|\leq\delta^{-1}
. Consequently, the preceding quantitative estimate (
1.8
) verifies that the ellipticity and boundedness condition (
1.7
) is satisfied with constants
\lambda(C_{1},\delta,p)=\lambda\delta^{p}
and
\Lambda(C_{2},\delta,p)=\Lambda(p-1)\big(\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\big)^{p-2}
in the case where
p\geq 2
.
The ellipticity constant
\lambda
vanishes in the limit
\delta\downarrow 0
, leading to a lack of ellipticity in the closed unit ball
\overline{B_{1}}
, while the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian becomes unbounded in the case where
p>2
when
|\xi|
increases. In the case
p=2
, the constant
\Lambda
only depends on
C_{2}
but is independent of
\delta
, while
\Lambda
depends on
\delta
and
C_{2}
when
p>2
. By following a similar reasoning in the sub-quadratic setting
1<p<2
, a different state of affairs to the super-quadratic case
p>2
applies, where the smallest eigenvalue of
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{p}(x,t,\xi)
vanishes for large
|\xi|
and the largest eigenvalue becomes unbounded whenever
\xi
approaches the unit sphere
\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\xi|=1\}
from outside.

1.3.
Definition of weak solution

At this point, we present our notion of a weak solution to equation (
1.1
).

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
satisfy the structure conditions (
1.6
) and (
1.7
). A measurable function

u\in C^{0}\big(0,T;L^{2}_{\loc}(\Omega)\big)\cap L^{\infty}_{\loc}\big(0,T;W^{1,\infty}_{\loc}(\Omega)\big)

is a local weak solution of equation (
1.1
), if the integral identity

(1.9)

\iint_{\Omega_{T}}\big(-u\partial_{t}\phi+\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,t,Du),D\phi\rangle\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\iint_{\Omega_{T}}f\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

holds true for any test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega_{T})
.

Remark 1.2
.

Without any additional conditions imposed on
\mathcal{F}
, for instance
p
-growth, we are forced to consider weak solutions that are
a priori
locally Lipschitz continuous in
\Omega_{T}
. Indeed, for the prototype example (
1.2
) and more generally, also for functions satisfying standard
p
-growth assumptions, with a right-hand side
f
belonging to a suitable Orlicz space, it has been established in
[
1
]
that weak solutions exhibit a locally bounded spatial gradient in
\Omega_{T}
in the super-critical case
p>\frac{2n}{n+2}
. Their result continues to hold true in the sub-critical range
1<p\leq\frac{2n}{n+2}
if weak solutions are assumed to be locally bounded resp. integrable to a certain exponent in
\Omega_{T}
, cf.
[
14
]
. Due to the general form of
\mathcal{F}
and the structure conditions (
1.7
), without additional information on the growth rate of
\xi\mapsto\mathcal{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)
, such as
p
-growth, we cannot derive quantitative energy estimates for weak solutions to (
1.1
). Already to ensure convergence of the integral involving the solution
u
in (
1.9
), further knowledge about the qualitative behavior of
\mathcal{F}
would be necessary. Consequently, we focus on weak solutions that are
a priori
of class

C^{0}\big(0,T;L^{2}_{\loc}(\Omega)\big)\cap L^{\infty}_{\loc}\big(0,T;W^{1,\infty}_{\loc}(\Omega)\big),

which aligns with the treatment of the elliptic setting
[
10
,
11
,
29
,
30
]
.

1.4.
Main result

In this section, we present the main result of our paper, along with a subsequent corollary.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
f\in L^{n+2+\sigma}(\Omega_{T})
for some
\sigma>0
, and

u\in C^{0}\big(0,T;L^{2}_{\loc}(\Omega)\big)\cap L^{\infty}_{\loc}\big(0,T;W^{1,\infty}_{\loc}(\Omega)\big)

be a local weak solution to (
1.1
) under structure conditions (
1.6
) and (
1.7
), where
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes a bounded and convex set with
0\in\inn E
on which
\mathcal{F}
degenerates. Then, there holds

\mathcal{K}(Du)\in C^{0}(\Omega_{T})

for any
\mathcal{K}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\mathcal{K}\equiv 0
on
E
.

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.3
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
hold true. If there additionally holds

\nabla\mathcal{F}\in C^{0}(\Omega_{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}),

then we have

\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,t,Du)\in C^{0}(\Omega_{T},\mathbb{R}^{n}).

1.5.
Strategy of the proof

In this section, we delineate the fundamental steps leading to our main regularity result, that is Theorem
1.3
. We start by introducing a cutoff for the functional
\mathcal{F}
when the values of
|\xi|
exceed a certain threshold
L<\infty
. We then redefine
\mathcal{F}
to
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
, ensuring that the mapping
\xi\mapsto\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)
remains constant for points where
|\xi|\geq L
, a property that holds true for any
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
. The threshold
L
in fact corresponds to the essential supremum of
|Du|
within a compactly contained cylinder
Q_{R}=Q_{R}(y_{0},s_{0})\Subset\Omega_{T}
, where

u\in L^{\infty}_{\loc}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}_{\loc}(\Omega))

is taken to denote an arbitrary local weak solution to equation (
1.1
), as defined in Definition
1.1
. Given our definition of weak solution, indeed it follows that
L=\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R})}<\infty
. With this construction in place, any local weak solution
u
to the equation (
1.1
) can be regarded as a local weak solution to the redefined equation

\partial_{t}u-\divv\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,Du)=f\qquad\text{in }Q_{R}.

In general however, the truncated function
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
fails to be convex, which we require for the structure conditions (
1.7
) to hold true. This problem is overcome by adding a suitable convex function
\Phi
to
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
, such that the sum
\hat{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\tilde{\mathcal{F}}+\Phi
is once again convex. Furthermore, we enhance the diffusion term of the original equation (
1.1
) by adding the scaled Laplacian term
\varepsilon\Delta u
, with
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
serving as a small approximation parameter. This modification results in a more regularized version of (
1.1
), where we denote the corresponding functional as
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}
, which is elliptic across all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to
\xi
and avoids degeneracy within the closed unit ball. It is important to note that the ellipticity constant generally depends on the parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Moreover, the obtained mapping
\xi\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi)
exhibits quadratic growth across
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for any
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
. In the next step, we examine the unique weak solution

u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0}),u+W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)

to the weak formulation of the approximating equation on the cylinder
Q_{R}=Q_{R}(y_{0},s_{0})\Subset\Omega_{T}
, where we impose a Cauchy-Dirichlet condition on
\partial_{p}Q_{R}
, that involves the weak solution
u
of equation (
1.1
). The uniform ellipticity of the regularized equation enables us to establish the existence of second-order weak spatial derivatives, leading to the regularity

u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});W^{1,\infty}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)\cap L^{2}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});W^{2,2}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)

for the approximating solutions. Additionally, owing to the quadratic growth of
\xi\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi)
for any
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
, we derive a quantitative local
L^{\infty}
-gradient bound for
Du_{\varepsilon}
, with the
L^{\infty}
-gradient norm essentially controlled from above by the
L^{2}
-gradient norm, up to constants and additive terms. This expedient matter of fact allows us to establish a local uniform bound for
\|Du_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}
in the cylinder
Q_{R}
with respect to the approximating parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

The primary challenge that follows is to prove Theorem
3.9
, which asserts the local Hölder continuity in
Q_{R}
of the function

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})\coloneqq\frac{(|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}-(1+\delta))_{+}}{|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}}Du_{\varepsilon}

for any
\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. The Hölder exponent
\alpha_{\delta}\in(0,1)
and the positive Hölder constant
C_{\delta}
both depend on the data and the parameter
\delta\in(0,1]
, yet remain independent of
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. The mapping
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}
vanishes within the slightly larger set
\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\xi|_{E}\leq 1+\delta\}
, while maintaining positivity outside the set
\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\xi|_{E}>1+\delta\}
. As noted previously in
[
3
]
, it is beneficial to examine the function
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})
because in the region where
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}
is positive, the equation described by (
1.1
) exhibits uniform ellipticity, with the ellipticity constant only depending on the parameter
\delta\in(0,1]
but not on
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. The proof of Theorem
3.9
requires a meticulous approach to both the non-degenerate and degenerate regime. In the non-degenerate regime, the set of points where
\partial_{e^{*}}u_{\varepsilon}
is close to its supremum is large in measure for at least one element
e^{*}\in\partial E^{*}
, while conversely, the degenerate regime encompasses a subset of points where
\partial_{e^{*}}u_{\varepsilon}
is rather far away from its supremum for any
e^{*}\in\partial E^{*}
. The set
E^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the unit ball in the dual norm induced by the convex set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The reader is provided with further information in Section
2.2
. In the non-degenerate regime, our objective is to deduce a lower bound for
|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}
within the smaller cylinder
Q_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(z_{0})\Subset Q_{2\rho}(z_{0})\Subset Q_{R}\Subset\Omega_{T}
. This result is accomplished by leveraging the measure-theoretic information that characterizes the non-degenerate regime, which is assumed to hold on
Q_{\rho}(z_{0})
. By differentiating the regularized equation, we deduce that for each
e^{*}\in\partial E^{*}
, the function
\partial_{e^{*}}u_{\varepsilon}
serves as a weak solution to a linear parabolic equation that exhibits elliptic and bounded coefficients. Importantly, the ellipticity constant for this equation is independent of
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and relies solely on the parameter
\delta\in(0,1]
as well as the provided data. This matter of fact enables us to apply established excess-decay estimates for parabolic De Giorgi classes, as referenced in
[
19
]
. In the degenerate regime, we exploit the fact that

v_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(\partial_{e^{*}}u_{\varepsilon}-(1+\delta))_{+}^{2},

serves as a sub-solution to a linear parabolic equation that incorporates elliptic and bounded coefficients, which again allows us to derive a parabolic De Giorgi class-type estimate. This strategy yields a quantitative reduction of the supremum of
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})
over a smaller cylinder, as delineated in Proposition
3.11
. By intertwining the results of both Propositions
3.10
and
3.11
through a sequence of shrinking nested cylinders, we adeptly navigate between the non-degenerate and degenerate regimes, adjusting our approach based on the measure-theoretic information in each step. The transition between these regimes is crucial, shaping the subsequent analysis. If the non-degenerate regime is applicable at a certain step, this condition persists. However, moving to a smaller cylinder within the degenerate regime yields uncertainty regarding whether the subsequent step will fall under the non-degenerate or degenerate regime. Once Hölder continuity of
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})
has been established for any
\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, we can invoke Arzelà-Ascoli’s theorem and first pass to the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
to obtain that
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du)
is also locally Hölder continuous in
Q_{R}
, with the Hölder exponent and constant dependent on
\delta
and the given data. Moreover, by subsequently also passing to the limit
\delta\downarrow 0
, we conclude that the limit function

\mathcal{G}(Du)=\frac{(|Du|_{E}-1)_{+}}{|Du|_{E}}Du

is locally uniformly continuous in
Q_{R}
. At this point, however, the quantitative control over the Hölder exponent and constant is lost, leaving uncertainty about the optimal modulus of continuity of the limit function
\mathcal{G}(Du)
. Ultimately, the main regularity Theorem
1.3
can be derived from these findings. For further insight, we encourage the reader to consult the reasoning employed in
[
4
,
30
]
for the proofs of both Theorem
3.9
and Theorem
1.3
.

1.6.
Novelties and Significance

In comparison to known
C^{1}
-regularity results for widely degenerate parabolic equations, where the article by Bögelein, Duzaar, Giova, and Passarelli di Napoli
[
4
]
is the only available result to our knowledge, our main result, Theorem
1.3
, is new even in the case where the diffusion term in the considered equation (
1.1
) does not depend on the space-time variable
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
. Nevertheless, the flexibility of our proof strategy, that is drawn from the pioneering works of De Giorgi, DiBenedetto, Friedman, and Uhlenbeck, also allows us to treat the more general case where the diffusion term does depend on
(x,t)
under the structure assumptions (
1.6
) - (
1.7
). Moreover, in contrast to the parabolic result of
[
4
]
, where the degeneracy set is given as the unit ball, while for gradient values
\{|Du|>1\}
their equation satisfies standard
p
-growth conditions, we establish Theorem
1.3
for equations that not only degenerate on a general bounded convex subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with the property that
0\in\inn E
, but also may exhibit arbitrary growth for gradient values
\{|Du|_{E}>1\}
. This generality was previously achieved in the elliptic setting by Colombo and Figalli
[
11
]
, and subsequently by the author in
[
30
]
with equations allowed to depend on the spatial variable
x\in\Omega
.

The core difficulty of the present article lies in proving Theorem
3.9
, which establishes the local Hölder continuity of the approximating mappings
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})
within
Q_{R}(y_{0},s_{0})\Subset\Omega_{T}
for any
\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Our main contribution, however, resides in the non-degenerate regime treated in Section
5
, where we begin by establishing a lower bound for
|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}
in Proposition
5.1
. This is the main ingredient for the proof of Proposition
3.10
. The proof of Proposition
5.1
mirrors the strategy developed by Kuusi and Mingione in
[
25
]
, built upon a De Giorgi–type iteration argument. In the setting of widely degenerate equations, a careful choice of test function is crucial in order to obtain an appropriate Caccioppoli-type estimate: although the approximating equation is non-degenerate due to the presence of the scaled Laplacian term
\varepsilon\Delta u_{\varepsilon}
, one must avoid introducing dependence on the parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
in the quantitative constants and in the data. Therefore, our test function in Proposition
5.1
is designed not only to suppress small gradient values, but also to exclude excessively large gradient values. This leads to an
a priori
insufficient measure-theoretic information expressed in (
5.11
), which does not guarantee a lower bound for
|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}
in general. It is precisely at this juncture that an additional argument becomes necessary, one that leverages the expedient regularity property

(\partial_{e^{*}}u_{\varepsilon}-(1+\delta))^{2}_{+}\in C^{0}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});L^{2}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)

for any
e^{*}\in\partial E^{*}
,
\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, which is inferred in Lemma
4.4
a similar manner to that used by DiBenedetto
[
14
]
.

1.7.
Plan of the paper

Our paper is structured in the following way: in Section
2
, we will begin by introducing the notation and framework, along with supplementary material required for later sections, as well as our definition of a weak solution to equation (
1.1
). Following this, in Section

1.3

3.9

3.10
as well as Proposition
3.11

and
3.11
will be addressed in the subsequent Sections
5
and
6
, concluding the article.

2.1.
Notation and setting

Throughout this paper,

\Omega_{T}

\partial_{p}\Omega_{T}\coloneqq\big(\overline{\Omega}\times\{0\}\big)\cup\big(\partial\Omega\times(0,T)\big),

whenever it turns out convenient. The standard scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted by
\displaystyle\langle\cdot\rangle
and the dyadic product of two vectors

C
or
C(\cdot)
and their dependence is described solely on their variables, not their specific values. However, it is possible for constants to vary from one line to another without further clarification.

We define the open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with radius
\rho>0
and center
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

B_{\rho}(x_{0})\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\penalty\ |x-x_{0}|<\rho\}.

By a point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we always refer to
z_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})
where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

x_{0}=0
, it will be common to simplify notation by writing
B_{\rho}
instead of
B_{\rho}(x_{0})
.
Additionally, we may omit the vertex of the cylinder for convenience. The
general (backward) parabolic cylinder
with vertex
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is defined as

Q_{R,S}(z_{0})\coloneqq B_{R}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-S,t_{0}],

with the standard (backward) parabolic cylinder with vertex
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
given by

Q_{\rho}(z_{0})=B_{R}(x_{0})\times\Lambda_{\rho}(t_{0})\coloneqq B_{\rho}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-\rho^{2},t_{0}].

Next, we will introduce the
parabolic distance
between
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
, where
z_{1}=(x_{1},t_{1})
,
z_{2}=(x_{2},t_{2})\in\Omega_{T}
, that is given by

d_{p}(z_{1},z_{2})\coloneqq|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\sqrt{|t_{1}-t_{2}|}.

For any
f\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T},\mathbb{R}^{k})
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we use the common abbreviation for the average integral of
f
. Let
A\subset\Omega
such that
\mathcal{L}^{n}(A)>0
. Then, we define the slice-wise mean
(f)_{A}\colon(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
as follows

\displaystyle(f)_{A}(t)\coloneqq\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.24571pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.65785pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.07782pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-4.91403pt}}\!\int_{A}f(\cdot,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\mbox{for a.e.\penalty\ $t\in(0,T)$}.

Since many of the functions in this paper are continuous with respect to the time variable, that is,
f\in C^{0}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
, the slice-wise mean value is well-defined for all
t\in(0,T)
. In a similar fashion, we denote the mean value of
f
on some measurable set
E\subset\Omega_{T}
with
\mathcal{L}^{n+1}(E)>0
by

\displaystyle(f)_{E}\coloneqq\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-6.23328pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-5.58163pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-4.09418pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-3.32918pt}}\!\iint_{E}f(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

If it is evident from the context, the vertex
z_{0}
may sometimes be omitted to simplify notation.

The operators
\nabla\mathcal{F}
and
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}
denote the first and second order derivatives of the function
\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)
with respect to
\xi
in the region
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{E}
. Meanwhile,
\partial_{x}\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)
signifies the derivative of
\nabla\mathcal{F}
with respect to the spatial variable
x
. Occasionally, we will refer to
\nabla\mathcal{F}
simply as
\mathcal{H}
, and both notations will be used interchangeably throughout the discussion. The terms
spatial gradient
,
gradient
, and
spatial derivative
will all be used interchangeably without any distinctions. To avoid confusion and to clearly differentiate between derivatives pertaining to weak solutions
u
of equation (
1.1
) and derivatives of the function
\mathcal{F}
incorporated in the diffusion term, we will adopt the following conventions: weak partial derivatives of
u
will be denoted by
D_{i}u
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, directional derivatives
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
\partial_{v}u\coloneqq\langle Du,v\rangle
whenever this quantity exists, and its weak gradient will be denoted by
Du
. In contrast, we will use
\nabla\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{H}
for the first order derivative of
\mathcal{F}
and
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}
for its Hessian. To distinguish between spatial and time derivatives, we will indicate the latter as
\partial_{t}u
, provided that
u\colon\Omega_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
possesses a (weak) derivative with respect to the time variable
t
. It is important to note that no distinction will be made between classical and weak derivatives, with context providing clarity regarding the intended meaning. For any other differentiable mapping
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we may use either
Df
or
\nabla f
to represent its derivative, depending on the context.

[

4
,
30
]
. For
\delta\geq 0
, we define

(2.1)

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(\xi)\coloneqq\frac{(|\xi|_{E}-(1+\delta))_{+}}{|\xi|_{E}}\xi\qquad\text{for\penalty\ $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

In the case where
\delta=0
, we shall simply write

(2.2)

\mathcal{G}(\xi)\coloneqq\mathcal{G}_{0}(\xi)=\frac{(|\xi|_{E}-1)_{+}}{|\xi|_{E}}\xi\qquad\text{for\penalty\ $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

The following bilinear form on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
naturally arise by differentiating the weak form (
1.9
) with respect to the spatial variable
x
. For arbitrary
\eta,\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let

(2.3)

\mathcal{B}(\cdot,\xi)(\eta,\zeta)\coloneqq\langle D^{2}\mathcal{F}(\cdot,\xi)\eta,\zeta\rangle\qquad\text{for\penalty\ $(x,\xi)\in\Omega_{T}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{E})$}.

2.2.
Convex sets and the Minkowski functional

In this article, we consider a bounded and convex set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in\inn{E}
, which serves as the set of degeneracy for the equation presented in (
1.1
). The convexity of
E
is crucial as it induces a map on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which will be particularly important for deriving energy estimates in Sections
3
–
6
. Furthermore, the inclusion of
0\in\inn E
is essential for the proof of Lemma
2.5
, as it ensures the Lipschitz continuity of the Minkowski functional
|\cdot|_{E}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined by

\displaystyle|\xi|_{E}\coloneqq\inf\{t>0:\xi\in tE\},\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
tE\coloneqq\{tx:x\in E\}
. For further insights into the properties of the Minkowski functional, we direct the reader to
[
28
]
, particularly pages 109 and 119. Since the set
E
consists of points satisfying
|\xi|_{E}\leq 1
, it corresponds to the unit ball with respect to the Minkowski functional. We also introduce its dual map, defined as follows:

(2.4)

\displaystyle|\xi|_{E^{\prime}}\coloneqq\sup\limits_{|e|_{E}\leq 1}\langle\xi,e\rangle=\sup\limits_{e\in E}\langle\xi,e\rangle

along with the unit ball corresponding to this dual map:

(2.5)

\displaystyle E^{*}\coloneqq\overline{B}_{1}^{|\cdot|_{E^{\prime}}}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\xi|_{E^{\prime}}\leq 1\}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle\xi,e\rangle\leq 1\penalty\ \forall e\in E\}.

As established in
[
11
, Section 3]
, we obtain the following alternative representation formula for the Minkowski functional:

(2.6)

\displaystyle|\xi|_{E}=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\vskip-1.42271pt\\
e^{*}\in E^{*}\end{subarray}}{\sup}\langle\xi,e^{*}\rangle=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\vskip-1.42271pt\\
e*\in\partial E^{*}\end{subarray}}{\sup}\langle\xi,e^{*}\rangle

for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A key implication of this representation is that, in several energy estimates, we will weakly differentiate a given equation in the direction
e^{*}
, where
e^{*}\in\partial E^{*}
. This allows us to derive information about the directional derivatives
\partial_{e^{*}}u=\langle Du,e^{*}\rangle
. Since
e^{*}\in E^{*}
can be chosen arbitrarily, we may pass to the supremum over all
e^{*}\in E^{*}
to obtain estimates for the Minkowski functional
|Du|_{E}
of the (weak) gradient of
u
. We denote the (weak) directional derivative in any direction
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
\partial_{v}u
, where we use the representation formula

(2.7)

\displaystyle v=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}e_{i},

with
(e_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n}
being the standard base of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v_{i}
denoting the respective coefficients of
v
. Thus, for a.e.
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\partial_{v}u=\langle Du,v\rangle
.
It is also evident from the definition that
|\cdot|_{E}
is homogeneous for any
\lambda>0
, satisfying
|\lambda\xi|_{E}=\lambda|\xi|_{E}
for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Additionally, we have
|0|_{E}=0
, and since
0\in\inn{E}
, it follows that
|\xi|_{E}<\infty
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given that
0\in\inn{E}
and
E
is bounded, there exist radii
0<r_{E}\leq R_{E}<\infty
, such that

(2.8)

\displaystyle B_{r_{E}}(0)\subset E\subset B_{R_{E}}(0),

where
r_{E}>0
is chosen as the largest radius and
R_{E}>0
denotes an arbitrary but fixed radius with this property. Consequently, from the definition of (
1.5
), we can deduce that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\frac{|\xi|}{R_{E}}\leq|\xi|_{E}\leq\frac{|\xi|}{r_{E}}

for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Finally, for any
\delta>0
, we define the outer parallel set of
E
at a distance
\delta>0
as:

(2.10)

\displaystyle E_{\delta}\coloneqq\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\xi|_{E}\leq 1+\delta\}.

2.3.
Mollification in time

According to our definition, weak solutions may not necessarily exhibit weak differentiability with respect to the time variable. In order to overcome this lack of regularity, we introduce the standard regularization technique involving Steklov-means, where we refer to
[
8
]
for some properties and additional information. Given a function
f\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
and
0<h<T
, we define its
Steklov-average

[f]_{h}
by

(2.11)

[f]_{h}(x,t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}f(x,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}&\mbox{for\penalty\ $t\in(0,T-h)$},\\
0&\mbox{for\penalty\ $t\in[T-h,T)$}.\end{cases}

Rewriting the identity (
1.9
) in terms of Steklov-means
[u]_{h}
of
u
and integrating by parts, yields

(2.12)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\{t\}}\big(\partial_{t}[u]_{h}\phi+\langle[\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,t,Du)]_{h},D\phi\rangle\big)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega\times\{t\}}[f]_{h}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x

for any test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
and any
t\in(0,T)
.

3.1.
Regularizing the equation

As performed in a similar fashion for the elliptic setting in
[

, Proof of Theorem 1.1]
and
[

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)
satisfies quadratic growth with respect to the gradient variable

\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with an ellipticity constant depending on the parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
in general. We consider a cylinder
Q_{R}=Q_{R}(y_{0},s_{0})\Subset\Omega_{T}
and let

u\in L^{\infty}_{\loc}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}_{\loc}(\Omega))

denote an arbitrary weak solution to (
1.1
). In what follows, we will always omit the vertex
(y_{0},s_{0})
of the cylinder
Q_{R}=Q_{R}(y_{0},s_{0})
in any given quantity. For the further course of this section, we denote

(3.1)

K\coloneqq\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R})}<\infty.

Moreover, we set

(3.2)

\tilde{K}\coloneqq\|\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times(B_{K+2R_{E}}))}<\infty,\qquad L\coloneqq\tilde{K}+1<\infty,

where
R_{E}>0
is given in (
2.8
). The additional constant quantity
1
is chosen arbitrarily and could be replaced with any positive real number. Next, let
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\mathbb{R}_{>0})
denote a smooth cut-off function that satisfies

\Psi(s)=s\qquad\mbox{for\penalty\ $s\in[0,\tilde{K}]$},\qquad\Psi(s)=L\qquad\mbox{for\penalty\ $s\in[L,\infty)$}

as well as

|\psi^{\prime}(s)|+|\psi^{\prime\prime}(s)|\leq C_{\Psi}\qquad\mbox{for all\penalty\ $s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$},

where
C_{\Psi}>0
. For any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we consider the redefined function

(3.3)

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)\coloneqq\Psi(\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)).

Due to this construction, there holds

(3.4)

\mathcal{F}(x,t,Du(x,t))=\Psi(\mathcal{F}(x,t,Du(x,t)))=\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,Du(x,t))

for any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and, moreover, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)\Psi^{\prime}(\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)

\displaystyle=(\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)\otimes\nabla\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi))\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi))+\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)\Psi^{\prime}(\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{E}

for any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
. Since
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)
is positive definite for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{E}
and any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
, the mapping
\xi\mapsto\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{E}
is strictly convex for any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
. Therefore,
\xi\mapsto\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)
is coercive on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
, such that for any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
the truncated function
\xi\mapsto\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)
remains constant on the set
Q_{R}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{N(x,t)})
for some

N(x,t)\geq K+2R_{E}.

We define

(3.6)

\displaystyle N\coloneqq\sup\limits_{(x,t)\in Q_{R}}N(x,t)\geq K+2R_{E}

and observe that
N<\infty
due to assumption (
1.7
) that holds for any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
. Thus, according to the preceding reasoning,
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)
is constant for any
(x,t,\xi)\in Q_{R}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{N})
. In particular, for the truncated function
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
there hold the following estimates

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{N})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Psi}\|\nabla\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times B_{N})},

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times(\mathbb{R}_{N}\setminus E_{\delta}))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Psi}\|\nabla\mathcal{F}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times B_{N})}+C_{\Psi}\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times(B_{N}\setminus E_{\delta}))}

for any
\delta>0
. Here,
E_{\delta}
denotes the outer parallel set of
E
as defined in (
2.10
). Therefore, the preceding
L^{\infty}
-estimates for both
\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nabla^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
only depend on the constant
C_{\Psi}
and the
L^{\infty}
-bounds for
\nabla\mathcal{F}
and
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}
respectively. Next, we set

(3.8)

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\max\{\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times(\mathbb{R}_{N}\setminus B_{K+R_{E}}))},1\}<\infty

and consider a convex function
\Phi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that satisfies

(3.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Phi(\xi)=0&\mbox{for any\penalty\ $|\xi|\leq K+R_{E}$},\\
|\nabla\Phi(\xi)|\leq(2C_{\mathcal{F}}+1)|\xi|&\mbox{for any\penalty\ $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$},\\
\langle\nabla^{2}\Phi(\xi)\eta,\eta\rangle\geq(C_{\mathcal{F}}+1)|\eta|^{2}&\mbox{for any\penalty\ $|\xi|\geq K+2R_{E}$},\\
|\nabla^{2}\Phi(\xi)|\leq 2C_{\mathcal{F}}+1&\mbox{for any\penalty\ $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}\end{cases}

for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, we set

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)\coloneqq\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)+\Phi(\xi)\qquad\mbox{for\penalty\ $(x,t)\in Q_{R},\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

and note that
\mathbb{R}^{n}\ni\xi\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)
is convex across
\mathbb{R}^{n}
due to the preceding construction of
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
and
\Phi
. In fact, on the set
Q_{R}\times B_{K+2R_{E}}
we have
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)=\mathcal{F}(x,t,\xi)
, such that, since
\Phi
is convex,
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
is convex across
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. On the complementary set

Q_{R}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{K+2R_{E}}),

by exploiting (
3.8
) as well as (
3.9
), there holds the ellipticity estimate

(3.10)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla^{2}\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)\eta,\eta\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)\eta,\eta\rangle+\langle\nabla^{2}\Phi(\xi)\eta,\eta\rangle\geq|\xi|^{2}

for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\hat{\mathcal{F}}
, we have the estimate

(3.11)

\displaystyle|\nabla^{2}\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)|\leq 3C_{\mathcal{F}}+1\qquad\mbox{for\penalty\ $(x,t)\in Q_{R},\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{K+R_{E}}$}.

As in
[
30
, Section 3.1]
, we further infer that the redefined mapping
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies the set of structure conditions (
1.6
), where condition
\eqref{fregularity}_{4}
holds true with a quantitative bound

(3.12)

\displaystyle|\partial_{x_{i}}\nabla\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C(C_{\Psi},N,R_{E})+C_{\Psi}\underbrace{\|\partial_{x}\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times B_{N})}}_{\leq C(C_{\Psi},N,r_{E})}\underbrace{\|\nabla\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times B_{N})}}_{\eqqcolon\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},C_{\Psi},N,r_{E})

for any
i=1,\ldots,n
, any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
, and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In turn, we used the mean value theorem to estimate the quantity involving
\partial_{x}\mathcal{F}
further above. In particular, the structure constant
C=C(\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},C_{\Psi},N,r_{E})
does not depend on the size of
|\xi|_{E}
. Additionally, the ellipticity condition (
1.7
) for
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
holds true. For any
\delta>0
we find positive constants
\hat{\lambda}=\hat{\lambda}(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta)
and
\hat{\Lambda}=\hat{\Lambda}(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta)
with
0<\hat{\lambda}\leq\hat{\Lambda}
, such that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\qquad\hat{\lambda}(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta)|\eta|^{2}\leq\langle\nabla^{2}\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)\eta,\eta\rangle\leq\hat{\Lambda}(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta)|\eta|^{2}\qquad\text{for any\penalty\ $1+\delta\leq|\xi|_{E}\leq\delta^{-1}$}

for any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
. Since
u
is a weak solution to equation (
1.1
),
u
is in particular a weak solution to

\partial_{t}u-\divv\nabla\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,Du)=f\qquad\mbox{in\penalty\ $Q_{R}$}.

This is a direct consequence of (
3.2
) as we have that
\hat{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}
in
Q_{R}\times B_{K}
. However,
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
does not admit uniform ellipticity on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, necessitating an additional approximation in order to obtain a truly elliptic equation on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For an approximating parameter
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, we set

(3.14)

\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi)\coloneqq\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)+\varepsilon\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}|\xi|^{2}\qquad\mbox{for\penalty\ $(x,t)\in Q_{R},\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

and note that
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}
is indeed elliptic with respect to
\xi
on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the case where
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, with the ellipticity constant depending on the parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
in general. Here, the quantity
\frac{1}{2}|\xi|^{2}
denotes the convex function of the well-known
2
-energy. Consequently, we set

(3.15)

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi)\coloneqq\nabla\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi)=\big(\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,\xi)+\varepsilon\xi\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\qquad\mbox{for any\penalty\ $(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}$,\penalty\ $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

as well as

\nabla^{2}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi)=\big(\nabla^{2}\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)+\varepsilon I_{n}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\qquad\mbox{for any\penalty\ $(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}$,\penalty\ $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{E}$}.

In the spirit of (
2.3
), we set

(3.16)

\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi)(\eta,\zeta)\coloneqq\langle\nabla^{2}\hat{\mathcal{F}}(x,t,\xi)\eta,\zeta\rangle+\varepsilon\langle\eta,\zeta\rangle\qquad\mbox{for any\penalty\ $(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}$,\penalty\ $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{E}$}

for any
\eta,\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Additionally, we have that
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies quadratic growth, i.e. there holds

(3.17)

\displaystyle|\nabla\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi)|\leq(\varepsilon+C_{\Psi}\|\nabla\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R}\times B_{N})})(1+|\xi|)\leq C(C_{\mathcal{F}},C_{\Psi},\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}})(1+|\xi|)

for any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, resp.

(3.18)

\displaystyle|\nabla\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(x,\xi)|\leq(2C_{\mathcal{F}}+1)|\xi|.

for any
(x,t)\in Q_{R}
and
|\xi|>K+2R_{E}
. The latter is a consequence of the definition of
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
. In order to simplify notation, we shall omit the constant
C_{\Psi}
in any quantity depending on
C_{\Psi}
from this point on.

We will now state certain structure estimates that will turn out essential for the subsequent discussion.

The following lemma serves as a crucial preliminary tool, providing a monotonicity estimate for
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}
. This estimate is a direct consequence of the ellipticity condition (
3.13
), which holds for any
1+\delta\leq|\xi|_{E}
and for all
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and
\delta>0
. For any
(x,t)\in\Omega
and any
\tilde{\xi},\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\xi|_{E}\geq 1+\delta
there holds

(3.19)

\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\tilde{\xi})-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi),\tilde{\xi}-\xi\rangle\geq\bigg(\varepsilon+C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta)\frac{r_{E}(2|\xi|_{E}-(2+\delta))}{2|\xi|_{E}(R_{E}+r_{E})}\bigg)|\tilde{\xi}-\xi|^{2}.

Moreover, for any
\tilde{\xi},\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the quantity on the left-hand side of (
3.19
) is non-negative.

As a consequence of Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
3.1
, we obtain the next auxiliary result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and
\delta>0
. For any
(x,t)\in\Omega
and
\tilde{\xi},\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
there holds

\displaystyle\varepsilon|\tilde{\xi}-\xi|^{2}+|\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(\tilde{\xi})-\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(\xi)|^{2}\leq C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta,R_{E},r_{E})\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,\tilde{\xi})-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,\xi),\tilde{\xi}-\xi\rangle.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and
\delta>0
. For any
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
and any
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
1+\delta\leq|\xi|\leq\delta^{-1}
there holds

(\varepsilon+\lambda(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta))|\eta|^{2}\leq\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi)(\eta,\eta)\leq(\varepsilon+\Lambda(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta))|\eta|^{2}.

Proof.

The estimate is an immediate consequence of the ellipticity assumption (
3.13
) and the definition of the bilinear form
\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}
according to (
3.16
).
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The estimates from Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
also to hold true in the case where
|\xi|_{E}\leq 1
without the constants
C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta)
,
\lambda(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta)
,
\Lambda(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta)
.

Next, let us consider the unique weak solution

u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{0}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0}),L^{2}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)\cap L^{2}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0}),u+W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)

to the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem

(3.20)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}-\divv\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon})=f&\mbox{in\penalty\ $Q_{R}$},\\
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\quad\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad u_{\varepsilon}=u&\mbox{on\penalty\ $\partial_{p}Q_{R}$}.\end{cases}

Since the redefined equation satisfies quadratic growth according to (
3.17
), it is indeed sufficient to consider approximating solutions
u_{\varepsilon}
thar are
a priori
assumed to be of class

C^{0}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0}),L^{2}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)\cap L^{2}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0}),u+W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big).

The respective weak formulation of (
3.20
) results in

(3.21)

\iint_{Q_{R}}(-u_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\phi+\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon}),D\phi\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\iint_{Q_{R}}f\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

for any test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(Q_{R})
. The ellipticity property of
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}
enables us to obtain higher regularity of the form

u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});W^{1,\infty}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)

for the approximating solutions
u_{\varepsilon}
. Additionally, we obtain local quantitative estimates for
Du_{\varepsilon}
in
Q_{R}
, both of which are stated in form of Proposition
3.6
and Proposition
3.7
respectively. In fact, we immediately infer from
[
14
, Chapter V, Theorem 3.3 & Remark 3.1]
that
u_{\varepsilon}
is bounded in
Q_{R}
for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, i.e. there holds
u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(Q_{R})
, which is a consequence of the quadratic growth (
3.17
) and the fact that the boundary datum
u\in L^{\infty}(Q_{R})
itself is bounded within
Q_{R}
. Indeed, there holds

(3.22)

\displaystyle\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R})}\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R})}<\infty

for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

Before stating quantitative local
L^{\infty}_{\loc}(Q_{R},\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});W^{1,2}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}),\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)
-gradient estimates for
Du_{\varepsilon}
on
Q_{R}
, the subsequent energy estimate turns out expedient, which states a uniform
L^{2}
-gradient bound with respect to the parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
for the approximating solutions
u_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. There holds the uniform energy estimate

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{R}}|Du_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\iint_{Q_{R}}\big(1+|Du|^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x

with a constant
C=C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},R_{E},r_{E})
.

Proof.

We subtract the weak forms in Steklov-means of (
1.9
) for
u
from the respective weak formulation (
3.21
) in Steklov-means for
u_{\varepsilon}
. This yields

\int_{B_{R}}\big(\partial_{t}[u-u_{\varepsilon}]_{h}\phi+\langle[\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du)-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon})]_{h},D\phi\rangle\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=0

for any
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{R})
. and any
t\in\Lambda_{R}(s_{0})
. Subsequently, we integrate the latter with respect to
t\in\Lambda_{R}(s_{0})
to obtain

(3.23)

\iint_{Q_{R}}\big(\partial_{t}(u-u_{\varepsilon})\phi+\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du)-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon}),D\phi\rangle\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=0

for any
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{R})
. Now, we test the preceding weak formulation (
3.23
) with
\phi=[u-u_{\varepsilon}]_{h}
. Note that
\phi
is indeed an admissible test function due to the definition of
u_{\varepsilon}
and through a standard approximation argument. For the term involving the time derivative, we have

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{R}}\partial_{t}[u-u_{\varepsilon}]_{h}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{Q_{R}}\partial_{t}[u-u_{\varepsilon}]^{2}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{B_{R}\times\{s_{0}\}}[u-u_{\varepsilon}]^{2}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Passing first to the limit
h\downarrow 0
yields

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{h\downarrow 0}\iint_{Q_{R}}\partial_{t}[u-u_{\varepsilon}]_{h}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{B_{R}\times\{s_{0}\}}|u-u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Letting also
h\downarrow 0
in the remaining diffusion term and adding the quantity
\varepsilon\langle Du,Du-Du_{\varepsilon}\rangle
on both sides of the inequality, we end up with

(3.24)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{R}\times\{s_{0}\}}|u-u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\iint_{Q_{R}}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du)-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon}),Du-Du_{\varepsilon}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\iint_{Q_{R}}\langle Du,Du-Du_{\varepsilon}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Since the first term leads to a non-negative contribution, we discard it. Then, there remains

(3.25)

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{R}}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du),Du\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\iint_{Q_{R}}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon}),Du_{\varepsilon}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{Q_{R}}\big(\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon}),Du\rangle+\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du),Du_{\varepsilon}\rangle\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

The first quantity on the left-hand side of the preceding estimate is again non-negative due to Lemma
3.1
and is discarded. We recall that
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies (
3.13
). The second term on the left-hand side is also non-negative for the same reason, allowing us to pass to the subset of points
Q_{R}\cap\{|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}\geq\frac{3}{2}\}
and bound the term below by employing Lemma
3.1
to obtain

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{R}}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon}),Du_{\varepsilon}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\iint_{Q_{R}}\bigg(\hat{C}\frac{2|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}-\frac{5}{2}}{|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}}|Du_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\scalebox{1.3}{$\chi$}_{\{|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}\geq\frac{3}{2}\}}+\varepsilon|Du_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\iint_{Q_{R}}\bigg(\hat{C}\frac{2|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}-\frac{5}{2}}{|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}}(|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}-\mbox{$\frac{3}{2}$})^{2}_{+}+\varepsilon|Du_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\iint_{Q_{R}}\hat{C}(|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}-\mbox{$\frac{3}{2}$})^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\varepsilon\iint_{Q_{R}}|Du_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

with a positive constant
\hat{C}>0
that depends on
C_{\mathcal{F}},R_{E},r_{E}
. In turn, we also used (
2.9
). Next, we bound the first term contained in the integral on the right-hand side of (
3.25
) further above by exploiting the quadratic growth of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}
according to (
3.17
) and also Young’s inequality, which yields

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{R}}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon}),Du\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{Q_{R}}\big(C(1+|Du_{\varepsilon}|)|Du|+\varepsilon|Du_{\varepsilon}||Du|\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{Q_{R}}\bigg(\frac{\hat{C}}{4}(|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}-\mbox{$\frac{3}{2}$})^{2}_{+}\,+C|Du|^{2}+C\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\iint_{Q_{R}}\big(|Du_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+|Du|^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

with a constant
C=C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},R_{E},r_{E})
. The remaining second integral term on the right-hand side of (
3.25
) is estimated in a similar way. After reabsorbing all quantities involving
(|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}-\mbox{$\frac{3}{2}$})^{2}
into the left-hand side of (
3.25
), we end up with

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{R}}\big((|Du_{\varepsilon}|_{E}-\mbox{$\frac{3}{2}$})^{2}_{+}+\varepsilon|Du_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\leq\varepsilon C\iint_{Q_{R}}|Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+C\iint_{Q_{R}}(1+|Du|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

for some constant
C=C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},R_{E},r_{E})
, from which the claimed energy bound can be readily inferred by another application of (
2.9
).
∎

As a next step, we aim to establish the higher regularity

u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});W^{1,\infty}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)\cap L^{2}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});W^{2,2}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big),

where we commence with the treatment of the

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Then, there holds

Du_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});W^{1,2}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}),\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)\cap L^{\infty}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});L^{2}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}),\mathbb{R}^{n})\big).

Moreover, for any cylinder
Q_{2\rho}(z_{0})\Subset Q_{R}
with
\rho\in(0,1]

(3.26)

\displaystyle\esssup\limits_{t\in\Lambda_{\frac{\rho}{4}}(t_{0})}\int_{B_{\frac{\rho}{4}}(x_{0})\times\{t\}}|Du_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\iint_{Q_{\frac{\rho}{4}}(z_{0})}|D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}}\Bigg(\iint_{Q_{\rho}(z_{0})}(1+|Du_{\varepsilon}|)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\rho^{2}\iint_{Q_{\rho}(z_{0})}|f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\Bigg)

with a constant
C=C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},N,n,r_{E})
.

Proof.

We establish the quantitative
L^{2}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});W^{1,2}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0})\big)\cap L^{\infty}_{\loc}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0});L^{2}_{\loc}(B_{R}(y_{0}),\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)
-gradient estimate (
3.26
) in the usual way through employing the notion of difference quotients. Let us consider a cylinder
Q_{2\rho}(z_{0})\Subset Q_{R}
and a parameter
\sigma
small enough, such that there holds
|\sigma|\in(0,\dist(Q_{2\rho}(z_{0}),\partial Q_{R}))
for any
z\in Q_{2\rho}(z_{0})
. The finite difference of
u_{\varepsilon}
with respect to
x
in direction
x_{i}
and increment
\sigma\neq 0
is given by

\tau_{i,\sigma}u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\coloneqq u_{\varepsilon}(x+\sigma e_{i},t)-u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)

for a.e.
(x,t)\in Q_{2\rho}(z_{0})
and any

. In order to simplify notation, we shall write
u=u_{\varepsilon}
and
\tau_{\sigma}u=\tau_{i,\sigma}u_{\varepsilon}
. We test the weak form (
3.21
) with
\tau_{-\sigma}\phi
for an arbitrarily chosen test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(Q_{2\rho}(z_{0}))
and perform a change of variables, leading to

\iint_{Q_{R}}(-\tau_{\sigma}u\,\partial_{t}\phi+\langle\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du),D\phi\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=-\iint_{Q_{R}}f\tau_{-\sigma}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Integrating by parts in the evolutionary term, we obtain

\iint_{Q_{R}}(\partial_{t}(\tau_{\sigma}u)\phi+\langle\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du),D\phi\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\iint_{Q_{R}}f\,\tau_{-\sigma}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

for any
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(Q_{R})
. Indeed, this calculation is only formal and can be made rigorous by a Steklov-average procedure similar to Lemma
3.5
. In the preceding equation, we choose
\phi=\zeta\tau_{\sigma}u
, where
\zeta=\psi^{2}(x,t)\eta(t)\in W^{1,\infty}_{0}(Q_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(z_{0}),[0,1])
and
\psi\in C^{2}(Q_{\frac{\rho}{2}(z_{0})},[0,1])
denotes a smooth cut-off function vanishing on the parabolic boundary
\partial_{p}(Q_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(z_{0}))
with
\psi\equiv 1
in
Q_{\frac{\rho}{4}}(z_{0})
,
|D\psi|\leq\frac{8}{\rho}
,
|D^{2}\psi|\leq\frac{64}{\rho^{2}}
, and
|\partial_{t}\psi|\leq\frac{32}{\rho^{2}}
, while
\eta\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R},[0,1])
is a Lipschitz continuous function in time subject to

\displaystyle\eta(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\mbox{for\penalty\ $t\in(-\infty,\kappa]$},\\
1-\frac{t-\kappa}{\delta}&\mbox{for\penalty\ $t\in(\kappa,\kappa+\delta]$},\\
0&\mbox{for\penalty\ $t\in(\kappa+\delta,\infty)$}\end{cases}

for an arbitrarily chosen
t_{0}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}<\kappa<\kappa+\delta<t_{0}
with
\delta>0
. This choice of test function
\phi
can be justified by an approximation argument. We begin with the treatment of the term involving the time derivative where we integrate by parts, which yields

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{R}}\partial_{t}(\tau_{\sigma}u)\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{Q_{R}}\partial_{t}(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{Q_{R}}(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\partial_{t}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\iint_{B_{R}\times(\kappa,\kappa+\delta]}(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\psi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\iint_{Q_{R}}(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\psi\partial_{t}\psi\eta,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Passing to the limit
\delta\downarrow 0
, we thus obtain for a.e.
\kappa\in\Lambda_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(t_{0})
the following

(3.27)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}\times\{\kappa\}}

\displaystyle(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\psi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}\langle\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du),D[\psi^{2}\tau_{\sigma}u]\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\psi\partial_{t}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}f\,\tau_{-\sigma}[\psi^{2}\tau_{\sigma}u]\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

where we abbreviated
Q_{\kappa}\coloneqq B_{R}\times(t_{0}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4},\kappa]
. By exploiting the commutativity of finite differences with weak derivatives, the diffusion term yields two quantities

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}\langle\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du),D[\psi^{2}\tau_{\sigma}u]\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}\psi^{2}\langle\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du),\tau_{\sigma}Du\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+2\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}\langle\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du),\tau_{\sigma}u\psi D\psi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

In the second term on the right-hand side of the preceding equality, we again integrate by parts through a change of variables and shift the finite difference from the vector field
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}
onto the quantity
\tau_{\sigma}u\psi D\psi
. This way, we achieve

(3.28)

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}\langle\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du),D[\psi^{2}\tau_{\sigma}u]\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}\psi^{2}\langle\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du),\tau_{\sigma}Du\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-2\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du),\tau_{-\sigma}[\tau_{\sigma}u\psi D\psi]\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

The finite difference apparent in the first integral term on the right-hand side above can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du)

\displaystyle=\tau_{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du)+\varepsilon\tau_{\sigma}Du(x)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\tau_{\sigma}Du(x)+\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x+\sigma e_{i},t,Du(x+\sigma e_{i},t))-\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du(x+\sigma e_{i},t))

\displaystyle\quad+\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du(x+\sigma e_{i},t))-\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du(x,t))

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{II}+\mathbf{III}

for a.e.
(x,t)\in Q_{\kappa}
. The first term
\mathbf{I}
and the third term
\mathbf{III}
remain unchanged. The second term
\mathbf{II}
is bounded above with aid of the Lipschitz assumption
\eqref{fregularity}_{4}
, according to (
3.12
), as the latter implies that
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
remains constant whenever
|\xi|\geq L
for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, also recalling (
3.6
), we estimate

\mathbf{II}\leq C(\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},N,r_{E})|\sigma|.

For the second integral quantity in (
3.28
), we use a sort of product rule for finite differences and rewrite the latter as

\displaystyle\tau_{-\sigma}[\tau_{\sigma}u\psi D\psi](x,t)

\displaystyle=\tau_{\sigma}u(x,t)\psi(x,t)D\psi(x,t)-\tau_{\sigma}u(x-\sigma e_{i},t)\psi(x-\sigma e_{i},t)D\psi(x-\sigma e_{i},t)

\displaystyle=\tau_{-\sigma}\tau_{\sigma}u(x,t)\psi(x,t)D\psi(x,t)+\tau_{-\sigma}[\psi D\psi](x,t)\tau_{\sigma}u(x-\sigma e_{i},t)

\displaystyle=\tau_{-\sigma}\tau_{\sigma}u(x,t)\psi(x,t)D\psi(x,t)-\tau_{-\sigma}[\psi D\psi](x,t)\tau_{-\sigma}u(x,t),

which holds true for a.e.
(x,t)\in Q_{2\rho}(z_{0})
. This term is bounded above by applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, while the term involving the datum
f
in (
3.27
) is further estimated by taking its modulus. Combining our calculations, we thus obtain

(3.29)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}\times\{\kappa\}}(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\psi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\varepsilon\int_{Q_{\kappa}}\psi^{2}|\tau_{\sigma}Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}\psi^{2}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du(x+\sigma e_{i},t))-\hat{\mathcal{H}}(x,t,Du(x,t)),Du(x+\sigma e_{i},t)-Du(x,t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\leq C|\sigma|\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}\psi^{2}|\tau_{\sigma}Du|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}|f||\tau_{-\sigma}[\psi^{2}\tau_{\sigma}u]|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+C(\varepsilon+1)\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}(1+|Du|)|\tau_{-\sigma}[\psi D\psi]||\tau_{-\sigma}u|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+C(\varepsilon+1)\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}(1+|Du|)\psi|D\psi||\tau_{-\sigma}\tau_{\sigma}u|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\psi\partial_{t}\psi,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\tilde{\mathbf{I}}+\tilde{\mathbf{II}}+\tilde{\mathbf{III}}+\tilde{\mathbf{IV}}+\tilde{\mathbf{V}}

with
C=C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},N,r_{E})
, for a.e.
\kappa\in\Lambda_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(t_{0})
. The third term on the left-hand side of the preceding estimate is non-negative due to the second assertion of Lemma
3.1
and thus can be discarded. The quantities

\psi

[
20

. The first term
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
is estimated by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{I}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\int_{Q_{\kappa}}\psi^{2}|\tau_{\sigma}Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{C}{\varepsilon}|\sigma|^{2}|Q_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(z_{0})|.

Next, the second term involving the datum

\psi
and

and also that
\psi
is supported within

20

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{II}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}|\sigma|^{2}\iint_{Q_{\rho}(z_{0})}|f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\varepsilon}{8|\sigma|^{2}}\iint_{B_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4},\kappa]}|\tau_{-\sigma}[\psi^{2}\tau_{\sigma}u]|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}|\sigma|^{2}\iint_{Q_{\rho}(z_{0})}|f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\iint_{B_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4},\kappa]}|D[\psi^{2}\tau_{\sigma}u]|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{III}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon+1)\rho^{2}\iint_{B_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4},\kappa]}(1+|Du|)^{2}|\tau_{-\sigma}[\psi D\psi]|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

with a constant

. For the fourth term

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{IV}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon+1)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\bigg(\iint_{B_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4},\kappa]}(1+|Du|)^{2}\psi^{2}|D\psi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\iint_{B_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4},\kappa]}|\tau_{-\sigma}\tau_{\sigma}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\rho^{2}}\frac{\varepsilon+1}{\varepsilon}|\sigma|^{2}\iint_{Q_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(z_{0})}(1+|Du|)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\iint_{Q_{\kappa}}|\tau_{\sigma}Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\tilde{\mathbf{V}}
is estimated by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\rho^{2}}|\sigma|^{2}\iint_{Q_{\rho}(z_{0})}|Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

At this point, we reabsorb all quantities involving the finite difference
\tau_{\sigma}Du
into the left-hand side of (

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}\times\{\kappa\}}(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\psi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{Q_{\kappa}}\psi^{2}|\tau_{\sigma}Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{C|\sigma|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\rho^{2}}\Bigg(\iint_{Q_{\rho}(z_{0})}(1+|Du|)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\rho^{2}\iint_{Q_{\rho}(z_{0})}|f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\Bigg)

for a.e.
\kappa\in\Lambda_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(t_{0})
, with a constant
C=C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},N,n,r_{E})
. For the remaining two quantities on the left-hand side, we exploit the fact that

on
B_{\frac{\rho}{4}(x_{0})}

\kappa\in\Lambda_{\frac{\rho}{2}}(t_{0})

\displaystyle\esssup\limits_{t\in\Lambda_{\frac{\rho}{4}}(t_{0})}\int_{B_{\frac{\rho}{4}}(x_{0})\times\{t\}}(\tau_{\sigma}u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\iint_{Q_{\frac{\rho}{4}}(z_{0})}|\tau_{\sigma}Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

with

. The term in brackets on the right-hand side is bounded uniformly with respect to
\sigma
, allowing us to utilize another classical result on difference quotients, cf.
[
20

was chosen arbitrarily, we obtain the claimed gradient estimate (

Proposition 3.7
.

Let

. Then, there holds

\esssup\limits_{Q_{\rho}(z_{0})}|Du_{\varepsilon}|\leq C\Bigg(\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-6.23328pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-5.58163pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-4.09418pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}}\kern-3.32918pt}}\!\iint_{Q_{2\rho}(z_{0})}|Du_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1\Bigg)

3.6

19

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{2\rho}(z_{0})}

3.3

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and

be the unique weak solution to (

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})\to\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du)\qquad\text{in\penalty\ $L^{2}(Q_{R},\mathbb{R}^{n})$ as\penalty\ $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$.}

Proof.

By following the approach taken in the proof of Lemma
3.5
, we again infer the energy estimate (
3.24
), i.e. there holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{R}\times\{s_{0}\}}|u-u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\iint_{Q_{R}}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du)-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon}),Du-Du_{\varepsilon}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\iint_{Q_{R}}\langle Du,Du-Du_{\varepsilon}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

An application of Lemma
3.2
and also Young’s inequality, after discarding the non-negative first term on the left-hand side in the preceding estimate, yields

\displaystyle\varepsilon\iint_{Q_{R}}|Du_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle-Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\iint_{Q_{R}}|\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta,R_{E},r_{E})\iint_{Q_{R}}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du)-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t,Du_{\varepsilon}),Du_{\varepsilon}-Du\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\int_{B_{R}}|Du_{\varepsilon}-Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C(C_{\mathcal{F}},\delta,R_{E},r_{E})\,\varepsilon\int_{B_{R}}|Du|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

After reabsorbing the first term on the right-hand side of the above inequality into the left-hand side, we note that importantly the constant

does not depend on the parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1]

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Similarly to before, let us consider the arbitrarily chosen cylinder
Q_{R}=Q_{R}(y_{0},s_{0})\Subset\Omega_{T}
and let

u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}\big(\Lambda_{R}(s_{0}),u+W^{1,\infty}_{0}(B_{R}(y_{0}))\big)

denote the unique weak solution to the Dirichlet problem (
3.20
) for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.
Further, let
\delta\in(0,1]
and consider a cylinder
Q_{\rho}(z_{0})\Subset Q_{R}\Subset\Omega_{T}
. In this setting, Theorem
3.9
is applicable, which asserts that
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})
is Hölder continuous in
\overline{Q}_{\rho}(z_{0})
with a Hölder exponent
\alpha_{\delta}\in(0,1)
and a Hölder constant
C_{\delta}
that depend on the data

\displaystyle\alpha_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\delta}(C_{\mathcal{F}}(\delta),\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},\delta,\|f\|_{L^{n+2+\sigma}(Q_{R})},M,N,n,R,r_{E},\sigma)\in(0,1),

\displaystyle C_{\delta}

\displaystyle=C_{\delta}(C_{\mathcal{F}}(\delta),\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},\delta,\|f\|_{L^{n+2+\sigma}(Q_{R})},M,N,n,R,R_{E},r_{E},\sigma)\geq 1.

According to Lemma
3.2
there holds the convergence
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})\to\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du)
in
L^{2}(Q_{R})
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. Thus, there exists a subsequence
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,1]
with
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon_{i}})\to\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du)
a.e. in
Q_{R}
as
\varepsilon_{i}\downarrow 0
. As
(\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon}))_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
is locally uniformly equicontinuous in
Q_{R}
and also locally uniformly bounded in
Q_{R}
, we may apply the theorem of Arzelà-Ascoli, allowing us to pass to the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, which yields that the limit function
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du)
itself is Hölder continuous in
\overline{Q}_{\rho}(z_{0})
with Hölder constant
C_{\delta}
and Hölder exponent
\alpha_{\delta}\in(0,1)
.

Furthermore, due to (
2.9
) and Lemma
2.4
, we estimate

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du)-\mathcal{G}(Du)|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\frac{(|Du|_{E}-(1+\delta))_{+}}{|Du|_{E}}Du-\frac{(|Du|_{E}-1)_{+}}{|Du|_{E}}Du\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|Du|}{|Du|_{E}}\big|(|Du|_{E}-(1+\delta))_{+}-(|Du|_{E}-1)_{+}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{$\frac{1}{r_{E}}$}\big|(|Du|_{E}-(1+\delta))_{+}-(|Du|_{E}-1)_{+}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{$\frac{1}{r_{E}}$}\delta

for a.e.
(x,t)\in\overline{Q}_{\rho}(z_{0})
. This verifies that the family
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du)\to\mathcal{G}(Du)
is uniformly continuous in
\overline{Q}_{\rho}(z_{0})
. As a consequence, the limit function
\mathcal{G}(Du)
itself is uniformly continuous in
\overline{Q}_{\rho}(z_{0})
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\kappa>0
with

(3.47)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}(Du(z_{1}))-\mathcal{G}(Du(z_{2}))|<\varepsilon\qquad\mbox{for any\penalty\ $x,y\in\overline{Q}_{\rho}(z_{0})$ with\penalty\ $d_{p}(z_{1},z_{2})<\kappa$}.

Let
\mathcal{K}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote an arbitrary continuous function that vanishes inside the bounded and convex set of degeneracy
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, according to the statement of Theorem
1.3
. Since there holds

u\in L^{\infty}_{\loc}\big(0,T;W^{1,\infty}_{\loc}(\Omega)),

there exists a positive constant
0<M<\infty
, such that
|Du|\leq M
a.e. in
\overline{Q}_{\rho}(z_{0})
. Then, there exists a modulus of continuity
\omega_{M}\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on
\overline{B}_{M}
with

|\mathcal{K}(\xi)-\mathcal{K}(\eta)|\leq\omega_{M}(|\xi-\eta|)\qquad\mbox{for any\penalty\ $\xi,\eta\in\overline{B}_{M}$}.

We now distinguish between two cases. First, we assume that
|Du(x)|_{E}\leq 1+\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. If
|Du(x)|_{E}\geq 1
, we use the fact that
\mathcal{K}\equiv 0
on
E
and the homogeneity of the Minkowski functional
|\cdot|_{E}
, which yield

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}(Du(x))|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathcal{K}(Du(x))-\mathcal{K}\Big(\frac{Du(x)}{|Du(x)|_{E}}\Big)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{M}\bigg(\Big|Du(x)-\frac{Du(x)}{|Du(x)|_{E}}\Big|\bigg)

\displaystyle=\omega_{M}\bigg(\frac{|Du(x)|}{|Du(x)|_{E}}(|Du(x)|_{E}-1)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{M}(R_{E}\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

If there holds
|Du(x)|_{E}\leq 1
, then the above estimate holds true trivially. The modulus of continuity (
3.47
), the triangle inequality (
2.1
), and also (
2.9
) yield

\displaystyle(|Du(y)|_{E}-1)_{+}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{G}(Du(y))|_{E}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{G}(Du(y))-\mathcal{G}(Du(x))|_{E}+|\mathcal{G}(Du(x))|_{E}

\displaystyle<\mbox{$\frac{\varepsilon}{r_{E}}$}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max\{\mbox{$\frac{1}{r_{E}}$},1\}\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

This implies in particular

|Du(y)|_{E}\leq 1+2\max\{\mbox{$\frac{1}{r_{E}}$},1\}\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

Thus by following the very same steps as above and exploiting (
2.9
) once more, we obtain
|\mathcal{K}(Du(y))|\leq\omega_{M}\big(2R_{E}\max\big\{\frac{1}{r_{E}},1\big\}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\big)
. By combining the estimates for
|\mathcal{K}(Du(x))|
and also
|\mathcal{K}(Du(y))|
, there holds

|\mathcal{K}(Du(x))-\mathcal{K}(Du(y))|\leq\omega_{M}(R_{E}\sqrt{\varepsilon})+\omega_{M}\big(2R_{E}\max\{\mbox{$\frac{1}{r_{E}}$},1\}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\big)\leq 2\omega_{M}\big(2R_{E}\max\{\mbox{$\frac{1}{r_{E}}$},1\}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\big),

such that
\mathcal{K}(Du)
is continuous in
\overline{Q}_{\rho}(z_{0})
in the case where
|Du(x)|_{E}\leq 1+\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. In the remaining case
|Du(x)|_{E}>1+\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, we may apply Lemma
2.5
, yielding that

\displaystyle|Du(x)-Du(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(R_{E},r_{E})}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{G}(Du(x))-\mathcal{G}(Du(y))|

\displaystyle\leq C(R_{E},r_{E})\sqrt{\varepsilon},

which further implies

|\mathcal{K}(Du(x))-\mathcal{K}(Du(y))|\leq\omega_{M}(C(R_{E},r_{E})\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

Consequently, we have verified that
\mathcal{K}(Du)
is continuous in
\overline{Q}_{\rho}(z_{0})
. Since all cylinders
Q_{\rho}(z_{0})\Subset Q_{R}
were chosen arbitrarily, the composition
\mathcal{K}(Du)
is continuous in the entire space-time domain
\Omega_{T}
, finishing the proof.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

, the control of the quantitative Hölder exponent is lost in the limit as

To our knowledge it remains an open question even for the model equation (

is actually locally Hölder continuous in

in

for any Hölder exponent

in the elliptic setting that for a certain function

there exist Lipschitz continuous functions

that vanish inside

is not Hölder continuous for any Hölder exponent

The degenerate regime

Proposition 6.1

(Expansion of positivity)
.

Let
\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
denote the weak sub-solution defined in (

such that whenever the assumption

\displaystyle|B_{\rho}\cap\{1-\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\tilde{t})>L\}|\geq\alpha|Q_{\rho}|

is satisfied, then either for all times

there holds

or we have

with a constant

C=C(C_{\mathcal{F}}(\delta),\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},\delta,\|f\|_{L^{n+2+\sigma}(Q_{R})},M,N,n,R,r_{E},\sigma).

Proof.

The result follows directly from the De Giorgi class-type energy estimates from Lemma
4.3
in Section
4
and the arguments presented in
[
19
, Chapter 12, Section 10.1 Proof of Theorem 10.1. Preliminaries]
and
[
19
, Chapter 12, Section 10.2 Proof of Theorem 10.1. Expansion of Positivity]

5.2

[

are satisfied in our setting. Consequently, the expansion of positivity stated in Proposition
6.1
immediately follows from these references.

However, it is important to highlight that, in

the latter is given by

|Q_{\rho}(z_{0})\cap\{\partial_{e^{*}}u_{\varepsilon}\leq 1+\delta+(1-\nu)\mu\}|\geq\nu|Q_{\rho}(z_{0})|.

For the re-scaled function
\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}
, this translates to the measure-theoretic information

(6.2)

\displaystyle|Q_{\rho}\cap\{\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}\leq(1-\nu)^{2}\}|\geq\nu|Q_{\rho}|.

The preceding upper bound for
\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}
in (
6.2
) can be estimated further above by

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}\leq(1-\nu)^{2}\leq 1-\nu^{2},

due to
\nu\in(0,1)
. The measure condition (
6.2
) thus implies that

\displaystyle|Q_{\rho}\cap\{\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}\leq 1-\nu^{2}\}|\geq\nu|Q_{\rho}|.

This measure-theoretic information in particular implies the existence of a time
t_{0}\in\big(-\rho^{2},-\frac{1}{2}\nu\rho^{2}\big]
, such that there holds

(6.3)

\displaystyle|B_{\rho}\cap\{\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t_{0})\leq 1-\nu^{2}\}|\geq\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\nu|B_{\rho}|,

since otherwise a contradiction is obtained as follows

\displaystyle\nu|Q_{\rho}|

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\int_{-\rho^{2}}^{0}|B_{\rho}\cap\{\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\leq 1-\nu^{2}\}|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{-\rho^{2}}^{-\frac{1}{2}\nu\rho^{2}}|B_{\rho}\cap\{\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\leq 1-\nu^{2}\}|\,\mathrm{d}t+\displaystyle\int_{-\frac{1}{2}\nu\rho^{2}}^{0}|B_{\rho}\cap\{\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\leq 1-\nu^{2}\}|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\nu|Q_{\rho}|\big(1-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\nu\big)+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\nu|Q_{\rho}|

\displaystyle=\nu{\textstyle\big(1-\frac{1}{4}\nu\big)}|Q_{\rho}|.

Consequently, the measure-theoretic information (
6.3
) allows an application of the expansion of positivity stated in Proposition
6.1
with the choice
\alpha\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\nu
,
\Gamma\coloneqq\frac{1}{4}\nu
, and
L\coloneqq\nu^{2}
. This way, we obtain the existence of a parameter
\eta\in(0,1)
that depends on

\eta=\eta(C_{\mathcal{F}}(\delta),\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},\delta,\|f\|_{L^{n+2+\sigma}(Q_{R})},M,N,n,\nu,R,r_{E},\sigma),

such that either there holds
\eta\nu^{2}\leq C\rho^{\beta}
, or we have

(6.4)

\tilde{v}_{\varepsilon}\leq 1-\eta\nu^{2}\qquad\mbox{a.e. in\penalty\ $B_{\rho}\times\big(-\frac{1}{4}\nu\rho^{2},0\big]$}.

The bound (
6.4
) can be translated to the original solution
u_{\varepsilon}
itself, which yields

\partial_{e^{*}}u_{\varepsilon}-(1+\delta)\leq\sqrt{1-\eta\nu^{2}}\mu\qquad\mbox{a.e. in\penalty\ $B_{\rho}(x_{0})\times\big(t_{0}-\frac{1}{4}\nu\rho^{2},t_{0}\big]$}.

Due to the fact that
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{4}]
, there holds
\sqrt{1-\eta\nu^{2}}\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
, ensuring a lower bound for the given quantity. In particular, way may choose
\sqrt{1-\eta\nu^{2}}\in\big[2^{-\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2}},1\big)
, as stated in Proposition
3.11
. Moreover, by choosing a radius
\hat{\rho}>0
small enough in dependence on the given data

(C_{\mathcal{F}}(\delta),\hat{C}_{\mathcal{F}},\delta,\|f\|_{L^{n+2+\sigma}(Q_{R})},M,N,n,\nu,R,r_{E},\sigma),

such that
C\tilde{\rho}^{\beta}<\eta\nu^{2}
, the first alternative of Proposition
6.1
never applies. Eventually, since
e^{*}\in\partial E^{*}
remains chosen arbitrarily up to this point, we pass to the supremum over all
e^{*}\in\partial E^{*}
, and use (
2.6
) and the definition of
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}
, to obtain

|\mathcal{G}_{\delta}(Du_{\varepsilon})|_{E}\leq\kappa\mu\qquad\mbox{a.e. in\penalty\ $Q_{\tilde{\nu}\rho}(z_{0})$},

with
\tilde{\nu}\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{2}
and
\kappa\coloneqq\sqrt{1-\eta\nu^{2}}\in\big[2^{-\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2}},1\big)
. This finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎
