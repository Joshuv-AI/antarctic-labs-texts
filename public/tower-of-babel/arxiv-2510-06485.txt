Spectral Triples for Hensel-Steinitz Algebras
Shelley Hebert, Slawomir Klimek, Matt McBride

Abstract
. We explicitly construct Fredholm modules and spectral triples representing any element of K K -homology groups of Hensel-Steinitz algebras.



1.
Introduction

Hensel-Steinitz algebras, introduced in
[
6
]
, are natural C
∗
-algebras associated to multiplication maps of the ring of
s
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. A Hensel-Steinitz algebra is defined as a crossed product of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
by a monomorphism with hereditary range defined in equation (
2.1
) below. It should be noted that Hensel-Steinitz algebras, despite similar definitions, are quite different than the algebras introduced in
[
3
]
. Hensel-Steinitz algebras are one of many interesting examples of C
∗
-algebras with connections to number theory, see for example
[
4
]
,
[
7
]
.

Hensel-Steinitz algebras can be related to some familiar C
∗
-algebras which allows for computation of their
K
-theory and
K
-homology groups. However, it is often not easy to pinpoint projections and unitaries generating the
K
-theory and Fredholm modules or spectral triples generating the
K
-homology groups. The purpose of this note is to describe in detail such Fredholm modules and spectral triples representing any element of
K
-homology groups of Hensel-Steinitz algebras. The
K
-theory was already sufficiently described in
[
6
]
. While
K
-theory and
K
-homology groups of C
∗
-algebras are part of their non-commutative topology, constructions of explicit representatives belong to their non-commutative geometry.

The first part of this note is a short summary of some of the results from
[
6
]
.
The second part on
K
-homology, Fredholm modules, and spectral triples for Hensel-Steinitz algebras is new, however it contains ideas and techniques inspired by a similar study in
[
11
]
.

2.
Hensel-Steinitz Algebras

For
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
s\geq 2
, the space of
s
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is defined as consisting of infinite sums:

{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}=\left\{x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}s^{j}:0\leq x_{j}\leq s-1,j=0,1,2,\ldots\right\}\,.

We call the above expansion of
x
its
s
-adic expansion. Note that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is isomorphic with the countable product
\prod{\mathbb{Z}}/s{\mathbb{Z}}
and so, when the product is equipped with the Tychonoff topology, it is a Cantor set. It is also a metric space with the usual norm,
|\cdot|_{s}
, which is defined as follows:

|0|_{s}=0\textrm{ and, if }x\neq 0,|x|_{s}=s^{-n},

where
x_{n}
is the first nonzero term in the above
s
-adic expansion.
Moreover,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
is an Abelian ring with unity with respect to addition and multiplication with a carry,
for more details, see
[
10
]
.

The main object of interest in this note is the map
\alpha:C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
defined by

(\alpha f)(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &f\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)&&\textrm{ if }s|x\\
&0&&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

(2.1)

Notice that

\alpha(1)(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&&\textrm{ if }s|x\\
&0&&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

is the characteristic function of the subset of the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
consisting of those elements that are divisible by
s
. It follows from equation (
2.1
) that the range of
\alpha
is equal to
\alpha(1)C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
. Thus
\alpha
is a monomorphism with hereditary range and in particular, all well known definitions of a crossed product by an endomorphism coincide in this case, see
[
12
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
5
]
.
With this, the Hensel-Steinitz algebra, denoted by
HS(s)
and defined in
[
6
]
, is the following crossed product:

HS(s):=C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}.

It is useful to realize
HS(s)
as a concrete C
∗
-algebra. Let
H=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
\{E_{l}\}_{l\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be its canonical basis, then since
{\mathbb{Z}}
is a dense subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
, the mapping
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\to B(H)
,
f\mapsto M_{f}
, given by

M_{f}E_{l}=f(l)E_{l}

is a faithful representation of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
.

Let
V
be the
s
-adic shift operator defined on
H
via

VE_{l}=E_{sl}\,.

A simple calculation verifies that

V^{*}E_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{l/s}&&\textrm{ if }s|l\\
&0&&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

There is an isomorphism between C
∗
-algebras, see
[
6
]
for details:

HS(s)=C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}}\cong C^{*}(V,M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))\,.

This follows because
V
and
M_{f}
satisfy the relations of the Stacey’s crossed product in
[
16
]
:

V^{*}V=I\textrm{ and }VM_{f}V^{*}=M_{\alpha f}\,

(2.2)

Additionally, the algebra
C^{*}(V,M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}))
carries a natural gauge action defined by maps
\rho_{\theta}
with
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
which is given by

\rho_{\theta}(a)=e^{2\pi i\theta\mathbb{P}}ae^{-2\pi i\theta\mathbb{P}}.

(2.3)

Here, given
l\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and writing
l=s^{m}l^{\prime}
with
l^{\prime}
not divisible by
s
, the diagonal operator
\mathbb{P}
is defined by:

\mathbb{P}E_{s^{m}l^{\prime}}=mE_{s^{m}l^{\prime}},\ \ \ \mathbb{P}E_{0}=0.

The gauge action satisfies
\rho_{\theta}(V)=e^{2\pi i\theta}V
and that
\rho_{\theta}(M_{f})=M_{f}
.
Then, the O’Donovan condition for crossed products implies that we have
HS(s)\cong C^{*}(V,M_{f})
.

3.
The Structure of Hensel-Steinitz Algebras

To understand the structure of
HS(s)
we first look at an ideal, denoted by
I_{s}
, which is the kernel of the representation
\pi_{0}
in
B(H)
defined by
\pi_{0}(M_{f})=f(0)I
and
\pi_{0}(V)=v
, where
v\in B(H)
is the standard bilateral shift

vE_{l}=E_{l+1},

so that

I_{s}:=\textup{Ker }\pi_{0}.

The ideal
I_{s}\subseteq HS(s)
can also be described as the subalgebra generated by the operators
V^{m}M_{f}
,
m\geq 0

and
M_{f}(V^{*})^{-m}
,
m<0
such that
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
with
f(0)=0
.

Let
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}
be the unit sphere in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
, and notice that if
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
has the
s
-adic expansion:

x=x_{0}+x_{1}s+x_{2}s^{2}+\cdots

then,
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}
if and only if
x_{0}\neq 0
. Observe that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}
is both a closed and open subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be the C
∗
-algebra of compact operators in a separable Hilbert space, the following isomorphism of C
∗
-algebras is shown in
[
6
]
:

I_{s}\cong C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}\,.

We outline an argument from that paper since some of the notation is useful later. Given nonzero integer
l
, decompose
l=s^{m}l^{\prime}
, with
l^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
,
|l^{\prime}|_{s}=1
. For
\lambda\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
, define the multiplication operator
m_{\lambda}:H\to H
by the following

m_{\lambda}E_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\lambda(l^{\prime})E_{l}&&\textrm{if }l\neq 0\\
&0&&\textrm{if }l=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.1)

Similarly, given a sequence
\chi=\{\chi(l)\}\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})
and the decomposition above, define
\mu_{\chi}:H\to H
by

\mu_{\chi}E_{l}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\chi(m)E_{l}&&\textrm{if }l\neq 0\\
&0&&\textrm{if }l=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.2)

Then, we have the following isomorphisms of
C^{*}
-algebras:

C^{*}(M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),f(0)=0)\cong C^{*}(m_{\lambda}\mu_{\chi}:\lambda\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}),\chi\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}))\cong C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\otimes c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})\,.

Since
m_{\lambda}
commutes with
V
and
\mu_{\chi}
, the following two C
∗
-algebras are isomorphic:

\displaystyle C^{*}(V^{n}M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}),f(0)=0,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})

\displaystyle C^{*}(m_{\lambda}:\lambda\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}))\otimes C^{*}(V^{n}\mu_{\chi}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},\chi\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})).

Finally, we have the following isomorphism:

C^{*}(V^{n}\mu_{\chi}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0},\chi\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}))\cong\mathcal{K}\,,

since the matrix units can be easily constructed from
V^{n}\mu_{\chi}
and they generate the algebra.

This leads to the short exact sequence describing the structure of
HS(s)
:

0\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}\to HS(s)\to C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})\to 0,

(3.3)

which is instrumental in further analysis of the Hensel-Steinitz algebra.

4.
Smooth Subalgebras

Our next goal is to introduce natural smooth subalgebras of Hensel-Steinitz algebras
HS(s)
, see
[
6
]
.
We first define a smooth subalgebra of the ideal
I_{s}
. This is done by using Fourier coefficients of elements of
HS(s)
with respect to the gauge action given by equation (
2.3
).

We again use the decomposition of nonzero elements of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
as
s^{m}x
,
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
|x|_{s}=1
and, given
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
with
f(0)=0
, we put

F(m,x)=f(s^{m}x).

The smooth subalgebra
I_{s}^{\infty}
of
I_{s}
is defined as the space of
a\in I_{s}
with a norm convergent series expansion:

a=\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{F_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}M_{F_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n},

(4.1)

where
F_{n}\in C_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
satisfy a rapid decay (RD) condition:

I_{s}^{\infty}:=\left\{a\in I_{s}:\{\underset{x}{\textrm{sup}}|F_{n}(m,x)|\}_{m,n}\textrm{ is RD}\right\}\,.

Recall from Proposition 2.1 in
[
9
]
, that the space of smooth compact operators
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}
can be described as

\mathcal{K}^{\infty}=\left\{\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}x_{n}+\sum_{n<0}x_{n}(V^{*})^{-n}:x_{n}\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}),\{x_{n}(m)\}_{m,n}\text{ is RD}\right\}\,.

We have the following isomorphism of algebras:

I_{s}^{\infty}\cong C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}^{\infty}.

Notice that the elements of
I_{s}^{\infty}
don’t have any extra regularity in the
\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}
coordinates, which is required for the construction of spectral triples below. For that purpose, the simplest requirement is Lipschitz continuity.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be a Lipschitz function and define

L(f)=\textrm{inf}\{k:|f(x)-f(y)|\leq k|x-y|_{s})\},

called the Lipschitz constant of
f
. Let
C_{L}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
be the Banach
*
-algebra of Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}
equipped with the norm:

\|f\|_{L}:=\underset{x}{\textrm{sup}}|f(x)|+L(f).

Using the notation from equation (
4.1
), we define

I_{s,L}^{\infty}:=\left\{a\in I_{s}:\{\|F_{n}(m,\cdot)\|_{L}\}_{m,n}\textrm{ is RD}\right\}\,.

so that

I_{s,L}^{\infty}\cong C_{L}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}^{\infty}.

For
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
, a Toeplitz operator
T(\phi)
acting on
H
is given by

T(\phi)=\sum_{m\geq 0}\phi_{m}V^{m}+\sum_{m<0}\phi_{m}(V^{*})^{-m}

(4.2)

where
\phi_{m}
are the
m^{\textrm{th}}
Fourier coefficients of
\phi
. Since
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
, it follows that the sequence
\{\phi_{m}\}
is RD and the above series is norm convergent.

We define the smooth Hensel-Steinitz algebra,
HS^{\infty}(s)
by

HS^{\infty}(s)=\{a\in HS(s):a=T(\phi)+c\text{ with }\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}),c\in I_{s}^{\infty}\}\,.

It was verified in
[
6
]
that
HS^{\infty}(s)
is a smooth subalgebra of
HS(s)
and in particular it is stable under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements. Below we also need the following
*
-subalgebra of
HS(s)
:

HS^{\infty}_{L}(s)=\{a\in HS(s):a=T(\phi)+c\text{ with }\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}),c\in I_{s,L}^{\infty}\}\,.

Notice that this is a smooth, dense subalgebra of
HS(s)
and we will refer to it as a smooth-Lipschitz subalgebra.

5.
K-Theory

We review the
K
-theory of
HS(s)
from
[
6
]
. It can be computed from the six-term exact sequence in
K
-theory induced by the short exact sequence given by equation (
3.3
).

Denote by
C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
the group of continuous functions on
\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}
with values in
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Since
I_{s}\cong C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})\otimes\mathcal{K}
, the stability of
K
-theory and the known
K
-theory of continuous functions on totally disconnected compact spaces shows that

K_{0}(I_{s})\cong K_{0}(C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}))\cong C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})\quad\text{and}\quad K_{1}(I_{s})\cong K_{1}(C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}))\cong 0.

The explicit stability isomorphism of
K_{0}(C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}))\cong K_{0}(I_{s})
is given by

p\mapsto p\otimes(I-VV^{*}).

The six-term exact sequence in
K
-theory simplifies to:

0\to{\mathbb{Z}}\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})\to K_{0}(HS(s))\to{\mathbb{Z}}\to 0.

By
[
6
]
, the range of the map
{\mathbb{Z}}\to C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
is precisely the constant functions in
C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Taking the quotient, we obtain the short exact sequence

0\to C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})/{\mathbb{Z}}\to K_{0}(HS(s))\to{\mathbb{Z}}\to 0,

which can be easily seen to split. This implies that the
K
-theory of
HS(s)
is given by

K_{0}(HS(s))\cong(C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})/{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}\quad\text{and}\quad K_{1}(HS(s))\cong 0.

It is convenient to naturally identify
C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})/{\mathbb{Z}}
with
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
where
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
are those functions in
C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
which vanish at
1\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}
:

C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})=\{f\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}}):f(1)=0\}.

To proceed, we need the generators of
K_{0}(HS(s))
.
From the above calculation we see that, other than the class of identity, all other
K_{0}(HS(s))
classes are generated by the projections

m_{1_{X}}(I-VV^{*})\in I_{s},

(5.1)

where
X
is a closed and open subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
not containing
1\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}
, and
1_{X}
is its characteristic function.

6.
K-Homology

From reference
[
1
]
, Section 22.3, we know that the bootstrap category
\mathcal{N}
of separable nuclear C
∗
-algebras contains all commutative C
∗
-algebras, all AF C
∗
-algebras, and is closed under tensor products and extensions. It follows that
HS(s)
is in
\mathcal{N}
and we can use the Universal Coefficient Theorem of
[
15
]
. Since
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
is free (see below and also exercise 7.7.5 in
[
8
]
) the
K
-homology can be identified with the groups of of homomorphisms from the
K
-theory to
{\mathbb{Z}}
and we obtain

K^{1}(HS(s))\cong 0\quad\text{and}\quad K^{0}(HS(s))\cong\Hom(C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus{\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{Z}})\cong\Hom(C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}.

In the formula above, the last factor of
{\mathbb{Z}}
is generated by the homomorphism
\eta:K_{0}(HS(s))\to{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\eta(I)=1\ \ \textrm{ and }\ \ \eta(I-VV^{*})=0.

(6.1)

It remains to study
\Hom(C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}})
.

6.1.
Generators

Next, we describe a convenient set of generators of
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
. For
n=0,1,2,\ldots
and
0\leq x<s^{n}
consider the following functions on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
:

1_{(n,x)}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&&\textrm{if }s^{n}|(z-x)\\
&0&&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

Those are characteristic functions of open and closed subsets of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
. Any two of those sets are either disjoint or one is a subset of the other. Moreover, linear combinations of functions
1_{(n,x)}
are dense in
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
and any function in
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s},{\mathbb{Z}})
is a finite integer combination of
1_{(n,x)}
’s. However,
1_{(n,x)}
’s are not independent since

1_{(n,x)}=\sum_{l=0}^{s-1}1_{(n+1,x+ls^{n})}.

Since
\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}
is both closed and open in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
we can think of
C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
as the subspace of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})
of functions vanishing outside of
\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}
. From the definition, if
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}
has the
s
-adic expansion:

x=x_{0}+x_{1}s+x_{2}s^{2}+\cdots

then,
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{s}^{\times}
if and only if
x_{0}\neq 0
. It follows that
1_{(n,x)}\in C(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
if and only if
s
does not divide
x
and
1_{(n,x)}\in C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
if additionally
x>1
.

To proceed further we consider the set
T\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}
given by

T:=\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}:x\geq 2,s\nmid x\}.

For
x\in T
we define

1_{(x)}:=1_{(n(x),x)},

where
n(x)\in{\mathbb{N}}
is defined so that
s^{n(x)-1}<x<s^{n(x)}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

The collection of functions
\{1_{(x)}\}
for all
x\in T
form free generators of the group
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
. In other words,
any element
f\in C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
can be uniquely written as a finite sum:

f=\sum_{x\in T}f_{(x)}\,1_{(x)}

where
f_{(x)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

As noticed above, linear combinations of the
1_{(n,x)}
’s with
x\in T
generate
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and since continuity of a function in
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
is equivalent to the function being locally constant, any function in
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
is a linear (integer) combination of
1_{(n,x)}
’s with
x\in T
. Thus, to establish the existence part of the proposition we need to express the functions
1_{(n,x)}
in terms of the functions
1_{(x)}
’s.

We proceed by induction in
n
. The base case of
n=1
is immediate by definition.
For the inductive step, suppose
1_{(n,x)}
can be expressed in terms of the
1_{(x)}
’s and consider the function
1_{(n+1,x)}
. If
s^{n}<x<s^{n+1}
, then by definition we have that
1_{(n+1,x)}=1_{(x)}
. On the other hand if
2\leq x<s^{n}
, we have that

1_{(n+1,x)}=1_{(n,x)}-\sum_{l=1}^{s-1}1_{(n+1,x+ls^{n})}=1_{(n,x)}-\sum_{l=1}^{s-1}1_{(x+ls^{n})},

which, by assumption, shows the inductive step and hence existence.

To establish uniqueness suppose that

\sum_{x\in T}f_{(x)}\,1_{(x)}(z)=0.

We evaluate the sum on the left-hand side of the above equation at
z=2,3,\ldots
and prove recursively that
f_{(x)}=0
.

Consider

0=\sum_{x\in T}f_{(x)}1_{(x)}(2)=\sum_{x\in T}f_{(x)}1_{(n(x),x)}(2)

for
s^{n(x)-1}\leq x<s^{n(x)}
. Note that
1_{(n(x),x)}(2)=1
if
s^{n(x)}
divides
x-2
and is zero else. Since
x-2<s^{n(x)}
, it follows that from the above sum that
f_{(2)}=0
. Following this procedure recursively, we conclude that
f_{(x)}=0
for all
x\in T
and hence the uniqueness follows.

∎

6.2.
Special Homomorphisms

There are two classes of particularly interesting elements of the group
\Hom(C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Define
e_{(y)}\in\Hom(C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}})
for
y\in T
to be the “dual” homomorphisms to the generators
1_{(x)}
of
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
:

e_{(y)}(1_{(x)})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&&\textrm{if }y=x\\
&0&&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

The significance of
e_{(y)}
is that any homomorphism
\Phi\in\Hom(C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}})
can be uniquely written as a possibly infinite sum:

\Phi=\sum_{y\in T}\phi_{(y)}\,e_{(y)}

where
\phi_{(y)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. The infinite sum above is always well-defined because when evaluating
\Phi
on
f\in C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
the resulting sum is always finite.

Another important class of homomorphisms are the evaluation maps
\delta_{z}
,
z=0,1,2,\ldots
:

\delta_{z}(f):=f(z).

Notice that if
z\notin T
but
x\in T
then
\delta_{z}(1_{(x)})=0
and so, for
z\notin T
,
\delta_{z}
is a zero homomorphism on
C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}})
. However, below it is still convenient to use that notation of evaluations at
z\not\in T
.

Let
x
be a natural number, if
n(x)
is such that
s^{n(x)-1}\leq x<s^{n(x)}
we set:

\gamma(x):=x\textrm{ mod }s^{n(x)-1},

so that
0\leq\gamma(x)<s^{n(x)-1}
.
Notice that there are
x\in T
such that
\gamma(x)\not\in T
. In fact, from the definition,
\gamma(x)=0
for
x=2,3,\ldots s-1
. Additionally,
\gamma(x)=1
if
x
is of the form

x=1+s^{n-1}+ys

for
n\geq 2
and
y=0,1,\ldots,s^{n-1}-s^{n-2}-1
. In all other cases if
x\in T
then
\gamma(x)\in T
.

The two classes of homomorphisms are closely related by the following formulas.

Proposition 6.2
.

With the above notation we have

\delta_{z}=e_{(z)}+e_{(\gamma(z))}+e_{(\gamma^{2}(z))}+\cdots+e_{(\gamma^{i}(z))}+\cdots\textrm{ and }e_{(z)}=\delta_{z}-\delta_{\gamma(z)}.

In the first formula, the sum is such that
\gamma^{i}(z)\in T
. In the second formula, the homomorphism
\delta_{\gamma(z)}
might be zero.

Proof.

Fix
z\in T
. For
s^{n(x)-1}<x<s^{n(x)}
we have that

\delta_{z}(1_{(x)})=\delta_{z}(1_{(n(x),x)})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&&\textrm{ for }s^{n(x)}|(z-x)\\
&0&&\textrm{ else}.\end{aligned}\right.

We want to determine for which
x
we have
\delta_{z}(1_{(x)})
is non-zero. First observe that to get nontrivial
\delta_{z}(1_{(x)})
, we must have
x\leq z
since otherwise
0<x-z<s^{n(z)}
and so
s^{n(z)}
cannot divide
x-z
. In particular, for a fixed
z
, there are only finitely many
x
such that
\delta_{z}(1_{(x)})\neq 0
. We determine them inductively in decreasing order.

The first is
x=z
and it is the only one such that
s^{n(x)}=s^{n(z)}
. The next possible
s^{n(x)}
is
s^{n(z)-1}
and for such a choice we must have
s^{n(z)-1}
dividing
x-z
and hence

x=z\textrm{ mod }s^{n(z)-1}=\gamma(z).

Repeating this reductive process results in the first formula. The second formula is a direct consequence of the first.
∎

7.
Fredholm Modules

The goal of this section is to describe in detail Fredholm modules representing any element of the
K
-homology groups of Hensel-Steinitz algebras.

7.1.
Definitions

An odd Fredholm module over a unital C
∗
-algebra
A
, see for example
[
8
]
, is a triple
(H,\rho,{\mathcal{F}})
where
H
is a Hilbert space,
\rho:A\to B(H)
is a
*
-representation, and
{\mathcal{F}}\in B(H)
satisfies

{\mathcal{F}}^{*}-{\mathcal{F}},\ I-{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\in\mathcal{K}(H),\textrm{ and }[{\mathcal{F}},\rho(a)]\in\mathcal{K}(H)

for all
a\in A
, where
\mathcal{K}(H)
is the algebra of compact operators in
B(H)
. Similarly, an even Fredholm module is the above information, together with a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-grading
\Gamma
of
H
, such that

\Gamma^{*}=\Gamma,\ \Gamma^{2}=I,\ \Gamma{\mathcal{F}}=-{\mathcal{F}}\Gamma,\ \textrm{and }\Gamma\rho(a)=\rho(a)\Gamma

for all
a\in A
. It follows that
H=H_{ev}\oplus H_{odd}
,
\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd}
. Without the loss of generality, we can assume that
{\mathcal{F}}
is self-adjoint, so it has the form:

{\mathcal{F}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&G\\
G^{*}&0\end{bmatrix}

(7.1)

where
G
maps
H_{odd}
to
H_{ev}
.
Classes of odd Fredholm modules over
A
form the K-homology group
K^{1}(A):=KK^{1}(A,\mathbb{C})
, while classes of even Fredholm modules over
A
form the K-homology group
K^{0}(A):=KK^{0}(A,\mathbb{C})
.

For any C
∗
-algebra
A
and
i=0,1
there are pairings between
K_{i}(A)
and
K^{i}(A)
, see
[
2
]
. For our purpose we only need the pairing of the class in
K_{0}(A)
of a projection
P
in the algebra
A
and a Fredholm module
(H_{ev}\oplus H_{odd},\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd},{\mathcal{F}})
. It is given by

\langle[P]_{0},(H_{ev}\oplus H_{odd},\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd},{\mathcal{F}})\rangle=\textrm{Index}(\rho_{ev}(P)G\rho_{odd}(P)),

where the index above is of the restricted operator

\rho_{ev}(P)G\rho_{odd}(P):\textrm{Ran}(\rho_{odd}(P))\to\textrm{Ran}(\rho_{ev}(P))\,.

7.2.
Constructions

First we describe a simple Fredholm module the class of which in
K
-homology

K^{0}(HS(s))
is the homomorphism
\eta
of equation (
6.1
). In this one-dimensional Fredholm module the even Hilbert space is just zero while the odd is
{\mathbb{C}}
. The even representation of
HS(s)
and the operator

Notice that this representation is zero on the ideal

and in particular it is zero on the generators of

coming from that ideal. It follows that the corresponding index is zero. On the other hand, the range of the odd representation is one-dimensional which implies that the index of

Next, given a homomorphism

Informally, we build the representation

More precisely, the Hilbert space for this module is a separable Hilbert space with distinguished basis labeled in the following way.
H_{odd}

\phi_{(y)}>0

E^{{odd},-}_{(z,k,l)}

\phi_{(z)}<0

for all

E^{{ev},-}_{(z,k,l)}

\phi_{(z)}<0
,

and

Since

is a crossed product by an endomorphism:

HS(s)=C({\mathbb{Z}}_{s})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{N}},

to define a representation of

we need to represent its generators and verify that they satisfy the relations of Stacey’s crossed product given by equation (
2.2

\rho_{ev}

\rho_{ev}(M_{f})E^{{ev},+}_{(y,j,l)}=f(s^{l}\gamma(y))E^{{ev},+}_{(y,j,l)},\ \ \ \ \rho_{ev}(M_{f})E^{{ev},-}_{(z,k,l)}=f(s^{l}z)E^{{ev},-}_{(z,k,l)},

The definition of

\rho_{odd}(M_{f})E^{{odd},+}_{(y,j,l)}=f(s^{l}y)E^{{odd},+}_{(y,j,l)},\ \ \ \ \rho_{odd}(M_{f})E^{{odd},-}_{(z,k,l)}=f(s^{l}\gamma(z))E^{{odd},-}_{(z,k,l)}

It ie easy to see that the relations in equation (

∗

We also consider an operator

given on basis elements by:

GE^{{odd},+}_{(y,j,l)}=E^{{ev},+}_{(y,j,l)}\textrm{ and }GE^{{odd},-}_{(z,k,l)}=E^{{ev},-}_{(z,k,l)}.

The definition implies that

Theorem 7.1
.

With the above notation,
(H_{ev}\oplus H_{odd},\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd},{\mathcal{F}})
where
{\mathcal{F}}
is given by equation (
7.1
), is an even Fredholm module over
HS(s)
. The class of this module in
K
-homology

K^{0}(HS(s))\cong\Hom(C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}
is the homomorphism
\Phi\oplus 0
.

Proof.

From the definitions it follows that
{\mathcal{F}}^{2}=I
and
{\mathcal{F}}
is self-adjoint. Thus, to check that
(H_{ev}\oplus H_{odd},\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd},{\mathcal{F}})
is a Fredholm module we need to verify that
[{\mathcal{F}},\rho(a)]\in\mathcal{K}(H)
for all
a\in HS(s)
.

From the structure of
HS(s)
any element is of the form
a=T(\phi)+c
with
\phi\in C({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
and
c\in I_{s}
. This task can be simplified in the following way. Since compact operators form a closed ideal in
B(H)
, by continuity it is enough to verify that
[{\mathcal{F}},\rho(a)]\in\mathcal{K}(H)
for
a
in a dense subalgebra of
HS(s)
. A convenient choice are finite sums of the form

a=\sum_{m\geq 0}\phi_{m}V^{m}+\sum_{m<0}\phi_{m}(V^{*})^{-m}\,+\,\sum_{n\geq 0}V^{n}M_{F_{n}}+\sum_{n<0}M_{F_{n}}(V^{*})^{-n},

see equations (
4.1
) and (
4.2
).

Next, notice that
{\mathcal{F}}
and
\rho(V)
commute:

[{\mathcal{F}},\rho(V)]=0.

This is because, on basis elements,
\rho(V)
shifts the
l
index while formulas for
G
and
G^{*}
are independent of
l
.

Further simplification comes from the observation that the commutator with
{\mathcal{F}}
is a derivation and consequently, the Leibniz rule implies that it is enough to check that
[{\mathcal{F}},\rho(a)]\in\mathcal{K}(H)
for algebraic generators of the subalgebra.

For
p\geq 0
let
\chi_{p}\in c_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})
be a sequence given by
\chi_{p}=\{\delta_{mp}\}_{m\geq 0}
.
Notice that if

f(s^{m}l)=1_{(x)}(l)\chi_{p}(m)\textrm{ and }f(0)=0,

with
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times}
, then

M_{f}=m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{p}}.

Notice also that

I-VV^{*}=\mu_{\chi_{0}}.

(7.3)

It follows from the structure of the ideal
I_{s}
and formulas (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) that the operators of the form:

m_{1_{(x)}}V^{n}\mu_{\chi_{p}}\textrm{ and }m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{p}}(V^{*})^{n},

x\in T
,
p\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, form generators of the ideal
I_{s}
. In view of our previous remarks on Leibniz rule for the commutator with
{\mathcal{F}}
as well as the fact that
{\mathcal{F}}
and
\rho(V)
commute we reduce our problem to verifying that

[{\mathcal{F}},\rho(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{p}})]\in\mathcal{K}(H).

A direct calculation for any
a\in HS(s)
yields

[{\mathcal{F}},\rho(a)]=\begin{pmatrix}0&G\rho_{odd}(a)-\rho_{ev}(a)G\\
G^{*}\rho_{ev}(a)-\rho_{odd}(M_{f})G^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, we only have to check that
G\rho_{odd}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{p}})-\rho_{ev}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{p}})G
is a compact operator from
H_{odd}
to
H_{ev}
.

Notice on the “odd” basis elements we have the following formulas:

\displaystyle(G\rho_{odd}(M_{f})-\rho_{ev}(M_{f})G)E_{(y,j,l)}^{odd,+}=(f(s^{l}y)-f(s^{l}\gamma(y)))E_{(y,j,l)}^{ev,+}

\displaystyle(G\rho_{odd}(M_{f})-\rho_{ev}(M_{f})G)E_{(z,k,l)}^{odd,-}=(f(s^{l}\gamma(z))-f(s^{l}z))E_{(z,k,l)}^{ev,-}.

We study the above equation for
M_{f}=m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{p}}
and the “odd/plus” basis elements. By Proposition
6.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\chi_{p}(l)(1_{(x)}(y)-1_{(x)}(\gamma(y)))E_{(y,j,l)}^{ev,+}

\displaystyle=(\delta_{y}(1_{(x)})-\delta_{\gamma(y)}(1_{(x)}))E_{(y,j,p)}^{ev,+}=e_{(y)}(1_{(x)})E_{(y,j,p)}^{ev,+}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{(x,j,p)}^{ev,+}&&\textrm{ if }y=x\textrm{ and }l=p\\
&0&&\textrm{ else.}\end{aligned}\right.

The same happens with the “odd/minus” basis elements except for a difference of a minus sign. Thus
G\rho_{odd}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{p}})-\rho_{ev}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{p}})G
is a finite rank operator and in particular, it is compact. This establishes that
(H_{ev}\oplus H_{odd},\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd},{\mathcal{F}})

is an even Fredholm module over
HS(s)
.

Finally, we need to verify that the index homomorphism for this Fredholm module coincides with
\Phi\oplus 0
. It is enough to verify that on the identity and on the other generators
m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}}
’s of
K_{0}(HS(s))
where
x\in T
, see formulas (
5.1
) and (
7.3
).

Since the operator
G:H_{odd}\to H_{ev}
is invertible it has zero index and so the Fredholm module
(H_{ev}\oplus H_{odd},\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd},{\mathcal{F}})
pairs trivially with the class of identity in
K_{0}(HS(s))
. Non-trivial pairing happens on the other generators of
K_{0}(HS(s))
as computed below.

Let the operator

B_{x}:\textrm{Ran}(\rho_{odd}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}}))\to\textrm{Ran}(\rho_{ev}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}}))

be given by

B_{x}=\rho_{ev}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}})G\rho_{odd}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}}).

We want to compute its index. We first look at its domain.

Using the definition of
\rho
on the “odd” basis elements yields

\rho_{odd}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}})E_{(y,j,l)}^{odd,+}=\delta_{y}(1_{(x)})E_{(y,j,0)}^{odd,+}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\rho_{odd}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}})E_{(z,k,l)}^{odd,-}=\delta_{\gamma(z)}(1_{(x)})E_{(z,k,0)}^{odd,-}.

Using Proposition
6.2
we see that

\delta_{y}(1_{(x)})=(e_{(y)}+e_{(\gamma(y))}+\cdots+e_{(0)})(1_{(x)})\textrm{ and }\delta_{\gamma(z)}(1_{(x)})=(e_{(\gamma(z))}+e_{(\gamma^{2}(z))}+\cdots+e_{(0)})(1_{(x)}).

Consequently, we have

\textrm{Ran}(\rho_{odd}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}}))=\textrm{span}(X_{1}\cup X_{2})

where

X_{1}=\{E_{(y,j,0)}^{odd,+}:x=\gamma^{q}(y),\textrm{ for some }q\geq 0\}\textrm{ and }X_{2}=\{E_{(z,k,0)}^{odd,-}:x=\gamma^{q}(z),\textrm{ for some }q\geq 1\}.

Calculating on the “odd” basis elements yields

B_{x}E_{(y,j,0)}^{odd,+}=\delta_{\gamma(y)}(1_{(x)})\delta_{y}(1_{(x)})E_{(y,j,0)}^{ev,+}\quad\textrm{and}\quad B_{x}E_{(z,k,0)}^{odd,-}=\delta_{z}(1_{(x)})\delta_{\gamma(z)}(1_{(x)})E_{(z,k,0)}^{ev,-}.

We can now determine the kernel of
B_{x}
on the range of
\rho_{odd}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}})
. If
x=y
, it follows that

e_{(\gamma(y))}(1_{(x)})=e_{(\gamma^{2}(y))}(1_{(x)})=\cdots=e_{(0)}(1_{(x)})=0.

On the other hand, if
x=\gamma^{q}(y)
for
q\geq 1
then

\delta_{y}(1_{(x)})\delta_{\gamma(y)}(1_{(x)})\neq 0.

Consequently we have that the kernel of
B_{x}
restricted to the range of
\rho_{odd}(m_{1_{(x)}}\mu_{\chi_{0}})
is equal to the following set:

\textrm{span}\{E_{(y,j,0)}^{odd,+}:j=1,\ldots\varphi_{x}\}.

Thus we have that

\textrm{dim}(\textrm{Ker}(B_{x}))=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\varphi_{x}&&\textrm{ if }\varphi>0\\
&0&&\textrm{ if }\varphi<0.\end{aligned}\right.

A completely analogous calculation shows that

\textrm{dim}(\textrm{Ker}(B_{x}^{*}))=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0&&\textrm{ if }\varphi>0\\
&-\varphi_{x}&&\textrm{ if }\varphi<0.\end{aligned}\right.

Putting these together shows that
\textrm{ind}(B_{x})=\varphi_{x}=\Phi(1_{(x)})
, as claimed.
∎

8.
Spectral Triples

The unbounded analogues of Fredholm modules are called spectral triples
[
14
]
.
They are important tools in noncommutative geometry. In the definition of a spectral triple, as compared to a Fredholm module, we usually suppress mentioning the Hilbert space
H
as it is implicit in the definition of a representation
\rho
. The new element is a dense
*
-subalgebra
\mathcal{A}
of a C
∗
-algebra
A
. See reference
[
13
]
for a discussion of smooth subalgebras associated to spectral triples.

8.1.
Definitions

A spectral triple for a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
A
is a triple
(\mathcal{A},\rho,\mathcal{D})
where
\rho:A\rightarrow B(H)
is a representation of
A
on a Hilbert space
H
,
\mathcal{A}
is a dense
*
-subalgebra of
A
, and
\mathcal{D}
is an unbounded self-adjoint operator in
H
satisfying:

(1) for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
\rho(a)
preserves the domain of
\mathcal{D}
,

(2) for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the commutator
[\mathcal{D},\rho(a)]
is bounded,

(3) the resolvent
(1+\mathcal{D}^{2})^{-1/2}
is a compact operator.

Moreover,
(\mathcal{A},H,\mathcal{D})
is said to be an even spectral triple if there a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-grading of
H
, such that
H=H_{ev}\oplus H_{odd}
,
\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd}
and

\mathcal{D}=\begin{bmatrix}0&D\\
D^{*}&0\end{bmatrix}.

An even spectral triple for a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
A
determines a homomorphism from
K_{0}(A)
to
{\mathbb{Z}}
. For our purpose we only need the formula for this homomorphism for the
K_{0}(A)
class of a projection
P
in the algebra
A
. As for Fredholm modules it is given by an index

P\mapsto\textrm{Index}(\rho_{ev}(P)D\rho_{odd}(P)).

8.2.
Constructions

Let
\Phi\in\Hom(C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}})
be a homomorphism given by equation (
7.2
). Also, let
H_{ev}\oplus H_{odd}
and
\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd}
be the Hilbert space and the representation from Theorem
7.1
. We use those objects to define a spectral triple over
HS(s)
corresponding to
\Phi\oplus 0
.

Consider an operator
D:\textrm{dom }D\subset H_{odd}\to H_{ev}
given on basis elements by:

DE^{{odd},+}_{(y,j,l)}=\Lambda(y,l)E^{{ev},+}_{(y,j,l)}\textrm{ and }DE^{{odd},-}_{(z,k,l)}=\Lambda(z,l)E^{{ev},-}_{(z,k,l)},

with
\Lambda(y,l)
to be determined later and
\textrm{dom }D
is the maximal domain of
D
. We need

\Lambda(y,l)\to\infty

for the resolvent
(1+\mathcal{D}^{2})^{-1/2}
to be a compact operator.

Consider first the question of boundedness of the commutators in two special cases:
[\mathcal{D},\rho(V)]
and
[\mathcal{D},\rho(M_{F})]
with
F\in C_{0}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times})
and is Lipschitz in the second coordinate. Those commutators are off diagonal with respect to the grading on
H
and the matrix elements can be explicitly computed. Indeed, we have:

(D\rho_{odd}(V)-\rho_{ev}(V)D)E^{{odd},+}_{(y,j,l)}=(\Lambda(y,l+1)-\Lambda(y,l))E^{{ev},+}_{(y,j,l+1)}

and similarly for
E^{{odd},-}_{(z,k,l)}
. It follows that

\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(V)]\|=\underset{y\in T,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}{\textrm{sup}}\,|\Lambda(y,l+1)-\Lambda(y,l)|.

(8.1)

The other commutator calculation is analogous:

(D\rho_{odd}(M_{F})-\rho_{ev}(M_{F})D)E^{{odd},+}_{(y,j,l)}=\Lambda(y,l)(F(l,y)-F(l,\gamma(y)))E^{{ev},+}_{(y,j,l)}

and similarly for
E^{{odd},-}_{(z,k,l)}
. It follows that

\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(M_{F})]\|=\underset{y\in T,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}{\textrm{sup}}\,|\Lambda(y,l)|\,|F(l,y)-F(l,\gamma(y))|\leq\underset{y\in T,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}{\textrm{sup}}\,|\Lambda(y,l)|\,\|F(l,\cdot)\|_{L}|y-\gamma(y)|_{s},

where we used Lipschitz continuity of
F
.
From the definition, if
s^{n(y)-1}\leq y<s^{n(y)}
then
\gamma(y)=y\textrm{ mod }s^{n(y)-1}
and so

|y-\gamma(y)|_{s}\leq s^{-(n(y)-1)},

which gives

\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(M_{F})]\|\leq\underset{y\in T,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}{\textrm{sup}}\,|\Lambda(y,l)|\,\|F(l,\cdot)\|_{L}\,s^{-(n(y)-1)}.

(8.2)

A simple choice of
\Lambda
so that the norms in equations (
8.1
) and (
8.2
) are finite and
\Lambda(y,l)\to\infty
is

\Lambda(y,l)=c_{1}l+c_{2}s^{n(y)}

(8.3)

for any positive constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
.

Theorem 8.1
.

With the above notation
(HS_{L}^{\infty}(s),\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd},\mathcal{D})
is an even spectral triple. The class of this spectral triple in
K
-homology

K^{0}(HS(s))\cong\Hom(C_{\{1\}}(\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{\times},{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}

\Phi\oplus 0
.

Proof.

Notice that
I_{s,L}^{\infty}
is a Fréchet algebra with respect to the following usual RD norms

\|a\|_{N}:=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}},\,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}(1+|m|+|n|)^{N}\,\|F_{n}(m,\cdot)\|_{L}.

where
a
is given by the series from equation (
4.1
). Similarly,

is a Fréchet space with norms

\|\phi\|_{N}:=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(1+|m|)^{N}\,|\phi_{m}|.

where
\phi_{m}
are Fourier coefficients of
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
. We say that an element of
HS_{L}^{\infty}(s)
is
polynomial
if it is given by a finite sum in formulas (
4.1
) and (
4.2
). Notice that the polynomial elements of
HS_{L}^{\infty}(s)
form a dense subalgebra.

By the definition of the maximal domain of
D
we have that

\displaystyle\textrm{dom }D=\{\Omega\in H_{odd}:\|D\Omega\|^{2}<\infty\}=

\displaystyle\left\{\Omega=\sum_{y,j,l}\omega_{(y,j,l)}^{+}E_{(y,j,l)}^{odd,+}+\sum_{z,k,l}\omega_{(z,k,l)}^{-}E_{(z,k,l)}^{odd,-}:\sum_{y,j,l}\Lambda^{2}(y,l)|\omega_{(y,j,l)}^{+}|^{2}+\sum_{z,k,l}\Lambda^{2}(z,l)|\omega_{(z,k,l)}^{-}|^{2}<\infty\right\}

and similarly for the adjoint.
\textrm{dom }D
is a Hilbert space with the inner product given by
\|D\Omega\|^{2}
in the above expression. Notice that finite linear combinations of the basis elements form a dense subspace in
\textrm{dom }D
.

We need to verify that
\rho(a)
preserves the domain of
\mathcal{D}
and
[\mathcal{D},\rho(a)]
is bounded for all
a\in HS_{L}^{\infty}(s)
. Notice that the polynomial elements of
HS_{L}^{\infty}(s)
preserve the dense subspace of finite linear combinations of the basis elements. Since we have

\|\mathcal{D}\rho(a)\Omega\|\leq\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(a)]\Omega\|+\|\rho(a)\mathcal{D}\Omega\|\leq\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(a)]\|\|\Omega\|+\|a\|\|\mathcal{D}\Omega\|,

we see that estimates on
[\mathcal{D},\rho(a)]
in terms of Fréchet norms of
a
would imply that
\rho(a)
preserves the domain of
\mathcal{D}
by an approximation argument.

For
\Lambda
given by formula (
8.3
) we have by equation (
8.1
):

\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(V)]\|\leq c_{1}

which implies that

\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(V^{m})]\|\leq c_{1}|m|

for every
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Consequently, for
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
and the Toeplitz operator
T(\phi)
given by equation (
4.2
) we get

\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(T(\phi))]\|\leq c_{1}\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|m|\,|\phi_{m}|\leq c_{1}\|\phi\|_{1}.

Inserting
\Lambda
from formula (
8.3
) into inequality (
8.2
) we can estimate as follows:

\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(M_{F})]\|\leq\underset{l\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}{\textrm{sup}}\,s(c_{1}l+c_{2})\|F(l,\cdot)\|_{L}\leq\sum_{l\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}s(c_{1}l+c_{2})\|F(l,\cdot)\|_{L}.

Consequently, for
a\in I_{s}
given by equation (
4.1
) we obtain

\|[\mathcal{D},\rho(a)]\|\leq\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}},\,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}(s(c_{1}l+c_{2})+c_{1}|n|)\,\|F_{n}(l,\cdot)\|_{L}\leq\textrm{const}\|a\|_{1}.

By the previous remarks, those estimates prove that
(HS_{L}^{\infty}(s),\rho_{ev}\oplus\rho_{odd},\mathcal{D})
is an even spectral triple.

Finally, the index calculation for the spectral triple is completely analogous to the index calculation for the Fredholm module in the proof of Theorem
7.1
as the extra non-zero
\Lambda(y,l)
factor does not change the kernel and cokernel of the corresponding operators.
