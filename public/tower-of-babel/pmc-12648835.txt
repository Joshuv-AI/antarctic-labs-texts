Background

Endophthalmitis is a clinical condition which involves inflammation affecting the inner coats of the eye as a result of intraocular colonisation by infectious agents with involvement of the vitreous humour as a key clinical implication [1]. Its clinical presentation can be subdivided into endogenous and exogenous subtypes. The endogenous form involves the spread of infection from a primary site in the body to the eye via the bloodstream. The primary infection sites include: urinary tract, lower respiratory tract, skin and organ abscesses. Diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression and intravenous drug use are noteworthy clinical associations.

Endophthalmitis is recognised as a vision-threatening condition. Prompt diagnosis and timely intervention is fundamental in preventing permanent vision loss. Its presentation, diagnosis and management are of critical importance in the realisation of optimal clinical outcomes. Fungal presentations are rare compared with their bacterial counterparts, and there is a dearth of level 1 evidence on the topic of fungal causes. This review serves to update the literature on the initial diagnosis and management of this sight-threatening condition.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old gentleman was referred to our ophthalmology service with persistent eye pain and reduced visual acuity affecting the left eye in the aftermath of a recent history of trauma to this eye involving a barbed wire. This injury was a penetrating injury with a self-sealing wound which occurred two months previously. His medical history was significant for primary hypertension which was managed on lercanidipine 10 mg once daily orally. Ocular history was notable for previous right-sided cataract surgery and cystoid macular oedema affecting the left eye.

He had received appropriate treatment for uveitis in the immediate aftermath of his monocular traumatic injury. This involved topical steroid prednisolone 1% and cycloplegic cyclopentolate 1% therapy for a four- week period. Clinical examination of the anterior segment revealed left conjunctival injection, resolved anterior uveitis with no visible hypopyon. Posterior view revealed an absence of visible lens artefact, ghost vessels in the left fundus along with clumps in the vitreous on the left side. At this point, clinical suspicion for exogenous fungal endophthalmitis was the impression.

The initial uveitis had subsided on referral to our department. The presenting visual acuity in the left eye was 6/36 unaided. Pinhole improvement optimised this to 6/30 and normal intraocular pressure was apparent following assessment. An ultrasound B-scan was performed owing to hazy posterior segment view. B scan (Ultrasound of the eyeball) and CT orbit excluded the presence of an intraocular foreign body. Blood results were unremarkable for Full Blood Count, ESR, ANA, Lyme serology, Toxoplasmosis, syphilis and bartonella. The patient then underwent an anterior chamber tap and inject using vancomycin 1% and ceftazidime 5% and Amphotericin B. The antifungal agent was introduced owing to clinical suspicion for fungal pathology owing to occupational exposure and suggestive features on widefield Optos imaging.

The recent clinical history involving a barbed wire influenced the decision to include voriconazole. Figure 1 shows an image which illustrates the colony formation in the macula adjacent to the optic disc suggestive of fungal aetiology. Figure 2 shown below illustrates hypo-fluorescence of the left macula. We commenced a course of oral voriconazole and arranged a follow-up review one week thereafter. At the follow-up review, a clinical decision to admit the patient and perform a core vitrectomy was made. A diagnostic vitrectomy was performed - under local anaesthetic - and vitreous samples were extracted. The vitreous culture revealed the rare Rhizomucor species as the causative organism. Susceptibility testing proved sensitivity to the Amphotericin B pharmaceutical.

Discussion and conclusion

This case report details a rare case of Rhizomucor miehei endophthalmitis, in the setting of sustained corneal trauma. The patient was initially treated for acute anterior uveitis with the aid of topical steroids and cycloplegic therapy. The subsequent management plan involved an intravitreal combination therapy of ceftazidime 5% and vancomycin 1% in addition to the oral anti-fungal voriconazole. Broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy was clinically justified prior to the release of actual culture results and this methodology found its place in this case. The clinical decision to proceed to diagnostic vitrectomy was undertaken alongside anti-fungal intravitreal therapy. The magnitude of the infection coupled with the substantial decrease in visual acuity and associated occupational exposure justified the decision to move towards vitrectomy intervention. A vitreous free sample was harvested from the left eye and subsequently cultured Rhizomucor miehei. The resulting susceptibility report highlighted vulnerability to Amphotericin B, posaconazole and isavuconazole. The patient was closely followed up in clinic after with visual acuity improving to 6/30 at the four-week follow-up.

The procedure of vitrectomy provides distinct advantages over conservative medical therapy in the definitive management of endophthalmitis. Specifically, it reduces the inflammatory load within the eye, provides the extraction of potentially diagnostic specimens, enables direct inspection of the retina, permits definitive treatment and expedites visual rehabilitation [2]. There are two large-scale studies that convey rather contrasting endpoints in this context. The Early Vitrectomy Study (EVS) published in 1995 advocates for vitrectomy in severe cases, detailing those with visual acuity ‘perception of light’ at initial presentation as opposed to vitreous tap and intravitreal antibiotic therapy [3]. In this study, a total of 420 patients who had endophthalmitis diagnosed within 6 weeks of cataract surgery were investigated. A 2 × 2 factorial design was created with cohorts randomised to either vitrectomy or systemic antibiotic treatment groups [3]. The findings revealed no difference in final visual acuity or media clarity with or without the use of systemic antibiotics.

In contrast to the EVS findings, the complete and early vitrectomy for endophthalmitis (CEVE) study in 2020 encourages complete vitrectomy including posterior vitreous detachment induction in order to prevent exudates settling over the macula [4]. However, this approach comes with an iatrogenic break formation risk. Clinical features and microbiological factors were retrospectively reviewed in 62 patients who were treated for acute post-cataract surgery endophthalmitis which occurred within three weeks of cataract surgery in Alabama [4]. Complete and early vitrectomy is a safe and effective initial treatment for acute cataract post endophthalmitis. When accompanied by systemic antibiotics and retreatment of recurrent media opacification, it improves recovery of 20/40 or better visual acuity by approximately 50% compared to a predominantly tap-and-inject treatment paradigm [4]. CEVE is recommended for fundus-obscuring endophthalmitis cases whenever the view is inadequate to rule out significant macular concern.

Antibiotics remain at the forefront of therapeutics in exogenous endophthalmitis with specific attention drawn towards fourth generation fluoroquinolones [5]. In this case, the broad spectrum combination of vancomycin and ceftazidime were chosen as the empirical treatment of choice. Vancomycin is a glycopeptide antibiotic active against gram positive bacterial species works via irreversible binding to D-alanyl-D-alanine moieties of NAM and NAG peptides resulting in bacterial cell wall synthesis inhibition [6]. Ceftazidime is a third generation cephalosporin which delivers proven activity against gram negative ocular isolates. While the threat of antibiotic resistance remains, structural alterations within the cephalosporin class have shown superior efficacy against gram negative bacteria [7].

The clinical suspicion for fungal pathology prompted the timely introduction of voriconazole. Voriconazole is understood as a triazole antifungal agent responsible for broad-spectrum activity against Aspergillus, Candida and a variety of other fungal species. It holds 96% bioavailability following oral administration and reaches peak concentration 2 to 3 h post-administration [8]. There is a necessity to monitor drug blood levels while on this medication with regular attention directed towards liver function tests, in view of recognised toxicity.

Corticosteroids represent a pharmaceutical class of immediate anti-inflammatory actors, and an inevitable ingredient in the treatment of endophthalmitis. Nonetheless, they remain an unsuitable modality in fungal cases, as they enhance fungal growth secondary immunosuppression mechanisms. The role of intravitreal steroids remains contentious with safety demonstrated with dexamethasone therapy, but studies showing negligible therapeutic effect [9].

Rhizomucor miehei is a thermophilic, widely distributed saprophytic mould and its enzymes are exploited as biocatalysts in the biotechnology zeitgeist [10]. It is rarely documented as a human pathogen and is better recognised as a cause of mastitis and bovine mycotic abortion within Veterinary Medicine [11]. The incidence of mucormycosis is rising – in global terms – and most especially in immunocompromised individuals [12]. An accurate diagnosis is elusive to many clinicians, owing to its subtle and sometimes misleading clinical presentation. The literature reports that treatment modalities for this condition are limited to agents such as posaconazole, isavuconazole and time-aged Amphotericin B [13].

Exogenous fungal endophthalmitis cases are uncommon are typically associated with trauma, eye surgery and infectious keratitis [14]. Aspergillus represent the most common causative agent followed by the Candida species [15]. Research in the USA has proven the uncommon nature of fungal keratitis with reports proving < 2% of all microbial keratitis cases [16]. Topical steroid use, corneal laceration suturing, size of corneal ulcer and hypopyon presence are the biggest risk factors for developing endophthalmitis with Fusarium and Aspergillosis the most commonly reported [17]. Our case of Rhizomucor endophthalmitis lies in contrast to those typically reported in the literature. Our patient reported left visual acuity 6/30 unaided at the most recent follow-up and responded successfully to Amphotericin B therapy. Here, we describe the culture of Rhizomucor miehei isolate from a vitreous sample, the first of its kind in the literature as a cause of exogenous fungal endophthalmitis.

Learning points

Fungal endophthalmitis is a rare and diagnostically challenging presentation. Prompt empirical therapy is essential in the management of endophthalmitis, given the propensity for sight-altering sequalae in affected patients. Fungal endophthalmitis – although often unexpected - should be considered in cases of traumatic eye injuries.
