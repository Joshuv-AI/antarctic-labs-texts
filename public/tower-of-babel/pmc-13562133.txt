Digital Health in Obesity Care: Current Evidence, Challenges, and Future Directions
Authors: Sherry Pagoto, Richard Bannor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewThe purpose of this review is to highlight areas in which digital technologies including mobile apps, wearables, telehealth, and AI have improved obesity care, where gaps remain, and future directions.Recent FindingsTechnology is being used in myriad ways to support lifestyle, pharmacological, and surgical obesity care. Mobile apps and wearables reduce the burden of behavior change strategies (e.g., self-monitoring) and telehealth eliminates numerous barriers to in-person obesity care. However, low engagement is a persistent problem with digitally-delivered interventions. An emerging area is the use of AI in obesity care to glean personalized insights from data collected by wearables and mobile apps and to deliver lifestyle coaching.SummaryTechnology increases access to obesity care, facilitates personalized care, and reduces burden. Future research should examine ways to improve engagement, to design adaptive interventions that are responsive to patients’ evolving care needs, and to facilitate implementation in healthcare settings.

Introduction

Obesity prevalence has risen from 23% in 1988 to 42.5% in 2017 before leveling off for nearly 10 years [1]. During that period, myriad technologies have emerged to facilitate obesity care including telephone counseling in the 1990’s [2], internet-delivered lifestyle interventions in the 2000’s [3], smartphones and wearables in the 2010’s [4, 5], and telehealth [6] and AI in the 2020’s [7, 8]. Although the rise of obesity co-occurred with the rise of these technologies, before 2010 few technologies for obesity were widely available and those that were mainly served as self-monitoring devices [9]. To have population impact, technologies must increase access to evidence-based care, be scalable, affordable, and integrated into the healthcare system. Between 2015 and 2022, commercial digital lifestyle interventions became widely available [10] and telehealth reimbursement rules were relaxed [11]. This increased access to lifestyle interventions and reduced barriers to pre- and post-metabolic and bariatric surgical (MBS) care. In 2021, a major breakthrough occurred in obesity care: the FDA approved semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist medication for obesity [12]. Shortly thereafter, the demand for anti-obesity medication (AOM) exploded and digital platforms to support medication users proliferated. Obesity care may be on the precipice of another major breakthrough with the emergence of generative artificial intelligence (AI) in 2023. AI has myriad applications in obesity care, from AI-assisted mobile apps and wearables to AI chatbots. Technology is rapidly transforming lifestyle, pharmacological, and surgical obesity care. This review highlights progress, challenges, psychosocial outcomes (e.g., quality of life, mood, weight stigma), research gaps, and future directions for digital health in obesity care.

Mobile Apps

In 2008, the first diet tracking app (Lose It) debuted in the Apple Store [13] and shortly thereafter dozens followed. Diet tracking apps were the first health-related apps to appear in the market with early versions providing free and convenient ways for users to track diet, physical activity, and weight [14]. Diet tracking apps are popular in the marketplace with over 3.6 billion downloads in 2024 [15] and a global market that is worth $4.6 billion and expected to double by 2034 [16]. Diet tracking apps have largely replaced traditional forms of calorie tracking and many now charge users for advanced features such as nutrition and meal planning, coaching, barcode scanners, ad-free browsing, workout plans, photo-based meal recognition, and AI tools [17]. Mobile apps are used to support self-guided weight management attempts, lifestyle interventions and pharmacological and surgical obesity care.

Mobile Apps in Lifestyle Interventions

Although early trials revealed that diet tracking apps were not efficacious in producing clinically significant weight loss as standalone interventions [4], they have become a staple of evidence-based lifestyle interventions. In fact, numerous studies have shown that diet tracking is the strongest predictor of weight loss in lifestyle interventions [18]. Unfortunately, diet tracking adherence is generally low [19–21]. For example, in a lifestyle intervention trial only 68% of participants tracked ≥ 6 days per week during the first week and this fell to 21% by the end of 3 months [20]. Consumers who download diet tracking apps from the marketplace have even lower adherence, with estimates of only 32% using the app for a week, 14% for a month, 7% for 3 months, and 2% for one year [22]. Studies have tested lower burden diet tracking apps that involve taking photos of meals [23, 24] or tracking meal patterns [25], meal size [26], specific eating habits [27], or traffic light foods [28], but none have outperformed conventional diet tracking apps in adherence or weight loss outcomes. One study showed that prescribing occasional breaks from diet tracking apps and using that time to coach users on how to apply what they learned from tracking resulted in higher adherence and possibly more weight loss [29]. Future research is needed to determine the diet tracking app prescription that maximizes adherence and weight loss.

Some commercial weight loss apps provide comprehensive lifestyle interventions with notable examples including Weight Watchers [30] and Noom [31]. Efficacy data for these apps is growing. In randomized trials, the Weight Watchers app was shown to outperform standard guidance (41% vs. 20%) [32] and an online newsletter at 1 year (26% vs. 13%) in percentage of participants losing ≥ 5% weight at 6 months [33]. The Noom app was shown to outperform an education control group with 23% vs. 12% of participants losing ≥ 5% weight at 4 months, respectively [34]. The only trial that reported app engagement found that participants reported using the Weight Watchers app on average “never to a few times per month” at 3 months [33]. Although weight loss outcomes are modest and engagement appears to wane by 3 months, mobile apps have provided many millions of people access to lifestyle interventions [35]. Future research should examine factors that predict disengagement and test adaptive interventions that provide strategies to prevent disengagement before it occurs [36].

Mobile Apps to Support Anti-Obesity Medication (AOM)

Many weight loss apps now include features for patients using AOMs. Weight Watchers [37], Noom [38], MyNetDiary [39], and MyFitnessPal [40] include features such as protein trackers, side effect and water tracking, meal plans tailored for suppressed appetites and GI side effects, community forums, and custom macronutrient targets. Weight Watchers and Noom also offer specialized coaching for AOM users. New mobile apps designed specifically for AOM users have also emerged (e.g., Shotsy [41], MeAgain [42], GLP1 Tracker [43]) and they provide shot day reminders and tools for tracking medication adherence and protein intake, mapping injection site rotations, managing side effects, and estimating medication levels in the blood. These apps have been rapidly taken up by AOM users with Shotsy, launched in 2024, reporting over 1 million downloads [41] and MeAgain [42], launched in 2025 reporting > 421 K users. Research is needed to examine whether mobile apps improve medication adherence, protein intake, hydration, and medication tolerance in AOM users, and reduce medication discontinuation. How best to support patients using AOMs with mobile technology is an important area for future research given that AOM discontinuation rates are high [44] and discontinuation can lead to loss of health benefits [45].

Mobile Apps in Metabolic and Bariatric Surgical (MBS) Care

In randomized trials, mobile apps have been tested for their impact on pre-surgical preparation completion [46], nutrition adherence [47], complications [48], physical activity [49], weight loss [48], readmission rates, and length of stay [50]. Although mobile apps can provide in-the-pocket support to patients during all phases of MBS care, randomized trials have not found evidence that the mobile apps tested have improved most of these outcomes. Two trials found effects of mobile apps on post-surgery physical activity in MBS patients but neither revealed effects on weight loss [47, 49]. A third trial provided evidence that app engagement is associated with better outcomes [46]. In that trial, pre-surgical patients were randomized to a mobile app that provided pre-operative support including checklists, appointment reminders, and automated alerts for missed appointments or standard care. While no differences were found in pre-operative preparation completion, high app engagers had higher rates of completion compared to low app engagers (76% vs. 20%) [46]. Future research should examine barriers to MBS patient engagement in mobile apps throughout the care continuum, the ideal timing to introduce mobile apps during MBS care, and app features most likely to meet patient needs and improve MBS outcomes.

Wearable Devices

Wearables are devices worn on the body (e.g., smartwatches, rings, sensors, body cameras, and eyeglasses) to track physical activity, sleep, and/or physiological metrics [51]. A survey on wearable use in 2025 found that nearly half of US adults (46%) owned a wearable device, with smartwatches accounting for the vast majority [52]. Wearable use rates were high with 83% of users wearing their device ≥ 5 times per week. The survey also found high rates of long-term use with 23% reporting to have used a wearable for 5 + years, 24% for 3–5 years, 28% for 1–3 years, and 23% for less than 1 year [52]. Passive tracking via wearables seems to attract more engagement than mobile apps, the latter of which put the burden on users to manually input data. Survey respondents also revealed that the most common metrics tracked were physical activity (35%), sleep (26%), and heart rate (21%) [52]. Wearable use was also found to be associated with high rates of use of other digital health technologies, such that 74% of wearable owners reported to have used health apps and 44% reported to have used AI health chatbots. This suggests that wearable users are likely highly motivated to learn about and improve their health (i.e., “worried well”) and may not be representative of patients with chronic disease. Although wearables have great potential to support clinical care, 70% of users acquire their devices outside of healthcare settings, which is likely because wearables are not routinely integrated into obesity care. In the research literature, wearables have been examined in the context of lifestyle interventions to increase physical activity and weight loss.

The most common wearables used in lifestyle interventions are physical activity trackers but whether they improve weight loss in populations with obesity remains an open question. While a systematic review revealed that wearable-supported physical activity interventions are efficacious at increasing physical activity [53], few comprehensive lifestyle interventions have been designed to evaluate whether wearables improve weight loss outcomes. A landmark trial in 2016 compared lifestyle interventions with and without a physical activity wearable and found that the group that received wearables lost less weight at 2 years [5]. The investigators raised questions about whether real-time physical activity feedback might make physically active participants compensate by increasing caloric intake and/or might make less physically active participants feel discouraged and anxious [54]. Systematic reviews reveal meager evidence for wearables on weight loss outcomes. One systematic review of wearable interventions for adults with cardiometabolic conditions revealed that wearables significantly increased physical activity, especially when combined with coaching, however, no effects were found on weight [55]. Similarly, a 2026 systematic review of randomized trials comparing wearable interventions to control conditions in adults and youth with overweight or obesity revealed that wearables produced significant effects on weight in youth but not adults, but they did produce modest effects on waist circumference in adults [56]. These findings are consistent with a systematic review of wearables on long-term weight loss outcomes in lifestyle intervention trials which found no differences between wearable and comparator interventions in long-term weight loss [51]. Wearable use may have adverse effects including discouragement, anxiety, fatigue, compulsive use, and desensitization of feedback. In fact, the term “techno-hypochondria” was coined in 2026 to provide a conceptual framework for the negative impacts of wearables [57]. Assistive AI systems that personalize, contextualize, and convert wearable data into actionable insights may have the potential to maximize the benefits of wearables while minimizing adverse effects [58]. Future research is needed to test intervention strategies, including AI systems, that increase the impact of wearables on both physical activity and weight loss.

Telehealth

Telehealth refers to clinic visits performed remotely via the telephone or videoconference software. Telehealth coverage was expanded during the pandemic such that Medicare [59] and most state Medicaid programs [60] now cover telehealth obesity care. Telehealth removes many common barriers to obesity care including transportation, lack of local providers, caregiving responsibilities, and impaired mobility [61]. In obesity care, telehealth has become increasingly popular for the delivery of lifestyle interventions, pre- and post-surgical visits in MBS clinics, and AOM management.

Telehealth in Lifestyle Interventions

Telehealth delivery of lifestyle interventions reduces the burden of this care model which involves numerous visits over the course of 6 months or longer [62]. The CDC National Diabetes Prevention Program currently recognizes 1,483 telehealth lifestyle interventions in the US [63]. Despite the advantages, studies comparing videoconference-delivered lifestyle interventions to in-person versions find no differences in attendance or outcomes. In one non-randomized study of 43 military family members, no differences were found in weight loss between participants enrolled in a 12-week videoconference lifestyle intervention compared to those enrolled in an in-person version, and attendance was high (90%) but equivalent in both groups which suggests this sample likely had few barriers to in-person care [64]. Another randomized trial recruited 150 low-income adults from diverse backgrounds (65% African American) from federally qualified health centers, randomized them to videoconference or in-person lifestyle interventions, and found no differences in attendance between groups [6]. Further, neither group outperformed enhanced usual care (i.e., access to diet and exercise coaching at the community center) in weight loss, which suggests that videoconference delivery did not address the unique barriers to participation in this underserved population. Neither of these trials was powered for non-inferiority so findings may have been due to lack of power. In a non-randomized trial that was powered for non-inferiority, weight loss outcomes from a sample of 160 predominantly white women who received a videoconference lifestyle intervention were compared to outcomes from gold standard in-person lifestyle intervention (i.e., 8% weight loss) [65]. Videoconference participants lost 7% of their weight and non-inferiority was confirmed. Participants attended on average 78% of videoconference visits. The literature is still lacking trials that provide head-to-head comparisons of videoconference and in-person lifestyle interventions using a randomized design and powered for non-inferiority. Thus far it appears that videoconference delivery may not improve upon outcomes or attendance, especially for marginalized populations who may have barriers that extend beyond the ability to attend groups. Asynchronously-delivered lifestyle interventions may be particularly convenient for marginalized populations by allowing people to participate in online discussions and modules anytime [66]. Several studies have examined asynchronous lifestyle interventions using private groups on social media platforms [67–70] including one trial that compared this approach to in-person delivery. Findings revealed that while the in-person intervention outperformed the asynchronous intervention at 6-months, no differences were observed by 12 months but criteria for non-inferiority were not met [71]. The cost of treatment delivery was 18% less in the asynchronous condition and a greater percentage of asynchronous participants rated their treatment condition as convenient. Further research is needed on the efficacy of asynchronous care in marginalized populations, however even the most convenient intervention delivery modality may not overcome community-level and systemic barriers to healthy diet (e.g., food swamps) and physical activity (e.g., neighborhood walkability) which are key drivers of poor weight loss outcomes in marginalized populations.

Telehealth and Anti-Obesity Medication

A joint advisory by four obesity and nutrition professional organizations emphasized the need for telehealth-delivered lifestyle support for patients on AOMs [72]. The global market for AOMs is valued at over $100 billion and is expected to exceed $200 billion by 2030 [73]. Following the approval of semaglutide for obesity in 2021, direct-to-consumer telehealth companies proliferated to meet the rapidly rising demand. In 2025, Novo Nordisk, the manufacturer of semaglutide, launched an online pharmacy, enabling several telehealth weight management companies to provide direct-to-patient home shipments of semaglutide to further meet the demand [74]. Many commercial platforms provide digital lifestyle support to users through virtual meetings, mobile apps, and remote coaching. Studies show less than optimal engagement in such programs but that high engagers tend to lose more weight. One study examined participation in 4 virtual lifestyle support sessions for AOM patients and found that only 37% attended ≥ 3 sessions [75]. Even less engagement was observed in a cohort study of > 100,000 patients prescribed AOMs in a national digital weight loss service in the UK that provides coaching, a mobile app, and an AI assistant [76, 77]. They found that only ~ 6% of patients were considered “digitally engaged.” While engagement was low, engagers lost 26% more weight than non-engagers. More research is needed to examine ways to improve engagement in telehealth lifestyle support programs for AOM users. Given that 50% of patients initiating an AOM discontinue within 1 year [77], lifestyle interventions provided following discontinuation are greatly needed. Discontinuation may be a motivational window of opportunity in AOM users who are likely to be fearful of weight regain. Further research is needed to determine the optimal timing for telehealth-delivered lifestyle support for AOM users.

Telehealth in Metabolic and Bariatric Surgical Care

MBS care involves numerous visits over the course of a year or more with physicians, surgeons, dietitians, and psychologists. This puts a substantial burden on patients, especially those who live far from a MBS center. Not surprisingly, only 1% of patients who are candidates for MBS surgery receives it [78], likely in part due to the time commitment involved in care. Telehealth services can greatly reduce patient burden by eliminating travel for pre-operative assessments, nutrition and behavioral counseling, and post-operative visits. One review of telehealth in MBS surgery patients from marginalized backgrounds found that telehealth increased visit show rates, reduced pre-surgery drop-out, and resulted in greater improvements in diet and physical activity [79]. In terms of safety, a cohort study compared MBS patients whose preoperative care was entirely delivered via telehealth to those who received traditional in-person care and found no differences in major adverse events, length of stay, hospital readmission, and ER visits [80]. A systematic review found that telehealth MBS programs also appear to produce similar weight loss outcomes as traditional care [81]. Increasing the availability of telehealth MBS care appears to increase access to care while producing the same if not better outcomes as traditional care.

Artificial Intelligence

AI chatbots are conversational agents powered by large language models that use natural language processing to generate human-like responses to queries and perform complex tasks such as information synthesis, problem solving, prediction, and decision support [82]. ChatGPT, the first widely available generative AI chatbot was released in 2023. Since then, AI use in daily life quickly became ubiquitous. AI chatbots have proliferated for both general and health use, with applications across myriad health conditions including obesity. A 2025 survey found that 25% of US adults reported having used an AI chatbot for health advice and the most common health topics were nutrition and exercise, with 59% of health advice-seeking users seeking advice on these topics [83]. AI has myriad applications in obesity care, including patient education, measurement and prediction of diet and physical activity, and intervention delivery.

AI Chatbots for Patient Education

Given that the internet is the most common source patients turn to for weight management and nutrition information [84], high rates of AI chatbot use for this purpose are not surprising. A systematic review found that studies on AI chatbot outputs about obesity, AOMs, MBS, and lifestyle interventions are generally highly accurate [85]. The review also reported evidence that AI chatbot outputs provide guideline-adherent recommendations. However, AI chatbot outputs were less accurate in the context of complex medical profiles. Research is needed to examine how people act upon information they gather from AI chatbots about obesity treatments and whether chatbot use leads to lifestyle changes, improved medication adherence, and/or greater interest in obesity care.

AI in Measurement and Prediction of Diet and Physical Activity

AI can process data collected from wearables and mobile apps to assess and predict contextual factors affecting dietary choices and physical activity. A longstanding challenge in the obesity literature has been the development of dietary measures that are accurate, inexpensive, and low burden. Traditional methods (e.g., food frequency measures, 24-hour recalls) are time-consuming to implement and suffer from recall and underreporting biases [86]. AI-powered dietary assessments use computer vision for image recognition and deep learning, natural language processing, and machine learning to estimate dietary intake [87]. A systematic review of AI-powered dietary assessments revealed comparable or higher accuracy in food identification and portion size quantification compared to food frequency measures, 3-day food diaries, and 24-hour dietary recalls [88]. A recent study compared an AI-powered image-based dietary assessment app to doubly labeled water, the most accurate benchmark for energy intake in adult women with obesity in free living conditions [89]. Findings revealed that the AI app underestimated energy intake by 25% or on average about 817 calories per day due to variable photo quality, food misclassification, and algorithm portion size estimate errors. Studies are needed to sharpen the accuracy of AI dietary assessments and to examine their utility in both epidemiological studies and lifestyle interventions.

AI has also been used to improve the utility of wearable physical activity data. Wearables use accelerometers, gyroscopes, heart rate monitors, and GPS to estimate physical activity duration, intensity, and energy expenditure; however, such data on its own does not provide insights about determinants and predictors of physical activity. The type and duration of physical activity and its relationship with physiological, psychological, and environmental variables vary throughout a 24-hour period and while all of these factors can now be measured via wearable technology, more complex analytic methods are needed to glean insights from this complex data. Machine learning is being applied to wearable data to generate actionable insights as it has the ability to classify patterns, identify clusters, and regress outcomes from a multitude of variables to identify their independent contributions [90]. Machine learning is now being used to identify relationships between step counts, physical activity duration, intensity, heart rate variability, sleep duration and quality, and contextual factors [90]. For example, the wearable device WHOOP uses AI to analyze sleep, heart rate variability, resting heart rate, stress, and activity data to predict sleep need, physical recovery status, and readiness for physical activity [91]. Clinical trials are needed to test whether insights produced by AI models can increase physical activity in patients with obesity.

Lifestyle Coaching AI Chatbots

AI chatbots have been developed to provide lifestyle coaching that is adherent to evidence-based protocols and provides users 24/7 personalized support at lower cost than human coaching. Lifestyle coaching chatbots have proliferated in the commercial market (e.g., Noom Welli, Lark Health, Vida Health, and Livongo) including some that specifically support AOM patients (e.g., Noom Med, WeightWatchers Clinic, Ro). A 2025 systematic review of 7 randomized trials of AI chatbots for lifestyle coaching revealed inconclusive results for efficacy but high acceptability among users [92]. Results should not be generalized beyond the AI chatbots used in those trials given how frequently AI models are updated. A landmark 2026 randomized controlled non-inferiority trial compared referrals to an AI chatbot designed to deliver the Diabetes Prevention Program lifestyle intervention asynchronously or a human-delivered version via videoconference-delivered visits and found comparable rates of clinically significant weight loss, physical activity, and HbA1c at 1 year in participants with overweight or obesity and prediabetes [8]. The AI chatbot had higher uptake rates than the human-delivered version (93.4% vs. 82.7%) which provides additional evidence for asynchronous lifestyle interventions. Observational studies of users of commercial AI lifestyle chatbots also provide important insights into their potential. A retrospective analysis of 3,933 patients with pre-diabetes who enrolled in Lark Health’s Diabetes Prevention Program AI chatbot found that while only 10% met a threshold of minimal engagement, 40% of those who completed ≥ 4 educational lessons lost ≥ 5% of their baseline weight at 1 year [93]. While uptake was low, greater engagement was associated with better outcomes. An important question for future research is how to improve uptake and engagement in AI lifestyle chatbots, particularly among marginalized populations, as this would enhance the impact of such a cost-effective and scalable intervention approach.

Psychosocial Impacts of Digital Interventions for Obesity

Traditional lifestyle interventions improve psychosocial outcomes such as quality of life [94], depression [95], anxiety, and stress [96], but less is known about the impact of digital interventions for obesity on psychosocial outcomes given the dearth of trials that measure these outcomes. In trials that measure psychosocial outcomes, findings are mixed and effects appear to depend on the target population and the type of intervention. For example, in a systematic review of 38 randomized trials of digital obesity interventions in which at least one psychosocial outcome was measured, most did not find effects on those outcomes [97]. However, trials of samples with depression and/or binge eating disorder found effects on depression [98, 99] and eating disorder symptoms [100, 101], respectively. The systematic review also found that a trial of a digital lifestyle intervention tailored for men found effects on quality of life [102], a trial testing digital acceptance and commitment therapy found effects on weight self-stigma and mental health [103], a trial of online exercise classes found effects on quality of life [104], and a trial of a digital mindfulness intervention found effects on perceived stress but not weight loss [105]. The strongest evidence for impacts of digital lifestyle interventions on psychosocial outcomes comes from two trials of Weight Watchers’ digital program which revealed significant impacts on a host of psychosocial outcomes including quality of life, well-being [106], weight-bias internalization, self-compassion [107], sleep quality, hunger, and food craving [106]. A third trial revealed effects of the Weight Watchers virtual behavioral program for incretin therapy users on quality of life, well-being, weight-bias internalization, depression, perceived stress, and self-compassion [107]. Trials of digital obesity interventions that have improved psychosocial outcomes typically involve human coaching and may not generalize to stand alone digital interventions (e.g., mobile apps). Indeed, a systematic review of 7 digital lifestyle interventions with no human coaching found no effects on quality of life [108]. As discussed elsewhere, some commercial mobile apps for weight management have been found to include stigmatizing language and imagery [109] which could have negative impacts on psychosocial outcomes such internalized weight stigma. Because generative AI has been found to exhibit bias toward stigmatized medical conditions [110], studies are needed to examine the presence of weight bias in AI chatbots. A systematic review of outcomes of AI coaching platforms for obesity reported no psychosocial outcomes, which leaves a significant gap in the literature [111]. Psychosocial outcomes should be routinely measured in digital obesity interventions, especially those that do not include human coaching.

Conclusion

Technology has changed how obesity care is delivered and although it has great potential to increase the reach of care, thus far, it has not increased the efficacy of lifestyle, pharmacological, or surgical treatments. Mobile apps and wearables are among the most studied but their impact has been limited by low engagement which could be due to myriad factors including low perceived value or relevance, poor design features, lack of human support, user burden, privacy concerns, digital literacy, inadequate use or deployment of evidence-based behavioral strategies, and possibly unintended negative effects on psychosocial factors (e.g., weight stigma). Engagement is particularly low in digital platforms for AOM users which raises questions about the appropriate intervention targets, timing, and outcomes. Given the high AOM discontinuation rates, digital support that prevents medication discontinuation and facilitates weight loss maintenance should be a priority. Future research should conceptualize engagement as a dynamic process, incorporate frequent assessment of engagement to identify who disengages, when and why, and develop and test adaptive interventions that flag and address declining engagement as it occurs [36]. Such work could leverage data collected by mobile apps and wearables (e.g., weight, body composition, diet, physical activity, sleep, and environmental conditions) to inform predictive models and intervention development.

AI has the potential to advance obesity care by integrating data from disparate sources (e.g., mobile apps, wearables, electronic health records) to produce personalized, adaptive interventions according to phenotypes, genotypes, treatment response, and patient preferences. AI-powered systems have the potential to predict disengagement and treatment response to more efficiently move patients with obesity through the care continuum based on their evolving needs. Although AI can be used to develop highly personalized interventions, personalization may not guarantee better engagement or outcomes. Head-to-head trials are needed to examine what type of personalization, if any, improves engagement and outcomes. Because digital interventions that include a human component tend to have better engagement and outcomes, future research should determine whether this is also the case in AI-powered interventions and, to improve cost-effectiveness, identify the characteristics of patients who benefit with and without a human-in-the-loop.

The next generation of research on digital solutions must be conducted via a health equity lens that evaluates algorithmic biases, digital literacy, and the extent to which solutions affect inequities. Digital interventions for obesity should also be designed with society-level drivers of obesity in mind, including poor access to healthy food, built environments that present barriers to physical activity, poor access to care, and social vulnerability. Further, few digital solutions for obesity care have been integrated into real-world practice which signals a need for implementation research that examines how to plan, execute, and adapt digital programs into healthcare and community settings. Finally, the future of digital obesity care will require interoperability across mobile apps, wearables, electronic health records, and telehealth platforms to produce a system of coordinated care that links patients, providers, and healthcare systems.

Mathioudakis N, Lalani B, Abusamaan MS, et al. An AI-Powered Lifestyle Intervention vs. Human Coaching in the Diabetes Prevention Program. JAMA. 2025;334(23):2079. doi:https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2025.19563.○ This paper describes the first large, randomized trial of an AI chatbot-delivered lifestyle intervention versus a human delivered lifestyle intervention in adults with overweight or obesity and prediabetes published in a major medical journal. Findings revealed that participants referred to the AI chatbot were more likely to initiate treatment and the AI chatbot produced non-inferior outcomes relative to human coaching at 1 year.
