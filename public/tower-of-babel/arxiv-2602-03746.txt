Factor-balancedness, linear recurrence, and factor complexity
Bastián Espinoza and Manon Stipulanti, Pierre Popoli

Abstract
. In the study of infinite words, various notions of balancedness provide quantitative measures for how regularly letters or factors occur, and they find applications in several areas of mathematics and theoretical computer science. In this paper, we study factor-balancedness and uniform factor-balancedness, making two main contributions. First, we establish general sufficient conditions for an infinite word to be (uniformly) factor-balanced, applicable in particular to any given linearly recurrent word. These conditions are formulated in terms of 𝒮 \mathcal{S} -adic representations and generalize results of Adamczewski on primitive substitutive words, which show that balancedness of length-2 factors already implies uniform factor-balancedness. As an application of our criteria, we characterize the Sturmian words and ternary Arnoux–Rauzy words that are uniformly factor-balanced as precisely those with bounded weak partial quotients. Our second main contribution is a study of the relationship between factor-balancedness and factor complexity. In particular, we analyze the non-primitive substitutive case and construct an example of a factor-balanced word with exponential factor complexity, thereby making progress on a question raised in 2025 by Arnoux, Berthé, Minervino, Steiner, and Thuswaldner on the relation between balancedness and discrete spectrum.



Keywords
: Combinatorics on words, balancedness, factor-balancedness, uniform factor-balancedness, discrepancy, Sturmian words, Arnoux–Rauzy words, linearly recurrent words, substitutions, decisiveness, factor complexity, Toeplitz words.

: 68R15.

Introduction

The study of

Their research introduces balancedness as a fundamental property linking the combinatorics of the so-called

This perspective laid the foundations for what has since become a recurrent theme in combinatorics on words (for instance, see

In the theory of aperiodic order, balancedness is an important regularity property of Delone sets, tilings, and quasicrystals, providing a quantitative measure of how uniformly points or tiles are spread; for instance, see
[

It is also a recurring notion in uniform distribution theory

In topological dynamics on the Cantor set and ergodic theory, the Gottschalk–Hedlund theorem relates the balancedness of clopen sets to cohomological properties and to the existence of topological eigenvalues
[

From a computational viewpoint, balancedness is connected to certain optimization and operations research problems; for instance, see

In arithmetic and number theory, it appears under the name of bounded remainder sets and is closely related to continued fractions (for instance see
[
35
]

[

Originally stated in the field of number theory
[

This conjecture is verified for small values of

Finally, in the context of billiard dynamics, the complete unbalancedness is linked to the notion of topological weak mixing.

In combinatorics on words and symbolic dynamics, balancedness is a quantitative measure of how uniformly factors occur in a given infinite word.

The seminal paper of Rauzy

This work extends, to a two-dimensional setting, the connection between arithmetic geometry and combinatorics on words, which was already present in the Sturmian case. Rauzy’s paper initiated a large body of subsequent research.
In particular, a long series of works aim at generalizing this interplay to broader families of infinite words.
This line of research includes the introduction of Arnoux–Rauzy words

, the refutation of the conjecture asserting their letter-balancedness in all cases

Further extensions were developed for other families, such as Brun words
[
22
]
.
Recently, Berthé, Cecchi–Bernales, and Espinoza establish necessary and sufficient conditions for letter-balancedness of primitive substitutive words in terms of incidence matrices and so-called extension graphs, see
[

.

Instead of considering letters, one may turn to factors.
Given an integer

If each factor of

When the bound

The study of factor-balancedness and its uniform version is the main focus of this paper.

Contrary to letter-balancedness, which depends only on the abelianization of words, factor-balancedness depends on finer combinatorics and therefore cannot be reduced to the study of letters in general.
However, Adamcwzeski
[
1
]
proves that the factor-balancedness of primitive substitutive words is equivalent to balancedness for length-

Related to famous families of words, Fagnot and Vuillon
[

show that Sturmian words are factor-balanced and give conditions under which they are even uniformly factor-balanced.
In addition, many works are dedicated to investigating the links between letter-balancedness and factor-balancedness of different lengths.
For instance, Berthé and Cecchi–Bernales
[
14

prove that letter-balancedness implies factor-balancedness in dendric words.
This family of words has gained interest in recent years and generalizes Arnoux–Rauzy words and codings of interval exchange transformations; for instance see
[

,
18
,
17
,
37
,
38
]
.
Later on, the result of Berthé and Cecchi–Bernales was generalized by Berthé et al.

to primitive unimodular proper
\mathcal{S}
-adic subshifts.
More recently, Arnoux et al.
[
7
]
connect factor-balancedness with the Pisot conjecture, which asserts that any irreducible Pisot substitution generates a symbolic dynamical system with pure discrete spectrum; see
[
3
]
for a general overview of this conjecture.
In this context, Arnoux et al.
[
7
, Question 18.2]
ask which are the factor-balanced infinite words generating a symbolic system with a discrete spectrum.
Regarding the relationship between the original notion of letter-balancedness and its extension to factor-balancedness, we also mention the works of Poirier and Steiner
[
51
]
and that of Quéffelec
[

]
.

Although uniform factor-balancedness is stronger than factor-balancedness, Adamczewski’s results
[

2

As a consequence, many classical infinite words belong to this class, e.g., the Tribonacci word, and more generally, any substitutive Arnoux-Rauzy word
[
8
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, this property explicitly appeared only two decades later as a computational hypothesis in
[

]
, motivated by the use of the theorem-prover

[

]
, which is built on the connection between first-order logic and automata theory.
From this hypothesis, Couvreur et. al.
[

]
prove that the two-dimensional generalized abelian complexity of an automatic uniform factor-balanced word is regular, in the sense of Allouche and Shallit
[
4
]
.
Furthermore, authors of
[

]
also prove the following explicit result about the Tribonacci word, generalizing that of
[
54
]
concerning letter-balancedness.

1.1
[
27
]
.

The Tribonacci word is uniformly factor-balanced with constant
2
.

However, Adamczewski’s general theorem appears to be largely underutilized for factor-balancedness in the literature.
This became apparent when the authors of
[
27
]
presented their work to audiences in combinatorics on words, dynamical systems, and numeration systems, where uniform factor-balancedness (and in particular Theorem

This observation constitutes the main motivation for the present work.
Our goal is to bring uniform factor-balancedness back into focus by revisiting known results, clarifying its scope and significance, and extending the current understanding of this property, as well as providing some new general results on factor-balancedness.

1.1.
Our contributions

In this paper, we contribute to the theory of factor-balancedness and uniformly factor-balancedness in two different ways.

First
, in
Section

5
, we provide conditions under which linearly recurrent words are (uniformly) factor-balanced.
We give our result here in
Theorem

1.2
but we refer the reader to
Section

5
for the necessary definitions and notation that appear in the statement. Note that a congenial sequence as stated below always exists for a given linearly recurrent word.

1.2
.

Let
{\bf{x}}
be a linearly recurrent word and
(\tau_{n},\,a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a congenial sequence generating
{\bf{x}}
satisfying Conditions

(
P
)

,

(
F
)

, and

(
D
)

.
Let

denote the limit of
(\tau_{n,m}(a_{m}))_{m>n}

Suppose that there exists a sequence

Our approach to

1.2
is based on the rich theory of

-adic representations of linearly recurrent words, and is explained in
Section

5.1
.
Another crucial ingredient of our approach is the notion of decisiveness in

. This allows us to reduce in

5.3
the problem of studying factor-balancedness to that of letter-balancedness in an adequate desubstitution of the original word.

Theorem

can be applied to any primitive substitutive word, since such a word is linearly recurrent
[
29
]
. In this case, we obtain in
Section

5.3
an equivalence between uniform factor-balancedness and balancedness of length-
2
factors.

{proposition}

Let
{\bf{x}}
be the fixed point of a primitive substitution. Then each length-2 factor of
{\bf{x}}
is balanced in
{\bf{x}}
if and only if the word
{\bf{x}}
is uniformly factor-balanced.

This phenomenon already appears in the work of Adamczewski, following directly from
[
1
, Theorem 22]
.

More importantly, another application of
Theorem

1.2
provides a full characterization of binary and ternary Arnoux–Rauzy words that are uniformly factor-balanced, which we discuss in
Section

6
. All necessary definitions are introduced in
Section

3.6
.
Also note that the binary case, i.e., about Sturmian words, was already considered in
[
33
]
.

1.3
.

An Arnoux–Rauzy word over an alphabet of at most
3

Second
, in
Section

7
, we study links between factor-balancedness and factor complexity.
We first prove in
Section

7.1
that substitutive factor-balanced words have linear factor complexity, and in the more specialized

7.1
we describe a special substitutive structure for these words.
Furthermore, with
Theorem

1.4

This reasoning is based on Toeplitz words, whose construction is recalled in
Section

7.2
.

1.4
.

There exists an infinite word that is factor-balanced and has exponential-growth factor complexity.
In particular, the symbolic dynamical system generated by
{\bf{x}}
has positive topological entropy and thus cannot have pure discrete spectrum.

This makes progress towards a question raised by Arnoux et al. in

by showing that not all factor-balanced symbolic system have discrete spectrum.

Finally, the reader will find in
Section

3
the necessary background for the paper; in particular, subsections dedicated to definitions about words, substitutions, the close connection between discrepancy and balancedness,

-adic representations, Sturmian words, and Arnoux–Rauzy words.

Section

contains generalities on (uniform) factor-balancedness, especially its stability by invertible higher block codes and its relationship to uniform recurrence in infinite words.
Instead of finishing the paper with some open problems, we propose some directly below, in
Section

.

2.
Open problems

In this section, we gather some open questions we came up with during the writing of the paper, which motivates further research on the topic of (uniform) factor-balancedness.

In
Section

5
,
Theorem

1.2
provides sufficient conditions for a linearly recurrent word to be (uniformly) factor-balanced.
As a natural question, we ask whether there are necessary conditions for this to hold.

2.1
.

What are the necessary conditions on linearly recurrent words to be (uniformly) factor-balanced?

To answer this question, it appears that some recognizability hypothesis is required. In
Section

6
, we fully characterize uniformly factor-balanced Sturmian and ternary Arnoux–Rauzy words.
One can naturally wonder whether the property extends to any size alphabet.

2.2
.

Is it possible to characterize which Arnoux–Rauzy words are uniformly factor-balanced over any
d
-ary alphabet, with
d\geq 2
?

Note that three letters were already a limitation in
[
12
]
, so the general case might be more intricate and new methods might be necessary to tackle the question.

In
Section

7.3
, we explicitly provide an example of a factor-balanced word with an exponential-growth factor complexity. One may wonder the following finer question.

2.3
.

Can every exponential-growth factor complexity be attained with factor-balanced infinite words?

Our Toeplitz construction does not allow us to obtain uniformly factor-balanced examples with exponential-growth factor complexity without substantial modifications.
Moreover, all known examples of uniformly factor-balanced words have linear factor complexity.
It is therefore natural to ask the following.

2.4
.

Is it possible to construct a uniformly factor-balanced infinite word with a superlinear-growth factor complexity?

By
Section

7.1
, no (uniformly) factor-balanced word with super-linear factor complexity can be substitutive.
Then, one has to use different words, as we did to prove
Theorem

1.4
with Toeplitz words.

Preliminaries

An

To distinguish finite from infinite words, we write the latter in bold.

denote the

A word

For any integer

.

Consider an infinite word

and a finite word

.
The

\mu_{\bf{x}}(u)

is defined, if it exists, as the following limit

When the limit exists, we say that

We say that

Finally, we say that

An infinite word
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}

.
The word

(1)

3.2.
Substitutions

Given an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, a

is a map
\tau

\mathcal{A}

that is compatible with the concatenation, and usually its extension from
\mathcal{A}^{*}

If there exists a letter

such that the first letter of
\tau(a)
is
a
,
\lim_{n\to+\infty}|\tau^{n}(a)|=+\infty
, and
|\tau(b)|\neq 0
for every letter
b
, then the limit
\tau^{\omega}(a):=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\tau^{n}(a)
exists and is called the
fixed point of
\tau
starting by
a
.
In that case, we say that the produced infinite word is
substitutive
.

A substitution
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
is
primitive
if there exists an integer
k
such that for all letters
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
,
a
is a factor of
\tau^{k}(b)
.
If
{\bf{x}}
is substitutive,
then one can easily check that
{\bf{x}}
is uniformly recurrent whenever the involved substitution is primitive.

A set
\mathcal{L}
of words over
\mathcal{A}
is
factor-closed
if, whenever
u
is a factor of
v\in\mathcal{L}
, then
u\in\mathcal{L}
.
It is
biextensible
if
u\in\mathcal{L}
implies that
aub\in\mathcal{L}
for some letters
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
.
For a substitution
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
, consider the set
L
formed by all words
u\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
occurring in some
\tau^{n}(a)
, where
n\geq 1
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then,
L
contains a unique inclusion-maximal factor-closed and biextensible set of words, which we call the
language generated by
\tau
and denote by
\mathcal{L}(\tau)
.
We say that
\mathcal{L}(\tau)
satisfies a given property (e.g., recurrent or
P
-power-free) if for every
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathcal{L}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\tau)
,
{\bf{x}}
also satisfies this property.

A letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
is
bounded
for
\tau
if
\bigl(|\tau^{n}(a)|\bigr)_{n\geq 0}
is bounded.
We let
B_{\tau}
denote the set of bounded letters for
\tau
, and define
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}=\mathcal{A}\setminus B_{\tau}
, the set of
growing letters
for
\tau
.
The substitution
\tau
is
growing
if
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}=\mathcal{A}
, i.e., every letter is growing.
We also say that
\tau
is
quasi-primitive
if there exists
n\geq 1
such that
b
occurs in
\tau^{n}(a)
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
.
This is equivalent to the notion of
l
-primitiveness
found in
[
45
,
59
]
.
Note that, for growing substitutions,
\tau
is primitive if and only if
\tau
is quasi-primitive.
We say that
\tau
is
quasi-uniform
if there exists
C>0
such that

|\tau^{n}(a)|\leq C\,|\tau^{n}(b)|\kern 5.0pt\text{for all $n\geq 1$ and $a,b\in\mathcal{A}$.}

Finally,
\tau
is
tame
if and only if
\mathcal{L}(\tau)\cap\mathcal{B}_{\tau}^{*}
is finite.
This definition is equivalent to the original notion of tameness, as shown in
[
45
, Theorem 2.9]
and
[
59
, Proposition 3.17]
.

3.3.
Discrepancy and balancedness

For an infinite word
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
u\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
such that its frequency
\mu_{\bf{x}}(u)
in
{\bf{x}}
exists, we define its
discrepancy
by
D_{\bf{x}}(u)=\sup_{n\geq 0}D_{\bf{x}}(u,n)
, where

\displaystyle D_{\bf{x}}(u,n)=\sup\bigl\{\big||w|_{u}-|w|\mu_{\bf{x}}(u)\big|:w\in\mathcal{L}_{n}({\bf{x}})\bigr\}\in\mathbb{R}.

The
balance function
of
u
in
{\bf{x}}
is defined by
B_{\bf{x}}(u)=\sup_{n\geq 0}B_{\bf{x}}(u,n)
, where

\displaystyle B_{\bf{x}}(u,n)=\sup\big\{\big||t|_{u}-|s|_{u}\big|:t,s\in\mathcal{L}_{n}({\bf{x}})\big\}.

The basic relationship between the discrepancy and balance function is given by the following lemma, which adapts
[
1
, Proposition 7]
from the letter case (expressions of the form
|u|_{a}-|v|_{a}
with
a\in\mathcal{A}
) to the word case.

{lemma}

Let
{\bf{x}}
be an infinite word and
u\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
.

(1)

If
u
is
C
-balanced in
{\bf{x}}
, then the frequency
\mu_{{\bf{x}}}(u)
of
u
in
{\bf{x}}
exists uniformly, and the discrepancy
D_{\bf{x}}(u)
is bounded by
C
.

(2)

If the discrepancy
D_{\bf{x}}(u)
is bounded by
C
, then
u
is
2C
-balanced in
{\bf{x}}
.

Proof.

We prove the first item of the statement.
Assume that
u
is
C
-balanced in
{\bf{x}}
.
For
z\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
of length
n+|u|-1
, we define
g(z)=|z|_{u}/n
, and we let
m_{n}
(resp.,
M_{n}
) denote the minimum (resp., maximum) value of
g(z)
for
z\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
of length
n+|u|-1
. First, let us show that

(1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}M_{\ell}\leq M_{n}

for every
n\geq 0
.
For
w\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
of length
\ell+|u|-1
with
\ell\geq n
, we let
k=\lfloor\ell/n\rfloor
.
For each
i\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
, we let
z_{i}
denote the factor of
w
of length
n+|u|-1
occurring at positions
in
and we let
z_{k}
be the suffix of
w
starting at position
kn
.
Since
|w|_{u}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}|z_{i}|_{u}
, we get

g(w)=\frac{|w|_{u}}{\ell}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{|z_{i}|_{u}}{\ell}=\frac{n}{\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}g(z_{i})+\frac{|z_{k}|_{u}}{\ell}.

Since
|z_{k}|\leq n
, we get
g(w)\leq\frac{nk}{\ell}M_{n}+\frac{n}{\ell}
, from which (
1
) follows.
Similarly, we can prove that

(2)

\displaystyle m_{n}\leq\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}m_{\ell}

for every
n\geq 0
.
Now, since
u
is
C
-balanced in
{\bf{x}}
, we have that
M_{n}-m_{n}\leq\frac{C}{n}
for every
n\geq 0
.
From both Inequalities (
1
) and (
2
), we deduce that the frequency
\mu_{\bf{x}}(u)
of
u
in
{\bf{x}}
exists and is the limit of both sequences
(m_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.
By Inequalities (
1
) and (
2
) one more time, we have
m_{n}\leq\mu_{\bf{x}}(u)\leq M_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
, which implies that
D_{\bf{x}}(u)\leq C
using the bound
M_{n}-m_{n}\leq\frac{C}{n}
.

We turn to the proof of the second item of the statement.
Assume that
D_{\bf{x}}(u)
is bounded by
C
.
Then, for
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
of the same length
\ell
, we have

\big||w|_{u}-|w^{\prime}|_{u}\big|\leq\big||w|_{u}-\mu_{\bf{x}}(u)\ell\big|+\big||w^{\prime}|_{u}-\mu_{\bf{x}}(u)\ell\big|\leq 2C,

which shows that
u
is
2C
-balanced in
{\bf{x}}
.
∎

3.4.
\mathcal{S}
-adic representations

An efficient way to represent low-complexity words is through limits of compositions of substitutions.
We recall here the basic definitions, which will be central in what follows.

Let
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of alphabets, and for each
n\geq 0
, consider a substitution
\tau_{n}\colon\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}_{n}^{*}
.
Assume that there exists a sequence
(b_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of letters such that
b_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
for each
n\geq 0
and each image
\tau_{n}(b_{n+1})
begins with
b_{n}
.
Following the terminology of Berthé, Karimov, and Vahanwala
[
20
]
, we call
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{n},b_{n})_{n\geq 0}
a
congenial sequence
.
For
n>m\geq 0
, we abbreviate

\tau_{m,n}=\tau_{m}\circ\tau_{m+1}\circ\tau_{m+2}\circ\dots\circ\tau_{n-1}.

Because
\tau_{n}(b_{n+1})
begins with
b_{n}
, it follows that
\tau_{m,n}(b_{n})
is a prefix of
\tau_{m,n+1}(b_{n+1})
.
Hence, for each
m\geq 0
, the limit

{\bf{x^{(m)}}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{m,n}(b_{n})

exists in
\mathcal{A}_{m}^{\mathbb{N}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*}
.
Moreover, one verifies that

(3)

{\bf{x^{(m)}}}=\tau_{m,n}({\bf{x^{(n)}}})\quad\text{for all $n>m\geq 0$.}

Our main object of study is
{\bf{x}}\coloneqq{\bf{x^{(0)}}}
, which we call the
word generated by
\boldsymbol{\tau}
.
In this case,
\boldsymbol{\tau}
is said to be an
\mathcal{S}
-adic expansion
of
{\bf{x}}
.
The auxiliary infinite words
{\bf{x^{(m)}}}
for
m\geq 1
play a crucial role in the analysis of
{\bf{x}}
.

3.5.
Sturmian words

The prototypical example of
\mathcal{S}
-adic expansions is the one for Sturmian words obtained by Morse and Hedlund
[
47
]
, which we describe now.
We first introduce Sturmian words, following the presentation of Berthé, Holton, and Zamboni
[
19
]
.

Let
\alpha
be an irrational number in
(0,1)
.
Consider the two two-interval exchange transformations
R_{\alpha}:[-\alpha,1-\alpha)\to[-\alpha,1-\alpha)
and
\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}:(-\alpha,1-\alpha]\to(-\alpha,1-\alpha]
respectively defined by

R_{\alpha}(z)=\begin{cases}z+\alpha,&\text{if }z\in[-\alpha,1-2\alpha);\\[6.0pt]
z+\alpha-1,&\text{if }z\in[1-2\alpha,1-\alpha);\end{cases}

and

\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(z)=\begin{cases}z+\alpha,&\text{if }z\in(-\alpha,1-2\alpha];\\[6.0pt]
z+\alpha-1,&\text{if }z\in(1-2\alpha,1-\alpha].\end{cases}

Both can be considered as rotations of angle
2\pi\alpha
, since these are conjugate, after identification of points
-\alpha
and
1-\alpha
, to a circle rotation.

A
Sturmian word

{\bf{x}}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

of slope
\alpha
is the forward itinerary (with respect to the natural partition) of a point
\rho\in[-\alpha,1-\alpha]
, called the
intercept
of
{\bf{x}}
, under the action of one of these transformations, i.e.,

(1)

either for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{n}=0
if and only if
R_{\alpha}^{n}(x)\in[-\alpha,1-2\alpha)
,

(2)

or for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{n}=0
if and only if
\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}^{n}(x)\in[-\alpha,1-2\alpha)
.

If
z=0
, then both definitions above produce the same word
{\bf{x}}
, which is called, in this case, the
characteristic Sturmian word of slope
\alpha
.

We now describe the
\mathcal{S}
-adic expansion of characteristic Sturmian words. For this, let
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\colon\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}^{*}
be the substitutions defined by

\theta_{0}\colon\begin{cases}0&\mapsto 0,\\
1&\mapsto 01;\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{1}\colon\begin{cases}0&\mapsto 10,\\
1&\mapsto 1.\end{cases}

We further define, for
n\geq 2
,
\theta_{n}=\theta_{0}
if
n
is even, and
\theta_{n}=\theta_{1}
if
n
is odd. The following result is classical, first proven by Morse and Hedlund
[

]
.
The version cited here follows Berthé, Holton, and Zamboni
[

]
, and is presented using the language introduced in the previous subsection.

{proposition}

Let
\alpha
be an irrational number in
(0,1)
with continued fraction expansion
[0;a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
.
For

if

3.6.

\big||u|_{w}-|v|_{w}\big|\leq\sum_{w^{\prime}\in W}\big||u^{\prime}|_{w^{\prime}}-|v^{\prime}|_{w^{\prime}}\big|\leq\mathrm{Card}(W)\max_{w^{\prime}\in W}C_{w^{\prime}}.

This proves (
4
) for
K\coloneqq\max_{w^{\prime}\in W}C_{w^{\prime}}
, from which it follows that
\pi({\bf{x}})
is factor-balanced.

Next, we prove the second part of the statement, for which it is enough to show that the constants
\mathrm{Card}(W)
and
K
appearing in (
4
) are bounded independently from
w
.
If
{\bf{x}}
is furthermore uniformly
C
-factor-balanced, then
K\coloneqq\max_{w^{\prime}\in W}C_{w^{\prime}}
is bounded from above by
C
.
Thus, it is enough to show that
\mathrm{Card}(W)
is bounded independently from
w
.
Suppose that
\pi
has an inverse
\rho
, i.e.,
\rho
is an
\ell
-block code such that
\rho(\pi({\bf{x}}))=x_{k+\ell}x_{k+\ell+1}\cdots
.
Set
S=\{s\in\mathcal{L}(\pi({\bf{x}}))\cap\mathcal{B}^{|w|+k+\ell}:\rho(s)\in W\}\cap\mathcal{L}(\pi({\bf{w}}))
.
Then, for any
s\in S
,
\pi(\rho(s))=w
is a suffix of

.
This shows that
S\subseteq\{t\,w:t\in\mathcal{B}^{k+\ell}\}
, from which it follows that
\mathrm{Card}(S)\leq\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{B}^{k+\ell})
.
As
\rho(S)=W
, we get
\mathrm{Card}(W)\leq\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{B}^{k+\ell})
.
We conclude that
K
is bounded independently of
w
, as desired.
∎

{remark}

Notice that the first item of the previous proposition fails if we only consider letter-balancedness. For example the Thue–Morse word is letter-balanced while the period doubling word is not. Indeed, define
\tau
as
\tau(1)=10
and
\tau(0)=11
, then
{\bf{x}}=\tau^{\omega}(1)
is the period doubling word. Define
u_{0}=1
and
u_{n+1}=\tau^{2}(u_{n})1
for

, i.e.,

u_{n}=\tau^{2n}(1)\,\tau^{2(n-1)}(1)\cdots\tau^{2}(1)\,1.

Let us show that

. It is clear for

, and if
u_{n}\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
, then
u_{n}a\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
for some

, so
\tau^{2}(u_{n}a)=\tau^{2}(u_{n})\tau^{2}(a)
is in
\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
as well. Since both
\tau^{2}(0)
and
\tau^{2}(1)
both start with

,
u_{n+1}=\tau^{2}(u_{n})1
is a prefix of
\tau^{2}(u_{n})\tau^{2}(a)
, and hence it belongs to
\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
.

We now prove that the letter
1
is not balanced in
{\bf{x}}
by means of estimating its discrepancy. The frequency
\mu_{1}
of
1
in
{\bf{x}}
is given by the normalized right Perron eigenvector of the incidence matrix of
\tau
, i.e.,
\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\

; in this case,
\mu_{1}=2/3
. The incidence matrix of

is

\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix}2+4^{n}&2(4^{n}-1)\\
4^{n}-1&2\cdot 4^{n}+1\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

|u_{n}|_{1}-\mu_{1}|u_{n}|=\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\left(|\tau^{2i}(1)|_{1}-\mu_{1}|\tau^{2i}(1)|\right)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\left(2\cdot 4^{i}+1-2\cdot 4^{i}\right)=\frac{n+1}{3},

that is, the discrepancy of

is unbounded.

We now show that factor-balancedness forces recurrence in infinite words to be uniform.

{proposition}

Let
{\bf{x}}
be a factor-balanced infinite word and let
{\bf{y}}
be a recurrent infinite word with
\mathcal{L}({\bf{y}})\subseteq\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
.

(1)

The word
{\bf{y}}
is uniformly recurrent.

(2)

For every recurrent infinite word
{\bf{y^{\prime}}}
with
\mathcal{L}({\bf{y^{\prime}}})\subseteq\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
, we have
\mathcal{L}({\bf{y}})=\mathcal{L}({\bf{y^{\prime}}})
.

In particular, every factor-balanced recurrent infinite word is uniformly recurrent.

Proof.

Let
{\bf{y}},{\bf{y^{\prime}}}
be recurrent infinite words with
\mathcal{L}({\bf{y}}),\mathcal{L}({\bf{y^{\prime}}})\subseteq\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
, and fix an arbitrary factor
t\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{y}})
.
We claim that

(5)

there exists
\ell\geq 1
such that every length-
\ell
factor of
{\bf{y^{\prime}}}
contains
t
.

Let us first show that Claim (
5
) implies the statement.
By Claim (
5
),
t\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{y^{\prime}}})
and, moreover,
t
occurs in
{\bf{y^{\prime}}}
with gaps bounded by
\ell
.
Since
t
was arbitrary, we obtain
\mathcal{L}({\bf{y}})\subseteq\mathcal{L}({\bf{y^{\prime}}})
.
By symmetry (interchanging
{\bf{y}}
and
{\bf{y^{\prime}}}
), we get
\mathcal{L}({\bf{y}})=\mathcal{L}({\bf{y^{\prime}}})
, and every word of this common set appears with bounded gaps in both
{\bf{y}}
and
{\bf{y^{\prime}}}
.
In particular, taking
{\bf{y^{\prime}}}={\bf{y}}
yields Item (1).

It remains to prove Claim (

).
As
t\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
, let

be the balance constant of
t
in
{\bf{x}}
, i.e., for any two factors

of equal length, we have
\big||u|_{t}-|v|_{t}\big|\leq C_{t}
.
Because
{\bf{y}}
is recurrent and

, there exists a factor
p
of
{\bf{y}}
containing at least

occurrences of

.
Set

.
Now let

be any factor of
{\bf{y^{\prime}}}
with
|w|=\ell
.
Then

, so by the factor-balancedness of
{\bf{x}}
,

|w|_{t}\geq|p|_{t}-\big||w|_{t}-|p|_{t}\big|\geq|p|_{t}-C_{t}.

Since
|p|_{t}\geq C_{t}+1
, we obtain

.
Thus

occurs in every length-

, which shows Claim (

).
∎

We now provide an example of a word that is uniformly recurrent but not balanced.

{example}

Consider the so-called

{\bf{c}}=0012001212012\cdots

.
Indeed, it is known that

generates a

.

In combinatorics on words, we say that a nonempty word

overlaps itself
if

contains more than two occurrences of

.
Otherwise we say that

does not overlap itself

contains exactly two occurrences of

{lemma}

[
[
44
, Chapter 1]
]

A word is primitive if and only if it does not overlap itself.

{lemma}

Let
{\bf{x}}
be an infinite word and

be nonempty.
If
u
is
C
-balanced in
{\bf{x}}
for some

and

is eventually periodic of period dividing
|u|
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
u
is primitive.
We argue by contradiction and we assume that
{\bf{x}}
is not eventually periodic of period dividing
|u|
.
Then, since
u^{2C+3}\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
, there exists a word
w\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
of length
|u^{2C+3}|
having
u^{C+1}
as a prefix, but not
u^{C+2}
.
We decompose

, with
|t|=|u|
and
|s|=(C+1)|u|
; note that

.
Now,

contains at least
C+2
occurrences of
u^{C+2}
, so, since

is
C
-balanced in
{\bf{x}}
, every factor of length
|u^{2C+3}|
of

has at least two occurrences of

.
This applies in particular to

; let

be two different positions at which

occurs in

On the one hand, we notice that

since, otherwise,

would be a prefix of

, contradicting the choice of

.
This implies that if

, then

overlaps with itself, which by

is impossible since

is primitive.
Therefore,

.
On the other hand, we trivially have that

.
We conclude that

.
Thus, the intervals

occurs overlap in a window of size at least

∎

Therefore, from the previous lemma, we prove the following two results about power avoidance.

-power-free.

is not

-power-free, then it is eventually periodic by

.
Since
{\bf{x}}
is recurrent, this implies that it is periodic, contradicting the hypothesis.

{lemma}

Let
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
be

-power-free for an integer

.
Then,

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that

By the Pigeonhole principle, there exist two occurrences of

starting at positions

and

∎

5.
Factor-balancedness in linearly recurrent words

The objective of this section is to characterize which linearly recurrent words are uniformly factor-balanced.
Our approach relies on an
\mathcal{S}
-adic description of these words, due to Durand
[
29
]
and introduced in
Section

5.1
.
The specific substitutive structure of linearly recurrent words allows us to reduce the problem of studying factor-balancedness to that of letter-balancedness across the different levels of the structure.
The resulting characterization is given in
Theorem

1.2
, whose proof is presented in
Section

5.3
.
As an immediate corollary, we recover Adamczewski’s analogous result for substitutive words
[
1
,
2
]
, namely
Section

1.1
.

5.1.
Substitutive description of linearly recurrent words

We recall the reader that, for a congenial sequence
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{n}\colon\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}_{n}^{*},\,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, the notation
\tau_{m,n}
, for
n>m\geq 0
, stands for the composition
\tau_{m}\circ\tau_{m+1}\circ\dots\circ\tau_{n-1}
.
Furthermore, we say that
\tau_{m,n}
is
positive
if
b
occurs in
\tau_{m,n}(a)
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
b\in\mathcal{A}_{m}
.

The following classical theorem gives a concrete characterization of infinite words that are linearly recurrent in terms of a substitutive structure.
Recall that a substitution
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
is
left-proper
if there exists a letter
a
such that each
\tau(b)
begins with
a
.

5.1
[
29
]
.

An infinite word
{\bf{x}}
is linearly recurrent if and only if there exists a congenial sequence
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{n},\,a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
generating
{\bf{x}}
satisfying:

(\mathbf{P})

There exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\tau_{n,n+k}
is positive for all
n\geq 0
;

(\mathbf{F})

The set
\{\tau_{n}:n\geq 0\}
is finite; and

(\mathbf{L})

The substitution
\tau_{n}
is left-proper for all
n\geq 0
.

The prototypical example of a congenial sequence
\boldsymbol{\tau}
as in
Theorem

5.1
corresponds to substitutive ones.
More precisely, suppose that
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
is a left-proper primitive substitution and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then, the constant sequence
\boldsymbol{\tau}\coloneqq(\tau,\,a)_{n\geq 0}
is congenial and satisfies the three conditions of
Theorem

5.1
.
This shows that any fixed point of a left-proper primitive substitution is linearly recurrent.
In fact, any fixed point of any primitive substitution (not necessarily proper) is linearly recurrent
[
29
]
.
Therefore, linearly recurrent words generalize primitive substitutive words.
This generalization is vast, since there are only countably many substitutive words but uncountably many linearly recurrent words.
For instance, among Sturmian words, the substitutive ones correspond to slopes that are quadratic algebraic numbers, whereas the linearly recurrent ones correspond to slopes whose continued fraction coefficients are bounded (an uncountable set of zero Lebesgue measure), see
Theorem

6.1
.

We now collect several facts about linearly recurrent words and the substitutive structure in
Theorem

5.1
. Recall that, for a substitution
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
, we use the notation
|\tau|=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\tau(a)|
.

{proposition}

[
[
31
,
29
]
]

Let
{\bf{x}}
be a linearly recurrent word generated by the congenial sequence
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{n},\,a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and satisfying Conditions

(

)

and

(

)

of

.
Then, there exists

such that:

(1)

and

.

Proof.

The set

is finite by hypothesis, so there exists

Moreover, Lemma 8 in

5.2.
Decisiveness

We now introduce a concept that plays a crucial role in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
along with the properties that will be used in its proof.

{definition}

Let
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
be an infinite word and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
A substitution
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
is
k
-decisive in
{\bf{x}}
if there exists a map
r\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}^{k}
such that
\tau(b)
starts with
r(a)
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
with
ab\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x}})
.
For
u\in\mathcal{B}^{*}
of length
|u|\leq k+1
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we define the notation
q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,a)
as follows.
For
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we let
r_{|u|}(a)
denote the prefix of length
|u|-1
of
r(a)
, and then set

(6)

q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,a)=|\tau(a)\,r_{|u|}(a)|_{u}.

We can rephrase decisiveness as follows: it is the property that if a letter
a
appears in
{\bf{x}}
at some position
i
, then this determines not only that the block
\tau(a)
appears in
\tau({\bf{x}})
at the expected position
j\coloneqq|\tau({\bf{x}}_{[0,i)})|
, but also that the extended block
\tau(a)r(a)
that appears at position
j
.

Decisiveness can be seen a generalization of properness, as described next. Recall that a substitution
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
is left-proper if there exists a letter
a
such that each
\tau(b)
,
b\in\mathcal{A}
, begins with
a
. Note that in this case,
\tau
has a unique fixed point, namely,
\tau^{\omega}(a)
.

{proposition}

Let
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
be a left-proper substitution, and in
{\bf{x}}
its fixed point. Then
\tau
is
1
-decisive in
{\bf{x}}
.

Proof.

By setting
r(b)=a
for
b\in\mathcal{A}
, this shows that a left-proper substitution is always
1
-decisive in its unique fixed point.
∎

More generally, for any substitution
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
, its length-2 sliding block code is
k
-decisive in any of its fixed points, with
k=\min\{|\tau(a)|:a\in\mathcal{A}\}-1
. Furthermore, if a substitution
\tau
is
1
-decisive in a certain
{\bf{x}}
, then
\tau^{n}
is
k_{n}
-decisive in
{\bf{x}}
, where
k_{n}=\min\{|\sigma^{n}(a)|:a\in\mathcal{A}\}
.

One important property of decisive substitutions is that they allow us to compute the occurrences of a given short word
u
in
\tau({\bf{x}})
using only the occurrences of letters in
{\bf{x}}
and some data that depend on
u
, but not on the position where
u
occurs.
The next lemma makes this precise.

{proposition}

Let
\tau\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
be a
k
-decisive substitution in
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Suppose that
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
occurs at position
i\in\mathbb{N}
in
{\bf{x}}
, and let
p=|\tau({\bf{x}}_{[0,i+|w|)})|
be the natural position at which
\tau(w)
ends in
\tau({\bf{x}})
.
Then, for every
u\in\mathcal{L}(\tau({\bf{x}}))
of length
|u|\leq k+1
, we have

(7)

|\tau(w)\,\tau({\bf{x}})_{[p,p+|u|)}|_{u}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|w|_{a}\,q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,a).

Proof.

We need to count occurrences of
u
whose starting position lies in the interval
[p-|\tau(w)|,p+|u|)
of
\tau({\bf{x}})
. For each
j\in\{i,i+1,\dots,i+|w|-1\}
, consider
\tau(x_{j}x_{j+1})=\tau(x_{j})\tau(x_{j+1})
.
Since
\tau
is
k
-decisive and
|u|\leq k+1
, the first
|u|-1
letters of
\tau(x_{j+1})
are the fixed word
r_{|u|}(x_{j})
, independent of

.
Hence the number of occurrences of
u
that start in
\tau(x_{j})
(possibly crossing into
\tau(x_{j+1})
) equals

\bigl|\tau(x_{j})\,r_{|u|}(x_{j})\bigr|_{u}=q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,x_{j}).

These sets of starting positions when

varies in
\{i,i+1,\ldots,i+|w|-1\}
are disjoint and together cover exactly the starts of
u
in the interval
[p-|\tau(w)|,p+|u|)
.
Therefore,

|\tau(w)\,\tau({\bf{x}})_{[p,p+|u|)}|_{u}=\sum_{i\leq j<i+|w|}q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,x_{j})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|w|_{a}\,q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,a)

since

.
∎

:

\big|q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,w)-\mu_{u}|\tau(w)|\big|

.

For

, let

, and note that

We can compute

The second term in the right-hand side is small:
From

4

, so, since
|\varepsilon_{a}|\leq B
,

(11)

\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\varepsilon_{a}\,q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,a)\leq B\cdot\frac{P\|\tau\|}{|u|}.

Next, we show that the first term in the right side of (

) is close to

.
Using the definition of

, we can write

|\tau(w)|=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|w|_{a}\,|\tau(a)|=|w|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nu_{a}\,|\tau(a)|+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\varepsilon_{a}\,|\tau(a)|.

From this and the definition of
\mu_{u}
in (
8
), we obtain

\Big||w|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nu_{a}\,q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,a)-\mu_{u}|\tau(w)|\Big|=\mu_{u}\Big|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\varepsilon_{a}\,|\tau(a)|\Big|.

Note that

by

4
, so
\mu_{u}\leq P/|u|
.
This and

give the estimate:

(12)

\Bigl||w|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nu_{a}\,q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,a)-\mu_{u}|\tau(w)|\Bigr|\leq\frac{P}{|u|}\cdot B\cdot\|\tau\|.

Finally, we can bound
\big|q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,w)-\mu_{u}|\tau(w)|\big|
by first replacing

using (

) and then estimating the remaining terms with (

) and (

), i.e.,

\big|q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,w)-\mu_{u}|\tau(w)|\big|=\Big||w|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nu_{a}q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,a)-\mu_{u}|\tau(w)|+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\varepsilon_{a}q_{\tau,{\bf{x}}}(u,a)\Big|\leq\frac{2BP\|\tau\|}{|u|},

as desired.
∎

Observe that

The case where

Now, assume that

and suppose that

,
\beta=\sigma_{b}

Observe that
\sigma_{a}\circ\sigma_{b}

Therefore, (

Now, since

has bounded weak partial coefficients, there is an increasing sequence

\displaystyle T(\tau_{0,n_{j}+2})=T(\tau_{0,n_{j}}\circ\tau_{n_{j}}\circ\tau_{n_{j}+1})\leq\frac{1}{2}T(\tau_{0,n_{j}})+2

for every

.
Moreover, we have

for all

\displaystyle T(\tau_{0,n_{j}+2})\leq\frac{1}{2}T(\tau_{0,n_{j}})+2

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}T(\tau_{0,n_{j-1}+2})+\frac{C-2}{2}+2,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}T(\tau_{0,n_{j-1}+2})+\frac{C}{2}+1

for every

For all

Between the indices

and

7.
Factor-balancedness and factor complexity

The goal of this section is to exhibit certain links between the (uniform) factor-balancedness and factor complexity.
It is well known that linearly recurrent words possess a linear factor complexity
[
29
]
, so it seems natural to also investigate this matter regarding factor-balancedness. Let us mention that the previous work
[
14
]
of Berthé and Cecchi Bernales also focuses on infinite words with low complexity, since dendric words have at most linear complexity.
In this paper, we prove that substitutive factor-balanced words have linear factor complexity (see
Section

7.1
), even if the fixed point is not uniformly recurrent.
Remember from a result of Pansiot
[
49
]
that substitutive words can reach quadratic factor complexity.
Finally, in
Section

7.3
, we also provide an example of a factor-balanced word with exponential factor complexity using Toeplitz words, whose construction is recalled and illustrated in
Section

7.2
.

7.2.
Toeplitz words

A
Toeplitz word
is a uniformly recurrent infinite word
{\bf{x}}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a period
p\geq 1
for which
x_{n+kp}=x_{n}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
We present a method to construct Toeplitz words following
[
30
]
, which is based on
[
39
]
.
We also warn the reader that this definition is more general than that of “Toeplitz words” presented, for instance, in
[
24
]
. Finally, we refer to the survey of Downarowicz
[
28
]
for more details.

Let us fix an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.
The construction depends on two parameters.
First, we need an increasing sequence
(p_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of positive integers such that

(\mathbf{T\!p_{1}})

The integer
p_{n}
divides
p_{n+1}
for all
n\geq 0
.

For
n\geq 1
, define the integer
q_{n}=p_{n}/p_{n-1}
.
The second parameter is a sequence
(W_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of sets of words with
W_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{p_{n}}
and satisfying the following: for every
n\geq 0
,

(\mathbf{T\!p_{2}})

Each
w\in W_{n+1}
is a concatenation
w=\phi_{n}(w,1)\phi_{n}(w,2)\cdots\phi_{n}(w,q_{n})
of
q_{n}
words from
W_{n}
; and

(\mathbf{T\!p_{3}})

There exists
w_{n}\in W_{n}
that is a prefix of each word in
W_{n+1}
.

Observe that the last property ensures that
w_{n}
is always a prefix of
w_{n+1}
.
Since
|w_{n}|=p_{n}
is increasing, we obtain an infinite word

{\bf{x}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}.

If we furthermore assume that the parameters satisfy, for every
n\geq 1
,

(\mathbf{T\!p_{4}})

The set
\{\phi_{n}(w,i):1\leq i\leq q_{n}\}
equals
W_{n}
for every
w\in W_{n+1}
,

then
{\bf{x}}
is uniformly recurrent, and
{\bf{x}}
is a Toeplitz word.

{example}

We exemplify the definitions by showing that the fixed point of the period doubling substitution is a Toeplitz word.
Let
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
and
p_{n}=4^{n}
for
n\geq 0
, which satisfy

(

⁢
T

p
1

)

.
We inductively define the sets of words
W_{n}=\{u_{0}(n),u_{1}(n)\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{p_{n}}
as follows.
First,
u_{0}(0)=0
and
u_{1}(0)=1
, and then we set

(30)

\begin{cases}u_{n+1}(0)&=u_{n}(0)\,u_{n}(1)\,u_{n}(0)\,u_{n}(0),\\
u_{n+1}(1)&=u_{n}(0)\,u_{n}(1)\,u_{n}(0)\,u_{n}(1),\end{cases}

for
n\geq 0
.
Conditions

(

⁢
T

p
2

)

and

(

⁢
T

p
4

)

are satisfied by the very construction, and

(

⁢
T

p
3

)

holds as well with

.
Therefore, the sequence
(u_{n}(0))_{n\geq 0}
of words converges to a uniformly recurrent word
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
, which is a Toeplitz word.

is exactly the fixed point of the

(

⁢
T

p
4

)

.

Observe that the words
\phi_{n}(w,i)
in

(

⁢
T

p
2

)

essentially define substitutions.
We now formalize this idea, as it will be highly convenient to describe the Toeplitz construction using the language of substitutions.

Let us fix a sequence
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of alphabets such that

and

is in bijection with

Then, these bijections translate the decompositions

)

(\mathbf{T\!p^{\prime}_{1}})

for every

(\mathbf{T\!p^{\prime}_{4}})

Every

occurs in

for every

Condition

(

⁢

{remark}

We carefully warn the reader that the notation

in the framework of Toeplitz does not refer to the notation of inducing tuples from the previous sections of the paper. Since we do not use both of these objects simultaneously, what the notation

means should be clear regarding the context.

7.3.
Exponential factor complexity

Our aim is to prove
Theorem

1.4
.
We thus construct a factor-balanced Toeplitz word
{\bf{x}}
whose complexity function
p_{\bf{x}}
satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log p_{\bf{x}}(n)>0,

i.e.,
{\bf{x}}
has a positive entropy.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{0}
be an alphabet. Consider two sequences of positive integers
(k_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(r_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with
r_{n}\leq 2k_{n}
and that will be fixed later. We inductively construct
\tau_{n}\colon\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}_{n}^{*}
as follows.

First, we simply set
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{0}
and
\tau_{0}\colon\mathcal{A}_{1}\to\mathcal{A}_{0}
to be the identity map, i.e.,
\tau_{0}(a)=a
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, and we extend it to a substitution by concatenation.
Suppose that
\tau_{n-1}
has been defined.
Fix a primitive word
v_{n}u_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{*}
with
|u_{n}|=|v_{n}|=r_{n}
.

Let
W_{n}
be the set of all words
w\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{*}
such that:

(1)

The word
w
begins with
u_{n}
and ends with
v_{n}
;

(2)

Each
a\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
occurs exactly
k_{n}
times in
w
, i.e.,
|w|_{a}=k_{n}
;

Note that the second condition implies that
|w|=k_{n}|\mathcal{A}_{n}|
for all
w\in W_{n}
.
We let
\tau_{n}\colon\mathcal{A}_{n+1}\to W_{n}
to be any bijection between the alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{n+1}
and
W_{n}
, and then extend
\tau_{n}
to a substitution
\tau_{n}\colon\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}_{n}^{*}
.

{example}

Let us describe how the first step works on the simple example where
r_{1}=2
,
k_{1}=4
,
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{0,1\}
,
u_{1}=01
, and
v_{1}=10
. Then
w\in W_{1}
if

,
|w^{\prime}|_{0}=|w^{\prime}|_{1}=2
. Thus, there are six choices for

0011,0101,0110,1001,1010,1100
. Finally, we define
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\{0,1,\ldots,5\}
and
\tau_{1}:0\mapsto 0011,1\mapsto 0101,\ldots,5\mapsto 1100

It is not difficult to see that the sequence
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of substitutions defined above satisfies the Toeplitz conditions, so they define Toeplitz words
{\bf{x^{(m)}}}
according to (
31

We set
{\bf{x}}={\bf{x^{(0)}}}
.

7.1
.

The word
{\bf{x}}
is factor-balanced. Moreover, there exists a choice of sequences
(r_{n},k_{n})
such that the word

has exponential-growth factor complexity.

Observe that

1.4
directly follows from the previous statement.

{lemma}

For every
m\geq 0
,
{\bf{x^{(m)}}}
is
(4k_{m})
-letter-balanced.

Proof.

Fix
m\geq 0
, let
u\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x^{(m)}}})
and
a\in\mathcal{A}_{m}
. It is enough to prove that

(33)

\left||u|_{a}-k_{m}\,\frac{|u|}{|\mathcal{A}_{m}|}\right|\leq 2k_{m},

as then for any other
v\in\mathcal{L}({\bf{x^{(m)}}})
of length
|v|=|u|
, the previous inequality yields

\big||u|_{a}-|v|_{a}\big|\leq\left||u|_{a}-k_{m}\,\frac{|u|}{|\mathcal{A}_{m}|}\right|+\left|k_{m}\,\frac{|v|}{|\mathcal{A}_{m}|}-|v|_{a}\right|\leq 4k_{m}.

Let us prove (
33
).
Observe that, by construction,
{\bf{x^{(m)}}}
is an infinite concatenation of words of the form
\tau_{m}(b)
for
b\in\mathcal{A}_{m+1}
.
In particular, since
u
occurs in
{\bf{x^{(m)}}}
, it must occur in
\tau_{m}(b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell})
for some word
b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell}\in\mathcal{A}_{m+1}^{*}
with
\ell\geq 1
.
We take such a word of minimal length
\ell
. By construction, for any
b\in\mathcal{A}_{m+1}
,
\tau_{m}(b)
satisfies

\begin{cases}|\tau_{m}(b)|=k_{m}|\mathcal{A}_{m}|,\\
|\tau_{m}(b)|_{a}=k_{m}.\end{cases}

Therefore, since
u
occurs in
\tau_{m}(b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell})
, we have the following upper bounds

\begin{cases}|u|\leq\ell k_{m}|\mathcal{A}_{m}|,\\
|u|_{a}\leq\ell k_{m}.\end{cases}

Now, by the minimality of

, either
\ell\leq 2
or
\tau_{m}(b_{2}b_{3}\cdots b_{\ell-1})
occurs inside

.
In both cases, we obtain the lower bounds

\begin{cases}|u|\geq(\ell-2)k_{m}|\mathcal{A}_{m}|,\\
|u|_{a}\geq(\ell-2)k_{m}.\end{cases}

From the previous inequalities we deduce that

\begin{cases}(\ell-2)k_{m}\leq|u|_{a}\leq\ell k_{m},\\
\frac{|u|}{k_{m}|\mathcal{A}_{m}|}\leq\ell\leq\frac{|u|}{k_{m}|\mathcal{A}_{m}|}+2.\end{cases}

Therefore, plugging the second inequalities in the first, we obtain

\frac{|u|}{|\mathcal{A}_{m}|}-2k_{m}\leq|u|_{a}\leq\frac{|u|}{|\mathcal{A}_{m}|}+2k_{m}.

This proves (

Remember that

is independent of

.
Suppose that all

is sufficiently large,

corresponds to the number of words

such that

(i)

The word

starts with

and ends with

.

(ii)

We have

for all

.

Since

, we have that

is exactly the multinomial coefficient

. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\ell_{n+1}=(1+o(1))\left(\ell_{n}^{\ell_{n}(k_{n}-2)}\frac{\sqrt{\ell_{n}}}{(2(k_{n}-2)\pi)^{(\ell_{n}-1)/2}}\right).

Then for sufficiently large

, we have

(35)

\ell_{n+1}>\ell_{n}^{\ell_{n}k_{n}}\frac{1}{(2\pi\ell_{n}^{2}k_{n})^{\ell_{n}}}.

Using the bound in (
35

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{n+1}}\log(\ell_{n+1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{p_{n}k_{n}\ell_{n}}\log\left(\ell_{n}^{\ell_{n}k_{n}}\frac{1}{(2\pi\ell_{n}^{2}k_{n})^{\ell_{n}}}\right),

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log(\ell_{n})}{p_{n}}-\frac{1}{p_{n}k_{n}}\log(2\pi\ell_{n}^{2}k_{n}),

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log(\ell_{n})}{p_{n}}-\frac{2}{p_{n}k_{n}}\log(\ell_{n})-\frac{1}{p_{n}k_{n}}\log(2\pi k_{n}),

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log(\ell_{n})}{p_{n}}\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{n}}\right)-\frac{3}{p_{n}}.

Thus, by iterating the previous inequality, we obtain for all

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{n}}\log(\ell_{n})\geq\frac{1}{p_{2}}\log(\ell_{2})\prod_{2\leq i\leq n-1}\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{i}}\right)-S_{n},

where

Since

, we have
p_{i}\geq 2^{i}
for all
i\geq 2
. And we have

, then the sum

is finite.
Now, we choose
(k_{n})_{n\geq 2}
such that

is finite and non-zero, e.g.,

for all
n\geq 2
. Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{n}}\log(\ell_{n})\geq\frac{1}{p_{2}}\log(\ell_{2})\prod_{i\geq 2}\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{i}}\right)-S,

since
\prod_{2\leq i\leq n-1}\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{i}}\right)\geq\prod_{i\geq 2}\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{i}}\right)
and
S_{n}\leq S
.
Finally, we choose
|\mathcal{A}|=\ell_{1}=\ell_{0}
and
k_{1}
as below.
Recall that
p_{1}=1
,
p_{2}=p_{1}\ell_{1}k_{1}=|\mathcal{A}|k_{1}
, and by (
34
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{2}}\log\ell_{2}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|k_{1}}\bigg(|\mathcal{A}|(k_{1}-2)\log(|\mathcal{A}|)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\log(|\mathcal{A}|)

\displaystyle-\left.\frac{|\mathcal{A}|-1}{2}\log(2\pi(k_{1}-2))+O\left(\frac{1}{k_{1}}\right)\right)

So, first choose
\mathcal{A}
such that
\log|\mathcal{A}|\prod_{i\geq 2}\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{i}}\right)>S
.
Then, as

, we obtain

\frac{1}{p_{2}}\log\ell_{2}=\log|\mathcal{A}|\prod_{i\geq 2}\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{i}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{k_{1}}\right).

In the end, we may choose

such that

\frac{1}{p_{n}}\log p_{{\bf{x}}}(p_{n})\geq\frac{1}{p_{n}}\log|\mathcal{A}_{n}|\geq\frac{1}{p_{2}}\log(\ell_{2})\prod_{i\geq 2}\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{i}}\right)-S>0.

Therefore, the sequence

remains bounded away from zero, which means that
{\bf{x}}
has exponential-growth factor complexity, as desired.
∎
