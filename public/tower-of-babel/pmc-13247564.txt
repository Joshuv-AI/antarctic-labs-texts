A dataset of preclinical tooth preparation images
Authors: Mohammad Fraiwan, Rami Al Fodeh, Dalal Shamiyah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents a curated dataset of multi-view digital images of full-coverage crown preparations performed on artificial teeth in a preclinical dental education setting. The dataset comprises composite images generated from three predefined photographic views (facial, occlusal, and lingual) for each tooth preparation, along with corresponding expert annotation records. The presented evaluation results reflect qualitative, image-based expert judgment rather than direct instrument-based quantitative measurements.Tooth preparations were completed by fourth-year undergraduate dental students as part of routine preclinical training, collected retrospectively from routine teaching activities, and documented using digital photography. Each tooth preparation was evaluated using a predefined educational rubric commonly applied in preclinical prosthodontic teaching. The released annotation data include a dichotomous pass/fail outcome, a feedback-oriented ordinal score, and a numeric score ranging from 0 to 5, reflecting the number of criteria met. Reference annotations were provided by a specialist prosthodontist. Image files and annotation records are linked through standardized naming to ensure traceability across dataset components.The dataset is intended for reuse in dental education research, the development and testing of image-based assessment methods, and machine learning applications focused on automated feedback and evaluation of preclinical tooth preparations. No patient data or clinical variables are included.Keywords: Tooth preparation, Preclinical prosthodontics, Image-based evaluation, Artificial intelligence, Deep learning

Background

Preclinical tooth preparation is a core component of undergraduate dental education, where students develop fundamental skills related to fixed prosthodontics. Assessment of these preparations is traditionally performed by instructors using predefined criteria and grading rubrics [1,2]. With increasing dental student cohort sizes in many institutions, instructors are often required to assess large numbers of preparations within limited timeframes, which may affect the consistency and timeliness of feedback provided to students [3].

To support assessment and feedback in preclinical training, digital tools have been introduced, including systems based on high-resolution optical scanners [1,4,5]. Although such systems can provide objective and detailed evaluations, they often require specialized hardware and supporting infrastructure, which may limit their accessibility in low-resource educational settings [2,5,6]. As a result, alternative approaches that rely on photographic acquisition using widely available equipment remain of interest, but are less commonly reported in the form of open, reusable datasets.

Within this context, curated image-based datasets can support educational research and the development of assessment methodologies. The dataset presented in this article was compiled to document tooth preparations performed by fourth-year dental students during preclinical training. Images were acquired using digital photographic methods and annotated according to predefined educational criteria commonly applied in prosthodontic teaching.

Data Description

The dataset comprises digital image files and corresponding annotation records documenting full-coverage crown preparations performed on artificial teeth in a preclinical educational setting. All data are organized in a structured manner designed to enable straightforward navigation and linkage between image and annotation components. Image data

The image dataset is organized into four top-level directories corresponding to tooth class (i.e., Incisors, Canines, Premolars, and Molars), each containing files for individual tooth preparations within that category. The distribution of preparations across tooth classes is summarized in Table 1. For every tooth sample, three standardized photographic views—occlusal, facial, and lingual—were acquired and stored as source images. These three views were also combined into a single composite image arranged in a fixed side-by-side layout, which serves as the primary visual representation of each preparation. Composite images are stored in JPG format and named using a standardized identifier that encodes tooth class and a numeric index. Filenames follow the convention [number]_I for incisors, [number]_C for canines, [number]_P for premolars, and [number]_M for molars. The original source images are in JPG format and use an extended naming scheme that appends a third field indicating the view: O (occlusal), F (facial), or L (lingual). For example, the filename 6_C_L.jpg denotes the lingual view of the sixth canine sample.

The Mendeley Data repository contains four separate files. The first file, “Annotation.xlsx”, includes the evaluation criteria and corresponding results for each composite image (referenced by filename), in addition to summary statistics. This file contains six sheets. Four identically structured sheets correspond to the four tooth types. Each of these sheets includes three main sections (columns). The first section contains the file name, which serves as a unique preparation identifier across the entire dataset. The second section contains the feedback labels for the five evaluation dimensions (see Table 2), represented as binary values (0 or 1). The third section contains the evaluation scores, organized into four subcolumns corresponding to the annotation scales (see Table 3 for the definitions and acceptable values of each scale). The fifth sheet provides basic descriptive statistics for the preparations, including counts and percentages. The sixth sheet contains a detailed description of the evaluation rubric, corresponding to the criteria and scales presented in Table 2, Table 3. The second file, “Stitched Images.zip,” is a compressed archive containing four subfolders organized by tooth type (Canines, Incisors, Molars, and Premolars); each folder includes the composite multi-view images following the specified naming convention. The third file, “Separate View Images.zip”, is similarly organized into four tooth-type subfolders, each containing three individual view images (facial, occlusal, and lingual) per preparation, labeled according to the same naming convention. The fourth file, “stitching.py” contains the python code used to combine the images from the three separate views into one image per preparation.

Annotation data

Annotation data consist of evaluations assigned to each tooth preparation based on a predefined educational rubric commonly applied in preclinical prosthodontic teaching. Each preparation was reviewed as a composite image and evaluated across five assessment domains: axial wall reduction, occlusal reduction, retention and resistance form, margin clarity and continuity, and undercut evaluation. No instrument-based quantitative measurements were performed, and this evaluation reflects qualitative, image-based expert judgment.

To standardize the evaluation of tooth preparations in the dataset, each preparation was evaluated across five predefined assessment domains: axial wall reduction, occlusal reduction, retention and resistance form, margin clarity and continuity, and undercut evaluation (Table 2). Assessments were performed through qualitative comparative visual inspection of the images, using anatomical landmarks, preparation geometry, and relative proportions of the tooth structure as reference cues in the absence of direct physical measurements. Each domain was scored using a binary scheme (1 = meets standard, 0 = does not meet standard). The five domain scores were then summed to generate a preparation quality score ranging from 0 to 5. Based on this total score, three complementary annotation scales were derived: a discrete numeric score representing the total number of criteria met (0-5), a dichotomous pass-fail outcome (≥3 = pass; ≤2 = fail), and an ordinal category describing preparation quality (excellent-maintain, good-minor adjustments, needs improvement, or redo required), reflecting the type of qualitative feedback typically provided to students during preclinical prosthodontic training. All annotation values represent reference evaluations provided by a single specialist prosthodontist and are reported as per-tooth entries. The evaluation rubric and associated scoring scales are summarized in Table 2, Table 3.

File linkage and internal consistency

Consistency across dataset components is maintained using identical tooth identifiers in both image file names and annotation records. Each composite image corresponds to exactly one annotation row in the Excel workbook. Only preparations with complete composite images were included.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data were generated in the conservative dentistry simulation laboratories at the Faculty of Dentistry, Jordan University of Science and Technology (JUST), as part of preclinical training. Tooth preparations were completed by fourth-year undergraduate dental students on artificial teeth (NISSIN Dental Products, Japan) mounted on typodont jaws and collected retrospectively from routine teaching activities. No samples were excluded, and each student contributed one tooth preparation per type. However, not all of them contributed to all tooth types. Thus, the number of distinct students was 533. All preparations were full-coverage crown preparations. No patient involvement or patient-related variables were included in the data collection process.

Following completion of the tooth preparations, digital images were captured to document the final preparation morphology from three predefined views: occlusal, facial, and lingual. A uniform blue dental apron background was used during photography to provide contrast and reduce visual distractions. Image acquisition was performed by a single operator using a digital single-lens reflex camera (Canon EOS 250D; Rebel SL3, Canon Inc., Japan) equipped with a macro lens (Sigma 105 mm f/2.8 DG Macro HSM OS, Canon EF mount) and a ring flash (Canon Macro Ring Lite MR-14EX). Images were captured in JPG format at the camera’s native resolution using autofocus. Manual exposure mode was used with standardized settings of f/5.6 aperture, 1/125s shutter speed, and ISO 100. The ring flash provided uniform illumination to reduce shadowing across preparation surfaces. No software-based steps were undertaken to normalize the orientation of images.

Following image acquisition, cropping was applied to the original images. Crops were defined to preserve the entire visible tooth preparation while removing extraneous background elements and surrounding artifacts present in the unedited photographs. After cropping, each source image was resized to 400 × 400 pixels (width × height). Composite images were then synthesized, automatically using a Python code, by arranging the three standardized views side by side—facial on the left, lingual in the center, and occlusal on the right—yielding a final composite resolution of 1200 × 400 pixels. No other preprocessing operations were performed on the images. Two of the authors performed quality control and checked that the stitching process indeed combined the correct images and that the cropping did not unintentionally removed parts of the preparation

Tooth preparations were subsequently annotated using a predefined educational rubric (Tables 2 and 3). A calibration step needs to be conducted during development of the annotation framework to align interpretation of the evaluation criteria. Furthermore, inter-rater reliability testing needs to be performed during this step. This is because before the actual annotation process begins, the evaluators need to undergo a standardization or alignment process to ensure that all annotators interpret the preparation criteria consistently. For example, evaluators may initially differ in how they judge concepts such as undercut presence, taper adequacy, occlusal reduction, or margin smoothness. During the calibration step, the annotators need to review representative preparation examples together, discuss disagreements, clarify ambiguous cases, and align their understanding of the rubric definitions and scoring scales. This process helps reduce inter-rater variability and improves annotation consistency and reliability across the dataset. In this work, the released dataset contains reference annotations provided by a single specialist prosthodontist and no reliability testing was performed. Preparations were reviewed as composite images using a digital image viewer, and annotation values were recorded in a structured electronic format.

Limitations

The dataset reflects tooth preparations performed by fourth-year undergraduate dental students during preclinical training; therefore, the range and distribution of preparation quality correspond to a specific educational level and may not represent variability associated with more experienced operators. In addition, the distribution of tooth types within the dataset is not fully balanced, with certain categories (e.g., molars) more heavily represented than others (e.g., incisors). Furthermore, some categories of the results may not have a substantial number of samples. These imbalances may limit the representativeness of the dataset across all tooth types and could influence model performance or generalizability.

Image acquisition was conducted using a handheld camera without standardized angulation, working distance, or positioning aids, introducing variability in image orientation and framing across samples. Although the same equipment and general imaging conditions were used throughout, some images demonstrate lower visual quality due to factors such as focus variation or positioning at the time of capture, which may affect clarity in a subset of cases. Furthermore, due to the low-texture and reflective nature of white dental surfaces, conventional photographic imaging may not fully capture subtle grooves and micro-topographic depth variations. As a result, certain fine preparation details may appear less pronounced in 2D multi-view images, which may influence annotation precision and model performance on subtle surface features.

Another limitation of the dataset arises from the use of 2D photographic images to document inherently three-dimensional tooth preparations. While the multi-view composite images were designed to capture multiple perspectives of each preparation, subtle micro-topographic variations in surface depth may not always be fully represented in 2D projections. This limitation is particularly relevant to the evaluation of undercuts, which typically requires a three-dimensional assessment of axial wall convergence and internal spatial relationships. Shallow or localized undercuts may therefore be less detectable when evaluated from fixed photographic angles, which may potentially affecting the precision of this specific rubric domain. Future work could address this limitation by incorporating 3D scanning or photogrammetric reconstruction techniques to provide volumetric representations of the preparations and enable more precise geometric assessment.

Finally, the released annotation dataset includes reference scores provided by a specialist prosthodontist. Therefore, alternative interpretations are not represented, limiting assessment of annotation variability and potential subjectivity across evaluators. Furthermore, the lack of actual measurements of the preparation shortcomings may open the door for subjective interpretation and human errors. Future expansion of the dataset will aim to further balance the number of images from different tooth types and include more annotators along with the required reliability analysis.

Usage Note

Due to the natural distribution of the collected samples, the dataset exhibits a class imbalance across both tooth types and evaluation outcomes. In particular, incisors are underrepresented relative to molars and premolars, and the proportion of passing preparations is comparatively low for certain tooth categories. Researchers using this dataset for machine learning tasks should account for this imbalance during model development. Common mitigation strategies include data augmentation techniques (e.g., geometric transformations such as rotation, scaling, and horizontal flipping), class-weighted loss functions, oversampling of minority classes, or synthetic data generation methods. Applying these approaches can help reduce model bias toward majority classes and improve the generalization performance of models trained on the dataset.
