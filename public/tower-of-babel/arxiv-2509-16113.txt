A generalized canonical metric for optimization on the indefinite Stiefel manifold
Dinh Van Tiep 1, Duong Thi Viet An 2, Nguyen Thi Ngoc Oanh 2, Nguyen Thanh Son 2

Abstract
Various tasks in scientific computing can be modeled as an optimization problem on the indefinite Stiefel manifold. We address this using the Riemannian approach, which basically consists of equipping the feasible set with a Riemannian metric, preparing geometric tools such as orthogonal projections, formulae for Riemannian gradient, retraction and then extending an unconstrained optimization algorithm on the Euclidean space to the established manifold. The choice for the metric undoubtedly has a great influence on the method. In the previous work [D.V. Tiep and N.T. Son, A Riemannian gradient descent method for optimization on the indefinite Stiefel manifold, arXiv:2410.22068v2[math.OC]], a tractable metric, which is indeed a family of Riemannian metrics defined by a symmetric positive-definite matrix depending on the contact point, has been used. In general, it requires solving a Lyapunov matrix equation every time when the gradient of the cost function is needed, which might significantly contribute to the computational cost. To address this issue, we propose a new Riemannian metric for the indefinite Stiefel manifold. Furthermore, we construct the associated geometric structure, including a so-called quasi-geodesic and propose a retraction based on this curve. We then numerically verify the performance of the Riemannian gradient descent method associated with the new geometry and compare it with the previous work.

Contribution

It is the main motivation source of the present paper to answer these questions. Namely, by converting the Lyapunov matrix equation into a linear equation using the Kronecker product and vectorization operator and by asking that the resulting equation is solving-free, we first achieve a general one-parameter family of metrics that fulfill our expectation; the canonical(-like) metric in
EdelAS98
;
GSAS21
certainly belongs to this class. Second, we explain in detail why in the case of the orthogonal Stiefel manifold, the Lyapunov matrix equation does not appear during the computation of the Riemannian gradient by examining the formulation of the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space under the tractable metric. Third, we go further by characterizing the normal space to the manifold, deriving closed-form expressions for the orthogonal projections onto the tangent and normal spaces, and the Riemannian gradient of the cost function. Moreover, we construct a so-called quasi-geodesic curve associated with the obtained metric which in turn allows us to propose a retraction. Finally, these tools are invoked in a Riemannian gradient descent scheme for running several numerical examples.

Preliminaries

In this section, we recall basic facts about the indefinite Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{iSt}_{A,J}(k,n)
which can also be shortened as
\mathrm{iSt}_{A,J}
or
\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)

.

Given matrix
A
as introduced in the beginning, let
\mathrm{i}_{+}(A)
and

respectively denote the number of positive and negative eigenvalues of

. It was stated in

\mathrm{i}_{+}(J)\leq\mathrm{i}_{+}(A)\mbox{ and }\mathrm{i}_{-}(J)\leq\mathrm{i}_{-}(A).

We always assume that (
2
) holds when working with this manifold.

Then, it was shown in

, Prop. 2.2)
that the set

) is a closed, embedded submanifold of

For

, let us denote by

a full-rank matrix satisfying

The following statements hold due to

, Lem. 2.3)
:

i)

E
is nonsingular and

E^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}JX^{T}A\\
(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp})^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}\end{bmatrix}.

(3)

ii)

For any

there are

and

where

There holds

(5)

Next, recall that the tangent space at

is the set

T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)=\left\{\dot{\varphi}(0)\;:\;\varphi:(a,b)\ni 0\longrightarrow\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)\text{ is smooth and }\varphi(0)=X\right\}.

In

TiepSon2025
, Prop. 2.4)
, it has been shown that

\displaystyle T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)

\displaystyle=\left\{Z\in{\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}:Z^{T}AX+X^{T}AZ=0\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{XW+A^{-1}X_{\perp}K:JW\in\mathrm{Skew}(k),K\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times k}\right\}.

(6)

In

, Sect. 3)

-smoothly dependent matrix

\langle Z_{1},Z_{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{g}_{\mathbf{M}_{X}}}\coloneq\mathrm{tr}\left(Z_{1}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{X}Z_{2}\right)

The computation of

Based on this formula, the Riemannian gradient was given in

where

is some smooth extension of

Ignoring the computational cost for

by, e.g., considering the Euclidean metric, one can show that the total cost for computing the Riemannian gradient

3
A generalized canonical metric on
\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)

As introduced, we will determine a form of
\mathbf{M}_{X}
to eliminate the need of solving the Lyapunov equation (
10
). To this end, we simplify the coefficient
X^{T}A\mathbf{M}_{X}^{-1}AX
of the equation. The identity
E^{T}AX=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}J\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
suggests considering
\mathbf{M}_{X}^{-1}\in\mathrm{SPD}(n)
as

\mathbf{M}_{X}^{-1}=E\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{1}&\Gamma_{2}\\
\Gamma_{2}^{T}&\Gamma_{3}\end{bmatrix}E^{T},\ \Gamma_{1}\in\mathrm{SPD}(k),\;\Gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times(n-k)},\;\Gamma_{3}\in\mathrm{SPD}(n-k).

This results in
X^{T}A\mathbf{M}_{X}^{-1}AX=J\Gamma_{1}J.
Consequently,
the equation (
8
) reduces to

2\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY)=(J\Gamma_{1}J)U_{X,Y}+U_{X,Y}(J\Gamma_{1}J).

(11)

For a greater detail, let us convert this matrix equation into a linear equation using vectorization operator and Kronecker product
HornJ91
. Namely, for a matrix
M=(m_{ij})\in{\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}
,
\mathrm{vec}(M)\in\mathbb{R}^{nk}
is generated by stacking the columns of
M
to make a column vector and the Kronecker product of
M
and
N\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
, denoted by
M\otimes N\in\mathbb{R}^{np\times kq}
, is the matrix consisting of
nk
block matrices
m_{ij}N
for
i=1,\ldots,n,j=1,\ldots,k
. In view of
(
HornJ91
, Lem. 4.3.1)
, (
11
) is equivalent to

(I_{k}\otimes(J\Gamma_{1}J)+(J\Gamma_{1}J)\otimes I_{k})\mathrm{vec}(U_{X,Y})=\mathrm{vec}(2\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY)).

We say that a square system of linear equations is
solving-free
if its coefficient is a scalar matrix, i.e., the product of a (nonzero) scalar and the identity matrix. Thus, its solution is merely the product of the reciprocal of the scalar and the right-hand side. Accordingly, a sufficient condition for
obviating the need of solving
the Lyapunov matrix equation (
8
) is that
I_{k}\otimes(J\Gamma_{1}J)+(J\Gamma_{1}J)\otimes I_{k}

has
the form
2\rho I_{k^{2}}
, where
\rho
is a nonzero constant parameter. It follows that
J\Gamma_{1}J=\rho I_{k}
which, together with the condition
J^{2}=I_{k}
, yields
\Gamma_{1}=\rho I_{k}
. For the symmetric positive-definiteness of
\mathbf{M}_{X}^{-1}
, we set
\rho>0
. One can verify that in this case, the solution to (
11
) is
U_{X,Y}=\frac{1}{\rho}\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY).

Furthermore, we set
\Gamma_{2}=0
for the ease of argument. Consequently,
\mathbf{M}_{X}^{-1}
takes the form

\mathbf{M}_{X}^{-1}=E\begin{bmatrix}\rho I_{k}&0\\
0&\Gamma_{3}\end{bmatrix}E^{T}=\rho XX^{T}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}\Gamma_{3}X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}.

(12)

Moreover, taking (
3
) into account, we also have

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{X}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}JX^{T}A\\
(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp})^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\rho}I_{k}&0\\
0&\Gamma_{3}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}JX^{T}A\\
(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp})^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\rho}AXX^{T}A+X_{\perp}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}\Gamma_{3}^{-1}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}.

(13)

In the framework of the tractable metric whose representing matrix has just been obtained, the targeted
generalized canonical metric
applied to two tangent vectors
Z_{i}=XW_{i}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}K_{i}
,
i=1,2,
at any
X\in\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
is

g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}(Z_{1},Z_{2})\coloneqq\mathrm{tr}\left(Z_{1}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{X}Z_{2}\right)=\dfrac{1}{\rho}\mathrm{tr}(W_{1}^{T}W_{2})+\mathrm{tr}(K_{1}^{T}\Gamma_{3}^{-1}K_{2}).

(14)

Obviously,
g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}
is a non-degenerate bilinear form defined on
T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
and dependent upon both
\rho
and
X_{\perp}
. To make this a Riemannian metric, except for the special parameter
\rho
,
it is essential to require that
\mathbf{M}_{X}
depends only on
X\in\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
and this dependence is smooth. To this end, we will impose additional conditions on
X_{\perp}
in terms of
\Gamma_{3}
. If we choose

\left(X_{\perp}^{T}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}=\Gamma_{3}

(15)

then the second term of (
13
) becomes

X_{\perp}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}X_{\perp}\right)\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}=\left(A(I_{n}-XJX^{T}A)\right)^{2}

\mathbf{M}_{X}=\frac{1}{\rho}AXX^{T}A+\left(A\left(I_{n}-XJX^{T}A\right)\right)^{2}.

(16)

Alternatively, we can take

\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}X_{\perp}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}=\Gamma_{3}.

(17)

Accordingly, it follows that

\displaystyle X_{\perp}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}\Gamma_{3}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}

Thus,

13
) is symmetric positive-definite, so we can extend the inner product
g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}
on
T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
to an inner product on
{\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}
which is given by
g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}(Z_{1},Z_{2})=\mathrm{tr}(Z_{1}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{X}Z_{2}),
for all
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}.

The normal space to
\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
at
X
with respect to
g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}
is defined by

T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)_{\rho}^{\perp}\coloneqq\left\{N\in{\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}:g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}(N,Z)=0\text{ for all }Z\in T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)\right\}.

The following statement characterizes the elements of this space. Although the metric (
13
) depends on
X_{\perp}
, the elements of the normal space, as we will see, do not.

Proposition 1

T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)_{\rho}^{\perp}=\left\{XU:JU\in\mathrm{Sym}(k)\right\}.

Proof

Let
N=XU_{N}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}K_{N}
with
U_{N}\in{\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}},

K_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times k}
as in (
4
).
For each
Z\in T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n),
we have
Z=XW_{Z}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}K_{Z},
with
JW_{Z}\in\mathrm{Skew}(k),K_{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times k}
as in (
6
). By (
14
), we have

\displaystyle g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}(N,Z)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\rho}\mathrm{tr}(U_{N}^{T}W_{Z})+\mathrm{tr}(K_{N}^{T}\Gamma_{3}^{-1}K_{Z})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\rho}\mathrm{tr}\left[(JU_{N})^{T}(JW_{Z})\right]+\mathrm{tr}(K_{N}^{T}\Gamma^{-1}_{3}K_{Z}).

Therefore,
g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}(N,Z)=0
, for any
JW_{Z}\in\mathrm{Skew}(k)
and any
K_{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times k},

if and only if

K_{N}=0
and
JU_{N}\in\mathrm{Sym}(k)
since
\mathrm{Skew}(k)
is the orthogonal complement of
\mathrm{Sym}(k)
with respect to the Euclidean product on
{\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}
. Therefore,
N=XU_{N}
with
JU_{N}\in\mathrm{Sym}(k)
if and only if
N\in T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)_{\rho}^{\perp}
which is also the expected characterization.
∎

Next, we present formulae for the orthogonal projections
\mathscr{P}_{X}
and

\mathscr{P}_{X}^{\perp}
with respect to the inner product
g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}
onto
T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
and
T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)^{\perp}_{\rho},
respectively.

Proposition 2

Given any
Y\in{\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}},
the orthogonal projections of
Y
onto
T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
and
T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)^{\perp}_{\rho}
are respectively given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{X}(Y)

\displaystyle=Y-XJ\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY)

(18)

\displaystyle=XJ\mathrm{skew}(X^{T}AY)+(I_{n}-XJX^{T}A)Y

(19)

and

\mathscr{P}_{X}^{\perp}(Y)=XJ\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY).

(20)

Proof

From (
6
) and Proposition
1
, the images of
Y
should take the forms

\mathscr{P}_{X}(Y)=XW_{Y}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}K_{Y}
and
\mathscr{P}_{X}^{\perp}(Y)=XU_{Y},
in which
JW_{Y}\in\mathrm{Skew}(k),

K_{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times k},
and
JU_{Y}\in\mathrm{Sym}(k).
In view of (
7
), it holds that

Y=XW_{Y}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}K_{Y}+XU_{Y}.

(21)

Left multiplying this identity by
X^{T}A
yields

X^{T}AY=JW_{Y}+JU_{Y}
.
Therefore,
\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY)=JU_{Y}
leads to
\mathscr{P}_{X}^{\perp}(Y)=XU_{Y}=XJ\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY)
which proves (
20
).

The identity (
18
) follows from (
21
),

\mathscr{P}_{X}(Y)=XW_{Y}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}K_{Y}=Y-XU_{Y}=Y-XJ\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY).

Since
-\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY)=\mathrm{skew}(X^{T}AY)-X^{T}AY,
(
19
) is a consequence of (
18
).
∎

3.2
Riemannian gradient

We discuss now the Riemannian gradient of the cost function
f
at
X\in\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
with respect to the generalized canonical metric
g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}
constructed in Section
3
. Recalling that it is the unique vector of
T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
, denoted by
\mathrm{grad}_{gc}f(X)
, satisfying

g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}(\mathrm{grad}_{gc}f(X),Z)=\textsc{D}\bar{f}(X)[Z]=\langle\nabla\bar{f}(X),Z\rangle\text{ for all }Z\in T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n),

where
\bar{f}
is any smooth extension of
f
around
X
in
{\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}.

We have the following formula of
\mathrm{grad}_{gc}f(X).

Proposition 3

The Riemannian gradient of the cost function
f
at
X
in
\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
is given by

\mathrm{grad}_{gc}f(X)=\rho\,XJ\mathrm{skew}(JX^{T}\nabla\bar{f}(X))+A^{-1}X_{\perp}\Gamma_{3}X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}\nabla\bar{f}(X).

(22)

Proof

In view of the definition of the Riemannian gradient and (
14
), there holds

\langle\mathbf{M}_{X}\mathrm{grad}_{gc}\bar{f}(X),Z\rangle=g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}\left(\mathrm{grad}_{gc}\bar{f}(X),Z\right)=\textsc{D}\bar{f}(X)[Z]=\langle\nabla\bar{f}(X),Z\rangle,

for any
Z\in{\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}.
Therefore, from (
12
),

\mathrm{grad}_{gc}\bar{f}(X)=\mathbf{M}_{X}^{-1}\nabla\bar{f}(X)=\left(\rho XX^{T}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}\Gamma_{3}X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}\right)\nabla\bar{f}(X).

It follows from
(
AbsiMS08
, Eq. (3.37))
and (
19
) that

\displaystyle\mathrm{grad}_{gc}f(X)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{P}_{X}(\mathrm{grad}_{gc}\bar{f}(X))

\displaystyle=XJ\mathrm{skew}\biggr(X^{T}A\left(\rho XX^{T}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}\Gamma_{3}X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}\right)\nabla\bar{f}(X)\biggr)

\displaystyle\quad+\;(I_{n}-XJX^{T}A)\left(\rho XX^{T}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}\Gamma_{3}X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}\right)\nabla\bar{f}(X)

\displaystyle=\rho XJ\mathrm{skew}\left(JX^{T}\nabla\bar{f}(X)\right)+\left(\rho XX^{T}+A^{-1}X_{\perp}\Gamma_{3}X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}\right)\nabla\bar{f}(X)

\displaystyle\quad-\rho XX^{T}\nabla\bar{f}(X)

\displaystyle=\rho XJ\mathrm{skew}\left(JX^{T}\nabla\bar{f}(X)\right)+A^{-1}X_{\perp}\Gamma_{3}X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}\nabla\bar{f}(X).

This proves the identity (
22
).
∎

It is stressed that in practice, the appearance of
X_{\perp}
in (
22
) needs to be removed. This must be made in accordance with the
condition imposed in either (
15
) or (
17
).
Consider first the case
\left(X_{\perp}^{T}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}=\Gamma_{3}
. It is readily checked that

X_{\perp}\Gamma_{3}X_{\perp}^{T}=X_{\perp}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}=I_{n}-X(X^{T}X)^{-1}X^{T}.

Hence, the expression (
22
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{grad}_{gc}f(X)=

\displaystyle\rho\,XJ\mathrm{skew}(JX^{T}\nabla\bar{f}(X))

(23)

\displaystyle+A^{-1}\left(I_{n}-X(X^{T}X)^{-1}X^{T}\right)A^{-1}\nabla\bar{f}(X).

In the case that
\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}X_{\perp}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}=\Gamma_{3}
, taking (
5
) into account, it follows that

\displaystyle A^{-1}X_{\perp}\Gamma_{3}X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}X_{\perp}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}X_{\perp}\left(X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}X_{\perp}\right)^{-1}X_{\perp}^{T}A^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(I_{n}-XJX^{T}A\right)\left(I_{n}-XJX^{T}A\right)^{T}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{grad}_{gc}f(X)=

\displaystyle\rho\,XJ\mathrm{skew}(JX^{T}\nabla\bar{f}(X))

(24)

\displaystyle+\left(I_{n}-XJX^{T}A\right)\left(I_{n}-XJX^{T}A\right)^{T}\nabla\bar{f}(X).

If matrix
A
is dense and has no special structure, the computational cost for
A^{-1}(\cdot)
in (
23
) is generally comparable to
n^{3}
. Therefore in this case, using (
24
) is more advantageous. One can see that the computation of (
24
) requires
2n^{2}k+10nk^{2}-nk-3k^{2}
flops. Obviously, when
k
is approaching
n
, this cost is cheaper than that caused by (
9
). This will be seen in the numerical examples presented in Section
6
.

Before closing this section, we discuss the reason for some closed-form expressions of the orthogonal projections in popular instances. Indeed, our general setting here and the tractable metric in
(
TiepSon2025
, Sect. 3)
allow a clear explanation. First, for the orthogonal Stiefel manifold, the canonical metric proposed in
(
EdelAS98
, Subsect. 2.3)
is a special case of our proposed metric (
14
) when
\rho=2
and
\Gamma_{3}=I_{n-k}
. Therefore, there is no surprise when the associated orthogonal projection on the tangent space is in closed-form; namely,
(
EdelAS98
, Eq. (2.4))
is a special instance of (
19
). Alternatively, if the Euclidean metric is used, i.e.,
\mathbf{M}_{X}=I_{n}
, equation (
8
) immediately admits the explicit solution
U_{X,Y}=\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}Y)
, which together with
(
TiepSon2025
, Prop. 3.2)
, yields
(
EdelAS98
, Eq. (2.4))
. Second, for the generalized Stiefel manifold, it was suggested in
ShusA2023
;
WangDPY2024
to choose
\mathbf{M}_{X}=A
. This results in the explicit solution
U_{X,Y}=\mathrm{sym}(X^{T}AY)
to (
8
). In view of this,
(
TiepSon2025
, Prop. 3.2)
implies
(
ShusA2023
, Eq. (3.18))
and
(
WangDPY2024
, Eq. (4.3))
. Note, however, that unlike our derivation,
(
ShusA2023
, Eq. (3.18))
was obtained from
(
EdelAS98
, Eq. (2.4))
via a change of variable.

This explanation is also applicable to the geometry of the symplectic Stiefel manifold under the canonical-like metric
GSAS21
as a special case of the tractable metric
GSS2024_2
.

4
Quasi-geodesics with respect to the generalized canonical metric

A geodesic on a manifold is a smooth curve with vanishing acceleration. In the dawn of Riemannian optimization, the solution is updated by searching along the geodesic which goes through the current solution in an appropriate tangent direction
EdelAS98
;
Gaba82
;
Smit94
. In the Euclidean space, zero acceleration means
\ddot{X}(t)=0
which implies that
X(t)=\dot{X}(0)t+X(0)
which is a straight line. That is, geodesics can be viewed as a generalization of lines in the Euclidean space to the Riemannian manifold. In a general setting, the notion of acceleration depends on the Riemannian connection
(
AbsiMS08
, Sect. 5.4)
and therefore the differential equation determining geodesics is usually much more involved. This makes the task of finding an explicit formula for the geodesic, given initial information
X(0),\dot{X}(0)
, very difficult. Fortunately, in optimization, only local information encoded by geodesics and its feasibility are
interesting due to their usage in updating the iterate. Thus, one usually retains these features in approximating geodesics which results in the so-called
quasi-geodesics
, see, e.g.,
JurdML2020
;
KrakMLB2017
;
NishA2005
for more approaches to the quasi-geodesics on the Stiefel manifold.

In this paper, we follow a procedure similar to that of
(
GSAS21
, Subsect. 4.3)
, which was adopted from
(
EdelAS98
, Subsect. 2.2)
. Namely, we replace the acceleration that should be formulated in accordance with the Riemannian connection associated with the proposed generalized canonical metric by the one corresponding to the Euclidean metric, which turns out to be
\ddot{Y}(t).
In view of the normal vectors formulated in Proposition
1
, we define the quasi-geodesic with respect to the
generalized canonical metric
g_{\rho,X_{\perp}}
to be any solution
Y_{\rho}(t)\in\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
of the initial value problem

\ddot{Y}(t)+Y(t)U(t)=0,\ Y(0)=X\in\mathrm{iSt}(k,n),\ \dot{Y}(0)=Z\in T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n),

(25)

with some
U(t)
such that
JU\in\mathrm{Sym}(k)
for all
t\geq 0
, although the obtained result apparently holds for
t<0
too. In this section, a closed-form solution to this equation is presented.
For the sake of brevity, we refrain from writing the variable
t
in our presentation.

As the first step, we show that for the equation (
25
) to have a solution,
U(t)
must have a special form. Consecutively differentiating with respect to
t
both sides of the identity
Y^{T}AY=J
twice
yield

\displaystyle\dot{Y}^{T}AY+Y^{T}A\dot{Y}

\displaystyle=0,

(26)

\displaystyle\ddot{Y}^{T}AY+2\dot{Y}^{T}A\dot{Y}+Y^{T}A\ddot{Y}

\displaystyle=0,

(27)

respectively. Substituting
\ddot{Y}=-YU
from (
25
) into (
27
) gives

(-YU)^{T}AY+2\dot{Y}^{T}A\dot{Y}+Y^{T}A(-YU)=0

(28)

or
\dot{Y}^{T}A\dot{Y}=JU
due to the symmetry of
JU
. Hence,
U(t)=J\dot{Y}^{T}A\dot{Y}
and therefore, the equation (
25
) becomes

\ddot{Y}+YJ(\dot{Y}^{T}A\dot{Y})=0.

(29)

Next, let us set
W\coloneqq Y^{T}A\dot{Y}
and
V\coloneqq\dot{Y}^{T}A\dot{Y}
. Thanks to the relation (
26
),

W\in\mathrm{Skew}(k)
for
t\geq 0.
Moreover, using (
29
), we get

\dot{W}=V+Y^{T}A\ddot{Y}=V-Y^{T}AYJV=V-V=0,\mbox{ for }t\geq 0.

Hence,
W(t)=W(0)\eqqcolon W_{0}\in\mathrm{Skew}(k)
for
t\geq 0
. We proceed to determine
V
which is symmetric. Taking the derivative of
V
with (
29
) in mind, we have that

\displaystyle\dot{V}

\displaystyle=\ddot{Y}^{T}A\dot{Y}+\dot{Y}^{T}A\ddot{Y}=(-YJV)^{T}A\dot{Y}+\dot{Y}^{T}A(-YJV)

\displaystyle=-VJW+WJV=W_{0}JV-VJW_{0}.

We will show that the initial value problem

\dot{V}=W_{0}JV-VJW_{0},\ V(0)=V_{0},

(30)

admits a solution
V(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-tW_{0}^{T}J}V_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}.
Indeed, differentiating such
V(t)
yields

\displaystyle\dot{V}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{e}^{-tW_{0}^{T}J}W_{0}^{T}JV_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}-\mathrm{e}^{-tW_{0}^{T}J}V_{0}JW_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}

\displaystyle=-W_{0}^{T}J\mathrm{e}^{-tW_{0}^{T}J}V_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}-\mathrm{e}^{-tW_{0}^{T}J}V_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}JW_{0}

\displaystyle=-W_{0}^{T}JV-VJW_{0}=W_{0}JV-VJW_{0}.

Here, we invoke the skew-symmetry of
W_{0}
and the identity
M\mathrm{e}^{tM}=\mathrm{e}^{tM}M
for any real square matrix
M
and any real number
t
.

Next, we present a technical lemma, which is similar to
(
GSAS21
, Lem. 4.6)
.

Lemma 1

If
\Theta\in\mathrm{Skew}(k)
and
\Gamma\in\mathrm{Sym}(k),
then for any
t\in\mathbb{R},
it holds that

\left(\mathrm{exp}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}J\Theta&J\Gamma\\
I_{k}&J\Theta\end{bmatrix}\right)\right)^{T}\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{exp}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}J\Theta&J\Gamma\\
I_{k}&J\Theta\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{bmatrix}.

(31)

Proof
For the sake of convenience, let us set
\Lambda\coloneq\left[\begin{smallmatrix}J\Theta&J\Gamma\\
I_{k}&J\Theta\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and the function on the left hand side of (
31
) as
u(t)\coloneq\left(\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Lambda\right)\right)^{T}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Lambda\right)
. Then, it follows that

\dot{u}(t)=\left(\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Lambda\right)\right)^{T}\biggr(\Lambda^{T}\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{bmatrix}\Lambda\biggr)\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Lambda\right).

By assumption, one can easily check that

\Lambda^{T}\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{bmatrix}\Lambda=0.

Therefore,
\dot{u}(t)=0
which leads to the fact that
u(t)=u(0)=\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{bmatrix},
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}.

∎

Now, we are ready to give the explicit formula for a solution to the problem (
25
).

Proposition 4

Given
X\in\mathrm{iSt}(k,n),Z\in T_{X}\mathrm{iSt}(k,n).
The initial value problem

\ddot{Y}(t)+Y(t)J\dot{Y}(t)^{T}A\dot{Y}(t)=0,\ Y(0)=X,\ \dot{Y}(0)=Z,

(32)

has a unique global solution in
\mathrm{iSt}(k,n)
given by

Y_{\rho}(t)=Y_{\rho}^{\mathrm{qgeo}}(t,Z)\coloneqq[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}JW_{0}&-JV_{0}\\
I_{k}&JW_{0}\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}},

(33)

where
W_{0}=Y_{\rho}^{T}(0)A\dot{Y}_{\rho}(0)=X^{T}AZ
and
V_{0}\coloneq\dot{Y}_{\rho}^{T}(0)A\dot{Y}_{\rho}(0)=Z^{T}AZ.

Proof

First, let us assume that the problem (
32
) has a global solution in
\mathrm{iSt}(k,n),
denoted also by
Y_{\rho}(t)
. It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(Y_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\dot{Y_{\rho}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}+Y_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}JW_{0}.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\dot{Y}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\ddot{Y_{\rho}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}+\dot{Y}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}JW_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(-Y_{\rho}(t)JV\right)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}+\dot{Y}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}JW_{0}

\displaystyle=-Y_{\rho}(t)J\mathrm{e}^{-tW_{0}^{T}J}V_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}+\dot{Y}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}JW_{0}

\displaystyle=-Y_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}JV_{0}+\dot{Y}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}JW_{0},

in which we have invoked (
30
) to replace

V\coloneq\dot{Y_{\rho}}^{T}A\dot{Y_{\rho}}
by its explicit form
V=\mathrm{e}^{-tW_{0}^{T}J}V_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}

when
Y_{\rho}(t)\in\mathrm{iSt}(k,n),
for all
t\geq 0.

Therefore,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[Y_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}\;\;\dot{Y}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}\right]=\left[Y_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}\;\;\dot{Y}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}\right]\begin{bmatrix}JW_{0}&-JV_{0}\\
I_{k}&JW_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(34)

This basic equation admits an explicit solution from which we obtain that

\displaystyle Y_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=\left[Y_{\rho}(0)\;\;\dot{Y}_{\rho}(0)\right]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}JW_{0}&-JV_{0}\\
I_{k}&JW_{0}\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}

\displaystyle=[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}JW_{0}&-JV_{0}\\
I_{k}&JW_{0}\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}},

which is the expected formula.

Next, we will show that this is indeed a global solution to the problem (
32
). For brevity, we set
\Psi\coloneq\left[\begin{smallmatrix}JW_{0}&-JV_{0}\\
I_{k}&JW_{0}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. In view of (
33
), it holds that

\dot{Y}_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}+Y_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}JW_{0}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(Y_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}\right)=[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\Psi\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

This leads to

\displaystyle\dot{Y}_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}=

\displaystyle[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\Psi\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}\\
0\end{bmatrix}-[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}\\
0\end{bmatrix}JW_{0}

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\biggr(\begin{bmatrix}JW_{0}\\
I_{k}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}JW_{0}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\biggr)=[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\begin{bmatrix}0\\
I_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

Repeating this procedure with
\dot{Y}_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}
, we obtain

\ddot{Y}_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}+\dot{Y}_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}JW_{0}=[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\Psi\begin{bmatrix}0\\
I_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

Using (
35
), this identity gives

\ddot{Y}_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{tJW_{0}}=[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\begin{bmatrix}-JV_{0}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(36)

On the other hand, using the expression for
\dot{Y}_{\rho}
in (
35
), we receive the identities

\displaystyle\dot{Y}_{\rho}^{T}A\dot{Y}_{\rho}=

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}\right)^{T}[0\;\;I_{k}]\left(\mathrm{exp}(t\Psi)\right)^{T}\begin{bmatrix}X^{T}\\
Z^{T}\end{bmatrix}A[X\;\;Z]\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\begin{bmatrix}0\\
I_{k}\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{e}^{-tW_{0}^{T}J}[0\;\;I_{k}]\left(\mathrm{exp}(t\Psi)\right)^{T}\begin{bmatrix}J&W_{0}\\
-W_{0}&V_{0}\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\begin{bmatrix}0\\
I_{k}\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{e}^{-tW_{0}^{T}J}[0\;\;I_{k}]\left(\mathrm{exp}(t\Psi)\right)^{T}\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{bmatrix}\Psi\mathrm{exp}\left(t\Psi\right)\begin{bmatrix}0\\
I_{k}\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-tJW_{0}}

\displaystyle=

A Riemannian gradient descent method

To minimize a continuously differentiable cost function
f
on the indefinite Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{iSt}_{A,J}
,

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}f(X)\ \mbox{ s.t. }\ X^{T}AX=J,

(42)

given an initial point
X_{0}\in\mathrm{iSt}_{A,J}(k,n)
, one consecutively updates the approximate solution by

X_{j+1}=\mathcal{R}_{X_{j}}(-\tau_{j}\mathrm{grad}f(X_{j})),

where
\mathcal{R}_{X_{j}}(\cdot)
is a retraction at
X_{j}
,
\mathrm{grad}f(X_{j})
denotes the Riemannian gradient of the cost function
f
at
X_{j}
with respect to a chosen Riemannian metric
\mathrm{g}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and
\tau_{j}>0
is a step size to be determined. To compute the step size, we combine a nonmonotone line search technique with the alternating Barzilai-Borwein strategy on an embedded submanifold, see
BarB88
;
HuLWY2020
;
IannP18
;
ZhanH2004

Discussion

Through examples presented, we can first see that, all newly established theoretical materials work properly and compatibly with the existing ones. Second, as expected, for the case that
k
is not small, the schemes using the generalized canonical metric always need less time to be convergent (with respect to the given
rstop
) compared to the ones using the Euclidean metric; even in the case of Procrustes problem, within at most 2000 iterations, only the schemes with the generalized canonical metric converge. Third, the quasi-geodesic retraction apparently is less feasible than the Cayley retraction. This fact might come from the instability of the scaling and squaring algorithm invoked by
expm
in MATLAB which occasionally occurs as pointed out in
GuetN2016
.

7
Conclusions

To avoid having to solve the Lyapunov matrix equation during the computation of
Riemannian gradient for optimization on the indefinite Stiefel manifold, we proposed the generalized canonical metric and constructed the necessary associated material for running a Riemannian gradient descent scheme, including a retraction based on a quasi-geodesic corresponding to the new metric. The presented numerical examples verified the theoretical findings and suggested that, using the generalized canonical metric with the Cayley retraction is perhaps the most potential combination since it needs less time to reach a desired approximate solution and delivers better feasibility.
