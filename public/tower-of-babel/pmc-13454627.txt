Explainable artificial intelligence with pyramid vision transformer model for multi-class malignant cell classification on cytology slides
Authors: Hussain Alshahrani, Radwa Marzouk, Mona Almofarreh, Majdy M. Eltahir, Hana Almagrabi, Randa Allafi, Nadiyah Almutairi, Saad A. AlOwayyed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Digital pathology involves the digitisation of histology slides, which is vital in modern healthcare, especially for cancer detection and diagnosis. Still, the manual analysis of these digital slides by expert pathologists is time-consuming and prone to inconsistencies. This workload often results in subjective grading and a lack of agreement between different pathologists (inter-operator variability) and even with the same pathologist over time (intra-operator variability). With the advent of whole slide imaging (WSI) and high computational ability, the application of deep learning (DL), particularly convolutional neural networks, has developed rapidly in the past decades in the domain of digital pathology. Therefore, this study introduces a Hybrid Temporal Deep Learning Network for Automated Malignant Cell Classification in Cytology Slides (HTDL-MCCCS) model. The objective is to improve diagnostic consistency, reduce workload, and enable scalable screening in resource-limited clinical settings. The HTDL-MCCCS model consists of four integrated stages: First, slide pre-processing is performed using stain normalisation, artefact removal, patch extraction, and nuclei segmentation to enhance morphological clarity. Second, the pyramid vision transformer (PVT) is utilised to learn robust cell-level features from unlabeled slide patches. Third, the bidirectional temporal recurrent unit (BiTRU) is employed for the automated classification of malignant and benign cells. Lastly, a detailed analysis using Grad-CAM is included to support clinical interpretability and trust. A wide-ranging simulation analysis of the HTDL-MCCCS methodology portrayed superior outcomes of 98.16% and 96.42% under the SIPaKMeD and Herlev datasets.

Introduction

Microscopic diagnosis and classification are essential prerequisites for treating all forms of cancer. It is carried out subjectively on tissues or cells through a microscope or cytologically by qualified individuals, mainly pathologists1. Intellectual cytotechnicians can support in pre-screening cytologic specimens. Tissue acquisition for biopsy requires local anaesthesia and the use of scalpels or needles2. They are noninvasively acquired either through a brush mucosal surface (bile ducts, oral, conjunctival, pharyngeal, bronchial, laryngeal, perianal, or cervical) or in body fluids (sputa, serous effusions, bronchial secretions, cerebrospinal fluid, and urine)3. Patient adherence to the recommendation of cytological analysis of a specified doubtful lesion is accordingly greater than for histopathological analysis. Due to their painlessness and non-invasiveness, cytological analysis is also utilised for large-scale screening4. More prominent and recognised cytologic tests are for cancer, as reported by the Papanicolaou test, also called the Pap smear. The Pap smear test looks for plenty of cancer cells and analyses these cells under a microscope for any abnormalities5. Apart from that, widespread cancer cell-positive cytological identification is further evaluated by histological individuals under specimens of the removed specific lesions, and the diagnostic efficacy gains higher levels6. Cytopathology introduces a field of pathology and requires significant diagnostic training7. Screening cytological specimens requires great attention, with time taken and tiring8. Accordingly, the accessibility of a computational microscope that must have the ability to mechanically screen cytological tissues from different parts of the human body for the existence of cancer tissues and their indications is advantageous9.

It could not change cytopathologists or cyto-technicians, who only gain their time and focus on its complex research to resolve desirable diagnostic issues and validate suspicious or positive diagnoses10. Current developments in artificial intelligence (AI) as well as deep learning (DL) have innovated the area of medical diagnosis, presenting effective tools to overcome the intrinsic limitations of conventional cytology11. The above-mentioned drawbacks, namely the labour-intensive nature of manual assessment, inter-observer variability, and subjectivity in analysis, may cause diagnostic errors and interruptions in patient care12. Especially, DL shows outstanding capabilities in evaluating difficult medical images, giving the possibilities to automate and improve diagnosis procedures13. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are developed as a basic structure in this field, confirming extreme efficiency in different cell classification and detection tasks14. This automatic dissimilarity can decrease the dependence on biased visual analysis, possibly resulting in more reliable and constant diagnoses15. Likewise, CNNs have demonstrated potential in the detection of white blood cells (WBCs), a critical stage to monitor immune system function and diagnose haematological disease16. Moreover, DL-based techniques exhibit robust performance in leukocyte identification, while faced with challenging image conditions like the existence of artefacts or low image quality17. Such capacities lead to the emergence of more scalable and reliable diagnostic medical devices18.

To address the challenges of unreliable, poor slide quality, and morphological variability, and constrained diagnostic precision in malignant cell classification, this research introduces a Hybrid Temporal Deep Learning Network for Automated Malignant Cell Classification in Cytology Slides (HTDL-MCCCS) methodology. The HTDL-MCCCS architecture incorporates advanced slide pre-processing, vision transformer-based feature extraction, DL-driven classification, and XAI to provide accurate and robust diagnostic support. Proposed model architecture

The HTDL-MCCCS model introduces a robust method for classifying cell malignancy from cytology slides. Figure 1 presents the comprehensive architectural design of the HTDL-MCCCS technique, demonstrating the incorporation of four primary sub-steps: the slide pre-processing step, the feature extraction step, the classification step, and the integration of Grad-CAM.

These steps are envisioned to accurately classify cell malignancy, which makes the model more robust and versatile for real-time use.

Step 1: Pre-processing: Applied stain normalisation, artefact removal, patch extraction, and nuclei segmentation to standardise slide quality, eliminate redundant information, and identify crucial biological structures for precise computer-aided analysis and diagnosis.

Step 2: Feature Extraction: Utilised PVT to capture hierarchical cell-level features from pre-processed slides by using a pyramid structure for processing information at multiple scales.

Step 3: Classification: The BiTRU is designed to perform accurate classification. This hybrid model accurately classifies cells into benign and malignant.

Step 4: Grad-CAM: Grad-CAM, an XAI model utilised to present transparency and interpretability to complex DL models employed in fields like medical image analysis, including the classification of malignant cells in cytology slides.

Transformer-based feature learning

For feature extraction, the PVT is deployed to learn reliable cell-level features from unlabeled slide patches. The PVT manages global and local visual attributes in its procedure of extraction. PVT collects vital image elements from the entire region of the image without reliance on local process, making it suitable for analysing the spread of diagnostic markers32. In several analysis scales, PVT differentiates better image components with its capability to recognise an extensive imagery pattern that aids clinical specialists in interpreting cytology slide data. The multi-scale design framework allows for the processing of intricate patterns or shape features. The transformer‐based method allows more effective processing of higher-resolution slides, leading to enhanced outcomes.

In the training process, the PVT method accepted its weights from ImageNet, along with substantial data. The technique employs image processing features to concentrate on identifying. The improved parameters of containers received imagery for component identification, which led to improved image recognition capability. The PVT-Small version is employed, balancing higher representation capability with computation efficacy for clinical image categorisation tasks. The structure of the PVT method comprises many layers of transformer encoder in every layer, analysing image patches from particular levels of scale. Transformers in every encoded layer send their outcome to the following phases, thus creating integrated hierarchical aspects. In contrast to standard vision transformers, the PVT integrates a hierarchical framework that gradually lessens the spatial resolution of mapping attributes through many phases. Performance evaluation parameters

To calculate the execution of the proposed classification technique, standard performance measures were utilised. In the specified class distribution, the succeeding measures are employed to ensure an extensive assessment:

Accuracy (Overall Success Rate (OSR)): Calculate the entire ratio of properly categorised instances.20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:OSR=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}$$\end{document}

Here, TP and TN imply true positives and true negatives; however, FP and FN signify false positives and false negatives.

Precision (Positive predictive Value (PPV)): Assess the ratio of properly recognised positive samples among all positive instances.21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:PPV=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$$\end{document}

Sensitivity (True Positive Rate (TPR)): Assess the capability of the method to recognise actual positive instances.22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:TPR=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$\end{document}

High recall assures that the method does not miss a substantial number of instances.

F-Statistic: It presents a balanced measurement of precision and recall:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F-Statistic=2\times\:\frac{Precision\times\:Recall}{Precision+Recall}$$\end{document}

ROC-AUC: The ROC curve plots the true positive rate (TPR) against the false positive rate (FPR) through many threshold settings. The AUC determines the overall discriminative proficiency of the model through each probable classification threshold.24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:FPR=\frac{FP}{FP+TN},\:TPR=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$\end{document}

Conclusion

In this manuscript, a novel HTDL-MCCCS model is presented for improving diagnostic consistency, reducing workload, and facilitating scalable screening in resource-constrained clinical settings. The HTDL-MCCCS framework encompasses four phases: Initially, slide pre-processing is carried out using stain normalisation, artefact removal, patch extraction, and nuclei segmentation to improve morphological clarity. Next, the PVT is deployed for learning effective cell-level features from unlabeled slide patches. Additionally, the BiTRU is developed for the automated classification of malignant and benign cells. Finally, XAI analysis utilising Grad-CAM to support clinical interpretability and trust. To demonstrate the superiority of the HTDL-MCCCS methodology, experiments were conducted on three benchmark cervical cytology datasets, namely SIPaKMeD, Herlev, and APCData. The comprehensive simulation analysis highlights the enhanced performance and efficiency of the proposed HTDL-MCCCS method compared with recent state-of-the-art models. A wide-ranging simulation analysis of the HTDL-MCCCS methodology portrayed superior outcomes of 98.16 and 96.42% under the SIPaKMeD and Herlev datasets. These outcomes demonstrate that the HTDL-MCCCS approach offers greater classification performance, enhanced robustness across multiple datasets, and improved computational efficiency. Therefore, the proposed framework offers a reliable and scalable solution for automated cervical cytology analysis and can be efficiently applied to large-scale medical image-based diagnostic systems. Therefore, the HTDL-MCCCS technique is found to be an efficient and robust tool for classifying malignant cells from cytology slides. Limitations can be observed due to the dependence on small datasets, restricted generalisation, and lack of real-world cytology slides, mitigating clinical applicability. Moreover, most studies have no multi-centre validation, limiting robustness across diverse laboratories. Future work should concentrate on collecting larger, diverse datasets and integrating the models with WSI for enhancing real-world performance and reliability.
