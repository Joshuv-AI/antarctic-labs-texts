Pseudoephedrine for Central Sympathetic Insufficiency
Authors: Jessica Wcislo, Andrew Posen, Jared Davis
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Various autonomic nuclei mediate control over the sympathetic nervous system. Specifically, stimulation of the posterior hypothalamus results in activation, increasing blood pressure (BP) and heart rate (HR).12 Herein, we describe a patient with lesions in the posterior hypothalamus associated with reduced sympathetic drive, and treatment with a readily available sympathomimetic drug as a temporary solution.

A 40-year-old male was found outside of a police station in a state of confusion. His reported medical history included diabetes mellitus, human immunodeficiency virus infection, and cancer with recent brain biopsy. Following transport to a nearby hospital, a CT scan of the head demonstrated an abnormality in the left basal ganglia. MRI with contrast sequencing revealed a 3 cm ring-enhancing lesion in the sellar and basal forebrain, extending between the left and right midbrain and in the left-posterior hypothalamus (Fig. 1). These findings prompted transfer to our institution’s neurosurgical intensive care unit (NSICU) for evaluation.

The patient arrived with temperature 33.6℃, HR 60–80 bpm, and BP 76/58 mm Hg. Due to concern for multifactorial shock, he received antibiotics, norepinephrine, and corticosteroids. A lumbar puncture with an opening pressure of 19 cm H2O and mildly elevated protein (77 mg/dL) was otherwise unremarkable. No infection was identified and antibiotics were discontinued.

Outside records detailed a central nervous system (CNS) lymphoma diagnosed by stereotactic neurosurgical biopsy and flow cytometry positive for Epstein–Barr virus. Having previously received two cycles of chemotherapy, consultation with hematology-oncology favored re-initiation, however this was delayed by ongoing need for vasoactive support. Goal BP was achieved with norepinephrine, but profound bradycardia persisted (35 to 45 bpm). Norepinephrine was transitioned to simultaneous dopamine and isoproterenol to restore both normotension and baseline chronotropy. Attempts to wean led to recurrence of marked hypotension and bradycardia. Electrocardiography and echocardiography were overtly normal. Consultation by Neurocritical Care and Electrophysiology Cardiology attributed bradycardia to low sympathetic and high vagal tone. This is most consistent with mass effect created by the CNS lymphoma on the posterior hypothalamus, decreasing sympathetic outflow. Since the bradycardia was asymptomatic and chronotrope-responsive, he was not a candidate for electrical pacing.

After two days of dopamine and isoproterenol dependence, enteral pseudoephedrine (PSE) was trialed. Isoproterenol was stopped after the first dose and dopamine was discontinued shortly after the second (Supplementary Fig. 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). Residual bradycardic episodes in the first 24 hours prompted increasing the dose to 60 mg every 6 hours, which prevented further episodes. The patient remained off intravenous vasoactives and transferred services for initiation of chemotherapy. PSE was eventually tapered for a total 12-day course.

PSE is a stereoisomer of ephedrine primarily used as a sinus decongestant.345 It also distributes to the brain and stimulates the cardiovascular system by promoting catecholamine release and directly agonizing alpha and beta-adrenergic receptors.67

Our patient had decreased sympathetic output due to a posterior hypothalamic mass effect, with incomplete response to steroids and norepinephrine, necessitating simultaneous dopamine and isoproterenol. Chemotherapy would be the most definitive treatment, but was precluded by dependence on intravenous vasoactives and delicate hemodynamic monitoring in the NSICU. While midodrine, theophylline, and pyridostigmine represent alternative options, our patient’s clinical syndrome was mediated by neurogenic central insufficiency of both alpha and beta-adrenergic outflow. Therefore, PSE was chosen for its activity at both receptor pathways and found to be effective.

Based on our review of published literature, the application of PSE in a patient with a posterior hypothalamic and neurogenic sympathetic deficiency is unique. PSE has been used in patients with autonomic dysfunction, specifically due to cervical spinal cord insults.8910 In those cases, the daily dose averaged between 120–240 mg, similar to what we found effective. PSE is orally administered as a tablet or can be crushed and made into solution, serving as a useful therapy to supplant intravenous vasoactives.

There are variables potentially confounding our observed benefit with PSE. Corticosteroids, including fludrocortisone, were administered for a clinical syndrome mimicking adrenal insufficiency, unconfirmed by objective evidence. These may have contributed to the patient’s improvement, although unlikely, because supplementation for three days preceding PSE’s initiation did not attenuate vasopressor dependence. Upon starting PSE, isoproterenol was abruptly weaned and incremental benefits were seen as the dose increased. Unfortunately, imaging correlating reduced mass effect with PSE’s tapering following chemotherapy is unavailable.

We described a case of insufficient sympathetic outflow due to a posterior mass effect necessitating vasoactive therapy. Treatment with PSE facilitated a shortened NSICU course, expediting access to curative chemotherapy. For patients affected by neurogenic hypotension and bradycardia due to posterior hypothalamus pathology, we suggest PSE as a temporizing measure.
