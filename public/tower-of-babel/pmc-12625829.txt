Racial and ethnic variation in perceived relative harm of tobacco and nicotine products: an experimental study
Authors: Teresa DeAtley, Matthew D Stone, Andrea C Johnson, Melissa Mercincavage, Andrea C Villanti, David W Wetter, Janet Audrain-McGovern, Cristine Delnevo, Andrew A Strasser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionConverging lines of research indicate that the public misinterprets the relative harms associated with different tobacco and nicotine products. These misperceptions may contribute to persistent tobacco-related health disparities. To inform regulatory actions in a growing tobacco market, it is essential to understand how harm perceptions differ across tobacco and nicotine products among racial and ethnic groups to ultimately decrease disparities.MethodsAn online sample (n=897) of US adults completed a discrete choice experiment, known as a Maximum Difference Likelihood task, which is an orthogonally balanced approach designed to assess harm perceptions of 18 tobacco products that span a risk continuum. Participants selected the most and least harmful products. We estimated probability-scaled relative harm perception scores using a multinomial logit hierarchical Bayesian analysis. We then conducted linear regressions to look at differences by race and ethnicity using estimated marginal means and contrasts for each product while controlling for smoking status, age and sex.ResultsThe analyses revealed that participants’ harm perceptions do not align with the established scientific literature for combustibles, smokeless tobacco and non-combustible tobacco products. We observed low harm perceptions for products with high population rates of use, such as cigarillos and little cigars for non-Hispanic (NH) Black or African American, and Latino individuals. We observed high harm perceptions for products with low population use, such as plug tobacco for NH Asian individuals, e-cigarettes for NH Black or African American and Latino individuals and nicotine replacement therapy for NH Black or African Americans.ConclusionGiven the known tobacco-related health disparities among people of color, our results underscore the need to correct the tobacco product harm misperceptions among certain races and ethnicities, such as NH Black or African Americans, Latinos, NH Asians and NH Indigenous or mixed-race individuals.Keywords: Public Health, Cross-Sectional Studies, Community Health, Population Surveillance

Introduction

Among the estimated 46 million adults in the USA who use tobacco products, population-level survey data reveals distinct patterns of commercially available tobacco product use among racial and ethnic groups. Data from the 2021 National Health Interview Survey documents that non-Hispanic (NH) lack individuals have the highest reported rates of cigar and pipe use. Whereas NH White individuals have the highest reported rate of smokeless tobacco use and the second highest rate of e-cigarette use.1 Collectively, these differential patterns of tobacco use underscore the importance of assessing factors that influence use, such as harm perceptions.

Differential patterns of tobacco use occur against a backdrop of steady proliferation of emerging tobacco and nicotine products. Over the last decade, cigarette consumption has declined, while new products such as electronic cigarettes, heated tobacco products (HTPs) and oral nicotine products have entered the US market.2 3 Entry of these new products has been linked to industry ‘transformation’ efforts that have publicly focused on increasing sales of new products, some of which are lower in risk4 than combustible cigarettes while doing little to reduce sales of more harmful tobacco products.5 One concern is that some of the new products that have entered the market have received marketing authorisation from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as modified-risk tobacco products (MRTP). However, research indicates that individuals often misunderstand the relative risks and harms of MRTP claims,6 7 further complicating how individuals equate the relative harms of tobacco and nicotine products within a diverse product landscape. Misperceptions may increase the health risks associated with tobacco use or may reduce the effectiveness of harm reduction strategies, such as using nicotine replacement therapy to help an individual quit.

Substitution of reduced-risk products, a form of harm reduction, is a key approach aimed to reduce tobacco-related morbidity and mortality. Harm reduction strategies within a tobacco regulatory framework assume that tobacco and nicotine products fall on a continuum of risk, with some posing higher relative harm to others.8 To date, general consensus has been reached that while no form of tobacco is safe for human health, the use of smokeless tobacco presents fewer health risks than combustible cigarettes, and that non-combustible tobacco products contain lower levels of toxicants than combustible tobacco products.9 In the field of tobacco regulatory science, harm reduction refers to the development of strategies that effectively move individuals away from using the most harmful tobacco products to those that have less negative health effects or using cessation aids to quit completely.

Accurate knowledge about the harms of tobacco product use has been linked to meaningful health promotion behaviours10 such as cessation11 and thought processes, such as intention to quit.12 Recent scientific interest has focused on the consequences of the public’s misperception about the health risks of nicotine.13 Lower perceived harm has been linked to a higher likelihood of tobacco product use among US adults.14 Given that regulatory efforts in the USA are structured to facilitate the use of less harmful products and discourage the use of the most harmful products,15 achieving the full public health impact of these efforts may be influenced by tobacco users and non-users’ knowledge and beliefs about nicotine in relation to specific products.13 Importantly, longitudinal data from the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health (PATH) study has shown that misperceptions about nicotine may have a negative impact on harm reduction efforts, such as quit success or decreased likelihood of using cessation aids.16 Furthermore, beliefs about nicotine are strongly linked to the perceived harms of specific products.17

Very little research has focused on understanding the tobacco product harm perceptions among racial and ethnic minorities,6 despite high rates of combustible tobacco use among these populations. Existing research has documented that relative to NH White individuals, racial and ethnic minorities are more likely to believe nicotine causes cancer.18 Similarly, Nyman et al found that African Americans believe that cigarillos are less addictive than cigarettes; yet, it is still unclear why this perception is held.19 None of these studies tested harm perceptions across a continuum of currently available tobacco and nicotine products with varying levels of potential health harms to assess relative comparisons among different racial and ethnic groups. The goal of our study was to systematically examine the perceived health harms in the emerging and diverse tobacco marketplace to better understand how racial and ethnic minorities perceive different tobacco and nicotine products that span a continuum of risk, to inform future tobacco regulatory efforts.

Study data and methods

This study uses data from a discrete-choice task that was administered at the end of two independent online crowdsourcing experiments conducted through Prolific between July and October 2022.20 One experiment assessed perceptions of tobacco products with and without modified risk claims, varying in nicotine content.6 The second experiment evaluated the impact of different potential regulatory actions (e.g., graphic health warning labels) on perceived worth and willingness to pay for cigarette packs. After participants completed the primary task of each respective experiment on Qualtrics, participants were redirected to Sawtooth Lighthouse Studio to complete a discrete choice experiment with a Maximum Difference (MaxDiff) Likelihood task design. Briefly, a discrete choice experiment is a tool that can be used to understand individual preferences when presented with a series of options that may vary by specific attributes. In a MaxDiff Likelihood task design, participants are forced to rank order all items from least preferred to most preferred.

Once participants from both surveys were routed to the Sawtooth Lighthouse Studio platform, participants were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either a full task or a modified task. In the modified task, participants were able to indicate familiarity with the product before taking the task. The MaxDiff Likelihood task was developed collaboratively between the principal investigators of each pilot study (authors TD, MDS) and used identical task procedures. The results from the full MaxDiff Likelihood task are the focus of the current paper.

Ethics statement

This study was determined to be of minimal risk to participants and was deemed eligible for expedited review. Given that this was an experimental study and data was collected from participants online, it was not appropriate to involve participants in the design, conduct or reporting of these research findings.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Sample

In the modified risk experiment, eligible individuals were aged 21–65 years old and had a self-reported smoking status of people who have never smoked, people who currently smoke, people who have recently smoked or other. A person who had never smoked was defined through Prolific as smoking fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. A person who currently smokes was defined as smoking at least five cigarettes a day for at least 1 year. A person who has recently smoked was defined as an individual who smoked five cigarettes a day for less than 1 year. ‘Other’ was a prescreening eligibility criterion for the survey that was only available to people who indicated they smoked. To meet our purposive race and ethnicity sampling, people who smoke and people who had never smoked completed separate surveys and were identified with separate prescreeners. This study oversampled the following US-based racial and ethnic demographic groups: NH Black or African American, NH Asians (inclusive of Pacific Islander, Southeast Asian, East Asian and South Asian), Latinos, and American Indians or Alaskan Natives (AI/AN).6 Sampling for this study was determined by power for appropriate demographic subgroup analysis, where we aimed to recruit 250 individuals per racial and ethnic subgroup. In this study, 1639 individuals were screened for eligibility, 5% of participants were excluded because they did not finish the survey. While not mutually exclusive, an additional 0.1% had a bot score of less than 0.5 based on Qualtrics reCAPTCHA software and 0.9% did not respond to attention checks.4 The health warning labels experiment included participants aged 21–65, with a self-reported smoking status of people who currently smoke and people who have recently smoked using Prolific’s predefined criteria. People who have never smoked were not eligible to participate in the health warning label experiment. This experiment recruited a stratified sample relative to the US population by sampling participants from four US geographical regions (South, West, Midwest and Northeast). For the health warning label experiment, simulation-based power calculations in Sawtooth Lighthouse Studio indicated a target sample size of 1575 participants to provide 80% power at 95% confidence to detect small utility differences. All participants who completed the harm perception or graphic health warning label surveys were automatically linked to the MaxDiff Likelihood task, as this was a required field to complete the parent survey. Once in the Sawtooth Lighthouse Studio platform, participants were randomised to the full task or partial task.

The full Maximum Difference Likelihood task

The full MaxDiff Likelihood task was designed to assess the perceived harm of 18 different nicotine and tobacco products encompassing a spectrum of risk regardless of familiarity with the products. The 18 products were as follows: nicotine replacement therapy (FDA-approved nicotine lozenge and nicotine gum), snus, snuff, plug tobacco, loose leaf chewing tobacco, hookah, roll your own cigarette, little cigar, cigarillo, clove cigarette, cigar, bidi, e-cigarettes (one with traditional nicotine salt, one with synthetic nicotine salt, and one with nicotine salt), HTP and a conventional non-flavoured manufactured cigarette.

At the start of the task, participants read a brief description explaining that the task would address their perceptions of various tobacco and nicotine products with a series of different images. Participants were randomly assigned to either the full task or relevant items in a dynamic task. The dynamic task was conditioned on the following question: Which of the following tobacco products are you familiar with (e.g., have knowledge of, have heard of, or have used before)? In the dynamic task, participants were only queried on the products they indicated familiarity with. Given the exploratory nature of our research question, this paper focuses on the results from the full task only. Participants randomised to the full task were given a unique set of 19 questions, each question comprised of 4 products representing 18 unique products. For each question, participants were asked to select the product that they thought was the most harmful and the least harmful before proceeding to the next question. Based on prior work,21 question sets were selected from a pool of 300 partial profile design variants. Design variants were created to achieve orthogonal balance (Lighthouse Studio V.9.8.1; Sawtooth Software, Provo, Utah, USA) and ensured orthogonal balance (each product appeared an equal number of times with every other product), level balance (each product was shown the same number of times across sets) and varied presentation order (items appeared in different positions across sets).21 22 This design ensured that each product was evaluated three times per respondent while preserving statistical efficiency for estimating relative harm perceptions. Each product was displayed with a generic, unbranded image and a label (eg, ‘nicotine gum’, ‘heated tobacco product’), but no additional descriptions were provided. The present manuscript focuses on the full task to establish a baseline harm perception across all products, regardless of familiarity. A separate analysis is planned to compare perceptions of products that participants reported being familiar with versus those they were not familiar with.

Stimuli

Tobacco product images were pulled from the web, specifying the product type and using the search terms ‘unbranded’ and ‘plain background’. Each image was placed on a white background and resized. One image was used for each tobacco product. Four products retained a marketing element; for the HTP, the product name, IQOS, was shown on the heat stick holder and the charging device. For the e-cigarette with nicotine salt image, Puffbar was visible on the bottom of the product. Lastly, for nicotine gum and nicotine lozenge, we opted to display the products in their packaging as opposed to the singular lozenge or piece of gum. For consistency, we choose to use generic Amazon brands for the two nicotine replacement therapy products. To minimise visual attention and attraction differences for products that were brand-free, we chose generic images and colours that were not bright or eye-catching (e.g., products that were black or grey).

Statistical analyses

Our primary aim was to systematically examine the relative harm perceptions of various tobacco and nicotine products, focusing on differences by race and ethnicity.

To link relevant demographic information from Qualtrics to Sawtooth, we created one master file for a complete case analysis. Our master file included a priori covariates that were used in Sawtooth to generate the estimated probability-scaled harm perception scores for all 18 products. We controlled for smoking status, age, sex, race and ethnicity, and parent study experiment condition. If a participant’s prolific ID was present in both surveys, we used the data for the MaxDiff task from the survey that was completed first based on time-stamped information from Qualtrics. Smoking status was recoded as: people who smoke cigarettes (people who currently smoke, people who have recently smoked, other people who smoke) and people who have never smoked. Race and ethnicity were collapsed into the following demographic groups: NH Black or African American, Latino, NH Asian, NH Indigenous or mixed-race or NH White.

Probability-scaled relative harm perception scores were estimated using a multinomial logit hierarchical Bayesian (HB) analysis. HB analysis is commonly used in choice experiments because it combines information from the whole sample with data from each individual, producing more reliable estimates of how people rank products while still capturing differences between individuals. Bayesian approaches rely on repeated sampling from probability distributions (iterations) to estimate stable parameter values, similar to repeatedly running the model until results stop changing. The initial samples are often unstable, so a ‘burn-in’ period is used to discard early iterations before the model reaches a stable distribution. In this study, the model was run for 40 000 iterations (20 000 burn-in and 20 000 inference), following recommended standards in Sawtooth Lighthouse Studio. Convergence was checked by confirming that parameter estimates stabilised over the sampling process. As a sensitivity check, we also ran models with fewer iterations (20 000 total) and found nearly identical outcomes, giving us confidence in the robustness of our findings. To quantify the relative harm perceptions across products, scores were ratio-scaled with probability scores across products summing to 100. To distinguish the relative harm perception score differences by race and ethnicity, ordinary least squares regressions were run. We then estimated the marginal means and pairwise contrasts for each product with a Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons. All models controlled for age, sex and smoking status. In line with prior studies,4 23 we combine race and ethnicity, which served as our primary predictor of interest. We organised our results by product category for ease of interpretation and reported the standardised mean differences (contrasts) that were statistically significant at a p value of less than 0.05 and the marginal means at or below a p value of less than 0.05.24 Analyses were conducted in Lighthouse Studio25 and Stata.26

Study results

The task was completed by an online sample (n=897) of US adult people who smoke and people who have never smoked. The average age of all participants (both people who smoke and people who have never smoked) was 35.7 (SD=11.0) years. We had an almost equal number of females (n=444, 49.5%) and males (n=453, 50.5%) participate in the task. Slightly over half of our sample had a college education or above (n=495, 55.2%) and identified as people who currently smoke or people who have recently smoked (n=472, 52.6%) compared with those who identified as people who have never smoked (n=425, 47.4%). Overall, 38.1% (n=342) of individuals identified as NH White, 17.9% (n=161) identified as NH Black or African American, 22.2% (n=199) identified as Latino, 19.1% (n=172) identified as NH Asian and 2.7% (n=23) identified as NH Indigenous or mixed-race.

Overall, cigarettes had the highest relative probability of being perceived as the most harmful product (11.08%), followed by cigars (9.55%), snuff (8.07%) and plug tobacco (8.17%). Across all products, e-cigarettes were perceived as being lower in harm relative to cigarettes, starting with an estimated marginal mean of (4.09%) for e-cigarettes with traditional nicotine salts, followed by e-cigarettes with synthetic nicotine salts (3.69%) and e-cigarettes with nicotine salts (3.68%). IQOS, nicotine gum and nicotine lozenge were considered the three least harmful products relative to all other tested products with an estimated marginal mean of (2.31%), (0.36%) and (0.46%), respectively (table 1).

Discussion

The present study contributes to tobacco regulatory science by seeking to understand differences in relative harm perceptions of tobacco and nicotine products among racial and ethnic minorities that span the harm continuum; and highlights where these efforts can be further targeted. Overall, our pattern of results illustrates the complexities of harm perceptions versus actual harms across the continuum of tobacco and nicotine products and how this differs across a diverse population. Development of messages that communicate complex scientific concepts accounting for the full continuum of harm may reduce inaccurate harm perceptions for specific products.

Several combustible tobacco products were consistently perceived as being lower in harm relative to combustible cigarettes: roll your own, little cigars, cigarillos and bidis. Importantly, products in this category are known to deliver the highest levels of harmful chemicals.8 Given that harm perceptions are an important determinant of tobacco use behaviour, which is linked to initiation, experimentation and patterns of use, it is important to address misperceptions of specific products that have higher use among specific racial and ethnic groups.27 For example, data from both the PATH survey and the National Adult Tobacco Survey show that cigarillo and cigar use is high among individuals who identify as NH Black or African American (82.6%), reflecting a substantial majority of cigar users.28 29 Furthermore, longitudinal cigar-smoking patterns are higher for cigarillos among NH Black or African American and Latino individuals in terms of past 30-day smoking, daily use and established use compared with traditional and filtered cigar use.28 Differences in product use prevalence appear to be consistent with the low harm perceptions we observed for NH Black or African American and Latino individuals. Furthermore, the estimated marginal (table 3) and contrasts (table 2) across several combustible tobacco categories, including manufactured cigarettes, were lower for Indigenous and mixed-race individuals compared with the referent group. Given that AI and AN individuals are more likely to smoke manufactured cigarettes than other racial and ethnic minorities,30 our work suggests that this demographic group may be underestimating the overall harm of the most harmful tobacco and nicotine products, combustibles.

Our results indicate that several incorrect harm perceptions are held for specific smokeless tobacco products. While not without risk, studies that have explored quantifying levels of reduced risk for these products have equated certain smokeless products like snus to be up to 95% less harmful than cigarettes.31 In our study, relative to other products, we observed that NH Black or African Americans and Latinos perceived snus to be more harmful. Similarly, NH Asian individuals perceived plug tobacco to be relatively more harmful than other tobacco and nicotine products. While NH Asian individuals have low prevalence rates of smokeless tobacco product use,32 studies have noted that significant heterogeneity exists within Asian American subgroups for consumption of various tobacco products.33 Structural level factors, such as marketing disparities at the point of sale, may partially explain inaccurate harm perceptions around smokeless tobacco products. Studies have documented that smokeless tobacco use may be targeted in neighbourhoods with fewer Black34 and Asian American residents.35

Our findings expand the existing literature on harm perceptions of e-cigarettes among racial and ethnic minorities.36 In contrast to prior studies, e-cigarettes were perceived as being lower in harm compared with manufactured cigarettes and the other combustible tobacco products that were tested among Latino, NH Black or African American individuals. However, within e-cigarette type comparisons, it was revealed that NH Black or African American and Latino individuals still perceive these products as being higher in harm than NH White individuals. Elevated harm perceptions for e-cigarettes may, in part, explain the low uptake of e-cigarettes among these demographic groups.37 Furthermore, our findings are among the first to report on the perceived harm of IQOS for racial and ethnic minorities. While IQOS is perceived as being only slightly more harmful than nicotine replacement therapy, NH Black or African American and NH Indigenous and mixed-race individuals perceive this product to be more harmful than NH White individuals, indicating that some racial and ethnic groups might be more sceptical of new and emerging products than others, despite federally approved MRTP claims for IQOS and specific e-cigarettes. Lastly, NH Black or African Americans were the only demographic group to perceive nicotine replacement therapy as more harmful than NH White individuals, which corroborates previous studies that have documented hesitancy and lack of trust for nicotine replacement therapy among African American people who smoke.38 This is particularly important as the FDA has recently released industry guidance on developing nicotine replacement therapy drug products,39 which have been historically underused by racial and ethnic minorities due to scepticism of the pharmaceutical industry and healthcare system distrust.

While it is important that everyone is provided with correct health risk information for tobacco products, our results suggest that there are some groups that may not be privy to such information, may not know how to interpret existing information or may distrust the sources of information. For example, we saw complicated patterns of misperceptions where African-Americans perceived more harmful products like little cigars as less harmful, while lower-harm products (e-cigarettes, nicotine replacement therapy) were perceived as more harmful. Such pairings of misperceptions could lead to continued use of combustibles in this population rather than use of nicotine replacement therapy.

Limitations

The methodological approach is a strength of this study. By forcing participants to make trade-off choices between the most and least harmful products, our approach yields greater discrimination among items as compared with a traditional monadic ratings scale which can suffer from floor and ceiling effects. The resulting ratio-scaled probability scores provide not only the rank order of perceived risk across products but the magnitude of the risk between products, giving us a detailed understanding of individuals’ harm perceptions with a focus on important demographic characteristics across a wide range of tobacco and nicotine products. Study limitations include the use of a convenience sample limited to one crowd-sourcing platform. Furthermore, our approach pooled data from two separate studies, which can introduce bias between study designs that were administered prior to the task. We did not limit the task to tobacco and nicotine products that participants were familiar with, thus limiting the generalisability of the results; however, we plan to examine this area in future work. We were also unable to test the full range of commercially available and FDA-approved tobacco products but chose a range of products that represent a continuum of risk. While the reported relative harm perceptions and contrasts were modest, small effect sizes can still produce meaningful improvement at a population level when misperceptions are addressed, and misperceptions may cumulatively contribute to broader tobacco-related health disparities if they remain uncorrected. We included clove cigarettes in our product list which are currently banned and now sold as clove little cigars, which limits our ability to interpret this product’s harm perceptions. However, our results may hold utility for other countries that have yet to ban these products. Lastly, we were unable to recruit a sample that was sufficient to elucidate harm perception patterns for Indigenous or mixed-race individuals as originally intended. While this manuscript establishes baseline harm perceptions across all tobacco and nicotine products, regardless of familiarity, a planned secondary analysis will directly compare perceptions of products participants reported being familiar with versus those they were not. This work will extend our findings by clarifying how familiarity shapes harm perceptions and may influence product uptake. Such analyses will be directly relevant for regulatory agencies like the FDA, which aim to develop communication strategies that correct misperceptions and provide clear guidance on the relative risks of tobacco and nicotine products.

Policy implications

The FDA’s regulatory framework is still focused on reducing combustible tobacco product use. If we consider the known tobacco-related health disparities for different racial and ethnic groups, our results underscore the need to provide tailored messaging on tobacco product risk and harm with specific consideration for race and ethnicity, such as cigarillos and little cigars for NH lack and African American and Latino individuals. This aligns with the FDA’s stated priorities to develop methods and messages for communicating complex scientific concepts, such as the relative risks and harms of tobacco use along a continuum of harm.40

Conclusion

We observed notable differences between the current evidence on health harms and relative harm perceptions reported by a diverse sample of tobacco users and non-tobacco users. We consistently observed that non-cigarette, combusted products were perceived as less harmful among two or more racial and ethnic groups compared with NH White individuals. Additionally, we report on specific non-combustible tobacco and nicotine products that were perceived as more harmful among racial and ethnic minorities. Given the consistency between harm beliefs and the prevalence of specific tobacco product use, there is a need to correct relative harm perceptions for specific combustible tobacco products and smokeless tobacco products. Future research should focus on correcting these relative harm misperceptions, especially among racial and ethnic groups with notably high use of specific combustible tobacco products.
