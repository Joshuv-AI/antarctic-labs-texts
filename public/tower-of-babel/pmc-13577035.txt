Sleep‐Autonomic Interactions in Parkinson's Disease and Multiple System Atrophy: A Comparative Study Using Structural Equation Modeling
Authors: Bora Jin, Yoonkyung Lee, Seungho Lee, Byeol‐A Yoon, Young Jin Jeong, Sang‐Myung Cheon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson's disease (PD) and multiple system atrophy (MSA) are α‐synucleinopathies with overlapping autonomic and sleep dysfunction, but distinct trajectories.ObjectivesThis study compared sleep and autonomic profiles in PD and MSA and elucidated disease‐specific associations using structural equation modeling (SEM).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed drug‐naïve patients with PD and MSA presenting with sleep complaints. All participants underwent polysomnography (PSG), autonomic function tests, and cardiac [I‐123] metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy. Significant parameters with moderate correlations (Spearman's ρ ≥ 0.35) were included in SEM to examine domain relationships.ResultsA total of 136 PD and 67 MSA patients were included. Patients with PD were older, with comparable sex, disease duration, and diabetes mellitus frequency. In PSG, patients with MSA showed lower‐stage N3 sleep, higher apnea–hypopnea index (35.4 vs. 23.2 events/h), and lower minimum oxygen saturation. Autonomic testing revealed that the MSA group demonstrated greater orthostatic blood pressure drops and higher total composite autonomic severity scores. Cardiac MIBG uptake was significantly reduced in the PD group. A directional SEM model in MSA revealed a significant association between autonomic dysfunction and increased sleep disruption (β = −0.68, P = 0.006), with substantially greater explanatory power than observed in PD (R 2 = 0.54 vs. 0.29).ConclusionsBoth autonomic and sleep parameters were significantly more impaired in patients with MSA than in those with PD. SEM further indicated that these domains were closely linked in MSA, with greater autonomic dysfunction associated with poorer sleep quality. This highlights the need for integrated management of both domains.Keywords: AFT, MSA, PD, PSG, SEM

Parkinson's disease (PD) and multiple system atrophy (MSA) are distinct neurodegenerative disorders within the spectrum of α‐synucleinopathies, differing in their underlying pathology and rate of progression. 1 , 2 Patients with MSA and PD frequently experience various sleep disturbances, such as rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD), insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and restless legs syndrome. 3 , 4 The occurrence and clinical impact of these symptoms differ substantially between the two disorders. Patients with MSA report more pronounced nighttime problems than patients with PD, with higher sleep fragmentation and more frequent RBD. 5 Sleep disturbances have been associated with poorer quality of life (QoL) and negative prognostic implications, particularly in patients with MSA. 6 , 7

Autonomic dysfunction varies in prevalence, pattern, and severity across synucleinopathies. 8 In PD, constipation (~65%) and urinary symptoms (~34%) are most common, with neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (nOH) in ~30% of patients. 9 , 10 In contrast, MSA exhibits greater autonomic severity, with nOH in 70%–80% of patients (often more pronounced than in PD) and frequent urinary and bowel symptoms (both 87%). 11 , 12

Both sleep and autonomic disturbances exhibit distinct disease‐specific profiles in patients with PD and MSA. Accumulating evidence suggests a close relationship between these domains. In a recent study of patients with PD, REM density was analyzed in relation to multiple nonmotor symptoms, including cognitive function and heart rate variability, yet no significant association was observed. 13 Nevertheless, other investigations have demonstrated that PD patients with RBD exhibit greater autonomic dysfunction compared to those without RBD, as assessed by autonomic symptom questionnaires, 14 and a prior report showed a strong association between RBD and OH in PD. 15 Similarly, a study conducted in an MSA cohort demonstrated that patients with preceding RBD symptoms are more likely to experience earlier or more prevalent autonomic onset, including symptomatic OH, suggesting a possible sleep‐autonomic interaction. 16 However, few studies have examined whether this sleep‐autonomic relationship differs between the two disorders. Therefore, this study aimed to compare sleep and autonomic profiles in drug‐naïve patients and characterize disease‐specific associations using structural equation modeling (SEM).

Patients and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective cross‐sectional study included 136 drug‐naïve patients with PD and 67 with MSA who presented with sleep‐related complaints. In this study, “drug‐naïve” was defined as having no prior exposure to dopaminergic medications. All patients fulfilled the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) clinical diagnostic criteria for PD or MSA. 17 , 18 Sleep‐related complaints were defined as the presence of at least one of the following: snoring, excessive daytime sleepiness, dream‐enactment behavior, initial insomnia, sleep fragmentation, or nocturnal pain and muscle cramps. Comprehensive medication and past medical histories were reviewed for all participants. Patients were not taking dopaminergic agents or pressor agents (medications for orthostatic hypotension). Regarding other medications, participants were not taking medications known to affect sleep architecture or respiratory patterns. Although 5 patients reported a past medical history of insomnia upon detailed history‐taking, none of these patients were currently taking hypnotic medications. No patients reported a past medical history of depression. Some patients with comorbid hypertension (HTN) may have been taking antihypertensive medications, as HTN was not an exclusion criterion. As part of the baseline assessment, all enrolled patients underwent standard polysomnography (PSG), a standardized autonomic function test (AFT), and cardiac [I‐123] metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy.

Autonomic Function Test

Patients abstained from exercise for 24 hours and from caffeine, nicotine, and food for 6 hours, as well as from autonomic‐altering medications for 48 hours before testing. The standardized AFT protocol consisted of four components assessed using the Finometer Pro (FMS, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) and the Quantitative Sweat Measurement System (WR Medical Electronics Co., Maplewood, MN, USA). These components included the following:(1) heart rate response to deep breathing (HRDB) and expiratory–inspiratory (E:I) ratio, (2) Valsalva maneuver (VM) for Valsalva ratio (VR) and pressure recovery time (PRT), (3) head‐up tilt test (HUTT) to identify nOH per current consensus criteria, 18 and (4) quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART). 19 The HUTT was performed in all patients except for 2 patients with PD and 2 with MSA, whereas the other AFT components were completed in all participants. Autonomic deficits were quantified using the 10‐point Composite Autonomic Severity Scale (CASS), adjusted for age and sex, comprising adrenergic (0–4), sudomotor (0–3), and cardiovagal (0–3) domains. 20

Polysomnography

Standard PSG was performed in all participants according to the standardized protocol previously established at our center. 21 The recording was performed using the Respironics Alice 5/6 Diagnostic Sleep System (Philips Respironics, Murrysville, PA, USA) or Natus Sleepworks software (Natus Medical Inc., Middleton, WI, USA). Sleep stages were scored according to the 2007 American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) criteria, and the following sleep parameters were calculated: sleep efficiency (%), sleep latency (minutes), REM sleep latency (minutes), apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), periodic limb movement index (PLMI), and arousal index (AI). RBD was diagnosed according to the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, third edition (ICSD‐3), criteria, requiring the presence of REM sleep without atonia on PSG and a history of dream‐enactment behaviors. 22 OSA was diagnosed based on the ICSD‐3 criteria, defined as either an AHI ≥5 events per hour accompanied by symptoms of excessive daytime sleepiness, witnessed apneas, or cardiometabolic comorbidities, or an AHI ≥15 events per hour regardless of symptoms. 22 The Periodic limb movements during sleep (PLMS) index (PLMI), defined as the number of periodic limb movements per hour of sleep, was calculated. 23

Cardiac I‐123 MIBG Scintigraphy

Early and delayed cardiac MIBG scintigraphy were performed 30 minutes and 3 hours after the intravenous injection of 111 MBq of I‐123 MIBG, respectively. 24 The planar images were obtained using a dual‐head gamma camera equipped with a low‐energy high‐resolution parallel‐hole collimator (eCAM, Siemens Healthcare, Knoxville, TN, USA). Medications that could affect MIBG uptake were withheld for 24 hours before the administration of I‐123 MIBG. Relative cardiac uptake was determined by setting the region of interest (ROI) in the anterior view. The entire left ventricular ROI was manually drawn, and a rectangular ROI was set in the upper mediastinal area. The delayed heart‐to‐mediastinum ratio (dHMR) was calculated by dividing the mean count density per pixel of the left ventricular ROI by that of the mediastinal ROI.

Statistical Analyses

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were performed using the χ2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and the independent t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, depending on the data distribution. Spearman's correlation analyses were performed separately for sleep and autonomic variables in the PD and MSA groups. SEM was employed to delineate the systemic interplay between autonomic and sleep domains. This approach allows for the simultaneous estimation of multiple interconnected pathways while minimizing measurement error through the use of latent constructs. 25 Variables were selected for SEM if they exhibited significant correlation (ρ ≥ 0.35, P < 0.05). Furthermore, sleep measures with high factor loadings in an exploratory SEM were incorporated to ensure model robustness. The final latent constructs comprised the following: (1) autonomic dysfunction, indicated by orthostatic blood pressure (BP) changes, CASS, and PRT; and (2) impaired sleep profiles, indicated by AHI, REM latency, N2 proportion, minimum oxygen saturation, and sleep latency. Each latent construct included at least three indicators to ensure adequate identification of the model. In MSA, two model specifications were evaluated: a directional path (Auto → Sleep) and a nondirectional covariance (Auto ~~ Sleep). Model fit was assessed using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). To validate the SEM findings, additional multivariate regression analyses were performed with the AHI as the dependent variable after being adjusted for age, sex, and disease duration. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and SEM was conducted using the lavaan package. Differences were considered statistically significant at two‐tailed P‐values <0.05.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics with Sleep‐Autonomic Profiles

A total of 203 drug‐naïve patients (136 PD, 67 MSA) with sleep‐related complaints were included. The most prevalent complaints in both PD and MSA were dream‐enactment behavior (77.9% vs. 77.6%), snoring (78.7% vs. 44.8%), and excessive daytime sleepiness (72.1% vs. 37.3%), followed by sleep‐onset insomnia, sleep fragmentation, and nocturnal pain and muscle cramps. Both groups were in early disease stages (mean disease duration ~2 years) with comparable sex distribution, although patients with PD were significantly older (P = 0.003). Comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus (DM), kidney disease, and malignancy, showed no significant intergroup differences. Regarding PSG parameters, both groups exhibited reduced sleep efficiency and prolonged REM latency. Although sleep architecture was mostly similar, the MSA group showed a significantly lower proportion of stage N3 sleep (P = 0.009) and more severe sleep‐disordered breathing, evidenced by higher AHI (P = 0.001), AI (P = 0.036), and lower minimum oxygen saturation (P < 0.001). The PLMI was also notably higher in MSA (P = 0.002). However, the clinical prevalence of RBD (64.7% vs. 64.2%), OSA (80.1% vs. 89.6%), and PLMS (44.1% vs. 47.8%) was high in both the PD and MSA groups, with no significant intergroup differences observed (all P > 0.05).

Regarding cardiac sympathetic function, the dHMR on cardiac MIBG scintigraphy was significantly lower in the PD group than in the MSA group (P < 0.001). In the AFT, although cardiovagal subscores and related indices showed no significant intergroup differences, the MSA group exhibited significantly greater systolic and diastolic BP drops during the HUTT (both P < 0.001). PRT was also significantly prolonged in patients with MSA compared to those with PD (P < 0.001). Consequently, both adrenergic and sudomotor subscores were significantly higher in the MSA group (P < 0.001 and P = 0.006, respectively), resulting in a significantly higher total CASS (P < 0.001). Structural Equation Modeling of Sleep‐Autonomic Interactions

In the PD group, SEM analysis did not reveal any statistically significant association between the prespecified latent constructs representing the autonomic and sleep domains. By contrast, a significant latent association was identified in the MSA group under the directional model. For the MSA group, two model specifications were evaluated: directional path (Auto → Sleep) and nondirectional covariance (Auto ~~ Sleep). The directional pathway model demonstrated a significant association from autonomic dysfunction to increased sleep disruption (β = −0.68, P = 0.006), indicating that greater autonomic dysfunction, particularly orthostatic BP drops during HUTT, was associated with higher AHI values. Under the alternative covariance specification, which examined the association without imposing a directionality, a comparable negative association was observed (β = −0.68, P = 0.052), approaching but not reaching statistical significance. The overall model fit remained consistent across both specifications (CFI = 0.960, TLI = 0.945, RMSEA = 0.062, and SRMR = 0.067). Discussion

This cross‐sectional study investigated sleep and autonomic domains in patients clinically diagnosed with PD and MSA using quantitative assessments, including AFT, cardiac MIBG scintigraphy, and PSG. These findings reveal that within α‐synucleinopathies, the sleep‐autonomic relationship is more pronounced in MSA, where greater autonomic dysfunction is significantly associated with increased sleep disruption. Even without a defined causal direction, the covariance between these constructs reached marginal significance, suggesting a potential physiological linkage. Finally, these findings suggest that targeted management of one domain may benefit the other, offering a multidimensional approach for symptom control.

This study included a large, well‐characterized cohort of patients with PD and MSA, offering a more systematic evaluation than previous reports. Regarding sleep parameters, the MSA group showed more prominent sleep‐disordered breathing along with higher PLMI and AI, indicating more severe sleep fragmentation and poorer overall sleep continuity than the PD group. The prevalence of RBD in MSA has been reported to range from 46.7% to 76.9%, 26 , 27 with a systematic review estimating 68.8%–100% (pooled prevalence: 88%). 28 In the present study, RBD prevalence in the MSA group was 64%, which is lower than typically reported. Given that nearly 90% of our MSA patients had sleep apnea, which is considerably higher than the 78.8% reported previously, 29 this elevated prevalence likely reflects our recruitment strategy of enrolling patients with sleep‐related complaints, thereby enriching for those with sleep‐disordered breathing. The resulting high prevalence of OSA in our cohort is consistent with severe respiratory disturbances contributing to sleep fragmentation and REM sleep suppression, thereby reducing the observed prevalence of RBD in this MSA cohort compared to published reports.

Although sleep efficiency, sleep latency, and REM latency did not differ significantly between the groups, both groups demonstrated clear alterations in sleep architecture compared to normative data. Stage N3 sleep percentages in our cohort were 5.63% (PD) and 5.01% (MSA), substantially lower than healthy adult normative values (20.4%). 30 However, these reduced N3 values are consistent with published reports in early‐stage PD (8%) and MSA (10.2%) populations, 26 , 31 with notable heterogeneity across studies (ranging 1.1%–10.2% in MSA depending on sleep‐disordered breathing severity). 29 This variation likely reflects differences in patient populations, disease stage, and comorbid sleep pathology. Prior studies similarly report that sleep disturbances are a common nonmotor manifestation of PD and MSA, with both groups exhibiting increased N1/N2 sleep, reduced N3/REM sleep, prolonged REM latency, and decreased sleep efficiency. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 Although these common alterations likely stem from shared neurodegeneration in brainstem structures, such as the locus coeruleus, pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus, and raphe nucleus, 34 the observed differences in clinical sleep disturbances suggest that sleep mechanisms involve complex, multilevel interactions beyond shared neurodegenerative pathways.

Autonomic parameters revealed that the MSA group exhibited more pronounced sudomotor abnormalities, larger orthostatic BP drops, and prolonged PRT. Although cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction in PD is predominantly postganglionic, whereas MSA primarily involves central preganglionic pathways, 35 recent studies suggest that peripheral involvement may also occur in MSA. 36 , 37 This discrepancy reflects the more aggressive nature of MSA, where accelerated neurodegeneration leads to broader involvement of multiple autonomic pathways. 37 Consequently, disease severity, rather than lesion site alone, may contribute to the extent of autonomic dysfunction.

Among the various types of sleep disturbance, RBD is one of the most common. It is thought to result from a breakdown of the REM sleep regulatory circuitry within the brainstem. 38 Notably, the brainstem also harbors key neural networks responsible for autonomic regulation, suggesting that the mechanisms underlying REM sleep and autonomic control are anatomically and functionally intertwined. 39 Given these clinical and pathophysiological considerations, an important question emerges as to how the interplay between sleep disturbances and autonomic dysfunction manifests across PD and MSA. However, studies examining these two domains within an integrated framework remain scarce. A recent study that assessed sleep and autonomic functions in small samples of PD and MSA primarily focused on comparing parameters based on the presence or absence of RBD within each disease group, rather than examining disease‐specific differences in autonomic‐sleep interactions. 40

To examine these interrelationships further, SEM was applied using comprehensive objective measures from both the sleep and autonomic domains. Rather than examining individual associations in isolation, SEM constructs latent variables that represent underlying domains by integrating information from multiple indicators, thereby allowing us to examine associations between higher‐order constructs while accounting for measurement error. The sleep domain was represented by the AHI, stage N2 proportion, sleep latency, REM latency, and minimum oxygen saturation, whereas the autonomic domain included systolic and diastolic BP drops, PRT, and total CASS scores. These variables were selected as the representative indicators of each functional domain. SEM revealed that this relationship was not significant in the PD group, whereas in the MSA group, a significant association emerged; greater autonomic dysfunction, largely explained by orthostatic BP drop during HUTT, was linked to increased sleep disruption, primarily driven by respiratory instability as reflected by higher AHI. Under the alternative covariance specification within the same framework, a marginally negative correlation was observed between the two latent constructs, indicating that sleep‐autonomic interactions exist to some degree in MSA. The significant directional path suggests influence within the sleep‐autonomic axis rather than temporal causality, consistent with our cross‐sectional design. In a sensitivity analysis using multivariate regression with AHI as the dependent variable and systolic and diastolic BP drop, PRT, and total CASS score as independent variables (adjusted for age, sex, and disease duration), the explanatory power of the MSA model (adjusted R 2 = 0.54) was substantially greater than that of the PD model (adjusted R 2 = 0.29), supporting the prominence of sleep‐autonomic interplay in MSA.

Although sleep‐disordered breathing has been shown to contribute to autonomic dysfunction in prior studies, such evidence derives primarily from PD cohorts, 41 , 42 , 43 with limited direct comparisons between disease groups. The more prominent sleep‐autonomic association observed in MSA compared to PD could partly reflect confounding effects of sleep‐disordered breathing‐related autonomic alterations. However, to our knowledge, this represents one of the first attempts to systematically examine and statistically compare sleep‐autonomic relationships across two synucleinopathies using quantitative objective measures. These findings underscore the disease‐specific nature of sleep‐autonomic interactions and highlight the potential clinical significance of this relationship in MSA. Nevertheless, external validation in independent cohorts would strengthen these observations and further clarify the mechanisms underlying sleep‐autonomic interactions in both PD and MSA.

AHI is a representative marker of OSA, a condition that can be effectively managed with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy. This finding has important clinical implications, particularly in MSA, where autonomic dysfunction, especially neurogenic orthostatic hypotension, often proves difficult to control. 35 Moreover, pharmacological treatments for orthostatic hypotension can induce supine hypertension, which has been associated with an increased risk of target organ damage, cardiovascular events, and premature death. 44 Optimizing sleep quality through CPAP may offer a complementary approach to symptom management, a hypothesis warranting prospective studies. Given that autonomic dysfunction is a major determinant of both the QoL and mortality in MSA, 35 addressing modifiable sleep disturbances represents a clinically meaningful avenue for comprehensive symptom management. It is noteworthy that a previous study in patients with OSA has demonstrated associations between OSA and autonomic nervous system alterations, with treatment showing reductions in sympathetic overactivity. 45 However, these investigations were not conducted in the context of synucleinopathies. The present findings extend this understanding by examining the interplay between sleep disturbances and autonomic dysfunction specifically within the context of synucleinopathies, representing a distinct line of investigation with different pathophysiological implications.

This study had several strengths. First, unlike many previous studies that relied on simple questionnaires, it employed quantitative and objective measures of both the sleep and autonomic domains in all patients. Second, a relatively large sample of drug‐naïve patients in the early stages of the disease was enrolled, which allowed us to minimize the confounding influence of antiparkinsonian medication on sleep and autonomic outcomes.

Nevertheless, this study had several limitations. First, the inclusion criterion of patients presenting with sleep‐related complaints represents an important selection bias. This recruitment strategy may enrich for patients with sleep‐disordered breathing (such as OSA), RBD, and sleep fragmentation, potentially inflating the reported prevalence of these sleep disturbances compared to unselected early‐stage PD and MSA cohorts. Consequently, these sleep and autonomic profiles may not be fully generalizable to all patients with newly diagnosed PD or MSA in the general population. Second, although overall past medical history (including comorbid conditions such as DM, HTN, kidney disease, and malignancy) did not differ significantly between the groups, they were not completely excluded. In particular, for DM, data on disease duration and glycemic control were lacking. Additionally, some patients with HTN may have been taking antihypertensive medications, which could potentially influence orthostatic BP changes during HUTT testing. Third, standardized motor severity assessments, such as the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale or the Unified Multiple System Atrophy Rating Scale, were not available. Because MSA typically presents with more severe motor manifestations than PD, motor severity may have confounded the observed sleep‐autonomic relationship. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the stronger association observed in MSA reflects mechanisms intrinsic to MSA or is partly attributable to greater overall motor burden. Fourth, the cross‐sectional design of this study precludes establishing definitive causality between autonomic dysfunction and increased sleep disruption. Future longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate how this interplay evolves across disease progression. Fifth, although all patients fulfilled the MDS clinical diagnostic criteria for PD or MSA, early disease stages are known to have diagnostic instability between these conditions, particularly in patients presenting with prominent sleep and autonomic complaints. Clinical misclassification, while present to some degree in any study without pathological confirmation, may introduce bias in the observed between‐group differences. Depending on misclassification patterns, such bias could attenuate true differences toward the null or, conversely, exaggerate apparent differences. Future prospective studies with long‐term follow‐up and biomarker validation would strengthen confidence in the associations observed in this cohort.

Despite these limitations, these findings suggest differential sleep‐autonomic patterns between PD and MSA. Given that MSA represents an aggressive and rapidly progressive synucleinopathy, these results highlight the potential for integrated management strategies that simultaneously address both sleep and autonomic dysfunction, which may ultimately improve patient outcomes. Future longitudinal studies incorporating motor and broader nonmotor domains in more refined cohorts are required to validate and expand upon these findings.
