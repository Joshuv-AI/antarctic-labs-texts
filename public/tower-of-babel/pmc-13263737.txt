Homozygous variegate porphyria: Two cases misdiagnosed as erythropoietic protoporphyria
Authors: Chenqing Wang, Narmene Bensaber, Lina Rebeiz, Benny Tran, Silvia Tortorelli, Kevin Kyle, Lenore K. Yalom, Akshata Moghe, Karl E. Anderson, Rebecca K. Leaf, Amy K. Yeung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Homozygous variegate porphyria (HVP) is an ultra-rare porphyria caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in PPOX. It typically presents with early childhood onset of cutaneous photosensitivity, including blistering, skin fragility, scarring, and poorly defined neurologic symptoms. We report two unrelated patients with childhood-onset photosensitivity, blistering, scarring, modest zinc-predominant erythrocyte protoporphyrin elevation, elevated plasma porphyrins that were predominantly protoporphyrin, and homozygous novel PPOX variants, findings consistent with homozygous variegate porphyria. Both patients had initially been diagnosed with erythropoietic protoporphyria because of photosensitivity and elevated total erythrocyte protoporphyrin, despite fractionation demonstrating a slight zinc predominance rather than metal-free predominance. Genetic testing revealed that both patients were homozygous for PPOX variants: c.223G>T p.Val75Phe in Case 1 and c.1070A>C p.Asp357Ala in Case 2. Neither variant was present in population genetic datasets nor previously reported in patients with variegate porphyria, and both were classified as variants of uncertain significance. A plasma fluorescence scan was performed for Case 2 and showed a peak at 624 nm, specific for variegate porphyria. A comprehensive porphyria gene panel, ideally with plasma fluorescence scanning, should be performed when total erythrocyte protoporphyrin is substantially elevated but predominantly zinc-chelated, to ensure accurate diagnosis of homozygous porphyrias such as HVP.

Introduction

Homozygous variegate porphyria (HVP), resulting from biallelic protoporphyrinogen oxidase (PPOX) pathogenic variants, is an ultra-rare disorder associated with early childhood onset of photosensitivity, including blistering, skin fragility, and scarring. Patients may also develop neurologic symptoms that have been poorly defined [1], [2], [3]. Due to the rarity of homozygous VP, it may be misdiagnosed as other more common porphyrias.

Variegate porphyria (VP) is an autosomal-dominant acute hepatic porphyria caused by one pathogenic variant in PPOX [4], [5], [6]. Clinical manifestations include adult-onset cutaneous blistering photosensitivity and/or acute neurovisceral attacks, including severe abdominal pain [6]. It has decreased penetrance, and symptoms begin after puberty, typically in adulthood [4], [5].

Erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP) is a recessively inherited porphyria caused by pathogenic variants in ferrochelatase (FECH), resulting in painful cutaneous light sensitivity that begins early in childhood [7], [8]. X-linked protoporphyria (XLP) is clinically identical to EPP, but it is caused by gain-of-function variants in ALAS2 [7]. Blistering and scarring are not prominent in either [7], [9]. Both EPP and XLP patients have elevated total erythrocyte protoporphyrin levels [8]. However, in EPP, the total erythrocyte protoporphyrin is typically <5% zinc-chelated, with the rest metal-free. In contrast, in XLP, the zinc erythrocyte protoporphyrin component is typically 10–50%, with the rest metal-free [8]. In recessive porphyrias besides EPP, the total erythrocyte protoporphyrin is elevated, but is predominantly zinc rather than metal-free [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15].

FECH c.315-48T>C is a common hypomorphic allele present in >95% of EPP patients [16], [17], [18]. In combination with a rare pathogenic FECH variant, it can cause EPP but has no known phenotype on its own [18]. While this variant is well recognized for its role in EPP pathogenesis when present in trans with a pathogenic FECH variant, its role in other porphyrias remains undefined.

Here, we report two unrelated patients with homozygous variegate porphyria caused by novel homozygous PPOX variants, each presenting with childhood-onset photosensitivity, blistering skin findings, and zinc-predominant erythrocyte protoporphyrin elevation. Both were initially misdiagnosed with EPP because of photosensitivity and elevated total erythrocyte protoporphyrin. We describe the key clinical, biochemical, and genetic features of these two HVP cases.

Case reports

Case 1

Case 1 is a 26-year-old woman who developed photosensitivity at age 4–5 and was diagnosed with EPP at age 6.

Since early childhood, she experienced cutaneous and gastrointestinal symptoms within 20–30 min of light exposure, including indoor lighting, with symptoms lasting for hours after onset. Her initial symptoms include nausea and headache, followed by cutaneous tingling and heat, then erythema. During severe episodes, her skin became painful, sensitive to touch, mildly swollen, and sometimes pruritic. As a child, she frequently developed blisters on her hands, but not in recent years. Still, even minor skin injuries healed slowly and left scars. Prolonged light exposure was associated with severe stomach pains and headaches.

She had no weakness, seizures, or dark urine. She was adopted and does not have any information about her biological family's medical history.

The patient underwent focused genetic testing for protoporphyria, which identified the common FECH c.315-48T>C allele, with no pathogenic variants in ALAS2. Based on this, and her elevated total erythrocyte protoporphyrin, she was diagnosed with EPP, despite her atypical skin symptoms and her slight zinc erythrocyte protoporphyrin predominance. She participated in an EPP clinical trial while she was thought to have EPP.

Given her atypical clinical and biochemical features, many of which were not consistent with EPP or XLP, a 10-gene porphyria panel was performed. This was positive for a homozygous PPOX c.223G>T p.Val75Phe variant, as well as the FECH c.315-48T>C hypomorphic allele. The PPOX variant was predicted to be deleterious by SIFT and PolyPhen and had a high CADD score. It was absent from population genetic databases, had not previously been reported in patients with variegate porphyria, and was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). Based on her clinical, biochemical, and genetic findings, she was diagnosed with homozygous variegate porphyria.

Because Case 1 was evaluated during a screening visit for an EPP clinical trial rather than in a dedicated clinical diagnostic evaluation, urine porphyrins, plasma fluorescence scanning, and stool porphyrins were not obtained at that visit.

Case 2

A 34-year-old woman was diagnosed with EPP at the age of 2–3. Around that time, she started experiencing pruritus, skin peeling, and scabbing following light exposure. The symptoms progressed to severe burning pain of the hands and face with continued light exposure. After approximately 25 min of bright sunlight, symptoms would begin and last for 1 day or longer. She reported that even certain indoor lighting triggered symptoms.

In addition to cutaneous symptoms, she experienced intermittent abdominal pain occurring 2–3 days per week, lasting approximately 25 min per episode. She also experienced episodic bilateral hand numbness, tingling, and weakness, as well as intermittent leg numbness and weakness severe enough to prevent ambulation for 15–30 min. Moreover, she reported frequent headaches, brain fog, and concerns about her memory.

Her parents were first cousins, and there was no known family history of porphyria.

Biochemical testing demonstrated elevated erythrocyte protoporphyrin levels that were 60% zinc-chelated (Table 1). Plasma total porphyrins were elevated at 3.3 μg/dL (reference <1 μg/dL), with plasma protoporphyrin being 2.7 μg/dL. Plasma fluorescence scanning showed a peak at 624 nm, specific for VP [19], [20]. Her urine ALA, urine PBG, and urine porphyrins were all normal on repeated testing (Table 1). Ferritin was within the reference range.

Given that several clinical and biochemical features were atypical for EPP, including the predominance of zinc erythrocyte protoporphyrin, a comprehensive 10-gene porphyria panel was performed. Genetic testing revealed a homozygous PPOX c.1070A>C p.Asp357Ala variant, as well as the common low-expression FECH allele c.315-48T>C. Similar to the variant in case 1, the PPOX variant was predicted to be deleterious by SIFT and PolyPhen and had a high CADD score. It was absent from population genetic databases, had not previously been reported in patients with variegate porphyria, and was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). Based on her clinical, biochemical, and genetic findings, she was diagnosed with HVP.

Neurologic evaluation, including an MRI of the brain and cervical spine, electromyography, and a skin biopsy evaluating small fiber neuropathy, was all unrevealing. Additional neuropathy-focused genetic testing identified two ADAMTS2 variants (the gene responsible for Ehlers–Danlos syndrome type VIIC) of uncertain significance; these were considered incidental findings of unclear clinical relevance and are included for completeness.

Discussion

We report two unrelated patients with childhood-onset photosensitivity, blistering and scarring of the skin, modest zinc-predominant erythrocyte protoporphyrin elevation, elevated plasma protoporphyrin, and homozygous novel PPOX variants, consistent with homozygous variegate porphyria. These features align with prior reports of HVP, in which early-onset cutaneous disease, elevated erythrocyte protoporphyrin with zinc predominance, and variable neurologic manifestations have been described. In one of our patients, a plasma fluorescence peak at 624 nm provided additional biochemical support for VP [19], [20]. Although both patients had initially been diagnosed with EPP because of childhood photosensitivity and elevated total erythrocyte protoporphyrin, the zinc predominance of their erythrocyte protoporphyrin should have raised suspicion for an alternative diagnosis.

In the United States, PPOX enzymatic assays are not clinically available; therefore, diagnostic confidence relies on concordant clinical, biochemical, and genetic findings. Although both variants remain formally classified as VUS, several features support their likely clinical relevance, including absence from population databases, damaging in silico predictions, and the presence of a different homozygous PPOX variant in each of two unrelated patients with clinical and biochemical findings consistent with HVP. Notably, one patient's variant is PPOX c.1070A>C p.Asp357Ala, which has a genetic position only two base pairs from a variant that has been reported multiple times in patients with HVP, PPOX c.1072G>A p.Gly358Arg [21], [22].

The skin symptoms in our patients are consistent with those previously reported in homozygous VP. These skin symptoms include blistering, scarring, and skin fragility beginning in early childhood [1], [22], [23], [24], [25]. While these symptoms can be severe, sometimes resembling those of congenital erythropoietic porphyria, the lesions and skin involvement are often milder in HVP than in CEP, as in our cases.

Notably, neurologic symptoms have been reported in some but not all HVP cases. The symptoms vary widely and have been poorly characterized. Murphy et al. described two unrelated patients with HVP who both demonstrated growth retardation and short stature [23]. Kaiser et al. reported two siblings with HVP who had developmental delay, short stature, and epilepsy, and conducted a literature review of 16 patients with biallelic PPOX variants [22]. In that literature review, 11 of 16 patients had neurodevelopmental abnormalities in addition to cutaneous findings. Reported neurologic symptoms included developmental delay, nystagmus, epileptic seizures, and sensory neuropathy [22].

While neurologic symptoms were reported in both of our patients, we did not identify objective neurologic abnormalities on evaluation, and in Case 2 urine ALA and PBG were normal. It remains uncertain whether these symptoms are due to HVP or another etiology. The neurologic symptoms reported by our patients were less severe and less objectively characterized than those most commonly described in previously reported HVP cases, which more often include developmental delay, seizures, and nystagmus. Abdominal pain was also reported in both patients, although acute abdominal pain attacks have not been reported in previously published HVP cases [1], [22]. However, reported neurologic manifestations in biallelic HVP do not appear to correlate consistently with urinary ALA and PBG, as neurologic or neurodevelopmental abnormalities have been described in cases with both normal and elevated urinary ALA and PBG [22], [23], [24]. A similar pattern has been reported in biallelic HMBS disease, in which chronic neurologic or neurodevelopmental manifestations have been described despite urinary ALA and PBG levels that may be normal or only modestly elevated [26]. The mechanisms and biochemical correlates of the neurologic and neurodevelopmental abnormalities in these patients are not understood and should be the topic of future study.

While elevations of erythrocyte protoporphyrin have been reported in cases of HVP, it has typically been reported to be much less than 1000 μg/dL, as in our cases [14], [22], [23]. In all cases where erythrocyte protoporphyrin fractionation was performed, zinc erythrocyte protoporphyrin predominated. Severe iron deficiency anemia can increase zinc erythrocyte protoporphyrin; however, although one patient had ferritin consistent with iron deficiency, the degree of iron deficiency would not increase erythrocyte protoporphyrin to the levels seen in these patients. In one report of two patients with HVP, the zinc fraction was 60–70% among their two cases, and another reported >80% zinc protoporphyrin [15], [23]. In our cases, the zinc fraction was slightly lower at 54–60%. We speculate this may have been influenced by the presence of FECH c.315-48T>C in both patients. Because FECH catalyzes the insertion of both iron and zinc into protoporphyrin, reduced FECH activity would be expected to increase the metal-free fraction, which may contribute to diagnostic confusion when total erythrocyte protoporphyrin is elevated, and the metal-free and zinc fractions are similar in magnitude [7], [18].

In case reports of homozygous variegate porphyria, initial misdiagnosis with CEP has been reported, but initial misdiagnosis with EPP has not, to our knowledge [23], [24]. Our cases had several clinical similarities with EPP patients, including childhood onset, cutaneous painful light sensitivity, and, in some EPP cases, sensitivity to indoor lighting [7], [9]. However, there were striking features that were not consistent with EPP. These include elevated erythrocyte protoporphyrin with a slight predominance of zinc erythrocyte protoporphyrin, total erythrocyte protoporphyrin that is lower than most EPP patients, and obvious cutaneous blistering and scarring.

In patients evaluated for EPP or XLP, fractionation of total erythrocyte protoporphyrin into metal-free and zinc components is essential to ensure accurate diagnosis, because zinc predominance excludes EPP and makes XLP unlikely [27]. The differential diagnosis should instead include biallelic porphyrias, including HVP, hepatoerythropoietic porphyria [biallelic UROD], ALAD porphyria [biallelic ALAD], and congenital erythropoietic porphyria (CEP) [biallelic UROS] [10], [11], [12], [13], [15], depending on the clinical presentation. Multigene porphyria testing should be pursued rather than limited to FECH and ALAS2. Plasma fluorescence scanning can provide additional supportive evidence when available, with a peak around 626 nm being characteristic of VP, whereas the EPP peak is typically around 634 nm [19], [20].

These cases further support that homozygous variegate porphyria can present with childhood-onset photosensitivity, blistering and scarring skin findings, zinc-predominant erythrocyte protoporphyrin elevation, and variable neurologic symptoms. Recognition of this pattern is important, as patients with elevated erythrocyte protoporphyrin but zinc predominance warrant broader evaluation for porphyrias other than EPP.
