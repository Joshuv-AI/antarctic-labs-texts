LncRNA MIR22HG promotes fracture healing through down-regulation of miR-10a-5p
Authors: Luna He, Shuchao Zhai, Hurong Du, Wenlong Zhang, Zeyuan Liu, Wei Liu, Meini Cen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelayed fracture healing (DFH) is a common postoperative complication in fractured patients, impairing affects quality of life and increasing financial burden.ObjectivesThis study explored the clinical significance of long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) MIR22HG in DFH and its potential mechanisms in promoting fracture healing.MethodsA total of 145 patients with normal healing and 132 patients with DFH were enrolled. Quantitative PCR was used to detect the expression levels of MIR22HG, miR-10a-5p, and osteogenic differentiation-related genes. ROC curves and logistic analysis were performed to evaluate the predictive value of MIR22HG. Using the hFOB 1.19 osteogenic differentiation model, the effects of MIR22HG silencing and co-inhibition of MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p on osteogenic differentiation were explored, and the proliferation and apoptosis were detected via CCK-8 assay and flow cytometry.ResultsSerum MIR22HG were decreased, while miR-10a-5p was elevated in DFH patients. MIR22HG could distinguish DFH patients and predict DFH occurrence. In the hFOB 1.19 osteogenic differentiation model, silencing MIR22HG reduced the expression of osteogenic differentiation markers, inhibited cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis. MIR22HG negatively regulated miR-10a-5p, and miR-10a-5p effectively reversed the inhibitory effect of MIR22HG silencing on hFOB 1.19 cell function.ConclusionMIR22HG promotes fracture healing by sponging miR-10a-5p. Serum MIR22HG is a potential marker for the clinically assisted prediction of DFH occurrence. MIR22HG/miR-10a-5p axis provides a potential molecular target for fracture healing treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06488-3.

Introduction

Tibial fractures are a common type of clinical fracture. In addition to conventional surgical interventions and physical therapy [1], advancements in minimally invasive techniques have reduced complication rates [2]. Nevertheless, delayed healing or non-union remains a concern, affecting approximately 5% to 20% of patients and increasing the risk of secondary procedures or limb dysfunction [3]. The fracture healing process is complex, and in-depth studies of its underlying mechanisms are essential to optimize clinical therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have provided novel insights into disease mechanisms [4]. Notably, lncRNA MIR22HG has attracted attention due to its unique involvement in skeletal disorders. Research indicates that MIR22HG may serve as a prognostic biomarker for osteosarcoma (OS) and regulate OS cell functions [5]. In osteoarthritis (OA), MIR22HG expression is decreased in chondrocytes, and its overexpression alleviates OA progression [6]. Furthermore, MIR22HG is down-regulated in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) from osteoporotic (OP) mice, and its knockdown inhibits osteogenic processes [7]. Additionally, MIR22HG is also involved in regulating bone resorption via effects on osteoclast activity and bone metabolism in OP [8, 9]. Despite its significance in the skeletal system, the exact role of MIR22HG in fracture healing remains unclear.

LncRNAs can modulate target genes expression by sponging microRNAs (miRNAs) [10]. Among these, miR-10a-5p is a key regulator in orthopedic contexts. In OS, miR-10a-5p acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting tumor proliferation [11]. In a rat model of spinal cord injury (SCI), suppressing miR-10a-5p enhances BMSCs differentiation and improves motor function [12]. miR-10a-5p is up-regulated in OA, exacerbating cartilage degradation [13] and influencing cartilage repair [14]. Furthermore, miR-10a-5p is a potential therapeutic target for OP [15], as its up-regulation impairs osteogenic differentiation [16]. However, whether miR-10a-5p participates in fracture healing regulation as a downstream target gene of MIR22HG requires further validation.

Therefore, this study analyzed serum MIR22HG levels and their clinical predictive value in DFH patients. Combined with cell function experiments, this research investigated the role of the MIR22HG/miRNA-10a-5p regulatory axis in fracture healing. The results are expected to provide potential markers for clinical prediction of healing outcomes and lay a theoretical foundation for molecular targeted therapy.

Patients and methods

Research subjects and grouping

This research employed a retrospective design to gather clinical data. A total of 277 patients with tibial fractures treated at Shanxi Bethune Hospital between 2021 and 2024 were selected. The inclusion criteria comprised: (1) unilateral traumatic fractures; (2) time from injury to treatment ≤ 72 h (meeting internal fixation criteria); and (3) complete imaging data. The exclusion criteria included: (1) multiple fractures or pathological fractures; (2) history of fractures, osteoporosis, or bone tuberculosis; (3) autoimmune diseases, malignant tumors, or metabolic disorders (except diabetes mellitus); (4) use of bone metabolism-influencing drugs within 6 months; and (5) severe hepatic or renal insufficiency.

Clinical investigations examining the healing duration of tibial fractures have identified 12 weeks post-surgery as a critical time point for assessing early healing, during which pronounced callus formation is typically evident in the majority of cases exhibiting normal healing [3]. Consequently, patients with indistinct fracture lines, continuous bone callus formation on imaging within 12 weeks post-treatment, and absence of local tenderness or abnormal activity were classified into the normal fracture healing (NFH) group (n = 145). Prior research has indicated that 16 weeks serves as an early warning threshold for delayed healing of tibial fractures, facilitating timely intervention. The presence of a distinct fracture line and the absence of significant callus formation at this juncture suggests a considerable delay in the healing process [17]. Furthermore, the 16-week timeframe extends the observation period by an additional four weeks beyond the 12-week mark, thereby allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of fracture healing and mitigating the potential for misinterpretations arising from individual variations in healing rates. Accordingly, patients with distinct fracture lines, minimal bone callus formation, and observable gaps/sclerosis at the fracture site on imaging within 16 weeks post-treatment were categorized into the delayed fracture healing (DFH) group (n = 132).

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and their families, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Bethune Hospital.

Clinical data and serum collection

Upon enrollment, patient data was systematically collected by researchers who had undergone uniform training, utilizing a standardized case report form. The general demographic information collected encompassed age, height, and weight (for Body Mass Index calculation), gender, smoking history, and alcohol consumption history. Disease-related data included fracture side (left or right), fracture severity, osteosynthesis method (open or closed reduction), the type of internal fixation employed (plate or intramedullary nails), and presence of diabetes mellitus.

A 5 mL of venous blood was collected into a RNase-free vacuum blood collection tube to prevent exogenous enzymatic contamination. Following stratification at room temperature (no more than 30 min), the blood was centrifuged at 3,000 r/min for 15 min at a temperature of 4 °C to isolate the upper layer of serum. These low-temperature procedures effectively suppress endogenous RNase activity released from blood cell rupture, thereby minimizing degradation of lncRNA. To mitigate the potential impact of repeated freeze-thaw cycles on the assay results, the serum samples were aliquoted into sterile, RNase-free cryovials using small-volume packaging to ensure single-use per experiment. Samples were then rapidly stored at -80 °C in an ultra-low temperature freezer to fully inactivate any residual RNase and maintain long-term lncRNA stability.

Cell culture and differentiation induction

The human osteoblast cell line hFOB 1.19 (Procell, China) was cultured in DMEM/F12 (HyClone, USA) complete medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), 1% antibiotic solution (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, Solarbio, China), and 0.3 mg/mL G418 (Sigma, USA). The cells were incubated at a regular incubator. The specific parameters were 37 °C, with 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity.

Cells from the 3rd to 5th generation were seeded in 6-well plates. Following a 24-hour period for cell attachment, the medium was replaced with osteogenic differentiation induction medium (Cyagen, China). The osteogenic differentiation induction medium was supplemented with the following key osteogenic inducers: 50 µg/mL vitamin C phosphate, 10 mM sodium β-glycerophosphate, and 0.1 µM dexamethasone, in addition to the complete medium. The cells were collected 14 days post-induction.

Cell transfection

Small interfering RNA sequence targeting MIR22HG (si-MIR22HG, 5’-CCUACUUGGACAAGCUCAUTT-3’), as well as transfection sequences miR-10a-5p inhibitor (5’-CACAAAUUCGGAUCUACAGGGUA-3’) and miR-10a-5p mimic(5’-UACCCUGUAGAUCCGAAUUUGUG-3’) were designed and synthesized by EditGene Technology Co. Ltd. in Guangzhou, China. The negative control (NC) groups including si-NC (5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’), inhibitor-NC (5’-CAGUACUUUUGUAGUACAA-3’) and mimic-NC (5’-UUGUACUACACAAAAGUACUG-3’) were established to exclude non-specific effects.

The hFOB 1.19 cells in the logarithmic growth phase after osteogenic differentiation induction were seeded in 6-well plates. Transfection was performed when cell confluence reached 70% − 80% using 40 pmol of constructs and 7.5 µL of FuGENE® SI or RNAimax® (NewbioScience, China). Specifically, si-MIR22HG and its NC were subjected to transfection using FuGENE® SI transfection reagent, while RNAimax® was employed for the transfection of miR-10a-5p mimic, inhibitor, and their corresponding NC. The cells were subsequently collected 72 h post-transfection for subsequent assays.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from serum utilizing a serum/plasma total RNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, China) and from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The lysis buffer provided in the kit contains an intrinsic RNase inhibitor, which immediately neutralizes residual RNase activity upon sample lysis. Concurrently, DNase I treatment was applied to eliminate genomic DNA contamination and prevent interference with downstream analysis. Furthermore, the silica-membrane-based spin column included in the kit enables efficient enrichment of lncRNA, minimizes RNA loss during purification, and utilizes RNase-free TE buffer during elution to prevent post-extraction degradation. The entire RNA extraction process was conducted on ice, and all consumables were certified RNase-free, ensuring protection against exogenous RNase contamination throughout the operational workflow.

Utilizing RNA as a template, an appropriate commercial kit was selected for the synthesis of cDNA. The cDNA synthesis for lncRNA and mRNA was conducted using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China), whereas the synthesis of cDNA for miRNA was carried out with the Mir-X miRNA First Strand Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). The procedures adhered strictly to the guidelines provided by the respective reagent kits to ensure optimal sensitivity and accuracy in detection.

To validate the effectiveness of this experimental strategy, we performed quality control assessments. The coefficient of variation (CV) of Ct values for the internal reference gene GAPDH across all serum samples was below 5%, indicating consistent RNA extraction efficiency and absence of significant RNA degradation. Additionally, 20 randomly selected samples (10 from NFH group and 10 from DFH group) underwent three independent qPCR replicates, yielding a CV of less than 8% for MIR22HG relative expression levels. These results further confirm that the extracted lncRNA is sufficiently stable to meet the requirements of subsequent analyses.

qPCR was performed using Hieff UNICON® Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China) in a 20 µL reaction system on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, USA). Reaction conditions: an initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. GAPDH (for lncRNA/mRNA) or U6 (for miRNA) served as internal references, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primers for the reactions were designed, synthesized, and validated by GeneWiz Technology Co., Ltd. in Beijing, China. The amplification primer sequences for the relevant genes were as follows: MIR22HG (forward 5’-TGGAGACCAGTGAAGGACCA-3’ and reverse 5’-ACATCTCGGAGGCAGAAAGC-3’), miR-10a-5p (forward 5’-ACCCTGTAGATCCGAATTTGTGTAA-3’ and reverse 5’-AGAGCGGAGTGTTTATGTCAACT-3’), GAPDH (forward 5’-GCACCGTCAAGGCTGAGAAC-3’ and reverse 5’-TGGTGAAGACGCCAGTGGAC-3’), U6 (forward 5’-GATCAGGCCAGTGGAAATGT-3’ and reverse 5’-GTGTGTTTCGTGCAACCATC-3’), RUNX2 (forward 5’-CCACCGAGACCAACAGAGTC-3’ and reverse 5’-TCACTGTGCTGAAGAGGCTG-3’), OSX (forward 5’-CCTCCTCAGCTCACCTTCTC-3’ and reverse 5’-GTTGGGAGCCCAAATAGAAA-3’), OCN (forward 5’-TCCTTTGGGGTTTGGCCTAC-3’ and reverse 5’-CCAGCCTCCAGCACTGTTTA-3’), as well as OPN (forward 5’-AGCCACATCGCTCAGACACC-3’ and reverse 5’-TGAAATTCATGGCTGTGGAA-3’).

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

The binding sites between MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p were predicted utilizing the ENCORI database. Oligonucleotide fragments containing wild-type (WT, 5’-ACCAGGCCUGGAGAGCACAGGGUC-3’) and mutant (MUT, 5’-ACCAGGCCUCCUGAGCUGUCCCAC-3’) binding sequences were cloned into the pmirGLO vector (Promega, USA). Recombinant plasmids (100 ng) were co-transfected with 50 nM miR-10a-5p mimic or inhibitor into hFOB 1.19 cells (2 × 104 cells per well in a 96-well plate). Following a 48-hour incubation period, luciferase activity was assessed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay System (Promega, USA).

RNA binding protein Immunoprecipitation (RIP)

After culturing hFOB 1.19 cells for 14 days, the binding of Ago2 to MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p was detected using an RIP assay kit (Millipore, USA). The following antibodies were used: Ago2 antibody (1:50, Abcam, UK) and IgG antibody (1:100, Abcam, UK). The cells were lysed in an equal volume of RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) at 4 °C on an ice bath for five minutes, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min. 10% of the cell extract was taken as the input group and the remainder was used for co-precipitation experiments. Magnetic bead-antibody complexes were prepared according to the kit instructions, washed with 900 µL of RIP washing buffer, resuspended, and then 100 µL of the cell extract was added and the mixture incubated overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the sample was washed and the magnetic bead-protein complex collected. The complex and input group samples were digested with proteinase K (Beyotime, China) and the RNA extracted. The expression levels of MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p were detected by qPCR.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity test

After osteogenic differentiation induction and transfection, hFOB 1.19 cells were collected. A 100 µL of pre-cooled cell lysis buffer (containing 1% Triton X-100) was added to lyse the cells on ice for 30 min. The lysate was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The ALP activity was measured using an ALP assay kit (Nanjing JianCheng, China) with optical density (OD) value at 405 nm detected via a microplate reader (Spectramax, USA). Relative activity was calculated using a standard curve and normalized.

Alizarin red S (ARS) staining analysis

The HFOB 1.19 cells, which had undergone osteogenic differentiation induction and cell transfection, were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After rinsing with deionized water, 40 mM ARS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added and the cells were stained in the dark for 30 min. The ARS bound to the cells was then dissolved in 10% acetic acid and the OD value measured at a wavelength of 415 nm using a spectrophotometer to reflect the amount of calcium deposited.

Cell proliferation assay

Following the osteogenic differentiation induction and transfection, hFOB 1.19 cells were seeded into 96-well plates. At specified time points of 0, 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Beyotime, China) was added to each well, and plates were incubated for 2 h. The OD value at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, with blank wells (medium devoid of cells) as the baseline.

Cell apoptosis detection

The hFOB 1.19 cells were collected after osteogenic differentiation induction and transfection. The cells were washed 3 times with pre-cooled PBS, centrifuged at 1,000 r/min for 5 min, and resuspended in 100 µL of 1× Binding Buffer. 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of propidium iodide (PI) were added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. 400 µL of Binding Buffer was added, and apoptosis was analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (excitation wavelengths: Annexin V-FITC at 488 nm and PI at 535 nm), with 10,000 cells counted per sample. Apoptosis rates were quantified using FlowJo software.

Statistical methods

Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical data were reported as the number of cases and percentage (%). The analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Continuous data between two groups were compared using the t-test, and among multiple groups using one-way or two-way ANOVA, with pairwise comparisons via the Tukey test. The chi-square test was utilized for categorical data. Correlations between variables were assessed via Pearson’s correlation. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to analyze the predictive value of MIR22HG. A P < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistically significant differences.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the subjects

There were no statistically significant differences in general demographic data and most clinical characteristics between the two groups (P > 0.05). The groups were well balanced. Regarding disease-related factors, a total of 46 patients (34.85%) in the DFH group presented with diabetes history, which was significantly higher than the 24 patients (16.55%) in the NFH group, with this difference reaching statistical significance (P = 0.001).

This study was based on fracture pattern complexity to determine complete fractures, where the bone’s continuity was completely interrupted and the fracture line ran through the entire diameter. This resulted in the bone splitting into two or more independent parts and was accompanied by comminuted changes (fracture fragments ≥ 3), as well as lateral, shortening and other displacements. This made treatment difficult, resulted in poor stability and damaged the blood supply and anatomical relationships. Due to the greater complexity of these fractures and the resulting difficulty in achieving functional recovery, the fractures were more severe. Therefore, the severity of fractures was assessed based on the occurrence of complete fractures. The proportion of complete fractures was also significantly higher in the DFH group (P = 0.022, Table 1), indicating more severe fractures in DFH patients.

Serum MIR22HG levels and the diagnostic and predictive significance of serum MIR22HG

Serum MIR22HG levels significantly lower in patients belonging to the DFH group when compared to those in the NFH group (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A).

Fig. 1The serum MIR22HG levels in NFH and DFH group (A). The diagnostic value of serum MIR22HG for DFH (B) and the prediction of risk factors for the DFH occurrence (C). ***, P < 0.001

The serum MIR22HG levels in NFH and DFH group (A). The diagnostic value of serum MIR22HG for DFH (B) and the prediction of risk factors for the DFH occurrence (C). ***, P < 0.001

ROC curve analysis indicated that serum MIR22HG effectively distinguished NFH and DFH patients (P < 0.001), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.888, surpassing the optimal diagnostic threshold of 0.8. At the optimal cut-off value, the sensitivity was 71.97% and the specificity was 88.28% (Fig. 1B).

The study population was stratified into non-diabetic and diabetic subgroups according to the presence or absence of diabetes, and ROC curve analyses were subsequently performed. In the non-diabetes subgroup, the AUC of serum MIR22HG for predicting DFH was 0.837, with a sensitivity of 76.74% and a specificity of 73.55% (P < 0.001, Supply 1 A). In the diabetes subgroup, the AUC was 0.807, with a sensitivity of 67.39% and a specificity of 87.50% (P < 0.001, Supply 1B). The AUC values in both subgroups remained consistently above 0.8, indicating that serum MIR22HG demonstrates robust predictive performance for DFH, irrespective of diabetic status.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified age, diabetes mellitus history, and serum MIR22HG levels as independent factors associated with DFH occurrence. Specifically, for each one-year increase in age, the likelihood of developing DFH escalated by a factor of 1.953 (P = 0.018). Furthermore, patients with diabetes mellitus history had a 2.726-fold increased risk of DFH compared to those without such a history (P = 0.032). Additionally, a one-unit decrease in serum MIR22HG levels increased the risk by 7.143-fold in the risk of DFH (P < 0.001). This ‘one unit’ referred specifically to the numerical unit calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method of relative expression. The low serum MIR22HG expression, diabetes mellitus history, and advanced age emerged as significant risk factors for DFH occurrence (Fig. 1C).

The establishment of osteogenic differentiation induction model and MIR22HG Silencing model

Throughout the process of inducing osteogenic differentiation, the mRNA levels of the key osteogenic differentiation marker genes RUNX2, OSX, OCN, and OPN in hFOB 1.19 cells increased over time (P < 0.001, Fig. 2A). Additionally, the relative levels of ALP, an indicator of osteoblast activity, demonstrated a gradual increase during induction (P < 0.001, Fig. 2B). These results suggest that the hFOB 1.19 osteogenic differentiation model has been successfully established. Within this model, MIR22HG expression exhibited a gradual up-regulation trend in relation to the induction duration (P < 0.01, Fig. 2C). The calcium deposition increased substaconntially on the 14 d after osteogenic induction (P < 0.001, Fig. 2E).

In the established osteogenic differentiation cell model, silencing MIR22HG significantly reduced its levels in hFOB 1.19 cells (P < 0.01, Fig. 2D). This process was highly efficient, successfully creating a cell model with reduced levels of MIR22HG.

The analyses revealed that the mRNA levels of RUNX2, OSX, OCN, and OPN were significantly reduced (P < 0.001, Fig. 3A). Additionally, ALP synthesis was significantly decreased (P < 0.01, Fig. 3B).

Serum miR-10a-5p levels and its targeting relationship with MIR22HG

Serum levels of miR-10a-5p were markedly higher in patients in the DFH group than in the NFH group (P < 0.001, Fig. 4A). Correlation analysis revealed a significant inverse relationship between MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p levels in the serum of DFH patients (r = -0.632, P < 0.001, Fig. 4B).

Predictions from the ENCORI database indicated the presence of a complementary binding site for miR-10a-5p within the 3’-UTR region of MIR22HG (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, a dual luciferase reporter assay demonstrated that the overexpression of miR-10a-5p significantly reduced the luciferase activity of the WT-MIR22HG plasmid group compared to the control group (P < 0.01). Conversely, inhibiting miR-10a-5p led to a significant increase in luciferase activity (P < 0.001), with no significant alterations observed in the luciferase activity of the MUT-MIR22HG group (P > 0.05, Fig. 4D). Additionally, the results of the RIP experiment showed that the relative enrichment factors of MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p in the Ago2 antibody group were significantly higher than in the IgG control group (P < 0.001, Fig. 4E), confirming the presence of MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p in the same complex.

MIR22HG regulates the differentiation of osteoblasts via sponging miR-10a-5p

In the established osteogenic differentiation cell model, silencing MIR22HG resulted in a significant increase in miR-10a-5p levels in hFOB 1.19 cells (P < 0.01). Furthermore, concurrently silencing both MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p led to a notable decrease in the levels of the latter (P < 0.001, Fig. 5A), restoring them to control group levels.

Individual silencing of MIR22HG markedly diminished the levels of critical markers associated with osteogenic differentiation, inhibited cellular proliferation, and promoted apoptosis, thereby obstructing the osteogenic differentiation process (Fig. 3). Conversely, following the simultaneous inhibition of MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p, there was a significant rebound in RUNX2, OSX, OCN, and OPN mRNA levels in hFOB 1.19 cells (P < 0.001, Fig. 5B), as well as substantial restoration of ALP synthesis (P < 0.001, Fig. 5C).

Cell function assay results indicated that co-inhibiting MIR22HG and miR-10a-5p significantly counteracted the suppression of hFOB 1.19 cell proliferation (P < 0.001, Fig. 5D) and the pro-apoptotic effects associated with MIR22HG silencing (P < 0.001, Fig. 5E and F). Furthermore, the MIR22HG silencing-weakened ARS staining level was considerably reversed by the miR-10a-5p co-inhibition (P < 0.05, Fig. 5G).

Discussion

Although emerging therapeutic strategies hold promise for improving fracture prognosis, delayed healing or nonunion remains a challenge in some patients [17]. LncRNAs offer a promising avenue for precision medicine, given their high sensitivity, capacity for non-invasive monitoring, and multifaceted roles in gene regulation [18]. Consequently, identifying blood-based markers is crucial to advancing our understanding and management of fracture healing.

Recent research has indicated that non-coding RNAs are crucial regulatory elements in the context of musculoskeletal disorders. For instance, miRNAs are involved in the repair processes of tendon injuries by modulating extracellular matrix metabolism and influencing tendon cell differentiation [19]. Furthermore, the differential expression of miRNAs in osteoarthritis has the potential to serve as both a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target [20]. siRNAs contribute to the maintenance of tendon homeostasis by regulating genes associated with tendon fibrosis [21]. In the case of rheumatoid arthritis, siRNAs can specifically target and inhibit inflammatory cytokines, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response [22]. Additionally, siRNAs have been shown to enhance bone health in osteoporosis by regulating genes involved in bone metabolism [23]. Advances in molecular technologies have revealed that numerous lncRNAs are involved in regulating fracture healing, and they are expected to be potential markers of DFH. For instance, reduced LINC-PINT expression slows DFH progression, underscoring its value as an early diagnostic indicator [24], while elevated LINC00339 in DFH patients not only predicts DFH onset but also modulates osteoblast function [25]. Increased serum levels of OIP5-AS inhibit fracture healing, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for DFH [26]. Additionally, osteoblasts exhibit 1,878 up-regulate and 1,842 down-regulated lncRNAs, which influence DFH by affecting osteogenic differentiation [27]. MIR22HG, previously identified as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target in OS [5], shows reduced expression in OA and OP patients, linked to chondrocyte apoptosis and impeded osteogenesis [6, 7]. Prior research has indicated that parameters associated with stress-induced hyperglycemia can act as independent predictors of DFH, aligning with the findings of this investigation [28]. This suggests a potential interplay between metabolic disorders and MIR22HG in the regulation of fracture healing. Notably, the non-diabetes subgroup benefits from high sensitivity, enabling effective identification of DFH cases, whereas the diabetes subgroup achieves a lower risk of misdiagnosis due to its higher specificity. These findings not only further support the prior conclusion from multivariate logistic regression analysis that MIR22HG is an independent predictor of DFH but also provide more tailored clinical insights for risk stratification of DFH among fracture patients with or without diabetes. The observed reduction in serum MIR22HG in DFH patients here suggests a protective role in fracture healing, while its strong discriminatory ability for DFH—coupled with its identification as a risk factor alongside advanced age and comorbid diabetes—supports its potential as a diagnostic marker and clinical predictor for DFH, though its regulatory mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Osteoblasts and their differentiation are essential for fracture healing. The normal expression of osteoblast differentiation markers is essential for completing proliferation, differentiation, and bone matrix mineralization; aberrant expression hinders bone repair and leads to DFH [29]. Furthermore, prior research has established a positive correlation between enhancements in bone microstructure and the efficiency of healing [30]. ALP, a marker of osteoblast activity, promotes matrix mineralization, and reduced ALP activity impairs osteogenesis, delaying fracture healing [31]. In our hFOB 1.19 osteogenic differentiation model, the concurrent up-regulation of osteogenic markers (RUNX2, OSX, OCN, OPN), ALP activity, and MIR22HG over time suggests a functional link between MIR22HG and osteogenic progression. The reduction in osteogenic markers, impaired cell proliferation, and increased apoptosis following MIR22HG silencing confirm its role as a key promoter of osteogenic differentiation—consistent with findings that MIR22HG knockdown inhibits osteogenesis in OP [7]. Mechanistically, MIR22HG may influence fracture healing by regulating the proliferation–apoptosis balance and the functional activity of osteoblasts, potentially via pathways such as PI3K/AKT or Wnt/β-catenin [5, 8, 9]. It is hypothesized that MIR22HG might involve in bone differentiation and metabolism through these pathways, thereby affecting fracture healing.

Recent studies have highlighted miRNAs as regulators of fracture healing, acting through processes such as osteoblast differentiation, chondrocyte function, angiogenesis, and apoptosis [32]. Furthermore, miRNAs have been also shown to influence bone metabolism through the regulation of transcription factors [33]. For instance, miR-7-5p overexpression enhances osteoblast viability and ALP activity in vitro and activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in vivo to promote fracture healing [34], while miR-21 targets TGF-β1/Smad and ERK pathways to activate osteogenic/angiogenic genes and inhibit chondrocyte apoptosis [35, 36]. As previously described, miR-10a-5p inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to promote chondrocyte apoptosis, and its suppression enhances osteogenic mineralization in vitro and new bone formation in vivo [13, 14, 16]. Furthermore, numerous studies have substantiated that lncRNAs function as miRNA sponges, playing a significant role in the regulation of bone metabolism [25] and the process of fracture healing [37]. In this study, the up-regulation of miR-10a-5p in DFH patients’ serum—along with its direct target binding and negative regulatory relationship with MIR22HG—suggests a functional interplay. Co-inhibition of both reversed the inhibitory effects of MIR22HG silencing on osteogenic differentiation, indicating that MIR22HG may relieve miR-10a-5p-mediated suppression of downstream osteogenic pathways (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin) by sponging miR-10a-5p, thereby enhancing osteogenic marker expression and accelerating fracture healing. Current intervention strategies for fracture healing have demonstrated some efficacy in promoting bone repair; however, they are not without limitations. For instance, while the integration of growth factors and biomaterials has been shown to facilitate healing, a notable 5% to 20% of patients continue to experience delayed healing [38]. Additionally, although 3D printing-assisted surgical techniques have been associated with a reduction in complication rates, their effectiveness is limited in the context of complex fractures, such as those occurring in diabetic patients [39]. Furthermore, while mesenchymal stem cell transplantation has the potential to modulate the immune microenvironment and enhance bone repair, the mechanisms underlying its regulation of osteogenic differentiation require further investigation [40]. The identification of the MIR22HG/miR-10a-5p regulatory axis in this study offers novel molecular markers and potential therapeutic targets for fracture healing, which may serve to augment existing intervention strategies and provide a theoretical basis for personalized precision treatment.

While this study illuminates the role of the MIR22HG/miR-10a-5p axis in fracture healing, limitations exist. It relied solely on an in vitro cell model, lacking in vivo validation in animals. Furthermore, this study was conducted on patients with tibial fractures, so the applicability to other fracture types requires verification. Future research will verify the in vivo function of the MIR22HG/miR-10a-5p axis by constructing animal fracture models and using a combination of high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics methods to explore downstream target genes and pathways. Furthermore, we plan to include patients with various types of fractures to increase sample diversity and improve the translational applicability of the research. In addition, this study failed to identify and validate the downstream osteogenic targets of miR-10a-5p. It also lacked target function and rescue experiments, resulting in incomplete ceRNA pathways. Subsequently, potential osteogenic targets of miR-10a-5p will be screened by combining bioinformatics databases with osteogenic pathways, and binding will be verified through a dual luciferase assay. Target overexpression/knockdown experiments will be used to clarify the role of target gene in osteogenic regulation. Rescue experiments will verify whether the target can reverse the inhibition of MIR22HG silencing on osteogenic differentiation. This will improve the understanding of the role of MIR22HG as a ceRNA in regulating fracture healing.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that MIR22HG promotes osteogenic differentiation and accelerates fracture healing by targeting miR-10a-5p. Serum MIR22HG could potentially be used as a potential clinical marker for predicting DFH. This regulatory axis provides novel insights into fracture healing mechanisms and lays the foundation for targeted molecular therapies.
