Covering rings by proper ideals
Malcolm Hoong Wai Chen, Eric Swartz, Nicholas J. Werner

Abstract

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we assume rings to be associative, but not necessarily commutative nor to have a multiplicative identity. Our goal is to determine when such a ring can be expressed as a finite union of its proper left, right, or two-sided ideals, as well as the cardinality of such a minimal cover.

The motivation for this problem comes from group theory. A group
G
is said to be
coverable
if there exists a collection of proper subgroups of
G
whose union is equal to
G
. It is an easy exercise to show that a group is never the union of two of its proper subgroups, and a classical result due to Scorza
[
25
]
shows that a group is the union of three proper subgroups if and only if it has a quotient isomorphic to the Klein four-group. Later, Cohn
[
6
]
began to systematically study the
covering number

\sigma(G)
of a group
G
, which is the minimum number of subgroups required to cover
G
. Cohn showed that there exists a group with covering number
p^{d}+1
for every prime
p
and integer
d\geqslant 1
, and Tomkinson
[
29
]
showed that there is no group with covering number seven. The covering numbers of groups have been studied extensively, and all integers
N\leqslant 129
that are not the covering number of a group were determined in
[
10
]
, which also contains a survey of the related work for groups.

This motivated inquiry into a broader question: What are the covering numbers of other algebraic structures? This has been studied for vector spaces
[
5
,
14
]
, modules (over commutative rings)
[
11
,
13
]
, loops
[
9
]
, and semigroups
[
8
]
. We refer the reader to the survey article
[
12
]
for more details.

The related question for rings has also received significant attention. This was first initiated by Lucchini and Maróti
[
17
]
, who determined all rings with covering number three. Building upon a series of earlier work
[
3
,
7
,
22
,
26
,
27
]
, the recent article
[
28
]
completely determined which integers can occur as covering numbers of rings. For a ring
R
, let
\sigma(R)
be the minimum number of proper subrings of
R
necessary to cover
R
(if such a cover exists). The approach in
[
28
]
is based on the observation that if
R
has a two-sided ideal
I
such that
R/I
has a cover, then it can be lifted to a cover of
R
, so
\sigma(R)\leqslant\sigma(R/I)
. A ring
R
is called
\sigma
-elementary
if the inequality
\sigma(R)<\sigma(R/I)
is strict for all
I\neq\{0\}
. Since any coverable ring is either
\sigma
-elementary, or has a proper
\sigma
-elementary residue ring with the same covering number, knowledge of
\sigma
-elementary rings allows us to characterize coverable rings and determine their covering numbers. A classification of all
\sigma
-elementary rings is given in
[
28
, Thm. 1.3]
.

While many questions on the covering numbers of groups remain open, if one restricts to covers by
normal
subgroups there is an elegant result
[
2
, Thm. 1]
(see also
[
1
]
): a group
G
is the union of proper normal subgroups if and only if it has a quotient isomorphic to
C_{p}\times C_{p}
(the direct product of two cyclic groups of prime order
p
); the corresponding covering number
\eta(G)
is
p+1
for the smallest such
p
. Our investigations in this paper are prompted by the ring-theoretic analogue, namely covers by proper ideals.

Motivated by the preceding discussions, we make the following definitions.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
R
be a ring. The
left
(resp.
right, two-sided
)
ideal covering number

\eta_{\ell}(R)
(resp.
\eta_{r}(R)
,
\eta(R)
) is the cardinality of a minimal cover of
R
by proper left (resp. right, two-sided) ideals, if such a cover exists, and
\infty
otherwise.

We say that
R
is
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary
(resp.
\eta_{r}
- or
\eta
-elementary
) if
\eta_{\ell}(R)<\eta_{\ell}(R/I)
(resp.
\eta_{r}(R)<\eta_{r}(R/I)
,
\eta(R)<\eta(R/I)
) for every nonzero two-sided ideal
I
of
R
.

We use the notation
\eta_{*}(R)
and
\eta_{*}
-elementary
in a statement which would apply to any of
\eta_{\ell}
,
\eta_{r}
, or
\eta
. Note that an
\eta_{*}
-elementary ring is coverable by the relevant type of ideal, which we will refer to as
*
-ideals
.

We remark that any unital ring cannot be covered by proper ideals, as any ideal containing the multiplicative identity is the whole ring. It follows that
\eta_{*}
-elementary rings are necessarily nonunital. However, as we shall see, any
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary (resp.
\eta_{r}
-elementary) ring that is not
\eta
-elementary contains a left (resp. right) multiplicative identity.

Notation
.

Let
C_{p}
denote the cyclic group of order
p
,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
the finite field of order
q
(a prime power), and
M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
the ring of
n\times n
matrices with entries from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Covers by two-sided ideals were first considered independently by O’Neill
[
20
]
and Parmenter
[
21
]
; in each paper, the authors provide characterizations of rings with covers by two-sided ideals. In contrast, covers by left (resp. right) ideals were, to the best of our knowledge, first studied in the preprint
[
4
]
, which suggested that

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\right\}\leqslant M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})

is an
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary ring for every prime
p
. In this present work, we generalize these results, providing a complete classification of
\eta_{*}
-elementary rings in each case as well as determining their ideal covering numbers. A ring
R
that is coverable by a finite number of
*
-ideals has an
\eta_{*}
-elementary residue ring with the same ideal covering number. Hence, our classification completely characterizes rings that are coverable by a finite number of proper ideals, and determines all possible ideal covering numbers of such rings.

Since every commutative
\eta_{\ell}
- and
\eta_{r}
-elementary ring is also
\eta
-elementary, it suffices to consider the noncommutative case when studying covers by one-sided ideals. Our main theorems are the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

R
is an
\eta
-elementary ring if and only if
R^{2}=\{0\}
and
(R,+)\cong C_{p}\times C_{p}
for some prime
p
. Moreover,
\eta(R)=p+1
.

From Theorem
1.2
, we recover
[
20
, Thm. 1]
and
[
21
, Thm. 2]
:
R
is coverable by finitely many two-sided ideals if and only if
R
has a finite homomorphic image
S
such that
S^{2}=\{0\}
and
(S,+)
is a nontrivial noncyclic group. We remark that Theorem
1.2
can be proved independently by appealing to
[
20
, Thm. 1]
or
[
21
, Thm. 2]
—which reduces the problem to covering a noncyclic abelian group—and applying a result of Cohn
[
6
, Thm. 2]
. We include our proof of Theorem
1.2
for completeness.

The situation for rings with a cover by left or right ideals is far more interesting.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
R
be a noncommutative ring.

(1)

R
is
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary if and only if

R\cong\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&v\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}:A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\text{ and }v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\right\}\leqslant M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

for some prime power
q
and integer
n\geqslant 1
. We have
\eta_{\ell}(R)=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}
.

(2)

R
is
\eta_{r}
-elementary if and only if

R\cong\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
v^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}:A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\text{ and }v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\right\}\leqslant M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

for some prime power
q
and integer
n\geqslant 1
. We have
\eta_{r}(R)=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}
.

Observe that it suffices to prove (1), since the proof of (2) follows by passing to the opposite ring. Moreover, if
R
is the ring of Theorem
1.3
(1) and
S=M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, then
R
is a left
S
-module. Under this identification, the left
S
-submodules of
R
are exactly the left ideals of
R
(see Lemma
4.1
). Thus, we have produced the first example of a module over a noncommutative ring with covering number
\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}
.

Corollary 1.4
.

For every prime power
q
and integer
n\geqslant 1
, there exists a left (resp. right)
M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
-module
M
such that the covering number of
M
by left (resp. right)
M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
-submodules is
\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}
.

This generalizes the known results on covers of modules over commutative rings. In
[
13
, Thm. 2.2]
, it is shown that if
R
is a commutative unital ring and
M
is an
R
-module that admits a finite cover by proper
R
-submodules, then the covering number of
M
is
q+1
for some prime power
q
. Our work shows that the determination of covering numbers of modules is still an open problem. It would be interesting to know if there are other integers that are covering numbers of modules.

2.
Preliminaries

It is well known that a nonunital ring
R
of characteristic
p
can be embedded into a unital ring
R^{\prime}
: let
R^{\prime}:=\mathbb{F}_{p}\times R
with multiplication given by the rule

(n_{1},r_{1})(n_{2},r_{2}):=(n_{1}n_{2},n_{1}r_{2}+n_{2}r_{1}+r_{1}r_{2}).

Notice that, for any
(n,r)\in R^{\prime}
,
(1,0)(n,r)=(n,r)(1,0)=(n,r)
, so
R^{\prime}
has a multiplicative identity, and there is a natural identification of
R
with
\{0\}\times R\subseteq R^{\prime}
, so we may view
R
as a subring of
R^{\prime}
. Such an extension
R^{\prime}
is sometimes called the
Dorroh extension
of
R
, although this name is sometimes reserved for the extension
\mathbb{Z}\times R
with analogous multiplication. Throughout this paper,
R^{\prime}
will always refer to the extension of
R
defined above.

We prove one basic result that will be used later.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
I
is any two-sided ideal of
R
, then
\{0\}\times I
is a two-sided ideal of
R^{\prime}
. In particular,
\{0\}\times R
is a two-sided ideal of
R^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For any
(n,r)\in R^{\prime}
and
(0,i)\in\{0\}\times I
, we have
(n,r)\cdot(0,i)=(0,ni+ri)
and
(0,i)\cdot(n,r)=(0,ni+ir)
, both of which are in
\{0\}\times I
. The result follows.
∎

If
R
is finite, then the structure of
R^{\prime}
is determined by the Wedderburn-Malcev Theorem (sometimes also called the Wedderburn Principal Theorem); see e.g.
[
23
, Sec. 11.6, Cor. p. 211]
or
[
18
, Thm. VIII.28]
. To describe the theorem, we need to define the Jacobson radical of a ring. There are a number of common equivalent definitions for the Jacobson radical of a unital ring. For a nonunital ring, the definition is more obscure. We follow the approach taken in
[
15
, Sec. 4]
.

If we define the operation
\circ
on a (not necessarily unital) ring
T
by
a\circ b\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}a+b-ab
, then
(T,\circ)
is a monoid with
0
as the identity element. An element of
T
is called
left (
resp.
right) quasi-regular
if it has a left (resp. right) inverse in the monoid
(T,\circ)
. A subset
I\subseteq T
is called
left (
resp.
right) quasi-regular
if every element of
I
is left (resp. right) quasi-regular. As in
[
15
, Sec. 4, Exercise 4]
, we have the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

The
Jacobson radical
of a (not necessarily unital) ring
T
is

\mathscr{J}(T)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{a\in T:Ta\text{ is left quasi-regular}\}.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that
\mathscr{J}(T)
is a two-sided ideal containing all left (resp. right) quasi-regular ideals of
T
. In particular, if
T
has a multiplicative identity, then
\mathscr{J}(T)
is the intersection of all maximal left (resp. right) ideals of
T
, which agrees with the usual characterizations of the Jacobson radical of a unital ring.

Theorem 2.4
.

(Wedderburn-Malcev Theorem)
Let
T
be a finite unital ring of characteristic
p
. Then, there exists an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-subalgebra
S
of
T
such that
T=S\oplus\mathscr{J}(T)
, and
S\cong T/\mathscr{J}(T)
as
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebras. The ring
S
is semisimple: for some
t\geqslant 1
,
S\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d_{i}})
, where each integer
d_{i},n_{i}\geqslant 1
.

An immediate consequence of the definition of
R^{\prime}
is that a subset
I\subseteq R
is a left ideal of
R
if and only if it is a left
R^{\prime}
-module. When viewing left ideals in this light, the following classical result will be useful:

Lemma 2.5
.

(Nakayama’s Lemma)
[
15
, Lem. 4.22]

Let
T
be a unital ring and
I
be a left ideal of
T
. Then,
I\subseteq\mathscr{J}(T)
if and only if for any finitely generated left
T
-module
M
and
N
a submodule of
M
,
M=N+IM
implies that
M=N
.

To use Nakayama’s Lemma, we need to know about the Jacobson radical of
R^{\prime}
. As it turns out, it is simply the Jacobson radical of
R
.

Lemma 2.6
.

\mathscr{J}(R^{\prime})=\{0\}\times\mathscr{J}(R)
.

Proof.

The following proof is adapted from and analogous to that of
[
24
]
. First, since
\{0\}\times R
is a maximal ideal of
R^{\prime}
by Lemma
2.1
,
\mathscr{J}(R^{\prime})\subseteq\{0\}\times R
.

Let
(0,x)\in\{0\}\times\mathscr{J}(R)
. By Lemma
2.1
,
\{0\}\times\mathscr{J}(R)
is a two-sided ideal of
R^{\prime}
. For any
(n,r)\in R^{\prime}
, we have
(n,r)(0,x)=(0,nx+rx)\in\{0\}\times\mathscr{J}(R)
. Since
nx+rx\in\mathscr{J}(R)
, it is left quasi-regular in
R
and hence left quasi-regular in
R^{\prime}
. Indeed, if
y
is the left inverse of
nx+rx
in
(R,\circ)
, then
(0,y)
is the left inverse of
(n,r)(0,x)
in
(R^{\prime},\circ)
. Thus,
\{0\}\times\mathscr{J}(R)\subseteq\mathscr{J}(R^{\prime})
.

Now, let
(0,x)\in\mathscr{J}(R^{\prime})
. Then, for every
(n,r)\in R^{\prime}
, there exists
(m,y)\in R^{\prime}
such that
(n,r)(0,x)+(m,y)-(m,y)(n,r)(0,x)=(0,0)
. We have

\displaystyle(m,y)

\displaystyle=(m,y)(n,r)(0,x)-(n,r)(0,x)

\displaystyle=(0,mnx+mrx+nyx+yrx-nx-rx)\in\{0\}\times R.

This gives
m=0
. So, for any
r\in R
, we may choose
n=0
and then
(0,y)\circ(0,r)(0,x)=0
in
R^{\prime}
. This implies
rx+y-yrx=0
in
R
, i.e.,
rx
is left quasi-regular and
x\in\mathscr{J}(R)
. The result follows.
∎

We close this section by demonstrating a form of Theorem
2.4
that holds for finite nonunital rings in characteristic
p
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
R
be a finite (not necessarily unital) ring of characteristic
p
and Jacobson radical
J
. Then, there exists a subring
S
of
R
such that
R=S\oplus J
and
S
is either
\{0\}
or semisimple.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.4
and Lemma
2.6
, there exists a semisimple subalgebra
S^{\prime}
of
R^{\prime}
such that

R^{\prime}=S^{\prime}\oplus\mathscr{J}(R^{\prime})=S^{\prime}\oplus(\{0\}\times J)

and

S^{\prime}\cong R^{\prime}/\mathscr{J}(R^{\prime})=R^{\prime}/(\{0\}\times J).

Since
\{0\}\times R
is an ideal of
R^{\prime}
by Lemma
2.1
, we have

R/J\cong(\{0\}\times R)/(\{0\}\times J),

so
R/J
is isomorphic to an ideal of the semisimple ring
S^{\prime}
. By
[
18
, Thm. VIII.5]
,
R/J
is either
\{0\}
or semisimple. Since
S^{\prime}\cap(\{0\}\times J)=\{0_{R^{\prime}}\}
, there exists an ideal
\{0\}\times S
of
S^{\prime}
such that

\{0\}\times R=(\{0\}\times S)\oplus\mathscr{J}(R^{\prime})=(\{0\}\times S)\oplus(\{0\}\times J),

i.e.,
R=S\oplus J
, where
S
is either
\{0\}
or semisimple.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

Note that while
S
(when nonzero) is isomorphic to a semisimple—hence unital—ring, we cannot immediately conclude that the action of
S
on
J
is nontrivial. That is, in the decomposition of Proposition
2.7
, it is possible that
SJ=JS=\{0\}
. This differs from the behavior of
S
and
J
in the decomposition
T=S\oplus J
of Theorem
2.4
, in which
1_{S}=1_{T}
and
SJ=JS=J
.

3.
Basic properties and reduction theorems

In this section, we establish some results for minimal covers by left and two-sided ideals, for the structure of
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary and
\eta
-elementary rings. Note that any statement regarding covers by left ideals has a natural analogue for right ideals. After proving Theorem
1.2
, which classifies all
\eta
-elementary rings, we focus solely on
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary rings.

We begin with a variation on
[
30
, Thm. 2.2]
, which gives a sufficient condition for the covering number of a direct product of rings to be equal to the covering number of one of the direct factors.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
R
be a finite ring such that
R=\prod_{i=1}^{t}R_{i}
, where each
R_{i}
is a ring. Assume that
*
-ideals of
R
respect this decomposition. That is, for each
*
-ideal
L\subseteq R
, there exist
*
-ideals
L_{i}\subseteq R_{i}
such that
L=\prod_{i=1}^{t}L_{i}
.

(1)

Each maximal
*
-ideal
M
of
R
has the form

M=R_{1}\times\cdots\times R_{i-1}\times M_{i}\times R_{i+1}\times\cdots\times R_{t}

for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant t
, where
M_{i}
is a maximal
*
-ideal of
R_{i}
.

(2)

\eta_{*}(R)=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant t}(\eta_{*}(R_{i}))
.

(3)

If
R
is
\eta_{*}
-elementary, then
t=1
.

Proof.

(1) Let
M
be a maximal
*
-ideal of
R
. By assumption,
M=\prod_{i=1}^{t}L_{i}
, where each
L_{i}
is a
*
-ideal of
R_{i}
. If there exist two indices
j\neq k
such that
L_{j}\neq R_{j}
and
L_{k}\neq R_{k}
, then let
M^{\prime}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}L_{i}^{\prime}
, where
L_{j}^{\prime}=R_{j}
and
L_{i}^{\prime}=L_{i}
for

. We have
M\subsetneqq M^{\prime}\subsetneqq R
, which contradicts the maximality of

. The result follows.

(2) This is identical to the proof of
[
30
, Thm. 2.2]
with each use of “maximal subring” replaced with “maximal

-ideal”.

(3) Suppose that
R
is
\eta_{*}
-elementary, but

. Then, each direct factor
R_{i}
occurs as a residue ring

of
R
for some nonzero two-sided ideal

of
R
. Since
R
is
\eta_{*}
-elementary,
\eta_{*}(R)<\eta_{*}(R_{i})
for each

. This contradicts (2).
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
R
be an
\eta_{*}
-elementary ring with minimal cover

by
*
-ideals. If
I
is a two-sided ideal of

that is contained in

for each

, then
I=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Assume that
I
is contained in
L_{i}
for each

. Then, each
L_{i}/(L_{i}\cap I)=L_{i}/I
is a proper
*
-ideal of
R/I
, and

form a cover of
R/I
. If
I\neq\{0\}
, then
\eta_{*}(R)=m\geqslant\eta_{*}(R/I)
, a contradiction to
R
being
\eta_{*}
-elementary.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

(1)

If
R
admits a finite cover by
*
-ideals, then
R
contains a two-sided ideal
I
of finite index such that
\eta_{*}(R)=\eta_{*}(R/I)
.

(2)

If
R
is an
\eta_{*}
-elementary ring, then
R
is finite and has prime characteristic.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that

, their intersection contains a two-sided ideal

-ideal cover of

(2) Assume that

-elementary. By (1),

:

. However, by

-ideals

is a minimal cover of

∎

We can now begin to derive structural results on

.

Lemma 3.4

If

-elementary ring, then

.

Proof.

By Lemma

,

. Then,

∎

Henceforth, we will define

Lemma 3.5

(1)

If

-elementary ring, then

-elementary ring, then

(1) Assume that

We now have everything necessary to classify

Proof of Theorem

.

(\Leftarrow)
Assume that

(\Rightarrow)

We claim that

Lemma 3.6
.

Let

be an
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary ring.

(1)

Both

and

are two-sided ideals of

, and

.

(2)

.

(3)

.

(4)

For every left ideal

, where

and

are left ideals of the rings

and

Proof.

(1) By Lemma
3.5
,

. With this is mind, it is routine to check that

and

are two-sided ideals of

. If
S=\{0\}
, then

, which implies

by Lemma

. Hence,

, and the result follows. So, assume

. Both

and

are subsets of

. For the reverse containment, let

. To show that the sum is direct, let

(2) This follows from (1) and Proposition

(3) Consider the multiplication of two elements of

,

(s_{1}+x_{1}+k_{1})(s_{2}+x_{2}+k_{2})=s_{1}(s_{2}+x_{2})=(s_{1}+x_{1})(s_{2}+x_{2}).

From this, it follows that

is closed under both left and right multiplication by elements of

.

(4) Let

be a left ideal of

. The containment

. Lastly,

Proposition 3.7

Let

\eta_{\ell}
-elementary ring. Then either

Let

Proposition 3.8
.

Let

be a noncommutative

-elementary ring. Then,

, where

. In particular,

By Lemma

,

4.
An infinite family of
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary rings

Let

be a prime power and

an integer. We now construct examples of nonunital rings

which are the union of finitely many proper left ideals, show that they are
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary, and prove that
\eta_{\ell}(R)=q^{n}+q^{n-1}+\cdots+q+1=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}
.

Throughout this section, let

be the set consisting of all
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrices with entries from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
whose last row is zero, which is the ring described in Theorem
1.3
(1). Then,

•

R
is a ring under matrix addition and multiplication.

•

Elements of
R
can be represented as
(A|v)
, where
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is a column vector. Multiplication in
R
follows the rule

(A|v)(B|w)=(AB|Aw).

•

Under the representation above, let
e=(I_{n}|0)
, where

is the

identity matrix. Then,

is a left identity of

, but is not a right identity. We have

as rings. Let

.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
L\subseteq R
. Then,
L
is a left ideal of
R
if and only if
L
is a left
S
-submodule of
R
.

Proof.

In both implications, closure under addition is trivial.

(\Rightarrow)
This is clear, because
S\subseteq R
and
RL\subseteq L
.

(\Leftarrow)
Assume that
L
is a left
S
-submodule of
R
. Let
r\in R
and
\ell\in L
. Since
e
is a left identity of
R
,
r\ell=(er)(e\ell)=(ere)\ell
, and this is in

because

.
∎

The following facts about
S
-modules hold because
S
is a (semi)simple ring.

•

S
-modules are semisimple. Hence, a nonzero
S
-module is a direct sum of simple
S
-modules.

•

Since
S\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, simple

-modules are isomorphic (as

-modules) to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. In particular, all such simple modules have size

. Consequently, any finite

-module must have order

for some

.

•

S
-modules are injective. So, if
L
and

are

-modules and

, then there exists an
S
-module

such that

.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

be a left ideal of

. Then,

is a maximal left ideal of

if and only if
|L|=q^{n^{2}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma

,

is an

-module. Since

is an injective

-module, there exists an

-module

such that

. The

is itself semisimple, so

is maximal if and only if

is simple, if and only if

.
∎

Next, we define two families of left ideals in

.

Definition 4.3
.

For each

(viewed as a column vector) and linear subspace

, and there are no restrictions on

Lemma 4.4
.

(1)

For each

.

(2)

.

.

Proof.

(1) It is straightforward to check that

. Since

, the left ideal

is generated by

, so

.

(2) This follows from (1).

(3) Clearly,

.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

(1)

For each linear subspace

.

(2)

.

(3)

Let

.

(4)

(1) Let

. For maximality, let

(2) Fix a linear subspace

. Let

b_{n-1}^{T}&0\\

Then,

. Next, given,

.

(3) This follows from the observation that for any linear subspaces

such that the right nullspaces of

are exactly

, respectively.

(4) This is clear from the definition of

.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

The left ideal covering number of

Proof.

By Lemmas

4.5

and

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, which is (using Gaussian binomial coefficients)
\binom{n}{1}_{q}=1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}
. So,
\eta_{\ell}(R)\geqslant q^{n}+q^{n-1}+\cdots+1
. On the other hand,
\eta_{\ell}(R)\leqslant q^{n}+q^{n-1}+\cdots+1
because the collection of all left ideals
L_{v}
and
N_{V}
forms a cover of
R
by Lemmas
4.4
(3) and
4.5
(4). We conclude that
\eta_{\ell}(R)=q^{n}+q^{n-1}+\cdots+1=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}
.
∎

It remains to demonstrate that
R
is
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary. For this, we will describe all of the two-sided ideals of
R
.

Lemma 4.7
.

The Jacobson radical of
R
is
\mathscr{J}(R)=\{(0|v):v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\}
, and this is the only proper nonzero two-sided ideal of
R
.

Proof.

Let
J=\{(0|v):v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\}
. Then,
J
is a proper nonzero subset of
R
, and it is easy to see that
J
is a two-sided ideal of
R
. We will show that
J
is the only such ideal with these properties. Let
I
be any proper nonzero two-sided ideal of
R
.

Suppose that there exists
(A|0)\in I
with
A\neq 0
. Then,
I
contains the two-sided ideal of
M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
generated by
A
, which is equal to
M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
itself. Hence, the left identity
e
of
R
is in
I
, and since
IR\subseteq I
, we see that
I=R
. This is a contradiction. So, whenever
(A|0)\in I
,
A
must be 0.

Now, let
(B|v)\in I
. Then,
(B|0)=(B|v)e\in I
. By the previous paragraph,
B=0
, so each element of
I
has the form
(0|v)
. Thus,
I\subseteq J
. Since
I
is nonzero and
M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
acts irreducibly on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we must have
I=J
.

Finally,
\mathscr{J}(R)
is a two-sided ideal of
R
. This ideal is nonzero because
J\subseteq\mathscr{J}(R)
, and it is proper because
(I_{n}|0)\notin\mathscr{J}(R)
. Thus,
\mathscr{J}(R)=J
.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

The ring
R
is
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.6
,
R
is coverable by left ideals. By Lemma
4.7
,
\mathscr{J}(R)
is the only proper nonzero two-sided ideal of
R
. The residue ring
R/\mathscr{J}(R)\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is unital, and hence not coverable by left ideals. Therefore,
R
is
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Because
R
contains a left identity,
R
cannot be covered by right ideals or two-sided ideals. Hence,
\eta_{r}(R)=\eta(R)=\infty
.

5.
Structure of noncommutative
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary rings

Throughout this section, let
R
be a noncommutative
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary ring. By Proposition
3.8
,
R=S\oplus J
, where
S
is a subring of
R
which is semisimple of characteristic
p
and
J=\mathscr{J}(R)
is a left
S
-module. Furthermore,
JR=\{0\}
and

R\cong\left\{\begin{pmatrix}s&j\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}:s\in S\text{ and }j\in J\right\},

where the operations are usual matrix addition and multiplication.

The purpose of this section is to prove that
S
is a simple ring and
J
is a simple left
S
-module. Consequently, we can deduce that
S\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
for some prime power
q
and integer
n\geqslant 1
, and that
J\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. This then corresponds to one of the
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary rings constructed in Section
4
, which will complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proposition 5.1
.

S
is a simple ring. Thus,
S\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
for some prime power
q
and integer
n\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

Since
S
is semisimple, we have
S=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}S_{i}
for simple unital rings
S_{1},\ldots,S_{t}
that mutually annihilate one another (that is,
S_{i}S_{j}=\{0\}
whenever
i\neq j
). We will show that
t=1
. For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant t
, let
e_{i}
be the multiplicative identity of
S_{i}
, so that
e=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{t}
and
e_{i}e_{j}=0
when
i\neq j
. Also, for each
i
let
J_{i}=S_{i}J
(it is possible that
J_{i}=\{0\}
). We have
J=SJ
and

R=S\oplus SJ=\Big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}S_{i}\Big)\oplus\Big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}S_{i}\Big)J=\Big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}S_{i}\Big)\oplus\Big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}J_{i}\Big)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}(S_{i}\oplus J_{i}).

We know that
e_{i}e_{j}=0
when
i\neq j
, and
JR=\{0\}
, so the rings
S_{i}\oplus J_{i}
and
S_{j}\oplus J_{j}
mutually annihilate one another when
i\neq j
. It follows that, as rings,
R\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}(S_{i}\oplus J_{i})
. We will show that left ideals of
R
respect this decomposition.

Let
L
be a left ideal of
R
. For each
i
, let
L_{i}=L\cap(S_{i}\oplus J_{i})
, which is a left ideal of
S_{i}\oplus J_{i}
. Certainly,
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}L_{i}\subseteq L
. For the reverse inclusion, assume that
a\in L
. Since
a\in R
, we may write
a=\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}
, where each
a_{i}\in S_{i}\oplus J_{i}
. Then,
e_{i}a=e_{i}a_{i}=a_{i}\in S_{i}\oplus J_{i}
. But,
e_{i}a\in L
, so
a_{i}\in L_{i}
. Thus,
a=\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}L_{i}
, and so
L=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}L_{i}
.

We can now apply Lemma
3.1
(3) and conclude that
t=1
. Thus,
S\cong R/J
is a simple unital ring.
∎

Next, suppose that
S\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
for some prime power
q
and integer
n\geqslant 1
. Let
e
be the multiplicative identity of
S
, which is also a left identity of
R
. Since
S
is simple, we may express
J
as
J=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\lambda}L_{i}
, where
\lambda\geqslant 1
and each
L_{i}
is a simple left
S
-module. So,
|L_{i}|=q^{n}
for each
i
. Our goal is to show that
\lambda=1
.

Note that since
JR=\{0\}
, any left ideal contained in
J
is a two-sided ideal of
R
. In particular,
I=\bigoplus_{i=2}^{\lambda}L_{i}
(which is
\{0\}
if
\lambda=1
) is a two-sided ideal of
R
, and
R/I
is isomorphic to the ring of Section
4
. So,
R
is coverable by left ideals and
\eta_{\ell}(R)\leqslant\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}=\binom{n+1}{1}_{q}
.

Definition 5.2
.

For each
x\in J
, let
S_{x}:=\{A+Ax:A\in S\}
. When
\lambda\geqslant 2
, let
\widehat{J}_{1}:=\bigoplus_{i=2}^{\lambda}L_{i}
and
\widehat{J}_{2}:=L_{1}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=3}^{\lambda}L_{i}
. Finally, when
\lambda\geqslant 2
and
x\in L_{1}
, define
T_{1,x}:=S_{x}\oplus\widehat{J}_{1}
; and if
x\in L_{2}
, then define
T_{2,x}:=S_{x}\oplus\widehat{J}_{2}
.

We will show that each
T_{1,x}
and each
T_{2,x}
is a maximal left ideal of
R
with a two-sided ideal complement in
R
. By the next lemma, each such left ideal must be part of any minimal cover of
R
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
R
be an
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary ring with minimal cover
I_{1},\ldots,I_{m}
by left ideals. If
R=M\oplus I
for some maximal left ideal
M
and two-sided ideal
I\neq\{0\}
of
R
, then
M=I_{k}
for some
k
.

Proof.

If
M\subseteq I_{k}
for some
k
, then
M=I_{k}
by maximality and we are done. So, suppose that
M\not\subseteq I_{k}
for all
k
. Then,
I_{1}\cap M,\ldots,I_{m}\cap M
is a cover of
M
by left ideals, so
\eta_{\ell}(R)=m\geqslant\eta_{\ell}(M)=\eta_{\ell}(R/I)
, a contradiction to
R
being
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that
\lambda\geqslant 2
.

(1)

For each
x\in J
,
S_{x}
is a left ideal of
R
, and
S_{x}\cong S
as rings.

(2)

Let
x\in L_{1}\cup L_{2}
. Then,
T_{1,x}
and
T_{2,x}
are both maximal left ideals of
R
. If
x\in L_{1}
, then
R=T_{1,x}\oplus L_{1}
; and if
x\in L_{2}
, then
R=T_{2,x}\oplus L_{2}
.

(3)

Each
T_{1,x}
and
T_{2,x}
is unique. That is, if
x\in L_{1}
and
y\in L_{2}
, then
T_{1,x}\neq T_{2,y}
; if
x,y\in L_{1}
and
x\neq y
, then
T_{1,x}\neq T_{1,y}
; and if
x,y\in L_{2}
and
x\neq y
, then
T_{2,x}\neq T_{2,y}
.

Proof.

(1) Let
x\in J
. It is clear that
S_{x}
is closed under addition. Given
r\in R
, we may write
r=B+y
, where
B\in S
and
y\in J
. Then, for any
A+Ax\in S_{x}
, we have
r(A+Ax)=BA+BAx\in S_{x}
. Finally, one may verify that the map
S\to S_{x}
sending
A\in S
to
A+Ax\in S_{x}
is a ring isomorphism.

(2) We prove this for
T_{1,x}
; the proof for
T_{2,x}
is similar. Assume that
x\in L_{1}
. By (1),
S_{x}
is a left ideal of
R
, so
T_{1,x}
is a sum of left ideals, hence itself is a left ideal. For maximality, note that left ideals of
R
correspond to left
S
-submodules of
R
, and any maximal left
S
-submodule of
R
has order
q^{n(n+\lambda)-n}=q^{n(n+\lambda-1)}=|T_{1,x}|
. Lastly, we show that
T_{1,x}\cap L_{1}=\{0\}
. If
y\in T_{1,x}\cap L_{1}
, then
y=A+Ax
for some
A\in S
. So,
A=y-Ax\in S\cap J=\{0\}
, which means that
y=0
. Thus,
T_{1,x}\subsetneqq T_{1,x}\oplus L_{1}
, and by maximality
T_{1,x}\oplus L_{1}=R
.

(3) When
x\in L_{1}
and
y\in L_{2}
, the left ideal
T_{1,x}
contains nonzero elements of
J_{2}
, while
T_{2,y}
does not. Similarly,
T_{2,y}
contains elements of
L_{1}
that are not in
T_{1,x}
. So,
T_{1,x}\neq T_{2,y}
. In the case where
x,y\in L_{1}
, suppose that
T_{1,x}=T_{1,y}
. Recalling that
e
is the identity element of
S
, we see that
e+x\in T_{1,x}=T_{1,y}
. So,
e+x=A+Ay
for some
A\in S
. The condition
S\cap J=\{0\}
forces
A=e
and
x=y
. Similarly, for
x,y\in L_{2}
, having
T_{2,x}=T_{2,y}
implies that
x=y
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

J
is a simple left
S
-module. Thus,
J\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\lambda\geqslant 2
. As noted before Definition
5.2
,
\eta_{\ell}(R)\leqslant\binom{n+1}{1}_{q}
. Since
|L_{1}|=|L_{2}|=q^{n}
, by Lemma
5.4
(3),
R
contains
2q^{n}
left ideals of the form
T_{1,x}
or
T_{2,x}
. Recall that because
JR=\{0\}
, both
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are two-sided ideals of
R
. So, by Lemma
5.4
(2), each
T_{1,x}
and each
T_{2,x}
is a maximal left ideal of
R
with a two-sided ideal complement in
R
.

Since
R
is
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary, by Lemma
5.3
, each
T_{1,x}
and each
T_{2,x}
must be part of any minimal cover of
R
by left ideals. Thus,
\eta_{\ell}(R)\geqslant 2q^{n}
. This is a contradiction, because
\binom{n+1}{1}_{q}<2q^{n}
. Therefore,
\lambda=1
and the result follows.
∎

We complete the paper with our proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

First, it suffices to prove the result for
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary rings, since the proof of (2) follows from passing to the opposite ring. Let
R
be a noncommutative
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary ring. By Lemma
3.3
(2),
R
is finite and has characteristic
p
. By Proposition
3.8
,
R=S\oplus J
, where
J
is the Jacobson radical of
R
,
S\neq\{0\}
is semisimple,
SJ=J
, and by Lemma
3.5
,
JR=\{0\}
. By Proposition
5.1
,
S
is simple, i.e.,
S\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
for some prime power
q
and integer
n\geqslant 1
. By Proposition
5.5
,
J
is a simple left
S
-module, thus
J\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. Finally,
R
is actually
\eta_{\ell}
-elementary by Corollary
4.8
, and
\eta_{\ell}(R)=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}
by Proposition
4.6
.
∎
