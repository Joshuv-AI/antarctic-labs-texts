Introduction

Within medical education, established forums for shared learning including informal ones remain highly valuable. Since the early models described by James Paget and later formalised by William Osler in the nineteenth century, journal clubs (JCs) have provided a structured and collegiate arena for collaborative academic discussion and critical appraisal.1 Over time, this foundations of this model has persisted and expanded worldwide.2 More than a century later, JCs continue to play an important role in medical education, although they now exist in many different forms.3

This evolution has occurred alongside broader developments in medicine, including advances in technology and an increasing emphasis placed on evidence-based medicine (EBM) within both undergraduate and postgraduate curricula. The educational benefits of JCs have been documented across multiple studies, and although they are often perceived as only informal meetings, substantial work has been undertaken to refine their structure and optimise their educational impact.4 While JCs remain widely used throughout medical education, their format has evolved considerably. Traditional face-to-face meetings now coexist with online, hybrid, and social media-based models, while educational strategies such as flipped-classroom learning and emerging artificial intelligence (AI) tools continue to reshape how JCs are delivered. Despite these developments, the literature remains fragmented, with contemporary evidence dispersed across different JC formats, educational settings, and emerging technologies. Although previous reviews have examined aspects of JCs, many predate the widespread adoption of virtual, hybrid, social media-based, flipped classroom, and AI-assisted models. The aim of this narrative review is to address this gap by synthesising the contemporary evidence, examining the factors associated with successful implementation, and exploring future directions for journal club education.

Methods

A narrative review of the published literature was conducted to identify relevant articles relating to JCs in medical education. The primary aim was to examine contemporary formats, factors associated with successful implementation, and emerging future directions.

The literature search was performed by the first author on 17 May 2026 using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar. No publication date restrictions were applied, and only articles published in English were considered eligible. The search strategy combined keywords including “journal club”, “medical education”, “evidence-based medicine”, “social media”, “online journal club”, “virtual journal club”, “flipped classroom”, and “artificial intelligence” using Boolean operators (AND/OR) as appropriate for each database. In addition, the reference lists of included articles were manually screened to identify further relevant publications.

Original research articles, systematic reviews, narrative reviews, and other relevant scholarly publications such as commentaries and editorials were all considered for inclusion. Studies were selected based on their relevance to the objectives of the review, with particular emphasis on contemporary JC models, educational outcomes, implementation strategies, and future developments.

Contributions from both medical and surgical specialties were included because principles are broadly applicable across disciplines and specialty-specific literature is relatively limited. Findings were synthesised narratively and organised into themes relating to educational foundations, contemporary formats, factors associated with successful implementation, and future directions.

Educational Foundations of Journal Clubs

The enduring presence of JCs within medical education reflects their continued educational relevance and adaptability. Indeed, such is their perceived importance that JCs have previously been described by researchers as both a “cognitive sandbox” and a “signature pedagogy” as well as an enduring “educational legacy” within medicine.5–7 Despite substantial changes in healthcare delivery and medical training, JCs continue to provide a structured forum for collaborative learning and critical engagement with scientific literature.

Evidence-Based Medicine and Critical Appraisal

As EBM has become increasingly central to modern clinical practice, clinicians face growing expectations to remain up to date with the rapidly expanding scientific literature. JCs provide an environment in which participants can critically evaluate emerging evidence while simultaneously developing skills in critical appraisal, study design, research methodology, and statistical analyses.8 In a randomised trial involving general surgeons, MacRae et al found that participants receiving a JC intervention incorporating article review, online discussion, and structured methodological critique demonstrated significantly greater critical appraisal skills than those provided with articles alone.9 This randomised study provides strong evidence that structured JCs can improve objective educational outcomes, specifically critical appraisal skills. However, these are findings from a single study only and other studies with the same level of evidence remain limited.

Beyond the acquisition of knowledge, JCs can help participants develop practical strategies for managing the growing volume of medical information. Pato et al described them as a model for lifelong learning, arguing that the skills developed through critical appraisal and literature evaluation support ongoing professional development beyond formal training. The authors further emphasised the importance of equipping learners with methods to efficiently identify, assess, and organise relevant evidence, enabling clinicians to remain current in an era of information overload.10 Of note, these conclusions are based primarily on expert opinion and observation rather than comparative intervention studies and should therefore be interpreted within that context.

The social aspect of JCs is also worthy of note and relates to Wenger´s Communities of Practice (CoP) theory.11 This highlights four interconnected processes: community (a shared purpose), meaning (understanding constructed through discussion), identity (professional development through participation), and practice (knowledge built through shared clinical experience). In a case study involving video-recorded journal club sessions and participant interviews, Quinn et al identified all four elements of CoP. The authors highlighted the importance of senior clinician attendance, noting that experienced members contributed valuable clinical insights and feedback while promoting the active engagement of junior participants.12

Participation in JCs has also been shown to positively influence clinicians’ reading behaviours and engagement with contemporary medical literature, while facilitating the translation of evidence into clinical practice.13 This is particularly important given the recognised gap between the generation of new knowledge and its implementation in clinical practice.14 JCs represent a highly adaptable educational model. Their ability to evolve in response to changing educational needs, technologies, and modes of delivery may help explain their enduring presence within undergraduate and postgraduate medical education programmes worldwide.

Characteristics, Advantages, and Limitations of Contemporary Journal Club Formats

Virtual and Online Journal Clubs

A particularly novel feature of some contemporary JCs is the direct involvement of the actual study authors within discussions. De Cates et al described a model in which authors were invited to participate in sessions or provide a commentary in advance.5 This approach allows participants to gain insights into study design, methodology, and interpretation that may not be apparent from the published manuscript alone, while also fostering interaction between learners and researchers. Social media (SoMe) platforms were used extensively for advertising and recruitment. The authors concluded that this model improved inclusivity and accessibility. However, these reported benefits are based largely on observational studies and participant experience. A particularly novel aspect was the inclusion of a patient representative within the discussion panel.

Online JCs gained popularity during COVID-19 pandemic, which accelerated their development. As highlighted by Awied et al, they allow for larger audiences although some attendees can feel intimidated by this. Discussion intensity can also be less, and technical issues do arise.15 Moreover, Balamurali et al, refer to the reduced spontaneity and interpersonal interaction associated with online JCs.16

Social Media Based Journal Clubs

Social media (SoMe) is now embedded within the day-to-day lives of clinicians. As such, it is perhaps unsurprising that it has also emerged as a platform for hosting JCs.17 Twitter, for example, has been used given it can serve as a microblogging platform. Thangasamy et al reported finding at least 17 of such JCs in the Twitter arena.18 One example is the International Urology Journal Club that operated by having a monthly article to be discussed asynchronously over a 48 hour period.19 In a follow up survey, 81% of participants felt it supported their growth in EBM. The study also applied Kirkpatrick’s four-level evaluation model (reaction, learning, behaviour, and results) to assess the educational impact of their JC. These findings demonstrate strong evidence of learner satisfaction and engagement (Kirkpatrick Level 1), with some evidence of behavioural change (Level 3). However, objective improvements in knowledge acquisition or patient outcomes have not yet been consistently demonstrated.

Advantages of SoMe include global reach, opportunities for networking and collaboration, and a format that aligns well with the communication preferences of younger generations. Social media–based journal clubs also provide accessible and time-efficient platforms for professional discussion, dissemination of EBM and continuing education. However, caution is required, as participant identity and expertise may be difficult to verify, discussions can be challenging to moderate effectively, and formats may need adaptation to maintain educational quality. Platforms such as Twitter are additionally limited by character restrictions, may present a learning curve for some users, and can allow dominant voices to disproportionately influence discussions.20 Engagement and interaction may also wane over time.

Hybrid Journal Clubs

Hybrid models combining online, and in-person participation have also been proposed as an alternative approach, aiming to balance accessibility with the benefits of face-to-face interaction. Although conceptually attractive, there is currently limited evidence demonstrating that hybrid models achieve truly superior educational outcomes compared with traditional or fully virtual JCs.

As for online JCs, virtual attendance has also been promoted as an advantage from a carbon footprint perspective.16

Flipped Classroom Journal Clubs

The “flipped classroom” approach involves participants reviewing the article beforehand to enable a more in-depth and interactive discussion during the journal club session.21 This can promote a shift from surface to deep learning.22 However, evidence supporting improved objective educational outcomes remains limited, with most published studies reporting learner engagement and perceived educational benefit rather than measured improvements in performance.

Factors that can Contribute to Successful Journal Club Implementation

Preparation is recommended through prior dissemination of the selected article, although approaches vary considerably. Traditionally in JCs, one person prepares and presents a summary of the study during the session, with participants often undertaking minimal prereading, if any. This can be problematic if the individual leading the session has limited prior experience and selects a poor-quality article or is unable to critically appraise it effectively.23 This may have a knock-on effect, reducing the educational value for other participants and potentially leading to overly critical feedback from senior discussants or supervisors, which can negatively affect the presenter’s confidence.

More broadly, contemporary JC design has increasingly shifted towards learner-centred educational models.24 Rather than focusing solely on the critical appraisal of individual articles, modern journal clubs may seek to promote collaboration, participation, and shared learning among attendees. Educational theory has framed journal clubs as the aforementioned CoPs, where knowledge is developed through discussion and interaction between learners with varying levels of experience. Accordingly, the format adopted should reflect the needs and educational objectives of the target audience rather than adhering rigidly to traditional structures. Notwithstanding this, structured appraisal tools are increasingly being incorporated into journal clubs to guide discussion and promote a more systematic evaluation of the literature. Common examples include the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklists and the JAMA Users’ Guides to the Medical Literature, often complemented by reporting frameworks such as CONSORT and PRISMA. These tools can help participants assess study methodology, validity, and clinical applicability, while providing less experienced learners with a structured approach to critical appraisal.

The Mayo Clinic shared their experiences from 24 years of running a JC. The authors highlighted several factors that contributed to its success, including regular scheduling and shared responsibility for presenting articles.25 They also recorded sessions, allowing discussions to be archived and improving flexibility and accessibility for participants across different institutions and time zones.

A recurring theme within the literature is the importance of balancing trainee autonomy with senior faculty involvement.24 While experienced clinicians provide valuable expertise and mentorship, excessive reliance on faculty-led discussion may limit engagement from junior participants. Conversely, journal clubs led entirely by inexperienced trainees may lack sufficient depth or structure. Successful models therefore often combine learner ownership with facilitative support from senior colleagues. Indeed, those leading the sessions can function more as facilitators, reflecting the Socratic method of teaching through probing questions and guided discussion rather than passive learning. This approach may also help reduce some of the hierarchical barriers that can inhibit discussion within traditional journal clubs. Facilitated discussion encourages broader participation and allows attendees to contribute perspectives and uncertainties that may otherwise remain unspoken in more didactic formats.24 Khan et al also advocate for the study discussion to be centred around a core clinical problem that participants are currently encountering in their own practice, helping to improve the clinical relevance and applicability of the journal club discussion.23

Collectively, these findings suggest that successful JCs are characterised not by any single format, but by their ability to engage participants actively, encourage collaborative learning, and align educational activities with the needs of their audience. Traditional, virtual, and hybrid models may all be effective when these principles are incorporated into their design.

Taken together, the available literature provides the strongest support for improvements in learner engagement and perceived educational value, whereas evidence demonstrating objective educational outcomes remains comparatively limited. Much of the published evidence is derived from single-centre studies, descriptive reports, and participant surveys, with relatively few studies directly comparing different journal club formats. Consequently, although the available evidence is encouraging, conclusions regarding the superiority of individual journal club models should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Different JC formats may be suited to different educational contexts. For example, traditional and flipped classroom models may be particularly appropriate for undergraduate and residency training where structured discussion and faculty facilitation are desirable. Virtual and hybrid models may be advantageous for interinstitutional collaboration and geographically dispersed learners, whereas social media (SoMe) may facilitate wider professional networking and international communities. The choice of format should be ideally shaped by the educational objectives, learner characteristics, and available resources.

Challenges and Future Directions

Artificial intelligence (AI), including large language models (LLMs), has, like SoMe, become more integrated into everyday life.26 These technologies have been proposed as tools to assist JCs, for example by helping prepare materials for meetings, a task often considered time-consuming. However, Shrebati et al argue that caution is needed, as reliance on such tools may lead to atrophy of cognitive skills and impede creativity.6 As highlighted by Tazelaar et al, human review remains essential to verify the accuracy of AI-generated content and minimise the risk of hallucinations or misleading interpretations produced by generative AI tools.25,27 At present, evidence supporting the educational effectiveness of AI-assisted journal clubs remains largely theoretical or based on expert opinion, highlighting the need for prospective evaluation of their impact on learning outcomes.

Conclusion

JCs remain a valuable component of medical education. They facilitate critical appraisal, support EBM, promote collaborative learning, and help bridge the gap between research and clinical practice. While their format has evolved considerably, their core educational functions remain the same. Successful implementation appears to depend less on the specific format adopted and more on aligning the JC with learners’ needs and educational objectives. Participant engagement, preparation, clinical relevance, and effective facilitation are all elements to foster.

Although the available evidence is encouraging, the quality of the supporting evidence remains variable, and future research should therefore prioritise evaluation of objective educational outcomes across different JC models.
