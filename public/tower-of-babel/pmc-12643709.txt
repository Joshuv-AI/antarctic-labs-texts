The Split Latissimus Free Flap for Reconstruction of Defects Around the Ankle: Exploring the Potential of the Workhorse
Authors: Georgios Karamitros, Alexander Hahn, Raymond A. Pensy, Mark J. Gage, Gregory A. Lamaris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:The latissimus dorsi muscle flap is a widely used flap for soft tissue reconstruction. It is supplied by the thoracodorsal artery, which has a consistent intramuscular branching, allowing for flap division into 2 or more segments. We present a case of a 17-year-old woman with a complex lower extremity soft tissue defect involving the plantar foot and posterior ankle following a motorcycle accident. Reconstruction was performed using a latissimus dorsi musculocutaneous flap that was split between the transverse and descending branches of the thoracodorsal artery to create 2 independently perfused hemiflaps. The transverse portion resurfaced the Achilles tendon, whereas the descending portion covered the plantar wound. This case highlighted the utility of the split latissimus dorsi flap in reconstructing irregularly shaped, adjacent defects in the lower extremity with a single flap. Although fasciocutaneous flaps have gained popularity, the latissimus dorsi remains a reliable and adaptable workhorse flap due to its size, consistent anatomy, and minimal donor-site complications.

The latissimus dorsi is a widely used muscle for extremity reconstruction.1 In most cases, the thoracodorsal artery splits intramuscularly into a transverse and a descending branch, coursing along the cephalad and lateral muscle border, respectively.2

This constant branching pattern of the artery allowed the development of several variations in flap harvest technique.1,3–5 First described by Tobin et al,3 the latissimus dorsi can be split into 2 units, each supplied by the descending and transverse thoracodorsal artery branches. That seminal study led to the development of techniques of harvesting only a portion of the muscle, sparing the residual muscle’s vascular supply, innervation, and function.1,6

Furthermore, even if the entire muscle is harvested, it can be separated into multiple subunits based on the thoracodorsal artery branching pattern.4,5,7 Herein, we describe using the split latissimus dorsi for reconstructing an irregularly shaped lower extremity defect spanning the posterior aspect of the ankle and the plantar aspect of the foot.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 17-year-old woman sustained a degloving foot injury. After wound debridement, the resulting defect spanned the plantar aspect of the foot and the posterior aspect of the ankle (Fig. 1). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the soft tissue defect of the plantar aspect of the foot with exposed plantar fascia, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E517.) Given the irregular defect shape, we used a split latissimus flap for reconstruction.

Soft tissue defect of the left foot following degloving injury, after debridement to healthy tissue margins. The wound spans the posterior aspect of the ankle, exposing the Achilles tendon, and the plantar aspect of the foot, exposing the plantar fascia.

The muscle was harvested in the standard fashion based on the thoracodorsal bundle. The descending and transverse thoracodorsal branch course was marked on the flap surface in situ using a handheld Doppler, and the muscle was split between the 2 vessels (Fig. 2). The flap was transferred to the leg using the posterior tibial bundle as the recipient vasculature. The transverse branch segment lined the Achilles tendon, whereas the descending branch segment resurfaced the plantar foot. A split-thickness skin graft was used to cover the muscle. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10; 9 months following reconstruction, she was back to regular activities using normal footwear (Figs. 3, 4). DISCUSSION

Soft tissue reconstruction around the ankle and foot is challenging; paucity of local tissues requires free tissue transfer.8 Both fasciocutaneous and muscle flaps have been described for lower extremity reconstruction, and the debate on the ideal flap is ongoing.8–10 Historically, muscle flaps were used more frequently in traumatic extremity wounds, as the robust perfusion was expected to help with bony union and prevent infection; recent studies have shown fasciocutaneous flaps to be as effective in limb salvage, with no difference in complication rates, osteomyelitis, or bony union.9,10 Ultimately, flap selection relies on the unique defect characteristics and patient-specific reconstructive needs, as both muscle and fasciocutaneous flaps have advantages in terms of donor-site morbidity and the ability to provide thin, pliable tissue to match the recipient site.8–10

Of all the muscle flaps described for extremity reconstruction, the latissimus dorsi is the most frequently used.9 Its large surface area; superficial location among the back musculature; consistent pedicle anatomy; straightforward flap elevation; and low donor-site morbidity, except for seroma formation and potential shoulder weakness, all account for its popularity. The consistent intramuscular branching pattern of the thoracodorsal artery allows division of the flap into separate, independent units.2 In 94% of cases, the neurovascular trunk bifurcates into 2 branches, each coursing along the cephalad and lateral border of the muscle, whereas in only 6% of cases, the neurovascular trunk gives 3 or 4 major proximal branches.2 This understanding led to the description of the split latissimus dorsi myocutaneous flap to cover separate defects around the shoulder and upper extremity.3 Subsequently, muscle splitting techniques have been described for harvesting only a segment of the latissimus dorsi, while preserving the perfusion and innervation of the remaining muscle to minimize donor-site morbidity and retain residual muscle function.1,6

Pertaining to the reconstruction of extremity wounds, taking advantage of the branching pattern of the thoracodorsal artery can expand the potential of the latissimus dorsi when the entire muscle unit is harvested. Chiang and Wei4 first described the harvest of 2 segmental free flaps for coverage of 2 separate lower extremity defects. Furthermore, to maximize flap length, Wei and Lin5 described the widely split latissimus dorsi muscle flap where the 2 hemiflaps—each based on the descending and transverse branch—can be sequentially linked in a chain fashion to reconstruct defects up to 50 cm in length. More recently, Borschel7 described a free latissimus dorsi muscle divided into 3 subunits for single-stage coverage of the hand and 3 adjacent fingers. Herein, we showcase the application of the split latissimus dorsi flap for the reconstruction of 2 adjacent soft tissue defects along the posterior aspect of the ankle and plantar aspect of the foot. The branching pattern of the thoracodorsal artery can be ascertained intraoperatively with transillumination7 or a handheld Doppler, and the course of the different branches can be marked on the surface of the latissimus dorsi, allowing for safe division of the muscle without compromising its perfusion and for reliable harvest of the entire muscle unit. Splitting the muscle into 2 fan-shaped hemiflaps allowed us to cover the irregularly shaped wound with only 1 flap, simplifying the reconstructive plan. The thin, pliable tissue of the muscle achieved optimal coverage of the wound, whereas the subsequent atrophy allowed the use of normal footwear and return to regular activities and sports. The only revision needed was the excision of the skin island, which is routinely harvested in our practice for flap monitoring in the immediate postoperative period, as it allows the early identification of vascular compromise.10

In conclusion, we present the reconstruction of a challenging lower extremity wound covered with a split latissimus dorsi muscle flap. The consistent anatomy of the thoracodorsal artery allows the division of the muscle to cover irregularly shaped defects. Although the debate over the ideal extremity reconstruction flap endures and the enthusiasm surrounding fasciocutaneous flaps grows, the latissimus dorsi is still a workhorse in the armamentarium of the reconstructive surgeon.
