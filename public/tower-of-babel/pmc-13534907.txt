Eyes on the water: How local mariners can accurately detect a submerged human proxy in shallow nearshore coastal water with modern recreational fish finders
Authors: Britny A. Martlin, Lynne S. Bell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt has recently been demonstrated that modern recreational fish finders with high‐frequency (800 kHz) Sidescan transducers can detect submerged human bodies. This ubiquitous technology is widely used on the water and has the potential to be utilized for the rapid detection of submerged decedents in any waterbody. This research investigates whether, with minimal training, the boating public can use modern recreational fish finders to accurately locate a submerged human proxy in a shallow nearshore coastal location. One human‐shaped plastic proxy was submerged, resting on the water bottom, at a depth of ~8 m in a semi‐sheltered bay with a silty bottom. Ten parallel searches were completed by nine local volunteers using four different recreational vessels. Volunteers reported the location of the proxy when seen on the fish finder. Three vessels were equipped with recreational fish finders capable of high‐frequency (800 kHz) Sidescan and Downscan sonar. One vessel was equipped with low‐frequency (200 kHz) conventional down‐looking sonar. High‐frequency Sidescan sonar provided clear and distinctive visuals of the submerged proxy in all volunteer sonograms. Most reported locations were <10 m of the target location. This research demonstrated that local mariners with mixed boating experience can successfully use recreational fish finders with high‐frequency (800 kHz) Sidescan transducers to identify and report accurate locations of a submerged human proxy in a shallow (<20 m), nearshore coastal environment. This sophisticated recreational technology provides the potential to enhance community awareness and involvement for the early detection of submerged decedents.

INTRODUCTION

To recover a body from the water, first, the body must be found. In British Columbia (B.C.), 26% of individuals who have gone missing in the coastal ocean are never recovered; the lowest recovery rate of all aquatic environments [1]. Submerged decedents that are recovered successfully from the coastal ocean are mainly recovered within the first day [1, 2]. Rapid detection is imperative for a successful recovery. Bodies can travel great distances in the ocean prior to recovery [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9], but when found within the first 24 h, the body is often found in the submersion location or very close to the submersion location [1, 2]. After 24 h of submersion in the ocean, it becomes far less likely that a missing submerged decedent will be found [1]. It is likely their body will be displaced by currents and tides until washing near the shore or sinking into great depths from which it will not resurface [1, 6, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Even in shallow waters, a body can quickly become invisible to the naked eye.

Commercial Sidescan sonar has been increasingly in use for the detection of submerged human remains [17, 18, 19, 20, 21], while recreational fish finders have only begun to be investigated for this purpose [22]. Modern recreational fish finders are sophisticated units, which are already widely in use, can attach to almost any vessel, are hull‐mounted, and incorporate multiple sonar technologies (including Sidescan sonar), and have GPS receivers built directly into them [22] which are accurate within ~5–10 m, with position accuracy of 15–20 m 95% of the time [23, 24]. High‐frequency (800 kHz) recreational Sidescan sonar has previously been successful at detecting submerged bodies in shallow waters [22], and allows large areas of the water bottom and column to be searched quickly from the surface.

Engagement of the public for the detection of submerged decedents has not yet been attempted. SAR teams in B.C. are widely made up of volunteers, who are a specific group of specially trained individuals [25, 26, 27], and private companies with sonar equipment have been hired by families to search for missing decedents in the water [28, 29], but the boating public has until now been overlooked. Modern fish finders incorporating high‐frequency Sidescan transducers are already widely used by the public on multiple waterbodies, including the coastal ocean and inland lakes and rivers, and are an untapped resource which could be employed for early detection of submerged decedents.

Recent analysis completed in B.C. by Martlin and Bell [2024] demonstrated (1) that there is a very short period of opportunity (<24 h) within which to locate a submerged decedent in the coastal ocean, and (2) that decedents submerged in the coastal ocean are the least likely to be recovered. Rapid detection is necessary for the greatest possibility of recovery in the coastal ocean. Subsequent work by Martlin and Bell [2025] thoroughly validated recreational fish finder technology for the rapid detection of submerged decedents. Systematic testing of the technology was completed using a single recreational fish finder attached to a single small vessel in a controlled freshwater location, and outdoor lake and coastal ocean environments using multiple proxies and living human participants. The results of this previous work demonstrated that human bodies and proxies can be detected with a recreational fish finder using high‐frequency (800 kHz) Sidescan sonar both in the water column and on the water bottom in shallow depths (<20 m) in both lakes and the coastal ocean [22].

Engaging the public with their own recreational sonar technology to assist with the rapid detection of submerged decedents is a logical next step. The boating public who already operate these sophisticated recreational fish finders are currently an untapped resource who could act as ‘eyes on the water’ to rapidly locate a missing submerged decedent in shallow waters, anywhere recreational boaters and anglers frequent. Engagement of the public could result in the detection of submerged decedents prior to them being displaced from the submersion location or sinking into great depths from which they may not be found. The current research builds on previous results [1, 22] to determine if members of the boating public who already use this technology on their own vessels can assist with the early detection of submerged decedents in shallow water contexts. This study assesses the ability of local mariners with mixed experience to accurately detect a submerged human‐shaped proxy submerged in a semi‐controlled, shallow (~8 m) nearshore coastal environment using modern recreational fish finders on multiple recreational vessels. The aim is to determine if, with minimal training, the boating public can correctly and accurately identify a decedent submerged in shallow waters using recreational fish finder technology.

Recruitment was completed through local fishing clubs, Facebook groups, marinas, and fishing supply stores. Recruitment material was shared online, and verbal snowball recruitment was encouraged. Although the response was positive, this research required a specific set of individuals and could not be completed over a long period of time, thus limiting the number of participants who could engage with the research within the required time.

Materials

A Lowrance HDS Live 12 fish finder with dual‐frequency Active Imaging 3‐in‐1 Transducer was attached to a Wilderness Systems Tarpon 120 kayak as the main survey vessel. This research setup was previously validated by Martlin and Bell [2025] for the detection of submerged proxies and living human participants. This fish finder represents an easily available unit with a transducer that incorporates multiple sonar types (Sidescan and Downscan) at high resolution frequencies (800 kHz), which can be viewed and recorded simultaneously on a single monitor, while also navigating and recording GPS waypoints using the in‐system navigational charts. The unit was provided on loan from Navico Canada for the completion of this research.

To determine if local mariners with mixed experience can be called upon to detect a submerged decedent utilizing their own vessels and recreational equipment, multiple vessels and equipment types were included in this research alongside the main survey vessel. The three volunteer vessels were motorized: (1) a 12′ inflatable, hard‐bottomed Zodiak with rear‐mounted portable outboard motor with tiller handle, with hull‐mounted Lowrance HDS fish finder and Active Imaging Transducer, allowing for 800 kHz Sidescan and Downscan; (2) a 17’ Boston Whaler centre console powerboat, with hull‐mounted Lowrance Elite 9 fish finder and Active Imaging Transducer, allowing for 800 kHz Sidescan and Downscan; and (3) a 27’ Northwestern centre console powerboat, with hull‐mounted Garmin ECHOMAP Ultra fish finder and GT24UHD Transducer, which only allowed 200 kHz frequency down‐looking sonar to be utilized. Four participants used the kayak, two participants used the 12′ Zodiak, two participants used the 17′ powerboat, and two participants used the 27′ powerboat. Participant 2 used both the kayak and the Zodiak (Table 2).

One human proxy measuring 183 cm in height was utilized for this research (Figure 2). This proxy was chosen as it has previously been validated as a comparable proxy for submerged human bodies and has been successfully detected using the same recreational search vessel and transducer setup [22]. This proxy was made of a hard plastic torso with 10 cm corrugated plastic piping attached for arms and legs, and a 5‐liter plastic bottle used as a head. The proxy was clothed in quick‐dry hiking pants, a raincoat, and rubber boots and was weighted to rest supine on the water bottom, using a sandbag attached to the underside of the torso and fishing weights zip‐tied onto the arms and legs.

Human proxy: (A) construction and (B) clothed in quick‐dry hiking pants, a raincoat, and rubber boots.

The drop location and defined search area were chosen to ensure a bottom composition of sand or silt, clear of obstructions, with a shallow depth of ~8 m. No bottom obstructions ensure the proxy is not obscured on the sonogram, and shallow depths result in the clearest sonar images [17, 19, 22]. The confirmed search area was digitally marked using GPS waypoints (N48 35.841, W123 23.081; N48 35.821, W123 23.055; N48 35.821, W123 23.144; N48 35.803, W123 23.084) (Figure 3) and totaled ~264m2.

Map of the search area depicting the parallel search pattern used to detect the submerged target: Beginning at marker A, moving across the search area, completing parallel transects, and finishing at marker D. Maps were created with ArcGIS Pro (version 3.0).

The target was positioned within the search area at N48 35.819, W123 23.088 by dropping the target from the surface. A fender was attached to the target on 15 m of weighted line to allow for recovery. The weighted line ensured that the fender floated away from the target location and did not get in the way of participant transects. A parallel search of the defined search area was completed after placing the target, ensuring the target was detectable and located at the correct GPS coordinates and allowing for comparison between volunteer and researcher sonar images.

Participant protocol

Prior to each search, volunteers were shown a selection of still images obtained during previous research using the high‐frequency recreational fish finder [22] to demonstrate what the submerged proxy looks like on sonar (Figure 4). Each corner of the search area was digitally marked on each volunteer's fish finder as a GPS waypoint prior to their search. Volunteers were instructed to complete straight and parallel transect lines in reverse directions at slow speeds until full coverage of the search area was completed (Figure 3), as outlined for aquatic searches with commercial Sidescan sonar [17]. Locations were reported as GPS waypoints every time volunteers suspected they saw the target on the sonogram. Volunteers who utilized the research equipment, rather than their own, were shown how to create a GPS waypoint on the fish finder by hovering the cursor over the target and saving the location as a GPS waypoint. The nature of the parallel search with wide Sidescan swath widths means that the submerged target is visible on the sonogram during multiple transects as the search vessel moves across the search area (Figure 5), as such, multiple waypoints could be reported for the target during a single search. Sonar transects were completed using the highest frequency available (Table 2), and Sidescan swath widths were set to 30 m. Searches were conducted at speeds between 1.2 and 3.7 knots (Table 2).

Three Sidescan sonar images obtained from Participant 2 (a)'s search, all depicting the submerged target on different transects during the same search: (A) depicts the target ~22 m to the right of the search vessel (SVI = Good), (B) depicts the target ~20 m to the right of the search vessel (SVI = Fair), and (C) depicts the target ~10 m to the left of the search vessel (SVI = Poor). Dependent upon how close the search vessel was to the submerged target during each transect, the target's position within the sonogram is altered to reflect the distance it is from the search vessel.

Eight participant searches were completed with Sidescan‐capable transducers, viewing both Sidescan and Downscan simultaneously at 800 kHz frequency. Two participant searches utilized a conventional down‐looking sonar with no Sidescan capability, at only 200 kHz (Table 2). Sonar logs were recorded for seven searches as .sl2 files onto external memory cards. Participants 5 and 6 used a fish finder which did not allow for recording of the sonar log; video recordings of the fish finder monitor were made instead. Participant 3's search was not recorded due to technical difficulties. Search patterns and waypoint creation were also directly observed. After each search was complete, recordings and GPS waypoints were shared for analysis. Feedback was also requested from participants post‐search to gauge how they perceived the task, compared to how they performed, gaining an understanding of what they did and why.

Post‐processing analysis

Reported GPS locations were uploaded to ArcGIS Pro for analysis using WGS 1984 as the GPS Coordinate system. Post‐processing of sonar logs was completed in Reefmaster Waypoint Manager. Each reported location was compared to the actual target location, and reported sightings were compared to the actual number of times the target was visible in the sonar log. Visuals of the target were exported as .png files and assessed using the Sonar Visibility Index (SVI) (Table 3) from zero (None) to three (Good), which was used in previous research [22]. Additionally, the presence of a sonar shadow was scored as either present or absent.

RESULTS

A total of 38 GPS waypoints were reported by volunteers, with 31 within the defined search area. Most waypoints were <10 m of the actual target location. The target was visible in all Sidescan sonar logs, four Downscan sonar logs, and was not visible on conventional down‐looking sonar.

Sonar technology

The use of high‐resolution Sidescan provided the majority of target sightings (Figure 6), and the clearest images of the submerged target (Figures 7 and 8). The target was visible in participant Sidescan sonar logs 23 times. All participant Sidescan logs showed the target multiple times. In contrast, the target was only visible on participant Downscan logs six times, in only four participant Downscan logs, and was never visible on low‐frequency conventional down‐looking sonar (Figure 6).

Within a single search, target visuals received differing scores on the SVI scale. Of the 23 times the target was visible on volunteer Sidescan recordings (Table 4; Figure 8), 14 (60.9%) were scored Fair (9; 64.3%) or Good (5; 35.7%) for visibility. Those sightings which were scored as Good clearly demonstrate the distinctive human shape of the target, with easily defined head and legs and a brighter sonar return compared to the water bottom (Figure 8). Participants were able to obtain Sidescan images of the target, which were of comparable quality to Sidescan images collected prior to volunteer search commencement (Figure 7). Twenty‐one (91.3%) Sidescan images of the target had a visible shadow present (Figure 8), which were helpful for visual detection; however, they did not offer greater detail regarding target shape.

Downscan logs demonstrated a clear lack of target visibility. Of the six times the target was visible on volunteer Downscan recordings (Table 5), 5 (83.3%) were scored Poor for visibility, with only one (16.7%) scoring Fair and none scoring Good (Figure 9). Although the target was detected using Downscan by four volunteers, the resulting images lacked clear target definition, causing difficulty when determining what was being seen on the sonogram (Table 5; Figure 9). Only one participant (7) reported a location every time the target was visible on the Sidescan sonogram, and only when the target was visible on the sonogram (Figure 6). Of these five instances, in post‐processing, two scored Good, one scored Fair, and two scored Poor (Table 4). The target was never visible on the Downscan log (Table 5). Participant 7, although not with the most years of experience, was the most confident in interpreting the Sidescan sonogram and reporting locations of the target.

Four participants reported locations less frequently than the actual times the target was visible. Participants 1 and 4 both reported only one location each. The target was visible on the Sidescan sonogram of Participant 1 three times, two scoring Fair, one Poor, and the Downscan sonogram once, scoring Poor (Tables 4 and 5). Similarly, the target was visible in Participant 4's Sidescan log four times, all scoring Poor (Table 4), and just once on the Downscan log, scoring Fair (Table 5). As all Sidescan visuals were scored as Poor, it is likely that these were not recognized in real time by the participant. Participant 9 reported four locations (Figure 6), although the target was visible on the sonar log five times: three times on the Sidescan log, two scoring Fair and one scoring Good (Table 4), and twice on the Downscan log, scoring Poor (Table 5). During Participant 2 (a)'s search, they reported a location three times (Figure 6), although the target was visible five times; three times on Sidescan, one each scoring Poor, Fair, and Good (Table 4), and twice on Downscan, both scoring Poor (Table 5). However, the participant noted that they only recognized the target on the Sidescan sonogram during their search.

Four participants reported locations more frequently than actual times the target was visible. During Participant 2(b)'s search they reported a location four times; however, the target was visible only twice on the Sidescan sonogram, both scoring Fair (Figure 6, Table 4). Participant 8 reported a location 14 times during their search. The target was only visible on Participant 8's Sidescan log three times (Figure 6), of which each image scored Poor, Fair, and Good on the SVI scale (Table 4). Participant 8 did correctly identify the submerged target; however, they also reported six additional locations within the search area and five locations outside of the search area, which denoted a submerged crab trap (Figure 10). Participant 8 made it clear that while they have a decade of experience (Table 2), they less often utilized the fish finder, suggesting that participant overzealousness may be expected from individuals who are less experienced in discriminating between targets.

Participants 5 and 6 both utilized the conventional low‐frequency down‐looking sonar. Both participants reported two locations, with one location reported by each participant outside of the search area. Re‐examination of the video recording for both searches demonstrated no visual of the target on the low‐frequency down‐looking sonar (Figure 6).

Location accuracy

Over half the waypoints reported within the search area were <10 m of the actual target location (20, 64.52%). Waypoints were most often reported between 5–10 m from the actual target location (12, 38.71%), with 8 waypoints (25.81%) reported <5 m of the actual target location. Eleven waypoints (35.48%) were reported >10 m from the target, but within the search area, four of which (19.05%) were reported between 11–15 m of the actual target location. Seven waypoints were reported outside of the search area (one each by Participants 5 and 6, and five by Participant 8). Six reported locations, which fell outside of the search area, were reported over a submerged crab trap, which was not part of this research. Waypoints outside the search area were not included in the analysis.

All of the locations reported by Participant 7 were <10 m of the actual target location. Participant 3 reported a single location, which correctly identified the actual location of the target to <5 m. Six of nine (66.7%) waypoints reported by Participant 8 were <10 m of the actual target location. Participant 9 reported four locations, all <15 m from the actual target location, and three of which were <10 m (Table 6). The location reported by Participant 5 inside the search area was <5 m of the actual target location; however, the video record showed no clear visual of the target (Figure 10). During search 2 (a), Participant 2 reported a location three times, two <10 m of the actual target location and one between 10–15 m of the actual target location (Table 6). During search 2(b), Participant 2 reported a location four times, two of which were <5 m of the actual target location (Table 6). Both locations reported by Participant 6 were >10 m from the actual target location. Participants 1 and 4 created only a single waypoint each, both >15 m from the target (Table 6).

Vessel use

Four volunteers utilized the main survey vessel, while three other volunteer vessels were utilized by the additional five volunteers. One individual participated using two different vessels on two different days (Table 2).

Due to the size of the search area, the smaller vessels were able to more easily complete the parallel search pattern, as they could make quicker turns for completion of multiple straight transects. Larger vessels had to complete turns outside of the search area to ensure straight parallel transects. The 27′ powerboat required a large turning radius and so was often outside of the search area and had to reposition prior to completing the next transect. Further, the 27′ vessel completed transects of the search area relatively quickly due solely to its size, spending much of its time turning and repositioning. The 17′ powerboat similarly completed turns far outside the search area but required a smaller turning radius, so was able to more quickly return to the search pattern. Volunteers who used the kayak and the 12′ Zodiak were more easily able to complete tighter turns, resulting in turns often happening within or right at the edge of the search area. Variation in speed occurred mainly during turns and repositioning efforts.

Participants completed between 6 and 15 transects within the search area, and search patterns varied slightly, with some overlapping transects and some directional variation between transects (Figure 11). One participant using the kayak struggled to maintain an appropriate search pattern (Figure 11E), which, although they passed near the submerged target, likely resulted in the single reported location, which did not correspond with the actual target location. Two participants (Figure 11C,H) noted the target on the sonogram and completed extra transects around the target to obtain further visuals. Regardless of slight changes in the search pattern completed during each participant's search, the target was visible on all participant's Sidescan sonar logs. Using Sidescan sonar for underwater searches allows the use of a wide swath width, covering large areas of the water bottom in one transect. Large swath widths ensure that complete bottom coverage is attained, even with some variability in search pattern.

DISCUSSION

This research demonstrates that with minimal instruction, local mariners with mixed experience can successfully and accurately detect a submerged human proxy in a shallow (~8 m) nearshore coastal environment using modern recreational fish finders with high‐frequency (800 kHz) Sidescan transducers. This technology has been previously validated by Martlin and Bell [2025] for the detection of submerged bodies and proxies in multiple environments. The current research proves that the public who already use this sophisticated technology is an untapped resource who could be successful at rapidly locating a missing decedent submerged in shallow water.

Previous research has demonstrated that the coastal ocean is one of the most difficult environments to find submerged individuals due to the short window of opportunity (<24 h) within which an individual can be expected to be located [1]. Successfully completing this research has demonstrated that targets submerged in the coastal ocean can be accurately detected by the public within 5–10 m using modern recreational technology.

Sonar technology

Only modern fish finders incorporating transducers with high‐frequency Sidescan sonar should be used for the detection of missing submerged decedents. These results are in line with previous work utilizing fish finders for the detection of submerged bodies and proxies, which also suggested high‐frequency Sidescan as the most appropriate sonar technology for this purpose [22]. All participants' Sidescan logs captured images of the submerged target, and Sidescan provided clearly defined images of the target regardless of the user. Positioning a vessel directly above an unknown target is extremely difficult; likely the reason why the target in this study was only visible on four participant Downscan logs. Using a wide swath width with Sidescan allowed volunteers to be at variable distances from the target and obtain clear visuals, similarly noted in research utilizing commercial Sidescan [19].

Multiple elements can impact target visibility, including boat speed and continuity of speed, surface movement, direction of travel, direction of the transducer in relation to the target, meandering transects, and bottom composition. Tidal movement, waves, and strong currents can also cause unexpected boat movement, which may impact the sonar visual as well as pull a vessel off course [17]. Further, detection using Sidescan sonar becomes far more difficult as the body becomes disarticulated and loses distinctiveness of shape, and can be further encumbered if the body is obscured by bottom features [19]. Rapid response using a wide swath width and high frequency for shallow water searches of large areas provides the greatest possibility of obtaining a defined visual of the target. Further, identification of submerged bodies using recreational Sidescan technology is not limited to coastal zones or bodies resting on the bottom. More distinctive sonogram visuals are easier to obtain in still, freshwater lakes and when the body is within the water column [19, 22].

Participant geolocation accuracy

The results from this study have demonstrated that local mariners with mixed experience can (1) identify a submerged human‐shaped target, and (2) report locations which are accurate to <10 m of the actual target location.

Identification

Identification of the intended target on the real‐time sonogram is of utmost importance for the accurate reporting of its location. Variation in sonogram image quality, along with participant experience level, likely impacted how many times a target was identified by each participant. It is likely that those target visuals that scored poorly on the SVI scale were often overlooked by volunteers, as more defined sonogram images are easier to differentiate in real time. Further, when the body is resting on the bottom, it can be difficult to differentiate it from the surrounding environment, especially with the resulting small sonar shadow [22]. High‐frequency (800 kHz) Sidescan sonar and parallel transects provide the best opportunity for a clearly defined image of the target on the sonogram. Those volunteers with greater sonar experience were also more easily able to correctly identify the target, even when target visuals were of poorer quality.

Search volunteers must be decisive about what they report as a potential body. Less familiarity with normal underwater disturbances caused difficulty when differentiating between the “human” target and other anomalies, which led to false reports. An expanded reference manual including a larger dataset of non‐target images would better assist individuals in the future with differentiating between the target and other anomalies, giving volunteers the confidence to discriminate between possible human targets and other objects, regardless of experience level.

Location accuracy

Reported locations clustered within a 10 m radius of the target, proving that recreational fish finders can be used by citizens with mixed experience to accurately locate a submerged target. Even when the same volunteer was correctly reporting the target location multiple times, position accuracy varied within a 10 m radius of the target. Recreation‐grade GPS receivers are not always 100% accurate, causing geolocation variation within ~10 m [23, 24], suggesting that much of the geolocation variation seen in this study was a result of the technology itself. When volunteers could correctly identify the target, reported locations were well within accepted geolocation accuracy. If a missing decedent can be detected prior to being greatly displaced, providing a radius of 10 m offers a small target area for a subsequent recovery operation to begin.

Vessel use

All recreational vessels in this research were used successfully; however, the size and type of vessel should be considered based on the terrain. Smaller, more agile vessels allowed the parallel search to be completed with greater ease, as did the motorized vessels. Greater maneuverability allowed for tighter turns between transects and less repositioning time. Larger vessels can be used for searching large areas, but vessels <20 feet in length will likely continue to prove the most useful in multiple environments. Motorized vessels also allowed for greater control, especially when moving against ocean tides and currents. Kayaks may prove the most useful for shallow shoreline searches, in confined areas, or in river systems with difficult‐to‐navigate terrain, where submerged obstructions might hinder motor‐powered vessels [30].

The future of community detection

The widespread use of modern recreational fish finders provides a force of local boaters who are already using this technology and could act as ‘eyes on the water’ to help rapidly locate a submerged decedent. There is a short period of opportunity within which to locate a submerged individual in the coastal ocean (<24 h) [1]. The best chance of recovery in the coastal regions is if the individual is located quickly, and in shallow nearshore waters. It is also likely that a submerged body will be displaced until either washing near to shore or sinking into great depths from which they will not resurface [1]. Local members of the boating community are already on the water in the same areas in which individuals are likely to go missing due to recreational activities and could respond immediately when a person has gone missing in the water, locating the decedent prior to their body being displaced from the area.

Community engagement could be professional‐led or community‐led. Locals are often alerted to missing persons on land, and SAR teams in Canada are widely volunteer‐based [27]. On land, the B.C. Emergency Alert System is currently used to send community alerts to smartphones. A similar alert system could alert local boaters who are already in the vicinity of a submersion incident to assist with the initial detection of missing decedents in shallow waters, prior to a professional dive team recovery operation. Public engagement could also be particularly useful in isolated communities where immediate professional response might be more difficult, or in areas without professional response teams. Awareness surrounding the use of recreational technology to accurately detect submerged decedents could lead to communities proactively creating an action plan to manage the detection of submerged decedents prior to the arrival of official search and recovery agencies. The rapid detection of the body is the first step to a successful recovery. If a missing submerged decedent can be rapidly detected by the community, a recovery operation can begin at the reported sighting.

CONCLUSION

Modern recreational fish finders with high‐frequency Sidescan capable transducers that local boaters are already equipped with on their own vessels are a potential resource, which are not currently being used for underwater detection. The public who already uses this sophisticated technology could act as ‘eyes on the water’ for the rapid detection of submerged decedents. This research builds on previous work by Martlin and Bell [2025], which demonstrated that modern recreational fish finders can be used to detect submerged bodies and proxies in shallow waters (including lakes and the coastal ocean). The current research has now proven that the boating public can use these modern recreational fish finders with high‐frequency (800 kHz) Sidescan sonar on a variety of vessels to accurately detect a submerged human proxy.

There is a very short period of opportunity within which to locate a submerged decedent, especially in the ocean. To enhance the chances of recovery from the ocean, missing decedents should be found within the first hours after submersion. Completing this research in the nearshore, coastal ocean proves that submerged targets can be detected by the public using recreational fish finders in the most difficult location, with the shortest window of opportunity. The engagement of the public who already use this sophisticated sonar technology could enhance search efforts and provide another avenue for the rapid detection of submerged decedents, particularly in areas that have limited resources or where rapid response by officials is difficult. The public who are already using this technology could assist with locating a missing decedent prior to their body being greatly displaced. Increasing community awareness concerning the potential of this technology could empower local communities to create their own rapid submersion response plan to provide increased early response to submersion incidents involving missing decedents.
