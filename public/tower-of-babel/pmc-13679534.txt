Cytokine- and Airflow-Based Clustering Identifies COPD Phenotypes with Differing Quantitative CT Features
Authors: Qing Wang, Yinan Yao, Mengling Xia, Jingyang Huang, Xinhong Huang, Yuan Ye, Jianya Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous disease with distinct inflammatory profiles that may be associated with key pathological features, including emphysema and airway wall remodeling. This study aimed to identify COPD subgroups based on circulating pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine activity and airflow limitation and to examine their clinical, physiological, and quantitative CT characteristics.MethodsDemographic characteristics, symptom scores, pulmonary function parameters, and quantitative CT indices were collected from patients with stable COPD. Serum levels of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Cluster analysis was performed using a Gaussian mixture model (GMM).ResultsA total of 319 stable COPD patients were included. Four distinct clusters were identified. Cluster 1 (Severe-low inflammation) was characterized by the lowest circulating inflammatory cytokine levels, the greatest symptom burden, the most severe airflow limitation, an increased exacerbation frequency, and the most extensive emphysema. Cluster 2 (Mild-low inflammation) exhibited low levels of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, mild symptoms, relatively preserved lung function, and a low frequency of annual exacerbations. Cluster 3 (Moderate-elevated inflammation) had moderately elevated levels of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, with intermediate mMRC and CAT scores, pulmonary function impairment and exacerbation frequency. Cluster 4 (Extremely high inflammation) was characterized by markedly elevated levels of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, intermediate symptom burden, relatively preserved pulmonary function, pronounced airway wall thickening and a high frequency of moderate-to-severe exacerbations. Further cytokine ratio analysis revealed an IL-6-related shift in Clusters 3 and 4 and an IL-17-related shift predominantly in Cluster 3.ConclusionIntegrating cytokine profiles with airflow limitation identified four exploratory COPD phenotypes, highlighting a potential dissociation between systemic inflammation and disease severity. External and longitudinal validation is needed to confirm their reproducibility and clinical relevance.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a chronic inflammatory airway disorder primarily characterized by persistent and progressive airflow limitation. Upon activation, various inflammatory cells release an abundance of pro-inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and tissue-degrading proteases, which drive the progression of airway inflammation and irreversible destruction of distal airspaces, thereby accelerating the onset and worsening of bronchitis and emphysema.1,2 Recent studies have underscored the marked heterogeneity in the inflammatory profile of COPD. Long regarded as a relatively homogeneous condition predominantly driven by smoking and exposure to harmful particulates, accumulating evidence now reveals substantial diversity among COPD patients in clinical manifestations, disease trajectory, treatment responses, and prognosis.3,4 This profound heterogeneity undermines the traditional uniform management strategy and emphasizes the imperative for refined phenotyping or endotyping grounded in underlying biological mechanisms to facilitate personalized therapeutic interventions.

To address this heterogeneity, data-driven clustering approaches have been increasingly applied to COPD phenotyping. Previous studies based on clinical, physiological, and quantitative imaging variables have identified subgroups differing in symptoms, comorbidities, exacerbation risk, airflow limitation, emphysema, and airway abnormalities.5–7 Other studies incorporating systemic or airway inflammatory biomarkers have revealed distinct inflammatory patterns among patients with COPD.8,9 However, relatively few have examined the coordinated variation of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines in relation to physiological impairment and quantitative CT features. In particular, the relationship between systemic inflammatory profiles and structural abnormalities, including emphysema-related parenchymal destruction and airway wall changes, remains incompletely characterized. Exploring this relationship requires consideration of both inflammatory mediators and the counter-regulatory signals within the COPD inflammatory network.

Within this network, pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines play complementary roles in the heterogeneous pathophysiology of COPD. The pro-inflammatory cascade, involving cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, IL-17, and IL-33, is associated with neutrophil recruitment, airway epithelial dysfunction, mucus hypersecretion, and lung tissue destruction, contributing to recurrent acute exacerbations and the progression of emphysema.10–14 Concurrently, endogenous anti-inflammatory mechanisms exert counterbalancing effects. Novel anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-27, IL-35, and IL-37, serve as key players in modulating immune response: IL-27 inhibits Th17 cells and promotes regulatory T cells differentiation,15,16 whereas IL-35 and IL-37 have been implicated in the regulation of inflammatory responses in COPD.17,18 The dynamic balance, or imbalance, between these opposing pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory forces likely shapes the distinct inflammatory milieu in individual patients, giving rise to varied clinical phenotypes. Therefore, moving beyond single or limited biomarkers to systematically analyze the network of both anti- and pro-inflammatory factors offers a promising perspective for unraveling the inflammatory heterogeneity of COPD.

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted an unsupervised clustering analysis in a cohort of patients with stable COPD. We combined a composite score representing the shared variation in circulating pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines with FEV1% predicted to identify inflammatory-physiological phenotypes. We hypothesized that the resulting inflammatory-physiological clusters would exhibit differing symptom and exacerbation burdens, additional pulmonary function characteristics, and structural lung abnormalities. The identified clusters were subsequently characterized using clinical variables and quantitative CT measurements. This approach was intended to provide further insight into COPD heterogeneity from the complementary perspectives of systemic inflammatory activity and airflow limitation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

Patients with stable COPD were consecutively recruited from the respiratory outpatient clinic of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Zhejiang University from Jan 2025 to Jan 2026. COPD was diagnosed according to the Global Initiative of Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD).19 Stable COPD was defined as the absence of acute worsening of respiratory symptoms and no requirement for additional systemic treatment beyond routine maintenance therapy. Briefly, the inclusion criteria for stable COPD patients were as follows: 1) age≥40 years, 2) the ratio of post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) to forced vital capacity (FVC) <0.70; 3) no acute worsening of respiratory symptoms and no requirement for antibiotics or systemic corticosteroids for an exacerbation within the preceding 4 weeks. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) confirmed or suspected respiratory tract infection; 2) pulmonary malignancy, asthma or pulmonary diseases other than COPD, including bronchiectasis, interstitial lung disease, sarcoidosis, silicosis, pulmonary embolism, and cystic fibrosis; 3) use of antibiotics or systemic corticosteroids within 4 weeks before entering the study; 4) major diseases of non-respiratory system, such as diabetes mellitus, severe cardiovascular disease, neurological disease, or hepatic and renal dysfunction. 5) any prior or current treatment with biologic targeted therapy.

At enrollment, baseline characteristics including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, and number of exacerbations in previous year were collected. Exacerbations were identified according to previous studies.20 Moderate exacerbations were defined as those requiring treatment with antibiotics and/or oral corticosteroids, whereas severe exacerbations were defined as those requiring admission to hospital.

Pulmonary function test (PFT) was performed using a spirometer (Quark PFT, COSMED, Rome, Italy) under standard conditions according to ATS/ERS technical standards.21 Each participant performed at least three acceptable and repeatable spirometric maneuvers. The largest FEV1 and FVC values obtained from technically acceptable maneuvers were retained for analysis. Complete blood count was also measured. All participants completed COPD assessment test (CAT) questionnaire and Modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale. The total CAT score was calculated for each individual by summing the points for each CAT variable, ranging from 0 to 40. mMRC scale is a validated 5-point questionnaire ranging from 0 to 4, reflecting the severity of dyspnea.22

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IIT Ethics Approval No. 0171). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

The blood samples from patients were collected, centrifuged at 2000 rpm 20 min, and stored at −80°C after separation. The frozen serum was defrosted before analysis. The serum levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-27, IL-35 and IL-37) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, IL-17 and IL-33) were measured in duplicate using commercial ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance was measured using an iMark microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). The mean of the two replicate measurements was used for analysis.

CT Acquisition and Quantitative Analysis

CT scans were performed with a 64-slice multidetector CT scanner (Philips Brilliance 64 Slice, Philips Medical Systems) according to a standardized protocol. Quantitative airway and emphysema measurements were performed using SYNAPSE VINCENT software (FUJIFILM Medical, Tokyo, Japan).23 For airway analyses in the COPD patients, the segmental bronchus was defined as the 3rd generation airway. Following automatic luminal segmentation of the airway tree, all bronchi from the right apical and posterior basal segments were manually identified by tracing the airways from 3rd to 6th generation. Airway parameters, including wall thickness (WT), wall area (WA), and wall area percentage (WA%, calculated as wall area divided by total bronchial cross-sectional area × 100%), were averaged for the 4th to 6th generation bronchi in these segments. To evaluate the severity of emphysema, the lung fields were segmented, and the percentage ratio of voxels below −950 Hounsfield units (HU) to all the voxels in the lung was calculated as the low attenuation volume percent (LAA-950%).

Unsupervised Clustering via Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)

To identify distinct inflammatory-physiological phenotypes of COPD, we performed unsupervised clustering using a Gaussian mixture model (GMM). GMM is a probabilistic approach that assumes that the observed data arise from a finite mixture of Gaussian distributions with unknown parameters. Unlike K-means, which assigns each participant to the nearest centroid and generally favors approximately spherical clusters with similar within-cluster variance, GMM allows clusters to differ in their covariance structures and estimates the posterior probability of membership in each cluster. Because inflammatory activity and airflow limitation may form overlapping and variably dispersed patterns in heterogeneous COPD populations, this probabilistic framework was considered more appropriate than a purely distance-based approach.

Before clustering, the seven circulating cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, IL-17, IL-27, IL-33, IL-35, and IL-37) were individually standardized using z-score transformation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed on the standardized cytokine matrix. The first principal component (PC1), which explained 72.27% of the total cytokine variance, was retained as the composite cytokine activation score and oriented so that higher values represented greater overall cytokine activity. Consistent with the primary inflammatory focus of this exploratory study, the original PC1 score was multiplied by 1.5 and combined with standardized FEV1% predicted in the GMM. This multiplier represented an exploratory modeling choice rather than a uniquely optimal weight. The PC1 loading coefficients are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

GMMs with full covariance matrices were fitted using two to seven mixture components. Model parameters were estimated using the expectation maximization algorithm. The full covariance structure was selected to allow flexible cluster geometry and potential correlations between the inflammatory and lung-function dimensions. Each model was fitted using 20 random initializations, a maximum of 100 iterations, a covariance regularization value of 0.01, and a fixed random seed of 42. Patients were assigned to the component with the highest posterior membership probability.

The number of mixture components was determined by jointly considering information criteria, posterior classification probabilities, model stability, and clinical interpretability. Models with two components mainly separated patients according to inflammatory status and did not adequately capture heterogeneity in COPD. Although the Akaike Information Criterion continued to decrease as the number of components increased, the improvement beyond four components was limited and was accompanied by progressive subdivision of clinically similar groups. In contrast, the Bayesian Information Criterion increased with additional components, indicating that more complex models were not favored after penalization for model complexity. Therefore, the four-component solution was retained as an exploratory and clinically interpretable representation because it provided additional phenotypic granularity while maintaining an acceptable minimum cluster size and avoiding excessive fragmentation. The four-cluster solution was visualized in the two-dimensional GMM input space defined by the weighted composite cytokine PC1 score and standardized FEV1% predicted.

Internal Cluster Validation and Phenotype-Level Robustness

Internal validation included overall and cluster-specific silhouette coefficients, repeated model fitting using 100 different random seeds, and cluster stability analysis using 200 bootstrap resamples. For each bootstrap sample, data preprocessing and GMM fitting were repeated, and the refitted clusters were matched to the reference solution. Overall agreement was quantified using the adjusted Rand index (ARI), and cluster-specific stability was assessed using Jaccard similarity. Because Cluster 4 contained only 17 patients, additional targeted analyses were performed to determine whether this subgroup was driven by a single patient or extreme cytokine measurements. Each Cluster 4 patient was sequentially excluded, followed by repetition of the complete clustering procedure. Cytokine values were also winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles before model refitting. In addition, the participant with the maximum observed value of each cytokine was sequentially excluded, and the complete clustering procedure was repeated after each exclusion. Sensitivity analyses using alternative scaling specifications were performed to assess whether the principal clustering structure depended on the exact relative contribution of the cytokine dimension.

Phenotype-level robustness was further evaluated by determining whether the key clinical and imaging contrasts observed in the original four-cluster solution were directionally preserved after each deterministic sensitivity analysis and across 200 bootstrap refits. For each contrast, robustness was summarized as the number and proportion of refitted solutions in which the original direction was retained after optimal cluster-label matching. The directional robustness of the key cytokine ratio patterns was assessed similarly by determining the proportion of 200 bootstrap refits in which the cluster-specific median ratio contrasts observed in the original solution were retained.

Assessment of Potential Confounding

Age, sex, BMI, and smoking pack-years were evaluated as potential confounders. Their distributions were compared across the original clusters using the chi-square test for sex and one-way ANOVA for age and BMI, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for smoking pack-years. As a sensitivity analysis, each cytokine was residualized for these four covariates before PCA and fixed four-component GMM refitting using both raw and log1p-transformed cytokine values. Agreement with the reference solution was evaluated using the ARI and cluster-specific Jaccard similarity. Multivariable logistic regression was additionally used to examine whether Cluster 3 membership was directly associated with these covariates. Models compared Cluster 3 with all other clusters and separately with Cluster 2. Individual P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and the joint contribution of the four covariates was evaluated using a likelihood-ratio test.

Post-Clustering Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python software, version 3.14. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-cluster comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Mann–Whitney U-test were used for post hoc pairwise comparisons and p values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cluster 1: Severe-Low Inflammation Phenotype

Patients in this cluster exhibited the highest mMRC and CAT scores, indicating the most severe symptoms. They also had the highest frequencies of overall exacerbations and moderate-to-severe exacerbations in the previous year. In terms of lung function, this group showed the worst ventilatory function, including the lowest percent predicted FEV1, FVC and maximal expiratory flow (MEF) 50, as well as the poorest diffusion capacity (DLco%), while the percent predicted residual volume (RV%) was lower than that in cluster 2. Quantitative CT measurements revealed the highest percentage of LAA-950%, indicating the most extensive emphysema. However, airway wall area and thickness were not significantly increased (Figure 3). Thus, the imaging profile of this cluster was characterized predominantly by extensive emphysema rather than increased airway wall measurements. Notably, despite their severe symptoms, physiological impairment, and extensive emphysema, these patients had relatively low circulating levels of the measured pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Cluster 2: Mild-Low Inflammation Phenotype

This cluster displayed the lowest mMRC and CAT scores, as well as the fewest overall and moderate-to-severe exacerbations in previous year, reflecting the mildest symptom burden among the four clusters. Lung function was the best overall, with the highest percent predicted FEV1 and FVC. Small airway parameters (MEF50 and MEF25), as well as diffusion capacity were also relatively preserved. CT imaging showed the least extensive emphysema and generally limited structural abnormalities (Figure 3). Levels of anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory cytokines were similarly low in this group. Thus, these patients presented with mild symptoms, minimal impairment in lung function and structure, and a low circulating level of inflammatory cytokines.

Cluster 3: Moderate-Elevated Inflammation Phenotype

Patients in this cluster had intermediate mMRC and CAT scores, with the frequencies of both total and moderate-to-severe exacerbations in the previous year falling between those of Clusters 1 and 2. Ventilatory parameters (FEV1%, FVC%, MEF50% and MEF25%) and diffusion capacity (DLco%) were also intermediate between Clusters 1 and 2, while RV% was comparable across clusters. On pulmonary imaging, the extent of emphysema (LAA-950%) was intermediate between Clusters 1 and 2, and airway wall thickness did not differ significantly from the other two clusters (Figure 3). Levels of both anti- and pro-inflammatory factors were significantly higher than those in Clusters 1 and 2, indicating elevated circulating cytokine activity. Collectively, these data characterize Cluster 3 as a phenotype of intermediate disease severity and heightened systemic inflammation.

Cluster 4: Extremely High Inflammation Phenotype

This cluster demonstrated markedly elevated levels of both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory cytokines, with approximately 3- to 4-fold increases compared to Clusters 1 and 2. Clinically, mMRC and CAT scores, as well as the frequency of moderate-to-severe exacerbations, were significantly higher than those in Cluster 2 and comparable to those in Cluster 1. Regarding lung function, ventilatory parameters, including FEV1%, FVC%, and MEF50%, were relatively well preserved, with significantly better values than those in Cluster 1 and comparable to those in Cluster 2. Diffusion capacity (DLco%) showed a similar pattern. On pulmonary imaging, the extent of emphysema was between Clusters 1 and 2 and comparable to Cluster 3 (Figure 3). Airway wall thickness was numerically greatest in Cluster 4 and was significantly greater than that in Cluster 1, suggesting prominent small airway wall thickening in this cluster. Overall, Cluster 4 was characterized by substantially elevated systemic inflammatory marker levels, greater symptom burden and exacerbation frequency, increased airway wall thickness, and relatively preserved pulmonary function.

Internal Validation and Sensitivity Analyses

The four-cluster solution showed moderate overall separation, with an overall silhouette coefficient of 0.361. The solution was highly reproducible across 100 random-seed refits (mean ARI=0.997) and maintained substantial overall agreement with the reference clustering across 200 bootstrap resamples (mean ARI=0.774). Mean cluster-specific Jaccard similarities were 0.835, 0.850, 0.592, and 0.685 for Clusters 1–4, respectively. Targeted leave-one-out analysis of Cluster 4 yielded a mean Jaccard similarity of 0.979, with cluster sizes ranging from 15 to 17. Winsorization, exclusion of cytokine maxima, and alternative cytokine-axis scaling also produced broadly comparable solutions. In particular, an equal-variance specification showed high agreement with the reference solution (ARI=0.960), with Cluster 1 remaining highly consistent and the high-inflammatory Cluster 4 remaining identifiable. Together, these findings support the reproducibility of the overall four-cluster structure, while identifying lower membership stability in Cluster 3 and some variability in Cluster 4. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Phenotype-Level Robustness

The principal clinical and imaging contrasts of Clusters 1 and 4 were generally retained across sensitivity analyses. In bootstrap refits, Cluster 1 retained the lowest FEV1% predicted and highest LAA-950% in 93.5% and 90.0% of solutions, respectively. Cluster 4 retained higher FEV1% predicted and greater airway wall thickness than Cluster 1 in 95.0–100% of refits, and higher symptom scores and a greater previous-year moderate-to-severe exacerbation burden than Cluster 2 in 94.5%-100%. The key IL-6- and IL-17-related ratio directions were retained in 89.0–100% and 86.5–90.0% of refits, respectively. Thus, the principal clinical and structural contrasts remained consistently observable despite variation in exact cluster membership. Detailed directional robustness results are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Assessment of Potential Confounding

Age and BMI differed across the original clusters (P=0.036 and P=0.043), whereas sex and smoking pack-years did not. After covariate residualization, the raw- and log1p-scale solutions showed ARIs of 0.808 and 0.656, respectively. Cluster 1 remained comparatively stable, whereas Cluster 3 and the precise membership of Cluster 4 were more sensitive to adjustment. However, multivariable logistic regression found no significant association between the measured covariates and Cluster 3 membership, either jointly or individually (joint P=0.609), with similar findings when Cluster 3 was compared with Cluster 2 (joint P=0.147). Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

In this study, we used a GMM to cluster patients according to a composite score of circulating pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines and FEV1% predicted, and subsequently characterized the resulting clusters using symptom burden, exacerbation history, additional pulmonary function parameters, and quantitative CT measurements. The most informative contrast was between Cluster 1, characterized by severe airflow limitation, extensive emphysema, and low circulating cytokine levels, and Cluster 4, characterized by markedly elevated cytokine levels, relatively preserved lung function, and greater airway wall thickness. These findings suggest that circulating cytokine activity, physiological impairment, and structural lung abnormalities may represent related but distinct dimensions of COPD heterogeneity.

Cluster 1 was characterized by severe airflow limitation, extensive emphysema, substantial symptom burden, and relatively low circulating cytokine levels. Previous studies have suggested that physiological impairment in advanced emphysema primarily reflects accumulated parenchymal destruction and small-airway loss24 and may not parallel current systemic inflammatory activity.25,26 Booth et al also reported reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in terminal bronchioles in GOLD grade 4 COPD compared with earlier disease stages.27 Although these tissue-level findings are distinct from circulating cytokine measurements, they provide a biological context for the combination of extensive emphysema and low circulating cytokine activity observed in Cluster 1. In our study, airway wall thickness in Cluster 1 was not significantly greater than that in Clusters 2 or 3. This may reflect relatively limited airway wall remodeling; alternatively, airway distortion and reduced visibility in severely emphysematous, hyperinflated lungs may limit quantitative assessment and obscure between-cluster differences. Overall, Cluster 1 represents an exploratory emphysema-predominant phenotype with low circulating cytokine activity. This combination raises the possibility of limited benefit from certain anti-inflammatory interventions, a hypothesis requiring prospective evaluation.

In contrast, Cluster 2 combined low circulating cytokine activity with mild symptoms, preserved lung function, and limited structural abnormalities on quantitative CT, representing a clinically coherent mild phenotype. These findings underscore the importance of preventive and nonpharmacological management, including smoking cessation, regular physical activity, and pulmonary rehabilitation when clinically indicated.19 The low circulating cytokine activity also suggests that inflammatory profiles may be useful for stratification in future trials of anti-inflammatory therapies. However, this cross-sectional study cannot establish slower disease progression or reduced benefit from anti-inflammatory treatment.

In contrast to the low-cytokine profiles of Clusters 1 and 2, Clusters 3 and 4 showed higher circulating levels of both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, although their relative cytokine configurations differed. Cluster 3 occupied an intermediate position in the cross-sectional distributions of symptom burden, physiological impairment, and structural abnormalities, while showing a relatively IL-17-oriented pattern, reflected by its lower IL-27/IL-17 and IL-37/IL-17 ratios. This pattern may be biologically and clinically relevant because previous studies have linked IL-17-related responses to mucus hypersecretion, airway remodeling, and reduced corticosteroid responsiveness in COPD, whereas IL-27 and IL-37 have been reported to suppress Th17-related and broader inflammatory responses.15,18,28–32 Thus, Cluster 3 may not simply represent an intermediate-severity phenotype but may also exhibit a distinct relative inflammatory configuration more permissive to IL-17-related activity. These findings raise the hypothesis that patients within this cluster may differ in inflammatory mechanisms and corticosteroid responsiveness, warranting prospective mechanistic and therapeutic evaluation.

Cluster 4 combined markedly elevated circulating cytokine levels with relatively preserved lung function, greater airway wall thickness, and a greater previous year moderate-to-severe exacerbation burden than the mild Cluster 2. In contrast to the low-cytokine, emphysema-predominant Cluster 1, this subgroup highlights a potential dissociation between inflammatory activity and physiological impairment. The lower IL-27/IL-6 and IL-35/IL-6 ratios suggest a relative shift toward IL-6-associated inflammatory activity, a pattern of biological interest given previous associations between circulating IL-6, exacerbation burden, and adverse COPD outcomes.10,25,33 Although this IL-6-related pattern was also observed in Cluster 3, the additional IL-17-oriented pattern in Cluster 3 suggests that the two cytokine-high groups may exhibit different relative inflammatory configurations. Together, the cytokine profile and airway wall thickening make Cluster 4 a subgroup of particular interest for future trials evaluating inflammatory phenotype-guided treatment strategies, although treatment responsiveness cannot be inferred from the present findings.34

Internal validation supported the overall reproducibility of the four-cluster solution, with moderate separation and differing levels of cluster-specific stability. The partial overlap is compatible with the continuous nature of cytokine activity and airflow limitation, while the clusters capture clinically interpretable patterns within this heterogeneity. Cluster 1 was comparatively robust, and the high-inflammatory pattern of Cluster 4 persisted across the principal sensitivity analyses, although its precise membership remained partly sample-dependent. Cluster 3 showed greater sensitivity to resampling and covariate adjustment, which may reflect less sharply defined boundaries within the continuous inflammatory-physiological distribution. Nevertheless, its principal IL-17-related ratio directions were retained in most bootstrap refits, supporting the descriptive value of this exploratory inflammatory pattern. In the assessment of potential confounding, multivariable logistic regression did not detect significant associations between original Cluster 3 membership and age, sex, BMI, or smoking pack-years, although modest associations cannot be excluded. These findings support the validity of the identified clusters as exploratory COPD phenotypes. Although the relative weighting of the cytokine dimension was an exploratory modeling choice, the principal Cluster 1 and Cluster 4 phenotypes were retained under alternative scaling specifications. This suggests that the major biological and clinical contrasts were not solely created by a single precise weighting value, although the membership of some patients remained scale-dependent.

Previous studies have highlighted the partly independent dimensions of COPD heterogeneity. Agustí et al described variability in systemic inflammation among patients with comparable pulmonary abnormalities,25 while Vanfleteren et al identified distinct biomarker-based clusters despite broadly similar airflow limitation.26 Zou et al demonstrated heterogeneous CT patterns of emphysema, airway wall thickening, and small-airway disease.35 In contrast, our study jointly considered selected pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine activity and airflow limitation, using quantitative CT for subsequent structural characterization rather than cluster definition. This approach linked inflammatory-physiological profiles to structural differences, particularly the contrast between Clusters 1 and 4.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, this cross-sectional study measured cytokines at a single time point, precluding assessment of within-patient biological variability, temporal cluster stability, and prognostic significance. Second, circulating cytokine levels may not fully reflect compartment-specific inflammation within the airway wall or distal lung parenchyma. Third, the two-dimensional clustering framework was designed to examine the focused relationship between circulating cytokine activity and airflow limitation but may not capture the full multidimensional heterogeneity of COPD. Fourth, although age, sex, BMI, and smoking pack-years were evaluated and patients with overt acute respiratory infection or exacerbation were excluded, residual confounding from subclinical infection, medication exposure, treatment adherence, comorbidities, and other unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Fifth, Cluster 3 and, to a lesser extent, the precise membership of Cluster 4 were sensitive to resampling and covariate adjustment. Moreover, the cytokine ratio patterns were derived from the reference four-cluster solution and should be regarded as exploratory, although their principal directions were frequently retained in the bootstrap analyses. Accordingly, the identified phenotypes should be considered hypothesis-generating, and their reproducibility, temporal stability, clinical relevance, and incremental value relative to higher-dimensional approaches require confirmation in larger independent and longitudinal cohorts.

Conclusions

Our exploratory clustering analysis identified COPD phenotypes with differing combinations of circulating cytokine activity, airflow limitation, symptoms, and quantitative CT features. The contrast between the low-cytokine, emphysema-predominant Cluster 1 and the cytokine-high Cluster 4 with relatively preserved lung function and greater airway wall thickness suggests a potential dissociation between systemic cytokine activity and physiological or structural disease severity. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require independent external, longitudinal, and mechanistic validation.
