A rare case of prostatic malakoplakia with multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli: a case report
Authors: Qing Yang, Xiaokang Chen, Guoxian Kou, Xiaoxi Ji
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Prostatic malakoplakia is an uncommon chronic inflammatory disorder, tumor-like but non-cancerous, the diagnosis of which pivots crucially on the identification of characteristic Michaelis-Gutmann bodies within the pathological tissue. We hereby present an inaugural case report of prostatic malakoplakia concurrent with sepsis caused by multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli, verified through blood culture and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS). The pathogenesis might be associated with infections by Escherichia coli, immune system irregularities, or lysosomal dysfunction. Although the patient had no chronic underlying diseases, he presented early with sepsis and multi-organ dysfunction. This case emphasizes the imperative to further investigate the association between malakoplakia and Escherichia coli, the necessity for prompt diagnosis, and the supportive role of mNGS, and the treatment strategy focuses on rapid control of infection and systemic inflammatory response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10144-2.

Introduction

Prostatic malakoplakia is a rare chronic inflammatory disease, with fewer than 20 cases documented in conjunction with urinary tract infections (Escherichia coli) [1] and no reported cases of association with sepsis involving multidrug-resistant E. coli. It is currently believed that prostatic malakoplakia is associated with acquired impairments in the lysozyme functions of histiocytes and macrophages, which lead to functional deficits [1–3]. The accumulation of undigested bacteria and bacterial components can form characteristic basophilic lysosomal inclusion bodies, known as Michaelis-Gutmann bodies [4–6].

The specific pathogenetic mechanisms underlying prostatic malakoplakia is still unclear. Abundant research suggests that the genesis and evolution of malakoplakia might be associated with infections from Gram-negative rods bacteria, amongst which E. coli holds dominion as the most ubiquitous [3, 4, 6]. However, the nature of E. coli and its role in the onset and progression of diseases remain to be clarified. Current research suggests that it may be related to abnormalities in macrophage function caused by E. coli infections [7].

We are first to report the diagnosis, treatment, and outcome of a case of prostatic malakoplakia complicated by multiple drug-resistant E. coli infection. The patient was hospitalized with principal symptoms of dysuria and fever. Radiological examination indicated a nodule in the peripheral zone of the prostate, not excluding the possibility of prostate cancer. Finally, definitive diagnosis of prostatic malakoplakia concurrent with sepsis (multidrug-resistant E. coli) was achieved through blood cultures, prostatic biopsy histopathology, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS). The patient was treated with claforan and meropenem for the infection, ultimately improving and being discharged from the hospital.

Case illustration

A 74-year-old male was admitted to the hospital with complaints of urinary retention for three days and fever for over a day, with no prior history of malignancy, diabetes, insulin resistance, or infectious diseases. Digital rectal examination (DRE): The prostate volume is enlarged, and a hard nodule measuring approximately 1.5 cm in size can be palpated on the right lobe, with tenderness and shallow central sulcus. Urine analysis showed leukocytes at 11,827.7 cells/µL and bacterial count at 9,657.7 cells/µL; procalcitonin (PCT) was 55.86 µg/L, total PSA (TPSA) was 50.96 ng/mL, and free PSA (FPSA) was 11.59 ng/ml; blood urea 9.93 mmol/L, serum creatinine 153 μmol/L, and eGFR 39.6 ml/min/1.73m2. Imaging with chest and abdominal CT revealed slight exudation around both kidneys and nodules on the periphery of the prostate. Prostate MRI with enhancement indicated prostatic hyperplasia accompanied by inflammation, with peripheral nodules, measuring approximately 1.7 cm × 1.5 cm. ADC showed obvious focal low signal intensity, while high-b-value DWI demonstrated high signal intensity. The maximum diameter was greater than 1.5 cm, and the lesion exhibited uneven and significant enhancement on contrast-enhanced scans. Peripheral nodules not excluding the possibility of prostate cancer (Fig. 1). After 4.43 days of hospitalization, blood cultures indicated multi-drug resistant E. coli. Prostate tissue mNGS revealed (Table 1): E. coli (sequence number 127,455). Prostate needle biopsy indicated (Fig. 2): chronic inflammation in the cord-like prostate tissue, gland destruction, with a notable aggregation of histiocytes in the stroma. Histiocytes contained PAS-positive granules and round basophilic cytoplasmic inclusions (Michaelis-Gutmann bodies) [1], with focal suppurative inflammation, leading to the diagnosis of prostatic malakoplakia. The patient was treated with ceftriaxone and meropenem, the patient's symptoms improved, follow-up blood cultures turned negative, PCT dropped to 0.31µg/L, TPSA to 4.578 ng/ml, FPSA to 0.731 ng/ml, and serum creatinine to 64.5μmol/L. The patient was discharged in a better condition and continued oral cefuroxime treatment for six days post-discharge. Discussion

Prostate malakoplakia is an exceedingly rare chronic inflammatory condition, believed to result from bacterial engulfment and lysosomal dysfunction. Although benign, it exhibits tumor-like growth and often mimics prostate cancer in clinical assessments and multiparametric MRI, making differentiation challenging [8]. The patient exhibited a significant increase in PSA and prostate PI-RADS system score [8] was 5 points, which did not rule out the possibility of prostate cancer. At present, the diagnosis of this disease primarily hinges on pathological examination, particularly through the identification of Michaelis-Gutmann bodies in affected tissues [8–10]. The case we have chronicled revealed prominent Michaelis-Gutmann bodies via PAS staining of pathological tissue, corroborating the diagnosis. To date, there have been no documented cases of prostatic malakoplakia coexisting with sepsis (due to multidrug-resistant E. coli); our report addresses this gap.

The pathogenesis of malakoplakia remains incompletely elucidated, with several highly regarded potential mechanisms currently proposed: 1) Microorganisms may play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis, particularly E. coli, which has been identified in over two-thirds of cases [1, 11]; 2) Abnormal or altered immune responses, such as immune suppression or chronic conditions like diabetes mellitus [1]; 3) Macrophage dysfunction due to lysosomal defects [10]. Previous studies have indicated that lysosomal functional impairment in malakoplakia leads to compromised bactericidal activity in macrophages, resulting in persistent infections and an increased risk of bacteremia and sepsis [10].

The blood culture results of the patient under consideration indicated a multi-drug resistant E. coli infection, which was also detected in prostate pathological tissues through mNGS (sequence count of 127,455), demonstrating homology with the E. coli strain cultivated from the blood. This correlation indirectly demonstrates that the pathogenesis of malakoplakia in this case may be associated with an E. coli infection. Consistent with previously reported studies, bacteremia (E. coli) plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of malakoplakia. Most patients with malakoplakia may also have comorbidities such as malignant tumors, diabetes and/or insulin resistance, or immunodeficiency diseases (Table.S1) [12]. However, this patient does not have a history of malignant tumors, immunodeficiency, or other similar conditions. At the onset of the illness, the patient exhibited symptoms of sepsis coupled with multiple organ dysfunction (thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, and cardiac failure). This implies that, aside from the infection from the multi-drug resistant E. coli, a systemic inflammatory response triggered by sepsis (PCT 55.86 μg/L) may also mediate macrophage reactive changes and lysosomal dysregulation, consequently leading to malakoplakia.

Based on the probable pathogenesis of malakoplakia, the treatment strategy for the patient centers on swiftly controlling the infection and the systemic inflammatory response. The patient, afflicted with urinary difficulties and fever upon hospitalization, is highly suspected of having a urinary tract infection. Typically dominated by Gram-negative bacteria, such infections are initially addressed with empirical antibiotic therapy using claforan in the emergency setting. Post-admission, the patient scored a 3 on the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), indicative of sepsis [13], prompting an upgrade to Meropenem for antimicrobial treatment. Blood cultures revealed the presence of E. coli, whereas urine cultures returned negative. To ascertain the source of infection more precisely, mNGS was performed on prostate pathological tissues without contraindications [14]. Compared to conventional microbiological tests, mNGS boasts a higher positivity rate, providing faster and more accurate diagnostic information. In cases where a highly suspected infection locus exists yet conventional pathogenic tests return negative, mNGS serves as an advantageous alternative examination [15–17].

The results of the mNGS suggest high sequence numbers for E. coli and Nocardia farcinica. E. coli, commonly seen in intra-abdominal exterior infections and urinary tract infections [18], lines up with the blood culture outcomes, thus it is highly plausible that it serves as the pathogen. Conversely, Nocardia farcinica is an opportunistic pathogen found in the natural environment, typically associated with lung, skin, and subcutaneous tissue infections; given the lack of congruity with the clinical manifestations of this patient, its pathological role is less likely [19]. Additionally, its prevalence in former paraffin section samples suggests that it might be associated with the introduction of contaminants from the embedding materials utilized in the paraffin section preparation process. The pathogenic possibilities for Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Human papillomavirus type 16 are minimal, given the relatively low sequence count detected for these entities.

Following hospital admission, the patient underwent an initial urine culture. Unfortunately, the patient did not strictly follow the urine sample collection guidelines during the sampling process, resulting in the growth of contaminated bacteria in the final urine culture results. However, the patient's blood culture revealed the presence of multidrug-resistant E. coli at 4.43 days. Antibiotic susceptibility testing indicated negativity for ESBL detection, with susceptibility to carbapenems, third-generation cephalosporins, and cefuroxime, while resistance was observed to fluoroquinolones and penicillin-based antibiotics, amongst others. Considering the antibiotic sensitivity tests, mNGS results, and amelioration of clinical symptoms, continued treatment with meropenem was administered, which significantly improved and stabilized the patient's condition. Post-discharge, the patient’s infection was managed with a step-down therapy of cefuroxime, corroborated by antimicrobial susceptibility and prostate tissue mNGS findings. In the follow-up year post-discharge, there was no recurrence. Therefore, swift pathological and microbiological diagnostics are imperative for establishing the most effective treatment strategy for prostatic abscesses, ensuring a good prognosis.

In summary, for individuals without a history of malignancies, diabetes/insulin resistance, or immunodeficiency, multidrug-resistant E. coli infection and a strong systemic inflammatory response might play a crucial role in the occurrence and development of prostate malakoplakia. However, this case report does not prove a causal relationship between multi-drug resistant E. coli infection, lysosomal function defects, and prostatic Michaelis-Gutmann bodies. The hypothesized mechanisms in this case should be interpreted with caution. Further research is required to elucidate the specific mechanisms linking multidrug-resistant E. coli to the formation of Michaelis-Gutmann bodies.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel report of a rare case of prostatic malakoplakia complicated with sepsis (multidrug-resistant E. coli), documenting both the diagnosis and treatment. We have utilized mNGS to indirectly substantiate the hypothesis that the pathogenesis of prostatic malakoplakia may be associated with an infection caused by E. coli. Concurrently, the mNGS results have provided significant evidence to aid in diagnostic confirmation and efficacy evaluation. Expedited etiological clarification through susceptibility testing, timely assessment of pathological tissues, and recognition of the characteristics of malakoplakia are imperative for selecting appropriate treatment strategies and achieving a favorable prognosis.
