Genetics-Informed Mapping Identifies a CRIM1-Associated Endocardial Inflammatory Remodeling State in Acute Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Ruyi Ruan, Xiaofeng Xu, Quan Liu, Fu Xiang, Lan Luo, Lijie Shi, Quankun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute myocardial infarction (AMI) reflects inherited susceptibility and inflammatory remodeling, but the cellular contexts linking genetic risk to disease remain unclear.Material/MethodsWe integrated a meta-transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) with a human cardiac single-nucleus RNA-sequencing atlas contained 11 individuals (5 AMI and 6 donor) to identify genetics-informed cellular programs. Composite program states were defined by global score quartiles. A fixed 5-gene panel was evaluated for nucleus-level endocardial low-transcriptional-state (Endo_LTS) vs endocardial high-transcriptional-state (Endo_HTS) discrimination within the AMI endocardium using 5-fold leave-1-patient-out cross-validation. Functional follow-up used CRIM1 silencing in hypoxia-treated human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived endocardial endothelial-like cells and complementary peripheral blood analyses.ResultsThe endocardium exhibited the most prominent infarction-associated increase in TWAS-anchored program activity, with expansion of program-high states and higher CytoTRACE scores. A consensus 5-gene panel (RPS8, PLEC, CFDP1, CRIM1, TNS2) was identified. Among 2163 AMI endocardial nuclei from 5 patients, the state classifier included 364 Endo_LTS and 751 Endo_HTS nuclei; 1048 Endo_MTS nuclei were excluded. Pooled out-of-fold ROC-AUCs ranged from 0.665 to 0.831. The panel also showed discriminatory value in an independent peripheral-blood AMI-vs-control cohort. CRIM1 was prioritized as a candidate linked to the remodeling program. CRIM1 silencing attenuated ACTA2/α-SMA, vimentin, LDHA, CCL2, and VEGFA and partially restored CD31, whereas TGF-β remained elevated.ConclusionsThese findings identify a genetics-informed endocardial inflammatory remodeling state in AMI and define a 5-gene surrogate of its activated state. CRIM1 is prioritized as a candidate linked to selected inflammatory, metabolic, and structural outputs. Persistent TGF-β elevation after CRIM1 silencing argues against a simple linear regulatory model and indicates that further mechanistic validation is required.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term heart failure worldwide [1,2]. Beyond acute ischemic injury, infarction initiates a complex cascade of inflammatory activation, vascular dysfunction, and tissue remodeling that shapes subsequent repair and clinical outcome [3]. Although many of these responses have been described at the tissue level, the specific cellular states that coordinate pathological remodeling after AMI are still incompletely understood. Recent single-cell and single-nucleus studies have revealed marked heterogeneity across cardiac lineages, suggesting that discrete stress-responsive programs may emerge in selected cell populations during infarction rather than uniformly across the myocardium [4–7].

In parallel, human genetic studies have identified numerous loci associated with coronary artery disease and myocardial infarction risk [8], but the biological interpretation of these signals remains challenging. Transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) provide a useful framework for linking polygenic risk to gene regulation [9–11], yet TWAS signals alone do not specify the cellular contexts in which genetically anchored programs become active. Integrating TWAS with high-resolution cardiac cell atlases may therefore help bridge inherited susceptibility and pathological tissue responses by localizing disease-associated transcriptional programs to specific cell types and states.

Among cardiac lineages, endothelial and endocardial compartments are of particular interest because of their exposure to circulating cues and their roles in inflammatory recruitment, barrier function, and structural adaptation after injury [12,13]. These compartments may serve not only as passive interfaces, but also as active participants in post-infarction remodeling. However, whether inherited AMI risk converges preferentially on an endocardial state, and whether such a state is linked to inflammatory, metabolic, and mesenchymal-like remodeling features, remains unclear. A clearer definition of these programs may also provide more compact molecular surrogates for disease-associated cell states and help prioritize candidate regulators for downstream validation.

Here, we integrated meta-transcriptome-wide association analysis with a human cardiac single-nucleus RNA-sequencing atlas to identify genetics-informed transcriptional programs associated with acute myocardial infarction and to determine their cellular localization. Program activity was preferentially increased in the endocardium, where program-high states expanded and were accompanied by greater transcriptional plasticity. A multi-algorithm strategy yielded a 5-gene surrogate of this state, and CRIM1 was prioritized for functional follow-up. Analyses in hypoxia-treated human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived endocardial endothelial-like cells [14,15] were consistent with a contribution of CRIM1 to selected inflammatory, glycolytic, and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT)-like outputs, but do not establish CRIM1 as a master regulator or therapeutic target.

Material and Methods

Study Design

This study integrated population-level genetic association signals with single-nucleus transcriptomic profiling to identify cell-state–specific programs associated with AMI. The workflow consisted of a meta-TWAS, projection and composite scoring in a human cardiac single-nucleus dataset, multi-algorithm derivation of a 5-gene panel, patient-grouped evaluation of endocardial low-transcriptional-state (Endo_LTS) vs endocardial high-transcriptional-state (Endo_HTS) discrimination within the AMI endocardium, separate patient-level pseudobulk AMI-vs-donor differential-expression analysis, peripheral-blood assessment, and CRIM1 follow-up in an hiPSC-derived endocardial endothelial-like hypoxia model.

Meta-TWAS and Pathway Enrichment Analysis

A meta-TWAS was performed using the FUSION framework to prioritize genes whose genetically regulated expression was associated with AMI. Genome-wide association study summary statistics for AMI and precomputed cis-eQTL weights from cardiovascular-relevant tissues were used as input. FUSION was applied according to the standard pipeline to obtain gene-level association statistics, including Z scores and P values. To evaluate coordinated biological processes associated with the TWAS signal distribution, the complete ranked TWAS Z-score vector was subjected to gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) in R using the clusterProfiler package. Normalized enrichment scores were calculated, and pathways with adjusted P values < 0.05 were considered significant unless otherwise specified.

Single-Nucleus RNA-Sequencing Data Processing and Cell Annotation

Single-nucleus RNA-sequencing data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus under accession number GSE270788 [16]. The processed discovery object contained 11 individuals (5 AMI and 6 donor). Raw matrices were processed in Scanpy. Nuclei with fewer than 500 detected genes, genes detected in fewer than 10 nuclei, and nuclei with mitochondrial transcript proportions of 10% or less were excluded. Counts were normalized to 10 000 per nucleus and log-transformed, followed by highly variable gene selection, scaling, principal component analysis, neighborhood graph construction, UMAP visualization, and Leiden clustering. Clusters were annotated using canonical markers and the annotated object was transferred to R/Seurat for subsequent analyses. The retained endocardial compartment included 2532 nuclei from 10 individuals (5 AMI and 5 donor). One donor in the discovery object contributed no retained endocardial nuclei.

TWAS-Anchored Program Activity Scoring

To map TWAS-prioritized genes to single-nucleus transcriptional states, program activity was quantified using 5 complementary methods: AddModuleScore, UCell, Percent Expressed, Mean Expression, and JASMINE. Method-specific scores were z-transformed, min-max normalized within method to the interval [0,1], and summed to generate a composite program score for each nucleus. Quartile cut-points calculated across all 15 441 scored cardiac nuclei (Q1 = 1.4762; Q3 = 2.5358) defined a low-TWAS activity state (LTS; score ≤ Q1), medium-TWAS activity state (MTS; Q1 < score < Q3), and high-TWAS activity state (HTS; score ≥ Q3). The corresponding global counts were 3861 LTS, 7719 MTS, and 3861 HTS nuclei. These labels were incorporated into the metadata for state-distribution, trajectory, CytoTRACE, communication, pathway, metabolic, and classifier analyses.

Trajectory Inference and Cellular Plasticity Analysis

To investigate dynamic state transitions, pseudotime trajectories were inferred from reduced-dimensional embeddings using the Slingshot R package. Cellular plasticity was evaluated using CytoTRACE, which estimates relative differentiation status based on transcriptional diversity. In this study, CytoTRACE scores were interpreted conservatively as indicators of injury-associated transcriptional plasticity. Correlations between CytoTRACE and the composite program score were assessed in the endocardial compartment.

Multi-Algorithm Feature Selection

To identify a compact signature associated with the program-high endocardial state, normalized endocardial expression data were analyzed using 7 feature-selection algorithms: least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), adaptive best subset selection (ABESS), random forest, decision tree, Boruta, gradient boosting machine (GBM), and XGBoost. Candidate genes selected by each method were compared, and the intersection across all 7 methods defined the consensus 5-gene panel. This panel was then treated as a fixed predictor set in the leave-1-patient-out model-fitting loop.

Pseudobulk Differential Expression Analysis

To reduce pseudo-replication bias in disease-level differential expression, endocardial nuclei were aggregated by patient using AggregateExpression in Seurat. Endocardial pseudobulk profiles were available from 10 contributing individuals (5 AMI and 5 donor); 1 donor in the discovery object contributed no retained endocardial nuclei. The resulting count matrix was analyzed with DESeq2, using a negative binomial model. This pseudobulk AMI-vs-donor analysis was separate from the nucleus-level Endo_LTS-vs-Endo_HTS classifier.

Machine-Learning Modeling and Cross-Validation

The supervised program-state task was nucleus-level classification of Endo_LTS vs Endo_HTS within AMI endocardium; each nucleus was an observation, and patient identity was the cross-validation grouping variable. Of 2163 AMI endocardial nuclei from 5 patients, 1048 Endo_MTS nuclei were excluded, leaving 1115 classifier observations: 364 Endo_LTS (32.65%) and 751 Endo_HTS (67.35%). Endo_MTS nuclei were not merged with Endo_HTS. The same fixed 5 predictors (CRIM1, PLEC, TNS2, CFDP1, and RPS8) were entered in every fold.

Five leave-1-patient-out iterations were performed. In each iteration, all nuclei from 1 patient formed the test set, and all nuclei from the other 4 patients formed the training set, preventing nuclei from the same patient from appearing in both sets. All held-out patients contained both classes; training sets ranged from 561 to 1108 nuclei and test sets from 7 to 554 nuclei. Models included binomial logistic generalized linear model (GLM), radial-kernel support vector machine (SVM) with probability output, random forest with 200 trees, and XGBoost with fixed settings (100 rounds, maximum depth 3, learning rate 0.1, column subsampling 0.8, and row subsampling 0.8). Pooled out-of-fold predictions were used to calculate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall curves. Models refitted to all 1115 nuclei were used only for feature-importance summaries.

External Cohort Validation and Nomogram Construction

To assess blood-based translational relevance, the independent peripheral-blood dataset GSE48060, consisting of 31 patients with AMI and 21 controls (n = 52), was analyzed [17]. Expression profiles of the 5 genes were compared between AMI and control samples. A multivariable diagnostic model and nomogram were constructed using the rms package; calibration was evaluated by 1000 bootstrap repetitions, and clinical utility by decision-curve analysis. Because this blood analysis used AMI-vs-control status rather than endocardial Endo_LTS-vs-Endo_HTS labels, it was treated as a separate cross-tissue assessment and not as direct external validation of the program-state classifier.

Cell-Cell Communication Analysis

Cell-cell communication in the AMI data was analyzed using CellChat. The grouping variable retained Endo_LTS, Endo_MTS, and Endo_HTS as separate activity-defined endocardial labels together with the other annotated cardiac populations. Ligand-receptor interactions, signaling directionality, and network centrality were inferred using the built-in reference database, with representative incoming and outgoing interactions involving Endo_HTS summarized. These analyses were state-level inferences and did not compare CRIM1-positive with CRIM1-negative cells.

Network-Based Virtual Perturbation

To predict the regulatory effect of CRIM1 loss, scTenifoldKnk was applied to construct a gene regulatory network and perform in silico knockout analysis. The perturbed network was compared with the unperturbed network to identify downstream transcriptional changes associated with CRIM1 ablation. Differentially perturbed genes were subsequently subjected to functional enrichment analysis.

Clinical Sample Collection and Peripheral Blood Processing

Peripheral venous blood samples were collected from patients with AMI (n = 20) and non-AMI controls (n = 20) at Huaihua Central Hospital. Whole blood was collected into EDTA-treated tubes. Plasma was separated via centrifugation and stored at -80 °C. Total RNA was extracted from whole blood using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, RR037A). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed utilizing PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A25742). The expression levels of CRIM1, RPS8, PLEC, CFDP1, and TNS2 were quantified utilizing the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the reference gene GAPDH. Gene-specific primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST and synthesized by Tsingke.

Plasma concentrations of TGF-β1, VEGFA, and CCL2/MCP-1 were measured using Quantikine ELISA kits (R&D Systems; TGF-β1: DB100B, VEGFA: DVE00, CCL2: DCP00) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

hiPSC-Derived Endocardial Endothelial-Like Cell Culture, Hypoxia, and RNA Interference

The commercially available hiPSC line iPS (IMR90)-4 (WiCell) was maintained on Matrigel-coated plates in mTeSR Plus medium. At 80% to 90% confluence, directed endocardial differentiation was initiated in a monolayer. Briefly, mesoderm was induced with 8 μM CHIR-99021 for 48 hours, followed by endocardial specification using 10 ng/mL bone morphogenetic protein 10 (BMP10) and 50 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor until day 14. Successful differentiation was verified by the expression of canonical endothelial (CDH5, PECAM1, KDR) and endocardial-specific (NFATC1, NPR3, NRG1, MEIS2, TMEM100) markers. The resulting cells were subsequently maintained on fibronectin-coated plates (10 μg/mL) in EGM-2 medium (Lonza) at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

For hypoxia experiments, hiPSC-derived endocardial endothelial-like cells were incubated in a hypoxia chamber (1% O2) for 24 hours, with normoxic cells maintained in parallel as controls. Targeted CRIM1 knockdown was achieved via transfection with a CRIM1-targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA; Tsingke) or a non-targeting control siRNA utilizing Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, 13778075).

RT-qPCR and Immunoblotting

Total RNA from cultured hiPSC-derived endocardial endothelial-like cells was isolated using TRIzol Reagent. cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR were executed as described above. Gene-specific primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST and synthesized by Tsingke. Relative mRNA expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method against the endogenous control GAPDH. For protein expression analysis, cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were probed with primary antibodies against CRIM1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-73860) and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; Cell Signaling Technology, 19245). Immunoreactive bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence.

Quantification of Secreted Factors

Following designated treatments, hiPSC-derived endocardial endothelial-like cell culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged to remove cellular debris, and stored at −80 °C. Secreted concentrations of C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2)/monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), and transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) were quantified using respective human Quantikine enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (R&D Systems; DCP00, DVE00, and DB100B/DB100C) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Ethics Statement

This study involving human participants was approved by the Ethics Committee of Huaihua Central Hospital. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and local institutional guidelines.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were performed using the t test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by appropriate post hoc testing.

For transcriptomic analyses, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method where appropriate. Correlations were assessed using Pearson or Spearman methods depending on data distribution. All tests were 2-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Results

Meta-TWAS Identifies AMI-Associated Transcriptional Programs Linked to Inflammatory and Remodeling Pathways

To obtain a genetics-informed overview of AMI, we first performed a meta-TWAS and summarized gene-level association signals across the genome. The resulting Manhattan-like profile showed that AMI-associated signals were distributed across multiple loci rather than concentrated in a small number of regions, consistent with a polygenic transcriptional architecture (Figure 1A). Among the prioritized genes were MAP3K9, CRYZ, SNX19, NEAT1, and MARVELD1 (Figure 1B).

To determine which biological processes were represented by the ranked TWAS signal distribution, we next applied threshold-free GSEA to the full TWAS Z-score vector. This analysis identified enrichment of pathways related to cardiac stress adaptation and remodeling, together with multiple immune- and cytokine-associated programs (Figure 1C). Among the most prominent categories was positive regulation of cytokine production involved in immune response (Figure 1D). These findings suggest that the inherited transcriptional architecture of AMI converges not only on stress-remodeling pathways, but also on inflammatory programs that may shape post-infarction tissue responses.

TWAS-Anchored Program Activity Localizes to the Endocardium and Expands Into a Program-High State in AMI

To localize TWAS-prioritized signals across the infarcted heart, we analyzed a human cardiac single-nucleus RNA-sequencing atlas and identified 25 transcriptionally distinct clusters, which were further consolidated into 13 major cardiac lineages based on canonical marker genes (Figure 1E, 1G). Comparison of donor and AMI samples showed clear remodeling of the infarct microenvironment, including expansion of myeloid and fibroblast populations, thereby providing the cellular context for subsequent mapping of TWAS-anchored program activity.

We next quantified TWAS-anchored program activity at single-nucleus resolution using a 5-method consensus scoring framework. The strong concordance across scoring approaches supported the robustness of the composite program score (Figure 2A, 2B). Across major cardiac cell types, the endocardium showed the largest infarction-associated increase in composite score (Figure 2C). When baseline engagement and disease-associated score shift were jointly considered, the endocardium remained the most responsive compartment (Figure 2D). Consistently, atlas-level visualization showed a global upward shift in program activity in AMI relative to donor samples.

To further resolve heterogeneity within this compartment, endocardial nuclei were stratified using the global quartile rules defined above. Among 2532 retained endocardial nuclei, 510 were LTS, 1221 were MTS, and 801 were HTS. The AMI endocardium contained 364 LTS, 1048 MTS, and 751 HTS nuclei, whereas the donor endocardium contained 146 LTS, 173 MTS, and 50 HTS nuclei (Figure 2E). UMAP visualization indicated that these states formed a structured continuum rather than a random distribution (Figure 2F), and pseudotime analysis further supported an ordered state transition (Figure 2G). Thus, AMI was associated with redistribution toward higher program activity and expansion of the HTS endocardial state.

We next investigated whether this state transition was accompanied by altered transcriptional plasticity. CytoTRACE analysis showed progressively higher scores with increasing program activity (Figure 2H), and the composite program score was positively correlated with CytoTRACE (Figure 2I). Consistent with this, HTS nuclei exhibited the highest CytoTRACE scores among the 3 activity states. These results suggest that TWAS-anchored program activation in AMI is preferentially localized to the endocardium and is accompanied by expansion of a more transcriptionally plastic program-high state.

A Compact 5-Gene Surrogate Captures the Program-High Endocardial State and Prioritizes CRIM1

To derive a compact molecular surrogate of the program-high endocardial state, we applied 7 feature-selection strategies spanning penalized regression, tree-based learning, and boosting-based models to normalized endocardial expression data (Figure 3A). LASSO identified a stable penalty region by cross-validation, random forest showed convergence of out-of-bag error with increasing tree number, and Boruta, decision tree, ABESS, GBM, and XGBoost yielded partially overlapping but broadly consistent feature rankings. Across these independent methods, the intersected feature set converged on a consensus 5-gene panel consisting of RPS8, PLEC, CFDP1, CRIM1, and TNS2 (Figure 3B). This overlap-based strategy reduced reliance on any single selection framework and generated a compact surrogate of the activated endocardial state.

We next examined whether this 5-gene panel tracked the program-high endocardial state identified above. At single-nucleus resolution, donor-vs-AMI comparison showed infarction-associated shifts across the panel within the endocardium (Figure 3C). Differential expression analysis further identified CRIM1 and PLEC as the most strongly upregulated genes in the endocardial compartment, whereas CFDP1 and TNS2 also showed increased expression and RPS8 did not exhibit a significant increase in the single-cell comparison (Figure 3D). Across the full cardiac atlas, the 5 genes showed preferential enrichment in the endocardial compartment rather than uniform pan-cellular expression (Figure 3E). Consistent with this, atlas-level feature plots provided additional visualization of their global and condition-specific expression patterns in AMI and donor samples. Together, these results support the 5-gene panel as a localized surrogate of the program-high endocardial state rather than a nonspecific infarct signature.

To reduce single-nucleus pseudo-replication in disease-level differential expression, we first performed a separate patient-level pseudobulk analysis of endocardial nuclei from 10 contributing individuals (5 AMI and 5 donor). Principal component analysis, the pseudobulk heatmap, and the differential-expression volcano plot showed an AMI-associated transcriptomic shift; CRIM1 and PLEC remained the most robustly increased panel components. The program-state classifier was a distinct nucleus-level analysis restricted to the 5 patients with AMI. After excluding 1048 Endo_MTS nuclei, it compared 364 Endo_LTS with 751 Endo_HTS nuclei. Patient-grouped leave-1-patient-out cross-validation yielded pooled out-of-fold ROC-AUCs of 0.762 for GLM, 0.665 for SVM, 0.820 for random forest, and 0.831 for XGBoost (Figure 3F). Precision-recall curves were also examined because the state classes were imbalanced. These nucleus-weighted estimates are interpreted as exploratory internal evaluation of a fixed panel.

Finally, we assessed whether the endocardium-derived panel had a detectable correlate beyond cardiac tissue. Boxplots show the independent GSE48060 peripheral-blood cohort (31 AMI and 21 controls; n = 52) [17]. The combined 5-gene panel showed discriminatory value for blood-based AMI-vs-control status, while CRIM1 and PLEC showed the clearest individual signals (Figure 3G). Because tissue source and outcome differed from the Endo_LTS-vs-Endo_HTS task, this analysis is presented as a separate cross-tissue assessment rather than direct external validation of the endocardial state classifier.

CRIM1 Marks an Endocardial State Associated With Inflammatory, Metabolic, and Structural Remodeling

CRIM1 and PLEC were both retained as prominent components of the 5-gene panel. CRIM1 was selected for downstream interrogation because the pre-perturbation evidence additionally converged on preferential endocardial enrichment, increasing expression along the inferred activity trajectory (Figure 4A), correlation with the composite score (Figure 4B), alignment with the inflammatory, hypoxic, glycolytic, growth-factor, and cytoskeletal context of the HTS state, and prior vascular and BMP-related biology that provided an experimentally tractable hypothesis.. The subsequent circulating-mediator correlations, virtual perturbation, and siRNA findings provided post-selection support and were not treated as original selection criteria. PLEC remains a co-prioritized panel component that warrants dedicated mechanistic evaluation.

We then characterized the pathway context of the activity-defined AMI endocardial states. Gene set variation analysis comparing Endo_HTS with Endo_LTS showed enrichment of hypoxia, glycolysis, inflammatory-response, and TGF-β signaling signatures in Endo_HTS (Figure 4C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis highlighted IL-17, NF-κB, Toll-like receptor, tumor necrosis factor, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways (Figure 4D), while Gene Ontology analysis identified leukocyte migration, chemotaxis, tissue remodeling, and actin filament organization (Figure 4E). Because these comparisons were defined by the composite activity state rather than CRIM1 positivity alone, they establish the context in which CRIM1 increased but do not demonstrate CRIM1-specific pathway control.

CellChat analysis used activity-defined AMI endocardial labels (Endo_LTS, Endo_MTS, and Endo_HTS) together with the other annotated cardiac populations. Network centrality and representative incoming and outgoing interactions involving Endo_HTS were summarized (Figure 4F). These state-level inferences support altered communication by the program-high endocardial state but do not establish CRIM1-specific communication effects or causal directionality.

Network-based virtual perturbation was used as a hypothesis-generating analysis. Simulated CRIM1 loss predicted lower hypoxia, glycolysis, and inflammatory-response pathway activities, whereas predicted TGF-β signaling increased (Figure 4G). At the gene level, virtual CRIM1 ablation was associated with increased SERPINE1 and lower predicted CCL2, VEGFA, ACTA2, and TGFB1 expression (Figure 4H). These model-derived associations prioritize selected outputs for experimental testing but do not establish CRIM1 as a single upstream controller of the endocardial program.

Peripheral Blood Analysis Supports a Systemic Correlate of the 5-Gene Surrogate and CRIM1-Associated Inflammatory Signaling

We next investigated whether the endocardium-derived 5-gene surrogate had a detectable correlate in the circulation. In the external peripheral blood cohort, CRIM1, PLEC, CFDP1, and TNS2 were increased in AMI samples relative to controls, whereas RPS8 did not show a significant difference (Figure 5A). Consistent with this pattern, the combined 5-gene panel showed better discrimination than did representative single genes in the same cohort (Figure 5B), supporting its value as a compact systemic correlate of the endocardial program rather than a tissue-specific substitute.

To further examine the inflammatory context associated with CRIM1, we measured circulating mediators linked to remodeling and leukocyte recruitment. Plasma TGF-β, VEGFA, and CCL2 were all elevated in AMI compared with controls (Figure 5C). In parallel, peripheral blood CRIM1 expression was positively correlated with each of these mediators, with the strongest association observed for CCL2 (Figure 5D). These findings support a systemic inflammatory correlate of the CRIM1-associated program identified in the endocardium. Taken together, the peripheral blood data do not directly establish endocardial specificity, but they provide independent support that the 5-gene surrogate, and CRIM1 in particular, is linked to a broader AMI-associated inflammatory state detectable beyond cardiac tissue.

CRIM1 Silencing Attenuated Hypoxia-Induced EndMT-Like Remodeling, Glycolytic Activation, and Inflammatory Secretion in hiPSC-Derived Endocardial Endothelial-Like Cells Despite Persistent TGF-β Upregulation

To test the functional relevance of CRIM1 in a batch-validated endocardial-relevant hypoxic setting, we established an in vitro model using hiPSC-derived endocardial endothelial-like cells exposed to hypoxia with or without CRIM1 silencing (Figure 6A). Under hypoxic conditions, CRIM1 expression increased markedly, whereas transfection with small interfering RNA targeting CRIM1 (si-CRIM1) effectively reduced its expression (Figure 6B), confirming efficient knockdown in this experimental system.

Next, we examined markers related to endothelial remodeling. Hypoxia induced ACTA2 (α-SMA) expression, and this response was significantly attenuated after CRIM1 knockdown at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 6C, 6D). Consistent with this, hypoxia decreased the endothelial marker CD31 and increased the mesenchymal marker vimentin (VIM), whereas CRIM1 silencing partially restored CD31 and reduced VIM expression (Figure 6E). These findings support attenuation of a hypoxia-induced EndMT-like and cytoskeletal remodeling response after loss of CRIM1.

We then examined metabolic and secretory outputs. Hypoxia-induced lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) expression decreased after CRIM1 knockdown (Figure 6F), and hypoxia-induced CCL2 and VEGFA secretion was attenuated (Figure 6G, 6H). In contrast, TGF-β did not decrease; it remained elevated and increased further in the hypoxia + si-CRIM1 group (Figure 6I). Together with the ACTA2/α-SMA, VIM, and CD31 results, these findings are consistent with a contribution of CRIM1 to selected inflammatory, glycolytic, and structural outputs in this hypoxic in vitro model. The discordant TGF-β response argues against a simple linear CRIM1-to-TGF-β mechanism and is compatible with parallel, feedback, or compensatory regulation.

Discussion

A central challenge in AMI research is that disease-associated signals are often interpreted at the level of bulk tissue or individual genes, whereas the biologically more informative question is where and in which cellular states these programs are deployed after injury. In the present study, we integrated meta-TWA analysis with a human cardiac single-nucleus atlas and found that TWAS-anchored program activity localized preferentially to the endocardium, where it expanded into a program-high and more transcriptionally plastic state after infarction. This state-based interpretation is consistent with recent human and spatial multiomic studies showing that post-infarction remodeling is organized into distinct cellular neighborhoods and disease-associated cell states rather than uniform responses across the myocardium [18–20]. Framed in this way, our findings extend genetics-informed prioritization toward a cell-state-resolved model of AMI remodeling and nominate the endocardium as a privileged site where inherited risk-linked transcriptional programs may be translated into local inflammatory remodeling.

The endocardial emphasis of our data is biologically plausible. Although conventional models of AMI have focused primarily on cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and infiltrating leukocytes [3], emerging evidence suggests that the endocardium is not merely a passive anatomical lining but an active injury-responsive interface. In particular, recent spatial multiomic studies demonstrated that leukocytes can access the infarcted heart through the endocardium and identified local upregulation of von Willebrand factor as a mediator of early myeloid recruitment in the subendocardial infarct zone [12]. These observations are highly relevant to our results, because the endocardial program identified here was enriched for inflammatory pathways, leukocyte migration-related signatures, and stronger inferred cell-cell communication. Together, these findings support a broader view in which the endocardium functions as an immunologically active compartment that helps organize early inflammatory traffic and remodeling after infarction, rather than serving only as a structural boundary.

Our results also fit more closely with a stress-adapted, EndMT-like remodeling state than with a complete and stable mesenchymal conversion. In the infarct setting, endothelial cells are increasingly recognized as highly plastic and capable of transient phenotypic reprogramming. Single-cell studies by Tombor et al showed that endothelial cells after MI acquire a temporary mesenchymal-like state accompanied by inflammatory and metabolic adaptation, before returning toward baseline rather than committing to a permanent mesenchymal fate [13]. Likewise, transcriptomic profiling of cardiac endothelial cells across post-MI time points has shown an early response centered on metabolic change, pro-inflammatory activation, cell-cycle entry, and later acquisition of proangiogenic and mesenchymal features [21]. Our own findings are concordant with this framework: the program-high endocardial state combined hypoxia, glycolysis, inflammatory activation, cytoskeletal remodeling, and increased CytoTRACE scores, while CRIM1 silencing attenuated ACTA2/α-SMA, VIM, LDHA, CCL2, and VEGFA and partially restored CD31. Taken together, these data support the interpretation that the relevant biology is a plastic, injury-associated endothelial/endocardial remodeling mode rather than categorical proof of a full lineage switch.

Within this framework, CRIM1 was prioritized for functional follow-up, while PLEC remained a co-prioritized panel component. The choice was based on convergence across endocardial localization, trajectory and composite-score association, HTS pathway context, and prior vascular biology, rather than classifier importance alone. CRIM1 is upregulated during endothelial tube formation and is required for efficient capillary-like morphogenesis in vitro [22]; Crim1 also contributes to retinal vascular stability through endothelial VEGFA autocrine signaling [23] and to coronary vascular endothelial development and homeostasis [24]. At the molecular level, CRIM1 can bind BMP family ligands and modulate their processing and delivery to the cell surface [25]. Circulating correlations, virtual perturbation, and siRNA results subsequently strengthened the rationale for further study but did not form part of the initial candidate choice or establish therapeutic validity.

Taken together, the data favor a context-dependent remodeling model rather than a single-pathway mechanism. Association and trajectory analyses locate CRIM1 within an activity-defined state but do not establish directionality, and the CellChat results describe state-level rather than CRIM1-specific communication. Virtual CRIM1 loss generated predictions that were partly concordant with siRNA-mediated attenuation of ACTA2/α-SMA, VIM, LDHA, CCL2, and VEGFA and partial restoration of CD31. However, TGF-β remained elevated or increased further after silencing, indicating that these outputs can diverge and that parallel, feedback, or compensatory inputs are likely. Such context dependence is characteristic of TGF-β signaling [26]. CRIM1 is therefore best viewed as a prioritized candidate that may contribute to selected outputs within a plastic remodeling network, not as a master regulator of a unitary pathway.

The translational implications of this work should likewise be framed at the level of state biology rather than biomarker enthusiasm. The 5-gene panel defined here is best viewed as a compact surrogate of a tissue-localized endocardial inflammatory remodeling state, not as proof that circulating blood measurements directly recapitulate endocardial specificity. Still, the fact that the combined panel retained discriminatory value in peripheral blood suggests that a local cardiac remodeling program may leave a measurable systemic trace. This may be clinically relevant because inflammatory trajectories after AMI are heterogeneous, and blood-accessible surrogates of tissue-defined remodeling states may ultimately prove more informative than isolated markers or single-pathway readouts. In addition, our use of hiPSC-derived endocardial endothelial-like cells improves the anatomical relevance of the functional validation compared with conventional coronary endothelial models. This is supported by prior work showing that human pluripotent stem cells can be directed toward an endocardial endothelial population with characteristic endocardial features and the capacity to undergo endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition-like responses [14,15,27].

A broader clinical framework may also consider constitutional phenotypes that were not measured in this study. Central adiposity is associated with MI risk across populations [28], while pectus excavatum, as a representative thoracic morphological phenotype, has been associated with altered cardiac kinetics, biventricular function, and myocardial deformation indices [29]. Such features could plausibly modify hemodynamic loading or the systemic inflammatory-metabolic environment in which endocardial states emerge. However, the present study included no anthropometric, thoracic-imaging, or patient-specific hemodynamic measurements and therefore cannot test interactions between constitutional anatomy and the CRIM1-associated program. Future cohorts integrating standardized anthropometry, thoracic and cardiac imaging, hemodynamic phenotyping, genetics, and cell-resolved molecular profiling will be needed to evaluate this possibility.

Several limitations should be emphasized. First, although the program-state classifier contained 1115 nuclei, its independent biological units were only 5 patients with AMI, and nucleus contributions were highly unequal. Grouped leave-1-patient-out validation prevented nuclei from the held-out patient from entering training, but pooled nucleus-level predictions weight patients according to their retained nucleus counts. In addition, the 5-gene predictor set was fixed before the leave-1-patient-out model-fitting loop; thus, leave-1-patient-out evaluates patient-held-out fitting of this predefined panel but does not independently revalidate the upstream 7-algorithm selection workflow. The reported AUCs should therefore be considered exploratory, particularly because small-sample cross-validation can be unstable [30,31]. GSE48060 addresses a different tissue and AMI-vs-control endpoint and is not direct external validation of Endo_LTS-vs-Endo_HTS classification. Second, TWAS and the association, trajectory, communication, and virtual-perturbation analyses do not establish causal directionality. Third, the siRNA experiment supports selected outputs only in the tested hiPSC-derived hypoxic model; persistent TGF-β elevation and the absence of in vivo intervention preclude designation of CRIM1 as a validated therapeutic target. Finally, the spatial boundaries and temporal persistence of the HTS state remain unresolved. Larger patient-level cohorts, nested evaluation of the complete selection pipeline, spatial multiomics, lineage-aware perturbation, and mechanistically resolved in vivo studies are required.

Conclusions

In this study, meta-transcriptome-wide association analysis and human cardiac single-nucleus RNA-sequencing identified a genetics-informed endocardial inflammatory remodeling state in AMI. TWAS-anchored program activity localized preferentially to the endocardium and expanded into a program-high, transcriptionally plastic state. A consensus 5-gene panel (RPS8, PLEC, CFDP1, CRIM1, and TNS2) provided a compact surrogate of this state and showed exploratory patient-grouped discrimination of Endo_HTS vs Endo_LTS nuclei, together with a separate blood-based AMI-vs-control signal. CRIM1 was prioritized as a candidate associated with selected inflammatory, glycolytic, and EndMT-like outputs. Silencing attenuated several hypoxia-induced outputs in vitro, but persistent TGF-β elevation indicates a nonlinear and incompletely resolved regulatory architecture. These findings support further mechanistic and preclinical study of CRIM1 rather than establishing it as a therapeutic target.
