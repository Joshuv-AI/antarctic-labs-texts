Tissue-specific isoform usage and gene expression revealed through RNA-seq and ATAC-seq in developing cattle
Authors: Sarem F. Khilji, Shangqian Xie, Gabrielle M. Becker, Katie A. Shira, Morgan R. Stegemiller, Julia L. Woods, Janet E. Williams, Lauren E. Christensen, Denise E. Konetchy, Darren E. Hagen,, Gordon K. Murdoch, Stephanie D. McKay, Brenda M. Murdoch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEstablishing a comprehensive characterization of the regulatory landscapes of cattle tissues facilitates a better understanding of the biological mechanisms responsible for condition-dependent phenotypes that drive tissue-specific gene regulation, developmental processes, and responses to environmental or physiological cues. This can be achieved through the characterization of gene expression, transcript usage and open chromatin accessibility. While much of this work has been done in human and biomedical model species, there is a lack of research in cattle that jointly characterizes chromatin accessibility and transcript usage alongside gene expression to define tissue-specific regulatory landscapes in cattle. Samples of prefrontal cortex (PFC), liver (L), and gracilis skeletal muscle (SM) were collected from four 3-month-old Angus steers and subjected to ATAC-seq and RNA-seq analyses. Differential gene expression (DGE) and transcript usage (DTU) were quantified across the tissues and evaluated in PFC-L, PFC-SM, and L-SM pairwise comparisons.ResultsIn total, 4,840, 4,151, and 3,663 genes were identified as differentially expressed, while 209, 236, and 154 genes exhibited differential transcript usage in PFC-L, PFC-SM, and L-SM comparisons, respectively. The genes CLTA, SLC25A3 and P2RX5 were highlighted for DTU between tissues, and further comparison of predicted protein sequences identified structural differences between CLTA and P2RX5 isoforms suggesting tissue-specific roles. Differential chromatin accessibility analysis revealed 2,576, 1,820, and 2,960 differential peaks in promoter regions across the same pairwise tissue comparisons. Functional enrichment revealed that PFC regulatory genes were associated with synaptic formation, myelination, and neurodevelopment, L genes with homeostatic regulation and lipid metabolism, and SM genes with muscle differentiation, myonuclear addition, and hypertrophy. These patterns aligned with ATF, HNF, and MEF2 motif enrichment in PFC, L, and SM promoter peaks, respectively. Furthermore, the integration of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq results revealed 62 genes in PFC, 93 genes in L, and 59 genes in SM as potential tissue-specific regulatory genes.ConclusionsThese findings provide insight into the complex regulatory mechanisms governing biological processes in the PFC, L, and SM of young Angus steers. This work integrates chromatin accessibility, transcript usage, and gene expression across multiple physiologically important cattle tissues, providing a comprehensive view of tissue-specific regulatory landscapes and generating a resource to support future studies in cattle genomics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12252-y.

Introduction

Advances in genomic technologies, including sequencing and bioinformatic tools, alongside improvements in data management and breeding strategies have accelerated genetic improvement in cattle. Foundational efforts like the human Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project and gene atlas initiatives have pioneered data-rich approaches to investigate the genetic variation underlying complex biological functions [1, 2]. Large-scale gene expression atlases have been instrumental in characterizing gene expression across multiple tissues and providing foundational insights for the cattle genomics community [3–5]. These efforts have dramatically advanced our understanding of tissue-specific gene expression across breeds. However, despite their value, these broad surveys often only focus on gene-level expression and do not capture isoform-level variation, alternative splicing patterns, or the chromatin accessibility landscape.

Isoform level differences are known to affect the protein sequence and its downstream localization, function and interaction [6–8]. Recent studies have demonstrated the significance of differential transcript and isoform usage in various biological contexts. For instance, recent research revealed isoform switching in genes related to tumor suppression and lipid metabolism, alongside alterations in long non-coding RNA expression linked to aging and disease resistance in dwarf mice [9]. Investigations into Alzheimer's disease in humans identified isoform switches in crucial genes like amyloid-beta precursor protein in affected versus non-affected brain tissue [10]. Additionally, studies on Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis infection in Holstein cows uncovered significant differential alternative splicing events associated with immune response genes [11]. While these examples are associated with disease or pathologies, it should be emphasized that it is extremely likely that tissue-specific splice variant expression contributes to functional specialization of healthy tissues as well.

Chromatin accessibility also plays a central role in transcriptional regulation by influencing the ability of transcription factors and other regulatory proteins to bind DNA. Regions of open chromatin often correspond to active promoters, enhancers, and other regulatory elements, whereas closed chromatin can restrict access and suppress transcriptional activity [12]. Integrating chromatin accessibility profiles with RNA-seq–based gene expression data provides a more complete view of the regulatory landscape across tissues, linking structural features of the genome to transcriptional outcomes. Recent studies have demonstrated the value of this integrative approach, identifying genes and transcription factor binding sites associated with intramuscular fat deposition in pigs [13] and revealing accessibility patterns linked to liver-specific gene expression in human progenitor cells [14].

Despite these advances, chromatin accessibility profiling and isoform-level transcript, together in conjunction with gene expression analyses, remain relatively underrepresented in cattle genomics research. Existing studies have predominantly concentrated on skeletal muscle or have relied on secondary analyses of publicly available datasets, rather than on coordinated multi-tissue experimental designs [15, 16]. Moreover, approaches that primarily rely on RNA-seq data constrain our ability to directly link transcriptional output with the underlying chromatin architecture that regulates it. While all such studies are valuable, further investigating these regulatory patterns can help elucidate the biology supporting trait diversity seen within various livestock species [17–19].

To address these limitations, this study focuses on three physiologically important tissues: the prefrontal cortex (PFC), liver (L), and skeletal muscle (SM) in four 3-month-old Angus steers. This developmental stage was intentionally selected as it represents a key window of rapid postnatal growth, during which the tissues undergo extensive development. At this age, calves are still pre-weaning but beginning to transition metabolically and developmentally toward maturity, making this an ideal time point to capture dynamic, tissue-specific regulatory activity. The selected tissues were chosen for both their physiological importance and their representation of the major tissue types and developmental origins: skeletal muscle, derived from the mesoderm; prefrontal cortex, representing nervous tissue derived from the ectoderm; and liver, representing connective tissue derived from the endoderm.

Physiologically, the liver is a key organ in regulating nutrient metabolism, plasma protein synthesis and release, as well as bile acid production [20]. The liver receives first pass nutrients via the portal vein and is critical for post-prandial storage of lipids including volatile fatty acids, and liberation of these nutrients as needed between meals [21]. As such, liver maturation is a critical component and transitions away from prenatal maternal nutrients and metabolic regulation to independent internal control. In fact, hepatic dysfunction and disease such as fatty liver and liver abscesses are some of the most prevalent production issues in production livestock. The prefrontal cortex regulates dietary patterns of feed acquisition, ingestion, digestion and mediates learned behavioral responses to ambient conditions critical for livestock survival [22, 23]. In beef cattle, SM is arguably the most economically important tissue as it directly contributes to the production and yield of marketable meat. By its sheer mass (35–65%) of a production animal by weight, it contributes significantly to metabolism, nutrient requirements and as mentioned previously, is the main contributor to commercial beef products [24]. At three months of age these tissues are rapidly maturing, growing and optimizing their functionality towards independent nutrient management, utilization, health and functional organization.

By integrating gene expression profiling with chromatin accessibility mapping and applying isoform-level differential transcript usage in PFC, L, and SM at the pre-weaning (3-month) stage, this study provides a mechanistic view of early-life tissue specialization that has not been previously comprehensively characterized in cattle. The findings will contribute to a deeper understanding of the genetic regulation of important tissues and improving livestock genetics.

Materials and methods

Tissue samples collection

The use of young beef calves as the source of the experimental tissues was deliberate in that it is well accepted that during this early phase of life, the growth rate and tissue remodeling are both extensive and phenotypically distinct for tissues with unique functions. All experimental tissues were collected from Angus based calves purchased from the University of Idaho Nancy M Cummings Research Extension and Education Center. The calves were from the same pasture bred cow herd, therefore the calves are likely related, however parentage was not used to determine these potential relationships. Three-month-old Angus steers (n = 4) were humanely harvested under the pre-approved Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-2020–61) guidelines at the University of Idaho. The calves used in this study were pre-weaned calves with the potential of consuming some feedstuffs while at the cows side. The calves were separated from their dams the evening prior to slaughter the following morning at 8:00 am. All calves were housed at the University of Idaho campus under the same environmental conditions and cow feeding regiment. On the day of harvest, the steers were transported less than a mile to the University of Idaho meat harvesting facility, where they were harvested immediately upon arrival by highly trained personnel. A licensed veterinarian was present to ensure all calves were healthy. The steers were harvested as per IACUC and USDA standard cattle euthanasia protocol. Briefly, captive bolt was used to render the steers unconscious and euthanized via exsanguination. Samples of the PFC, L and SM tissues were collected and flash frozen within 30 min postmortem. Each tissue was dissected by qualified animal scientists and rinsed with 1 × Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) as needed, then aliquoted into 1.5-mL cryovials, and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Tissue samples were stored at −80 °C until processing.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from frozen PFC, L and SM tissues using the Quick-RNA Miniprep Plus kit (cat # R1057, Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to manufacturer's instructions. The RNA samples were provided to LC Sciences (Houston, TX, USA) for mRNA library preparation (poly A enrichment) and 150-bp paired-end (PE) sequencing. The RNA integrity was assessed by Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, CA, USA), with RIN numbers > 7.40. The libraries were constructed using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 sequencing platform (2 × 150 bp).

RNA sequence quality control, mapping and quantification

Quality assessment of the raw reads from RNA-seq was performed using FASTQC (v0.11) [25]. Following quality control, reads were aligned to the Bos taurus genome assembly ARS-UCD1.3 [26] using TopHat2 (v2.1.1) [27]. The parameters used for the alignment included allowing up to 20 multiple alignments per read (-g 20) and permitting a maximum of two mismatches in the segment mapping (-N 2). Sequence reads with unique mapping alignments were carried forward and filtered (-q 50) for low quality reads using samtools. The average Phred score adopted for our samples after this was 36.1. Sequencing reads were assigned to genomic features using annotation features present in the ARS-UCD1.3 genome assembly. The number of reads mapped to each gene was quantified using the read summarization program featureCounts [28]. Normalized read counts for differential expression were obtained using the median of ratios method from DESeq2 [29]. Variance-stabilized counts obtained using the VST (variance stabilizing transformation) function in DESeq2 were used to construct a principal component analysis (PCA) to visualize sample clustering.

ATAC-seq sample processing and sequencing

Frozen tissue samples were submitted to the High-Throughput Epigenomics Core at the University of California, San Diego, for ATAC-seq library preparation and sequencing. Tissue homogenization was performed by freezing the samples in liquid nitrogen and grinding them into a fine powder using a pre-chilled mortar and pestle. Nuclei were then prepared by resuspending the pulverized tissue in nuclear permeabilization buffer, followed by incubation at 4 °C to permit optimal permeabilization. The nuclei were subsequently pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in Tagmentation Buffer. Nuclei counts were performed to ensure consistency, targeting approximately 50,000 nuclei per reaction. These nuclei were then exposed to the Tagmentation enzyme at 37 °C to fragment the DNA. After tagmentation, the fragmented DNA was purified and subjected to PCR amplification. To prepare the DNA libraries for sequencing, size selection to enrich fragments of approximately 200–1,000 bp was conducted using SPRIselect beads. Quantification and quality assessment of the libraries were executed using the Qubit fluorometer and the TapeStation system, respectively. Prepared libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 system (2 × 50 bp) for data generation [30].

ATAC sequence quality control, mapping and quantification

Data processing for ATAC-seq began with an initial quality assessment of raw reads using FASTQC (v0.11) [25]. Adapter sequences were trimmed from the raw reads using the fastp option with default parameters [31]. The trimmed reads were then aligned to the Bos taurus genome assembly ARS-UCD1.3 [26] using Bowtie2 [32] with default parameters. Following alignment, mitochondrial DNA reads were removed using samtools [33]. To eliminate PCR duplicates, deduplication was performed with Picard [34] with default parameters. Further, non-uniquely mapping reads were filtered out using samtools [33], retaining reads with a mapping quality score of 10 or higher. Peaks representing regions of accessible chromatin were identified using MACS2 [35] with a p-value cutoff of 0.01, a read shift value of 75, and an extension size set to 150. Normalized read counts from Diffbind (v3.10.1) [36] were also used to construct a PCA plot to visualize sample clustering. Fragment size distribution and analysis was examined using deepTools v3.5.4 [37].

Differential gene expression

Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis between the PFC, L, and SM tissue samples was performed using the DESeq2 (v1.40.2) [29] package in R (v4.3.1) [38]. A pre-filtering step excluded genes with a combined non-normalized read count greater than 50 across all 12 samples to reduce noise and improve statistical power. The median of ratios method in DESeq2 [39] was utilized to obtain normalized read counts for the remaining genes and then to perform pairwise expression comparisons between each of the three tissue types. All reported P-values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to correct for multiple testing. Genes with a padj < 0.05 and log2 fold change > 2 were reported as significantly differentially expressed. Volcano plots were constructed using the R package EnhancedVolcano (v1.18.0) [40]. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the PANTHER (v19.0) [41] (Protein analysis through Evolutionary Relationships) database classification system. The Bos taurus reference proteome used for the analysis was “UP000009136 9913 BOVIN Bos taurus,” as provided by the PANTHER database. Visualizations of GO terms were made with the R package ggplot2 (3.5.1) [42].

To obtain a broad overview of potential chromosomal-level patterns in gene expression, significantly differentially expressed genes from the RNA-seq analysis were assigned to their respective chromosomes for each tissue type. For each tissue, DEGs were identified from pairwise comparisons against each of the other two tissues for chromosome-level visualization. For each chromosome, the log₂ fold change values of these DEGs were plotted and used to calculate the interquartile range (IQR) and quartile values (Q1, Q3) to summarize expression variability and identify outliers. To further assess potential chromosomal-level patterns, Manhattan-style scatter plots were generated by plotting the log₂ fold change of each DEG against its corresponding chromosome number and position, for significantly upregulated and downregulated genes.

Differential transcript usage

To quantify transcript isoform abundance from RNA-seq reads, Salmon (v1.4.0) [43] was employed as a transcriptome-wide quantifier, aligning reads to the reference transcriptome index using a quasi-mapping method to estimate individual transcript (isoform) abundance in transcripts per million (TPM). Transcript isoform abundance were then analyzed with the R package IsoformSwitchAnalyzeR [44], which detects isoform switches using DEXSeq [45]. The data were filtered using stringent criteria, including a mean normalized gene expression cutoff of 100 (from DESeq2 normalization), an isoform expression cutoff of 5 TPM, a differential isoform fraction (dIF) cutoff of 0.2, and a q-value threshold of > 0.01. These thresholds were deliberately chosen to be stringent to reduce the potential of false positives. By requiring moderate gene expression, isoform abundance, and at least 20% difference in isoform redistribution, and statistical false discovery rate of < 0.01, we aimed to prioritize the isoform switches with the highest degree of confidence. Representative examples of detected differential transcript usage (DTU) genes were selected for visualization and verification using ggsashimi [46] to illustrate the patterns identified in the analysis through Sashimi plots. To further investigate the potential functional implications of identified isoform switches, representative isoforms for selected genes were analyzed at the protein level. Predicted protein structures were generated using AlphaFold2 (v1.5.5) [47], and structural differences between isoforms were assessed by aligning and superimposing models in ChimeraX (v1.9) [48]. Visual inspection focused on identifying conformational changes or domain rearrangements that may reflect isoform-specific functional variation. In parallel, isoform sequences were analyzed using InterProScan (5.73–104.0) [49] to compare protein family and domain annotations, providing additional insight into potential functional consequences associated with differential transcript usage.

Differential accessibility, peak annotation, visualization and motif analysis

Differentially accessible regions were identified using Diffbind [36] with default parameters, defining significance as log2 fold change > 2 and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value < 0.01. Bigwig signal tracks were generated with deepTools [37] to visualize the distribution of accessible chromatin regions across the genome. Motif enrichment analysis and peak annotation were conducted using Homer [50], which facilitated the identification of enriched motifs and functional elements within the peaks. ChIPseekr v1.36.0 was used to annotate the differential peaks using the ARS-UCD1.3 annotation file, categorizing genome-wide functional regions into promoter, exon, intron, downstream, intergenic, 5’ untranslated region (UTR), and 3’ UTR. Peaks were associated with genes according to their proximity to transcription start sites (TSS), with peaks located ± 3 kb from the TSS considered as potential gene promoters.

Integration of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data

Following the analysis of differential gene expression (RNA-seq) and chromatin accessibility analysis (ATAC-seq) for each pairwise tissue comparison, significantly upregulated genes from both datasets for each tissue type were compared to affirm that upregulated genes coincide with promoter accessibility. A Spearman correlation analysis was then performed in R using the log2 fold change values from the RNA-seq and ATAC-seq datasets. The correlation was calculated only for genes that were significantly upregulated in the RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analyses (log₂ fold changes ≥ 2, padj ≤ 0.01), rather than across the entire genome. For genes exhibiting a significant log2 fold change difference in the RNA-seq pairwise comparisons, the highest promoter peak log2 fold change from the corresponding ATAC-seq analysis was used to compare. To identify putative tissue-specific regulatory genes, the following criteria were applied: (1) The gene must be differentially expressed (RNA-seq) with a log2 fold change ≥ 2 and padj ≤ 0.01 compared to both other tissue types; (2) The gene's expression in the other two tissues must have a mean normalized count ≤ 5, based on DESeq2’s median of ratios method; and (3) The gene must exhibit an enriched promoter peak (log2 fold change ≥ 2 and padj ≤ 0.01) in ATAC-seq data relative to the other two tissue types.

Tissue-consistent genes were stringently defined as those with low variability in expression across all animals and tissue types [51]. To qualify as consistent, a gene needed to have a coefficient of variation (CV = [standard deviation/mean] < 2), a fold change (between the maximum and minimum expression value) < 2, and a mean expression level lower than the maximum expression minus two times the standard deviation [52].

RNA-Seq and ATAC-seq data statistics

Approximately 65 million paired-end reads per sample were generated from ATAC-seq (65,422,472 ± 685,115) (Table 1). Reads were aligned with an overall alignment of 98.95% ± 0.38%. PCA conducted on variance-stabilized read counts revealed clear tissue-specific accessibility profiles, with PC1 capturing 86% of the total variance and PC2 accounting for an additional 12% (Fig. 1C). To gain an overview of how the peaks were distributed across the genome, open chromatin peaks were assigned to previously described genome-wide functional regions. These regions included 5′ UTRs, intergenic regions, promoters, introns, exons, 3′ UTRs, and potential downstream elements. Most peaks (> 98%) were found in promoters, intergenic, exonic, and intronic regions (Fig. 1D). Fragment size analysis showed that the majority of fragments were < 250 bp in length (Supplemental Fig. 1) and were found within ± 3 kb of the TSS, suggesting that the accessible chromatin regions were connected with transcription (Fig. 1E and Supplemental Fig. 2).

Differential gene expression profiles and functional enrichment

A total of 4,840 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between PFC and L (PFC-L), 4,151 genes between PFC-SM, and 3,663 genes between L-SM were identified with padj < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|> 2 (Fig. 2A) (Supplemental File 1). The gene expression patterns of DEGs and their relationships across tissue types revealed some consistent gene expression between tissues (Fig. 2B). By examining genes that were common across these pairwise comparisons for each tissue type (e.g., genes upregulated in PFC as compared to both L and SM), we identified the significantly upregulated and downregulated genes common to each tissue (Fig. 2C). A total of 1,876 genes in the PFC, 1,468 genes in the L, and 924 genes in the SM were significantly upregulated. In contrast, 421 genes in the PFC, 462 genes in the L, and 380 genes in SM were significantly downregulated, with respect to each tissue. As such, upregulated genes in PFC were primarily associated with neuronal functions, including neurogenesis and trans-synaptic signaling, while downregulated genes were linked to metabolic pathways. Upregulated genes in L were enriched in metabolic and immune responses, with neuronal functions downregulated. Differential transcript usage

To further explore the potential difference of transcripts between tissue types, a differential transcript usage (DTU) analysis was performed. In total, 209, 236, and 154 DTU genes were identified in PFC-L, PFC-SM, and L-SM comparisons, respectively (Supplemental File 2). Of these, 54, 71, and 45 genes were identified as both DTU and DEG in PFC-L, PFC-SM, and L-SM, respectively (Fig. 3A), while 155, 165, and 109 genes were identified as DTU but not as DEG. Two genes (SLC25A3 and CLTA) were highlighted as representative for each category (Fig. 3B). For SLC25A3, SM exhibits a much higher overall gene expression compared to PFC and L (Fig. 3C). SM appears to predominantly utilize the NM_174657.2 isoform, which skips gene exon four. In contrast, PFC and L favor expression of the XM_005206513.3 isoform, which skips gene exon 3. This isoform-specific expression pattern is evident despite the differences in overall gene expression between the tissue types. Distinct exon usage patterns and isoform variants are also evident in CLTA, with different exon skipping or inclusion events observed in each tissue. Specifically, the isoform predominant in PFC (NM_174022.2) includes all seven gene exons, while isoform XM_005210062.2 in L skips gene exons five and six and isoform XM_005210060.2 in SM skips gene exon five (Fig. 3C). Within PFC, the NM_174022.2 isoform is prominently utilized as evidenced by the high expression of exons five and six in comparison to L and SM. Both L and SM were detected as primarily using the XM_005210062.2 isoform while L also has some signal for the XM_005210060.2 isoform. These findings highlight the importance of identifying DTU patterns among both differentially expressed and non-differentially expressed genes. Integrated analysis of ATAC-seq with RNA-seq

Tissue-specific regulated genes from transcription and chromatin accessibility

In addition to identifying genes with significant differential accessibility and/or expression, we integrated high-confidence ATAC-seq peaks with RNA-seq expression data to pinpoint putative tissue-specific regulated genes. Genes were selected if they showed robust differential expression (log₂ fold change ≥ 2, padj ≤ 0.01) and corresponding enrichment in promoter accessibility (log₂ fold change ≥ 2, padj ≤ 0.01) relative to both other tissue types. To ensure tissue specificity, we further filtered for low expression variability (CV < 2, fold change < 2) and minimal background expression (mean normalized count ≤ 5) in non-target tissues. Using these criteria, we identified 387, 177, and 103 tissue-specific genes in PFC, L, and SM, respectively. Among these, 62, 93, and 59 genes had at least one enhanced peak within a promoter region in the ATAC-seq data (Fig. 7A). GO analysis of those genes illustrated distinct biological processes associated with each tissue (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, transcription binding factor binding motifs were identified (Fig. 7C). Motifs of the PFC gene subset revealed significant enrichment of several regulatory elements. Notably, Nuclear Factor Y (NYF) and Engrailed Homeobox 1 (EN1) motifs were each present in 23.65% of target sequences, both of which have been previously associated as key regulators during brain morphogenesis [53, 54]. In L, the HNF family was highly enriched, with HNF4a found in 18.01% of target sequences and HNF1B and Hnf1 present at 14.94% and 13.41%, respectively. The CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein alpha (CEBP) motif, which is important in adipogenesis and metabolic processes [55], was enriched in 19.16% of target sequences. In the SM, muscle-related transcription factors were prominent, particularly the MEF2 family, with Mef2b showing the highest enrichment at 37.72%, followed by MEF2D (26.35%), MEF2C (22.14%), and MEF2A (14.47%). Discussion

Comprehensive understanding of different tissue types facilitates the elucidation of the biological functions and regulatory mechanisms that underpin gene expression related to complex traits [56]. Previous studies utilizing gene expression analyses have indicated that alterations in the transcriptome contribute to the phenotypic variation observed among diverse species [57]. Similarly, analyzing chromatin-wide patterns of accessibility can reveal which genomic regions are available to various cellular machinery, thereby playing a crucial role in expanding our understanding of the regulation of gene expression [58]. The three tissues examined in this study were expected to exhibit obvious variations in both their gene expression and chromatin accessibility profiles, as supported by a body of previous research [59, 60]. This study examined these differences in greater depth, providing a more detailed report of common and tissue-specific expression attributes.

Differential gene expression

Analysis of the most highly expressed genes in each tissue type revealed a striking pattern: the top 50 genes of liver and skeletal muscle accounted for a large amount of the total expression of those tissues (33% and 43%, respectively), while the top 50 genes of prefrontal cortex accounted for a much smaller percentage (13%). This finding aligns with existing knowledge of the higher complexity and heterogeneity of several brain tissues at the molecular level [61, 62]. In humans, skeletal muscle and liver express similar numbers of genes (11,276 and 11,392, respectively), while cerebellum has a considerably greater gene count (13,298) [63]. Of the tissues reported by Ramsköld and colleagues (2009), only breast, lymph node and testes expressed a greater number of genes than cerebellum. In contrast to the more specialized functions of skeletal muscle and liver, where a smaller set of genes tend to dominate the expression profile, the prefrontal cortex exhibits a more dispersed expression pattern. This diversity in gene expression is crucial for supporting the wide array of cellular functions and complex regulatory mechanisms required for neuronal activities and brain functions. Similar expression patterns have been observed in other mammals, including humans, where a significant portion of the genome is expressed in the brain to accommodate its complex structural and functional needs [61, 62]. The GO analysis of each of these top 50 gene subsets returned terms that are related to the expected specific physiological functions of each tissue system. Differential gene expression analysis further confirmed that the top 50 genes were consistently among the most upregulated genes in each tissue type.

Differential gene expression profiles were used to identify genes that were upregulated across each tissue type. Notable genes such as myelin basic protein (MBP) and proteolipid protein 1 (PLP1) stand out due to their high expression levels and known roles in coding for the primary constituents of the myelin sheath [64]. Additionally, the GPM6A and GPM6B paralogs were identified as are involved in neuron development and synaptic plasticity [65]. As expected for animals of this age, central nervous system maturation was occurring at the time of sampling as evidenced by high expression of DCAF8, a gene known to influence cellular proliferation and remodeling [66]. This was accompanied by high expression of QKI, a gene responsible for myelination [67], APP essential for axonogenesis and neurite growth [68] and ITGA7 that regulates cell matrix and blood–brain barrier integrity [69]. Other notable genes that were identified and reported to be associated with aggressive behavior, including SCD, PDLIM4, PAMR1, SLC24A2 and BRINP1 [22, 70].

Genes from the Serpin superfamily were prominent, with SERPINA1, SERPINA3, SERPINA3-1, SERPINC1, and SERPING1 ranked among the top 50 most highly expressed genes in the liver. Serpins inhibit serine proteases of the chymotrypsin family via a unique "suicide substrate" mechanism, playing a critical role in maintaining liver homeostasis [71]. Genes related to the fibrinogen family, including FBG, FGG, and FGA, playing critical roles in blood clotting and inflammation [72], were also among the top expressed genes in the liver. High C8G expression in liver was noted and is critical towards the establishment and retention of functional hepatic anatomical structure as supported by reduction in expression being associated with hepatic steatosis [73].

Genes, ATP2A1, TMOD4, LMOD3, RYR1, MEF2C, PKM and MYBPC2 were identified and are pivotal components in the regulation of intracellular pathways governing muscle contraction and muscular development as observed in previous research [74]. The tropomyosin (TPM) family of genes, essential for muscle contraction and filament maintenance [75], also exhibited enriched expression in skeletal muscle, aligning with previous analyses in beef cattle [3]. Genes associated with oxidative phosphorylation were enriched, particularly members of the Cytochrome C oxidase (COX) family, including COX17, COX14, COX8B, COX7A1, COX7A2, COX5A, COX412, COX5B, and COX7C [76, 77]. The myosin gene family (MYH2, MYH1, MYL1, MYLPF, MYH7, MYBPC2, and MYOT) and Titin gene family (TTN, TNNT1, TNNT3, TNNI2, TNNC1, and TNNC2), were also identified, underscoring their critical roles in skeletal muscle function and architecture. ITGA7 was also highly expressed, which is known to play a physiological role in secondary muscle fiber formation, alignment and myoblast migration [78], as were PDLIM1 for muscle function and development and CAPZB an actin binding protein that regulates actin filament alignment [79]. Overall, many of the observed differential expression results support expected tissue-specific changes whereas others provide novel insight into putative maturational processes that are relevant to the PFC, L and mixed-fiber type SM. It is expected that the PFC are maturing through synaptic formation, myelination and energy production; SM is prioritizing energy metabolism, sarcomere growth and modification as well as contractile properties; hepatic (L) cells are maturing through growth, enhanced nutrient processing as well as both anabolic (protein synthesis, glycogenesis) and catabolic metabolism, along with some structural modifications.

Differential isoform usage

In higher-level eukaryotic organisms, the complexity of gene expression is exemplified by the ability of a single gene to produce numerous distinct transcripts (isoforms) through the differential usage of splice sites, transcription start sites, and polyadenylation sites [80]. This study identifies potential isoforms unique to each tissue type, revealing several key genes that have a significant isoform composition difference between tissue types. For instance, the P2RX5 gene demonstrates variance in isoform composition between prefrontal cortex and skeletal muscle: the NM_001304573.1 transcript constitutes 60% of P2RX5 isoform expression in prefrontal cortex, while the XM_010815950.3 transcript accounts for approximately 60% in skeletal muscle. Structural modeling and domain analysis of these isoforms revealed that the prefrontal cortex-associated NM_001304573.1 isoform contains two additional α-helices and an extra transmembrane domain not present in the skeletal muscle-enriched isoform. This suggests a potential divergence in membrane integration or receptor activity, supporting the idea that isoform switching may have functional implications in tissue-specific signaling. The P2RX5 gene encodes P2X5, a member of the purinergic receptor family of ATP-activated, ligand-gated ion channel proteins with known roles in brain [81] and skeletal muscle [82]. The P2RX gene family (P2RX1–7) encodes the corresponding P2X1–7 protein subtypes, which are expressed in various cell types in the brain, including neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells, and microglia cells in the brain [81, 83, 84]. P2X5 activation in muscle satellite cells has been found to inhibit proliferation and promote differentiation and P2X1, P2X2 and P2X4-6 have been reported as expressed in different developmental stages of muscle [82] tissues of several species. Similarly, the CLTA gene exhibits distinct isoform expression, with the XM_005210062.2 isoform being predominantly expressed in the liver, and the NM_174022.2 isoform containing all gene exons being predominantly expressed in the prefrontal cortex. CLTA was found to have structural differences between isoforms and has previously been found to have additional exon expression in neuronal cell types [85] which may impact the transportation rate of clathrin across neurons [86]. The structural differences predicted between CLTA isoforms may contribute to the performance of different roles between tissues. While neither P2RX5 nor CLTA was detected in the DEG analysis between tissue types, the DTU analysis illuminated these important differences. Conversely, SLC25A3 and TSC22D4 are both examples of genes identified in both DTU and DEG analyses. Within skeletal muscle for SLC25A3, the NM_174657.2 transcript exhibited higher expression relative to the other two tissue types, which primarily expressed the XM_005206513.3 isoform. SLC25A3 encodes a mitochondrial phosphate carrier protein that has been shown to have differential isoforms expression in skeletal muscle of other species [87, 88]. TSC22D4 is an intrinsically disordered protein growth factor that has key interactions with the AKT signaling pathway (which also was identified as a DTU gene); however, isoform-specific functions have not been well characterized. We found preferential usage of the NM_001075860.2 isoform in prefrontal cortex compared to the XM_005225131.4 isoform in skeletal muscle and liver. These findings align with previous analyses and highlight the necessity of additional data collection to better understand the implications of DTU in tissues. Future studies utilizing long-read RNA data may provide further insights as short-read sequencing may not accurately capture isoform-level variation.

Differential accessibility in promoter regions

The chromatin accessibility profiles for each tissue type revealed peak signal enrichment predominantly concentrated around TSS regions, consistent with findings from previous studies [13, 89]. Pairwise comparisons revealed tissue-enriched peaks associated with genes with relevance to expected tissue function. Several SOX (SRY-related HMB-box) transcription factor binding motifs were enriched in the prefrontal cortex. The SOX gene family has been previously reported to govern several developmental mechanisms including neurogenesis [90], oligodendrocyte development [91], and male development [92, 93]. Whereas, HNF4 and FOXA motifs were significantly enriched in liver, and are both strongly associated with hepatocyte differentiation and overall liver function [94]. Transcription factor binding motifs were enriched for MEF2 family members which are myocyte enhancer factors known for their roles in muscle cell differentiation in skeletal muscle. Specifically, MEF2c’s involvement in the differentiation of the neurons [95], bone formation [96], and vascular development [97] further supports the developmental processes likely occurring within the sampled steers. While other tissue-specific transcriptional regulators likely play a critical role in tissue maturation, these by nature are tightly regulated rather than constitutively expressed. Therefore, our specific sampling time may have missed the up- and down-regulation of some transcription factors.

Tissue specific gene accessibility and expression

The sampling of young, rapidly growing steers provided the opportunity to identify tissue-specific changes in gene accessibility and expression. We anticipated that increased promoter accessibility would generally coincide with higher transcriptional output, resulting in positive concordance between RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data. Given this expectation, we next examined whether tissue-specific patterns of promoter accessibility and transcription aligned with the physiological demands of young, rapidly maturing animals. The maturation of young animals requires metabolic changes. Several genes directly associated with metabolism, energy and cell signaling were elevated in skeletal muscle. These include; acylphosphatase 2 (ACYP2) involved in cellular energy metabolism and hydrolysis of intracellular ion pumps [98], aldolase A (ALDOA) a glycolytic enzyme [99], ATPA2 (muscle-specific SERCA pump) [100], dynactin (DCTN2) in cellular transport [101], EPN1 involved in nutrient endocytosis [102], FGL1 modulation of insulin sensitivity [103], IDH3B & muscle pyruvate kinase (PKM) essential TCA cycle enzymes [104, 105], as well as the VDAC3 voltage-dependent anion channel [106]. These support the physiological maturation of SM, through improved nutrient management, acquisition and catabolism.

A total of 388 tissue-specific genes were identified in the prefrontal cortex, 177 were identified in the liver, and 103 were identified in skeletal muscle. Ontology analysis reveals distinct biological processes for each tissue. Prefrontal-cortex tissue-specific genes are primarily associated with neural processes, including synaptic signaling and neuron differentiation, while skeletal muscle-specific genes are enriched in pathways related to skeletal system morphogenesis and transcriptional regulation. The metabolically relevant terms in the liver were related to lipid metabolism and intestinal absorption. Some of the most highly expressed tissue-specific L genes were FOXA2 and FOXA3, which play significant roles in metabolic homeostasis [107] and liver injury and regeneration [108]. This was also observed to be consistent with ATAC-seq data. Several genes from the cytochrome P450 family (CYP7A, CYP2B6, CYP4F2, and CYP11A1) were also associated with tissue-specific peaks. This gene family is known for their critical role in the metabolism of cholesterol and fatty acids [109], and marked differences between human and livestock species are reported [110]. Several genes from this family were also significantly upregulated in the liver compared to the prefrontal cortex and skeletal muscle. Other studies have surveyed these genes with the objective of developing therapeutic drugs for use in cattle species [111]. Furthermore, 62, 93, and 59 tissue-specific genes were identified through both RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analyses in prefrontal cortex, liver, and skeletal muscle, respectively. The enriched motifs within these promoter regions revealed important insights into tissue-specific transcriptional regulation. The prefrontal cortex analysis identified the presence of Activating Transcription Factor (ATF) motifs, such as ATF2, which regulate stress response, apoptosis, and cell cycle control [112]. Octamer (OCT) factors, including OCT11, are involved in pluripotency and differentiation, playing key roles in development and cellular identity, with some involvement in the nervous system [113]. Within the liver, key regulators like HNF1 and HNF4a were prevalent, emphasizing their well-established roles in metabolic control and liver-specific transcription [114]. Once again, we observed the presence of FOX TF binding motifs. Although the GO terms for liver-specific genes were not as statistically significant, the presence of these transcription factors does support the functions of the identified tissue-specific genes. In skeletal muscle, transcription factors such as MEF2c, MEF2d, and SIX2 were enriched, corresponding with expected muscle differentiation, hypertrophy and developmental processes [115].

Conclusion

By jointly profiling transcription, open chromatin, and isoform usage across PFC, L, and SM at this early developmental stage, this study illustrates tissue-specific regulatory programs in rapidly growing pre-weaned Angus steers. Isoform-resolved analyses identified variants with predicted protein-level consequences, including altered domains or signal peptides, exemplified by SLC25A3 and P2RX5. Concordant promoter accessibility and motif enrichment implicate canonical regulators consistent with known tissue biology. By examining these mechanisms, alongside standard gene expression analyses, we present a more comprehensive overview of early-life tissue specific gene regulation. This study reveals that the PFC is intensely invested in complex synaptic formation and functional efficiency associated with myelination, whereas the L is optimizing its capacity to maintain homeostasis (serpins) and process nutrients such as cholesterol and lipids (CYP’s). Perhaps as expected, this analysis confirmed that SM tissue is prioritizing its accretionary growth through differentiation, myonuclear addition and hypertrophy (MEF’s). These findings augment our understanding of the transcriptome and further establish strong groundwork for future, more targeted research, that aims to more specifically elucidate the functional implications of these gene expression patterns.
