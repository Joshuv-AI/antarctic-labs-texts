Serum Lipid Profile and Electrolytes Reference Intervals for Apparently Healthy Children and Adolescents in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Ousman Mohammed, Melkitu Kassaw, Endalkachew Befekadu, Letebrhan G/Egzeabher, Yosef Tolcha, Feyissa Challa, Adisu Kebede, Genet Ashebir, Mehari Meles, Fatuma Hassen, Biruk Zerfu, Dessie Abera, Abiy Belay, Fikirte Aboneh, Daniel Hailu, Workabeba Abebe, Kassu Desta, Mistire Wolde, Aster Tsegaye
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAccurate reference intervals generated from an apparently healthy population and stratified by crucial variables such as age and gender are required to guarantee appropriate interpretation of test results. Since there were no local reference intervals in the study area, the present study aimed to establish reference intervals on serum lipid profiles and electrolytes for children and adolescents in Addis Ababa.MethodsThis community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from April to October 2019. Laboratory analysis was performed using the automatic biochemical analyzer Cobas 6000 (c501) from Roche. According to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, reference intervals for lipid profile and electrolyte tests for apparently healthy children and adolescents were established. We used a non‐parametric method to calculate the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles with a 90% confidence interval.ResultsIn children, the reference intervals for serum potassium, sodium, chloride, calcium, magnesium, and phosphate in mmol/L were 4.37–5.20, 137–145.50, 101.90–107.90, 2.34–2.70, 0.74–0.97, and 1.42–1.85, respectively; and for total cholesterol, triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein, and high‐density lipoprotein, the respective values were 100.76–171.70, 44.16–126.36, 60.60–105.60, and 31.60–53.70 in mg/dL, for both genders. For adolescents, the reference intervals were 4.03–5.58, 137–146, 98.90–120.90, 2.39–2.70, 0.73–0.96, and 0.96–1.80 for serum potassium, sodium, chloride, calcium, magnesium, and phosphate in mmol/L, respectively; and 97.20–189.10, 40.50–143.60, 41.70–120.90, and 21.30–57.0 in mg/dL for total cholesterol, triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein, and high‐density lipoprotein, respectively, for both genders.ConclusionThe established reference intervals in the current study revealed that both the lower and upper limits contradicted the manufacturer values as well as the available literature. The study also discovered significant gender differences in reference values for TC, TG, LDL‐C, potassium, phosphate, and chloride in the adolescent age groups.

Introduction

Lipid profile tests are used for screening for atherosclerotic risk and in the diagnosis and treatment of dyslipidemia. The onset of this disease occurs during the first years of life, and the manifestation of hyperlipidemia in childhood is associated with atherosclerosis at an older age. The differences in factors like body fat distribution, the activity of enzymes involved in lipolysis, and insulin response can lead to population differences in serum lipid concentrations [1, 2]. Electrolytes are widely distributed in body fluids and maintain many physiological functions. Serum electrolyte imbalance for various reasons requires urgent management and otherwise leads to mortality and morbidity [3].

The reference interval (RI) is the interval between which 95% of the values of a reference population fall, with the remaining 5% falling outside of the limits [4]. For evidence‐based clinical diagnostics and healthcare delivery, the proper selection and monitoring of a wide range of biochemical parameters are critical [5, 6]. Accurate reference intervals obtained from a healthy population and stratified by key factors such as age and gender are required to guarantee proper interpretation of test results. However, establishing population‐based reference intervals can be difficult due to the difficulties in selecting and recruiting a large number of representative healthy individuals, the time and resources needed, and the significant physiological changes that occur during childhood and adolescence [7, 8, 9, 10]. Children and adolescents are more prone to experiencing physiological changes that affect typical analyte concentrations, such as physical size, organ maturity, bodily fluid compartments, nutrition, and metabolism. These noticeable variations during childhood make it essential to establish age‐and gender‐specific reference intervals, which enhance the clinical laboratory service [11, 12].

The vast majority of reference intervals used in Africa today are derived from populations outside the continent and may not be reflective of population values elsewhere [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Discrepancies in RI values have been noted when compared to healthy African population values [18, 19]. Furthermore, differences in various characteristics have been documented across distinct African ethnic groups. Despite these disparities, there is a scarcity of information on biochemical reference intervals for healthy children and adolescents in Sub‐Saharan Africa [20, 21, 22, 23]. Interpretation of laboratory data based on non‐representative reference intervals is potentially troublesome for clinical care and may result in mismanagement, underestimation, or overestimation of illness states. The proper medical evaluation of children is largely dependent on the correct interpretation of all laboratory results combined with data collected through physical examinations and medical histories. Relevant reference intervals, ideally established from a representative population, are required for such an interpretation of numerical laboratory results [24, 25, 26, 27, 28].

The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC) also recommended that every indigenous population have their own set of reference limits for each analyte derived from healthy representative individuals [7, 29]. However, there are no published serum lipid profile and electrolyte reference intervals for children and adolescents in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. This study aimed to address critical gaps in serum lipid profile and electrolyte reference intervals for children and adolescents in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Following CLSI guidelines, we determined variations by gender and age to establish comprehensive reference ranges. Thus, it is very essential for early detection, close monitoring, and correction of dyslipidemia and electrolyte abnormalities in order to make quick and accurate decisions.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

The study was conducted on apparently healthy children and adolescents living in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Addis Ababa is the capital city of Ethiopia and the largest city in the country by population, with an area of 527 km2. The city has, in recent years, seen a robust annual growth rate, and population counts are growing closer to 5.3 million, with a male‐to‐female ratio of 0.9 [30]. From April to October 2019, children and adolescents aged 5 to 17 were chosen at random from the community by health extension workers. Community meetings were held to explain the aims of the study to the community and its leaders. Apparently, healthy individuals aged between 5 and 17 who voluntarily participated in the study were included. Participants experiencing an acute illness and having chronic diseases like diabetes mellitus, heart disease, anemia, thyroid disease, liver disease, and cancer of any type were excluded from the study. Individuals with hemo‐ and intestinal parasites, the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), syphilis, pharmacologically active substances, smoking, alcohol consumption, or obesity were also excluded from the study (Figure 1).

A flow chart showing study participant selection.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Technique

CLSI recommends a minimum of 120 samples to establish a reference interval with a 90% CI by using a non‐parametric method [7]. This number does not include any losses or deletions of observations. So, assuming 30% of apparently healthy participants do not qualify for reference interval determination for various reasons, a total of 522 participants were enrolled to meet the minimum requirement of 120 individuals in each partition. The initial stratification of reference individuals by age and gender was made based on previously published reference interval studies [31, 32, 33] together with a graphical inspection of our data. Participants were partitioned by age groups: 5–12 and 13–17 years of age. Furthermore, the last age group was further sub‐grouped by gender because of gender‐related differences during the pubertal period [31, 32, 33].

With the help of the central statistical agency, four sub‐cities were established, namely Arada, Kirkos, Akaki‐Kality, and Yeka. The probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling method was employed, where the size depends on the number of woredas in a sub‐city. The study participants were selected from randomly selected woredas (the smallest administrative units) under the respective sub‐cities through both school and village‐to‐village mobilization by health extension workers and well‐trained data collectors. Once volunteer participants fulfill the eligibility criteria, they are invited to go to nearby health facilities or temporary specimen collection sites for interviews using structured questionnaires and to facilitate blood, urine, and stool sample collection.

Data Collection Procedure

Both an a priori and a posteriori sampling method were used, whereby individuals assessed whether they fulfilled the well‐defined exclusion and inclusion criteria before and after recruiting the reference participants. In the data collection process, there was an initial interview by a health extension worker, along with the data collectors, to collect general health information on lifestyle habits and medical history, followed by a visit to a nearby health center to obtain samples and a physical examination. Prior to blood collection, volunteers were requested to fast overnight for 10–12 h. The participants and/or their parents or guardians were interviewed, and participants were asked to be measured for their heights, weights, and mid‐upper arm circumference. Height was measured without shoes in a standing position on a height measuring scale to the nearest 0.1 cm, and weight was measured with a digital scale to the nearest 0.1 kg after taking off any additional layers of clothing, shoes, and anything in their pockets. After 5 min of rest, blood pressure was checked in triplicate, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Socio‐demographic and clinical data were collected using structured questionnaires by trained data collectors. A health officer carried out the physical examination and anthropometric measurements. Moreover, volunteers were asked to sit for 15–20 min prior to collection in order to avoid variation due to postural influence and physical stress. Finally, sample collection was performed from 8:30 to 11:00 a.m., and small children were given the first chance during the phlebotomy procedure.

Sample Collection

By explaining the blood drawing procedure to the participants, about 5 mL of blood samples were collected from each individual in a serum separator tube following standard operating procedures, and then the samples were transported from the collection area to the laboratory by packing them inside the icebox. These containers were used to transport the serum under controlled conditions, minimizing temperature fluctuations that could affect sample integrity. The serum separator tube blood sample was centrifuged within 1 h of collection for 2–3 min at 1500–3000 g, then immediately separated and stored at −80°C until analysis. In addition, stool and midstream urine sample examinations were performed on‐site. As a practical matter, samples were collected and handled properly and in a manner consistent with the routine practices followed for patient specimens.

Laboratory Analysis and Quality Control

All tests were performed using the Cobas 6000 (c501) (Roche Diagnostics, GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) automatic chemistry analyzer at the Ethiopian Public Health Institute, which is a nationally accredited reference laboratory. Moreover, the clinical chemistry reference laboratory is enrolled in the One World Accuracy Proficiency Program for Clinical Chemistry Test Analysis. The Cobas 6000 (c501) clinical chemistry analyzer was checked for delivering correct results by using commercial controls. Likewise, pooled serum samples were run alongside the participants' samples. Additionally, we implemented a comprehensive quality control technique that included three types of serum controls: normal, pathologically low, and pathologic high. This method guaranteed the accuracy and reproducibility of our analytical measurements over a wide range of clinically relevant concentrations. Moreover, to minimize inter‐assay variability in our lipid profile and electrolyte measurements at the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI), we implemented several quality control measures. We calculated inter‐assay coefficients of variation (CVs) from these quality control samples, aiming to maintain CVs below 15%. EPHI also participates in external proficiency testing schemes to ensure alignment with international standards. The clinical chemistry laboratory adheres to strict protocols for sample handling, including standardized procedures for freezing, thawing, and centrifugation. Additionally, quality control charts are employed to identify any out‐of‐control runs and establish acceptable bounds of variability for our assays.

The analytical performance of all assays was strictly assessed, and the participant's sample was analyzed only when all assays were acceptable. The concentrations of four lipid profile tests, such as total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), were determined by an enzymatic, colorimetric method. An ion‐selective electrode (ISE) indirect potentiometric method was used to measure the serum electrolytes sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), and chloride (Cl−). However, the colorimetric endpoint method was used to measure magnesium (Mg2+), calcium (Ca2+), and phosphate (PO4−) (Table 1). Reagents, calibrators, and controls were obtained from the manufacturer of the instrument and regulated in accordance with Roche Diagnostic instructions. Besides, calibration was also run daily before testing sodium, potassium, and chloride. Serum indexes such as hemolysis, lipemia, and icterus were detected automatically with the Cobas analyzer. Infectious disease screening tests were conducted at the Ethiopian National Blood Bank (ENBB) using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) testing technologies. During laboratory analysis, all the necessary procedures and steps were followed based on the manufacturer's instructions. Furthermore, serum samples were subjected to a single freeze–thaw cycle during the biochemical analysis process. In general, to ensure the accuracy and precision of the test results, all pre‐analytical, analytical, and post‐analytical precautions were taken into consideration.

Data Analysis and Interpretation

The data was analyzed in accordance with the CLSI C28‐A3 Guidelines [7]. After cleaning, editing, and double‐checking for completeness, the data was entered into the Statistical Package for Social Science version 23 (SPSS version 23.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) for statistical analysis. The serum lipid profile and electrolyte reference intervals for apparently healthy children and adolescents were calculated using non‐parametric methods because the majority of the analytes were not distributed normally. First, we applied inspection of the reference observation distribution via histogram and scatter plot, followed by identification of the outliers in interquartile ranges (IQR: Q3–Q1; Q1: lower quartile, Q3: upper quartile). Moreover, the data were tested for the normality of its distribution by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. At levels of < Q1–1.5 IQR and/or > Q3 + 1.5 IQR were considered outliers and discarded manually following the Box and Whisker plot and were not included in the final reference estimation. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The mean, median, and standard deviation (SD) were calculated. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles with corresponding 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for each analyte were determined using the bootstrap technique. Biochemical parameter variations with gender and age were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. A two‐sided p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

Five hundred and twenty‐two apparently healthy individuals participated in the study, with an equal number of male and female ratios of N = 261 (50%) each. Of the total study samples, 54.6% (285/522) were in the adolescent age group (13–17 years), and the rest, 45.4% (237/522), were children (5–12 years). The numbers of children in the age groups of 5–12 years were relatively smaller as compared to 13–17 years since the study established separate reference intervals for the adolescent group. Participants were divided into age cohorts of ≥ 5 to < 13 years and ≥ 13 to ≤ 17 years. The oldest age cohort was further subdivided by gender. Regarding the ethnicity of the study participants, Amhara 183 (35.1%), Oromo 77 (14.8%), South Nation nationality people 108 (20.7%), Tigre 31 (5.9%), and the rest 123 (23.6%) were of mixed origin. All were students, and about 97% were born in Addis Ababa.

Age and Gender Distribution, and Screening Results of the Reference Population

Figure 2 shows a bar chart depicting the age and gender distribution in the reference population. In terms of lifestyle, practically all study participants had a normal weight; none were classified as overweight or underweight. None of the volunteers were addicted to alcohol, chewing gum, or smoking. One participant with a hepatitis B virus infection and one syphilis‐positive individual were excluded from the study. Furthermore, 20 people with high‐sensitive C‐reactive protein (hsCRP) values greater than 3 mg/L were excluded, but no one was eliminated because of HIV, HCV, malaria testing, intestinal parasites, or urine chemical tests, as all these tests were negative. As a result, before the lipid and electrolyte values were assessed, 500 children and adolescents met the qualifying criteria.

Establishment of Reference Intervals on Serum Lipid Profile and Electrolytes for Children and Adolescents

Different numbers of outliers were excluded from each analyte before determining reference intervals. The reference values for four lipid profiles and six serum electrolytes are shown below. In the adolescent group, gender partitions were performed since significant differences were observed between the genders. Adolescent females had high TC, TG, LDL‐C, and Ca2+ values in both the lower and upper limits. And females also had higher HDL‐C, K+, Cl−, and Na+ values at the upper limit than did males. On the other hand, adolescent male participants had a slightly high value of phosphate at the upper limit. In both limits, males had higher Mg2+ and Cl− levels than females. Furthermore, the serum electrolyte values between children and adolescents were very similar except for phosphate. In both the lower and upper limits, the low‐age cohort (5–12 years) had higher PO4− concentrations than adolescents (13–17 years). As for the lipid profile, children had relatively higher concentrations of TC, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, and TG in the lower limit than adolescents' combined values (Table 2).

Moreover, the mean, median, SD, and RI (2.5th–97.5th percentiles) for children and adolescents were determined for each serum lipid and electrolyte parameter. Using the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test, statistically significant differences (p‐value < 0.05) between male and female adolescents were observed for PO4−, TC, TG, K+, Cl−, and LDL cholesterol. As shown in Table 2, the median concentrations and RI for TC were 130.8 mg/dL (100.76–171.70), for TG 81.8 mg/dL (44.16–126.36), HDL‐C 35.70 mg/dL (31.60–53.70), LDL‐C 73.10 mg/dL (60.60–105.60), potassium 4.57 mmol/L (4.37–5.20), sodium 141 mmol/L (137–145). The median (RI) values for the analytes in adolescents were for TC 134.4 mg/dL (97.25–189.10), TG 77.20 mg/dL (40.55–143.60), HDL‐C 38 mg/dL (21.30–57), LDL‐C 74 mg/dL (41.70–120.95), potassium 4.74 mmol/L (4.03–5.58), sodium 141 mmol/L (137–146), chloride 103.50 mmol/L (98.95–108.50), calcium 2.55 mmol/L (2.39–2.70), magnesium 0.84 mmol/L (0.73–0.96–1.80), for combined genders. Furthermore, this study found that there were significant differences between age groups in the median concentration of phosphate, potassium, and calcium (p‐value < 0.05). Likewise, with the exception of HDL‐C, there were no significant differences between age groups in the median concentration of the lipid profile. On the other hand, the combined gender median sodium and chloride ion concentrations between children and adolescents were identical.

Proportion of Misclassified Values in Children and Adolescents

Table 3 compares manufacturer‐provided reference intervals with established serum lipid profiles and electrolyte parameter RIs to show the proportions of misclassified values in children and adolescents. For both age partitions, misclassifications were seen in all of the analytes. A significant difference was observed for HDL cholesterol. According to the findings, among children aged 5 to 12, there were, respectively, 74.4%, 36%, 24.2%, 18.2%, and 16.9% misclassified results for HDL‐C, Mg2+, K+, Cl−, and PO4−. Additionally, among 12‐ to 17‐year‐old adolescents, misclassified values for HDL‐C, Ca2+, K+, Mg2+, and LDL‐C were found to be 74.6%, 39.7%, 19.5%, 13.8%, and 10.9%, respectively. In the younger age cohort, sodium ions had the lowest proportion of misclassified values (1.04%) versus 2.2% in the adolescents (Table 3).

Comparison of Currently Established Reference Intervals in Children and Adolescents With Other Published Data

The Cobas platform (Roche Diagnostics) was utilized for all of the tests, which may have helped to harmonize methodology‐dependent discrepancies, but it was still extremely likely that the reference intervals between the current study and other prior studies would differ. Serum electrolytes like potassium and phosphate also differed significantly from the Western reference intervals. There was no agreement between studies for children's concentrations of HDL‐C, TC, TG, and LDL‐C. Additionally, in comparison to the majority of other research, the present study's TC and HDL‐C levels were lower. On the other hand, there were similarities for LDL‐C RIs within lower limits across various nations (Table 4).

Discussion

Because precise reference intervals are required for the proper interpretation of any numeric laboratory test findings, CLSI and IFCC recommended that RIs be produced for each region and locality [7, 29]. Because obtaining samples from healthy children might be challenging, creating reference intervals using the direct technique can be problematic for children. As a result, reference intervals provided by instrument manufacturers mostly generated in Western countries, are employed in Ethiopia. As a consequence, the current work focuses on developing reference intervals for serum lipids and electrolytes in children and adolescents from Addis Ababa for the first time using direct approaches. The current study found statistically significant gender differences in TC, TG, LDL‐C, potassium, phosphate, and chloride in the 13–17 age groups.

Electrolytes are vital for maintaining physiological balance and supporting functions like nerve transmission and muscle contraction. Establishing reference intervals for blood electrolytes and lipid profiles is crucial for diagnosing and managing electrolyte imbalances. Accurate benchmarks enhance clinical test interpretation and management of electrolyte‐related health issues, highlighting the need for localized data in pediatric health monitoring. Relevant literature underscores the importance of precise electrolyte measurements in understanding various health disorders [49, 50, 51].

The increase in potassium concentration, which increased with maturity towards adolescence in the present study, likely reflects different stages of physiological changes during maturation [31]. Compared to values determined for other populations, the K+ values for our study populations were higher than those reported from manufacturer's values for Canada [34], Sweden [35], Denmark [36], and Guinea [37], both at the upper and lower limits. Potassium RI also has a higher lower limit than in the USA [38], Tanzania [39], and Ghana [40]. In line with this study, exactly the same figure was obtained for the upper limit of potassium in a Tanzanian study [39]. The established RIs for Tanzanian [39] and Ghanaian [40] males and females were lower than the current findings. Consistent with the current finding, a study done in Ghana found that females had a slightly higher K value in the upper limit. The difference between the current results and earlier findings could be related to variations in inclusion and exclusion criteria, nutrition, or genetics. Moreover, an increase in potassium concentration with maturity towards adolescence likely reflects physiological changes during growth and development. However, it is important to note that pre‐analytical factors, such as tourniquet use during blood collection, can also contribute to variations in serum potassium levels [41, 42, 52, 53].

Regarding serum sodium ions, the current study determined an interval that was almost similar for both genders and age ranges. Evidence from this study and previous investigations suggests that sodium levels peak early in life and then remain stable. The current study's lower and upper RI for sodium were relatively higher than the manufacturer's values and those established in Canada [34], Germany [43], Australia [44], Tanzania [39], and Guinea [37] in both genders and age groups. However, the current study's sodium upper limit was lower than the findings from Ghana [40]. The level of sodium was inconsistent among different African countries, including the current study. This inconsistency may be explained by the great variations in salt consumption in various areas [45]. In agreement with the present finding, adolescent females had slightly higher upper limit values than males in the Zimbabwean study [46, 47].

Similarly, the chloride RIs in the current study were higher in both limits than those stated in the manufacturer's leaflet and in studies from Canada [34] and the United States [38], but lower in the upper limit than those reported from Australia [44] and Ghana [40]. The RI results in both chloride limits were found to be completely different between the Kenyan study and the current study [48]. According to findings including current work, adolescent females had slightly higher chloride concentrations in the upper limit than males. Sodium and chloride are particularly sensitive to salt intake; this is the most likely explanation for this major difference in results. This could also be explained by variations in aldosterone levels and genetic factors, as well as lifestyle factors such as hot weather, prolonged exposure to sunlight, and sweating rate [45, 54, 55].

Through adolescence, serum calcium levels gradually rose, with male and female children aged 5–12 years having the lowest levels. The current study showed no appreciable gender differences between boys and girls. In comparison to studies in Canada [34], Australia [44], Sweden [35], Guinea [37], and Tanzania [39], relatively greater calcium concentrations were found in the current study in both the lower and upper ranges, while there was also better agreement with the manufacturer's leaflet values. Overall, variations in blood vitamin D or parathyroid hormone may be the primary causes of variations in calcium concentration (PTH). Most likely, it could also be brought on by dietary issues or racial disparities [42, 56, 57].

Regarding the RI values of magnesium, the current children had lower values at the upper limit than other studies in Canada [34], Australia [44], Germany [43], and Tanzania [39]. However, it was relatively higher than the values provided by the manufacturer. As for adolescents, the magnesium values were higher than in a study from Denmark [36] but lower than in a Tanzanian study [39]. On the other hand, a higher concentration of phosphate among children was observed. Hence, a significant increase in phosphate in the early stages of life in children is attributed to bone growth [58]. In line with this finding, other studies revealed that children had high phosphate values [34, 35, 37, 38, 39, 43]. Comparing the present phosphate RI, children had a lower upper limit than reported RIs from Germany [43], Australia [44], Canada [34], Guinea [37], and Tanzania [39]. Likewise, our adolescents' phosphate values were higher in both genders compared to values from Pakistan [59] and Denmark [36]. However, it was lower at both the lower and upper limits than in the Kenyan study [48]. The variations in electrolyte RIs could be brought on by variations in the age limits of the reference population in different studies, as well as the analyzer, technique of analysis, diet, way of life, and genetics.

With the exception of HDL‐C in the older age group, gender‐associated variations were seen in the current investigation for all lipid profile tests. In both biological genders, the levels of LDL‐C, HDL‐C, TC, and TG rose with age. Studies from Guinea [37] and Tanzania [39] also came to similar conclusions. In contrast, the current children's TC RI values were lower than those in earlier Canadian [34], Swedish [35], American [38], and Danish [36] investigations in both the upper and lower ranges. On the other hand, the TC RI limit obtained in the current study was marginally larger on the upper limit side when compared to studies from Guinea [37] and Tanzania [39]. However, TC's RI value was higher than a finding from Ghana [40] in both the lower and upper limits. Similarly, at the adolescent age, the current findings on TC are also lower than reports from the USA [38], Germany [43], and Denmark [36]. Additionally, only the upper limit of TG levels in children and adolescents was less than that reported in their Canadian [34], USA [38], Australia [44], Swedish [35], Guinean [37], Tanzanian [39], and Ghanaian [40] counterparts. This difference may be due to variations in the number of participants, exclusion criteria for conditions that likely affect lipid levels, partition, and differences in both statistics and laboratory methods. Variations in gene polymorphisms that regulate lipid metabolism can have an effect on serum lipid levels. Food, social status, lifestyle, and exposure to different environmental conditions all have vital effects. For instance, dietary differences can impact lipid profiles, while varying levels of physical activity and stress might also contribute to these discrepancies. Understanding these factors is crucial for developing context‐specific health interventions [60, 61]. The LDL‐C upper reference limit for children in the current study was lower than that for Canada [34], Denmark [36], and Tanzania [39], while being somewhat similar to that from a study conducted in Zimbabwe [46, 47]. The current work revealed that female adolescents had a relatively higher LDL‐C than males. This finding contradicts the known fact that the estrogen hormone decreases the LDL‐C concentration in female adolescents. High LDL‐C and low HDL‐C levels are thought to be better predictors of the occurrence and recurrence of myocardial infarction and stroke, as well as premature and severe cardiovascular disease [62, 63, 64, 65]. As a result, the current study discovered that the lower and upper limits of HDL‐C were lower in children and adolescents than in all prior studies [34, 35, 38, 39, 46, 47]. Children and adolescents are particularly susceptible to physiological changes that influence typical analyte concentrations, including factors such as growth, organ maturation, fluid distribution, nutrition, and metabolism. The low HDL‐C concentration may be attributed to variations in nutrition, lifestyle, body posture, and genetic factors [66]. A slight increase in total cholesterol and LDL‐C upper limits from infancy to adolescence may be explained by increased demand for steroid hormone synthesis and is connected with physiologic development [67, 68, 69]. Overall, the established reference intervals for total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL‐C, and HDL‐C were lower in this study when compared to Western RIs. Additionally, the observed differences in lipid profile reference intervals between this study and other studies underscore the need for further investigation into the underlying physiological mechanisms, including the role of diet, lifestyle, and genetic factors. According to the current findings, the manufacturer's RIs or other RIs established elsewhere might have resulted in incorrect diagnosis and management of patients. Furthermore, misclassifications were seen in all analytes for both age divisions, and HDL cholesterol showed the biggest disparity.

Strengths and Limitations

In this study, we recruited a large study population outside of hospital settings. Moreover, biochemical analysis was performed with Cobas c501 at a nationally accredited laboratory. However, the lack of RIs from birth to 5 years of age was the limitation.

Conclusion and Recommendation

This study is the first effort to establish the RIs for fasting lipids and serum electrolytes in children and adolescents aged between 5 and 17 years in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The current reference intervals will aid in the early assessment of lipid and electrolyte abnormalities in child and adolescent populations. The researchers discovered significant gender differences in RIs for TC, TG, LDL‐C, potassium, phosphate, and chloride in the 13–17 age groups. Furthermore, significant differences between age groups were seen for HDL‐C, phosphate, potassium, and calcium concentrations. The observed gender variations in reference values for lipid profiles and electrolytes in teenagers are most likely caused by a mix of causes, the majority of which are connected to puberty. During puberty, hormonal changes significantly affect lipid metabolism and electrolyte balance. The present study thus supports the requirement for the adoption of locally specified reference intervals. We suggest that these data could be applied to decision‐making, epidemiological studies, and intervention studies to interpret an individual's results. After that, for the proper interpretation of laboratory findings, any healthcare provider must follow these RI values. At the national level, more research is suggested, including for children under 5 years old, to determine the biochemical RIs for the Ethiopian population.
