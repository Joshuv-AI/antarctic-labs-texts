Non-Hermitian Generalization of Rayleigh-Schrödinger Perturbation Theory
Wei-Ming Chen, Yen-Ting Lin, Chia-Yi Ju

Abstract
While perturbation theories constitute a significant foundation of modern quantum system analysis, extending them from the Hermitian to the non-Hermitian regime remains a non-trivial task. In this work, we generalize the Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory to the non-Hermitian regime by employing a geometric formalism. This framework allows us to compute perturbative corrections to eigenstates and eigenvalues of Hamiltonians iteratively to any order. Furthermore, we observe that the recursion equation for the eigenstates resembles the form of the Girard-Newton formulas, which helps us uncover the general solution to the recursion equation. Moreover, we demonstrate that the perturbation method proposed in this paper reduces to the standard Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory in the Hermitian regime.

II
Geometric Formalism of Quantum Mechanics

In this section, we review the geometric formalism of (non-)Hermitian quantum mechanics
[
29
,
13
]
and its direct consequence, namely, an emergent dimension
[
30
]
. In this context, the physical system under consideration evolves with respect to the parameters
t
(time) and
q
(a physical parameter) as parallel transports.

To be more specific, the states in the Hilbert space are constrained by the Schrödinger equation, i.e., a parallel transport in the
t
-direction,

\nabla_{t}|\psi(q,t)\rangle=\bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+iH(q,t)\bigg]|\psi(q,t)\rangle=0,

(1)

where
\nabla_{t}
is the connection or covariant derivative in the
t
-direction.
An important aspect of this formalism is the Hilbert space equipped with the metric
G(q,t)
, which enables the definition of a more general inner product in the Hilbert space,

\langle\psi_{1}(q,t)|G(q,t)|\psi_{2}(q,t)\rangle,

(2)

where
\bra{\psi_1(q, t) }
is the Hermitian conjugate of
\ket{\psi_1(q,t)}
. Note that when
G(q,t)
is the identity, this definition reduces to the familiar form encountered in Hermitian quantum systems.
To express this more concisely, we define

\left|\psi(q,t)\right\rrangle=\ket{\psi(q, t)}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\llangle\psi(q,t)\right|=\bra{\psi(q, t)}G(q,t).

(3)

We therefore treat the double brackets
\left|\psi(q,t)\right\rrangle
and
\left\llangle\psi(q,t)\right|
as states and dual state, respectively, in a Hilbert space.
Moreover, by requiring that the state normalization is independent of the parameters
q
and
t
, i.e.,

\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\left\llangle\psi(q,t)\vphantom{\psi(q,t)}\right|\kern-2.58334pt\left.\psi(q,t)\vphantom{\psi(q,t)}\right\rrangle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left\llangle\psi(q,t)\vphantom{\psi(q,t)}\right|\kern-2.58334pt\left.\psi(q,t)\vphantom{\psi(q,t)}\right\rrangle=0,

(4)

we obtain the governing equations on the metric
G(q,t)
being

\nabla_{t}G(q,t)=iG(q,t)H(q,t)-iH^{\dagger}(q,t)G(q,t),

(5)

and

\nabla_{q}G(q,t)=iG(q,t)K(q,t)-iK^{\dagger}(q,t)G(q,t),

(6)

and the state evolution along the
q
-direction being

\nabla_{q}\ket{\psi(q,t)}=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial q}+iK(q,t)\right]\ket{\psi(q,t)}=0.

(7)

Although
K(q,t)
is not yet determined at this stage, from its role indicates that it is the evolution generator in the
q
-direction, or the
q
-generator. It is worth mentioning that even without an explicit expression or governing equation, we can show that the
q
-generator
K(q,t)
is not unique. As an example, one can add a phase to the state, i.e.,
|\psi(q,t)\rangle\rightarrow e^{Aq}|\psi(q,t)\rangle
, without altering the physics, but it modifies
K(q,t)
in Eq. (
7
) to
K(q,t)+A
. Therefore, the
q
-generator
K(q,t)
is gauge-dependent (a more precise argument can be found in
[
29
,
30
]
).

To find the explicit expression for
K(q,t)
, we turn our attention to the local curvature
\mathcal{F}=F_{t,q}dt\wedge dq
, where

iF_{t,q}\ket{\psi}=[\nabla_{t},\nabla_{q}]\ket{\psi}=0,

(8)

for any quantum state
\ket{\psi(q, t)}
, where the last equality comes from Eqs. (
1
) and (
7
). In other words, the local curvature
\mathcal{F}
always vanishes.

As a consequence, from
F_{t,q}=0
, we obtain the relation between the operators
H
and
K
, namely

i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}K(q,t)-i\frac{\partial}{\partial q}H(q,t)+[K(q,t),H(q,t)]=0.

(9)

This equation serves as our starting point for performing the perturbation computation. As we will see later, the perturbation in the Hamiltonian can be encoded in
K(q,t)
. While
K(q,t)
is gauge-dependent, we can choose suitable gauge choices for eigenstates perturbation. Hence, each term of the Hamiltonian eigenvalue perturbation can be expressed using the operator
K(q,t)
.

III
Geometric Perturbation

For simplicity, we focus on the non-degenerate perturbation theory for the eigenstates of a time-independent Hamiltonian. In this case, the time evolution of the eigenstates can be written as

\left|\psi_{n}(q,t)\right\rrangle=e^{-ih_{n}(q)t}\left|n(q)\right\rrangle,

(10)

where
\ket{n(q)}=\ket{\psi_n(q, t = 0)}
is the
n
-th eigenstate of the Hamiltonian, i.e.,

H(q)\left|n(q)\right\rrangle=h_{n}(q)\left|n(q)\right\rrangle,

(11)

with
h_{n}(q)
representing the
n
-th eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian. To find a
q
-generator
K(q,t)
that works well with the perturbation, we impose the adiabatic gauge-fixing condition
[
30
]
,

\left[\partial_{t}K(q,t),H(q)\right]=0.

(12)

This choice ensures that the states evolve adiabatically, such that Eq. (
11
) is preserved. The condition, together with Eq. (
9
), leads to

\partial_{t}^{2}K(q,t)=0,

(13)

which allows us to decompose
K(q,t)
into

K(q,t)=K_{1}(q)t+K_{0}(q).

(14)

From the decomposition, it is easy to see the operator
K_{1}(q)
is completely fixed by the gauge-fixing condition in Eq. (
12
), while
K_{0}(q)
retains some gauge freedom
[
31
]
(i.e.,
K_{0}(q)
can be shifted by
\Delta K
, where
[\Delta K(q),H(q)]=0
).

From now on, since the operators
H(q)
,
K_{1}(q)
, and
K_{0}(q)
, as well as the states
\left|n(q)\right\rrangle
, depend solely on the parameter
q
, we will omit this dependence in our expressions for simplicity. Additionally, we will omit the
q
- and
t
-dependence in
K(q,t)
to further simplify the discussion.

By substituting the decomposition into Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
), we obtain in turn the following commutation relations, respectively:

\displaystyle\left[H,K_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=i(K_{1}-\partial_{q}H),

(15)

\displaystyle\left[H,K_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

(16)

Since
K_{1}
and
H
commute with each other, they share the same eigenstates. To compute the eigenvalue perturbation, it is important to determine the eigenvalue of the corresponding eigenstate when
K_{1}
acts on it.

By applying the parallel transport in the
q
-direction Eq. (
7
) to the time-independent Hamiltonian eigenstate Eq. (
10
),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nabla_{q}\big(e^{-ih_{n}t}\left|n_{q}\right\rrangle\big)

(17)

\displaystyle=e^{-ih_{n}t}\big(-it\partial_{q}h_{n}+\partial_{q}+iK\big)\left|n\right\rrangle

\displaystyle=e^{-ih_{n}t}\Big[-it\big(\partial_{q}h_{n}-K_{1}\big)+\big(\partial_{q}+iK_{0}\big)\Big]\left|n\right\rrangle,

where, in the last equality, we have used the gauge-fixing decomposition Eq. (
14
). Since terms with different powers in
t
are independent, this implies,

\displaystyle K_{0}\left|n\right\rrangle

\displaystyle=i\partial_{q}\left|n\right\rrangle,

(18)

\displaystyle K_{1}\left|n\right\rrangle

\displaystyle=\partial_{q}h_{n}\left|n\right\rrangle.

(19)

These results are consistent with the previous discussion:
K_{0}
is not a gauge-invariant operator, as it generates
q
-translations in eigenstates (which are not physical observables), whereas
K_{1}
is a gauge-invariant operator, as it generates
q
-translations in eigenvalues (which are physical observables).

Next, we proceed to compute the perturbative corrections to the eigenstates and their corresponding eigenvalues. Assume
q
is small and expand all relevant quantities in Taylor series,

\displaystyle K_{0}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{j}K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}},~~K_{1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{j}K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}\,,~~H=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{j}H^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}\,,

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pth_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{j}h_{n}^{(j)}\,,~~~\left|n\right\rrangle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{j}\left|n^{(j)}\right\rrangle.

(20)

The upper indices in the parentheses denote the orders in the Taylor expansion.

Given the expansion, Eq. (
18
) allows us to express a given state correction as a linear combination of
K_{0}
acting on other state corrections. More precisely, for order
q^{k-1}
, Eq. (
18
) implies,

\left|n^{\text{\tiny($k$)}}\right\rrangle=-\frac{i}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j-1$)}}\left|n^{\text{\tiny($k-j$)}}\right\rrangle,~~~~~~k\geq 1.

(21)

This re-expression can be performed iteratively, so that any correction to a perturbed state can be expressed as a linear combination of products of
K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}
’s acting on the unperturbed state
\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle
. Interestingly, Eq. (
21
) closely resembles the form of the Girard-Newton formulas (see Appendix
A
). Specifically, it can be obtained by replacing
s_{j}
with
\left|n^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}\right\rrangle
and
p_{j}
with
iK_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j-1$)}}
in Eq. (
61
), respectively. Therefore, just as
s_{j}
can be represented by
p_{j}
’s and
s_{0}
,
\left|n^{\text{\tiny($k$)}}\right\rrangle
can also be expressed in terms of
K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}
’s and
\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle
. However, one must be careful with the ordering: unlike the terms in the standard Girard-Newton formulas,
K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}
’s are generally non-commutative operators, and the states must also be placed after the
K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}
’s. Therefore, the ordering of
K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}
’s must be taken into account when solving for the eigenstates from Eq. (
21
). As a result, Eq. (
21
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left|n^{\text{\tiny($k$)}}\right\rrangle=\frac{\mathbb{B}_{k}\Big((0!)\cdot(-i\,K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}),(1)!\cdot(-i\,K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}),\dots,(k-1)!\cdot(-i\,K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($k-1$)}})\Big)}{k!}\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle,

(22)

with
\mathbb{B}_{k}
being the
dual non-commutative Bell polynomials
, where the recursive definition can be found in Eq. (
66
) in Appendix
A
.
For clarity, we provide some examples below:

\displaystyle\left|n^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\right\rrangle

\displaystyle=-iK_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle,

\displaystyle\left|n^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}\right\rrangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Big[\left(K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right)^{2}+iK_{0}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\Big]\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle,

(23)

\displaystyle\left|n^{\text{\tiny($3$)}}\right\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{6}\Big[\left(K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right)^{3}+iK_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}

\displaystyle\hskip 49.50795pt\quad+2iK_{0}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}-2K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}\Big]\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle.

Once again, it is evident that the corrections to the eigenstates depend only on
K_{0}
, highlighting the gauge dependence of the eigenstates.

Our target is to determine the gauge-invariant quantity, namely the corrections to the Hamiltonian eigenvalues, which can be computed using Eq. (
19
).
For order
q^{k-1}
, the corresponding equation is

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big(K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($j-1$)}}-jh_{n}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}\Big)\left|n^{\text{\tiny($k-j$)}}\right\rrangle=0.

(24)

By sandwiching this equation with
\left\llangle n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|
, we derive the recursion formula for the corrections to the eigenvalues, namely,

\displaystyle h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($k$)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($k-1$)}}]]_{nn}}{k}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($j-1$)}}\mathbb{B}_{k-j}]]_{nn}-h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}[[\mathbb{B}_{k-j}]]_{nn}}{(k-j)!k},

(25)

where the double square brackets are defined as

\displaystyle[[\cdots]]_{mn}

\displaystyle\equiv\left\llangle m^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|\cdots\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle=\bra{\p{m}{0}}G^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\cdots\ket{\p{n}{0}}.

(26)

We list some results of the Hamiltonian eigenvalue corrections for reference,

\displaystyle h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}

\displaystyle=[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}]]_{nn},

\displaystyle h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big([[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}]]_{nn}-i[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}]]_{nn}+i[[K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}]]_{nn}h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\Big)

\displaystyle h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($3$)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\Big(2[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}]]_{nn}-2i[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}]]_{nn}-i[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}]]_{nn}

\displaystyle~~~~~\quad-[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\left(K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right)^{2}]]_{nn}+i[[K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}]]_{nn}h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}

\displaystyle~~~~~\quad+[[\left(K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right)^{2}]]_{nn}h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}+4i[[K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}]]_{nn}h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}\Big).

(27)

Generally speaking, we can compute the
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
operators to obtain the corresponding eigenvalue corrections order by order.

IV
Special Case: Linear Perturbation

In this section, we apply the aforementioned setup to the most commonly encountered situation, namely, a linear perturbation:

H=H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}+qH^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}.

(28)

By expanding Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) in powers of
q
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bigg[H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}+qH^{\text{\tiny($1$)}},~{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}}q^{j}K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}\bigg]=i{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}}q^{k}K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($k$)}}-H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}},

(29)

\displaystyle\bigg[H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}+qH^{\text{\tiny($1$)}},~{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}}q^{j}K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}\bigg]=0.

(30)

By matching the coefficients of each order of
q
, the above equations become

\displaystyle\left[H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}},K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right]=i\left(K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}-H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\right)

(31)

\displaystyle\left[H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}},K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right]=0

(32)

\displaystyle\left[H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}},K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($\ell+1$)}}\right]+\left[H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}},K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($\ell$)}}\right]=iK_{1}^{\text{\tiny($\ell+1$)}}

(33)

\displaystyle\left[H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}},K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($\ell+1$)}}\right]+\left[H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}},K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($\ell$)}}\right]=0,

(34)

for
\ell>0
. Using Eqs. (
31
)-(
34
) and the fact that

\displaystyle[[AB]]_{nn}=\sum_{m}[[A]]_{nm}[[B]]_{mn},

(35)

it is straightforward to show that the first three corrections to the eigenvalues shown in Eq. (
27
) become

\displaystyle h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}

\displaystyle=[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}]]_{nn},

\displaystyle h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}]]_{nn},

(36)

\displaystyle h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($3$)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}]]_{nn}-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}]]_{nm}[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}]]_{mn}}{h_{m}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}-h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}},

where the gauge-dependent
K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($j$)}}
’s either canceled with each other or were replaced by the gauge-independent
K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($\ell$)}}
’s.

As a matter of fact, Eqs. (
31
)-(
34
) also allow the perturbative corrections in Eq. (
36
) to be expressed in terms of
H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}
. For instance, Eq. (
31
) renders

\displaystyle\begin{split}h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}&=[[K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}]]_{nn}\\
&=[[\left(H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}-i\left[H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}},K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right]\right)]]_{nn}\\
&=[[H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}]]_{nn}-i\left(h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}-h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right)[[K^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}]]_{nn}\\
&=[[H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}]]_{nn},\end{split}

(37)

where we have used
H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle=h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle
and
\left\llangle n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}=h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\left\llangle n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|
in the calculation. Analogously, straightforward calculations show that

\displaystyle\begin{split}h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}&=\left\llangle n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle,\\[8.53581pt]
h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}&=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\left\llangle n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\left|m^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle\left\llangle m^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle}{h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}-h_{m}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}},\\[2.84526pt]
h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($3$)}}&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\neq n\\
\ell\neq n\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\llangle n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\left|m^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle\left\llangle m^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\left|\ell^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle\left\llangle\ell^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle}{\left(h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}-h_{m}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right)\left(h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}-h_{\ell}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right)}-h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\left\llangle n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\left|m^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle\left\llangle m^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle}{\left(h_{n}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}-h_{m}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right)^{2}}.\end{split}

(38)

Some important ingredients for deriving the equations above can be found in Appendix
B
.

It is worth noting that if
H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}
is a Hermitian Hamiltonian, the above perturbation corrections can be reduced to the familiar results from the Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory by taking
G^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}=\mathbbm{1}
, so that
\left|n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle=\ket{\p{n}{0}}
and
\left\llangle n^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right|=\bra{\p{n}{0}}G^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}=\bra{\p{n}{0}}
.

Therefore, the proposed perturbation scheme not only reproduces the well-known results in the Hermitian regime, but also extends its validity into the non-Hermitian regime. (Note that the proposed scheme fails when
H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}
is at an EP because Eq. (
35
) is not valid in this case.)

V
A Non-Trivial Example

Here we apply our formalism to a toy model,

\displaystyle H=H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}+qH^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}+q^{2}H^{\text{\tiny($2$)}},

(39)

where
q
is the expansion parameter, and

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36958ptH^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}=h\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle H^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}=\alpha_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}i&0\\
0&-i\end{array}\right),~~~H^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}=\alpha_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),

where
h
,
\alpha_{1}
, and
\alpha_{2}
are fixed constants.

Solving Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) iteratively with the expansion Eq. (
20
), we have

\displaystyle K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}&-\dfrac{\alpha_{1}}{2h}+a_{2}\\
\dfrac{\alpha_{1}}{2h}+a_{2}&a_{1}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle K_{1}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{3}&a_{4}\\
a_{4}&-2ia_{2}\dfrac{\alpha_{1}}{h}+a_{3}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle K_{1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{1}^{2}-2\alpha_{2}h}{h}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle K_{0}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}a_{5}&-\dfrac{\alpha_{1}^{3}-3\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}h}{2h^{3}}+a_{6}\\
\dfrac{\alpha_{1}^{3}-3\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}h}{2h^{3}}+a_{6}&-2ia_{4}\dfrac{\alpha_{1}}{h}+a_{5}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle K_{1}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}

\displaystyle=-i\frac{\alpha_{1}^{3}-2h\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}{h^{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right),

where the
a_{i}
’s are constants that correspond to the gauge degrees of freedom, which contribute only to the amplitudes of the eigenstates but not the eigenvalues. Direct calculation using Eq. (
V
), the fact that
H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}
is Hermitian so that
G^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}=\mathbbm{1}
, and the unperturbed eigenstates from
H^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}
,

\left|+^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\end{array}\right),~~~\left|-^{\text{\tiny($0$)}}\right\rrangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-1\end{array}\right),

(56)

yields

h_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny($1$)}}=0,~~~h_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny($2$)}}=\mp q^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2h}-\alpha_{2}\right),~~~h_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny($3$)}}=0.

(57)

Notice that the
a_{i}
’s do not appear in the eigenvalue perturbation, as expected. As shown here, we provide a systematic method for computing the perturbations order by order.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we develop a perturbation scheme based on the geometric formalism
[
28
,
29
]
. In addition to perturbing the states, we also perturb the matrices
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
, which are related to their ascendant, the metric of the Hilbert space
G
. The perturbation in eigenstates can then be characterized by the recursion equations Eq. (
21
).

As the recursion relations of eigenstates take a form similar to the well-known Girard-Newton formulas, and the Girard-Newton formulas can be related to Bell polynomials, this observation inspires us to express each order of eigenstate correction in terms of dual non-commutative Bell polynomials. With these results, we further derive the recursion equation for eigenvalues Eq. (
25
). This result can be applied to any type of polynomial perturbation.

As a consistency check, when considering the linear perturbation of a Hermitian Hamiltonian, Eq. (
28
), the results in Eq. (
27
) and Eq. (
36
) are equivalent to those obtained from Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory. Nevertheless, for concreteness, a simple example with linear and quadratic perturbations is provided. It is straightforward to consider other polynomial perturbations as well.

In conclusion, the perturbation scheme presented in this paper can be applied to Hermitian systems,
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric systems, pseudo-Hermitian systems, and even beyond. However, the method generally does not work at exceptional points (EPs). On one hand, the non-trivial metric of Hilbert space of non-Hermitian systems becomes singular at the EPs
[
31
]
. On the other hand, we need to generalize our method for the case where the eigenvalues are degenerate and the eigenvectors coalesce.

Recent studies
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
on the pertubation around EPs in non-Hermitian system primarily relied on the series expansions of exact eigenstates to derive perturbed results, rather than perturbing the system from unperturbed eigenstates. To advance research in this area, the development of more general and systematic methods remains highly anticipated.

Lastly, since the resemblance to the Girard-Newton formulas has been incorporated as a novel element in this perturbation formalism, it would be interesting to explore whether this new ingredient can provide additional input to the quantum mechanics bootstrap program
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
.

Appendix A
Girard-Newton Formulas and Bell Polynomials

As mentioned in the main text, Eq. (
21
) takes a form similar to the so-called Girard-Newton formulas. For clarity,
we review the Girard-Newton formulas in the appendix.
Consider a monic polynomial
f(x)
of degree
k
with roots
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
\dots
,
x_{k}
,

f(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-x_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j-k}x^{j},~~\mathrm{with}~s_{0}=1,

(58)

where, in the second equality, the equation is rewritten using the
elementary symmetric polynomials
,

s_{j}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=(-1)^{j}\sum_{\resizebox{3729359}{}{$1\leq m_{1}<\dots<m_{j}\leq k$}}x_{m_{1}}\cdots x_{m_{j}},

(59)

The
s_{i}
’s form a basis for every symmetric polynomials in
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
\dots
,
x_{k}
. where as the
power sums

p_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}x^{i}_{j},

(60)

form another basis. The Girard-Newton formulas gives a relationship between the two bases as

s_{k}=-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{k}s_{k-j},~~~~k\geq 1.

(61)

Actually, one can express
s_{j}
’s (
j\neq 0
) in terms of
p_{j}
’s and
s_{0}
. For the purpose, the general form of
s_{j}
can be represented by using
the so-called
Bell polynomials

B_{j}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{j})
,

s_{j}=\frac{1}{j!}B_{j}(-p_{1},-(1)!p_{2},\dots,-(j-1)!p_{j})s_{0}.

(62)

We refer the reader to
[
40
]
and therein for the details of Bell polynomials and their non-commutative generalization mentioned here.
By definition, the Bell polynomials satisfy the recursion equation (we omit the argument
p_{1}
,
\dots
,
p_{k}
for simplicity),

B_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(k\atop j\right)B_{k-j}p_{j+1}~~\mathrm{with}~~B_{0}=1~~\mathrm{and}~~k\geq 0.

(63)

For the generalizing to the case when
p_{i}
’s become non-commutative variables, it is good to decompose a Bell polynomial
B_{k}
into the sum of
homogeneous Bell polynomials

B_{k,j}
,

B_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}B_{k,j},

(64)

where for a collection of integers
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle B_{k,j}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k})=k!\sum_{|m|=j\atop{||m||=k}}\prod_{r=1}^{k}\frac{1}{r!}\frac{(p_{m_{r}})^{m_{r}}}{m_{r}!},

(65)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~|m|\equiv m_{1}+m_{2}+\dots+m_{k},

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~||m||\equiv m_{1}+2m_{2}+\dots+km_{k}.

The definition of Bell polynomials, Eq. (
63
) can be generalized to non-commutative Bell polynomials
\mathbb{B}_{k}
by promoting
p_{j}
’s to some matrices
\mathbb{P}_{j}
’s,

\mathbb{B}_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(k\atop j\right)\mathbb{B}_{k-j}\mathbb{P}_{j+1}~~\mathrm{with}~~\mathbb{B}_{0}=\mathbbm{1}~~\mathrm{and}~~k\geq 0.

(66)

\mathbb{B}_{k}
is the
dual non-commutative Bell polynomials
defined in
[
40
]
.
The solution of
\mathbb{B}_{k}
to the recursion equation is

\mathbb{B}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{B}_{k,j},

(67)

where,

•

for
j=1
,

\hskip-91.04872pt\mathbb{B}_{k,1}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}\dots,\mathbb{P}_{k})=\left(\mathbb{P}_{1}\right)^{k},

(68)

•

for
1<j<k
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k,j}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}\dots,\mathbb{P}_{k})

(69)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{2}\dots m_{j}=1}^{k}\left(k-1\atop m_{2}\right)\left(m_{2}-1\atop m_{3}\right)\cdots\left(m_{j-1}\atop m_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\mathbb{P}_{k-m_{2}}\mathbb{P}_{m_{2}-m_{3}}\cdots\mathbb{P}_{m_{k-1}-m_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{m_{k}},

•

for
j=k
,

\hskip-99.58464pt\mathbb{B}_{k,k}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}\dots,\mathbb{P}_{k})=\mathbb{P}_{k}.

(70)

The dual non-commutative Bell polynomials can be used to represent the solution of perturbed eigenstates in the main text.
