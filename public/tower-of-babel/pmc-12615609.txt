Diurnal variation of wearable device-based heart rate variability in the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort study
Authors: Carsten Skarke, Wei Yang, Daohang Sha, Nicholas F Lahens, Tamara Isakova, Mark Unruh, Rajat Deo, Eunice Carmona-Powell, John H Holmes, Elaine Ficarra, Jing Chen, Jiang He, Hernan Rincon-Choles, Vallabh Shah, Chi-yuan Hsu, Amanda H Anderson, James P Lash, Mahboob Rahman, the CRIC Study Investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLittle is known about the prognostic value of continuous, out-of-clinic biometric monitoring of cardiovascular function in chronic kidney disease (CKD). In this study, a mean (±SD) of 50.3 ± 9.3 h of EKG recordings from wearable BioPatch devices was collected from 458 participants across seven Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort centers. Multivariable linear regression showed that diabetes was associated with 7.4 ms lower Standard Deviation of NN Intervals (SDNN) compared to non-diabetic participants (p = 0.001). Higher proteinuria (uPCR ≥ 0.2) was associated with 5.73 ms lower SDNN compared to lower proteinuria (p = 0.027). This study represents the largest dataset to date evaluating SDNN, a key heart rate variability metric using wearable EKG technology in CKD. Our findings highlight that specific clinical and demographic factors significantly influence HRV in this population. These results provide a critical foundation for future work to determine whether time-specific HRV metrics can serve as predictive biomarkers for cardiovascular risk and clinical outcomes in CKD.Subject terms: Medical research, Diseases, Cardiovascular diseases, Kidney diseases

Introduction

Kidney and heart function are regulated by the molecular circadian clock to adapt to time-specific changes in physiological needs over the 24-h cycle to maintain a healthy homeostasis1,2. Loss of time-dependent physiology associates, for example, with higher mortality in hypertensives with a non-dipping compared to a dipping blood pressure phenotype3. Misalignment of the sleep-wake rhythms in healthy volunteers in a nightshift model increased blood pressure and inflammatory markers while suppressing heart rate variability4 and blunted rhythmic gene expression profiles relevant for cellular immune response, apoptosis, cell proliferation and differentiation5.

The molecular circadian clock is challenging to study under real-world conditions because this oscillator and its downstream rhythmic effects are modulated by environmental and behavioral components, that is, light exposure, sleep, food intake, and social interaction for the most parts. Examining biological rhythms in wrist temperature in the UK Biobank6, we argued that roughly 60% of the 6 °C fluctuations within 24 h can be attributed to the circadian clock, while 25–30% are driven by behavioral and environmental factors. Since the latter factors are usually non-randomly distributed over the 24-h cycle and can vary significantly between individuals, they introduce substantial measurement noise. To address this, we developed the human chronobiome study paradigm, where, despite these limitations, we can discern time-specific phenotypes through phenomic and multi-omic outputs in small cohorts of volunteers under real-world conditions7.

Data streams from wearable devices are increasingly used in clinical medicine for risk prediction and stratification8 and create opportunities for clinical phenotyping and determining disease risk. However, little is known about the prognostic value of out-of-clinic biometric monitoring of heart health in chronic kidney disease (CKD)9. Under controlled clinical conditions, we observed a clear association between heart rate variability (HRV) and risk of mortality in QRS complexes over 10 s from a 12-lead resting electrocardiogram measured at baseline in 3245 Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) participants with a median 4.2 years of follow-up10. A particular aspect we are interested in is to understand how the diurnal variability observed in time-series measurements relates to future disease risk. In the CRIC study, we found that rhythmic profiling of 24-h blood pressure (BP) traces identified subgroups of CRIC participants at higher risk of cardiovascular death. CRIC participants with a history of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and absent cyclic components in their BP profile had at any time a 3.4-times higher risk of cardiovascular death than CVD patients with cyclic components present in their BP profile (HR: 3.38, 95% CI: 1.45–7.88, p = 0.005)11.

Recently, we demonstrated feasibility for out-of-clinic evaluation of HRV in a two-center cross-sectional sample of CRIC participants and healthy controls12. We found preliminary evidence of deconsolidated rhythmic organization of HRV where, for example, the time-of-day dependent modulation of RR-intervals was lower in participants compared to controls. This work provided the roadmap for the current larger cross-sectional sample of CRIC participants. The goal of the current study was to confirm feasibility of obtaining HRV data in a large patient population, and to study the association between HRV and its time-specific variability associated with demographics, disease phenotypes, and therapeutic drug use. This adds to the growing body10–12 of predictive and time-specific phenotyping in CRIC participants using real-world sampling strategies.

Results

The cross-sectional cohort consisted of 458 participants with valid BioPatch measurements collected from seven CRIC centers between 2019 and 2023. The study population had a mean age (and standard deviation) of 68.6 ± 9.7 years, while 45% were female, 49.3% had diabetes, and 30.8% had pre-existing cardiovascular diseases. The study participants were in general healthier than those who consented to participate in the CRIC Phase 4 (Supplementary Table 1). Each participant contributed a mean (±SD) of 50.3 ± 9.3 h of BioPatch EKG recordings, including the heart rate variability (HRV) output SDNN (Standard Deviation of Normal-to-Normal intervals). SDNN is the standard deviation of the RR intervals under conditions of a sinus rhythm. We split the multi-day data recordings of this cohort by SDNN tertiles into low SDNN (≤33.7 ms; n = 152), mid SDNN (33.8–48.8 ms; n = 153), and high SDNN categories (≥49.0 ms; n = 153). For comparison, the normative reference value clinically established for short-term SDNN is 50 ± 16 ms13 and low SDNN values have been associated with increased cardiovascular risk14. The distribution of age, sex, race, and ethnicity were similar between the tertiles (p > 0.05, Table 1).

Disease burden manifests in low heart rate variability

About half of the overall cohort had diabetes. A total of 65.8% of the participants in the low SDNN category were diabetic, compared to only 36.6% in the high SDNN category (Table 1). Hemoglobin A1C levels also differed significantly between participants in the lowest SDNN tertile (6.85 ± 1.69%), compared to the mid- and high-SDNN tertiles (6.18 ± 0.98% and 6.05 ± 1.10%, respectively, p < 0.0001, Fig. 1, Table 1). In the multivariable linear regression, the presence of diabetes was significantly associated with a 7.4 ms lower SDNN compared to non-diabetic participants (p = 0.001, Table 2) after adjusting for other covariates. Those who had a higher level of urine protein-creatinine-ratio (uPCR ≥ 0.2) had 5.73 ms lower SDNN compared to those who had a lower level of uPCR (<0.2) (p = 0.011, Table 2).

Participants with eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 had 5.32 ms higher SDNN compared to those with eGFR ≥ 45. However, this is mainly explained by a significant interaction between uPCR and eGFR (p value for test of interaction = 0.023). Participants with compromised kidney function and high level of proteinuria (eGFR < 45, uPCR ≥ 0.2) showed a substantially lower SDNN of 41.9 ms compared to 53.6 ms in participants with compromised renal function but low level of proteinuria (eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, uPCR < 0.2, Supplementary Table 2). This is reflective of how the presence of proteinuria in CKD potentiates the risk for CVD and underscores the emergence of proteinuria as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality15. Of note is that this effect was less pronounced in participants with a kidney function ≥45 mL/min/1.73 m2 (SDNN of 41.9 ms in participants with high level of proteinuria compared to 43.8 ms in participants with low level of proteinuria).

The findings using the rhythm-adjusted mean of SDNN (derived as MESOR [midline-estimating statistic of rhythm] from fitting a cosine function) were similar (Supplementary Fig. 1a, c, Supplementary Table 3a, Supplementary Table 4, with the schematic diagram presented in Supplementary Fig. 1d). Notably, participants with high level of proteinuria had a 5.15 ms lower SDNN MESOR compared to participants with low level of proteinuria (p = 0.027, Supplementary Table 4).

Disease-specific cyclical patterns in HRV

In the cosinor modeling (Supplementary Fig. 1d), men were over-represented in the high-SDNN amplitude tertile compared to the low- and medium-SDNN amplitude tertiles (62.7% vs. 53.3% and 49%, respectively; p = 0.047, Supplementary Table 3b). Since sex-specific modulation of short-term SDNN has been documented13, we used splines to identify differences in the shape of curves. We found that men compared to women displayed higher SDNN amplitude over the 24-h time frame (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2a) which replicates known physiology13. In the multivariable linear regression, females were associated with a 2.23 ms lower SDNN amplitude compared to males (p = 0.005, Table 2).

Disease-specific cyclical patterns in heart rate variability.

Estimated tracings for Standard Deviation of NN intervals (SDNN) from the mixed effects models show time-specific differences between (a) male and female participants, (b) participants with high and low proteinuria levels, for (c) diabetic status, (d) history of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), and (e) beta blocker use.

We observed differences in the proportion of participants with diabetes across the tertiles of SDNN amplitude (42.5% in high tertile vs. 59.9% and 45.8% in the low and middle tertiles; p = 0.006, Supplementary Table 3b). After the multivariable adjustment, participants with diabetes had a 1.45 ms lower SDNN amplitude compared to those without diabetes (p = 0.074, Table 2).

For time of peak SDNN levels, or acrophase (Supplementary Fig. 1d), we split the multi-day data by SDNN quartiles into early morning (0am–6am; n = 159), morning (6am–12pm; n = 188), afternoon (12pm–6pm, n = 74) and evening categories (6pm–12am; n = 37, Supplementary Table 3c). In the multivariable linear regression modeling for acrophase, BMI and beta blocker use were significantly associated with acrophase shifts in peak SDDN levels (phase advance by 0.11 h (6.6 min) per unit of BMI increase (p = 0.007); phase delay by 1.36 h for beta blocker users, p = 0.018; Table 2). Participants with diabetes showed a trend for a 1-h phase delay (p = 0.06). Though these relationships could be confounded by sleep times, chronotypes16, indication, and other factors, these findings could suggest an altered cardiac autonomic state and thus may introduce a rhythm-based risk factor for CVD17. Note that age was already adjusted in the model.

In the spline analysis, curve shape differences supportive of acrophase shifts were evident for sex (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2a). Participants with a higher level of uPCR or diabetes had overall lower SDNN compared to non-diabetic participants; however, with similar acrophases (Fig. 2b, c). The curve shape for participants with a history of cardiovascular diseases was significantly blunted compared to those who had no history of cardiovascular diseases (p = 0.01, Fig. 2d); however, the SDNN acrophase occurred at a similar time of day (around 5 AM). Finally, the spline analysis confirmed that the shape of SDNN-over-time curve was significantly different between beta blocker users and non-users (p = 0.0005, Fig. 2e).

Discussion

This study assembled the largest dataset to date of SDNN as an index for heart rate variability from out-of-clinic wearable devices in the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC). The cohort structure allowed us to confirm sex- and disease-specific modulation of heart rate variability, most notably for diabetes and HbA1C levels as well as for kidney damage (uPCR). Differences in the time-specific variance of heart rate variability were evident for sex, disease and therapeutic drug use, where beta blocker use was associated with elevated heart rate variability (other cardiovascular drugs failed to show an association with SDNN metrics). Notably, the increased SDNN observed in beta blocker users compared to non-users is in line with the beta-blocker-induced increase of parasympathetic activity, resultant in increased SDNN18. The value of the present study is that this sets the stage to explore in this CRIC population the predictiveness of HRV metrics for future CVD events and other future clinical outcomes.

The strengths of this study design include that the BioPatch devices were worn by CRIC participants during their activities of daily living, that participants wore these devices for at least two complete circadian cycles (over 48 h) to enhance detection and credibility of rhythmic features19, and that data were collected in a multi-center effort. These characteristics strengthen the applicability of our findings to the general CKD population and beyond.

SDNN values in the present study are consistent with normative data developed clinically from short-term EKG recordings13 and align well with our preliminary results based on 48-h recordings12, though at much higher precision. The heterogeneity of the study cohort allowed us to determine associations of SDNN summary and rhythmic readouts with demographic factors, renal function and comorbidities. Here, as expected, the significantly lower SDNN in CRIC participants with diabetes stood out as a risk factor. Diabetes per se has been associated with lower SDNN compared to healthy controls (effect size = ±0.65; 95%CI ± 0.83 to ±0.47; p < 0.001 with moderate heterogeneity of I2 = 65.1%) as reported in a meta-analysis of 25 studies with a sample size of ≈1400 with T2DM and ≈1600 healthy controls20. It is known that annual mortality rates increase dramatically as SDNN decreases. This is described in a similarly powered study in chronic heart failure from the UK using 24-h ambulatory EKGs determined risk categories with the mortality rising from 5.5% for patients with SDNN > 100 ms to 12.7% for SDNN between 50 and 100 ms, up to 51.4% for SDNN < 50 ms21. The mean of SDNN 50.3 ± 9.3 in the present study is situated right at the threshold where the upward relationship between lower SDNN and increased risk of mortality (13%) transitions into the much steeper association of very low SDNN and high mortality risk (51%). In the context of the present study, the ~4 ms the SDNN was significantly lower in CRIC participants with diabetes potentially exposes them to a disproportionately higher risk of mortality. Prognostic thresholds for SDNN monitored over 24 h, (<50 ms unhealthy, 50–100 ms compromised health, >100 ms healthy), have been proposed by Shaffer and Ginsberg22 based on the mortality rate of patients surviving acute myocardial infarction14. Less clear, however, is the clinical relevance of smaller SDNN differences. 19-year-olds living with T1DM for 10 years displayed a reduced cardiac autonomic nervous activation quantified as a 10 ms lower SDNN compared to healthy controls23.

The shape of heart rate variability over the course of 24 h allows for the time-specific characterization of disease risk phenotypes. Our data highlights, for example, that in CRIC participants with diabetes SDNN levels were lower over the entire 24-h day compared to CRIC participants without diabetes. In contrast, participants with CVD showed reduced SDNN levels primarily during the nighttime hours compared to participants without CVD. This time-specific phenomenon has been associated with increased risk for stroke24 and cardiovascular events25. Acrophase is a dynamic variable which can shift to adapt to environmental changes during transmeridian travel, for example, or can be desynchronized by prolonged misalignment of sleep-wake rhythms with the environment as is the case in long-term shift work. For rotating shift workers, the SDNN showed a phase delay of 3–4 h and a shorter phase angle to physical activity peaking in the afternoon hours26. This leads to a temporally misaligned autonomic nervous system. This has been described for healthy volunteers exposed to simulated shift work schedules in a controlled laboratory setting where circadian misalignment was associated with 5.6 mmHg higher sleep-time systolic blood pressure, 8–15% lowered wake-time cardiac vagal modulation, and 7% decreased 24-h urinary excretion rate of epinephrine (without affecting the overall 24-h urinary norepinephrine excretion rate)4.

This sets the stage for future studies in this CRIC subpopulation to parse how time-specific features of heart rate variability associate with renal function, CVD events, and mortality, which are not yet feasible due to limited follow-up time. Notably, we established the framework to address this approach in the UK Biobank where we were able to predict the future onset of 73 diseases by discerning rhythmic features in wrist body temperature traces collected with wearable fitness trackers under the noisy conditions of daily life6.

Limitations for the present study include that this is a correlative study with variables limited to demographic factors, comorbidity, and renal function. Given the observational nature of the study, residual confounding needs to be considered. The short follow up period of just two years for some of the participants does not yet allow association studies between HRV parameters and clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, we established a paradigm to leverage wearable digital health technology to monitor time-dependent cardiovascular health in participants with CKD. This platform is essential to develop clinically actionable, digital biomarkers to improve management of CVD in CKD.

Methods

Multi-center study design to deploy wearable devices

The design, inclusion and exclusion criteria of the CRIC study have been published previously27,28. In brief, participants where included if they had not reached ESRD (end-stage renal disease) and consented to participate in Phase 4 of the CRIC study starting in 2018, but excluded if they were a resident in a nursing home, non-ambulatory, equipped with heart pacemaker or automatic defibrillator, presented with a skin rash or irritation at the site of wearable placement, Modified Mini-Mental State Examination <80, or ESRD. This study used the Zephyr BioPatch (Zephyr Technology, Annapolis, MD, USA), distributed by Medtronic Corporation (Minneapolis, MN, USA). This research-grade wearable device records cardiovascular, and respiratory activity. The target observational time was set to 48 h to cover two consecutive 24-h circadian cycles in order to enhance the detection of biological rhythms. Participants received a plug-in charging cradle and were instructed to remove the BioPatch device from its chest-mounted holder after ~24 h of use to recharge the device for approximately two hours followed by inserting it back into the BioPatch holder on the chest for continued recordings. Positioning of the two EKG snap electrodes to hold the BioPatch holder in place followed standard EKG guidelines for V1 and V2, i.e. V1 corresponds to the right fourth intercostal space; and V2 corresponds to the left fourth intercostal space. Prior to application of the snap electrodes skin was cleaned with rubbing alcohol and shaved if necessary.

Study participants in comparison to CRIC study population

We first divided the raw data into hourly intervals. Within each one-hour interval, we calculated the average indices of SDNN as the metric of HRV for this study. SDNN was defined as the Standard Deviation of Normal-to-Normal (NN) Intervals measured in the EKG trace from normal R-R intervals in a rolling window of 300 beats (~5 min). Quality control led to the exclusion of n = 125 participant recordings. A majority of those (n = 86) had episodes of ≥5-hour consecutive missingness of HRV traces (Supplementary Fig. 2) which compromised confidence in the analysis of diurnal heart rate variability. We compared the characteristics of study participants with those who consented to participate in CRIC Phase 4 but were not included in this study (Supplementary Table 1). The current study population is a little bit healthier than those not included in the study. The mean age of the included participants was 68.6 years, compared to 69.6 years for those not included. 49.3% of the included participants had diabetes, compared to 55.5% of those not included. 89.9% of the included participants had hypertension, compared to 94.3% of those not included. However, the kidney function measures were similar, with a mean eGFR of 51.1 in the included participants and 52.4 in those not included. The median uPCR levels were 0.17 for the included participants and 0.2 for those not included. In addition, the Hispanic ethnicity group is overrepresented in the current study (16.4% vs. 11.7%). Finally, the study participants had a higher history of cardiovascular disease (69.2% vs. 58.7%) compared to those not included in the study.

Data analysis

We modeled the SDNN using two approaches. Our first approach was to implement cosinor analysis for rhythm detection29 to quantify MESOR (midline-estimating statistic of rhythm), amplitude (extent of the rhythm’s variation), and acrophase (time of the peak in the rhythm) (Supplementary Fig. 1d) for each individual. The acrophase delineates where on the 24-h clock time the peak of a sinusoidal curve lands. Since CRIC participants are monitored in the outpatient environment and entrained to a 24-h rhythm, period in this model is fixed at 24 h. We compared the participants between different categories defined by the tertiles of the average SDNN, MESOR and MESOR amplitude. Since heart rate variability typically reach peak levels throughout the early morning hours12,26, we also compared study participants in four categories defined by the SDNN acrophase values: early morning (0AM-6AM), morning (6AM–12PM), afternoon (12PM–6PM) and evening hours (6PM–12AM). We then fit separate multivariable linear regression models to identify factors that are associated with average SDNN, SDNN MESOR, amplitude, and phase. The models included the following covariates: age, sex, race/ethnicity, diabetic status, hypertension, history of cardiovascular disease, smoking status, BMI, eGFR, uPCR, and beta blocker use.

The second approach was to fit linear mixed effects models on the hourly SDNN data directly. In the model, we used restricted cubic splines for the time variable to allow a flexible diurnal pattern within the day instead of forcing it to be an exact sinusoidal shape. The model included a random intercept at an individual level to account for repeated measures from the same participant. We adjusted for the same covariates as in the first approach. Additionally, we included the interaction between a specific categorical covariate (e.g., diabetes) and all spline terms for time to estimate group-specific daily SDNN patterns and test if the shape differs between groups.

In sensitivity analyses, we varied the length of the time interval (e.g., 30-min and 10-min time windows instead of an hourly window) for summarizing the raw data and evaluated how sensitive the analyses were with respect to the different time intervals, and found similar results.
