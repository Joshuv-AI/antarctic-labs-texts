Riociguat as inpatient rescue therapy for acute decompensation of inoperable chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension

Background

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a progressive and under-recognised cause of pulmonary hypertension (PH), characterised by persistent obstruction of pulmonary arteries by organised thrombi. This chronic obstruction leads to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, right ventricular strain, and ultimately right heart failure. Although the incidence of CTEPH after acute pulmonary embolism (PE) is relatively low (0.5–3%), the burden of the disease is significant given the high morbidity if untreated. Diagnosis often relies on a ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan, followed by confirmation with right heart catheterisation (RHC) and pulmonary angiography.1

Pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) is potentially curative and significantly improves outcomes, yet not all patients are surgical candidates due to the distribution of thrombi or the presence of comorbidities. For inoperable patients, Riociguat, a soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) stimulator, has been shown to improve 6 min walk distance (6MWD), WHO functional class, and haemodynamic parameters.2 3 However, its application in acutely decompensated inpatients remains sparsely reported.

Case presentation

A man in his 70s, a retired coal miner with a history of asbestos exposure and no history of smoking, presented to the local emergency department with progressive dyspnoea and syncope. His medical history included Mobitz Type II atrioventricular block with a permanent pacemaker in situ, polymyalgia rheumatica, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), vitamin B12 deficiency, hypertension and non-obstructive coronary artery disease.

Investigations

A CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA) revealed multiple bilateral lobar pulmonary emboli (PE). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed a D-shaped left ventricle with reduced left ventricular systolic function, with an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 35–45%, a dilated right ventricle with mildly reduced systolic function, a severely dilated right atrium, and a dilated inferior vena cava (IVC) with mild tricuspid regurgitation (TR). The right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) was estimated at 86 mm Hg+RAP. He was discharged on rivaroxaban 20 mg once daily and referred to the heart failure, cardiology and respiratory outpatient clinics.

At follow-up, the patient reported WHO functional class III–IV dyspnoea and fatigue, with a gradual decline since his initial diagnosis. He had commenced long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT): 1 L/min at rest for 16 hours/day, 4 L/min with exertion indoors via concentrator, and 8 L/min with exertion outdoors via cylinder. A cardiac MRI showed an LVEF of 55% and a right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF) of 23%, with severely dilated right heart chambers and severely impaired right ventricular function, raising strong suspicion of PH. Outpatient investigations were organised including pulmonary function tests (PFTs), a V/Q scan, RHC and a referral to the Irish National Pulmonary Hypertension Unit was made.

PFTs demonstrated a forced vital capacity (FVC) 119%; forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁) 114%; FEV₁/FVC 75.71%; and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) 93%. A V/Q scan demonstrated extensive bilateral mismatched perfusion defects, consistent with CTEPH (figure 1).

Prior to review at the National Pulmonary Hypertension Unit, the patient re-presented locally with worsening dyspnoea, productive cough with haemoptysis and progressive bilateral lower limb oedema.

Physical examination revealed normal systolic blood pressure and heart rate, with an oxygen saturation of 89% on 4 L/min via nasal cannula. Cardiac auscultation identified a holosystolic murmur. Clinical signs of heart failure were present, including elevated jugular venous pressure (JVP) and bilateral lower limb pitting oedema. Abdominal examination was unremarkable. His weight had increased from an estimated dry weight of 81 kg to 90 kg.

Arterial blood gas analysis on 4 L/min oxygen identified pH 7.51, PaO₂ 6.79 kPa, PaCO₂ 4.03 kPa and SpO₂ 85%. Full blood count, white cell count (WCC) and C-reactive protein (CRP) were within normal limits. Serum N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) was elevated at 7020 pg/mL. Repeat TTE demonstrated a severely dilated right atrium and ventricle, increased RVSP, moderate TR and a non-collapsing IVC. A repeat CTPA showed chronic right lower lobe subsegmental embolism unchanged from previous imaging, with findings in keeping with chronic thromboembolic disease.

Differential diagnosis

The patient presented with exertional dyspnoea and a syncopal episode. Acute PE was initially suspected, and CTPA confirmed bilateral lobar emboli, prompting anticoagulation. However, the findings on transthoracic echocardiography were suggestive of chronic pulmonary hypertension. TTE demonstrated a D-shaped left ventricle, severe dilation of the right atrium and right ventricle, right ventricular hypertrophy with depressed systolic function, and moderate tricuspid regurgitation. The RVSP was estimated at 86 mm Hg+RAP. These findings triggered further investigation.

V/Q scan revealed multiple mismatched segmental perfusion defects with preserved ventilation, characteristic of CTEPH. Right heart failure was considered given the reduced RV systolic function and chamber enlargement, and the patient was treated with diuretics. However, the underlying mechanism was most likely secondary to chronic pulmonary vascular obstruction rather than isolated cardiac pathology.

At this stage, treatment was initiated and follow-up with right heart catheterisation was organised to confirm the diagnosis of CTEPH.

Treatment

Despite optimisation of diuretic therapy, the patient’s respiratory status deteriorated requiring high-flow oxygen support via AIRVO (FiO₂ 83%, flow rate 50 L/min) for 14 days. The case was discussed with the National Pulmonary Hypertension Unit at the Mater Misericordiae University Hospital. Riociguat was initiated as an inpatient, rescue treatment at a starting dose of 0.5 mg three times daily (three times a day) and titrated fortnightly to a maximum of 1.5 mg three times a day, with close monitoring of blood pressure, renal function, and oxygenation.

The patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement with Riociguat therapy within 72 hours of commencing treatment. He was successfully weaned off AIRVO and transitioned back to his baseline LTOT: 1 L/min at rest, 4 L/min with exertion indoors, and 8 L/min outdoors. NT-proBNP decreased from 7020 pg/mL to 3720 pg/mL. He tolerated the up-titration well, with no adverse events observed. He was discharged, and follow-up was arranged at the National Pulmonary Hypertension Unit.

Outcome and follow-up

Post-discharge, he underwent further evaluation including RHC, which confirmed a haemodynamic profile consistent with CTEPH (mean right atrial pressure (RAP) 11 mm Hg; right ventricular pressure (RVP) 82/5 mm Hg; pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) 70/38 mm Hg; mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) 40 mm Hg; pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) 8 mm Hg; pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR): 10 Wood units) (table 1). Pulmonary angiographic findings were also compatible with CTEPH, demonstrating bilateral dilatation of the main pulmonary arteries. On the right, there was marked narrowing of the right lower lobe pulmonary artery consistent with chronic thromboembolism, with multilevel proximal segmental occlusions. These lesions resulted in secondary sublobar and segmental perfusion defects throughout the right lung, most pronounced in the lower lobe, with less extensive abnormalities seen in the right upper lobe. On the left, segmental stenoses were identified in branches to both the upper and lower lobes, with complete occlusions in the left upper lobe and left lung base. These abnormalities resulted in poor peripheral lung perfusion, with multilevel segmental and subsegmental deficits most marked at the bilateral lung bases.

The case was subsequently reviewed in a multidisciplinary setting at the National Pulmonary Hypertension Unit and in consultation with the Royal Papworth team in the United Kingdom. Given insufficient surgically accessible thromboembolic burden and coexisting left ventricular dysfunction, the patient was deemed unsuitable for PEA or balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA). Riociguat was continued as medical therapy for inoperable CTEPH, with ongoing specialist follow-up.

Discussion

PEA is the treatment of choice and remains the gold standard for patients with operable CTEPH. It can significantly improve pulmonary haemodynamics, with reductions in PVR of up to 65%, and enhance functional capacity. Outcomes in experienced centres are excellent, with perioperative mortality below 2.5% and 3-year survival rates approaching 90%.3

For patients with inoperable disease or persistent/recurrent pulmonary hypertension following PEA, BPA is now an established treatment option. BPA is particularly useful for distal pulmonary artery obstructions not amenable to surgery and has been shown to improve haemodynamics (PVR reduction of 49–66%), right ventricular function, and exercise capacity.3

Medical therapy also plays a role in selected patients. Riociguat, a sGC stimulator, was evaluated in a phase three randomised controlled trial in patients with inoperable CTEPH or with persistent/recurrent pulmonary hypertension after PEA. After 16 weeks, it significantly improved 6MWD and reduced PVR by 31% compared with placebo.2 Similarly, subcutaneous treprostinil improved 6MWD at 24 weeks in patients receiving high-dose therapy, as demonstrated in another phase three trial.4

Management of CTEPH is increasingly shifting toward a multimodal, individualised approach. Expert centres integrate surgical, interventional, imaging and medical expertise to develop tailored treatment plans for each patient, with clear outcome monitoring.5

There is currently no clinical trial data evaluating the safety, efficacy, or routine use of riociguat as inpatient rescue therapy for acute decompensation in inoperable CTEPH. However, its potential role in acute settings has been reported in the literature. This includes a case report describing successful weaning of two critically ill neonates with severe pulmonary hypertension from inhaled nitric oxide after failure of conventional therapy, suggesting a possible application in acute settings.6 Furthermore, a randomised trial in patients with pulmonary hypertension and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) found that a single dose of riociguat was well tolerated and led to improvements in stroke volume and right ventricular dimensions, although without significant change in mean pulmonary artery pressure.7

This case supports the emerging view that riociguat, while typically initiated in stable outpatients, may be effectively and safely used in acutely decompensated inpatients with CTEPH. Initiating riociguat in this context requires multidisciplinary input and vigilant monitoring due to the potential for hypotension or worsening right heart failure. In our case, the patient’s Pro-BNP reduction and improved functional capacity reinforce its potential role even during acute decompensation, despite optimal diuretic and supportive therapy.

Learning points

Riociguat may offer a valuable therapeutic option in selected inpatients with inoperable CTEPH. This case highlights its potential utility to rescue acute decompensation of group 4 pulmonary hypertension, beyond current outpatient guidelines and underscores the importance of early specialist involvement and individualised management in pulmonary hypertension care.
