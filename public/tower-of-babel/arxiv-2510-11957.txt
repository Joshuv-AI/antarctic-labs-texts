Furstenberg systems of certain sequences of superpolynomial growth Thanks: The research project is implemented in the framework of H.F.R.I call “3rd Call for H.F.R.I.’s Research Projects to Support Faculty Members & Researchers” (H.F.R.I. Project Number: 24979).
Andreu Ferré Moragues and Andreas Koutsogiannis

Abstract
. We give examples of sequences defined by smooth functions of intermediate growth, and we study the Furstenberg systems that model their statistical behavior. In particular, we show that the systems are Bernoulli. We do so by studying exponential sums that reflect the strong equidistribution properties of said sequences. As a by-product of our approach, we also get some convergence results.

1.
Introduction

The seminal proof of Szemerédi’s theorem using ergodic theory due to Furstenberg
[
12
]
initiated a new method to study problems in combinatorics: through the use of his, now classical, correspondence principle, one can translate the problem of finding patterns in large subsets
E\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}
into recurrence properties on a measure preserving system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
for a given set
A\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mu(A)>0
.

It was soon realized that one can actually use a slight variant of the aforementioned principle to study statistical properties of bounded sequences
a:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
(and more generally, properties of finite collections of bounded sequences
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
) via convenient dynamical models.

For example (cf.
[
13
, Theorem 3.13]
), the statistical behavior of the sequence
(n^{2}\alpha)_{n}
on the torus
\mathbb{T}
can be modeled via the measure preserving system
({\mathbb{T}}^{2},m_{\text{Leb}},S)
, where
S:{\mathbb{T}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
is given by

S(x,y):=(x+\alpha,y+x),\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{T}}.

Indeed, given
f\in C({\mathbb{T}}),
in order to study the statistical properties of the sequence
a(n):=f(n^{2}\alpha),
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{r}a(n+h_{i})=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}g(S^{h_{i}}(x,y))\ dm_{\text{Leb}}(x,y),

where
g(x,y)=f(y)
, and the equality above holds for all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all
h_{1},\dots,h_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
This is an example of what we will call a
Furstenberg system
(see Definition
2.2
below). The study of such systems is especially important and has received a lot of attention recently in view of the existing connection with problems in analytic number theory. More precisely, one can see Chowla’s conjecture as the claim that the Liouville function
\lambda
can be modelled with a Bernoulli system, or that the Möbius function
\mu
can be modeled by a direct product of a procyclic system and a Bernoulli system (see
[
5
,
20
,
21
]
). What is known currently are some partial results; we refer the reader to
[
9
,
10
,
14
]
.

There is also recent work on the topic of Furstenberg systems, showcasing the importance of this topic in recent years (see for example
[
11
,
16
]
). These show the attention that Furstenberg systems have received, highlighting their relevance in the development of ergodic theory, especially taking into account, as we mentioned, its very important and strong connections with number theory (see also
[
19
]
for its connection with Sarnak’s conjecture), although they are of interest for its own sake as well.

Expanding on the aforementioned connection, Frantzikinakis
[
7
]
, the work of whom is the main source of inspiration for the present article, dealt with iterates that come from smooth functions in a Hardy field (in fact, in the examples under consideration, the functions are all of the
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
class). In particular
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
determines the structure of Furstenberg systems with respect to sequences of polynomial growth rate.

By this we mean that the sequence grows slower than some
(n^{k})_{n},

.

In this article, we will go in a slightly different but also very interesting (and less explored) direction: we will look into iterates of the form
(n^{\log^{c}n})_{n}
and
(\alpha\lfloor n^{\log^{c}n}\rfloor)_{n},
for

and

As is easy to check, the sequence
(n^{\log^{c}n}=\exp(\log^{c+1}n))_{n}
grows faster than any polynomial, but it is of subexponential growth because of the logarithm. In contrast with Frantzikinakis’s results, the systems we obtain are all Bernoulli (Theorem
1.1
). This latter result follows through the study of exponential sums and a strong equidistribution result on a multidimensional torus of shifts of the original sequence (Theorem
1.4
) which also helps us derive some corollaries with a flavor of topological dynamics (Corollary

Dealing with sequences of intermediate growth has remained a widely open topic (cf. the open problems in
[
6
]
and
[
8
]
for such sequences of iterates) precisely because traditional approaches and many crucial tools that one usually uses to show more general convergence (and consequently recurrence results), such as the ubiquitous van der Corput trick, can no longer be used. Hence, one has to consider alternative approaches, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 1.10 (i)]
, where Frantzikinakis showed that for distinct
c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\in(0,1/2)
the sequences
({\left\lfloor n^{\log^{c_{1}}n}\right\rfloor})_{n},\ldots,({\left\lfloor n^{\log^{c_{r}}n}\right\rfloor})_{n}
, are jointly ergodic for every ergodic nilsystem. He achieved this via the main result on joint ergodicity of sequences (
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
) which allows one to prove the property of joint ergodicity by showing that said sequences enjoy the equidistribution and seminorm estimates properties. Because in his setup, the underlying system is a nilsystem, the seminorm estimates trivally hold, and because of the fact that the
c_{i}
’s are distinct, he can trivially apply
[
4
, Theorem 2]
to deduce the required equidistribution.

On the other hand, one should be careful when aiming for more general results. As it turns out, the choice and properties of the sequence of intermediate growth in this paper is important, as
[
3
, Theorem 1.6]
shows that even equidistribution might fail when one considers general Hardy field iterates of intermediate growth.

1.3.
Structure of the paper

The structure of the paper is as follows. In section
2
, we give the necessary background that is required to follow the rest of the paper: some basics on measure preserving systems, the relevant definitions for one of the main objects of study: Furstenberg systems, and also some necessary basic results on Hilbert space splittings and equidistribution of real-valued sequences on tori.

Section
3
is the bulk of the paper and concerns the proofs of some intermediate results on bounds of derivatives of the functions of interest. These are based on the ideas
of the proof of Theorem 2 of
[
4
]
, but they have to be modified and adapted so that we can actually deal with the linear combinations that naturally appear when we start considering the averages that determine a Furstenberg system.

The aforementioned results help us prove, in Section

, the main exponential sum theorem (Theorem
1.4
) which in turn implies the equidistribution properties of our sequences (Theorem
1.3
). Once these results are established, we can use them to characterize the Furstenberg systems associated to the corresponding sequences with intermediate growth (Theorem

1.4.
Open problems

It is natural to consider, as is done in
[
7
]
, the study of Furstenberg systems associated to sequences of the form
d(n):=f(T^{\lfloor G(n)\rfloor}x)
, where
x\in X
is a “typical” point of a measure preserving system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
,
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
and
0<c<1/2
.

In order to be able to obtain a result similar to
[
7
, Theorem 1.6]
or
[
7
, Corollary 1.7]
one would need to have seminorm control for averages of the form

\lim_{N\to\infty}\E_{n\in[N]}f(T^{\lfloor G(n+h_{1})\rfloor}x)\cdots f(T^{\lfloor G(n+h_{r})\rfloor}x).

This is currently beyond our reach, as the sequence
(\lfloor G(n)\rfloor)_{n}
grows too fast for the van der Corput trick to be helpful, and we cannot even attempt to prove a strong stationarity type of property for them by reducing the problem to nilsystems.

However, given the independence that shifts of the function
G(x)
enjoy, and the strong equidistribution results we were able to prove, it is natural to expect the result to be true, so we leave it as a conjecture.

.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be a measure preserving system,

is such that the Furstenberg system associated to

If one were able to prove Conjecture

2.
Background

2.1.
Measure preserving systems

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
be a probability space. Let
T_{1},\dots,T_{r}:X\to X
be commuting invertible measure preserving transformations. We will say that the tuple
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T_{1},\dots,T_{r})
is a measure preserving system. Oftentimes we will only use single transformation measure preserving systems, i.e., we will have
r=1
. Recall that a system is ergodic if the only sets invariant under the action generated by
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{r}\rangle
have either full or zero measure.

For other common concepts in ergodic theory, such as conditional expectations, factor, Bernoulli system, joining, entropy, and disjoint systems we refer the reader to Section 2 of
[
15
]
.

2.2.
Furstenberg systems

The purpose of this subsection is to present some basic facts about one of our main objects of study in this paper: Furstenberg systems. We will follow the presentation in
[
7
]
adapted to our slightly simpler context.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
a:{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a sequence. We say that
a

admits correlations
if there exists a subsequence of increasing natural numbers
(N_{k})_{k}
such that the limits

\lim_{k\to\infty}\E_{n\in[N_{k}]}a^{\varepsilon_{1}}(n+h_{1})\cdots a^{\varepsilon_{r}}(n+h_{r})

exist for all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, all distinct
h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all
(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{r})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}\setminus\{\vec{0}\}
.

Even though Furstenberg systems can be defined over different sequences of intervals, we will only be interested in those arising from the standard intervals
[1,N]
and subsequences of those.

Remark
.

It is worth pointing out that if
a
admits correlations, then, by letting
S:(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
be the shift map
S(y_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}:=(y_{n+1})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
one can show that the weak* limit
\lim_{k\to\infty}\E_{n\in[N_{k}]}\delta_{S^{n}a}
exists.

We are now in position to give the definition of Furstenberg system.

Definition 2.2

(Furstenberg system of a sequence)
.

Let
a:{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a sequence that admits correlations on
([N_{k}])_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Put
X:=(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
, equipped with the product topology,
\mathcal{B}:=\text{Borel}(X)
, and
S:X\to X
be the shift map as defined above. Let
\mu:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\E_{n\in[N_{k}]}\delta_{T^{n}a}
be a weak* limit point of the sequence of measures
\left(\E_{n\in[N]}\delta_{T^{n}a}\right)_{n}
. We say that the system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
is a
Furstenberg system
of
a
on
([N_{k}])_{k}
.

We say that the Furstenberg system of
a
is
unique
if
a
admits correlations on the traditional intervals
([N])_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Remarks
.

(1) If we are given a sequence
a:{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we simply extend it in any way to a sequence
a:{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
; the measure
\mu
is not affected at all by this extension.

(2) It is shown in
[
2
]
that under some natural restrictions, such Furstenberg systems are unique, in the sense that they do not depend on the construction of the measure space, but are rather characterized by the statistical properties of the sequences.

We will be especially interested in determining when the measure
\mu
is the product measure
\bigotimes_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}m_{\text{Leb}}
. To this effect, we have the following result, which is a consequence of the Stone-Weierstrass theorem.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
a:{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a sequence and
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be a Furstenberg system for
a
. Let
F:X\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be given by
F(x):=x(0).
Then, the measure
\mu
is determined by the values:

\int_{X}T^{h_{1}}F^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots T^{h_{r}}F^{\varepsilon_{r}}\ d\mu,

for all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, all distinct
h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and all
(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{r})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
.

Proof.

The polynomials
z^{n}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1},n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
generate a
C^{*}
algebra of
C(\mathbb{S}^{1})
that separates points. Therefore, with our notation above, we have that the family

\mathcal{A}:=\{T^{h_{1}}F^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots T^{h_{r}}F^{\varepsilon_{r}}:r\in{\mathbb{N}},\text{ distinct }h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}},(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{r})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}\}

generates a
C^{*}
-algebra that separates points in
X
.

Now, by the Stone-Weierstrass theorem, the
C^{*}
-algebra generated by these functions is exactly equal to
C(X)
, and by the Riesz-Fischer theorem, the measure
\mu
is determined by its values on
C(X)
. Thus, by linearity of
\mu
, knowing its values on
\mathcal{A}.

∎

Proposition
2.3
allows us to deduce a convenient characterization of sequences whose Furstenberg system is unique and Bernoulli.

Corollary 2.4

Let

2.4.
Equidistribution

We recall now some basic definitions of equidistribution that we shall use in the sequel.

Definition 2.8
.

We say that a sequence
(x_{n}=(x_{n}^{1},\ldots,x_{n}^{r}))_{n}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{r}
is

It is important to note that in this result and the next ones, we will keep assuming that we can work with

Before giving a proof of Proposition

3.1.1.
Examples

Although it will perhaps not be fully apparent in the proof, the driving force behind the computations is, first and foremost the sum of the integer coefficients. If this sum is not
0
, regardless of the shifts (assuming, as we can, that this sum of coefficients

Example 3.6

Suppose we have a function

of the form

Then, clearly

since

G(x+1)\leq 1.1G(x)
for

some objective
x_{0}
(it only really depends on our “choice” of

and

and of

will also depend on

Combining all our previous comments and realizations, it is

\displaystyle F(x)\geq G(x)-3.3G(x)+3G(x)=0.7G(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}G(x).

This would be a good enough lower bound, even though with some care one can improve on the

The main reason we cannot use this argument right away in the proof of Proposition

Next, we illustrate the sorts of different behaviors that arise immediately when the sum of the coefficients is null (
\Sigma=0
). We first show an example of coefficients we will label as “balanced”.

Example 3.7

Consider the following expression for a “balanced”

Then, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle F(x)=\int_{x}^{x+1}\int_{t}^{t+1}G^{\prime\prime}(s)\ ds\ dt\geq\int_{x}^{x+1}G^{\prime\prime}(t)\ dt\geq G^{\prime\prime}(x),

given that any derivative of

This example showcases how the coefficients can “conspire” to force

to have slower growth. In fact, this argument could be applied straight away for the “balanced” cases of Proposition
3.5
. The issue is actually being able to identify when the coefficients are or are not such that they admit an integral representation like the one above (more on this in an example below).

On the other hand, it is possible for the sum of coefficients to be
0
, and such that a single application of the mean value theorem is enough.

Example 3.8

Suppose we have
F(x):=-G(x+2)+3G(x+1)-2G(x)

Then

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-G(x+2)+3G(x+1)-2G(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(G(x+2)-G(x+1))+2(G(x+1)-G(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-G^{\prime}(\xi_{x})+2G^{\prime}(\tau_{x})\geq 2G^{\prime}(x)-G^{\prime}(x+2)\geq 0.9G^{\prime}(x),

where we used the mean value theorem to find the

and

and the fact that
G^{\prime}(x+2)\leq 1.1G^{\prime}(x)

(we used the mean value theorem to find

Next, we would once again use that

Note that, once more, the issue with the generalization is the uniformity that we would need for these bounds for the derivatives of

It is also important to notice that the distances in the jumps provided by the

Example 3.9

Assume that

F(x):=G(x+4)-3G(x+1)+2G(x).

We have

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(x+4)-3G(x+1)+2G(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(x+4)-G(x+1)-2(G(x+1)-G(x))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 3G^{\prime}(x+1)-2G^{\prime}(x+1)=G^{\prime}(x+1),

once again by using the increasing properties of

Moreover, what determined who “won” in the battle of signs, depended both on the coefficients and the length between the shifts of the

However, things can be very different even if they appear similar. Namely, the “balanced” behavior can also be achieved, even if the coefficients do not look, a priori, like

Example 3.10

Consider the function
F(x):=2G(x+2)-3G(x)+G(x-4).

Then

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2G(x+2)-3G(x)+G(x-4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(G(x+2)-G(x))-(G(x)-G(x-4))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{x}^{x+2}G^{\prime}(t)\ dt-\int_{x-4}^{x}G^{\prime}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle\int_{x-2}^{x}G^{\prime}(t)\ dt-\int_{x-4}^{x-2}G^{\prime}(t)\ dt+\int_{x-2}^{x}G^{\prime}(t)\ dt-\int_{x-4}^{x-2}G^{\prime}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle 2\int_{x}^{x+2}\int_{t-4}^{t-2}G^{\prime\prime}(s)\ ds\ dt\geq 4\int_{x}^{x+2}G^{\prime\prime}(t-4)\ dt\geq 8G^{\prime\prime}(x-4)\geq 4G^{\prime\prime}(x).

In order to prove Proposition
3.5
, overcoming the difficulties mentioned above, we begin by first showing the following result, which can be interpreted as a suitable change of basis.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
F
be as defined above and let
s\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
. Let
\Delta
denote the finite difference operator, i.e.,
\Delta H(x):=H(x+1)-H(x)
for a given function
H:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
. Then, there exists some polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
such that

F^{(s)}(x)=p(\Delta)G^{(s)}(x).

Proof.

We first begin with a reduction: without loss of generality we will assume that all the
h_{i}\geq 0
. We can do this by shifting the sum by the smallest of the
h_{i}
that is negative. This does not change any result in the sums, because of the Følner property of the intevals
[1,N]
.

Let us now suppose that
0\leq a=\max\{h_{i}:i=1,\dots,r\}
. Then, note that given a function
H:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
we have (denoting by
I
the identity operator)

\begin{cases}IH(x)=H(x)\\
\Delta H(x)=H(x+1)-H(x)\\
\Delta^{2}H(x)=H(x+2)-2H(x+1)+H(x)\\
\vdots\\
\Delta^{a}H(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{a}(-1)^{j}\binom{a}{j}H(x+a-j).\end{cases}

Applying these equations to
G(x)
we obtain a way to write all of
G(x),G(x+1),\dots,

G(x+a)
in terms of powers of the difference operator, already given in an upper echelon form. This means that we can solve the relevant system of equations backwards, allowing us to write

F^{(s)}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{a}d_{i}\Delta^{a}G^{(s)}(x),

for some
d_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where we may assume that the non-zero coefficients are bounded away from
0
with constants depending only on the
\alpha_{j}
and
h_{j}
appearing in the definition of
F
.
∎

We will also need some growth rate of the functions
\Delta^{k}G
, and a comparison between the growth rates of the derivatives of
G
and shifts of it. We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
s\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
, let
k\in\{0,\dots,a\}
, and let
G
be as defined in Proposition
3.5
above. Then,

G^{(s+k)}(x+k)\geq\Delta^{k}G^{(s)}(x)\geq G^{(s+k)}(x)\quad(N+a<x\leq 2N-a).

Proof.

Indeed, observe that the following equality holds by induction:

\Delta^{k}G^{(s)}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\dots\int_{0}^{1}G^{(s+k)}(x+u_{1}+\dots+u_{k})\ du_{1}\dots du_{k},

so since
G^{(s+k+1)}(x)
is positive on the indicated range by Lemma
3.3
, it follows that
G^{(s+k)}
is increasing, and thus

G^{(s+k)}(x+k)\geq\Delta^{k}G^{(s)}(x)\geq G^{(k+s)}(x),

as desired.
∎

We are now in position to give the proof of the lower bound we claimed in Proposition
3.5
.

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

We begin by using Lemma
3.11
so we have

F^{(s)}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{a}d_{i}\Delta^{a}G^{(s)}(x).

Next, let
\tau=\min\{i\in\{0,\dots,a\}:|d_{i}|>0\}
. Therefore, by Lemma
3.12
we can write

F^{(s)}(x)\geq d_{\tau}G^{(s+\tau)}(x)-\lambda\sum_{j=\tau+1}^{a}G^{(s+j)}(x+a),

where
\lambda=\max\{|d_{j}|:j\geq\tau+1\}
. Next write

F^{(s)}(x)\geq d_{\tau}G^{(s+\tau)}(x)\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{d_{\tau}}\sum_{j=\tau+1}^{a}\frac{G^{(s+j)}(x+a)}{G^{(s+\tau)}(x)}\right).

By Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.4
, we can give bounds for each of the summands (
j\geq\tau+1
):

\displaystyle\frac{G^{(s+j)}(x+a)}{G^{(s+\tau)}(x)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(s+j)!}{(s+\tau)!}\cdot\frac{2^{s+j+1}G(3x+3a)}{\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{s+j}}\cdot\frac{2x^{s+\tau}}{G(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4^{s+j+1}\frac{(s+j)!}{(s+\tau)!}\cdot\frac{1}{x^{j-\tau}}\cdot\exp(\log^{c+1}(3x+3a)-\log^{c+1}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4^{(c+1)\log^{c}(x+a)+a+1}(s+a)^{a-\tau}\cdot\frac{1}{x}\cdot\exp\left((3x+3a)(c+1)\frac{\log^{c}(3x+3a)}{x}\right),

by the mean value inequality. This last expression only depends on
x,c
and
a
(in other words, the values of the
h_{i}
). These are known a priori, which is crucial for the uniformity in
s
that we need in the proofs later.

It is easy to check that the last expression tends to
0
as
x\to\infty
, but again, we remark that this is the case depending only on the aforementioned parameters. This means that for a certain
x_{0}(c,h_{1},\dots,h_{r})
it will be that this last quantity is bounded above by
\frac{d_{\tau}}{2\lambda}
, which implies that

F^{(s)}(x)\geq\frac{d_{\tau}}{2}G^{(s+\tau)}(x).

It follows that

\frac{F^{(s)}(x)}{s!}\geq\frac{d_{\tau}}{2}\cdot\frac{G^{(s+\tau)}(x)}{s!}=\frac{d_{\tau}}{2}\cdot\frac{(s+\tau)!}{s!}\cdot\frac{G^{(s+\tau)}(x)}{(s+\tau)!}\geq\frac{d_{\tau}}{4}\cdot\frac{G(x)}{x^{s+\tau}},

on the specified range, as we wished to show.
∎

4.1.
Exponential sums

We can use Theorem

Proof of Theorem
1.4

Using the dyadic summation trick we can write:

\E_{n\in[N]}e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\alpha_{j}G(n+h_{j})\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\log_{2}N\rfloor+1}\sum_{\frac{N}{2^{k+1}}<n\leq\frac{N}{2^{k}}}e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\alpha_{j}G(n+h_{j})\right).

Next, Theorem
3.1
allows us to estimate the inner dyadic sums (always for

actually get shrunk into

4.2.

We will use Theorem

.

, it is enough to show that

Next, we move to the sequence

Proof of Corollary

.

4.3.
A von Neumann type result

In this last subsection we will give the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Using Theorem
2.5
, we have
L^{2}(\mu)=L^{2}(\mu)_{\text{inv}}\oplus L^{2}(\mu)_{\text{erg}}.
For
f\in L^{2}(\mu)_{\text{inv}}
we have that
T_{1}^{\lfloor G(n+h_{1})\rfloor}\cdots T_{r}^{\lfloor G(n+h_{r})\rfloor}f=f,
so, it suffices to show that for

we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T_{1}^{\lfloor G(n+h_{1})\rfloor}\cdots T_{r}^{\lfloor G(n+h_{r})\rfloor}f\right\|_{2}=0.

This follows from Theorem
2.6
and Proposition
4.1
. Indeed,

\begin{split}&\quad\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ T_{1}^{\lfloor G(n+h_{1})\rfloor}\cdots T_{r}^{\lfloor G(n+h_{r})\rfloor}f\right\|^{2}_{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}\left\langle\left(\prod_{j=1}^{r}T_{j}^{\lfloor G(n+h_{j})\rfloor-\lfloor G(m+h_{j})\rfloor}\right)f,f\right\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{r}}e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\alpha_{j}(\lfloor G(n+h_{j})\rfloor-\lfloor G(m+h_{j})\rfloor)\right)\;d\nu_{f}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{r}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\alpha_{j}\lfloor G(n+h_{j})\rfloor\right)\right|^{2}\;d\nu_{f}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})\end{split}

which goes to

as

since
f\in L^{2}(\mu)_{\text{erg}},
whence, by Corollary
2.7
we have
\nu_{f}(\{(0,\ldots,0)\})=0.

∎
