Introduction

Coronary ischaemia typically presents with chest discomfort, dyspnoea, or pressure-like discomfort with radiation.1 However, older adults and individuals with poorly controlled metabolic risk factors tend to develop atypical symptoms such as syncope or visual disturbances.2 Recognizing such non-classical presentations is clinically important to avoid a delay in diagnosis of significant underlying coronary artery disease (CAD). This case highlights how systemic metabolic comorbidities can obscure the clinical presentation and delay recognition of significant coronary disease.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old man with a history of uncontrolled hypertension and no prior cardiac workup presented following a motor vehicle collision (MVC) precipitated by a transient episode of complete vision loss with a patient-reported small, illuminated brown halo in bilateral visual fields. He denied pre-accident chest pain, palpitations, or dyspnoea. The patient was evaluated at a community hospital emergency department under trauma protocol. Weight at presentation was 116.9 kg, with body mass index of 34 suggesting class I obesity.

The patient’s history was notable for uncontrolled hypertension but no known cardiac disease, diabetes, or smoking history. There was no family history of CAD.

The prodrome leading to this patient’s MVC was considered most consistent with either syncope or seizure. Differential diagnoses at presentation considered ischaemic stroke, transient ischaemic attack, vasovagal syncope, cardiac arrhythmia, silent myocardial ischaemia, and acute coronary syndrome with relevant manifestations such as carotid artery stenosis.

As per trauma protocol, computed tomography (CT) scan of the head and cervical spine was completed as well as X-ray imaging of the chest/abdomen/pelvis, revealing no acute post-traumatic findings. At this point, the prodrome was considered most consistent with a syncopal episode, and more targeted workup was initiated.

A completed 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) at emergency department (ED) presentation demonstrated ST-segment depression in leads V3-V6 as well as in leads I and aVL, consistent with anterolateral ischaemia. Additionally, pathologic Q waves were noted in leads III and aVF, suggestive of a prior inferior myocardial infarction (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiogram was completed and revealed grade I diastolic dysfunction of the left ventricle with an ejection fraction of 55%–60%, mild sclerotic thickening of the aortic valve, and trivial regurgitation of the aortic, mitral, and tricuspid valves. There were no significant wall motion abnormalities noted when assessing the left ventricle. High-sensitivity troponins (hs-troponins) were also found to be mildly elevated on admission (Table 1; normal <20 ng/L). Additional lab findings completed during patient workup included an elevated haemoglobin (16.8 g/dL; normal range 12.5–16.3 g/dL), haematocrit (49%; normal range 37%—47%), creatinine (1.46 mg/dL; normal range 0.7–1.3 mg/dL), and a low estimated glomerular filtration rate (53 mL/min/1.73 m2; normal >60 mL/min/1.73 m2). A completed lipid panel revealed abnormal findings; cholesterol was normal at 198 mg/dL (normal <200 mg/dL), triglycerides were high at 174 mg/dL (normal <150 mg/dL), HDL was normal at 40 mg/dL (normal >40 mg/dL), LDL was high at 123 mg/dL (normal <100 mg/dL), and the cholesterol/HDL ratio was borderline high at 5.0 (normal <5.0). Thyroid-stimulating hormone testing also revealed a measurement of 27.98 µIU/mL (normal 0.4–5.33 µIU/mL), suggesting hypothyroidism, and a haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) measurement was revealed to be 6.0% (normal <5.7%), suggesting prediabetes.

Given the combination of anterolateral ST-segment depressions, up trending and multiple cardiovascular risk factors, coronary angiography was pursued to evaluate for obstructive coronary disease, and (hs-troponin) levels, left heart catheterization revealed severe multivessel coronary artery disease (MVCAD) (Figure 2), including 80% stenosis in the mid left anterior descending (LAD) artery, 95% stenosis after diagonal 2 branch in LAD, 99% lesion in proximal obtuse marginal (OM) 2, chronic 100% occlusion of the proximal-to-mid right coronary artery, with left-to-right collaterals, and mild disease in the left main and left circumflex artery.

The patient was transferred to a tertiary care centre, deemed a suitable surgical candidate, and underwent coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) ×4, 1 week after the initial encounter. The procedure included left internal mammary artery to distal LAD, saphenous vein graft (SVG) to diagonal 2 and OM2, SVG to right posterior descending artery, endoscopic vein harvest from the right leg, and sternal plating alongside placement of epicardial pacing wires.

The patient’s post-operative ICU course was uncomplicated. He was extubated and weaned off vasopressors quickly, without arrhythmias or infections. He was mobilized with physical therapy and underwent diuresis appropriately for postoperative hypervolemia. Continuous in-patient telemetry monitoring did not reveal sustained dysrhythmias during hospitalization. Post-operative labs showed improvement in creatinine and stable electrolytes. Post-operative management included levothyroxine for subclinical hypothyroidism, rosuvastatin and ezetimibe for dyslipidemia, metoprolol for hypertension, and counselling recommended for prediabetes.

The sternal incision healed well, and he ambulated independently without supplemental oxygen. The patient was discharged home in stable condition in 1 week following the CABG with follow-ups arranged for the following primary care, cardiology, and cardiothoracic surgery.

Discharge medications included aspirin 162 mg daily, rosuvastatin 20 mg nightly, ezetimibe 10 mg daily, metoprolol tartrate 12.5 mg daily, furosemide 40 mg daily × 7 days, levothyroxine 25 mcg daily, acetaminophen pro re nata (PRN; ‘as needed’), and polyethylene glycol PRN.

Discussion

This case illustrates the importance of considering silent ischaemia in patients presenting with syncope without classic anginal symptoms. Despite no prior cardiac history, the patient was found to have extensive MVCAD, requiring urgent surgical revascularization. Early cardiac evaluation enabled timely diagnosis and intervention, emphasizing the value of a low threshold for cardiovascular workup in high-risk individuals.

The patient’s visual symptoms raised consideration of both ophthalmologic and neurologic aetiologies.3,4 The initial presentation of transient visual disturbance and presumed syncope suggests transient cerebral hypoperfusion as a unifying mechanism.5 This may have occurred secondary to ischaemia-induced arrhythmia, or from global hypoperfusion in the setting of MVCAD.6 Additionally, autonomic dysregulation associated with myocardial ischaemia may have contributed to transient hypotension and reduced cerebral perfusion.7

Although ischaemia-related visual symptoms are recognized, definitive localization is limited by the absence of focal neurologic deficits and advanced neuroimaging. While CT of the head was unremarkable, magnetic resonance imaging may have provided greater sensitivity for detecting small infarcts or posterior fossa pathology.8 Hence, for this patient, the absence of advanced neuroimaging represents a limitation.

Notably, continuous telemetry monitoring during hospitalization did not demonstrate sustained arrhythmias. However, transient or intermittent arrhythmias may not be captured during a limited monitoring period and therefore cannot be definitively excluded as a contributing factor. Prior studies have shown that arrhythmia-related syncope is frequently underdiagnosed with short-term monitoring, as transient events may not occur during inpatient monitoring.9

Atypical presentations of CAD have been well described across high-risk populations. Silent myocardial ischaemia may present with syncope or neurologic symptoms due to transient cerebral hypoperfusion or arrhythmia.2 Contemporary studies and clinical guidelines further emphasize that a substantial proportion of patients with acute coronary syndromes present without chest pain, exhibiting non-classical symptoms such as syncope or dizziness, which are associated with delays in diagnosis and worse clinical outcomes.10

Untreated hypothyroidism, dyslipidemia, prediabetes, and obesity underscore the systemic nature of atherosclerosis. Hypothyroidism contributes to dyslipidemia and endothelial dysfunction, while obesity and prediabetes further promote insulin resistance, inflammation, and vascular injury, accelerating plaque progression.11–13 High-intensity lipid-lowering therapy has been associated with modest regression of plaque burden in intravascular ultrasound and computed tomography angiography studies, although this effect is variable and dependent on treatment intensity and degree of LDL reduction.14 Each 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL cholesterol is associated with a 22% reduction in major cardiovascular events.15

In this patient, the synergistic effects of uncontrolled hypertension, hypothyroidism, prediabetes, dyslipidemia with a particularly elevated LDL count, and obesity likely contributed to progressive atherosclerosis. While the exact mechanism remains uncertain, clinicians should consider silent myocardial ischaemia in patients presenting with syncope or transient visual disturbances. Early cardiovascular evaluation and risk stratification remain essential for preventing adverse outcomes.
