Unpacking Factors Affecting Medical Students’ Engagement in Educational Activities at University: A Mixed-Method Study
Authors: Reyhane Golbaf, Mahboobeh Khabaz Mafinejad, Morteza Taji, Reza Hosseini Dolama, Elizabeth Krajic Kachur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionA key component of engaging students in educational activities is identifying the factors that influence their motivation. This practice is particularly useful given the crucial role the educational system plays in student support.Methods and MaterialsThis multi-phase mixed-methods study in Tehran University of Medical Sciences consisted of three sequential phases: In the first phase, a literature review was conducted to identify factors reported in the literature that affect student involvement in university educational programs. In the second phase, a focus group discussion was carried out with 10 health professions students to learn about students’ views and the motivating factors affecting them. In the third phase, a three-round modified Delphi study was conducted among health professions students at TUMS to determine which of the identified factors met the a priori ≥4 inclusion threshold. In this phase, 45 students take part in round 1. 41 and 39 students took part in round 2 and 3.ResultsBased on the extracted codes from the focus group meeting and the first round of Delphi, the factors were categorized into five areas: “Providing opportunities for gaining personal and professional benefits”, “Providing opportunities for encouragement and appreciation”, “Providing opportunities for collaboration and receiving support”, “Providing opportunities for personal and professional development” and “having a sense of interest, autonomy, and usefulness”. In the second Delphi round out of different domains, “feeling useful” was rated as the most important motivational factor, followed by “impacting the future career” and “having the opportunity to learn and strengthen the acquisition of skills”, while “gaining fame” and “availability of educational materials” received the lowest ratings. In the third round, 73% of participants agreed with the final list of motivational factors without modification.ConclusionOne of the most important responsibilities of health professions education institutions is motivating students during the educational process through the identification of proper motivational factors. Our results can offer a guide to educational policymakers and administrators.

Introduction

It’s not just a person’s abilities that determine their function; motivation plays a crucial role in this process. Motivation energizes and directs individuals’ participation and performance.1 The processes that strengthen and guide behavior stem from both internal and external motivation.2 Student engagement has behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions, making it a complex construct.3 So despite considerable research attention, it still lacks a unified conceptual definition and is understood differently across educational and psychological perspectives.4

Student engagement in academic activities refers to strengthening student involvement in policy-making, leadership, and management of educational activities; establishing constructive communication with officials, professors, and academic staff; and, finally, participating in development projects.5,6 This type of student engagement is often conceptualized as a partnership model, because students are regarded as active stakeholders who share responsibility with faculty.7

Accordingly, one of the main duties of universities is to provide mechanisms and opportunities for as many students as possible to participate in improving the quality of education.8

The evidence suggests that students’ participation in university is influenced by various variables, including their personality, attitude, capabilities, and motivations.9 From a psychological perspective, student motivation and participation are closely related. Motivation encompasses unobservable factors, whereas participation involves observable behaviors. Although the factors affecting each may seem different, motivation is considered the basis for strengthening student participation in the university.3 Considering the impact of motivation on students’ performance, it is necessary to pay more attention to the motivational components of students in different dimensions of academic participation.10 In this regard, identifying and strengthening the effective factors that motivate health professions students to participate in university activities is more critical due to the high concentration of educational programs.11

Along with the various functional areas of the university, promoting student engagement in educational activities (eg, participation in curriculum planning, educational research, and mentoring) is one of the primary missions of academic institutions. On the other hand, the use of motivational stimuli yields satisfactory results in education and is recognized as a factor in enhancing students’ effort and efficiency in educational activities.12 Based on this, one of the essential tasks of academic institutions in involving students in various fields, including education, is to identify the effective factors that motivate them, which provides the basis for realizing the fundamental goals of improving the quality of education.3

It is essential to differentiate between students’ engagement in educational activities in universities and other activities such as engagement in the teaching-learning process. Engagement in the teaching-learning process focuses on students’ engagement in their own learning, which includes being present in classes, participating in class activities, performing assigned learning activities, and interaction with teachers and other learners.13 In turn, engagement in educational activities relates to students’ participation in education as partners in the process of education development and improvement through curriculum development, educational research, mentoring, policy making, leadership, and educational decision making.14 As it can be seen, both types of engagement imply active participation of students; however, these types of engagement differ in purpose and role of participants in the process. Consequently, motivation for participation in educational activities and teaching-learning process can differ and require separate research.

The review of the evidence reveals that it is challenging to identify the factors that affect students’ motivation to participate in educational activities at universities. In recent years, some universities in the world have provided more credit for making decisions about providing necessary incentives for students’ participation in educational activities.6 However, defining and identifying motivational factors that affect participation in the process of promoting educational activities, unlike participation in research, is not straightforward. In addition, most of the studies conducted in the field of student engagement have emphasized the motivational factors involved in the participation of students in the teaching-learning process.15,16 A recent systematic review continue to highlight this gap, with most research in medical education still concentrated on classroom-based active learning strategies rather than participation in other educational development activities.17

More specifically, and in the Middle East region, medical student engagement has been the subject of a number of recent studies, but these studies have been largely limited to a specific area, namely student research engagement.18,19 In Iran, a national qualitative study identified the country’s centralized medical education system and the prevailing cultural atmosphere as barriers to student engagement and reported faculty support as a strong motivator for engagement.20 Other studies have reported barriers such as inappropriate clinical environments and limited interaction between faculty and students,21 decreased learning motivation related to cultural-economic factors,22 and barriers to participation in a specific activity such as scientific competitions.23 However, these studies are scattered and each has only addressed a certain limited field (research, clinical education, a region, or a single activity) and the motivational factors affecting medical students’ engagement have not been systematically identified and prioritized in any of them.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to address this gap by identifying and prioritizing the factors that motivate students’ participation in university educational activities using a three-stage mixed design: a literature review to examine the international literature; a focus group discussion to uncover more factors from the students’ perspective; and a modified Delphi technique to obtain a prioritization among students. The findings of this study are expected to provide valuable insights for understanding the factors that influence student engagement and may help educational stakeholders develop strategies to promote greater student participation in educational activities.

Methods and Materials

This study employed a multi-phase mixed-methods design consisting of three sequential phases:1 literature review,2 qualitative inquiry using a focus group discussion, and3 a three-round modified Delphi. Each phase built on the findings from the previous one. The literature review was conducted first to identify motivational factors reported in earlier research. The focus group discussion was then conducted to explore participants’ views on the identified issues and to identify motivational factors that might have been overlooked in the literature review. Finally, the results from the literature review and the focus group discussion, along with the responses from the first round of the Delphi technique, were used to construct the list of motivational factors assessed in the subsequent Delphi rounds. The phases were sequentially connected, with results from each phase informing the next and ultimately leading to the formation of motivational factors and domains. In this study, qualitative data were analyzed using MAXQDA 2020, while quantitative data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.

Two researchers (MKM, MT) independently reviewed the full texts of the articles (Including six in English and one in Persian) and extracted codes using conventional content analysis. In this qualitative analysis method, which is conducted to identify key points,13 the related literature is reviewed using inductive reasoning, and information is extracted without specific interpretation. This usually forms the basis for the subsequent stages of research.24 Relevant meaning units related to motivational factors were identified in each article. When a concept was explicitly labeled by the authors of the respective article, their original terminology was used and when no label was provided, the researchers assigned a code based on the content of the text.

After independent coding, the two researchers compared their codes to identify similarities and differences. In cases of discrepancies during the coding process, consensus was reached through discussion between the two researchers. This phase did not aim to generate any category or subcategory; rather, the extracted codes and concepts served as inputs for the subsequent phases, where they were further explored, refined, and integrated.

Phase 2: Qualitative Exploration Through a Focus Group Discussion

At this stage, a two-hour focus group meeting was convened according to AMEE Guide No. 91.25 The focus group method facilitates the elucidation of students’ perspectives by creating opportunities for discussion. In the focus group, participants interact to develop and present their opinions, generating more creative and in-depth insights.26 Purposive sampling was used to invite students to the focus group session. The inclusion criteria were being a health professions student at Tehran University of Medical Sciences and having participated in at least one of the university’s educational activities within the past year. The exclusion was unwillingness to participate in the study criteria To ensure maximum diversity in the sample, efforts were made to include students with varied gender and academic backgrounds. Sixty percent of the 10 participants were female (n = 6), 50% were undergraduate students (n = 5), and the remaining participants were postgraduate students.

The justification for choosing 10 participants in the focus group is based on the recommendation by Tang and Davis (1995). In their findings, the number of participants should range between 4 to 12 participants depending on practical considerations and objectives of the research. Since the methodology used in the research involves mixed methods approach, the focus group was mainly meant for confirmation and interpretation purposes. The structured format and appropriate time management made the session duration sufficient for a 10-participant group. Participants from diverse disciplines and academic levels maximized variation in perspectives. Thus, a 10-participant group was considered adequate and appropriate for the study objectives.27

The focus group meeting was held at the participants’ appointment in a private conference room of university, where distractions from the outside were kept at a minimum level. Before the meeting, guidelines outlining the rules for student participation, including protocols for engaging in discussions and exchanging opinions, were provided to the participants. The discussion was moderated by an instructor specializing in medical education (MKM), and this instructor had never previously been in any educational, supervisory, or evaluative role in relation to the participants. Open-ended questions were designed and compiled by the research team before the meeting. These questions included: What motivational factors at the individual level make you motivated to participate in the educational activities of the university?What motivational factors at the organizational level make you motivated to participate in the educational activities of the university?

With participants’ consent, the meeting was audio-recorded, and notes were taken of the comments and suggestions by another member of the research team (MT).

Data saturation was assessed continuously during the focus group discussion by examining within-group saturation. The moderator kept track of the discussion in order to identify whether the participants were coming up with new ideas or themes that could help answer the research questions raised. When the discussion only confirmed previously mentioned information within-group data saturation was considered to have been reached.28

After the focus group interview, the audio-recorded data was transcribed verbatim and independently read several times by two researchers (M.K.M. and M.T) to gain a deep understanding of the content. Units of meaning relevant to motivational factors were identified, and the process of open coding was performed.

Similar concepts were grouped into provisional subcategories through iterative discussion. Any disagreement regarding the codes was resolved through consultation with a third researcher. Moreover, using the percentage agreement method we calculated inter-Coder Reliability (ICR) in which an agreement percentage of 80% was considered acceptable.29

Phase 3: Three-Round Modified Delphi Process

To identify and reach a consensus on the factors that motivate students to engage in educational activities, a modified Delphi technique was employed.30 The reason for choosing the modified Delphi technique is that, in the second round of Delphi, the results of the literature review and the focus group were combined with those of the first round. In the second round, these combined results were then presented to the participants to identify priorities. In the Delphi technique, the sampling method for students was purposive, aiming for maximum diversity. The inclusion criteria were students studying at Tehran University of Medical Sciences who had experience working in the student committee for education development or had participated in at least one university or college education development activity. Students from various academic levels, with a gender distribution, were invited to participate in the Delphi study. Students were recruited from multiple disciplines, including medicine, dentistry, pharmacy, and nursing. The questionnaire was distributed electronically via Email to 61 participants: 40 females (65%) and 21 males (35%), with 13 postgraduates (21%) and 48 undergraduates (79%). A three-week timeframe was allocated for responding to and returning the completed questionnaire, with two reminders sent at one-week intervals during this period to prompt a response.

In the first round of the Delphi study, with the participation of 45 students resulting a response rate of 73%, students’ perspectives on motivational factors influencing their engagement in university educational activities were collected via an open-ended questionnaire; therefore, no predefined items were presented for rating in this round.

To analyze the qualitative responses, conventional content analysis was used. The findings from the literature review, focus group discussion, and the open-ended responses from the first Delphi round were subsequently compiled and reviewed together. Overlapping or conceptually similar factors were compared and grouped, and the resulting factors were organized into broader motivational domains. This integrated list was then used in the subsequent Delphi rounds, in which participants evaluated and prioritized the factors and domains and consensus was assessed. Thus, the final set of motivational factors and domains was derived from the integration and refinement of evidence across all three phases.

In the second round of the modified Delphi, 41 students (31 females and 10 males) out of 45 (participants of previous round) completed the survey, resulting in a response rate of 91%. Participants included 9 postgraduates and 32 undergraduate students.

To produce the final list of domains and factors, the research team held a two-hour consensus session in which the codes obtained from the literature review (Phase 1), focus group discussion (Phase 2), and open-ended responses from the first Delphi round were compiled into a single comprehensive list.

In this session, codes that referred to a common underlying concept were merged into groups by reviewing each code individually; this judgment was made based on a comparison of the operational definition of each code across different sources. The codes within each group were then grouped into subcategories by screening for duplicate or overlapping items.

Finally, the subcategories were reviewed to see if they shared a common conceptual basis and were clustered into broad thematic domains that reflected internal or external sources of motivation. According to self-determination theory, domains were finally examined in terms of being internal and external Internal domains are those in which the primary source of motivation is the activity itself or personal growth and a person’s sense of control (regardless of external consequences), and external domains are those in which the primary source of motivation is an outcome, reward, or recognition provided by others or the institution (university).1

Any disagreements among team members during this merging process were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. This process resulted in a final list of 33 items across five domains, which were presented to participants for prioritization in the second round of Delphi, in which the domains and subset codes for each category were provided to the participants, and students were asked to rate each item based on their perspective, ranging from 1 (meaning “least important”) to 5 (meaning “most important”).

After receiving the responses, the mean and standard deviation of each item were calculated, with a mean score of four or above out of five considered eligible for retention. The mean scores were used as the main indicator of the importance of each item, as the mean represents the overall level of perceived importance of that item based on the assessments of all participants. This criterion was chosen by adapting from similar studies that considered an average score of ≥4 out of 5 as the initial criterion for identifying high-importance items.31,32 However, this criterion was not considered a final or definitive criterion and was put to a vote in the third round.

In the third round of the Delphi process, 39 students (29 women and 10 men) participated, with a response rate of 86%. 9 participants of this round were postgraduates and 30 were undergraduates.

The items were presented to the participants again, along with the statistics and their proposed status. They were asked to indicate their agreement using three options: “I agree with the final list of motivational factors”, “I agree with the condition of making the proposed amendments”, or “I disagree with the extracted list”. In this round, 39 students (29 women and 10 men) participated, and because the required level of agreement (70%) was achieved, the Delphi study was stopped. This level of consensus was determined by the research team based on a review of similar Delphi studies and published methodological guidance on defining consensus.22–24

In our study we employed Several strategies to enhance the trustworthiness of the findings, including the application of Guba and Lincoln’s criteria, member checking, reflexivity, and maintenance of an audit trail. These strategies are described in detail below.

To ensure the trustworthiness of the data, Guba and Lincoln criteria were used as a framework.33 For credibility, participants were selected with the aim of achieving a diversity of perspectives, data were collected through Focus Group sessions using a structured guide, and member checking was performed after transcription with every participant of the focus group. For dependability, the data collection and analysis steps were carried out systematically using a pre-designed guide. For confirmability, the methods section included a detailed account of the research steps to facilitate auditability. For transferability, the study context, participants, and data collection and analysis steps were clearly described. Authenticity, was established by ensuring the researcher’s competency in performing each step of study and by full disclosure of any kind of potential conflicts of interest.

During the focus group session, a summary of the notes and initial codes extracted from the discussion were provided to the participants to ensure the initial accuracy of the codes and to provide explanations or corrections if necessary. After a more detailed analysis, the identified codes and findings were sent to the participants for review and confirmation.

Before starting the data collection and analysis, each member of the research team was asked to record in writing their initial assumptions and beliefs about possible motivational factors (reflexivity). An audit trail was also maintained throughout the study to record the analysis process and the main decisions related to coding, categorizing, and integrating the findings.

A group of studies referred to the value and importance of the task; that is, the extent to which an individual considers the work or activity to be valuable and meaningful. The role of a supportive environment, social relationships, external rewards, a sense of belonging, and self-actualization were also identified as complementary motivators in some studies. Phase 3: Modified Delphi Process

During a 2-hour meeting attended by the research team members, all the findings from the literature review (31 codes), the focus group meeting (13 codes), and the first round of Delphi (26 codes) were compiled, compared, and merged, resulting in 33 final items for the second round of Delphi. These items were categorized into five main categories. The factors related to external motivation were placed in three categories: “Providing opportunities for gaining personal and professional benefits”, “Providing opportunities for encouragement and appreciation”, “Providing opportunities for collaboration and receiving support”. Additionally, the factors related to internal motivation were placed in two categories: “Providing opportunities for personal and professional development” and “Having a sense of interest, autonomy, and usefulness”.

Of the 33 items rated in the second Delphi round, 20 items (60.6%) met the retention criterion (mean ≥ 4.00) and were proposed for inclusion in the main list, while 13 items fell below this threshold and were proposed for exclusion.

Discussion

Student engagement plays a crucial role in the development of educational systems;40 therefore, understanding and identifying motivational factors that affect active student involvement in universities is vital for educators, managers, and educational policymakers. In this study, we investigated the motivational factors that influence student engagement in education. Motivation refers to the internal processes that lead individuals to initiate and maintain certain behaviors.41 Motivation plays a vital role in student engagement. When students are motivated, they are more likely to contribute to the institution’s progress and development actively. By examining both internal and external motivational factors, educators and educational policymakers can employ strategies that enhance student participation and yield positive educational outcomes. While prior research has largely examined motivation in classroom, this study contributes a prioritized, consensus-based framework that clarifies the factors that are involved in motivating students toward educational activities.

Before we discuss the findings of the current study, it is important to note that our findings only reflect participants’ perceived importance of factors rather than their effect on actual engagement. The Delphi method captures consensus on what students believe motivates them, which may not fully correspond to what actually drives their behaviour.

Based on our findings, the category of students’ motivation to participate in educational development activities is multifaceted and dependent on many factors. According to students’ opinions, the most important subcategories related to personal and professional benefits were “the impact of educational activity on the future career” and “the possibility of publishing an article”. This issue highlights the importance of recognizing that students are seeking to make educational activities an opportunity to enrich their future endeavors. By designing and assigning educational activities related to their future careers, students can create a safe platform for improving their knowledge, developing professional skills, and identifying interests (Madan and Teitge 2013), which has numerous advantages for both students and the university.42 One possible reason for this finding is that when students are encouraged to publish their articles, they gain a better sense of success and recognition for their work. According to a study by Ali Asghar Hayat et al (2018), this practice not only increases their self-confidence but also encourages them to actively participate in improving their learning.9 This emphasis on career-related benefits may be particularly prominent in the Iranian academic context, where publication is formally very important for academic promotion, and this may strengthen students’ motivation to engage in scholarly activities beyond what has been reported in Western studies. This finding is probably related to a structural feature of the Iranian medical education system, as the publication of articles by students directly affects their professional future and job opportunities. This creates an institutional incentive that is less prominent in countries where job opportunities are less dependent on research output. Actually, considering the available literature, this finding partly diverges from a comparative study of Romanian and international medical graduates where peer role models and resource constraints (time and budget) were identified as the main drivers of research participation, suggesting that the motivation associated with publication may be influenced by the specific academic incentive structures of each institution. This suggests the importance of the culture that governs each organization.43

Through active participation in academic activities, students not only deepen their learning and enhance their professional skills but also gain personal benefits, such as increased job readiness and networking opportunities.44 In general, motivational factors are effective in shaping students’ participation in educational activities to improve their future careers. Erdogan’s study (2019) shows that by identifying this factor, educational officials can create an environment that empowers students to achieve academic success and excellence in their future careers.44 The results of the present study align with those of other studies conducted in this field. Seeking credit and fame was another motivating factor investigated in this category, and it plays a significant role in students’ active participation in university educational activities. According to Festeu, the desire to earn credit, recognition, and social status can be a strong motivator for students to participate actively in educational projects. External rewards, such as credit, recognition, and social status, can significantly affect learners’ willingness to contribute to educational development.

Another practical dimension in motivating students was providing opportunities for encouragement and appreciation. The factors related to “receiving educational encouragement facilities” and “seeing the activities performed by the individual” were found to be the most important. Incentive facilities are rewards offered to students to recognize their participation in the university’s educational activities. This facility can take multiple forms, such as providing scholarships, offering opportunities to participate in extracurricular courses, and presenting academic awards. It appears that educational institutions can promote a sense of belonging and ownership among students by providing incentive facilities. According to Sansgiri et al, providing direct incentives yields more effective feedback on students’ performance than indirect incentives.45 Another challenge is ensuring the sustainability and long-term impact of incentive facilities in maintaining and improving learners’ motivation for continued participation.46 According to Desi et al, while these facilities may initially create enthusiasm and increase learners’ participation, there is a need to evaluate their long-term effectiveness. Furthermore, one limitation of providing incentive facilities is the potential negative impact on learners’ intrinsic motivation. When students focus solely on obtaining rewards or benefiting from incentives, their genuine passion for learning and for personal and institutional growth may diminish. As a result, it is necessary to strike a balance between providing external rewards, such as opportunities for encouragement and appreciation, and fostering students’ intrinsic motivation to maintain their long-term participation.1,47,48 In this regard, to maximize the effectiveness of incentive programs, it is suggested that incentives be tailored to students’ diverse needs and interests.

Another factor that can be investigated in this category is the effect of verbal incentives on strengthening learners’ motivation to participate in university educational activities. Verbal incentives involve using positive reinforcement, such as praise, to foster and sustain students’ motivation to participate in educational activities. Studies have shown that effective use of verbal incentives by professors and managers can significantly increase student participation.49 This can be attributed to several factors, including the psychological effect of positive feedback that increases students’ self-esteem and makes them more confident about their abilities. Verbal incentives also create a supportive learning environment in which students feel valued and appreciated for their efforts, fostering a sense of belonging and intrinsic motivation.50 In general, by strategically employing verbal incentives, instructors may increase students’ willingness to participate in university educational activities while fostering a positive learning environment that supports students’ academic growth and development. It is noteworthy that verbal incentives may have an impact comparable to, or even greater than, costlier material incentives It can show that for institutions with limited capacity, low-resource interventions, such as ongoing praise from faculty, can be a cost-effective strategy.

Another effective factor is receiving positive feedback from professors, which can profoundly influence students’ participation in university educational activities. According to DeVahl et al, this can be a corrective and strengthening solution in which students will feel valued, approved, and encouraged to participate in expressing their ideas and opinions by receiving feedback from professors. It appears that providing constructive feedback by professors on the activities carried out by students, focusing on reinforcing their strengths and offering suggestions to improve their performance in academic activities, may foster a growth mind set and support performance.51 In addition, providing positive feedback strengthens the internal motivation of learners by creating a sense of success and personal satisfaction, and encourages them to enhance their learning experiences, thereby improving the quality of education.1 Furthermore, visible student activities help strengthen interactions among students by creating a vibrant culture in the university environment.9,52 The interesting thing to note in this category was that receiving financial incentives was ranked lower in terms of importance. This issue can be a response to high-level human needs in students according to Alderfer’s theory.53 Based on this, students consider such opportunities as a platform to showcase their abilities and a chance to improve themselves as much as possible, rather than solely to earn financial rewards.54 The relatively low ranking of financial incentives may also reflect some specific features of the Iranian context, including free or low-cost public medical education and a relatively secure career future for physicians. This could therefore reduce the importance of financial rewards compared to systems where students are required to pay more for education.

Concerning the third category of motivational factors related to providing opportunities for cooperation and receiving support, what motivates students more than any other factor is “receiving support from officials”, “possibility of receiving expert advice”, and “existence of a positive and constructive atmosphere in the university”. Creating a positive and constructive atmosphere in universities is a motivating factor consistently highlighted in numerous studies. This issue can be considered in line with Bandura’s theory, which posits that the environment, the individual, and behavior interact as a trinity that shapes behavior.55 Without a supportive atmosphere, students may not be expected to be active and participate. In such situations, experienced professors can be expected to act as compassionate counselors for relatively inexperienced but highly interested students. This finding is consistent with recent evidence emphasizing the value of structured student-staff partnerships in curriculum developments.56 They similarly report that a supportive and collaborative institutional culture enhances student motivation to engage. This result also aligns with Lakkala’s study, which identifies the support of professors, along with a positive organizational atmosphere, as important factors in student engagement.57 The findings of Rabbani et al showed that the university’s social context (including the professor’s emotional support, autonomy support, and peer emotional support) affects students’ participation in several ways. The findings indicate that background factors, such as conditions at educational institutions, are related to individual characteristics (independence, ability, and communication), and these processes influence students’ motivation.37 Therefore, the institution’s supportive atmosphere is one of the factors influencing learners’ motivation to participate in educational activities. Norouzi et al investigated the relationship between university atmosphere and its dimensions with students’ academic motivation and showed that there is a positive relationship between university atmosphere and students’ academic motivation, and acknowledged that the higher the university’s communicative, cooperative, and supportive atmosphere, the higher the motivation of students can rise.47 Regarding the present findings, this triple interaction is reflected in how faculty support (an environmental factor) interacts with students’ sense of responsibility (an individual factor) to create active participation (a behavioral outcome); it seems that the lack of any one of these elements, especially institutional support, is sufficient to disrupt this cycle.

Another effective factor in this category is the “support of educational officials” and the “possibility of receiving expert advice from experts”. The support of educational officials, combined with the opportunity to receive specialized advice from experts, was perceived by students as strengthening participation in educational activities. It seems that this issue will not only strengthen the sense of being seen in the institution but also enhance their capabilities. The encouragement and support of students in conducting educational research by Education Development Centers (EDCs) can be effective in developing educational activities. In the field of individual growth and development, the ability to communicate with experts and consult them to improve skills is a crucial factor in student motivation. In this regard, it is worth noting that, by challenging students, development activities provide an opportunity for their growth and advancement, and students view such cases as a chance to enhance their performance and educational synergy. The importance of interpersonal relationships with specialists in the category of students’ participation has been emphasized previously in Lester’s study.58 This can also be understood in the sense that educational activities are new and questionable for students because they have less experience and expertise in the field. Access to experts provides students with a series of informal guidance that may reduce this uncertainty and, in turn, lower the perceived barrier to participation. It should be noted that the choice of support from educational authorities as a motivating factor may partly reflect the organizational culture in Iran. In an organization with a strong organizational structure, support from a higher hierarchical authority is particularly important; this factor is probably less prominent in educational systems with different organizational structures.

Another factor influencing students’ motivation in this category was “observing their academic and skill development”. The motivation to progress is one of the most significant motivations that lead students to engage in educational activities.59

Finally, according to the participants in the present study, one of the most important factors guiding a student towards university educational activities is their mentality. Our findings indicate that “feeling useful and responding to personal interests” is one of the most important factors of the fifth category. This point aligns with Ainley’s perspective, which considers interest as a key factor in student engagement.60 Therefore, creating a wide range of activities for students and allowing them to choose one according to their interests, rather than imposing it on them, will be a motivating approach.61 Interest plays a crucial role in student engagement.11,12,47,59,62 Furthermore, supporting active students by inducing a sense of usefulness will serve as a motivating factor. The factor most highly prioritized by participants as relevant to their motivation and participation in this study was the sense of usefulness. When students are allowed to actively participate in improving their learning environment, this strengthens their sense of agency. Involving students in decision-making processes, such as curriculum planning or the development of educational policy, not only empowers them but also fosters a deeper understanding of the existing processes and conditions.47,62 Additionally, a sense of usefulness instills responsibility in students, encouraging them to take ownership of their education and engage in active collaboration with professors and fellow students. Ultimately, when students feel empowered to shape their educational experiences, they become more motivated and enthusiastic learners who actively participate in the development of education at all levels.63 In this regard, a study at the University of Minho (2025) also showed that educational pathways that support autonomy can generate higher intrinsic motivation for students. These findings reflect the findings of our study in a broader international sample.64 As a result, it seems that efforts to understand students and satisfy their psychological needs should be more on the agenda of universities. In some cases, students need to be able to feel effective internally and to be able to follow what they feel in their hearts in the real world. Educational planners and administrators should be aware that dictating a series of things that students have no interest in may lead to achieving goals in the short term, but in the long term, it can destroy students’ motivation and enthusiasm. Unlike the more structural and institutional factors described above, the centrality of autonomy and sense of usefulness in our findings reflects basic psychological needs and has been documented in diverse educational and cultural contexts; this suggests that such factors are likely generalizable beyond the Iranian context.

When examining the factors that affect students’ motivation to engage in academic activities, it is crucial to consider prevailing cultural context. Culture may impact individuals’ motivations.65 According to global web resources like “Hofstede”, Iranians are characterized by several cultural traits: Firstly, they pursue success for increased prosperity. Secondly, they value hierarchy within organizations and institutions. Thirdly, they are group-oriented, placing high importance on loyalty within groups. Lastly, they value self-control and autonomy.66 These aspects confirm our findings and may correspond, respectively, to indicators such as “providing opportunities for gaining personal and professional benefits”, “providing opportunities for encouragement and appreciation”, “providing opportunities for collaboration and receiving support from others”, and “providing opportunities for personal and professional development” It should be noted that this cultural fit is interpretive and not tested in this study; future research that directly measures cultural dimensions along with motivational factors will be needed to confirm these fits.

Overall, our findings suggest a useful distinction between context-specific and potentially generalizable factors. Factors such as the importance of publication, hierarchical support, and low importance of financial incentives appear to be related to the structural features of the Iranian medical education system. In contrast, factors rooted in basic psychological needs—namely, autonomy, competence, and affiliation—are aligned with components of autonomy theory that have been documented in a variety of international contexts, suggesting that these dimensions may be important beyond the present context.

One of the strengths of this study is the identification and prioritization of motivational factors that drive student engagement in university educational activities, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been explored in any prior study. Additionally, a multi-stage mixed-methods study was employed to identify and reach consensus on a list of influential factors, a step beyond merely compiling participants’ opinions.

Limitations and Future Directions

There are some limitations in the current research. First, the current study is performed in a single setting, which might limit the generalizability and transferability of the results in other educational settings. Second, some operational constraints may have resulted some studies being missed in the literature review phase. The small number of participants in the focus group discussion (n=10) might not cover all the opinions of different fields of study and different levels of education. Also, the use of purposive sampling and inclusion of students with prior interest in educational activities might cause some biases in the study sample and result in the selection of more involved students. Besides, the presence of peers and faculty moderator in the focus group might cause some social desirability or conformity. The 73% response rate in the first Delphi round may have introduced non-response bias, as students who did not participate may have held different views from those who responded, although to minimize non-response, two reminders were sent during each round, and participants were allowed to continue their participation in subsequent rounds. The panel used for Delphi rounds is biased as well since it consists mostly of undergraduate students (79%) and females (65%). The predefined cut-off value for consensus (≥4.00 mean) might have resulted in the omission of some important but slightly lower-scoring items. However, the results of Delphi consensus do not show the causality between factors, but only their perceived importance. It is unknown how the motivational factors relate to students’ behaviors and how they are influenced by changes in the context of education. We would also want to emphasize that this study cannot establish whether the identified factors actually lead to increased engagement; it only presents what students believe to be motivating.

In the future, researchers are suggested to perform multicenter longitudinal studies with diverse samples, investigate the barriers to sustaining student motivation, take the viewpoints of the faculty members, educational administrators, and policymakers into account, and use interventional designs to explore the relationship between perceived motivational factors and actual engagement behaviors.

The transferability of these findings to other contexts should be considered with caution. At the regional level, given the relative cultural and structural similarities between Iran and some Middle Eastern countries, these findings may be transferable to medical schools in the region, although this assumption needs to be tested. In contrast, factors rooted in basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) are more likely to generalize to international medical education contexts. In general, it is suggested that institutions interested in applying these findings first assess their local priorities using a similar method to avoid inappropriate generalization.

Conclusion

Our single-center study of health professions students with prior involvement in educational development activities identified and prioritized five key categories of motivational factors perceived by these students as relevant to their engagement. However, although these findings will provide important information when implementing such programs in institutions with similar characteristics, the results cannot be extrapolated to other medical students or educational systems without taking into account differences in culture and structure. Based on our study, educational policymakers are advised to: (a) develop educational programs that link students to their careers to increase the perceived relevance of the program for them; and (b) develop an organized process for providing recognition that incorporates both incentives and feedback to make students feel useful and in control of the educational improvement process.
