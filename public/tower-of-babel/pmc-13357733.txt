Mitochondrial translocation of MDM2 and TFAM reprograms metabolism in treatment-refractory cancers
Authors: Jie Qing Eu, Nur Afiqah Binte Mohamed Salleh, Jayshree Hirpara, Naoto Ohi, Emiri Omori Takaki, Tuan Zea Tan, Ju Ee Seet, Susan Swee-Shan Hue, Shu Jun Chan, Dorothy Xi Yue Lim, Lingzhi Wang, Regina Tong Xin Wong, Azhar Ali, Yaw Chyn Lim, Boon-Cher Goh, Li Ren Kong, Shazib Pervaiz, Andrea LA Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) are frontline therapies for oncogene-addicted cancers, yet metabolic rewiring frequently drives acquired resistance. Here, we identify a mitochondrial trafficking mechanism that regulates oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) dependence in TKI-resistant tumours. Using resistant cell models and patient-derived materials, we demonstrate that OXPHOS activation is regulated by an AKT-driven, competitive interaction between mitochondrial MDM2 and the mitochondrial transcription factor TFAM at mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Mechanistically, adaptive AKT activation promotes cytosolic redistribution of MDM2 with reciprocal accumulation of TFAM in mitochondrial, enhancing mtDNA transcription and OXPHOS activity. To validate this mitochondrial-cytosolic exchange, we develop a quantitative, high-resolution imaging approach to map MDM2 and TFAM localization. In a TKI-resistant clinical cohort (n = 76), we revealed a positive correlation between AKT activation, MDM2 phosphorylation and TFAM mitochondrial trafficking, defining a spatial, subcellular biomarker signature of metabolically reprogrammed TKI resistance. Pharmacologic disruption of the AKT-MDM2-TFAM signaling axis reverse TKI resistance, linking mitochondrial genome regulation to therapy resistance and suggesting a metabolic vulnerability for combinatorial targeting.Subject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Oncology

Introduction

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are frontline therapies for oncogene-addicted cancers, yet durable responses are limited by the emergence of acquired resistance. In EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs), acquisition of secondary gatekeeper mutations such as T790M mutation1,2 or amplifications of bypass signaling axis3,4 are associated with tumour relapse. Beyond genetic alteration, metabolic reprogramming towards oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) has emerged as an alternative resistance mechanism. In this context, enhanced mitochondrial respiration supports mesenchymal transition, cell proliferation in tumours, and the bioenergetic and macromolecular anabolic demands of resistant tumors5–7. Although certain molecularly defined resistance mechanisms, such as MET amplification in NSCLC8,9, can be addressed with rational drug combination, metabolically reprogrammed subsets remain largely undruggable.

We and others have previously shown that prolonged TKI exposure in oncogene-driven tumours, including EGFR-mutant NSCLC and BRAF-activated malignant melanoma, induces OXPHOS activation through mitochondrial biogenesis and Complex I upregulation6,7,10. Targeting OXPHOS not only suppressed tumour growth but also restored TKI sensitivity, as demonstrated in models of NSCLC, melanoma, and chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). In CML, the FDA-approved mitochondrial translation inhibitor tigecycline synergizes with imatinib to eradicate resistant drug resistant progenitor cells11. These findings underscore OXPHOS dependence as a targetable metabolic vulnerability; yet, the upstream mechanism of this metabolic switch remains elusive, thus limiting biomarker-guided stratification of TKI-resistant patients for targeted intervention.

The mitochondrial transcription factor TFAM is essential for facilitating the transcription and maintenance of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which encodes 13 subunits of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC). Specifically, TFAM not only binds to the promoter regions to maintain mtDNA transcription and mtDNA homeostasis but also regulating mitochondrial respiration11. Recent study reported that the MDM2 oncoprotein translocate to the mitochondria and suppress OXPHOS by competing with TFAM for mtDNA binding in a p53-independent, non-canonical fashion12. However, cytosolic MDM2 can also sequester the complex I subunit NDUFS1, inhibiting mtDNA transcription and OXPHOS activity13,14, highlighting the context-dependent nature of MDM2’s mitochondrial functions. Coupled with our prior observations of TFAM upregulation in OXPHOS-driven TKI-resistant cells10, these findings suggested that dynamic MDM2-TFAM exchange might regulate mitochondrial respiration during TKI resistance.

Here, we delineate a mitochondrial trafficking mechanism in which AKT-driven phosphorylation of MDM2 regulates its cytosolic redistribution and competitive interaction at mtDNA with TFAM. We show that adaptive AKT activation – resulting from TKI resistance – promotes TFAM enrichment in mitochondria, thereby enhancing mtDNA transcription and OXPHOS activity. Using a high-resolution imaging platform and a cohort of TKI-refractory clinical specimens, we validate this MDM2-TFAM shuttling mechanism and establish a spatial, subcellular biomarker signature that may stratify tumours with metabolic resistance. Our findings uncover an AKT-MDM2-TFAM signalling axis that connects mitochondrial function to therapy resistance and propose a metabolic vulnerability for combinatorial targeting in TKI-resistant cancers.

Results

MDM2 relocates from mitochondria to cytoplasm in TKI resistance

We previously reported that TKI-resistant models of EGFR-mutant NSCLC (gefitinib-resistant HCC827; HCC827-GR) and BRAF-mutant melanoma (vemurafenib-resistant A375, A375-VR) acquired high OXPHOS activity and become sensitive to OXPHOS inhibitors10. This metabolic phenotype deviates from the classical Warburg effect15,16, and was not addressed by a metabolic gene signature in our genomic profiling of resistant cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

MDM2 translocation to the mitochondria downregulates mitochondrial complex I activity by suppressing mitochondrial respiration13. We hypothesized that MDM2 subcellular trafficking might be altered during acquisition of TKI resistance to drive metabolic switch to OXPHOS. Subcellular fractionation in our cellular models revealed that MDM2 predominantly resides in the mitochondria in the parental HCC827 and A375 cells, but shifted to the cytoplasmic fraction in HCC827-GR and A375-VR (Fig. 1A). The total MDM2 protein expression remained unchanged (Fig. 1B), indicating that altered translocation rather that protein abundance accompanies TKI resistance. Given our previous findings of TFAM as a mediator of OXPHOS switch10, we next examined the protein expression and localization of TFAM. In contrast to MDM2, TFAM was upregulated in the mitochondrial fraction of TKI-resistant cells (Fig. 1B). Given that the mRNA expression of TFAM remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 1B), this indicates that the increase of TFAM expression is not driven by transcriptional upregulation.

Our subsequent high-resolution immunofluorescence confirmed these observations. In the parental HCC827 cells, MDM2 colocalised strongly with the mitochondrial marker TOM20, but showed marked reduced colocalization in HCC827-GR cells (Manders’ coefficient 0.597 vs. 0.361, p = 0.0232; Pearson’s colocalization coefficient 0.796 vs. 0.443, p = 0.0065) (Fig. 1C, D). Conversely, TFAM-TOM20 colocalization increased in HCC827-GR cells (Manders’ coefficient 0.217 vs. 0.547, p = 0.0081; Pearson’s colocalization coefficient 0.215 vs. 0.751, p = 0.0008) (Fig. 1E, F). Similar reciprocal shifts were observed in A375 and its resistant A375-VR counterpart (Supplementary Fig. 1C). These findings suggest that TKI resistance is accompanied by MDM2 export from mitochondria and compensatory TFAM accumulation, consistent with enhanced mitochondrial transcription and OXPHOS activity.

MDM2 and TFAM competitively bind mtDNA to regulate OXPHOS

MDM2 inhibits mitochondrial transcription by occupying mtDNA promoter12, whereas TFAM promotes mtDNA transcription in a sequence-specific manner17 (Supplementary Fig. 2A). To evaluate whether competitive binding underlies the reciprocal localization of MDM2 and TFAM, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays at the light-strand promoter region. We observed that MDM2-mtDNA binding was significantly reduced, whereas TFAM-mtDNA binding was significantly increased in TKI-resistant cells compared with parental cells (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Given that MDM2 and TFAM knockouts are embryonically lethal18–20, we employed small interfering RNA to acutely deplete each protein individually to probe their functional interplay. Silencing TFAM in TFAM-high HCC827-GR cells induced mitochondrial relocalization of MDM2 (Pearson’s coefficient = 0.438 vs 0.775, p = 0.031) (Fig. 2A, B). Conversely, MDM2 knockdown in parental HCC827 cells drastically enhance TFAM mitochondrial colocalization, whereas MDM2 overexpression did not observe any significant changes (Fig. 2C, D). Interestingly, silencing of MDM2 in HCC827-GR cells – where TFAM is mitochondrial bound – did not increase mitochondrial TFAM further, but MDM2 overexpression reduced mitochondrial TFAM (Fig. 2C, D). Collectively, these reciprocal effects support a model in which MDM2 and TFAM compete for mtDNA binding to modulate mitochondrial biogenesis.

Although MDM2 is commonly known for p53 degradation, its functional role of MDM2 is reportedly p53-independent12,13. Consistent with this, neither p53 silencing nor overexpression affected subcellular distribution of MDM2, or TFAM expression in parental or resistant HCC827 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In addition, we observed no change in the expression levels of both p53 and p53-related protein p16 between HCC827 and HCC827-GR cells (Supplementary Fig. 3B), confirming that MDM2 trafficking during TKI resistance occurs independently of p53.

MDM2- TFAM equilibrium determines OXPHOS activity and mitochondria transcription

To determine how reciprocal mitochondrial localization of MDM2 and TFAM affects respiration function in mitochondria, we measured oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in parental and TKI-resistant models. In parental HCC827 and A375 cells, which retained higher mitochondrial MDM2 (Fig. 1A), MDM2 silencing increased OCR to levels comparable to those of their resistant counterparts (Fig. 3A, B). In contrast, MDM2 silencing had minimal effect in TKI-resistant HCC827-GR and A375-VR cells (Fig. 3A, B). TFAM silencing led to the opposite phenotype, as demonstrated by reduced OCR in both parental and resistant cells, with a more pronounced reduction in HCC827-GR (Fig. 3C, D). These results indicate that TKI-resistant cells rely on TFAM to sustain OXPHOS activity, whereas mitochondrial MDM2 restrains respiration in parental cells.

We next examined the individual effects of MDM2 and TFAM on mtDNA transcription mtDNA-encoded complex I subunits (MT-ND1-6). Consistent with earlier work showing that mitochondrial MDM2 inhibits mtDNA transcription12, MDM2 knockdown significantly increased MT-ND transcripts in HCC827 parental cells, but had modest effect in HCC827-GR cells (Fig. 3E). In contrast, TFAM knockdown markedly reduced MT-ND transcripts in HCC827-GR cells (Fig. 3F).

To investigate whether the altered mtDNA transcription reflected changes in mtDNA abundance, we quantified mtDNA copy number. MDM2 silencing did not alter mtDNA copy number in either parental or resistant cells, whereas TFAM silencing reduced mtDNA copy number only in HCC827-GR cells (Fig. 3G). Similar observations were obtained in A375 and A375-VR models (Supplementary Fig. 4). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that mitochondrial biogenesis respiratory function is governed by a context-dependent equilibrium between MDM2 and TFAM. Specifically, MDM2 constrains OXPHOS in TKI-sensitive cells, whereas TFAM drives mtDNA replication and transcription in resistant cells.

MDM2 phosphorylation at Ser166/Ser186 correlates with TKI resistance

We next examined the mechanism disrupting mitochondrial translocation of MDM2 when cells acquired resistance. Total MDM2 mRNA and protein expressions were unchanged between parental and HCC827-GR cells (Figs. 1A and 4A), suggesting the involvement of post-translational regulation instead of expression level in the translocation of MDM2. As MDM2 stability21 and subcellular trafficking22 are modulated by phosphorylation of its functional residues, we profiled site-specific phosphorylation in our cellular models. In resistant HCC827-GR and A375-VR, we observed increased phosphorylation of MDM2 at Ser166 and Ser186, but not at Ser395 site (Fig. 4B). It has been previously reported in tumour cells that these sites promote subcellular translocation of MDM2 and pro-survival signaling22.

Given that Ser166 is a known AKT target23,24, and that Ser395 is driven by ATM to activate p53-driven apoptosis23,25,26, we hypothesized that PI3K/AKT activation underlies MDM2 phosphorylation in resistant models. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of RNA-seq data from resistant versus parental cells. The data revealed significant up-regulation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, along with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), JAK-STAT, and KRAS signalling pathways, in both TKI-resistant cancer models (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 5). Consistently, we detected elevated AKT1 expression and Ser473 phosphorylation (Fig. 4D) as well as an increase in membrane-associated p-AKT (Ser473) and total AKT expression in the resistant cells (Fig. 4E). The concordance between the RNA-seq and protein profiling confirmed the activation of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway. This aligns with clinical-reported observation of PI3K/AKT hyperactivation in treatment-resistant NSCLC and breast cancers27,28.

These findings support a novel model in which PI3K/AKT-mediated phosphorylation of MDM2 at Ser166/Ser186 drives its displacement from mitochondria, thereby relieving MDM2-dependent repression of mitochondrial transcription and enabling TFAM-dependent OXPHOS in TKI-resistant cells.

TKI-resistant cells exhibit selection vulnerability to AKT inhibition

AKT is known to directly phosphorylates MDM2 at Ser166, Ser186 to promote tumour growth23,24, and have been implicated in mitochondrial regulation29,30. Building on our finding that AKT-driven phosphorylation of MDM2 displaces it from mitochondria, we hypothesized that AKT functions as an upstream driver of OXPHOS activation in TKI-resistant cells. As clinically approved OXPHOS inhibitors remain limited, we postulated that targeting upstream kinases mediating mitochondrial respiration might provide a more tractable therapeutic strategy TKI-resistant tumours.

To identify pathway dependencies, we performed a 515 anti-cancer compounds high-throughput drug screen encompassing several signalling cascades – including PI3K/AKT, JAK/STAT, and MAPK – using our TKI-resistant models. Both HCC927-GR and A375-VR cells demonstrated striking sensitivity to PI3K/AKT inhibitors compared with other targeted agents (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 6A). By contrast, susceptibility to inhibitors of JAK/STAT3 and other pathways remained largely unchanged between resistant and parental cells (Supplementary Fig. 6B–H), consistently with our previous findings that OXPHOS activation is independent of STAT3 activity10. Among the PI3K/AKT inhibitor, the allosteric AKT compound MK-2206 and GSK690693 showed the most pronounced differential activity in resistant cells, whereas the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 produced no observable difference between parental and resistant cells (Fig. 5B, C, Supplementary Fig. 6G). These results suggest the specific dependency on AKT, rather than upstream PI3K, in metabolically reprogrammed TKI-resistant tumours.

Disruption of AKT-MDM2 axis restores TKI resistance through OXPHOS suppression

We wondered whether inhibition of AKT signalling would impair MDM2 phosphorylation and OXPHOS activity. Treatment of HCC827-GR cells with MK-2206 resulted in dose-dependent decrease of MDM2 Ser166 phosphorylation (Fig. 5D). Conversely, overexpression of AKT1 in parental HCC827 cells reduced MDM2 mitochondrial translocation, as indicated by a decrease in the MDM2-TOM20 colocalization (Fig. 5E, F). Genetic knockdown of AKT1 in HCC827-GR cells resulted in a reciprocal increase in MDM2 mitochondrial translocation (Fig. 5E, F). Functionally, both AKT1 silencing and MK-2206 treatment significantly reduced OCR levels in resistant HCC827-GR cells, but not in parental HCC827 (Fig. 5G, H), confirming that AKT activity is indeed necessary to sustain MDM2 phosphorylation and OXPHOS in the resistant state.

The clinical development of OXPHOS inhibitors has so far yielded limited benefit31,32, largely because clinical response is short-lived with unknown cause. We previously demonstrated that the first-generation OXPHOS inhibitor OPB-51602, produces transient suppression of mitochondrial respiration in TKI-resistant NSCLC cells10. Consistently, the second-generation clinical-stage OPB-111077 (OPC)33 reduced basal OCR in both HCC827 and HCC827-GR cells within 2–4 h, but failed to maintain suppression at 24 h (Fig. 6A), indicating incomplete or reversible OXPHOS inhibition, likely through metabolic compensation. Given that AKT1 activity alone is sufficient to sequester MDM2 in the cytoplasm and sustain OXPHOS, we reasoned that combinatorial inhibition of AKT and OXPHOS treatment could prevent this adaptive metabolic escape and prolong response duration. Indeed, combining OPC with the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 produced sustained OXPHOS inhibition in HCC827-GR for up to 72 h (Fig. 6B). This highlights that a durable metabolic inhibition in metabolically reprogrammed cells could be achieved only through simultaneous targeting of both pathways.

To evaluate long-term effects on tumorigenic potential, we assessed the anchorage-independent growth under different permutations of OXPHOS inhibitor (OPC), AKT inhibitor (MK2206), and EGFR TKI (gefitinib). As expected, HCC827 cells remained sensitive to gefitinib, while HCC827-GR cells were selectively inhibited by OPC or MK2206 (Fig. 6C, D). Notably, neither OPC-gefitinib nor MK-2206-gefitinib combination completely suppressed colony formation in resistant cells (Fig. 6C, D), suggesting a metabolic bypass mechanism in these cells. As HCC827-GR has been shown to be OXPHOS-dependent and a combination of OPC and MK-2206 induced sustained OXPHOS inhibition (Fig. 6B), we investigated the effects of this combination on cell survival. In contrast, the combination of OPC and MK2206 induced synthetic lethality in HCC827-GR cells, as demonstrated by the complete ablation of colony formation (Fig. 6C, D). These findings identify AKT as a key mechanistic node that maintain OXPHOS dependence in TKI-resistant cells to mediate cellular survival, and support a therapeutic strategy of dual AKT/OXPHOS blockade to overcome metabolic resistance.

Tissue validation of reciprocal mitochondria localization of MDM2 and TFAM in TKI-refractory tumours

To translate our mechanistic discoveries into a clinically actionable biomarker strategy, we assessed whether reciprocal mitochondrial trafficking of MDM2 and TFAM could serve as a spatial signature of TKI resistance in EGFR-mutant NSCLC tumours. Using multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC), we profiled treatment-naive (n = 40) and TKI-refractory (n = 36) tumours, including 22 paired tumour samples collected before and after the development of resistance. As current biomarker strategy primarily focus on single IHC staining to observe changes in protein expression changes, we developed a subcellular-based approach by co-staining with the mitochondrial marker TOM20 to track protein redistribution. In one representative pre-TKI sample, MDM2 (green) strongly colocalized with the mitochondrial marker TOM20 (red), producing a yellow overlap signal indicative of mitochondrial localization. Following TKI resistance, this MDM2-TOM20 colocalization was markedly diminished, accompanied by a reciprocal increase in TFAM-TOM20 colocalization (white), as quantified by the inform tissue analysis pipeline (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Fig. 7A). Our analysis on the paired tissues revealed that this reciprocal subcellular shift – loss of mitochondrial MDM2 with increased mitochondrial TFAM – was observed in 21 of 22 cases (Supplementary Fig. 7), highlighting MDM2-TFAM trafficking as a robust spatial marker of acquired TKI resistance.

Across the unpaired cohort, treatment-naive tumour cohort displayed predominantly cytoplasmic MDM2 with limited mitochondrial fraction (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Fig. 7B). In contrast, TKI-refractory tumours showed a marked decrease in MDM2-TOM20 colocalization (mean Pearson’s correlation coefficient difference: −0.2249 ± 0.02481, p < 0.0001), indicating a loss of MDM2 mitochondrial translocation despite stable total MDM2 expression (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Fig. 7B). Conversely, TFAM-TOM20 colocalization significantly increased (mean Pearson’s correlation coefficient difference: 0.2144 ± 0.02211, p < 0.0001), suggesting enhanced TFAM mitochondrial localization in TKI-refractory tumours (Fig. 7C, Supplementary Fig. 7C). The reciprocal changes support a model in which MDM2 and TFAM dynamically exchange between the cytoplasm and mitochondria to sustain OXPHOS during TKI resistance.

To further connect this spatial signature to the upstream AKT-MDM2 mechanism, we quantified AKT activity in paired NSCLC tumours (n = 20) by comparing for the expression of AKT phosphorylation at Ser473 (pAKT; yellow) and MDM2 phosphorylation at Ser166 (pMDM2; red) in CK+ tumour regions. TKI-refractory samples showed significant upregulation of pAKT (mean difference = 26.6 ± 12.8, p = 0.0259) and pMDM2 (mean difference = 29.2 + 16.5, p = 0.0466) (Fig. 7D, E). Notably, pMDM2 positively correlated with pAKT in EGFR-mutant NSCLC tumours (r2 = 0.238, p = 0.088) (Fig. 7F). This is consistent with our in vitro observations that AKT activation promotes cytosolic retention of MDM2 and mitochondrial accumulation of TFAM.

Together, these findings establish reciprocal MDM2-TFAM trafficking not only as a mechanistic driver of OXPHOS dependence, but also as a clinically measurable biomarker to detect metabolic reprogramming in TKI-resistant tumours. We aim to provide a rationale for patient stratification, specifically in trials testing dual AKT/OXPHOS blockade.

Discussion

Acquired resistance to TKIs remains a major clinical challenge in the treatment of oncogene-addicted cancers. Among the diverse resistance mechanisms identified, a metabolic shift toward OXPHOS has emerged as a recurrent hallmark of therapy-refractory tumours6,10,11, allowing cancer cells to meet their bioenergetic demands under therapeutic stress. Although OXPHOS inhibitors have revealed this metabolic dependency as a therapeutic vulnerability in vitro, their clinical impact has been limited by dose-limiting toxicities and the inherent metabolic plasticity of tumours31,32. These limitations underscore the importance of identifying upstream regulatory nodes that drive OXPHOS activation and could be therapeutically targeted with greater precision.

In this study, we uncover a previously unrecognized mechanism in which AKT-driven cytosolic retention of MDM2 relieves mitochondrial repression of TFAM, thereby enhancing mitochondrial transcription and sustaining OXPHOS in TKI-resistant tumours. While hyperactivation of the PI3K-AKT pathway is a well-established mediator of TKI resistance34–38, likely through PIK3CA mutations, PTEN loss, or AKT amplification39,40, our work reveals that AKT activation alone – independent of PI3K – can act as a metabolic driver by regulating mitochondrial protein trafficking. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of AKT, but not PI3K, selectively suppressed OXPHOS activity and reversed TKI resistance in vitro, highlighting AKT as a critical metabolic gatekeeper in this context.

Mechanistically, our findings integrate and extend prior current knowledge of the non-canonical mitochondrial functions of MDM2. MDM2 is best known as a nuclear E3 ligase that promotes p53 degradation, but recent studies have suggested that MDM2 can also localize to mitochondria to repress mtDNA transcription and inhibit ETC activity. We show that, in TKI-resistant cells, phosphorylation of MDM2 by AKT at Ser166/186 promotes its cytosolic retention, thereby disinhibiting TFAM and enhancing mtDNA transcription, complex I activity, and OXPHOS. Importantly, the increase in mitochondrial TFAM observed in resistant cells does not appear to arise from altered TFAM turnover mechanisms. As part of our mechanistic investigation, we examined two established regulators of TFAM stability41, the mitochondrial Lon protease (LonP1) and the mtDNA replisome component POLG2, and detected no expression differences between parental and resistant cells (Data not shown). These observations support a model in which TFAM accumulation is driven primarily by altered competition with MDM2, rather than changes in synthesis or degradation pathways. Together, this finding directly supports a causative link between AKT activation, MDM2 trafficking, and TFAM-dependent mitochondrial rewiring, potentially revealing a tractable therapeutic axis in TKI resistance.

Therapeutically, this pathway presents opportunities for rational combinatorial targeting. Direct OXPHOS inhibitors, such as OPB-51602 and its second-generation derivative OPB-111077, show transient suppression of mitochondrial respiration but fail to achieve durable responses due to metabolic compensation33. In addition, OXPHOS inhibitors lead to intrinsic toxicities, including lactic acidosis and peripheral neuropathy42. While TFAM is a direct driver of mitochondrial respiration, its inactivation is embryonically lethal and currently lacks druggable biochemical assays43. Existing MDM2 inhibitors, such as Nutlin-3 and its derivatives, are developed for specific targeting of p5344, and to our knowledge, no inhibitors of MDM2 phosphorylation have yet been developed for cancer therapy. By contrast, our findings show that dual inhibition of OXPHOS and AKT achieved sustained suppression of oxygen consumption and induced synthetic lethality in TKI-resistant cells, effectively overcoming metabolic plasticity. Importantly, AKT inhibitors – including capivasertib, ipatasertib, and the allosteric inhibitor MK-2206 – are already in clinical development45,46, providing a feasible path to translate these findings into therapeutic strategies.

This was further substantiated by the increased efficiency of colony formation inhibition with OXPHOS and AKT inhibitors in TKI-resistant cancers, potentially eradicating metabolically driven treatment resistance.

Beyond the mechanistic and therapeutic insights, our study introduces a novel biomarker strategy for patient stratification. Using mIHC, we developed a subcellular localization-based assay to quantify the reciprocal mitochondrial trafficking of MDM2 and TFAM as a spatial signature of TKI resistance. Unlike conventional biomarkers that focus solely on expression changes, this method captures dynamic changes in protein compartmentalization, which – in the context of our study models – directly reflects metabolic state and therapeutic vulnerability. Using a cohort of EGFR-mutant NSCLC tumours as proof-of-concept, we demonstrated that decreased mitochondrial MDM2 and increased mitochondrial TFAM strongly correlate with TKI-refractory disease, establishing a translatable methodology for incorporating subcellular trafficking biomarkers into clinical diagnostics. Such an approach could guide the selection of patients most likely to benefit from AKT/OXPHOS–targeted therapies and represents an important step toward precision oncology grounded in spatial proteomics, with the potential to expand to other cases such as kinases and E3 ligases is fundamental.

While our findings establish a novel AKT-MDM2-TFAM signalling axis as a driver of OXPHOS-dependent TKI resistance, several limitations warrant consideration. First, we currently lack in vivo or organoid models that capture the dynamic emergence of MDM2 trafficking during the transition from TKI sensitivity to resistance, limiting the mechanistic dissection of temporal events that underlie resistance development. Second, our clinical validation was restricted to EGFR-mutant NSCLC, and it remains uncertain whether this mechanism extends to other oncogene-driven malignancies. Finally, although our mIHC assay provides a robust tissue-based readout of mitochondrial trafficking, its broader clinical application may be constrained by the need for serial tumour sampling. This approach is most feasible in settings where repeated biopsies of relapse lesions are practical, and prospective studies will be required to determine whether such a strategy can reliably predict early resistance in real-world patient cohorts.

Cell Culture

HCC827 (ATCC, CRL-2868, RRID:CVCL_2063), HCC827-GR, and H1975 (ATCC, CRL-5908, RRID:CVCL_1511) cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA), while A375 (ATCC, CRL-1619, RRID:CVCL_0132) and A375-VR cells were cultured in DMEM high-glucose media (Nacalai Tesque, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS), and 1% L-glutamine (Gibco, USA). To maintain resistance to the respective TKI, HCC827-GR and A375-VR were cultured with 2 µM µM gefitinib and vemurafenib, respectively, in the cell culture media. All the cells were kept at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cell lines used in this study were authenticated via STR profiling and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using MycoStrip® (Invivogen, rep-mys-10) monthly.

High throughput screening (HTS) of compound libraries

HTS of anti-cancer and metabolic compound libraries (Selleckchem #L3000, #3700) was performed in collaboration with Dr. Giridharan Periyasamy (Genome Institute of Singapore). The Selleckchem Anti-cancer compound Library (#L3000) and Selleckchem Metabolism Library (L3700) were used for high-throughput chemical screening. All compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Approximately 1500 cells were seeded per well in a 384-well plate 24 h prior to drug treatment. For high-throughput studies, cultures were dispensed (50 μL) into 384-well plates using a MultiFlo dispenser, while drugs were 100-fold diluted in DMSO and maintained at 1% (v/v) across all drug concentrations and controls. Cell viability was assayed using CellTiter-Glo luminescent (Promega, USA) after 72 h of treatment. Luminescence signals were detected using a TECAN Spark® multi-mode plate reader with an integration time of 1000 ms.

RNA-Seq and GSEA Analysis

RNA samples were extracted using RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Whole-transcriptome sequencing of the mRNA was performed using Novogene. Paired-end reads were aligned to the hg38 genome using STAR v2.5.3a (RRID:SCR_015899)47, and transcript quantification was performed using RSEM v1.3 (RRID:SCR_013027)48 based on Gencode v45 annotation. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the R v4.4.0 package EBseq v1.28.0 (RRID:SCR_001085)49. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) v4.3.250 was performed on FPKM gene expression data using hallmark gene sets51.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) cell blocks were prepared for IHC staining of cell lines at the basal level. Briefly, the cells were spun down at 2500 rpm for 5 min and rinsed with 1XPBS. Cell pellets were obtained by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 5 min, and an equal volume of plasma (Sysmex Asia, Japan) was added to resuspend the cell pellet. Thrombin (Sysmex Asia, Japan) was added to the mixture to form a clot, which was fixed in 10% formalin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) overnight. After fixation, the clot was placed in a tissue processor, embedded in paraffin, and dried overnight at 37 °C. IHC staining was performed using a BOND-MAX Fully Automated IHC Staining System (Leica, Germany) using an optimized protocol and antibody dilution.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Approximately 30,000 cells were seeded into an 8-well chamber (Ibidi, Germany) and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cell culture media were aspirated and rinsed with 1X PBS before fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at 37 °C. The slides were blocked with blocking buffer (5% BSA, 0.3% Triton-X in 1XPBS) for 30 min and stained with primary antibodies at an optimized dilution in capturing buffer (2.5% BSA, 0.3% Triton-X, 1XPBS) overnight at 4 °C with shaking. The cells were subsequently stained with secondary antibodies and DAPI for 2 h at room temperature and imaged using a ZEISS Microscope.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

FFPE tissues were prepared for mIHC on 4 µm coated slides and performed using a BOND-MAX Fully Automated IHC Staining System (Leica, Germany) with an optimized protocol and antibody dilution with the following dye and antibody pair (AKOYA Biosciences and Biotium): For the 4-plex mIHC colocalization panel, four rounds of heat-induced antigen retrieval, primary antibody incubation, and Opal fluorophore incubation were carried out for each marker in the following sequence: MDM2, TFAM, TOM20, and pan-cytokeratin (CK). Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed by heating slides at 95 °C for 20 min with Bond Epitope Retrieval Solutions (Leica Biosystems). Antibodies used in this panel included anti-MDM2 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-965, RRID:AB_627207) with Opal 520 fluorophore (AKOYA Biosciences, FP1487001KT), anti-TFAM antibody (Abcam, ab119684, RRID:AB_10971668) with Opal 570 fluorophore (AKOYA Biosciences, FP1488001KT), anti-TOM20 antibody (Proteintech, 11802-1-AP, RRID:AB_2207533) with Opal 620 fluorophore (AKOYA Biosciences, FP1495001KT), and anti-pan cytokeratin antibody (Abcam, ab27988, RRID:AB_776222) with CF680R (Biotium, 92196).

For the 4-plex mIHC AKT panel, four rounds of heat-induced antigen retrieval, primary antibody incubation, and Opal fluorophore incubation were carried out for each marker in the following sequence: phospho-MDM2 (Ser166), phospho-AKT (Ser473), MDM2, and pan-cytokeratin (CK). Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed by heating slides at 95 °C for 20 min with Bond Epitope Retrieval Solutions (Leica Biosystems). Antibodies used in this panel included anti-phospho-MDM2 (Ser166) antibody (Abcam, ab131355, RRID:AB_11129223) with Opal 520 fluorophore (AKOYA Biosciences, FP1487001KT), anti-phospho-AKT1 (Ser473) antibody (Abcam, ab8932, RRID:AB_306146) with Opal 570 fluorophore (AKOYA Biosciences, FP1488001KT), anti-MDM2 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-965, RRID:AB_627207) with Opal 620 fluorophore (AKOYA Biosciences, FP1495001KT), and anti-pan cytokeratin antibody (Abcam, ab27988, RRID:AB_776222) with CF680R (Biotium, 92196).

Imaging and analysis were performed using the PhenoImager HT system (AKOYA Biosciences) and inFORM Tissue Analysis Software (AKOYA Biosciences). Pan-cytokeratin was used as a tumour marker for tissue segmentation, and the H scores of MDM2, p-MDM2 (Ser166), p-AKT (Ser473), and TFAM were calculated within the tumour regions identified by pan-cytokeratin staining (Supplementary Fig. 8A). TOM20, a mitochondrial marker, was used to assess the mitochondrial signal, and the H score for TOM20 was calculated to ensure consistent intensity levels before performing colocalization measurements (Supplementary Fig. 8B). The H-score calculation was performed using the workflow illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 8C.

Subcellular Fractionation

Approximately 2.5 million cells were seeded onto a 150 mm dish and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cell culture supernatant was aspirated and rinse with 1X cold PBS before scraping on ice. Cells were centrifuged at 1200PRM for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell supernatant was aspirated and the cell pellet was resuspended in Buffer A incubated on ice for 15 min. Homogenization was performed using a Dounce tissue grinder set with 40 strokes per cell lysate, and the lysates were spun down at 1000RCF for 10 min at 4 °C. Cell supernatants containing mitochondrial and cytosolic proteins were transferred to fresh Eppendorf tubes and spun down at 10,000RCF for 15 min at 4 °C. Cell supernatants containing cytosolic proteins were transferred to fresh tubes, while cell pellets containing mitochondrial proteins were resuspended in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer and stored at −80 °C for immunoblotting.

Immunoblotting

Protein estimation was performed using a Coomassie Blue Plus (Bradford) Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Lysates were denatured in loading dye (62.5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 2% (w/v) SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.01% (w/v) bromophenol blue, 1.25 M DTT), and 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) followed by transfer to PVDF membranes using the wet transfer method. The membrane was blocked with a 5% Blotting-Grade Blocker (Bio-Rad, USA) for an hour at room temperature on a shaker before probing with appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 40 °C. The membrane was washed three times with TBS + 0.1% Tween 20 before probing with isotype-specific secondary IgG-HRP for an hour. After washing with TBS + 0.1% Tween 20, the membranes were exposed to WesternBright Quantum HRP Substrate (Advansta, USA), and signals were detected using a Chemidoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Uncropped and unprocessed blots are provided in Supplementary Figs. 9–13.

Transfection

Approximately 150,000–200,000 cells were seeded for transfection and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Transfection of siRNA was performed using jetPRIME® transfection reagent (Polyplus, France) for 48 h, while transfection of the DNA plasmid was performed using FuGENE HD transfection reagent for 48 h (Promega, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell Viability Assay

Approximately 30,000 cells/well were seeded onto a Greiner 96-wells black microplate and incubated overnight at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR) Measurement

Cells were plated at respective optimal densities in Seahorse XF 24-well plates one day prior to measurement and incubated overnight at 37 °C, 5% CO2. The final drug concentration is shown in the figures, whereas substrate concentrations were 1 μM for oligomycin and FCCP, 0.5 μM for rotenone and antimycin A, and measurements were performed on a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer (Agilent, USA) and normalized to protein concentration (µg/µL).

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP was performed using the suggested cross-linking protocol from Abcam with optimization. Briefly, cross-linking of proteins to DNA was performed by adding formaldehyde dropwise to a final concentration of 0.75% and rotating at room temperature for 10 min. Glycine was subsequently added and incubated with shaking at room temperature before harvesting cells in cold 1X PBS. The cells were spun down at 1000RCF for 5 min at 4 °C, and the cell pellet was resuspended in ChIP lysis buffer and incubated on ice for 10 min. Lysates were sonicated to shear the DNA and centrifuged at 8000RCF for 10 min at 4 °C. Approximately 50 µg of DNA was used for the subsequent immunoprecipitation assays. Primary antibodies were added at the optimal concentration and rotated at 4 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 30 µL Dynabeads G was added to each tube and incubated overnight with rotation at 4 °C. Immunoprecipitated samples were centrifuged at 2000RCF for 1 min, and the supernatant was aspirated. Cell pellets were washed in low-salt, high-salt, and LiCl wash buffers, and the supernatant was aspirated each time. DNA was extracted and purified after adding elution buffer, 5 M NaCl, 10 mg/ml RNase A, and 20 mg/ml proteinase K (Invitrogen, USA) using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

mtDNA quantification using Real-Time PCR (RT-PCR)

Cells were collected and rinse with 1XPBS before performing DNA extraction using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed using SYBR™ Select Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative quantification (RQ) was performed using the Comparative ΔΔCT method. HPRT was used as an internal normalization control. The primer sequences are listed in Table 3.

Tissue sample study analysis

Archival FFPE tissue blocks were retrieved from the National University Hospital (NUH) Tissue Repository (TR). Tissue blocks were retrieved at two time points: diagnostic (pre-TKI) and after progression on TKI treatment (post-TKI), and sectioned onto coated slides for downstream analysis. Patient consent for archival retrieval was obtained before tissue retrieval. The use of human tissue samples was approved by National University Hospital Tissue Repository Domain Specific Scientific Review Working Committee (NUH TR DSSR) (approval number: TR DSSR 2021-027-6). To reduce bias, tissue samples were assigned slide codes, and staining was performed before the slide codes were matched to pre-TKI or post-TKI status and their respective subject ID. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines and regulations set forth by NUS-IRB, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives prior to sample collection. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed using n = 3 biological replicates, and each biological replicate was analyzed in technical triplicate where applicable. All quantifications of microscopy experiments are performed on more than 10 cells per biological replicate. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software v10.3.0. Student’s t-test was performed to compare the two groups to calculate the significance of the differences. When comparisons across more than two groups were required, two-way ANOVA was employed to calculate the significance differences. For dose response curve comparison, non-linear regression was performed, and EC50 fold shift was quantified to calculate the significance of the shift in the dose-response curve. Mander’s and Pearson’s colocalization coefficient of the immunofluorescent-stained cells were calculated using ImageJ (RRID:SCR_002285) and JaCoP52. For mIHC analysis of tissue samples, Pearson’s colocalization coefficient was calculated using Celleste Image Analyzer software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and inFORM® Tissue Finder (AKOYA Bioscience) advanced image analysis software using the workflow illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 6.
