Age at menopause and risk of sarcopenia in postmenopausal women: evidence from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Yachai Li, Liang Qin, Yan Wang, Shentai Li, Li Ling, Nan Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMenopausal age is closely associated with women's health, but research on its association with sarcopenia risk remains insufficient. Our study aimed to explore how menopausal age affects the risk of sarcopenia in postmenopausal women.MethodsBased on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database, we included 3,410 postmenopausal women from 2011–2015. Participants were divided into five groups according to menopausal age: premature menopause (< 40 years), early menopause (40–44 years), intermediate menopause (45–49 years), normal menopause (50–55 years), and late menopause (> 55 years). Sarcopenia was identified using the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS 2019) criteria. Mixed-effects logistic regression models were used to analyze the association between menopausal age and sarcopenia.ResultsThe study included 3,410 postmenopausal women with a mean age of 64.39 ± 8.68 years and overall sarcopenia prevalence of 35.40%. Compared to the average age at menopause group (50–55 years; 1,593/3,410; 46.70%), premature menopause (127/3,410; 3.70%), early menopause (385/3,410; 11.30%), intermediate menopause (1,098/3,410; 32.20%), and late menopause (207/3,410; 6.10%) were all associated with increased sarcopenia risk. Compared to normal menopause (50–55 years), early menopause (40–44 years), intermediate menopause (45–49 years), and late menopause (> 55 years) were associated with statistically significant increased sarcopenia risk, while premature menopause (< 40 years) showed a non-significant elevated risk (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 0.89–2.21, p = 0.1438). This association was stronger among women with late menarche, rural residence, married status, non-retirement, or low education levels.ConclusionsBoth earlier and later than average menopausal age may be associated with increased sarcopenia risk in Chinese postmenopausal women, although the association for premature menopause did not reach statistical significance in fully adjusted models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-026-02358-1.Keywords: Age at menopause, Middle-aged and elderly women, Sarcopenia, Women's health

Introduction

Sarcopenia, an age-related condition characterized by progressive decline in muscle mass and function, commonly affects individuals over 40 years of age and has emerged as a significant public health challenge in aging societies worldwide [1]. Research demonstrates that sarcopenia not only substantially increases the risk of falls, fractures, and disability, but is also closely associated with various chronic diseases including cardiovascular disease and diabetes, thereby seriously compromising quality of life and life expectancy among older adults [2, 3]. Studies suggest that multiple social and biological factors may contribute to sex differences in sarcopenia, including women's longer life expectancy compared to men [4], lower levels of physical activity [5], nutritional disparities [5], and female-specific reproductive risk factors such as pregnancy and menopause [6]. From an epidemiological perspective, sarcopenia affects approximately 10–16% of older adults globally, with higher prevalence rates observed in women (17%) compared to men (12%) [7].

Recent studies have increasingly explored the relationship between reproductive factors and sarcopenia risk. Estrogen plays a crucial role in promoting protein synthesis, inhibiting protein degradation, and maintaining muscle fiber integrity [8–10]. However, menopause leads to declining estrogen levels, potentially disrupting muscle function and accelerating muscle mass loss. Previous studies have found that earlier age at menopause is associated with poorer muscle function and accelerated muscle mass decline [11, 12]. However, the mechanisms underlying this association may be far more complex than simple estrogen deficiency. Other potential mechanisms may include age-related decline in physical activity levels, changes in nutritional intake and body composition, increased systemic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation associated with the menopausal transition [13, 14]. The average age at menopause among Chinese women is approximately 48.5 years, but with significant individual variation, with about 3.5% and 10% of women experiencing menopause before ages 40 and 45, respectively, and approximately 13% after age 55 [15, 16].

Existing research on the relationship between age at menopause and sarcopenia has primarily focused on the effects of premature menopause (< 40 years). A meta-analysis demonstrated that compared with women of normal age at menopause (> 45 years), women with early menopause (< 45 years) had significantly reduced muscle mass, while those with premature ovarian insufficiency (< 40 years) showed further declines in muscle strength and function [17]. These studies are predominantly cross-sectional in design, have limited sample sizes, and are mainly conducted in high-income Western countries [18, 19]. In contrast, Chinese women experience earlier natural menopause (mean age 48–49 years) compared to Western populations (≥ 50 years) [20], with a higher proportion experiencing premature (< 40 years, 3.4–4.1%) and early menopause (40–45 years, 6.8–11.6%) [16, 21]. China also differs from high-income countries in having lower awareness and utilization rates of hormone therapy (HT) [22, 23]. Hormone therapy, through estrogen supplementation, may help preserve skeletal muscle mass by maintaining muscle protein synthesis and reducing protein breakdown, thereby potentially modifying the relationship between menopausal age and sarcopenia risk [24, 25]. These population-specific characteristics may result in different effects of menopause on muscle health in Chinese women compared to findings from Western studies. Moreover, evidence regarding risk differences across various menopause age intervals (40–55 years), potential effects of late menopause (> 55 years), and heterogeneity of these associations across different population characteristics remains scarce and inconsistent.

In this study, we utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to evaluate the association between different ages at menopause and sarcopenia risk, and analyzed the development trends of sarcopenia in a nationally representative aging cohort from 2011 to 2015.

Methods

Study population

We utilized data from the CHARLS, which employed a multistage stratified sampling design. Baseline data were collected during 2011–2012, covering 450 communities (villages) across 150 counties (districts) nationwide, successfully recruiting 17,708 respondents from 10,257 households, ensuring national representativeness. CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal survey designed to examine the social, economic, and health circumstances of Chinese residents aged 45 and older [26]. The survey collects comprehensive data including demographics, health status, physical measurements, and functional assessments. Follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020, respectively [26]. CHARLS allows participants to withdraw due to death, relocation, refusal to continue participation, and simultaneously recruits new eligible participants as needed to maintain sample representativeness and study continuity.

Since CHARLS publicly released physical examination data for the first three waves, we selected these three time points for analysis. During 2011–2015, CHARLS included 12,925 female participants, of whom 10,166 participated in menopause-related visits. After data screening, 4,575 participants had complete sarcopenia data, and 3,808 remained after excluding those with missing covariates. After further excluding participants lacking specific menopause age information, 3,449 eligible study subjects were obtained. To ensure accuracy of natural menopause timing, we further excluded the following populations: (1) 29 women with a self-reported history of breast, ovarian, cervical, or endometrial cancer; (2) 10 women with menopause age below 28 years. Finally, 3,410 women were included as the analytical sample (Fig. 1).

Menopause age

In CHARLS, information on women's menopausal status and timing was collected through self-reported questionnaires, primarily asking "Have you experienced menopause?" and "When did you start menopause?" Following established classifications in menopause research [20], participants were categorized into five groups based on age at menopause: premature menopause (< 40 years), early menopause (40–44 years), intermediate menopause (45–49 years), normal menopause (50–55 years), and late menopause (> 55 years). Following previous studies [27], we selected the 50–55 years group as the reference group for statistical comparison.

Identification of sarcopenia

Sarcopenia was identified using the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia criteria (AWGS 2019), which are based on three core indicators [28]: muscle strength, skeletal muscle mass, and physical performance. Muscle strength was assessed through handgrip strength testing, calculating the average of maximum grip strength values from both hands, with female grip strength < 18 kg defined as reduced muscle strength. Skeletal muscle mass was evaluated using appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASM/Ht2). ASM was calculated using a validated prediction equation developed for Asian populations [28, 29]: ASM = 0.193 × body weight + 0.107 × height (cm)—4.157 × sex—0.037 × age—2.631, then standardized by dividing by height squared to obtain ASM/Ht2 (kg/m2). This equation has been validated against DXA measurements and is recommended for use in large-scale epidemiological studies where direct body composition assessment is not feasible [30, 31]. A reduction in muscle mass is identified when the index falls below the 20th percentile threshold. Physical performance was assessed using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), comprising three tests: balance test, walking speed, and chair stand test, with total scores ranging from 0–12 points, and scores ≤ 9 points defined as declined physical performance [28]. Sarcopenia was identified as low muscle mass accompanied by either low muscle strength or poor physical performance [28].

Covariates

Based on previous studies [18, 27], we included multiple potential confounding factors as covariates for adjustment. Demographic characteristics included age (continuous variable, in years), residential area (urban, rural), and ethnicity (Han, other ethnicities). Socioeconomic characteristics included education level (primary school or below, middle school, high school or above), marital status (married, other), retirement status (retired, not retired), and household size (number of household members). Reproductive health-related factors included number of surviving biological children and age at menarche. Lifestyle factors included smoking status (current smoking, non-smoking) and alcohol consumption status (current drinking, non-drinking). Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with all VIF values below 5 (Table S1), indicating no significant multicollinearity issues.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. For between-group comparisons, continuous variables were analyzed using analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis test according to data distribution, and categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square test.

Given the longitudinal nature of our study with repeated measurements on the same individuals across three waves (2011, 2013, 2015), we employed mixed-effects logistic regression models to account for within-subject correlation and between-subject heterogeneity [32, 33]. This modeling approach allows for the inclusion of all available observations while appropriately handling the dependence structure of repeated measures data [33]. The association between menopause age and sarcopenia was assessed using a series of mixed-effects logistic regression models with sarcopenia status as the binary outcome variable. Follow-up time was operationalized as years since baseline and was treated as a continuous variable in all models. Random slope effects for time were not included as preliminary analyses indicated minimal variance in individual trajectories, consistent with established practice in similar longitudinal studies [27]. In all models, menopause age groups, time, and their interaction terms, along with covariates, were treated as fixed effects, while individual-specific random intercepts were included to account for clustering of repeated observations within the same participant. To examine the robustness of associations and minimize potential confounding, we constructed three progressively adjusted models following established practices in epidemiological research [29, 34]. Model 1 adjusted only for basic demographic factors: age, urban/rural residence, and ethnicity. Model 2 additionally adjusted for socioeconomic characteristics: education level, marital status, and retirement status. Model 3 (fully adjusted model) further adjusted for reproductive and lifestyle factors: household size, number of surviving biological children, age at menarche, smoking, and alcohol consumption status. To test whether the association between menopause age and sarcopenia changed over time, all models included interaction terms between menopause age groups and time. Random effects for individuals were included in all models to control for correlation among observations within the same study subject. To further explore patterns of sarcopenia risk changes over time, predicted probabilities of sarcopenia for each menopause age group at baseline 2011, 2-year follow-up 2013, and 4-year follow-up 2015 were generated based on the fully adjusted mixed-effects model (Model 3), with random effects set to zero and covariates held at their mean or modal values to obtain population-average estimates [35, 36].

To explore the influence of different population characteristics on the association between menopausal age and sarcopenia, we conducted pre-specified subgroup analyses based on Model 3 Subgroup analyses included age at menarche groups divided by sample median (early menarche, late menarche), residential area groups (rural, urban), marital status groups (married, unmarried/widowed), retirement status groups (not retired, retired), and education level groups (middle school or below, high school or above).

To assess potential nonlinear dose–response relationships between menopause age and sarcopenia risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was employed. A nonlinear model was constructed based on ordinary logistic regression, incorporating menopause age in RCS form into the regression equation, with 4 knots positioned at the 10th, 35th, 65th, and 90th percentiles of menopause age distribution. Using menopause age 52 years (65th percentile) as the reference value, the model simultaneously adjusted for all covariates (Model 3). Likelihood ratio tests were used to evaluate whether significant nonlinear associations existed between menopause age and sarcopenia risk, and dose–response relationship curves were plotted to display trends in association strength.

Sensitivity analysis

To verify the robustness of main study results, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, we restricted the analytical sample to women with menopausal age within the 30–60 years range to exclude extreme menopause age values that might affect result stability [27]. Natural menopause before age 30 is extremely rare (< 0.1%) and typically represents pathological premature ovarian insufficiency rather than natural variation [37, 38]. Second, we employed a three-category menopause age classification (early menopause < 45 years, normal menopause 45–55 years, late menopause > 55 years) to evaluate the impact of different grouping methods on study conclusions [39, 40]. This simplified classification aligns with widely used definitions in epidemiological studies, where menopause before age 45 is considered early menopause and is associated with increased health risks [39, 40]. Third, we excluded participants aged < 55 years at baseline to reduce potential confounding effects of correlation between age and menopause age on results. Additionally, we progressively added depression symptom scores (CESD-10) and total household income to Model 3 to test result robustness.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and sarcopenia prevalence

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of study subjects grouped by menopausal age. The study included 3,410 postmenopausal women with a mean age of 64.39 ± 8.68 years. Among them, 127 women (3.7%) experienced premature menopause (before age 40), 385 women (11.3%) experienced early menopause (ages 40–44), 1,098 women (32.2%) reached menopause at ages 45–49, 1,593 women (46.7%) reached menopause at ages 50–55, and 207 women (6.1%) experienced late menopause (after age 55). The overall sarcopenia prevalence was 35.4% (95% CI: 33.8%−37.0%). The sarcopenia prevalence varied across menopause age groups: the early menopause group had the highest prevalence at 43.1%, followed by the late menopause group at 43.5% and the premature menopause group at 39.4%, while the 50–55 years menopause group had the lowest prevalence at 31.5%. Over 90% of women were Han Chinese, and the majority had education levels of middle school or below (85.2%). More than 60% of women resided in rural areas, with the late menopause group having the highest proportion of rural residence at 65.7%. Regarding marital status, 76.8% of women were married, and 87.7% had not yet retired. In terms of lifestyle habits, 93.2% of women were non-smokers, and 87.5% did not drink alcohol. The mean age at menarche was 16.11 years, average household size was 3.38 people, and the average number of living children was 3.15 across all menopause age groups.

Association between menopause age and sarcopenia risk

Table 2 presents the results of multivariable-adjusted mixed-effects logistic regression models examining the association between menopause age and sarcopenia risk, with women aged 50–55 years at menopause as the reference group. Across all models, menopause occurring earlier or later than 50–55 years was consistently associated with higher sarcopenia risk. The magnitude of associations gradually decreased from Model 1 to Model 3, suggesting that socioeconomic and lifestyle factors partially mediate these associations. In the fully adjusted Model 3, compared to women with menopause at ages 50–55 years, women with premature menopause (< 40 years) had 40% higher odds of sarcopenia (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 0.89–2.21, p = 0.1438), though this did not reach statistical significance. Women with early menopause (40–44 years) had 54% higher odds (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.17–2.03, p = 0.0023), women with intermediate menopause (45–49 years) had 33% higher odds (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.09–1.61, p = 0.0045), and women with late menopause (> 55 years) had 46% higher odds (OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.03–2.07, p = 0.0337). Notably, the early menopause group (40–44 years) demonstrated the strongest and most consistent association across all models (all p < 0.01). Table 2 also presents interaction effects between menopause age groups and time, but none of the menopause age groups showed statistically significant interactions (all p > 0.05), suggesting stable associations over the 4-year follow-up period.

Temporal trajectories of sarcopenia probability

Figure 2 illustrates the predicted sarcopenia probability distributions across menopause age groups over the 4-year follow-up period, derived from the fully adjusted mixed-effects model (Model 3). At baseline (2011), women with premature menopause (< 40 years) and late menopause (> 55 years) exhibited the highest predicted sarcopenia probabilities (41.8% and 42.9%, respectively), while women with normal menopause timing (50–55 years) had the lowest probability (34.0%). However, distinct temporal trajectories emerged across groups during follow-up. Women with premature menopause showed a substantial decline in predicted sarcopenia probability over time (from 41.8% at baseline to 13.0% at 4-year follow-up), whereas women with late menopause demonstrated a progressive increase (from 42.9% to 50.2%). By the 4-year follow-up (2015), the late menopause group had surpassed all other groups, attaining the highest predicted sarcopenia probability (50.2%), while the normal menopause group maintained consistently lower risk throughout the follow-up period (declining from 34.0% to 27.2%). These divergent temporal patterns suggest that menopause timing influences not only baseline sarcopenia risk but also the trajectory of risk evolution over time, with important implications for risk stratification and targeted prevention strategies in different menopause age groups.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses revealed important heterogeneity in the association between menopause age and sarcopenia risk across different population characteristics, with all analyses using menopause at age 50–55 years as the reference group. Among women with late menarche (> 16 years), premature menopause (< 40 years) and early menopause (40–44 years) were associated with 88% (OR = 1.88; 95%CI: 1.01–3.50, p = 0.0467) and 64% (OR = 1.64; 95%CI: 1.10–2.43, p = 0.0135) increased sarcopenia risk, respectively, whereas among women with early menarche (≤ 16 years), only the intermediate menopause group (45–49 years) showed significant association (OR = 1.49; 95%CI: 1.14–1.95, p = 0.0034), suggesting that late menarche may amplify the adverse effects of early reproductive cessation on muscle health (Table S2). Geographic residence also modified the menopause-sarcopenia relationship: rural women demonstrated elevated risk in early menopause (40–44 years: OR = 2.27; 95%CI: 1.37–3.76, p = 0.0015) and intermediate menopause (45–49 years: OR = 1.74; 95%CI: 1.23–2.44, p = 0.0016) groups, while urban women only exhibited increased risk in the late menopause group (> 55 years: OR = 1.53; 95%CI: 1.00–2.34, p = 0.0480), indicating distinct vulnerability patterns between urban and rural populations (Table S3). Marital status further stratified risk, with married women showing elevated sarcopenia risk across early (OR = 1.59; 95%CI: 1.15–2.18, p = 0.0048), intermediate (OR = 1.27; 95%CI: 1.02–1.59, p = 0.0361), and late (OR = 1.62; 95%CI: 1.08–2.41, p = 0.0191) menopause groups, whereas unmarried women demonstrated significant association only in the intermediate menopause group (OR = 1.50; 95%CI: 1.01–2.24, p = 0.0460), suggesting that marital status may influence the menopause-sarcopenia relationship through differences in social support or lifestyle factors (Table S4). Similarly, non-retired women exhibited elevated sarcopenia risk in early (OR = 1.53; 95%CI: 1.15–2.03, p = 0.0038), intermediate (OR = 1.27; 95%CI: 1.04–1.56, p = 0.0199), and late (OR = 1.53; 95%CI: 1.07–2.20, p = 0.0214) menopause groups, while retired women showed significant association only in the intermediate menopause group (OR = 2.16; 95%CI: 1.09–4.28, p = 0.0278), highlighting potential differences in occupational or lifestyle exposures between these groups (Table S5). Most notably, educational attainment emerged as a strong effect modifier: women with lower secondary education or less demonstrated significantly elevated risk across early (OR = 1.50; 95%CI: 1.12–2.00, p = 0.0065), intermediate (OR = 1.30; 95%CI: 1.06–1.59, p = 0.0128), and late (OR = 1.46; 95%CI: 1.01–2.09, p = 0.0416) menopause groups, whereas women with upper secondary education or above showed no significant associations in any menopause age group, suggesting that higher education may confer protective effects against menopause-related muscle loss, potentially through better health literacy, healthcare access, or healthier lifestyle behaviors (Table S6).

Dose–response relationship

Figure 3A displays the dose–response relationship between menopause age and sarcopenia risk based on multivariable-adjusted restricted cubic spline regression model estimates. Menopause age showed a "U-shaped" nonlinear association with sarcopenia risk (p for overall = 0.0002, p for nonlinear = 0.0003). Women with normal menopause at ages 50–55 had the lowest sarcopenia risk (31.6%), while women with premature menopause (< 40 years, 39.4%) and late menopause (> 55 years, 45.4%) both exhibited higher sarcopenia prevalence. Among these, the early menopause group (ages 40–44) had the highest prevalence, reaching 43.3% (Fig. 3B).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our study results. First, when restricting the analysis to women with menopause age 30–60 years, women with early menopause (ages 40–44) and intermediate menopause (ages 45–49) maintained significantly increased sarcopenia risk, while the risk association for late menopause (> 55 years) was slightly attenuated but still showed similar trends (Table S7). Second, when using a simplified three-category classification strategy, women with early menopause (< 45 years) had significantly increased sarcopenia risk (OR = 1.34; 95%CI: 1.06–1.70, p = 0.0145), while the risk increase for late menopause (> 55 years) did not reach statistical significance (Table S8). Third, after excluding women with baseline age < 55 years, the risk patterns across menopause age groups remained consistent, with the risk association in the early menopause group becoming more pronounced (Table S9). Finally, additional adjustment for depressive symptoms, household income, and their combined model all yielded results consistent with the main model, confirming the robustness of the primary findings (Table S10).

Discussion

Based on nationally representative longitudinal data from China, we identified a non-linear relationship between age at menopause and sarcopenia risk. Early menopause, intermediate menopause, and late menopause were all associated with elevated sarcopenia risk, suggesting that abnormal menopausal timing may constitute a risk factor affecting muscle health in later life among Chinese women. Furthermore, premature and early menopause (combined < 45 years) affected 15% of Chinese women in our study, a proportion slightly higher than observations reported in high-income countries [20], highlighting the burden of sarcopenia risk associated with early menopause faced by Chinese women. Additionally, the marginal association between late menopause and accelerated increase in sarcopenia risk over time warrants attention. These findings indicate that abnormal menopausal timing may serve as a marker for increased sarcopenia risk in later life among Chinese women, though causality cannot be inferred from observational data.

Research on the relationship between menopausal age and sarcopenia remains relatively limited in China, yet existing evidence has demonstrated the complexity of this relationship. A prospective cohort study of chronic diseases involving 2,059 middle-aged and elderly individuals from Guangzhou [41] found that both early and late menopause were associated with lower postmenopausal muscle mass compared to women experiencing menopause at typical ages. Our findings are generally consistent with international research. Studies from Spain, Poland, and other populations have collectively shown that women experiencing menopause before age 45–50 years face significantly higher sarcopenia risk and poorer muscle health indicators compared to those with later menopause [42–44]. These findings underscore the importance of menopausal timing as a risk factor for sarcopenia across different ethnic and geographic populations. While most studies focus on early menopause, emerging evidence suggests bidirectional effects. The International Mobility in Aging Study (IMIAS) conducted a retrospective analysis of 775 postmenopausal women aged 65–74 years from Albania, Brazil, Colombia, and Canada, revealing that women with premature menopausal age (< 40 years) had lower grip strength, while women with late menopausal age (≥ 55 years) had faster walking speed, indicating a non-linear relationship between menopausal age and muscle function [18]. Our study provides additional epidemiological evidence supporting a non-linear association between menopausal age and sarcopenia risk in a nationally representative Chinese sample. Given that sarcopenia encompasses a prodromal period that may extend up to 10 years, longer-term follow-up is needed to fully capture the long-term relationship between menopausal age and muscle health.

We found that the association between menopausal age and sarcopenia was stronger in specific populations, providing important clues for individualized prevention strategies. Among women with late menarche, the risks associated with premature and early menopause were greater, potentially reflecting the cumulative effects of reproductive endocrine axis dysfunction on muscle metabolism [45, 46]. This may also be related to a shorter reproductive lifespan and consequently fewer years of estrogen's protective effects on muscle. From a clinical perspective, women with both late menarche and early menopause may represent a high-risk group warranting earlier and more intensive sarcopenia screening and prevention interventions. Stronger associations were observed among rural women, married women, non-retired women, and women with low educational attainment. These findings may be related to factors such as nutritional status, physical activity patterns, socioeconomic status, and healthcare accessibility. Nutritional factors may play a particularly important role in explaining the elevated sarcopenia risk among Chinese women, especially those in rural areas. National nutrition surveys have documented declining dietary protein intake among older Chinese adults over recent decades, with rural residents consuming significantly less protein than their urban counterparts [47, 48]. Additionally, vitamin D deficiency affects 70.3% of Chinese older adults aged 65 years and older, substantially higher than the 17.4% observed in the United States [49]. These nutritional inadequacies may increase vulnerability to sarcopenia and partially explain the population-level differences in risk trajectories observed in our study. The interaction between suboptimal nutritional status and hormonal changes following menopause may further compound adverse effects on muscle health [13, 50]. Research has demonstrated that lower educational levels and socioeconomic status are closely associated with earlier menopausal age and more frequent vasomotor symptoms [51]. Furthermore, rural women may simultaneously face the dual pressures of malnutrition and heavy physical labor, resulting in more pronounced negative effects of hormonal changes on muscle health [52, 53]. The risk of sarcopenia in postmenopausal women can be effectively mitigated through adopting healthy lifestyle modifications, including ensuring adequate protein intake, vitamin D supplementation, and maintaining regular physical activity [54, 55]. These findings emphasize the importance of considering social determinants when developing sarcopenia prevention strategies, particularly for women in disadvantaged socioeconomic positions.

This study has several limitations. The CHARLS questionnaire did not provide a clear definition of menopause. While menopause is typically defined as the absence of menstrual periods for 12 consecutive months or more, participants may have incorrectly reported their age at menopause due to recall bias, which could result in misclassification and bias the observed associations in either direction. Second, we were unable to distinguish between women who experienced natural versus surgical menopause. Although we excluded women with a history of ovarian, cervical, or endometrial cancer, this approach may still result in misclassification. Third, the exclusion of 767 participants due to missing covariate data may have introduced selection bias, as missing data in large-scale surveys are unlikely to be completely random and may be associated with participant characteristics such as age, education, and rural residence. Furthermore, while we adjusted for factors such as age at menarche, age, and education level, we were unable to adjust for several important potential confounders, including physical activity, nutritional status, and hormone therapy use. Physical activity data were collected through sampling rather than universal assessment in the first three waves of CHARLS (2011, 2013, 2015), and comprehensive nutritional data were not available in the dataset. The lack of information on menopausal hormone therapy use represents a limitation in fully characterizing the relationship between menopausal age and sarcopenia risk. Additionally, our study participants had a mean of 3.15 biological children, reflecting reproductive patterns predominantly shaped before China's one-child policy implementation. This generation experienced substantially different reproductive trajectories compared to more recent cohorts, which may have implications for long-term muscle health [56, 57]. The generalizability of our findings to younger Chinese women requires cautious interpretation and validation in future studies.

This study has several limitations. First, age at menopause was self-reported and may be subject to recall bias; additionally, we could not distinguish between natural and surgical menopause. Second, the sample sizes for the premature menopause group (< 40 years, n = 127) and late menopause group (> 55 years, n = 207) were relatively small, and the non-significant association for premature menopause (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 0.89–2.21) may reflect low statistical power rather than a true absence of association. Third, due to the observational design, causality cannot be established, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., physical activity, nutritional status, and menopausal hormone therapy use) cannot be ruled out. Fourth, sarcopenia was identified using a validated prediction equation rather than direct DXA measurements, which may have introduced measurement error; moreover, the exclusion of 767 participants with missing covariate data may have introduced selection bias, and the generalizability of our findings to younger cohorts requires caution.

Conclusion

In this study, we found a non-linear relationship between female menopausal age and sarcopenia risk among middle-aged and elderly Chinese women. Extreme menopausal ages were associated with higher sarcopenia risk, and late menopause may be associated with more rapid risk progression over time. Future health interventions should consider incorporating extreme menopausal age as an important risk factor for later-life muscle health in women within this population.
