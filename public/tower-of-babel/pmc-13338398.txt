Investigation of progressive failure and instability in stratified rock slopes based on the NMM
Authors: Dawen Tan, Tao Wan, Chunyao Hou, Heng Cheng, Hongyi Zhang, Jingjie Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Numerical Manifold Method (NMM) introduces two independent cover systems—mathematical and physical—allowing discontinuities associated with crack propagation to be represented in a natural manner. To clarify the instability pattern and crack evolution of stratified rock slopes under top loading, this study implements a quadrilateral NMM framework incorporating the LT criterion, formulated from the Mohr–Coulomb strength theory, together with a damage factor. The LT criterion is used to determine the onset and propagation of cracks, while the damage factor serves to distinguish crack types. The proposed framework is first examined through a Brazilian splitting test, which confirms its capability to resolve crack development in a transparent and physically consistent way. It is then applied to systematically investigate the failure pattern and crack propagation characteristics of stratified slopes containing cracks of different inclinations or randomly distributed bedding cracks under overload conditions. The results show that, as crack inclination increases, the failure pattern of top-loaded stratified slopes shifts from shallow bedding-parallel shear failure to deep sliding–tensile composite failure. For slopes with randomly distributed bedding cracks, progressive failure follows a sequence of dispersed crack initiation, coalescence, penetration, and subsequent layered sliding–splitting. The proposed method captures the complex discontinuous failure process with high fidelity, demonstrating its effectiveness for stability analysis of stratified slopes with inherent cracks. The findings provide a theoretical basis for hazard assessment and protective design of top-loaded stratified rock slopes.Keywords: Numerical manifold method, Strength criterion, Stratified rock slopes, Failure mode, Crack propagation mechanismSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Cracks are a major cause of material failure and play a critical role in the stability and durability of geotechnical structures. In rock mechanics, analyzing cracks is essential for understanding and predicting failure processes. The presence of cracks within rock masses reduces the stability of geological strata, significantly diminishes the load-bearing capacity of rock structures, and may even induce catastrophic collapse. Therefore, understanding crack behavior, especially the dynamic processes and mechanisms of crack propagation, is essential for ensuring the safety and integrity of geotechnical systems.

To address the challenges associated with crack propagation, a variety of numerical approaches have been developed. These approaches can be broadly classified into continuum mechanics methods, discontinuum mechanics methods, and hybrid techniques. Within the continuum framework, widely used methods include the finite element method (FEM), boundary element method (BEM), generalized/extended FEM (GFEM/XFEM), and phase-field models. In particular, phase-field fracture formulations provide a diffusive representation of cracks and avoid explicit crack tracking, and recent developments have advanced mixed-mode phase-field modeling for rock-like materials and extended phase-field strategies to discontinuous rock-slope problems1–3. Discontinuum methods are specifically designed to handle crack growth and material discontinuities, including meshfree methods, particle methods, and peridynamics. Hybrid approaches combine the advantages of both continuum and discontinuum frameworks, exemplified by the Numerical Manifold Method (NMM)4–7, the combined finite-discrete element method (CDEM)8, and the finite-discrete element method (FDEM)9,10. In addition to physics-based numerical methods, data-driven and surrogate-model approaches have been developed to accelerate slope stability assessment by learning mappings from geometry and geotechnical parameters to stability-related outputs, providing efficient approximations when repeated analyses are required11,12.

The Numerical Manifold Method (NMM), proposed by Genhua Shi13, provides the advantage of handling both continuum and discontinuum problems within a unified framework. It combines the finite element method, suitable for continuum domains, with discontinuous deformation analysis methods designed for non-continuous problems14,15, enabling natural simulation of crack propagation without relying on techniques commonly used in the extended finite element method (XFEM), such as level-set methods or generalized Heaviside functions. Notably, updating level sets becomes a major challenge when multiple cracks are present16.

NMM has been widely applied to investigate crack propagation. Tsay et al.17 proposed a local mesh refinement and automatic remeshing scheme for NMM. Chiou et al.18 applied a virtual crack extension method to predict propagation paths of mixed-mode fractures. Yang et al.19 developed an optimized cover refinement strategy for NMM and validated it using benchmark examples under complex loading. Liu and Zheng20 introduced a multilayer cover method with structured grids for local integration. To improve accuracy, Ma et al. incorporated asymptotic crack-tip functions into the global NMM approximation to capture singularities, enabling simulation of complex crack patterns and propagation. Zheng et al.6 applied an MLS-based NMM to reduce static multi-crack propagation simulation to a nonlinear complementarity problem (NCP). Wu and Wong21 and Yang et al.22 used NMM’s contact-handling capability together with the Mohr–Coulomb crack initiation criterion to investigate the effects of friction angle and cohesion on propagation of compression-closed cracks.

Stratified rock slopes, characterized by layered geological structures, are prone to instability due to inherent discontinuities such as bedding planes and joints, which significantly influence failure mechanisms under various loading conditions. Classical studies have laid the foundation for understanding these slopes, focusing on kinematic analyses of failure modes like planar sliding, wedge failure, and toppling, often employing limit equilibrium methods and early numerical simulations to evaluate stability factors and the role of joint orientations23–27. These works highlighted the critical impact of structural fabric, shear strength reduction, and external factors like groundwater on slope deformation, providing analytical frameworks for block-flexure toppling and underground openings in jointed masses. In recent years, research has advanced to incorporate time-dependent degradation processes, discrete element modeling, and finite element approaches to simulate crack initiation, propagation, and coalescence in complex interlayered systems28–32. For instance, investigations into non-persistent joints and soft-hard interlayers have revealed shear-slip behaviors and stability decreasing as joint density increases or rock strength varies29,32. However, while these studies effectively capture overall failure patterns and safety factors through tools like UDEC and FLAC3D, there remains a gap in detailed analyses of crack types (e.g., tensile vs. shear) and the underlying propagation mechanisms28–32, particularly in top-loaded scenarios with random bedding cracks, which limits comprehensive hazard predictions for engineering applications.

NMM has proven to be a powerful tool for analyzing discontinuous deformations and simulating crack propagation in various geotechnical applications33,34. However, most previous studies focus on crack propagation patterns, with less attention to crack types and propagation mechanisms. In the present work, the strength criterion and a damage factor are used to determine crack propagation and identify crack types, aiming to investigate the instability failure modes and crack propagation mechanisms of stratified rock slopes under top-loading conditions.

Weak form: spatial and temporal discretization

Spatial discretization based on the NMM approximation

In the Numerical Manifold Method (NMM), numerical analysis is carried out within two overlapping cover systems: the physical and the mathematical covers. Interpolation is performed on the mathematical covers, while numerical integration is conducted over the manifold elements formed by the intersections of the overlapping physical covers. The basic concepts and further details of NMM can be found in Refs.13,22. In this work, the fundamentals of the NMM follow established formulations in the literature. The term ‘quadrilateral NMM framework’ refers to our implementation using a structured orthogonal quadrilateral background discretization. This discretization provides (i) regularity and consistency with uniform element size and high shape quality, improving the stability of numerical integration and contact iterations; (ii) reduced mesh-direction dependence, helping mitigate mesh-induced bias and step-to-step oscillations of incremental crack-growth directions; and (iii) implementation convenience because joints are introduced as geometric entities without requiring complicated boundary-fitted meshing.

Owing to the intrinsic cutting feature of NMM, nodes located on the two sides of a crack belong to different manifold elements and therefore carry independent degrees of freedom. This enables a natural representation of the displacement jump across the crack.

Damage indicator

Criteria derived from linear elastic fracture mechanics, such as the maximum tangential stress criterion and the maximum hoop stress criterion, are widely used to predict crack initiation and propagation. However, these criteria are mainly formulated for tensile or tensile–shear crack-tip conditions and typically require a non-negative mode-I stress intensity factor (). During progressive failure of rock masses, the local crack-tip stress state may evolve into a compressive–shear regime, under which the above criteria may become less reliable and may lead to step-to-step oscillations of the predicted propagation direction (numerically manifested as a zigzag trajectory) in incremental crack-growth simulations21,22. Therefore, the LT criterion, which is established on a Mohr–Coulomb–type strength condition and is applicable to pure tension, tension–shear, and compression–shear stress states, is adopted to determine the crack propagation direction and increment22.

A damage indicator is further defined according to the Mohr–Coulomb criterion to identify crack types and clarify propagation mechanisms. The stress state at the center of each manifold element represents the stress of the entire element. Numerical examples

Validation example—Brazilian disk splitting test

The Brazilian disk splitting test is widely used to indirectly determine the tensile strength of brittle rocks and to evaluate their mechanical behaviour. In this study, the test is reproduced to verify the capability of the quadrilateral-element Numerical Manifold Method in modelling dynamic crack propagation, and to illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed damage-based indicator for identifying crack types and interpreting propagation mechanisms. Here, the term ‘quadrilateral-element NMM’ denotes the structured orthogonal quadrilateral discretization adopted in our NMM implementation rather than a new theoretical formulation of NMM.

The geometry of the specimen and the corresponding numerical model are shown in Fig. 3. The disk has a diameter of 100 mm. Each loading platen is 6.2 mm in length, forming a central angle of 7.2° at the disk centre. A pre-existing flaw with a length of 30 mm is introduced at an inclination angle of θ = 45° relative to the horizontal. The load is applied through two rigid plates positioned at the upper and lower boundaries of the disk. The plates measure 100 mm × 4 mm, and the average loading rate is 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ m/step.

Geometry of the Brazilian disk test and its numerical model.

The material parameters used in the analysis are: elastic modulus E = 10 GPa, Poisson’s ratio ν = 0.25, density ρ = 2500 kg/m3, internal friction angle φ = 40°, cohesion c = 5 MPa, tensile strength σₜ = 0.5 MPa, and a penalty stiffness of 100 GPa. The pre-cut flaw and the contact between the disk and the rigid plates are modelled as frictionless interfaces without cohesion or tensile resistance. The time increment is set to Δt = 0.001 s.

As illustrated in Figs. 4 and 5, the crack‐growth pattern obtained with the quadrilateral manifold elements aligns closely with both the reported DDA simulations35 and the experimental observations36. In addition, Fig. 6 shows that the damage indicator introduced in this study delineates a clear tensile-damage zone ahead of and surrounding the propagating crack during the Brazilian splitting process. This evidences that the fracture is tensile in nature and verifies the effectiveness of the indicator in identifying crack types and elucidating their propagation mechanisms.

Progressive failure simulation of stratified slopes with randomly distributed cracks

To investigate the progressive failure characteristics of stratified rock slopes under conditions closer to reality, 34 random straight cracks were generated within the upper trapezoidal region subjected to top loading. The crack lengths were randomly distributed between 1 and 2 m, with orientations concentrated around 20° (allowing a ± 3° deviation), and the cracks were uniformly distributed throughout the region. The geometry, boundary conditions, and numerical model of the slope with randomly distributed cracks are shown in Fig. 16. The geometric dimensions, boundary conditions, and material parameters are identical to those used in "Failure modes of stratified slopes with uniformly distributed cracks" section.

The progressive failure of a rock slope containing randomly distributed bedding-parallel cracks under top loading follows a systematic evolution, beginning with the independent propagation of multiple cracks, followed by local coalescence, the formation of oblique through-going cracks, development of principal cracks, and layered sliding along bedding planes, as shown in Figs. 17 and 18. In the initial stage (e.g., s), the upper portion of the slope is subjected to tensile-shear stresses induced by the top load, triggering independent activation of the internal random bedding-parallel cracks. Subsequently ( s), adjacent cracks rapidly coalesce, forming a localized damage zone in the upper slope. The damage then propagates along the bedding planes toward the slope surface, producing oblique through-going cracks ( s). From s onward, the damage bands extend downward, gradually developing single or multiple principal through-going cracks, while the slope exhibits layered sliding along the bedding planes. Ultimately, a network of principal cracks penetrates all layers, driving the overall slope instability.

Throughout this process, the random distribution of bedding-parallel cracks dictates the initiation sites of failure, the bedding structure governs the spatial pattern of layered sliding, and the top load provides the driving force for sustained damage propagation and crack coalescence. These results clearly demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method in capturing the failure mechanisms of stratified rock slopes.

Conclusions

A quadrilateral manifold element-based numerical manifold method, incorporating the LT crack propagation criterion and a damage factor derived from the Mohr–Coulomb strength theory, systematically investigates the instability and crack propagation mechanisms of stratified rock slopes under top loading. The key findings are summarized as follows:

Bedding inclination exerts a significant and non-monotonic influence on the failure characteristics of top-loaded bedding rock slopes. As increases from to , the failure mode gradually transitions from shallow bedding-controlled shear failure to deep-seated curved sliding–tensile composite failure. Meanwhile, the number of slip surfaces increases from one to three, the principal slip surface progressively migrates downward, and the overall failure depth correspondingly increases. Under the conditions considered in this study, shows a pronounced delaying effect on crack penetration, whereas the case exhibits a stronger tendency toward late-stage accelerated instability and may therefore be regarded as a relatively unfavorable inclination condition.

This process ultimately produces a network of principal through-going cracks that drives overall slope instability. Despite the above findings, several limitations and applicability conditions should be noted. The simulations focus on the propagation and interaction of pre-existing discontinuities and consider crack growth only, while crack nucleation is not included. In addition, the present study is conducted within a two-dimensional framework and therefore does not capture three-dimensional effects or coupled processes (e.g., hydro-mechanical interactions). Accordingly, the proposed method is mainly applicable to bedding-controlled stratified rock slopes, where progressive failure is dominated by discontinuity sliding/detachment under top loading. Future work will extend the framework to coupled processes and three-dimensional problems.
