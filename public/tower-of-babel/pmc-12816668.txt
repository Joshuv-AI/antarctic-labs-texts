Egg mass gelatinous envelopes of yellow goosefish Lophius litulon protect the internal eggs and larvae from salinity stress and desiccation
Authors: Tomoya Ishikawa, Mitsuhiro Nakaya, Yuta Eno, Tetsuya Takatsu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Some teleosts, including yellow goosefish Lophius litulon, spawn gelatinous egg masses, but the biological function of the gelatinous matrix remains poorly understood due to limited experimental access. This study tested whether the gelatinous membrane of L. litulon egg masses, which naturally float just below the sea surface in coastal areas, protects eggs and larvae from abiotic stress. Salinity stress experiments using 782 and 605 eggs were conducted. Embryos retained within gelatinous membranes maintained high survival and normal growth after 45 and 90 h of exposure to 50% diluted salinity, whereas those removed showed significant reductions (two-way ANOVA; p < 0.001 and = 0.015, respectively). Additionally, experiments using 449 and 599 larvae examined responses to 24-hour exposure under desiccation and elevated temperature conditions (2–3 °C above the appropriate temperature). The gelatinous matrix retained moisture, showing stable survival and growth (two-way ANOVA; p = 0.838 and 0.804, respectively), although heat stress reduced survival (two-way ANOVA; p < 0.001). These results indicate that the gelatinous matrix offers physical protection from some types of environmental stress, likely an adaptive trait for survival in variable coastal habitats. This study provides the first experimental evidence of such protective functions in gelatinous egg masses of marine fishes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32075-x.

Introduction

Three groups of marine fishes that are non-monophyletic, namely Scorpaeniformes, Ophidiiformes and Lophiiformes have been confirmed to spawn gelatinous egg masses1–4. Benefits of enveloping eggs in gelatinous matrix have been discussed from several perspectives. One proposed advantage is the optimization of fertilization efficiency by preventing sperm dispersion5,6. In addition, gelatinous envelopes have been suggested to reduce predation risk by increasing the patchiness of eggs and larvae7 and by making the egg masses unpalatable to predators1. Gelatinous egg masses are also known in polychaetes, cephalopods, pelecypods and gastropods8–10. The gelatinous egg masses of invertebrates have been shown to provide protection to the inside embryos against abiotic environmental stresses11. In contrast, for marine fishes, the relationship between gelatinous envelopes and environmental stressors has not been experimentally examined, partly because opportunities to collect egg masses are scarce and experimental investigations into their ecology and development remain in their infancy.

Yellow goosefish Lophius litulon (Lophiiformes; Lophiidae) is a large-sized demersal fish distributed in temperate, coastal western Pacific12–14. They are confirmed to spawn ribbon-shaped floating gelatinous egg masses (here termed as “egg veils”; Supplementary Fig. S1A) consisting of gelatinous chambers that envelop 1–3 embryos with liquid arranged in a single layer15,16 (Supplementary Fig. S1B, C). The larvae emerge from the egg veil as they develop morphology and become neutrally buoyant for the subsequent life stage in the ocean17. During the time that eggs and larvae spend in the egg veil (here termed as the egg-veil-duration) is considered to be ca. 8–15 days depending on the water temperature, with higher temperatures resulting in shorter durations18,19. Appropriate temperature range for the egg-veil-duration is suggested 12–23 °C19, and almost cover the sea surface temperature range that egg veils have been collected in previous studies15–21. Because the eggs have oil globule19, and body density of the larvae is lower than that of natural salinity during egg-veil-duration (1.016 g/cm3)17, the egg veils have been found only at intertidal zone22 or immediately below the sea surface15–21. Considering that the egg veils are distributed just below the sea surface of coastal regions, we assumed that they are exposed to abiotic environmental stresses and hypothesized that the gelatinous membrane protects the inner embryos. However, there is only one report about the relationship with environmental stress, which tested tolerance against continuous thermal stress to understand the appropriate temperature range19.

Here, we examined whether the egg veils provide: (1) tolerance to low salinity seawater diluted by precipitation and river discharge as observed in Gadidae (Gadiformes) fish larvae23; and (2) tolerance to short-term high temperature and air exposure which imitate the conditions of stranding in the intertidal zone for this species22. Through these tests, our objective was to assess the existence of potential benefits of gelatinous egg masses beyond enhancing fertilization efficiency and reducing predation risk5–7, in order to deepen our understanding of reproductive strategies in marine fishes.

Results

Low salinity exposure experiment

We evaluated the protective function of the gelatinous membrane in L. litulon by comparing the survival and growth of embryos either retained within egg veil (hereafter referred to as the “Veil” group) or artificially removed from it (the “Particle” group) under conditions of abrupt salinity fluctuation (Fig. 1A–C; detailed in “Materials and methods” section). Three salinity conditions were applied (Fig. 1D): all veils and egg particles were prepared at salinity 32, then assigned to one of three treatments, constant low salinity (16; Const16), and two fluctuating conditions in which eggs were exposed to salinity 16 for 45 or 90 h before being shifted to salinity 32 (Exp16for45h and Exp16for90h, respectively).

High temperature and air exposure experiment

We examined whether the gelatinous membrane provides protection by conducting experiments that combined the presence or absence of temporal heat stress with exposure or non-exposure to air (Fig. 4). Effect of the high temperature exposure of egg veils (25 °C: 2–3 °C above the appropriate temperature19 for 24 h on the survival rate was significant (two-way ANOVA; F1, 20 = 48.73, p < 0.001) and the mean survival rate was 53% compared to the control conditions (Fig. 5A). Effect of the air exposure and interaction of the air exposure and the high temperature exposure on the survival rate was not confirmed (two-way ANOVA; F1, 20 = 0.04, 3.53, p = 0.838, 0.075, respectively). The deformity rates and NLs were not significantly affected by the temperature conditions, the air exposure and their interaction (Fig. 5B, C; two-way ANOVA; deformity rates: F1, 20 = 0.85, 2.20, 1.45, p = 0.367, 0.154, 0.236; NLs: F1, 20 = 3.27, 0.06, < 0.001, p = 0.086, 0.804, 0.986, respectively).

Discussion

Abrupt salinity fluctuation and air exposure did not affect the survival and growth of the eggs and larvae of L. litulon enveloped by the egg veils. High temperature exposure did, however, affect the survival. Our results demonstrated the hypothesis that gelatinous envelopes of the egg veils of L. litulon protect the enveloped eggs and larvae from some environmental stresses. While such protection is known with respect to gelatinous egg masses of invertebrates11,24, the function of the gelatinous matrix of fish egg mass has not been verified. This study newly suggests protection against abiotic environmental stresses as an additional adaptive advantage of gelatinous egg masses in marine fishes, in addition to their roles in enhancing fertilization efficiency5,6 and reducing predation7.

The protective role of egg veils against abrupt salinity fluctuation was demonstrated by the significant interaction between salinity treatments and the presence or absence of the gelatinous membrane. Similar protective functions of gelatinous egg masses against salinity stress have been reported in the gastropods (Melanochlamys diomedea25 and Ziminella salmonacea26, and polychaetes (Marphysa gravely27 and Diopatra variabilis28. Larval fish of typically oceanic species such as Boreogadus saida and Eleginus gracilis (Gadiformes; Gadidae) exhibit strong tolerance to extremely low salinity (2–5) for up to 24 h23. Such tolerance against salinity stress has been interpreted as an adaptation to coastal water or brackish water subject to rapid salinity fluctuations11,23,26–28. Because river water and rainfall decrease the surface salinity of coastal waters29,30, egg veils of L. litulon, which are frequently found near the surface15–21, are also expected to be exposed to such salinity fluctuation. Osmoregulation outside the normal habitat salinity, especially under abrupt fluctuations, demands greater energy expenditure31–33, which may explain the reduced survival rates and shorter NLs observed under fluctuating salinity in the Particle group. Therefore, unlike Gadidae larvae that must directly endure abrupt salinity changes23, the gelatinous membrane of L. litulon egg veils may buffer eggs and larvae from such abrupt fluctuations, reducing the osmotic stress and conserving energy for osmoregulation. Although, previous observations of egg veils have been limited to just below the sea surface15–21 or in an intertidal zone22, future studies should consider the potential influence of local oceanographic conditions, such as stratification and current patterns, on their vertical distribution.

In the low-salinity exposure experiment, both survival and growth in Const16 were relatively higher than in groups subjected to abrupt salinity increases. Moreover, the values observed in Const16 were comparable to previous findings at normal salinity of 32–33 (NL at Stage21: 5.32 ± 0.46 mm17; survival rate at 18 °C: 71 ± 14%19; mean ± standard deviation). These results indicate that L. litulon eggs and larvae are more sensitive to abrupt salinity increases than to abrupt decreases or stable exposure at salinity 16.

Consistent across the survival rate, growth rate, and deformity rate, we found that the performance under salinity elevation was poorer in the larval stage than in the egg stage when the gelatinous membrane was removed. This suggests that the gelatinous membrane plays a particularly important role in buffering salinity stress during the larval stage. Therefore, it can be proposed that enveloping until larval stage within a gelatinous matrix, as observed in Ophidiiformes species7 and Lophius (Lophiiformes) species17 represents an adaptive advantage that enhances tolerance to environmental fluctuations. Based on the survival curves, the pre-mouth-opening larval stage may represent a developmental window during which salinity stress incurred during embryogenesis becomes apparent. Because Const16 showed similar survival and growth between Veil and Particle, the impacts of gelatinous membrane removal, pipette handling and difference in the aeration condition on the embryos are unlikely to have exceeded those of salinity stress. As tolerance to salinity stress in marine teleost eggs and larvae is known to vary by developmental stage23,34, further studies across ontogenetic stages are needed to clarify how salinity affected L. litulon eggs and larvae.

The survival, deformity and growth rate were not significantly influenced by 24 h of air exposure, imitating the situation that the egg veils were remain in the intertidal zone22. During temporary stranding events caused by tidal fluctuations or wind in coastal habitats, such protection likely reduces the risk of desiccation of the egg veils and may allow survival if they are re-submerged before larval emergence. In L. litulon, the gelatinous membranes preserved an inner liquid environment during air exposure, enabling larvae to maintain respiration within the narrow chambers despite the absence of surrounding water. This suggests that the gelatinous membrane of egg veils may facilitate flexible oxygen exchange between the inner chamber and the external environment. Similar protection against desiccation has been reported for the gelatinous egg masses of the oval squid Sepioteuthis lessoniana collected at 50 m depth in the coastal water, and the membrane has been interpreted as primally functioning a barrier against water pressure24. Lophiiformes species that spawn gelatinous egg masses, inhabiting a wide range of depths4,35,36, may have evolved egg masses capable of maintaining favorable internal conditions under diverse pressure conditions, as has been suggested for S. lessoniana. However, further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms of substance transport across the gelatinous membrane and effect of pressure.

Protection against high temperature exposure was not confirmed within the temperature range used in this study. In the assumption of this study, if terrestrial air temperature is 2–3 °C higher than appropriate temperature for the egg-veil-duration19, the survival of embryos within egg veils that have been exposed to air between high tides would be difficult, even if they later drift offshore. Although we referred to previous records of L. litulon22, few data are currently available to estimate the frequency of egg veil stranding events for this species. In other scenario, it can also be inferred that survival would become difficult if the egg veils were transferred far from the spawning grounds, and remained for one day in waters exceeding their appropriate temperature range. Further field surveys and simulation-based analyses are therefore needed to evaluate these possibilities. In gastropods, only egg masses of M. diomedea have been revealed the presence of thermally protective proteins11 and references therein. The protection against inappropriate temperature stress has been revealed less frequently than that of salinity stress and desiccation11. It may be difficult for marine organisms to obtain protection against thermal stress in a gelatinous matrix.

This study clarifies the survival strategies of L. litulon provided by gelatinous egg masses from the aspect of abiotic environment stresses. Since newly emerged larvae have been reported to selectively feed on rotifers Synchaeta vorax37 commonly found in low-salinity or coastal areas38–40, it is possible that the egg veils should remain near the shoreline, where environmental fluctuations are intense, until the end of the egg-veil-duration. Under such conditions, they are more likely to be exposed to the types of stresses assumed in this study. In the future, we will investigate the relationship between oceanographic conditions and transportation of egg veils of L. litulon, considering the insights of this study.

Materials and methods

Ethical aspect

The experiments were conducted under the approval of the institutional committee as outlined in the Hokkaido University Manual for Implementing Animal Experimentation. All procedures adhered strictly to relevant guidelines and regulations, ensuring ethical standards were maintained. The study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Invasive procedures were carried out using commonly accepted methods in fish research to minimize distress, and the specific treatments performed at each step are described below.

Field collection and preparation

A total of four egg veils of L. litulon were collected by a scooping net at a coastal station in Aomori Prefecture, Japan (41°29’N, 141°01’E) in late May to mid-July, 2023. A part of the egg veils (ca. 15 cm squares) was kept in a plastic bag (2 L volume) with surrounding seawater (ca. 1 L) with oxygen and then transported to Hokkaido University, Hakodate by truck and ship taking approximately four hours. The egg veils were reared in a plastic tank with ca. 10 L of artificial seawater (Tetra Marine Salt Pro, Spectrum Brands Japan Inc.; Japan, 15–18 °C; salinity: 32–33) with gentle aeration until examination. In all experimental procedures, the artificial seawater was dissolved with streptomycin sulfate at a concentration of 50 ppm to relieve bacterial stress and the antimicrobial effects of gelatinous envelopes suggested in a freshwater fish Perca fluviatilis41.

Low salinity exposure

Two independent experiments were conducted using 782 and 605 L. litulon eggs at blastula stage (Stage 1019 which is generally known to be one of the most sensitive stages during fish embryonic development42, with each batch derived from a separate egg veil. These egg veils were designated as “1B” and “2B” for reference throughout the study. To assess the protective function of the gelatinous membrane against salinity stress, we compared the salinity tolerance of eggs that remained enclosed within the gelatinous membrane (hereafter referred to as the “Veil” group) with those that were separated from it (the “Particle” group). Each egg veil was cut into approximately 2 cm squares using scissors, with each piece containing about 50 eggs (Fig. 1A, B).

When the egg veils were cut in artificial seawater, eggs were released from the gelatinous matrix and collected using a pipette to constitute the Particle group. Fifty eggs from each group were placed in 500 mL plastic tanks filled with artificial seawater under three different salinity conditions (Fig. 1C, D; Table 1). A constant salinity condition was applied: salinity was maintained at 16 (Const16). A low-salinity treatment of 16 was chosen to simulate a strong yet ecologically realistic freshening event (e.g., precipitation or river discharge)29,30, corresponding to an approximately 50% dilution that imposes a clear osmotic challenge while remaining below the acute lethal threshold, as confirmed by preliminary trials, thereby providing sufficient stress intensity to reveal any protective effect without causing immediate, wholescale mortality. In the two experimental conditions, salinity was initially set at 16 for 45 or 90 h and then shifted to 32; the 45 h exposure corresponds to the embryo stage (Stage 1219 of eggs (Exp16for45h), and the 90 h exposure to the yolk-sac larval stage before mouth opening (Stage 16–1717 (Exp16for90h). Accordingly, we set up salinity treatments consisting of a control with no salinity elevation and treatments in which salinity elevation occurred during either the egg or larval stage, allowing us to compare the effects of the timing of salinity elevation. Each treatment condition was replicated in two tanks per batch, resulting in four replicates in total. Early disintegration of the gelatinous envelope occurred in one replicate tank of the Veil group under the Const16 treatment in 2B. Because the embryos were enveloped for a shorter period, their environmental conditions may have differed. Thus, data from this tank were excluded, leaving three replicates for the Veil group under Const16.

At 45 and 90 h after the onset of the experiment, 100% of the tank water was replaced with artificial seawater adjusted to the target salinity (Fig. 1D; Table 1). The veil pieces and egg particles were transferred using tweezers and a pipette, respectively. All tanks were incubated at a temperature maintained between 17 °C and 18 °C using a constant-temperature water bath or incubator. The mean incubation temperature ± standard deviation was 17.5 ± 0.4 °C in 1B experiment and 18.1 ± 0.1 °C in 2B experiment, which are within the appropriate range for the development19. Gentle aeration was provided for Veil tanks, whereas no aeration was applied to Particle tanks. This was because preliminary observations showed that, without aeration, embryos located far from the edge of the egg veil tended to experience hypoxia and die, while aeration increased mortality of larvae without gelatinous membrane once the pelvic fins and notochord had developed. Detailed results of these trials will be reported elsewhere. To prevent hypoxic conditions in Particle tanks, eggs were spatially separated. Dead embryos and early-emerged individuals were removed and recorded twice daily (07:00–07:30 and 15:00–15:30). The experiment was continued until five days after hatching, which corresponds to the end of the egg-veil-duration (Stage 2117. Larvae at five days after hatching were anesthetized using MS-222 (Sigma-Aldrich LLC, USA) and fixed in 5% neutral buffered formalin for further analysis.

High temperature and air exposure

Two independent experiments were conducted using 449 and 599 L. lituon larvae, respectively, each derived from a separate egg veil, to examine whether the gelatinous membrane surrounding the larvae provides protection against thermal stress and desiccation. The study focused on mid to late yolk-sac larvae (from Stage 19 to Stage 2114, as this developmental stage is expected to be especially sensitive to air exposure and high temperatures for two main reasons. First, oxygen consumption markedly increases after hatching in many fish species43,44, and the rate is further accelerated at higher temperatures45,46, making larvae particularly susceptible to oxygen limitation under air exposure and heat stress. Second, the gelatinous membrane becomes weakened during the later phase of the egg-veil-duration. These conditions were therefore considered appropriate for testing whether the gelatinous membrane, even in a degraded state, could retain internal fluid and protect larvae from desiccation and oxygen depletion under air exposure and heat stress.

Each veil was cut into approximately 2 cm square pieces using scissors, with each piece containing around 50 larvae. From each veil, 12 veil pieces with no dead individuals were selected for the experiment. Six of these pieces were subjected to air exposure by placing a single piece at the center of a dry, lidded Petri dish for 24 h without water (Fig. 4). After exposure, dead larvae were removed using a fine needle, and the veil piece was transferred into a bottle containing 200 mL of artificial seawater maintained at 18 °C with a salinity of 32.0.

The remaining six pieces from each veil were directly placed into bottles containing 200 mL of artificial seawater, sealed with cling film to prevent evaporation. Half of the Petri dishes and bottles from each condition were exposed to elevated temperatures for 24 h using an incubator, where the mean ± standard deviation of air temperature was 25.2 ± 1.0 °C and 25.9 ± 1.4 °C, and relative humidity was 57 ± 1% and 60 ± 3% for each veil, respectively. These temperatures were approximately 2–3 °C above the appropriate incubation temperature range for the egg veils19. The other half were kept in a control incubator maintained at 18.0 ± 0.2 °C, including the 24 h air exposure.

Following the initial 24-hour exposure, all veil pieces were placed in artificial seawater adjusted to 18 °C and incubated under the same conditions for the remainder of the experiment. No water exchange or aeration was performed during the experimental period. Dead and early-emerged individuals were removed and counted twice daily (07:00–07:30 and 15:00–15:30). The experiment continued for 96 h from the start, corresponding to the end of the egg-veil-duration. Surviving larvae at the conclusion of the experiment were fixed in 90% ethanol for subsequent analysis.

Data analysis

Notochord length (NL) of fixed larvae was measured from the anterior tip of the snout to the posterior tip of the notochord in 0.01 mm using a stereomicroscope. Larvae exhibiting clear morphological abnormalities, particularly cranial stunting, were excluded from the analysis of NL. For each tank, larval survival rate, deformity rate and mean NL were calculated and used as response variables in subsequent statistical analyses. We conducted a two-way ANOVA with respect to the salinity treatment and the condition if the eggs are enveloped in the gelatinous membrane for the low salinity exposure experiments, as well as with respect to exposed temperature and the condition if the egg veils were exposed to the air. After the two-way ANOVA, a post hoc Tukey’s HSD test was used to compare treatments. Data were organized and summarized using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation), and statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.0 (R Core Team). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.
