The link between dietary inflammation and hyperuricemia: what is the mediating role of insulin resistance and abdominal obesity?
Authors: Weili Liu, Chuyan Feng, Lin Yang, Ke Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe investigated the serial mediating roles of insulin re-sistance (IR) and abdominal obesity in the association between the Dietary Inflamma-tory Index (DII) and hyperuricemia (HUA), and whether abdominal obesity moder-ated this pathway.MethodsWe analyzed data from 8,232 adults in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2007–2016). The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index served as a surrogate for IR. We employed serial mediation and moderated me-diation models.ResultsHigher DII was associated with increased HUA risk. This re-lationship was serially mediated through the pathway: DII → TyG index → abdominal obesity → HUA. This indirect pathway accounted for 52.3% of the total effect, with abdominal obesity being the most potent mediator (contribution: 31.0%). Importantly, the mediating effect of the TyG index was significant only in individuals with a normal waist circumference (β = 0.0016, 95% CI: 0.0006, 0.0026) but was attenuated to non-significance in those with abdominal obesity.ConclusionOur findings suggest that IR and abdominal obesity may serially mediate the link between a pro-inflammatory diet and HUA. The me-diating role of IR appears to be prominent in individuals without abdominal obesity, whereas in those with obesity, obesity itself becomes the dominant factor. However, given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn. These findings support developing stage-specific HUA prevention strategies, targeting insulin sensitivity or weight control based on an individual’s obesity status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01143-y.Keywords: Hyperuricemia, Dietary inflammation index, Triglyceride glucose, Triglyceride glucose-waist circumference, Abdominal obesity, Mediation

Introduction

Hyperuricemia (HUA) is a metabolic disorder characterized by elevated serum uric acid (SUA) concentrations resulting from overproduction and/or impaired renal excretion [1]. HUA is defined as SUA levels ≥ 7.0 mg/dl (416.0 µmol/L) in males or SUA ≥ 6.0 mg/dl (357.0 µmol/L) in females [2]. HUA is a globally prevalent disease, particularly in high-income and middle-income countries, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life and and its prevalence is increasing worldwide [2]. According to epidemiological reports, the prevalence of HUA among different populations worldwide ranges from 2.6% to 36%, and is rapidly increasing in both developed and developing countries, highlighting its significant public health burden [3]. Epidemiological and clinical studies have linked HUA to the development of various diseases, including chronic kidney disease, fatty liver, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, insulin resistance, obesity, type 2 diabetes (T2D), and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [4–6]. Notably, T2D and HUA are increasingly recognized as coexisting metabolic disorders with shared pathophysiological mechanisms, such as insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and lipid metabolic dysfunction, forming a complex bidirectional relationship [7]. HUA is associated with multiple risk factors, including diet, alcohol consumption, medication use, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and obesity [6].

Dietary factors can adjust inflammation levels through pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory mechanisms [8], potentially influencing the development of hyperuricemia [9–11]. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) is an innovative, literature-based dietary tool designed to assess the inflammatory potential of diets across populations [12]. It has been widely used in clinical research to explore the association between dietary inflammatory potential and various diseases [13, 14]. Moreover, previous studies have preliminarily confirmed that a higher DII score, indicative of a pro-inflammatory diet, is associated with an increased risk of hyperuricemia and its adverse outcomes [15–18].

Although the association between DII and HUA has been preliminarily established, the specific biological mechanisms underlying this relationship require further elucidation. In fact, accumulating evidence has begun to outline parts of this complex network. On the one hand, pro-inflammatory diet were considered key environmental factor in the potential induction of IR [19, 21]. Meanwhile, IR is also an important risk factor for HUA. Multiple studies have demonstrated that IR can directly elevate SUA levels by reducing renal uric acid clearance [22–24], thereby contributing to the development of HUA. Currently, the gold standard for assessing IR is the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique [25]. However, this method is complex and costly, limiting its clinical application. In this study, we used the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) and triglyceride-glucose index-waist circumference (TyG-WC) as surrogate markers for IR. The TyG index evaluates insulin sensitivity by combining two biomarkers: triglycerides and fasting glucose [26, 27], while the TyG-WC index further incorporates waist circumference, thus encompassing information on both metabolic disturbances and central obesity [28–32]. On the other hand, obesity—particularly abdominal obesity—significantly correlates with the progression of HUA [19–21]. Obesity not only increases uric acid production but also decreases its excretion [22, 33]. Importantly, abdominal obesity is inextricably linked with IR and a potential mediator linking diet and hyperuricemia [22, 23, 34]. Mechanistic studies indicate that dysfunctional adipose tissue acts as an endocrine organ, secreting various bioactive adipokines and pro-inflammatory mediators that link visceral obesity to systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation [35, 36]. Consequently, this visceral fat dysfunction may promote hyperuricemia by reducing renal uric acid clearance and enhancing hepatic uric acid synthesis [37, 38]. These dual mechanisms underscore the need to jointly consider IR and abdominal obesity in the potential mediating pathways.

However, although numerous previous studies have demonstrated associations between pro-inflammatory diets and IR [19]as well as obesity [39, 40], and both IR [41]and obesity [42] have been identified as independent risk factors for HUA, no research to date has integrated dietary inflammation, insulin resistance, and abdominal obesity into a single framework to systematically investigate their potential sequential mediating and synergistic effects in the development of HUA.

To address this gap, we propose a novel “Diet–Metabolism–Morphology” framework that systematically investigates the sequential and moderating roles of insulin resistance and abdominal obesity in the relationship between dietary inflammation and HUA. Using cross-sectional data from the 2007–2016 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), we hypothesized the following hypotheses: (1) The Sequential Mediation Hypothesis: Insulin resistance (proxied by TyG and TyG-WC) and abdominal obesity function as successive mediators in a cascade, wherein a pro-inflammatory diet (elevated DII) is associated with increased insulin resistance, which may then be linked to abdominal obesity, ultimately correlating with the risk of HUA; and (2) The Moderation Hypothesis: Abdominal obesity acts as a crucial effect modifier on this inflammatory-metabolic axis, potentially modifying the magnitude of the association between insulin resistance and hyperuricemia.

Through this framework, we aim not only to move beyond traditional single-factor analyses but also to provide an innovative theoretical perspective on the mechanisms associated with HUA. Furthermore, this model may lay a scientific foundation for precision prevention strategies tailored to distinct population phenotypes.

Methods

Study population

This study was a cross-sectional analysis based on data from NHANES in 2007 ~ 2016. NHANES is a survey program conducted biennially that employs a multistage probability sampling design to collect nationally representative data. The survey protocol was reviewed and approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board of the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 46,685 individuals were initially included. Participants were excluded if they were younger than 20 years (n = 18,538) or pregnant women (n = 317). We further excluded participants with missing data on the two 24-hour dietary recalls (n = 5,401), waist circumference (n = 4,843), serum uric acid (n = 777), and TyG or TyG-WC indices (n = 8,577). Finally, the study cohort comprised 8,232 participants. The flowchart of study population selection was shown in Fig. 1.

Dietary inflammatory index

DII was developed by Shivappa [12] in 2014 as a scoring method for assessing the potential inflammatory levels of dietary components. The DII incorporates a total of 45 food parameters. This study used two 24-hour dietary recall interviews, with the first conducted at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC) and the second via telephone or email 3–10 days later. We calculated DII by using the average of the two 24-hour recall interviews, incorporating data on 27 food: alcohol, Vitamin B12, Vitamin B6, Beta-carotene, Caffeine, Carbohydrates, Cholesterol, Kilocalories, Total Fat, Fiber, Folic Acid, Iron, Magnesium, Total Monounsaturated Fatty Acids, Niacin, Omega-3 Fatty Acids, Omega-6 Fatty Acids, Protein, Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids, Riboflavin, Saturated Fat, Selenium, Thiamin, Vitamin E, Vitamin C, Vitamin D, and Zinc. DII scores range from negative to positive with a higher score indicating a more pro-inflammatory diet and a lower score signifying a diet with stronger anti-inflammatory effects.

Abdominal obesity

Human body parameters were measured by trained technicians at the MEC following standardized protocols. Abdominal obesity was classified according to the recommendations of the International Atherosclerosis Society and the International Chair on Cardiometabolic Risk Working Group [43], based on waist circumference(WC). Participants were categorized into three groups: normal weight, overweight, and obesity. Normal weight was defined as WC ≥ 80 cm for women and ≥ 90 cm for men; overweight as ≥ 90 cm for women and ≥ 100 cm for men; and obesity as ≥ 105 cm for women and ≥ 110 cm for men [44].

Hyperuricemia

Serum uric acid concentrations were measured using the DxC800 module and timed endpoint method during the 2007–2016 NHANES cycle. Hyperuricemia was defined as a serum uric acid level of at least 7 mg/dL in men and at least 6 mg/dL in women [45].

Covariates

Data on covariates included age, sex, race (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, and other race), marriage status (married, living with partner, never married, and other), educational attainment (low high school, college, and above college), and family poverty income ratio (PIR). Physical activity(PA) was categorized into three groups according to weekly metabolic equivalent (MET) minutes: ≤600 MET-min/week, 600–1200 MET-min/week, and ≥ 1200 MET-min/week. Smoking status was defined as current smokers (who had at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and reported currently smoking), former smokers (≥ 100 cigarettes in lifetime but not currently smoking), and never smokers (< 100 cigarettes in lifetime). Drinking status was defined as three groups: No drinker (fewer than 12 drinks in the past year, fewer than 12 drinks in lifetime, or average weekly drinking frequency of zero over the past 12 months), light-to-moderate drinker (men < 2 drinks/day, women < 1 drink/day), and heavy drinker (men ≥ 2 drinks/day, women ≥ 1 drink/day). Hypertension was defined as: (1) systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg; (2) a positive response to the question “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have hypertension?”; (3) taking anti-hypertensive drugs. Diabetes was defined as: (1) fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (2) a positive responses to question “You ever been told by a doctor or health professional that you have diabetes or sugar diabetes?”; (3) using of diabetes medication or insulin. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined as a positive response to the question “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told that you had congestive heart failure/coronary heart disease/angina/heart attack/stroke?”. Cancer was defined as a positive response to the question “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told that you had cancer or a malignancy of any kind?”.

Statistical analysis

In accordance with NHANES analytic guidelines, all analyses accounted for the complex sampling design and sampling weights. Weighted analyses were conducted using recalibrated dietary weights (two-day dietary weights divided by five). All statistical procedures were performed in R software (version 4.3.1). Continuous variables were described using means(standard deviations), and categorical variables were presented as counts(percentages). Student’s t-test and the chi-square test were applied for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

Weighted multivariable logistic regression was first employed to construct three types of models to assess the associations between DII and HUA, with TyG, TyG-WC, and abdominal obesity considered separately. Three models were constructed for each type: Crude model, without any covariates; Model 1, adjusted for demographic characteristics (age, sex, race, marriage status, education, and PIR) and lifestyle factors (PA, smoking, and drinking); Model 2, further adjusted for chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, CVD, and cancer). DII, TyG, and TyG-WC were categorized into quartiles, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group.

Secondly, weighted multivariable logistic regression model was used to investigate the association between DII and abdominal obesity. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were then used to evaluate the dose–response relationships between (a) DII and HUA; (b) DII and TyG/TyG-WC; (c) TyG/TyG-WC and HUA.

The mediation analyses were carried out using the “PROCESS” package in R. In single-mediator analysis, the potential mediating roles of TyG, TyG-WC, and abdominal obesity in the association between DII and HUA were examined separately. In serial mediation, DII was treated as the independent variable, TyG and TyG-WC as the first mediating variable, abdominal obesity as the second mediating variable, and HUA as the outcome variable. Finally, a moderated mediation was constructed to examine whether abdominal obesity modified the mediating role of TyG or TyG-WC. For all mediation models, statistical significance was determined when the 95% bootstrap confidence interval (based on 1,000 bootstrap samples) did not include zero.

Hierarchical multivariate logistic regression models were used for subgroup analysis to explore the potential modifying role of race (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, and other race) on the associations of DII, TyG, TyG-WC, abdominal obesity, and HUA.

A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval that does not include zero indicated statistical significance.

Mediating role of TyG, TyG-WC and abdominal obesity

We constructed three single mediation models to examine the mediating effects of TyG, TyG-WC, and abdominal obesity on the association between DII and HUA. The results are shown in Fig. 3; Table 4. In all three single mediation models, both the total effect and the direct effect of DII on HUA were significant (p < 0.001). After adjusting for all covariates, TyG (β = 0.0018, 95% CI: 0.0011–0.0026), TyG-WC (β = 0.0032, 95% CI: 0.0021–0.0044), and abdominal obesity (β = 0.0026, 95% CI: 0.0015–0.0037) were all significant mediators, with mediation proportions of 11.4%, 19.6%, and 16.8%, respectively.

The moderated effect of abdominal obesity in the mediation model

To further explore the potential interaction between WC and TyG, we conducted a moderated mediation to test whether abdominal obesity moderated the three pathways in the model: DII → HUA; DII → TyG; TyG → HUA. Results are shown in Fig. 5; Table 6. In all three pathways, abdominal obesity did not exhibit statistically significant moderation (p > 0.05). Among participants without abdominal obesity, the mediating effect of TyG was significant (β = 0.0016, 95% CI: 0.0006–0.0026). However, this indirect effect was not significant in participants with overweight (β = 0.0013, 95% CI: − 0.0001 to 0.0028) and obesity (β = − 0.0002, 95% CI: − 0.0014 to 0.0009). These findings suggest that the mediating effect of TyG in the association between DII and HUA was significant only in individuals with normal WC. As abdominal obesity severity increased, this metabolic mediating pathway gradually attenuated, becoming statistically non-significant in individuals with obesity.

Subgroup analysis

To further examine the robustness of the associations between DII, TyG, TyG-WC, and abdominal obesity with HUA across different racial groups, we conducted a series of subgroup analyses by race, with results presented in Supplementary Table S1-S3. Subgroup analysis results indicated that the direction of association between DII, TyG, TyG-WC, abdominal obesity, and increased odds of HUA remained highly consistent across all racial subgroups. Discussion

In this study, we systematically investigated the complex interplay among the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), insulin resistance, abdominal obesity, and hyperuricemia. By testing a “Diet–Metabolism–Morphology” framework, our findings suggest that the adverse association between a pro-inflammatory diet and uric acid homeostasis may be sequentially mediated—potentially initiated by insulin resistance (measured by TyG) and subsequently linked to abdominal obesity. Crucially, abdominal obesity emerged not only as the pivotal potential mediator in this modeled cascade but also as a distinct effect modifier, influencing the magnitude of the diet-metabolic association.”

Our analysis supports the hypothesis that diet-driven inflammation is a pivotal upstream correlate of HUA, consistent with prior work [16, 42, 46]. However, our study moves beyond simple association by dissecting and quantifying the statistical pathways. We found that abdominal obesity was the most powerful single mediator in our model, accounting for nearly one-third (30.97%) of the total observed effect, followed by the direct effect of insulin resistance (TyG, 14.84%). This quantification suggests a potential hierarchical cascade wherein insulin resistance may represent early metabolic disturbances, which are subsequently associated with the presence of abdominal obesity within this pathological chain.

The biological plausibility underlying these associations is multifaceted. While our statistical analysis adopts a sequential framework, we recognize that the relationship between insulin resistance and abdominal obesity is intricate and may involve bidirectional pathways [7]. Notwithstanding this complexity, the positioning of insulin resistance as an antecedent in our serial mediation model aligns with established physiological mechanisms. Specifically, elevated insulin levels exert a potent anabolic effect, promoting lipogenesis and inhibiting lipolysis [47]. This effect is particularly pronounced in visceral adipose tissue due to its high metabolic activity, leading to preferential fat accumulation and thereby creating a self-perpetuating vicious cycle of metabolic dysfunction [48, 49]. Concurrently, a pro-inflammatory diet is hypothesized to act as an initial contributing factor; by promoting the release of systemic cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) that directly disrupt insulin receptor signaling, it may promote the systemic insulin resistance required initiate this theoretical metabolic cascade [50–52].

Regarding the final step of our mediation model, the link from abdominal obesity to hyperuricemia is likely facilitated by a dual mechanism involving increased urate production and reduced renal excretion. Visceral adipose tissue functions not merely as an energy depot but as a metabolically active endocrine organ. Its excessive expansion leads to local hypoxia and accelerated cell turnover, which promotes purine degradation and increases endogenous uric acid production [53, 54]. Furthermore, dysfunctional adipose tissue exhibits altered expression of aquaporins (AQP7) that disrupts glycerol metabolism [55], while concurrently secreting specific pro-inflammatory mediators such as complement factor H (fH) and serum amyloid A (SAA) [35, 36]. These factors maintain a chronic inflammatory status that drives lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [56]. Crucially, this oxidative stress—amplified by oxidized lipoproteins and urate-induced inflammation—precipitates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) dysfunction, thereby compromising nitric oxide bioavailability and vascular homeostasis [57, 58]. Ultimately, this metabolic milieu, coupled with insulin-mediated sodium retention, synergistically impairs renal tubular function, thereby reducing uric acid excretion [59, 60].

Notably, the lack of statistical significance in the alternative pathway (DII → TyG-WC → Abdominal Obesity → HUA) provides internal validation for our structural model. Since the TyG-WC index inherently incorporates waist circumference, placing abdominal obesity as a downstream mediator introduces statistical multicollinearity and logical redundancy. This null finding supports the plausibility of our conceptual framework—“Diet → Metabolism → Morphology”—suggesting that within this statistical model, metabolic dysfunction (IR) is better positioned as an antecedent variable rather than a consequence of the morphological manifestation of abdominal obesity.

Crucially, the moderated mediation analysis reveals that the association between insulin resistance (TyG) and hyperuricemia is conditional on obesity status. We observed that the mediating effect of TyG was robust in individuals without abdominal obesity but attenuated to non-significance in those with established abdominal obesity. This statistical interaction suggests a potential “masking” or “saturation” effect: in the absence of abdominal obesity, diet-induced insulin resistance appears to be a primary correlate of hyperuricemia. However, once abdominal obesity is established, the overarching metabolic influence of visceral adiposity may overshadow the specific contribution of the TyG index. These findings point towards the potential utility of a stratified management strategy: for individuals without obesity, interventions targeting dietary quality and insulin sensitivity might be prioritized, whereas for those with those with abdominal obesity, the management of visceral adiposity appears to be the central therapeutic goal.

Strengths of our study include the use of a large, representative NHANES sample and the application of serial mediation analysis to dissect the ‘Diet–Metabolism–Morphology’ pathway. However, limitations exist. First, Due to the cross-sectional design of this study, we cannot definitively establish causality or confirm the temporal sequence of the proposed mediation pathways. The cascade modeled in our study represents a statistical association based on theoretical plausibility, rather than a proven longitudinal progression. Second, self-reported dietary data are subject to recall bias. Third, while we adjusted for major comorbidities, granular data on urate-lowering medications were unavailable. Nevertheless, the consistency of our results across subgroups suggests that residual confounding from medication use is unlikely to drive the primary associations. Therefore, future prospective cohort studies and Mendelian randomization analyses are warranted to confirm the causal nature of this “Diet-Metabolism-Morphology” cascade and to explore the role of genetic predispositions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study characterizes a statistical association linking a pro-inflammatory diet to hyperuricemia, suggesting a potential sequential mediation process involving insulin resistance and abdominal obesity. Our findings propose a potential “shift in metabolic drivers”: while insulin resistance appears to be a primary correlate in individuals without obesity, the influence of visceral adiposity emerges as a predominant correlate once abdominal obesity is established. Despite the inability to establish causality from this cross-sectional data, these insights support the theoretical basis for stratified prevention strategies, suggesting that interventions might prioritize dietary quality and insulin sensitivity for individuals without obesity, whereas visceral adiposity management should be the central therapeutic goal for those living with abdominal obesity.
