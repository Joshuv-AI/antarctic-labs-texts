Analysis of the clinical characteristics of patients with Kawasaki disease complicated with cholestasis
Authors: Pingping Yang, Lingzhe Meng, Jing Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo explore the clinical characteristics, related factors, and prognosis of Kawasaki disease (KD) combined with acute febrile cholestasis and improve the understanding of the liver complications of KD to avoid misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the demographic, clinical, and laboratory data of 1803 patients with KD between January 2019 and July 2023 in our hospital. Based on the presence of cholestasis, patients were divided into the cholestatic and control groups. Logistic regression analysis was performed for the statistically significant indicators between the two groups to examine the risk factors for KD with coronary artery abnormalities (CAA) and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) unresponsiveness. Additionally, patients with KD and cholestasis were compared with patients with acute febrile cholestasis due to other causes during the same period.ResultsCompared to the control group (n = 1720), patients in the cholestatic group (n = 83) were older, had higher levels of white blood cell count (WBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), D-dimer, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), a shorter fever duration, and high incidences of IVIG unresponsiveness and CAA. KD was the leading cause of acute febrile cholestasis in children (72.6%). In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, younger age, cholestasis, hypoalbuminemia, and a high NT-proBNP level were risk factors for IVIG unresponsiveness, whereas male sex, longer fever duration before treatment, and high alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and CRP levels were risk factors for CAA.ConclusionKD with cholestasis was associated with a higher risk of IVIG unresponsiveness and coronary artery abnormalities. KD was the leading cause of acute febrile cholestasis in children. Attention to the possibility of KD is warranted in children with acute febrile cholestatic jaundice, especially if associated with elevated WBC, CRP, and D-dimer levels, or hypoalbuminemia.Keywords: Kawasaki disease, Acute febrile cholestasis, Hydrocholecystis, IVIG unresponsiveness, Coronary artery abnormalities

Introduction

Kawasaki disease (KD), also known as the mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome, is an acute febrile systemic vasculitis of unknown etiology that usually affects children under 5 years of age. It involves the small and medium arteries of various systems, causing multisystem complications, and affects mainly the coronary arteries, being one of the main causes of acquired heart disease in children [1]. The diagnosis of KD is based on clinical findings, without specific diagnostic laboratory tests. However, nearly 20% of KD cases are atypical, increasing the risk of missed diagnosis or misdiagnosis, and leading to delayed treatment and an elevated risk of coronary artery abnormalities (CAA) [2]. Therefore, challenges remain in the early diagnosis of KD with systemic complications. The liver is rich in blood vessels and is an organ commonly involved in KD. Previous literature [3] shows that 5–20% of children with KD present hepatobiliary involvement, mainly manifested as abdominal pain, enlarged liver, and hepatitis, while acute febrile cholestasis is relatively rare. KD presenting with cholestasis has been only described in few case reports [4–9], and no analysis of a large sample is currently available. This study aimed to summarize the clinical characteristics, related factors, and prognosis of KD with cholestasis to guide the clinical diagnosis and treatment of the disease.

Materials and methods

Study population

We retrospectively analyzed the demographic, clinical, and laboratory data of 1803 patients with KD (1103 males and 700 females) who were admitted to the Pediatrics Department of the Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University between January 2019 and July 2023. The male: female ratio was 1.58:1. The median patient age was 27 months (range, 15–36). KD occurred mainly in infants, which accounted for approximately 64% of the cases. Among all cases, 315, 839, 523, and 126 cases occurred in patients aged < 1 year, 1–3 years, 3–6 years, and > 6 years, respectively. A total of 163 patients (9.0%) showed no response to intravenous immunoglobulin(IVIG), and 176 patients (9.8%) presented CAA. Based on the presence of cholestasis, the patients were divided into the cholestatic (n = 83) and control groups (n = 1720).

Research indicators

Clinical data included basic patient information, fever duration before treatment, laboratory tests [white blood cell count(WBC), platelet count(PLT), C-reactive protein(CRP), erythrocyte sedimentationrate(ESR), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide(NT-proBNP), and D-dimer, liver function tests [alanine aminotransferase(ALT), aspartateaminotransferase(AST), and total bilirubin(TBIL)], hepatobiliary ultrasound findings, and treatment outcomes [hospital length of stay(LOS), response to IVIG, and presence of CAA]. The two groups were analyzed and compared, and the statistically significant indicators underwent logistic regression to examine the risk factors for KD with CAA and IVIG unresponsiveness.

Additionally, the data of patients with KD and cholestasis were compared with those of patients with acute febrile cholestasis due to other causes during the same period.

Diagnostic criteria

KD, IVIG unresponsiveness, and CAA were defined in accordance with the 2017 American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of KD [10].

The diagnostic criteria for cholestasis were the following: serum direct bilirubin (DBIL) > 17.1 µmol/L, regardless of whether DBIL exceeded 20% of the TBIL level [11].

Exclusion criteria

Patients with the following characteristics were excluded from the study: (1) recurrent KD; (2) receipt of IVIG or glucocorticoid therapy before admission; (3) with a history of liver function abnormalities; (4) with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection; (5) with other systemic or metabolic diseases.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 20.0 statistical software was used for all statistical analysis. Normally distributed data are expressed as means ± standard deviation and between-group differences were assessed using the independent-sample t-test. Non-normally distributed data are expressed as medians (25th percentile, 75th percentile) and between-group comparisons were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Countable data are expressed as percentages (%), and the χ [2] test was used for between-group comparison. Variables that showed a significant association in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, and the results are presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Analysis of related factors between the cholestatic and control groups

Patients in the cholestatic group (n = 83) had a higher mean age than those in the control group [36.0 months (range, 24.0–60.0) vs. 26 months (range, 14.–36), respectively; P < 0.05]. The male: female ratio was 1.52:1, and no significant differences were found in sex compared to the control group. In the cholestatic group, fever duration before admission was (4.72 ± 1.72) days, shorter than that of the control group [(6.17 ± 2.78) days](P < 0.05). The hepatobiliary ultrasound results of 63 patients with KD and cholestasis were normal, whereas 13 patients presented with an enlarged liver, and 7 with gallbladder edema; of these, 58 patients were admitted to the hospital with acute febrile jaundice as the first manifestation.

Compared with the control group, the cholestatic group had significantly higher levels of WBC [(14.56 vs. 12.50)×109/L], CRP [(102.00 vs. 54.28) mg/L], ESR [(79.0 vs. 64.50) mm/h], D-dimer [(693.0 vs. 378.0) µg/L], NT-proBNP [(1267.0 vs. 394.5) pg/mL], ALT [(181.0 vs. 21.0) U/L], AST [(87.0 vs. 25.0) U/L], and TBIL [(43.6 vs. 4.6) µmol/L]; longer LOS [(10.76 vs. 8.04) days]; higher incidence of IVIG unresponsiveness (27.7% vs. 8.1%); and higher incidence of CAA (18.1% vs. 9.4%) (P < 0.05 for all), as shown in Table 1.

Comparison of KD with cholestasis and other types of acute febrile cholestasis

Thirty-two cases of acute febrile cholestasis with etiologies other than KD occurred during the same period; the median age in this group was 48.0 months (range, 30.25–84.0), which was higher than that of the KD group (P < 0.05). Eighteen patients were male, and the male: female ratio was 1.29:1. The average febrile days before treatment were (4.72 ± 1.72) days, without a significant difference compared to the KD group, as shown in Table 2. Of the 32 patients, 12 had cholecystic wall thickening or hydrocholecystis. Serological evaluation was performed to exclude hepatitis virus infection, metabolic diseases, and other autoimmune diseases; 16 patients were diagnosed with viral cholestatic hepatitis (13 caused by Epstein-Barr virus, 1 by herpes simplex virus, 1 by influenza A virus, and 1 by adenovirus), 4 with mycoplasma infection, 3 with bacterial infection, 1 patient with Chlamydia infection, 2 with drug-induced cholestatic liver injury due to acetaminophen, 1 with choledochal cyst, 1 with symptomatic cholelithiasis, and 4 with unknown etiology. A total of 115 cases of cholestasis occurred in the two groups, and the 85 cases of cholestasis caused by KD represented the most common cause of febrile cholestasis in children (72.6%).

No significant difference in PLT, γ-glutamyltranspeptidase(GGT), and bilirubin levels were found between the two groups. Although the KD group had higher WBC, CRP, and D-dimer levels and lower albumin values, their aminotransferase levels were lower than that of children with acute febrile cholestasis with other etiology (P < 0.05).

Influencing factors for IVIG unresponsiveness

IVIG unresponsiveness occurred in 163 cases (9.04%) of all the total patients, with a median age of 21.0 months (range, 14.0–35.0), which was lower than that in the responsive group [27.0 months (range, 15.0–36.0)]; P < 0.05. Twenty-three cases (14.1%) in the responsive group presented combined cholestasis, higher than the number of cases in the unresponsive group. Albumin, ALT, AST, GGT, bilirubin, CRP, NT-proBNP, and D-dimer levels were significantly associated with IVIG unresponsiveness (P < 0.05; Table 3). These factors were included as independent variables in the multivariate logistic regression model, with IVIG unresponsiveness as the dependent variable. The results showed that younger age, presence of cholestasis, lower albumin levels, and higher NT-proBNP levels were risk factors for IVIG unresponsiveness (P < 0.05; Table 4).

Influencing factors for CAA

163 cases of CAA occurred (9.76%) in all the total patients. In the CAA group, boys accounted for 71.0% of cases, higher than the male proportion in the non-CAA group. Compared to the non-CAA group, the CAA group presented more fever days before treatment [(6.01 ± 2.57) days vs. (6.90 ± 4.06) days respectively) and more cases of cholestasis (15 cases; 8.5%; P < 0.05). Albumin, ALT, AST, WBC, PLT, CRP, NT-proBNP, and D-dimer levels were significantly associated with CAA (P < 0.05; Table 5). These factors were included as independent variables in the multivariate logistic regression model, and CAA was included as the dependent variable. The results showed that male sex; more fever days before treatment; and higher ALT, PLT, and CRP levels were risk factors for CAA (P < 0.05; Table 6).

Discussion

KD is an acute self-limited systemic vasculitis that can cause extensive and diverse involvement of arteries in the heart, lungs, liver, gastrointestinal tract, and other organs [12]. Liver dysfunction as a complication usually occurs in the acute phase of KD, with liver involvement ranging from asymptomatic transaminase elevation to severe cholestatic hepatitis and gallbladder hydrops [13]. Eladawy et al. [13] found that elevation in transaminase, GGT, and bilirubin levels were the most common manifestations of liver dysfunction in KD, with detection rates in 240 KD cases of 37.3%, 37.2% and 13.4% respectively. KD complicated with liver function impairment hinders diagnosis and delays treatment. CAA occur in 15–25% of untreated patients (14–15). IVIG therapy reduces the incidence of CAA to 5% during the first 10 days of the disease [16]; therefore, the timely diagnosis and treatment of KD complicated by liver function impairment, especially cholestasis, is essential. However, most cases of KD with cholestasis in literature to date are cases reports [4–9].

The association between KD and cholestasis has been increasingly understood [4–9]. A retrospective study of 24 children with acute febrile cholestatic jaundice found that KD was the second most common cause of febrile jaundice in children (21%), ranking second only to viral infections [17]. The current study included 115 children with acute febrile cholestasis in which KD (72.6%) was the leading cause, followed by viral infection (13.7%), especially Epstein-Barr virus, and mycoplasma (3.4%) was the third cause. Compared to febrile cholestasis from other etiologies, patients with cholestasis caused by KD were younger and presented higher WBC, CRP, and D-dimer levels and lower albumin and transaminase levels. Therefore, in children with acute febrile cholestasis, especially when accompanied by high WBC, CRP, and D-dimer levels and hypoalbuminemia, KD should be suspected.

The pathogenesis of cholestasis in KD remains unclear, but is presumed to be related to intrahepatic cholangitis or obstruction caused by lymphadenectasis, edema of the gallbladder wall, and inflammation of the liver and gallbladder serous membrane [7, 18]. Hepatic biopsy in patients with KD and cholestasis has revealed fragmented liver tissue with accentuated lobular architecture, bile duct hyperplasia, bile duct cell injury with predominant neutrophilic granulocytes, acidophilic bodies, occasional biliary thrombi around the lumina of bile duct cells with ductular proliferation, and evidence of cholangiocyte injury featuring acute cholangiolitis, causing portal vein dilation and acute cholangitis [4]. In some patients, acute hydrocholecystis suggested that inflammatory damage to bile duct cells may extend from the small to the large bile duct [17].

Currently, the incidence of obstructive jaundice caused by gallbladder edema in patients with KD is reportedly approximately 13%, with most patients being asymptomatic. In most cases, the effusion resolves spontaneously without surgical intervention (7, 13, 18–19). However, because of the onset of abdominal or liver symptoms in the early days of some atypical cases of KD, misdiagnosis as a hepatobiliary or gastrointestinal disease, such as hepatitis or acute gallstone cholecystitis, is common. In a case report by Luzzato et al. [20], a patient with obstructive jaundice had evidence of cholecystectomy hydrops and intrahepatic duct dilation and underwent percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage. Falcini et al. [6] reported cases in which cholecystectomy was performed in patients with febrile cholestasis and gallbladder effusion. If KD is diagnosed early, invasive measures can be avoided, as gallbladder effusion and liver dysfunction are temporary conditions associated with KD. In the present study, seven patients (8.2%) with cholestasis showed thickening of the gallbladder wall or gallbladder edema on ultrasound. However, after IVIG treatment, the gallbladder effusion absorbed spontaneously.

Studies have shown that liver biochemical indexes return to normal within 4 days after IVIG treatment [21], and the median recovery time for liver enzymes ranges from 2 to 99 days [22]. In this study, the average recovery time of transaminase and bilirubin levels was (5.14 ± 2.96) days and (2.61 ± 1.55) days, respectively, in KD complicated with cholestasis.

In this study, patients with KD and cholestasis were more likely to develop CAA and IVIG unresponsiveness, compared to those in the control group, which is consistent with previous findings (23–24). Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that younger age, hypoalbuminemia, hyperbilirubinemia, and higher NT-proBNP levels were risk factors for IVIG unresponsiveness, whereas male sex, long duration of fever, and higher ALT, PLT, and CRP levels were risk factors for CAA. Therefore, although liver function is not a diagnostic tool for KD, it may reflect the severity of systemic inflammation to some extent and, thus, serve as a prognostic marker for IVIG unresponsiveness or CAA (13, 23–24). Therefore, attention should be paid to the examination of liver function in children, as early diagnosis and treatment can reduce complications and improve prognosis.

Some limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, due to the retrospective nature of the study, complete data were not available for some patients. Second, the examination testing time could not be unified because the patients visited the hospital during different periods of fever. Third, this was a single-center study, which requires further evaluation in multi-regional and multi-center studies.

In conclusion, KD, combined with cholestasis is associated with a higher risk of IVIG unresponsiveness and CAA. Moreover, KD is the most common cause of acute febrile cholestasis in children. Attention to the possibility of KD is warranted in cases of febrile cholestatic jaundice in children, especially in those associated with elevated WBC, CRP, and D-dimer levels, or hypoalbuminemia.
