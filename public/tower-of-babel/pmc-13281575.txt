Integrating family-based interventions into specialty court programs: qualitative guidance from sex trafficking specialty court participants and their supportive family members
Authors: Aaron Murnan, Jane Mahon, Gwen Jackson, Symone Pate, Susan Thompson, Christina Stewart, Jennifer Brown, Sarah Manchak, Melinda Butsch Kovacic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe intersection of sex trade involvement and substance use disorder is a deeply complex and tragic issue for many women, as decades of research reflect that traditional substance use treatment modalities often fail to meet many of these women’s unique needs. Sex trafficking specialty court programs constitute a unique and promising avenue to deliver evidence-based practices to treat substance use disorders and to support health and well-being among this largely service disconnected and marginalized population. A growing body of evidence suggests these specialty court programs may be strengthened by the inclusion of family-based interventions. A critical preliminary step to the potential integration of family interventions is the development of processes to identify and engage supportive family members, as well as guidelines on discerning which family members’ inclusion is likely to be beneficial versus detrimental. The current study provides direct insights from sex trafficking specialty court participants and their family members regarding family involvement within specialty court treatment programming and potential family therapy services.MethodsFifteen family dyads consisting of one sex trafficking specialty court program participant paired with a supportive family member (identified by program participants) were invited to participate in interviews exploring best practices for identifying and engaging supportive family members in sex trafficking specialty court programming. Inductive Content Analysis was used to analyze interview transcript data.ResultsFour themes were identified: (1) characteristics and dynamics within familial relationships; (2) selection criteria for family members to be invited to participate; (3) barriers to successful family participation; and (4) perceptions of family therapy.ConclusionsThis study provides insights to inform efforts to engage family supports through the development and integration of family interventions that may bolster substance use treatment success among sex trafficking specialty court participants.Keywords: Sex trafficking specialty court programs, Treatment courts, Substance use treatment, Family intervention

Introduction

Women in the sex trade have been disproportionately affected by the opioid epidemic as sex trading and opioid use (along with use of other substances) commonly co-occur and create feedback loops that unfold numerous consequences to their health and well-being (Argento et al., 2015; Gilchrist et al., 2005; Surratt et al., 2006). Harsh public perceptions of sex trading are common and hinder treatment engagement and disclosure of sex trading (Duff et al., 2015), yet stigmatization largely ignores the unique constellation of risk factors that often underly and precipitate sex trade involvement, such as childhood abuse, early onset substance use, poverty, homelessness, and early school dropout (Murphy, 2010). Many of these women seek substance use treatment, yet their treatment success is markedly low (Nuttbrock et al., 2004). These shortcomings of substance use treatment attempts are exacerbated by a lack of evidence-based practice established in partnership with women who have lived experience with sex trading and substance use.

Individuals who have challenges with mental health and/or problematic substance use who encounter the criminal legal system are sometimes eligible to participate in specialized court programs. These programs are often referred to as treatment or recovery courts, which cater services to specific populations. One such court program developed over the last few decades is sex trafficking specialty courts (Kulig & Butler, 2019; Marlowe et al., 2016). Similar to other specialty court programs, participation in these programs has many benefits, including comprehensive, wrap-around treatment and support for malleable factors underlying victimhood, such as substance use, unaddressed mental health, and other risks for criminal recidivism (Liles et al., 2016).

While under supervision of these courts, participants are often expected to abide by a number of rules and restrictions. One such example is banning communication with others who have histories of problematic substance use and/or a criminal record (Corbett, 2015; Petersilia, 1997). These types of social contact bans are grounded in research identifying antisocial associations as a leading risk factor for recidivism (Akers & Jennings, 2019; Bonta & Andrews, 2016; Pratt et al., 2010; Thomas, 2016). Given the complexities within women’s larger family systems, (e.g., substance use or familial trafficking), limited court resources, and a lack of evidence-based guidance, these social restriction policies are most feasibly applied in a broad and wide-reaching manner (Doherty, 2015; Karteron, 2022) However, wide-spread application of these policies ignore the possibility of potential benefits and supportive elements within some women’s familial relationships within their larger family systems.

The gendered pathways literature (Adler, 1975; Boppre et al., 2018); DeHart et al., 2014; Salisbury & VanVoorhis, 2009) has repeatedly demonstrated that women’s involvement in criminal activity is highly influenced by their familial and intimate partner relationships—complex relationships that can both facilitate antisocial activity and reduce recidivism in criminal activity. This is particularly true among women in the sex trade, as some women report experiences of manipulation, exploitation, and being trafficked by romantic partners and/or family (Horning & Sriken, 2017; Le & Wyndham, 2022; Sprang & Cole, 2018; Wheeler, 2022), while also experiencing trauma bonds with those individuals (Casassa et al., 2024). When considered in isolation, these findings suggest the presence and maintenance of family relationships may inherently jeopardize women’s specialty treatment court progress and place them at further risk for continued victimization.

On the other hand, women in the sex trade who engage in substance use treatment have also identified the presence of supportive family members who promote their treatment success and have expressed desires for substance use treatment approaches that can enhance successful re-integration into these family relationships, cultivate familial support, and catalyze family-level changes to support their long-term recovery after they leave treatment (Murnan, 2021; Murnan & Ferber, 2020; Murnan & Holowacz, 2020). Given social support is strongly related to lower rates of criminal recidivism, reduced substance use and mental health symptoms, as well as improved relationships with professionals within the criminal legal system; all of which are strong predictors of success within these programs (Hogue et al., 2022; Kennealy et al., 2012; Manchak et al., 2014; Spohr et al., 2016); strengthening relationships with supportive family members could have a meaningful impact on women’s ability to maintain long-term reductions in substance use and risk behaviors post-treatment.

Within the substance use treatment literature, extensive research among general treatment-seeking samples reflects family interventions are effective at promoting long-term reductions in substance use (Hogue et al., 2022; Rowe, 2012). By engaging family members into treatment, these programs are able to address correlated family factors such as family support, conflict, functioning, and cohesion; as well as catalyze new, more supportive family interactions and environments to promote long-term substance use and well-being (Hogue et al., 2022; Rowe, 2012). For example, within family drug courts––another type of specialty court designed for women dually involved with the criminal and family court systems––family interventions have shown success at improving participants’ clinical (e.g., substance use and mental health) and familial outcomes (e.g., family functioning, parenting practices, child welfare; Dakof et al., 2009; Green et al., 2007; Worcel et al., 2008). Furthermore, a small yet growing body of research suggests family-based interventions may be well-situated to address salient and unique risk factors for substance use treatment and criminal recidivism among women in the sex trade (Murnan et al., 2017; Nichols et al., 2025).

The prospect of integrating family-based interventions into sex trafficking specialty court programs is a complex endeavor that requires careful consideration and evidence-based approaches. These programs appropriately prioritize creating a treatment environment in which women can focus on their recovery without exposure to manipulation, coercion, force, fraud, or abuse that may characterize their experiences prior to program entry. This reality is difficult to hold in balance with participants’ strong desires for family interventions and a robust body of research supporting family-based interventions as beneficial in substance use treatment (Murnan et al., 2017; Murnan & Ferber, 2020; Nichols et al., 2025). Implementation efforts are hindered by limited guidance on how to identify and engage family members who can meaningfully support participants’ recovery. A critical first step, therefore, is the development of processes to assess and engage supportive family members in ways that are responsive to women’s desire for family engagement yet also center participant safety and treatment success. Importantly, these efforts should be guided by perspectives and stated preferences of women with lived experience, ideally generated through community-partnered research approaches.

The current study is a part of a larger community partnered project leveraging co-design methodologies that aims to adapt and test a family intervention targeting opioid use and family factors for sex trafficking specialty court program participants in a large midwestern city. The current study addresses gaps in the literature by reflecting insights from sex trafficking specialty court participants and their family members regarding family involvement in their court programming via the creation of a hypothetical scaffolded family intervention. A co-design team comprised of women with lived experience was assembled to help guide study design and conduct as well as iteratively interpret finding. This study seeks to gain insights on women and families’ perspectives on identifying and engaging supportive family members into family interventions as part of sex trafficking specialty court programming.

Method

Overview

Sex trafficking specialty court program participants, who identify as women and their named supportive family members (n = 15 family dyads; 30 individuals) were invited to participate in separate, semi-structured qualitative interviews that focused on current relationships with their family members, including the participating family member. Participants were also asked to provide feedback on how family interventions can differentiate family members who would be supportive of women’s substance use treatment progress and those who may impede or interfere with women’s treatment success. Participants shared their insights on potential barriers to successfully implementing a family intervention within a sex trafficking specialty court program. This study marks the first inquiry with these dyads to focus on how providers can make determinations about protective and harmful family inclusion as well as successfully identify and engage supportive family into specialty court programming via family intervention. The study utilized Inductive Content Analysis of transcribed qualitative interviews. The methods were conducted in line with Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (O’Brien et al., 2014).

Approach

Co-design methodologies

To support this work, the first author, who is trained and licensed as a family therapist with prior expertise working with women with histories of sex trading and substance use, assembled a co-design team comprised of four women with lived experience and the director of a sex trafficking specialty court program to inform all phases of the study design. To be eligible, individuals needed to have participated in a sex trafficking specialty court program and report prior opioid use and engagement in the sex trade industry. Prior to starting, the first author met with prospective co-design team members and reviewed study details, answered questions, and then reviewed and obtained informed consent. The first author provided training including the planned research methodology, co-designers’ roles, and intervention design. Co-design team members were compensated $100 per meeting via a reloadable debit card. Initial meetings focused on rapport building, learning, and co-creating rules and expectations for the team’s meetings. Expectations and rules included schedules for meetings; location of meetings; how members would treat and communicate with one another; assigning group roles to different members to disperse power; how the team would address potential disagreements or mistakes; and steps to address members’ not adhering to co-created rules. The team also co-created and established a desired group culture, which included things like valuing and respecting each member’s knowledge, approaching discussions with curiosity and empathy, and allowing set time before/after meetings for members to discuss on-going challenges in their own lives and receive support. The co-design team worked together to design and inform various aspects of the study across all study phases. Specifically, co-designers assisted with designing interview scripts/demographic surveys and brainstorming recruitment/engagement strategies. Throughout the qualitative methodology, the co-design team refined initial copies of the qualitative codebook, reviewed, refined, and contextualized preliminary findings and interpretations, as well as collaborated to draft sections of the current manuscript. The co-design team expressed a strong preference for data collectors to be trusted figures as opposed to university personnel and insisted that a trusted specialty court program staff member be trained to conduct the qualitative interviews to ensure women and families felt comfortable, as well as reflect the commitment of the court to creating a family program. To support this request, one of the court staff joined the study team and was provided research training and on-going supervision (see Data Collection below for more details). Additional details about the co-design team and methodologies can be found elsewhere (Murnan et al., 2026).

Sample

Fifteen family dyads (n = 15 dyads; 30 individuals) were recruited from a sex trafficking specialty court program in a large midwestern city. Each dyad was comprised of a: (a) sex trafficking specialty court participant and (b) a supportive family member they selected/invited. In order to be eligible, women needed to be: (1) at least 18 years of age, (2) currently participating in a sex trafficking specialty court program, (3) met Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders – 5 (DSM-5; American Psychiatric Association, 2013) criteria for an Opioid Use Disorder (OUD), (4) report current or prior sex trade involvement, (5) be able and willing to identify a supportive family member willing to participate, and (6) consent to audio-recorded interviews. Inclusion criteria for family members identified by participants included being at least 18 years old and willing to consent to an audio-recorded interview. Interviews were conducted separately with each dyad member to reduce potential emotional distress and encourage transparency about current familial relations and prospective family integration into specialty court treatment programming. All participants were recruited and participated between October, 2023 and April, 2024.

Participants involved in sex trafficking specialty court programming were between the ages of 27 to 48 years of age (M = 36.24; SD = 6.56). Racial identity among court participants were as follows: 9 identified as White, 4 identified as multi-racial, and 2 identified as Black. Eleven specialty court participants (73.3%) identified themselves as having experienced sex trafficking at some point, whereas 20% (n = 3) did not (1 preferred not to answer). Eighty six point 7% of specialty court participants reported having at least one child, with their number of children ranging from 1 to 6 (M = 2.62; SD = 1.71). The specialty court participants’ supportive family members included 6 mothers, 4 adult children (2 sons and 2 daughters), 3 sisters, and two romantic partners and fathers to their children (1 husband and 1 partner). Procedures

Court staff shared study details with program participants and referred interested participants to study staff. The first fifteen women who expressed interest were referred to study staff. Study staff then conducted a brief eligibility screening to confirm prospective participants met eligibility criteria. All study participants who were screened met eligibility criteria. Once eligibility was confirmed, study visits were flexibly scheduled with participating dyads to review and obtain informed consent and complete interviews and a brief demographic survey. Study staff conducted interviews individually with participating dyad members at private locations of their choice which included: private offices at the court program, trusted local service providers serving this population, or within participants’ homes. As part of the study visit, interviewers reviewed study procedures as well as reviewed and obtained informed consent via REDCap. As part of the informed consent process, participants were assured of the voluntary nature of their participation, they could stop at any time, and their participation would have no impact on their treatment programming. Confidentiality protocol and exceptions to confidentiality were thoroughly reviewed to best inform prospective participants’ decisions to participate or not. Participants were offered a $100 gift card as compensation for their time. The University of Cincinnati's Institutional Review Board approved all study procedures prior to data collection.

Data collection

Following consent procedures, participating dyads then completed a demographic questionnaire and a semi-structured in-person interview. The demographic questionnaire included information regarding racial and ethnic identity, age, biological sex, education, as well as lifestyle risk factors (e.g., criminal justice involvement, substance use, sex trade industry participation). The interviews utilized a variety of questions: introductory, follow-up, and probing in order to gather comprehensive participant accounts. Because little is known about the topic of interest, the interviewer purposefully avoided making assumptions about topics being queried and used open-ended questions to elicit more open responses. Interview scripts and procedures were the same for specialty court participants and family participants. Interviews lasted approximately one hour and were audio-recorded. In line with co-design team recommendation and prior work (Murnan et al., 2024), the first author hired and trained a trusted program staff member to conduct qualitative interviews. The identified interviewer had prior experience conducting clinical and qualitative research interviews with this population as well as extensive experience in advocacy work on behalf of women in the sex trade. The interviewer received additional training from the first author in the areas of qualitative interviewing methodologies, ethical research, and the details of this study. The first author held weekly meetings with the study interviewer to monitor progress, discuss emergent questions, and concerns. Survey and interview data were stored on OneDrive to ensure security and confidentiality of data.

Data analyses

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, de-identified, double-checked for quality assurance, and then permanently destroyed. All coders had prior experience with qualitative coding procedures and were provided training and consistent supervision by the first author to enhance reliable, stable, accurate, and replicable codes. Coders’ training included training on the study protocol, qualitative methods, and relevant literature to build their knowledge on sex trade involvement and substance use (see e.g., Berends & Johnson, 2005; Braun & Clarke, 2013; Cole, 1988). Interview transcripts were analyzed using Inductive Content Analysis (Elo & Kyngäs, 2008; Lauri & Kyngäs, 2005), which includes three phases: preparation, organization, and reporting. Inductive Content Analysis methods were the same for all dyad members (specialty court program participants and family participants) and coded together.

During the preparation stage, two coders with prior qualitative experience, immersed themselves in the data by reading through all the interview files several times, taking notes, and holding regular meetings to iteratively refine and organize common themes in order to draft an initial codebook. Initial study coders stepped away midway through the preparation phase and were replaced with two new doctoral-level student coders, who finalized the first draft of the codebook. In the organization stage, these two coders conducted open coding with a subset of interviews. The first author and coders met regularly to discuss similar and differing codes to inform codebook refinement and replicable coding practices, after reviewing each interview. Following several iterations, a revised version of the codebook was reviewed with co-design team, who provided insights on revisions, organization of codes, and further refinement. This informed a final revised version of the codebook. Once formal coding began, coders used NVivo software to review all interview transcripts. The coders read each interview and highlighted text that reflected themes identified within the codebook. Then the coders met to discuss and compare their codes, placement of codes, themes, and questions for each interview prior to coding the next interview. When diverging codes occurred, coders would discuss discrepancies until a consensus was reached. If an agreement could not be reached between the coders, the first author would be triangulated to assist. Any interview with a cumulative inter-rater reliability below 0.90 or containing a single code with an inter-rater reliability below 0.80 was re-coded. This did not occur after the practice coding phase. After all interviews were coded, the coding team began to prepare findings for dissemination. Prior to drafting components of the manuscript for publication, the co-design team reviewed results to ensure and promote validity and credibility of findings.

Results

Several qualitative themes were identified within participating family dyads responses related to their current familial relationships and shared insights about identifying and engaging supportive family members into women’s substance use treatment. Four broad categories were identified: (a) characteristics of familial relationships necessary to contextualize a family intervention; (b) selection criteria for family members to participate; (c) potential interpersonal and structural barriers to family participation; and d) perceptions of family therapy. In line with prior qualitative work, many participants referred to involvement in the sex trade and/or substance use as engagement in “the life(style),” thus this constellation of interconnected experiences together is presented herein using such terminology (Dalla, 2000). Identified themes within participant interviews, including sex trafficking specialty court participants and supportive family members are summarized below. In addition to reporting total frequencies for all 30 participants, the authors present counts for the sex trafficking specialty court participants who discussed themes are indicated by ‘ST =’ and counts for participating supportive family members are indicated by ‘F =’.

Family relationship characteristics

Almost all participants described their familial relationships as complicated and nuanced in nature (n = 27; ST = 14; F = 13), in which the relationships included unresolved issues and prior damage, as well as strong relational ties, support, and encouragement towards their treatment efforts. The presence of shared or intergenerational patterns within families in “the life” (n = 21; ST = 9; F = 12) was a recurrent theme. Specifically, participants discussed the presence of shared familial experiences of criminal justice involvement, using substances together and engaging in the sex trade industry that persisted across generations. Even so, no active illicit substance use was reported among chosen family participants. Larger family norms surrounding involvement in “the life” often clashed with substance use treatment norms and goals. Participants described these shared circumstances as complicating their relationships due to long-term relational damage (n = 17; ST = 12; F = 5) in some cases perceived to be related to using substances and/or working in the industry together. Some participants reflected feeling “full of guilt and shame” and as though they “didn’t want to face” their supportive family members. Family participants detailed deep-seated fear and distrust stemming from specialty court participants stealing from them, jeopardizing their safety, prior incidents of overdose, and long periods of no-contact or disappearances while actively in “the life”. Two family members who were both mothers of specialty court participants noted constant, long-standing worries regarding whether their “child is alive or dead.” Another family participant detailed shame, fear, and frustration related to family and friends regularly asking them about their daughter, who engaged in the sex trade industry within their shared neighborhood.

Despite the presence of relational damage, nearly every participant emphasized the strong presence of support within these relationships (n = 29; ST = 14; F = 15) and the ways family currently supported specialty court participants’ treatment progress and long-term success. Family members provided support in myriad ways, such as providing transportation to treatment programming, childcare, financial assistance, and positive emotional reinforcement for treatment progress. Some family members traveled great distances to visit and offer encouragement during residential treatment phases. One specialty court participant attributed her family’s support as critically influential in reducing her feelings of isolation and making treatment tolerable:

“Oh my gosh, like, I blossomed. Like, I had their support and, um, I felt loved and a part of, and I felt like I was helping my mom with life by being better and giving her hope and stuff.”

In another case, motivation from family members was described as the main factor that kept her in treatment on difficult days:

“… on really hard days, I have to remind myself like ‘my mom believes in me and she needs me and I don’t want to put her through that pain again.’ That’s what gets me through it… and I feel like only family can really do that… give that kind of motivation.”

The supportive elements of these relationships with parents, siblings, or adult children were deemed critical, as participants pointed out that without familial support and motivation, they would remain vulnerable to relapse and re-engagement in the sex trade industry or potential sex trafficking situations post-treatment. Supportive acts by family members illustrated that while relationships may present complexities, these chosen family members constituted sources of support and care that aided women’s progress in treatment. As one court participant explained:

“… I know the first couple times that I tried to get clean, my family wasn’t there and I just had no urge to do it. ‘Cause what was the point? They wasn’t gonna be there anyway. So I think it’s huge to have your family’s support.”

Similar sentiments were echoed by family participants regarding their potential inclusion in specialty court participants’ treatment. Eight family participants expressed hope to reinforce specialty court participants’ desires to build better lives and achieve their goals. Specifically, family participants expressed desires to develop a better understanding of triggers and strategies to better support participants’ success. Despite the complicated nature of family dynamics and presence of relational damage, family dyads consistently reaffirmed the importance of family support and relationships as well as their commitment to supporting one another while in treatment and after treatment.

Selection criteria for inclusion of family members in treatment programming

Participants discussed potential selection criteria or considerations of family members’ personal qualities and behaviors to inform guidelines on who may be invited to participate in a hypothetical family therapy with sex trafficking specialty court participants. Participants noted several key considerations for family members inclusion, such as the family members’ prior versus active substance use and/or involvement in the sex trade (n = 17; ST = 12; F = 5); previous roles in women’s exploitation or abuse (n = 13; ST = 13; F = 0); benefits of prior lived experience with substance use and the sex trade industry (n = 8; ST = 7; F = 1); as well as their interpersonal characteristics (n = 14; ST = 10; F = 4) and willingness to participate and support women’s treatment success (n = 19; ST = 11; F = 8). Participants frequently reiterated the importance of empowering sex trafficking specialty court participants to make informed decisions regarding which family members to include in their treatment through individualized selection processes.

Active participation in ‘the Life’

Slightly more than half of the participants (n = 17; ST = 12; F = 5) suggested the exclusion of family members with histories of using substances or involvement in the sex trade industry. Participants noted the potential for someone actively involved in “the life” to interfere with their success, whereas others expressed concern that folks “nodding off”’ or ‘using street slang could be triggering to women who are making progress in substance use treatment. Interestingly, 9 of these 17 participants also challenged this as a blanket policy, insisting that individuals who have histories of involvement and are not currently active in “the life” should not be inherently excluded. In fact, it was noted that family members who shared lived experiences and have changed their lives could enhance treatment, as these individuals can readily relate to the challenges women faced, whether related to the sex trade, struggles with substance use, the criminal justice system, or treatment (n = 8). Participants envisioned these shared experiences would break down barriers for talking about trauma and treatment challenges and generating empathy/understanding. One specialty court participant described these benefits in potentially participating with her sister:

“… she’s been to prison. And through different services that I know that the courses that we could go through can help her and her personal life. And I can be open. And she won’t hide anything. She’ll keep it 100. Like no sugar coating [laughs].”

While participants held firm distinctions of active vs. prior substance use for themselves and in their own recovery, they noted that for other women it may be appropriate for individuals who are actively using to participate if one of the following caveats were true: (1) they were committed to making changes in their own substance use and sex trading behaviors or (2) they were highly committed to supporting women’s treatment success and were able to attend sessions sober. The importance of someone’s willingness to change was detailed by one participant as:

“…People who have no desire to exit the life – whether it be the street life or the sex industry – if you have no desire, I don’t think that somebody can fabricate that for you…You have to want it. Want to either kind of get out or just anything. Otherwise, you’re just kind of like… None of the information is gonna be… It’s like not gonna be able to penetrate that – that steel wall that’s been created to be in the street life and all of that. It won’t be able to be broken.”

While sobriety in session was considered mandatory; participants noted that including family members who use substances could help these individuals connect with needed resources; “learn about living clean”; and “see a different way of life and to see that people are getting clean and staying clean.” As noted in the previous quote, specialty court participants expressed empathy towards family members’ resistance to treatment and being vulnerable through therapy, which was described as a “steel wall,” in which involvement in the streets often leads to a “tough guy” or “hard body” persona, making it increasingly difficult to accept help. Sex trafficking specialty court participants wondered if family participants may not want to be portrayed as “soft” or “vulnerable” as they are accustomed to an “all or nothing”, “survival” mindset, but could be successful at “opening up” over time.

Often, the main difference sex trafficking specialty court participants noted between themselves, and family members with shared experiences in “the life” was that they had been arrested and subsequently connected with substance use treatment services, whereas their family members may not have had that opportunity. As such, specialty court participants had a great deal of empathy and understanding for their family members. One participant described:

“I love my mom. Without my mom, I don’t think – I definitely wouldn’t be the person that I am today. ‘Cause now I see a lot more of me in her, my mom was – she has a lot of mental issues and stuff and trauma that she had when she was a kid. So, it’s like… for her to turn around and take care of 8 kids, and have to do everything, and like, I dunno. It’s – it’s – I see, I never realized how similar me and her were.”

Exploitative or abusive family members

Participants acknowledged that sexual abuse and serious violence perpetrated by family members as well as instances of introducing women into the sex trade require careful consideration (n = 13; ST = 13; F = 0). While none of the family members selected had perpetuated sexual abuse against specialty court participants; it was noted by specialty court participants (n = 11; ST = 11; F = 0) that family members who were “abusers” or contributed to women’s trafficking victimhood should be excluded. Specific emphasis was placed on excluding family members who raped or sexually assaulted women as children or were identified as their first trafficker. For example, one court participant stated: “I mean, a lot of the girls, their parents… beat them violently or something like that. And I feel like it will just trigger the client more than it will help them.” Another participant shared, “… people are sexually abused by their family, trafficked by their family, like… Sometimes family members are abusers so… like in that instance [they should be excluded].” Interestingly, 11 participants, including 9 of those who stated such family members should be excluded, also suggested circumstances should be individualized, reinforcing other court participants’ autonomy over who is ultimately involved in their recovery. One court participant stated: “Honestly, I feel like if that’s who they’re wanting – so that way they can build that relationship back – then that should be good.” It is important to note that these caveat instances were in the context of discussing other women’s treatment (not their own) and included scenarios where prior physical or emotional (but not sexual) abusers felt remorse and were no longer behaving in abusive, manipulative, or coercive fashions, in these instances participants felt their inclusion could be appropriate. Sex trafficking specialty court participants expressed great empathy and consideration for family members who grew up in similar situations, where they were constantly surrounded by addiction and involved in the sex trade themselves at early ages. They perceived family therapy could represent a good opportunity to heal and re-establish new relationships and norms together. This was summarized by one specialty court participant reflecting:

“Cause sometimes, too, abusers – especially if it’s a parent or something – they, in rare cases, are like ‘I didn’t know that this affected you like that. I was just doing the best that I could.’ Some of them are addicts themselves or are in the sex industry themselves so it’s like ‘I was just doing what I knew, and what I was comfortable with. And I’m willing to change because now I know.’”

Interpersonal characteristics

Participants noted the importance of interpersonal considerations, such as family members’ interpersonal characteristics (n = 14; ST = 10; F = 4) and willingness to participate (n = 19; ST = 11; F = 8) as critical factors that would influence the quality of engagement with the family therapy program. Several court and family participants described personality traits like aggression, defensiveness, stubbornness, narcissism, manipulativeness, unreliability, being actively violent, and/or overall closed-mindedness as factors that may prevent their family members from successfully engaging in family therapy. They often described these types of family members as holding “old-fashioned mindsets” that lead to “shutting down” difficult conversations or responding with hurtful or stigmatizing remarks. Alternatively, specialty court participants felt that incorporating family members who were unwilling to participate or “there because they feel like they have to be there” would be counterproductive. One participant described including these types of family members as “like talking to a wall. You’re not gonna get anywhere with it”. On the other hand, participants pointed out prioritizing the inclusion and engagement of family members who were naturally “open-minded”; “honest”; “understanding”; “able to listen”; “compassionate”; “loving”; “not judgmental”; and “self-aware”. However, it was noted that those who were motivated and willing to participate in family therapy would likely be a good fit, as they could learn some of these skills via therapy participation.

Respecting women’s autonomy in decision-making

A consensus among participants seemed to form around the importance of flexibility (n = 27; ST = 14; F = 13) to think through these issues and make decisions about who to invite and details of family integration into their treatment. Participants highlighted how different family members could positively or negatively influence their treatment and how the selection process should be individualized. One participant emphasized that these decisions could be best supported by “someone to kind of guide and mediate” difficult decisions, such as “trained professionals;” however, women should have power to make decisions regarding their treatment success as opposed to have decisions made for them. Three family participants explicitly emphasized the importance of respecting and promoting the autonomy of sex trafficking specialty court participants in collaborating with treatment staff on decisions related to selecting family members to engage in their substance use treatment as well as processes for their engagement.

Barriers to program participation

Many participants identified potential interpersonal (n = 27; ST = 15; F = 12) and structural barriers (n = 29; ST = 15; F = 14) that may influence successful participation among family members. Interpersonal barriers discussed included many of the same factors described above in the discussion around interpersonal characteristics for consideration related to eligibility criteria and included personality traits such as being aggressive, defensive, judgmental, narcissistic, manipulative, coercive, or actively violent. Participants also highlighted structural barriers that could inhibit family members’ meaningful engagement in family therapy. The two most common structural barriers mentioned included timing or schedule conflicts (n = 22; ST = 11; F = 11) and a lack of stable transportation (n = 20; ST = 11; F = 9). For example, one family participant discussed how “schedule is a big thing” and that ensuring “everybody is prepared for the topics” is important when involving family members. Several court and family participants also expressed how childcare could serve as a barrier to family therapy participation for family (n = 9; ST = 5; F = 4). It was emphasized that the program being associated with the courts may scare family members away due to fear of getting in trouble; feeling “leery”, or “freeze up” (n = 5; ST = 2; F = 3). This fear may be especially present among this population as many participants’ family members noted histories of criminal justice involvement and/or problematic substance use. Overall, these potential barriers are important to consider when determining what family members’ inclusion would be beneficial as well as how the program can help alleviate some of these challenges.

Family perceptions of family therapy

Participating dyad members expressed strong support for and the perception that family therapy would be beneficial towards the long-term substance use treatment success of sex trafficking specialty court participants (n = 23; ST = 14; F = 9) and would constitute a more approachable strategy to improve their family relationships (n = 9; ST = 4; F = 5). Participants described family therapy as an avenue to navigate difficult discussions, repair relational damage, and cultivate family support in a “less stressful”, “less awkward” and a “safe environment.” One participant described how family therapy would “open their [family members] mind a little bit” and show them that they are “trying to grow into a better version” of themselves. Family therapy was described by others as making these processes “less overwhelming and scary.” Participants perceived it as beneficial to have therapists’ support to “guide” and “direct” the family in creating new family interaction patterns “without judgement and bypassing prior resentments”. One participant felt family therapy would help “build a stronger foundation with family and your support outside of [specialty court program name].” Another participant expressed previously wanting to do family therapy while in treatment but pointed out it is not an offered option. In other instances, participants felt adding a family therapy into sex trafficking specialty court programs could be “life changing” by “offering a foundation.” In another instance, one participant felt that family therapy would lead to more family support and connection and would be protective for women from risk of becoming the victim of sex trafficking in the future. As she stated:

“It’s like you go from having that to like not having…. so you gotta make sure that your support is strong. And I feel like that’s the – that’s like the number one place to start - is your own family. And you gotta have – you have to have it. Like… being a wanderer or feeling lost is what gets you out there. I mean, really, it is. It’s what happened. That’s the kind of people that traffickers even prey on. It’s like… the young teenager – y’know, young girls or women who don’t have support… y’know… so they try to offer that. So, it’s like I just really. I just can’t stress that enough.”

Two court-involved participants noted the delivery of a family intervention should occur later in treatment (specialty court consists of four phases) when women were more advanced in their recovery

“to make sure that they’re there in their recovery before cause then they can see their family and then want it for their family and then something goes bad, you know what I mean? And then they’re relapsed or gone or depressed… you don’t want Phase 1s [to be engaging in family intervention].”

Participants also suggested individualized therapy sessions between therapist and court participants as well as therapist and family members occurring separately, prior to working together as a family (n = 21; ST = 13; F = 8). These sessions were described to serve multiple functions including preparing court participants for families’ sessions. Additionally these sessions were also thought to help family members acclimation to a therapeutic environment and build trust with the therapist as well as an opportunity for them to gain knowledge and develop skills to best support productive family sessions.

Discussion

Findings elevate the perceptions and preferences of sex trafficking specialty court participants, and their identified family supports. Findings offer insights to growing discussions about potential benefits of family interventions for women in the sex trade and their integration into sex trafficking specialty court programs. Current findings also offer insights and key considerations to inform strategies for successfully and acceptably identifying and integrating supportive family members into women’s treatment via these modalities.

Selecting family members

Decisions regarding which family members to invite into women’s treatment require careful consideration. Participants emphasized the importance of excluding family members who had perpetuated sexual abuse, were actively involved in “the life,” or were unable to attend sessions sober or engage productively. Women similarly cautioned against involving family members perceived as manipulative, coercive, narcissistic, unwilling to engage in therapy, or uninterested in their long-term success. These perspectives align with specialty court priorities and best practices in the substance use and family science literature, which caution against including active abusers or individuals who continue to struggle with substance use due to documented resistance to change, manipulation of professionals, and continued harm to women following family intervention (Katz, 2025; Yllo & Lawrence, 1993). For this population, involving individuals who seek to coerce, control, or exploit women would undermine treatment and pose serious risks to both their short- and long-term wellbeing.

At the same time, women emphasized the importance of autonomy in decisions about family inclusion. They expressed openness to including family members with histories of perpetuating abuse or who were actively involved in “the life” in the context of other women’s treatment if those women believed such involvement to be appropriate. These views reflect a strong commitment to flexible, individualized women-centered decision-making; however, also suggest structured support from trusted treatment providers in this decision making would be advantageous. Ultimately, family selection processes should balance women’s empowerment and autonomy with structured support from treatment staff who can provide guidance and assist women in evaluating which family relationships are likely to support recovery and which may undermine it.

Engaging families may be most appropriate later in treatment, once women in specialty court programming are more stable within their own recovery, and through a stepped-approach. Participants recommended a stepped-approach in which therapists first meet individually with participants prior to convening family sessions. This approach allows therapists to build rapport, provide education, prepare dyads for family work and support participants in clarifying expectations, while also assessing readiness and identifying family members whose involvement may jeopardize treatment progress.

Notably, all participants in the current study were able to identify at least one supportive family member willing to participate, who met suggested criteria they outlined. Although some of these individuals reported histories of problematic substance use, criminal justice involvement, or participation in the sex trade, they were no longer engaged in these behaviors. Rather they were described as providing substantial support to women and expressed willingness to actively support program success. These findings suggest that women can identify family members who serve as protective influences within broader family systems that may also include non-supportive or harmful individuals. Participants further noted that family members with prior lived experience were often less judgmental and easier to communicate with, indicating that such experiences when accompanied by meaningful lifestyle changes may represent unique strengths for family therapy. Programs may therefore benefit from recognizing prior lived experiences and on-going recovery among family members as potential protective resources.

Addressing common barriers by meeting families “Where They Are At”

Women and their family members described numerous structural barriers to their successful engagement/participation, including transportation, childcare, busy schedules, and distrust/fear of the criminal legal system for which sex trafficking specialty court programs are housed. Structural barriers require careful attention for family programs to be feasible. To date, the only study evaluating family therapy among women in the sex trade who seek substance use treatment employed an ecologically informed model that paired family therapy with case management services, actively linking families to resources that addressed their basic needs rather than relying solely on referral (Murnan et al., 2017; Slesnick & Zhang, 2016). Further, families in that study were also provided transportation support in the form of direct transportation from therapists as well as gas cards/bus passes. Family therapy was also provided via in-home and in-office settings. It may be the case that some type of childcare support option may be required to ensure supportive family members can attend family therapy sessions and their dependents can be safely looked after. Decades of research reflects that traditional intervention modalities often fail to meet the unique needs of this population. As such, clinicians and provider systems working with this population would be well-served to accept the onus of “meeting these families where they are at.” This is likely to require flexible scheduling, empathy and understanding when last-minute changes need to be made, and individualized accommodation for families to ensure that they can actively engage in family therapy in a way that fits their family circumstances and overcomes any existing structural barriers.

Potential benefits of family therapy

Findings are well-aligned with growing recommendations for incorporating family-based interventions into the treatment programming of women in the sex trade, including victims of sex trafficking (Bounds et al., 2023; Le et al., 2018; Marburger & Pickover, 2020; Murnan & Ferber, 2020; Murnan et al., 2017; Nichols et al., 2025). Participant dyads expressed strong enthusiasm for incorporating family therapy in sex trafficking specialty court programs, viewing it as an approachable way to address familial influences on treatment success and build upon existing support. Unlike individual approaches, family therapy targets first-order change (individual behavior) and second-order change (changes in the family system) simultaneously to further support and enforce first-order changes long-term (Becvar & Becvar, 1999). While individual-based approaches may successfully catalyze first-order changes in substance use behaviors and participation in the sex trade industry while women are actively in treatment, many participants struggle to maintain these first-order changes following treatment when they are disconnected from supportive treatment environments. Specifically, women report challenges encountered when second-order changes within their family and social support circles have not been made and they re-acclimate into these environments after treatment (Murnan, 2021; Murnan & Ferber, 2020). Family therapy addresses these obstacles by integrating supportive family members into treatment to address family conflict, promote familial support, and bolster collaborative familial environments away from treatment that can reinforce and better promote sustained reductions in substance use and sex trade involvement.

Although many women may lack family members who meet traditional criteria for supportive treatment involvement due to pervasive trauma and adverse experiences, excluding family altogether neglects the reality that women must reintegrate into social environments following treatment. Social belonging and support are central to long-term recovery (Jones et al., 2024; Owens & McGrady, 2014), and reliance on isolation as a protective strategy may heighten post-treatment vulnerability albeit not while under treatment purview. While risks related to familial trafficking, abuse, and coercion are significant, carefully designed family programming can identify and cultivate protective alliances and mitigate isolation within otherwise hazardous environments, ultimately supporting women’s sustained recovery. Clearly further research is needed, but findings provide initial insights into potential strategies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered within the evaluation of this study’s findings. First, all participants were recruited from one large sex trafficking specialty court program. It may be the case that regional or programmatic differences exist within the preferences and perspectives of program participants that could not be captured. Second, only women who were able to identify a supportive family member willing to participate with them were eligible. It may be the case that some women interested in family therapy did not express interest in the study due to a lack of supportive family members. It is worth noting that no prospective participants screened were deemed ineligible for any reason including not having a family member willing to participate with them.

Despite these limitations, the findings contribute to a very small but growing body of literature seeking to inform best practices regarding the viability, development and integration of family-based intervention to support the health and well-being among women impacted by the commercial sex trade industry, such as sex trafficking specialty court participants (Bounds et al., 2023; Marburger & Pickover, 2020; Murnan & Ferber, 2020; Murnan et al., 2017; Nichols et al., 2025). Specifically, this study provides insights into the development of strategies for identifying and integrating supportive family members into sex trafficking specialty court program participants’ treatment for substance use disorders. Given the exploratory nature of this study and lack of prior research in this area, it is critically important the application of the current findings be done with great caution and intention. However, we view our findings as an important step towards informing the potential development of family interventions for this population.

Conclusions

Current findings emphasize the presence of supportive family members within the lives of sex trafficking specialty court participants and insights towards their integration into treatment programming. While these families may be complicated and nuanced, due to shared traumatic or adverse experiences, environmental risk, and prior relational damage; current participants successfully identified supportive family members who were great sources of motivation and support towards their treatment. Given treatment goals among these individuals and prior success of family therapy interventions with this population (Murnan, 2021; Murnan & Holowacz, 2020), incorporating family members into interventions as part of sex trafficking specialty court programs represents a unique and promising strategy to bolster participants short- and long-term success. Findings from this study can inform practices within sex trafficking specialty court programs to collaborate with participants to identify and engage supportive family into their treatment through the development and testing of evidence-based family interventions.
