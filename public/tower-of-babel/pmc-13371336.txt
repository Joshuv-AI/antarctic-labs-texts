Interactive effects of vitamin D status and visit-to-visit systolic blood pressure variability on 12-month cognition and mild cognitive impairment in older adults with hypertension
Authors: Linya Zhao, Pengran Wang, Hongmei Zhao, Nuan Xiao, Lili Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVisit-to-visit systolic blood pressure variability (BPV) has been linked to cognitive decline, but inter-individual susceptibility may depend on modifiable factors such as vitamin D status. We investigated whether baseline serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] modifies the association between systolic BPV and 12-month cognition among older adults with hypertension.MethodsIn this single-center prospective cohort, participants were recruited from May 2023 to June 2024 and followed for 12 months, adults aged ≥ 65 years with hypertension underwent baseline 25(OH)D measurement and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) at baseline and Month 12. BPV was calculated from clinic SBP values during Months 0–10 (≥ 6 visits required). Average real variability of SBP (ARV-SBP) was the primary BPV metric; standard deviation (SD-SBP), coefficient of variation (CV-SBP), variability independent of mean (VIM-SBP), and range-SBP were examined in sensitivity analyses. Average real variability of SBP (ARV-SBP) was the primary BPV metric. The primary analysis used ANCOVA-style multivariable linear regression for Month 12 MoCA including ARV-SBP (per 1 SD), 25(OH)D (per 10 ng/mL, centered), and their interaction, adjusting for baseline MoCA and prespecified covariates. A screening-based, algorithm-defined MCI status at Month 12 was analyzed using modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors.ResultsAmong 176 participants (mean age 73.1 years), 73 (41.5%) had low vitamin D status [25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL] and 48 (27.3%) met algorithm-defined MCI status at Month 12. Higher ARV-SBP was associated with lower Month 12 MoCA (β=−0.22, 95% CI − 0.37 to − 0.07 β), and 25(OH)D significantly modified this association (interaction β = 0.18, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.32; p = 0.01 β), indicating attenuation of BPV-related cognitive disadvantage at higher vitamin D levels. The interaction remained directionally consistent across SD-SBP, CV-SBP, VIM-SBP, and range-SBP sensitivity models (all nominal p ≤ 0.04 ; FDR q ≤ 0.04 ). In joint-exposure analysis, low vitamin D status plus high BPV was associated with a higher probability of meeting algorithm-defined MCI status versus 25(OH)D ≥ 20 ng/mL with low BPV (adjusted RR = 4.14, 95% CI 1.93 to 8.90), while additive interaction was suggestive but imprecise (RERI = 1.94, 95% CI − 0.10 to 6.03).ConclusionsBaseline 25(OH)D was associated with heterogeneity in the observed association between clinic visit-to-visit systolic BPV and 12-month cognition in adults aged ≥ 65 years with hypertension. These findings support attention to BP stability and low vitamin D status as a potential marker of cognitive vulnerability in older hypertensive outpatients, while causal inference and supplementation effects require confirmation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04956-9.

Introduction

With population aging, late-life cognitive disorders, including mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia, represent a rapidly expanding global health burden, with dementia prevalence projected to increase substantially through 2100 due to increased absolute number of older adults [1, 2]. In parallel, hypertension remains highly prevalent worldwide, and treatment/control gaps persist across regions and age groups [3]. Among older adults, approximately 65% of those aged 60–79 years and about 80% of those aged ≥ 80 years are estimated to have hypertension [4]. These treatment and control gaps also vary geographically and by healthcare access, with lower control rates in under-resourced settings and among older adults with less consistent follow-up [3, 5, 6]. Hypertension is consistently recognized as a modifiable risk factor in contemporary dementia-prevention frameworks [7]. Prospective evidence further indicates that adverse blood pressure phenotypes across the life course, including elevated midlife blood pressure and certain late-life blood pressure patterns, are associated with higher risks of cognitive impairment and dementia; in meta-analysis, effect estimates generally fall in the approximately 1.19- to 1.55-fold range across relevant blood pressure phenotypes [8]. Accordingly, global clinical guidance emphasizes sustained blood pressure control and risk stratification in routine hypertension care [5, 6, 9].

Beyond mean blood pressure, visit-to-visit blood pressure variability (VVV-BPV) captures longer-term hemodynamic instability and has prognostic significance independent of average systolic blood pressure in large observational analyses [10]. Evidence summarized in major reviews indicates that increased short- and long-term BPV is linked to target-organ damage and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [11]. Mechanistically, repeated fluctuations in systemic pressure can challenge cerebral autoregulation and promote intermittent hypo- or hyperperfusion, creating a plausible pathway from BP instability to microvascular injury and neuronal stress [12]. Consistent with this neurovascular stress model, higher VVV-BPV has been associated with progression of white matter hyperintensities among older individuals with hypertension [13]. In older adult cohorts, greater visit-to-visit BPV has also been associated with worse cognitive performance independent of mean blood pressure, suggesting that BP stability may matter for brain function beyond average BP reduction alone [14]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis further reported that higher systolic BPV was associated with higher odds of dementia or cognitive impairment [15].

Vitamin D deficiency is common worldwide, with marked geographic and demographic variability, and remains a frequent issue in older adults who often have reduced sun exposure and multiple comorbidities [16]. Global reviews emphasize that older age is a key risk context for low 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentrations [17]. Although vitamin D is biologically linked to vascular homeostasis, meta-analytic evidence from randomized supplementation trials shows limited or heterogeneous effects on endothelial function and arterial stiffness-related measures [18]. As highlighted in a recent critical appraisal, many large vitamin D randomized trials have followed pharmaceutical-trial conventions despite nutrient-specific challenges such as relatively replete baseline 25(OH)D concentrations, modest dose contrasts, supplementation in control groups, and analyses that do not account for achieved 25(OH)D levels; these design features may contribute to null or heterogeneous findings [19]. Neuroscience-oriented reviews describe plausible vitamin D–related pathways involving neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neurodegenerative processes [20]. However, evidence from ancillary cognitive studies in older adults reports no overall cognitive benefit of vitamin D3 supplementation over short follow-up, which motivates hypothesis-driven observational work to test whether baseline vitamin D status identifies subgroups more vulnerable to hemodynamic stressors such as VVV-BPV in older hypertensive outpatients [21].

Accordingly, the present study aimed to determine whether baseline vitamin D status modifies the relationship between visit-to-visit systolic blood pressure variability (BPV) and short-term cognitive outcomes. Vitamin D may influence susceptibility to BPV-related neurovascular stress through effects on endothelial function, arterial stiffness, neurohormonal pathways, and neural resilience. Our primary hypothesis was that baseline serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] interacts with systolic BPV such that higher BPV would be associated with lower 12-month global cognition, measured by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), with a stronger adverse association among participants with lower 25(OH)D concentrations. As a secondary, exploratory aim, we examined whether the joint presence of low vitamin D status and higher BPV identifies a higher-risk phenotype for algorithm-defined MCI status at 12 months by estimating adjusted risk ratios across joint exposure groups; additive interaction (RERI) was treated as exploratory because it depends on dichotomizing continuous exposures and can be unstable in modest samples.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This single-center prospective observational cohort enrolled community-dwelling older adults with hypertension who attended outpatient care at Affiliated Hospital of Hebei University with recruitment conducted from May 2023 to June 2024 and 12-month follow-up for each participant. For the present analysis, participants were additionally required to have ≥ 6 eligible clinic BP visits during Months 0–10; in this outpatient setting, visits typically occurred at approximately monthly intervals for routine follow-up and treatment adjustment. Hypertension was defined as a documented clinician diagnosis and/or current antihypertensive treatment, or average office BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg confirmed on at least two separate visits using standardized measurement procedures, consistent with contemporary international guidance for office BP thresholds [5, 9]. All participants were aged ≥ 65 years and completed baseline clinical assessment and cognitive testing, with planned reassessment at 12 months. Participants were not enrolled if they had known dementia, acute delirium, or other conditions that would preclude valid cognitive testing at baseline.

The protocol conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of Affiliated Hospital of Hebei University [Approval No.: HDFYLLKY2024013]. All participants provided written informed consent prior to any study procedures.

Vitamin D measurement and definitions

At baseline, venous blood was collected and serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] was measured in the hospital laboratory using a chemiluminescent immunoassay on the Roche Elecsys Vitamin D total on cobas e601/e801 (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 5% and 8%, respectively, with routine internal quality-control procedures and calibration. Because 25(OH)D exhibits seasonal variation, with mean peak-to-trough differences of approximately 9.6 ng/mL reported in community-dwelling older adults [22], season of blood draw was recorded (spring, summer, autumn, winter) and included as a covariate in regression models.

Vitamin D exposure was specified a priori in two complementary ways: as a continuous measure modeled per 10 ng/mL (mean-centered for interaction modeling) and as a categorical measure defining low vitamin D status as 25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL and higher vitamin D status as ≥ 20 ng/mL. We use “low vitamin D status” to acknowledge that cut-points differ across frameworks and that some references define deficiency at lower thresholds [23].

Blood pressure measurement and visit-to-visit BP variability

Clinic blood pressure was measured at routine outpatient visits using a standardized protocol with a validated automated oscillometric device (Omron HBP-1300, Kyoto, Japan), calibrated/verified according to hospital biomedical engineering procedures. After ≥ 5 min of seated rest, 2–3 measurements were obtained at 1-minute intervals with an appropriately sized cuff and the arm supported at heart level. Visit-level SBP and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was defined as the mean of last two readings. Visit-to-visit BPV was computed from all eligible SBP values recorded from baseline through Month 10, excluding Month 12 measurements to avoid contamination by concurrent cognitive assessment. The BPV observation window was defined as the time (days) between the first and last eligible BP visits during Months 0–10, and mean SBP and mean DBP were calculated as the arithmetic mean across included visits.

Visit-to-visit BP variability estimated from routine clinic measurements is a pragmatic exposure that can reflect biological hemodynamic variability, but also treatment adjustments, adherence patterns, and visit timing. The relative contribution of medication initiation/intensification, missed doses, visit timing, and other unmeasured factors could not be partitioned with the available data; accordingly, clinic BPV should be interpreted as a composite marker of longer-term BP stability in outpatient care rather than a pure physiological signal, and any nondifferential nonphysiological component would most likely attenuate associations [11].

The prespecified primary BPV metric was the average real variability of SBP (ARV-SBP) [24], calculated as ARV = (1/(n − 1))·Σ|SBP(i+1) − SBP(i)| across consecutively ordered visits. ARV-SBP was standardized (z-score) for regression modeling so that effect estimates corresponded to a 1-SD difference in ARV-SBP. Four additional SBP variability metrics were evaluated in sensitivity analyses to assess robustness to BPV operationalization and comparability with prior literature: SD-SBP (standard deviation of SBP across visits), CV-SBP (100 × SD/mean SBP), VIM-SBP, and range-SBP (maximum SBP – minimum SBP). Cognitive assessment and definition of mild cognitive impairment

Global cognition was assessed at baseline and Month 12 using the Chinese version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [25], administered by trained assessors under standardized conditions. Total MoCA score (0–30, higher scores indicating better cognition) was used as the primary cognitive measure. Education correction for MoCA scoring was applied according to the test manual/validated local procedure. Chinese versions of MoCA have been validated for screening in older Chinese adults [26]. The prespecified primary outcome was Month 12 MoCA analyzed with baseline MoCA included as a covariate to improve precision and reduce regression-to-the-mean.

Algorithm-defined MCI status at Month 12 (screening-based)

Because a full neuropsychological battery and multidisciplinary adjudication were not performed, “MCI” in this study represents an operational screening-based status at Month 12. Month 12 MCI status required: (1) objective cognitive impairment on MoCA; (2) no known dementia at baseline and no evidence of acute delirium at Month 12 (clinical evaluation and chart review); and (3) preserved functional independence by interview and chart review (no dementia-level dependence). The functional independence interview guide was developed for this study (English-version is provided as Supplementary File S1). Month 12 MCI analyses should be interpreted as follow-up status unless baseline MCI is explicitly classified and excluded in incident analyses [27].

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance to vascular risk and cognition and were measured at baseline unless otherwise specified. Demographic covariates included age, sex, and years of education. Diabetes mellitus was defined by documented diagnosis and/or glucose-lowering medication use. Renal function was summarized by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73m2) and modeled per 10 mL/min/1.73m2 in regression analyses. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) with screen-positive depressive symptoms defined as GDS-15 ≥ 5 [28]. Antihypertensive medication use at baseline was recorded by class and summarized descriptively as the number of antihypertensive classes, with class-specific baseline use additionally tabulated for angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, and β-blockers (BBs), and mean SBP (per 10 mmHg) was included as a covariate in regression models to separate BP level from BP variability. Oral vitamin D supplementation status was not systematically captured in the available sources records and therefore could not be included as an adjusted covariate. Season of blood draw was modeled with spring as the reference category.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when approximately normally distributed and median [interquartile range, IQR] when skewed, and categorical variables are summarized as n (%). Baseline characteristics and BP/BPV measures were compared between low and higher vitamin D groups using independent-samples t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. These comparisons are descriptive and were not used for covariate selection. The primary inferential focus was the prespecified ARV-SBP × 25(OH)D interaction in the Month 12 MoCA model; other analyses were treated as sensitivity or exploratory analyses.

The primary analysis tested effect modification by vitamin D using an ANCOVA-style multivariable linear regression model with Month 12 MoCA as the dependent variable and baseline MoCA, ARV-SBP (per 1 SD), baseline 25(OH)D (per 10 ng/mL, mean-centered), and their interaction (ARV-SBP × 25(OH)D) as key predictors, adjusting for age, sex, education, diabetes, eGFR (per 10 units), depressive symptoms (GDS-15 ≥ 5), mean SBP (per 10 mmHg), and season of blood draw. Nominal two-sided p values and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported for coefficients, and the prespecified primary inference is the ARV-SBP × 25(OH)D interaction. Because baseline 25(OH)D was measured before the Months 0–10 BPV accumulation window and was hypothesized to modify vulnerability to BP instability, mediation analysis was not prespecified.

Assessment of functional form (linearity/nonlinearity)

The primary model specified ARV-SBP and 25(OH)D as linear terms (with mean-centering for interaction interpretability) to prioritize transparency and clinical interpretability. We evaluated model assumptions using residual diagnostics and inspected partial residual plots for key continuous predictors.

Adjusted predicted Month 12 MoCA values were generated across the observed ARV-SBP range at prespecified vitamin D concentrations (12, 20, and 28 ng/mL), representing a deficient level, the study cut-point, and an approximately typical level in this cohort, to visualize effect modification. Predictions set continuous covariates to cohort means and categorical covariates to their reference categories. Robustness to BPV metric choice was evaluated by refitting the same adjusted model with SD-SBP, CV-SBP, VIM-SBP, and range-SBP (each standardized per 1 SD) and summarizing interaction estimates, nominal p values, and Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) q values across the five BPV metrics.

For the secondary binary outcome, risk ratios (RRs) for algorithm-defined MCI status at Month 12 were estimated using modified Poisson regression with robust (sandwich) standard errors. Joint exposure categories were defined by low vitamin D status (25(OH)D < 20 vs. ≥ 20 ng/mL) and high vs. low BPV (high ARV-SBP defined as ≥ the cohort median), with “25(OH)D ≥ 20 & low BPV” as the reference group, and adjusting for age, sex, education, baseline MoCA, and mean SBP. Additive interaction was quantified using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI = RR11 − RR10 − RR01 + 1) [29]. Bootstrap resampling (2,000 replicates) was used to obtain a 95% CI and p value for RERI. All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05, and analyses were performed using R (version 4.4).

Results

Participant flow and analytic cohort

Among 199 enrolled older adults with hypertension, 184 completed the Month 12 cognitive assessment, and 176 participants met the prespecified BPV eligibility criterion of ≥ 6 eligible clinic BP visits during Months 0–10 and comprised the analytic cohort (Fig. 1). In the analytic cohort, low vitamin D status (baseline 25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL) was present in 73 participants (41.5%; Table 1). The median number of BP visits used for BPV estimation was 9 (IQR 7–10), and the BPV observation window averaged 281 ± 29 days (Table 2). By Month 12, 48 of 176 participants (27.3%) met algorithm-defined MCI status.

Baseline characteristics by vitamin D status

Baseline characteristics were broadly similar between participants with low vitamin D status and those with 25(OH)D ≥ 20 ng/mL, with no statistically significant differences in age, sex distribution, education, BMI, diabetes prevalence, eGFR, depressive symptoms, baseline MoCA, or baseline antihypertensive class count (Table 1). Baseline use of ARBs, CCBs, diuretics, ACE inhibitors, and BBs was also similar between vitamin D groups (all p ≥ 0.36; Table 1). As expected, baseline 25(OH)D concentrations were substantially lower in the low vitamin D group (15.8 ± 2.7 ng/mL) than in the ≥ 20 ng/mL group (31.6 ± 9.9 ng/mL; p < 0.001), while the distribution of season of blood draw did not differ between groups (Table 1).

BP visit structure and BP variability metrics

Across the cohort, mean SBP and DBP during Months 0–10 were 141.4 ± 11.5 mmHg and 78.0 ± 7.2 mmHg, respectively, and systolic BP variability was moderate (ARV-SBP 11.7 ± 3.7 mmHg, SD-SBP 13.8 ± 4.2 mmHg, CV-SBP 9.8 ± 3.0%, VIM-SBP 13.6 ± 3.7 units, and range-SBP 32.6 ± 11.4 mmHg; Table 2). Compared with participants with 25(OH)D ≥ 20 ng/mL, those with low vitamin D status had higher ARV-SBP (12.4 ± 3.4 vs. 11.2 ± 3.7 mmHg; p = 0.03) and higher VIM-SBP (14.2 ± 3.5 vs. 13.1 ± 3.8 units; p = 0.048), with a similar direction for SD-SBP (14.5 ± 3.9 vs. 13.3 ± 4.4 mmHg; p = 0.06), CV-SBP (10.4 ± 2.9% vs. 9.4 ± 3.1%; p = 0.03), and range-SBP (34.8 ± 10.8 vs. 31.0 ± 11.3 mmHg; p = 0.04). The number of BP visits and the BPV observation window duration were similar by vitamin D status (Table 2).

Primary outcome: vitamin D modifies the association between systolic BPV and Month 12 cognition

In the prespecified multivariable ANCOVA-style regression model, higher ARV-SBP (per 1 SD) was associated with lower Month 12 MoCA after adjustment for baseline MoCA, demographics, diabetes, eGFR, depressive symptoms, mean SBP, and season (β = −0.22, 95% CI − 0.37 to − 0.07; p = 0.005; β Table 3). Baseline 25(OH)D (per 10 ng/mL, centered) was positively associated with Month 12 MoCA (β = 0.21, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.36; p = 0.004; β Table 3), and the ARV-SBP × 25(OH)D interaction term was statistically significant (β = 0.18 for the interaction between ARV-SBP [per 1 SD] and 25(OH)D [per 10 ng/ml, centered], 95% CI 0.05 to 0.32; p = 0.01 Table 3), indicating attenuation of the adverse ARV-SBP association at higher vitamin D levels. Adjusted predictions showed a steeper decline in Month 12 MoCA with increasing ARV-SBP at lower vitamin D concentrations (Fig. 2). Beyond the primary exposures, baseline MoCA, age, education, eGFR, depressive symptoms, and season were associated with Month 12 MoCA in adjusted models (Table 3).

Robustness of effect modification across BPV metrics

The vitamin D × BPV interaction was directionally consistent when SD-SBP, CV-SBP, VIM-SBP, and range-SBP were used instead of ARV-SBP in otherwise identical adjusted regression models (Fig. 3). Interaction coefficients were positive for SD-SBP (β = 0.17, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.31; p = 0.01; FDR q = 0.03 β), CV-SBP (β = 0.16, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.30; p = 0.02; FDR q = 0.03 β), and for VIM-SBP (β = 0.16, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.30; β p = 0.02; FDR q = 0.03), and range-SBP (β = 0.15, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.30; p = 0.04; FDR q = 0.04 β), consistent with attenuation of the negative BPV–MoCA association at higher 25(OH)D levels.

Secondary outcome: algorithm-defined MCI status at Month 12 and joint exposure patterns

Algorithm-defined MCI status at Month 12 occurred most frequently among participants with joint exposure to low vitamin D status and high BPV (25/39, 64%) compared with 25(OH)D ≥ 20 ng/mL and low BPV (6/54, 11%), 25(OH)D ≥ 20 ng/mL and high BPV (7/49, 14%), and low vitamin D status with low BPV (10/34, 29%; Table 4). In modified Poisson regression adjusted for age, sex, education, baseline MoCA, and mean SBP, the low vitamin D status/high BPV group had a higher risk of MCI compared with the reference group (adjusted RR 4.14, 95% CI 1.93 to 8.90; p < 0.001; Table 4), whereas the low vitamin D/low BPV group showed an elevated but imprecise association (adjusted RR 2.06, 95% CI 0.87 to 4.90; p = 0.10; Table 4) and the 25(OH)D ≥ 20/high BPV group did not differ from the reference (adjusted RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.45 to 2.95; p = 0.78; Table 4). Additive interaction analysis suggested excess risk beyond additivity (RERI 1.9), but the bootstrap 95% CI included 0 (− 0.1 to 6.0) with borderline evidence (bootstrap p = 0.06), indicating that additive interaction could not be confirmed definitively in this sample (Table 4).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort of older adults with hypertension, our principal finding was that baseline vitamin D status modified the association between visit-to-visit systolic BPV and 12-month cognitive performance, such that higher BPV was associated with lower Month 12 MoCA particularly at lower baseline 25(OH)D levels. In the fully adjusted ANCOVA-style model, ARV-SBP was inversely associated with Month 12 MoCA, and the statistically significant ARV-SBP×25(OH)D interaction indicated attenuation of BPV-related cognitive disadvantage with higher vitamin D. This pattern was visually consistent in adjusted predictions, where the estimated BPV–MoCA slope was steepest at 25(OH)D = 12 ng/mL and progressively weaker at 20 and 28 ng/mL, supporting clinically interpretable effect modification. Importantly, the direction and magnitude of the interaction were robust across alternative BPV operationalizations, with concordant positive interaction estimates when BPV was defined using SD-SBP, CV-SBP, VIM-SBP, and range-SBP, strengthening confidence that the observed modification was not driven by a single BPV metric choice. Although the primary ARV-SBP coefficient was numerically modest at the individual level, the interaction implies larger separation at lower 25(OH)D concentrations; the expected difference across a 2-SD ARV-SBP contrast was approximately 0.9 MoCA points at 25(OH)D = 12 ng/mL versus approximately 0.3 points at 28 ng/mL. We therefore interpret the effect size as modest for an individual but potentially meaningful as a population-level vulnerability signal in clinically at-risk older adults.

Vitamin D may plausibly modify the impact of visit-to-visit BPV on cognition through vascular “reserve” mechanisms in hypertension. In our cohort, higher systolic BPV was associated with lower Month 12 MoCA, and this adverse association was significantly attenuated at higher baseline 25(OH)D, suggesting that low vitamin D may mark increased vulnerability to BP instability. A biologically coherent pathway is supported by human data linking lower vitamin D status with impaired endothelial function and greater arterial stiffness, factors that can amplify hemodynamic lability and reduce buffering of pressure fluctuations [30]. Meta-analyses of randomized trials report mixed and generally modest effects of vitamin D supplementation on endothelial function and arterial stiffness, with substantial heterogeneity across trials and the possibility that any benefit may be more apparent in vitamin D-deficient subgroups [18, 31, 32]. In parallel, hypertension-related cerebrovascular injury frameworks emphasize that hypertension disrupts cerebral vessel structure/function and may promote vascular cognitive impairment and MCI [33]. Because BP variability has been associated with markers of cerebral small vessel disease in population-based meta-analyses and has also been linked to Alzheimer’s disease biomarker profiles in older adults, low vitamin D could plausibly lower cerebrovascular resilience and magnify the cognitive impact of BP instability [34, 35].

Our findings also fit within, and extend, the existing literature on BPV and cognitive outcomes in older adults with vascular risk. Longitudinal meta-analytic evidence indicates that higher long-term BPV is associated with increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia, supporting BPV as a clinically meaningful exposure beyond mean blood pressure [15, 36]. Large observational cohorts likewise report associations between visit-to-visit BPV and poorer cognitive trajectories, including results from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis [37], while a pooled analysis of three national prospective cohorts specifically among patients with hypertension reported faster cognitive decline with higher long-term BPV even among treated individuals [38]. Age-dependent work suggests BPV may be particularly informative in late life for dementia risk, which is consistent with our older cohort and the relatively high Month 12 MCI proportion [39]. The baseline mean MoCA below 25 likely reflects a clinically vulnerable outpatient hypertension cohort with modest educational attainment rather than established dementia, and it underscores that our sample was enriched for individuals at elevated short-term cognitive risk.

Prospective observational work has linked low vitamin D status to higher risk of incident dementia and Alzheimer’s disease [40]. Evidence from randomized trials has been mixed, and analyses from the D-Health trial did not show cognitive benefit from monthly vitamin D supplementation in largely vitamin D–replete older adults [41]. One potential explanation is that vitamin D status may function less as a universal cognitive enhancer and more as a modifier or marker of susceptibility to vascular instability. In our joint-exposure analysis, participants with low vitamin D status and high BPV had a higher probability of meeting algorithm-defined MCI status than those with 25(OH)D ≥ 20 ng/mL and low BPV, while additive interaction was suggestive but imprecise. We did not perform mediation analysis because our hypothesis and temporal ordering were centered on effect modification: baseline 25(OH)D preceded BPV ascertainment and was not modeled as an intermediate generated by BPV. In the absence of repeated vitamin D measures or a specified causal pathway from BPV to subsequent 25(OH)D to cognition, an interaction framework was more appropriate than mediation for the present data structure.

This study also has several strengths. These include the prospective design, repeated clinic BP measurements with a prespecified Months 0–10 exposure window preceding Month 12 cognitive assessment, adjustment for baseline cognition and mean SBP, and concordant findings across multiple BPV indices. This study has several limitations warrant cautious interpretation. The single-center design may limit external validity, and residual confounding remains possible because vitamin D status is correlated with unmeasured factors (e.g., frailty, outdoor activity, physical activity or sedentary behavior, sun exposure, supplement use, and diet) that also relate to cognitive outcomes. BPV was estimated from routine outpatient measurements and may reflect measurement noise, visit timing, and treatment adjustments in addition to physiological variability; the ≥ 6-visit requirement strengthens BPV estimation but may select for individuals with more frequent healthcare utilization, and the absence of time-varying medication adherence or titration data means that the nonphysiological contribution to clinic BPV cannot be quantified directly. Second, the binary MCI endpoint was operationalized as a screening-based, algorithm-defined status at Month 12 rather than a fully adjudicated clinical diagnosis, and we did not conduct a separate incident-MCI analysis excluding baseline MCI. Oral vitamin D supplementation was not systematically recorded, precluding direct assessment of whether supplementation attenuated the observed interaction. Finally, the number of MCI events limited precision for additive interaction estimates, as reflected by wide confidence intervals. Future work should include multicenter validation, longer follow-up, richer characterization of treatment changes and adherence, and mechanistic measures (e.g., neuroimaging markers of small vessel disease).

In conclusion, in this prospective observational cohort of adults aged ≥ 65 years with hypertension, baseline 25(OH)D was associated with heterogeneity in the observed association between clinic visit-to-visit systolic BPV and 12-month global cognition. The combination of low vitamin D status and elevated BPV was associated with a higher probability of meeting algorithm-defined MCI status at Month 12. Clinically, these data support closer attention to BP stability and recognition of low vitamin D status as a potential marker of susceptibility when monitoring cognition in older hypertensive outpatients, while not implying a proven benefit of vitamin D supplementation itself. Larger multicenter studies with longer follow-up and more detailed exposure characterization are needed to confirm these findings and clarify mechanisms.
