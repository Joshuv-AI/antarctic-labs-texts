Efficiently Computed Marginal Likelihoods for Multivariate Mixture Models
Martin Metodiev, Marie Perrot-Dockès, Pierre Latouche, Adrian E. Raftery

Abstract
We propose a new methodology for efficiently computing marginal likelihoods for univariate and multivariate mixture models. The estimator that this methodology proposes computes the marginal likelihood from Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) samples, is consistent, asymptotically normal and of finite variance. In addition, it is invariant to label switching, does not require posterior samples from hidden allocation vectors, and is efficiently approximated, even for an arbitrarily high number of components. Its computational efficiency is based on an asymptotically optimal ordering of the parameter space, which can in turn be used to provide useful visualisations. We test it in simulation settings where the true marginal likelihood is available analytically. It performs well against state-of-the-art competitors, even in multivariate settings with a high number of components. We demonstrate its utility for inference and model selection on univariate and multivariate data sets.

1
Introduction

Choosing the right number of components for a mixture model is a topic of much discussion
[see
24
,
12
, for reviews]
. The marginal likelihood, also known as the evidence or the normalising constant, is a Bayesian model choice criterion that can be used for this task and that is optimal from a Bayesian point of view. More precisely, if
G
denotes the number of mixture components,
Z(G)
the marginal likelihood, and
\Pi(G)
the prior on
G
, choosing
G
such that it maximises
Z(G)\Pi(G)
minimises the model selection error rate on average over
\Pi

[
38
]
.

Unfortunately, the marginal likelihood often does not possess an analytic expression. A variety of algorithms have been proposed for its computation, none of which is clearly seen as a gold standard
[see
45
, for a review]
. In addition, there is an almost complete lack in the literature of marginal likelihood estimation techniques for mixture models that are multivariate and contain a high number of components. The goal of this article is to present a marginal likelihood estimator that can be used in this setting and that satisfies the following three desiderata: it is precise, generic and efficiently approximated as the number of components increases.

Marginal likelihood estimation

Available approaches for marginal likelihood estimation do not satisfy the three desiderata described before. These include bridge sampling
[
47
]
(when using a generic, non-symmetric proposal such as the bridge sampling implementation of
Gronau et al. [32]
) and Chib’s estimator
[
14
]
, which have been observed to be imprecise when used to estimate the marginal likelihood of mixture models
[
54
,
12
]
.

Many precise estimators are not generic: they either require sampling schemes different from a simple MCMC sample from the posterior
[
67
,
21
, e.g., the proposals of]
, specific Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) samplers that can obtain samples from hidden allocation vectors
[
56
,
63
,
59
,
35
, e.g., the proposals of]
or randomly permuted MCMC samples
[
23
,
25
, e.g., the proposals of]
. A truly generic estimator would not have to depend on these restrictions. Indeed, there are many popular samplers that do not provide samples from hidden allocation vectors or randomly permuted parameters, such as the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm implemented in Stan
[
68
]
, the classical Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
[
50
,
36
]
and the tempered MCMC algorithm
[
53
]
.

Precise and generic estimators have been proposed in
Berkhof et al. [9]
,
Lee and Robert [42]
,
Celeux et al. [12]
. Unfortunately, these estimators quickly become intractable as the number of mixture components increases. This may be because their computational complexity is related to the total number of permutations that can be applied to the labels of the parameters. Indeed, this number increases superexponentially, leading to extremely large values, even for standard datasets. For example, in
Bouveyron et al. [10]
a dataset is analysed for which the true number of components is estimated to be
G=15
. The number of permutations is equal to
G!=15!
in this case, which is over one trillion. Dealing with this superexponential increase may well be the central bottleneck to these marginal likelihood estimation procedures.

Our proposal

Building on the ideas of
Berkhof et al. [9]
and
Reichl [60]
, we propose a new methodology for efficiently computing marginal likelihoods in univariate and multivariate mixture models with a high number of components, while still adhering to the three desiderata of precision, generality and computational efficiency. The estimator that this methodology proposes is

•

precise, since it is consistent, asymptotically normal, and optimal in a certain sense, even for the multimodal posterior densities that typically appear in mixture models,

•

generic, since it is compatible with any MCMC algorithm that provides a sample that converges in distribution to the posterior distribution of the parameters,

•

efficiently approximated via a novel graph-theoretic algorithm, even when the number of mixture components is large.

Outline of the paper

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we describe previous work on which our estimator, the truncated harmonic mean estimator (THAMES) for mixture models, is built. Then, we introduce the THAMES for mixture models in Section
3.1
and explain how it can be computed efficiently in Section
3.2
, using the principle of overlap graphs to obtain an ordering constraint. In addition, we propose a model choice criterion, the criterion of overlap (CO), and show how to implement an identity from
Nobile [55]
,
Nobile [56]
in Section
3.3
to better deal with the phenomenon of empty components. We compare the THAMES to state-of-the-art estimators in simulation studies, where the true marginal likelihood is known, in Section
4.1
, and on real datasets, in Section
4.2
. We conclude with a discussion in Section
5
.

2
Previous work relating to the THAMES

Let
\theta
denote the model parameter of dimension
R
and
Y
the data. The Bayesian framework is considered, where
\pi(\theta)
is the prior probability density function (PDF) and
L(\theta)=p(Y|\theta)
is the likelihood.

Let
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
be a MCMC sample that converges in distribution to the posterior distribution of the parameters. The methodology that we propose builds on a class of estimators that are evaluated on a truncated harmonic mean of a function of the MCMC sample. This class of estimators has the following form, where
V(A)
denotes the volume of a set
A
:

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}=\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
\theta^{(t)}\in A\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(A)}{\pi(\theta^{(t)})L(\theta^{(t)})}.

(1)

Under mild assumptions on
A
,
\hat{Z}^{-1}
converges to the reciprocal of the marginal likelihood
[
49
]
. However, the choice of
A
is crucial, since it can heavily influence the variance of
\hat{Z}^{-1}
, and thus the speed of its convergence.
Metodiev et al. [49]
suggested setting

\displaystyle A=E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}=\{\theta|(\theta-\hat{\theta})^{\intercal}\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\theta-\hat{\theta})<c^{2}\},

(2)

an ellipsoid centred at an estimate of the posterior mean
\hat{\theta}
and scaled by an estimate of the posterior covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}
. Notice that the posterior sample is split:
\hat{\theta}
and
\hat{\Sigma}
are computed using the first half (or portion, more generally), while the marginal likelihood estimator is computed from the second half. This choice of
A
was justified by the assumption of asymptotic normality of the posterior distribution, which is ensured in many standard settings
[
37
,
29
,
65
,
51
, see, e.g.,]
. However, in the context of mixture models, the posterior is highly multimodal, even as the size of the dataset increases, so asymptotic posterior normality does not hold. Another choice of
A
is needed.

The following theorem shows that, under mild assumptions, an optimal choice of
A
is given by an
\alpha
-highest-posterior-density (HPD) region. The proofs of this and any other theorem presented in this article are given in the supplementary material.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that
\pi(\theta)L(\theta)
is continuous on its support and that
\pi(\theta)L(\theta)
is non-constant, in the sense that the volume of each level set
V(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)=q\})=0
for all
q>0
. Let

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}=\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>q_{\alpha}\},

(3)

where
q_{\alpha}
is the constant for which the posterior probability of
\{\theta\in H_{\alpha}\}
is equal to
\alpha
. If the draws
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}\sim p(\theta|Y)
are independent, there exists a constant
\alpha\in(0,1]
such that
A=H_{\alpha}
minimises the variance of
\hat{Z}^{-1}
over all measurable sets
A
, conditional on
Y
.

Remark 1
.

The assumption that
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
is an i.i.d sample from the posterior distribution is rarely satisfied exactly, but should be satisfied approximately, for example if a sufficiently large burn-in period is used and the sample is sufficiently heavily thinned.

Unfortunately, the volume of
H_{\alpha}
is needed for the computation of
\hat{Z}^{-1}
. We know of no simple way to compute this volume in general.
Robert et al. [62]
suggested using a convex hull which contains
H_{\alpha}
, but approximating the volume of a convex hull is difficult when the dimension of the parameter space is large. This is avoided by the proposal of
Reichl [60]
, who suggested to instead use a set that is sufficiently close to
H_{\alpha}
, but whose volume is easy to compute, by taking the intersection of the ellipsoid
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
with
H_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}

\displaystyle=E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}\cap\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>\hat{q}_{\alpha}\}

\displaystyle=\{\theta|(\theta-\hat{\theta})^{\intercal}\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\theta-\hat{\theta})<c^{2},\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>\hat{q}_{\alpha}\},

where
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
is the empirical
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the sample of unnormalised posterior values.
This proposal requires additional Monte Carlo simulations to compute the volume of
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
. This is done by using a technique that was successfully employed by
Sims et al. [66]
in reciprocal importance sampling: an i.i.d sample
\theta^{(1)}_{\text{Unif}},\dots,\theta^{(N)}_{\text{Unif}}
from the uniform distribution on
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
is simulated to calculate

\displaystyle\hat{V}(B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha})=V(E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c})\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\mathds{1}_{\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>\hat{q}_{\alpha}\}}(\theta^{(j)}_{\text{Unif}}).

(4)

Reichl [60]
implemented this estimator and obtained good results in a variety of situations. In particular, in the context of mixture models, it performed well, but only after adding additional MCMC simulations from random permutation sampling via a technique from
Frühwirth-Schnatter [22]
. This runs against our desideratum of generality.

3
Methods

3.1
Truncated harmonic mean estimation for mixture models

The standard mixture model setting is considered, where the data
Y\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times d}(\mathbbm{R})
consist of an i.i.d sample of
n
different
d
-dimensional observations,
Y=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
, with a distribution that is a mixture of distributions from the same family,

\displaystyle Y_{i}|\xi,\tau\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}\sum^{G}_{g=1}\tau_{g}f(\cdot|\xi_{g}),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(5)

where
G>1,\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G})
is a matrix containing the mixture component parameters,
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{G}>0,\sum^{G}_{g=1}\tau_{g}=1
are the mixture proportions represented by the vector
\tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{G})
, and
f(\cdot|\xi_{g})
is a density characterised by
\xi_{g}
. We are interested in estimating the marginal likelihood
Z(G)
for different values of the number of mixture components
G
. The full parameter is denoted by
\theta=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G},\tau_{1},\dots\tau_{G-1})
.

If the prior distribution of
(\tau_{g},\xi_{g})
does not depend on the index
g
, which is commonly the case and an assumption that we make from now on, the posterior distribution is symmetric. This results in the well-known label-switching problem, where some parts of the MCMC sample randomly switch their labels. Luckily, it is possible to simulate a sample whose labels are not switched by relabelling, e.g., with Stephens’ algorithm
[
70
]
or the ECR algorithm
[
58
]
. In the former, the sample is permuted such that it minimises the posterior expected loss under a class of loss functions, while the latter is based on permuting the sample such that it follows the law of a non-symmetric distribution. The two algorithms give similar results in our experience.

Let
\theta^{(1)\star},\dots,\theta^{(T)\star}
denote the posterior sample after relabelling. To ensure that the marginal likelihood estimator computed on this sample is still consistent, a procedure suggested in
Berkhof et al. [9]
is used. This procedure averages over the permutations
P_{1},\dots,P_{G!}
of the the posterior sample, which results in

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\textup{S}}(G)=\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
P_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})\in A\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(A)}{\pi(\theta^{(t)\star})L(\theta^{(t)\star})},

(6)

This estimator is symmetric, in the sense that its value does not change when the values of the parameters change. This makes it invariant to the problem of label switching. This invariance ensures consistency and asymptotic normality of the estimator under standard conditions on the MCMC sample, due to the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
is ergodic and converges in distribution, as
T\to\infty
, to the symmetric posterior
p(\theta|Y)
of a mixture model. Let
\theta^{(1)\star},\dots,\theta^{(T)\star}
denote a relabelling of
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
. For any fixed choice of
A
for which
\pi\cdot L
is bounded away from 0, in the sense that a
q>0
exists such that
\pi(\theta)\cdot L(\theta)\geq q
for all
\theta\in A
, any symmetric estimator defined by Equation (
6
) almost surely converges to the reciprocal marginal likelihood
Z^{-1}(G)
as
T\to\infty
. If
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
is also ergodic of degree 2, then
\sqrt{T}(\hat{Z}^{-1}(G)-Z^{-1}(G))
converges, as
T\to\infty
, in distribution to the normal distribution with mean equal to 0 and variance depending on the choice of
A
.

Remark 2
.

The previous theorem presumed that the MCMC sample was an i.i.d sample from the posterior distribution, an assumption that is typically not true exactly. This theorem does
not
make such an assumption. In particular, it ensures that the marginal likelihood estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal even when it is computed on a relabelled sample, that is not i.i.d. The only assumptions in this theorem related to the MCMC sample are standard assumptions such as ergodicity.

Our proposal

We suggest combining the proposal of
Reichl [60]
with the symmetrisation procedure used by
Berkhof et al. [9]
to obtain the THAMES for mixture models,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\text{THAMES}}(G)=\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
P_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})\in B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/\hat{V}(B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha})}{\pi(\theta^{(t)\star})L(\theta^{(t)\star})}.

(7)

We compute the volume of
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
by setting
N=T
in Equation (
4
). Note that a naive computation of the full sum over the
G!
permutations is not necessary, since most permutations will evaluate to 0. We elaborate on this in the next section.

The THAMES for mixture models (hereafter just THAMES) is symmetric, consistent and asymptotically normal as the size
T
of the simulations increases, under standard assumptions on the MCMC sample.
1
1

1

This follows from Theorem
3.1
: setting
A=B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
implies that
\pi\cdot L
is bounded away from 0 by definition, so the conditions of this theorem are fulfilled, conditional on the first half of the posterior sample that is used to define
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
. In addition, the volume is estimated by a Monte Carlo sample, which means that the THAMES is equal to the estimator given in this theorem with
A=B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
, distorted by the multiplicative ratio
\frac{V(B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha})}{\hat{V}(B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha})}
. However, since the multiplicative ratio almost surely converges to one, consistency and asymptotic normality are still preserved due to Slutsky’s theorem.
Only the relabelled posterior sample is required for the computation of the THAMES, with the first half being used to estimate the posterior mean and covariance matrix.

The THAMES possesses two tuning-parameters,
c\in(0,\infty)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and we can use asymptotic results to choose them in an optimal way. In fact, the following theorem implies that a value of
c
that is asymptotically equivalent to
\sqrt{R+1}
leads to a version of the THAMES that minimises a lower bound on the variance over all mixtures of truncated harmonic mean estimators, under some assumptions. The theorem shows optimality for a version of the THAMES that uses an ellipsoid as a truncation set. However, in practice we use an intersection between an ellipsoid and an
\alpha
-HPD region to ensure robustness of the THAMES. The value of
\alpha
is chosen such that the truncation set of the THAMES excludes posterior tail behaviour that is very different from the behaviour of a multivariate Gaussian, which is the distribution that is used in the following theorem. This is done by minimising the Kolmogorov distance with respect to this distribution. For the exact procedure that we use to choose
\alpha
, see Supplement B.

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider a convex combination of truncated harmonic mean estimators,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\text{Mix}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
\theta^{(t)}\in A_{k}\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(A_{k})}{\pi(\theta^{(t)})L(\theta^{(t)})},

(8)

where
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{K}
are weights that sum to 1 and
A_{1},\dots,A_{K}
are truncation sets within the posterior support. Suppose that the posterior density
p(\theta|Y)
is a Gaussian mixture,
p(\theta|Y)=\sum^{K}_{k=1}a_{k}\textup{MVN}_{R}(\theta;m_{k},S_{k}),
where
\text{MVN}_{R}(\theta;m_{k},S_{k})
denotes the Gaussian law of dimension
R
with mean
m_{k}
and covariance matrix
S_{k}
. If the draws
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
from the posterior distribution are independent, an upper bound on the variance of
\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\text{Mix}}
, conditional on
Y
, is minimised by
A_{k}=E_{m_{k},S_{k},c_{R}},\omega_{k}=a_{k},
where
c_{R}
is asymptotically equivalent to
\sqrt{R+1}
, i.e.,
\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{c_{R}}{\sqrt{R+1}}=1
.

Remark 3
.

When choosing an ellipsoid as a truncation set, the weights in Equation (
8
) are equal to
\omega_{k}=1/G!
so long as the posterior density of the relabelled sample is unimodal, since this leads to
G!
modes in the posterior due to label switching. This isn’t necessarily the case, since the phenomenon of genuine multimodality, that is, multimodality in the posterior density of the relabelled sample, can be observed
[
34
]
. In such cases, where the number of genuine modes is larger than 1, it may be useful to obtain a more optimal version of the THAMES by averaging different versions of the THAMES, each centred in a different genuine mode. These different genuine modes can for example be determined by applying the R package mclust
[
64
]
to the relabelled posterior sample. One could also use the approach of
Naderi et al. [52]
to determine the different ellipsoids over which one should average the different versions of the THAMES. However, the assumption of only
G!
symmetric modes may very well be justified by the large sample behaviour of the posterior: given an identifiable (up to permutations) mixture model, the posterior itself can often be observed to converge to a mixture of
G!
multivariate Gaussian distributions, as pointed out in
Frühwirth-Schnatter [24]
. This is exactly the condition under which this theorem is verified.

Remark 4
.

The assumptions of this theorem are that the MCMC draws independently follow the posterior distribution and that the posterior is equal to a mixture of multivariate normal distributions. The former assumption may well hold approximately, for example if a sufficiently large burn-in is used and the MCMC chain is sufficiently heavily thinned. The latter assumption also usually holds approximately, since any distribution can be approximated by a mixture of multivariate Gaussians, under few assumptions
[
4
]
.

The definition of the THAMES is given in Equation (
7
). In the rest of the section we present a multistep methodology to efficiently approximate the THAMES. While this methodology — unlike Equation (
7
) — is not simple, it drastically reduces the computational complexity of the THAMES, far below the superexponential complexity of other symmetric estimators used in this setting. A full pseudo-code implementation of this approximation is provided in Supplement B.

3.2
Calculating the THAMES for mixture models efficiently

A naive computation of Equation (
7
) would require
G!
computation steps, one for each permutation of the
G
different labels. This rapidly becomes infeasible computationally as
G
increases. However, in practice, many of these computations can be avoided due to the fact that the support
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
is contained within a very simple geometric object, the ellipsoid
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
. We can hence reduce computation time by selecting a restricted number of components and only summing over those permutations which respect the order of these components within the ellipsoid
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
. The selection of the components is done via graph-theoretic tools, while the permutations are determined via quadratic discriminant analysis
[
28
, QDA; see for example]
.

In the next three subsections, we propose a way to efficiently approximate the THAMES. First, we apply the principle of
``
overlap graphs
"
to choose a maximum set of components that do not overlap. Then, we use this set to construct an ordering constraint via QDA. We use this ordering constraint to efficiently approximate the THAMES by limiting the number of permutations required in the computation. We emphasise that overlapping components are determined in the parameter space and not in the observation space, since the objective is to reduce the computation cost of Equation (
7
), which is computed on the parameters from the posterior.

3.2.1
Visualising the posterior distribution via overlap graphs

Since the radius
c=\sqrt{R+1}
is taken as suggested by
Metodiev et al. [49]
,
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
roughly estimates a 50%-HPD-region on the relabelled posterior distribution. For more details, we refer the reader to
Metodiev et al. [49]
. In such a region, two components should not be overlapping if none of the components is superfluous. It can efficiently be verified (see Supplement B) if the hyperplane
\{\theta|\xi_{g_{1}}=\xi_{g_{2}}\}
crosses the ellipsoid
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
. If this is the case, we say that there is overlap between the components
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. This could be an indicator of either
g_{1}
or
g_{2}
being superfluous since they could be the same component.

Fig 1:
Example of two overlap graphs; an edge is added whenever there is overlap between 2 components.

Figure
1
shows two
``
overlap graphs
"
, where each node is a component and an edge is added between two nodes if there is overlap between the corresponding components. In Model 1, there is no overlap between all components, so there are no edges present. In Model 2, there is overlap between the pairs (1,2), (2,4), (4,3) and (3,1), so there are at most two non-overlapping components present. Please note that overlap graphs identify which mixture components have distinct parameters. In this sense, they achieve a different goal than most techniques that visualise the uncertainty of a cluster
[
73
,
18
,
41
,
5
, see for example]
.

Using graph-theoretic techniques on an overlap graph, we can find a set that approximates a maximum independent set, i.e., a maximum set of non-overlapping components. In Model 2 of Figure
1
such a set is, for instance, given by components 1 and 4. This set can be determined via the build_cover_greedy function in the gor package
[
3
]
and is denoted by
I(G)
. Note that the complexity of build_cover_greedy is quadratic with respect to
G
. As we are going to see,
I(G)
will strongly help reducing the computational cost of Equation (
7
). Interestingly, the size
|I(G)|
can also be interpreted as an estimate of the number of distinct (i.e., non-overlapping) components. Thus, we define the criterion of overlap (CO) as

\displaystyle\text{CO}=\#\begin{subarray}{c}\text{distinct}\\
\text{components}\end{subarray}-\#\begin{subarray}{c}\text{overlapping}\\
\text{ components}\end{subarray}=|I(G)|-(G-|I(G)|).

The CO is related to the goal of determining the number of distinct components (i.e., clusters) instead of the number of true components. The difference between these two different goals was pointed out in
Baudry et al. [6]
. While determining the number of components
G
that maximises the marginal likelihood is useful in the latter case, choosing
G
such that it maximises the CO is useful in the former. The set
I(G)
that determines the CO is also going to be used to define an ordering constraint to estimate the marginal likelihood: nodes outside of
I(G)
cannot be ordered together with nodes inside
I(G)
and should thus not be used directly to define the constraint. The constraint will be constructed via QDA, and will in turn be used to quickly compute the THAMES.

3.2.2
Applying QDA to the posterior sample

Our aim is to find a classification of a component parameter
\xi_{g}
such that the labels of two component parameters differ if, and only if, their classifications differ. This is done using discriminant analysis.

Focusing on the mixture component parameters in
\xi
, discriminant analysis can be performed on the relabelled posterior sample because the labels of the parameters are known by construction. Indeed,
\xi_{1}
are the parameters of component
1
,
\xi_{2}
of component
2
, and so on. Thus, a labelled sequence of size
G\cdot T
from the relabelled posterior sample is given by

\displaystyle\xi_{1}^{(1)\star},\dots,\xi_{G}^{(1)\star},\dots,\xi_{1}^{(T)\star},\dots,\xi_{G}^{(T)\star}.

In the following, QDA is applied to this sequence. The idea is that QDA should be able to distinguish two samples from two component parameters if, and only if, they are concentrated around two different values.

Let
\hat{\xi}_{1},\dots,\hat{\xi}_{G}
and
\hat{S}_{1},\dots,\hat{S}_{G}
denote the estimated component means and covariance matrices on the second half of the posterior sample, respectively. Let
\text{MVN}_{u}(\xi_{g^{\prime}};\hat{\xi}_{g},\hat{S}_{g})
denote the multivariate normal distribution of dimension
u
with mean
\hat{\xi}_{g}
and covariance matrix
\hat{S}_{g}
, evaluated at
\xi_{g^{\prime}}
. Then, any value
\xi_{g^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{u}
(of which the true component
g^{\prime}
is unknown) will be assigned to

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}})=\text{argmax}_{g}\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}),

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}})=\frac{\frac{1}{G}\text{MVN}_{u}(\xi_{g^{\prime}};\hat{\xi}_{g},\hat{S}_{g})}{\sum_{\tilde{g}=1}^{G}\frac{1}{G}\text{MVN}_{u}(\xi_{g^{\prime}};\hat{\xi}_{\tilde{g}},\hat{S}_{\tilde{g}})}.

(10)

Note that the cluster proportions are always estimated as
\frac{1}{G}
by construction. Thus,
\xi^{(t)\star}_{g^{\prime}}
is assigned the wrong label if
\hat{g}(\xi^{(t)\star}_{g^{\prime}})\neq g^{\prime}
.

It should also be noted that, if
G
is the true number of components, the relabelled posterior distribution should converge to the true value as the size of the data increases. Moreover, the condition needed to apply QDA is asymptotic normality, and posteriors are often asymptotically normal when converging to the truth
[
37
,
29
,
65
,
51
]
. Since this condition is often met, we argue that an asymptotically optimal ordering constraint can be defined via QDA.

3.2.3
Defining the ordering constraint

In the literature, ordering constraints have been employed to ensure identifiability
[
61
,
69
,
43
,
27
, see for example]
. However, the specific choice of the constraint can be of crucial importance
[
13
]
. Results from the previous two sections will be applied to construct a constraint that is useful for our purposes, in the sense that the constraint will mostly hold within an approximate 50%-HPD-region.

An approximate 50%-HPD-region was used to define the set
I(G)
such that a constraint could hold on this region for all of the components of
I(G)
. We now restrict the set of components over which the QDA allocation probabilities are computed. Thus, conditioning the QDA output on
I(G)
gives the probability
\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}|I(G))=\frac{\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}})}{\sum_{\tilde{g}\in I(G)}\hat{w}_{\tilde{g}}(\xi_{g^{\prime}})}
for all
g\in I(G),g^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,G\}
.

Given normality, the assignment
\hat{g}
and the uncertainty assessment
\hat{w}
meet the optimality results provided by QDA
[
28
]
. With this in mind, we combine this assignment
\hat{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}|I(G))
with its probability
\hat{w}_{\hat{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}|I(G))}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}|I(G))
to obtain the summary measure
W(\xi_{g^{\prime}})
:

\displaystyle W(\xi_{g^{\prime}})=\hat{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}|I(G))+1-\hat{w}_{\hat{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}|I(G))}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}|I(G)).

W
distinguishes the components in
I(G)
by construction: if
\xi_{g^{\prime}}^{(t)\star}
is assigned to the component
\hat{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}^{(t)\star}|I(G))
by QDA, it is placed within
[\hat{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}^{(t)\star}|I(G)),\hat{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}^{(t)\star}|I(G))+1]
by
W
. It is moved further away from
\hat{g}(\xi_{g^{\prime}}^{(t)\star}|I(G))
if its placement was assigned with low certainty.

Fig 2:
The values of
\mu,\sigma
and
W
for a relabelled sample
\xi^{(1)\star},\dots,\xi^{(T)\star}=(\mu^{(1)\star},\sigma^{(1)\star}),\dots,(\mu^{(T)\star},\sigma^{(T)\star})
from the posterior of the galaxy dataset of size
T=10,000
and different values of

; the acceptance regions of QDA are shown via a grid in the background

The role of the function
W
can be explained via Figure
2
: it shows the different relabelled MCMC simulations of the component parameter
\xi=(\mu_{1},\sigma_{1}),\dots,(\mu_{G},\sigma_{G})
of the galaxy dataset (see Section
4.2
) in conjunction with the values of
W
. Ordering by
\mu
would not be enough in the case that
G=2
, since its values overlap, although the values of
\sigma
do not. Ordering by
\sigma
would not be enough when
G=3
for the converse reason. However, the values of
W
differ for most points whenever they are in a different component. The exception is component 4 in the case that
G=4
: it is superfluous, and was thus not included into the calculation of
W
.

W
is used to define the subspace

\displaystyle\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G}:W(\xi_{1})\leq\dots\leq W(\xi_{G})\}.

(11)

This subspace will be used to subdivide the parameter space in
G!
different subspaces
J_{1},\dots,J_{G!}
created by permuting the ordering constraint, e.g.,
J_{1}=\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G}:W(\xi_{1})\leq W(\xi_{2})\leq\dots\leq W(\xi_{G})\},J_{2}=\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G}:W(\xi_{2})\leq W(\xi_{1})\leq\dots\leq W(\xi_{G})\}
and so on. This subdivision will be used for the computation of the THAMES in Equation (
7
).

3.2.4
Efficiently computing the THAMES

Fig 3:
A toy example to illustrate the computation of the THAMES; the points that lie in the ellipsoid can be counted by applying
P_{1},P_{2}
or
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
.

Let
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{G!}
denote the
G!
different ordering algorithms that map
\theta
to the different permutations
J_{1},\dots,J_{G!}
of the subspace defined in Equation (
11
), respectively, by permuting the labels of
\theta
. Note that these are different from
P_{1},\dots,P_{G!}
, since
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{G!}
always enforce some version of Equation (
11
) independent of the value of
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G})
. For example,
\psi_{1}
may order
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G})
to
(\xi_{1}^{\psi_{1}},\dots,\xi_{G}^{\psi_{1}})
such that
W(\xi_{1}^{\psi_{1}})\leq W(\xi_{2}^{\psi_{1}})\leq\dots\leq W(\xi_{G}^{\psi_{1}})
for all values of
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G})
, while
\psi_{2}
would result into
W(\xi_{2}^{\psi_{2}})\leq W(\xi_{1}^{\psi_{2}})\leq\dots\leq W(\xi_{G}^{\psi_{2}})
for all values of
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G})
.

The difference between
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{G!}
and
P_{1},\dots,P_{G!}
is shown in Figure
3
in the case that
G=2,T=10
and that there are only two parameters
\theta^{(t)\star}=(\mu_{1}^{(t)\star},\mu_{2}^{(t)\star})
, one for each component, with
W
replaced by the conceptually much simpler function
W^{\prime}(\mu_{g})=\mu_{g}
. Computing Equation (
7
) for
W^{\prime}
boils down to counting the number of times that
\psi_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star}),P_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})
falls in the ellipse for any
o=1,\dots,G!
. One can either apply
P_{1}
(the identity) and
P_{2}
(flipping the
x
- and
y
-axis), or
\psi_{1}
(ordering the points such that
\mu_{1}^{(t)\star}\leq\mu_{2}^{(t)\star}
for all
t=1,\dots,T
) and
\psi_{2}
(ordering the points such that
\mu_{2}^{(t)\star}\leq\mu_{1}^{(t)\star}
for all
t=1,\dots,T
). The outcome will be the same: 9 points are counted to be in the ellipse for some
\psi_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star}),P_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})
, but computing
\psi_{2}(\theta^{(t)\star})
could be avoided entirely since the ellipse does not cross the identity line.

We are going to determine which of
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{G!}
to evaluate through the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{G!}
denote the
G!
different ordering algorithms that map
\theta
to different permutations of the ordering constraint

\displaystyle W(\xi_{1})\leq\dots\leq W(\xi_{G}),

respectively, by permuting its labels. Let
\Delta
denote the adjacency matrix defined by
\Delta_{g_{1},g_{2}}=1
if
W(\xi_{g_{1}})<W(\xi_{g_{2}})
for all
\xi_{g_{1}},\xi_{g_{2}}
that satisfy the ellipsoidal constraint on
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
, and let
\Omega
denote any index set which includes the collection of all topological orderings on the graph defined by
\Delta
. The THAMES is equal to

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\textup{THAMES}}(G)=\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o\in\Omega}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1\\
\psi_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})\in B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha})}{\pi(\theta^{(t)\star})L(\theta^{(t)\star})}.

(12)

Remark 5
.

A choice of
\Omega
can be computed exactly, through a series of quadratic optimality problems that can be resolved in polynomial time with respect to
G
and
T
(see Supplement B). However, it is conceptually simpler and computationally more inexpensive to verify the inequalities
W(\xi_{g_{1}})<W(\xi_{g_{2}})
approximately by re-using the Monte Carlo sample from Equation (
4
). This approximation is used in all computations performed in this article. See Supplement B for details on these computations and the computation of
\Omega
, which can be done in polynomial time with respect to the size of
\Omega
. Note that any measurable function
W
could have been used to construct
\Omega
.
W
was chosen by QDA to target a function for which the inequalities
W(\xi_{g_{1}})<W(\xi_{g_{2}})
are fulfilled for as many pairs
(g_{1},g_{2})
as possible, thus reducing the size of
\Omega
.

The parameters
c
and
\alpha
can always be chosen such that the set
\Omega
is small enough and that the THAMES can be computed in a reasonable amount of time through Equation (
12
): the smaller the chosen ellipsoid is, the more the overlap decreases. See Supplement B for more details. In practice we found that this is rarely necessary, since few of the different orderings need to be evaluated. This is likely due to the fact that the components overlap less and less as the size of the data
n
increases.

3.3
The THAMES for high dimensional mixture models in the case of empty components

Aside from the computational challenges already discussed, employing mixture models in high dimensional settings can pose further difficulties. In particular, when the number of components is large, we run the risk of overfitting the data, resulting in empty components assigned to no data points. The posterior distribution of these empty component parameters is often highly diffuse, which makes it difficult to fit an ellipsoid around them, a technique that is vital for the precision of the THAMES. In this section, we will show how this problem can be sidestepped.

The component
g
of a posterior sample entry
\theta^{(t)}
is identified as possibly being empty if the product of conditional proportions

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\hat{z}_{i,g}^{(t)})\text{, where }\hat{z}_{i,g}^{(t)}=\frac{\tau_{g}^{(t)}f(Y_{i};\xi_{g}^{(t)})}{\sum_{\tilde{g}=1}^{G}\tau_{\tilde{g}}^{(t)}f(Y_{i};\xi_{\tilde{g}}^{(t)})},

is not close to zero. This may be true for a large value of the total number of components
G
, but is going to be less and less likely as
G
decreases. Thus, the empty component eventually disappears when simulating from the posterior of a sufficiently small model. In addition, when the probability of having an empty component is large, it is also efficiently estimated. Suppose that a Dirichlet prior with parameters
(e_{1},\dots,e_{G})>0
is used for the proportions. We use an identity from
Nobile [55]
,
Nobile [56]
to recursively estimate the THAMES through the following theorem.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that a Dirichlet prior with parameters
e_{1},\dots,e_{G}>0
is used for the proportions and that
\hat{Z}(G-1)
is a consistent estimator of
Z(G-1)
. If
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G}
are a priori independent and their prior distribution does not depend on the total number of components, then

\displaystyle\hat{Z}(G)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\sum^{G}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)\Gamma\left(n+\sum^{G-1}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)}{\Gamma\left(n+\sum^{G}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)\Gamma\left(\sum^{G-1}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)}\cdot\hat{Z}(G-1)\cdot\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{0}(G)},

(13)

with

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{0}(G)=\frac{1}{G}\sum^{G}_{g=1}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\hat{z}_{i,g}^{(t)}),\quad\hat{z}_{i,g}^{(t)}=\frac{\tau_{g}^{(t)}f(Y_{i};\xi_{g}^{(t)})}{\sum_{\tilde{g}=1}^{G}\tau_{\tilde{g}}^{(t)}f(Y_{i};\xi_{\tilde{g}}^{(t)})}

is a consistent estimator of
Z(G)
as long as
\hat{p}_{0}(G)
is a consistent estimator of its posterior mean.

Remark 6
.

Note that
\hat{p}_{0}(G)
is an arithmetic mean, which implies that it is a consistent estimator of its posterior mean under standard conditions on the MCMC sample
[
71
]
. Thus, we can simply check if
\hat{p}_{0}(G)
exceeds some upper threshold (we chose the threshold
1/T
in this article), and reduce the estimation procedure to the estimation of the marginal likelihood of the lower dimensional model if this is true.

It should be noted that this technique can only be used if
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G}
are a priori independent and their prior distribution does not depend on the total number of components. We used this technique whenever this condition was fulfilled, which was the case in all multivariate Gaussian mixture models, and computed the THAMES without this technique otherwise.

4
Experiments

We now demonstrate the utility of the THAMES for mixture models on simulated and real datasets.
For conciseness, we limit the model descriptions to the definition of the mixture models and the definition of the datasets only. More detailed descriptions, such as the exact prior distributions used, are presented in Supplement C.

4.1
Simulations

We perform simulations for two settings in which the marginal likelihood can be either computed analytically or easily approximated with a high degree of accuracy. Formulas for these exact or approximate solutions are given in Supplement D. In the first, the exact expression of the marginal likelihood is computed in a brute-force manner, which can be done due to the low number of data points. In the second, the marginal likelihood is computed for a high number of extremely distinct components.

5
Discussion

We presented the THAMES, a marginal likelihood estimator for mixture models. It is consistent, asymptotically normal, symmetric, and is computed on the relabelled MCMC parameters, without the need of simulations from the hidden allocation vectors. Its simplicity is exemplified by the fact that it only requires two inputs: the unnormalised log-posterior density (i.e., the logarithm of the prior times the likelihood) and the relabelled MCMC sample from the posterior. A function that takes exactly those two arguments is implemented in the R-package
thamesmix

[
48
]
.

The THAMES uses the symmetrisation method that was used in
Berkhof et al. [9]
and is tractable in the number of components, unlike other estimators that use this method. It should be noted that adjustments presented in
Berkhof et al. [9]
and
Lee and Robert [42]
already decrease the computation time of symmetrised estimators, but they do not lower it below
O(G!)
, which is the case for the THAMES. For example, we only needed to evaluate less than
10^{-6}
percent of the permutations over which we sum in the case that
G=15
for the galaxy dataset. However, for large
G
, higher overlap implies that the ellipsoid onto which the THAMES is defined will have to be shrunk to ensure that the THAMES can be computed in a reasonable amount of time, thus increasing the variance of the THAMES.

The THAMES also uses an identity from
Nobile [55]
,
Nobile [56]
to deal with empty components in cases in which this identity is applicable. While this identity was always applicable for the multivariate mixture models that we used, it is not applicable in cases in which the prior distributions on the components are not independent, such as the cases in which a hierarchical prior is used. This was the case for the univariate datasets used in
Richardson and Green [61]
, where such a hierarchical prior was used on the variance parameters. In our simulations, the THAMES still performed well on these datasets, presumably because precisely such a hierarchical prior ensures that the probability of empty components occurring is low. Nevertheless, obtaining an identity that is similar to the identity from
Nobile [55]
,
Nobile [56]
but that applies to all mixture models would be interesting future work.

There are specific aspects of the THAMES — such as the fact that its support is a union of ellipsoids — that make it easy to compute, so we conjecture that our method of computation can be extended to more intricate, but similar marginal likelihood estimators
[
66
,
74
,
46
,
52
, e.g.,]
. It should also be noted that one of these estimators, the ECMLE, was introduced and compared to the THAMES in
Naderi et al. [52]
, though not in the context of mixture models. Indeed, while the prior distribution is a mixture in one of the examples presented in that article, the likelihood is not. Thus, that comparison is not relevant to our paper, which focuses on marginal likelihood estimation in the context of mixture models, where the likelihood of the data, not the prior distribution, is a mixture.

Finally, we want to emphasise that our estimator can be applied to very general high dimensional and multivariate mixture models. This opens up a new avenue of numerous applications. One interesting application may be to further adapt the THAMES for the stochastic block model
[
75
,
57
]
, in which only the complete data likelihood is available.

A.1 Proving that an optimal truncation set is given by a HPD region

The following theorem proves that the variance of the estimator

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}=\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
\theta^{(t)}\in A\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(A)}{\pi(\theta^{(t)})L(\theta^{(t)})},

is minimised by an
\alpha
-HPD region under conditions on which this region is well-defined, if the samples
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
independently follow the posterior distribution. The condition on the distribution of
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
is rarely satisfied exactly, but should be satisfied approximately, for example if a sufficiently large burn-in period is used and the sample is sufficiently heavily thinned.

Theorem 5.1

(Theorem 2.1 in the main article)
.

Assume that
\pi(\theta)L(\theta)
is continuous on its support and that
\pi(\theta)L(\theta)
is non-constant, in the sense that the volume of each level set
V(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)=q\})=0
for all
q>0
. Let

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}=\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>q_{\alpha}\},

(16)

where
q_{\alpha}
is the constant for which the posterior probability of
\{\theta\in H_{\alpha}\}
is equal to
\alpha
. If the draws
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}\sim p(\theta|Y)
are independent, there exists a constant
\alpha\in(0,1]
such that
A=H_{\alpha}
minimises the variance of
\hat{Z}^{-1}
over all measurable sets
A
, conditional on
Y
.

Proof.

Let
A
be an arbitrary set of non-zero, finite volume that is contained in the posterior support. Because of independence and unbiasedness, the variance of
\hat{Z}^{-1}
is given by

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\hat{Z}^{-1}|Y]

\displaystyle=\text{Var}\left[\left.\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
\theta^{(t)}\in A\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(A)}{\pi(\theta^{(t)})L(\theta^{(t)})}\right|Y\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T/2}\text{Var}\left[\left.\frac{\mathds{1}_{A}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A)}{\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)})}\right|Y\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T/2}\text{E}\left[\left.\left(\frac{\mathds{1}_{A}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A)}{\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)})}\right)^{2}\right|Y\right]-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T/2}\int_{A}\left(\frac{1/V(A)}{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)}\right)^{2}p(\theta|Y)\;d\theta-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}\int_{A}\frac{1/V(A)^{2}}{p(\theta|Y)}d\theta-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2},

where
\mathds{1}_{A}(\theta^{(1)})
denotes the indicator function of
A
evaluated at
\theta^{(1)}
. We are going to show that there exists a level
\alpha
such that
\hat{Z}^{-1}
has an equal- or lower variance under
H_{\alpha}
.

Proving that there exists an
\alpha
such that
H_{\alpha}
is optimal

Let
H_{\alpha}=\{\theta|p(\theta|Y)\geq q_{\alpha}\}
denote an
\alpha
-HPD region. Since
V(H_{\alpha})
is continuous, we can choose
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle V(H_{\alpha})=V(A).

We have just shown that the variance of
\hat{Z}^{-1}
is, up to translation, proportional to

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\hat{Z}^{-1}|Y]\propto\int_{A}\frac{1/V(A)^{2}}{p(\theta|Y)}d\theta.

(17)

Comparing this to the same integral of
\hat{Z}^{-1}
under
H_{\alpha}
gives

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\hat{Z}^{-1}|Y]

\displaystyle\propto\frac{1}{V(A)^{2}}\int_{A}\frac{1}{p(\theta|Y)}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V(H_{\alpha})^{2}}\int_{A\cap H_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{p(\theta|Y)}d\theta+\frac{1}{V(H_{\alpha})^{2}}\int_{A\backslash H_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{p(\theta|Y)}d\theta

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{V(H_{\alpha})^{2}}\left(\int_{A\cap H_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{p(\theta|Y)}d\theta+\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}}V(A\backslash H_{\alpha})\right).

This is due to the fact that
A\backslash H_{\alpha}=\{\theta\in A|p(\theta|Y)<q_{\alpha}\}.
Using this and the fact that
V(A\backslash H_{\alpha})=V(H_{\alpha}\backslash A)
one can similarly conclude

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V(H_{\alpha})^{2}}\left(\int_{A\cap H_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{p(\theta|Y)}d\theta+\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}}V(A\backslash H_{\alpha})\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V(H_{\alpha})^{2}}\left(\int_{A\cap H_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{p(\theta|Y)}d\theta+\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}}V(H_{\alpha}\backslash A)\right)\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V(H_{\alpha})^{2}}\int_{A\cap H_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{p(\theta|Y)}\;d\theta+\frac{1}{V(H_{\alpha})^{2}}\int_{H_{\alpha}\backslash A}\frac{1}{p(\theta|Y)}\;d\theta=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V(H_{\alpha})^{2}}\int_{H_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{p(\theta|Y)}\;d\theta.

It follows that for any set
A
with
V(A)\in(0,\infty)
that is contained in the posterior support an
\alpha
exists such that the variance of
\hat{Z}^{-1}
is lower or equal when choosing
H_{\alpha}
instead of
A
. This finishes the proof.

Please note that
H_{\alpha}
is well-defined and
V(H_{\alpha})
is strictly increasing and continuous. This follows from the conditions given in the theorem. We could not find a direct proof in the literature (though the result is probably well-known), so we give one here.

Proving that
H_{\alpha}
is well-defined and
V(H_{\alpha})
is strictly increasing and continuous

Let
q\in(0,\infty)
be arbitrary. Let
P(\cdot|Y)
denote the probability measure of the posterior distribution. It is well-known that any measure is continuous, in the sense that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}P(A_{i}|Y)=P\left(\left.\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\right|Y\right)

for any decreasing series of sets
A_{1}\supseteq A_{2}\supseteq\dots

[
39
]
. Thus

\displaystyle\lim_{\tilde{q}\uparrow q}P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>\tilde{q}\}|Y)=P\left(\left.\bigcap_{\tilde{q}\uparrow q}\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>\tilde{q}\}\right|Y\right)

\displaystyle=P(\{\theta|\forall\tilde{q}<q:\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>\tilde{q}\}|Y)=P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)\geq q\}|Y)=P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>q\}|Y),

for all
q\in\mathbb{R}
due to the fact that
V(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)=q\})=0
. Since
V(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)=\tilde{q}\})=0
holds as well, it is also the case that

\displaystyle\lim_{\tilde{q}\downarrow q}P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>\tilde{q}\}|Y)

\displaystyle=1-\lim_{\tilde{q}\downarrow q}P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)\leq\tilde{q}\}|Y)

\displaystyle=1-\lim_{\tilde{q}\downarrow q}P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)<\tilde{q}\}|Y),

and we can use the same technique as before to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{\tilde{q}\downarrow q}P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>\tilde{q}\}|Y)=P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>q\}|Y).

Since we have just shown that the function
q\mapsto P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>q\}|Y)
is continuous, and since it tends to
0
as
q\to\infty
, to
1
as
q\downarrow 0
, it follows that the equation

\displaystyle P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>q|Y)=\alpha

(18)

has at least one solution. In addition, the function is also strictly decreasing in the interval
(\inf_{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>0}\pi(\theta)L(\theta),\sup_{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>0}\pi(\theta)L(\theta))
,

since for any
\inf_{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>0}\pi(\theta)L(\theta)<q_{1}<q_{2}<\sup_{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>0}\pi(\theta)L(\theta)

\displaystyle P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>q_{1}\}|Y)

\displaystyle-P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)>q_{2}\}|Y)

\displaystyle=P(\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)\in(q_{1},q_{2})\}|Y)>0,

because the set
\{\theta|\pi(\theta)L(\theta)\in(q_{1},q_{2})\}
has non-zero volume due to the fact that
\pi(\theta)L(\theta)
is continuous and non-constant. It follows that Equation (
18
) has only one solution, defined as
q_{\alpha}
. We can use the exact same technique to show that the volume
V(H_{\alpha})
is strictly increasing and continuous, since the only property of
P(\cdot|Y)
that we used is that it is a measure, and the volume is also a measure.
∎

A.2 Proving that the THAMES is consistent and asymptotically normal

Theorem 5.2

(Theorem 3.1 in the main article)
.

Suppose that
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
is ergodic and converges in distribution, as
T\to\infty
, to the symmetric posterior
p(\theta|Y)
of a mixture model. Let
\theta^{(1)\star},\dots,\theta^{(T)\star}
denote a relabelling of
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
. For any fixed choice of
A
for which
\pi\cdot L
is bounded away from 0, in the sense that a
q>0
exists such that
\pi(\theta)\cdot L(\theta)\geq q
for all
\theta\in A
, any symmetric estimator of the form

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\textup{S}}(G)=\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
P_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})\in A\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(A)}{\pi(\theta^{(t)\star})L(\theta^{(t)\star})},

almost surely converges to the reciprocal marginal likelihood
Z^{-1}(G)
as
T\to\infty
. If
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
is also ergodic of degree 2, then
\sqrt{T}(\hat{Z}^{-1}(G)-Z^{-1}(G))
converges, as
T\to\infty
, in distribution to the normal distribution with mean equal to 0 and variance depending on the choice of
A
.

Proof.

First, let us note that
\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\textup{S}}(G)
is symmetric, which implies that it is not affected by changes in labels. Thus, this estimator computed on the relabelled sample is exactly equal to the same estimator on the same sample, that is not relabelled:

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\textup{S}}(G)=\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
P_{o}(\theta^{(t)})\in A\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(A)}{\pi(\theta^{(t)})L(\theta^{(t)})},

By exchanging the order of summation,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\textup{S}}(G)=\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{t=T/2+1}^{T}\frac{\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\mathds{1}_{A}(P_{o}(\theta^{(t)}))/V(A)}{\pi(\theta^{(t)})L(\theta^{(t)})},

where
\mathds{1}
denotes an indicator function.

Due to
Tierney [71, Theorems 3 and 4,]
, the statement of the theorem is correct if the expectation of
\frac{\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\mathds{1}_{A}(P_{o}(\theta))/V(A)}{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)}
is equal to
Z^{-1}(G)
if
\theta\sim p(\theta|Y)
. Symmetry of the posterior implies that
\theta\sim p(\theta|Y)
is equivalent to
P_{o}(\theta)\sim p(\theta|Y)
for any
o\in\{1,\dots,G!\}
. It follows that

\displaystyle E\left[\frac{\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\mathds{1}_{A}(P_{o}(\theta))/V(A)}{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}E\left[\frac{\mathds{1}_{A}(P_{o}(\theta))/V(A)}{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}E\left[\frac{\mathds{1}_{A}(\theta)/V(A)}{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)}\right]=E\left[\frac{\mathds{1}_{A}(\theta)/V(A)}{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)}\right].

Bayes’ rule implies

\displaystyle E\left[\frac{\mathds{1}_{A}(\theta)/V(A)}{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)}\right]=\int_{A}\frac{p(\theta|Y)/V(A)}{\pi(\theta)L(\theta)}\;d\theta=\int_{A}\frac{p(\theta|Y)/V(A)}{Zp(\theta|Y)}\;d\theta=Z^{-1}(G).

This concludes the proof.
∎

A
1-p
confidence interval can thus be calculated by combining the confidence interval of
\hat{V}(B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha})
(which follows a scaled binomial distribution as it is proportional to a sum of i.i.d Bernoulli random variables) with the confidence interval of the above RIS estimator, which approximately follows a normal distribution, under mild assumptions on the MCMC sample. Since these estimators are independent, their confidence intervals can be combined using standard inequalities. Since we are usually interested in the logarithm of the THAMES, the logarithmic
\sqrt{1-p}
confidence intervals of these independent estimators can simply be added to obtain a
1-p
confidence interval. When we use the identity from
Nobile [55]
,
Nobile [56]
to recursively estimate the THAMES, the upper and lower bound are recursively estimated as well, using the same identity.

A.3 Proving that the variance of the THAMES minimises an upper bound

Optimality results were obtained in
Metodiev et al. [49]
in the case that
K=1
, under the assumption of normality of the posterior. We are going to present results in the case that the posterior is a mixture of normal distributions. This case is important because any density can be approximated by such a mixture, under few assumptions
[
4
]
.

Theorem 5.3

(Theorem 3.2 in the main article)
.

Consider a convex combination of truncated harmonic mean estimators,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\text{Mix}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
\theta^{(t)}\in A_{k}\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(A_{k})}{\pi(\theta^{(t)})L(\theta^{(t)})},

(19)

where
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{K}
are weights that sum to 1 and
A_{1},\dots,A_{K}
are truncation sets of the different estimators. Suppose that the posterior density
p(\theta|Y)
is a Gaussian mixture,
p(\theta|Y)=\sum^{K}_{k=1}a_{k}\textup{MVN}_{R}(\theta;m_{k},S_{k}),
where
\text{MVN}_{R}(\theta;m_{k},S_{k})
denotes the Gaussian law of dimension
R
with mean
m_{k}
and covariance matrix
S_{k}
. If the draws
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}
from the posterior distribution are independent, an upper bound on the variance of
\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\text{Mix}}
, conditional on
Y
, is minimised by
A_{k}=E_{m_{k},S_{k},c_{R}},\omega_{k}=a_{k},
where
c_{R}
is the minimising constant determined in
Metodiev et al. [49]
, which is asymptotically equal to
\sqrt{R+1}
, i.e.,
\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{c_{R}}{\sqrt{R+1}}=1
.

Proof.

Because of independence, unbiasedness, the Cauchy-Schwartz and the QM-AM inequality, the variance of
\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\text{Mix}}
is given by

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\text{Mix}}|Y]=\text{Var}\left[\left.\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
\theta^{(t)}\in A_{k}\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(A_{k})}{\pi(\theta^{(t)})L(\theta^{(t)})}\right|Y\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T/2}\text{Var}\left[\left.\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A_{k})}{\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)})}\right|Y\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{unbiased}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T/2}\text{E}\left[\left.\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A_{k})}{\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)})}\right)^{2}\right|Y\right]-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{linearity}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\omega_{j}\text{E}\left[\left.\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{k}\cap A_{j}}(\theta^{(1)})/(V(A_{k})V(A_{j}))}{(\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)}))^{2}}\right|Y\right]-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Cauchy Schwartz}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K}\sqrt{E\left[\left.\omega_{k}^{2}\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A_{k})^{2}}{(\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)}))^{2}}\right|Y\right]E\left[\left.\omega_{j}^{2}\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{j}}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A_{j})^{2}}{(\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)}))^{2}}\right|Y\right]}

\displaystyle-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{QM-AM}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\frac{K^{2}}{T/2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}^{2}\omega_{j}^{2}E\left[\left.\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A_{k})^{2}}{(\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)}))^{2}}\right|Y\right]E\left[\left.\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{j}}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A_{j})^{2}}{(\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)}))^{2}}\right|Y\right]}

\displaystyle-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}=

\displaystyle\frac{K^{2}}{T/2}\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}^{2}E\left[\left.\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A_{k})^{2}}{(\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)}))^{2}}\right|Y\right]\right)-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}=

\displaystyle\frac{K}{T/2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}^{2}E\left[\left.\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A_{k})^{2}}{(\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)}))^{2}}\right|Y\right]-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}.

We can use the fact that
p(\theta|Y)\geq a_{k}\text{MVN}_{R}(\theta;m_{k},S_{k})
for any
k
to get

\displaystyle\frac{K}{T/2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}^{2}E\left[\left.\frac{\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}(\theta^{(1)})/V(A_{k})^{2}}{(\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)}))^{2}}\right|Y\right]-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}=

\displaystyle\frac{K}{T/2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}^{2}\int_{A_{k}}\frac{p(\theta|Y)/V(A_{k})^{2}}{(\pi(\theta)L(\theta))^{2}}\;d\theta-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}=

\displaystyle\frac{K}{T/2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}^{2}\int_{A_{k}}\frac{p(\theta|Y)/V(A_{k})^{2}}{(Zp(\theta|Y))^{2}}\;d\theta-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}=

\displaystyle\frac{KZ^{-2}}{T/2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}^{2}\int_{A_{k}}\frac{1/V(A_{k})^{2}}{p(\theta|Y)}\;d\theta-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{KZ^{-2}}{T/2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{a_{k}}\int_{A_{k}}\frac{1/V(A_{k})^{2}}{\text{MVN}_{R}(\theta;m_{k},S_{k})}\;d\theta-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}.

\text{MVN}_{R}(\theta;m_{k},S_{k})
is itself a density, and the integral above is exactly the integral found in the proof of Theorem 1, Equation (
17
). Thus, this integral is proportional to the variance of a THAMES estimator for which the posterior density is exactly normal. This case has been dealt with in
Metodiev et al. [49, Theorem 1]
, and the integral is indeed minimised by
E_{m_{k},S_{k},c_{R}}
. The transformation formula implies that the integral is independent of
k
in this case, since

\displaystyle\frac{K}{T/2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{a_{k}}\int_{E_{m_{k},S_{k},c_{R}}}\frac{Z^{-2}/V(E_{m_{k},S_{k},c_{R}})^{2}}{\text{MVN}_{R}(\theta;m_{k},S_{k})}\;d\theta-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}=

\displaystyle\frac{K}{T/2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{a_{k}}\int_{E_{0_{R},I_{R},c_{R}}}\frac{Z^{-2}/V(E_{0_{R},I_{R},c_{R}})^{2}}{\text{MVN}_{R}(\theta;0_{R},I_{R})}\;d\theta-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2}=

\displaystyle\frac{K}{T/2}\int_{E_{0_{R},I_{R},c_{R}}}\frac{Z^{-2}/V(E_{0_{R},I_{R},c_{R}})^{2}}{\text{MVN}_{R}(\theta;0_{R},I_{R})}\;d\theta\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{a_{k}}\right)-\frac{Z^{-2}}{T/2},

and the quadratic mean is minimised by
\omega_{k}=a_{k}
.
∎

A.4 Proving that we can efficiently compute the THAMES

Theorem 5.4

(Theorem 3.1 in the main article)
.

Let
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{G!}
denote the
G!
different ordering algorithms that map
\theta
to different permutations of the ordering constraint

\displaystyle W(\xi_{1})\leq\dots\leq W(\xi_{G}),

respectively, by permuting its labels. Let
\Delta
denote the adjacency matrix defined by
\Delta_{g_{1},g_{2}}=1
if
W(\xi_{g_{1}})<W(\xi_{g_{2}})
for all
\xi_{g_{1}},\xi_{g_{2}}
that satisfy the ellipsoidal constraint on
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
, and let
\Omega
denote any index set which includes the collection of all topological orderings on the graph defined by
\Delta
. The THAMES is equal to

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\textup{THAMES}}(G)=\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o\in\Omega}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1\\
\psi_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})\in B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha})}{\pi(\theta^{(t)\star})L(\theta^{(t)\star})}.

(20)

Proof.

The key identity for this proof is that for every
t
and for every function
\iota
of
\theta

\displaystyle\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\iota(P_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star}))=\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\iota(\psi_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})).

It is true because the functions
\psi_{o},P_{o}
each assign a different permutation to
\theta
. This can be illustrated via Figure
8
: let
T=10
,
\theta^{(t)\star}=(\mu^{(t)\star}_{1},\mu^{(t)\star}_{2})
, and
\iota(\theta^{(t)\star})=\mathds{1}_{E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}}(\theta^{(t)\star})
. In this case, we are simply counting the number of times that
P_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})
is in
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
. This can be done by applying
P_{1},P_{2}
one by one and counting each time (plots A and B), or by ordering every parameter such that
\mu_{1}^{(t)}>\mu_{2}^{(t)}
via
\psi_{1}
, and then switching the order to
\mu_{2}^{(t)}>\mu_{1}^{(t)}
via
\psi_{2}
(plots C and D). In fact,
\psi_{2}
can be avoided entirely in Figure
8
since
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
does not cross the identity line.

Since
\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\text{\text{THAMES}}}
is also a sum over all permutations of
\theta^{(t)}
, we can replace
P_{o}
by
\psi_{o}
to get

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{-1}_{\text{THAMES}}(G)=\frac{1}{G!}\sum_{o=1}^{G!}\frac{1}{T/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=T/2+1,\\
\psi_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})\in B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}\end{subarray}}^{T}\frac{1/V(B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha})}{\pi(\theta^{(t)\star})L(\theta^{(t)\star})}.

Suppose that
\psi_{o}
is such that
W(\xi_{g_{1}}^{\psi_{o}})>W(\xi_{g_{2}}^{\psi_{o}})
, but
\Delta_{g_{1},g_{2}}=1
. This immediately implies that
\psi_{o}(\theta^{(t)\star})
will not be included in the sum for any
\theta^{(t)\star}
, since
W(\xi_{g_{1}})\leq W(\xi_{g_{2}})
for all
\xi_{g_{1}},\xi_{g_{2}}
satisfying the ellipsoidal constraint on
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
, and thus in particular in
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
. Thus, the sum only includes orderings which respect the partial ordering implied by
\Delta
. This is how topological orderings are defined. Since
\Omega
only excludes those parts of the series that would be excluded anyway, this finishes the proof.
∎

A.5 Proving that we can avoid dealing with empty components

Theorem 5.5

(Theorem 3.4 in the main article)
.

Suppose that a Dirichlet prior with parameters
e_{1},\dots,e_{G}>0
is used for the proportions and that
\hat{Z}(G-1)
is a consistent estimator of
Z(G-1)
. If
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G}
are a priori independent and their prior distribution does not depend on the total number of components, then

\displaystyle\hat{Z}(G)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\sum^{G}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)\Gamma\left(n+\sum^{G-1}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)}{\Gamma\left(n+\sum^{G}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)\Gamma\left(\sum^{G-1}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)}\cdot\hat{Z}(G-1)\cdot\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{0}(G)},

(21)

with

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{0}(G)=\frac{1}{G}\sum^{G}_{g=1}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\hat{z}_{i,g}^{(t)}),\quad\hat{z}_{i,g}^{(t)}=\frac{\tau_{g}^{(t)}f(Y_{i};\xi_{g}^{(t)})}{\sum_{\tilde{g}=1}^{G}\tau_{\tilde{g}}^{(t)}f(Y_{i};\xi_{\tilde{g}}^{(t)})}

is a consistent estimator of
Z(G)
as long as
\hat{p}_{0}(G)
is a consistent estimator of its posterior mean.

Proof.

The data has the following latent variable representation:

\displaystyle C_{i}|\tau

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}\text{Multinom}_{G}(1,\tau),

\displaystyle Y_{i}|\xi,C_{i,g}=1

\displaystyle\sim f(\cdot;\xi_{g}),

with the hidden matrix of allocation vectors
C=(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times G}(\{0,1\}).

Nobile [55]
,
Nobile [56]
showed that

\displaystyle\frac{Z(G)}{Z(G-1)}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\sum^{G}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)\Gamma\left(n+\sum^{G-1}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)}{\Gamma\left(n+\sum^{G}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)\Gamma\left(\sum^{G-1}_{g=1}e_{g}\right)}\cdot\frac{1}{1-p(\exists i:C_{i,G}=1|Y)}.

(22)

As pointed out by
Nobile [56]
,

\displaystyle 1-p(\exists i:C_{i,G}=1)=p\left(\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i,G}=0\right|Y\right),

the posterior probability that component
G
is empty. By the law of total probability

\displaystyle p\left(\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i,G}=0\right|Y\right)=\int p\left(\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i,G}=0\right|Y,\theta\right)p(\theta|Y)\;d\theta

The distribution of
C_{i}
given
Y,\theta
is i.i.d multinomial with probabilities

\displaystyle z_{i,g}=\frac{\tau_{g}f(Y_{i},\xi_{g})}{\sum_{\tilde{g}=1}^{G}\tau_{\tilde{g}}f(Y_{i};\xi_{\tilde{g}})},\quad g=1,\dots,G.

This simplifies the integral to

\displaystyle\int p\left(\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i,G}=0\right|Y,\theta\right)p(\theta|Y)\;d\theta=\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-z_{i,G})p(\theta|Y)\;d\theta,

which is in turn equal to the posterior expectation of
\prod^{n}_{i=1}(1-z_{i,G})
. By the law of large numbers,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\prod^{n}_{i=1}(1-\hat{z}_{i,G}^{(t)})

(23)

is a valid estimator of this expectation. Due to the symmetry of the posterior, choosing any other component
g=1,\dots,G
instead of the last component
G
would give the exact same result. Averaging over all of these versions produces a symmetric estimator. This estimator is exactly equal to
\hat{p}_{0}
. Combining the unbiasedness of
\hat{p}_{0}
with the consistency of
\hat{Z}(G-1)
gives the assertion.

∎

Supplement B: details on the algorithm

B.1 Choosing the tuning-parameters

We choose
c=\sqrt{R+1}
, following the results of
Metodiev et al. [49]
. The hyperparameter
\alpha
could be chosen via grid-search over
(0,1)
such that it minimises the empirical variance of the THAMES. However, while this approach may work, it is computationally expensive, since we would have to approximate the volume of
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
via a new Monte Carlo sample for each value that
\alpha
can take on the grid. Instead, we suggest choosing
\alpha
such that the posterior distribution is well-behaved on
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
. We take this to mean that the distribution of the sample from the posterior within
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
is as close as possible to a normal distribution, truncated such that at most 50 percent of the posterior sample is within
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
. This is because the tails of the posterior can cause difficulties in reciprocal importance sampling estimators and the tails of the normal distribution are particularly well behaved in the case of the THAMES if they are cut off at 50 percent, as was shown in
Metodiev et al. [49]
.

If the posterior is normal, the negative unnormalised log-posterior values

\displaystyle\eta^{(1)},\dots,\eta^{(T)}=-\log(\pi(\theta^{(1)})L(\theta^{(1)})),\dots,-\log(\pi(\theta^{(T)})L(\theta^{(T)}))

follow, if they are truncated to an HPD region, a truncated, shifted and scaled
\chi^{2}
-distribution with
d
degrees of freedom. This is because the quadratic form

\displaystyle(\theta^{(t)}-m)^{\intercal}S^{-1}(\theta^{(t)}-m)

follows a
\chi^{2}
-distribution if
m
and
S
are the true posterior mean and covariance matrix, respectively.

We set
\alpha
such that it minimises the Kolmogorov distance against this
\chi^{2}
-distribution. Let
F
denote the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of
\eta^{(1)},\dots,\eta^{(T)}
conditioned on the
\alpha
-HPD region
H_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle F(\eta)=\frac{1}{\sum^{T}_{t=1}\mathds{1}_{H_{\alpha}}(\theta^{(t)})}\sum^{T}_{t=1}\mathds{1}_{H_{\alpha}}(\theta^{(t)})\cdot\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\eta]}(\eta^{(t)}),

with
\mathds{1}
denoting the indicator function, and let
\chi_{M,v}^{2}
denote the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the shifted and scaled
\chi^{2}
distribution truncated on
[0,\max\{\eta^{(1)},\dots,\eta^{(T)}\}]
with
d
degrees of freedom, mean
M
and variance
v
.
The Kolmogorov distance
[
76
, see for instance]
on
H_{\alpha}
is given by

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in H_{\alpha}}\left|\chi^{2}_{\hat{M}_{\alpha},\hat{v}_{\alpha}}(-\log(\pi(\theta)L(\theta)))-F(-\log(\pi(\theta)L(\theta)))\right|,

(24)

where the mean and variance parameter are computed using the moment-estimators
\hat{M}_{\alpha},\hat{v}_{\alpha}
on the values of
\eta^{(1)},\dots,\eta^{(T)}
for which the corresponding parameters are within
H_{\alpha}
. We minimise this distance by evaluating Equation (
24
) on some subset of the grid
\{\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}\}
for all sensible values of
\alpha
, with
\alpha
being capped at 50 percent for the reasons that we mentioned before (for more details on this technique, see the following subsection).

Summary

Choosing
\alpha
such that it minimises Equation (
24
) has many advantages. An optimal value is easy to compute, since it can be efficiently approximated by evaluating at most
T^{2}
different values due to the discreteness of
F
. It also conforms with previous results from
Metodiev et al. [49]
: if the posterior is exactly normal, the Kolmogorov distance is going to approach
0
if
\alpha=1
, which implies
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}=E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
. This means that the asymptotically optimal set derived by
Metodiev et al. [49]
for the case that the posterior is exactly normal is also going to be chosen by our method if this case occurs. Finally, if the posterior is not normal, our method chooses
\alpha
such that the THAMES is well-behaved, in the sense that the Kolmogorov distance is going to penalise extreme outliers.

Even without this optimisation algorithm, we have observed that choosing
c=\sqrt{R+1}
and
\alpha=0.5
lead to good results, which is in congruence with similar observations from
Metodiev et al. [49]
and
Reichl [60]
.

B.2 Choosing the grid for
\alpha

We want to choose
\alpha
such that it minimises the Kolmogorov distance

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in H_{\alpha}}\left|\chi^{2}_{\hat{M}_{\alpha},\hat{s}_{\alpha}}(-\log(\pi(\theta)L(\theta)))-F(-\log(\pi(\theta)L(\theta)))\right|.

We do this by first choosing a grid for the different values of
\alpha
, and then, for each value on the grid, approximating the supremum via another grid search over
\theta
, respectively.

Regarding the choice of the grid for
\alpha
, it is important to note that the above function is itself an estimator of the distance between the true CDF of the normalised log posterior values, approximated by
F
, and the CDF of the
\chi^{2}
-distribution. For this reason, we have experienced that small values of
\alpha
caused the function to be very volatile and unreliable, since almost all of the normalised log posterior values are excluded in this case. We have experienced that this can be avoided by only evaluating values of
\alpha
that are larger than
0.2
.

In addition, the function
F
is constant for most values of
\theta
, excluding the values of the posterior sample itself,
\{\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)}\}
. In turn, this leaves us with a possibility of
T
different values of
\alpha
to check, but we chose to thin the sequence by a step-size of 100, since we made the experience that this greatly reduces computation time with no loss in the efficiency of the THAMES. Regarding the choice of the grid for
\theta
, we chose the full grid, since the computation time was quite small, even for a large grid. If the chosen
\alpha
is larger than 0.5, it is then truncated to 0.5, to be in accordance with
Metodiev et al. [49]
.

B.3 Numerically verifying that
\xi_{g_{1}}=\xi_{g_{2}}
lies in
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}

\xi_{g_{1}}=\xi_{g_{2}}
lies in
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
if, and only if, the minimum

\displaystyle\text{min}_{\theta:\xi_{g_{1}}=\xi_{g_{2}}}(\theta-\hat{\theta})^{\intercal}\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\theta-\hat{\theta})

(25)

is smaller or equal to
c^{2}
. This is a quadratic optimisation problem, which can be solved in polynomial time since
\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}
is positive definite, for example by using one of the algorithms given in
Goldfarb and Idnani [30]
,
Goldfarb and Idnani [31]
. We do this using the quadprog R package
[
72
]
.

B.4 Efficiently computing the THAMES

The matrix
\Delta
is constructed as follows: if it has been established that
\xi_{g_{1}}=\xi_{g_{2}}
for
g_{1},g_{2}
and for some pair of values
(\xi_{g_{1}},\xi_{g_{2}})
that follows the ellipsoidal constraint given by
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
, then
\Delta_{g_{1},g_{2}}=0
since
W(\xi_{g_{1}})=W(\xi_{g_{2}})
in that case. If not, then we test
W(\xi_{g_{1},\text{Unif}}^{(t)})<W(\xi_{g_{2},\text{Unif}}^{(t)})
with
(\xi_{g_{1},\text{Unif}}^{(t)},\xi_{g_{2},\text{Unif}}^{(t)})
being from the Monte Carlo sample that is simulated uniformly on
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
. If there exists a
j
such that the condition is false, then
\Delta_{g_{1},g_{2}}
is set to 0. It is set to 1 otherwise. The law of large numbers guarantees
\Delta_{g_{1},g_{2}}
will be evaluated accurately if the size of the Monte Carlo sample
N
is large enough.

The choice of
\Omega
that is used in this article is constructed via an algorithm that returns all topological orderings on the graph defined by
\Delta
. An algorithm that does this in polynomial time with respect to the size of
\Omega
is given in
Knuth and Szwarcfiter [40]
. We implemented a version of this algorithm.

A very rough upper bound on the size of
\Omega
is given by
G!/|I^{\prime}(G)|!
, where
|I^{\prime}(G)|
denotes the number of vertices of the longest path in the graph generated by
\Delta
. This is because there are
|I^{\prime}(G)|+1
possibilities of choosing the position of the first node that is not in
I^{\prime}(G)
,
|I^{\prime}(G)|+2
possibilities of choosing the position of the second node and so on, and the order of the nodes within
I^{\prime}(G)
is fixed. Note that
I^{\prime}(G)
can be computed by determining the shortest path of a graph with adjacency matrix
-\Delta
, by applying a shortest-path algorithm that can handle negative edges, such as the Bellman–Ford algorithm
[
19
,
7
]
. This is possible since
\Delta
cannot have cycles, since the relation
W(\xi_{g_{1}})<W(\xi_{g_{2}})
is transitive. We use the shortest.path function from the igraph package
[
16
,
2
,
17
]
for this task.

Whenever
G!/|I^{\prime}(G)|!
passes 50,000, we take this as a cue that the THAMES cannot be evaluated in a reasonable amount of time. In this case, we iteratively shrink the ellipsoid until the bound passes 50,000. This is done by repeatedly dividing
c
by 2 and, if the ellipsoid ends up containing no samples due to this shrinkage, setting
\hat{\theta}
to the mode of the second half of the posterior sample and restarting the process. This makes sure that the ellipsoid always contains at least one point, the mode of the second half of the posterior sample. To make sure that
W
can differentiate this point after
\hat{\theta}
has been shifted, we train QDA only on those sample points that are within the ellipsoid in this case. Note that
\Omega
can also be chosen such that it allows an exact computation of the THAMES. We do not use this choice, since we consider the approximate choice presented above to be sufficiently precise, and much simpler than the exact one. The exact choice is still explained here to showcase that an exact computation of the THAMES is possible in a feasible amount of time.

An exact computation of
\Omega

A different choice of
\Omega
can be computed by determining
\Delta
via a variety of inequalities. If these inequalities hold for a given value of
g_{1},g_{2}
, they imply
W(\xi_{g_{1}})<W(\xi_{g_{2}})
for all values of
(\xi_{g_{1}},\xi_{g_{2}})
that follow the ellipsoidal constraint given by
E_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c}
. Thus, these inequalities can be used to construct
\Delta
like in the previous case, but without using the approximate verification through the Monte Carlo sample. This construction of
\Delta
will thus give a choice of
\Omega
through which the THAMES can be computed exactly, and not just approximately. In the following text, it is explained how these inequalities can be derived.

We have
W(\xi_{g_{1}})<W(\xi_{g_{2}})
if, and only if

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{1}}|I(G))+1-\hat{w}_{\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{1}}|I(G))}(\xi_{g_{1}}|I(G))<\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{2}}|I(G))+1-\hat{w}_{\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{2}}|I(G))}(\xi_{g_{2}}|I(G))

Suppose that
\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{1}}|I(G))
and
\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{2}}|I(G))
are different natural numbers. In this case, the above inequality holds if, and only if

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{1}}|I(G))<\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{2}}|I(G)),

(26)

since the remainders of the sums are within
(0,1)
.
\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{1}}|I(G))
and
\hat{g}(\xi_{g_{2}}|I(G))
are different natural numbers if, and only if

\displaystyle\text{argmax}_{g\in I(G)}\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g_{1}}|I(G))\neq\text{argmax}_{g\in I(G)}\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g_{2}}|I(G)).

this is the case if, and only if

\displaystyle\text{argmax}_{g\in I(G)}\frac{\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g_{1}})}{\sum_{\tilde{g}\in I(G)}\hat{w}_{\tilde{g}}(\xi_{g^{\prime}})}\neq\text{argmax}_{g\in I(G)}\frac{\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g_{2}})}{\sum_{\tilde{g}\in I(G)}\hat{w}_{\tilde{g}}(\xi_{g^{\prime}})}.

Since the denominators are the same, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\text{argmax}_{g\in I(G)}\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g_{1}})\neq\text{argmax}_{g\in I(G)}\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g_{2}}).

Since
\sum_{g}\hat{w}_{g}=1,\hat{w}_{g}>0\forall g
, this inequality holds if for all values of
\xi_{g_{1}},\xi_{g_{2}}
within the ellipsoid there exists a number
g_{1}^{\prime}
such that
\hat{w}_{g_{1}^{\prime}}(\xi_{g_{1}})>0.5
and another number
g_{2}^{\prime}>g_{1}^{\prime}
such that
\hat{w}_{g_{2}^{\prime}}(\xi_{g_{2}})>0.5
. The inequality holds because the former statement implies that
\text{argmax}_{g\in I(G)}\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g_{1}})=g_{1}^{\prime}
and
\text{argmax}_{g\in I(G)}\hat{w}_{g}(\xi_{g_{2}})=g_{2}^{\prime}
for all values of
\xi_{g_{1}},\xi_{g_{2}}
within
B_{\hat{\theta},\hat{\Sigma},c,\alpha}
.

By definition,
\hat{w}_{g_{1}^{\prime}}(\xi_{g_{1}})>0.5
if, and only if

B.5 The full algorithm

A full description of an approximation of the THAMES for mixture models is given in Algorithms
1
and
2
. These algorithms have been constructed by combining the computational details given in Supplement B with the broader overview given in the main part of this chapter. Algorithm
2
recursively calls Algorithm
1
, and is always used whenever the prior on the mixture proportions is Dirichlet,
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{G}
are a priori independent and the prior distribution does not depend on the total number of components. If these conditions are not fulfilled, Algorithm
1
is used directly.

In addition, there are three edge cases in which the THAMES is undefined or cannot be computed in a feasible amount of time:

(1)

none of the second half of the posterior sample is included in the truncation set, in which case the THAMES is undefined. This can be avoided by setting the centre of the ellipsoid to the posterior mode of the second half of the posterior sample.

(2)

the volume estimator of the truncation set is equal to 0, in which case the THAMES is undefined. This can happen if the truncation ellipsoid does not contain enough of the posterior HPD region, in which case the probability of the Monte Carlo sample taking values in this region is too small. This can be avoided by decreasing the size of the ellipsoid, by, e.g., by dividing its radius by 2.

(3)

the permutation subset
\Omega
is too large to allow a feasible computation time of the THAMES. This can also be avoided by decreasing the size of the ellipsoid, since this decreases the overlap of the mixture parameters within the ellipsoid and hence the size of
\Omega
.

A sufficient repetition of the modifications outlined above ensures that all three edge cases can be avoided, and that the THAMES can be computed.

C.1 Simulations on univariate Gaussian mixtures

An analytic solution of the marginal likelihood was computed in the case that the number of parameters and data points is very small
[
24

, such that the performance of the THAMES could be compared to other state-of-the-art estimators.

Model

We consider a univariate Gaussian mixture model,

\displaystyle Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}|\mu,\sigma,\tau\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\tau_{g}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{g},\sigma^{2}_{g}),

where only the parameters

C=(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times G}(\{0,1\})
denote the matrix of hidden allocation vectors. An analytic solution was computed and a Gibbs sampler was constructed using the following theorem. It is proven in Supplement
D
.

Theorem 5.6
.

If the prior is set to the distribution with the following symmetric densities,

\displaystyle\pi(\mu)=\prod_{g=1}^{G}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{g};0,1),\quad\pi(\sigma_{g})=\begin{cases}1&\sigma_{g}=1,\\
0&\textup{else},\end{cases}\quad\pi(\tau_{g})=\begin{cases}1&\tau_{g}=1/G,\\
0&\textup{else,}\end{cases}

(32)

then the marginal likelihood is given by

\displaystyle p(Y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{C_{1},\dots,C_{n}}p(Y|C_{1},\dots,C_{n})p(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})

(33)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G^{n}}\sum_{C_{1},\dots,C_{n}}\textup{MVN}_{n}\left(Y;0_{n},I_{n}+O\right),

where

if

O_{i,j}=0
else. The conditional distributions of

\displaystyle\mu_{g}|C,Y

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{ind.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i,g}Y_{i}}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i,g}},\frac{1}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i,g}}\right)

\displaystyle C_{i}|\mu,Y

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{ind.}}}{{\sim}}\textup{Multinom}_{G}\left(1,\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(Y_{i}-\mu_{1}\right)^{2}\right)}{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(Y_{i}-\mu_{g}\right)^{2}\right)},\\
\vdots,\\
\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(Y_{i}-\mu_{G}\right)^{2}\right)}{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(Y_{i}-\mu_{g}\right)^{2}\right)}\end{pmatrix}\right).

Settings

In all of the following scenarios, we set

such that the formula given in Theorem

In addition, even though our prior assumptions were symmetric, we wanted to test how our estimator performs in the case that the data is simulated from a distribution that is not symmetric, in the sense that its components have distinct means and proportions. For all scenarios, we simulated 50 different datasets from the fixed parameters

C.2 Simulations on multivariate Gaussian mixtures

In this section, the performance of the THAMES was assessed in the case that the number of components

Setting

To ensure that Equation (

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=0.00001,

\displaystyle p(\mu|Y,C_{1},\dots,C_{n})\propto p(\mu)p(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})p(Y|\mu,C_{1},\dots,C_{n})

D.2
Highly accurate
marginal likelihood
approximation
for an extremely well separated dataset

We know that the marginal likelihood is given by

\displaystyle Z(G)=\sum_{C_{1},\dots,C_{n}}p(Y|C_{1},\dots,C_{n})p(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})\simeq G!p(Y|C^{0})p(C^{0})

if
p(Y|C^{0})\simeq 0
for all but one allocation matrix
C^{0}
(and its permutations). The expression
p(C^{0})
is equal to
[
24
,
35

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(G)}{\Gamma(G+n)}\prod^{G}_{g=1}\Gamma\left(1+\sum^{n}_{i=1}C_{i,g}^{0}\right).

First of all, let us note that (denoting
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{G}),\Sigma=(\Sigma_{1},\dots,\Sigma_{G})
)

\displaystyle p(Y|C^{0},\tau,\mu,\Sigma)=p(Y|C^{0},\mu,\Sigma),

since the proportions are rendered redundant by the knowledge of the component membership.
p(Y|C^{0})
can thus be further decomposed into

\displaystyle p(Y|C^{0})

\displaystyle=\int p(Y|C^{0},\mu,\Sigma)\pi(\mu,\Sigma)\;d(\mu,\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i=1}^{G}p(Y_{i}|C^{0},\mu,\Sigma)\pi(\mu,\Sigma)\;d(\mu,\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\prod^{G}_{g=1}\int p_{g}(Y|\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})\pi_{g}(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})\;d(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})=\prod^{G}_{g=1}Z_{g}(G),

where
\pi_{g}
denotes the prior on
(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})
and

\displaystyle p_{g}(Y|\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})=\prod_{i:C_{i,g}=1}p(Y_{i}|C^{0},\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g}).

This is because
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are independent given
C^{0},\mu,\Sigma
and
(\mu_{1},\Sigma_{1}),\dots,(\mu_{G},\Sigma_{G})

Because

for which

with length

G

\displaystyle\int p_{g}(Y|\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})\pi_{g}(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})\;d(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})=\frac{p_{g}(Y|\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})\pi_{g}(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})}{p_{g}(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g}|Y)}
