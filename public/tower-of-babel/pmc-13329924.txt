Pulmonary Vein Isolation: Comparing different energy sources – independent or complementary use?
Authors: Marina Marins, Carina Muresan, Maya Verma, Atul Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulsed-field ablation (PFA) has rapidly emerged as a non-thermal modality for pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) in the treatment of atrial fibrillation. By delivering high-voltage, microsecond-scale electrical pulses, PFA produces irreversible electroporation of cardiomyocytes while preserving the extracellular matrix of adjacent structures. Compared with conventional thermal modalities, namely radiofrequency (RF) and cryoablation, PFA offers shorter procedural times, comparable or superior efficacy for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and a markedly reduced risk of esophageal injury, persistent phrenic nerve palsy, and pulmonary vein stenosis, as demonstrated in numerous randomized controlled trials and large registries.However, PFA introduces unique limitations: hemolysis with potential renal injury, acute and delayed coronary vasospasm when ablating near the coronaries, the need for deep sedation or general anesthesia in most current systems, and uncertain durability of non-pulmonary vein lesions including the mitral and cavotricuspid isthmuses. Thermal ablation retains advantages for lesions beyond the pulmonary veins matter most particularly along the cavotricuspid and mitral isthmuses.Dual-energy catheters that combine RF and PFA, and sequential colocalized RF + PFA strategies, are now in clinical evaluation and may allow operators to create stacked lesions which may offer benefits for lesion durability. PFA is poised to become the dominant energy source for PVI, but in current clinical practice it is best viewed as complementary to, rather than fully replacing, thermal ablation.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Pulsed-field ablation, Pulmonary vein isolation, Radiofrequency ablation, Cryoablation, Dual-energy ablation

Introduction

Pulsed-field ablation (PFA) has recently emerged as a novel, non-thermal modality for the treatment of cardiac arrhythmias. This technique involves the delivery of high-intensity electrical fields to the myocardial tissue, resulting in disruption of cellular membrane integrity and the initiation of a cascade of biological events that ultimately lead to targeted cell death and subsequent fibrotic tissue replacement over time [1].

PFA uses nano-to microsecond pulse width and high voltage amplitude pulses to induce irreversible electroporation of cell membranes. Unlike conventional thermal ablation methods (radiofrequency and cryothermal), PFA achieves myocardial cell destruction without heating or freezing which confers a degree of tissue selectivity, preferentially ablating cardiac myocytes while sparing adjacent structures [2].

Although electroporation for cardiac tissue was first reported in the 1980s, the technology entered clinical use for the treatment of atrial fibrillation in the late 2010s, with first-in-human studies published in 2019 and 2020 [3]. Subsequent clinical trials and large multicenter registries have demonstrated that PFA efficacy is non-inferior to conventional thermal ablation techniques, with similar efficacy and safety endpoints, showing comparable rates of freedom from atrial fibrillation recurrence at one year [4]. Moreover, PFA has been associated with a reduced risk of collateral tissue injury, including esophageal damage, phrenic nerve palsy, pulmonary vein stenosis, and shorter procedural durations, resulting in reduced anesthesia time and decreased patient exposure to risks inherent to invasive ablation procedures [5].

However, newer concerns over PFA delivery have emerged such as hemolysis, acute renal injury, coronary spasm, and delayed coronary spasm occurring hours to days after the procedure which have been associated with fatal outcomes. Although the occurrence of these complications is rare, so too were some of the fatal complications of thermal energy, such as esophageal fistula.

This article discusses the factors supporting PFA as a potential preferred energy source for pulmonary vein isolation and explores the clinical scenarios in which conventional thermal ablation techniques may continue to play a role.

The biophysics

Mechanism of PFA

The basic mechanism of PFA is the delivery of nano-to microsecond-scale, high-voltage electrical pulses to cardiac tissue, which induces irreversible electroporation of cell membranes. This process forms nanoscale pores in the sarcolemma, leading to loss of membrane integrity, leakage of intracellular contents, and subsequent cell death by necrosis or apoptosis [6].

The ablation pulse waveform has multiple variable components that influence ablation efficacy. Although many systems employ similar pulse characteristics, each proprietary system has other non-generalizable features. For instance, the PFA generator incorporates high voltage capacitors capable of delivering precisely controlled voltages over very brief pulse durations in a repetitive fashion. Controllable parameters include the peak delivered voltage (usually more than 1500–2200 V baseline to peak), voltage waveform shape and tilt, pulse duration (nanoseconds to milliseconds), phasicity (monophasic or biphasic), interphase delay, interpulse delay, pulse number, and number of pulse trains (a group of pulses grouped together). In addition to that, the pulse form needs to be delivered through a catheter into the cardiac tissue. The shape of the catheter, size of the electrodes, number of electrodes, shorting of electrodes, and sequence of activation can all affect the electrical field and magnitude of ablation effect. Finally, tissue characteristics such as fiber orientation and scarring play a critical role in the electric field production and tissue effect [7]. Therefore, any combination of parameters associated with a specific catheter design may have its own intensity of effects and should be tested for both beneficial and unwanted outcomes [1].

To isolate pulmonary veins for ablation of atrial fibrillation (AF), multiple applications (typically 8-12) are required for each vein. The entire pulmonary vein isolation procedure with PFA is often completed in less than 60 min due to rapid lesion creation [6,8,9].

Another key factor determining creation of successful lesions with PFA is contact of the electrode to the cardiac tissue. Although PFA is a field and can create lesions up to 2–4 mm away from the tissue, these lesions will be sub-optimal. This is why for multipolar PFA catheters, procedure workflow emphasizes overlapping catheter positions and optimized contact using intracardiac echocardiography, mapping, impedance-based contact sensing, and fluoroscopy to ensure effective lesion creation without gaps [8]. Contact force is less relevant for optimal PFA delivery, particularly for large footprint catheters. Even point-by-point catheters may not create any additive effect of PFA beyond 10 g of contact force [10].

Although PFA creates lesions via a non-thermal ablation mechanism, the underlying physical process (i.e., high current delivered through a resistive load) induces a variable degree of tissue temperature increase. A study evaluating the temperature profile of focal point, monopolar biphasic PFA ablation demonstrated energy-dependent detectable temperature changes at the tissue surface as well as at 3 mm and 7 mm depths; however internal tissue temperatures did not exceed 2.8 °C above baseline. These findings reveal that the focal monopolar biphasic energy delivered by PFA technology retains a favorable thermal safety profile, where it does not produce a temperature rise that will damage vascular, neural and esophageal tissues near ablated myocardium [11]. Furthermore, the duration of heat exposure occurs in the order of milliseconds, which is insufficient to result in coagulative necrosis, allowing for sparing of the extracellular matrix. Should neural or esophageal tissue damage occur, the extracellular matrix of these tissues can be repopulated with progenitor cells [12]. However, poor design of electrodes and catheters can cause excessive heating—particularly at the edges of electrodes—which can cause char, blood boiling, coagulation, and potentially collateral tissue damage [13]. Therefore, it should not be assumed that every PFA formula associated with a particular catheter is non-thermal [14].

Current PFA systems typically achieve tissue ablation depths of 4–6 mm. This is supported by studies using standard biphasic, bipolar PFA configurations with delivery of high-voltage trains (1500–2000 V) [10]. Newer PFA systems, which utilize higher energy settings or modified electrode configurations, can achieve significantly greater tissue depths. Gasperetti et al. observed a progressive increase in the maximal lesion depth with an increase in the voltage used for lesion delivery with linear association (300–1200 V to produce 1–5 mm lesion depths) [15]. Furthermore, voltage amplitude is a critical determinant of thermal effects; current settings of 19, 22 and 25 A were evaluated with higher currents producing greater ablation depths while maintaining minimal thermal effects with temperature rises of 4.7 ± 1.5 °C over baseline at 25 A [11]. For instance, focal monopolar PFA systems with 25 A current have demonstrated lesion depths of 8.2 ± 1.6 mm in porcine ventricular tissue [16]. This contrasts with radiofrequency ablation, which at 30 W/30 s produces temperature spikes up to 70 °C at 3 mm tissue depth while not necessarily attaining a depth of 8 mm [11].

Although the PFA non-thermal mechanism seems to eliminate typical complications associated with thermal ablation, such as atrio-esophageal fistula, pulmonary vein stenosis, and persistent phrenic nerve palsy, there are few complications that have been reported exclusively with the use of PFA [5].

One concerning PFA-specific complication is hemolysis. This is caused by extraneous field being released into the blood stream resulting in electroporation of red cells [17]. When defined by any reduction in haptoglobin >10 mg/dL, hemolysis can be detected in approximately 95% of PFA cases overall, with 34% showing significant hemolysis (haptoglobin ≤25 mg/dL) and 13% showing severe hemolysis (haptoglobin ≤10 mg/dL) [18]. Most studies revealed that the level of hemolysis was directly proportional to the number of applications, with clinical manifestations, such as renal failure or hemoglobinuria, typically occurring in cases exceeding 60 applications with a pentaspline system and variable circular system. Newer catheter designs can reduce hemolysis by insulating the blood-facing surfaces or by reducing the size of the catheter tip itself [12,19].

Although the administration of a large volume of fluid post-ablation (2 L) seems to prevent kidney failure, this approach is primarily aimed at mitigating the consequences of hemolysis rather than directly preventing its occurrence. It will not eliminate sub-clinical renal injury and consequences associated with plasma free hemoglobin [14] such as platelet activation through oxidative stress and direct interaction with platelet surface receptors. Additionally, the depletion of nitric oxide by free hemoglobin removes an important endogenous platelet inhibitor, further promoting platelet activation creating a prothrombotic state but also predisposing to systemic vasospasm [20].

Another recognized complication of PFA is coronary artery spasm, particularly when ablation is performed near a coronary vessel, such as right coronary artery during cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) ablation or left circumflex artery when performing mitral isthmus (MI) ablation. While angiographic evidence of coronary vasospasm is frequently observed when PFA is delivered in close proximity to coronary arteries, particularly during CTI ablation, most episodes are transient and clinically silent. In contrast, clinically significant ischemic events attributable to coronary spasm appear to be rare, with reported incidence around 0.14% [21]. Mark TK Tam et al. published a study evaluating the changes in coronary artery after PFA for atrial flutter and interestingly, no patient developed severe spasm when treated parenterally with very high doses of nitroglycerin (NTG 1–3 mg IV bolus and repeat for additional lesion time), before applying electroporation along the CTI. Nevertheless, despite suppression of acute angiographic vasospasm with prophylactic nitroglycerin, follow-up imaging studies have reported modest reductions in coronary luminal area at 3 months. The clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain, as no consistent association with myocardial ischemia or adverse coronary events has yet been demonstrated [22]. Therefore, high doses of NTG do not seem to eliminate the long-term consequences of coronary vasospasm. Studies suggest that 6.5 mm is the minimal safe distance from coronaries to PFA application to prevent vasospasm [23], but the degree to which it occurs, and the minimal safe distance will likely end up being very system specific based on catheter size and energy parameters.

In addition to proximity associated spasm, rare cases of delayed coronary vasospasm occurring hours to days after PFA have been reported, including isolated cases associated with severe clinical consequences such as cardiac arrest [24]. However, the available evidence is limited to case reports and small series, and the true incidence and casual mechanisms remains incompletely defined. There are probably many contributors to this phenomenon including a genetic pre-disposition to spasm [25], modification of autonomic tone during ablation, and depletion of nitric oxide caused by hemolysis [26].

Thermal ablation

Among thermal ablation modalities, radiofrequency (RF) and cryoablation are the most well established and extensively validated techniques in clinical practice.

Radiofrequency

RF works by delivering alternating current at cycle lengths of 300–750 kHz. This current induces resistive heating of the adjacent myocardial tissue, resulting in thermal injury and coagulative necrosis when tissue temperatures reach approximately 50 °C. The ablation lesion is typically 5–6 mm in diameter and 3–4 mm deep with standard catheters, but larger lesions can be achieved using irrigated catheters, larger electrodes, or very long application times [27].

The duration of RF application for atrial tissue varies based on the power strategy employed. The maximum lesion size is typically achieved within 1 min, as the half-time lesion growth is approximately 5–15 s depending on power used [28]. Studies using 20–35 W commonly applied energy for approximately 30 s per location [29]. Shorter duration may be achieved with higher power settings. A large multicenter study demonstrated use of 45–50 W for 2–15 s per lesion and the Q-Efficiency trial examined very high power (90 W) short duration ablation with applications ≤4 s, both with low complications rates, demonstrating comparable safety profile to conventional ablation [30,31].

RF energy delivery may lead to excessive tissue heating which can cause steam pops, char formation, cardiac perforation, and stroke. The heat can also affect adjacent anatomical structures near the ablation site. In the context of atrial fibrillation ablation, where the primary objective is pulmonary vein isolation, rare but potentially severe complications may occur, including symptomatic esophageal injury (5–10%), phrenic nerve injury (0.4%), pulmonary vein stenosis (<1%) or atrial-esophageal fistula (0.1% to 0.25%) [4,14]. Technological advances such as open-irrigated catheters to create larger lesion depth by allowing sustained high-power delivery [32], contact force sensing, and local impedance monitoring, and ablation indices have improved lesion durability, procedural safety, and overall outcomes [33]. Consequently, radiofrequency ablation has long been the standard ablation modality.

Cryoablation

The biophysics of cryoablation delivers rapid cooling to produce cellular injury and cell death. During the freezing phase, extracellular ice crystal formation occurs first, creating hyperosmolar conditions that drive water out of cells, leading to cell dehydration and osmotic stress. As temperatures drop below −40 °C, intracellular ice crystals form, causing mechanical disruption of organelles and plasma membranes, resulting in irreversible cell damage and apoptosis. Following this cascade of events, the thawing phase ensues, during which ice crystal coalescence increases osmotic gradients, and rewarming leads to microvascular injury, endothelial damage, platelet aggregation, and microthrombus formation, ultimately resulting in ischemic tissue necrosis [34].

For balloon catheter cryoablation, a 3 to 4-min application time is the established standard, with most clinicians employing a “freeze-thaw-freeze” technique using at least one additional 4 min “bonus” cycle at successful locations to extend lesion size [35]. Repetitive freeze-thaw cycles significantly enhance cryoablation efficacy by increasing ablation zone size, improving cell death, and reducing the transition zone between viable and necrotic tissue. Adding a second freeze increases the median lesion width by 4 mm and depth by 3–5 mm in both healthy and scar tissue [36].

Electrophysiologically, transient conduction block and slowing of conduction velocity occur in tissue cooled from 37 to −20 °C, but permanent lesions form only in regions cooled below approximately −40 °C. These lesions are characterized by well-demarcated zones of fibrosis surrounded by areas of microvascular injury and apoptosis [37].

The most important and most frequent complication of cryoablation for atrial fibrillation ablation was phrenic nerve injury, notably more common with cryoablation than with radiofrequency [38], at a rate of approximately 1.5–3% [39]. This is likely due to the placement of the balloon device deeper into the right sided veins and extension of the freezing towards the phrenic nerve. Pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade rates are similar with a risk of 0.4–0.8%. Other complications may occur less frequently such as pulmonary vein stenosis (<0.5%), stroke or transient ischemic attack (<0.2%), and atrioesophageal fistula (<0.05%) [39,40]. Atrioesophageal fistula may be less common with cryoablation because of more shallow and uniform tissue depth (3–4 mm), and less likelihood of destroying the extracellular matrix of adjacent esophageal tissue.

Pulmonary vein isolation for atrial fibrillation ablation

In recent decades, atrial fibrillation ablation has been primarily performed using radiofrequency energy or cryoablation to achieve pulmonary vein isolation (PVI).

Secondarily, current evidence suggests that safety seems to be improved by PFA by reducing phrenic nerve and esophageal injury, although new but rare complications have arisen.

Remapping studies reported encouraging rates of PVI durability with PFA, such as IMPULSE, PEFCAT and PEFCAT II, initial first-in-human studies, showing that PVI durability improved progressively with successive waveform refinements achieving 84.1% of patients with durable PVI at 3 months remapping and their reassessment over one year follow-up with low rate of atrial arrhythmia recurrence [3,40]. Contemporary data from actual procedures, including findings from the MANIFEST REDO STUDY, have demonstrated pulmonary vein reconnection rates comparable to those observed with thermal ablation [41]. These outcomes may reflect factors such as inadequate lesions overlap, suboptimal catheter-tissue contact, and operators desire to complete procedures very quickly. Collectively these findings suggests that lesion durability remains highly dependent on catheter design, energy delivery characteristics and operator technique.

Registries have shown substantially reduced procedural times and low incidences of severe complications. The multicenter study MANIFEST-US evaluated the safety profile of the pentaspline PFA catheter on over 40,000 patients. PFA demonstrated no evidence of esophageal fistula or pulmonary vein stenosis. The major complication rate was 0.6%, mostly vascular adverse events and pericardial tamponade [5]. Comparing MANIFEST 17K, the European registry with 17,642 patients, to MANIFEST-US reveals improvement over time, with reductions in major (0.98% to 0.63%) and minor (3.2% to 2.1%) adverse events, likely reflecting learning and procedural refinements [42]. REAL-AF registry focusing on procedures performed with Varipulse (Biosense Webster) variable loop circular catheter demonstrated low incidence of acute procedural complications (0.4%) which compares favorably with previous pivotal trials like AdmIRE, with primary safety event rate of 2.9%, and inspIRE with no reports on primary adverse events. In terms of procedure times, all trials showed short total procedure time (between 42.4 and 97.8 min), a reasonable rate of zero-fluoroscopy ablation, and same-day discharge [[43], [44], [45]].

Randomized trials for paroxysmal AF have shown similar efficacy and safety between PFA and thermal ablation but consistently reduced procedural times. In the ADVENT trial, single-procedure success at one year was 73.3% for PFA and 71.3% for thermal ablation, with no statistically significant difference. In terms of safety, PFA was noninferior to conventional thermal ablation for the primary safety endpoint, with procedure related serious adverse events occurring in 2.1% of PFA patients vs. 1.5% of thermal ablation patients. Importantly, no esophageal complications, phrenic nerve injury, or pulmonary vein stenosis were observed in this trial, complications historically associated with thermal ablation [4]. The BEAT PAROX-AF trial similarly found no difference in single procedure success at 12 months between PFA (77.2%) and RF ablation (77.6%). The BEAT PAROX-AF trial also observed fewer serious adverse events with PFA (3.4%) compared to RF (7.6%) [46].

Complication rates for PFA versus cryoballoon ablation are also low [47]. In the SINGLE SHOT CHAMPION trial, PFA was noninferior to cryoballoon ablation with atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence occurring in 37.1% of PFA patients vs. 50.7% of cryoballoon patients. AF burden was similar in both groups. Furthermore, the composite safety endpoint (cardiac tamponade requiring pericardiocentesis, persistent phrenic nerve palsy >24 h, serious vascular complications requiring intervention, stroke or TIA, atrioesophageal fistula, or death) occurred in 1% of PFA patients vs. 1.9% of cryoballoon patients. The tissue-selective energy delivery and short application times of PFA may offer potential safety and efficacy benefits over cryoablation although some limitations have to be taken into consideration, including that only the pentaspline PFA catheter was used and follow-up was limited to one year [48]. Meta-analyses confirm comparable or slightly lower recurrence rates for PFA versus thermal modalities [49].

For persistent atrial fibrillation, early clinical studies indicate that PFA achieves pulmonary vein isolation efficiently and with a favorable safety profile, but large-scale randomized data are still emerging [2]. The ADVANTAGE AF Phase 2, which used continuous rhythm monitoring with cardiac monitors, found that after PFA (pulmonary vein isolation and posterior wall ablation), freedom from atrial arrhythmia recurrence at one year was 73.4% using traditional intermittent monitoring, and 52% using continuous monitoring for ≥30 s episodes. An atrial arrhythmia burden <0.1% and episode duration <1 h were associated with lowest healthcare use. Freedom from symptomatic recurrence were high (85.3%), and major adverse events were rare (2.4%) [50,51]. These outcomes are consistent with prior thermal ablation trials, such as PRECEPT, which reported similar symptomatic recurrence rates for persistent atrial fibrillation [50].

In persistent atrial fibrillation patients, adding linear ablation lesions (cavotricuspid isthmus, mitral line and roof or posterior wall) beyond pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) has shown inconsistent benefits (See Fig. 1). The SPHERE Per-AF trial evaluated a novel dual-energy lattice-tip system combining PFA and RF energy and comparing it to conventional RF ablation. This dual system enabled more frequent use of linear lesions and significantly reduced ablation and energy application times across all lesion types. While outcomes suggest a trend toward higher 12-month effectiveness when combining PVI with linear lesions versus PVI alone, particularly with the new technology, the differences were not statistically significant and subgroup sizes were small. Overall, the findings are exploratory, indicating potential advantages of advanced ablation strategies, yet supporting the need for further randomized trials to confirm their clinical benefit [52].

Major energy sources used for ablation of atrial fibrillation. Thermal modalities (radiofrequency and cryoablation) were the standard and are now being replaced by pulsed field ablation (PFA). PFA avoids some o the traditional complications of thermal ablation but introduces new problems. PFA is best for pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) but isthmus ablation may be more safely performed with thermal energy.

From a practical standpoint, adoption of PFA requires consideration of procedural workflow, anesthesia resources, and operator training. Compared with conventional RF ablation, PFA workflows are generally simplified because lesion delivery is rapid and less dependent on precise point-by-point catheter manipulation. Most commercially available systems use multipolar catheters capable of encircling the pulmonary veins with a limited number of applications, reducing left atrial dwell time and potentially improving laboratory throughput. The ADVENT trial and multiple meta-analyses report procedure times reduced by 40–45 min versus RF and 9–44 min versus cryoablation [4]. The EU-PORIA registry also reinforced the success rate combined with excellent safety and short procedure times with PFA ablation in real-word patients with atrial fibrillation [53].

Despite these workflow advantages, anesthesia requirements remain an important consideration. Current microsecond-based PFA systems frequently induce skeletal muscle contraction, diaphragmatic stimulation, cough, and patient discomfort during pulse delivery. Consequently, many centers perform PFA under deep sedation or general anesthesia, often with neuromuscular blockade. This may limit implementation in centers where anesthesia support is not routinely available [9].

However, newer catheter designs and waveform configurations have demonstrated the feasibility of conscious sedation in selected patients, suggesting that future generations of PFA systems may reduce procedural resource utilization while preserving efficacy and safety [40]. For instance, the VOLT-AF IDE trial demonstrated that conscious or deep sedation was feasible and associated with increased procedural efficiency compared to general anesthesia, and there was no difference in acute success between sedation and general anesthesia groups. This is a significant finding, as it suggests that the Volt PFA system catheter “balloon-in-basket” design, tissue contact stability, and waveform characteristics may mitigate the muscle contractions, cough and pain [14].

Successful implementation of PFA also requires dedicated operator training. Durable lesion formation remains dependent on appropriate catheter positioning, adequate tissue contact, sufficient lesion overlap, and familiarity with system-specific energy delivery characteristics. Early registry data suggest that procedural outcomes continue to improve with increasing operator experience and refinement of workflow protocols. Thus, while PFA offers important advantages over conventional thermal modalities, its long-term success in routine clinical practice will depend on continued refinement of procedural workflows and accumulation of operator experience.

Four PFA systems are now commercially available: FARAPULSE (Boston Scientific) pentaspline catheter, PulseSelect (Medtronic) over-the-wire circular catheter, VARIPULSE (Biosense Webster) mapping-integrated circular catheter, and Sphere-9 (Medtronic) lattice-tip focal catheter. The pentaspline catheter has shown shorter procedure time and has the most extensive clinical evidence with high efficiency rates. It consists of a bipolar biphasic catheter and has a configuration that ranges from a partially deployed catheter (“basket” configuration) to a fully deployed catheter (“flower” configuration). It is mainly used for PVI with efficacy at 1 year reported as 73.3% for paroxysmal AF and 73.4% in permanent AF, as previously mentioned. It can also be used concomitantly for PW as shown in the ADVANTAGE AF Phase 1 trial. Evaluations for pentaspline catheter efficacy in CTI ablation are currently ongoing. The tradeoff is that the procedures demonstrated higher fluoroscopy use and greater hemolysis compared to other systems [56].

Alternatively, the loop catheter consists of a bipolar, biphasic, fixed circular catheter (Medtronic PulseSelect™) or variable circular catheter (Biosense Webster Varipulse™). VARIPULSE has shorter fluoroscopy times with the AdmIRE trial demonstrating >25% of procedures performed with zero fluoroscopy, this system is particularly advantageous for operators who prioritize a mapping-guided workflow, complex PV anatomy or settings where radiation minimization matters. PulseSelect by Medtronic may be preferred when minimizing hemolysis is a priority, as patients with baseline renal impairment or anemia. The HEMO-PFA study showed that PulseSelect had the smallest haptoglobin decline among the three systems (FARAPULSE, VARIPULSE and PulseSelect) with PVI times comparable to VARIPULSE [54,56]. Both systems have well-established role for PVI, with a 1-year efficacy superior to >60% for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation as per the PULSED AF and INSPIRE trials [57]. Reports of successful use for non-PV targets are still emerging.

Finally, the Sphere-9 (Medtronic lattice tip) is the only system with focal PFA catheter, making it suited for point-by-point ablation beyond PVI, such as targeting specific triggers, linear lesions or redo procedures where discrete gaps need to be addressed. The first human study enrolled 178 patients, 70 persistent AF and 108 paroxysmal AF, with 77.9% and 78.3% freedom from arrhythmia at 12 months, respectively, with individualized lesion sets (anterior LA, posterior wall, roof, mitral line and CTI). It has also integrated high-density mapping capability that allows simultaneous mapping and ablation.

With the result of one-year outcomes of VOLT-AF trial, Volt system (Abbott) has secured FDA approval in late 2025 complementing the CE Mark awarded early that year. It consists of a ballon-in-basket PFA ablation system designed to address the limitations of first generation PFA including tissue contact, hemolysis and mapping integration. First in-human studies have shown promising results with acute PVI close to 100%, and the 12 month freedom from documented atrial arrhythmia in 84.2% (PAF) and 67.8% (PeAF) and low rates of complications with no clinically relevant hemolysis or kidney injury (61).

Therefore, the selection of the PFA system should consider the patient's clinical profile, the operator's experience, as well as the team's workflow and the type of anesthesia available. Each system possesses unique characteristics, with distinct advantages and limitations that should be evaluated individually according to the specific needs of the patient and procedure.

Combined thermal and PFA usage

Although PF represents a major advance in PVI, its current limitations with non-PV targets accentuate the persistent need for RF energy, prompting the development of dual-energy systems capable of delivering both modalities through the same catheter. Such advancement would bring a new level of flexibility to physicians, allowing them to customize their approach during ablations by tailoring to anatomical obstacles and tissue specifics.

To address this, dual-energy catheters are currently being developed and studied. The Dual Energy THERMOCOOL SMARTTOUCH™ SF Catheter, conceived by Johnson and Johnson, has been used in the SmartfIRE trial, a prospective multicenter European study addressing patients with drug-refractory paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. The 12-month follow-up results, published in August 2025, have shown freedom from symptomatic and asymptomatic arrhythmia in 71.5% of patients [54]. The Sphere-9 Catheter, developed by Medtronic, was studied in the SPHERE Per-AF trial, a 2024 randomized, single-blinded, non-inferiority trial evaluating only a single energy approach (PF) compared to dual-energy approach (PF and RF) in patients with persistent atrial fibrillation [52]. The FOCALFLEX and FLEXPULSE IDE trials both aim to evaluate the Tactiflex Duo Ablation Catheter, developed by Abbott, with an estimated completion of results planned respectively for March 2026 and April 2026.

The advent of dual-energy catheters seeks to integrate the versatility of RF with the safety profile of PF, with the potential of improving procedural success in complex ablation cases. In particular, dual energy catheters may have benefits in creating lesions along common flutter isthmuses.

Common isthmuses

The mitral and cavotricuspid isthmuses are common adjuvants to PVI for AF ablation (See Fig. 1). Both structures can be challenging to block because of tissue thickness. They are also both near to the coronary arteries, introducing a risk of coronary spasm or injury.

Cavotricuspid isthmus

Cavotricuspid isthmus (CVI) ablation is often performed during PVI to treat coexisting typical atrial flutter. Challenges during CTI ablation are associated with the thickness of the muscular tissue and the irregularity of the surface of the isthmus creating “hills and valleys”, which can be tough to ablate. The CTI is also very close to the distal RCA. Because ablation lesions in this region may be delivered near epicardial coronary vessels, thermal ablation has been associated with coronary artery injury; however, such complications are uncommon and typically present as subclinical coronary stenosis.

On the other hand, it has been reported that PFA can induce coronary artery vasospasm, seen during CTI ablation due to its anatomical proximity to the coronaries. CTI ablation studies have demonstrated right coronary artery spasm in up to 100% of patients, importantly, these observations are largely based on systematic angiographic assessment, and the majority of spasm were transient and not associated with clinical ischemia. Administration of prophylactic nitroglycerin, either intracoronary or intravenous, effectively prevented severe spasm [21]. Management typically involves substantial or continuous nitroglycerin administration, sometimes requiring vasopressor support to maintain hemodynamic stability. Nonetheless, the risk of coronary spasm is not entirely mitigated.

Emerging clinical evidence from the ADVANTAGE study regarding efficacy of PFA at the CTI, demonstrated acute bidirectional block in 98.6% of cases. Freedom from typical atrial flutter was seen in 96.4% of patients at the 1-year mark [51]. Despite encouraging results, robust evidence for consistent CTI block across current PFA systems is lacking, mainly because most commercially available catheters are optimized for PVI rather than for ablation along the CTI. A dual-energy approach, in which RF is used for CTI ablation, therefore remains advantageous due to its established efficacy and safety throughout the years.

Mitral isthmus

Mitral isthmus ablation is not uncommonly used for management of perimitral atrial flutter. Using an endocardial approach with a RF catheter, an ablation line is forged from the left inferior pulmonary vein to the lateral mitral annulus. However, large myocardial thickness variability along the isthmus introduces difficulty when creating a durable block across the isthmus. Epicardial blood vessels, consisting of the left circumflex artery and the coronary sinus, may also cause convective cooling of the subepicardial muscle fibers limiting ablation depth with RF. Lastly, epicardial connections, such as the ligament of Marshall, containing the vein of Marshall and the Marshall bundle, can prevent completion of a conduction block during mitral isthmus ablation.

PFA has been attempted for mitral isthmus block. Although acute success rates can be high, long-term durability appears low, with sustained block observed in fewer than 6% of cases [55]. Coronary vasospasm involving the circumflex artery has been reported in up to 41% of patients undergoing PFA, whereas such events have not been observed with radiofrequency (RF) ablation. The use of high or continuous doses of prophylactic intracoronary or intravenous nitrates may reduce the risk of coronary spasm, but this comes at the cost of hypotension and may not completely eliminate the issue.

Because of these procedural challenges, around 30% of cases require a hybrid approach, combining both an endocardial and epicardial approach to achieve complete line block. PFA can be used in the portions of the isthmus that are distant from the coronary artery (the proximal portion of the mitral isthmus line from the left inferior vein towards the annulus) while RF is used in the regions closer to the annulus and hence, the circumflex artery.

Sequential RF and PFA lesions

A sequential approach combining radiofrequency (RF) and pulsed field (PF) energy has been proposed [56]. Greater lesion depth can be achieved, particularly when RF is delivered over a short interval. The effectiveness of this method relies on the choice of catheter and the specifics of the PFA formula and the RF duration. In one study (Verma et al.), stacked applications of PFA on RF or vice versa created deeper lesions than either energy alone [56]. The RF duration used in this study was short, and the application of PFA was done immediately afterwards. However, there are studies from other catheters which have failed to show the same effectiveness of stacking [57].

Conclusions

Overall, pulsed field ablation (PFA) has demonstrated non-inferior efficacy, and promising safety outcomes, together with consistently shorter procedural times compared with conventional thermal ablation for pulmonary vein isolation. However, current evidence remains highly dependent on catheter design, waveform characteristics, and operator experience, limiting the generalizability of results across all PFA platforms. Furthermore, data regarding long-term lesion durability and efficacy for non-pulmonary vein targets remain limited and continue to evolve.
