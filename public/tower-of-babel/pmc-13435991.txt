Residency taught me medicine: practice taught me everything else
Authors: Dominika Jegen
Published: 2026

10 years ago, I completed residency training believing I was finally prepared for independent practice.

I knew how to evaluate chest pain, manage hypertension, suture a laceration and navigate countless other clinical scenarios. Residency had provided the medical knowledge and procedural skills necessary to begin practice. Yet, as many physicians discover, competence at graduation is not the endpoint of professional development but the beginning of it.

Recent literature recognises the transition from residency to independent practice as a distinct developmental stage characterised by increasing autonomy, evolving professional identity, and challenges extending beyond clinical competence to communication, practice management and uncertainty.1 2 Professional identity formation continues long after formal training concludes, shaped by clinical experience, reflection, role modelling and the communities in which physicians practice.3

The transition from residency to practice revealed an important distinction. Residency taught me medicine. Practice taught me everything else.

Although many publications have examined continuity of care, resilience, physician well-being, communication and professional identity individually, fewer have considered how these lessons converge during the first decade of independent family medicine practice. This perspective synthesises those experiences into a practical framework for understanding the transition from residency to mature clinical practice, emphasising lessons that are difficult to teach through formal curricula but emerge through longitudinal patient relationships, reflection and lived experience.

Many of the lessons that have shaped my first decade as a family physician did not come from textbooks, board examinations or clinical guidelines. They emerged through relationships with patients, conversations with colleagues, interactions with learners, experiences outside medicine and the gradual accumulation of perspective that only time can provide.

Relationships as the foundation of care

Continuity of care is widely recognised as one of the defining characteristics of family medicine.4 5 During training, continuity was often discussed in operational terms: patient panels, scheduling systems and longitudinal care models. With experience, I came to appreciate continuity differently.

Several years into practice, I relocated to a city an hour away. A small number of patients chose to transfer their care to my new clinic. What struck me was not their willingness to travel, but how little needed to change once they arrived. Their histories, diagnoses and medications were familiar. More importantly, the trust we had built over years of caring for one another remained intact. Care continued seamlessly because the relationship had already been established. They did not need to retell their stories from the beginning, and no physician needed months or years to reconstruct the context behind every decision.

That experience fundamentally changed how I viewed family medicine. Relationships are not simply a component of care. They are often the mechanism through which care becomes effective. Relationship-centred care recognises that healing occurs within the context of human connections and that meaningful relationships influence both patient and physician experiences.6

Trust develops gradually through repeated interactions. It is reinforced when physicians listen carefully, remember details that matter and follow through on commitments. Contemporary evidence continues to support the value of personal continuity in primary care, including associations with improved outcomes, lower utilisation and reduced costs.4 5

Over time, I learnt that reliability is itself a therapeutic intervention. Patients may not remember every recommendation, but they remember whether their physician was present, attentive and trustworthy. Their trust not only improved their experience of care; it also made me a better physician for them.

Listening beyond the presenting complaint

Early in practice, I often viewed my role primarily as solving problems. Over time, I realised that understanding the problem frequently requires listening longer than expected.

Patients rarely arrive as textbook cases. A visit scheduled for hypertension may become a conversation about caregiving stress. A discussion of chronic pain may reveal grief, loneliness, anxiety, financial hardship or family conflict. The presenting complaint often represents only part of the story.

Listening is sometimes described as a soft skill. In reality, it is one of the most important clinical skills physicians possess in any discipline. Relationship-centred care recognises the importance of communication, empathy and understanding patients’ perspectives.6

As my career progressed, I became less focused on directing conversations and more attentive to understanding the concerns underlying them. Some of the most meaningful interventions I have made began not with a diagnosis or prescription but with sufficient time and attention for patients to explain what truly worried them.

I recall a young man who presented with persistent back pain several weeks after a snowmobiling accident. As the conversation continued, he casually mentioned numbness below his abdomen. Further questioning uncovered additional neurologic symptoms that had seemed unrelated to him. An urgent MRI ultimately revealed spinal lymphoma. The diagnosis emerged not from a dramatic presentation but from taking the time to listen beyond the initial complaint.

With increasing years in practice, I have come to appreciate that listening is not separate from diagnosis; in many cases, it is the diagnostic process itself.

Curiosity, humility and lifelong learning

One of the most liberating lessons of practice has been recognising that expertise does not require possessing every answer.

Medicine is too complex and evolves too rapidly for any physician to master completely. Professional growth depends less on certainty than on maintaining intellectual curiosity and a commitment to lifelong learning. The concept of adaptive expertise emphasises not only efficiency in familiar clinical situations but also the capacity to adapt, reflect and learn in the face of complexity and uncertainty.7

Teaching students and residents has reinforced this lesson repeatedly. Learners ask questions that challenge assumptions, expose knowledge gaps and encourage reflection. I still remember a resident asking why we routinely approached a common clinical problem in a particular way. My initial answer was little more than habit and experience. Revisiting the evidence afterward led me to modify aspects of my own practice. Moments like these remind me that teaching is often reciprocal. Some of the most important lessons of my career have come from learners willing to ask simple questions that challenged comfortable assumptions.

Curiosity extends beyond clinical knowledge. Experiences outside medicine have often strengthened my ability to connect with patients. Travel, reading, teaching and community engagement have broadened my understanding of perspectives different from my own.

I recall caring for a patient who had recently immigrated from a country I had visited years earlier. A brief conversation about a place we both knew transformed the encounter. The discussion was not medically relevant, yet it fostered trust and connection in a way no clinical question could. We discussed local food, island life and the ocean. That encounter reminded me that genuine curiosity often creates connection more effectively than any communication technique.

Such experiences reinforced the importance of cultural humility. Cultural humility emphasises self-reflection, openness, and a lifelong commitment to understanding patients’ experiences and perspectives.8 Understanding patients requires more than understanding disease; it requires genuine curiosity about the lives they lead.

Humility, uncertainty and resilience

My understanding of resilience evolved substantially during the transition from training to independent practice.

Early in my career, I viewed resilience primarily as the ability to withstand adversity. Experience led me to a different understanding. Professional growth often occurs through challenges rather than in their absence. Difficult encounters, administrative burdens, unexpected setbacks and periods of self-doubt are common features of medical practice.

At the same time, practice taught me humility. Residency builds confidence, as it should, but experience teaches how often certainty must coexist with doubt. Even after years in practice, clinical situations continue to challenge assumptions and expose limitations in knowledge and judgement.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, I volunteered at a tertiary care centre in New York City. I cared for a patient admitted to a COVID-19 unit whose clinical deterioration appeared most consistent with septic shock, and I initiated empiric antibiotic therapy accordingly before the work-up even began. Further evaluation later revealed a perforated stomach requiring urgent surgery. The experience reinforced a lesson that medicine continues to teach repeatedly: even thoughtful clinical reasoning can be incomplete, and maintaining diagnostic humility remains essential throughout a career.

Looking back, some of the most important lessons of practice emerged not during periods of confidence but during periods of uncertainty. Eventually, I came to appreciate that most challenges are temporary, even when they feel overwhelming in the moment. More importantly, I learnt that resilience is not solely an individual attribute. Supportive colleagues, mentors, friends and family members play essential roles in helping physicians navigate difficult periods.

Burnout and diminished well-being remain significant concerns across medicine. Contemporary literature emphasises the importance of both individual and organisational approaches to supporting physician well-being and sustaining careers in medicine.9 Reflection, connection and perspective often prove more valuable than endurance alone.

Resilience, I came to realise, is not the absence of mistakes, uncertainty, or difficult experiences. It is the willingness to continue learning from them.

Taken together, these experiences reshaped my understanding of what it means to practice medicine—not simply as a technical profession, but as a lifelong process of learning, growth and human connection.

Conclusions

Reflecting on my first decade in practice, I have come to appreciate that becoming a physician and becoming a person are deeply intertwined journeys.

Residency taught me how to diagnose disease, interpret evidence and manage illness. Practice taught me how to build relationships, listen carefully, remain curious and continue growing throughout a career.

The longer I practice, the less I define expertise by the ability to provide immediate answers and the more I define it by the ability to remain curious, reflective and willing to learn.

These reflections also have implications beyond individual practice. Residency programmes may benefit from placing greater emphasis on longitudinal mentorship, reflective practice, communication, diagnostic humility and preparing trainees for the relational and emotional dimensions of independent practice. Transition-to-practice initiatives and faculty development programmes may likewise help early-career physicians navigate uncertainty, strengthen professional identity formation, and cultivate resilience through structured mentorship and reflection, consistent with emerging literature emphasising structured support for professional identity formation and the transition to independent practice among early-career physicians.1–3 10

Residency taught me medicine.

Practice taught me everything else.
