Biomechanical rational for development of cervical kyphosis deformity: a finite element analysis
Authors: Fangchen Jin, Jiali Li, Zixuan Liu, Baoge Liu, Zhi Yang, Juying Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAlterations in cervical curvature can redistribute stress and strain among intervertebral discs (IVDs), facet joints, and neck muscles, potentially initiating degenerative changes that contribute to the development of cervical kyphosis. This study aims to investigate the biomechanical effects of cervical kyphotic deformity, with a particular focus on the stress and strain variations in facet joints and IVDs, to elucidate the underlying mechanical mechanisms of cervical kyphosis.MethodsA finite element (FE) model of the lordotic cervical C2–C7 motion segment was constructed based on computed tomography (CT) images of a healthy adult. The model was modified to represent three distinct cervical spine alignments: lordotic cervical spine, straightened alignments, and cervical kyphosis. Under physiological loadings conditions, stress and strain were analyzed across these states to assess their biomechanical impact on the facet joints and IVDs.ResultIn a lordotic cervical spine, the highest stress and strain were concentrated at the C3-C4, C4-C5, and C5-C6 facet joints. In contrast, under cervical kyphosis, the C2-C3 and C6-C7 segments demonstrated a substantial increase in stress and strain. Facet joint stresses increased by up to 71.10-fold at C2–C3 and 356.12-fold at C6–C7 compared with the lordotic model. Furthermore, kyphotic alignment elevated IVD stress, which was consistently concentrated in the anterior region of the C2-C3 disc across all loading conditions.ConclusionThe C2-C3 and C6-C7 segments are subjected to increased mechanical loads in straightened cervical alignment and kyphotic deformity, thereby increasing their susceptibility to facet joint degeneration. Furthermore, cervical kyphosis is associated with accelerated disc degeneration, with stress concentration predominantly occurring in the anterior regions of the discs. These findings provide biomechanical insights into the progression of cervical spinal deformities and may help guide clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies.Keywords: Cervical spine, Kyphosis, Facet joint, Intervertebral disc, Finite element analysis

Introduction

Due to the increasing prevalence of electronic device use (reported in > 70% of adults) [1], prolonged smartphone or computer use has become common, placing continuous mechanical loads on the cervical spine and resulting in forward head posture (FHP). When the skull’s center of gravity shifts anteriorly, it moves in front of the vertebral column, increasing cervical flexion torque and thereby elevating the risk of cervical kyphosis [2]. Cervical kyphosis is characterized by sagittal misalignment of the cervical spine [3, 4], which increases mechanical stress on the IVDs and facet joints, accelerating the progression of degenerative spinal diseases. Patients with cervical sagittal imbalance, such as those with cervical kyphosis, are more likely to experience axial neck pain (ANP).

The maintenance of lordotic cervical curvature is influenced by various factors, including the elasticity of the muscles, ligaments, and discs, vertebral morphology, and the alignment of facet joints. In the lordotic cervical spine, approximately 36% of the axial load is borne by the vertebral body and discs, while 32% is supported by the facet joints, and the remaining load is distributed across the posterior ligamentous complex and cervical musculature, which collectively contribute to spinal stabilization [5]. Previous studies [6, 7] have demonstrated that lordotic alignment is associated with graeater cervical range of motion (ROM) and reduced disc pressure compared with non-lordotic alignment. In contrast, cervical kyphosis alters movement patterns and joint loading, leading to progressive degenerative of cervical structures [8, 9]. Sagittal misalignment also increases mechanical loading on the IVDs, potentially triggering inflammation and disc degeneration [10–12]. It may contribute to cervical instability and exacerbate mechanical stress on intervertebral discs, thereby accelerating degeneration and disc height loss in combination with other factors. Over time, cervical lordosis may become straightened or even converted into a kyphotic deformity [13]. Therefore, several animal models have been developed to investigate the biomechanical and pathological mechanisms of cervical kyphosis. Lee et al. [14] used cats and performed multilevel laminectomy to induce kyphotic deformity. Fields et al. [15] established a kyphosis model in white rabbits through laminectomy combined with spinal canal enlargement. Chen et al. [16] subjected rabbits to forward acceleration to establish a kyphotic curvature. However, marked anatomical and biomechanical differences between animal and human cervical pines limit the applicability of these models. Therefore, conclusion drawn from animal studies may not be directly translatable to humans [17]. Biomechanical analysis of the human cervical spine provides quantitative insight into spinal disorders and supports the development of treatment strategies [18]. However, conducting standalone biomechanical experiments on the cervical spine is costly and often infeasible in humans due to ethical and technical constraints. The FE method offers a well-validated computational approach to overcome these limitations. At present, FE models have been established for various cervical spine segments, enabling simulation based biomechanical analysis. Manickam et al. [18] developed a FE model of C3-C6 motion segments and calculated stress distributions in the C3-C4, C4-C5, and C5-C6 IVDs under flexion, extension, lateral bending and axial rotation loads. Cai et al. [19] established a three-dimensional nonlinear FE model of the C3-C7 cervical spine to evaluate the effects of different compressive loads on range of motion (ROM), joint forces, and intervertebral disc pressure (IDP). In addition, many studies have used FE models to investigate cervical kyphosis deformities caused by various pathogenic factors. Wei [20] first established a FE model of straightened cervical spine based on the lordotic C1-C7 motion segment to investigate how curvature affected on the biomechanics of the facet joints. Sang [21] constructed a C3-C7 FE model incorporating six morphological stages of progressive IVD degeneration to analyze the impact on the motion path of instantaneous center during flexion and extension. Rahman et al. [22] modified a lordotic C0-C7 FE model to explore the biomechanical impact of C5-C6 disc degeneration on the lower cervical spine. John et al. [23] examined how variations in ligament mechanical property influence cervical morphology, particularly its sagittal alignment. Focusing on cervical kyphosis secondary to atlantoaxial instability, Li et al. [24] developed a C0–C7 FE model to simulated six surgical fixation strategies evaluate their biomechanical performance. In terms of iatrogenic factors, Rahman et al. [25] analyzed the biomechanical consequences of two-level cervical disc replacement, while Ke et al. [26] developed a FE model simulating foraminal decompression and discectomy to investigate the etiological mechanisms of post-surgical cervical kyphosis.

Collectively, these studies have primarily focused on changes in cervical shape and curvature, as well as the interactions between IVDs, ligaments, and cervical muscles. However, only a limited number of investigations [20, 21] have investigated the biomechanical role of the facet joints in cervical deformity. This study aims to establish FE models of the C2–C7 segments in three cervical states: lordotic, straightened, and kyphotic. Through simulation, we evaluate the stress and strain distribution associated with cervical kyphotic deformity, focusing on its impact on facet joints and IVDs. Furthermore, we investigate the biomechanical factors contributing to structural alterations and functional impairment, thereby advancing the understanding of the mechanical mechanisms underlying cervical kyphosis. The findings of this research provides a scientific foundation for clinical diagnosis and treatment, with the potential to improve patient assessment and therapeutic planning.

Materials and methods

Development of geometric model

A 35-year-old healthy female volunteer (approved by Ethics Committee KY2020-073–02 of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University) with no history of cervical-related disease was selected. Axial CT scans of the C2-C7 motion segment were obtained using Toshiba 16-slice spiral CT Aquilion16 scanner, producing cross-sectional DICOM images with a slice thickness of 0.6 mm. The CT data were imported into Mimics 15.0 software for the reconstruction of a three-dimensional model of the C2 – C7 vertebrae. A predefined bone threshold range (226 to 3071HU) was applied to segment bone tissues. The Edit Mask tool was used to manually remove irrelevant pixels, and the C2-C7 vertebrae were isolated. The Region Growing tool further refined the segmentation to extract individual vertebral bodies. The initial 3D models were generated using the “Calculate 3D” function, smoothed, and then exported.

The preliminary models were imported into Geomagic Warp 2021 for further refinement. Within Geomagic, the mesh was smoothed using Relaxation tool, and defects such as self-intersections, sharp edges, and spikes were corrected using the Mesh Doctor function. The model surface was then converted from triangular meshes to NURBS surfaces to reduce mesh complexity.

The processed surface model was subsequently imported into ANSYS Workbench. In SpaceClaim, IVDs were modeled based on the adjacent vertebral endplates, following anatomical references. Joint structures, including the articular surfaces, joint capsules, and joint cavities, were also constructed to simulate soft tissue anatomy.

Development of lordotic cervical vertebra C2-C7 FE model

In the Model interface, springs were used to simulate the ligament, and the anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL), posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL), intertransverse ligament (IL), ligamentum flavum (LF), and capsular ligament (CL) were incorporated. Ligaments, as fibrous tissues, can only withstand tensile loads when stretched. Material properties were assigned to bone tissue, articular cartilage, ligaments, and IVD, as shown in Table 1 [27–29]. Coincident nodes were introduced at contact interfaces. The tetrahedral meshing method was used to mesh the model, with different minimum element sizes set for different regions: 15 mm for vertebrae, 4 mm for IVDs, and 2 mm for facet joints. Ten facet joints were established between adjacent vertebrae from C2 to C7. The final model consists of 155,720 units and 92,593 nodes.

Simulation of C2-C7 cervical deformity

The Cobb angle for a lordotic cervical spine is 25.5° [30]. Based on the geometric model of the lordotic C2-C7 cervical spine, relative positions and angular orientations of each vertebral body were adjusted to simulate the straightened (Cobb angle of 3°) and kyphotic (Cobb angle of −8°) alignments. The change in curvature was evenly distributed Cervical sagittal misalignment across the C2–C7 motion segment, rather than being localized to a single region. This was achieved by incrementally rotating and translating each vertebra to achieve the target Cobb angle while maintaining anatomical continuity and realistic intervertebral spacing. Corresponding morphological adjustments were also made to the intervertebral discs and facet joints to preserve biomechanical plausibility under physiological loading. All other model components—including material properties, meshing strategies, and ligament configurations—remained consistent with the validated lordotic model, ensuring that curvature was the only variable under investigation.

The straightened model consisted of 55,244 elements and 28,265 nodes, and the kyphotic model comprised 157,739 elements and 93,901 nodes. Therefore, FE models were established for two deformity states: cervical straightened and cervical kyphosis. Material properties

The material properties for the cortical bone, trabecular bone, intervertebral discs (IVDs), facet joints, and ligaments are listed in Table 1. The vertebral bodies, composed of cortical and cancellous bone, were modeled using tetrahedral elements and simplified as continuous, homogeneous, and isotropic linear elastic materials. The IVD consists of the annulus fibrosus (AF) and the nucleus pulposus (NP). The AF was modeled using tetrahedral shell elements, while the NP, which is gelatinous and containing 70–90% water, was represented as a linear viscoelastic material. Ligaments, including the anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL), posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL), intertransverse ligament (IL), ligamentum flavum (LF), and capsular ligament (CL), which can only withstand tensile loading, were simulated using spring elements [31, 32].

Boundary conditions and loading patterns

To accurately simulate the primary movements of the cervical spine, the model settings were aligned as closely as possible with experimental studies that evaluated cervical range of motion (ROM). For boundary conditions, the inferior surface of C7 and facet joints were fully constrained in all directions. A downward force of 40 N was applied on the superior surface of C2 to simulate the weight of the head, and a moment of 1 N · m was applied to simulate physiological loads including flexion and extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation.

Results and analysis

Model validation

The lordotic C2-C7 model developed in this study was validated by comparison with previously published in vitro experimental data. The ROM of the C2-C7 motion segment is presented in Fig. 2 and compared with the results of Panjabi et al. [33]. Axial rotation and lateral bending values represent the sum of left and right ROMs. The maximum ROM was observed at C3–C4 segment under flexion and lateral bending loads, and at C5–C6 segment under extension loads. The ROM values for flexion and extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation fell within the range reported by Panjabi et al. [33]. These results indicate that the lordotic C2-C7 model is consistent with in vitro findings and can reliably simulate cervical spine motion. Therefore, the three-dimensional FE model developed in this study is capable of accurately representing the biomechanics of the C2-C7 cervical segment.

Maximum stress and strain in the facet joint under different loads

Under flexion loading, as shown in Figs. 3 and 4, the maximum stress and strain were observed on the at the C3-C4, C4-C5, and C5-C6 facet joints segments in the lordotic model. In contrast, both stress and strain at C4-C5 decreased in straightened and kyphotic models. In the kyphotic model, the stress and strain at C3-C4 and C5-C6 showed a relative reduction, whereas they exhibited a pronounced increase at C2-C3 and C6-C7. The maximum facet joint stresses at C2–C3 and C6–C7 increased by up to 71.10- and 356.12-fold, respectively, compared with the lordotic mode.

Under extension loading, the C3–C4 facet joints exhibited the highest stress and strain across all models. In the straightened and kyphotic models, stress values at the upper (C2–C3) and lower (C6–C7) segments increased markedly. Specifically, in the kyphotic model, the maximum stress at C2–C3 increased to 2.7679 MPa 71.6 times higher than in the lordotic model—while the C6-C7 stress increased by 356.1-fold to 2.4882 MPa. Although absolute stress values remain within physiological thresholds, the relative increase suggests a substantial shift in load-bearing, which may predispose the C6–C7 segment to accelerated degeneration in kyphotic alignment.

Under lateral bending loading, C3–C4 and C4–C5 facet joints exhibited the highest stress in the lordotic model. In both deformity models, stress and strain at these segments remained relatively stable, whereas segments such as C2–C3, C5–C6, and C6–C7 showed marked increases. In the kyphotic model, C2–C3 exhibited the highest values, with 132.22 -fold and 128.85 -fold increases in stress and strain, respectively, compared to the lordotic model.

During axial rotation, as shown in Figs. 3 and 4, the C3-C4 facet joint exhibited the highest stress and strain in the lordotic model. These values decreased in the straightened model but remained elevated in the kyphotic model. Overall, cervical deformities resulted in significant increases in facet joint stress and strain, especially at C2-C3, C5–C6 and C6-C7.

These findings consistently demonstrate that cervical deformities shift the regions of maximal mechanical stress and strain from the mid-cervical facet joints (C3–C6) to the upper (C2–C3) and lower (C6–C7) segments (Fig. 5), indicating a redistribution of mechanical loads that may underlie the clinical pattern of segmental degeneration observed in non-lordotic cervical spines.

Maximum stress and strain in the cervical disc under different loads

As shown in Figs. 6, 7 and 8, under flexion loading, the highest IVD stress in the kyphotic model was observed at the C3-C4 segment. Compared with the lordotic model, the maximum strain at the C2–C3 and C6–C7 segments increased substantially in both the straightened and kyphotic models (Fig. 7), with the greatest strain observed at the C6–C7 in the kyphotic model. Overall, relative to the lordotic curvature, both cervical straightening and kyphosis produced higher IVD stress across all segments, with fold increases of up to 9.10 at C2–C3 and 39.60 at C6–C7. The stress distribution maps (Fig. 8) further demonstrated that, under flexion loading, stress was primarily concentrated in the anterior region of the IVDs. Furthermore, as cervical curvature shifted from lordotic to kyphotic, the anterior–posterior stress difference progressively increased, suggesting a redistribution of load toward the anterior disc regions.

Under extension loading, the maximum stress in the lordotic model remains localized at the C3-C4 IVD, distribution observed under flexion loading, similar to the distribution observed under flexion loading. In both the straightened and kyphotic models, the C3-C4 segment continues to exhibit the highest stress. However, cervical kyphosis results in a substantial increase in both stress and strain across all segments, indicating a more globally elevated loading condition.

Under lateral bending loading, the straightened cervical spine model exhibit decreased strain at the C4-C5 and C5-C6 IVDs compared to the lordotic model, while strain increases at the other segments. As the curvature progresses from the straightened to the kyphotic model, strain increased across all segments except for C4-C5, where a reduction was observed. The greatest increase occurred at the C6-C7 segment, where strain rose from 0.0048 mm/mm to 0.14158 mm/mm (approximately a 29.00-fold increase), making it the segment with the highest strain in the kyphotic model. The stress distribution (Fig. 8) exhibited a similar trend, with stress values increasing progressively as cervical curvature deformity advanced. Furthermore, the segment with the highest stress shifts from C5-C6 in the lordotic model to C3-C4 in the kyphotic model, indicating a redistribution of mechanical load under pathological curvature.

Under axial rotational loading, the maximum IVDs stress shifts from the C5-C6 segment to the C3-C4 segment in the kyphotic model (Fig. 6), with an overall increase in magnitude. In terms of strain, the lordotic model shows the highest value at the C5-C6 IVD. When the cervical spine is straightened, the maximum strain at C5-C6 decreases, whereas it increases in all other segments. In the kyphotic model, strain increases across all IVDs compared to the lordotic and straightened models.

Discussion

Construction of cervical spine model

In recent years, FE method has been widely applied in numerous biomechanical studies of cervical spine injuries [34, 35], enabling simulations that cannot be easily performed in vivo or in cadaveric models. CT-based modeling has become a predominant approach for such simulation [36]. In this study, a validated FE methodology, reconstructed from high-resolution CT data was developed to simulate cervical spine biomechanics under pathological curvature alterations. Compared to animal models [20], our human-derived model demonstrated ROM values within the range reported by Panjabi et al. [33] (Fig. 2), thereby minimizing interspecies variability. Based on the lordotic FE model, two additional models representing straightened cervical spine and kyphosis models were developed to comparatively assess the impact of cervical deformities on the facet joints and IVDs.

Cervical lordosis is typically lost progressively. Patient-specific models based on CT data can realistically capture anatomical variations across different deformity stages. However, a key limitation lies in the inability to standardize material properties across subject. For example, age-related degeneration may cause considerable variation in tissue properties between patients with differing curvatures. Such variability confounds the isolation of curvature-specific effects on facet joint and IVD biomechanics.

To address this issue, the present study first develops a lordotic cervical spine model. The FE models of the straightened cervical spine and kyphosis are then developed by directly modifying vertebral alignment and IVD height. This approach, based on the controlled-variable method, isolates changes in cervical curvature and geometric structure, enabling a more precise simulation of the biomechanical alterations in IVDs and facet joints during the progressive loss of cervical lordosis.

In this study, ligaments were represented as spring elements. Ligament stiffness is known to be affected by spinal conditions, external mechanical factors, and inter-individual variability. Mattucci et al. [31] reported stiffness values that varied according to these factors and showed numerical differences compared with those reported by Hua et al. [37]. To ensure consistency and physiological relevance, the stiffness properties of the ALL, PLL, LF, and CL were adopted from Mattucci et al. [31], after comparison with values documented in other literature [6, 38]. At present, no published studies have provided reference stiffness values for the IL. To address gap, we referred to the cervical spine models developed by Yoganandan et al. [32], in which ligaments were modeled using beam elements. Using their reported values for elastic modulus (E), cross-sectional area (S), and length (L), we calculated the stiffness of the IL with the formula K = E.S/L,​ and incorporated it into our model.

Criteria for model division

Cervical lordosis and kyphotic deformity lack universally accepted and clearly defined diagnostic criteria. Most studies categorize cervical spine alignment into lordotic, straightened, and kyphotic types. However, due to the absence of objective standards, such classifications often rely on subjective assessments of cervical morphology and are not consistently recognized [39].

In clinical practice, ome studies adopt quantitative measurements based on specific distances or angles. Common sagittal cervical parameters include: the C2–C7 Cobb angle, defined as the angle between the inferior endplates of C2 and C7; the T1 slope (T1S), defined as the angle between the superior endplate of T1 and the horizontal plane; and the C2–C7 sagittal vertical axis (C2–C7 SVA), defined as the horizontal distance between a plumb line dropped from the center of the C2 vertebral body and the posterosuperior corner of the C7 superior endplate [40].

In this study, cervical alignment was classified according to the C2–C7 Cobb angle. However, the definition of cervical deformity varies among researchers. For instance, Grob et al. [41] defined that a Cobb angle between + 4.0° and −4.0° (measured via the posterior tangent method) as straight, with values < −4.0° indicating kyphosis and >+ 4.0° indicating lordosis. In contrast, Xiao et al. [30] categorized patients as lordotic when the Cobb angle was ≥ 10°, straightened when it was between 0° and 10°, and kyphotic when it was < 0°. Further kyphosis subtypes were described in subsequent studies [42], but are not detailed here. Following the classification criteria of Xiao et al. [30], we ensured the selected models adhered to a range consistent with the overlap of different definitions. To achieve this, we adjusted the angles to align with the overlap of different classification standards. Accordingly, we adopted a lordotic model with a Cobb angle of 25.5°, a straightened model with 3.0°, and a kyphotic model with −8.0°.

Stress and strain in cervical facet joints and intervertebral discs

The cervical spine, the most mobile part of the spine, plays a crucial role in supporting the head, maintaining visual stability, enabling upright posture and effective obstacle avoidance. Any alteration in the cranial center of gravity can disrupt the biomechanical balance of the cervical spine, increasing paraspinal muscle tension and energy expenditure, thereby accelerating cervical degeneration [43]. The progressive loss of cervical lordosis results in spinal imbalance, shifting the head’s center of gravity forward and imposing abnormal mechanical loads, which in turn affect cervical kinematics and sagittal alignment [44].

This study demonstrates that cervical kyphosis leads to markedly higher maximum stress and strain in the facet joints under all physiological loads, with particularly increases under certain conditions. Specifically, the loss of cervical curvature increases facet joint stress by up to 71.10-fold at C2–C3 and 356.12-fold at C6–C7, indicating a mechanical explanation for the reported higher degeneration rates at these levels [20, 22]. Leng et al. [45] noted that the gravitational and muscular forces acting on the upper vertebrae are transmitted to the lower vertebral body as a combination of compressive and shear forces. Based on this theory, as the cervical spine transitions from a lordotic to a straightened and eventually kyphotic alignment, the head’s center of gravity may shift anteriorly, thereby increasing shear loading. As a result, the facet joints must resist greater shear forces to maintain balance, resulting in elevated mechanical stress [46]. This interpretation is consistent with our findings. Excessive loading of the facet joints may compromise cervical spine stability and contributes to the progression of cervical disorders [47, 48]. Given the reciprocal relationship between facet joint stress and cervical curvature, early clinical intervention is crucial to prevent further deterioration.

Our findings indicate that the lordotic cervical spine model exhibited higher maximum strain at the C3–C4 and C4–C5 facet joints under physiological loads. In contrast, strain at these levels remained relatively unchanged or decreased in the straightened and kyphotic models, while strain at the C2–C3, C5–C6, and C6–C7 showed fold increases ranging from 3.10 to 10.20 compared with the lordotic model. This suggests that mid—cervical facet joints (C3–C5) are comparatively stable under changes in alignment, whereas the upper and lower segments experience greater strain redistribution. Consequently, peak strain distribution shifts toward both ends of the cervical spine. These results are consistent with clinical observations by Park [49], who reported that degenerative changes most commonly occur at the C2–C3 and C5–C6 facet joints.

Similar to the facet joints, the IVDs are essential soft tissue structures responsible for load transmission s between vertebral segments and maintaining spinal stability during movement [5, 50]. In a physiologically lordotic spine, t the IVDs maintain proper alignment and contact relationships. However, pathological changes such as straightening or kyphosis alter vertebral alignment, resulting in modifications to load-bearing structures and contact surfaces. These changes disrupt the normal load transmission mechanism, leading to altered stress and strain distribution with the IVDs.

In the lordotic model, the maximum IVD stress was observed at C5–C6 segment due to C5’s position at the apex of the cervical curvature. As a biomechanical transition point, C5 converts compressive loads from the skull into tensile forces along the spine, increasing stress in the adjacent discs [51]. However, in the straightened and kyphotic models, peak stress consistently shifted to the C3–C4 segment, suggesting that C5 no longer serves as the apex or primary load-transfer point. Structural changes caused by kyphosis may lead to reversed curvature, diminishing the stabilizing function of the posterior facet joints. Consequently, mechanical loads are redistributed toward the anterior and middle columns of the spine, leading to increased stress concentration in the IVDs and accelerating disc degeneration [51]. This interpretation is further supported by clinical imaging data. Ling et al. [52] reported that among C3 to C6, the C5 vertebra exhibited the greatest posterior displacement in patients with cervical deformity. As C5 and C6 are also the most common levels of disc herniation, kyphosis-related misalignment may directly contribute to abnormal loading. In this context, Buyukturan et al. [53] demonstrated that cervical stabilization training effectively reduces neck pain and improve outcomes in patients with disc herniation, underscoring the clinical significance of segmental instability. Furthermore, previous studies [9, 54] have emphasized the critical role of physiological cervical lordosis in maintaining efficient spinal function and motion. In contrast, loss of lordosis and development of kyphosis are associated with abnormal spinal kinematics, increased mechanical loading, and progressive degeneration of intervertebral discs and facet joints.

During the progressive loss of lordotic cervical curvature, total stress across all IVDs increases, indicating that the IVDs are subjected to greater mechanical loads. This may be because cervical kyphosis leads to spinal imbalance and instability, which are early manifestation of IVD degeneration. These conditions can accelerate disc degeneration and result in a reduction in disc height [13]. The decreased distance between adjacent vertebrae further compresses the IVD, increasing their load. In addition, cervical imbalance weakens posterior tension, shifting the load anteriorly and further increasing pressure on the anterior portion of the discs [55]. This explains why, in the stress distribution map, some IVDs, particularly the anterior region of the C2–C3 segment, exhibit stress concentration in kyphotic deformity under all physiological loading conditions, including flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the model simplifies vertebral bodies as homogeneous and isotropic linear elastic materials. In reality, bone is heterogeneous and anisotropic, especially at the cortical–trabecular interface. This simplification may introduce localized inaccuracies in stress distribution. Nevertheless, as identical material properties were applied across all models (lordotic, straightened, and kyphotic), the comparative biomechanical trends remain valid for evaluating the effects of curvature alteration. Second, preload conditions representing in vivo muscle forces and compressive stabilization were not incorporated. Instead, only a static vertical head load and pure moments were applied. This omission may underestimate the role of active muscular control and passive stabilization in maintaining cervical alignment. Future studies should integrate follower load approaches and musculoskeletal coupling to better replicate physiological conditions. Third, the simulations were limited to static physiological loads, such as flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation. Dynamic effects, including cyclic or repetitive loading, viscoelastic time-dependent deformation, muscle activation patterns, and neuromuscular compensation mechanisms, were not considered. These factors are known to influence spinal biomechanics under daily activities and long-term degeneration, and their exclusion restricts the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, this study focuses solely on geometrical alignment alterations while holding all tissue material properties constant, without accounting for pre-stress from degeneration or chronic deformation. In clinical practice, changes in cervical curvature frequently co-occur with tissue degeneration, such as reduced disc hydration, cartilage wear, or ligamentous laxity. While the controlled-variable design allows isolation of curvature-related effects, it does not capture the complex interplay between anatomical degeneration and mechanical loading. Future modeling efforts should incorporate pathology-specific and age-related tissue property variations to enhance clinical relevance. Finally, although our findings are partially supported by prior biomechanical and imaging studies, direct clinical validation is lacking, with only limited studies to date directly correlating non-lordotic cervical alignment with accelerated degeneration [8, 9]. In particular, clinical studies correlating non-lordotic cervical alignment with increased degeneration at the C2–C3 and C6–C7 segments remain scarce. Moreover, direct dynamic validation of the model was not performed, and future experimental or in vivo studies are needed to confirm these findings. Thus, the present results should be regarded as biomechanical hypotheses that warrant further investigation and validation in both dynamic simulations and clinical settings.

Conclusion

This study developed and analyzed FE models of the cervical spine in lordotic, straightened, and kyphotic alignments to investigate the biomechanical impact of cervical curvature alterations. The results demonstrated that the loss of physiological lordosis—particularly in kyphotic deformity—led to a redistribution of mechanical loads from the mid-cervical segments (C3–C6) to the upper (C2–C3) and lower (C6–C7) cervical segments. At these regions, stress and strain in both the facet joints and IVDs increased markedly. These findings provide a biomechanical basis for early diagnosis and targeted intervention in patients with non-lordotic cervical spines, with the potential to reduce the risk of structural degeneration and functional decline.
