An update on the role of multimodal analgesia in perioperative pain management
Authors: Whitney N. Kuwamoto, Grace C. Eddy, Edward R. Mariano, Eric S. Schwenk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multimodal analgesia has become a foundational principle of acute perioperative pain management and enhanced recovery and involves the application of nonpharmacologic, pharmacologic, and/or interventional techniques. The overall goal of this approach is to apply a combination of different modalities that target discrete points along the pain pathway and thereby avoid the overreliance on opioids and associated side effects. Since our review in 2018, there have been significant developments that have prompted an updated review of multimodal analgesia. In this article, we identify and summarize important trends that influence the current approach to acute perioperative pain management as well as provide an updated clinical guide on multimodal analgesia with an emphasis on novel therapies. While a continued emphasis on multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia remains, additional evidence, newer regional techniques, and novel agents such as suzetrigine have emerged since our last review and have expanded options for perioperative pain management. In addition, special situations require specific recommendations, such as when designing a perioperative pain management plan for surgical patients who have opioid tolerance or live with opioid use disorder. These complex patients are at a higher risk for poor postoperative pain control and other adverse outcomes. This review includes suggested guidance on tailoring multimodal analgesia in this population with patient-specific therapies that can be added for appropriate indications and are supported by available evidence and guidelines.

Introduction

In the modern era of surgical care, routine use of multimodal analgesia has become a foundational principle of acute perioperative pain management [1]. Within the United States, the administration of multimodal analgesia for eligible surgical cases is a national quality measure [2], one of very few quality measures that directly relate to anesthesiologists. Implementation of multimodal analgesia has been associated with decreases in perioperative opioid use and complications after major surgery [3]. Given the data available at the time, we asserted in a 2018 review that designing an ideal perioperative pain management strategy should always start with multimodal analgesia [4], and we believe that this still holds true today in spite of the many advances in surgery, anesthesiology, and perioperative medicine in recent years.

At a minimum, multimodal analgesia is defined as the use of two or more analgesic therapies with different mechanisms of action with the goal of providing effective pain control [4], while avoiding the overreliance on opioids and associated opioid-related adverse effects. These modalities may be nonpharmacologic, pharmacologic, or interventional, with interventional techniques varying in their invasiveness and duration of action. When used optimally in combination, the elements of a multimodal analgesic regimen target different and discrete points along the pain pathway and may be adjusted to treat patients with specific pain subtypes [4,5].

In our initial review of multimodal analgesia, we addressed the core pharmacologic and interventional elements, which included the following nonopioid systemic analgesics: acetaminophen, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and cyclo-oxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitors, gabapentinoids, ketamine, magnesium, local anesthetics via surgical infiltration or peripheral nerve blocks, and lidocaine infusions. We also discussed appropriate indications and the judicious use of opioids [4]. However, healthcare worldwide continues to evolve, and there have been significant developments that have prompted this updated review of multimodal analgesia. In this paper, we provide an overview of important trends that influence the current approaches to acute perioperative pain management and an updated clinical guide on multimodal analgesia with an emphasis on new therapies.

The expansion of outpatient surgery

In recent years, given the rising costs of healthcare and projected increases in the number of total joint arthroplasty procedures performed per year, there has been a notable interest in transitioning arthroplasty to the outpatient setting with same-day discharge in many parts of the world [6]. In the United States, the proportion of outpatient arthroplasty has increased from < 1% in 2016 to approximately 20% in 2021 [7], although this change is not without risks [8]. The COVID-19 pandemic, which occurred after the publication of our original article [4], forced the temporary stoppage of all elective surgical procedures. Resuming those procedures during the ongoing pandemic prompted the re-evaluation of the risks and benefits of hospitalization, particularly the potential exposure to an infectious disease after undergoing a prosthetic joint implantation. This resulted in an increase in outpatient arthroplasty [9].

For patients discharged home on the same day after arthroplasty, perioperative pain management is a significant challenge given that intravenous analgesics are not available. Effective multimodal pain management in the outpatient setting should be easy to self-administer, promote early mobility and functional independence, minimize central nervous system adverse effects, and promote sleep [10].

Growing awareness of differential postoperative pain trajectories

Despite the wide range of therapeutic interventions available for postoperative pain management, there is a relative paucity of population-based observational data on postoperative pain trajectories [11]. The data available suggest that postoperative pain may vary both between surgical procedures and between individual patients having the same procedure [11,12]. Ideally, the proper application of acute perioperative pain management should be procedure- and person-specific based on the expected trajectories of pain and recovery after surgery.

Although advances in clinical pain research have not yet reached the point of individualized perioperative pain management plans, there is evidence to suggest that certain patient phenotypes may respond differently to specific interventions, and certain patient ‘tiers’ may require more analgesic modalities in their multimodal regimen than others. Vasilopoulos et al. [12] identified younger age, female sex, anxiety, and frequent pain behaviors, which were present on preoperative assessment, as risk factors for the high postoperative pain trajectory group in their study. This suggests that intentional preoperative pain assessment may be able to help guide the design of perioperative pain management regimens based on the ‘tier’ of patient. Preoperative identification of a particular pain phenotype, through examination of factors like baseline pain sensitivity, psychological comorbidities (e.g., anxiety, pain catastrophizing), and social support, may enable physicians to identify those patients at higher risk for severe acute pain and even tailor the perioperative pain management plan to their needs [13,14].

The emergence of fascial plane blocks for thoracic and abdominal surgeries

One of the most substantial changes to perioperative analgesia since our last review is the emergence of fascial plane blocks as alternatives to thoracic epidural analgesia for surgeries in the chest and abdomen. Fascial plane blocks target distal sensory and motor branches of peripheral nerves and may be useful as part of multimodal analgesia within enhanced recovery protocols, although they appear to be more effective for somatic pain than visceral pain [15–17]. Many novel blocks have been described including: the transversus abdominis plane, rectus sheath, quadratus lumborum, erector spinae plane (ESP), serratus anterior plane, and interpectoral/pecto-serratus plane (i.e., ‘Pecs II’) blocks [18]. One potential advantage of these blocks over traditional thoracic epidural analgesia in the setting of enhanced recovery is the avoidance of sympathectomy, which preserves blood pressure and decreases the administration of intravenous fluid [19,20]. Although data are limited, fascial plane blocks are performed in locations that are further away from the spinal cord, which decreases concern for clinically-significant bleeding complications that are generally associated with epidurals, especially when patients may be prescribed anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs postoperatively [21,22].

In breast and thoracic surgery, a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found that the serratus anterior plane block reduced both acute and chronic postsurgical pain [23]. A meta-analysis of ESP block studies found that they reduced postoperative pain after thoracic surgery similar to paravertebral blocks [24]. For patients undergoing abdominal surgery, there is mixed evidence regarding fascial plane blocks, with some articles supporting benefits of certain fascial plane blocks in pain and opioid reduction [25–27]; however, a recent randomized clinical trial reported no improvement in analgesia with both plain and liposomal bupivacaine for transversus abdominis plane blocks [28]. While epidural analgesia remains an established gold standard regional analgesic technique, particularly in major thoracic and abdominal surgeries, the trend in modern surgical practice continues to move towards less invasive techniques and rapid postoperative recovery, which has promoted the development of fascial plane blocks as alternatives with society guidelines supporting their use [29].

Routine or foundational elements of multimodal analgesia

Nonpharmacologic modalities

Nonpharmacologic modalities are foundational in multimodal analgesia (Table 1) and should be routinely employed unless contraindicated [5,30]. Examples include physical therapy, cryotherapy, compression, acupuncture, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, although robust evidence-based guidelines for nonpharmacologic analgesia have not been established [5]. Cognitive behavioral therapy may also have a role in mitigating some of the deleterious effects of postoperative pain in some patients [31]. These approaches support aspects of the biopsychosocial model of pain and may have a role in postoperative recovery from surgery.

Setting realistic expectations and providing patient and caregiver education regarding pain management prior to surgery are powerful nonpharmacologic interventions. While a pain-free postoperative experience is rarely feasible, there is evidence that positive preoperative expectations may lead to decreased postoperative pain [32]. Patients should be counseled that achieving a tolerable level of pain, one that allows for early activity and participation in rehabilitation, is a more realistic and meaningful goal [5].

Basic systemic nonopioid analgesics

Although covered in greater depth in our original review [4], acetaminophen and NSAIDs or COX-2 inhibitors continue to be recommended as routine components of multimodal analgesia (Table 1) [30,33]. In addition to the established effectiveness and safety for acetaminophen [34] and NSAIDs in the immediate postoperative period [35,36], these drugs are available in generic low-cost formulations. The potential role of NSAIDs in postoperative bleeding has been a controversial topic, but a meta-analysis by Bongiovanni et al. that included 74 articles showed no increase in risk for hematoma, return to the operating room for bleeding, or blood transfusion in patients who received NSAIDs when compared to patients who did not [37]. Caution is still warranted when considering the use of NSAIDs in patients with preexisting renal dysfunction [38].

Local anesthetics

Local anesthetics should be routinely administered in some form in any surgical procedure unless contraindicated (e.g., allergy) (Table 1). Options include infiltration by the surgeon at or near the surgical site, a peripheral nerve or fascial plane block, or a neuraxial block. Peripheral regional techniques can reduce postoperative pain and opioid use in various procedures [39–41], and the selection of technique, titratability, and duration of action (i.e., single-injection or catheter, use of adjuvants or extended-release formulations) should ideally be guided by the anticipated postoperative pain trajectory and expectations for recovery (e.g., location, activity). An extensive review of the various regional analgesic options is beyond the scope of this article.

Intravenous lidocaine infusions have been applied for perioperative analgesia and may be useful for cases in which a regional block is not provided [42,43]. Intravenous lidocaine has been incorporated into enhanced recovery protocols [44,45], with short-term dose-dependent benefits on postoperative pain [46]. An international consensus group did not provide a definitive recommendation regarding perioperative lidocaine infusions but did emphasize the importance of tailoring their use to individual patients and surgeries [43]. Lidocaine infusions have been shown to reduce inflammation [47], and a recent randomized clinical trial found reductions in both inflammation and pulmonary complications after thoracic surgery [48].

Opioids

Opioids remain an important element of multimodal analgesia, particularly as treatment for visceral pain, which may not be covered by other modalities (Table 1) [17]. In acute perioperative pain management, there are international consensus recommendations regarding safe opioid administration and avoidance of harm [49]. Some key recommendations include: a preoperative assessment of chronic opioid use; incorporation of patient function rather than pain intensity ratings alone in assessments of analgesic effectiveness; limiting the number of tablets prescribed at discharge; not initiating long-acting opioids in opioid-naive patients; counseling on the risks of opioids and safe disposal practices at discharge; and avoidance of automatic opioid refills. Following existing perioperative pain management guidelines [29,50] and practicing good opioid stewardship are highly recommended to maximize benefits and minimize complications.

Next level elements of multimodal analgesia that deserve special consideration

In addition to the routine or foundational elements of multimodal analgesia, there are classes of pain therapies or new drugs that may be employed for specific indications based on the patient and/or procedure (Table 1).

Selective voltage-gated sodium channel inhibitor

Suzetrigine is a new drug recently approved in the United States that selectively inhibits the voltage-gated sodium channel NaV1.8 in peripheral nerves and dorsal root ganglion sensory neurons, which are partially responsible for hyperexcitability. It is approved by the Food and Drug Administration for acute postoperative pain and is the first medication in this new class of nonopioid analgesics [51]. Suzetrigine is not thought to affect the central nervous system or interact with local anesthetics, which are non-selective sodium channel blockers [52,53]. In Phase 3 studies, suzetrigine has been demonstrated to produce superior analgesia to placebo and comparable analgesia to hydrocodone/acetaminophen with potentially fewer adverse effects [54]. It is currently being studied for diabetic peripheral neuropathy, a classic neuropathic chronic pain model [55]. Although promising, given its novel mechanism of action, its role in routine perioperative pain management, especially when used in combination with other established agents in a multimodal regimen, is not yet known but will be actively studied in the coming years.

Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor

As a serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, duloxetine is approved for the treatment of major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, fibromyalgia, chronic musculoskeletal pain, and diabetic peripheral neuropathy. It may be considered in patients with overlapping pain and mental health disorders but is contraindicated in individuals taking monoamine oxidase inhibitors and those with hepatic failure or severe renal impairment. Clinicians should monitor patients for suicidal ideation, serotonin syndrome, and potential drug interactions, particularly in elderly, pregnant, or breastfeeding patients [56]. Duloxetine as a perioperative analgesic is supported by two meta-analyses [57,58], which included total joint arthroplasty, but another meta-analysis did not find a clinically-important improvement in pain [59]. Therefore, duloxetine should not be routinely included in multimodal analgesic protocols, but may be considered as an added element for special indications in patients with the indicated chronic pain diagnoses having surgery, contraindications to other analgesic medications, or comorbid pain and mental health disorders.

Gabapentinoids

Gabapentinoids (e.g., gabapentin and pregabalin) act on voltage-dependent calcium channels to modulate excitatory neurotransmission centrally and peripherally [60]. These agents may reduce postoperative pain and opioid requirements, especially in patients with a neuropathic etiology of pain [61]. Respiratory depression can occur when gabapentinoids and opioids are combined [62], and side effects such as sedation, dizziness, and delirium may be more pronounced in the elderly [63]. Given available data which do not strongly support the analgesic benefit of gabapentinoids [64–66], they should not be given routinely in perioperative pain management. They may be appropriate to use in special cases, such as when patients are already taking them chronically or in select surgeries where nerve injury is likely, but duration of therapy should be limited with careful monitoring for side effects.

N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonists

Ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist but also modulates acetylcholine, gamma-aminobutyric acid, and opioid receptors, as well as voltage-gated calcium channels, making it a versatile analgesic [67]. It can be effective for patients undergoing painful surgery, are opioid-tolerant, or have a history of substance use disorder or depression [68–70]. Adverse effects when using subanesthetic doses are mild and generally tolerable [71]. Magnesium sulfate is a noncompetitive NMDA receptor antagonist. It has been shown to reduce postoperative pain, opioid requirements, and may prolong spinal blockade when used as an adjuvant with morphine or ketamine [4,30]. It has also been found to reduce intraoperative anesthetic requirements [72]. It is generally well tolerated at perioperative doses, although care should be taken in patients with renal insufficiency due to the risk of accumulation and toxicity. Adverse effects may include hypotension, bradycardia, nausea, vomiting, and, at higher concentrations, neuromuscular weakness, respiratory depression, arrhythmias, and prolonged time to tracheal extubation [73].

Special populations

Patients with opioid tolerance

Opioid-tolerant patients display altered physiology compared to opioid-naive patients. Some of the changes that occur include hypersensitivity to painful stimuli [74] and sleep-disordered breathing [75], which encompasses both obstructive and central sleep apnea. Opioid-tolerant patients experience worse perioperative outcomes, including inadequate analgesia, higher readmission rates, greater healthcare costs, and reduced odds of returning to work [76–79]. Realistic pain expectations should be discussed at the time of surgical planning, and every effort should be made to communicate and plan with the patient’s opioid prescriber, specifically around postoperative opioid prescribing upon discharge and outpatient follow-up. Multimodal analgesia is recommended for opioid-tolerant patients, both to improve analgesia and to minimize dose escalations of opioids. Of all the medications available to add to a routine multimodal regimen, ketamine has been shown to provide opioid-reducing analgesia in this patient population [80]. When available, an acute pain service can help improve analgesia, suggest opioid alternatives, and work with surgeons and other healthcare staff to develop safe opioid discharge regimens [78,79,81].

Patients with opioid use disorder

In the United States, overdose deaths from prescription opioids have flattened and even declined in recent years [82]. However, the overall opioid-related overdose death rate has continued to climb, largely driven by illicit fentanyl and other synthetic opioids. Patients living with opioid use disorder (OUD) can present challenges in the perioperative period, but there have been important recent advances. The American Society of Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine (ASRA Pain Medicine) strongly recommends the involvement of acute pain services in the perioperative care of patients with OUD [39]. In general, patients who are prescribed buprenorphine (partial mu opioid agonist and kappa antagonist) should continue it throughout the perioperative period for pain management and to reduce the risk of relapse [83]. This is a change from prior recommendations that suggested stopping the drug several days prior to painful surgeries [84].

Buprenorphine can be a very effective analgesic and several clinical studies [85,86] have demonstrated comparable analgesia between buprenorphine and morphine. If additional full mu-receptor agonists are indicated in a patient taking buprenorphine in the immediate perioperative period, hydromorphone or sufentanil may be used as these agents have high binding affinities for the mu- receptor [87]. While buprenorphine confers an overall low risk of respiratory depression, it is still possible, especially when combined with benzodiazepines and other sedating medications [88].

Other medications for OUD that may be encountered in the perioperative period include methadone and naltrexone. Similar to the perioperative management of buprenorphine, preoperative methadone dosing should be continued throughout the perioperative period, including the day of surgery [88]. One challenge with continuing outpatient methadone for OUD that is administered by a methadone clinic is that the dose may not be known by the patient, so outreach to the clinic will be necessary. Methadone can cause respiratory depression, particularly after a dose increase in a patient on a previously stable dose [88]. Monthly extended-release intramuscular naltrexone for OUD presents a unique challenge in the perioperative period due to the regular cadence of injections and long duration of action. Although experience and evidence remain limited, experts suggest that full mu-receptor agonists may only be partially effective during the first two to three weeks after the injection [88].

There is no single “best” approach at managing postoperative pain in this population, but the overall strategy should focus on maximizing nonopioid agents, incorporating regional anesthesia and analgesia whenever possible, using the lowest effective doses of opioids (full mu agonists) as rescue agents, and coordination with the physician prescribing OUD treatment before surgery and before discharge [89–91].

Conclusions

While a continued emphasis on multimodal analgesia remains, since our last review in 2018, additional evidence, newer regional techniques, and novel agents such as suzetrigine have emerged, and have expanded options for perioperative pain management. Special consideration should be given to designing perioperative pain management plans for surgical patients who take chronic opioids or live with OUD, both of whom are at higher risk for poorly controlled pain and worse postoperative outcomes. For these special populations and all patients who undergo surgery, designing the ideal perioperative pain management plan depends on consistent implementation of multimodal analgesia, founded in evidence-based modalities that can be used routinely and augmented by patient-specific therapies that can be added for appropriate indications.
