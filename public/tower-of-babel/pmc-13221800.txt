Catastrophic Antiphospholipid Syndrome
Authors: Sarah H. Choudhury, Ava Wexler, Manali Shah, Shamroz Farooq, Kevin S. McGrody
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an uncommon cause of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) in patients without traditional cardiovascular risk factors.Case SummaryA 50-year-old man with 1 week of urticaria presented with acute abdominal pain and inferior STEMI. Coronary angiography revealed thrombotic occlusion of the right coronary artery posterolateral branch with minimal atherosclerosis, treated with balloon angioplasty and thrombectomy. Despite reperfusion, he developed progressive mesenteric ischemia, left renal infarction, and right cerebellar infarction. Transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated a mobile aortic valve echodensity concerning for nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis. Laboratory testing revealed triple-positive antiphospholipid antibodies, concerning for catastrophic APS. He underwent multiple laparotomies with superior mesenteric artery thrombectomy and resection of necrotic jejunum, surviving with anticoagulation and multidisciplinary care.DiscussionThis case illustrates an arterial-predominant presentation of catastrophic APS initially manifesting as STEMI. Multiterritory arterial thromboses in patients without atherosclerotic disease should prompt evaluation for systemic thrombophilia.Take-Home MessageEarly anticoagulation and multidisciplinary intervention are essential to prevent ischemic complications.

History of Presentation

A 50-year-old man presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset, sharp left lower abdominal pain radiating to the back, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis, and recurrent throat tightness. He denied chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, or syncope. One week prior, he had developed diffuse urticaria and pruritus without identifiable triggers and had received outpatient prednisone from urgent care, with persistent symptoms.

On arrival, vital signs included temperature 37.8 °C, blood pressure 127/71 mm Hg, heart rate 93 beats/min, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation 97% on room air. He was diaphoretic, uncomfortable, and diffusely urticarial. Cardiovascular examination revealed regular rate and rhythm without murmurs, rubs, or gallops. The abdomen was soft with focal left lower quadrant tenderness. Neurologic examination was normal.

Electrocardiogram demonstrated 2 mm ST-segment elevations in leads II, III, and aVF (Figure 1). Point-of-care ultrasound revealed inferior wall hypokinesis. He was initially loaded with aspirin and ticagrelor, and subsequently given methylprednisolone, diphenhydramine, and famotidine.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of eczema and nephrolithiasis. He had no history of coronary artery disease, prior thromboembolism, autoimmune disorders, or anaphylaxis. He vaped nicotine and was a former cigarette smoker. He denied recent travel, immobilization, or illicit drug use.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial differential diagnosis for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) in a 50-year-old man with minimal cardiovascular risk factors included 1) atherosclerotic plaque rupture, 2) coronary vasospasm (Kounis syndrome given concurrent urticaria), 3) coronary embolism from cardiac source, 4) spontaneous coronary artery dissection, 5) coronary thrombosis from hypercoagulable state, and 6) Takotsubo cardiomyopathy.

After the development of multiterritory arterial thromboses, the differential expanded to include: 1) catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS); 2) thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura; 3) disseminated intravascular coagulation; 4) malignancy-associated hypercoagulability; 5) inherited thrombophilia; and 6) systemic vasculitis.

Investigations

Emergent coronary angiography revealed 100% occlusion of the right coronary artery posterolateral branch, successfully treated with balloon angioplasty and mechanical thrombectomy without stent placement (Figure 2). Despite successful reperfusion, abdominal pain worsened over 12 to 18 hours with progressive distension and tenderness. Laboratory tests showed rising liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase: 226 U/L, alanine aminotransferase: 104 U/L) and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein: 149 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate: 61 mm/h). Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated a 7 × 1 mm mobile linear echodensity on the noncoronary cusp of the aortic valve, concerning for nonbacterial thrombotic (Libman-Sacks) endocarditis, a known manifestation of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). Blood cultures (×4) remained negative, and extensive infectious work-up including Brucella, Q-fever, Bartonella, and Whipple polymerase chain reaction were negative.

Comprehensive thrombophilia evaluation revealed markedly elevated anticardiolipin IgG (94.1 GPL), β-2-glycoprotein I IgG (98.7 units), and positive lupus anticoagulant, establishing a high-risk antiphospholipid antibody profile.1 Remaining testing was negative for tryptase elevation, JAK2 mutation, disseminated intravascular coagulation, C1q antibodies, antinuclear antibodies, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, and rheumatoid factor.

Management

The patient was initiated on intravenous unfractionated heparin for anticoagulation, along with intravenous fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics (vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam), and continuation of a planned 7-day course of methylprednisolone. On hospital day 2, he underwent emergent exploratory laparotomy with superior mesenteric artery embolectomy for acute mesenteric ischemia. Serial abdominal re-explorations revealed progressive bowel ischemia, ultimately necessitating resection of 80 cm of necrotic mid-jejunum on hospital day 4. The remaining bowel was viable, and intestinal continuity was restored with primary abdominal closure on hospital day 5.

Given the diagnosis of APS with concern for CAPS, management followed established guideline-based recommendations. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient survived the acute APS episode with stabilization of multiorgan thrombosis after guideline-directed medical therapy and surgical intervention. Repeat CT head after therapeutic anticoagulation confirmed a right cerebellar infarct and additionally demonstrated 2 small cortical infarcts involving the right parietal and left temporal lobes. Despite these findings, he exhibited no persistent focal neurological deficits.

After multiple abdominal surgeries, the patient required total parenteral nutrition owing to bowel resection and postoperative ileus. Parenteral nutrition was gradually weaned as bowel function returned, and at the time of discharge, the patient was tolerating a full oral diet with regular bowel movements.

He was discharged on antiplatelet therapy with clopidogrel, high-intensity statin therapy with atorvastatin, and long-term anticoagulation in accordance with APS management guidelines, with plans to transition to warfarin targeting an international normalized ratio of 2 to 3.1 Ongoing outpatient follow-up was arranged with the hematology, cardiology, neurology, and general surgery teams for longitudinal management of APS, valvular disease, cerebrovascular sequelae, and short bowel syndrome, respectively.

Discussion

This case illustrates the importance of broadening the differential diagnosis for STEMI beyond atherosclerotic plaque rupture, particularly in patients without traditional cardiovascular risk factors. The American Heart Association scientific statement on myocardial infarction in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease (MINOCA) emphasizes that APS should be considered in younger patients presenting with acute coronary thrombosis, especially when accompanied by multiterritory arterial occlusion.2, 3, 4

The angiographic finding of isolated coronary thrombosis with minimal underlying atherosclerosis, followed by rapid progression to mesenteric, renal, and cerebral ischemia, raised concern for a systemic thrombotic process. In such presentations, multiterritory arterial thromboses should prompt evaluation for underlying autoimmune thrombophilia, including comprehensive antiphospholipid antibody testing. Although the patient did not meet criteria for a definite diagnosis of CAPS at presentation, the clinical course was most consistent with probable CAPS, supported by rapid multiterritory arterial involvement within 1 week and a high-risk, triple-positive antiphospholipid antibody profile.5,6 Confirmatory antibody testing at least 6 weeks apart was not yet available, precluding a definitive diagnosis.

TEE demonstrated a mobile echodensity on the noncoronary cusp of the aortic valve consistent with nonbacterial thrombotic (Libman-Sacks) endocarditis, supporting an embolic rather than primary atherosclerotic mechanism. Valvular involvement is well described in APS and is more prevalent among patients with double- or triple-positive antiphospholipid antibody profiles, further reinforcing the systemic nature of this thrombotic disease.

The patient's presentation was notable for preceding urticaria and allergic-type symptoms, which are not typical manifestations of CAPS. While cutaneous involvement occurs in approximately 50% to 54% of CAPS cases, urticaria is not a recognized cutaneous manifestation.6 However, these atypical features prompted early consideration of a systemic process rather than isolated coronary disease and may have represented an inflammatory trigger for the thrombotic storm, though this remains speculative.

Management of probable CAPS requires early multidisciplinary coordination and prompt initiation of combination therapy. In this patient, early anticoagulation and immunosuppression, combined with urgent coronary and mesenteric revascularization, were central to stabilizing multiorgan ischemia.1,3,6,7 Close collaboration among the cardiology, hematology, vascular surgery, general surgery, neurology, and infectious disease teams was essential to achieving a favorable outcome.

This case contributes to the literature by illustrating an arterial-predominant presentation of probable CAPS initially manifesting as STEMI in a patient without significant atherosclerotic disease. It verifies that APS should be considered in the differential diagnosis of acute coronary thrombosis in younger patients, particularly when accompanied by atypical systemic findings or multiorgan thrombotic involvement.2,4

Conclusions

CAPS can present initially as STEMI in patients without significant atherosclerotic disease and may involve rapid, multiorgan arterial thrombosis. Recognition of atypical inflammatory or cutaneous features can facilitate earlier diagnosis of a systemic thrombosis. Timely diagnosis, prompt anticoagulation, and coordinated multidisciplinary management are essential to limit organ injury and optimize outcomes in this high-risk population.
