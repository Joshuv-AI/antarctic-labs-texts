p p -Wasserstein distances on networks and 3D to 1D convergence
Martin Burger \orcidID 0000-0003-2619-2912 and Ariane Fazeny \orcidID 0009-0002-0008-3646 and Gilles Mordant \orcidID 0000-0003-1821-5168 and Jan-Frederik Pietschmann \orcidID 0000-0003-0383-8696

Abstract
We study transport distances on metric graphs representing gas networks. Starting from the dynamic formulation of the Wasserstein distance, we review extensions to networks, with and without the possibility of storing mass on the vertices. Next, we examine the asymptotic behavior of the static Wasserstein distance on a three-dimensional network domain that converges to a metric graph. We show convergence of the distance with a proof that is based on the characterization of optimal transport plans as c c -cyclically monotone sets. We conclude by illustrating our finding with several numerical examples.

Introduction

The theory of optimal transport, introduced by G. Monge in
Monge1781
and reformulated by L.V. Kantorovich in
Kantorovich1942
, is nowadays an ubiquitous tool in many areas of applied mathematics, statistics, and data science
PeyreCuturi2019
;
Santambrogio2015
;
Cuturi2013
;
Arjovsky2017
;
Panaretos2019
;
Kolouri2017
;
Frogner2015
;
Hallin2020MultivariateGT
;
lavenant2021towards
;
zhang2023manifold
. The theory arose from the “logistic” problem of moving mass from a source configuration to a target configuration at overall minimal cost. Here, the ground cost encodes the difficulty of transporting one unit of mass from a point
x
to a point
y
. As such, the overall optimal cost thus defines a metric (or more general distance functional) between probability distributions that leverages the ground metric, which makes it a versatile tool.

Additionally to the optimal transport plan, establishing how to reorganize the mass from the source to the target, it is sometimes possible to add a time component to the problem and track how the reorganization should evolve as time passes. The problem without the time component is often called the
static formulation
, while the other version of the problem is called the
dynamic formulation
, provided it exists.

This dynamic formulation further connects certain evolution equations to gradient flows in the space of probability measures endowed with the Wasserstein metric. This view, motivated by physical principles
JordanKinderlehrerOtto1997
;
JordanKinderlehrerOtto1998
;
Otto2001
;
MielkePeletierRenger2014
;
Mielke2023
, has had a tremendous impact in the PDE community. In the Euclidean case, a gradient flow is given by a smooth curve
x:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to{\Omega},~x{\left({t}\right)}=x_{t}
for some space domain
{\Omega}
, such that
\mathrm{d}x_{t}/\mathrm{d}t=-\nabla E{\left({x_{t}}\right)}
for
t\geq 0
, where
E:{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
is some (typically convex) energy function. Then the gradient flow
x_{t}
minimizes
E
following the steepest descent. If we now want to evaluate an energy function at not only a point in space
x_{t}\in{\Omega}
, but rather for a probability distribution
\rho_{t}\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\Omega}}\right)}
on the space domain
{\Omega}
, then in order to have a well-defined gradient of
E
with respect to probability distributions we need a way of quantifying the distance between two probability measures.

One popular metric for quantifying the distance (in the sense of optimal transport cost) between two probability measures is the the
Wasserstein distance
. Given a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and two probability measures
\mu,\,\nu\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\Omega}}\right)}
, the minimal total transport cost of going from
\mu
to
\nu
is given by

W_{p}{\left({\mu,\nu}\right)}\coloneqq\min_{\pi\in{\Pi\left({\mu,\nu}\right)}}{\left\{{\int_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}{\left\lvert\left\lvert{x-y}\right\rvert\right\rvert}^{p}{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi{\left({x,y}\right)}}\right\}}^{1/p},

(WDS)

where
{\Pi\left({\mu,\nu}\right)}
denotes the set of all joint probability distributions
\pi
on
{\Omega}\times{\Omega}
, with the respective marginals
\mu
and
\nu
, meaning that
\mu{\left({A}\right)}=\pi{\left({A,\Omega}\right)}
and
\nu{\left({B}\right)}=\pi{\left({\Omega,B}\right)}
for all Borel measurable sets
A,B\subset\Omega
. In the definition above, the ground cost, generally denoted by
c
, is the
p
-th power of the Euclidean distance. Further, this formulation of the Wasserstein distance solely involves an infimum over so-called couplings
\pi
on
{\Omega}\times{\Omega}
without any time component, thus it corresponds to the static formulation.

In the case of a non-convex domain
{\Omega}
, the formulation (
WDS
) generally also allows mass transport outside of
{\Omega}
, as the costs only consider the Euclidean distance. In order to restrict the feasible transport to the space domain
{\Omega}
, one can alternatively also define the static formulation of the Wasserstein distance as

W_{p}{\left({\mu,\nu}\right)}\coloneqq\min_{\pi\in{\Pi\left({\mu,\nu}\right)}}{\left\{{\int_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}d_{\Omega,p}{\left({x,y}\right)}^{p}{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi{\left({x,y}\right)}}\right\}}^{1/p},

(1)

where
d_{\Omega,p}
is the shortest path distance on
\Omega
given by

d_{\Omega,p}{\left({x,y}\right)}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in C^{1}{\left({{\left[{0,1}\right]}}\right)},\\
\xi{\left({0}\right)}=x,\,\xi{\left({1}\right)}=y\end{subarray}}{\left\{{\int_{0}^{1}{\left\lvert\left\lvert{\dot{\xi}{\left({t}\right)}}\right\rvert\right\rvert}_{p}^{p}{\,\mathrm{d}}t}\right\}}^{1/p}.

(2)

Here, the infimum is taken over all (continuous) curves
\xi
in
\Omega
which are connecting
x\in{\Omega}
to
y\in{\Omega}
.

The optimization problem (
WDS
) is linear in the coupling
\pi
, which also needs to fulfill the marginal constraints. Hence, it is natural to express the constraints by relying on Lagrange multipliers. Doing so, one obtains an “inf-sup” problem where it turns out that the
inf
and the
sup
can be exchanged. This crucial fact gives rise to a dual formulation of the problem, which we recall later in Theorem
3.1
.

A further important aspect is the notion of
c
-cyclical monotonicity of the set on which an optimal coupling
\pi^{\star}
for the problem (
WDS
) is concentrated, see Definition
6
. The
c
-cyclical monotonicity expresses the fact that the optimality of
\pi^{\star}
would be contradicted by the possibility to re-route the mass of
\pi^{\star}
to produce a coupling achieving a smaller overall cost. Such considerations thus induce strong geometric constraints on the support of
\pi^{\star}
, which will be leveraged in some of our following results.

In the seminal contribution of J.D. Benamou and Y. Brenier in 2000,
benamou2000computational
, the minimization problem (
WDS
) was shown to have an equivalent dynamic formulation. The main idea is to consider curves of probability measures
{\left({\rho_{t}}\right)}_{0\leq t\leq 1}\subset{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\Omega}}\right)}
over an artificial time interval
[0,1]
, which connect the two measures
\mu
and
\nu
, such that
\rho_{0}=\mu
and
\rho_{1}=\nu
. As mass is preserved along this evolution from
\mu
to
\nu
, the curves
(\rho_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
are characterized by solutions (in the sense of distributions) to the continuity equation, i.e.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}v_{t})=0,

(3)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\mu,\quad\rho_{1}=\nu,

for some (measure-valued) velocity field
v_{t}
. We denote the set of all such curves
{\left({\rho_{t}}\right)}_{0\leq t\leq 1}\subset{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\Omega}}\right)}
fulfilling (
3
) by
{\mathsf{CE}}{\left({\mu,\nu}\right)}
. Then, selecting the curve with minimal kinetic energy from
{\mathsf{CE}}{\left({\mu,\nu}\right)}
, thus corresponding to an optimal transport from
\mu
to
\nu
, one recovers the
2
-Wassertein distance, i.e.,

\displaystyle W_{2}{\left({\mu,\nu}\right)}=\inf_{{\left({\rho_{t},v_{t}}\right)}\in{\mathsf{CE}}{\left({\mu,\nu}\right)}}{\left\{{\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Omega}{\left\lvert{v_{t}}\right\rvert}^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}}\right\}}^{1/2}.

(2-WDD)

The connection of the dynamic formulation of the Wasserstein distance to the problem of gas transport within a complicated network is done by extending (
2-WDD
) to metric graphs as the domain
{\Omega}
, modeling a gas network. Roughly speaking, metric graphs are combinatorial graphs in which a one-dimensional interval is associated to each edge (see subsection
2.1
for a rigorous definition). Then, the continuity equation (
3
) can be used on each of the edges to ensure mass conservation in the individual pipes. However, to enforce global mass conservation on the entire network, additional coupling conditions at the vertices, corresponding to pipe junctions, have to be introduced.

So far, two approaches have been considered in the literature:

In
erbar2021gradient
, homogeneous Kirchhoff conditions were imposed. In this setting, the sum of mass entering the node is equal to the mass leaving it, which means that no mass is stored on the node and no additional gas is entering the network through the node. This also allows for a static formulation on the metric graph, analogous to (
WDS
), by choosing a metric graph as the domain
{\Omega}
and replacing the Euclidean norm distance by the distance (
GD
) on the metric graph, defined in subsection
2.1
.

The second approach, introduced in
burger2023dynamic
, allows for storage of mass on a node, utilizing a more general version of Kirchhoff’s law. Here, additional continuity equations on the vertices are imposed. It was then a striking observation, made in
Fazeny_2025
, and rigorously proven in
weigand2025pdiffusion
, that the ISO3 model for gas flow, given as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}+\partial_{x}{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}v^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}&=0\\
\partial_{x}p^{\mathsf{e}}{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}&=-\frac{\lambda^{\mathsf{e}}}{2D^{\mathsf{e}}}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}v^{\mathsf{e}}{\left\lvert{v^{\mathsf{e}}}\right\rvert}-g\rho^{\mathsf{e}}\sin{\left(\omega^{\mathsf{e}}\right)}\end{split}

(ISO3)

can be interpreted as a 3-Wasserstein gradient flow for a certain energy functional
E
. Here, the superscript-
{\mathsf{e}}
-notation corresponds to the restriction of the respective functions defined on the whole gas network to a single pipe
{\mathsf{e}}
. The derivation of the ISO3 model is described in detail in
, alongside an entire hierarchy of models for the gas flow within one pipe, all derived from the Euler equations system for compressible, inviscid fluids. Note that, here the pipe inclination angle
\omega_{e}
relative to the ground is used instead of the pipe hight function
b_{e}
. Both approaches for the coupling conditions at vertices,
erbar2021gradient
and
burger2023dynamic
, are based on a dynamic formulation of the Wasserstein distance on a metric graph, where the second approach is closely related to the study of transport on coupled domains of different dimension (the 1-dimensional edges and the 0-dimensional vertices), such as
monsaingeon2021new
;
CarioniKrautzPietschmann2025_PreferentialPaths
;
CarioniKrautzPietschmann2025_StarShapedGraphs
.

While the setting of metric graphs is a convenient way to model gas networks, it has the inherent limitation that pipes are modeled as one-dimensional entities, only, utilizing an averaging argument over the pipe cross sections. This gives rise to the question whether this approach can be justified by a limiting procedure, starting from a fully three-dimensional model and, in turn, letting the diameter of the pipes go to zero. We study this question in Section
3
and are indeed able to prove the convergence of the Wasserstein metric in its static formulation (corresponding to the case without mass storage on vertices). This serves as a starting point for further investigations of the convergence of the (equivalent) dynamic formulation and, subsequently, also the convergence of Wasserstein gradient flows.

2
Dynamic transport distances on metric graphs

Our next aim is to give a brief review of dynamic transport distances on metric graphs and their connection to the ISO3 model, following the results of
erbar2021gradient
;
Heinze2024
;
Fazeny_2025
;
weigand2025pdiffusion
.

2.1
Metric graphs

Using the notation of
Heinze2024
, we start with an undirected connected graph, which is a (finite) node set
{\mathsf{V}}
with edge set
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathsf{V}}\times{\mathsf{V}}
, such that if
{\mathsf{v}}{\mathsf{w}}\in{\mathsf{E}}
then
{\mathsf{w}}{\mathsf{v}}\not\in{\mathsf{E}}
. We use
{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{v}})
to denote the set of all edges adjacent to node
{\mathsf{v}}
. Based on this combinatorial graph
({\mathsf{V}},{\mathsf{E}})
, we construct a metric graph
{\mathsf{M}}
by fixing an orientation for each edge
{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}
and then associating this oriented edge with a finite line segment
[0,\ell^{\mathsf{e}}]\subset\mathbb{R}
, for some edge length
\ell^{\mathsf{e}}>0
.

To fix notations, given
{\mathsf{e}}={\mathsf{v}}{\mathsf{w}}
, we will call
{\mathsf{v}}

tail
and
{\mathsf{w}}

head
and give those the orientation

{\mathsf{n}}:{\mathsf{V}}\times{\mathsf{E}}\to\left\{-1,0,1\right\}\quad\text{with}\quad{\mathsf{n}}_{{\mathsf{v}}}^{\mathsf{e}}=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{if }{\mathsf{v}}\text{ is the tail node of }{\mathsf{e}};\\
0,&\text{if }{\mathsf{v}}\not\in{\mathsf{e}};\\
1,&\text{if }{\mathsf{v}}\text{ is the head node of }{\mathsf{e}}.\end{cases}

(4)

Note that the orientation of edges is used to determine the flow direction later on, with a positive sign of the velocity if the flow goes from head to tail, and a negative sign otherwise.

We associate the edge length
\ell^{\mathsf{e}}
to each
{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}
via the function

\ell:{\mathsf{E}}\to(0,\infty),\qquad\text{giving rise to the set}\qquad{\mathsf{L}}=\bigsqcup_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}{\left[{0,\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\right]}\,.

(5)

Here, and in the following,
\bigsqcup_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}{\left[{0,\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\right]}
denotes the disjoint union, also called coproduct, understood as the set of ordered pairs
\bigcup_{e\in{\mathsf{E}}}{\left\{{(e,[0,\ell^{e}])}\right\}}
.
The
metric graph
is now the triplet
{\mathsf{M}}={\left({{\mathsf{V}},{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}
, where in our application,
{\mathsf{V}}
represents the set of pipe junctions and pipe endpoints, while
{\mathsf{E}}
are the pipes of the gas network, which we assume to be finite and connected.

Definition 1
(1D distance on metric graph)

For
x,y\in{\mathsf{G}}
, we define the local distance as

d_{\text{loc}}{\left({x,y}\right)}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\left\lvert{x-y}\right\rvert},&\quad\text{if}~x,y\in{\left[{0,\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\right]},~\text{for the same edge}~{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}\\
+\infty,&\quad\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.,

and extend it to the whole metric graph
{\mathsf{G}}
with edges
{\mathsf{E}}
as

d{\left({x,y}\right)}:=\min_{\emptyset\nsubseteq{\left\{{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n}}\right\}}\subseteq{\left\{{1,2,\dots,k}\right\}}}{\left\{{\sum_{i=i_{1}}^{i_{n}}d_{\text{loc}}{\left({x_{i},y_{i}}\right)}}~\middle|~{\begin{array}[]{l}x_{i_{1}}=x,y_{i_{n}}=y,\\
\forall j\in{\left\{{1,2,\dots,n-1}\right\}}:\\
y_{i_{j}},x_{i_{j+1}}\in{\mathsf{V}},\,y_{i_{j}}\sim x_{i_{j+1}}\end{array}}\right\}}.

(GD)

The metric graph distance corresponds to the shortest path from
x\in{\mathsf{e}}_{i_{1}}
to
y\in{\mathsf{e}}_{i_{n}}
along the edges
{\mathsf{e}}_{i_{2}},{\mathsf{e}}_{i_{3}},\dots,{\mathsf{e}}_{i_{n-1}}
.

Note that with the results from
mugnolo2019actually
, we immediately obtain that
{\left({{\mathsf{G}},d}\right)}
is a geodesic space. Furthermore, the previously introduced Wasserstein distance is hence also well-defined in the metric graph setting, and by
erbar2021gradient
, we get that
{\left({{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)},W_{2}}\right)}
is a geodesic space as well. For general
p\geq 1
, in
lisini2007characterization
, it was shown that
{\left({{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)},W_{p}}\right)}
is a geodesic space.

2.2
Continuity equations

To obtain a dynamic formulation of optimal transport, we first need to introduce a notion of the continuity equation, generalizing (
3
) to metric graphs. Note that instead of using a velocity vector field, we introduce the momentum (also known as mass flux)
J_{t}=\rho_{t}v_{t}
. This has the advantage of rendering the continuity equation linear in
\rho_{t}
and
J_{t}
, and resulting in a convex optimization problem in (
2-WDD
).

We will separately discuss the case of mass storage on nodes, and the case with classical Kirchhoff conditions. We will closely follow the definitions and notations of
Heinze2024
, but also see
burger2023dynamic
;
Fazeny_2025
for properties of the Wasserstein metric with node mass storage and
erbar2021gradient
for the version with classical Kirchhoff conditions.

Mass storage on nodes

To arrive at a continuity equation structure on
{\mathsf{M}}
, we start with the definition of the continuity equation on the metric edges
{\mathsf{L}}
, for which we specify a family of time-dependent (and in the case of
j
signed) measures
\rho:[0,1]\to{\mathcal{M}}_{\geq 0}({\mathsf{L}}),~\rho{\left({t}\right)}\eqqcolon\rho_{t}
and fluxes
j:[0,1]\to{\mathcal{M}}({\mathsf{L}}),~j{\left({t}\right)}\eqqcolon j_{t}
. Here, we write the shortcut notation
\rho_{t}\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathsf{L}})
for
\rho_{t}={\left\{{\rho_{t}^{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathcal{M}}{\left({{\left[{0,\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\right]}}\right)}}\right\}}_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}
, and
j_{t}\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathsf{L}})
for
j_{t}={\left\{{j^{e}\in{\mathcal{M}}{\left({{\left[{0,\ell^{e}}\right]}}\right)}}\right\}}_{e\in{\mathsf{E}}}
and we call the pair
{\left({\rho,j}\right)}
a solution of the continuity equation if ut holds true that

\forall t\in{\left({0,1}\right)},\,x\in{\left({0,\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}:\quad\partial_{t}\rho_{t}^{\mathsf{e}}{\left({x}\right)}+\partial_{x}j_{t}^{\mathsf{e}}{\left({x}\right)}=0,\qquad\text{for all edges}~{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}},

(6)

where for all
{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}
we have measures
\rho^{\mathsf{e}}
and
j^{\mathsf{e}}
from the dual space of the continuous functions defined on
{\left[{0,\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\right]}
. Note that we do not impose any continuity or differentiability constraints at the nodes.

To obtain a mass preserving evolution, we complement the continuity equation with a local Kirchhoff condition, which receives the mass leaving from the metric edges due to the net-balance of the normal fluxes of
\{j^{\mathsf{e}}\}_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}
evaluated in the head- and tail nodes of the edges, and which acts as a reservoir for each of the metric edges.
Since, in general,
j_{t}\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathsf{L}})
does not posses a normal flux, we introduce a new set of variables
\bar{\jmath}^{\mathsf{e}}_{\mathsf{v}}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
for any
({\mathsf{v}},{\mathsf{e}})\in{\mathsf{V}}\times{\mathsf{E}}
, which are coupled in a weak sense by imposing the following integration by parts formula on the metric edges: For any
j^{\mathsf{e}}_{t}\in{\mathcal{M}}{\left({{\left[{0,\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\right]}}\right)}
with smooth density
j^{\mathsf{e}}_{t}(x)\;\mathrm{d}{x}
, we impose the identity

\int_{0}^{\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\varphi^{\mathsf{e}}(x)\partial_{x}j_{t}^{\mathsf{e}}(x)\;\mathrm{d}{x}=\sum_{{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}({\mathsf{e}})}\varphi^{\mathsf{e}}|_{{\mathsf{v}}}\,\bar{\jmath}^{\mathsf{e}}_{{\mathsf{v}},t}-\int_{0}^{\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\partial_{x}\varphi^{\mathsf{e}}\;\mathrm{d}j^{\mathsf{e}}_{t}.

(7)

Here, we write
{\mathsf{V}}{\left({{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}
for the set of the head- and tail node of a given edge
{\mathsf{e}}
and
\varphi:{\mathsf{L}}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes a test function, which is continuous across all nodes and continuously differentiable within the respective edge.

The net-balance is stored in the node density
\gamma_{\mathsf{v}}
for each node
{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}
and the
local Kirchhoff law
becomes

\forall{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}:\qquad\partial_{t}\gamma_{{\mathsf{v}},t}=\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{v}})}\bar{\jmath}^{\mathsf{e}}_{{\mathsf{v}},t}\qquad\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1].

(8)

We define the space of probability measures on
{\mathsf{M}}
as tuples
\mu_{t}=(\gamma_{t},\rho_{t})\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathsf{M}})\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{\geq 0}({\mathsf{V}})\times{\mathcal{M}}_{\geq 0}({\mathsf{L}})
such that
\gamma_{t}({\mathsf{V}})+\rho_{t}({\mathsf{L}})=1
. We are now in a position to introduce our notion of the continuity equation on a metric graph with node mass storage.

Definition 2
(Continuity equation,
Heinze2024

A family of curves
\mu=\left(\gamma,\rho\right):[0,1]\to{\mathcal{P}}({\mathsf{M}})
with a flux pair

if (

) and (

) hold, and
{\left({\mu,{\mathrm{j}}}\right)}
solves the abstract continuity equation

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}+\mathop{\mathsf{div}}\nolimits{\mathrm{j}}_{t}=0,

(9)

where for a.e.
t\in{\left({0,1}\right)}
, the measures
\mu
and

are form the dual space of the continuous functions defined on
{\mathsf{V}}\times{\mathsf{L}}
. For such a solution we then write
{\left({\mu,{\mathrm{j}}}\right)}\in{\mathsf{CE}}_{\mathsf{V}}
. In terms of duality, for
\Phi=(\phi,\varphi)\in C^{1}({\mathsf{V}}\times{\mathsf{L}})
the abstract version of the continuity equation is defined by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\bigg[\sum_{{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}}\phi_{\mathsf{v}}\gamma_{{\mathsf{v}},t}+\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}\int_{0}^{\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\mkern-8.0mu\varphi^{\mathsf{e}}(x){\,\mathrm{d}}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}_{t}\bigg]=

(10)

Kirchhoff case

In the case of classical Kirchhoff’s law at the nodes, we make use of the continuity equation structure introduced in
erbar2021gradient
, which is set on the quotient space

{\mathsf{G}}\coloneqq{\mathsf{L}}/\sim,

where the equivalence relation
\sim
identifies the head and tail nodes of different intervals corresponding to the same node. This means that we consider a situation where the edges are glued together at the nodes to form one connected space domain.

The main difference compared to the previous section is the use of test functions that are continuous over the complete graph. Thus, the boundary terms which were present in (
10
) disappear in the weak formulation, encoding a Kirchhoff condition on the fluxes.

Definition 3
(Continuity equation (Kirchhoff)
erbar2021gradient
)

A family of curves
{\left({\rho_{t},J_{t}}\right)}_{t\in[0,1]}
consisting of probability measures
\rho_{t}\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)}
and signed measures
J_{t}\in{\mathcal{M}\left({{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}
,
such that for all Borel sets
A
the map
t\mapsto{\left({\rho_{t}{\left({A}\right)},J_{t}{\left({A}\right)}}\right)}
is Borel measurable, is called a weak solution of
continuity equation on the metric graph with Kirchhoff condition
if for all test functions
\psi\in C^{1}{\left({{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}\cap C{\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)}
and a.e.
t\in{\left({0,1}\right)}
it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathsf{G}}\psi{\,\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}=\int_{\mathsf{L}}\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot{\,\mathrm{d}}J_{t},

(12)

where we assume

(1)

\forall\psi:\,t\mapsto\int_{\mathsf{G}}\psi{\,\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}
is absolutely continuous,

(2)

\int_{{\left({0,1}\right)}}{\left\lvert{J_{t}}\right\rvert}{\left({{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}{\,\mathrm{d}}t<\infty
.

We denote by
{\mathsf{CE}}_{K}(\rho^{0},\rho^{1})
the set of all weak solutions
{\left({\rho,J}\right)}
to (
12
) such that
\rho_{0}=\rho^{0}
and
\rho_{1}=\rho^{1}
.

Remark 1

The Kirchhoff condition is implicitly contained in the weak formulation (
12
). Indeed, if the flux would be sufficiently regular to allow for integration by parts, due to the continuity of the test function across nodes, we would obtain that

\sum_{e\in{\mathsf{E}}(v)}\bar{\jmath}^{\mathsf{e}}_{{\mathsf{v}}}=\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{v}})}j_{t}^{\mathsf{e}}{\left({{\mathsf{v}}}\right)}{\mathsf{n}}_{{\mathsf{v}}}^{\mathsf{e}}=0\qquad\text{for all}~{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}

must hold.

2.3
Action functionals and dynamic distances

We are now able to define dynamic transport distances based on the minimization of action functionals over curves which satisfy the respective continuity equations.

Mass storage on nodes

In view of our application, mass storage on nodes may have different interpretations. It is for example possible to use mass storage on nodes to model gas inflow and outflow for the network. To this end, one differentiates between boundary nodes
\partial{\mathsf{V}}
, where gas may enter or exit the network (as supply or demand), and interior nodes
\mathring{{\mathsf{V}}}
. Then, the set of interior nodes is defined as

\mathring{{\mathsf{V}}}={\mathsf{V}}\backslash\partial{\mathsf{V}},

which directly implies
\partial{\mathsf{V}}\cup\mathring{{\mathsf{V}}}={\mathsf{V}}
and
\partial{\mathsf{V}}\cap\mathring{{\mathsf{V}}}=\emptyset
so that each node is either a boundary node or an interior node.

For the set of boundary nodes
\partial{\mathsf{V}}
, it is possible to differentiate further between source nodes
\partial^{+}{\mathsf{V}}
, which supply the network with gas, and sink nodes
\partial^{-}{\mathsf{V}}
, at which gas is taken out of the network to meet given demands.
The inflow and outflow of gas into and out from the network can then be modeled via time-dependent boundary conditions at
\partial{\mathsf{V}}
. For the sake of readability, however, we will limit our exposition to the case without boundary nodes and refer to
Fazeny_2025
for details on those.

For given initial- and final mass distributions
\mu^{0}:=\left(\gamma^{0},\rho^{0}\right)
and
\mu^{1}:=\left(\gamma^{1},\rho^{1}\right)
, we define the dynamical formulation of the Wasserstein distance as

\displaystyle W_{{\mathsf{V}},p}{\left({\mu^{0},\mu^{1}}\right)}=

(13)

\displaystyle\min_{{\left({\mu,j}\right)}\in{\mathsf{CE}}_{{\mathsf{V}}}{\left({\mu^{0},\mu^{1}}\right)}}\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}\left(\int_{0}^{\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}h\left(j_{t}^{\mathsf{e}},\rho_{t}^{\mathsf{e}}\right){\,\mathrm{d}}\eta\right)+\sum_{{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}}h\left(\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{v}})}\bar{\jmath}^{\mathsf{e}}_{{\mathsf{v}},t},\gamma_{{\mathsf{v}},t}\right){\,\mathrm{d}}t\Bigg\}^{1/p},

where the
perspective function

h:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is given as

\left(a,b\right)\mapsto h\left(a,b\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\left\lvert a\right\rvert^{p}}{b^{p-1}},&\text{if}~b>0,\\
0,&\text{if}~b=a=0,\\
+\infty,&\text{if}~b<0~\vee~b=0,\,a\neq 0,\end{array}\right.

and it is responsible for penalizing negative densities and non-zero flux in the case of zero densities. Here,
\eta
denotes a reference measure and, abusing notation, we denote densities of
j^{\mathsf{e}}
and
\rho^{\mathsf{e}}
with respect to
\eta
still by the same symbols.

While (
13
) was introduced in
Fazeny_2025
, the gradient flows studied in
Heinze2024
are understood with respect to a different version of the Wasserstein distance. Indeed, in (
13
), the contributions at the nodes depend on

\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{v}})}\bar{\jmath}^{\mathsf{e}}_{{\mathsf{v}},t},

i.e., the net flux summing the contributions of all edges adjacent to the node
{\mathsf{v}}
. In contrast to that, in
Heinze2024
, each contribution is considered separately which yields the metric

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{{\mathsf{V}},p}(\mu^{0},\mu^{1})=

(14)

\displaystyle\min_{(\mu,j)\in{\mathsf{CE}}_{{\mathsf{V}}}(\mu^{0},\mu^{1})}\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}\left(\int_{0}^{\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}h\left(j^{\mathsf{e}}_{t},\rho^{\mathsf{e}}_{t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\eta\right)+\sum_{{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}}\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{v}})}h\left(\bar{\jmath}^{\mathsf{e}}_{{\mathsf{v}},t},\gamma_{{\mathsf{v}},t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\}^{1/p}.

Kirchhoff case

In the classical Kirchhoff case, we are considering curves
(\rho,J)\in{\mathsf{CE}}_{K}\left(\rho^{0},\rho^{1}\right)
for given initial- and final densities
\rho^{0},\rho^{1}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathsf{G}})
. The Wasserstein distance is then given by

W_{K,p}(\rho^{0},\rho^{1})=\min_{{\left({\rho,\rho v}\right)}\in{\mathsf{CE}}_{K}{\left({\rho^{0},\rho^{1}}\right)}}{\left\{{\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}\int_{0}^{\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}{\left\lvert{v_{t}}\right\rvert}^{p}{\,\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t}\right\}}^{1/p},

with
{\mathsf{CE}}_{K}{\left({\rho^{0},\rho^{1}}\right)}
as in Definition
3
.
In order for the right hand side of
J_{t}=v_{t}\,\rho_{t}
to be well-defined, we need a characterization of absolutely continuous curves in the
p
-Wasserstein space. This is given by a theorem proven in
weigand2025pdiffusion
, which requires the following two definitions.

Definition 4
(Absolutely continuous curve
ambrosio2005gradient
)

For
p\geq 1
, a curve
\zeta:{\left[{0,1}\right]}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)}
is called
p
-absolutely continuous in the
p
-Wasserstein space, if there exists a function
m\in L^{p}{\left({0,1}\right)}
such that for all
0<s\leq t<1
it holds true that

W_{p}{\left({\zeta{\left({s}\right)},\zeta{\left({t}\right)}}\right)}\leq\int_{s}^{t}m{\left({r}\right)}{\,\mathrm{d}}r.

(15)

Definition 5
(Metric derivative
ambrosio2005gradient
)

For every
p
-absolutely continuous curve (in the
p
-Wasserstein space)
\zeta:{\left[{0,1}\right]}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)}
, its metric derivative is defined as

\displaystyle{\left\lvert{\dot{\zeta}}\right\rvert}{\left({t}\right)}\coloneq\lim_{s\rightarrow t}\frac{d{\left({\zeta{\left({s}\right)},\zeta{\left({t}\right)}}\right)}}{{\left\lvert{s-t}\right\rvert}}

for
t\in{\left({0,1}\right)}
and it exists for a.e. such
t
, where
d
is the shortest path distance on the metric graph, see (
GD
).

In
ambrosio2005gradient
, it is proven that the metric derivative of a
p
-absolutely continuous curve exists for a.e.
t\in{\left({0,1}\right)}
and that
{\left\lvert{\dot{\zeta}}\right\rvert}\in L^{p}{\left({0,1}\right)}
. Furthermore,
{\left\lvert{\dot{\zeta}}\right\rvert}
satisfies (
15
) chosen as
m
, and
{\left\lvert{\dot{\zeta}}\right\rvert}
is minimal, meaning that for any other
m\in L^{p}{\left({0,1}\right)}
satisfying the equation, it holds true that
{\left\lvert{\dot{\zeta}}\right\rvert}{\left({t}\right)}\leq m{\left({t}\right)}
for a.e.
t\in{\left({0,1}\right)}
.

Theorem 2.1
(Absolutely continuous curves)

(i)

If
\rho:{\left[{0,1}\right]}\to{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)}
is an absolutely continuous curve in
{\left({{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)},W_{p}}\right)}

then there exists, for a.e.
t\in{\left({0,1}\right)}
, a vector field
v_{t}\in L^{p}{\left({\rho_{t}}\right)}
such that
\int_{\mathsf{G}}{\left\lvert\left\lvert{v_{t}}\right\rvert\right\rvert}{\,\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}\leq{\left\lvert{\dot{\rho}}\right\rvert}{\left({t}\right)}
and
{\left({\rho,\rho v}\right)}
is a weak solution to the continuity equation, as defined in
3
.

(ii)

If
{\left({\rho,\rho v}\right)}

is a weak solution to the continuity equation, as specified in
3
and

\int_{{\left({0,1}\right)}}\int_{\mathsf{G}}{\left\lvert\left\lvert{v_{t}}\right\rvert\right\rvert}{\,\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t<\infty,

then
\rho:{\left[{0,1}\right]}\to{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)}
is an absolutely continuous curve in
{\left({{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)},W_{p}}\right)}
and for almost every
t\in{\left({0,1}\right)}
, we obtain
{\left\lvert{\dot{\rho}{\left({t}\right)}}\right\rvert}\leq\int_{\mathsf{G}}{\left\lvert\left\lvert{{\mathrm{v}}_{t}}\right\rvert\right\rvert}{\,\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}
.

This result was already proven for
p\!=\!2
in
erbar2021gradient
, and it was generalized to
p\!\geq\!1
in
weigand2025pdiffusion
. It is an important result, as Theorem
2.1
(i) shows that every
W_{p}
-absolutely continuous curve
\rho_{t}
can be realized as a physical mass flow with an
L^{p}
-finite velocity field
v_{t}
and mass conservation, so
\rho_{t}
encodes a feasible optimal transport of the gas through the network. Moreover, Theorem
2.1
(ii) shows the opposite direction, meaning that having a mass flow with finite
p
-energy, which also satisfies the continuity equation, then the corresponding mass trajectory
\rho_{t}
is an
W_{p}
-absolutely continuous curve, thus any weak solution of
12
with an integrable velocity
v_{t}
is a feasible optimal transport. Regarding the dynamic formulation of the
p
-Wasserstein distance, this theorem ensures that the mass flux
J_{t}
from a weak solution of the continuity equation can be decomposed into
J_{t}=\rho_{t}\,v_{t}
.

2.4
Gradient flows on metric graphs

Since the introduction of Otto-Wasserstein gradient flow,
JordanKinderlehrerOtto1997
;
JordanKinderlehrerOtto1998
;
Otto2001
, many equations were recognized as gradient flows in Wasserstein-type metrics. We briefly review some results for equations set on metric graphs.

Mass storage on nodes

So far, gradient flows in the setting of mass storage on nodes have not been studied in relation to gas networks. Instead, linear drift-diffusion equations were considered in
Heinze2024
.
To introduce the system, we fix a positive reference probability measure
(\omega,\pi)\in{\mathcal{P}}_{+}({\mathsf{M}})
, where we assume
\pi
to have a density given by
\pi(\mathrm{dx})=e^{-P(x)}\mathrm{dx}
for some potential
P:{\mathsf{L}}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that
P^{\mathsf{e}}:{\left[{0,\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}\right]}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz for all
{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}
. With this, we consider for a.e.
t\in(0,1)
, the system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}=d^{\mathsf{e}}\partial_{x}\cdot\left(\partial_{x}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}+\rho^{\mathsf{e}}\partial_{x}P^{\mathsf{e}}\right),

\displaystyle\text{on }[0,\ell^{e}]\text{ for every }{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}},

(16a)

\displaystyle-d^{\mathsf{e}}\left.\Big(\partial_{x}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}+\rho^{\mathsf{e}}\partial_{x}P^{\mathsf{e}}\right)\Big|_{{\mathsf{v}}}\cdot{\mathsf{n}}_{{\mathsf{v}}}^{\mathsf{e}}=r({\mathsf{e}},{\mathsf{v}})\,\left.\rho^{\mathsf{e}}\right|_{{\mathsf{v}}}-r({\mathsf{v}},{\mathsf{e}})\gamma_{\mathsf{v}},

\displaystyle\text{for all }{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}},\,{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}},

(16b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\gamma_{\mathsf{v}}=\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{v}})}\Big(r({\mathsf{e}},{\mathsf{v}})\,\left.\rho^{\mathsf{e}}\right|_{{\mathsf{v}}}-r({\mathsf{v}},{\mathsf{e}})\gamma_{\mathsf{v}}\Big),

\displaystyle\text{for all }{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}\,.

(16c)

We set
\left.\rho^{\mathsf{e}}\right|_{{\mathsf{v}}}=\rho^{\mathsf{e}}(0)
if
{\mathsf{e}}={\mathsf{v}}{\mathsf{w}}
, and we set
\left.\rho^{\mathsf{e}}\right|_{\mathsf{v}}=\rho^{\mathsf{e}}(\ell^{\mathsf{e}})
if
{\mathsf{e}}={\mathsf{w}}{\mathsf{v}}
for some
{\mathsf{w}}\in{\mathsf{V}}
.
Moreover, we introduced the parameter
d^{\mathsf{e}}>0
as the diffusion constant on the metric edge
{\mathsf{e}}
,
r({\mathsf{e}},{\mathsf{v}})
as the jump rate from the endpoint of the metric edge associated with
{\mathsf{e}}
to an adjacent node
{\mathsf{v}}
, and
r({\mathsf{v}},{\mathsf{e}})
for the reverse jump rate from the node
{\mathsf{v}}
to the adjacent edge
{\mathsf{e}}
.

The main result of
Heinze2024
is that system (
16
) admits a gradient flow formulation, provided that the jump rates satisfy the
detailed balance condition
, with respect to the reference measure
(\omega,\pi)\in{\mathcal{P}}_{+}({\mathsf{M}})
, that is it holds

r({\mathsf{e}},{\mathsf{v}})\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{\mathsf{e}}\smash[t]{|_{\mathsf{v}}}}{\omega_{\mathsf{v}}}}=r({\mathsf{v}},{\mathsf{e}})\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\mathsf{v}}}{\pi^{\mathsf{e}}|_{\mathsf{v}}}}\eqqcolon{\mathscr{k}}^{\mathsf{e}}_{{\mathsf{v}}}\qquad\text{for all }{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}},\,{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}\,.

(17)

The driving entropy functional is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathsf{L}}}(\rho)+{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathsf{V}}}(\gamma)\coloneqq\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}{\mathcal{H}}\left(\rho^{\mathsf{e}}\middle|\pi^{\mathsf{e}}\right)+\sum_{{\mathsf{v}}\in{\mathsf{V}}}{\mathcal{H}}\left(\gamma_{\mathsf{v}}\middle|\omega_{\mathsf{v}}\right)\,,

(18)

where the relative entropy
{\mathcal{H}}
is defined as

{\mathcal{H}}\left(\mu\middle|\nu\right)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\int\eta\big(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu}\big){\,\mathrm{d}}\nu,&\text{if }\mu\ll\nu\,,\\
+\infty,&\text{else,}\end{cases}

(19)

where
\eta(r)\coloneqq r\log r-r+1
for
r\geq 0
.
Here and in the following, we use the convention that
0\cdot\log 0=0
.

Remark 2

As the above system (
16
) is a combination of diffusion on the metric edges and a reaction-like mechanism for the mass exchange between edges and nodes, the gradient flow structure also consists of two parts. While the edge dynamics can be captured by quadratic action functionals as introduced above, the reaction part requires a non-linear
\cosh
-type functional. Thus, even on a formal level, the combination of quadratic diffusion terms with non-linear reaction terms does not yield a metric structure, and instead a JKO approach as in (
20
), one uses an
\mathcal{R}^{*}
–
\mathcal{R}
formulation, see also
LieroMielkePeletierRenger2017
for details.

Kirchhoff case

In
Fazeny_2025
, it was shown that the (
ISO3
) model is a
W_{K,3}
-gradient flow with respect to the energy functional

\mathcal{E}{\left({\rho}\right)}\coloneqq\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{e}}{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}=\sum_{{\mathsf{e}}\in{\mathsf{E}}}\int_{0}^{\ell^{\mathsf{e}}}F^{\mathsf{e}}{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}+\frac{2D^{\mathsf{e}}g}{\lambda^{\mathsf{e}}}\sin{\left({\omega^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}x+d^{\mathsf{e}}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}{\,\mathrm{d}}x.

Here, the entropy density
F^{\mathsf{e}}
for each edge
{\mathsf{e}}
is defined based on the pressure-entropy relation

{F^{\mathsf{e}}}^{\prime\prime}{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}=\frac{2D^{\mathsf{e}}}{\lambda^{\mathsf{e}}}\frac{p^{\prime}{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}}{\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}

together with
F^{\mathsf{e}}{\left({0}\right)}\coloneqq 0
. Moreover, the integration constants
d_{e}
arise from the fact that even though the global mass within the network is conserved, the mass per edge can change between
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
.

In order to derive
p
-Wasserstein gradient flows with the defined energy
\mathcal{E}
, the optimality conditions of the
minimizing movement scheme

\rho_{{\left({i+1}\right)}\tau}=\text{arg}\min_{\rho\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathsf{G}}}\right)}}{\left\{{\frac{1}{p\,\tau^{p-1}}W_{K,p}^{p}{\left({\rho,\rho_{i\tau}}\right)}+\mathcal{E}{\left({\rho}\right)}}\right\}},

(20)

starting at the initial network state
\rho_{0}\coloneqq\rho_{0}
, for step size
\tau>0
are analyzed. It is then proven that for
\tau\rightarrow 0
, an appropriate time interpolation of the minimizers converges to a solution of the respective
p
-Wasserstein gradient flow.

The minimizing movement scheme provides a variational time discretization of the gradient flow associated with the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
in the
p
-Wasserstein space, where in each time step the new
\rho_{{\left({i+1}\right)}\tau}
is chosen such that on the one hand it is “close enough” to the previous network state
\rho_{i\tau}
(minimizing the transport cost), and on the other hand such that the new network state is a low energy state (as we aim at minimizing the energy
\mathcal{E}
).

Taking
\tau\rightarrow 0
in the optimality conditions, then the resulting equation system of the
p
-Wasserstein gradient flow is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}+\partial_{x}j^{\mathsf{e}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle j^{\mathsf{e}}\,{\left\lvert{j^{\mathsf{e}}}\right\rvert}^{p-2}

\displaystyle=-{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}^{p-1}\,\partial_{x}{\left({\mathcal{E}^{\prime}{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}}\right)}.

Plugging in our specific choice for the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
as well as
p=3
results in (
ISO3
), expressed by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}+\partial_{x}j^{\mathsf{e}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle j^{\mathsf{e}}\,{\left\lvert{j^{\mathsf{e}}}\right\rvert}^{p-2}

\displaystyle=-{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}^{2}\,{\left({\frac{2D^{\mathsf{e}}}{\lambda^{\mathsf{e}}\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\partial_{x}p^{\mathsf{e}}{\left({\rho^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}+\frac{2D^{\mathsf{e}}g}{\lambda^{\mathsf{e}}}\sin{\left({\omega^{\mathsf{e}}}\right)}}\right)}.

For the same Wasserstein distance, yet in the case
p=2
, the authors of
erbar2021gradient
rigorously proved that the drift–diffusion–aggregation equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta\rho+\nabla\cdot\Big(\rho{\left({\nabla V+\nabla W{\left[{\rho}\right]}}\right)}\Big)

with non-local interaction part

W{\left[{\mu}\right]}(x):=\int_{{\mathsf{G}}}W(x,y){\,\mathrm{d}}\mu(y)

is a gradient flow with respect to the metric
W_{K,2}
.
In this case, the energy functional is given as a sum of the logarithmic entropy and the non-local part

\mathcal{W}(\rho):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathsf{G}}\times{\mathsf{G}}}W(x,y){\,\mathrm{d}}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}\rho(y),

where
W:{\mathsf{G}}\times{\mathsf{G}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is symmetric and Lipschitz continuous.

3
Zero-width limit of gas networks

To prove our main results we need to introduce some concepts that can be found in most textbooks on the subject of optimal transport, such as
villani2008optimal
;
Santambrogio2015
. The major concepts introduced below were already motivated in the introduction.

Definition 6
(Cyclical monotonicity)

Let
X
,
Y
be arbitrary
sets, and take a cost function
c:X\times Y\to(-\infty,+\infty]
. A subset
\Gamma\subset X\times Y

is said to be
c
-cyclically monotone if, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and any family

(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{N},y_{N})
of points in
\Gamma
, the inequality

\sum_{i=1}^{N}c(x_{i},y_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}c(x_{i},y_{i+1}).

holds, using the convention
y_{N+1}=y_{1}
. A transference plan or coupling is said to be

c
-cyclically monotone if it is concentrated on a
c
-cyclically monotone set.

Definition 7
(
c
-concavity)

A function
\phi:Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is called
c
-concave if it
is not identically
-\infty
and there exists
\psi:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
,
such that

\phi(y)=\inf_{x\in X}\Big\{c(x,y)-\psi(x)\Big\}.

Theorem 3.1
(Kantorovich Duality)

Let
X
and
Y
be Polish spaces, let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
, and let

be lower semicontinuous. Then the following holds:

1.

Kantorovich duality:

\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,c):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}{\left\{{\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi}\right\}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi\in L^{1}(\mu),\,\psi\in L^{1}(\nu),\\
\phi(x)\,+\,\psi(y)\,\leq\,c(x,y)\end{subarray}}{\left\{{\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\right\}}.

(21)

2.

If c is real-valued and the optimal cost
\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,c)
is finite, then there exists a
\pi
-measurable
c
-cyclically monotone set

(closed if

is continuous) such that for any

the following statements are equivalent:

(a)
\pi
is optimal;

(b)
\pi
is c-cyclically monotone;

(c)
\pi
is concentrated on
\Gamma
;

(d) there exists a

-concave function

such that the equation

holds

For a proof we refer to Theorem 5.10 in

3.2
Main results

Let

{\varepsilon}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then,
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
corresponds to the 1D metric graph embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, thus its intrinsic dimension is 1.

For a well-defined limit
{\varepsilon}\to 0
of the densities, describing the distribution of gas in the network, we need probability measures
{\left({{\mu_{\varepsilon}}}\right)}_{\varepsilon},{\left({{\nu_{\varepsilon}}}\right)}_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({\mathbb{R}^{3}}\right)}
with support
{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
, which weakly converge to probability measures
{\mu_{0}},{\nu_{0}}\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathcal{N}_{0}}}\right)}
. These limits
{\mu_{0}}
and

correspond to lifted versions of probability measures
{\overline{\mu}},{\overline{\nu}}\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\overline{\mathcal{N}}}}\right)}
, where the mass is allocated only along the lines of
{\overline{\mathcal{N}}}
. This means that
\mu_{0}
and

can be written as

\mu_{0}=\overline{\mu}\otimes\delta_{x_{2},x_{3}=0}\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}}\right)}\quad\text{and}\quad\nu_{0}=\overline{\nu}\otimes\delta_{y_{2},y_{3}=0}\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}}\right)},

for the Dirac measure

\delta_{z_{2},z_{3}=0}{\left({Z}\right)}=\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\quad\text{if}~\exists z\in Z:~z_{2}=z_{3}=0,\\

In the following, for the support of the probability measures we will use the short-hand notation

.

3.3
Convergence on the entire network.

We first define a penalty function
\iota_{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, that incorporates the geometry of the 3D gas network
{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
, penalizing any transport outside of the network.
For a compact set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, let

\iota_{U}(x):=\begin{cases}\ 0,\quad\quad&\text{if}~x\in U,\\
\ +\infty,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

It is a standard result from convex analysis that
\iota_{U}(x)
is lower-semicontinuous when
U
is closed.

By
\mathcal{C}(x,y)
we denote the set of piecewise continuously differentiable curves
\gamma:{\left[{0,1}\right]}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3},\,t\mapsto\gamma_{t}
with endpoints
x,y
, i.e.,
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(x,y)
implies that
\gamma_{0}=x
,
\gamma_{1}=y
and
\dot{\gamma}_{t}:=\mathrm{d}\gamma_{t}/\mathrm{d}t
is well-defined for a.e.
t\in{\left[{0,1}\right]}
.

Define the cost function
{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y):\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
as

{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y):=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(x,y)}{\left\{{\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}_{t}\|^{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma_{t}){\,\mathrm{d}}t}\right\}}.

(22)

Importantly, remark that for two points
x,y
in the same pipe of the three-dimensional network, the infimum in (
22
) is attained and the optimal path
\gamma_{t}
is a straight line between
x
and
y
. The defined cost function is particularly attractive as it combines the aforementioned property, while ensuring that the cost function is well defined between any two points of the network and thus gives rise to a geodesic-type of distance.

The optimal transport problem on the 3D network is then given by

\operatorname{OT}{\left({{\mu_{\varepsilon}},{\nu_{\varepsilon}},{c_{\!\varepsilon}}}\right)}=\inf_{{\pi_{\varepsilon}}\in\Pi(\mu_{\varepsilon},\nu_{\varepsilon})}{\left\{{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y){\,\mathrm{d}}{\pi_{\varepsilon}}(x,y)}\right\}},

(OT)

as in (
21
).

Properties of the cost

First, notice that defining cost functions based on action minimizing curves is a classical theme in optimal transport, see for instance
(
villani2008optimal
, Chapter 7)
. The problem of interest can be thought of as plain optimal transport, where the trajectories
\gamma
need to avoid exiting the pipes, a problem conceptually not far from optimal transport in the presence of obstacles. In this last setting for instance,
CARDALIAGUET201143
relies on a similar
construction.

In a more computational context,
pooladian2024neural
proposed to study a similar problem where
\iota_{U}(x)
is replaced by a smooth function acting as a barrier. A smooth penalty function has indeed additional computational benefits, however it does not strictly enforce the condition of staying within the network, which could lead to
\gamma
“cutting the corner” at pipe intersections.

The introduced cost function relies on curves minimizing the
action

\mathcal{A}(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}_{t}\|^{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma_{t}){\,\mathrm{d}}t,

where the integrand is often called a
Lagrangian
.
This framework is covered in detail in
(
villani2008optimal
, Chapter 7)
. It can be easily verified that
\|v\|^{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}(x)

is convex and superlinear in
v
.

Lemma 2

For any
x\in\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon})
, the interior of
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
, assume that there is a unique geodesic
\gamma_{t}^{\star}
, i.e., a unique minimizer in the definition of
{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)
, joining
x
to a given
y\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
. Then the cost
{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)
is
C^{1}
in
x
and

\nabla_{x}{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)=-2\dot{\gamma}_{t}^{\star}\big|_{t=0}.

Proof

Under the assumption of a unique optimizer
\gamma_{t}^{\star}
in (
22
), we get

{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}t.

To obtain the derivative, first pick
x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
sufficiently close to
x
, such that there exists a unique geodesic connecting
x
and
x^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
. We will establish the limit as
x^{\prime}\to x
. Then, due to the continuous dependence of
\gamma^{\star}_{t}
on
x
, we can “slightly modify”
\gamma^{\star}_{t}
to become
\gamma_{t}^{\prime}
, which now connects
x^{\prime}
and
y
, instead of
x
and
y
, and thus

{c_{\!\varepsilon}}{\left({x^{\prime},y}\right)}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|{\dot{\gamma}_{t}}^{\prime}\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}t,

as the path
\gamma_{t}^{\prime}
might not be optimal.
The formula of first variation then gives

{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x^{\prime},y)\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\|\dot{\gamma}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}-2\langle\dot{\gamma}_{0}^{\star},x^{\prime}-x\rangle\right)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{o}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|\gamma_{t}^{\star}-\gamma_{t}^{\prime}\|\right),

which quantifies the difference between the action of the optimal curve connecting
x^{\prime}
and
y
, and the action of the slightly perturbed curve
\gamma_{t}^{\prime}
.

A suitable choice of
\gamma^{\prime}_{t}
ensures that
\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|\gamma_{t}^{\star}-\gamma^{\prime}_{t}\|=\|x^{\prime}-x\|
.
Therefore, we obtain by subtracting the equality
{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}t
from the above inequality

{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x^{\prime},y)-{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)\leq-2\langle\dot{\gamma}_{0}^{\star},x^{\prime}-x\rangle+\mathrm{o}\left(\|x^{\prime}-x\|\right),

which establishes that
-2\dot{\gamma}_{0}^{\star}
is an upper gradient of
{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)
.

By exchanging the roles of
x
and
x^{\prime}
, coupled with the continuity of the geodesics, a similar argument then yields a matching lower bound, and the claim follows.

Existence of an optimal map

To prove the existence of a unique coupling

\phi(x)+\phi^{c}(y)={c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)\quad\text{ for }(x,y)\in\operatorname{spt}(\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}).

The idea of deriving

Assuming differentiability of

Main results

We start by establishing that the optimal transport cost (
OT
) converges for
{\varepsilon}\to 0
.
To this end, let us first recall that a measure
\mu
is called atomeless if for all
\mu
-measurable sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\mu{\left({A}\right)}>0
, there exists a
\mu
-measurable set
B\subset A
with
0<\mu{\left({B}\right)}<\mu{\left({A}\right)}
.

Proposition 1

Assume that the sequences
({\mu_{\varepsilon}})
and
({\nu_{\varepsilon}})
are sequences of absolutely continuous probability measures with respect to the Lebesgue measure, that further converge weakly to atomeless probability measures
\mu_{0},\nu_{0}
supported on
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
. Then,

\operatorname{OT}({\mu_{\varepsilon}},{\nu_{\varepsilon}},{c_{\!\varepsilon}})\to\operatorname{OT}({\mu_{0}},{\nu_{0}},{c_{0}}),\quad\text{ as }{\varepsilon}\to 0.

Proof

Denote by
P
the projection of a point from
{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
to the closest point in
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
, with respect to the Euclidean distance, and in the case of the closest point in
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
not being unique (which can happen at the junction of pipes), pick an arbitrary point from the contenders in
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
.

For Lebesgue-almost every pair
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}\times{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
, it holds

{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)\leq{c_{0}}(P(x),P(y))+2{\varepsilon}^{2},

as this construction amounts to picking a non-optimal path between
x
and
y
. Recall that for any
\mu
-nullset
E
, integrating a function
f
with
f(x)=+\infty
for all
x\in E
evaluates at 0, so
\int_{E}f(x){\,\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)=0
. Therefore, we do not need the previous inequality for
x
or
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
.
This then yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}{c_{\!\varepsilon}}{\left({x,y}\right)}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\pi_{\varepsilon}}{\left({x,y}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}(P(x),P(y)){\,\mathrm{d}}{\pi_{\varepsilon}}(x,y)+2{\varepsilon}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}{\,\mathrm{d}}(P,P)_{\#}{\pi_{\varepsilon}}+2{\varepsilon}^{2},

where for any probability measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({X}\right)}
, the notation
F_{\#}\mu
for a measurable function
F:X\rightarrow Y
corresponds to the pushforward measure defined as
F_{\#}\mu\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({Y}\right)}
with
F_{\#}\mu{\left({B}\right)}\coloneqq\mu{\left({F^{-1}{\left({B}\right)}}\right)}
for measurable
B\subseteq Y
.

Now, for any coupling
{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
of the marginals
{\mu_{\varepsilon}},{\nu_{\varepsilon}}
, the term
(P,P)_{\#}\pi_{\varepsilon}
corresponds to a coupling between
P_{\#}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathcal{N}_{0}}}\right)}
and
P_{\#}{\nu_{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathcal{P}\!\left({{\mathcal{N}_{0}}}\right)}
. Optimizing over
{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
is thus equivalent to optimizing over couplings of
P_{\#}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}
and
P_{\#}{\nu_{\varepsilon}}
and so, we obtain

\inf_{{\pi_{\varepsilon}}\in\Pi(\mu_{\varepsilon},\nu_{\varepsilon})}{\left\{{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}{\,\mathrm{d}}(P,P)_{\#}{\pi_{\varepsilon}}}\right\}}=\inf_{\pi_{\varepsilon}^{P}\in\Pi(P_{\#}\mu_{\varepsilon},P_{\#}\nu_{\varepsilon})}{\left\{{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi_{\varepsilon}^{P}}\right\}}.

Further,
P_{\#}{\mu_{\varepsilon}},P_{\#}{\nu_{\varepsilon}}

converge weakly to
P_{\#}{\mu_{0}}={\mu_{0}}
and
P_{\#}{\nu_{0}}={\nu_{0}}
, by the continuous mapping theorem. Note that the assumption on the absence of atoms of the limits
{\mu_{0}},{\nu_{0}}
is required here, because of the absence of continuity of the projection
P
at junctions, which are not charged as
P
is continuous
{\mu_{0}}
- and
{\nu_{0}}
-almost everywhere.

One can then invoke Theorem 1.51 in
Santambrogio2015
(where we once again use the argument that
P_{\#}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus{\mathcal{N}_{0}})=0
and
P_{\#}{\nu_{\varepsilon}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus{\mathcal{N}_{0}})=0
for all
{\varepsilon}>0
, as well as
P_{\#}{\mu_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus{\mathcal{N}_{0}})=0
and
P_{\#}{\nu_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus{\mathcal{N}_{0}})=0
to circumvent the non-compactness of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
{c_{0}}
being non-continuous) to conclude that

\inf_{\pi_{\varepsilon}^{P}\in\Pi(P_{\#}\mu_{\varepsilon},P_{\#}\nu_{\varepsilon})}{\left\{{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi_{\varepsilon}^{P}}\right\}}\to\inf_{{\pi_{0}}\in{\Pi\left({{\mu_{0}},{\nu_{0}}}\right)}}{\left\{{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\pi_{0}}}\right\}}=\operatorname{OT}{\left({{\mu_{0}},{\nu_{0}},{c_{0}}}\right)},

as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
, which yields

\operatorname{OT}{\left({{\mu_{\varepsilon}},{\nu_{\varepsilon}},{c_{\!\varepsilon}}}\right)}\leq\operatorname{OT}{\left({{\mu_{0}},{\nu_{0}},{c_{0}}}\right)}.

In the other direction, for Lebesgue-almost every pair
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}\times{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
, we have

{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)\geq{c_{0}}(P(x),P(y))-K{\varepsilon}^{2},

where
K>0
is some constant depending on the network. This constant

is necessary as the network
{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
allows for shorter paths around a corner (at junctions) compared to the graph
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
. Since the number of pipes and junctions is finite, the amount of path length that can be “saved” in
{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
, compared to
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
, can be bounded from above.

The length of the path obtained by projecting first two points

on
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
, and then joining them by the shortest path on
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
must thus stay within a term

. The claim then follows from a similar argument as above. We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}{c_{\!\varepsilon}}{\left({x,y}\right)}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\pi_{\varepsilon}}{\left({x,y}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}(P(x),P(y)){\,\mathrm{d}}{\pi_{\varepsilon}}(x,y)-K{\varepsilon}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}{\,\mathrm{d}}(P,P)_{\#}{\pi_{\varepsilon}}-K{\varepsilon}^{2}

with the equality from before

\inf_{{\pi_{\varepsilon}}\in\Pi(\mu_{\varepsilon},\nu_{\varepsilon})}{\left\{{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}{\,\mathrm{d}}(P,P)_{\#}{\pi_{\varepsilon}}}\right\}}=\inf_{\pi_{\varepsilon}^{P}\in\Pi(P_{\#}\mu_{\varepsilon},P_{\#}\nu_{\varepsilon})}{\left\{{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}c_{0}{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi_{\varepsilon}^{P}}\right\}},

where the convergence of the right-hand-side term for
{\varepsilon}\to 0
gives us the desired inequality

\operatorname{OT}{\left({{\mu_{\varepsilon}},{\nu_{\varepsilon}},{c_{\!\varepsilon}}}\right)}\geq\operatorname{OT}{\left({{\mu_{0}},{\nu_{0}},{c_{0}}}\right)}.

Therefore, for
{\varepsilon}\to 0
, based on the sandwich theorem, we can conclude

\operatorname{OT}({\mu_{\varepsilon}},{\nu_{\varepsilon}},{c_{\!\varepsilon}})\to\operatorname{OT}({\mu_{0}},{\nu_{0}},{c_{0}}).\qedhere

The question of establishing the convergence of
\pi_{\varepsilon}
as

is thus a stability issue with respect to both measures

and cost
{c_{\!\varepsilon}}
. This question is addressed in Chapter 5 of

.

Our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 3.2

Let
{\left({\mu_{\varepsilon}}\right)}_{\varepsilon},{\left({\nu_{\varepsilon}}\right)}_{\varepsilon}
be sequences of probability measures with support

, and consider the sequence of cost functions
{\left({{c_{\!\varepsilon}}}\right)}_{\varepsilon}

).
Moreover, by

If

) for

weakly as
{\varepsilon}\to 0
.

{\pi_{0}}^{\star}

Proof

As
\mu_{\varepsilon}
and
\nu_{\varepsilon}
are weakly convergent sequences, by

\sup_{0<{\varepsilon}<1}{\left\{{{\mu_{\varepsilon}}{\left({\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash K_{\mu}}\right)}}\right\}},\sup_{0<{\varepsilon}<1}{\left\{{{\nu_{\varepsilon}}{\left({\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash K_{\nu}}\right)}}\right\}}<\xi.

Then, by Lemma 4.4 in
villani2008optimal
, the couplings
\pi_{\varepsilon}\in{\Pi\left({{\mu_{\varepsilon}},{\nu_{\varepsilon}}}\right)}
all lie in a tight set of
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

First, let us recall that optimizers
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
of (

) exist, i.e., the infimum is attained as the set of couplings is compact and
\pi\mapsto\int c{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi
is a lower-bounded, linear functional. Due to the tightness of the couplings, applying Prokhorov’s theorem to a sequence of optimizers

, we can extract a subsequence, still denoted
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
for simplicity, which converges weakly to some
\pi_{0}^{*}\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})

\pi_{0}^{*}
is an optimizer of (

) for
{\varepsilon}=0
, by showing that it is

-cyclically monotone.

Note that in
{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}\times{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
,
c_{\!{\varepsilon}}
is continuous and upper bounded, implying that
\operatorname{OT}{\left({{\mu_{\varepsilon}},{\nu_{\varepsilon}},{c_{\!\varepsilon}}}\right)}
is bounded as well. We can thus apply Theorem
3.1
and as
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
is optimal, each
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
is concentrated on a
{c_{\!\varepsilon}}
-cyclically monotone set. Moreover, for any
N\geq 1
pick arbitrary points
(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{N},y_{N})
from the support of
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
and denote this set of points by
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\otimes N}
. Additionally, define the set
C_{\!{\varepsilon}}(N)
as the set of all sets of
N
points
{\left\{{(\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\tilde{x}_{N},\tilde{y}_{N})}\right\}}
from
{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}\times{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{\!{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{\!{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i+1}),

(23)

with the convention
\tilde{y}_{N+1}=\tilde{y}_{1}
.

By the
{c_{\!\varepsilon}}
-cyclical monotonicity of
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
, the set
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\otimes N}
is thus concentrated on
C_{\!{\varepsilon}}(N)
.
Further, as the cost function
c_{\!{\varepsilon}}
is continuous on
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
,
the set of sets
C_{\!{\varepsilon}}(N)
is closed. As a next step, we show that
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\otimes N}
is also concentrated on another set. Let
\delta>0
and
N
be given, define

C_{\delta}(N)
as the set of all sets of
N
points
{\left\{{(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\hat{x}_{N},\hat{y}_{N})}\right\}}
from
{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}\times{\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{0}(\hat{x}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{0}(\hat{x}_{i},\hat{y}_{i+1})+\delta,

(24)

which corresponds to a
\delta
-approximate
c_{0}
-cyclical monotonicity. We now want to prove that, for
{\varepsilon}
small enough, the set
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\otimes N}
is concentrated on
C_{\delta}(N)
. We do so by distinguishing two cases: First, either at least one of the
x_{i}
’s or the
y_{i}
’s from
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\otimes N}
is not in
\mathcal{N}_{0}
, in which case both sides of the inequality (
24
) are
+\infty
and the inequality holds trivially. Second, if
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\otimes N}\subset{\mathcal{N}_{0}}\times{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
observe that for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
we get

{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x,y)\leq{c_{0}}(x,y),

which together with the
{c_{\!\varepsilon}}
-cyclical monotonicity of
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
, so (
23
), results in

\sum_{i=1}^{N}{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x_{i},y_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}{c_{\!\varepsilon}}(x_{i},y_{i+1})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}{c_{0}}(x_{i},y_{i+1}).

Finally,
c_{\!{\varepsilon}}(x,y)
converges uniformly to
c_{0}(x,y)
for
x,y\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
, so there exists
{\varepsilon}>0
small enough such that for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
we obtain

\lvert c_{\!{\varepsilon}}(x,y)-c_{0}(x,y)|\leq\frac{\delta}{N}.

Therefore,
(
24
) indeed also holds for all
x_{i},y_{i}
’s in
{\mathcal{N}_{0}}
, which yields
\operatorname{spt}{\left({\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\otimes N}}\right)}\subseteq C_{\delta}{\left({N}\right)}
for
{\varepsilon}
small enough. Since
C_{\delta}{\left({N}\right)}
is a closed set, the weak convergence of
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
to
\pi_{0}^{*}
also implies
\operatorname{spt}\big(\pi_{0}^{\otimes N}\big)\subseteq C_{\delta}{\left({N}\right)}
. To show that
C_{\delta}{\left({N}\right)}
is closed, a sublevel set argument for the function
f:{\left({\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\right)}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup{\left\{{+\infty}\right\}},\quad\big({\left({x_{1},y_{1}}\right)},\dots,{\left({x_{N},y_{N}}\right)}\big)\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{0}{\left({x_{i},y_{i}}\right)}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{0}{\left({x_{i},y_{i+1}}\right)}
with
C_{\delta}{\left({N}\right)}={\left\{{z}~\middle|~{f{\left({z}\right)}\leq\delta}\right\}}
can be used.

As the inequality (
24
) holds for all
\big({\left({x_{1},y_{1}}\right)},\dots,{\left({x_{N},y_{N}}\right)}\big)\in\operatorname{spt}{\left({\pi_{0}^{\otimes N}}\right)}
for all
\delta>0
, in the limit
\delta\to 0
, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{0}{\left({x_{i},y_{i}}\right)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{0}{\left({x_{i},y_{i+1}}\right)}

for all
\big({\left({x_{1},y_{1}}\right)},\dots,{\left({x_{N},y_{N}}\right)}\big)\in\operatorname{spt}{\left({\pi_{0}^{\otimes N}}\right)}
. Therefore,
\pi_{0}^{*}
is
c_{0}
-cyclically monotone, which guarantees the optimality of
\pi_{0}^{*}
in (
OT
) for
{\varepsilon}=0
by Theorem
3.1
.

Finally, in the case of a unique
\pi_{0}^{\star}
, we conclude the convergence of each subsequence
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
to
\pi_{0}^{\star}
, so that the whole sequence converges to
\pi_{0}^{\star}
by a classical subsequence argument.

Remark 4

In the somewhat trivial case of a network consisting of a single pipe, all the presented results still apply. The unique difference is that then the initial velocity is sufficient to characterize a geodesic, and uniqueness of the transport plan holds for each
{\varepsilon}>0
under the assumption of the measures
{\mu_{\varepsilon}},{\nu_{\varepsilon}}
having a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

3.5
Towards a dynamical understanding

The issue underlying the absence of uniqueness of optimizers
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
was pointed out above and relates to the fact that minimizing curves in the network change directions. Still, one intuits that minimal additional knowledge could suffice to still get a transport plan in “well-behaved” examples. To make a step in that direction and explain the open questions, let us consider the space-time lifting of the problem, i.e.,
we extend the measures
{\mu_{\varepsilon}}
and
{\nu_{\varepsilon}}
to
\mu_{\varepsilon}\otimes\delta_{t=0}
and
\nu_{\varepsilon}\otimes\delta_{t=1}
and utilize the cost function

\tilde{c}\big((x,s),(y,t)\big)=\inf\left\{\int_{s}^{t}\|\dot{\gamma}_{u}\|^{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}(\gamma_{u}){\,\mathrm{d}}u:\gamma(s)=x,\gamma(t)=y\right\},

for a piecewise absolutely continuous curve
\gamma_{u}
and
s,t\in{\left[{0,1}\right]}
.

From the characterization of the dual problem, if the following quantities were well-defined and the function

G_{(x,s)}(y,t):=(y,t)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}\tilde{c}\big((x,s),(y,t)\big)\\
\partial_{s}\tilde{c}\big((x,s),(y,t)\big)\end{pmatrix}

was invertible, then the optimal transport map in the space-time domain
is given by

T(x,t)=G_{(x,s)}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}\psi(x,s)\\
\partial_{s}\psi(x,s)\end{pmatrix},

where
\psi(x,s)
is an optimal dual potential.

3.6
Stability with respect to the gas network topology

An important question is to know how the optimal gas transport depends on the topology of the network, and in particular how the cost of transport would be affected in case of modifications of the network structure.

To better understand this, let us introduce the following setting. We consider two probability measures
\mu,\nu
that are supported on
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}:=\mathcal{N}_{0}\setminus\mathcal{M}
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a set of edges in the central portion of the graph, which are removed to get a new graph structure. Usually, one thinks of
\mu
as a source and
\nu
as a target, such that
\mu
is flowing to
\nu
through
\mathcal{M}
(at least partially). As a next step we can add new edges as a substitute for
\mathcal{M}
, which we denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
to get the new graph
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}:=\mathcal{N}_{0}\setminus\mathcal{M}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
. Note that we require
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
to be still connected to allow for a feasible transport. Then, we define the modified cost function

\tilde{c}(x,y):=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(x,y)}\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}_{t}\|^{2}+\iota_{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}(\gamma_{t}){\,\mathrm{d}}t,

as well as the modified optimal transport problem

\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,\tilde{c}):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}\tilde{c}{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi.

Recall that
\mu
and
\nu
are supported on
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}
and thus
\pi
is supported on
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}
, however the transport through the new section of the graph
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is accounted for in the cost function
\tilde{c}
as the feasible shortest-path curves
\gamma
live on the space domain
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
.

We denote by
O^{\star}
and
\widetilde{O}^{\star}
the set of optimal couplings in both problems, i.e.,

O^{\star}:=\left\{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu):\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}c{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}{c}{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi\right\},

\widetilde{O}^{\star}:=\left\{\tilde{\pi}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu):\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}\tilde{c}{\,\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\pi}=\inf_{\tilde{\pi}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}\tilde{c}{\,\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\pi}\right\}.

The following theorem relates the values of the optimal costs between the two situations, i.e., before and after modification of the gas network.

Theorem 3.3

In the above setting, it holds true that

\displaystyle\big|\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,\tilde{c})-\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,{c})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\max{\left\{{\inf_{\pi\in O^{\star}}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}|\tilde{c}-c|\mathrm{d}\pi,\inf_{\tilde{\pi}\in\widetilde{O}^{\star}}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}|\tilde{c}-c|\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}}\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{c}-c\|_{\infty}.

Remark 5

The middle bound expresses the natural intuition that one might have: The difference in cost between the two network topologies depends on the difference between the costs weighted by the mass transported between these points.

Remark 6
(Deleting one connection)

If the change consists in removing a single pipe within the network, then for all
x,y\in\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}
it holds true that

c(x,y)\leq\tilde{c}(x,y),

as each path that was going through this pipe now should be at least as long. Thus,

\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,\tilde{c})-\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,{c})\leq\inf_{\pi\in O^{\star}}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}(\tilde{c}-c)\ \mathrm{d}\pi^{\star}.

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
3.3
)

Plugging in the definitions of OT, we get the equality

\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,\tilde{c})-\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,{c})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}\tilde{c}{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi-\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}c{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi,

and then taking any
\pi^{\star}
that is an optimal coupling for the problem involving the cost
c
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,\tilde{c})-\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,{c})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}\tilde{c}{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi^{\star}-\underbrace{\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}c{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi}_{=\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}c{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi^{\star}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}(\tilde{c}-c)\ \mathrm{d}\pi^{\star}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}|\tilde{c}-c|\ \mathrm{d}\pi^{\star}.

So that, because of the arbitrariness of
\pi^{\star}\in\mathcal{O}^{\star}
,

\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,\tilde{c})-\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,{c})\leq\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{O}^{\star}}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}|\tilde{c}-c|\ \mathrm{d}\pi.

For the other direction, apply the same idea, picking a coupling
\tilde{\pi}^{\star}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}^{\star}
, to get

\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,c)-\operatorname{OT}(\mu,\nu,\tilde{c})\leq\inf_{\tilde{\pi}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}^{\star}}\int_{\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\times\check{\mathcal{N}}_{0}}|\tilde{c}-c|\ \mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}.

The first part of the claim follows. For the second part, note that
\|c-\tilde{c}\|_{\infty}\geq\int|c-\tilde{c}|{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi
, as
\int{\,\mathrm{d}}\pi=1
.

4
Numerical illustrations

In this final section, we exhibit (estimated) trajectories of a shortest path metric constrained to stay within the network. Based on a uniform grid in the network, we construct a graph linking each grid point (or pixel for the later representation) to its 8 surrounding nearest neighbors and itself with the following squared distance matrix

\begin{pmatrix}2&1&2\\
1&0&1\\
2&1&2\end{pmatrix}.

Then, given random points on the structure, we compute the cost matrix based on the shortest path between each pair of points (obtained using Dijkstra’s algorithm) and solve a linear assignment problem.

We finally represent the trajectories between the matched points in Figure
1
below. We clearly see the fact that in this example geodesics branch and also collapse. This explains why the standard proofs of uniqueness of transport plans do not apply —recall Section
3.3
—and why the construction in Section
3.5
would be required to derive a potential existence of a unique transport plan.
