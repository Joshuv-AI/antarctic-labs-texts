Impact of Food Ultra‐Processing on Cardiometabolic Health: Definitions, Evidence, and Implications for Dietary Guidance
Authors: Carlos A Monteiro, Eurídice Martínez Steele, Geoffrey Cannon
Published: 2024

Until recently, the impact of food processing on health was rarely studied. A search of papers in the PubMed database including the terms “food processing” or “processed food” or “ultra‐processed food” and cardiometabolic health–related conditions (“cardiovascular disease, risk or mortality,” “obesity,” “diabetes,” “hypertension,” “dyslipidemia,” or “metabolic syndrome”) in their titles retrieved zero records before 2014. Between 2014 and 2019, 17 records were retrieved; then a total of 103 between 2020 and 2024. A sufficient reason for the absence of studies on processed food consumption and health before 2014 was the absence of any standard food classification based on food processing. This limitation was overcome with the creation of the Nova food classification system, initially proposed in 2009 and published in final form in 2014. 1

THE NOVA FOOD CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM, THE CONCEPT OF ULTRA‐PROCESSED FOODS, AND THE ULTRA‐PROCESSED DIETARY PATTERN

Nova identifies food processing as all physical, biological, and chemical techniques used by industry on foods before they are consumed or prepared in or out of the home. Based on the extent and purpose of food processing, Nova classifies all foods and food products, consumed as such or as ingredients of freshly prepared dishes and meals, into 4 groups. 1

Nova group 1 (unprocessed or minimally processed foods) are edible parts of plants (fruits, seeds, leaves, stems, roots, tubers) or of animals (such as muscle, offals, eggs, milk), and also fungi, algae and water, after separation from nature or altered by mostly long‐established processes which largely retain the whole food structure (matrix) and do not add salt, sugar, or fat. These processes include removal of inedible or unwanted parts, cutting, drying, crushing, grinding, fractioning, roasting, boiling, pasteurization, refrigeration, freezing, placing in containers, vacuum packaging, and nonalcoholic fermentation.

Nova group 2 (processed culinary ingredients) are substances obtained directly from group 1 foods or from nature, such as oils, butter, lard, table sugar, honey, and salt, using also long‐established processes such as pressing, centrifuging, refining, extracting, mining, or drying. They are usually not consumed by themselves. They are used to prepare, season, mix, and cook group 1 foods, turning them into freshly prepared dishes and meals.

Nova group 3 (processed foods) are relatively simple modified foods manufactured by adding at least 1 group 2 ingredient (such as salt, sugar, or fat) to group 1 foods, using again long‐established processes such as canning, bottling, pickling, boiling, baking, and nonalcoholic fermentation. Common types of processed foods are canned animal and plant foods in brine, fruits in syrup, salted nuts and seeds, cheese, and freshly made breads.

Nova group 4 (ultra‐processed foods) are not modified whole foods such as those in group 1 and 3. They are industrial formulations made with substances extracted from group 1 foods (such as sugars, oils, and protein isolates), chemically altered food substances (such as modified starches, hydrogenated oils, hydrolyzed proteins), and classes of cosmetic additives (such as colors, flavors, and emulsifiers), mostly with little if any whole food included. Sequences of processes, such as hydrogenation, hydrolysis, extrusion, mechanical recovery, molding, prefrying, and other novel technologies exclusive to industry, create, alter, and combine the various ingredients and formulate the final products (hence “ultra‐processed”). Ultra‐processed foods include nonalcoholic sweetened drinks, sweetened and savory snacks, extruded breakfast cereals, mass‐produced packaged breads and other baked goods, reconstituted meat and cheese products, instant dishes, ready meals, and desserts.

The main purposes underlying the manufacture of minimally processed foods, processed culinary ingredients, and processed foods are to preserve natural (unprocessed) food, make it safe, facilitate and diversify its preparation, and enhance its sensory properties. In contrast, food ultra‐processing creates novel products liable to displace all the other Nova groups and thus dishes and meals made with them, and, as expected from any business innovation, to increase profits. To attract consumers, ultra‐processed foods are storable, ready‐to‐consume, and hyperpalatable. Usual long shelf‐life, low‐cost ingredients, and emphatic branding make them highly profitable. 1

The Nova system provides the metric that measures the extent to which dietary patterns are or have become ultra‐processed: the dietary share of ultra‐processed foods, or the proportion of total dietary intake as energy or weight from all Nova group 4 products. 2

Nationally representative dietary surveys data analyzed according to the Nova system show a large variation in the dietary share of ultra‐processed foods: from 4.9% of total energy intake in China in 2011 3 to almost 60% in the United States in 2018. 4 Repeated surveys show that everywhere across the globe, exposure to the ultra‐processed dietary pattern is increasing and fastest in countries where this exposure starts at relatively low levels, such as in South Korea, where the national dietary share of ultra‐processed foods went from 12.9% of total energy intake in 1998 to 32.6% in 2017. 5 Increases are slower in countries where this exposure starts at already very high levels, such as in the United States, where the dietary share of ultra‐processed foods among adults went from 53.5% in 2001 to 57% in 2018. 6 An analysis of findings from nationally representative dietary surveys conducted in 13 countries (Australia, Brazil, Canada, Chile, Colombia, France, Italy, Mexico, Portugal, South Korea, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, and the United States) showed that the dietary share of ultra‐processed foods was systematically associated with higher energy intake and higher levels of added sugars, total fat, and saturated fats, and lower levels of fiber, protein, and micronutrients. 7

EFFECTS OF THE ULTRA‐PROCESSED DIETARY PATTERN ON ADVERSE CARDIOMETABOLIC OUTCOMES AND POTENTIAL MECHANISMS

The relationship between exposure to the ultra‐processed dietary pattern, as defined by the Nova system, and cardiometabolic ill‐health outcomes, has been assessed by what are now a great number of observational studies and meta‐analyses of such studies, and some intervention and mechanistic studies.

A landmark umbrella review 8 of meta‐analytic evidence of the association between exposure to the ultra‐processed dietary pattern and increased adverse health outcomes has found direct associations in 32 of 45 meta‐analyses. Specifically regarding cardiometabolic health outcomes, the review found a direct association in 21 of 24 meta‐analyses. Using prespecified evidence classification criteria, convincing evidence was found for higher risks of incident cardiovascular disease–related mortality and type 2 diabetes (dose‐response). Highly suggestive evidence was found for higher risks of incident heart disease–related mortality and type 2 diabetes, and higher prevalence of obesity. Suggestive evidence was found for higher risk of incident cardiovascular disease events combined and higher prevalence of hypertension and abdominal obesity. Using the GRADE [Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (criteria)] system, which initially considers all observational studies as low quality, moderate quality evidence was found for higher dose–response risks of incident type 2 diabetes and overweight/obesity. 8

The association with overweight/obesity was reinforced by the findings of a National Institutes of Health (NIH) in‐patient, crossover, randomized, controlled trial that compared the ad libitum energy intake and changes in body weight and fat mass of participants following a 2‐week ultra‐processed diet (≈80% of ultra‐processed foods) and an unprocessed diet (zero ultra‐processed foods). The main findings showed a 2‐week weight gain of ≈1 kg, mostly fat mass, with the ultra‐processed diet, and a 2‐week weight loss of ≈1 kg, again mostly fat mass, with the unprocessed diet. 9

Nutrient Mechanisms

Some prospective studies performed formal mediation analysis to identify the proportion of the association between the ultra‐processed dietary pattern and increased cardiometabolic health outcomes that could be explained by the dietary content of specific nutrients. One cohort study conducted in Italy found that one‐fifth to one‐third of the association with cardiovascular mortality was mediated by sugar, while saturated fats, dietary cholesterol, and sodium had a negligible mediation effect. 10 A 3‐cohort US study found that 11.9% of the association with type 2 diabetes was mediated by dietary fiber, refined starch, added sugar, sodium, minerals, and partially hydrogenated oils. 11

A systematic review of 37 prospective studies on the association between the ultra‐processed dietary pattern and ill health, 26 of them including cardiometabolic health outcomes, provided additional evidence that the dietary content of critical nutrients only partially explains the association. This review found that 64 of 66 direct associations between greater ultra‐processed food consumption and increased risk of ill‐health outcomes remained statistically significant following adjustments for critical nutrients (eg, sodium, sugar, and saturated fat), nutrient‐dense food groups, or diet quality scores that combine nutrients and food groups. 12

The findings of the NIH trial also suggest the existence of nonnutrient mechanisms linking the ultra‐processed dietary pattern to cardiometabolic health outcomes. This is because the 2 diets studied were designed to match the presented calories, energy density, macronutrients, sugar, sodium, and fiber (while differing in added/intrinsic sugar and fiber and nonbeverage energy density). 9

Nonnutrient Mechanisms

Evidence for plausible nonnutrient dietary mechanisms comes from studies that have assessed the impact of consuming ultra‐processed foods on nonnutrient dietary attributes known to influence the risk of cardiometabolic ill‐health outcomes, as described below.

Total Energy Intake

The meta‐analysis of data from the nationally representative dietary surveys conducted in 13 countries, including the United States, predicted a total daily energy intake increase of 34.7 kcal (95% CI, 14.7–54.7) for each 10% increase in the energy share of ultra‐processed foods. 7 The NIH trial has shown that on the ultra‐processed diet, participants consumed ≈500 kcal more than when on the diet with no ultra‐processed foods. 9 Post hoc analyses of this trial suggested that this difference could be explained by higher content of hyperpalatable foods and higher energy density of solid foods in the ultra‐processed diet. 13

Health‐Protective Phytochemicals

Nationally representative US studies have shown that the quintiles of the dietary share of ultra‐processed food are linearly inversely associated with flavonoids (intake) 14 and phytoestrogens (urinary concentrations). 8

Xenobiotics

Nationally representative US studies have shown that, although not specific to ultra‐processed foods, neoformed contaminants generated by high temperatures and low moisture, such as acrylamide, and endocrine disruptors, such as bisphenols and phthalates, usually released from packaging materials, have been found in significant higher concentration in blood and urine samples, respectively, of participants with higher dietary share of ultra‐processed foods. 8

Food Additives

Experimental studies in humans show that emulsifiers, artificial sweeteners, and colors, cosmetic additives characteristic of ultra‐processed foods, cause microbiome dysbiosis and inflammation, 4 , 8 while cohort studies show prospective associations between higher consumption of artificial sweeteners 8 and several types of emulsifiers 15 with higher risk of cardiovascular diseases.

Altogether, the evidence of higher risk of adverse cardiometabolic health outcomes associated with the ultra‐processed dietary pattern fulfills 7 of 9 Bradford Hill criteria 16 to determine causation. These include: consistency (increased risk observed in many countries and settings by different researchers); strength (risk equivalent to the protection given by the Mediterranean Diet); temporality (most evidence provided by cohort studies); biological gradient (dose–response associations shown in many studies); plausibility (consistent with broad deterioration of diet quality); coherence (does not conflict with the natural history and biology of cardiometabolic diseases); and backed by experiment (in the case of body weight and fat mass gains).

IMPLICATIONS FOR DIETARY GUIDANCE

This robust evidence, and the worldwide growing displacement of long‐evolved dietary patterns based on Nova groups 1 through 3 by the ultra‐processed dietary pattern, even in countries where it already prevails, 6 means that dietary guidance to prevent cardiovascular diseases (and other ill health, and to promote good health and well‐being) should recommend against ultra‐processed diets, and therefore reduce the consumption of ultra‐processed foods. Until recently, guidance on healthy diets has focused on recommending the consumption of good sources of protein, complex carbohydrates, unsaturated fats, fiber, vitamins, and minerals and reduced intake of sugar, saturated fats, and sodium, with no explicit mention of food processing. Now, a growing number of countries, including France, Israel, India, and several in Latin America, also encourage consuming unprocessed or minimally processed foods and freshly prepared meals over ultra‐processed foods. 8 Similarly, the AHA 17 and the European Association for the Study of the Liver 18 advocated for this shift in 2021.

Should the consumption of all types of ultra‐processed foods be reduced? Although the evidence that ultra‐processed diets are grossly nutrient unbalanced is very consistent, 4 , 7 some ultra‐processed foods may include substantial amounts of health‐protective nutrients, such as fiber, protein, and calcium. Two examples are packaged breads with added bran or germ and emulsifiers and ready‐to‐drink “fruit” yogurts made from milk or milk‐derived substances, sugars or nonsugar sweeteners, and colors and flavors.

To exempt these ultra‐processed foods from the list of products to be reduced, evidence is needed that they are as healthy as equivalent homemade foods or less processed products (Nova groups 1 or 3). There is currently no such evidence. A few prospective studies that have attempted to isolate the effect of nutrient‐dense products within the ultra‐processed dietary pattern used analytical models that compared them with the average diet excluding ultra‐processed foods, not with the corresponding homemade or processed or minimally processed alternatives. 11 , 19

If a subgroup of ultra‐processed foods proves to be at least as good as an equivalent subgroup of nonultra‐processed foods in preventing the set of the many diseases associated with the ultra‐processed dietary pattern, the creation of an exception in the general dietary guidance to avoid substituting nonultra‐processed foods with ultra‐processed ones would be justified.

Meanwhile, pragmatic advice in countries where consumption of ultra‐processed food is very high, such as the United States and the United Kingdom, could provisionally omit some ultra‐processed foods from foods to avoid on the grounds that these have healthy ingredients and are less expensive or more available than better choices. Any such exceptions should not be identified as healthy.

Basing dietary guidance solely on nutrients overlooks the conclusive evidence on the ill effects of ultra‐processed dietary patterns on health outcomes beyond their nutrient profile. However, it would also be a mistake to focus dietary guidance exclusively on reducing ultra‐processed foods. As stated in the Brazilian Dietary Guidelines 20 and several other national food guides, as well as avoiding ultra‐processed foods, dietary guidance should recommend diets based on a variety of unprocessed or minimally processed foods, mostly plant‐sourced, and freshly prepared dishes cooked with healthy fats, herbs, and spices, while minimizing salt and added sugar, preferably enjoyed with company. These diets can include small amounts of processed foods containing low added fat, salt, and sugar.

Dietary guidelines are necessary but not sufficient. They need to be supported by social, economic, and environmental public policies and actions, including fiscal and statutory measures designed to make healthy unprocessed or minimally processed foods and freshly prepared dishes and meals more attractive, available, and affordable, and to tax and restrict the marketing of ultra‐processed foods, remove indirect and direct subsidies of these products, and prohibit their sales in settings such as schools and hospitals. Institutional food procurement must prioritize unprocessed or minimally processed foods. Culinary skills should be taught to boys and girls in schools. Shared family meal planning and preparation should be promoted by public mass‐media campaigns. As already done in several countries, both processed and ultra‐processed foods high in added sugar, sodium, or saturated fat should include prominent front‐of‐pack warning labels. 4 Warning labels addressing both unbalanced nutrient content and food ultra‐processing have shown good potential in guiding consumers to select healthy products. 4
