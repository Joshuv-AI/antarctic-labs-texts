Outcomes of Acute Ischemic Stroke Patients Undergoing Air Versus Ground Interhospital Transport in a Rural Area: A Retrospective Comparative Study
Authors: Eunji Park, Yong Sung Cha, Kang Hyun Lee, Sun Ju Kim, Chan Young Kang, Yae Jun Son, Oh Hyun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTimely reperfusion therapy is critical in acute ischemic stroke, yet prolonged transport remains a major barrier. This study compared the clinical outcomes and time intervals between helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) and ground emergency medical services (GEMS) in patients transferred for suspected stroke to a rural tertiary center in South Korea.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed adult patients (≥ 18 years) with acute ischemic stroke (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, code I63) transferred from referral hospitals at least 30 km away, between July 2013 and June 2021. Patients arriving over 24 hours from onset, transient ischemic attack cases, or those with incomplete records were excluded. After propensity score matching by age, sex, and referral hospital, data on demographics, National Institutes of Health Stroke Severity (NIHSS), modified Rankin Scale (mRS), transport times, and treatments (tissue plasminogen activator [tPA] or mechanical thrombectomy) were examined. Primary outcomes included rates of thrombolytic therapy and neurological outcomes at discharge (mRS ≤ 2).ResultsA total of 182 matched patients (91 HEMS, 91 GEMS) were analyzed. Baseline comorbidities were similar, although current smoking was more frequent in the HEMS group (29.7% vs. 15.4%). HEMS resulted in significantly shorter door-in-door-out time (60 vs. 83 minutes; P = 0.005) and interfacility transport time (39 vs. 51 minutes; P < 0.001), leading to a shorter onset-to-receiving-hospital interval. Despite these time advantages, no statistically significant differences were observed in overall tPA administration, mechanical thrombectomy rates, or discharge NIHSS and mRS scores. The most common reason for non-administration of tPA in both groups was arriving beyond the 4.5-hour window, reported more often in the GEMS group (72.8% vs. 42.9%; P < 0.001).ConclusionHEMS significantly reduced transport intervals and in-hospital time metrics for patients with acute ischemic stroke. However, faster arrival did not translate into a statistically significant improvement in thrombolysis or neurological outcomes compared to GEMS. Future research should refine patient selection for HEMS and optimize in-hospital workflows to fully leverage potential time savings and improve clinical outcomes.Keywords: Acute Ischemic Stroke, Ambulances, Helicopter, Emergency Medical Service, Therapeutic Thrombolysis, Thrombectomies, Factor, Time

INTRODUCTION

According to the Global Burden of Disease, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study, stroke is largely preventable and its incidence has been decreasing globally. However, as of 2019, stroke still remains the third leading cause of disability and death worldwide.1 In South Korea, stroke was the second most common cause of death after cancer in 2015.2 According to a 2018 report by the Epidemiologic Research Committee of the Korean Stroke Society, cerebrovascular diseases accounted for 29.6 deaths per 100,000 population, with ischemic stroke causing 9.1 deaths per 100,000 population.3

When a cerebral blood vessel is obstructed, blood flow is interrupted, leading to ischemic damage to brain cells. Therefore, the primary goal of acute ischemic stroke treatment is to promptly restore blood flow by minimizing the time from symptom onset to diagnosis and treatment. In particular, tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) must be administered within 4.5 hours of symptom onset in patients with ischemic stroke,4,5 making rapid transport during the pre-hospital phase critically important. There is a previous study indicating that for every 1-minute delay in transport, the likelihood of receiving intra-arterial thrombolytic therapy decreases by 2.5%.6 However, according to the 2018 Stroke Statistics in Korea by Kim et al.,3 the average time from symptom onset to hospital arrival for stroke patients in Korea was 6.2 hours, which is longer than the 2.8 hours reported in the United States. Furthermore, only 10.7% of all patients were eligible for tPA treatment.3

To facilitate the timely administration of thrombolytic therapy or when appropriate treatment is not available at a primary hospital, helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) may be utilized to transfer stroke patients to a stroke center.6 Several studies have reported advantages of air transport. For instance, it has been shown that 96.4% of acute ischemic stroke patients transported by air arrive at the final hospital within two hours,7 and the use of interhospital air transport has been associated with increased rates of thrombolytic therapy administration.8 Additionally, a domestic study comparing the outcomes of ischemic stroke patients transported by air and by ground found that air transport was associated with shorter hospital stays and reduced mortality rates.9 However, there are also numerous studies that do not support these findings. For example, in a study conducted by Funder et al.,10 which compared the outcomes of stroke unit patients transported by physician-staffed helicopters versus those transported by ground, the cumulative mortality rate was similar between the two groups, with 9.04 deaths per 100 patients in the ground transport group and 9.71 deaths per 100 patients in the air transport group. Moreover, a study conducted in Denmark reported that the “door to needle time,” which refers to the time from emergency department arrival to tPA administration, was actually faster in the ground transport group, with no significant differences in neurological outcomes or short- and long-term mortality between the air and ground transport groups.11 Consequently, the debate remains as to whether air transport for acute ischemic stroke patients contributes to better outcomes, with potential differences influenced by regional variations in HEMS operations or transport protocols.

There has been one report in Korea suggesting that air transport reduces mortality in acute ischemic stroke patients. However, no study has yet analyzed the factors that may influence patient outcomes during air transport in stroke patients. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the rates of thrombolytic therapy administration and neurological outcomes between acute ischemic stroke patients undergoing air versus ground transport for interhospital transfer and to identify the factors that may affect these outcomes.

METHODS

Emergency medical system characteristics in the study region

In the study region—Gangwon Province and northern Chungcheongbuk-do—suspected stroke patients are first managed by the national 119 ambulance service. Emergency medical technician (EMT) level 1 or 2 apply a standardized prehospital stroke scale, alert the destination hospital, and transport the patient by ground or air. When the initial hospital cannot provide definitive therapy, its physician contacts a higher-level center to arrange an interfacility transfer, which is carried out either by private ground ambulance or by the government-funded doctor-helicopter introduced in 2013.

The helicopter operates daily from sunrise to sunset with one emergency-medicine physician, one certified flight nurse or EMT level 1, and two pilots on board. The flight physician decides on deployment. Although the service also performs scene missions for patients who develop sudden focal deficits such as acute hemiparesis, the present study analyzes only interhospital transfers. In that context the helicopter is activated for patients with confirmed or strongly suspected cerebral infarction who are likely to benefit from intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy.

Most referring hospitals can obtain basic neuroimaging to diagnose ischemic stroke but lack stroke units, admission capacity, and often the staff required to administer tPA. The receiving institution, Yonsei University Wonju Severance Christian Hospital, functions as a comprehensive stroke center with 24-hour tPA, endovascular thrombectomy, neurosurgical backup, and a dedicated stroke intensive-care unit.

Setting & participants

This retrospective, observational study included adult patients (≥ 18 years old) with acute ischemic stroke (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, code I63) who were transferred from referring hospitals located at least 30 km away to a single tertiary institution in a rural area of South Korea. In Korea, all counties ‘gun’ are legally designated as rural, and any city ‘si’ that falls below the statutory thresholds for population size and density is classified as a rural-type small or medium-sized city. Most of the referring hospitals are located in such rural counties, while the remainder—including Jecheon, Chungju, Taebaek, and Yeongju—are situated in rural-type cities; together with the limited availability of internists, neurologists, and acute-stroke infrastructure across Gangwon Province, these characteristics justify defining our study region as a rural, medically underserved area. The study interval extended from July 2013 to June 2021. Because helicopter flights in Korea are limited to daytime hours (sunrise to sunset), only patients transferred during this period were considered. Exclusion criteria were: more than 24 hours between stroke onset and arrival at the receiving hospital, transient ischemic attack, stroke sequelae coded under I63, insufficient documentation in the medical record, and stroke onset within a hospital setting. Patients meeting inclusion criteria were categorized according to whether they were transported by HEMS or ground emergency medical services (GEMS). HEMS in this institution typically involves a physician specializing in emergency medicine and a nurse or EMT level 1, whereas GEMS transport from outlying centers often includes only a nurse or EMT level 1 or 2.

Data were extracted from electronic medical records and, for the HEMS group, from official air transport logs. Variables included demographic information (age, sex, medical history), as well as clinical data: National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores upon arrival and at discharge, defined time intervals for transport and treatment, and treatments received such as intravenous tPA or mechanical thrombectomy. Reasons for non-administration of tPA, including late arrival beyond 4.5 hours from symptom onset, mild symptoms, and intracerebral hemorrhage, were recorded. Time intervals included: door-in-door-out (DIDO), transport time from the referring to the receiving facility, onset to emergency department (ED) arrival at the final hospital, onset to injection (tPA), onset to groin puncture (mechanical thrombectomy), and ED to groin. A good neurological outcome was defined as mRS ≤ 2 at discharge.

Statistical analysis and ethics approval

Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviations or as medians with inter-quartile ranges, depending on normality and homogeneity of variance. Between-group comparisons employed the independent-samples t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous data and the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data.

Because patients ultimately transported by helicopter tended to originate from more distant hospitals and frequently showed greater neurological deficits at first medical contact—factors that can independently worsen prognosis and confound the association between transport mode and outcome—we applied propensity-score matching to approximate randomization and reduce selection bias. A 1:1 nearest-neighbour algorithm was selected because it provides the most robust covariate balance with minimal data loss; exploratory 1:2 matching increased sample size but produced significant imbalances in three referring hospital variables (P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 1) and was therefore not adopted. Sex, age, and referring hospital were chosen as matching variables because they are available before any transport decision, strongly influence stroke prognosis and transport allocation, and were 100% complete in our dataset. Importantly, referring hospital also serves as a surrogate for transport distance and travel logistics, ensuring that matched helicopter- and ground-transported patients originated from the same institutions and therefore shared comparable geographic contexts. Variables obtained after the allocation decision—such as admission NIHSS or onset-to-arrival intervals—were excluded from the matching algorithm to avoid post-treatment (collider) bias. Nevertheless, admission NIHSS remained well balanced after matching (GEMS vs. HEMS; 5.0 [2.0–14.0] vs. 7.0 [3.0–16.0]; P = 0.140), and incorporating NIHSS as an additional covariate in a sensitivity model did not alter the study’s primary outcomes.

In addition, because the clinical benefit of rapid transport is greatest when patients remain within the intravenous-thrombolysis window, we prespecified an “early-presenter” subgroup comprising patients who arrived at the referring hospital ≤ 2 hours after symptom onset. Using the same statistical tests applied in the overall matched cohort, we repeated all primary HEMS-versus-GEMS comparisons in this subgroup. Effect consistency was evaluated by comparing point estimates and their 95% confidence intervals between the full cohort and the early-presenter subgroup.

Matching was performed with the MatchIt package in R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), after which all remaining statistical analyses were conducted with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University Wonju Severance Christian Hospital (CR323109), with waived informed consent due to the retrospective nature of data collection.

RESULTS

From July 2013 to June 2023, 351 patients with acute ischemic stroke were transferred to the tertiary center via either air or ground transport. After excluding patients with stroke sequelae, transient ischemic attacks, or incomplete documentation, a final sample of 182 patients (91 HEMS, 91 GEMS) was analyzed following propensity score matching (Fig. 1). The entire cohort contained 56% men (n = 102), and the mean age was 71.91 ± 10.13 years. Baseline medical histories such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, and kidney disease did not differ significantly between the two transport groups (Table 1). However, current smoking was more common in the HEMS group (29.7%) compared to GEMS (15.4%, P = 0.021). The median transport distance in both groups was 65 km, with no substantial discrepancies in referral hospital distributions after matching (Table 2).

Rates of intravenous tPA administration at either the referring hospital or the receiving center were slightly higher in the HEMS group, although statistical significance was not reached. Analysis of time intervals demonstrated that HEMS conferred several advantages. The median DIDO time at the referring hospitals was 60 minutes (IQR, 43.0–82.0) in HEMS, significantly shorter than 83 minutes (IQR, 67.0–122.0) in GEMS (P = 0.005) (Table 5). Median transport time from the referring facility to the receiving center was also reduced for HEMS (39 minutes [32.0–48.0]) compared to GEMS (51 minutes [43.0–63.0], P < 0.001). Consequently, the median onset-to-receiving-ED interval in the HEMS group was about 85 minutes faster than GEMS (204 minutes vs. 289 minutes, P = 0.005). For mechanical thrombectomy, the onset-to-groin time was significantly shorter for HEMS (313 minutes vs. 410 minutes in GEMS, P = 0.023), and the ED-to-groin time followed a similar pattern (111 vs. 262 minutes, P = 0.016). A subset of patients who received tPA specifically at the receiving hospital also showed markedly reduced ED-to-groin times if air-transported (102 vs. 273 minutes, P = 0.030) (Supplementary Table 2).

A subgroup analysis examined patients who arrived at the first hospital within two hours of stroke onset. Among 50 individuals meeting this criterion, 18 were transported by GEMS and 32 by HEMS. No significant differences were noted in baseline comorbidities or transport distances among these early presenters. The rates of tPA and thrombectomy were comparable between the groups, but arriving outside the 4.5-hour window as a reason for non-administration of tPA was more frequent in the GEMS subset (30.8% vs. 4.0%, P = 0.038) (Table 6). Although NIHSS and mRS outcomes did not differ meaningfully between these subgroups, HEMS was again associated with a significantly shorter DIDO time (62 vs. 86 minutes, P = 0.011) (Tables 7 and 8). Notably, the median transfer time was shorter in HEMS than in GEMS (39 vs. 51 minutes, P < 0.001). The combined effect of faster DIDO and more efficient in-hospital processes contributed to significantly shorter onset-to-groin (256 vs. 405 minutes, P = 0.005) and ED-to-groin (102 vs. 251 minutes, P = 0.015) times in the HEMS group.

DISCUSSION

This study compared rates of thrombolytic therapy (tPA) administration, thrombectomy, and neurological outcomes among acute ischemic stroke patients transferred via HEMS versus ground GEMS. Although air transport substantially reduced transport intervals—including time to definitive treatment hospital arrival, in-hospital time to tPA administration, and time to thrombectomy—no statistically significant differences were observed in tPA administration rates, thrombectomy rates, neurological outcomes, or mortality between HEMS and GEMS groups. These findings remained consistent even in patients who arrived at the referring hospital within two hours of stroke onset. In acute ischemic stroke, early reperfusion through tPA or mechanical thrombectomy is crucial to improve neurological recovery.4,5 While the modestly higher administration rates of tPA and thrombectomy in the HEMS group did not reach statistical significance, the observed time savings underscore a clinically relevant advantage associated with air transport. Prior research similarly indicates that HEMS expedites interhospital transfer, which can facilitate quicker treatment.10 A domestic study also suggested that HEMS might reduce time to hospital arrival and potentially lower mortality.9 Air transport is especially beneficial in geographically remote regions where rapid ground transport may be impractical.

Despite these potential benefits, this study did not confirm significant outcome differences in revascularization success between HEMS and GEMS. As Diaz et al.12 noted, any time saved before hospital arrival can be offset if subsequent in-hospital workflows are delayed. Moreover, transport mode alone does not guarantee better prognosis.13 Several factors may explain why faster transport did not translate to statistically significant improvements in final clinical outcomes. First, although tPA and thrombectomy rates were numerically higher in the HEMS group, statistical significance was not reached. One possible reason is that patients selected for air transport often present with more severe strokes, which may require complex management. Indeed, the median NIHSS score was slightly higher in HEMS patients (7.0) than in GEMS (5.0), although the difference was not statistically significant. As Regenhardt et al.13 suggest, patients with greater stroke severity may be more influenced by the quality of in-hospital care than by mere reductions in transit time. Consequently, HEMS’s time advantage could be partially negated by the high clinical complexity of these patients.

Secondly, a substantial subset of the overall cohort was ineligible for tPA due to late arrival beyond 4.5 hours from symptom onset, mild neurological deficits, or intracerebral hemorrhage (Table 3). In particular, a notable 42.9% of HEMS patients still arrived outside the therapeutic window for thrombolysis, and other contraindications further limited eligibility for acute interventions. The apparently higher frequency of “mild neurological deficits” as a tPA-exclusion reason in the HEMS group (Table 3) may reflect overestimation of stroke severity or uncertainty about thrombolysis criteria at some referring hospitals. Because many rural facilities lack personnel formally trained to calculate the NIHSS and to apply intravenous-tPA guidelines, physicians often opt for helicopter transfer so that a stroke team at the comprehensive center can perform definitive assessment and decide on reperfusion therapy. Faster arrival does not inherently produce a clinical benefit when eligibility criteria are unmet, underscoring the importance of carefully selecting candidates for HEMS. Air transport requires considerable resources compared to GEMS, so prioritizing patients most likely to benefit from expedited care is key.14 Chalela et al.15 observed that although helicopter transport is generally safe post-tPA, contraindications reduce the potential utility of rapid transfer. Additionally, a significant number of patients transported for mechanical thrombectomy may ultimately be diagnosed with conditions other than true ischemic stroke.16 Similarly, Conroy et al.17 and Tal et al.14 argued that telemedicine protocols and prehospital screening can enhance the identification of candidates for advanced therapies, thereby improving outcomes. This is consistent with evidence suggesting that efficient triage and routing of likely tPA/thrombectomy candidates to specialized stroke centers can maximize benefit.8 In rural areas where referring hospitals often lack staff formally trained to calculate the NIHSS or apply tPA criteria, helicopter transfer may be requested even for patients with mild deficits so that a comprehensive stroke centre can re-evaluate eligibility. Triage accuracy could be improved through regular on-site or virtual training in stroke-severity assessment and reperfusion protocols, by providing concise field check-lists and standing orders that guide transport decisions, and by offering real-time teleconsultation with stroke specialists during the decision process. Focusing HEMS on patients who have a strong pre-hospital suspicion of large-vessel occlusion, remain within the tPA or thrombectomy window, and face long ground-transport times should enhance cost-effectiveness.

Third, this study’s focus on interhospital rather than scene-to-hospital transports may have introduced additional delays unrelated to the transport mode itself. According to Prabhakaran et al.,6 each interfacility transfer can slow time-sensitive interventions. Although HEMS reduces physical travel time, the cumulative effect of multiple handoffs may diminish any advantage over GEMS for particular patients. Finally, the relatively small sample size could limit the statistical power needed to detect differences in neurological outcomes, mortality, or revascularization success.

Interestingly, the lack of group differences in mortality and functional status (as measured by the NIHSS or mRS) remains notable, given time’s well-established role in limiting ischemic damage. Certain studies suggest that even when HEMS cuts transit time, in-hospital treatment delays can erase this benefit, possibly leading to comparable overall outcomes between transport modes.10 However, our study showed that not only were transport intervals faster for HEMS, but DIDO, onset-to-injection, and onset-to-groin times were also reduced, reflecting more efficient handoffs. A Danish investigation similarly found that physician-staffed helicopters accelerated coordination at the receiving center, leading to quicker stroke care.11 Yet, because we detected no statistically significant differences in discharge outcomes, it is possible that factors such as stroke severity (which appeared higher among air-transported patients) balanced out the time savings. Another consideration is that HEMS patients who did ultimately receive tPA or thrombectomy may have shown better results, but a specific subgroup analysis of only those who underwent definitive treatment was beyond the scope of this study.

There are notable limitations inherent in our retrospective design. First, transport modality was not randomized; clinicians may have chosen HEMS preferentially for severe cases or when ground travel was judged impractical. While we used propensity score matching to mitigate selection bias, differences in baseline severity and clinical urgency may remain. Randomized controlled trials could offer more definitive evidence, but are logistically challenging in emergent settings. Florez-Perdomo et al.18 emphasized in a systematic review that transfer outcomes depend on multiple contextual variables, arguing for large, multi-center prospective research. Second, we did not assess the specific levels of care delivered en route. According to Olson and Rabinstein,19 HEMS often provides more advanced interventions or monitoring than GEMS—particularly in regions where HEMS teams include specialized physicians—potentially influencing outcomes. Systematic measurement of in-transit care quality could clarify how these differences in clinical capability affect final results. Finally, our relatively small cohort limits power. Replicating this analysis with larger samples would help confirm whether HEMS truly improves tPA or thrombectomy rates and whether this translates to robust clinical benefits.

Overall, this study highlights that HEMS can significantly expedite the transfer of acute ischemic stroke patients between facilities, reducing both travel time and certain in-hospital processes. Although these benefits did not yield statistically significant improvements in neurological outcomes or mortality compared to GEMS, the observed time advantage may be valuable under optimal conditions. HEMS’s strategic use, especially in geographically isolated or resource-limited areas, could ensure that patients who are strong candidates for reperfusion therapy arrive rapidly and receive prompt intervention. Future investigations should refine the selection criteria for HEMS, explore its utility in broader populations, and characterize how specific treatment pathways—both during and after transport—can maximize the potential benefits of air evacuation. These findings may ultimately guide policy recommendations and resource allocation within emergency medical systems, particularly in countries like Korea where stroke remains a leading cause of mortality and regional disparities in care availability persist. By focusing on early identification of viable candidates for tPA or thrombectomy, refining in-transit treatment protocols, and coordinating seamless transfer to specialized centers, HEMS could serve as a pivotal component of stroke care for appropriately selected patients.
