New complementarity formulations for root-finding and optimization of piecewise-affine functions in abs-normal form
Yulan Zhang, Kamil A. Khan

Abstract
Nonsmooth functions have been used to model discrete-continuous phenomena such as contact mechanics, and are also prevalent in neural network formulations via activation functions such as ReLU. At previous AD conferences, Griewank et al. showed that nonsmooth functions may be approximated well by piecewise-affine functions constructed using an AD-like procedure. Moreover, such a piecewise-affine function may always be represented in an “abs-normal form”, encoding it as a collection of four matrices and two vectors. We present new general complementarity formulations for root-finding and optimization of piecewise-affine functions in abs-normal form, with significantly fewer restrictions than previous approaches. In particular, piecewise-affine root-finding may always be represented as a mixed-linear complementarity problem (MLCP), which may often be simplified to a linear complementarity problem (LCP). We also present approaches for verifying existence of solutions to these problems. A proof-of-concept implementation in Julia is discussed and applied to several numerical examples, using the PATH solver to solve complementarity problems.



1
Introduction

Nonsmooth functions arise in several applications, such as systems with discrete transitions between flow regimes or thermodynamic phase. Nonsmoothness may also arise in numerical methods that are applied to smooth problems, such as the well-known ReLU activation function in neural networks that are used for classification or regression. In the AD2012 conference, the first approaches
[
15
,
8
]
were presented for computing correct generalized derivatives for composite functions accurately and automatically, using new extensions of the standard vector forward mode of automatic/algorithmic differentiation (AD), enabling dedicated methods for nonsmooth root-finding and optimization that rely on generalized derivative information.

Subsequently, Griewank et al.
[
10
,
9
,
6
]
developed a
piecewise-linearization
variant of the forward AD mode. In a forward sweep through a computational graph, this variant locally approximates a composite nonsmooth function as a piecewise-affine function. Next, they proposed a new
abs-normal form
for piecewise affine functions (summarized in Section
2.2
below); this form encodes any piecewise-affine function as a collection of four matrices and two vectors. Again, this abs-normal form is to be constructed using a forward sweep through a computational graph, as illustrated in Example
1
below. Several properties and construction methods for the abs-normal form have been studied
[
24
,
16
,
1
,
11
,
12
]
, along with assessments of the form’s usefulness and efficacy
[
2
]
.

Complementarity formulations
[
3
]
encode discrete behavior quite differently to nonsmooth representations, and complementarity-based solvers such as
PATH

[
4
,
5
]
are powerful in practice. While a complementarity-based root-finding method was proposed for piecewise-affine functions in abs-normal form
[
9
]
, we will show here that this method may be improved: its requirements may be weakened significantly by exploiting the strict lower triangularity of one of the abs-normal form’s coefficient matrices. Hence, we aim to show that root-finding for the abs-normal form is equivalent to a mixed linear complementarity problem (MLCP), which in turn reduces to a linear complementarity problem (LCP) under an additional mild regularity assumption. Analogously, we will show that global optimization for a piecewise-affine function in abs-normal form is equivalent to a linear program with complementarity constraints (LPCC), which may be reformulated as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) via a big-M approach. We also present approaches for checking whether a global minimum exists at all. These new approaches have been implemented in Julia, and we present numerical examples as well. Each of these methods is a useful application of the abs-normal form, complementing recent AD-based approaches for automatically generating abs-normal forms of local approximations of nonsmooth functions, and extending the reach of these AD variants.

Hegerhorst-Schultchen et al.
[
12
]
also studied the optimization of unconstrained nonsmooth problems in a nonlinear generalization of the abs-normal form, demonstrating that these problems are equivalent to solving certain mathematical programs with equilibrium constraints (MPECs), particularly under a structural regularity assumption called the
linear independence kink qualification (LIKQ
).
Their work also extends to constrained nonsmooth nonlinear programming problems (NLPs), where both the objective function and constraints are represented in a genearlized abs-normal form. Overall, the authors explored how established concepts in MPEC theory, such as various stationarity conditions and constraint qualifications, apply to nonsmooth optimization problems in abs-normal form with embedded nonlinearities. Our new optimization formulation (
4.8
), on the other hand, focuses strictly on piecewise affine functions in abs-normal form but does not impose other structural assumptions. In particular, we do not consider constraint qualifications or stationarity conditions at all, although our piecewise affine requirement may be seen to play a similar role to a constraint qualification.

The remainder of this manuscript is structured as follows. Section
2
introduces abs-normal forms and an established complementarity-based root-finding approach. Section
3
presents our new root-finding formulations for piecewise-affine functions, and Section
4
presents analogous new optimization formulations. Section
5
summarizes our proof-of-concept Julia implementation (which is also available on GitHub), and Section
6
applies this implementation to several illustrative numerical examples.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

For any vectors
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
|\mathbf{p}|
will denote the componentwise absolute value, whose
{i}^{\text{th}}
component is
|p_{i}|
, and
\max(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
will denote an analogous componentwise bivariate “
\max
”, whose
{i}^{\text{th}}
component is
\max(p_{i},q_{i})
. Inequalities involving vectors are to be interpreted componentwise as well.

This article is concerned with continuous
piecewise-affine
(“
\mathcal{PA}
”) functions

\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, whose values are chosen from finitely many affine functions (called
affine pieces
). Several properties of piecewise affine functions were established by Scholtes
[
22
]
. If each affine piece of a
\mathcal{PA}
function

\mathbf{f}
is in fact linear, then
\mathbf{f}
is
piecewise linear
(
\mathcal{PL}
).

The
complementarity condition

\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{\mathbf{p}}\perp\mathbf{{q}}\geq\mathbf{0}
is equivalent to the following three conditions holding simultaneously:

\mathbf{p}\geq\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{q}\geq\mathbf{0}
, and
\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{q}=0
.

In optimization problems, constraints are marked with the phrase “subject to”, abbreviated to “s.t.”.

2.2
Abs-normal forms

Any
\mathcal{PA}
mapping may in principle be
represented as a composition of affine functions and absolute-value
functions
[
22
]
. This observation motivated Griewank et al.’s development of

abs-normal forms

[
9
,
10
]
,
which are described as follows, and effectively encode
\mathcal{PA}

functions as constant vector-matrix pairs.

Definition 2.1

[adapted from
[
9
,
10
]
]

Given a
\mathcal{PA}
function
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, there exist a number
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
matrices
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
,

\mathbf{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s}
, a strictly lower triangular matrix
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
, and vectors
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for which, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a unique vector
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}
for which

(2.1)

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}\\
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{b}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Z}&\mathbf{L}\\
\mathbf{J}&\mathbf{Y}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
|\mathbf{z}|\end{bmatrix},

The right-hand side of (
2.1
) is called an
abs-normal form
of
\mathbf{f}
.

Since
\mathbf{L}
is strictly lower triangular, observe that for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, the
{i}^{\text{th}}
row of (
2.1
) defines
z_{i}
in terms of
\mathbf{x}
and values
|z_{j}|
with
j<i
.

Griewank et al.
[
9
]
outlined how an abs-normal form may be constructed for a
\mathcal{PA}
function that is already expressed as a composition of affine functions and absolute-value operations. We illustrate this procedure in the following example.

Example 1

Consider the
\mathcal{PA}
mapping
f:x\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto x+\Big|2|3x+4|-5\Big|+6|7x-8|
. To construct an abs-normal form for
f
, we first construct a vector
\mathbf{z}
whose components are the arguments of the absolute-value operations in
f
’s definition, listed in the order these would be evaluated by a compiler or forward-AD-like procedure:

\displaystyle z_{1}

\displaystyle:=3x+4,

\displaystyle z_{2}

\displaystyle:=2|3x+4|-5=2|z_{1}|-5,

\displaystyle z_{3}

\displaystyle:=7x-8.

Then,
f
is written in terms of
x
and
|\mathbf{z}|
. For each
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

f(x)=x+|z_{2}|+6|z_{3}|.

At this point, the above equations may be collected into the following abs-normal form:

\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}\\
z_{2}\\
z_{3}\\
f(x)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}4\\
-5\\
-8\\
\phantom{-}0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}3&0&0&0\\
0&2&0&0\\
7&0&0&0\\
1&0&1&6\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
|z_{1}|\\
|z_{2}|\\
|z_{3}|\end{bmatrix}.

By inspection, this representation is consistent with (
2.1
) with the identifications:

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}4\\
-5\\
-8\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}3\\
0\\
7\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
2&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&6\end{bmatrix}.

Observe that
\mathbf{L}
is indeed strictly lower triangular, since in general, the above procedure will never define any
z_{i}
in terms of another
z_{j}
with
j>i
.

In the remainder of this manuscript, we aim to develop new complementarity-based methods for root-finding and optimization for the generic function in the following assumption.

Assumption 2.1

Consider a
\mathcal{PA}
function
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with the
abs-normal form (
2.1
).

To solve the problem of finding a root of
\mathbf{f}
, the following
complementarity-based approach was established
in
[
9
, Section 7.5]
.
In the following proposition, a
linear complementarity problem (LCP)
involves solving a condition of the form
\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{Mx}+\mathbf{q}\geq\mathbf{0}
for
\mathbf{x}
, and a
mixed linear complementarity problems (MLCP)
is an LCP coupled with linear equation systems. Established solvers including
PATH

[
4
,
5
]
can solve LCPs and variants including certain MLCPs.

Proposition 2.1
(from
[
9
]
)

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds, and assume
that
m=n
and
\mathbf{J}
is nonsingular. Define
the following quantities:

\mathbf{S}:=\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{J}^{-1}\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s},\qquad\hat{\mathbf{c}}:=\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{J}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}.

Then
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
if and only if there exist
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
which solve the following MLCP:

(2.2)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}&=&\hat{\mathbf{c}}+\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w})\\
\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{u}&\perp&\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbf{0}.\end{array}

Moreover, if
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})
is nonsingular, then
\mathbf{w}
solves the following LCP:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\leq(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{c}}+(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{S})\mathbf{w}\perp\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbf{0},

with
\mathbf{u}
still described by (
2.2
).

3
New root-finding formulations

The derivation of Proposition
2.1
does not actually make use of the fact that
\mathbf{L}
is strictly lower triangular. In this section, we will show that this triangularity allows for similar
root-finding formulations to Proposition
2.1
under significantly less restrictive assumptions. Observe that
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{L})

is unit lower triangular, and is therefore invertible. Moreover,
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{L})^{-1}

is straightforward to evaluate. The matrices and vectors in the
following definition will be useful in our subsequent development; these quantities all have the same dimensions as their namesakes.

Definition 3.1

Under Assumption
2.1
, define the following matrices and vectors:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle:=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{c},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{Y}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}

\displaystyle:=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{L})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{L}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{I}+\tilde{\mathbf{L}}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle:=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{Z},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{J}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{Y}\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}.

In the special case where
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
is nonsingular, define the
following quantities as well:

\check{\mathbf{c}}:=\tilde{\mathbf{c}}-\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\qquad\check{\mathbf{S}}:=\tilde{\mathbf{L}}-\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}.

Since
\mathbf{L}
is strictly lower triangular, the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}
is unit lower triangular.

We next illustrate these constructions for the function from Example
1
.

Example 2

Consider the
\mathcal{PA}
mapping
f:x\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto x+\Big|2|3x+4|-5\Big|+6|7x-8|
, whose abs-normal form (
2.1
) was constructed in Example
1
. Under Definition
3.1
, and using the matrices obtained in Example
1
, the following matrices and vectors may be computed directly:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}4\\
\phantom{-}3\\
-8\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}3\\
6\\
7\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
4&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-45\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{J}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}49\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}4&2&12\end{bmatrix}.

In this example,
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{c}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}6.8\\

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{S}}

The following theorem uses the constructions of Definition
3.1
in a new general root-finding result.

Theorem 3.1

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds, and consider the auxiliary matrices and vectors from Definition
3.1
.
Then,
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
if and only if there exists
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
for which
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})
solves the following MLCP:

(3.4)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{0}&=&\tilde{\mathbf{b}}+\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbf{w}\\
\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{w}&\perp&\tilde{\mathbf{c}}+\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbf{0}.\end{array}

Moreover, if
m=n
and
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
is nonsingular,
then
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
if and only if

(3.5)

\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{x}=-\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbf{w},

where
\mathbf{w}
solves the LCP:

(3.6)

\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{w}\perp\check{\mathbf{c}}+\check{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbf{0}.

Consider some fixed
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For the “only if” part of the theorem’s claim, consider the unique vector
\mathbf{z}
that is consistent with (
2.1
), and define
\mathbf{u}:=\max(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{z})
and
\mathbf{w}:=\max(\mathbf{0},-\mathbf{z})
. It follows that
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}
,
|\mathbf{z}|=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}
, and
\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{u}\perp\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbf{0}
.
Thus, the top block row of (
2.1
) becomes
\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w})
,
and so

(3.7)

\mathbf{u}=\tilde{\mathbf{c}}+\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{w}.

Beginning with (
2.1
), using the definitions of
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{w}
to eliminate
\mathbf{z}
throughout, and then using (
3.7
) to eliminate
\mathbf{u}
, we conclude that
\mathbf{w}
satisfies the following conditions simultaneously:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{b}}+\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbf{w},

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\perp\tilde{\mathbf{c}}+\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbf{0}.

Hence, if
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
, then
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})
solves the MLCP (
3.4
) as claimed.

Next, for the “if” part of the theorem’s claim, suppose that
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})
solves the MLCP (
3.4
). Define
\mathbf{u}
by (
3.7
), so that (
3.4
) yields
\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{u}\perp\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbf{0}
. Define
\mathbf{z}:=\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}
; it follows that
\mathbf{u}=\max(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{z})
and
\mathbf{w}=\max(\mathbf{0},-\mathbf{z})
, and so
|\mathbf{z}|=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}
. Now, (
3.7
) can be rearranged to yield
\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w})
, and so
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{L}|\mathbf{z}|
. Hence,
\mathbf{z}
is the unique vector that is consistent with (
2.1
), and so

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{J}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{z}|

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{J}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle\quad=\tilde{\mathbf{b}}+\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbf{0},

as claimed.

Next, if
m=n
and
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
is nonsingular, then (
3.5
) uniquely specifies
\mathbf{x}
as:

\mathbf{x}=-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbf{w}.

Substituting this expression for
\mathbf{x}
into the complementarity condition in the MLCP (
3.4
), and noting that (
3.5
) is equivalent to the equation in (
3.4
), we obtain (
3.6
) as required.

For instance, the following MLCP instance was constructed according to (

) for the function

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-45+49x+\begin{bmatrix}4&2&12\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\leq\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\

\displaystyle\perp\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}4\\

.

.

\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
for which

solves the following MLCP:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{0}&=&(\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{c})+(\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Z})\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{w}\\

4
New optimization formulations

This section adopts a similar approach to the previous section in order to optimize
\mathcal{PA}
functions with known abs-normal forms.
The following result shows that minimizing a function in abs-normal
form is equivalent to solving a
linear program with complementarity
constraints (LPCC)
in the sense of
[
13
,
7
,

]

4.2
Existence of minima

As defined in
[
20
]
,
horizon functions
will be
shown to characterize the existence of unconstrained global minima for
piecewise affine functions. First we establish some basic properties of horizon functions for
\mathcal{PA}
functions, and then we use these properties to obtain an MLCP that characterizes existence of a global minimum.

Definition 4.1
(adapted from
[
20
]
)

Given a continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the
horizon
function
for
f
is the mapping:

\displaystyle f^{\infty}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}:

\displaystyle\quad\bm{\xi}\mapsto\lim_{\delta\to{0}^{+}}\left(\inf\{\lambda f(\tfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\lambda}):\quad 0<\lambda<\delta,\quad\|\mathbf{x}-\bm{\xi}\|<\delta\}\right).

Any horizon function
f^{\infty}
is positively
homogeneous
[
20
, Theorem 3.2.1]
; thus,

f^{\infty}(0)=0
and

\inf_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})\in\{-\infty,0\}
. Intuitively, a horizon function describes how
f(\mathbf{x})
behaves when
\|\mathbf{x}\|
is large, and the graph of
f^{\infty}
is essentially a “highly zoomed-out” variant of the graph of
f
. Figure
1
illustrates the horizon function of a particular
\mathcal{PA}
function:

(4.10)

f(x)=x+\Big|2|x-2|-10\Big|+20.

The following lemma relates horizon function behavior to the existence of a global minimum
when
f
is
\mathcal{PA}
.

Figure 1:
The horizon function
f^{\infty}
(dashed) for the piecewise affine function
f
given by (
4.10
) (solid).

Lemma 4.1

Suppose that
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{PA}
, and choose
a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which
0\in\mathrm{int}(X)
. The function

f
has a (global) minimum in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if

\inf_{\bm{\xi}\in X}f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})=0
.

For the “only if” case, suppose that
\mathbf{x}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a minimum of
f
, and let

\phi:=f(\mathbf{x}^{\ast})
. Thus, for each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\delta>0
, and

\lambda\in(0,\delta)
, we have

\lambda f(\tfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\lambda})\geq\lambda\phi\geq-\delta|\phi|
.
It follows that, for each
\bm{\xi}\in X
,

\displaystyle f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to{0}^{+}}\left(\inf\{\lambda f(\tfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\lambda}):0<\lambda<\delta,\|\mathbf{x}-\bm{\xi}\|<\delta\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\delta\to{0}^{+}}(-\delta|\phi|)=0=f^{\infty}(\mathbf{0}),

and so
\inf_{\bm{\xi}\in X}f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})=0
.

Next, for the “if” case, suppose that
\inf_{\bm{\xi}\in X}f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})=0
, which implies
f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})\geq 0
for each
\bm{\xi}\in X
. Since
f^{\infty}
is positively homogeneous according to

[
20
, Theorem 3.21]
, and since
0\in\mathrm{int}(X)
, it
follows that
f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})\geq 0
for each
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By
[
22
, Proposition 2.2.3]
, there exists a finite collection
of polyhedra
P^{1},\ldots,P^{p}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which
f
is affine
on each
P^{i}
. Thus, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, the restriction of

f
to
P^{i}
may be
described as
f^{i}:y\in P^{i}\mapsto\langle{a}^{i},\mathbf{y}\rangle+b^{i}
for some

{a}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}b^{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
. By
[
22
, Theorem 2.1.2]
,
each
P^{i}
may be expressed as a Minkowski sum
C^{i}+R^{i}
, where

C^{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is compact and
R^{i}
(the
recession cone

of
P^{i}
) is a polyhedral cone.

Suppose, to obtain a contradiction, that

\langle{a}^{i},\mathbf{r}\rangle<0
for some
i
and some
\mathbf{r}\in R^{i}
. Then:

\displaystyle f^{\infty}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lim_{\delta\to{0}^{+}}\left(\inf\{\lambda f(\tfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\lambda}):\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\left.0<\lambda<\delta,\quad\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}\|<\delta,\quad(\tfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\lambda})\in P^{i}\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\lim_{\delta\to{0}^{+}}\left(\inf\{\langle{a}^{i},\mathbf{x}\rangle+\lambda b^{i}:\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\left.0<\lambda<\delta,\quad\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}\|<\delta,\quad(\tfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\lambda})\in P^{i}\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\langle{a}^{i},\mathbf{r}\rangle<0,

which contradicts the assumption that
f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})\geq 0
for each

\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus,
\langle{a}^{i},\mathbf{r}\rangle\geq 0
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}

and each
\mathbf{r}\in R^{i}
. Observe, moreover, that
\mathbf{0}\in R^{i}
. Lastly,
since each
C^{i}
is compact, there exists
\gamma^{i}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in C^{i}}f(\mathbf{x})
. Combining these observations,

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}}\left(b^{i}+\inf_{\mathbf{c}\in C^{i}}\langle{a}^{i},\mathbf{c}\rangle+\inf_{\mathbf{r}\in R^{i}}\langle{a}^{i},\mathbf{r}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}}\left(b^{i}+\langle{a}^{i},\bm{\gamma}^{i}\rangle+0\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}}f(\bm{\gamma}^{i}).

Thus, for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,
\bm{\gamma}^{i}
is a global minimum of
f
.

The above lemma depends heavily on the
\mathcal{PA}
assumption; it
is readily verified that the exponential mapping
x\mapsto e^{x}
has a
nonnegative horizon function but does not have a minimum in

\mathbb{R}
. The following lemma shows that horizon functions are
readily described for
\mathcal{PA}
functions with known abs-normal forms.

Lemma 4.2

Suppose that
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{PA}
. The function
f
has a global minimum in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if the equation system
f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})+1=0
has no solution
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Since
f^{\infty}
is positively homogeneous, there exists
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which
f^{\infty}(\mathbf{x})<0
if and only if there exists
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which
f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})+1=0
. At this point, the lemma follows immediately from Lemma
4.1
.

Lemma 4.3

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds with
m=1
. The horizon function

f^{\infty}
is
\mathcal{PL}
, and has the abs-normal form:

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\zeta}\\
f^{\infty}(\bm{\xi})\end{bmatrix}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Z}&\mathbf{L}\\
\mathbf{J}&\mathbf{Y}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\xi}\\
|\bm{\zeta}|\end{bmatrix}.

(Here,
\bm{\zeta}/\bm{\xi}
play the same role as
\mathbf{z}/\mathbf{x}
did in (
2.1
). We use differing notation here to distinguish a
\mathcal{PA}
function
f
from its horizon function
f^{\infty}
).

The piecewise linearity of
f^{\infty}
was established by Scholtes

22

; Scholtes calls the horizon function the “recession function”.

Now, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})
denote the unique value of
\mathbf{z}
that is consistent with (
2.1
). Suppose
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is fixed. Altering notation for consistency, Scholtes notes that there is some small
\nu>0
for which, for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
with
\nu>\lambda>0

\lambda f(\tfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\lambda})=\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x},

where
\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{{a}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{y}+b
is a particular affine piece of
f
(independent of
\lambda

\frac{1}{\nu}\mathbf{x}

\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}

|\mathbf{z}(\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{x})|=\bm{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{z}(\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{x})
whenever
\nu>\lambda>0
. At this point, if we start with (
2.1
), replace
\mathbf{x}
with
\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
with
\mathbf{z}(\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{x})

|\mathbf{z}(\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{x})|=\bm{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{z}(\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{x})
for all sufficiently small
\lambda>0
, and take the limit
\lambda\to 0^{+}
, we obtain this lemma’s claim.

\mathcal{PA}

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\cdot):d\mapsto\lim_{t\to{0}^{+}}\tfrac{1}{t}(f(x+td)-f(x))

is also a
positively homogeneous
\mathcal{PL}
approximation of
f
.
Note, however, that
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\cdot)
is not the same as
f^{\infty}
.
While, roughly,

f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\cdot)
describes the behavior of
f
near
\mathbf{x}
,
f^{\infty}

instead describes the behavior of
f
far from the origin.

The above lemmata can be combined to characterize existence of global minima for a

7
Conclusion

The above root-finding and optimization approaches are essentially new post-processing steps for the abs-normal form, and contribute to the practical utility of deploying the abs-normal form when considering “real-world”
\mathcal{PA}
functions. Recall that the abs-normal form itself is constructed via an AD-like procedure, and is also based on another piecewise-linearization AD procedure when a
\mathcal{PA}
approximation of a nonsmooth function is sought. Now that piecewise-affine root-finding and optimization have been expressed as standard complementarity problems or LPCCs, we expect that any future advances in solving LCPs, MLCPs, MILPs, and/or LPCCs will also enhance our capacity for computations involving
\mathcal{PA}
functions.
Future work may involve tailoring these methods to special cases, such as abs-normal forms that naturally represent convex functions or difference-of-convex functions (such as by
[
14
]
).
Future work may involve applying these methods to nontrivial application-oriented examples, perhaps involving neural networks, though this would require automation of the relevant function’s abs-normal form. (Our current implementation requires the abs-normal form as an input, and these were computed by hand in our numerical examples.) Since there is a new Julia interface
ADOLC.jl
v1.3.0
[
23
]
to the AD package
ADOL-C

[
25
]
, and since ADOL-C contains an implementation of piecewise linearization, perhaps these approaches could be chained together.
