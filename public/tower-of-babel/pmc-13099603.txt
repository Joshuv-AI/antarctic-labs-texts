WWOX maintains epidermal identity and suppresses EMT to prevent aggressive cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Tirza Bidany-Mizrahi, Kian Maroun, Mara Mancini, Osama Hidmi, Ihab Ansari, Jonathan Monin, Tal Keidar Haran, Alexander Maly, Gerry Melino, Eleonora Candi, Rami I. Aqeilan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) is an increasingly common cancer whose aggressive forms remain poorly understood. We identify WW domain–containing oxidoreductase (WWOX) as a central guardian of epidermal identity, stabilizing p63-driven transcriptional programs essential for epithelial homeostasis. Using conditional genetic models, transcriptomics, human tissue microarrays, and mechanistic assays, we show that loss of WWOX disrupts p63 activity, triggers epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and accelerates the evolution of aggressive tumors. These findings uncover a mechanistic WWOX–p63 axis that restrains cellular plasticity and malignant progression. Our work provides fundamental insights into squamous tumor biology and establishes WWOX and p63 as clinically relevant biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets in cSCC.

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) is the second most common form of skin cancer and its incidence has been rising steadily worldwide over the past decades (1, 2). The primary risk factor is ultraviolet (UV) radiation, with additional contributors including immunosuppression, fair skin, advanced age, exposure to chemical carcinogens, and infection with human papillomavirus (HPV) (1–4). cSCC arises from keratinocytes of the epidermis, particularly in the basal and suprabasal layers (5). In the United States, the lifetime risk of cSCC among white individuals is estimated at 14 to 20%, with reported increases of 50 to 200% over the last 30 y, a trend expected to continue with an aging population (1, 6, 7). A deeper understanding of the molecular etiology of cSCC is essential for improving its management and therapeutic intervention.

The WW domain-containing oxidoreductase (WWOX) gene, located at the common fragile site FRA16D, is frequently lost or downregulated in a wide range of malignancies (8–11), including cSCC (12). Our laboratory and others have demonstrated that WWOX loss is a hallmark of aggressive cancers such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), where it is associated with advanced stage and metastasis (13, 14). WWOX has also been implicated in suppressing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in ovarian, endometrial, and breast cancer cell lines (15–17). In murine models, WWOX deficiency alone can drive mammary tumorigenesis resembling basal-like breast cancer (BLBC), which is characterized by genomic instability and defective p53 signaling (18–20).

Functionally, WWOX interacts with more than 300 proteins and participates in diverse cellular processes including DNA repair, apoptosis, metabolism, and transcriptional regulation (10, 21–23). We and others have shown that loss of WWOX impairs ATM and ATR activation, compromising DNA damage response (DDR) signaling and promoting genomic instability (23, 24). Moreover, WWOX interacts with BRCA1, preventing its recruitment to the MRE11-RAD50-NBS1 (MRN) complex and shifting double-strand break repair from homologous recombination (HDR) toward nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) in breast cancers (25–28).

Although ΔNp63α, a key member of the p53 protein family, is recognized as a master regulator of epidermal development and differentiation (29, 30), the upstream mechanisms that sustain its stability and activity in vivo remain poorly defined. Loss of ΔNp63α expression characterizes mesenchymal-like cSCC, a phenotype associated with aggressive behavior and poor differentiation (31–34). WWOX has been implicated in diverse signaling pathways (9, 10), but its contribution to epidermal identity and p63 regulation has not been systematically investigated. While biochemical studies suggested that WW domain proteins can interact with ΔNp63α and influence its stability (10, 35, 36), whether this relationship plays a role in epithelial homeostasis and skin tumorigenesis in vivo is unknown. Furthermore, evidence from Wwox-null mice suggested altered p63 expression and impaired epidermal homeostasis (37), but the mechanistic and pathological significance of this observation had not been examined.

Here, we show using K14-Cre;Wwox;Trp53 conditional knockout (KO) mice that loss of WWOX accelerates p53-driven cSCC formation, resulting in poorly differentiated tumors with EMT features. Mechanistically, WWOX regulates p63 protein levels and transcriptional activity, thereby contributing to the maintenance of epithelial integrity and suppression of EMT. These findings, supported by mouse models, human tissue microarrays, and keratinocytes/cSCC cell lines, reveal a critical WWOX–p63 axis in cSCC and highlight WWOX as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

Results

Wwox/p53 DKO Mice Develop Earlier, More Frequent, and Poorly Differentiated cSCC with Marked Epidermal Hyperplasia at a Young Age.

cSCC originates from basal keratinocytes (Keratin 14+ cells) or from suprabasal cells derived from the basal layer of the epidermis (5). To specifically interrogate the role of WWOX in these cell populations, we generated a Keratin 14 (K14)-Cre conditional KO mouse model with either single or combined deletion of Wwox and Trp53 (38). A tdTomato reporter allele was included to trace Cre-mediated recombination (Fig. 1A). After confirming efficient recombination and loss of gene expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B), mice of all genotypes were monitored for spontaneous tumor development.

Consistent with previous observations, somatic loss of WWOX alone was insufficient to drive tumor formation (18, 19, 39–41) (Fig. 1B). However, deletion of one or both Wwox alleles in a p53+/fl background led to the development of cSCCs in ~40% of mice, whereas Wwox+/+; p53+/fl controls did not form tumors (Fig. 1C). Strikingly, in the p53fl/fl background, combined loss of one or both Wwox alleles markedly accelerated tumorigenesis: 100% of mice developed tumors, with a median onset of 225 and 251 d, respectively, compared to 65% incidence and a median onset of 371 d in p53fl/fl group, (Fig. 1D). Most tumors were cSCCs, while approximately 20% were mammary tumors, with some mice presenting with both tumor types. Together, these findings demonstrate that WWOX deficiency significantly accelerates tumor onset and progression in a p53-deficient context, underscoring WWOX as a key tumor suppressor.

To validate WWOX expression in the different genotypes, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on skin sections from wild-type (WT), p53 KO (p53fl/fl), Wwox+/fl; p53fl/fl and Wwoxfl/fl; p53fl/fl mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). In normal skin, WWOX was expressed in keratinocytes, consistent with its role as a tumor suppressor. WWOX staining was maintained in p53 KO tumors and, to a reduced extent, in Wwox+/fl; p53fl/fl lesions. By contrast, WWOX was undetectable in the Wwoxfl/fl; p53fl/fl DKO tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C).

We next investigated the histopathological characteristics of cSCCs arising in these mice. H&E analysis revealed that tumors from Wwox+/fl; p53fl/fl and Wwoxfl/fl; p53fl/fl mice were significantly less differentiated than those from p53fl/fl mice (Fig. 1 E and F). To assess early events preceding tumorigenesis, we examined skin from 100-d-old mice. Consistent with the enhanced tumorigenic potential, the epidermis of Wwoxfl/fl; p53fl/fl mice was markedly thicker and displayed pronounced hyperplasia compared with p53fl/fl mice (Fig. 1G). Although Wwox-deficient mice did not spontaneously develop cSCC, their epidermis was also significantly thicker than that of WT littermates (Fig. 1G).

To further evaluate early signs of transformation, we performed Ki67 staining on the 100-d-old mice, which revealed elevated proliferation in the epidermis of Wwoxfl/fl; p53fl/fl mice relative to p53fl/fl mice (Fig. 1H). We also used K14 (basal cell marker) and K10 (suprabasal marker) staining to distinguish proliferative from differentiating epidermal compartments (42). Epidermis from Wwox-deficient young mice contained a greater proportion of K14+ basal cells and fewer differentiated K10+ cells (Fig. 1I), consistent with a hyperproliferative, less differentiated state.

Given the established role of WWOX in DDR signaling, we hypothesized that WWOX deficiency would lead to elevated DNA damage, potentially explaining the early onset and high penetrance of tumors observed in our mouse model. Indeed, immunostaining of cSCCs revealed a significant increase in γH2AX foci, a marker of double-strand breaks, in tumors from Wwoxfl/fl; p53fl/fl mice compared with p53fl/fl mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Elevated γH2AX levels were also detected in keratinocytes from young WWOX-deficient mice prior to tumor onset, indicating that loss of WWOX promotes genomic instability early in epidermal development (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E).

Wwox KO mice do not spontaneously develop tumors; however, subjecting Wwoxfl/fl mice to the established multistage chemical carcinogenesis protocol, using DMBA-TPA treatment, resulted in numerous cSCC tumors and lesions over 12 wk (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Wwoxfl/fl mice developed lesions earlier and in greater quantities compared to WT mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C).

In addition to cSCCs, a subset of mice (~20%) developed mammary tumors, with some animals presenting with both tumor types. Although mammary tumors were not the primary focus of this study, we performed a preliminary histopathological assessment. Examination of H&E-stained sections revealed no clear differences in overall tumor aggressiveness scores among the different genotypes. However, mammary tumors arising in Wwox+/fl; p53fl/fl and Wwoxfl/fl; p53fl/fl mice tended to display increased nuclear atypia and larger tumor diameters compared with tumors from p53fl/fl mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 F and G).

Altogether, these findings show that WWOX alteration, particularly in the context of p53 deficiency, enhances epidermal proliferation, promotes poorly differentiated tumor phenotypes, and accelerates cSCC development. This indicates that WWOX is critical for maintaining epidermal homeostasis and suppressing malignant transformation.

Wwox-Deficient cSCCs and Young Epidermis Display Increased Tumorigenic Gene Expression and an EMT Signature.

To characterize molecular alterations associated with WWOX deficiency in cSCC and epidermal tissue, we performed RNA sequencing on tdTomato-expressing cells isolated from both spontaneous tumors and epidermis. Spontaneous cSCCs were collected from p53fl/fl (PKO) and Wwoxfl/fl;p53fl/fl (DKO) mice, dissociated into single-cell suspensions, and sorted based on tdTomato+ expression. As controls, epidermal tissues from aged WT mice were also processed. In parallel, epidermal cells from 100-d-old WT, Wwoxfl/fl (WKO), PKO, and DKO mice were isolated and subjected to the same workflow (Fig. 2A and Dataset S1).

Principal component analysis (PCA) of cSCC and aged epidermal samples showed clear segregation among WT, PKO, and DKO groups. In young epidermis, PCA also separated KO genotypes from WT, although the distinctions among KO subgroups were less pronounced (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed a substantial number of DEGs when comparing DKO vs. PKO in both cSCCs and young epidermis, and WKO vs. WT young epidermis (Fig. 2 B and C and Datasets S2–S4). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of DEGs from DKO vs. PKO cSCCs demonstrated significant enrichment of cancer-related pathways, including TNFα, KRAS, Notch, β-catenin/WNT, and EMT signaling (Fig. 2D). Similarly, analysis of young epidermis revealed enrichment for TNFα, KRAS, TGFβ, and UV-response pathways in DKO compared to PKO samples (Fig. 2E). Taken together, these data demonstrate that loss of WWOX promotes upregulation of tumorigenic gene programs and drives acquisition of an EMT signature in cSCC.

WWOX/p53 Double KO Promotes EMT and Cellular Plasticity in cSCC.

Given that EMT could underlie the poorly differentiated phenotype of DKO tumors, we examined this pathway more closely. Indeed, GSEA confirmed a robust increase in EMT-related signaling cascades in DKO cSCCs compared to PKO (Fig. 3A). To validate these findings at the protein level, we performed immunohistochemical staining for E-cadherin (epithelial marker) and Vimentin (mesenchymal marker) in cSCCs from PKO, Wwox+/fl;p53fl/fl, and DKO mice. Using tdTomato as a lineage tracer, we confirmed that tumor cells in PKO cSCCs maintained a fully epithelial phenotype (Fig. 3B). In contrast, tumors from DKO displayed marked heterogeneity, including epithelial and mesenchymal subpopulations, hybrid epithelial–mesenchymal cells (based on coexpression of epithelial (E-cadherin) and mesenchymal (Vimentin) markers), and in some cases fully mesenchymal phenotypes (Fig. 3 B and C). These findings demonstrate that WWOX deficiency in a p53-null background drives EMT and phenotypic heterogeneity, providing a mechanistic basis for the poorly differentiated nature of DKO cSCCs.

WWOX-Depleted Human Keratinocytes and cSCC Cells Are More Aggressive and Display Enhanced EMT Capabilities.

To validate our mouse model findings in human systems, we established nonmalignant and malignant keratinocyte cell lines with altered WWOX expression. Specifically, we used HaCaT, an immortalized adult human keratinocyte line, and A431, a human cSCC line, to model young epidermis and cSCC cells, respectively. WWOX was KO using CRISPR/CAS9 in both lines, and reexpression was achieved by rescue (Res) using Lenti-WWOX, in the presence or absence of p53 knockdown using shp53 (Fig. 4A). shp53 was used to better mimic the p53fl/fl background of our mouse model.

Functionally, A431 shp53 WWOX KO cells exhibited markedly enhanced survival and tumor growth potential both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 4 B and C). These cells also demonstrated significantly increased migratory and invasive capacity in transwell assays, consistent with the acquisition of EMT traits (Fig. 4 D and E). Under basal conditions, A431 shp53 or HaCaT shp53 cells lacking WWOX showed only modest changes in EMT markers, characterized by a mild reduction in epithelial gene expression and a moderate increase in EMT transcription factors and mesenchymal markers (Fig. 4 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C). However, upon induction of EMT with TGFβ treatment, these WWOX-deficient cells displayed a pronounced upregulation of mesenchymal markers, including Vimentin and Fibronectin, confirming an increased propensity to undergo EMT (Fig. 4 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Similar results were obtained on A431 and HaCaT cell lines without shp53 background (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–F)

Given that EMT facilitates metastatic dissemination, we next evaluated the metastatic capacity of A431 shp53 cells with WT, KO, or rescued WWOX expression. One million GFP-labeled cells were injected intravenously into immunodeficient mice. After 30 d, lung analysis by H&E staining and GFP imaging revealed that WWOX KO cells formed significantly larger metastatic lesions compared to WT or Rescued cells (Fig. 4H).

Together, these results demonstrate that WWOX loss in human keratinocytes and A431 cSCC cells promotes tumor aggressiveness, enhancing survival, growth, EMT plasticity, and metastatic potential.

WWOX Loss in Murine and Human cSCCs Leads to Diminished p63 Levels and Impaired Binding to Epithelial Gene Targets.

To uncover the mechanism by which WWOX loss promotes EMT, we examined its impact on the transcription factor ΔNp63, the predominant p63 isoform in the skin and a master regulator of epidermal development and epithelial identity (43–46). Loss of ΔNp63 expression is a hallmark of mesenchymal-like cSCC (32–34). and its enforced expression has been shown to restore differentiation in squamous tumors (31). Although ΔNp63 has context-dependent oncogenic and tumor-suppressive roles, it directly controls transcription of key epithelial genes that safeguard keratinocyte identity, including Cadherins, Perp, Desmoplakin, Par3, and MMP2 (29). Despite the established importance of ΔNp63, the upstream mechanisms that maintain its stability and function during tumorigenesis have remained unclear. Given the broad role of WWOX in protein networks and transcriptional regulation (9, 10, 21, 47), we hypothesized that WWOX deficiency may destabilize ΔNp63 and impair its binding to epithelial gene promoters, thereby driving EMT and aggressive cSCC.

We first examined whether WWOX associates with p63 in primary keratinocytes. To do this, we performed an in situ proximity ligation assay (PLA) in human neonatal epidermal keratinocytes (HEKn). Cells were stained with anti-p63 and anti-WWOX antibodies, and PLA signals were visualized by confocal microscopy. Strong punctate nuclear PLA signals were observed in HEKn cells transfected with control (scramble) siRNA, indicating close proximity between endogenous p63 and WWOX. Notably, siRNA-mediated knockdown of p63 significantly decreased the number of PLA-positive cells, confirming the specificity of the interaction (Fig. 5A). Quantification showed a substantial decrease in PLA-positive cells after p63 silencing. Effective p63 knockdown was verified by western blot analysis 48 h posttransfection. These results demonstrate close proximity between WWOX and p63 in keratinocytes, supporting their interaction, consistent with previous observations (48). To further support the association between WWOX and p63, we performed coimmunoprecipitation experiments in A431 cells. Immunoprecipitation of WWOX followed by immunoblotting for p63 revealed the presence of p63 in WWOX immunocomplexes in WWOX-expressing cells, whereas no signal was detected in control cells lacking WWOX (SI Appendix, Fig. S5I). These results indicate that WWOX and p63 are present within the same protein complex in cSCC cells.

We next determine whether WWOX loss impacts p63 expression and activity. In both human keratinocytes (HaCaT) and cSCC cells (A431), WWOX KO markedly reduced p63 protein levels compared to WT and WWOX-rescued cells (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5G). To assess the functional consequences of this reduction, we performed ChIP-seq in A431 shp53 cells. WWOX deficiency led to a striking decrease in p63 occupancy at its major target loci, including promoters of epithelial identity genes such as KRT14, KRT5, KRT1, ITGB4, COL17A1, and FZD6 (Fig. 5C), accompanied by diminished expression of several corresponding transcripts (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

To determine whether this effect is conserved in vivo, we examined cSCCs from PKO, Wwox+/fl; p53fl/fl, and DKO mice. While tumors from PKO mice retained robust p63 expression, WWOX-deficient tumors displayed a marked reduction in p63 staining (Fig. 5 D and E), consistent with WWOX serving as a stabilizer of p63 during epidermal tumorigenesis. This observation was true also for cSCC arising from the carcinogenic DMBA-TPA model, where Wwoxfl/fl mice display diminished p63 levels compared to WT mice cSCC (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D and E). Immunostaining for p63 showed a modest reduction in p63 levels in the mammary tumors from Wwox-deficient backgrounds relative to p53fl/fl tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 H and I), although these differences did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited number of tumors available for analysis.

We next extended this analysis to human tumors. IHC on tissue microarrays revealed that WWOX expression was strongly decreased in cSCC compared with normal epidermis (Fig. 5 F and G). Consecutive sections stained for p63 demonstrated a parallel reduction in p63 levels (Fig. 5 F–I). This concordant loss of WWOX and p63 highlights their functional interdependence and supports the idea that WWOX is required to maintain p63 activity and epithelial integrity.

Together, these results demonstrate that WWOX deficiency compromises both the stability and transcriptional function of p63 in murine and human cSCC. Loss of this regulatory axis provides a mechanistic explanation for the EMT phenotype, poor differentiation, and aggressive behavior characteristic of WWOX-deficient tumors.

Discussion

This study identifies WWOX as a critical tumor suppressor in cSCC and uncovers a mechanistic link between WWOX loss, destabilization of p63, and induction of EMT. Collectively, our findings position WWOX as a central safeguard of epidermal integrity, and highlight its disruption as a driver of aggressive cSCC.

Using conditional KO mouse models, we demonstrate that WWOX loss dramatically accelerates tumor onset and increases penetrance in a p53-deficient background, generating poorly differentiated tumors enriched with EMT signatures. These results extend the established view that WWOX deficiency sensitizes tissues to carcinogenesis in vivo (49, 50). Importantly, we reveal that WWOX loss in the epidermis induces hyperplasia and enhanced proliferation prior to tumor onset, consistent with observations that WWOX constrains keratinocyte proliferation and maintains epithelial homeostasis (37). Although mammary tumors were not the primary focus of this study, their occurrence in a subset of Wwox/p53 mutant mice is consistent with previous observations from our group (18, 25, 48) and others linking the WWOX–p63 axis to epithelial stemness and tumor progression (51, 52). These studies support a model in which disruption of WWOX–p63 regulatory networks may contribute to epithelial plasticity and tumor initiation across multiple epithelial tissues, including the mammary gland. Notably, the DMBA-TPA carcinogenesis experiments demonstrate that WWOX loss promotes tumor formation even in a p53-proficient background, supporting a tumor suppressor role for WWOX that extends beyond its cooperation with p53.

A key conceptual advance of this work is the demonstration that WWOX stabilizes p63-driven transcriptional programs that preserve epithelial identity. ΔNp63 is a master regulator of epidermal differentiation (29, 43–46), and its absence is a hallmark of mesenchymal-like cSCC (31–33). Notably, the role of p63 in cancer remains controversial and highly context-dependent, with evidence supporting both oncogenic and tumor-suppressive functions depending on tissue type, genetic background, and stage of disease (29). Our laboratory previously demonstrated that WWOX, via its first WW domain, interacts with ΔNp63 and stabilizes it by competing with the ITCH E3 ubiquitin ligase, thereby preventing ΔNp63 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation (53). Consistent with this mechanism, we confirmed the WWOX–p63 interaction in primary keratinocytes using a complementary proximity ligation approach and in WWOX-expressing A431 cells using immunoprecipitation experiments.

Our data further show that WWOX loss reduces p63 protein levels, diminishes genome-wide p63 binding, and suppresses transcription of epithelial gene targets. These effects mechanistically link WWOX deficiency to the acquisition of EMT traits, poor differentiation, and enhanced plasticity (Fig. 6). In human tissue microarrays, concurrent reduction of WWOX and p63 correlated with advanced tumor stage, underscoring the clinical relevance of this axis. These findings expand the paradigm of WWOX tumor suppression beyond its well-documented roles in DNA repair (23–28), TGFβ/SMAD3 signaling (54, 55), and other pathways (56–58). In line with a stabilization model, WWOX loss in our system results in reduced ΔNp63 protein levels without a corresponding decrease in p63 transcripts and is accompanied by diminished genome-wide p63 chromatin occupancy. Together with previous evidence that ITCH-mediated ubiquitination regulates ΔNp63 stability (48), these findings support a model in which WWOX preserves epithelial transcriptional programs by protecting ΔNp63 from proteasomal degradation (Fig. 6).

ΔNp63α has been widely described as an oncogenic driver in squamous carcinomas; however, accumulating evidence indicates that its function is context- and stage-dependent. While ΔNp63α can support early squamous tumorigenesis, its loss is frequently associated with EMT, dedifferentiation, and metastatic progression in advanced tumors (59, 60). Consistent with this view, ΔNp63 is a key regulator of epithelial lineage programs, and reduced ΔNp63 expression is commonly observed in poorly differentiated and mesenchymal-like squamous carcinomas (61, 62). Because ΔNp63 directly controls transcriptional programs required for keratinocyte proliferation, stratification, and differentiation, it plays a central role in maintaining epithelial architecture (63). Our findings support a model in which WWOX preserves ΔNp63-dependent transcriptional programs that maintain epithelial identity and restrain tumor plasticity and progression.

Another aspect of our study is the identification of epithelial- mesenchymal hybrid populations in DKO tumors. These intermediate cell states, detected by combined E-cadherin and Vimentin staining, are increasingly recognized as key mediators of cancer metastasis and therapy resistance (64–66). Our findings position WWOX as a barrier to EMT plasticity in squamous carcinogenesis. In vitro, WWOX-deficient keratinocytes and cSCC cells exhibited enhanced responsiveness to TGFβ stimulation, increased invasive capacity, and augmented metastatic colonization, linking WWOX loss to malignant dissemination.

Transcriptomic profiling further revealed profound alterations in oncogenic and microenvironmental pathways in WWOX-deficient epidermis and tumors, including enrichment for TNFα, KRAS, interferon, and EMT-associated signatures. These observations are consistent with reports that WWOX modulates inflammatory and survival signaling (37, 67) and suggest that its loss reshapes the tissue environment to favor tumor progression. Histological analyses also revealed increased fibrosis and collagen deposition in WWOX-deficient tumors, features associated with tumor stiffness and invasiveness (68).

Clinically, our findings have two major implications. First, WWOX and p63 may serve as biomarkers for aggressive cSCC, as their concurrent loss correlated with advanced stage. Second, the WWOX–p63 axis may represent a therapeutic vulnerability. Strategies aimed at restoring WWOX expression, stabilizing p63, or promoting epithelial differentiation may suppress EMT and restrain tumor progression. While direct reexpression of tumor suppressors remains technically challenging, emerging gene therapy and targeted protein stabilization approaches may provide future opportunities (69, 70). These may also include differentiation-based therapies that reinforce epithelial identity, inhibition of EMT-promoting pathways such as TGFβ signaling, toward which WWOX-deficient cells appear particularly sensitive, or approaches that modulate protein stability through targeted stabilization or degradation mechanisms. Together, these observations suggest that WWOX and p63 expression levels may also have value as prognostic or predictive biomarkers of tumor aggressiveness and therapeutic response.

In conclusion, our work reveals that WWOX regulates epidermal proliferation and epithelial identity through stabilization of p63-dependent transcriptional programs. Loss of WWOX drives EMT, poor differentiation, and metastasis, thereby promoting aggressive cSCC. These findings define the WWOX–p63 axis as a mechanistic safeguard of epidermal homeostasis and a clinically relevant determinant of skin cancer biology.

Methods

Mice.

Trp53fl/fl;Wwoxfl/fl (18, 41) mice were bred with K14-Cre mice (JAX) to generate double conditional KO (DKO) mice: K14-Cre; Wwoxfl/flTrp53fl/fland the other genotypes. After genotyping, the mice were tracked weekly for cSCC or mammary tumor formation and collected immediately when tumor detected. For the young mice epidermis, 100- to 120-d-old mice were used. Chemically induction of cSCC by DMBA-TPA treatment protocol (71) was performed on 7 to 9 wk old mice. After shaving the mice’s backs, DMBA treatment was followed by TPA promotion twice a week for 12 wk (both diluted in acetone). After killing, normal skin or tumors were fixed in 4% formaldehyde and processed for H&E staining, IHC, and immunofluorescence (IF). Our mice were handled in the specific pathogen free (SPF) animal facilities of the Hebrew university according to the ethical standards approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Cell Culture.

HaCaT and A431 cell lines were grown in DMEM or RPMI respectively (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), glutamine, and penicillin/streptomycin (Biological Industries). Human epidermal keratinocytes, neonatal (HEKn), were purchased from Thermo Fisher (C0015C) and grown in EpiLife medium (Thermo Fischer, MEPI500CA). All cells were grown at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were routinely authenticated and confirmed as Mycoplasma-free, and cell aliquots from early passages were used.

S clones for WWOX KO were produced using a lentiviral vector containing gRNA for WWOX exon 1 and selected by Blasticidin. Rescue clones were created from the WWOX KO cells using lentivirus containing CMV; WWOX overexpression vector and selected by G418 (Gibco 11811031). Clones of shp53 were done on WT/KO/Rescue cells transfected with PLKO.1 p53 shRNA vector and selected using Puromycin. For the PLA experiment, specific tp63 siRNA (CAGGUUGGCACUGAAUUCA) transfection for p63 silencing was performed with Lipofectamine RNAiMax Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were collected and analyzed 48 h after transfection.

TGFβ treatment for EMT induction.

Cells were grown on 5% serum media for 48 h prior to experimental start point, all the following treatments were done in 5% serum media as well. After 48 h, the cells were grown for 12 d under normal conditions (NT), with TGFβ treatment (5 ng/mL; 12D), or TGFβ treatment with SB431542 (TGFβ-R1 inhibitor, 1 µM; 12D+SB). TGFβ-R1 inhibitor was used as a control for TGFb induction. When reaching 70% confluency cells were split to prevent confluency affected cell adhesion.

PLA.

HEKn cells were fixed in formaldehyde and permeabilized with 0,1% Triton in PBS. The cells were probed with the following primary antibodies: anti-tp63 (ab735, Mouse, Abcam), anti-WWOX polyclonal antibody, and anti β-tubulin (ab15568, Rabbit, Abcam). The PLA staining was performed with Duolink In Situ Red Starter Mouse/Rabbit kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and analyzed with Stellaris-5 confocal system (Leica) and related LASX software.

Growth and Colonization Assay in Mice (Subcutaneous and Intravenous Injections).

For subcutaneous injections, one million cells suspended in RPMI, were injected subcutaneously in the back of 10-wk-old NOD/SCID mice and tracked every 2 to 3 d for their tumor volume. The volume was calculated by the tumor’s diameter measured via a caliper. After 19 d, mice were killed, and their tumors were analyzed. For intravenous injections one million cells suspended in HBSS, were injected in the tail vein of 8- to 12-wk-old NOD/SCID mice to study the colonization ability of cells in lungs. 30 d postinjection mice were killed and lungs were collected and fixated with 4% PFA for 1 h, 30% sucrose overnight, and OCT embedded. The lungs were sectioned using a cryostat and stained for DAPI nuclear counter stain. Lungs slides have been scanned by a digital slide scanner (3Dhistech) and quantified by Qupath (72).

Colony Formation Assay.

Cells were plated at a density of 300 cells in 6-well plates. After 7 d, the cells were fixed with methanol, stained with Giemsa, and counted for colonies. Three experimental repeats were done.

Immunohistochemical Staining of the TMA.

A skin cancer tissue microarray containing 10 samples of normal skin, 10 samples of malignant melanoma, 39 samples of cSCC, and 13 samples of cutaneous basal cell carcinoma was purchased from US Biomax (Cat. No. SK801c, Rockville, MD). Immunohistochemical staining for p63 and WWOX was performed using an anti-p63 antibody (ab735, Abcam), anti-WWOX antibody (73) with a dilution 1:700 for both of them. For staining, sections were dewaxed and rehydrated and incubated to block endogenous peroxidases in a 0.03% solution of hydrogen peroxide in methanol. Antigen retrieval was then performed by boiling the sample in 0.01 M citrate buffer pH 6.0 for 15 min in a microwave. The slides were incubated with an anti-p63 antibody (1:700), or -WWOX antibody (1:700) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using an UltraTek HRP antipolyvalent DAB staining system (ScyTek, Logan, UT), and the slides were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. The slides were scanned using a Leica Microscope DM6-B and processed with LASX Leica software.

Samples were scored in a blinded manner by a pathologist using a semiquantitative method. Cases were analyzed for staining intensity, which was scored as 0 (not detected), 1+ (weak), 2+ (intermediate), and 3+ (strong). For each case, the histological “H-score” (0 to 300) was calculated by multiplying the percentage of positive cells (0 to 100%) by the intensity (0 to 3).

Tissue Extraction, FACS, and RNA Sequencing.

After shaving, normal skin from young (100 d) or old (500 d+) mice was dissociated to single cells by Epidermis Protocol (74). Mice were tracked weekly for cSCC tumor development, when cSCC detected, mice were killed, tumors were extracted and dissociated to single cells by cSCC dissociation protocol (75). Cells were sorted for tdTomato+ cells by Sony SH800 cell sorter. After sorting RNA was extracted via Rneasy plus mini kit for RNA isolation (Qiagen; 74134).

mRNA library preparation and sequencing were carried out by the Genomic Applications Laboratory at the Core Research Facility of the Hebrew University, following a standard protocol. RNA quality was assessed using the TapeStation 4200 system (Agilent Technologies), High Sensitivity RNA ScreenTape (5067-5579) along with the Qubit® RNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen; Q32852). For library preparation, 5 ng of RNA from each sample was processed using the SMARTer® Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 - Pico Input Mammalian (Takara). Samples were eluted in 25 μL of 10 mM elution buffer, then all of them were pooled into 10 nM final concentration into a single tube. The library was sequenced using NovaSeq6000 machine (Illumina) targeting 40 million reads of single end reads of 122 bp. The BCL files (machine output) were converted to fastq files for bioinformatics analysis.

For library quality control, measures were applied to the raw FASTQ files, trimming low-quality bases (Q > 20), residual adapters, and filtering out short reads (L > 20). These measurements were performed using the Trim-Galore (v0.4.1) wrapper tool. File assessment before and after quality control was performed using FastQC (v0.11.5). The mouse transcriptome was mapped using salmon v0.13.1. Differential gene expression for the samples was explored using the R package (v1.16.1) and analyzed using DESeq2 (v1.28.1) (Datasets S1–S4).

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Sequencing (ChIP-seq).

ChIP-seq was performed as previously described (76). Briefly, A431 shp53 cells (~107) were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde (methanol free, Thermo Scientific 28906) for 10 min at room temperature and quenched with glycine, 125 mM final concentration. Fixed cells were washed twice in PBS and incubated in a lysis buffer (10 mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH = 8, and protease and phosphatase inhibitors) for 30 min on ice. Cells were sonicated using a bioruptor sonicator to produce chromatin fragments of ~200 to 300 bp. The sheared chromatin was centrifuged for 10 min at 20,000×g. From the supernatant, 2.5% were saved as input DNA and the rest was diluted in dilution buffer (50 mM TRIS-HCl pH8, 0.01% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, and 1% Triton X-100). The chromatin was immunoprecipitated by incubation with 5 μL of anti- ΔNp63 antibody [Rb polyclonal anti- ΔNp63 antibody (ab203826)]. Immune complexes were captured with protein G Dynabeads. Immunoprecipitates were washed once with low salt, twice with high salt buffer, and twice with LiCl buffer, and twice with TE buffer. The chromatin was eluted from the beads with 240 μL of elution buffer (100 mM sodium bicarbonate and 1% SDS) and incubated overnight at 65 °C to reverse the cross-linking. Samples were treated with proteinase K at 45 °C for 2 h. DNA was precipitated by phenol/chloroform/isoamylalcohol extraction. The ChIPed and the Input DNA were used to prepare libraries by Kappa Hyperprep kit and sequenced in Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

For ChIP-seq analysis, raw single-end reads were trimmed with Trim Galore! and aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using HISAT2, retaining only uniquely mapped reads with mapping quality ≥10. BAM files were sorted, indexed, and filtered with SAMtools. Normalized coverage and ChIP/Input enrichment tracks were generated with deepTools (bamCoverage and bamCompare) using read count normalization. Promoter-level enrichment (±1 kb from TSS) was quantified from normalized bigWig files with bigWigAverageOverBed (UCSC tools).

Statistical Analysis.

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. P values were determined by using the unpaired, two-tailed Student t test. P values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Detailed experimental procedures are provided in SI Appendix, Methods.
