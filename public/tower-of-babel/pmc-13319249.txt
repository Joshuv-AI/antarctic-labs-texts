High interobserver reliability of neutrophil phenotyping based on visual determination of CD16/CD62L expression patterns
Authors: Christian Thomas Hübner, Emma de Fraiture, Frederique van Eerten, Felix Karl-Ludwig Klingebiel, Nathalie Piaz, Hans-Christoph Pape, Leo Koenderman, Michel Paul Johan Teuben, Falco Hietbrink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAnalysis of neutrophil surface expression markers offers relevant insights into the inflammatory response following trauma. Recently a neutrophil phenotype classification based on CD16 and CD62L expression has been established. A seven-category visual classification system based on pattern recognition has been introduced for bedside use, but interobserver reliability has not been assessed to date.MethodsIn this study, 40 participants with varying medical backgrounds were asked to classify 50 CD16/CD62L dot plots using the seven-category scoring system. Participants received a standardized instruction manual for pattern recognition and category classification. Introduction

Neutrophils play a central role in the early innate immune response to tissue injury [1–3]. Advances in automated point-of-care flow cytometry have enabled rapid assessment of neutrophil surface marker expression in clinical settings [4–7]. Based on CD16 (FcγRIII) and CD62L (L-selectin) expression, subsets of neutrophils can be visualized in two-dimensional dot plots. These patterns reflect shifts in the circulating neutrophil population during acute inflammation and have been associated with both injury severity and clinical outcome [8–10].

A visual classification system for neutrophil phenotypes, ranging from Category 0 to Category 6, was previously introduced and validated using unsupervised clustering techniques [8]. This classification has been applied in several clinical studies to describe the inflammatory response following trauma or major surgery [11–14]. The categorization is based on visual pattern recognition of CD16/CD62L dot plots and is designed to be applicable at the bedside, without the need for complex software analysis [8].

Despite increasing adoption, the classification remains dependent on visual interpretation by the observer. To date, interobserver variability has not been formally assessed. The present study evaluates the consistency of visual categorization among individuals with varying levels of expertise. Observers include clinicians, researchers, and medical students, both with and without prior experience using the neutrophil phenotype classification system. This approach aims to determine whether experience is required for accurate and reproducible interpretation, and to assess the robustness of the method for broader clinical and scientific application.

Methods

Questionnaire design

This study was conducted as a self-administered survey assessing the visual classification of neutrophil phenotypes. Participants received an instruction manual that included background information on neutrophil biology, phenotypic changes in inflammation and trauma, and a detailed explanation of the classification system. The manual contained annotated examples illustrating how to interpret CD16/CD62L dot plots and apply the seven-category classification. In addition, the questionnaire collected demographic and professional background information, including gender, level of medical training, years of experience, and country of education and employment.

Each participant was asked to classify 50 anonymized random dot plots derived from trauma patients from a level 1 trauma center in Utrecht (The Netherlands). Plots displayed neutrophil expression patterns of CD16 and CD62L. Examples of neutrophil CD16/CD62L dot plots are provided in Fig. 1. No patient-identifiable information was included. The full questionnaire and instruction materials are provided in Supplementary Material 1. Participants completed the task either digitally or in printed format.

Definition of gold standard

The questionnaire was completed by three experienced raters who were involved in the development of the categorization system. All dot plots were independently evaluated by the three experts and assigned to one of the neutrophil categories. In cases of disagreement, the raters engaged in a personal discussion. If no consensus could be reached, the final decision was made by majority vote.

Study population

Participants were recruited through departmental meetings at two European trauma centers and via electronic distribution to medical students and other interested individuals. In-person sessions were supervised to ensure individual assessment without discussion or collaboration. No additional guidance beyond the instructional material and classification reference table was allowed during the rating.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages; continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Interobserver agreement was assessed using Fleiss’ Kappa, a statistical measure of reliability for multiple raters assigning categorical ratings. Interpretation of agreement levels followed conventional thresholds: κ ≤ 0.20 indicating poor, κ = 0.21–0.40 fair, κ = 0.41–0.60 moderate, κ = 0.61–0.80 substantial and κ > 0.81 almost perfect agreement beyond chance [15, 16]. Analyses were conducted in RStudio (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; version 2024.12.1 + 563).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 40 participants completed the visual neutrophil classification task (Table 1). The group included medical students (45%), residents (35%), attending physicians (18%), and one professor. Nearly all participants were working in the Netherlands (38%) or Switzerland (60%). The median age was 27 years (IQR 24–30), and 63% reported prior research experience. Roughly one-third had experience with flow cytometry (33%) or neutrophils (30%).

Classification accuracy and interobserver agreement

Interobserver agreement across the full seven-category system was moderate overall (κ = 0.473, 95% CI 0.396 to 0.540, p < 0.001), with category-specific values ranging from fair (κ = 0.391 and 0.398 for categories 0 and 1) to substantial (κ = 0.620 and 0.720 for categories 5 and 6).

A comparison of all participant ratings with the predefined gold standard across the seven neutrophil categories revealed substantial variation in classification rating among the categories. For instance, only 47.6% of the ratings for category 0 matched the gold standard. Categories 0, 1, and 3 were most frequently confused with one another, suggesting that early or mildly activated neutrophil phenotypes are more difficult to distinguish. In contrast, classification of higher categories (4-6) was more consistent. Notably, dot plots assigned to category 4 or higher by the gold standard were never misclassified as category 0, indicating that highly activated phenotypes are rarely mistaken for baseline patterns.

Performance metrics reflected these patterns: sensitivity was particularly low for category 0 (0.476), category 3 (0.625), and category 4 (0.607), indicating that mildly activated phenotypes were often missed. In contrast, categories 2 (0.838), 5 (0.787), and 6 (0.681) had higher sensitivity, suggesting better recognition. Specificity was generally high across all categories, ranging from 0.843 to 0.986, with the highest values observed for categories 2 and 6. Accuracy followed a similar trend, with lower values for category 0 (0.787) and higher values for categories 2 (0.969), 5 (0.934), and 6 (0.962). These findings support the notion that higher activation states are more readily and reliably identified than early-stage phenotypes. Figure 2 displays the confusion matrix. All ratings are reported in Supplementary Material 2.

Subsequently, the seven categories were grouped into a binary classification, “low” (categories 0–3) versus “high” (categories 4–6) inflammatory states, which improved agreement substantially. The corresponding confusion matrix is shown in Fig. 3. This binary classification yielded a Fleiss’ Kappa of 0.836 (95% CI 0.734 to 0.909, p < 0.001), indicating almost perfect agreement between raters. Additionally, the binary classification yielded a sensitivity of 0.915, specificity of 0.967, and an overall accuracy of 0.949, further highlighting the effectiveness of dichotomizing neutrophil phenotypes.

Discussion

This study evaluated the reliability of a visual neutrophil classification system based on CD16 and CD62L dot plots [8]. While the original seven-category system has been used in research during the past years to characterize the inflammatory response to trauma and surgery [11–14], its clinical utility depends on consistent and reproducible interpretation across observers. Our results demonstrate that visual classification into seven distinct categories yields moderate interobserver agreement, with most variability occurring in the lower and intermediate phenotypes. Importantly, agreement was best in the high immunophenotype categories, which were associated with an extensive inflammatory response and increased risk of complications. This suggests that the most clinically relevant cases, those with severe immune activation, are reliably identified across observers.

Observer training level and prior experience may influence classification performance, although patterns were not entirely consistent. Overall accuracy was comparable across attendings, residents, and students (Supplementary Material 3), but prior exposure to neutrophil phenotyping appeared most beneficial among residents. This suggests that some training levels may be more receptive to experience-based improvement, possibly reflecting differences in familiarity with immunological concepts or visual data interpretation. While these trends should be interpreted with caution due to limited group sizes, they indicate that targeted training could support more reliable classification, especially for those early in their clinical or research careers.

Differentiation between category 0 and categories 1 and 3 appeared to be particularly challenging. Gold standard category 0 was misclassified as category 1 in 34% and as category 3 in 15% of cases. Conversely, category 3 was frequently interpreted as category 0 (16%). This confusion might be explained by the similar visual patterns and the often indistinct subpopulations of neutrophils that serve as the primary distinguishing features between categories 0, 1, and 3.

In contrast, a simplified binary classification, distinguishing between low (categories 0–3) and high (categories 4–6) inflammatory states, resulted in almost perfect agreement among observers, regardless of their level of clinical or research experience.

These findings support the feasibility of application of a binary approach in inflammation detection in clinical settings when time and expertise may be limited. Importantly, the simplified classification had strong discriminatory power: plots classified by the gold standard as highly inflammatory were almost never misclassified as low. This indicates a low rate of false negatives.

Based on the literature, a binary classification may be clinically useful, as categories 0–3 were rarely associated with differences in Injury Severity Score (ISS), mortality, ICU admission or infection rates [8]. This simplified approach facilitates bedside application, where rapid recognition of patients at risk for immune-related complications, such as secondary infections, is essential for timely intervention [2, 3, 17].

Importantly, this does not preclude clinical value of the full seven-category classification. While more complex, it offers a more nuanced view of neutrophil activation dynamics and inflammatory progression, which may support both research and advanced clinical decision-making. This implies that while a binary tool may suffice for rapid triage, full-spectrum immune profiling could be enabled by structured training or automated algorithms. Visual classification strategies may thus serve as a foundation for explainable AI systems that replicate expert-level pattern recognition, enabling real-time immune monitoring even outside specialized centers.

One limitation of the current study is that the gold standard was defined by experts involved in the development of the classification system. This might introduce a bias with circular validation of the predefined categories. As the categories are a new concept, the missingness of independent reference standard is a matter. Future studies should evaluate the reproducibility of these findings using external experts.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that binary neutrophil classification is feasible. The high interobserver reliability suggests the potential use in clinical practice for rapid and reliable identification of high-risk patients.
