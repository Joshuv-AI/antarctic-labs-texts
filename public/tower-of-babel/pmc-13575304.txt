Patients in Every Practice: Health Status and Health Experiences of an International Sample of Adults Born Preterm
Authors: Michelle M. Kelly, Kathyrn King, Caitlyn Ng, Lauren Ingledow, Margaret Brace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDue to the increased survival and stable prevalence rates of preterm birth, every clinician, regardless of the patient population or practice setting, has cared for someone born preterm. Unfortunately, most healthcare teams fail to consider preterm birth as a risk factor to adult health, missing a critical opportunity to mitigate risk in this vulnerable population. The purpose of the study was to explore the health status and health experience of adults born preterm with comparisons between birth year cohorts and gestational age categories.DesignThe study utilized a cross‐sectional, quantitative descriptive comparative design, augmented by qualitative analysis of open‐ended questions.MethodsThe health status and health experience survey was developed in collaboration with leaders of an advocacy group for adults born preterm and based on the available research. Convenience, snowball sampling among members of the advocacy network and other preterm birth groups provided an international sample of participants (N = 80).ResultsChronic conditions or symptoms, representing health status, were reported by 85.7% of participants, with 75% of those participants taking medications for the condition. Participants born less than 32 weeks of gestation reported experiencing health conditions significantly more than those born at later gestations (91.8% vs. 62.5%, p = 0.008); there was no difference in birth year cohort. The most commonly reported conditions were respiratory, mental health, neurodevelopmental, and musculoskeletal. Two‐thirds of participants reported never being asked about preterm birth history by healthcare teams. Healthcare experiences were described as ambivalent or dismissive among those who chose to disclose preterm birth status to providers.ConclusionsChronic and comorbid conditions associated with preterm birth are common among adults born preterm. These conditions are responsive to prevention and mitigation strategies and, as such, failure to identify adults born preterm as a population susceptible to chronic health conditions results in unidentified, unmanaged risk.

Introduction

In the era of modern neonatal care, 95% of the 15 million infants born preterm each year survive to adulthood (Ward et al. 2024). With some variation among countries, the preterm birth rate has remained stable over the last decade (Bradley et al. 2025). Preter, birth is defined as birth before the 37 weeks of gestation, or more than 3 weeks prior to the due date. The timing of preterm birth is categorized into gestational age groupings, with birth before 28 weeks of gestation termed extremely preterm, between 32 and 28 weeks of gestation is termed very preterm, while birth between 32 and 34 weeks of gestation is termed moderately preterm, and birth between 34 and 37 weeks of gestation is termed late preterm (Ward et al. 2024). These categorizations enable researchers and providers to discuss outcomes associated with different gestational age groups. Globally, 85% of preterm births occur between 32 and 37 weeks of gestation, a period when survival is expected with access to special newborn care even if neonatal intensive care is not available (Bradley et al. 2025).

For today's healthcare teams, stable preterm birth rates and increased survival rates mean that every clinician, regardless of the patient population or practice setting, has cared for someone born preterm, most without asking about preterm birth as a component of past medical history or understanding the implications of such histories. In 2017, Raju et al. published a landmark executive summary of a workshop exploring the long‐term healthcare outcomes of preterm birth. Adult outcomes associated with preterm birth include impaired respiratory function, a 3–5‐fold increase of obstructive sleep apnea, hypertension, and increased risk of endocrine dysfunction, reduced nephrons resulting in impaired renal function and increased risk for neuropsychiatric conditions (Raju et al. 2017). The executive summary advocates for increased awareness about preterm birth outcomes among healthcare teams, improved communication between healthcare, educators and parents, and an urgent call for the documentation of gestational age and birth weight as a component of past medical history throughout the life span (Raju et al. 2017).

Despite the recommendations, in clinical practice, preterm birth history, including gestational age and birth weight, is only documented as part of past medical history from birth until approximately age 4. Some specialties such as obstetrics, pulmonology, and neurology are more likely than primary care to include preterm birth in a past medical history. Additionally, access to hospital birth records is quite variable, even with the proliferation of electronic health records. For example, in the United States (US), retention of medical records is governed by state law, concerned mainly with medical malpractice, with most states retaining minors' records until between 19 and 24 years of age, and adult records for 5 to 10 years after the last patient encounter (American Academy of Pediatrics 2025). The fragmentation of the US healthcare system, coupled with the variation in electronic health record systems, limits access to birth records in adulthood, making incorporation of preterm birth as a component of past medical history challenging.

Adult Health Status

Several literature reviews were published exploring the adolescent and adult health outcomes (such as Kelly and Griffith 2019; Kajantie et al. 2019; Crump 2020a) and mortality rates (Crump 2020b; Risnes et al. 2021) of individuals born preterm. These reviews reinforce the findings of Raju et al. (2017). Adults born preterm are at increased risk for alternations in respiratory (Course et al. 2024), cardiovascular (Greer et al. 2022), endocrine (Claffey et al. 2025), neurodevelopmental (Cheong et al. 2020), mental and behavioral conditions (Crump 2020a; Crump et al. 2021) compared to term born peers. Most of the long‐term outcome data originates from birth cohorts outside of the US (Raju et al. 2017). Respiratory outcomes are reported to have a geographic variation with Western Europe and the US experiencing poorer outcomes than Scandinavian countries (Course et al. 2024). Other health conditions follow similar geographic variations due to healthcare access and socioeconomic factors.

The types of conditions experienced by people born preterm were once described as high‐frequency, low‐severity conditions, the type that are experienced commonly by all, but occur at a noticeably higher rate in those born preterm (Aylward 2005). The intervening years of research suggest that some conditions occur earlier. For example, up to two‐thirds of adult chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) may be related to accelerated early decline in lung function related to developmental lung delays from preterm birth (Polverino and Sin 2026). Kelly and Brace (2024) report a 12% earlier diagnosis of diabetes in preterm low birth weight individuals compared to term born, normal birth weight. Failure to identify preterm birth as a component of past medical history precludes the ability to utilize that information in health risk and treatment plans. Delay in treatment is costly and modest increases in chronic health conditions pose a significant financial and resource burden to healthcare (Raju et al. 2017) and educational systems (Larsen et al. 2024; Louis et al. 2024) and also impair the health‐related quality of life of individuals (Kaplan and Hayes 2022).

Kelly et al. (2021) published recommendations for primary and specialty care clinicians to inform the use of preterm birth as a component of past medical history in clinical practice. Several authors and researchers have echoed the call for healthcare teams to illicit and utilize preterm birth history to greater understand individual risk of common chronic conditions in adulthood (such as Kelly et al. 2021, Girard‐Bock et al. 2021; Bush et al. 2025; Stegmann‐Woessner et al. 2025). Appreciation of the risks posed by preterm birth history is essential to health promotion and risk mitigation. Unfortunately, implementation of these recommendations has been elusive.

Healthcare Experience

The healthcare experience includes patient‐provider interactions which should build a rapport that generates a shared trust and respect (English et al. 2022). Primary care and general practice clinicians are at the forefront of both health promotion and chronic disease management (Reddy et al. 2020). Outside of the neonatal intensive care unit and early developmental or high‐risk follow‐up programs, there are no adolescent or adult specialty tasked with the care for people born preterm. Unfortunately, when preterm birth is not considered as a relevant component of past medical history, adults born preterm become an invisible, yet vulnerable group (Stegmann‐Woessner et al. 2025). This can result in mistrust in the healthcare system, evidenced by delays in seeking care for chronic conditions (Stegmann‐Woessner et al. 2025; Shukla et al. 2025).

Research Aims

The aim of the current research study was to explore the health experience of adults born preterm. The primary researcher and the leaders of the Adult Preemie Advocacy Network (APAN), an international adults born preterm advocacy group, have a well‐developed partnership that includes speaking at professional conferences, public scholarship and research. Through this collaboration, a mutual desire to formally explore the healthcare experiences of adults born preterm developed. This collaboration informed the research questions, as did the gap in existing literature on whether health status and health experiences differ across gestational ages and birth year cohorts. Previous studies have not explored these group differences, and it is unknown if observed health complications or healthcare experiences are similar across gestational age groups, or across birth years. This research moves the understanding of health status and healthcare experiences of adults born preterm forward.

Materials & Methods

Design/Sample

The current study explores the adult health status and healthcare experience of people born preterm, across gestational age and birth era cohort groups. The study utilized a cross‐sectional, quantitative descriptive comparative design, augmented by open‐ended responses. The questionnaire was informed by existing literature and refined based on feedback from the advocacy group directors. Language was modified to be acceptable to an international sample of people who spoke and read English. Snowballing recruitment of adults born preterm was conducted through international adults born preterm advocacy groups and social media pages. Direct email recruitment occurred through professional contacts of the researchers. The sample was limited to adults over the age of 18 and born less than 37 weeks of gestation who could respond in English. Participants who indicated a birth year after 2006 (less than 18 years of age at the time of the data collection) or gestational age > 37 weeks were automatically taken to the end of the survey. Demographic information included year of birth, country and region of birth, gestational age, birth weight, and sex.

To enable group comparisons, gestational age was categorized as birth at less than 32 weeks or birth equal to or greater than 32 weeks of gestation. Very or extremely premature infants, those born less than 32 weeks of gestation, are known to have increased neonatal medical complexity compared to infants born at later gestations. There has been an increased interest in understanding the outcomes of those born preterm at all gestational ages, as health risks are not absent in those born late preterm (34 to 36 weeks of gestation). Further APAN is comprised of people from a wide range of gestational ages, who share similar health stories across gestational ages. For this reason, it was decided that comparisons are based on gestational ages based on two categories: those born very or extremely preterm (< 2 weeks of gestation) and those born moderately preterm (> 32 weeks of gestation).

Birth years were categorized as pre‐surfactant (born before 1990) and post‐surfactant (born after 1990) (Philip 2005). Routine use of exogenous surfactant in the 1990s resulted in vastly increased survival for preterm infants at younger gestational ages (Philip 2005). As with gestational ages, APAN is comprised of participants across multiple generations. While exploring health experiences, the research team aimed to determine if reported experiences of the pre‐surfactant cohort would be appreciably different from the post‐surfactant cohort. Categorization has been used to mitigate recall bias in self‐report data particularly with adults born preterm (Nilsen et al. 2017). No artificial intelligence generating content tools were used to develop the survey or the manuscript.

Adult Health Status

Participant health status was explored through the yes/no question: Is your health an ongoing concern for you? Participants were asked to describe any chronic conditions or symptoms experienced, as well as medications used. Additionally, participants were asked if healthcare teams indicated that the chronic conditions were due to being born preterm, or if the participants believed the conditions were linked to preterm birth. The team utilized self‐report of chronic symptoms and conditions, independent of formal diagnosis, to assess health status. This was determined to be an important component of giving voice to the perception that many adults born preterm experience symptoms but fail to secure a formal diagnosis from healthcare teams.

Healthcare Experience

The APAN group helped to construct and revise questions to further explore participants' healthcare experience. It was important to the group to quantify if participants were asked about preterm birth history and that participants be given the opportunity to share more broadly about experiences with healthcare teams. To explore the healthcare experience, participants were asked two yes or no questions with text boxes for additional comments: (1) As an adult, have you ever been asked if you were born preterm? (2) As an adult, have you shared that you were born preterm with your healthcare team? Subsequently, two additional open‐ended questions posed were: What would you like healthcare teams to know or understand about being an adult born preterm? Is there anything else you would like to share about your life as an adult born preterm?

Full description of the qualitative analysis will be available in a subsequent publication.

Data Analysis

Data analysis included descriptive statistics to explore the sample characteristics including means, standard deviation, or percentages. Comparisons of sample groups were conducted using Pearson Chi‐Square or Fisher's Exact test as needed for low expected cell counts. Comparison groupings were dichotomized for gestational age: less than 32 weeks of gestation, greater or equal to 32 weeks of gestation, birth year era: pre‐surfactant or born before 1990, post‐surfactant or born after 1990. For 2 × 2 Chi‐Square tests, a target sample size of 80 participants would have 80% power (α = 0.05) to detect medium effect sizes (w = 0.31). All available data for each comparison were analyzed, excluding cases with missing data pairwise, and n's are reported in result tables. Significance p‐values and the overall alpha level (α = 0.05) were not adjusted for multiple testing. All categorical questions (yes/no) included a text response option to allow participants to elaborate as desired. Data analyses were conducted in StataSE, version 19.

Human Subjects Protection

This research was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board Office of Research Protections at Villanova University and designated an Exempt Category 2 study. Informed consent was provided through the recruitment email which included an explanation of the research, the survey, and voluntary participation. Consent to participate was indicated by clicking into the survey from the recruitment email.

Results

The survey was available from February to June of 2025 and resulted in a total sample (N = 80) that was primarily female (86%), with a mean age of 40 years (SD 12.3). Participants were grouped by birth year into pre‐surfactant (born prior to 1990) and post‐surfactant eras (born after 1990) with 66% born prior to the routine use of surfactant (mean age 46.5 years [SD 9.4]) and 34% born in the surfactant era (mean age 27.1 years [SD 5.3]). The sample included participants from several countries: 69% from the US, 5% from Canada, 17% from the United Kingdom, 6% from other European countries, and 2% from Australia. Adult Health Status

Health was reported as an ongoing concern for 78.8% of participants. There was no significant difference in reporting that health was a concern based on gestational age grouping or birth era grouping (Table 2). Chronic conditions or symptoms were reported by 85.7% of participants, and 75% of participants took one or more medications to treat those conditions. A significant difference between gestational age groups for having a chronic condition appeared with those born < 32 weeks being more likely to report chronic conditions (91.8% vs. 62.5%, p = 0.008). There was no significant difference in taking medication by gestational age grouping. There was no statistical difference in the birth year groupings for either chronic conditions or medication usage.

The responses of participants who reported experiencing chronic conditions or symptoms (n = 66) and medications (n = 58) were grouped by category (Figure 1). Several participants reported conditions and medications in more than one category. The most frequently reported category was respiratory (n = 39) with asthma, COPD/bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), and allergies commonly reported. Mental health conditions were the second most common condition (n = 38) which primarily consisted of anxiety and depression. Reported neurodevelopmental conditions were grouped separately (n = 37) consisting of attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and attention deficit disorder, neurodiversity/autism spectrum disorder, developmental/learning delays, and headaches/migraines. Musculoskeletal conditions were also frequently reported (n = 30) and consisted of joint pain and osteoporosis/bone disease.

Self‐reported conditions and medication usage categories.

Only 40.3% of participants reported being told the experienced health condition or symptom was related to preterm birth, while 92% of participants think that a health condition or symptom could be related to preterm birth. Participants born less than 32 weeks of gestation were more likely to report being told that health conditions were related to preterm birth (46.2% vs. 10%, p = 0.040). No significant difference by birth year grouping was identified.

Participants shared comments related to how health symptoms present: We are sometimes the exception to the rule (27 weeks gestation). Others reminded that frame of reference matters …adults born preterm have never known anything other than their baseline conditions…so can often think they are asymptomatic when actually they are not (24 weeks gestation). Participant comments reflected a feeling that their health status was atypical, unusual or different. A 42‐year‐old participant born at 32 weeks of gestation shared: I'm unsure of what my future will be like because of my end stage COPD. The uncertainty and fear are not limited to those who currently have diagnosed conditions:Beyond occasional shortness of breath, brain fog, and struggling to engage socially with life the older I've gotten, I have lived a pretty normal life with no adverse affects. I just worry a lot about my mental state and brain health… (26 weeks gestation).

Healthcare Experience

Sixty‐seven percent (67%) of participants were never asked if they were born preterm by healthcare teams (Table 2). There was no statistical difference based on gestational age grouping. However, post‐surfactant era participants were more likely to report being asked about preterm birth than older participants. Overall, 82% of participants reported sharing preterm birth history with healthcare teams. Those born less than 32 weeks of gestation reported sharing preterm birth history more frequently than those born at later gestational ages (93.4% vs. 44.4%, p < 0.001). No significant difference in birth year grouping was identified.

Open‐ended text responses from participants ranged from not knowing the information should be shared, to some health care providers reporting surprise at how well the participant was doing, to experiencing outright dismissal by health care teams. Several participants assumed that birth history and gestational age remained an accessible part of the medical record, so there would be no need to reshare the information. Others called for better understanding of preterm birth outcomes by healthcare teams and for that to extend to anticipatory guidance for families and people born preterm. Having more of an understanding of this myself at a younger age would have helped me not to believe all of this was some kind of problem with me or a character deficiency (28 weeks).

Several participants shared stories of clinician ambivalence or negating that anything that happened at birth could still be relevant to an adult of 30 or 40 years of age. One participant shared that providers …shrug it off and say because I'm 38 and in general good health, it's nothing to worry about (26 weeks gestation). That ambivalence sometimes progressed to being characterized as hypochondriacs or being overly concerned with symptoms. One participant shared: I get the impression they don't care or find it strange that I'm bringing it up. Part of me is concerned that this will get me viewed as, not a hypochondriac exactly, but someone overly concerned with “minor” health issues (27 weeks gestation).Participants described providers who asserted that there was no scientific evidence to connect preterm birth with adult health conditions. One participant simply stated: I'd like my concerns taken seriously (26 weeks gestation).

To counter the ambivalence and lack of awareness, some participants shared stories of bringing copies of research literature to appointments. This self‐advocacy was met with varying degrees of acceptance. One participant whose healthcare provider admitted having a preterm‐born child at home asked to keep copies of the resources. While another participant described: I offered bullet pointed information that was brief and to the point. He responded that he did not need to read it and he was not a medical student (26 weeks gestation).

Discussion

Adult Health Status

This study presents a novel health status and experience comparison across gestational age and birth era cohort. Unique to this sample was the larger percentage of those who were born before 1990 (66.2%), and those who were born less than 32 weeks of gestation (77.5%). This would suggest that 66% of the sample did not have the benefit of exogenous surfactant usage or advanced mechanical ventilation strategies (Philip 2005). Three‐quarters of the sample were at greater health risk based on gestational age. However, participants in this sample, for whom health is a concern (79%), did not differ by gestational age or birth era. Similarly, the reported incidence of taking medications (75% of total sample) did not differ by gestational age or birth era.

The lack of reported difference in health concerns, chronic conditions or symptoms, between gestational age and birth cohorts is important for healthcare teams to understand. Conventional research suggests that younger gestational ages are at increased risk of impaired outcomes (e.g., Cheong et al. 2021; Course et al. 2024), however, in adulthood, gestational age categories were less influential. Adults born preterm report health being a concern and taking medications similarly across groups. The similarity between groups adds to the literature and reinforces that adults born preterm are experiencing similar health status across gestational age groups, not just those born at earlier gestations.

The majority of adults born preterm in this study attribute their chronic conditions and symptoms to being born preterm. These findings are consistent with existing research exploring the health status of adults born preterm. Perez et al. (2020) conducted in‐depth interviews with adults (aged 20 to 54) born less than 33 weeks gestation in Germany. Participants reported that prematurity is an everyday concern, with 65% of participants reporting physical and 45% reporting mental health sequelae (Perez et al. 2020). Girard‐Bock et al. (2023) explored perspectives of adults born preterm in a sample of participants born between 1987 and 1996 in Montreal and recontacted as adults. The Montreal sample reported one or more adverse health conditions in 77% of the participants (Girard‐Bock et al. 2023).

Respiratory conditions and subsequent respiratory medication usage were the most common chronic conditions or symptoms in this sample. Impaired respiratory function is a hallmark of preterm birth, with well recognized associations between gestational age less than 32 weeks and reduced pulmonary function later in life (Du Berry et al. 2022). Pre‐surfactant era adults born at later gestational ages represent a group at increased risk of developing COPD (Du Berry et al. 2022). More recently, the risk of inadequate lung function in early adulthood has included those born at later gestations and without BPD diagnosis (Du Berry et al. 2026). Additionally, the reduced lung function experienced by people born preterm may require alternative treatment strategies. For example, even in childhood, inhaled corticosteroids provide only modest improvement in lung function for those born very preterm diagnosed with asthma, suggesting a need to distinguish between asthma and prematurity associated lung disease (Bradshaw et al. 2024). Bush et al. (2025) identified inadequate communication between providers related to the lifelong respiratory risk following preterm birth, even among adult allergists and pulmonologists. Course et al. (2024) call for healthcare teams caring for adults to support respiratory health of people born preterm. Supporting respiratory health includes health promotion activities such as reduced exposure to environmental toxins, particularly tobacco, and routine exercise (Du Berry et al. 2026).

Mental health conditions, like anxiety and depression, were reported by 58% of participants who reported chronic conditions. Neurodevelopmental conditions like ADHD and autism were reported by 56% of participants who reported chronic conditions. This is consistent with prior research identifying increased risk of adolescent to adult onset of anxiety, ADHD, and psychosis, with dose dependence increasing as gestational age decreased (Balit et al. 2025). A unique feature of the preterm phenotype of ADHD is one that is typically described as internalizing or inattentive rather than externalizing or hyperactive (Kelly et al. 2023). Both mental health and neurodevelopmental conditions are particularly susceptible to the caregiving environment and may be modified with proper support (Kelly et al. 2023). Balit et al. (2025) call for continued follow‐up beyond childhood, particularly for mental health conditions.

Musculoskeletal conditions, including joint pain and osteoporosis/bone disease, were reported by 45% of participants who experienced chronic conditions or symptoms. In the current sample, these symptoms did not appear to be related to a reported diagnosis of cerebral palsy or other mobility diagnoses. This finding, and the lack of a cerebral palsy diagnosis, is important for clinicians to realize that preterm born adults may experience joint pain and osteoporosis independent of a mobility‐related diagnosis. A systematic review by Deprez et al. (2024) describes alterations in skeletal muscle composition and function in adults born preterm, with significant reduction in muscle power as assessed by jump testing. Claffey et al. (2025) report poorer bone health and decreased bone composition in younger adults (aged 18 to 29) born preterm. Bone density is not expected to decline until after age 50 and would not be routinely evaluated in younger adults; however, this may need to be assessed earlier in adults born preterm. Muscle and joint pain, as well as osteoporosis, are conditions that have significant implications for overall health and quality of life and need to be managed adequately.

The symptoms and conditions experienced by our sample of adults born preterm are those that are additive but responsive to mitigation strategies. Respiratory compromise complicates physical activity but is also improved by a regular pattern of exercise. Similarly, immobility due to musculoskeletal pain, if not properly managed, will limit participation in healthy physical activities, exacerbating respiratory difficulties and reducing quality of life. Anxiety and depression, if not managed and supported, further influence quality of life.

Healthcare Experiences

Primary and general practice healthcare teams encounter people born preterm every day and are often the gatekeepers to symptom management. Our sample shows slight differences related to healthcare teams asking about preterm birth history, with 67% reporting never being asked about preterm birth history, compared to the 80% reported never being asked in the German sample (Perez et al. 2020). A desire for informed, specialized healthcare for people born preterm is evident from single center qualitative studies from the United States (D'Agata et al. 2022) and in Germany (Stegmann‐Woessner et al. 2025; Perez et al. 2020). Participant comments from this international sample add depth to the data by highlighting the need to address provider knowledge, ambivalence, and negative perceptions towards adults born preterm. Healthcare teams must understand the importance of preterm birth history and must communicate that risk to families of preterm infants. Across the life course, adolescents born preterm must be educated regarding the importance of this component of past medical history, similar to sharing other components of past medical history. Nurses, as consistent, accessible, and influential members of the healthcare team, must reinforce this message.

Participants held the misconception that past medical and family history, including neonatal course, would auto‐populate or be transferred from one provider encounter to another. The current participants shared the assumption that the information was “in the chart” so retelling the story was unnecessary. Further complicating these situations is the fragmentation of healthcare systems and the lack of unified national patient record systems in many countries.

Implications for Healthcare Teams

Failure to document preterm birth as a component of past medical history throughout the lifespan denies the importance of pre and postnatal exposures. Yet, without health care teams understanding that preterm birth influences future health, that link may be elusive. Therefore, recognition of the influence of preterm birth on adult health is needed to mitigate risk and maximize the potential of people born preterm. This requires not just asking about birth history when a patient is pregnant, seeking respiratory care, or living with a developmental delay. Preterm birth history, particularly gestational age, birth weight and neonatal course complexity, must be documented in a manner that will be retained by the electronic medical record throughout the patient's lifetime. Nurses must reinforce the importance of this with families at discharge from the neonatal intensive care unit, and throughout childhood. Additionally, healthcare teams need to utilize the history of preterm birth the way family history of heart disease, diabetes, or personal history of smoking is used, as a known risk to be mitigated when possible.

Future research involving adolescents and adults born preterm should incorporate symptomatology and patient experience to augment understanding of clinical diagnosis. The participants conveyed clearly that symptoms experienced by adults born preterm may not follow typical presentation patterns. There is a possibility that patterns may be established when preterm birth is connected to a patient's past medical history. These patterns may not be evident at the individual level; however, documentation of preterm birth as a diagnosis and component of past medical history will allow for empirical research with larger datasets to establish timing of symptoms, onset of disease, and track medication efficacy. Further, there is a call for healthcare teams to be diligent in listening to patients and investigating symptoms. Fatigue or weight fluctuations may be related to decreased respiratory function and exercise tolerance but may also be related to endocrine dysfunction or metabolic syndrome (Claffey et al. 2025). A constellation of factors influences health after preterm birth, such as healthcare access, socioeconomic environment, biologic factors, and other social determinants of health. Research models that include these components will help understand this interaction (Kelly and Li 2019; Kelly et al. 2023; Kelly and Brace 2024). In clinical practice, the understanding of these interactions may help identify means of mitigating compounding risk (Kelly et al. 2021).

Conclusion

Adults born preterm who experience chronic conditions and symptoms believe that their conditions are related to preterm birth and want healthcare teams to acknowledge and ask about preterm birth history. Nurses, and all members of the healthcare team, should ask and document gestational age, birth weight, and neonatal course. Asking the questions opens the door for discussion and validation that preterm birth is a relevant component of past medical history for all born preterm. The shared experience that was evident in this study calls healthcare teams to consider preterm birth as influencing future health for all born preterm, not just those born in later birth cohorts or born at younger gestational ages.

It is time to move past the call for more research to establish that risk exists; there is ample research to support that preterm birth is a lifelong condition. The focus must shift to targeted medications and treatments that will address the unique expression of common conditions experienced by people born preterm. Advocacy from patients, families, educators, researchers, and clinicians is required to bring about the necessary paradigm shift. A shift that recognizes preterm birth as a lifelong, relevant component of health history, and advances interventions that promote health and mitigate risk for people born preterm throughout the life course. The 2017 Executive Summary by Raju et al., the systematic reviews that followed, and subsequent clinical practice guidelines put forth by Kelly et al. in 2021 were the start of that process. It is time to collaborate with the preterm‐born community and support the educational and practice changes needed.
