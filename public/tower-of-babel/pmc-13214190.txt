Beyond assembly: functions of the coronavirus M protein
Authors: Christopher Kinyanjui Kariuki, Jordi Doijen, Mandeep Kaur Mann, Jiexiong Xie, Marnix Van Loock, Ellen Van Damme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoronaviruses are enveloped RNA viruses from the family Coronaviridae, notable for their crown-like spike glycoproteins. Though historically regarded as inconsequential, recent outbreaks, most notably COVID-19 caused by SARS-CoV-2, highlight their significant impact on global health. SARS-CoV-2 possesses a 27-31 kb, complex genome encoding multiple structural and non-structural proteins. The membrane/matrix (M) glycoprotein is the most abundant structural component of the viral envelope and confers the minimal requirement for virion formation. Its highly conserved structure, featuring three transmembrane domains, facilitates interactions with other viral proteins that are essential for assembly, budding, and maintaining the integrity of the viral envelope. Beyond its canonical functions in virion assembly and egress, emerging evidence suggests the M protein influences early infection processes, modulates host immune responses, and may serve as a promising antiviral target. This mini review consolidates current knowledge on the structure, multifunctional roles, and therapeutic potential of the coronavirus M protein, emphasizing the necessity for further research into its diverse functions throughout the viral lifecycle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03154-w.Keywords: Coronavirus, SARS-COV-2, Membrane/matrix (M) protein, Structural protein, Function, Antiviral, Therapy, Small molecule

Introduction

The coronaviruses (α-, β-, γ- and δ-) are enveloped viruses from the Coronaviridae family within the order of Nidovirales [1–3]. Their naming arises from the crown-like projections of spike (S) glycoproteins that outline their surface, which resembles a crown (corona) when viewed with electron microscopy [1]. Long considered as inconsequential pathogens, especially for healthy immunocompetent individuals, we now know that these viruses, especially β-Coronaviruses, can cause devastating respiratory disease outbreaks in human populations [4]. In the last two decades there were two epidemics, and one pandemic caused by β-Coronaviruses. In 2002-2003, around 8000 cases of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) resulted in 774 deaths globally [5]. In 2012, a first disease case caused by the Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV) was recorded in Saudi-Arabia. To this day, there have been 2600 confirmed cases of MERS with a fatality rate of 34% [6]. Finally, in 2019, a novel coronavirus, SARS-CoV-2, was responsible for the coronavirus diseases 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic lasting multiple years and resulting in more than 7 million deaths [7–9]. Although the COVID-19 pandemic was declared over by the World Health Organization (WHO), COVID-19 has taken its place as a general respiratory pathogen that persists in the global population [10, 11].

With one of the largest positive-sense, single-stranded RNA genomes (about 27-31 kilobases in length: Fig. 1), β-Coronaviruses have a complex genetic repertoire, which in turn contributes to a diverse set of viral proteins, critical for viral replication, interaction with host proteins and cellular pathways, and immune evasion [12]. These proteins are encoded on multiple open reading frames (ORFs) and can be divided into 16 non-structural proteins (encoded by long polyproteins, pp1a (nsp 1-11) and pp1b (nsp 12-16)) and 4 structural proteins (nucleoprotein (N), membrane /matrix (M), envelope (E) and spike (S)), and 11 accessory proteins (ORF3a, ORF3b, ORF3c, ORF3d, ORF6, ORF7a, ORF7b, ORF8, ORF9b, ORF9c and ORF10 [Fig. 1]) [13, 14].

The COVID-19 pandemic underlined the unmet need for effective antiviral drugs against coronaviruses. Initially, non-structural proteins such as the main protease (Mpro) [15, 16] and the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) [17] were prioritized as targets for drug discovery. These proteins have an essential role in viral replication, conservation across coronaviruses and lack of human analogues making them specific targets for antivirals [18–21]. In addition, the RdRp of influenza and hepatitis C virus have been successfully targeted with nucleot(s)ide analogues; and in the human immunodeficiency virus field, protease inhibitors were successful, especially in combination with RdRp inhibitors [22–24]. However, the structural M protein has recently emerged as an attractive druggable target.

The β-Coronavirus M protein is a viral membrane glycoprotein that interacts with other viral structural proteins and plays a pivotal role in maintaining the shape and integrity of the viral envelope [25, 26]. While the importance of M protein in assembly is clear, there is emerging evidence that the M protein has multiple functions throughout the viral life cycle and is also involved in early infection steps and modulation of innate immune responses. This review will focus on the M protein as a multifunctional protein with a prominent role in coronavirus infection.

Structure of the Sarbecovirus M protein

Among the Sarbecovirus lineage, the M protein is highly conserved, both in sequence and structure, with 99% similarity within SARS-CoV-2 variants, >98% similarity between bat SARS-CoV (SHC014 and WIV1), pangolin SARS-CoV (PgCoV) and SARS-CoV-2, as well as >90% similarity between SARS-CoV-2 and SARS-CoV [27–29]. Although MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV still share the same overall structural characteristics [27, 28, 30], recently published protein structures of SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 show that key features diverge [18, 31]. Multiple sequence alignment of alpha and beta coronavirus M proteins (Supplementary Figure S1) shows that the three transmembrane helices and the adjacent hinge region are highly conserved, while most sequence variation is concentrated in the C-terminal endodomain and low-complexity regions. Consistent with this, Zhang et al. (2022) demonstrated that M protein conserved residues cluster within the membrane-embedded three-helix bundle and the dimerization interface, whereas variable residues are primarily confined to solvent-exposed, flexible regions of the cytosolic domain positioned away from the dimer interface [32].

The dominant structural feature of the Sarbecovirus M protein is the occurrence of 3 hydrophobic transmembrane domains (Fig. 2) [32–34]. For the Sarbecovirus lineages, these 3 transmembrane domains anchor the protein within the viral envelope, with a short hydrophilic ecto-amino (NH3-) terminal domain protruding outside of the virion and a long amphipathic endo-carboxy (-COOH) terminal domain on the internal side of the virion [35, 36]. In contrast, in silico predictions suggested the MERS-CoV M protein contains two additional transmembrane loops, positioning both its N-and C-terminal domains outside of the virion [35].

However, a recent structure of MERS-CoV M protein, engineered by replacing select C-terminal residues with SARS-CoV-2 equivalents based on molecular dynamics energy calculations for antibody binding, reveals that the structural fold between MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 is conserved. In all cases, the protein forms a dimer with an extra-virion N-terminus, three transmembrane helices and an intra-virion C-terminal domain, where transmembrane 1 domain in one protomer is swapped to form a three-helix bundle with transmembrane 2 and 3 domains in the other protomer. While the overall structural fold of MERS-CoV M protein was conserved, notable differences in the relative arrangement of the transmembrane helices, residues and side chain orientations were observed [31]. The transmembrane region of the M protein is linked to the cytosolic domain via a turn-helix-turn motif. The positively charged intra-virion facing C-terminus of the M protein forms a β-sheet sandwich domain comprised of an inner (β3, β4, β5, and β8) and outer sheet (β1, β2, β6, and β7) [32], where the β-sheet domain dimerizes via the inner sheet to cap the intra-virion face of the transmembrane region. This domain has a highly conserved dileucine motif (218LL219), present in both Sarbecovirus and MERS-CoV lineages, and is required for efficient packaging of the nucleocapsid (N) protein into the virion particle [37].

The M protein exhibits flexibility and can adopt different conformational states (Fig. 2A, B) [38, 39]. This flexibility arises from the rearrangement between the transmembrane 1 and 2 domains around a highly conserved hinge region in transmembrane 3 domain, which is inserted into the cavity formed by the three-helix transmembrane bundle. This movement largely contributes to the pleomorphic nature of coronaviruses, as it enables virions to alter their size and shape [40]. The long-extended form of M protein (86 Å height x 50 Å width) is essential for scaffolding and assembly, whereas the short-bent form (72 Å height x 56 Å width) provides membrane flexibility for curvature modulation and particle elongation [32, 39, 40] The long form of M is more rigid due to the transmembrane and cytoplasmic arrangement which promotes tight M-M packing, and formation of a uniform, narrow curvature of the membrane effectively scaffolding membrane bending required to bud viral particles at the ERGIC. The short form functions as a regulator of membrane curvature, and due to its flexibility, packs less rigidly to accommodate a wider range of membrane curvature, allowing for particle elongation [39, 41] (Fig. 2C). The M protein generally exists in dimeric form, stabilized through aromatic residues, such as the highly conserved F95, and several leucine zipper motifs positioned at the interfaces between the transmembrane domains [37, 42]. These transmembrane regions form an interface through which the M protein interacts with itself (homotypic interaction) or with other (heterotypic interaction) viral proteins such as the S protein and E protein domains, while the cytoplasmic endodomain of M forms contacts with the C-terminal domain of N protein [43, 44]. The rigid M-M dimers in the long form results in regularly spaced anchor points which favors S protein binding and contributes to formation of smaller, spherical particles with higher S protein density, while maintaining M-N, and M-E interactions for ordered assembly [39, 45]. The short form of M retains surfaces for S, E and N binding; however, results in lower, variable S protein density, and M-N tethering that may be interrupted for RNP displacement. Studies show that E proteins can oligomerize into pentamers, a process influenced by post-translational modifications such as palmitoylation, and by the surrounding lipid environment. Pentameric E can create local planar patches of membrane to counteract excessive membrane bending stress produced by long form M-M dimers and are important for modulating membrane dynamics and final virion size and shape [41, 46]. The relative abundance of the short form M, long form M and E pentamers governs the local geometry of the budding site in the viral assembly pathway. In addition to post-translational modification of the E protein, the M protein undergoes N-glycosylation on its ectodomain. This glycosylation facilitates proper folding, stability and trafficking through the ER-Golgi and ERGIC secretory pathway [47, 48]. Acting in concert with the transmembrane targeting signals, glycosylation promotes M localization to the Golgi apparatus and ERGIC, where glycans are processed to complex forms that contribute to stability and retention at budding sites.

A potential drug candidate (JNJ-9676) was shown to specifically bind to the Sarbecovirus M protein, inhibiting the replication of both SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2, as well as bat coronaviruses SHC014 and WIV1; and pangolin coronavirus PgCoV, with an in vitro nanomolar efficacy [18, 19]. In a pre-exposure Syrian Golden hamster model (25mg/kg per kg,2x daily), JNJ-9676 demonstrated significant reduction in both viral load and infectious virions in the lung. Resistance mutations, such as P132S, S99A and N117K near the dimer interface suggested that the compound bound to this region. Structures of inhibitor-bound M protein revealed inhibitors bound between transmembrane helices 2 and 3 and the hinge region, and locked the M protein in a short form conformation or altered inhibited conformation, preventing the transition to the long form thereby preventing virion assembly [18, 19] (Supplementary Figure S2A showing 2D ligand interaction diagram). Interestingly, a second M protein inhibitor, CIM834, was identified from a phenotypic screen [19]. This molecule showed submicromolar activity against a panel of SARS-CoV-2 isolates and SARS-CoV, and similarly to JNJ-9676, showed low micromolar activity against HCoV-OC43 but lacked activity against MERS-CoV and a panel of α-Coronaviruses. In vivo, oral administration of CIM-834 at 100 mg/kg demonstrated efficacy in both a severe combined immune deficient (SCID) mouse model infected with SARS-CoV-2 B.1.35 (hCoV-19/Belgium/rega-1920/2021; EPI_ISL_896474, 2021-01-11) and in a hamster model infected with SARS-CoV-2 USA-WA1/2020. Treatment reduced infectious viral titres in the lung under both once -daily and twice -daily dosing regimens, in both prophylactic (intranasal infection and treatment at the same time) and therapeutic settings. (compound treatment 10, 24 and 48h post intranasal infection). Mechanistically, CIM835 was shown to have a late mechanism of action through locking the M protein into the short form thereby inhibiting virion assembly [19] (Supplementary Figure S2B showing 2D ligand interaction diagram).

M protein as a master regulator of assembly and egress

The M protein is the most abundant component of the Coronavirus viral envelope [34, 48, 49]. Together with the envelope (E) protein, the M protein confers the minimal requirements for formation of the virion [41, 50].

Replication of coronaviruses occurs entirely in the cytoplasm, with the four structural proteins translated from viral sub-genomic RNA transcripts generated from genomic RNA in the order 5´-pol-S-E-M-N [44]. The M protein is translated on membrane bound polysomes and inserted into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), from where it transits, together with the S and E proteins, into the endoplasmic reticulum-golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) [51]. After expression, the M protein congregates to the trans-Golgi as well as to the trans-Golgi network [52–55]. In this location, the M protein recruits and interacts with various viral proteins, such as the E protein and the S protein [51, 56]. The interaction with the E protein results in the formation of the viral envelope and is key for maintaining its structural integrity [51]. This interaction is described as the main driving force behind virion formation [53]. M protein also interacts with the S protein, stabilizing and facilitating its incorporation into the viral envelope [57]. In summary, the M protein is the linchpin for coronavirus virion assembly, given its pivotal protein-protein interactions that bridge all other viral proteins [56].

Eventually, the mature virions, encapsulating the viral RNA genome and the M protein within the viral envelope, are transported from the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane for viral budding and egress. During these processes, M protein also plays a role, through homotypic interaction as the M protein dimers cooperatively act to induce global membrane curvature through protein-lipid interactions, and through the pentameric E proteins that maintain membrane planarity [41]. The ribonucleoprotein/RNP (formed from the N protein and viral genomic RNA) then coalesces with the membrane bound M and E proteins, forming virions by budding into the ERGIC [51, 56]. The M-N interaction, which occurs selectively through the genomic RNA-bound N protein, is important for packaging the viral genomic RNA into new virions [58]. This results in viral budding into the space of the single-membrane vesicles (SMVs) [59], a membrane architecture that functions as the primary site of SARS-CoV-2 assembly and budding.

M protein in early stages of the viral lifecycle

While the best described functions of the M protein of the β-Coronaviruses are assembly and egress (Fig. 1C), there is growing evidence that the protein also has distinct functions early in the viral lifecycle. In fact, when JNJ-9676, an M protein inhibitor, was administered together with the viral inoculum (and subsequently removed), full inhibition of replication was observed suggesting that the M protein also has a crucial role before assembly begins [18]. However, the mechanism used remains unclear.

Upon binding of the virion to the human ACE2 (angiotensin I-converting enzyme-2) receptor, the S protein undergoes a conformational change to promote interaction with the receptor. Binding to the receptor then triggers further conformational changes in the S2 region of the S protein resulting in membrane fusion. In this process, the S protein is stabilized by the M protein as it undergoes the conformational changes triggered by proteolytic cleavage [60].

Coronavirus infection of one cell promotes further membrane fusions between the infected cells and neighbouring cells. Syncytia (giant cells), are large multinucleated cells occurring from virus induced cell-cell fusions and are observed over a diverse phylogenetic spectrum, including coronaviruses such as SARS-CoV-2 and MERS [61]. This mechanism allows viruses an evolutionary advantage, enabling them to evade antibody neutralization and the broader immune response [62]. However, unlimited formation of syncytia would result in massive cell death with pre-mature release of immature virions, triggering the host immune system. Therefore, mechanisms are in place to carefully manipulate the timing and size of syncytia formation. In coronavirus infections, syncytia are formed when spike proteins are present on the host cell surface, with an initial proteolytic cleavage at the S1-S2 boundary by furin. Shedding of the S1 subunit then exposes the S2’ cleavage site, through which spike activation is mediated by TMPRRS2 or Cathepsin L, leading to a conformational change that drives the fusion peptide into the target cells membrane with subsequent syncytia formation [63]. In an in vitro experimental model utilizing recombinant SARS-CoV-2 in HEK293T cells, co-expression of the M-protein with Spike (S) protein (or its variants) led to inhibition of Spike-triggered syncytia formation [60]. This occurred via a two-fold mechanism, with the first being the blocking of furin’s enzymatic activity thereby preventing the first cleavage of the S protein. Furin requires self-cleavage for activity, releasing the N-terminal catalytic domain into extracellular space and retaining the C-terminal fragment within the cell [64]. The M-protein interacts mechanistically with the C-terminus of furin, without influencing the Spike-furin interaction, but diminishing furin’s self-cleavage and thus enzyme activity [60]. Secondly, the M protein interacts with the TM3 and endo-domain of the S2 segment of the Spike (S) protein leading to the retention of the S protein within the cytoplasm thereby reducing its localization to the host cell membrane which again curbs uncontrolled syncytia formation [60]. However, the experimental approach mentioned here has inherent limitations, namely the reliance on an in vitro recombinant system, meaning that further experiments under authentic infection conditions are required to delve deeper into the mechanism by which the M-protein influences the S protein functionality in this regard.

M protein in innate immune evasion strategies

Actively replicating viruses are identified by the vertebrate cellular innate immune system through the recognition of viral RNA or DNA structures as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) in cytoplasm, either directly, or through autophagy [65]. Although the role of M protein in these processes remains to be further elucidated, the M protein has been demonstrated to impact innate immune signalling pathways, such as RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), type I interferon production and signalling, interferon regulatory factors (IRFs), toll-like receptors (TLRs) and the induction of autophagy [66].

Controversially, the M protein reduces the production of interferons and other antiviral cytokines by directly inhibiting signalling through RIG-I and MDA5, which are RLRs important for detecting viral RNA in the cytosol [38, 67, 68]. In an in vitro study, the recombinant and over-expressed SARS-CoV-2 M protein was shown to specifically target the RIG-I/MDA-5-dependent RNA-sensing pathway by inhibiting the activation of type I and III IFN production induced by RIG-I/MDA-5 signalling components, such as RIG-I, MDA-5, MAVS, TBK1, and IKKε [38, 67, 68]. This led to enhanced replication of a reporter virus system (VSV-eGFP) in a HEK293T cell line [38]. The application of a candidate compound such as JNJ-9676, which presumably locks the M-protein in an ‘unnatural’ conformation could influence how the protein interacts with the various components of the RIG-I/MDA-5-dependent RNA-sensing pathway. We hypothesize that such kind of interference may disturb a delicate balance, which could be enough to cause a shift in the production of type 1and type II IFN. However, the caveat for these results remains that the ectopic expression of a viral protein differs from the authentic in vivo infection in relation to its biological function. Further, the evidence of a role for M protein in immunity needs to further mature to fully understand these processes and how these processes may be impacted by treatment with direct acting antivirals.

The M protein has also been demonstrated to suppress the production of type I interferons (IFNs), including interferon-alpha (IFN-α) and interferon-beta (IFN-β) [69]. Coupled with the suppression of the interferon-beta (IFN-β) promoter, this contributes to the negative regulation of IFN-I production as well as impeding the downstream interferon-stimulated genes, which are crucial host antiviral factors [67, 68]. This inhibition leads to a reduced production of type I interferons and thus limits the expression of interferon-stimulated genes responsible for antiviral defense. Studies have also suggested that the M protein can inhibit the activation of transcription factors, such as interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), which are key regulators of IFN production [38]. It has been demonstrated that the M protein can disrupt the phosphorylation of the Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway, which is a critical pathway for interferon signaling [70]. However, there are conflicting reports that describe a stimulation of interferon production by the M protein via TLR-mediated processes [71]. Since the overall immune response to viral infection is an intricate and complex interplay of various factors, it is clear that further research is needed to discover in which way M protein finetunes antiviral response upon infection.

Although not fully understood, growing evidence attributes a role for the M protein throughout the coronavirus life cycle, starting very early in the infection process. It is therefore not surprising that the M protein interferes with cellular immunity, the first frontier upon infection. It should be noted that, particularly for structural proteins, the field is still developing, and most existing studies on the role of M protein in innate immunity rely on overexpression models rather than authentic infection in cellular settings, which may influence their outcomes.

Conclusions

The M protein was recently discovered as a novel antiviral target. It is highly conserved in the Sarbecovirus subfamily of the Coronaviridae and was shown to be druggable [18, 19] which suggests that M protein inhibitors may be of high value in the future. In this review, we aimed to bring together M protein functions beyond the most well described role in assembly and egress. Undeniably, structural biology of the M protein ties its conformation to its aggregator function and critical role in assembly, budding, envelope formation, and in maintaining the shape and integrity of the viral envelope. Interestingly, there is growing evidence of extended M protein function in early processes such as spike stabilization, syncytia formation, modulation of the endolysosomal pathway and immune modulation. This field is however still evolving and further scientific knowledge of M protein functions in all stages of the viral lifecycle are warranted.
