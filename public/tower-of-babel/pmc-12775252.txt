TCF7L2 is essential for the differentiation and invasive function of human extravillous trophoblast
Authors: Enoch Appiah Adu-Gyamfi, Joudi Salamah, Elisha Cheeran, Brandt Beck, Zaineb Ahmad, Bum-Kyu Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) play essential roles in placental development by anchoring the placenta, invading the maternal decidua, and remodeling spiral arteries. TCF7L2 is known to be expressed in human placental tissues and EVTs, and it controls EVT motility. However, the targets of TCF7L2 in trophoblasts and the mechanism by which it contributes to early trophoblast differentiation are largely unknown. Here, using trophoblast stem cells (TSCs), we investigate the expression patterns of TCF7L2 during trophoblast lineage differentiation, revealing that its expression gradually elevates throughout EVT formation. Loss-of-function studies uncover that TCF7L2 is implicated in the proliferation of TSCs and is essential for EVT formation. Conversely, overexpression of TCF7L2 hinders TSC differentiation into STs. We identify TCF7L2 binding sites across the genome in TSCs and EVTs. Integrative analyses of TCF7L2 targets with global gene expression profiles unveil that TCF7L2 facilitates EVT formation by directly activating extracellular matrix organization while suppressing genes linked to the cell cycle. Moreover, overlap analyses of TCF7L2 targets with those of other EVT factors reveal that TCF7L2 collaborates with other EVT factors to promote EVT formation. In summary, our findings highlight context-specific functions of TCF7L2 in the trophoblast lineage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05991-4.

Introduction

The placenta is a vital organ that facilitates nutrient and gas exchange between the mother and the developing fetus, supports hormonal regulation, and ensures immunological protection [1]. During early placental development, trophectodermal cells, which originate from the outermost epithelial layer of the blastocyst, give rise to diverse trophoblast cell types: cytotrophoblasts (CTs), syncytiotrophoblasts (STs), and extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) [2]. EVT-mediated remodeling of the uterine spiral arteries is fundamental for establishing the blood flow between the mother and the placenta [3]. EVTs invade maternal uterine tissue, remodel maternal spiral arteries into large-diameter vessels, and establish low-pressure blood flow to the placenta, ensuring constant nutrient and oxygen delivery [4]. Accumulating evidence suggests that defects in this process, such as insufficient invasion of decidual tissue or incomplete transformation of spiral arteries, can lead to the pathogenesis of obstetrical complications, including stillbirth, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), and preeclampsia (PE) [5–7]. Therefore, proper differentiation of trophoblasts into EVTs is crucial for a healthy pregnancy.

Transcription factor-7-like-2 (TCF7L2), also known as T-cell factor 4 (TCF4), is a member of the T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor (TCF/LEF) family, which includes LEF1, TCF7, and TCF7L1 in humans [8]. As a terminal executor of the Wnt signaling pathway, TCF7L2 facilitates embryonic organ development during embryogenesis [9, 10]. Prior studies have shown that TCF7L2 is implicated in various biological processes, such as insulin secretion, glucose metabolism, and adipogenic differentiation, and silencing TCF7L2 led to blood-vessel dysplasia and lipid accumulation [11–13]. In human placental development, immunohistochemistry of placental tissues and differentiating villous explant cultures revealed that TCF4 is weakly expressed in villous fibroblasts and term placenta, but strongly expressed in early gestation placenta, primary extravillous trophoblasts, and trophoblast cell lines [14]. A previous study conducted in the human trophoblastic choriocarcinoma cell line BeWo, which expresses GCM1, identified it as a novel target of the β-catenin/TCF complex [15]. The study further demonstrated that β-catenin/BCL9L/TCF4 signaling directly targets the GCM1/syncytin pathway to regulate cell fusion. Another study showed that Wnt/TCF-4 signaling could play a role as a crucial driver of extravillous trophoblast (EVT) differentiation, specifically promoting cell motility and the expression of promigratory genes using primary human cytotrophoblast and trophoblastic cell line SGHPL-5 [16]. These suggest that TCF7L2 may play important roles in early placental development. Despite the obvious expression patterns of TCF7L2 in placental tissue and their putative roles in trophoblast differentiation, definitive target genes of TCF7L2 have not been thoroughly identified in human trophoblasts. Moreover, the role of TCF7L2 in distinct trophoblast cell types and the mechanisms by which it regulates trophoblast lineage differentiation are only partially understood.

Here, we investigated the role of TCF7L2 in the self-renewal and differentiation of trophoblasts using TSCs as a model system. We found that TCF7L2 is essential for the proliferation of TSCs and EVT differentiation. Integrative analysis of TCF7L2 binding sites with global gene expression profiles revealed that TCF7L2 promotes EVT differentiation by directly activating EVT-specific genes while repressing cell cycle regulators. Our findings highlight the dual functions of TCF7L2 in trophoblast biology in a cell-context-dependent manner.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human TSCs were obtained from Dr. Takahiro Arima and cultured following previously established protocols [17]. In brief, the human TSCs were plated on dishes coated with 5 μg/ml Collagen IV (Corning) and maintained in TSC culture medium (TSCM). This medium is composed of DMEM/F12 (Gibco) supplemented with 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium-ethanolamine (Gibco), 0.2% fetal bovine serum (GeminiBio), 0.8 mM valproic acid (Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), 0.3% bovine serum albumin (GeminiBio), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.5% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), 0.5 μM A83-01 (Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), 0.5 μM CHIR99021 (Selleck Chemicals), 0.5 μM SB431542 (Stemcell Technologies), 5 μM Y27632 (Selleck Chemicals), 50 ng/ml epidermal growth factor (PeproTech), and 1.5 μg/ml l-ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich). Upon reaching 70–80% confluency, the cells were trypsinized with TrypLE (Gibco) for 10 min at 37 °C and reseeded on plates at a 1:3 ratio. The TSCs were cultured in an incubator set to 37 °C with 5% CO2, with the medium being refreshed daily.

TSC differentiation into STs and EVTs

To differentiate TSCs into STs or EVTs, we adhered to the previously established protocol [17]. Briefly, 2.5 × 105 cells were seeded in a 6 cm low-binding dish containing 3 mL of ST culture medium: DMEM/F12 supplemented with 50 ng/ml EGF, 0.3% BSA, 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 4% KSR, 2 μM forskolin, 0.5% Penicillin–Streptomycin, 1% ITS-X supplement, and 2.5 μM Y27632. On the third day, the medium was replaced with fresh medium. On the fifth day, the STs were collected and washed to remove dead cells. For EVT differentiation, TSCs were seeded in 6-well plates coated with 1 μg/ml Collagen IV at a density of 7.5 × 104 cells per well and cultured in 2 mL of EVT medium: DMEM/F12 supplemented with 4% KSR, 0.5% Penicillin–Streptomycin, 0.3% BSA, 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 1% ITS-X supplement, 7.5 μM A83-01, 2.5 μM Y27632, and 100 ng/ml NRG1. Shortly after suspending the cells in the medium, Matrigel (Corning) was added to a final concentration of 2%. On the third day, the medium was replaced with NRG1-free EVT medium, and Matrigel was added to a final concentration of 0.5%. On the sixth day, the medium was replaced with NRG1- and KSR-free EVT medium, with Matrigel added to a final concentration of 0.5%. The EVTs were harvested on the eighth day for further experiments.

Virus preparation and shRNA-mediated knockdown (KD) of TCF7L2

We purchased short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting TCF7L2 from Sigma-Aldrich. To generate shRNA virus, we transfected 7.3 × 10⁶ 293 T cells with 2.5 μg of TCF7L2 shRNA, 1.7 μg of Δ8.9, and 0.8 μg of VSVG using 15 μl of GenJet transfection reagent (SignaGen). After 12 h, the medium was replaced with the TSCM. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for another 36 h, after which the viral supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter, and used to infect TSCs. To knock down TCF7L2, 2.5 × 105 TSCs were infected with the viral particles in a 12-well plate. The infected cells were incubated overnight, and the medium was then replaced with fresh TSCM containing puromycin to select for infected cells. To assess the knockdown efficiency of TCF7L2 and profile global gene expression, cells were harvested three days post-infection to ensure complete knockdown of TCF7L2.

Cell proliferation assay

To assess the proliferation rate of TSCs, we employed the CCK8 kit (Abcam). Approximately 3 × 103 TSCs, treated with either TCF7L2 shRNA virus or a negative control virus (NC), were suspended in 100 µl of media and seeded in triplicate into 96-well plates. On days 0, 1, 2, and 3, 10 µl of the CCK8 solution was added to each well, followed by a 1.5-h incubation at 37 °C. Optical density was measured at 460 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Flow cytometric analysis of cell cycle and apoptosis

For cell cycle analysis, only TSCs were used, while both TSCs and EVTs were utilized for the apoptosis assay. In the cell cycle analysis, 1 × 105 TSCs treated with either TCF7L2 shRNA virus or the NC virus were fixed in 70% ethanol and incubated overnight at −20 °C. Following ethanol removal and PBS washing, the cells were incubated with 100 µl of Muse cell cycle reagent (Millipore, MCH100106) for 25–30 min at room temperature in the dark, then analyzed using the Muse flow cytometer (Luminex). For the apoptosis assay, 1 × 105 TSCs or EVTs treated with either TCF7L2 shRNA virus or the NC virus were incubated with 100 µl of Muse Annexin V & Dead Cell reagent (Millipore, MCH100105) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by analysis with the Muse flow cytometer.

Invasion assay

He cellular invasion assay was performed using a modified chamber-based technique [18]. Secure Seal Hybridization Chambers (Thermo Scientific, S24732) were UV-sterilized for one hour and affixed to 12-well plates. A mixture of Matrigel (Corning) and EVT differentiation medium at a 2:1 ratio was introduced into the chambers and polymerized at 37 °C for one hour. Next, 5 × 104 cells suspended in 30 μL EVT medium were added to each chamber and incubated for one hour at a tilted angle to ensure even cell–matrix contact. After cell adhesion, each well received 1 mL EVT medium, which was subsequently refreshed on days 3 and 6 as per the differentiation protocol. Invasion was monitored throughout using an EVOS microscope.

Stable cell line generation for inducible overexpression of TCF7L2

We engineered the pSBtet-GP vector (Addgene, plasmid #60495) by replacing the luciferase sequence with multiple cloning sites and adding a flag tag and biotinylation sequence at the N-terminal, creating what we call the pSBFB system. To establish stable cell lines, we transfected TSCs with the pSBFB vector containing TCF7L2, along with a vector encoding the BirA enzyme and the transposase-containing pCMV(CAT)T7-SB100 vector (Addgene, plasmid #34879). After 24 h, we selected the transfected cells using puromycin and G418. To induce the expression of the tagged protein, 1 µg/ml doxycycline (Fisher Scientific) was administered.

Protein extraction and western blot

The EVTs and STs were lysed in 4X Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, USA) supplemented with 2-Mercaptoethanol. The mixtures were then incubated at 90 °C for 10 min. We prepared SDS-PAGE gels at the appropriate concentration and used them to separate the proteins via electrophoresis. The separated proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad). To block non-specific binding sites, the membranes were soaked in 5% milk (Foodhold) for 60 min at room temperature. Overnight at 4 °C, the membranes were probed with primary antibodies (1 μg/ml) specific for GAPDH (Santa Cruz, sc-32233), MMP2 (Cell Signaling, 40994), HLA-G (Santa Cruz, sc-21799), CSH1 (Santa Cruz, sc-374266), CGB3 (Santa Cruz, sc-271062), and SDC1 (Santa Cruz, sc-390791), TCF7L2 (Cell Signaling, 2569). GAPDH served as the loading control. After three washes with PBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Active Motif) for 1.5 h at 25 °C. Following another three washes with PBST, protein bands were detected using ECL solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Starting with 500 ng of total RNA, cDNAs were synthesized using the SuperScriptTM III First Strand Synthesis SuperMix kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 18080400) and subsequently diluted 20-fold with DNase-free water. For each qRT-PCR reaction, 2 µl of cDNA was combined with the PowerTrackTM SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A46110). PCR primers, designed to span exon-exon junctions, were created using the Primer3 web tool (http://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3/). The sequences for all primers can be found in Supplementary Table S1. Cycle threshold (Ct) values for both samples and controls were normalized to GAPDH, serving as the loading control. Relative gene expression was then calculated as fold enrichment using the 2^-ΔΔCT method, based on the average of three replicates.

RNA sequencing and data processing

To profile global gene expression in human TSCs, we employed RNA-seq. Total RNA was extracted using the GeneJet RNA purification kit (Thermo Scientific, K0731). RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 500 ng of total RNA using the NEB Next Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, E7770) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In short, mRNAs were isolated from total RNA using the Magnetic mRNA Isolation Kit (oligo(dT) beads) (NEB, E7490), followed by cDNA synthesis with random primers and purification using NEBNext Sample Purification Beads (NEB, E7767). The ends of the purified double-stranded cDNAs were repaired, ligated with barcoded adaptors, and amplified via PCR. Sequencing of the RNA-seq libraries was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform. Paired-end reads were aligned to the human transcriptome (hg38) using the Salmon mapper (v1.10.1). Gene expression levels were quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) using the R library tximport (v1.18.0). Read counts were normalized using the median of ratios method with the R package DESeq2 (v1.30.1), and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on a P-adjusted value < 0.01 and |log2 fold change|> 1.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) sequencing

ChIP experiments were performed as previously outlined [19]. Briefly, TSCs were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 7 min at room temperature, followed by the addition of glycine (final concentration of 125 mM) for 5 min to neutralize the formaldehyde. The fixed cells were then sonicated using a Bioruptor (Diagenode) with cycles of 30 s on and 1 min off for a total of 10 min (repeated three times), resulting in sheared chromatin fragments averaging 250 bp. These fragments were used for immunoprecipitation with 10 µg of TCF7L2 antibody (Cell Signaling, 2569). The enriched ChIP materials were then used to create next-generation sequencing libraries using the NEB ChIP-seq library preparation kit (NEB, E7370L), and sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform.

ChIP-seq data processing

Paired-end 50 bp reads from ChIP-seq were aligned to the human genome assembly (hg38) using the Bowtie2 mapper [20], with low-quality reads (MapQ < 10) being filtered out using samtools [21]. The aligned reads were used for peak calling with the MACS3 peak caller [22] under default settings. Additional score filtering was performed to eliminate weak peaks through visual inspection. The human genome hg38 repeat-mask file was obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgTables), and peaks located in simple redundant regions of the genome were further excluded.

Consistent with the role of SEs [24, 25], RNA-seq data analysis revealed robust expression of TCF7L2 in both TSCs and EVTs, with the highest expression observed in EVTs (Supplementary Figure S1). We also confirmed higher transcript and protein levels of TCF7L2 in TSCs and EVTs compared to STs (Fig. 1B and C). Single-cell RNA sequencing data further showed that EVTs express the highest levels of TCF7L2 among trophoblast lineages, indicating that this expression pattern is conserved in vivo (Supplementary Figure S2). TCF7L2 is required for the proliferation of TSCs

To investigate the role of TCF7L2 in TSCs, we performed knockdown (KD) of TCF7L2 using two shRNAs targeting TCF7L2. We confirmed the reduction in TCF7L2 transcript and protein levels compared to an empty vector control using RT-qPCR and western blotting, respectively (Fig. 1D and 1E). KD of TCF7L2 did not cause any obvious morphological abnormalities in TSCs (Fig. 1F). We then examined the expression of well-known TSC marker genes. As shown in Fig. 1G, most marker genes did not exhibit significant changes in expression upon TCF7L2 depletion, except for TEAD4, which showed a moderate reduction. Given that one of the key characteristics of stem cells is their self-renewing capacity, we assessed the cell proliferation rate of TSCs upon KD of TCF7L2. The results revealed that TCF7L2-depleted TSCs exhibited a reduced proliferation rate compared to control TSCs (Fig. 1H). Consistently, the transcript level of the cell proliferation marker Ki67 significantly decreased, while DIO2, which negatively influences cell proliferation in trophoblasts [26], was induced (Fig. 1I). We further assessed cell cycle phases and apoptosis to determine if TCF7L2 affects specific phases of the cell cycle and apoptosis. The population at the S phase of the cell cycle diminished, whereas the G0/G1 phase population increased (Fig. 1J), indicating that TCF7L2 depletion in TSCs impairs cell proliferation. The percentage of late apoptotic cells also increased (Fig. 1K), along with reduced expression of anti-apoptotic genes (BCL2, SIRT1, and MCL1) (Fig. 2L). TCF7L2 promotes the differentiation of TSCs toward EVTs

Our study demonstrated that TCF7L2 is regulated by an SE and is strongly expressed in EVTs (Fig. 1A-1C). Time-course analysis of TCF7L2 expression during the differentiation of TSCs into EVTs revealed a gradual increase in TCF7L2 expression towards EVT differentiation (Fig. 2A). Based on this observation, we hypothesized that TCF7L2 may play important role in this process. To investigate the roles of TCF7L2, we first depleted TCF7L2 in TSCs and allowed them to differentiate into EVTs for 8 days. As shown in Fig. 2B, TCF7L2 depletion resulted in abnormal EVT morphology, in contrast to control EVTs which typically exhibit elongated protrusions. Correspondingly, TCF7L2-depleted cells exhibited significantly lower induction of EVT marker genes, including HLA-G [27], MMP2 [28], ASCL2 [29], FN1[30], ITGA5 [31], and DIO2 [27], compared to control cells, while failing to reduce TSC marker genes (TEAD4 and TP63) to the same extent as control cells (Fig. 2C and D). This suggests that they may be stalled in the intermediate stage of EVT differentiation. Consistently, the depletion of TCF7L2 inhibited the invasive capacity of cells into a Matrigel matrix (Fig. 2E). To establish a causal role for TCF7L2, we performed a rescue experiment. Restoration of TCF7L2 in TCF7L2-depleted cells partially rescued EVT differentiation and cell proliferation (Supplementary Figure S3). We also assessed apoptosis after 8 days of EVT differentiation, finding that a higher proportion of TCF7L2-depleted cells underwent apoptosis compared to control cells (Fig. 2F). Consistently, anti-apoptotic genes (BCL2, SIRT1, and MCL1) were significantly downregulated upon TCF7L2 depletion, while pro-apoptotic genes (TP53 and CDKN1B) were upregulated (Fig. 2G). In summary, these data strongly support that TCF7L2 is indispensable for the differentiation of TSCs into EVTs.

TCF7L2 inhibits ST formation

Unlike the formation of EVTs, where TCF7L2 levels increase, the expression of TCF7L2 gradually decreases during the differentiation of TSCs into STs (Fig. 3A). This observation led us to hypothesize that TCF7L2 might inhibit the differentiation of TSCs into STs. To test this hypothesis, we cloned TCF7L2 into a doxycycline-inducible expression vector and overexpressed it during ST formation. We confirmed the overexpression (OE) of TCF7L2 using qRT-PCR (Fig. 3B). Compared to control STs, TCF7L2-overexpressing cells exhibited smaller ST cell cluster than control cells, suggesting impaired cell fusion and ST formation (Fig. 3C). Consistent with the disrupted ST morphology, OE of TCF7L2 failed to induce the transcripts of multiple ST marker genes (CGA, CGB3, CSH1, ERVW1, GCM1, and SDC1) during ST formation (Fig. 3D). We also confirmed reduced protein levels of ST markers upon OE of TCF7L2 (Fig. 3E). Conversely, TSC marker genes (CDH1 and TEAD4) were relatively maintained in TCF7L2-overexpressing STs, suggesting incomplete differentiation of TSCs into STs (Fig. 3F). TCF7L2 enhances EVT formation by activating genes specific to EVTs while suppressing genes active in TSCs

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of global gene expression following the differentiation of TCF7L2-depleted TSCs into EVTs to elucidate the biological processes and pathways regulated by TCF7L2 during EVT formation. Initially, we examined overall expression patterns using correlation and principal component analysis (PCA), which revealed distinct clusters between TCF7L2-depleted and control EVTs (Fig. 4A and B). This indicates unique expression profiles in TCF7L2-depleted EVTs, which are cells differentiated from TCF7L2-depleted TSCs into EVTs. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis using DESeq2 identified 2,990 upregulated and 1,899 downregulated genes, with a cutoff of more than twofold expression change and a P-value < 0.01 (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Table S2). Gene ontology (GO) analysis of these DEGs highlighted enriched GO terms such as extracellular matrix organization, calcium signaling pathway, and circulatory system processes for downregulated DEGs, whereas cell morphogenesis, cell junction organization, and cell–cell adhesion, which are typically enriched terms in epithelial cells like TSCs, were enriched for upregulated DEGs (Fig. 4D). Additionally, cell type signature analysis disclosed that TCF7L2 depletion disrupts the cell signature of EVTs (Fig. 4E). Further analysis of the RNA-seq data using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) unveiled that TCF7L2-depleted EVT cells failed to effectively activate genes that are typically upregulated in EVTs and reduce the expression of genes that are usually downregulated during EVT formation (Fig. 4F). Additionally, these cells exhibited lower expression levels of genes involved in human placenta development and EVT formation (Fig. 4F). TCF7L2 directly suppresses genes linked to the mitotic cell cycle while promoting genes related to extracellular matrix organization during EVT formation

To identify the direct targets of TCF7L2 and unravel its regulatory roles in EVT formation, we conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by high-throughput sequencing (ChIP-seq). This approach uncovered tens of thousands of TCF7L2 binding sites in both TSCs and EVTs (Supplementary Table S3). Predominantly, these binding sites were situated at distal regions (upstream and intergenic) from transcription start sites (TSSs) in both cell types (Fig. 5A), suggesting that TCF7L2 tends to bind enhancers. Consistently, TCF7L2 binding sites were enriched with the enhancer signature of H3K27ac in both TSCs and EVTs (Fig. 5B). GO analysis of genes associated with TCF7L2 binding sites in TSCs and EVTs uncovered a considerable overlap in terms between the two cell types (Supplementary Figure S4), suggesting that many TCF7L2 binding sites are commonly shared. However, TSCs were particularly enriched with GO terms related to the regulation of cell–cell adhesion and the Wnt signaling pathway, whereas EVTs were associated with GO terms related to the regulation of cell migration and motility. To explore the regulatory functions of TCF7L2, we utilized BETA software [32] to determine whether it acts as a transcriptional activator or repressor. As illustrated in Fig. 5C, TCF7L2 serves dual roles as both an activator and a repressor. To understand how TCF7L2 facilitates the differentiation of TSCs into EVTs, we compared its binding sites between TSCs and EVTs, identifying three distinct clusters: C1 (TSC-unique sites), C2 (common sites), and C3 (EVT-unique sites) (Fig. 5E and F, and Supplementary Figure S5). Given that the C3 cluster represents newly emerged TCF7L2 binding sites in EVTs compared to TSCs, we hypothesized that the genes within this cluster play crucial roles in EVT formation. Interestingly, a comparison of gene expression in the C3 cluster between TSCs and EVTs revealed overall higher expression levels in TSCs than in EVTs, suggesting that most genes in the C3 cluster are relatively repressed in EVTs (Supplementary Figure S6). We further dissected these genes into two groups (upregulated and downregulated in EVTs compared to TSCs) using a 1.5-fold change cutoff. Consistently, while the majority of genes in the C3 cluster were downregulated in EVTs, a subset of genes was upregulated. GO term analysis of these two groups showed that genes directly repressed by TCF7L2 in EVTs were enriched in pathways related to Rho GTPase signaling, the mitotic cell cycle, and androgen receptor signaling (Fig. 5G). Conversely, genes directly activated by TCF7L2 in EVTs were enriched in pathways related to extracellular matrix organization and the RAC1 GTPase cycle, which are involved in cell differentiation and migration [33–35]. These findings suggest that TCF7L2 promotes the differentiation of TSCs into EVTs by directly curbing cell proliferation while enhancing cell migration and extracellular matrix organization.

Consistent with this, GATA2 knockdown in TSCs suppressed the C3 genes downregulated in EVTs while activating those upregulated in EVTs (Fig. 6C). Since TFs often collaborate by forming an enhanceosome to regulate cell-type-specific genes [37], we explored the relationship between TCF7L2 and other EVT regulators, including ASCL2, DLX3, DLX5, DLX6, GCM1, NRIP1, and ZNF439 [29, 38, 39]. Depleting any of these TFs reduced TCF7L2 expression (Fig. 6D), and reciprocally, depletion of TCF7L2 lowered the expression of these EVT regulators (Fig. 6E). This indicates that TCF7L2 and EVT factors mutually activate each other. Moreover, global gene expression profiles in EVTs derived from TSCs with each factor depleted showed similar expression patterns among them (Fig. 6F). Consistently, a comparison of genome-wide binding sites of these factors showed a strong positive correlation, suggesting that TCF7L2 shares its targets with other EVT factors (Fig. 6G). Target network analysis further confirmed direct regulation between TCF7L2 and other EVT factors (Fig. 6H). In summary, our findings demonstrate that TCF7L2 plays a pivotal role in EVT differentiation by cooperating with other key EVT regulators within an EVT-specific gene regulatory network.

TCF7L2 promotes EVT formation by controlling the NOTCH and WNT signaling pathways

It is known that WNT and NOTCH signaling play crucial roles in human placenta and trophoblast development [40]. Our ChIP-seq data analysis revealed that TCF7L2 binds near many WNT and NOTCH genes (Supplementary Figure S7). To investigate the influence of TCF7L2 on the WNT and NOTCH signaling pathways in trophoblasts, we examined the expression levels of WNT ligands in TSCs and EVTs using previously published RNA-seq data [38]. Among 19 WNT ligands, WNT5A, WNT6, WNT7A, WNT7B, WNT9A, WNT10B, and WNT11 were moderately to strongly expressed in either TSCs or EVTs (TPM > 1) (Fig. 7A). Comparing the expression between TSCs and EVTs, all WNT ligands except WNT7A and WNT10B showed higher expression in TSCs than in EVTs (Fig. 7A). Given the importance of WNT signaling in human placenta and trophoblast development [40], aberrant expression patterns of WNT ligands may contribute to impaired EVT differentiation. As shown in Fig. 7B, 6 out of 7 WNT ligands exhibited opposite expression patterns between TCF7L2-depleted EVTs and control EVTs, suggesting that depletion of TCF7L2 impaired proper WNT ligand expression, ultimately inhibiting TSC differentiation into EVTs. We also examined NOTCH expression in TSCs and EVTs. NOTCH1, NOTCH2, and NOTCH3 were expressed in TSCs, while only NOTCH2 is robustly expressed in EVTs (Fig. 7C). Similar to the significantly altered expression of WNT ligands in TCF7L2-depleted EVTs, NOTCH1 and NOTCH3 were upregulated, whereas NOTCH2 was downregulated (Fig. 7D). This expression pattern is entirely contrary to that observed in normal EVT formation, indicating that disrupted NOTCH expression may hinder TSC differentiation into EVTs. Discussion

TCF7L2 is a key transcription factor involved in numerous critical biological processes. It serves as the primary downstream effector of the Wnt signaling pathway and plays significant roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, metabolism, and disease development [12, 41, 42]. For example, TCF7L2 is implicated in regulating glucose metabolism. Variants in the TCF7L2 gene are linked to a higher risk of type 2 diabetes and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), conditions that can impact placental development and function [43, 44]. It has been reported that TCF7L2 is robustly expressed in human placenta tissue, especially in EVTs [14], and it plays a role in trophoblast motility [16]. However, the specific targets of TCF7L2 in TSCs and EVTs, as well as the mechanisms by which TCF7L2 modulates self-renewal and differentiation of TSCs, remain elusive. In this study, we showed that TCF7L2 is robustly expressed in TSCs, and its expression gradually increases during TSC differentiation into EVTs. Depleting TCF7L2 has minimal impact on the expression of TSC-specific genes, except for TEAD4. However, it significantly inhibits TSC proliferation, reduces the S-phase cell population, and increases apoptotic cells. This indicates that TCF7L2 promotes cell growth while preventing apoptosis in TSCs.

During EVT formation, progenitors undergo cell shape remodeling and invade the uterus [45]. A previous study reported that TCF7L2 is implicated in trophoblast motility [16]. Given the significant increase in TCF7L2 expression during EVT formation, we investigated its role in the differentiation of TSCs into EVTs. Our findings revealed that TCF7L2-depleted cells exhibited severe morphological defects and reduced expression of EVT marker genes, indicating that TCF7L2 is crucial for EVT formation. We explored the mechanisms by which TCF7L2 regulates EVT differentiation by analyzing global gene expression following TCF7L2 depletion and its binding sites in both TSCs and EVTs. TCF7L2 is known to bind to specific DNA sequences (5′-xCTTTGATx-3′) on the genome [8]. Consistently, we found an enriched TCF7L2 motif at the center of its binding sites near genes with significantly altered expression upon TCF7L2 depletion, indicating that TCF7L2 binding directly regulates its target gene expression. Intriguingly, BETA analysis disclosed that TCF7L2 can function as both a transcriptional activator and repressor. This aligns with previous findings that TCF7L2 exerts dual regulatory effects on target genes by interacting with either the transcriptional co-activator, β-catenin, or the transcriptional co-repressor, TLE/Groucho, which can recruit histone methyltransferases or histone deacetylases [46–49].

It has been reported that TCF7L2 binds to the promoter of GCM1, which is a key regulator for trophoblast syncytialization, and silencing TCF7L2 impairs the fusion of human trophoblast-like choriocarcinoma cells [15], suggesting that TCF7L2 may also play an important role in ST formation. Contrarily, we observed a decrease in TCF7L2 levels during ST formation, and its overexpression prevented TSCs from differentiating into ST, along with a failure to properly induce ST marker genes, including GCM1. This indicates a negative role of TCF7L2 in ST formation. This discrepancy may be due to different model systems, warranting further investigation.

Signaling pathways, such as NOTCH and WNT, play crucial roles in EVT differentiation [40]. We observed abnormal expression of WNT and NOTCH genes following the depletion of TCF7L2 during EVT formation and revealed that TCF7L2 binds near most of these genes, suggesting that TCF7L2 may directly regulate the WNT and NOTCH signaling pathways. Upon activation of the WNT signal, TCF7L2 can form a complex with β-catenin to regulate the expression of WNT target genes, including cell proliferation-associated genes [50]. In TSCs, activation of the canonical WNT signaling pathway with CHIR99021 is indispensable for the self-renewal of TSCs and CTB proliferation [17]. Depletion of TCF7L2 in TSCs may prevent TCF7L2 from forming a complex with the transcriptional coactivator, β-catenin, and activating WNT targets that are associated with the cell cycle in TSCs, which eventually hampers the proliferation of TSCs. Conditional deletion of Notch2 in mice impaired vessel remodeling and placental perfusion [51], suggesting its crucial roles in the endovascular invasion of spiral arteries. EVTs predominantly express NOTCH2 in the human placenta [52]. We observed that depletion of TCF7L2 impaired the proper induction of NOTCH2 during TSC differentiation to EVTs; therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that TCF7L2 promotes invasive trophoblast differentiation by regulating NOTCH2. In agreement with this, we found TCF7L2 binding sites near NOTCH2 in EVTs. Additionally, we found that TCF7L2 depletion during EVT formation failed to downregulate NOTCH1. Given that NOTCH1 can control column cell (CC) development [40, 53] and a rapid downregulation of NOTCH1 protein was reported in placental EVT (pEVTs), which is the distal region of the CC differentiated EVTs [54], sustained NOTCH1 may be attributed to abnormal differentiation of TSCs into EVTs.

Our data demonstrate TCF7L2 as a critical regulator of EVT differentiation. This finding, however, resides within a complex and seemingly contradictory landscape regarding the role of its upstream pathway, canonical WNT signaling. Early work in immortalized trophoblast models proposed that WNT/TCF7L2-dependent signaling promotes an invasive EVT phenotype [16]. In contrast, a recent study using TSCs established that canonical WNT/β-catenin signaling must be actively suppressed to enable EVT specification [55]. The apparent discrepancy between the requirement for the TCF7L2 and the dispensability of its canonical WNT/β-catenin presents a compelling paradox. This dichotomy can be resolved by non-mutually exclusive hypotheses. First, TCF7L2 may function primarily through β-catenin-independent, non-canonical mechanisms during EVT differentiation, potentially by partnering with other transcriptional co-regulators to control a distinct gene expression profile. Second, the requirement for WNT signaling may be highly context-dependent, varying between immortalized lines, primary cells, and TSC models due to differences in basal signaling states. Our results, which demonstrate that TCF7L2 depletion impedes EVT differentiation, firmly place this factor at the center of this mechanistic puzzle. Future work is essential to define the specific transcriptional partners of TCF7L2 in this context. Determining whether TCF7L2 drives EVT differentiation independently of β-catenin or interacts with other pathways will be critical for reconciling these models and achieving a unified understanding of human trophoblast lineage development.

Exploring the downstream targets of TCF7L2 could provide vital insights into early trophoblast development. Through ChIP-seq experiments, we identified tens of thousands of TCF7L2 binding sites across the genome in TSCs and EVTs. By comparing these sites between TSCs and EVTs, we discovered binding sites specific to EVTs that appear during TSC differentiation into EVTs. Interestingly, most genes associated with these binding sites were downregulated in EVTs, while a subset was upregulated. Signaling pathways involved in the regulation of cell cycle and proliferation, such as Rho GTPase signaling [56] and androgen receptor signaling [57], were enriched in the genes directly repressed by TCF7L2. Similarly, in human placental development, previous immunofluorescence analysis of placental tissues revealed the induction of TCF7L2 protein in the nucleus of non-proliferating EVTs [14]. In contrast, genes involved in cell differentiation and migration were enriched among those directly activated by TCF7L2 in EVTs. This suggests that TCF7L2 directly activates genes essential for EVT formation and cell migration while repressing cell proliferation, aligning with its dual role as a transcriptional activator and repressor [8]. Recent studies have reported multiple TFs (ASCL2, DLX3, DLX5, DLX6, NRIP1, and ZNF439) as key players in EVT differentiation [29, 38, 39]. Consistent with other EVT regulators, we found that EVT regulators and TCF7L2 share binding sites and regulate each other, forming an EVT-specific transcriptional gene regulatory network. The hierarchy and interaction of these critical EVT regulators in controlling EVT differentiation require further investigation.

Notably, TCF7L2 genetic variants—particularly the well-characterized rs7903146 polymorphism—are strongly associated with impaired glucose metabolism and an elevated risk of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [58, 59]. GDM itself is an established risk factor for subsequent complications, including IUGR [60], suggesting that TCF7L2 may contribute indirectly to these conditions. Beyond metabolic mechanisms, our findings, in conjunction with existing literature, indicate that TCF7L2 plays a vital role in placental development, particularly in regulating trophoblast differentiation and function. Although direct evidence linking TCF7L2 to preeclampsia remains limited, its involvement in key processes such as invasion and vascular remodeling suggests that altered expression or function could compromise placental formation and function. This provides a plausible biological basis for its potential contribution to placental syndromes such as preeclampsia and IUGR. Modulating TCFL7L2 activity or targeting downstream effectors within the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway may help restore normative placental development and function. Targeting metabolic pathways affected by TCF7L2 variants could aid in managing maternal glucose levels and reducing adverse pregnancy outcomes.

In summary, we discovered that TCF7L2 acts as a key regulator, switching between trophoblast proliferation and EVT differentiation by directly controlling cell cycle regulators and EVT-active genes in a context-dependent manner.
