Central and peripheral optical quality after FS-LASIK, SMILE, and phakic ICL implantation for moderate to high myopia
Authors: Ying Lin, Xin Wei, Baoyu Huang, Yu Li, Huanjun Su, Minli Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFS-LASIK, small-incision lenticule extraction (SMILE), and phakic intraocular lens (ICL) implantation are widely used for correcting moderate to high myopia, yet their effects on peripheral optical quality remain incompletely understood. This study compared central and peripheral optical outcomes after these three procedures using on-axis and off-axis wavefront measurements.MethodsThis retrospective comparative study included 598 eyes (18–35 years) that underwent FS-LASIK, SMILE, or phakic ICL implantation. Wavefront aberrations were measured preoperatively and at 6 months postoperatively, both centrally and at ± 30° eccentric fixation, using a modified Shack–Hartmann aberrometer. The measurements were reconstructed over a 4.5-mm pupil, ensuring consistency across subjects.Second-, third-, and fourth-order RMS aberrations, peripheral relative defocus, astigmatic vector J0, and horizontal coma were analyzed pre- and postoperatively, with inter-group comparisons and correlations with preoperative spherical equivalent (SE).ResultsAll procedures achieved high refractive accuracy with minimal surgically induced astigmatism. Second-order aberrations were substantially reduced in all groups. Third-order aberrations increased postoperatively, most prominently after FS-LASIK, followed by SMILE and then ICL implantation, while fourth-order changes were small and similar among groups. Peripheral relative defocus shifted toward hyperopia after all procedures, greatest after FS-LASIK. Induced higher-order aberrations and peripheral defocus were significantly correlated with preoperative SE, particularly in the FS-LASIK group.ConclusionsAlthough FS-LASIK, SMILE, and phakic ICL implantation all provided effective correction of moderate to high myopia, they were associated with different patterns of postoperative central and peripheral optical change. In this cohort, phakic ICL implantation showed relatively smaller changes in peripheral optical quality measures, whereas FS-LASIK was associated with greater spherical equivalent–dependent increases in higher-order aberrations and peripheral hyperopic defocus. Peripheral wavefront assessment may provide clinically relevant information beyond central aberrometry.Keywords: FS-LASIK, SMILE, Phakic ICL, Optical quality, Peripheral optics, Wavefront aberration, High myopia

Introduction

Refractive surgery is widely used to correct moderate to high myopia, with femtosecond-assisted laser in situ keratomileusis (FS-LASIK), small-incision lenticule extraction (SMILE), and posterior chamber phakic intraocular lens (ICL) implantation being among the most commonly performed procedures [1–3]. These techniques have demonstrated excellent refractive accuracy, safety, and patient satisfaction across a broad range of myopic corrections [4, 5]. However, due to fundamental differences in their surgical mechanisms—corneal tissue ablation in FS-LASIK, intrastromal lenticule extraction in SMILE, and additive intraocular correction in phakic ICL implantation—each procedure may induce distinct alterations in postoperative optical quality [6].

Wavefront aberrometry has provided important insight into optical changes after refractive surgery beyond conventional visual acuity outcomes [7]. Previous studies have shown that corneal refractive procedures can induce higher-order aberrations (HOAs), including coma and spherical aberration, even when best-corrected visual acuity remains excellent [8, 9]. SMILE has generally been reported to induce fewer HOAs than FS-LASIK, likely due to better preservation of anterior stromal integrity [10], whereas phakic ICL implantation tends to preserve corneal optics and visual quality because corneal shape is largely maintained [11, 12]. Notably, most prior investigations have focused predominantly on central (on-axis) optical performance.

Increasing evidence suggests that peripheral optical quality may also play an important role in visual performance, influencing contrast sensitivity, night vision, and overall visual comfort [13, 14]. In addition, peripheral refractive characteristics and off-axis aberrations have been implicated in ocular growth regulation and may be particularly relevant in young patients with moderate to high myopia [15, 16]. Clinical studies have further demonstrated measurable differences in peripheral defocus and higher-order aberrations in myopic eyes, highlighting the potential importance of peripheral optics in refractive assessment [17]. Despite this potential clinical relevance, peripheral wavefront measurements remain technically challenging, and standard aberrometers are not optimized for eccentric fixation. Consequently, comparative data regarding peripheral optical outcomes after different refractive procedures remain limited [18].

To address this gap, we employed a modified Shack–Hartmann aberrometer capable of reliable wavefront measurements at ± 30° eccentric fixation [19]. Using this approach, we evaluated both central and peripheral wavefront aberrations in a large cohort of patients who underwent FS-LASIK, SMILE, or phakic ICL implantation.

The purpose of this study was to compare central and peripheral optical quality after FS-LASIK, SMILE, and phakic ICL implantation in moderate to high myopia, and to examine the relationship between postoperative optical changes and preoperative spherical equivalent. By incorporating off-axis wavefront analysis, this study aims to provide clinically relevant information that may assist in procedure selection and postoperative optical assessment.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a retrospective observational design to quantify central and peripheral retinal image quality in myopic patients undergoing corneal or intraocular refractive correction. Wavefront aberrations were measured on-axis and at ± 30° eccentricities along the horizontal visual field before surgery and at 6 months postoperatively in patients who underwent femtosecond laser-assisted LASIK (FS-LASIK), small-incision lenticule extraction (SMILE), or phakic implantable collamer lens (ICL) implantation. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the participating institution. All patients provided written informed consent at the time of surgery, authorizing the use of their de-identified clinical data for research purposes.

A total of 598 patients aged 18–35 years were enrolled, with one eye from each subject randomly selected for analysis. The randomization was performed using a number generator, with odd numbers representing the right eye and even numbers representing the left eye. Inclusion criteria comprised stable refraction for at least one year, corrected distance visual acuity of 20/20 or better, and moderate to high myopia within standard surgical indications. Exclusion criteria included subjective astigmatism greater than 1.00 D, pupil diameter less than 4.5 mm, prior ocular surgery or trauma, ocular disease other than myopia or astigmatism, and systemic conditions known to affect corneal healing or visual function. Demographic and baseline refractive data were obtained from electronic medical records.

Surgical procedures

Clinical criteria for surgical selection (FS-LASIK vs. SMILE vs. ICL) are based on factors such as corneal thickness, refractive error, and individual patient suitability. These factors are evaluated to ensure the best surgical approach for achieving optimal visual outcomes.

All refractive procedures were performed by experienced surgeons following standardized clinical protocols.

FS-LASIK (Alcon Germany, EagleEye FS200 + EX500) first uses femtosecond laser to create the corneal flap, then uses excimer laser to ablate the corneal stroma to alter the corneal curvature, thus achieving refractive correction. In this study, the corneal flap diameter is approximately 8.5–8.8 mm, and the corneal flap thickness is approximately 100–110 μm; the excimer laser energy parameters are set according to the default settings of the device. The optical zone diameter of ablation is approximately 6.0–6.5 mm, and the transition zone is approximately 1.0–1.5 mm. After ablation, the corneal flap is repositioned to restore the corneal structure.

SMILE (Zeiss Germany, VisuMax500) uses femtosecond laser to cut a lenticule within the corneal stroma, which is then removed through a small incision to change the corneal refractive power. In this study, the corneal cap thickness is approximately 105–120 μm, the lenticule diameter is approximately 6.0–6.5 mm, the corneal cap diameter is approximately 7.5–7.8 mm, and the incision width is approximately 2 mm. The femtosecond laser energy and scanning parameters are set according to the recommended standard settings of the device.

ICL(EVO-ICL) surgery is typically performed by making a 3.0 mm corneal incision to implant the ICL lens, positioning it in the ciliary sulcus. The lens size selection method takes into account factors such as eye axial length, corneal curvature, pupil diameter, and expected vault height, and is determined by a combination of calculation formulas, the assistance of surgical equipment systems, and the surgeon’s experience. The annular axis alignment method ensures accurate lens positioning through preoperative corneal edge marking and intraoperative astigmatism marker alignment. The toric ICLs rotation after surgery should be controlled within a 5-degree range. Normally, postoperative ICL vault should be within the range of 250 μm to 750 μm, with the optimal vault around 500 μm to reduce the occurrence of complications [20],. In this study, the postoperative ICL vault was controlled within 200–500 μm.

All patients received routine postoperative topical antibiotics and corticosteroids according to institutional practice.

Wavefront measurement and peripheral assessment

This study used a modified iDesign Shack–Hartmann aberrometer(Johnson & Johnson Vision, United States). for off-axis wavefront measurements. A beam splitter and a fixed ± 30° eccentric fixation system were added, allowing the measurement beam to remain aligned with the optical axis while decoupling the fixation direction, enabling stable peripheral wavefront data acquisition (Fig. 1). To ensure wavefront reconstruction stability, an inscribed circle analysis strategy was used, with a circular region defined by the short axis of the elliptical pupil for Zernike decomposition. Under 30° eccentric conditions, this circle covered 87% of the pupil area, preserving most optical information and ensuring comparability between on-axis and off-axis measurements. All wavefront data were normalized to a 4.5 mm analysis pupil to minimize pupil size differences. The modified system was validated using a simplified physical eye model and human eyes, demonstrating reliable reproduction of both on-axis and off-axis optical behavior, and providing a stable platform for full-eye optical research.

In this study, defocus and coma were defined according to standard optical conventions. Defocus was represented by the Zernike defocus term (Z4), with positive values indicating myopic defocus (focal point anterior to the retina) and negative values indicating hyperopic defocus (focal point posterior to the retina). Horizontal coma was represented by the corresponding coma term (Z9), with the sign indicating the direction of asymmetry in the horizontal field. Peripheral relative defocus was derived from the wavefront defocus term and defined as peripheral defocus at ± 30° eccentric fixation minus central on-axis defocus at 0°. It was reported in diopters throughout for clinical interpretation, whereas micrometer units were reserved for wavefront aberration coefficients and RMS metrics.

Measurements were obtained under mesopic conditions. Wavefront data were reconstructed over a standardized 4.5-mm pupil diameter. Because the entrance pupil becomes elliptical during off-axis fixation, an inscribed circular analysis zone was used for peripheral reconstructions to preserve Zernike orthogonality and minimize geometric distortion. Consistency in vertex distance, head position, and pupil centration during eccentric fixation is ensured by pre-positioning and securing the head, along with a fixation target.

Wavefront analysis and outcome measures

Zernike polynomial decomposition up to the sixth order was applied to quantify lower- and higher-order aberrations. Outcome measures included changes in second-, third-, and fourth-order root mean square (RMS) aberrations, peripheral relative defocus, the astigmatic vector component J0, and horizontal coma at ± 30° eccentricity. Peripheral relative defocus and J0 were derived from Optical Society of America (OSA)–standard Zernike terms.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses compared postoperative changes in central and peripheral optical parameters among the three procedures. For continuous outcomes, inter-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When the overall ANOVA was significant, pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple testing. Within-group preoperative versus postoperative comparisons were assessed using paired t-tests. Associations between baseline spherical equivalent and surgically induced optical changes were evaluated using linear regression, and regression coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and corresponding P values were reported. Because multiple outcomes and field locations were analyzed, the results were interpreted with emphasis on the overall pattern of findings rather than on isolated nominal P values. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Model eye and human eyes verification test

The differences in LogMAR BCVA among the three groups of patients at 6 months postoperative were not statistically significant.

Off-axis wavefront aberrations were measured using a modified Shack–Hartmann aberrometer on a model eye. Axial defocus measurements (range 0 to − 14D) were highly consistent with theoretical predictions, with variability under 0.15D. For off-axis measurements at ± 30° eccentric fixation, relative defocus was + 0.18 ± 0.05D at − 30° and + 0.19 ± 0.03D at + 30°, closely matching the theoretical values. Primary spherical aberration (Z10) showed similar agreement, with measured values of − 0.68 ± 0.08 μm at − 30° and − 0.62 ± 0.07 μm at + 30°. The system demonstrated high repeatability with standard deviations under 0.05D for defocus and 0.08 μm for spherical aberration, confirming its accuracy for peripheral wavefront measurements (Fig. 2).

To assess the modified aberrometer’s accuracy for off-axis measurements, eccentric fixation at ± 30° was simulated using a model eye. Two optical parameters, relative defocus and primary spherical aberration (Z10), were analyzed. Theoretical predictions and measured values showed excellent agreement: relative defocus was + 0.18 ± 0.05D at − 30° and + 0.19 ± 0.03D at + 30°, while primary spherical aberration was − 0.68 ± 0.08 μm at − 30° and − 0.62 ± 0.07 μm at + 30°. Higher variability in Z6 may reflect HOA’s sensitivity to alignment changes.

In human subjects, peripheral wavefront measurements at ± 30° along the horizontal meridian showed reproducible group-level optical patterns consistent with established myopic peripheral optics. Relative peripheral defocus was hyperopic at both temporal and nasal eccentricities and became progressively more negative with increasing central myopia, with strong linear relationships to refractive group (R² = 0.846 temporally and 0.855 nasally). By contrast, relative J0 and horizontal coma showed stable nasal–temporal asymmetry but minimal dependence on refractive error (R² < 0.02 for both), indicating that the system captured expected peripheral optical behavior in vivo.

Central optical aberrations

On-axis wavefront analysis revealed a marked reduction in second-order RMS after all three procedures (Fig. 5). This decrease primarily reflects correction of lower-order refractive error. In contrast, third-order RMS aberrations increased after surgery, with the greatest increases observed after FS-LASIK, intermediate changes after SMILE, and the smallest changes after phakic ICL implantation. Fourth-order RMS aberrations showed only small postoperative increases and did not differ significantly among the three procedures.

Peripheral optical aberrations

Peripheral wavefront measurements at − 30° nasal and + 30° temporal eccentricities showed patterns broadly similar to those observed on-axis (Fig. 6). Second-order RMS aberrations were markedly reduced after surgery in both peripheral fields across all three procedures. In contrast, third-order RMS aberrations increased postoperatively, with the largest increases observed after FS-LASIK, intermediate changes after SMILE, and smaller changes after phakic ICL implantation. Fourth-order RMS changes in the peripheral field were small and did not differ substantially among the three groups.

Association between induced aberrations and preoperative spherical equivalent

The induction of higher-order aberrations was significantly associated with preoperative spherical equivalent (Fig. 8). On-axis analyses demonstrated that eyes with higher degrees of myopia experienced greater postoperative increases in third- and fourth-order RMS aberrations, with the strongest correlations observed in the FS-LASIK group, followed by SMILE and phakic ICL implantation. This relationship was more pronounced in the peripheral field. At − 30° nasal eccentricity, FS-LASIK showed the strongest associations between baseline myopia and induced aberrations, whereas SMILE demonstrated moderate correlations and phakic ICL implantation showed weaker associations. Temporal correlations at + 30° eccentricity followed a similar pattern, with slightly reduced slopes. Peripheral relative defocus

Peripheral relative defocus was calculated as the difference between peripheral defocus at − 30° or + 30° eccentric fixation and central on-axis defocus at 0°, and was reported in diopters. In the present dataset, the values plotted in Fig. 9 correspond to peripheral Z5-derived defocus minus the corresponding central Z5-derived defocus. Peripheral relative defocus became more negative after all three procedures (Fig. 9), indicating a postoperative shift toward relative peripheral hyperopic defocus. Preoperatively, relative peripheral defocus was approximately − 0.31 D in the nasal field and − 0.57 to − 0.59 D in the temporal field across groups. Postoperatively, it shifted further in the hyperopic direction, reaching approximately − 0.52 D and − 0.95 D in the FS-LASIK group, − 0.40 D and − 0.78 D in the SMILE group, and − 0.41 D and − 0.74 D in the phakic ICL group, at the nasal and temporal fields, respectively. The largest postoperative shifts were observed in the FS-LASIK group, followed by the SMILE group and then the phakic ICL group. Across all three procedures, the magnitude of relative peripheral hyperopic defocus was greater in the temporal field than in the nasal field.

Peripheral astigmatism and horizontal coma

Peripheral astigmatic vector component J0 remained consistently negative before and after surgery across all three procedures, with only minimal postoperative changes (Fig. 10A). A small nasal change in the FS-LASIK group reached statistical significance but was not clinically meaningful. Horizontal coma preserved its expected polarity, being positive in the nasal field and negative in the temporal field, with only minor postoperative shifts that were similar among FS-LASIK, SMILE, and phakic ICL implantation (Fig. 10B).

Summary of central and peripheral optical changes

Taken together, the results indicate that FS-LASIK, SMILE, and phakic ICL implantation all achieved excellent refractive outcomes and effectively corrected lower-order aberrations. However, the procedures differed in their effects on higher-order and peripheral optics. FS-LASIK induced the largest increases in higher-order aberrations and peripheral hyperopic defocus, SMILE produced intermediate changes, and phakic ICL implantation most consistently preserved both central and peripheral optical quality, particularly in eyes with higher preoperative myopia.

Discussion

In this study, we compared central and peripheral optical outcomes after FS-LASIK, SMILE, and posterior chamber phakic ICL implantation in moderate to high myopia using standardized on-axis and ± 30° off-axis wavefront measurements. Although all three procedures achieved excellent refractive accuracy and effective correction of lower-order aberrations, they differed substantially in their effects on higher-order aberrations and peripheral optics.

Central optical quality and higher-order aberrations

All procedures markedly reduced second-order aberrations on-axis, indicating accurate correction of spherical and astigmatic refractive errors. The substantial postoperative reduction in second-order RMS mainly reflects correction of lower-order refractive error. Therefore, second-order RMS should be interpreted primarily as an indicator of refractive correction rather than as a standalone measure of comparative postoperative optical quality. In contrast, third- and fourth-order aberrations increased postoperatively in a procedure-dependent manner. FS-LASIK induced the largest increase in higher-order aberrations, SMILE showed intermediate changes, and phakic ICL implantation was associated with relatively smaller changes in central optical quality measures [21, 22].

These differences are consistent with the known biomechanical and optical characteristics of each procedure. FS-LASIK, through corneal tissue removal and flap creation, may increase susceptibility to coma and spherical aberration, particularly in highly myopic eyes [23]. SMILE preserves the anterior corneal surface, which may partially mitigate higher-order aberration induction [24], while phakic ICL implantation leaves the cornea structurally intact, explaining its superior preservation of wavefront quality [25].

It is noteworthy that this study observed a slight undercorrection trend in the FS-LASIK group postoperatively. This phenomenon has been previously reported in high myopia correction and may be related to larger ablation depths and changes in corneal biomechanics [26–28]. Previous studies suggest that as the correction amount increases, the central ablation depth not only alters the curvature of the corneal front surface but may also affect postoperative refractive stability through tissue stress redistribution and epithelial remodeling [26–30]. Therefore, the slight undercorrection observed in FS-LASIK is not necessarily the result of “insufficient correction,” but rather may reflect continuous adjustments of the cornea during postoperative healing and biomechanical rebalancing. In contrast, SMILE, which primarily achieves refractive correction by removing a stromal lenticule, causes less direct disruption to the corneal surface, and its central refractive recovery process is less influenced by surface remodeling. ICL, as an intraocular additive optical correction strategy, relies more on lens positioning, the coupling state of the intraocular optical system, and the stability of individual intraocular parameters for refractive recovery.

Peripheral aberrations and field-dependent effects

Procedure-related differences were more pronounced off-axis than on-axis. FS-LASIK induced larger increases in peripheral third-order aberrations than SMILE and phakic ICL implantation, while fourth-order changes remained small and comparable among groups [31]. The dependence of induced aberrations on preoperative spherical equivalent was stronger in the peripheral field, particularly after FS-LASIK, suggesting greater sensitivity of peripheral optics to corneal shape modification in highly myopic eyes [32].

Peripheral astigmatism and coma

Peripheral astigmatic component J0 remained stable across procedures, with only minimal postoperative changes. Horizontal coma preserved its expected polarity pattern across all groups, with only small magnitude changes after surgery, consistent with previous peripheral wavefront studies [33]. Although no universally accepted clinical thresholds exist for peripheral aberration or refraction changes, we adopt the following criteria in this study to aid interpretation: an increase in coma (Z9) greater than 0.1 μm at a 4.5 mm pupil and a peripheral relative defocus shift greater than 0.5 diopters are considered clinically meaningful, as these ranges have been associated with perceptible declines in optical quality and contrast performance in prior adaptive optics and visual quality studies [34–38].

Clinical relevance and limitations

This study analyzed both central and peripheral wavefront aberrations within a unified measurement system and compared preoperative and postoperative changes across different refractive procedures, thereby extending optical assessment beyond the central visual field [39]. However, objective optical metrics do not fully reflect subjective visual experience, which may also be influenced by neural adaptation, contrast sensitivity, binocular vision status, and other factors [40, 41]. In addition, peripheral measurements were limited to the ± 30° horizontal meridian because of instrumental constraints, and no extrapolation beyond the measured locations was performed. Therefore, these measurements may not fully represent aberrations across the broader peripheral field. Future studies should include wider-field measurements and complementary techniques for a more comprehensive evaluation.

Several limitations should also be noted. First, the retrospective non-randomized design introduces the possibility of residual selection bias and confounding. Procedure choice was based on routine clinical indications and patient suitability rather than random allocation, and may therefore have been influenced by factors such as corneal thickness, refractive error, anterior chamber depth, and other clinical considerations. Although several baseline parameters were comparable among groups, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as comparative associations observed in this cohort rather than evidence of causal superiority of one procedure over another.

Conclusions

FS-LASIK, SMILE, and phakic ICL implantation all provided effective refractive correction in moderate to high myopia, but they were associated with different patterns of postoperative central and peripheral optical change. In this retrospective cohort, FS-LASIK was associated with larger increases in higher-order aberrations and peripheral hyperopic defocus, SMILE showed intermediate changes, and phakic ICL implantation showed relatively smaller changes in both central and peripheral optical parameters. These findings may provide useful objective optical information when considering refractive procedure selection for highly myopic young adults [42].
