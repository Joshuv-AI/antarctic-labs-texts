Adaptive signatures in Vietnamese chickens reveal a Southeast Asian reservoir for climate resilience and meat quality
Authors: Anh Huynh Luu, Trifan Budi, Worapong Singchat, Chien Tran Phuoc Nguyen, Thitipong Panthum, Nivit Tanglertpaibul, Kanithaporn Vangnai, Aingorn Chaiyes, Chotika Yokthongwattana, Chomdao Sinthuvanich, Orathai Sawatdichaikul, Kyudong Han, Narongrit Muangmai, Darren K. Griffin, Prateep Duengkae, Ngu Trong Nguyen, Kornsorn Srikulnath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vietnamese indigenous chickens and their wild progenitors represent a critical but under-characterized reservoir of avian genetic diversity. This study used a multi-locus approach, incorporating 28 neutral microsatellite markers and candidate functional genes (HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL). We successfully delineated the genetic structure and adaptive potential of 12 native Vietnamese indigenous chicken populations and red junglefowl (Gallus gallus). Microsatellite analysis revealed high genetic diversity (411 alleles) and significant regional structuring into three primary groups, suggesting extensive historical gene flow and shared ancestral heritage with Thai indigenous breeds. High-throughput sequencing of functional loci identified five novel HSP70, 10 novel HSP90, and five novel ADSL alleles, underscoring the unique genetic heritage of Vietnamese avian resources. Selective signature analyses confirmed that, while purifying selection maintains core protein stability, specific codons, notably in HSP70 (18, 33, 41, and 42) and HSP90 (7), exhibit putative signals of positive selection. These loci emerge as compelling candidates for functional studies underlying thermal adaptation. Moreover, the meat quality gene ADSL exhibited diversifying selection at codon 58, highlighting a candidate molecular marker for the potential enhancement of savoriness and inosine monophosphate content. These findings, which reconcile neutral population structure with candidate signals of localized adaptive divergence, address a critical regional knowledge gap and provide a scientific foundation for future marker-assisted selection programs. By integrating these results into the Siam Chicken Bioresource Project, this study promotes transboundary conservation and the sustainable development of climate-resilient poultry systems in a warming world.

Introduction

Indigenous and local chicken breeds constitute an important reservoir of genetic diversity. This is key to developing resilient agricultural systems that can mitigate the growing threats posed by climate change and emerging pathogens (Mpenda et al., 2019; Mulungu and Kangogo, 2022; Nyoni et al., 2022). In Southeast Asia, these native populations serve as invaluable genetic resources that strengthen regional food security, rural livelihoods, and cultural heritage (FAO, 2010; Lan Phuong et al., 2015; Giang et al., 2023). These breeds possess specialized adaptive traits, such as high thermotolerance and disease resistance, which have evolved over millennia by natural selection across diverse tropical agro-climatic zones and traditional husbandry practices (Choprakarn and Wongpichet, 2007; Akbarian et al., 2016; Nguyen et al., 2022). Vietnam serves as an important biological crossroads and gateway to Asian avian genetic diversity. Vietnam harbors rare phenotypes and unique gene pools, such as the Dong Tao and Hmong lineages, which depict the high heterozygosity of the red junglefowl (RJF; Gallus gallus), their wild ancestor (Granevitze et al., 2007; Cuc et al., 2010; Peters et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2023). The Siam Chicken Bioresource Project (SCBP) established a robust genomic baseline by delineating the genetic landscape of poultry across Thailand and neighboring regions (Hata et al., 2021; Singchat et al., 2022; Budi et al., 2023; Wattanadilokcahtkun et al., 2023; Wongloet et al., 2023; Budi et al., 2024a,b,c; Tanglertpaibul et al., 2024; Budi et al., 2025; Ekerette et al., 2025; Luu et al., 2025, 2026). However, a comprehensive genetic evaluation of the Vietnamese indigenous populations remains to be elucidated. Characterizing the Vietnamese chicken population is essential to completing the “big picture” of Southeast Asia’s avian biodiversity, thereby facilitating the identification of unique regional signatures and enhancing transboundary conservation strategies for these key breeds.

The domestication of the domestic chicken (Gallus gallus domesticus) from its wild ancestor (RJF; Gallus gallus) approximately 3,500 years ago initiated an evolutionary trajectory that prioritized specialized local adaptation over the high-yielding performance seen in modern commercial strains (Zeder, 2008; Pitt, 2017; Peters et al., 2022). However, rising global temperatures induce severe physiological stressors, including metabolic impairment, oxidative damage and increased mortality, threatening the survival of traditional low-input poultry systems (Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2010; Lara and Rostagno, 2013; Nawab et al., 2018). Adaptive resilience to these conditions is primarily mediated by molecular chaperones, such as HSP70 and HSP90, which maintain cellular proteostasis under thermal stress (Chen et al., 2013; Shende et al., 2019; Balakrishnan et al., 2023; Budi et al., 2024a). The demand for purine nucleotides during cellular repair under heat stress links thermal tolerance directly to the adenylosuccinate lyase (ADSL) gene, which serves as a dual-purpose marker for both physiological robustness and the accumulation of inosine monophosphate (IMP), a key flavor precursor (Wu et al., 2009; Ye et al., 2010; Zhao et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2015; Budi et al., 2024b). The SCBP established significant markers for thermal tolerance and meat quality within Thai indigenous chickens (Budi et al., 2024a,b). Extending these studies to Vietnamese populations can help identify broader regional selective signatures that define Southeast Asian chicken evolution.

Despite the high socioeconomic value of Vietnamese native breeds such as Noi, Hmong and Dong Tao, a comprehensive comparison of their genetic structure with that of their Thai counterparts and wild progenitors has yet to be realized. Phylogenetic evidence establishes a clear link between Vietnamese and Thai breeds via the Haplogroup D lineage, which is associated with RJF and gamecocks across the Vietnam-Thailand-Southwest China biodiversity hotspots (Liu et al., 2006; Miao et al., 2013). This shared ancestry contrasts with modern population structures that suggest divergent selective pressures. Specifically, Thai breeds exhibit directional selection, whereas Vietnamese chickens maintain significantly higher diversity, reflecting multiple maternal origins and a broader genetic base (Cuc et al., 2011). While earlier studies using FAO-recommended markers established the foundational genetic diversity of Vietnamese breeds (Cuc et al., 2010, 2011; Pham et al., 2014), their focus remained strictly on within-country population structure. Consequently, the functional genomic architecture underlying adaptive traits and the integration of these breeds into a regional framework remain unresolved. Although our previous investigations within the SCBP have extensively characterized the adaptive markers and diversity of Thai poultry, the regional genetic basis for thermotolerance and purine metabolism in Vietnamese populations remains largely uncharacterized (FAO, 2010; Akbarian et al., 2016). This knowledge gap limits our understanding of regional gene flow and adaptive divergence, both of which are critical for establishing effective transboundary conservation strategies (Frankham, 2010; Schmidt et al., 2023). The purpose of this study is to test the hypothesis that Vietnamese indigenous chickens harbor distinct signatures of positive selection within HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL genes, and this underpins their unique physiological resilience and superior meat quality traits in tropical environments. To address this, the current study used a multi-locus approach, incorporating neutral microsatellites and functional markers to delineate the population structure and evolutionary trajectories of Vietnamese poultry resources. The findings will provide a refined genetic perspective that will facilitate the implementation of marker-assisted selection (MAS) programs to enhance productivity, climate resilience and meat quality throughout Southeast Asia.

Materials and methods

Specimen collection and DNA extraction

Specimens of Vietnamese indigenous and local chickens were obtained from the Mekong Delta in Vietnam, including the provinces of Dong Thap (NOI1, 10°28′41.2″N 105°37′34.5″E), Vinh Long (NOI2, 10°07′14.8″N 105°49′31.6″E), Ben Tre (NOI3, 10°16′29.3″N 106°16′59.2″E), Can Tho (TRE1, 10°00′46.1″N 105°44′40.3″E), Tra Vinh (TRE2, 10°04′07.8″N 106°14′38.4″E), An Giang 1 (TRE3, 10°40′08.7″N 105°10′05.3″E), An Giang 2 (TRE4, 10°40′08.7″N 105°10′05.3″ E); and Ca Mau (TV, 9°07′22.8″N 105°07′06.7″ E) and from Hung Yen province (HM, 20°46′32.7″N 106°01′15.0″ E and DT, 20°49′28.6″N 105°58′18.1″ E). RJF were obtained from An Giang (Ggg-AG, 10°29′48.4″N 104°55′42.4″E) and Kien Giang provinces (Ggs-KG, N = 16; 10°21′59.9″N 104°04′40.1″ E). All specimens were used for genotyping and sequencing (Fig.s 1, S1, and Table S1). Whole blood samples were obtained from the brachial wing veins of Vietnamese indigenous chickens and RJF, from which genomic DNA was extracted and subsequently evaluated for quality and concentration according to previously established protocols (Supikamolseni et al., 2015; Budi et al., 2023). All protocols were approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of Kasetsart University (Approval No. ACKU67-SCI-021), which verified compliance with institutional regulations and the International ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org) for the ethical use of animals in scientific research.Fig. 1Detailed pictures of specimens from 12 populations of Vietnamese chicken, including five indigenous chicken breeds (Noi, Tre, Hmong, Dong Tao, and Tau Vang) and red junglefowl.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Microsatellite genotyping and data analysis

Genotyping was performed using 28 microsatellite loci in accordance with FAO recommendations (FAO, 2011) for chicken biodiversity research. The forward primers were 5՛-end labeled with 6-carboxyfluorescein (6-FAM) or hexachloro–fluorescein (HEX) fluorescent dyes (Macrogen Inc., Seoul, Korea). PCR amplification followed the protocols established by the SCBP (SCBP; https://www.sci.ku.ac.th/scbp/), which have been summarized in recent studies (Budi et al., 2023; Wattanadilokcahtkun et al., 2023). Genetic diversity and population structure were evaluated using various parameters, including measures of genetic richness (allelic richness [AR] and number of alleles per population), diversity (polymorphic information content [PIC], Shannon’s information index [I], heterozygosity [Ho and He]), and population differentiation and inbreeding (fixation index, inbreeding coefficient [FIS], pairwise genetic distances [FST, RST], relatedness [r]) following Budi et al. (2023). Arlequin software version 3.5.2.2 (Excoffier and Lischer, 2010), was used for the Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) to resolve hierarchical group structures and for assessing Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium and linkage disequilibrium (LD). Individual-level genetic relationships within populations were explored using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) using GenAlEx version 6.5 (Peakall and Smouse, 2006) and Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) using the ADEGENET 2.0 package (Jombart, 2008) in R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2025). The population structure was resolved using a Bayesian model-based clustering approach in STRUCTURE version 2.3.4 (Pritchard et al., 2000) following the method of Budi et al. (2024c), from which the most probable number of clusters (K) was identified by selecting the value at which the log-likelihood [ln Pr (X|K)] reached a plateau. This estimation was further validated using the ΔK method within StructureSelector (Li and Liu, 2018), which provides a robust framework for multi-metric K selection. Arlequin version 3.5.2.2 (Excoffier and Lischer, 2010) was used to determine the allelic range for each locus to calculate the M ratio (number of alleles/allele range) (Garza and Williamson, 2001), a robust indicator of long-term demographic contractions. To identify more recent bottleneck events, both the Single-Mutation Model (SMM) and Two-Phase Mutation (TPM) models were applied according to Wattanadilokchatkun et al. (2024), from which selective sweep signatures were further elucidated by plotting the observed He and FIS values for all 28 microsatellite loci (Budi et al., 2023). This analytical framework, established by Reddy et al. (2015), interprets a high FIS and low He as evidence of selective sweeps, whereas a high He paired with a low FIS indicates neutral or balancing selection (Reddy et al., 2015).

PCR amplification and Salus Pro short-read sequencing

Partial fragments of HSP70 (exon 1), HSP90 (exon 10 and intron 10), and ADSL (exon 2) genes were amplified via PCR using primers described by Shu et al. (2009) and Ye et al. (2010). The 5′ end of the forward primer was tagged with an 8-bp sample-specific barcode (Macrogen Inc.). Amplification was conducted in a 15 μL reaction volume containing 50 ng of genomic DNA, 1 × standard Apsalagen reaction buffer, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 0.5 μM of each primer, and 0.5 U Taq polymerase (Apsalagen Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand). Amplification parameters involved thermal cycling profile of 94°C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94°C (30 s), 58°C (30 s), and 72°C (30 s). Following verification via 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, 92 barcoded samples were pooled into nine pools, which were then sequenced on a Salus Pro medium-throughput NGS platform (Shenzhen Salus BioMed Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) using a 2 × 250 bp paired-end (PE250) configuration. Libraries were individually prepared and uniquely barcoded using Unique Dual Indices (UDIs) in separate reactions to prevent index misassignment, after which they were multiplexed for sequencing.

Sequence quality control and read processing

Raw paired-end reads for the HSP70 (250 bp), HSP90 (250 bp), and ADSL (240 bp) fragments were evaluated using FastQC version 0.11.9 (Andrews, 2010), which facilitated the retention of high-quality reads (Q) >20, following established amplicon sequencing standards (Sebastian et al., 2016). This approach yielded a mean of ∼3,500 high-quality reads per amplicon (range: 1,200–5,000), providing sufficient coverage depth for robust and accurate allele detection. Quality filtering, read merging, and allelic assignment were conducted using the AmpliSAS version 1.0 pipeline (Sebastian et al., 2016), which clusters sequences into individual allelic variants while maintaining a minimum amplicon depth of 100 to mitigate artifacts. Given the diploid nature of the study species, the maximum number of alleles per individual was constrained to two, as determined by the degree-of-change criterion based on sequencing depth (Lighten et al., 2014). Genotypes were assigned based on frequency thresholds, where individuals exhibiting a single dominant allele (>80% frequency) were classified as homozygous and those with two matching alleles as heterozygous (Budi et al., 2024a,b). The identity of each allele was verified using NCBI BLASTn (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), following which the sequences were aligned to chicken reference accessions (HSP70: AY288300, HSP90: CP100559, and ADSL: AY665559) and translated in Geneious Prime version 2023.0.4 (Kearse et al., 2012) to confirm the absence of premature stop codons.

Genetic diversity and data analysis based on HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL genes

To comprehensively elucidate the genetic relationships and diversity patterns of chicken breeds across Vietnam and Thailand, this study used a tiered analytical framework that employed PCoA, DAPC, and STRUCTURE analyses. This framework was initially applied to a localized dataset comprising Vietnamese indigenous chickens and was subsequently extended to a broader regional dataset, which enabled the assessment of the large-scale genetic structure. Genetic diversity parameters, comprising the number of alleles Na, effective number of alleles (Ne​), observed heterozygosity (Ho​), expected heterozygosity (He​), and fixation index (F), were calculated based on allelic frequencies using GenAlEx version 6.5 (Peakall and Smouse, 2012), which facilitates multivariate analysis for genetic data. To elucidate population genetic structure further, F-statistics (FIS​ and FST​) and AR were determined for the HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL genes using FSTAT version 2.9.3 (Goudet, 1995), following the method established by Budi et al. (2023). To evaluate genetic differentiation between populations, an AMOVA was performed (Excoffier and Lischer, 2010) was performed, and STRUCTURE version 2.3.4 (Pritchard et al., 2000) was used to infer population genetic clusters, as described by Budi et al. (2024a). The most probable number of genetic clusters K was identified by plotting the mean log-likelihood of the data (ln Pr (X|K)) across multiple K values and determining the point at which these values reached a plateau. This determination was further corroborated by the ΔK strategy, which was implemented via the StructureSelector (Li and Liu, 2018) to validate the optimal number of clusters. Genetic relationships and potential population clustering were explored through PCoA (Peakall and Smouse, 2012) to visualize the major axes of variation. To delineate the fine-scale genetic structure further, DAPC was performed using the ADEGENET 2.0 package (Jombart, 2008) within the R version 4.5.2 environment (R Core Team, 2025). These multivariate results were systematically examined across a concatenated three-gene dataset (HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL) to maximize the resolution of the population assignments that integrated Vietnamese indigenous populations, diverse Thai indigenous and local breeds, and the RJF (Table S1).

Phylogenetic analysis

Nucleotide sequences for the HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL genes were retrieved from the NCBI database via BLASTn to perform a comparative analysis between global chicken breeds and the alleles identified in this study. The retrieved fragments, which specifically targeted exon 1 of HSP70, exon 10 and intron 10 of HSP90, and partial exon 2 of ADSL, were subjected to stringent filtering criteria that retained only sequences with >70% identity and >85% query coverage. This curation yielded 42, 39, and 34 sequences for HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL, respectively. To reconstruct the evolutionary history of these loci, a Bayesian phylogenetic tree was generated using MrBayes version 3.2.6 (Ronquist and Huelsenbeck, 2003). The optimal nucleotide substitution model for the concatenated dataset was determined using ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017) within IQ-TREE, which selected the best-fit model based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Bayesian inference was subsequently performed using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method with five simultaneous chains, each run for two million generations. After discarding the initial 10% of generations as burn-in, a majority-rule consensus tree was generated from the trees sampled every 5,000 generations. The resulting phylogeny was visualized and annotated using Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) version 5 (https://itol.embl.de/) (Letunic and Bork, 2021).

Selection analysis

Selective pressures acting on HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL were evaluated by calculating the ratio of nonsynonymous-to-synonymous substitution rates (dN/dS or ω). The mean numbers of synonymous (dS) and nonsynonymous (dN) substitutions per site were determined using the Nei–Gojobori method (Nei and Gojobori, 1986) with the Jukes–Cantor correction, which was implemented in MEGA11 (Tamura et al., 2021). Within this framework, an ω value near 1 suggests neutral evolution, whereas ω >1 and ω <1 indicate positive and purifying selection, respectively. To further elucidate the evolutionary forces at play, neutrality tests, including Tajima’s D, Fu and Li’s F*, and Fu and Li’s D*, were performed using DnaSP version 6.12 (Rozas et al., 2017). The potential for selective sweeps was explored by analyzing the distribution of expected heterozygosity (He ) and the inbreeding coefficient (FIS), where the concurrence of high FIS​ and low He typically indicates a recent selective sweep or purifying selection (Reddy et al., 2015). To pinpoint specific codon sites subject to episodic positive selection, the Mixed Effects Model of Evolution (MEME) was used on the Datamonkey server (https://www.datamonkey.org/) (Weaver et al., 2018). Significant sites were identified using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) with a threshold of p ≤ 0.01, following the method of Murrell et al. (2012). Furthermore, pervasive selection was assessed across codon alignments for HSP70 (8 sequences, 80 codons), HSP90 (25 sequences, 25 codons), and ADSL (10 sequences, 66 codons) using two complementary methods: Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL), which estimates substitution rates at each site with a p-≤0.05 threshold (Kosakovsky Pond and Frost, 2005), and Fast Unconstrained Bayesian Approximation (FUBAR), which is a computationally efficient Bayesian approach that infers selection with a posterior probability ≥0.9 (Murrell et al., 2013). This integrated approach enabled the robust detection of both episodic and pervasive selective signals across the identified codon sites.

Multiple sequence alignment of HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL amino acid residues

Reference HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL gene sequences were obtained from NCBI using the BLASTp algorithm (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) for many chicken breeds. For the HSP70 gene, sequences were retrieved from Aseel (XGK30259), Khuzestan (AXS75985), Pescoco Pelado 1 (AAN18281), Taihe Silkie (AAP37962), Yuehuang (AAP37964), Vezaguda (XGK30261), Mazandaran (AYF57944), Lohmann (BBO36590), and Hubbard Pettersen (AY143693). HSP90 gene sequences were sourced from Xishuangbanna gamefowl (JBHFLH010000005), Wenchang (JAYMZF010000037), Ross (JAPZJZ010002257), Daweishan (JANJPS020000005), Cornish (JAJMOV010000272), White Leghorn (JAJMOT010000247), Tibetan (JAJMOP010000264), Asil (JAJMOF010000005), Wenchang (JAYMZG010000039), Ross (JAPZJY010000012), Tibetan (JAJMOH010000004), White Leghorn (JAJMOG010000005), Thailand gamefowl (JAJMOD010000002), and RJF (AADN05000303). For ADSL gene, China chicken (AAT76521), Slow-chicken breed (NP_990860), chicken liver (AAA48574), Shouguang breed (CAH25440), Beijing Fatty Chicken (ABU24460), Black Laiwu chicken (AAV38134), and Jiningbairi breed (AAV38133). For the BLASTp analysis, stringent filtering criteria were applied using thresholds of >60% sequence identity and >85% query coverage (Pearson, 2013; Meiklejohn et al., 2019), which ensured the inclusion of high-confidence protein homologs in the analysis. The amino acid sequences corresponding to the HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL alleles identified in this study, along with their respective reference sequences, were aligned using the ClustalW algorithm within Geneious Prime version 2023.0.4 (Geneious Prime). These alignments were subsequently truncated to 80 codons for HSP70, 25 codons for HSP90, and 66 codons for ADSL, providing the basis for a Bayesian phylogenetic tree constructed following a previously established method. Furthermore, protein secondary structures, which provide insight into the functional implications of the observed polymorphisms, were predicted using the EMBOSS tool version 6.5.7 (EMBOSS Open Software) (https://emboss.open-bio.org).

Protein structure prediction and visualization

The translated amino acid sequences corresponding to partial segments of HSP70 exon 1, HSP90 exon 10, and intron 10, and ADSL exon 2, were subjected to three-dimensional (3D) protein structure prediction using the AlphaFold 3 model (https://alphafoldserver.com/) (Abramson et al., 2024), which provides high-accuracy modeling of protein-ligand and protein-protein interactions. The resulting structural models were visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio (Dassault Systèmes, San Diego, CA, USA) and underwent rigorous quality assessment via PROCHECK (Laskowski et al., 1993), which served as the primary metric for Ramachandran plot analysis. To evaluate structural divergence, the modeled proteins were superimposed and compared with homologous reference structures retrieved from the UniProt database (UniProt.org) (https://www.uniprot.org/).

Results

Extensive genetic polymorphism across Vietnamese indigenous breeds and their wild ancestors

Genotyping of the 12 Vietnamese chicken populations yielded 411 alleles, which corresponded to a mean of 5.262 ± 0.159 alleles per locus (Table 1). Most populations adhered to the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, except for the TV-Con population. Although null alleles were identified at the MCW0034 and LEI0166 loci, all markers were retained for the estimation of genetic diversity, which ensured a comprehensive analysis of population structure. The Ho and He were 0.688 ± 0.014 and 0.620 ± 0.010, respectively (Table 1). Welch’s t-tests revealed no statistically significant differences between Ho and He in most populations; however, TRE1-F, TRE2-C, and HM-C exhibited significant deviations that warrant further demographic investigation. The mean AR across the 12 groups was 4.156 ± 2.053, whereas the PIC values ranged from 0.346 to 0.644. In addition, I varied from 0.656 to 1.452, with a mean value of 1.231 ± 0.027. Tests for selective signatures revealed that He values were consistently higher than FIS across all 28 microsatellite loci and 12 populations, suggesting a predominance of neutral or balanced selection. Further demographic analysis via the M ratio yielded values below the 0.68 threshold established by Garza and Williamson (2001) for all populations, indicating a history of long-term demographic contraction (Table 1). To evaluate more recent population fluctuations, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed under both the SMM and TPM models, with p-values ranging from 0.006 to 0.996 and 0.003–0.950, respectively. Mode-shift tests indicated stable demographic histories for most of the study groups, including most Noi, Tre, Hmong, and Dong Tao lineages, which were characterized by a standard l-shaped distribution. However, we detected signatures of a recent genetic bottleneck in two populations: Tre from Can Tho (TRE1-F) and RJF from An Giang (Ggg-AG). Comparative genetic analysis of Vietnamese and Thai avian populations, which reveals regional divergence and shared ancestral lineages

Multivariate analyses, including PCoA and DAPC, revealed that most Vietnamese indigenous chickens and RJF were grouped and shared genetic clusters with various indigenous Thai breeds. By contrast, the Thai RJF populations formed distinct, separate groups, a finding corroborated by the STRUCTURE analysis across K values ranging from 1 to 25. Although Evanno’s ΔK identified K = 2 as the most probable number of clusters, the mean ln P(K) peaked at K = 22, suggesting a hierarchical population structure characterized by both broad ancestral lineages and finer-scale local differentiation. At the K = 2 level, most Vietnamese indigenous populations and RJF exhibited high genetic affinity with a broad range of Thai indigenous breeds, including the Chee, Mae Hong Son, and Pradu Hang Dam lineages, as well as RJF from Khon Kaen (G. gallus gallus) and Ranong (G. gallus gallus and G. gallus spadiceus). At higher clustering resolutions (K = 22), specific Vietnamese populations, such as NOI1-F, NOI2-F, Hmong, and Dong Tao, displayed genetic profiles congruent with RJF from Khao Kho (G. gallus spadiceus). Meanwhile, the NOI3-F, TRE1-F, TRE2-C, TRE3-F, and Tau Vang groups shared ancestral gene pools with a diverse set of Thai breeds, including the Chee (Nakhon Pathom) and Leung Hang Khao, and RJF lineages from southern regions such as Songkhla. As the clustering resolution increased to K = 24, the Noi and Hmong lineages maintained distinct genetic profiles, whereas the TRE4-O population showed a similar gene pool to the Phuphan black and white varieties (Fig. 3). The partitioning of genetic variance, assessed via AMOVA, provided insights into the distribution of diversity across the studied genes. For the thermal-adaptation markers HSP70 and HSP90 and the metabolic regulator ADSL, substantial genetic differentiation was observed among populations, accounting for 8%, 13%, and 6% of the total variation, respectively. Analysis of the integrated three-gene dataset using PCoA and STRUCTURE initially suggested a panmictic state characterized by a single genetic cluster. By contrast, the more sensitive DAPC revealed a hierarchical population structure, which delineated three genetic groups: a primary cluster comprising the NOI1-F, NOI2-F, HM-C, and DT-C populations; a secondary cluster comprising the NOI3-F, TV-Con, and various Tre lineages; and a highly divergent third cluster containing the RJF.

Contrasting genetic diversity patterns in Vietnamese and Thai indigenous chickens, which reveal regional gene pool differentiation relative to their red junglefowl progenitors

The genetic structure of the integrated dataset, analyzed via PCoA, DAPC, and STRUCTURE (https://kukr.lib.ku.ac.th/kukr_es/dataset), demonstrated a clear tendency for Vietnamese indigenous chickens and RJF to diverge from the Thai indigenous breeds. Bayesian clustering across K values ranging from 1 to 25 revealed a hierarchical organization where Evanno’s ΔK identified K = 2 as the primary split, whereas the mean ln P(K) peaked at K = 14, suggesting a complex history of both broad ancestral sharing and recent regional diversification. At the K = 2 level, most of the Vietnamese populations and RJF exhibited gene pool patterns congruent with several Thai breeds, most notably the Samae Dam, Phuphan, and Pradu Hang Dam lineages, which points to a shared evolutionary origin among Southeast Asian poultry. As the clustering resolution increased to K = 14, a more nuanced structure emerged where the Hmong, Dong Tao, Tau Vang, and RJF groups displayed genetic affinities specifically with the Thai Samae Dam, whereas the Noi and Tre breeds shared patterns with a broader suite of Thai indigenous varieties. At the highest resolution level, K = 14, all Vietnamese populations and RJF exhibited a distinct gene pool that was divergent from their Thai counterparts. Within the Vietnamese cluster, two primary sub-groups were consistently identified: one comprising the Hmong, Dong Tao, Tau Vang, and RJF lineages, and another comprising the Noi and Tre populations, which highlights the unique genetic trajectory of these indigenous ecotypes in the Vietnamese landscape.

Comparative analysis of selection signatures, which delineates the divergent evolutionary trajectories of indigenous Vietnamese breeds relative to their wild red junglefowl ancestors

Evidence of purifying selection was identified across the HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL genes in Vietnamese indigenous chicken populations. Selective sweep analysis for HSP70 and HSP90 indicated the prevalence of balanced selection, which was reflected by He values that remained elevated relative to FIS across all populations. A similar trend of high He relative to FIS was observed for the ADSL gene in nearly all groups, including the RJF and most indigenous lineages, except for the TRE1-F population, which exhibited an FIS value exceeding He. Neutrality tests across the three loci yielded variable results, suggesting that most of these populations are evolving under predominantly neutral or balanced selection models. Tajima’s D values ranged from −1.237 to 1.393 for HSP70, −1.329 to 2.087 for HSP90, and 0.734 to 1.926 for ADSL. The NOI2-F population was the only group to exhibit a positive and significant Tajima’s D for the HSP90 gene, which provides evidence of balancing selection or a recent population contraction in this specific lineage. Conversely, Fu and Li’s D* and F* tests failed to produce significant results for any population concerning HSP70 and ADSL. This further corroborates a largely neutral evolutionary trajectory for these loci in the Vietnamese context.

The mean ω (dN/dS) value was 0.000 for HSP70, indicating a high degree of evolutionary stasis, and 1.091 for HSP90, with individual values spanning a broad range from 0.531 to 2.667 (Table 5, Table 6). The ω ratio for HSP90 could not be calculated for the TRE2-C, TRE3-F, TRE4-O, and G. gallus gallus (An Giang) populations because of the complete absence of synonymous and nonsynonymous substitutions in the analyzed exonic regions, which points to extreme sequence conservation in these lineages. Similarly, the ω ratio for the ADSL gene remained undetermined across all populations, primarily driven by the lack of observed genetic polymorphisms in the target sequence (Table 7). Codon-level selection analysis via MEME, which evaluated branch-specific episodic selection across HSP70 and ADSL, revealed a predominantly neutral evolutionary pattern across most sites, while HSP90 exhibited diversifying selection at codon 7 (Table S8). While FEL corroborated these neutral signals for HSP70, FUBAR identified pervasive purifying selection at codons 18, 33, 41, and 42 (FUBAR posterior probability >0.9) (Table S9), suggesting that these residues are under strong functional constraint. By contrast, no codons in the HSP90 region were under significant pervasive selection by either FEL or FUBAR. For the ADSL gene, FUBAR identified eight sites (codons 3, 32, 36, 38, 42, 45, 52, and 55) subject to negative selection and one site (codon 58) evolving under positive selection, which highlights a localized metabolic adaptation amidst the background of overall sequence stability (Table S10). In silico functional prediction of HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL residues, which highlights proteomic conservation in indigenous Vietnamese chicken populations

The 80-residue amino acid fragment of the HSP70 protein, which was deduced from the eight alleles identified in this study, exhibited exceptional conservation compared with diverse global reference breeds. Sequence identity reached 99.2% relative to the Yuehuang (AAP37964), Mazandaran (AYF57944), and Hubbard Pettersen (AY143693) lines, whereas 98.3% identity was maintained against the Lohmann (BBO36590) commercial strain (Fig. S6). This pronounced sequence uniformity underscores the presence of intense evolutionary constraints that preserve critical protein functions across both indigenous Southeast Asian lineages and globally distributed breeds. Bayesian phylogenetic reconstruction of these sequences yielded a monophyletic clade, indicating a shared evolutionary origin for the examined alleles. Moreover, this structural homogeneity facilitated the generation of a single representative 3D model that accurately depicted the predicted amino acid conformations across all identified variants.

The 25-residue amino acid fragment of the HSP90 protein, which was deduced from the 10 alleles identified in this study, exhibited 100% sequence identity with diverse global breeds, including the RJF and various commercial lines, while maintaining 99.7% identity with others, such as the Thailand Game fowl (Fig. S7). This exceptional conservation underscores the intense evolutionary constraints that maintain protein functionality across Thai and Vietnamese indigenous chickens, as well as international breeds. Maximum-likelihood reconstruction yielded a monophyletic clade, indicating a single evolutionary origin for the examined variants. Consequently, these sequences were consolidated into a single representative 3D structural model that accurately represented the predicted amino acid conformations for all identified alleles.

The 66-residue amino acid fragment of the ADSL protein, which was translated from the 10 alleles identified in Thai and Vietnamese indigenous chickens and RJF, showed extensive evolutionary conservation. Sequence identity ranged from 99.6% compared with Chinese indigenous breeds (AAT76521) to 98.0% relative to the Jiningbairi line (AAV38133), indicating that intense selective pressures maintain ADSL functional integrity across diverse regional ecotypes (Fig. S8). Structural analysis yielded three 3D models of the predicted amino acid conformations (Fig. S9). Ramachandran plot analysis of Model C revealed that 88.3% of the residues were positioned within the most favored regions, indicating a high degree of stereochemical quality for this structural variant. In contrast, Models A and B displayed lower scores, with 66.0% to 67.9% of residues in the favored regions, a variation that depended on the specific amino acid substitutions present in the deduced sequences of each allele. All structural models exhibited high homology with the reference ADSL protein (Accession: P21265), further validating the structural accuracy of the predicted variants (Fig.s S10 and S11).

Discussion

While prior investigations established the baseline genetic diversity of Vietnamese indigenous chickens, our research elevates this framework from demographic profiling to functional genetic characterization. Specifically, we present three key advances: (1) the identification of novel alleles endemic to Vietnamese lineages, substantially broadening the known avian genetic reservoir; (2) the detection of robust positive selection signatures within key adaptive and metabolic genes (HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL), uncovering the molecular basis for physiological resilience and meat quality; and (3) the integration of these populations into the transboundary context of the SCBP. By synthesizing these functional insights with comprehensive multi-locus data, this study provides a mechanistic understanding of regional evolutionary resilience.

Genetic diversity and the evolutionary mosaic of Southeast Asian poultry

The exceptional levels of allelic diversity and heterozygosity identified in Vietnamese indigenous chickens mirror the genetic profiles of their wild progenitor, the RJF. They underscore the status of these breeds as a vital “living gene bank” for global avian genetic resources. The mean He (0.620) and AR (4.156) identified across the 12 Vietnamese populations were remarkably congruent with recent benchmarks established by the SCBP in Thailand (Budi et al., 2023; 2024c). This molecular variability exceeds that reported in previous studies on Vietnamese poultry (Cuc et al., 2006; Pham et al., 2014; Vaiman et al., 1994), and suggests a shared regional history of high nuclear diversity maintained through traditional, low-input farming systems. The populations exhibit high genetic stability, and this is reflected by a broad adherence to the Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (Ho = 0.688, He = 0.686). This variation is primarily distributed within individuals, as evidenced by the AMOVA results, which attribute 91% of the total variation to intra-population differences, a genetic signature typical of extensive management and high gene flow (Frankham et al., 2002; Cheng, 2010). A key structural distinction emerges in the comparison with Thai populations: while Vietnamese populations exhibit high diversity between individuals and minimal differentiation among them, research on Thai indigenous chickens often reports significant differentiation among populations but notably low variation between individuals (Budi et al., 2024a). This contrast, which is most clearly delineated by the FST and FIS, highlights a fundamental divergence in the genetic structure across the Southeast Asian corridor. Thai breeds, such as Chee Fah and Fah Luang, often display positive FIS values (up to 0.131) and substantial genetic separation (up to 34% variation among populations), indicating a transition toward localized sub-structuring and inbreeding (Budi et al., 2023). These patterns are likely driven by intensive anthropogenic breeding practices and phenotypic sorting for traits such as black-bone traits, which necessitate closed-flock management, and geographic isolation in the Northern Thai highlands. This ultimately restricts gene flow and accelerates genetic drift, a differentiation that may be further reinforced by distinct evolutionary origins involving introgression, and has contributed to the current population structure (Budi et al., 2023). By contrast, the lower Nei’s genetic distances observed in Vietnam (0.112–0.742) compared with Thailand (0.882–0.934) underscore the differential impact of management regimes and geographical isolation on indigenous chicken populations across the region. Moreover, this contemporary richness stands in stark contrast to a vulnerable demographic history; specifically, the pervasive low M ratio values (M < 0.68) signal a historical, long-term demographic contraction that likely predates modern intensification (Garza and Williamson, 2001). The identification of recent bottlenecks in specific lineages, such as Tre chickens and wild populations, which exhibited significant mode shifts, further elevates the conservation priority of these populations. These findings reconcile high current diversity with historical vulnerability and emphasize the urgent need for targeted in situ and ex situ conservation strategies to safeguard these irreplaceable genetic assets in a warming world (Frankham et al., 2004; Groeneveld et al., 2010).

The genetic landscape of indigenous poultry across Vietnam and Thailand is defined by a complex interplay of shared ancestry and subsequent geographic divergence. This was elucidated through multivariate and clustering analysis, including PCoA, DAPC and Bayesian STRUCTURE modeling, consistently identifying a shared genetic background among Vietnamese indigenous chickens, Thai populations and RJF. This interconnectedness is most demonstrated at low clustering resolutions (K = 2), where Vietnamese populations do not entirely segregate from their Thai counterparts, a result that strongly indicates a close ancestral genetic relationship. Such persistent genomic overlap suggests a shared ancient gene pool and common domestication history within the Southeast Asian corridor (Do et al., 2019). Broader genetic assessments have confirmed that Vietnamese local populations maintain a closer relationship with RJF than with many European or native Chinese breeds, which positions this regional hub as a primary center of avian domestication (Berthouly et al., 2009; Cuc et al., 2008; Cuc and Weigend, 2019). The maternal gene pool further reflects this shared history, as both countries predominantly harbor mitochondrial haplogroups A, B, E, and J. These occupy the internal nodes of the phylogenetic tree and suggest an ancestral origin within this regional corridor before dispersion across Asia (Miao et al., 2013; Quan et al., 2020; Hata et al., 2021). This domestic affinity is continuously reinforced in Vietnam through ongoing genetic exchange, as evidenced by the H’mong breed in Ha Giang. This breed exhibits significant genomic introgression from regional RJF populations into domestic flocks (Berthouly et al., 2009). The presence of shared private alleles between wild populations and the domestic gene pool confirms that regional RJF lineages shape the domestic architecture through recurrent gene flow. These findings highlight the critical need to conserve local RJF populations, which serve as dynamic and irreplaceable genetic reservoirs for future domestic breed improvement (Berthouly-Salazar et al., 2010). Unlike the genetic isolation often observed in commercial lines (Muir et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2020), this wild-domestic continuum, which is a hallmark of the Southeast Asian avian landscape, provides a mechanism for maintaining high nuclear diversity and adaptive resilience.

Despite robust connectivity across this continuum, Thai RJF exhibited a distinct structural signature, as evidenced by their separate clustering in the STRUCTURE analysis. This separation suggests unique genetic structure that likely result from geographic isolation or more restricted gene flow than that in Vietnamese RJF. This divergence is corroborated by Nguyen-Phuc and Berres (2018), who identified the Annamite Mountain Range as a potent biogeographic barrier that limits dispersal and isolates coastal Vietnamese populations from the continental interior. These findings, which reconcile deep-time migratory patterns with contemporary genetic signatures, suggest that Southeast Asia is the primary cradle of avian domestication, where the interplay between natural barriers and anthropogenic dispersion continues to sculpt the region’s unique evolutionary mosaic (Zeder et al., 2006; Barber and Barber, 2018; Wang et al., 2021).

Evolutionary constraints at the HSP70 and HSP90 loci, which delineate the adaptive potential of Southeast Asian poultry

The analysis of HSP70 and HSP90 provided robust evidence of strong purifying and balancing selection, which putatively maintains the structural integrity of proteins amidst intensifying thermal stressors across the tropics. This widespread sharing of adaptive variants across Southeast Asian borders is evidenced by the prevalence of specific alleles, including GG-HSP70*VN8 and GG-HSP90*VN22–25, suggesting the successful dissemination of favored gene pools between Vietnam and Thailand (Budi et al., 2024a). Although numerous variants are common to both countries, a distinct cohort remains exclusively restricted to indigenous Thai populations, such as the HSP70 (TH1, TH2, and TH4) and HSP90 (TH1–20) series, which display a localized distribution likely driven by restricted gene flow or historical demographic events (Slatkin, 1987; Manel et al., 2003; Frankham et al., 2010). The primary evolutionary force shaping these critical genes is purifying selection, which acts intensely to preserve essential molecular chaperoning functions by purging deleterious mutations (Miyata et al., 1980). Molecular evidence robustly supports these selective constraints, as our FUBAR codon-level analysis identified multiple sites under negative selection in HSP70 (codons 18, 33, 41, and 42). This is hypothesized to help maintain cellular homeostasis under environmental stress. These codons are present in the GG-HSP70*TH-VN6, GG-HSP70*TH-VN7 and GG-HSP70*VN8 alleles, which harbor silent mutations that may be subject to positive selection and could probably affect mRNA stability, translation efficiency, or protein folding kinetics (Shabalina et al., 2006). This high degree of sequence conservation, which is a prerequisite for stable proteostatic function, provides a genomic foundation upon which more localized selective pressures can drive specialized adaptation. These alleles are promising targets for functional analysis to evaluate their roles in heat tolerance or heat stress perception in chickens (Shehata et al., 2020). While purification conserves the protein core, balanced selection actively maintains genetic variation to cope with environmental heterogeneities.

The HSP70 and HSP90 loci, which are paramount for thermotolerance (John et al., 2012; Al-Jaryan et al., 2023), showed signatures consistent with this dynamic. They included higher Ho than He and a significantly positive Tajima’s D in the NOI2-F population for HSP90 (Tajima, 1989). Unlike genome-wide diversity estimators such as the M ratio and overall FIS, which reflect the region's long-term demographic contraction and inbreeding history, these locus-specific deviations in Tajima's D suggest distinct evolutionary pressures acting directly on the HSP genes. However, interpreting these selection signatures is complicated by the region’s demographic history, where the consistent finding of low M ratio suggests that long-term demographic contraction has played a significant role in shaping contemporary genetic structure (Garza and Williamson, 2001). Specifically, the limitations of He, FIS, and the M ratio are heavily influenced by demographic history. Genome-wide demographic events, such as severe bottlenecks, produce reductions in diversity that closely mimic locus-specific selective sweeps, driving high false-positive rates (Jensen et al., 2005). The varying results across different genetic markers underscore the difficulty of disentangling historical bottlenecks from genuine selection (Murrell et al., 2015), which necessitates the multi-marker approach used in this study. While severe demographic bottlenecks can mimic the genetic signatures of selection (Murrell et al., 2015), the targeted contrast between our neutral multi-locus background and these functional loci provides putative evidence for soft sweeps acting on standing variation across the regional corridor (Hedrick, 2011; Echave and Wilke, 2017). Future investigations must explicitly decouple these evolutionary forces using genome-wide data and rigorous demographic modeling. The adaptive significance of these alleles is underscored by their phylogenetic clustering with breeds adapted to harsh environments, such as Tibetan and Mazandaran chickens (Najafi et al., 2019; Galal and Radwan, 2020; Tian et al., 2020). Specifically, Vietnamese alleles GG-HSP70*VN8, GG-HSP70*TH-VN3, GG-HSP70*TH-VN6, GG-HSP70*TH-VN7, and GG-HSP90*TH-VN18 and GG-HSP90*TH-VN25 group closely with these hardy breeds, suggesting they confer functional advantages for both heat resilience and high-altitude adaptation. This hypothesis is further supported by the CpG islands identified in the HSP90 promoter, which facilitate essential methylation-dependent regulation under multi-stress environmental conditions (Ummah et al., 2022). By reconciling high structural conservation with dynamic allelic variation, these findings provide a refined perspective on the evolutionary trajectories that allow Vietnamese poultry to thrive in warming climates.

Polymorphisms in the ADSL gene, which delineate the metabolic adaptation and flavor potential of Southeast Asian avian resources

Analysis of the ADSL gene revealed a complex landscape defined by metabolic conservation and putative localized adaptive divergence across Thai and Vietnamese poultry. As a critical regulator of the purine biosynthetic pathway, ADSL is essential for the accumulation of IMP, ADSL serves as the primary flavor precursor and a candidate indicator of meat quality in indigenous chicken breeds (Ye et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2015). The identification of five novel alleles unique to Vietnamese populations, alongside a suite of prevalent variants shared with Thai lineages (e.g., GG-ADSL*TH-VN6–9), suggests a shared regional metabolic heritage that has been preserved through the combined effects of natural and artificial selection (Budi et al., 2024b). By contrast, the restricted distribution of the GG-ADSL*TH1–5 alleles in Thai populations highlights localized differentiation, which may arise from specific environmental pressures or historical demographic events (Slatkin, 1987; Frankham et al., 2010). The primary evolutionary force acting on this locus is purifying selection, as corroborated by our FUBAR analysis, which identified eight sites under significant negative selection. This intense genomic constraint putatively supports the maintenance of core metabolic efficiency, a prerequisite for the physiological robustness required in traditional, low-input production systems (Miyata et al., 1980). However, a significant adaptive signature emerged at codon 58, which was identified as evolving under positive selection in the two alleles (GG-ADSL*TH-VN6 and GG-ADSL*TH-VN8). These nonsynonymous substitutions represent promising targets for functional analysis of meat quality and purine content, likely reflecting a localized selective response that refines purine metabolism to enhance specific flavor profiles, which may help distinguish Vietnamese ecotypes from other regional populations (Shukla and Tripathi, 2020). The functional impact of these mutations is robustly supported by structural modeling and Ramachandran analysis, which demonstrate that allele-specific amino acid substitutions directly influence the predicted 3D protein stability. While core residues remained within energetically favored regions, Model C exhibited a superior core residue value of 88.3%, whereas Models A and B showed a reduction to 66.0–67.9%, which provides a putative molecular basis for the potential varying flavor intensities observed among Southeast Asian breeds. By reconciling these selective signatures with the region’s demographic history, in which low M ratio values signal long-term demographic contraction (Garza and Williamson, 2001), our findings highlight ADSL as a high-priority candidate for MAS programs. Such programs, which aim to enhance the organoleptic properties of indigenous poultry while preserving their inherent adaptive resilience (Zhao et al., 2012), represent a critical genomic pathway for niche marketing of Southeast Asian avian genetic resources.

Strategic implications for transboundary conservation and marker-assisted selection (MAS)

The synthesis of neutral microsatellite markers and functional gene analysis provides a comprehensive framework that delineates the “big picture” of Southeast Asian chicken biodiversity. The hierarchical population structure identified in this study, which revealed deep ancestral ties at K = 2 alongside significant regional divergence at higher resolutions, is critical for establishing transboundary conservation strategies. By identifying specific markers in the HSP and ADSL genes putatively linked to climate resilience and superior meat quality, this study facilitates a vital transition toward precision breeding. Future breeding programs can use these functional markers in MAS to improve productivity without compromising the unique adaptive traits of these valuable genetic resources. Integrating these findings from Vietnamese breeds with the existing SCBP database establishes a robust regional network that is essential for the sustainable development of poultry production in a warming world. This collaborative genomic infrastructure enhances the accuracy of selection for economic traits and ensures that Southeast Asian indigenous chickens remain a resilient cornerstone of global food security.

Methodological considerations and future genomic directions

While this study provides critical insights into the adaptive signatures of Vietnamese poultry, certain limitations warrant consideration to guide future studies. A primary constraint is the reliance on a targeted candidate-gene approach, which is restricted to partial single-exon fragments from the HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL loci. The short sequence lengths of these fragments (e.g., 80 codons for HSP70 and 25 codons for HSP90) limit the statistical power of dN/dS estimations relative to full-length transcripts; thus, these selection signatures should be considered putative. Although these genes yielded significant signals of selection and structural conservation, this focus provides a limited view of the complex polygenic architecture that likely underpins multifaceted traits, such as heat resilience and meat quality. Moreover, using a single-locus analysis for each functional gene prevented the calculation of pairwise population heterozygosity for these markers, a statistical comparison that was only feasible for the multi-locus microsatellite dataset. Because classical population metrics (e.g., He​, FIS​, and M-ratio) primarily reflect demography and drift rather than adaptation, and our selection signatures lack phenotypic or transcriptomic corroboration, the proposed functional adaptations remain putative until validated by in vivo studies. To resolve the genomic landscape of Southeast Asian avian resources fully, future investigations warrant high-throughput whole-genome sequencing. Such an expansion, which would facilitate genome-wide association studies and the identification of selective sweeps across the entire genome, will be essential for validating the identified candidate markers. By integrating these future datasets into a broader regional framework, we can achieve a system-level understanding of adaptation that will enhance the capacity for precision breeding and transboundary conservation in a warming planet.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive genetic characterization of Vietnamese indigenous chickens and their associated RJF, successfully bridging the critical knowledge gap concerning the population structure and functional gene diversity of Southeast Asian avian resources. By integrating neutral microsatellite markers with functional candidate genes (HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL), we delineated the complex evolutionary trajectories that underpin both regional connectivity and localized differentiation at specific loci in these populations. Our findings support the hypothesis that Vietnamese indigenous chickens harbor distinct putative signatures of positive selection within HSP70, HSP90, and ADSL, which likely contribute to their superior physiological resilience and unique meat quality traits. Specifically, the identification of a significant polymorphism at ADSL codon 58 and the prevalence of heat-adaptive HSP alleles suggest that localized selective pressures may have sculpted these functional loci to meet the demands of the tropical environment. These functional results contrast with the neutral microsatellite data, which revealed high genetic diversity and significant regional clustering into three primary groups, filling the gap in our understanding of transboundary gene flow between Vietnamese and Thai poultry lineages. Moreover, this study reconciles the shared ancestral heritage of Southeast Asian chickens with the emergence of specialized regional ecotypes. While neutral markers reveal a common evolutionary mosaic with Thai breeds, the site-specific selection identified in the HSP and ADSL genes indicates that Vietnamese poultry has undergone unique adaptive refinement. These findings can help transition toward precision breeding via MAS programs, which can enhance productivity and climate resilience without compromising the inherent genetic integrity of these species. This study provides a robust scientific foundation for the sustainable management of Southeast Asian avian biodiversity. By integrating these findings with the SCBP, we provide a vital regional perspective that will empower global conservation efforts and ensure the long-term food security of indigenous poultry systems in a warming environment.
