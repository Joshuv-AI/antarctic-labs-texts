Dynamic J-Wave Pattern on ECG
Authors: Sofia Bezati, Effie Polyzogopoulou, Aristeidis Tsakanikas, John Parissis, Vasiliki Bistola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An 83-year-old man was transferred to the emergency department due to an altered level of consciousness and hypothermia. In this case report, we discuss the pathophysiologic basis, evolution, and differential diagnosis of J-wave pattern on the electrocardiogram.

An 83-year-old man with a history of coronary artery disease, arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, and hypothyroidism was transferred to the emergency department after he was found on the ground with an altered level of consciousness. At presentation, he had a normal respiratory rate (16 ipm), oxygen saturation of 95% on room air, low arterial blood pressure (88/43 mm Hg), severe bradycardia (32 beats/min), and low body temperature of 26 °C. Physical examination revealed Glasgow coma scale 10/15, rales on lung auscultation, and minimal injuries on upper and lower extremities. Arterial blood gas analysis showed mixed metabolic and respiratory acidosis (pH 7.22, pCO2 55 mm Hg, pO2 59 mm Hg, lactate 0.7 mmol/L, HCO3 22.5 mEq/L).Take-Home Messages•Hypothermia is associated with prolonged atrioventricular conduction, delayed ventricular depolarization, and repolarization inhomogeneity.•J-wave or Osborne wave is the most typical electrocardiographic finding, signals potential for life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, and is reversed last after normalization of PR and QT intervals.

Evolution of J-wave Pattern in Hypothermia

(A) Electrocardiogram (ECG) upon admission, core body temperature 26 °C, (B) ECG 20 hours after admission core body temperature 35 °C, (C) ECG 20 hours after admission core body temperature 36 °C.

Correct answer C.

ECG shows sinus bradycardia (32 beats/min), prolongation of PR (240 ms), and QTc (568 ms) intervals and wide QRS complex (160 ms) due to an extra deflection at the end of QRS, known as J-wave or Osborn wave (Figure 1A, arrows). J-wave is observed after the J-point, that is the junction of QRS and ST-T segment, denoting the end of depolarization and beginning of repolarization.1

The patient underwent a brain computed tomography, which showed no pathologic findings. Laboratory analysis revealed acute renal injury, normal serum calcium levels, and slightly increased but nonfluctuating high-sensitive troponin-T levels (51.6 pg/mL). Point-of-care cardiac ultrasound showed moderately depressed cardiac contractility without regional wall motion abnormalities. Therefore, hypothermia was considered the most appropriate diagnostic explanation of J-waves on ECG. After rewarming, J-waves remained on the ECG even after the core body temperature had reached 35 °C and although bradycardia (43 beats/min), PR (220 ms), and QTc (483 ms) had improved (Figure 1B, arrows). J-wave disappeared 48 hours after admission, while atrial fibrillation emerged (Figure 1C).

During hypothermia, as the core temperature drops below 32 °C, enhanced vagal tone suppresses the sinus node resulting in bradycardia and decreased conduction (prolonged PR). Alterations in ion channels have been described, causing delayed depolarization seen as wide QRS (phase 0, Na+ channels), J-waves (phase 1, prominent Ito current in epicardium), and delayed repolarization, which manifest as QT prolongation (phase 2, K+ channels). Cardiac contractility may be depressed.2 Pathophysiology of the J-wave involves dispersion of repolarization across the ventricular wall, where epicardial cells display an increase in width and amplitude of the notch of the action potential due to a more prominent Ito current during phase 1, whereas endocardial cells remain unaffected.3 J-wave should raise a concern of impeding life-threatening arrhythmias including ventricular fibrillation.

Although first described by John Osborn in hypothermic dogs and commonly documented in patients with hypothermia, J-wave is not pathognomonic of this condition. Hypercalcemia, cerebral hemorrhage, acute myocardial infarction, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, early repolarization, and Brugada syndromes may manifest with J-waves.1 Thus, thorough evaluation is necessary to unmask the underlying cause.
