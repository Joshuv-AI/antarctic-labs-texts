Association between psychological resilience and psychological distress in Chinese psychiatric nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zhaoqin Wang, Fengying Zu, Yanhong Zhang, Ying Gao, Chao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychiatric nurses in China face high psychological and occupational strain. Understanding the factors influencing their mental health is essential. This study aimed to determine the positive rate of psychological distress among Chinese psychiatric nurses and to examine the associations of psychological resilience and other psychosocial factors with psychological distress.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2024 to January 2025. Using an online survey, 436 psychiatric nurses from six hospitals in Jiangsu Province, China, were recruited. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12), and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC). Chi-square tests, Spearman correlation, and multivariate logistic regression were employed for analysis.ResultsOf 436 nurses invited, 412 provided valid responses (response rate: 94.5%). The positive screening rate for psychological distress (GHQ-12 score ≥ 3) was 38.38%. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that among Chinese psychiatric nurses, higher psychological resilience (OR = 0.948, 95% CI: 0.931–0.965, p < 0.001) was associated with lower odds of psychological distress. In contrast, more frequent weekly night shifts (≥ 3 vs. ≤ 2; OR = 2.452, 95% CI: 1.212–4.959, p = 0.013) and poorer self-rated health status (per level increase; OR = 1.711, 95% CI: 1.274–2.299, p < 0.001) were associated with a greater odds of psychological distress.ConclusionPsychological resilience, night shift frequency, and self-rated health status are significantly associated with psychological distress among Chinese psychiatric nurses. Interventions aimed at building resilience, optimizing work schedules, and promoting health may help protect their mental well-being.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04716-7.

Introduction

Psychiatric nursing is widely regarded as one of the most demanding specialties in healthcare. Nurses in this field are consistently exposed to intense and unique job stressors, such as managing patient aggression, witnessing traumatic events like patient suicide, and coping with the societal stigma attached to mental illness [1, 2]. This chronically high-stress work environment significantly increases their risk of psychological distress—a state of emotional suffering that includes symptoms of anxiety, depression, and general unease, and is a known precursor to more serious mental health conditions [3–5]. The psychological well-being of these nurses is not only a personal issue but also critical for patient safety and care quality. High levels of distress are linked to increased medical errors, reduced empathy, and higher staff turnover, which ultimately threaten the stability of healthcare services [6].

In China, nurses across all specialties face considerable job pressures, including heavy workloads, shift work, and emotional demands, which contribute to widespread mental health challenges [7, 8]. Recent studies report a concerning prevalence of psychological distress, sleep problems, and burnout among Chinese nurses [9, 10].​ This broader national context highlights a serious public health concern and suggests that psychiatric nurses, who work in an even more stressful clinical environment, may be at particularly high risk.

In response to these occupational hazards, it is essential to identify modifiable protective factors. The Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory​ offers a valuable theoretical framework for this purpose [11]. According to COR theory, individuals strive to obtain, retain, and protect valued resources (such as energy, time, and positive mood). Stress arises when these resources are threatened, lost, or not adequately recovered. Within this model, psychological resilience—defined as the capacity to adapt positively and maintain or recover mental health in the face of adversity [12]—can be seen as a key personal resource. It is thought to buffer the impact of stress by helping to conserve resources, foster new ones, and promote effective coping, thereby potentially stopping the “loss spiral” that leads to distress [13]. While resilience has been identified as a protective factor in various nursing groups [14–17], research focusing specifically on its relationship with psychological distress among Chinese psychiatric nurses, and especially studies that use a theoretical framework like COR theory to explain this relationship, remains limited and inconclusive. Much of the existing literature lacks a strong theoretical basis or does not fully consider how resilience is associated with other important work-related and health factors in this population.

To fill this gap, the present study aimed to investigate the positive rate of psychological distress among Chinese psychiatric nurses and to further explore the influence of psychological resilience and key social factors on their psychological distress, using the COR theory as a guiding framework. We hypothesize that higher levels of psychological resilience will be associated with better mental health outcomes. We further propose that factors such as night shift frequency and current health status will be independently associated with psychological distress in psychiatric nurses.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was designed using a convenience sampling method to recruit participants from six psychiatric hospitals in Jiangsu province, China. This method was selected due to its practicality and feasibility in reaching the target population within the designated data collection period, which spanned from October 2024 to January 2025. In the present study, questionnaires were distributed via Sojump, an online survey platform. Specifically, questionnaire links were sent through the nursing departments of the participating hospitals to head nurses, who then forwarded the links to nursing staff in their respective departments.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated based on the formula for cross-sectional studies:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N=({Z}^{2}\times\:P\left(1-P\right))/{\delta\:}^{2}$$\end{document}

Here, N represents the minimum required sample size, Z is the Z-score corresponding to the chosen confidence level, P is the estimated prevalence, and δ is the margin of error. With a confidence level of 95% (Z = 1.96), an estimated prevalence (P) of psychological distress issues of 0.23 based on a prior study [9], and a margin of error (δ) of 0.05, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 273. Accounting for a potential non-response rate of 20%, we aimed to recruit at least 342 participants. Furthermore, we used a common rule of thumb of 10 events per predictor variable to estimate the sample size for the logistic regression. With 6 predictor variables planned for the model, at least 60 positive events were required. Based on an expected prevalence of psychological distress of 0.23, the minimum sample size was calculated as 60 / 0.23 ≈ 261 participants. To account for potential invalid responses, the target sample size was increased by 20%. Consequently, the study aimed to recruit a minimum of 313 participants to ensure sufficient statistical power for detecting significant associations in the final regression model. After careful checking, a total of 412 valid questionnaires were collected from 436 psychiatric nurses in the present study, yielding an effective response rate of 94.5%. The final sample size (N = 412) exceeded the minimum requirement and provided adequate statistical power for the planned multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Participants

The eligibility criteria for participants included: (1) possession of a national nurse practitioner qualification certificate; (2) employment as an in-service psychiatric nurse at the hospital for at least one year; (3) self-reported absence of any history of drug abuse, mental disorders, or severe physical illnesses. Exclusion criteria included nursing personnel who were undertaking internships or advanced training. These individuals were excluded because their work experience, job responsibilities, and psychological stressors may differ substantially from those of full-time, in-service psychiatric nurses, potentially confounding the association between resilience and psychological distress in the target population. This study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Institutional Ethical Committee for Clinical Research of Nanjing Brain Hospital (Approval No. 2021-KY112-01). The protocol for the 2024–2025 data collection was covered under an amendment to this original approval. All participants were given written informed consent, and the data collected for this study have been ensured to be kept confidential.

Measurements

General Information Questionnaire:​ A self-designed questionnaire was used to gather demographic and occupational data, including gender, age, years of work experience, marital status, number of children, educational background, professional title, employment type, weekly night shift frequency, weekly exercise frequency, ways of confiding when facing distress, and self-assessment of current health status.

General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12):​ The GHQ-12 was originally developed by Goldberg in 1978 [18]. The Chinese Version of GHQ-12 [19], which has a good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha, 0.87), was employed to screen for general psychological distress. This instrument consists of 12 self-rated items, each offering four response options (A, B, C, D). A bimodal scoring method (0-0-1-1) was applied, where responses A and B were assigned 0 points, and responses C and D were assigned 1 point. The GHQ-12 score was dichotomized using the established cutoff of ≥ 3 to identify probable cases of psychological distress. This threshold is widely validated in Chinese and international populations, offering an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity for screening purposes [20–23]. While dichotomization may reduce statistical power, it aligns with the instrument’s common use in clinical and public health screening contexts and facilitates interpretation using odds ratios in logistic regression. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for the entire scale was 0.894.

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC):​ CD-RISC is a widely used self-report instrument designed to quantify psychological resilience [24]. The original standard version of the scale consists of 25 items. Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“always”). The total score is calculated by summing all items, ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting greater levels of psychological resilience. The Chinese version of the CD-RISC (C-CD-RISC) has been demonstrated to have good reliability and validity in Chinese populations [25]. The structure of the C-CD-RISC is conceptualized around three core factors, including Strength (13 items), Tenacity (8 items), and Optimism (4 items) [26]. Strength​ is a sense of personal competence, trust in one’s instincts, and control over one’s life. It involves self-efficacy, adaptability, and the ability to tolerate distress. Tenacity​ reflects determination, perseverance, and the ability to recover from adversity. It includes resilience, adaptability to change, and effective stress coping. Optimism​ is a positive attitude toward life and the future, even in difficulty. It involves focusing on strengths and maintaining hope. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for the entire scale was 0.859.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were implemented using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. For normally distributed continuous data, means and standard deviations were reported; for non-normally distributed data, medians and interquartile ranges were reported. The normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The chi-squared test for categorical variables was performed as a univariate analysis to explore potential associations between sociodemographic factors and psychological distress status, identifying candidate variables for the subsequent multivariate model. The multicollinearity between categorical variables was assessed using Cramér’s V coefficient, with values ranging from 0 to 1, where lower values indicate weaker associations. Spearman correlation analysis and logistic regression analysis were used to examine the influence of psychological and social factors on psychological distress. Given the exploratory nature of this study and sample size considerations, interaction effects between variables were not tested. The significance level was set to p < 0.05.

This revealed​ that nearly 40% of Chinese psychiatric nurses experience notable psychological distress issues. For further analysis, the participants were stratified by psychological distress status (based on the scores of GHQ-12). The distribution of demographic and psychosocial characteristics within each group was reported as percentages (see Table 2). Univariate analysis using the chi-squared test showed that professional title​ (χ²=9.547, p = 0.036), weekly night shift frequency​ (χ²=6.462, p = 0.011), weekly exercise frequency​ (χ²=14.710, p = 0.005), current health status​ (χ²=52.368, p < 0.01), and sleep duration​ (χ²=4.375, p = 0.043) were associated with psychological distress status in Chinese psychiatric nurses.

Logistic regression analysis

This study employed multivariate logistic regression to further investigate factors associated with psychological distress status, with the presence or absence of psychological distress issues as the dependent variable. Independent variables entered into the multivariate logistic regression model included psychological resilience (CD-RISC total score) and variables that showed significant associations with psychological distress in prior chi-square analyses: professional title, weekly night shift frequency, weekly exercise frequency, sleep duration, and current health status. The coding schemes for all independent variables are detailed in Table S2. The Cramér’s V coefficient among the categorical variables included in the logistic regression model, as well as the eta values between the categorical variables and the continuous variables, were reported in Table S3. All intercorrelations among the categorical variables were low (Cramér’s V < 0.2), and the effect sizes (η²) between the categorical and continuous variables were also modest (< 0.15), indicating that multicollinearity is not a substantial concern in this dataset. The results of multivariate logistic regression revealed that, after controlling for other variables, psychological resilience (β=-0.053, OR = 0.948, 95% CI: 0.931–0.965, p < 0.001), weekly night shift frequency (β = 0.897, OR = 2.452, 95% CI: 1.212–4.959, p = 0.013), current health status (β = 0.537, OR = 1.711, 95% CI: 1.274–2.299, p < 0.001) were significantly correlated with the psychological distress of Chinese psychiatric nurses (see Table 4). The results indicated that, with other variables held constant, for each one-point increase​ in the total score of CD-RISC, the odds of psychological distress decrease​ by 5.2%; nurses who work ≥ 3 night shifts per week​ have odds of psychological distress that are 2.452 times higher​ than nurses who work ≤ 2 night shifts per week; for each one-level increase​ in reported current health status, the odds​ of psychological distress increase by a factor of 1.711. The goodness-of-fit of the final logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, which indicated a satisfactory fit (χ² = 10.56, p = 0.228).

Discussion

In the present study, we primarily investigated the positive rate of psychological distress among Chinese psychiatric nurses and further revealed the associations of psychological resilience and key social factors with their psychological distress. Specifically, the main findings are as follows: (1) GHQ-12 revealed​ that nearly 40% of Chinese psychiatric nurses experience notable psychological distress issues; (2) factors such as psychological resilience (total score of CD-RISC), excessive frequency of weekly night shifts and poorer health status​ were associated with psychological distress status in Chinese psychiatric nurses.

In the present study, 38.38% of participating psychiatric nurses had problems with psychological distress. The rate is higher than that in the general population of Chinese nurses reported in a recent study (22.91%) [9]. Previous studies similarly revealed a relatively high rate of mental health among nurse practitioners in other countries [27–30]. This elevated prevalence likely stems from the unique and intense stressors of psychiatric settings, such as managing patient aggression, exposure to trauma, and workplace stigma. It confirms that psychiatric nursing is a high-risk occupation for mental health problems and warrants focused organizational and public health strategies.

Our finding that psychological resilience is associated with lower odds of psychological distress is consistent with broader nursing research. Previous studies have shown that a high level of psychological resilience can significantly alleviate burnout, anxiety, and depressive symptoms among nurses, while also enhancing their work engagement and job satisfaction [31–33]. Importantly, this study elucidates the protective role of psychological resilience within the specific context of Chinese psychiatric nursing. Psychological resilience, viewed as a dynamic adaptive capacity, may empower nurses to navigate the distinctive challenges of their work more effectively. For example, when confronted with patient aggression, resilient nurses might be more skilled at using de-escalation techniques and cognitively reframing incidents as part of their professional role, thereby reducing personal emotional toll. Similarly, in coping with the stigma attached to mental healthcare, psychological resilience may enhance​ professional identity and help nurses derive a sense of purpose, buffering against demoralization. This indicates that resilience-building interventions designed specifically for the realities of psychiatric nursing could be highly beneficial. Although resilience is generally associated with better mental health, the strength of this association can vary depending on the specific stressors and resources present in different work environments [34]. These variations highlight the context-dependent nature of these relationships and underscore the need for localized research and interventions [35].

This study also found that weekly night shift frequency and poorer self-rated health status were independent risk factors for psychological distress. A 24-year cohort study in China indicated that long-term engagement in night shift work is significantly associated with weight gain, increased risk of metabolic abnormalities, and reduced likelihood of healthy survival beyond the age of 70 among nurses, suggesting that night shifts may negatively impact long-term health by disrupting metabolic processes [36]. Additionally, night shift burden was reported to be closely linked to sleep issues and serves as a significant risk factor for depression and anxiety [37, 38]. Notably, psychological resilience was reported to protect against the negative impacts of night shifts [38–40]. The COR theory offers a coherent framework for understanding how these factors, together with psychological resilience, influence psychological distress. According to COR theory, night shift work acts as a chronic stressor that depletes resources, including physiological (circadian disruption), cognitive (fatigue), and emotional (impaired work-life balance) reserves. Concurrently, poor self-rated health reflects a state of diminished personal resources. Within this model, psychological resilience functions as an active resource management system. Highly resilient individuals are posited to be more proficient at: (1) Protecting existing resources​ through strategies like setting boundaries after work and using positive reappraisal to mitigate perceived threats; and (2) Gaining new resources​ by seeking support, finding meaning in challenges, and building self-efficacy from successful coping [13, 41]. Consequently, when facing the resource drain of night shifts, a nurse with high resilience and good health (a substantial resource pool) is better equipped to cope and may even experience post-traumatic growth. In contrast, a nurse with low resilience and poor health (a limited resource pool) is at high risk of entering a loss spiral, where initial resource depletion impairs the ability to prevent further losses, ultimately leading to psychological distress. This theoretical integration provides a mechanism that explains the interplay between our key variables, moving beyond simple association. While the COR theory provides a plausible framework for interpreting our findings, this proposed mechanism requires empirical validation in future studies.

Enhancing psychological resilience helps nurses better manage the persistent pressures arising from patient care complexity, workload, and shift work, thereby reducing the turnover rate [42]. Multi-level measures can be implemented to enhance the psychological resilience of psychiatric nurses. Previous study demonstrated that positive mental health and work stress significantly predict resilience, which suggests the important role of organizational and individual factors in fostering resilience [17]. At the organizational level, healthcare institutions should focus on creating a supportive work environment, optimizing scheduling systems, especially reducing consecutive night shifts, and providing structured training on stress management and resilience building. At the individual level, strategies like encouraging nurses to engage in regular physical exercise, learning self-regulation techniques, such as mindfulness-based stress reduction, and building strong social support networks have proven to be effective [43].

Several limitations should be considered in this study. First, as a cross-sectional design was employed, the findings only reflect associations at a specific time point and cannot establish causal relationships between psychological resilience and psychological distress. Second, the use of convenience sampling from hospitals within a single province may limit the generalizability of the results to psychiatric nurses in other regions or healthcare settings. Third, although self-reported questionnaires are practical for data collection, they may be subject to social desirability bias or subjective misinterpretation, potentially affecting the accuracy of responses. Fourth, our sample was predominantly female (90.78%), a distribution that reflects the actual gender composition of the nursing workforce in China, particularly in psychiatric settings. However, the underrepresentation limits gender-specific analyses and may affect the generalizability of findings to male psychiatric nurses, who may face distinct occupational stressors or resilience patterns. Future studies should consider oversampling male nurses to support meaningful gender-based comparisons. Furthermore, other potential influencing factors, such as specific workload metrics, organizational culture, and supervisory support, were not examined and should be considered in future research to provide a more comprehensive understanding. Future research should employ longitudinal or interventional designs to establish temporal precedence and causality, utilize randomized or stratified sampling to enhance representativeness, and consider incorporating objective measures to complement self-report data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed a notable prevalence of psychological distress (38.38%) among psychiatric nurses in Jiangsu, China. We identified psychological resilience as a significant protective factor, while frequent night shifts and poorer self-rated health were risk factors. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive strategies in psychiatric nursing management, including implementing resilience-building interventions (e.g., mindfulness training), optimizing shift schedules to reduce night shift frequency, and establishing proactive mental and physical health monitoring systems to safeguard the well-being of psychiatric nurses.
