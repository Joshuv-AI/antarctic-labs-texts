A multicenter single‐arm objective performance criteria trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of a dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device for severe cerebral venous sinus thrombosis
Authors: Shadamu Yusuying, Yan Wu, Ming Li, Jiangang Duan, Jian Chen, Yujiao Chen, Chen Zhou, Hetao Bian, Chuanhui Li, Chuanjie Wu, Ran Meng, Xunming Ji
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimApproximately 21% of patients with cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) are refractory to anticoagulation treatment and face poor prognosis. These patients may benefit from endovascular therapy. Although numerous studies have reported promising results with endovascular therapy, it remains challenging to obtain convincing evidence of its efficacy and safety due to the unique challenges posed by CVST, such as larger vessel diameters, distinct pathophysiology, and substantial clot burden. Therefore, dedicated endovascular therapy tools should be developed specifically for CVST. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device in patients with acute or subacute CVST.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter, single‐arm trial utilized an objective performance criterion design. Enrolled patients received treatment with a dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device to recanalize occluded sinuses or veins. The primary objective was to compare the immediate postoperative recanalization rate with a preset target value to determine whether the device met the clinical application needs. The trial plans to enroll 146 patients from 11 tertiary medical centers in China.DiscussionThis trial is expected to fill a critical gap in endovascular treatments for CVST and support the development of reliable, dedicated thrombectomy devices for future studies.Keywords: anticoagulation, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, endovascular treatment, thrombectomy devices, trial

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) has an estimated incidence of 3–5 cases per million people per year, primarily affecting young adults. 1 Patients with identified risk factors for CVST are at a high risk of developing thrombosis in the cerebral venous system due to systemic or local imbalances in the prothrombotic and thrombolytic processes. 2 Thrombus formation and progression in the cerebral dural sinuses or veins obstruct cerebral vein and cerebrospinal fluid circulation, ultimately leading to cerebral edema, cerebral ischemia, and intracranial hypertension as the cerebral venous system decompensates. 3 Increasing intracranial hypertension can result in papilledema in 28.0%–67.5% of patients with CVST, potentially causing decreased visual acuity or even acutely threatened vision. Cerebral edema or hemorrhagic infarction, resulting from occluded cerebral veins, may lead to seizures, severe neurological deficits, and death. Recanalization of thrombosed dural sinuses or veins may prevent permanent damage with most patients achieving favorable outcomes. 4 , 5 , 6

Guidelines from the United States and Europe recommend anticoagulation with heparin as the primary treatment for CVST, even in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) at baseline. 7 , 8 In the International Study on Cerebral Vein and Dural Sinus Thrombosis, a multinational (21 countries), multicenter (89 centers) prospective observational study, enrolled 624 patients with a 6‐month follow‐up and revealed that approximately 21% of patients had unsatisfactory outcomes. 5 Characteristics of cases refractory to heparin include extensive dural sinus thrombosis, deep venous system involvement, and a large thrombus burden. 9 Additionally, early evidence has shown that the rate of thrombus organization increases over time, rendering anticoagulation ineffective for chronic thrombi. 10 From a practical clinical standpoint, anticoagulation therapy is often inadequate for patients with acute‐onset, severe conditions due to the slow response of heparin, making rapid recanalization difficult. This limitation has led to the initiation of endovascular treatment (EVT).

EVT was first used in patients with widespread dural sinus thrombosis or progressive worsening of clinical symptoms, typically associated with severe CVST. 11 , 12 This severe condition, recognized as the decompensated phase of CVST, is unresponsive to conservative management, necessitating EVT to rapidly recanalize occluded veins or sinuses and restore cerebral venous blood flow. Numerous studies have demonstrated that EVT can effectively and safely manage CVST, achieving significant and rapid early recanalization and improving clinical outcomes. 13 , 14 , 15 EVT for CVST involves catheter‐based pharmacological thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, or a combination of both, each with its own characteristics.

Catheter‐based pharmacological thrombolysis, often using alteplase or recombinant tissue plasminogen activator, poses risks for patients with ICH during or after the procedure and may aggravate pre‐existing bleeding events. 16 , 17 Additionally, pharmacological thrombolysis may be ineffective against firm thrombi, and lysing large thrombi in the venous sinuses may take too long to prevent irreversible brain injury in severe cases. 18 In a study by Soleau et al., 19 of 10 patients who underwent chemical thrombolysis, 30% experienced hemorrhagic complications, and 60% showed clinical improvement. In contrast, patients who underwent mechanical thrombectomy demonstrated a lower hemorrhagic complication rate, with most (88%) recovering well. 19 Given the high recanalization rate and low risk of bleeding, many clinicians now select mechanical thrombectomy as the first‐line treatment for CVST. 14

The thrombolysis or anticoagulation for cerebral venous thrombosis (TO‐ACT) trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of EVT in patients with severe CVST but yielded negative results. 20 The lack of a strict EVT strategy and suboptimal device utilization for achieving recanalization were major contributors to these negative outcomes. The AngioJet device (Boston Scientific, Malborough, MA, USA), the most used device in the TO‐ACT trial, evacuates thrombi using high‐velocity saline jets and was initially designed for the peripheral vasculature. 21 However, studies have found that the use of AngioJet is associated with a lower likelihood of good outcomes and a higher risk of complications due to its extensive profile and the stiffness of the delivery system. 22 These deficits have driven clinicians to utilize new‐generation thrombectomy devices designed for arterial stroke. Data on these new devices showed favorable outcomes in patients treated with the Penumbra system (Penumbra Inc., Alameda, California, USA) and Solitaire FR device (eV3/Covidien, Irvine, CA, USA). 23 , 24 However, these devices are also not optimal for CVST due to size mismatches; typical aspiration catheters measure 2–3 mm in diameter, and the largest available stent retriever measures 6 mm, whereas the superior sagittal sinus has an average diameter of 10 mm in the occipital region. 25 , 26 As a result, complete thrombus removal is impossible with the available aspiration catheters and stent retrievers. 27

Due to the unique pathophysiology of CVST, intracranial venous clots often present with a large burden and high adhesion, requiring disruption and subsequent removal, rather than the approach used for arterial thrombectomy. 24 , 28 Given the limitations of repurposing peripheral or arterial thrombectomy devices, the size mismatches between available tools and cerebral veins or sinuses, and the unique pathophysiology of CVST, there is a clear need for a dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device.

To better remove thrombi, achieve rapid and sufficient recanalization, and ultimately improve clinical outcomes for patients with severe CVST, our team developed a dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device. We validated this device in a CVST animal model and conducted a prospective, single‐center, open‐label, randomized, controlled, noninferiority clinical trial (NCT05291585, unpublished data). This study further validated the device in a prospective multicenter cohort study.

This trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device in patients with severe CVST who are resistant to anticoagulation, using a prospective, multicenter, single‐arm objective performance criteria (OPC) design. The primary objective is to compare the immediate postoperative recanalization rate with a preset target value to determine whether the device meets clinical application needs.

METHOD

Design

This trial used a single‐arm OPC design without a contemporaneous control group. The process for determining the preset target value of the OPC design follows guidance from the Food and Drug Administration Center for Devices and Radiological Health. 29 , 30 This trial was conducted at 11 academic medical centers in China: Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing; Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing; China–Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University, Changchun; First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University, Shenyang; Inner Mongolia Autonomous People's Hospital, Hohhot; First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, Urumchi; Henan Provincial People's Hospital, Zhengzhou; Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan; Jiangxi Provincial People's Hospital, Nanchang; The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, Haerbin; and The First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University, Taiyuan.

All participating centers had substantial experience in diagnosing and treating CVST, and the corresponding clinicians were trained in programmatic exercises and equipment use. All centers uniformly utilized the dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device for enrolled patients with CVST, adhering to the inclusion and exclusion criteria with a 1‐month follow‐up, and recorded the pertinent data. The trial was approved by the medical and health ethics committees of all centers and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/; No. ChiCTR2200057621).

Clinical pathway

Suspected patients followed guidelines recommending magnetic resonance venography (MRV) or computed tomography venography (CTV) for CVST diagnosis. The management and monitoring of patients with CVST complied with established guidelines. 7 All patients diagnosed with CVST received a therapeutic dose of heparin as primary treatment, regardless of the presence of ICH at baseline. Routine blood work, screening for potential prothrombotic conditions, and d‐dimer assays were conducted for all patients. During anticoagulation treatment, two clinicians (each with at least 10 years of work experience in cerebrovascular disease) screened patients refractory to anticoagulation therapy who might benefit from EVT based on their condition and the reinspection of MRV/CTV (Figure 1).

Patient population

The screening criteria for eligible patients are as follows. The main inclusion criteria were: patients aged 18–70 years, in the acute or subacute stages based on clinical and radiographic confirmation (time from onset to diagnosis ≤28 days), and an expected survival period of >1 year. The main exclusion criteria were isolated cortical thrombosis or cavernous sinus thrombosis, a preonset modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score >2, and an imminent risk of transtentorial herniation. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Table 1. Final patient enrollment was contingent upon review by at least two chief physicians. All eligible patients were thoroughly informed, and only those who signed the consent form were included in the trial. Ineligible patients or those who declined participation returned to standard clinical care, active surveillance, or medical treatment.

Baseline variables for all enrolled patients were collected and included general information such as sex, age, and disease course; past medical history, including diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, and any history of stroke; clinical presentation, such as headache, seizures, and focal neurological deficits; causes or risk factors, including genetic prothrombotic conditions, acquired prothrombotic states, infections, and inflammatory diseases; mRS scores recorded preoperatively and at discharge; and imaging information, including the location and size of the ICH and the site of occlusion.

Interventional management

Characteristics of the dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device

The dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device is made from unique materials with excellent vascular compliance, supporting force, and biocompatibility. It can pass through tortuous venous sinus vessels to reach the thrombus along the microguidewire and is less likely to irritate the blood vessels. The device features a nickel‐titanium memory alloy stent specially designed for thrombectomy, providing excellent fatigue resistance. The stent mesh has a dense net‐woven structure, with proximal and distal ends that can capture thrombi during thrombectomy, preventing embolic events. Additionally, the device has a unique push structure that adjusts the stent's diameter, cutting and breaking the thrombus radially and axially, allowing it to remove large thrombi with high efficiency. The device can be paired with various negative‐pressure suction devices to further reduce operation time. The structure and composition of the device are shown in Figure 2.

Thrombectomy mechanism of the device

The dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device is delivered to the distal end of the thrombus. The retrieval stent is then opened by controlling the handle to match the diameter of the occlusive vein or sinus. The stent cuts and breaks the thrombus radially and axially, exfoliating thrombi adhered to the vascular wall, while negative‐pressure suction devices are used remove the exfoliated thrombi. This process rapidly reopens the occluded cerebral venous system and restores normal blood flow.

Interventional operation protocol

The Interventional Expert Committee approved the detailed and complete surgical protocol for each patient. The implementation of EVT for CVST is similar to that for arterial stroke. First, the neurosurgeon confirms the existence and location of dual sinus or vein thrombi using cerebral angiography via the internal jugular or femoral vein. Under the guidance of a guidewire, a catheter is introduced to the distal end of the thrombosed sinus, followed by stent thrombectomy, aspiration thrombectomy, or a combination of both to remove the thrombi.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary efficacy outcomes included: (a) Manipulation time: Time from the start of thrombectomy to the end of thrombus removal. (b) mRS score: mRS scores were recorded preoperatively, before discharge (within 14 days after surgery/before discharge), and 1 month after surgery. Prognosis was classified as favorable (mRS score: 0–2) or poor (mRS score: 3–6). (c) National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score: Scores were recorded preoperatively, before discharge (within 14 days after surgery or before discharge), and 1 month after surgery. (d) Glasgow Coma Scale score: Scores were recorded preoperatively, before discharge (within 14 days after surgery or before discharge), and 1 month after surgery. (e) Thrombosis recurrence: Magnetic resonance imaging (or computed tomography) combined with MRV (or CTV) was performed before discharge (within 14 days after surgery or before discharge) to assess the occlusion state of the dural sinus. (f) Cerebrospinal fluid pressure: Cerebrospinal fluid pressure was measured before and after discharge (within 14 days after surgery or before discharge). And (g) headache: Visual analog scale scores were recorded before and within 14 days after surgery.

Data management

Data collection

Data collection was completed using an electronic data capture (EDC) system, tested to meet quality management regulations for clinical trials of medical devices and the Technical Guidelines for Clinical Trial Data Management. Participants received training before the system's launch to ensure compliance with trial requirements. After activation, participants received account and password credentials, which they were responsible for keeping secure. Sharing account information or using someone else's account was prohibited.

Data verification and modification

The EDC system provides online and offline verification. When abnormal data are detected, the EDC system issues real‐time warnings to remind researchers to check the data. The data administrator conducts logical checks on the data saved on the server and publishes erroneous data as manual queries through the EDC system. Researchers were required to answer to address these queries, with supervisors reminding and assisting them regularly to ensure correct handling. The system records all queries and corresponding answers.

Database locking

The system was locked once data entry, submission, and query resolution were complete. The statistician generated a blind audit report based on the database. The sponsor, head of data management, and statistics personnel in charge needed to sign the database locking form if no further data modifications were required. Following this, the data administrator would lock the database. Once locked, the database could not be modified. However, if errors were discovered that affected key efficacy or safety indicators, the sponsor, head of data management, and statisticians in charge would confirm the need for unlocking and sign the database unlocking form. The data administrator would then modify the incorrect data based on the reasons for unlocking and perform quality control. After correcting the error, the database would be locked again, with the sponsor, head of data management, and statisticians signing the database locking form once more.

Data monitoring body

A third‐party contract research organization was responsible for developing a comprehensive and applicable monitoring plan, in accordance with the trial's requirements. This organization monitored trial quality according to the monitoring plan. Additionally, the sponsor conducted regular inspections of trial quality. The supervisor followed Standard Operating Procedures for Clinical Trial Monitoring of Investigational Medical Devices and ensured that the clinical trial was implemented according to the study protocol.

Statistical methods

Statistical design

This study used a prospective, multicenter, single‐group OPC design. The immediate postoperative recanalization rate was used to evaluate and is compare with the preset target value to assess the device's performance and determine whether it met the clinical application needs. The corresponding statistical hypothesis was as follows:

pT ≤ p0

pT > p0

Here, pT represents the expected recanalization rate of the dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device in this trial and p0 represents the preset target value. If the final result of this clinical confirmatory test meets the present target value, device's performance would be deemed sufficient for clinical application.

Statistical methods and analytical procedures

Descriptive analysis: Count data were analyzed for frequency and constituent ratio; measurement data were analyzed for mean (standard deviation), and median (25th and 75th percentiles, or range).

Baseline demographic analysis: Descriptive analysis was used. The likelihood ratio χ 2 test was used for possible comparisons between subgroups of count data. Fisher's exact test was performed if expected frequencies were less than five for more than 25% of the cells in the contingency tables. The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test and group t test were used for groups that met normality and homogeneity of variance.

Efficacy analysis: All patients who underwent device use were included in the analysis. For the primary efficacy indicators, the 95% confidence interval (CI) was estimated using exact probability and progressive standard methods, in addition to estimating the primary endpoint occurrence. The lower limit of the 95% CI was compared with the preset target value to determine whether the devices used in this trial met clinical applications requirements. The same analytical methods used at baseline were applied to the other efficacy indicators. For the intragroup comparisons of specific indicators, a paired t test was used for normally distributed measurement data, while the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used for nonnormally distributed data. For qualitative indicators, the McNemar paired χ 2 test was used.

Safety evaluation: The number and incidence rates of adverse events were described, detailing the performance and severity of all adverse events and their relationship with device utilization.

Primary endpoint: Statistical analysis was performed at a significance level of 0.025 (corresponding to the one‐sided confidence limit of the 95% CI), while the statistical test of other indicators was performed at a two‐sided significance level of 0.05 (unless otherwise specified). All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 statistical software (SAS Statistical Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Calculation of sample size

The immediate postoperative recanalization rate was the trial's primary endpoint. As there is a lack of data on similar dedicated devices, the preset target value of this trial was set at approximately 90% based on previous studies of thrombectomy devices. 13 , 15 The estimated immediate postoperative recanalization rate of the device was 97%. To ensure the safety of the device, considering that thrombectomy for CVST carries a 1%–5% risk of severe intracranial hemorrhage, the preset target value and estimated immediate postoperative recanalization rate were conservatively set at 88% and 95%, respectively. To calculate the sample size, the significance level was set at 2.5% (one‐sided) with a power of 80%, resulting in a calculated sample size of 138. Accounting for a 5% shedding rate, the final sample size was calculated to be 146 patients.

DISCUSSION

The breakthrough in arterial stroke thrombectomy in 2015 demonstrated that the development of effective and suitable thrombectomy devices is a crucial determinant of the success of clinical trials. 32 This perspective was also emphasized in the TO‐ACT trial, where it was noted that the development of novel and tailored thrombectomy devices would contribute to optimal recanalization through by enabling faster and more effective thrombus removal from the cerebral venous system (Figure 3). 20 For patients with CVST, many of whom are at critical stages in their family and professional lives, improving outcomes to help them return to normal activities is a shared goal between patients and clinicians. This underscores the need for developing a dedicated venous sinus thrombectomy device.

In this trial, we focused not only on the primary endpoint of immediate postoperative recanalization rate, as per the OPC design, but also on manipulation time and safety outcomes. This comprehensive approach was intended to validate the universality of the thrombectomy device across various scenarios involving different neurointerventional skills and patient conditions, building on findings from previous studies. While publishing the trial protocol early on was helpful for obtaining community feedback this effect diminished over time. Nevertheless, we decided to publish this trial protocol to promote patient recruitment, given the rare nature of CVST, which has slowed the recruitment process. We also aim to raise awareness and interest in more tertiary medical centers participating in clinical trials and provide a foundation for future interventional clinical studies.

This trial employed a single‐group OPC design for several reasons. First, there were no comparable devices available. No approved devices in China have been used for CVST thrombectomy, so the same devices could not be used as controls. Second, EVT for CVST is still in the exploratory stage, with no academic consensus on its efficacy and safety. Using other EVT devices or techniques as controls could raise significant scientific and ethical concerns. Third, pharmacological thrombolysis and anticoagulation control were not suitable. These treatments differ fundamentally from EVT in their approach to revascularization of CVST and fall under different medical application scopes. Using them as controls could introduce severe scientific, ethical, and data bias issues. Heterogeneous controls, particularly pharmacological thrombolysis and anticoagulant therapy, would also hinder the effective implementation of blinding and randomization, leading to a high dropout rate.

However, this protocol has some limitations. First, the classification system for immediate postoperative recanalization rate may be questioned. Due to the lack of a highly recognized method for evaluating this rate, we used the quantified classification system proposed by Qureshi et al. 31 to assess device efficacy. Second, OPC design has inherent flaws, including the lack of comparability between groups, which even complex statistical techniques cannot fully mitigate. Third, the inclusion and exclusion criteria may be seen as suboptimal. While scientific and convincing criteria for enrolling patients who could benefit from EVT are still lacking, our criteria was based on compelling evidence from the International Study on Cerebral Vein and Dural Sinus Thrombosis study and TO‐ACT trial. 5 , 20 Therefore, they were the best possible under the existing conditions. Finally, different thrombectomy techniques might yield varying immediate postoperative recanalization rates. The successful results of this trial may not be replicable in centers with minimal experience in stroke intervention therapy. However, patients with CVST are typically diagnosed at tertiary medical centers with significant interventional experience.
