Biosynthesis and heterologous production of the α-agarofuran scaffold of Celangulin V from Celastrus angulatus
Authors: Weiguo Li, Andong Zhu, Wei Li, Shengli Wang, Dongmei Liang, Xiaoguang Yan, Ruiqi Chen, Daoguang Tian, Ziming Liu, Zhaonong Hu, Shaopeng Wei, Yi Zhang, Mingzhang Wen, Qinggele Caiyin, Jianbin Yan, Jianjun Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Celangulin V is a widely used biopesticide derived from Celastrus angulatus, and features antifeedant and insecticidal properties as a dihydro-β-agarofuran (DHβAF) sesquiterpenoid. Its biosynthesis remains largely unexplored. Here, we assemble a chromosome-level and haplotype-resolved reference genome of C. angulatus, with each haplotype assembled into 23 pseudochromosomes and achieving scaffold N50 of 14.31 and 14.01 Mb, respectively. This high-quality genome reveals that a recent β whole-genome triplication (β-WGT) event occurred ~34.3 million years ago, and that the expansion of sesquiterpene synthases and cytochrome P450s from the CYP71BE family results from whole-genome duplication (WGD) event and tandem duplication, respectively. We identify CaTPS16 as a γ-eudesmol synthase, and show that CYP71BE416 further catalyzes the γ-eudesmol to tetrahydrofuran ring α-agarofuran for Celangulin V biosynthesis. We further achieve the de novo synthesis of α-agarofuran in Saccharomyces cerevisiae through combined coexpression of these genes. This study has significantly increases the available genomic resources of the Celastraceae family, improves our understanding of the biosynthetic origins and evolution of the tetrahydrofuran ring in DHβAF sesquiterpenoids, and enables its heterologous bioproduction in microbial chassis.

Introduction

Sesquiterpenoids with a dihydro-β-agarofuran (DHβAF) skeleton are characteristic natural products in the family Celastraceae, comprising over 500 identified compounds. They are classified into more than 70 groups based on the number, position, and stereochemistry of the hydroxy groups attached to the common tricyclic scaffold characterized by a trans-decalin ring (A and B rings) and a tetrahydrofuran ring (C ring)1. Due to their diverse molecular architectures, DHβAF sesquiterpenoids have a wide range of biological activities, including antitumor, multidrug resistance reversal, anti-HIV, immunosuppressive, and insect antifeedant and insecticidal activities2. Currently, natural extraction of DHβAF sesquiterpenoids from plants fails to meet market needs due to insufficient raw material supply. To our knowledge, only 15 of the more than 500 DHβAF sesquiterpenoids have been reported to be chemically synthesized1. While various strategies have been employed to synthesize this class of compounds chemically, little is known about the biosynthesis of the unique tetrahydrofuran ring from the Celastraceae.

Chinese bittersweet (Celastrus angulatus), a representative species of the Celastraceae family, is widely distributed and used in traditional Chinese medicine as a cancer treatment and as an insecticide in China3. Since 1986, Wu et al. have isolated and identified more than 60 sesquiterpenoids with DHβAF skeletons from the extracts of C. angulatus root bark and leaves4–6. Among these compounds, Celangulin V has been registered and commercialized as an insecticide in China and has effectively controlled many agricultural pests, including Pieris rapae (Lepidoptera) and Locusta migratoria manilensis (Orthoptera) by targeting the V-ATPase H subunit of these insects7. Currently, plant-derived Celangulin V is in short supply due to its low abundance in C. angulatus. The chemical synthesis of Celangulin V remains unrealized due to its complex stereochemical structure. Synthetic biology and metabolic engineering have emerged as powerful tools for the efficient synthesis of many structurally complex natural products in microbial hosts, including the colchicine alkaloid8 and the paclitaxel precursor baccatin III9. However, these efforts require a comprehensive understanding of the Celangulin V biosynthetic pathway.

The biosynthesis of DHβAF sesquiterpenoids starts with farnesyl diphosphate (FPP), with subsequent cyclization to the tetrahydrofuran ring by sesquiterpene synthases (STSs) and cytochrome P450s (CYPs). Then, the tetrahydrofuran ring is decorated by a series of reactions, including hydroxylation and acylation, to generate the final product by post-tailoring enzymes. Our previous transcriptomic analyses of C. angulatus10 identified candidate genes encoding STSs, CYPs, and BAHD acyltransferase for DHβAF sesquiterpenoid biosynthesis. Based on the above analysis, we hypothesize the biosynthetic pathway of Celangulin V (NW41) (Fig. 1a). However, experiments still need to verify the genes involved in the biosynthesis of Celangulin V. Here, we present the first high-quality and haplotype-resolved chromosome-level reference genome of C. angulatus to investigate DHβAF sesquiterpenoid biosynthesis. Comparative genome analyses reveal that STS duplicates created by recent WGD events and an expansion of the CYP71BE subfamily are crucial for the evolution of Celangulin V biosynthesis. Using an integrative approach combining genomic data, coexpression analyses, and functional validation, we identify CaTPS16 as a γ-eudesmol synthase and show that CYP71BE416 oxidizes γ-eudesmol to form the tetrahydrofuran ring α-agarofuran. In addition, we demonstrate the de novo synthesis of α-agarofuran in yeast by introducing these genes. This work provides foundational insights into the biosynthetic pathway of Celangulin V and the evolutionary mechanisms underlying tetrahydrofuran ring biosynthesis in the Celastraceae family.

Results

Proposed biosynthetic pathway of Celangulin V in C. angulatus and gene mining

First, we analyzed the chemical constituents of three organs of C. angulatus (root, leaf, stem and flower, Fig. 1b) by liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC/MS). At least six DHβAF sesquiterpenoids (NW31, NW32, NW35, NW41, NW49, and NW61) could be detected, which supported the validity of our proposed biosynthetic pathway. Subsequently, the contents of major compounds NW31, NW32, NW35, NW41, NW49, and NW61 in eleven tissue samples (roots at three developmental stages, leaves at three developmental stages, stems at two developmental stages and flowers at three developmental stages) were quantitatively determined (Supplementary Figs. 1–8). The results indicated the roots contained more abundant DHβAF sesquiterpenoids than the leaves and stem samples (Supplementary Table 1).

In order to obtain a complete list of candidate genes involved in the biosynthesis of Celangulin V, we sequenced, assembled, and annotated a chromosome-level genome of C. angulatus. The K-mer analysis of 78.31 Gb (244.41× coverage) Illumina paired-end short reads revealed that the genome size of C. angulatus was approximately 330 Mb with 0.61% heterozygosity based on 17 K-mer distribution. (Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Table 3). De novo assembly using ~44.07 Gb (144× coverage) of PacBio continuous long reads, followed by short-reads polishing and 43.40 GB of chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data (135× coverage) scaffolding (Supplementary Table 2), produced two haplotype-resolved assemblies anchored onto 23 pseudochromosomes for each haplotype (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 10). The final assembly size was 321.88 Mb for haplotype A (HA) and 321.47 Mb for haplotype B (HB), with scaffold N50 of 14.31 Mb and 14.01 Mb, respectively (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Table 5). Both haplotypes exhibited identical average GC content (35.2%) (Supplementary Table 5). High mapping rate of Illumina reads to HA (97.73%) and HB (97.74%) (Supplementary Table 6). Both haplotypes achieved a Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) analysis12 score of 98.9% for complete conserved homologs, confirming the high continuity and completeness of the assemblies (Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Table 7).

Protein-coding genes prediction identified 29,364 genes in HA and 29,090 in HB were annotated (Supplementary Table 5) with more than 97% functionally supported in at least one database of NR, InterPro, SwissProt, Pfam, EggNOG or TAIR (Supplementary Table 8). The completeness of the gene repertoire was assessed by BUSCO, showing 96.5% for HA and 94.9% for HB (Supplementary Table 7). Repetitive elements accounted for 74.59% (240.08 Mb) of HA and 75.87% (243.88 Mb) of HB (Supplementary Table 9). For the non-coding RNAs (ncRNA), 2260 non-coding RNA were identified in HA, and 2278 ncRNA were identified in HB (Supplementary Table 10).

While the two haplotypes share 98.57% genomic identify (Supplementary Fig. 11, Supplementary Table 11), a detailed synteny analysis revealed 188 collinear blocks disrupted by structural and sequence divergence, 13 inversions (374 kb in HA and 488 kb in HB) and 45 translocations (1317 kb in HA and 1262 kb in HB). The largest variants include a 140-kb inversion on chromosome 23 and a 56-kb translocation on chromosome 4 (Supplementary Fig. 12). 1,291,912 SNPs and 224,078 Indels (insertions and deletions shorted than 50 bp) have been detected. Of them, only 7962 (0.56%) SNPs and 10821 (4.83%) Indels affected protein sequences (Supplementary Data 1). Together, these results revealed that the two haplotypes were highly similar in terms of genome size and gene content, while retaining measurable heterozygosity in structural variations and sequence polymorphisms. In parallel, we carefully examined the distribution of assembly gaps in both haplotypes by screening for ambiguous base (“N”s) across all pseudochromosomes. Notably, no assembly gaps were detected in either haplotype, reflecting the high continuity of the chromosome-level assemblies and enabling precise mapping of structural variation. Therefore, unless otherwise specified, HA was used for subsequent analyses.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed a whole-genome triplication (β-WGT) event in C. angulatus

To determine the phylogenetic position of C. angulatus, we performed orthology inference based on the 17 representative plant species. In total, 21,148 orthogroups were identified, of which 8350 were shared by all species, including 682 single-copy orthogroups (Supplementary Fig. 13). In C. angulatus, a total of 26,369 genes were clustered into 13,693 orthogroups, included 11 species-specific orthogroups (Supplementary Table 12). A high-confidence phylogenetic tree was constructed using 628 single-copy orthologs. Based on the phylogenetic tree, we conducted gene family expansion and contraction analyses. Compared with the MRCA (most recent common ancestor) of C. angulatus and T. wilfordii, 181 expanded and 139 contracted gene families were identified with significant copy-number variation (p < 0.05) in C. angulatus (Fig. 2a). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the rapidly expanded gene families clustered in “metabolic pathway”, “biosynthesis of secondary metabolites” as well as “sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis” biosynthetic pathway (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results suggested that the expanded genes might be closely associated with secondary metabolites biosynthesis in C. angulatus, especially the sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis.

Accumulating evidence has shown that WGD and small-scale duplication (SSDs) events are important evolutionary forces in speciation, genome expansion and specialized functions13. To investigate WGD events in C. angulatus, the paralogous gene pairs in the collinear regions of C. angulatus and three other representative COM clade plant species (T. wilfordii, R. communis, and P. trichocarpa) as well as their orthologous gene pairs in the collinear genomic regions, were used to detect the Ks (synonymous substitution rate) and 4DTv (fourfold synonymous third codon transversion). The Ks distribution of intragenomic paralogs in C. angulatus exhibited two prominent peaks at approximately 2.08 and 0.20. The first peak at approximately 2.08 corresponds to the known core γ whole-genome triplication (γ-WGT) shared by all the eudicots. The second peak at approximately 0.20 indicated C. angulatus might undergo one recent WGD event after core eudicot γ-WGT (Fig. 2b). Genome-wide synteny analysis revealed extensive collinear blocks across multiple chromosome pairs in C. angulatus, accompanied by large-scale genomic rearrangement (Fig. 2c). A highly similar Ks distribution was observed in T. wilfordii, and pairwise synteny analyses revealed strong macrosynteny between the C. angulatus and T. wilfordii genomes (Fig. 2d), indicating a shared WGD history within the Celastraceae lineage. Consistently, Ks peak for orthologs between C. angulatus and T. wilfordii was approximately 0.08, suggesting their divergence occurred after this shared Celastraceae-specific WGD event. These results were further supported by 4DTv analysis (Supplementary Fig. 15).

To further investigate this polyploidization event in Celastraceae, we performed synteny analyses between C. angulatus genome and the Vitis vinifera (V. vinifera) genome, a reference genome that has not experienced any recent WGD after the core eudicot γ WGT events. We found that 78.2% of C. angulatus gene models are syntenic blocks corresponding to one V. vinifera gene region, covering 77.1 % of the V. vinifera gene space, among which 24% have three orthologous regions in C. angulatus, 29% have two, 19% have one (Supplementary Fig. 16). In addition, a typical 1:3 synteny relationship between C. angulatus genome and the V. vinifera was observed (Fig. 2e). Together, these results supported an Celastraceae-wide β-WGT. This β-WGT event was dated to approximately 34.3 MYA.

Gene duplication has been considered an important force for evolution14. In total, 25,542 duplicated genes were identified and classified into five categories: 21,284 WGDs (83.3%), 775 dispersed duplicates (3.03%), 1526 transposed duplicates (5.97%), 1419 tandem duplicates (5.55%), and 538 proximal duplicates (2.10%) (Supplementary Data 2). These results suggest that the WGD events played a significant role in the C. angulatus genome, with the majority of its genome being duplicated during evolution.

Identification of specific TPS genes involved in DHβAF-type sesquiterpenoids biosynthesis

Terpene synthases (TPSs) are pivotal enzymes in the terpenoid biosynthesis, and they play a central role in the formation of the diverse terpenoid carbon structures. The biosynthesis of DHβAF-type sesquiterpenoids begins with the cyclization of FPP under the catalysis of STSs. We analyzed C. angulatus TPS families and identified 41 TPS genes from the present genome using the reported HMM model (PF03936 and PF01397). We constructed a phylogenetic tree from the amino acid sequence alignment of TPSs and divided the TPS genes into five groups, namely TPS-a, TPS-b, TPS-c, TPS-e/f, and TPS-g, based on the reported classification of V. vinifera and Arabidopsis thaliana TPS genes15,16(Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 17). Among these, the TPS-a subfamily, which is primarily composed of STSs, contains genes that produce primarily mono- or double-ring sesquiterpenes17,18. Given that DHβAF-type sesquiterpenoids are double-ring structures, the TPS-a subfamily was identified as the target for mining relevant TPS genes in the C. angulatus genome. Twelve genes were assigned to the TPS-a family, all annotated as (-)-germacrene D synthase. Germacrene-type synthases are of particular interest due to the structural similarity of their products to DHβAF. These genes were designated CaTPS5-CaTPS16 (Supplementary Table 13). The expression levels (FPKM) of CaTPS6, CaTPS15, and CaTPS16 were highly correlated with the DHβAF sesquiterpenoid contents in C. angulatus roots, with Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) > 0.95 (Supplementary Table 14). To provide insights into DHβAF-type sesquiterpenoid biosynthesis, we have characterized the activity of all 11 CaSTSs in vitro. The purified proteins were incubated with precursor FPP, and the products were analyzed by GC/MS (Supplementary Fig. 18). Among them, the CaTPS5, CaTPS7, CaTPS8, CaTPS13, and CaTPS14 proteins generated germacrene D (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 19, and Supplementary Table 15). CaTPS6 and CaTPS16 proteins, on the other hand, produced the majority of γ-eudesmol, along with several minor products, including elemol, 7-epi-α-eudesmol, α-eudesmol, and cryptomeridiol (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 19, and Supplementary Table 15). Notably, the catalytic activity of CaTPS16 was higher than that of CaTPS6, making it the preferred candidate for further investigation. No enzymatic products were detected for CaTPS10, CaTPS11, CaTPS12, and CaTPS15. Among the sesquiterpene products generated, γ-eudesmol is a bicyclic sesquiterpene and structurally most similar to DHβAF, which is also a bicyclic compound and forms the backbone of Celangulin V. Interestingly, the cyclization modes of γ-eudesmol and DHβAF is identical, involving C1,C10- and C2, C7-cyclizations. In 1968, Marshall reported the chemical synthesis of DHβAF, in which epi-γ-eudesmol was a key intermediate20. Thus, we speculated that CaTPS6 and CaTPS16 were involved in the Celangulin V biosynthesis pathway, with γ-eudesmol serving as a critical intermediate.

We introduced CaTPS16 into the HO loci in strain Sc02721 and constructed a S. cerevisiae cell factory (LWG01): the level of γ-eudesmol produced was 80.2 mg/L. The MVA pathway provides FPP, a direct precursor for γ-eudesmol biosynthesis. As previously demonstrated, the FPP production has been improved by overexpressing the key genes in the MVA pathway to construct the strain Sc02721. Some studies have shown that the overexpression of upc2-1 could increase sesquiterpenes production in engineered S. cerevisiae22. In this study, we introduced the upc2-1 gene into the GAL80 loci in strain LWG01, generating strain LWG02. This modification improved the γ-eudesmol yield from 80.2 to 140.2 mg/L. Additionally, we replaced the ERG9 promoter with the glucose-inducible PHXT1 promoter in the yeast GAL80 disrupted regulatory system, allowing glucose to reallocate carbon flux from the growth pathway to the terpene synthetic pathway and boost terpene production23. The resulting engineered strain LWG03 was able to produce 342.32 mg/L γ-eudesmol (Fig. 4b).

Acetyl-CoA is the starting substrate of the MVA pathway for the heterologous synthesis of sesquiterpenes, and an insufficient acetyl-CoA supply often hampers the efficient biosynthesis of sesquiterpenes in yeast24. The synthetic non-oxidative glycolysis (NOG) pathway with xylulose-5-phosphate specific phosphoketolase (PK) and phosphotransacetylase (PTA) can transform glucose into three acetyl-CoA molecules with a 100% carbon yield25, compared to the mere 66% carbon yield of the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway. In the present work, we integrated Leuconostoc mesenteroides phosphoketolase (LmxPK) and Clostridium kluyveri phosphotransacetylase PTA (CkPTA) into the GPP1 locus of strain LWG03, as previously reported26. The γ-eudesmol production was increased to 693.7 mg/L in the resulting LWG04 (Fig. 4b). To increase the acetyl-CoA flux, we deleted the cytosolic malate synthase gene (MLS1) and the peroxisomal citrate synthase gene (CIT2) in LWG04, which leads acetyl-CoA to compete with the cytosolic MVA pathway26. The γ-eudesmol yield in the double-knockout strain LWG05 was improved to 1040.53 mg/L (Fig. 4b), representing a 1.29-fold increase over the previously reported highest yield (801.66 mg/L) obtained from a recombinant yeast strain expressing NnTPS10 from Nelumbo nucifera27. There is a 40% sequence similarity between NnTPS10 and CaTPS16 proteins (Supplementary Fig. 20). CaTPS16 and NnTPS10 specifically utilize FPP to produce distinct eudesmane-type sesquiterpenes, but not monoterpenes from GPP. (Supplementary Fig. 21). Steady-state kinetic analysis showed the catalytic efficiency of the CaTPS16 is 1.23-fold higher than that of the NnTPS10 (Supplementary Table 16). Lastly, the structure of γ-eudesmol, germacrene D (3), 7-epi-α-eudesmol (5) and cryptomeridiol (7) were further confirmed by NMR (Supplementary Figs. 22–25). The absolute configuration of γ-eudesmol was verified by electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectra as (2 R,4aR)-isomer (Supplementary Fig. 26), named (+)-γ-eudesmol, and future confirmed by optical rotatory dispersion (ORD) (Supplementary Table 17).

Specific CYP genes identification in C. angulatus via integrated genome and transcriptome analysis

The CYPs family is the largest enzyme family in plants and is involved in the synthesis of a variety of complex natural products. The homology of CYPs family genes is relatively low, at approximately 40%, and the functions of many remain unknown28. To gain insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the production of Celangulin V, we analyzed the overall CYP families in the C. angulatus genome and identified a total of 315 CYP genes using the reported HMM model (PF00067). These genes were roughly classified into A-type and non-A-type subfamilies (Supplementary Fig. 27). In the present research, most CYP genes involved in the biosynthesis of sesquiterpenes, including the CYP71, CYP76, CYP706 and CYP82 subfamilies, were found in the CYP71 clade29. A total of 101 CYP genes fall within the CYP71 clade (Fig. 5a). It has been proposed that pathway genes associated with specialized secondary metabolites often tend to exhibit similar expression patterns30. Given the positive correlation between CaTPS16 expression and Celangulin V biosynthesis in roots, we hypothesized that the CYP genes that catalyze subsequent steps from γ-eudesmol to DHβAF derivatives would exhibit similar root-specific expression patterns. Therefore, to refine the candidate CYP gene list, gene coexpression analysis was performed on the 101 CYP gene transcripts of the sequenced C. angulatus tissues, including the roots, flowers, stems and leaves, by using previously characterized CaTPS16 as the bait gene. Pathway genes involved in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites often exhibit expansion patterns31, which is a key strategy adopted to screen functionally associated genes from massive amounts of genome data and is employed by plants for environmental adaptation under the enhancement of secondary metabolite production32. Gene family expansion analysis demonstrated that 9 of the 23 candidate CYP genes underwent significant expansion. Notably, the most numerous genes, CYP71BE83 (CaA05G00613), CYP71BE414 (CaA11G00952), CYP71BE415 (CaA11G00954), CYP71BE404 (CaA11G00956), CYP71BE209 (CaA11G00957), CYP71BE211 (CaA11G00958), CYP71BE416 (CaA11G00959), and CYP71BE417 (CaA11G00960), except for CYP83F151 (CaA04G00594), belonged to the CYP71BE subfamily (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 18). These results suggest that the expansion of the CYP71BE subfamily played an important role in the evolution of Celangulin V biosynthesis in C. angulatus. Furthermore, we noticed that almost all the CaCYP genes were located on Chr11, except for CYP83F151 and CYP71BE83, which were located on pseudochromosomes 04 and 05, respectively.

We performed detailed microsynteny analyses of the CYP gene cluster located on Chr11 of C. angulatus and compared it with representative species from the Celastrales-Oxalidales-Malpighiales (COM) clade, including T. wilfordii, P. trichocarpa, and R. communis, as well as with the more distantly related eudicot V. vinifera (Fig. 5c). Our analysis shows that the CYP gene cluster in C. angulatus comprises 15 CYP genes arranged as a dense tandem array on ChrA11, including 7 candidate CYP genes (Fig. 5c), with multiple adjacent copies predominantly oriented in the same transcriptional direction. When comparing this genomic region with T. wilfordii, no detectable microsyntenic block encompassing the CYP gene cluster was identified, suggesting that this locus is either absent or has undergone extensive genomic rearrangement. As a result, a direct one-to-one comparison of tandem duplication patterns between these two Celastraceae species is not feasible. In contrast, conserved microsynteny of the CYP gene locus was clearly observed between C. angulatus and other COM clade members (Fig. 5c). These species share a homologous genomic segment containing CYP genes, indicating that a proto-CYP gene locus was likely present in the common ancestor of the COM clade. Notably, a homologous CYP gene-containing region was also detected in V. vinifera, despite its placement outside the COM clade. This observation suggests that the ancestral genomic context of the CYP gene locus predates the diversification of the COM clade and was already present in early eudicots.

Importantly, while this ancestral locus is broadly conserved, substantial differences in copy number and local gene organization are evident among species. C. angulatus exhibits a pronounced lineage-specific expansion of CYP genes through local tandem duplication, resulting in a markedly larger cluster than those observed in P. trichocarpa, R. communis, or V. vinifera. This pattern indicates that, following divergence from the common ancestor, the CYP gene locus in C. angulatus, including underwent extensive tandem duplication, likely contributing to functional diversification within this gene family (Supplementary Data 2). Thus, we speculated that these CYP genes could catalyze the hydroxylation of γ-eudesmol and were considered to be potential candidate genes for Celangulin V biosynthesis.

Functional identification of specific CaCYP genes

To investigate the biochemical activity of these CaCYPs, recombinant expression in S. cerevisiae was employed, as this method has proven to be successful for previous characterization31. For this purpose, full-length cDNAs for all 9 candidate CYP genes (CYP83F151, CYP71BE83, CYP71BE414, CYP71BE415, CYP71BE404, CYP71BE209, CYP71BE211, CYP71BE416 and CYP71BE417) together with AtCPR1 from A. thaliana were cloned and inserted into the pESC-Ura expression vector. The resulting constructs were transformed into the BY4741 strain, generating strains LWG06-LWG14, respectively. The in vitro assays were then performed using microsomal preparations from induced cultures of this recombinant yeast, with γ-eudesmol as a potential substrate. Notably, we found that CYP71BE209, CYP71BE211 and CYP71BE416 catalyzed the formation of α-agarofuran, a sesquiterpenoid containing an ether ring, as confirmed by its molecular weight (m/z = 220) and from comparison with MS data with the NIST database. Among these, CYP71BE416 exhibited the highest catalytic efficiency, with activity levels 1.23- and 1.08-fold higher than those of CYP71BE209 and CYP71BE211, respectively (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 28, and Supplementary Table 19). Moreover, CYP71BE416 presented higher expression levels than CYP71BE209 and CYP71BE416 in the root (Supplementary Fig. 29). For the CYP71BE404, the major product appeared to be the keto sesquiterpenoid carissone, which was generated by consecutive oxidations, as indicated by its molecular weight (m/z = 236) and mass spectrum (comparable to the standard from NIST). The minor product was identified as α-agarofuran. There is a 72.61% sequence similarity of CYP71BE416, CYP71BE211, CYP71BE209 and CYP71BE404 (Supplementary Fig. 30). No activity was observed for CYP83F151, CYP71BE83, CYP71BE414, CYP71BE415 and CYP71BE417.

The catalytic efficiency of CYP is a rate-limiting step that hinders the yield of the final product. The activity of CYPs depended on the redox coupling efficiency between the CYPs and CPRs33. Because there was a single-copy Artemisia annua CPR (AaCPR) gene in the original strain Sc02721, we deleted the AaCPR gene in LWG05 to generate an engineered strain LWG15, which was used to screen CPRs compatible with CYP71BE416. We analyzed C. angulatus CPR families and identified 23 CPR genes from the present genome using the reported HMM model (PF00667, PF00258 and PF00175). To narrow down the candidate CPR genes, we conducted coexpression analyses. Among the 23 CPR genes, the top 8 (CaCPR1-CaCPR8) with root-specific expression patterns were similar to CYP71BE416, CaTPS6, and CaTPS16 in the Celangulin V biosynthetic pathway, which were selected for the following study (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table 20). These CPR genes, along with four CPR genes from other species (AtCPR1, AtCPR2, TasCPR (CPR from Taxus cuspidata; GeneBank: AY571340), and TanCPR (CPR from Taxus chinensis; GeneBank: AY959320)), were coexpressed with CYP71BE416 in strain LWG15, respectively.

Among these, the strain LWG17 expressing CaCPR2 yielded the highest α-agarofuran production at 27.75 mg/L, which is consistent with the superior electron transfer efficiency of native CPR genes compared with that of other source species33 (Fig. 6c). Some studies have shown that the CYP-CPR fusion protein might enhance electron transfer efficiency and catalytic activity34,35. Subsequently, three different linkers (GGG, GSTSSG, and GSTSSGSG) were used to construct the CYP71BE416 and CaCPR2 fusion proteins in strain LWG15, respectively. The fusion protein CYP71BE416-GGG-CaCPR2 exhibited the highest α-agarofuran yield (43.89 mg/L) when expressed in strain LWG28 (Fig. 6d). Lastly, the CYP71BE416-GGG-CaCPR2 cassette was integrated into the YORWΔ22 site in LWG15, which was an effective integration site with high gene expression levels36. The final strain LWG31 was capable of producing 7.82 g/L of γ-eudesmol and 445.35 mg/L of α-agarofuran in a 5 L bioreactor (Fig. 6e), representing the highest titer reported to date in microbial cell factories. Lastly, the structure of α-agarofuran was further confirmed by NMR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 31). The absolute configuration of α-agarofuran was verified by electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectra as (1 R,6S,9 R)-isomer (Supplementary Fig. 32), named (±)-α-agarofuran.

Discussion

Celangulin V, a DHβAF sesquiterpenoid with a unique tetrahydrofuran ring, exhibits potent antifeedant and insecticidal activities and is widely used as a biopesticide in China7,37. DHβAF sesquiterpenoids with the specific tetrahydrofuran rings are widespread throughout the Celastraceae family and have marked biological activities38,39. Previous investigations of Celangulin V biosynthesis relied on transcriptome data, and researchers have identified only the relevant sesquiterpene synthases10. However, the biosynthesis of Celangulin V and the origin of the tetrahydrofuran ring have remained unknown. Here, we present the first chromosome-level and haplotype-resolved genome of C. angulatus with 321.88 Mb for haplotype A (HA) and 321.47 Mb for haplotype B (HB), and their scaffold N50 is 14.31 Mb and 14.01 Mb, respectively, providing a foundation for elucidating the biosynthesis of Celangulin V. To the best of our knowledge, our findings represent the first biosynthetic pathway for the tetrahydrofuran moiety in terpenes, opening various opportunities for biosynthesis involving the construction of the important heterocycle.

We identified a recent WGD in C. angulatus, and together with tandem duplication, these duplication events substantially contributed to the expansion of gene families associated with Celangulin V biosynthesis in the C. angulatus genome. Combined with genome and transcriptome analysis, we identified 12 STSs that belong to the TPS-a subfamily and were annotated as (-)-germacrene D synthase for further investigations. Most of these genes were expanded by the independent WGD events, except for CaTPS8, CaTPS9 and CaTPS15 (Supplementary Table 13). Interestingly, we demonstrated that CaTPS6 and CaTPS16 are responsible for producing γ-eudesmol (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, these genes exhibit high, root-specific transcriptional activity in C. angulatus, consistent with the site of Celangulin V accumulation. We also identified nine CYP genes in the CYP71BE subfamily through coexpression, gene expansion and tandem duplication analyses. Functional assays revealed that CYP71BE209, CYP71BE211 and CYP71BE416 catalyze the conversion of γ-eudesmol to α-agarofuran, forming the tetrahydrofuran ring characteristic of the Celastraceae family, and this structure is completely different from that of eudesmane-type sesquiterpene with its keto group or di-alcohol. The identification of CYP enzymes catalyzing unprecedented reaction types in plant sesquiterpene biosynthesis significantly advances our understanding of their diverse catalytic capacities and expands the product spectrum of the CYP71BE family.

More interestingly, the genome sequence reported here provides insight into the evolution of both the tetrahydrofuran ring-type sesquiterpenes and Celangulin V. With the increasing availability of sequencing data, it has become apparent that in many cases, the genes encoding biosynthetic enzymes for specialized metabolic pathways are not randomly distributed on the genome. Instead, they are physically linked in structures such as arrays, pairs and clusters40. CaTPS6, CaTPS16, CYP71BE209, CYP71BE211 and CYP71BE416 are not clustered in the C. angulatus genome. This observation is consistent with most plant biosynthetic pathway genes, which are not clustered together41, such as those encoding anthocyanins42 and benzylisoquinoline alkaloids in Coptis chinensis43. We identified a CYP (CYP71BE) tandem gene array on pseudochromosome 11 of the C. angulatus genome, including eight CYP71BE genes, through gene expansion analysis. Taken together, the absence of a syntenic CYP gene region in T. wilfordii, combined with its conservation and differential expansion in other COM species and in V. vinifera (Fig. 5c), indicates that the CYP gene cluster in C. angulatus has experienced local tandem duplication, potentially facilitated by the Celastraceae-wide β-WGT, resulting in a species-specific expansion. These results highlight distinct evolutionary trajectories of CYP gene families within Celastraceae.

Functional analysis of the CYP71BE genes revealed three distinct groups. One group contains CYP71BE209, CYP71BE211 and CYP71BE416: they have the same catalytic activity and can catalyze the formation of α-agarofuran from γ-eudesmol. Similarly, in the biosynthetic tandem arrays of oleanane-type triterpenoids, AeCYP72A935 and AeCYP72A936 are located on chromosome 9 of Aralia elata within 130 kb and perform the same activities44; the other group is CYP71BE404, whose major product is carissone, and the byproduct is α-agarofuran. The promiscuous activity of CYP71BE404 may have been an evolutionary starting point for pathway diversification45,46; the third group contains CYP71BE83, CYP71BE414, CYP71BE415 and CYP71BE417, and shows no detectable activity. The eight CYP71BE genes could have emerged from gene duplication throughout the evolution of C. angulatus and evolved different functions47. A noteworthy example is triptonide biosynthesis, for which TwCYP71BE83, TwCYP71BE85 and TwCYP71BE86 were found in tandem repeats on the chromosome CM023878 of T. wilfordii, and TwCYP71BE85 and TwCYP71BE86 were involved in the biosynthesis of the abeo-abietane core structure present in diterpenoid triepoxides; however, TwCYP71BE83 did not work48. SmCPS1 and SmCPS2 were tandemly duplicated genes in the tanshinone gene cluster, and they played a role in different tissues involved in the tanshinone biosynthesis31,49,50. The CYP tandem gene array may provide some intriguing insights into the evolution of sequential CYP-mediated steps in specialized metabolic pathways. For example, the tandem genes CYP82X2 and CYP82X1 are located in the noscapine gene cluster; however, functional analysis has revealed that each performs a highly specific and consecutive step in the noscapine biosynthesis51,52. Similarly, CYP76AH1 and CYP76AH3 are also quite closely related to each other, but they catalyze sequentially specific reactions in tanshinone biosynthesis53,54. CaCYPs may evolve different functions and perform continuous reactions in the biosynthesis of Celangulin V. Therefore, we will coexpress CYP71BE209, CYP71BE211, CYP71BE416, CYP71BE404 with CYP71BE83, CYP71BE414, CYP71BE415, CYP71BE417 and CYP83F151 in yeast, which may yield many key intermediates in Celangulin V biosynthesis and is an interesting aspect to study in the future.

In addition, we explored the application potential of CaTPS16 and CYP71BE416 in bioengineering and constructed an engineered S. cerevisiae to produce α-agarofuran (Fig. 6). These findings lay the foundation for identifying genes encoding subsequently active enzymes and reveal the great potential use of CaTPS16 and CYP71BE416 in bioengineering to produce Celangulin V in the future. To the best of our knowledge, CaTPS16 and CYP71BE416 are the first reported genes for the biosynthesis of the tetrahydrofuran ring in DHβAF-type sesquiterpenoids. This step represents a cornerstone for the study of DHβAF bioactive compounds and their pharmaceutical applications, providing a platform for producing clinically relevant derivatives.

In conclusion, this study significantly enhances the limited genomic resources of the Celastraceae family. The C. angulatus genome enabled the discovery of the pairing of the genes for the consecutively acting CaTPS16 and CYP71BE416, which initiate the characteristic tetrahydrofuran ring α-agarofuran formation involved in Celangulin V biosynthesis. This discovery has advanced our understanding of the biosynthetic origins of the tetrahydrofuran ring reported here and provided insight into the evolution of this characteristic heterocycle. In particular, this seems to have arisen from the expansion of a clade within the CYP71BE subfamily that underwent tandem duplication and neofunctionalization to catalyze the formation of the tetrahydrofuran ring. Accordingly, our study provides valuable insight into not only the more specific biosynthetic origins and evolutionary derivation of the medically relevant Celangulin V, but also the more general tetrahydrofuran ring of DHβAF sesquiterpenoids with agricultural and pharmaceutical applications.

Methods

Plant material and DNA sequencing

Fresh roots, leaves, stems, and flowers were collected from the natural range of C. angulatus (108.07°81′90″N, 34.27°24′26″) in the exposition park of Northwest A&F University in Yangling region, Xianyang city, Shaanxi Province, China. DHβAF sesquiterpenoids standards NW31, NW32, NW35, NW41, NW49, NW61 used in this study were provided by Prof. Shaopeng Wei from Northwest A&F University. The concentrations of DHβAF sesquiterpenoids in the root, stem, and leaf tissues of C. angulatus were determined by UPLC-MS analysis. Formic acid and acetonitrile (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were of UPLC grade. Samples were separated on a ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters, USA) on an ACQUITY Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatography instrument, and the column temperature was 35 °C. MS analysis was performed on XevoTM TQ-XS tandem quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, USA).

DNA was extracted from the roots of C. angulatus. The DNA quality was checked by agarose gel electrophoresis and a Qubit fluorimeter (Thermo Fisher). The paired-end libraries with a 500-bp insert length were prepared by following the Illumina protocols. Sequencing of the library was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 system. For the PacBio Sequel analysis, SMRTbell TM libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol for the sequencing platform. Four independent Hi-C libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 (PE125 bp) at Annoroad Gene Technology Co.

Genome size estimation

Quality control of the Illumina reads was performed using fastp v0.23.2 with default parameters. Then, Jellyfish v2.3.0 was employed to infer the genome size from the k-mer distribution, and the inferred genome size was when k-mer = 17 bp.

Genome assembly

Filtered long reads were assembled with NextDenovo v 2.3.1 and polished by Racon v1.4.20 with default parameters for three rounds. Then, FALCON_phase was performed to assemble the contigs. In order to anchor contigs onto chromosomes, the Hi-C reads were mapped onto the assembled contigs by Juicer v1.555 and the 3D-de novo assembly (3D-DNA) pipeline v.18011456. Finally, with the help of manual adjustment by JuiceBox57, 23 chromosomes were acquired. The completeness of the genome assembly was evaluated by using BUSCO v 5.2.1 with the embryophyte_odb10 dataset and mapping Illumina short-reads to the genome by using HISAT2 and BWA.

Repetitive sequences annotation

Repetitive sequences of C. angulatus genome were annotated using both homology-based search and ab initio prediction method. For homology-based prediction, we used RepeatMasker v4.1.1 to search against Repbase v20.05 with default parameters. For ab initio prediction, we used RepeatModeler v2.0.1 with the parameter ‘-engine ncbi’ to build a consensus sequence library. LTR_FINDER v1.07, LTRharvest v1.6.2 and LTR_retriver v2.9.5 were used to identify full-length long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) and build an LTR library with default parameters. Then, RepeatMasker v4.1.1 was used to search against two libraries to annotate the C. angulatus repeat elements. Finally, the repeat elements predicted by homology-based search and ab initio strategies were combined based on their coordination in the genomes.

Protein-coding gene annotation

After masking repetitive sequences, protein-coding gene prediction was performed by a combination of homology, de novo and transcript evidence-based approaches. Homologous gene prediction was performed by comparing C. angulatus protein sequences with A. thaliana, Oryza sativa, Solanum tuberosum, Solanum lycopersicum and Zea mays using Exonerate. De novo gene prediction was evaluated using various software including Augustus, GeneMark-ES and SNAP. A transcript evidence-based prediction approach was performed by obtaining the transcript data in the form of a de novo assembled transcriptome generated in Trinity58 and a reference-based assembly generated by Stringtie. Then, all the RNA-seq data were aligned to the assembled C. angulatus genome using PASA59. Gene model evidence from these methods was combined by Evidence Modeler (EVM)60 into a non-redundant set of gene structures.

The functional prediction of the predicted genes was analyzed by using a BLASTP homolog search against the NCBI non-redundant protein NR database and SwissProt. The prediction of gene sequence motifs and structural domains was performed using InterProScan v. The annotations of the GO terms and KEGG pathways of genes were obtained from eggnog-mapper.

Identification of noncoding RNA genes

The annotation of tRNA was performed using tRNAscan-SE v 2.0.11 with default parameters. rRNA annotation was conducted by RNAmmer v1.2. miRNA and snRNA genes were predicted by CMScan from INFERNAL v1.1.4 by searching against the Rfam database.

Analysis of haplotype variation

Minimap 2 (v.2.17-r941) was with parameter ‘-ax asm5’ used to perform whole-genome alignment between the two haplotypes. The alignments were then used to identify inversions, translocations, duplications, inverted translocations, and inverted duplications, as well as to detect structural variations (SVs) and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the genome using the SyRI v.1.6 pipeline with default parameters. Plotsr61 was used to visualize synteny, Annotation of SNPs and Indel was performed by snpEff (v 5.4a).

Genome synteny and whole-genome duplication (WGD) analyses

For detecting syntenic blocks for intra- and interspecies comparisons of C. angulatus in the assembled genome, we first conducted an all-against-all blast of primary protein sequences with parameters. Then, gene synteny and collinearity were performed using MCScanX62, and syntenic gene pairs were visualized using a Python version of the MCScan implemented in jcvi package (v1.0.6). We used KaKs_Calcuator version 2.063 to calculate synonymous substitution rate (Ks) with Yang-Nielsen (YN) model, fourfold synonymous third codon transversion (4DTv).

Phylogenetic analysis

The protein sequences from C. angulatus and 16 other species, including A. thaliana, Carica papaya, Prunus persica, R. communis, Theobroma cacao, Helianthus annuus, Populus trichocarpa, Poncirus trifoliata, T. wilfordii, Solanum tuberosum, Solanum melongena, Lactuca sativa, Cucumis sativus, V. vinifera, O. sativa, Fragaria vesca, were used for gene family clustering. All the genes of 17 species were filtered as follows: (1) whenever there was more than one transcript for one gene, only the longest transcript of the coding region was selected; (2) the genes coding region for proteins with more than 30 amino acids were selected for further analysis. Paralogous and orthologous gene families in these 17 species were identified by Orthofinder v2.2.764 running with the default setting, except that the ‘msa’ option was used.

Phylogenetic relationships were determined from 681 single-copy gene families using the maximum likelihood (ML) method. MUSCLE v was employed to align the protein sequences of these single-copy gene families, and then concatenated the alignments for each family into a super alignment matrix. The ML phylogenetic tree was constructed by RAxML v8.2.10 with 1000 bootstrap replicates using PROTGAMMAJTTX model. O. sativa was selected as an outgroup of the phylogenetic tree.

The resulting ML tree was then used as an input tree for the PAML MCMCTree program in PAML version 4.9i to estimate species divergence times. After a burn-in of 5,000,000 iterations, the MCMCTree process was performed 20,000 times with a sample frequency of 5000. To check convergence, two independent runs were performed. The divergence time between A. thaliana and C. papaya (68–72 million years ago)65, the divergence time between A. thaliana and P. trichocarpa (100–120 million years ago)66, the crown group of eudicots (125–161 Ma)67 and the crown group of angiosperms (195–246 million years ago)68 were served as calibrators.

The Café v5.0 software was used to infer the expansion and contraction of gene families. The gene family changes along each lineage in the phylogenetic tree were explored using a discrete approximation of the gamma distribution with “K = 3”. Finally, we compute the likelihood of transition from parent to child nodes based on gene family size in the phylogeny. The conditional probabilities were used as test statistics to calculate corresponding p-values for each lineage, with a p-value of 0.05 used to identify families that expanded and contracted markedly. Genes that belonged to specific and expanded gene families were subjected to functional analysis using GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses.

Genome duplication analysis

The DupGen_finder69 pipeline was used to classify the duplicate genes into WGD/segmental duplication (≥10 homologous gene pairs in collinear blocks), tandem (consecutive repeat), proximal (in nearby genomic region but adjacent within 10 genes), or dispersed (other modes than segmental, tandem and proximal) duplications with the default parameters. The remaining genes were defined as singletons. The genes in the five duplicate categories were further subjected to GO and KEGG analyses with go_profile (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost). The enrich items were selected according to an FDR criterion of 0.05. The dating of Celastraceae-β-WGT were estimated using the formula T = Ks/2r, where r was empirically calibrated based on fossil-calibrated divergence time of C.angulatus and T. wilfordii.

Transcriptome analysis

Raw RNA reads from flowers, leaves, stem as well as root were filtered and trimmed to yield clean reads using FastQC v0.11.9 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The obtained high-quality clean reads were then mapped to the assembled C. angulatus genome by HISAT v2.1.0, and the quantification of gene expression was calculated with StringTie. The Python script preDE.py built into StringTie was used to convert the quantification results into a count matrix. We averaged the abundance values from the biological replicates of each sample to obtain levels of gene expression.

Genes with transcripts per million (TPM) ≥ 1 in at least one of the samples were included in the analysis. Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs) for each bidirectional gene pair were calculated to quantify the corrections. Then, we ranked the PCCs values by mutual rank (MR) algorithm to identify the highly correlated gene pairs. Finally, gene pairs with MR ≤ 300 were referred to as co-expressed genes.

Genome mining for CaTPS, CaCYP and CaCPR gene family

For the identification and classification of CaTPS, CaCYP and CaCPR gene families, the corresponding conserved functional domains in the PFAM databases for each gene family were selected to establish a domain matrix for HMMER search in C. angulatus and other species genomes. For CaTPS genes family, two PFAM domains, PF01397 and PF03936, were used by HMMER search across different species genomes, including C. angulatus, T. wilfordii, P. trichocarpa, R. communis, A. thaliana, V. vinifera and O. sativa. Putative CaTPS candidates were required to match at least one Pfam domain. The retained CaTPS sequences were aligned by MAFFT v7.408 and alignments cleaned using GBlocks. The phylogenetic CaTPS tree was constructed using RAxML v 8.2.10 with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

For CaCYP, CaCPR genes family, PF0067 was used to identify CaCYP genes and PF00667, PF00258 and PF00175 were used to identify CaCPR genes in the C. angulatus genome by HMMER search. The classification of the CaCYP genes was executed by alignment with the CYP database using standard sequence similarity cut-offs, with definite standards of 97, 55, and 40% for allelic, subfamily and family variants, respectively. According to the standardized CYP genes nomenclature, CaCYP genes were divided into A-type and non-A-type. ML phylogenetic trees were constructed using the RAxML v 8.2.10 with homologous amino acide sequences.

Functional characterization of CaTPS genes

The codon-optimized CaTPSs were synthesized by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China) and inserted into the NcoI/XhoI restriction site of pET28a (+) vector (Merck Millipore). The well-constructed vectors were used to transform E. coli BL21 (DE3). Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl-1-thio-β-D-galactopyranoside (IPTG) at 16 °C for 20 h. Bacterial cells were lysed and the cleared supernatants (16,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C) were used for affinity purification on NI-NTA Resin column (Genescript, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The expected size of purified recombinant protein was confirmed by SDS-PAGE analysis.

Enzyme activity assays were performed in 500 μL active buffers (50 mM Tris−HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM DTT), in 2 mL glass tubes typically containing 50 μg purified proteins and 10 μM FPP. The reactions were incubated for 30 min at 30 °C. As controls, yeast transformed with an empty vector without CaTPS were used. Then, the reaction solutions were extracted by n-hexane for GC − MS analysis.

Functional characterization of CaCYP genes

The pESC-Ura vectors carrying CaCYP21-29 were each transformed into the yeast strain BY4741, which enables catalytic activity of CaCYPs by also expressing the A. thaliana NADPH-CYP reductase AtCPR1. BY4741 transformed with empty pESC-URA was employed as control. TEK buffer was prepared with 50 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 M KCl, pH 7.4. The cells were recovered by centrifugation at 2000 g for 5 min, resuspended in TEK to a concentration of 0.5 g wet cells per mL and left at room temperature for 5 min. The cells were again recovered by centrifugation and resuspended in TESB (0.6 M sorbitol in TE). Cells were broken up at 2 °C–6 °C by a cryogenic homogenizer. After centrifugation at 17,300 g for 15 min, microsomes were precipitated by adding NaCl to the supernatant to a final concentration of 0.15 M and polyethylene glycol PEG4000 to a final concentration of 0.1 g/mL. After centrifugation at 17,300 g for 15 min, pellets were resuspended in TEG (20% (v/v) glycerol in TE) to a final concentration of 40 mg/mL.

In vitro activity assays were performed in a 300 μL reaction system that included 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) and 1 mM NADPH, 0.5 mg microsomal protein, and 100 μM of the substrate. The reactions were incubated at 30 °C for 2 h with shaking, and then extracted with 300 μL ethyl acetate for GC − MS analysis.

Strain construction for de novo synthesis of α-agarofuran in yeast

For the production of α-agarofuran precursor γ-eudsemol, CaTPS16 were inserted into HO loci of strain Sc027, which was favorable for sesquiterpenoid synthesis and overexpressing eight key genes of the MVA pathway21. LmxPK, CkPTA, upc2-1 were overexpressed, MLS1 and CIT2 were knocked out, promoter ERG9 was replaced by HXT1. For the production of α-agarofuran, the recombinant plasmids pESC-URA containing CYP71BE416 and CPRs, and gene fragments CYP71BE416-linkers-CaCPR3 were chemically transformed into the yeast strain LWG07 separately. S. cerevisiae strains were transformed using the standard lithium acetate method. All plasmids, strains and primers used and generated in this study are summarized in the table of Supplementary Data 3, 4 and 5, respectively. The transformants were selected on SD medium without uracil for new strains. For shake-flask fermentation, each colony was grown in 50 mL SD medium containing 2% glucose and precultured at 30 °C for 120 h at 220 rpm. Ethanol was added at 36 and 60 h as the carbon source with a final concentration of 10 g/L. Yeast metabolites were extracted twice with 5 mL of ethyl acetate for GC − MS analysis.

GC-MS analysis

The pooled organic extract was dried via rotary evaporation and the residue was dissolved in 0.5 ml hexanes for GC-MS analysis (Thermo Fisher gas phase mass spectrometer ISQ-LT) using a DB-5 (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, non-polar column), HP-5MS (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, non-polar column), DB-Wax (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, polar column), and BP-20 (50 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, polar column). The injector temperature was 250 °C. One microliter of sample was injected in split mode (1:10), and the GC oven temperature program was applied with 1 mL/min nitrogen as the carrier gas: 70 °C for 2 min, 10 °C/min to 300 °C (DB-5, HP-5MS) or 230 °C (DB-Wax, BP-20), hold for 5 min. The MS scan range (m/z) was 35 to 350. Identification of the main components was based on comparison of their MS spectra and retention times with the standard and the NIST17 library. To further confirm these identifications, retention indices (RI) were calculated for all detected compounds by running an alkane standard mixture (C5 − C22), and were then compared to those reported in the NIST Chemistry WebBook.

Extraction, purification, and structure elucidation

To obtain sufficient amounts for structural analysis, LWG05 and LWG31 were used for fed-batch fermentation in a 5 L bioreactor (Shanghai Bailun Bio), respectively. 20 L cultures were grown and extracted. The fermentation was carried out at 30 °C with an air flow rate of 2 vvm. The dissolved oxygen (DO) was kept above 30% by controlling the agitation speed from 300 to 600 rpm. The feed solution was prepared and fed according to the previously described methods. The mixed feeding solution was added automatically by controlling the pH and DO. When the DO was above 50% and the pH was above 5.5, the feed solution was fed at a rate of 2 mL/min, and NH3·H2O was added automatically when the pH was dropped below 5.5. The feeding solution was added at approximately 15 h, when glucose was almost depleted.

The ethyl acetate phase was collected, concentrated using a rotary evaporator and dried with nitrogen. Then, the essential oil extracts were purified with silica gel column (ether/EtOAc=600:1 to 100:1) and the fractions were collected and analyzed by TLC and GC-MS. Isolated compounds were analyzed in deuterated chloroform by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy on a Bruker AVANCE III HD 400 MHz digital NMR spectrometer.

Theoretical ECD and ORD calculation

The ECD calculations for γ-eudsemol and α-agarofuran were performed with the Gaussian 16 program package. The conformational searching was conducted by the Conflex 8.0 software using the MMFF94 force field within an energy window of 7.0 kcal/mol. The conformers with the Boltzmann population above 0.5% were re-optimized at the B3LYP-D3(BJ)/6-311 G(d,p) level in vacuo. The ORD of γ-eudsemol was measured in chloroform, structural optimizations were carried out at B97XD/6-311 + G(d,p), and optical rotations at B97XD/Aug-cc-pVDZ, using the SMD solvation model to account for solvent effects.

Statistics and reproducibility

No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized.
