Contrast-enhanced ultrasound LI-RADS nonradiation treatment response assessment: applications, challenges, and future directions
Authors: David T Fetzer, Yuko Kono, Fabio Piscaglia, Christoph F Dietrich, Franca Meloni, Qiang Lu, Dirk-Andre Clevert, Andrej Lyshchik, Yasunori Minami, Jeong Min Lee, Tae Kyoung Kim, Hyun-Jung Jang, Masatoshi Kudo, Stephanie R Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) requires nuanced treatment response assessment to balance oncologic control with preservation of liver function. CT and MRI, cornerstones of response evaluation, are often limited by contraindications to their use or indeterminate findings. The recently introduced ACR LI-RADS Contrast-Enhanced Ultrasound (CEUS) Non-Radiation Treatment Response Assessment (TRA) algorithm offers a real-time, radiation-free alternative for evaluating treated HCC after ablation, transarterial therapies, or surgical resection. This review summarizes the CEUS TRA lexicon, criteria, and application across common clinical scenarios, emphasizing its value as a troubleshooting tool when conventional imaging is non-evaluable due to artifacts, contrast limitations, or heterogeneous post-treatment changes. We highlight pitfalls unique to CEUS interpretation, including poor lesion visibility, resection cavity complexity, and the inability to assess extrahepatic disease. Emerging areas include CEUS assessment after radiation-based therapies and the potential for integration of Kupffer-cell–specific contrast agents. Together, these developments position CEUS as an increasingly important modality for precise, timely, and individualized management of HCC following non-radiation locoregional therapy.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most common primary liver cancer, usually arises in the setting of chronic liver disease or cirrhosis. Its treatment is uniquely challenging because therapy must balance oncologic control with preservation of liver function (1). Contemporary HCC management emphasizes multidisciplinary evaluation and stage-adapted therapy using systems such as the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) classification (2, 3). The choice of therapy depends on HCC tumor stage and extent, as well as underlying liver function, patient performance status, and institutional expertise.

Following therapy, accurate assessment of HCC allows for timely re-treatment while minimizing unnecessary interventions–Delaying therapy may allow malignancy to progress to an advanced stage, while aggressive therapy may result in liver decompensation and failure. Precise treatment response assessment requires knowledge of the various available therapies and their potential effects on the treated HCC and surrounding liver parenchyma.

Curative-intent liver-related therapies (LRT) include surgical resection, ablation, and liver transplantation (Table 1). Surgical resection remains the preferred option for patients with preserved liver function and adequate future liver remnant, leaving behind a resection plane or cavity. For early-stage HCC (single nodule ≤ 2–3 cm), ablation is considered a valid first-line option with comparable outcomes to surgical resection, leaving behind an ablation cavity typically larger than the pre-treated lesion (4, 5). Liver transplantation offers both oncologic cure and treatment of underlying cirrhosis. However, candidates are generally required to be within the Milan criteria -- Downstaging with various locoregional therapies can expand eligibility for liver transplantation.

Locoregional endo-arterial LRT includes radiation-based and non-radiation based options. Non-radiation based options include Transarterial Embolization (TAE), including Chemoembolization (TACE), which can be administered with injection of iodized oil-based contrast (Lipiodol) (6). Radiation-based endo-arterial therapy includes Transarterial Radioembolization (TARE), which is gaining in popularity in many centers.

External beam Stereotactic Body RadioTherapy (SBRT) is also increasingly used, more often for unresectable tumors, vascular invasion, or when other locoregional therapies are contraindicated. Systemic therapy is generally reserved for patients with advanced stage of HCC, extrahepatic spread, vascular invasion, or a large intrahepatic burden unsuitable, refractory or progressing following locoregional treatment.

With all of these options, criteria are needed to accurately assess response to therapy, and to guide additional management decisions. The American College of Radiology (ACR) Liver Imaging, Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS) now contains several algorithms, each related to a specific clinical scenario and imaging modality, including HCC surveillance, diagnosis, and response to therapy, and has been adopted by the American Association for Study of Liver Disease (AASLD) (7). LI-RADS includes two algorithms for treatment response assessment (TRA) by CT/MRI, following either radiation-based and non-radiation based therapies (8). The non-radiation based TRA algorithm includes three categories: LR-TR Viable, LR-TR Nonviable, and LR-TR Equivocal. The radiation-based TRA algorithm includes a new category LR-TR Nonprogressing, instead of LR-TR Equivocal, given the dynamic changes in the treated lesion and surrounding hepatic parenchyma over a longer period of time (allowing for persistent APHE and washout that is stable or decreasing in size and enhancement and over time).

Unfortunately, CT and MRI may not always be possible due to renal dysfunction, contrast allergies or other contraindications. In addition, CT or MRI findings may be undetermined, with either non-evaluable observations due to image artifact, or because of indeterminate findings. In these cases, another assessment modality may be needed. Based on early studies involving the use of contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) in evaluating HCC after locoregional therapy (9, 10), the ACR LI-RADS CEUS Working Group published the LI-RADS CEUS Non-Radiation TRA in 2024 (11), mirroring the lexicon and response categories of the CT/MRI TRA algorithm. Early evidence supporting its use is promising (12). This article aims to review the CEUS TRA algorithm, highlight how CEUS can be used as a troubleshooting tool in specific clinical situations or after indeterminate results at CT or MRI, discuss ongoing challenges, and introduce future directions.

Technical tips for successful CEUS assessment

A successful CEUS assessment requires several key technical considerations (17). First, a good and consistent acoustic window to the treated lesion is key to ensure optimal image quality and assessment of the entire intralesional and perilesional area. Minimizing distance from skin-to-lesion, and minimizing artifacts such as from shadowing bowel, lungs, or ribs, improves image quality (16). Typically, CEUS is performed in the coronal plane, allowing the treated lesion to remain in view during normal patient respiration.

Optimal contrast dose may vary depending on the scanner and transducer used, while cardiac output and hepatic perfusion may impact timing of contrast arrival. After an initial injection, adjustments to contrast dose, as well as patient position and acoustic window, can be attempted for an additional injection to improve study quality. A subsequent injection can also be used to scan the treated lesion in another plane, further improving complete imaging of the lesion, and improving diagnostic confidence.

Contraindications to CT or MR imaging

In many cases, imaging by CT or MRI may be challenging or otherwise contraindicated. Typically, radiation exposure in adults with history of HCC is not a concern. However, poor renal function (such as glomerular filtration rate, GFR < 30 mL/min) may require alternative contrast-enhanced imaging modalities, which could include MRI or CEUS (Fig. 4). In addition, severe allergies to iodinated or gadolinium-based agents often requires an alternative imaging modality. Fortunately, adverse reactions from ultrasound contrast administration are exceedingly rare. CEUS is an excellent alternative for patients with significant contraindications to iodinated or gadolinium based agents.

Contrast mode image with B-mode (left) and contrast-only (right), in the arterial phase 30 s after contrast injection (A), show an ablation cavity slightly larger than the hyperechoic nodule without masslike intra-lesional or peri-lesional arterial phase hyperenhancement. In the late phase (B), no masslike perilesion washout is shown, qualifying as Treatment Response Assessment (TRA) category Nonviable (LR TR Nonviable)

Additional contraindications to MRI may include incompatible implanted devices, hardware, or other foreign bodies. A patient’s inability to lay flat for a prolonged period of time or suspend respiration, or severe claustrophobia, may also preclude the acquisition of a diagnostic MR examination. In these cases, US can often accommodate these patients.

Nonevaluable CT or MR imaging

In some cases, key series or phases are omitted or degraded by motion or other artifact such that major features including arterial phase hyperenhancement or washout cannot be determined. One of the most common causes for non-evaluable designation is the mistiming of the arterial phase. Since dynamic post-contrast CT and MR imaging is performed at specific timepoints after contrast administration, the ideal timepoint during the arterial phase during which the observation is the most enhanced may be missed. Since CEUS is a real-time imaging modality with high temporal resolution during the arterial phase, the risk of missing peak enhancement is negligible, particularly since ultrasound contrast may be re-injected during the same examination (Fig. 5).

Cautions and other considerations

While the LI-RADS CEUS Nonradiation TRA algorithm provides a rapid, non-invasive, and highly effective means of monitoring non-radiation locoregional therapy for HCC—as well as selected cases of non-HCC malignancies such as intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) and combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma (cHCC-CCA)—several technical and clinical pitfalls must be recognized to ensure accurate interpretation.

Poor visibility of the treated lesion on pre-contrast B-mode image

The fundamental principle of TRA lies in the precise correlation of post-treatment findings with the pretreatment lesion location. This process can be severely compromised when the treated lesion is poorly visualized on baseline B-mode ultrasound, particularly in the case of deep-seated or sub-diaphragmatic lesions. Acoustic shadowing from ribs, lung, or bowel gas may obscure the target, making it challenging to position the transducer optimally for continuous cine-loop acquisition during the critical arterial phase. If the lesion is not clearly seen on baseline B-mode though becomes apparent as a perfusion defect during the portal venous or late phase of the initial CEUS injection, a repeat injection can be performed. By focusing on the now-visible defect, the operator can specifically assess its arterial-phase enhancement patterns during the subsequent contrast injection, thereby improving diagnostic confidence.

Poor delineation of the resection cavity

Assessment of a resection cavity presents unique challenges distinct from those encountered with ablative therapies (18). A major pitfall in evaluating resection cavities is the presence of echogenic structures such as sutures or titanium clips, which can produce acoustic artifacts and potentially mimic or obscure true contrast enhancement on CEUS (16). Furthermore, gas within adjacent bowel loops can obscure the resection margin, limiting comprehensive evaluation. Correlation with B-mode images, and other cross-sectional imaging, may help define the resection cavity margin from other structures in close proximity to the cavity.

New observations distinct from the treated lesion

The detection of a new, untreated observation during a CEUS-based TRA is a clinically critical finding. In such cases, the focus of the examination should expand from treatment response assessment alone to include a standard diagnostic LI-RADS evaluation of the new lesion. The presence of a new LR-4 or LR-5 or LR-M lesion—even if the index treated lesion is classified as nonviable—indicates disease progression and may significantly alter subsequent management (Fig. 10).

Inability to screen for metastatic disease outside the liver

A critical limitation of CEUS in the oncologic context is its restricted field of view. Although CEUS excels at characterizing focal liver lesions and assessing their treatment response, it is not suitable for whole-body staging. This is particularly relevant in patients with high-risk features—such as large tumor size or persistently elevated serum alpha-fetoprotein levels—in whom the risk of extrahepatic metastasis is increased. While CEUS can confirm local tumor control, it does not evaluate the lungs, bones, lymph nodes, or peritoneum for metastatic involvement. Therefore, patient management should integrate CEUS findings with periodic contrast-enhanced CT and/or MRI based on individual risk stratification.

Similarly, CEUS TRA is not the modality of choice for the assessment of response to systemic therapies. Patients undergoing systemic therapy are usually in the advanced stage and therefore suffering from or at high risk of extraehapatic spread. Therefore, the focus on one single target may not be suitable as a general rule. However, in select cases, where uncertaintly remains about one single target and the verification of whether such a lesion is still viable or completely necrotic is clinically relevant, then CEUS TRA can become a key imaging modality. In such instances, the standard criteria listed herein for locoregional treatments may be applied, with caution.

Other considerations

It is essential to recognize that tumors are three-dimensional structures, and therefore the entire treated volume should be dynamically scanned during CEUS evaluation. Restricting the assessment to a single plane—even the presumed maximal cross-section—increases the risk of missing viable foci off plane. If necessary, a repeat contrast injection superimposed on the late phase can be performed with a different transducer orientation to detect any arterial hyperenhancement within the non-enhancing treatment zone, which may indicate residual viable disease. A repeat contrast injection in the late phase may improve the detection of intralesional recurrence.

Future directions

CEUS and radiation-based therapies

Recent years have witnessed an increasing utilization of radiation-based treatments for primary malignant liver tumors including TARE and SBRT (19). Regardless of the method of delivery, radiation-based therapies offer an effective method for reducing tumor burden. Cellular senescence, a unique therapeutic response to radiation, is mediated by injury to the cellular DNA, producing irreversible cellular damage with subsequent failure of cellular proliferation (20). Additionally, the senescent cells secrete pro-inflammatory factors which may affect both the tumor and the surrounding uninvolved liver parenchyma by inciting inflammation and further tissue damage.

Tumor response assessment following radiation requires special consideration: both the tumor and the peritumoral tissue require careful evaluation, recognizing that radiation effects are possible within both the tumor and surrounding parenchyma, and evolve over time (21). Importantly, post-treatment imaging may continue to show a tumor site with APHE and washout, similar to pretherapy scans, as the radiated senescent cells may not die for many months following therapy–This observation may be indistinguishable from viable tumor. Similarly, peritumoral reactive changes often show arterial hyperenhancement, most often in a geographic or wedge-shaped configuration. Residual/recurrent tumor may also exist within this area and show mass-like hyperenhancement with washout either within the tumor site or within the peritumoral tissue.

This common observation following TARE has thus required formation of aspecific category for TRA in CT and MRI called LR-TR nonprogressing to describe any tumor which continues to show mass-like APHE and washout with interval size stability or reduction over time–This suggests a favorable response and the anticipation is that over time, the tumor will eventually shrink and show reduced blood flow.

CEUS assessment following SBRT or TARE is not as yet incorporated into the official LI-RADS algorithms, although the application of the existing criteria for CT/MR in early studies may suggest that the same basic principles could apply and that their incorporation may occur in the future.

The essentials of such an algorithm include assessment for mass-like enhancement (any degree, any phase), and its change over time, in treated lesion or along its margin (Fig. 13). However, there are important considerations. The first is mentioned above and deals with the possibility that a tumor may continue to show APHE and washout for many months following TARE before the tumor declares its ultimate course.

Second, the nature of radiation therapy results in peri-lesional post radiation reactive change. This generally consists of geographic, perilesional, rim or otherwise wedge-shaped regions of tissue showing APHE and possibly even marked washout, frequently more generous in size than the arterial phase would suggest. The presence of APHE with washout is suggestive of malignancy although we now believe that likely the secreted pro-inflammatory factors are the major component resulting in the APHE and washout.

Radiation segmentectomy and radiation lobectomy, whether palliative or curative intent, are important aspects of TARE therapy which are beyond the scope of this manuscript. Regardless, these challenging assessments benefit from interval surveillance over many months following the radiation procedure.

Use of Kupffer cell agents

Certain microbubble-based contrast agents demonstrate a unique ability to be phagocytosed by hepatic Kupffer cells after the initial vascular phases, enabling prolonged visualization of the liver parenchyma (perfluorobutane microspheres; Sonazoid, GE HealthCare, Oslo, Norway). This agent allows for both vascular-phase and delayed Kupffer-phase imaging, functionally similar to a hepatobiliary phase of imaging in MRI achieved with agents such as Gadoxetate disodium. A contrast “defect” during the delayed Kupffer-phase of enhancement indicates a lesion failing to contain normal hepatic parenchyma, and can be used to screen for observations suspicious for HCC (improving sensitivity), complimenting the vascular-phase images currently used for HCC diagnosis.

However, the evaluation of treatment response in HCC relies primarily on changes in the degree and pattern of arterial phase enhancement. The absence of arterial enhancement in a treated lesion is a reliable indicator of successful therapy, whereas the presence of enhancement may signify residual, viable tumor. Fortunately, Kupffer phase defects can be re-evaluated with an additional injection of contrast and imaging during the vascular phase following reinjection, and the reappearance of arterial enhancement confirms tumor viability (22, 23). Furthermore, high–mechanical index (MI) contrast imaging with Sonazoid can be recommended for differentiating necrotic from viable areas, especially in hyperechoic or deep-seated HCCs, where conventional low-mechanical index CEUS often limits depth of penetration and may underestimate enhancement (23).

Conclusion

ACR LI-RADS has made a significant impact in the diagnosis and management of HCC, and newer management algorithms provide a framework for treatment response assessment. The new CEUS Non-Radiation TRA algorithm allows users to leverage the benefits of CEUS in the assessment of treatment response. CEUS may be used as a trouble-shooting tool when CT or MRI is impractical, not possible, or results are indeterminate. Specific considerations and challenges exist; however, several future opportunities may include the incorporation of specific Kupffer cell agents, and expanding the use of CEUS to radiation-based treatment response assessment.
