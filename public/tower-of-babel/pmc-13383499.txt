Comparison of mHealth-supported and centre-based cardiac rehabilitation in patients with stable coronary heart disease: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiaobing Yang, Jing Liang, Yuling Li, Jingmei Miao, Yanfang Xu, Wenjuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-intensity interval training (HIIT) is an effective exercise modality in cardiac rehabilitation; however, participation in conventional centre-based programs remains suboptimal due to barriers such as limited accessibility and adherence. Mobile health (mHealth) technologies may improve accessibility and supervision of exercise training. While previous studies have primarily focused on training modality, evidence comparing different delivery models of HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation remains limited. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of mHealth-supported versus centre-based delivery of a standardized HIIT cardiac rehabilitation program in patients with stable coronary heart disease (CHD).MethodsBetween December 2024 and April 2025, 98 adults with stable CHD eligible for Phase II cardiac rehabilitation were enrolled and randomly assigned to either the control group (n = 49) or the intervention group (n = 49). Both groups performed the same standardized HIIT protocol; the control group received supervised centre-based rehabilitation, whereas the intervention group performed mHealth-supported home-based training with real-time monitoring and individualized feedback via the Xin’ankang mHealth platform. Primary outcomes included the six-minute walk distance (6MWD), cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) parameters, and SF-36 quality of life scores. Secondary outcomes included anxiety, depression, and sleep quality.ResultsBaseline characteristics were generally comparable between groups, although minor differences were observed in certain SF-36 domains. After adjustment for baseline values, the intervention group demonstrated significant improvements compared to the control group in sleep quality scores (3.17 ± 2.09 vs. 4.83 ± 2.09), peak METs (5.83 ± 0.09 vs. 4.94 ± 0.09), anaerobic threshold METs (4.29 ± 0.07 vs. 3.53 ± 0.07), peak VO₂ (20.12 ± 0.30 vs. 16.70 ± 0.30 ml/kg/min), and 6MWD (563.83 ± 3.21 vs. 531.49 ± 3.21 m) (all P < 0.05). Improvements in SF-36 scores were observed in several domains, although no significant difference was found in vitality or mental health. Subgroup analyses revealed consistent improvements across age groups, while a sex-specific difference was observed for changes in VO₂ peak.ConclusionCompared with centre-based delivery of the same HIIT program, mHealth-supported HIIT was associated with greater improvements in cardiopulmonary function, exercise capacity, and quality of life in patients with CHD. These findings suggest that delivery modality may influence the effectiveness of HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation.Trial registrationClinical Trial Registration Number: ChiCTR2500104462, June 17, 2025.Keywords: Coronary heart disease, High-intensity interval training, Cardiac rehabilitation, Mobile health, Quality of life

Introduction

Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a key component of secondary prevention and long-term management for patients with cardiovascular disease [1]. Exercise-based CR has been shown to improve endothelial function, enhance cardiopulmonary fitness, reduce cardiovascular risk, and improve quality of life in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) [2]. Traditionally, moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) has been recommended as the primary exercise modality; however, a review of existing trials suggests that high-intensity interval training (HIIT) may produce greater improvements in peak oxygen uptake, exercise capacity, and health-related quality of life among cardiac populations [3]. HIIT typically involves brief bouts of vigorous exercise (85%–95% HR_max) interspersed with recovery periods at lower intensities (50%–70% HR_max), generating substantial cardiopulmonary benefits within relatively short sessions [4]. HIIT comprises various interval-based exercise protocols that vary in work duration, intensity, and recovery structure. While early HIIT models, particularly in untrained populations, used shorter work bouts with longer recovery periods (e.g., work-to-rest ratios approximating 1:2), cardiac rehabilitation studies adopted longer high-intensity intervals with relatively shorter active recovery periods.

Despite these promising advantages, the application of HIIT in routine cardiac rehabilitation remains limited, especially among patients with stable CHD, a population that often experiences reduced exercise tolerance and persistent functional impairment [5, 6]. Although centre-based supervised CR remains the standard model, participation rates are suboptimal due to barriers such as geographical distance, limited program availability, work-related constraints, and low long-term adherence [7, 8]. These challenges are particularly relevant for stable CHD patients, who represent a heterogeneous and increasingly younger population requiring long-term, sustainable rehabilitation strategies.

Digital health technologies, particularly mobile health (mHealth) platforms, offer a promising solution to these barriers by enabling remote monitoring, improving accessibility, and supporting individualized exercise prescription [9]. Previous systematic reviews and clinical studies have demonstrated the feasibility of mHealth-supported cardiac rehabilitation programs [10, 11]. However, most existing interventions have primarily relied on MICT and have several methodological limitations, including small sample sizes, absence of objective cardiopulmonary outcomes, limited evaluation of safety, and lack of randomized controlled designs [12]. Importantly, evidence supporting the integration of HIIT within mHealth-based rehabilitation remains scarce, particularly in patients with stable CHD. In addition, concerns regarding exercise intensity regulation and safety monitoring during remotely delivered HIIT programs have not been adequately addressed. These knowledge gaps underscore the need for structured approaches that combine HIIT with real-time physiological monitoring to ensure both safety and optimized training intensity.

Given these gaps, there is a need to evaluate whether different delivery models of HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation can achieve comparable or improved outcomes in patients with stable CHD. Therefore, this study compared two delivery approaches of a standardized HIIT cardiac rehabilitation program: mHealth-supported home-based training versus traditional centre-based supervised training. The study aimed to evaluate differences in cardiopulmonary function, exercise capacity, quality of life, psychological outcomes, and safety between the two approaches. We hypothesized that mHealth-supported delivery of HIIT would result in greater improvements in cardiopulmonary fitness and functional exercise capacity compared with centre-based HIIT rehabilitation. By focusing on the mode of delivery rather than the training modality itself, this study provides clinically relevant insights into optimizing the implementation of HIIT in cardiac rehabilitation for patients with stable CHD.

Methods

Study design

This study was a randomized, parallel-controlled, assessor-blind, single-center trial conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Participants

A total of 98 patients with stable CHD undergoing cardiac rehabilitation at our hospital between December 2024 and April 2025 were enrolled in this study and randomly assigned to either the control group or the intervention group (n = 49 per group). The reported period refers to patient recruitment; rehabilitation was initiated immediately after enrolment on a rolling basis, allowing all participants to complete the full intervention.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) a confirmed diagnosis of stable CHD in accordance with the 2019 European Society of Cardiology guidelines [13], with low to moderate exercise risk based on the American Association of Cardiovascular and Pulmonary Rehabilitation (AACVPR) risk stratification; (b) age ≥ 18 years with no upper age limit specified and a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) > 45%; (c) ability to comply with the exercise protocol, with written informed consent provided.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) the presence of severe pulmonary disease, renal insufficiency, musculoskeletal conditions that contraindicate physical activity, or other serious chronic illnesses (e.g., malignancies); (b) patients with neurological or neuromuscular disorders, cognitive impairment, or communication difficulties.

Baseline functional capacity was assessed in all participants prior to randomization using standardized cardiopulmonary exercise testing and the six-minute walk test. These assessments were used for baseline characterization and outcome evaluation rather than as inclusion criteria. Randomization after baseline assessment ensured comparable baseline fitness between groups.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using the formula for comparing two independent means: N=2(µα+µβ)2 σ2/ δ2. According to the study by Chen et al. [14] on the impact of exercise-based rehabilitation on cardiopulmonary function and prognosis in post-PCI patients, the peak metabolic equivalents (METs) after intervention were 5.17 ± 1.00 in the intervention group and 4.62 ± 0.93 in the control group. Assuming α = 0.05, β = 0.1, µα = 1.96, and µβ = 1.28, the estimated required sample size per group was 41, yielding a total of 82 participants. Considering an anticipated attrition rate of 10% to 20%, the sample size was increased to 98 participants.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned to the control or intervention group using a computer-generated random sequence. Allocation was concealed prior to group assignment. Due to the nature of the intervention, participants and exercise supervisors were not blinded to group allocation. However, outcome assessors and data analysts remained blinded throughout the study to minimize detection and analysis bias.

Outcome measures were collected at baseline and immediately after the 12-week intervention by blinded assessors.

Exercise adherence and quality control

Exercise adherence was defined as the proportion of completed valid training sessions during the intervention period. A session was considered valid if participants completed 30 min of prescribed HIIT. Participants were scheduled to complete 48 sessions over 12 weeks. Adherence was calculated as the number of completed sessions divided by the total expected sessions.

In the intervention group, adherence was recorded automatically via the mHealth platform, whereas in the control group, adherence was assessed through structured telephone follow-up. Although adherence was measured using different methods between groups, standardized definitions of valid sessions were applied to ensure comparability.

Data were entered into a dedicated database using a double-entry system to ensure accuracy. Discrepancies were resolved through verification procedures. Standardized protocols were followed to ensure consistency in data collection.

Intervention protocol

Both groups received a standardized HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation program using a treadmill-based 4 × 4-minute interval protocol. Each session consisted of four 4-minute high-intensity intervals performed at 85%–95% of age-predicted maximum heart rate (HR_max = 220–age), interspersed with 3-minute active recovery periods at 50%–60% HR_max (work-to-rest ratio ≈ 4:3) [15]. Although HIIT protocols vary considerably in structure, this longer-interval model has been extensively applied in cardiac populations and has demonstrated superior or comparable improvements in peak oxygen uptake relative to traditional moderate-intensity continuous training. Compared with shorter-interval models employing longer recovery periods, the 4 × 4-minute protocol allows patients to sustain a higher cumulative time at near-maximal oxygen uptake, a key driver of aerobic adaptation.

Training was conducted four times per week for 12 weeks, with each session lasting approximately 30 min. Exercise intensity progressed gradually based on baseline cardiopulmonary exercise testing and individual tolerance. Treadmill speed or workload was increased stepwise to achieve the target heart-rate range. If participants were unable to reach or sustain the prescribed intensity, progression was delayed or adjusted according to tolerance and safety considerations. The selection of this protocol in the present study was therefore based on its established efficacy, feasibility, and safety profile in patients with coronary heart disease.

Control group (Centre-Based HIIT)

Participants in the control group underwent conventional centre-based supervised HIIT rehabilitation, in addition to standard medical therapy and lifestyle education, including dietary guidance, medication adherence, and risk factor management.

Intervention group (mHealth-Supported HIIT)

Participants in the intervention group performed the same standardized HIIT protocol but with mHealth-supported delivery. After an initial supervised familiarization phase, participants transitioned to home-based training supported by the Xin’ankang mobile health platform. The platform enabled continuous heart rate and ECG monitoring, real-time data transmission, and remote supervision by clinicians. Exercise intensity adjustments followed the same predefined progression criteria as in the control group, with additional real-time feedback and individualized guidance provided through the mHealth platform. Monitoring and safety

During all training sessions, heart rate and ECG signals were continuously monitored. Alert thresholds were predefined based on CPET-derived target heart-rate ranges. Exercise intensity was adjusted dynamically to ensure participants remained within the prescribed intensity range. Exercise sessions were terminated if any of the following occurred: typical angina, significant symptoms (e.g., dyspnea, dizziness, pallor, or gait instability), or excessive blood pressure elevation (systolic blood pressure > 160 mmHg).

Development of the intervention

The intervention protocol was developed by a multidisciplinary team comprising cardiologists, rehabilitation therapists, and cardiac rehabilitation nurses. Expert consultations were conducted to ensure the scientific validity, safety, and feasibility of the program.

A pilot feasibility study involving 15 patients with stable CHD was performed prior to the main trial. Safety was assessed by monitoring exercise-related adverse events, while feasibility was evaluated based on participants’ ability to complete prescribed training sessions and adherence rates. No serious adverse events were observed, and the intervention was considered feasible. Findings from the pilot were used to refine the final intervention protocol, particularly with respect to exercise intensity progression, monitoring procedures, and participant instruction before home-based training. Based on these results, the final version of the intervention protocol was confirmed and implemented in the main study.

mHealth platform

Xin’ankang is a medical platform designed to support patients in self-monitoring heart rate during prevention and rehabilitation. It consists of a mobile application (Xin’ankang App), an electrocardiogram (ECG) recorder (model: CY-HR-02), and a physician interface. The wearable ECG device was used to continuously record ECG signals, heart rate, and cardiac rhythm during exercise sessions. All physiological data were transmitted wirelessly in real time to the mobile application and simultaneously uploaded to the physician interface, enabling continuous remote monitoring, accurate assessment of exercise intensity, and timely safety supervision.

Participants in the intervention group were trained to use the platform prior to home-based rehabilitation. During exercise sessions, physiological data were transmitted in real time, allowing clinicians to monitor exercise intensity and provide timely feedback when necessary.

Primary outcome measures

6MWD

6MWD was conducted according to established American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) guidelines. Participants were instructed to walk as far as possible within six minutes along a standardized indoor corridor. The total distance covered (6MWD) was recorded in metres. Heart rate and symptoms were monitored before and immediately after the test to ensure safety.

Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing (CPET)

CPET was performed using a symptom-limited incremental treadmill protocol in accordance with international guidelines. Breath-by-breath gas exchange analysis was used to measure VO₂ peak, METs, and anaerobic threshold (AT METs). Hemodynamic and electrocardiographic parameters were continuously monitored during testing.

SF-36

Health-related quality of life was assessed using the validated Medical Outcomes Study Short Form-36 (SF-36). The questionnaire comprises eight domains scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better perceived health status. The SF-36 has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity, with reported Cronbach’s α coefficients generally exceeding 0.80 in cardiovascular populations [16].

Secondary outcome measures

Anxiety and depression

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the validated Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item Scale (GAD-7), and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Both instruments are widely used and have demonstrated good internal consistency and validity in clinical populations. Total scores range from 0 to 21 for the GAD-7 and from 0 to 27 for the PHQ-9, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. Both instruments have demonstrated strong internal consistency, with reported Cronbach’s α coefficients typically greater than 0.85 [17].

Sleep quality

Sleep quality was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a validated instrument that assesses sleep quality over the previous month. The global PSQI score rangs from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. The PSQI has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in diverse populations (Cronbach’s α approximately 0.80) [18].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Baseline comparability between the control and intervention groups was assessed using independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests and graphical inspection of histograms.

The primary analysis of intervention effects was conducted using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with post-intervention values as dependent variables, group as a fixed factor, and baseline values as covariates. Adjusted means with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared (η²). For interpretative purposes, values of approximately 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 were considered to represent small, moderate, and large effects, respectively, based on conventional benchmarks. Subgroup analyses were performed using baseline-adjusted linear regression models with interaction terms to assess effect modification by sex and age. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 103 participants were initially enrolled in the study, with 52 assigned to the intervention group and 51 to the control group (Fig. 2). Among them, 2 participants withdrew due to personal reasons, 1 was lost to follow-up, and 2 were hospitalized for cerebrovascular events and thus discontinued the study. Ultimately, 98 participants completed the follow-up, with 49 in the intervention group and 49 in the control group, resulting in a loss-to-follow-up rate of 4.8%. Baseline demographic, CAD-related, and clinical characteristics demonstrated no statistically significant differences between the two groups (P > 0.05), indicating good comparability. Adherence to exercise intervention

Exercise adherence was assessed based on the completion of prescribed HIIT sessions. The intervention group demonstrated a higher exercise adherence rate compared with the control group (79% vs. 52%). Adherence was monitored via the mHealth platform in the intervention group and assessed through telephone follow-up in the control group. These findings suggest that the mHealth-supported delivery may be associated with improved adherence to prescribed exercise training.

Comparison of cardiopulmonary and functional outcomes after intervention

At baseline, no significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups in 6MWD, peak VO₂, peak METs, or AT METs (all P > 0.05), indicating good comparability (Table 2). After the intervention, the intervention group demonstrated significantly greater improvements than the control group across all cardiopulmonary and functional outcomes (all P < 0.001, Table 3). Specifically, peak VO₂ was significantly higher in the intervention group compared with the control group (F = 64.78, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.405), indicating a large effect size according to our thresholds. Similarly, significant between-group differences were observed for peak METs (F = 51.69, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.352), AT METs (F = 57.70, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.378), and 6MWD (F = 50.58, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.347), all of which correspond to large effects.

Comparison of quality of life outcomes after intervention

At baseline, most SF-36 domains were comparable between the intervention and control groups (all P > 0.05); however, significant differences were observed in the vitality (VT) and mental health (MH) domains (P < 0.05) (Table 2). Therefore, subsequent between-group comparisons were conducted using ANCOVA adjusting for baseline values (Table 5). After the intervention, the results showed that the intervention group demonstrated significantly greater improvements than the control group in several SF-36 domains, including physical functioning (PF) (F = 23.59, P < 0.001), role-physical (RP) (F = 70.95, P < 0.001), bodily pain (BP) (F = 73.65, P < 0.001), general health (GH) (F = 100.32, P < 0.001), social functioning (SF) (F = 73.24, P < 0.001), and role-emotional (RE) (F = 54.75, P < 0.001). In addition, a significant improvement was observed in health transition (HT) (F = 8.29, P = 0.005). In contrast, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed in VT (F = 0.04, P = 0.852) or MH (F = 0.002, P = 0.966), indicating that the effects of the intervention on quality of life were not uniform across all domains.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess whether the effects of the intervention on cardiopulmonary exercise capacity and functional performance differed by age or sex. Overall, the intervention group demonstrated significantly greater improvements than the control group in both VO₂ peak and 6MWD.

For changes in VO₂ peak (Fig. 3), significant improvements favoring the intervention group were observed across all sex and age subgroups. A significant interaction between treatment group and sex was observed (P for interaction = 0.003). The magnitude of improvement appeared to be greater in male participants (β = 4.23, 95% CI: 3.21–5.24; P < 0.001) than in female participants (β = 1.40, 95% CI: 0.12–2.68; P = 0.041). No significant interaction was observed between treatment group and age (P for interaction = 0.705).

Changes in VO₂ peak before and after the intervention by age and gender in the two groups

For changes in 6MWD (Fig. 4), significant improvements were consistently observed in the intervention group across all sex and age subgroups. No significant interaction was found between treatment group and sex (P for interaction = 0.891) or age (P for interaction = 0.512).

These results suggested that mHealth-supported HIIT was associated with consistent improvements across age groups, with a potential sex-related difference in peak VO₂ response; however, these findings were exploratory and should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial, we compared two delivery modalities of the same HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation program: mHealth-supported home-based rehabilitation versus conventional centre-based rehabilitation. The findings indicate that mHealth-supported delivery was associated with greater improvements in cardiopulmonary fitness and functional exercise capacity, while maintaining an acceptable safety profile in patients with stable coronary heart disease.

Impact of mHealth-supported HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation on cardiopulmonary function and physical performance

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated the efficacy of HIIT in improving cardiopulmonary fitness and exercise capacity in cardiac populations [19, 20]. Building on this evidence, the present study further suggests that the mode of delivery may influence the magnitude of these benefits. Participants in the mHealth-supported group exhibited significantly greater improvements in peak VO₂, peak METs, anaerobic threshold METs, and 6-minute walk distance compared with those receiving centre-based rehabilitation.

A notable finding of this study is the higher exercise adherence observed in the intervention group compared with the control group (79% vs. 52%). This difference in adherence may partly explain the superior improvements in cardiopulmonary outcomes. The integration of the mHealth platform enabled real-time monitoring of heart rate and electrocardiographic data, as well as timely feedback and remote supervision, which may have facilitated better compliance with prescribed training intensity and frequency [21]. However, these interpretations should be considered exploratory.

Impact of mHealth-supported HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation on quality of life outcomes

Reduced exercise capacity and long-term functional limitations are common among patients with CHD and are closely associated with quality of life (QOL). In the present study, improvements were observed in several domains of the SF-36 questionnaire in the intervention group compared with the control group, although not all dimensions demonstrated significant between-group differences. These findings highlight the heterogeneous impact of the intervention on health-related quality of life. Improvements appeared to be more pronounced in physical and role-related domains, whereas no significant differences were observed in vitality or mental health domains. This pattern suggests that mHealth-supported HIIT may exert stronger effects on physical functioning than on psychological dimensions.

Previous studies have also reported improvements in quality of life following HIIT-based rehabilitation [22–24]. The additional contribution of mobile health supervision may lie in its ability to provide continuous feedback and support during the behavior change process, allowing patients to monitor their responses to exercise and adjust training behaviors accordingly [25, 26]. Nevertheless, given that some SF-36 domains did not differ significantly between groups in the present study, these results should be interpreted with caution.

Impact of mHealth-supported HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation on psychological and sleep outcomes

Previous studies have reported mixed effects of exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation on psychological outcomes [27–30]. In the present study, no significant between-group differences were observed in anxiety or depressive symptoms, suggesting that mHealth-supported HIIT did not confer additional psychological benefits beyond those achieved with centre-based rehabilitation within the study period. This may be related to the relatively mild baseline symptom burden or the limited duration of follow-up. In contrast, a significant improvement in sleep quality was observed in the intervention group. This finding may indicate that sleep-related outcomes are more responsive to exercise-based interventions or may improve over shorter time frames than psychological measures. Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted cautiously given the sample size and follow-up duration.

Safety considerations of mHealth-Supported HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation

Safety is a central concern in the implementation of remote or technology-supported cardiac rehabilitation, particularly when HIIT is prescribed. In the present study, safety was addressed through careful patient selection, individualized exercise prescription based on CPET, and continuous physiological monitoring via the mHealth platform. Throughout the intervention period, no serious adverse cardiovascular events were observed. Continuous monitoring of heart rate and ECG allowed timely detection of abnormal responses and enabled appropriate adjustments to exercise intensity. These findings suggest that, when supported by appropriate patient selection, individualized exercise prescription, and real-time physiological monitoring, mHealth-supported HIIT can be implemented with an acceptable safety profile in patients with stable CHD.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, it was conducted at a single center with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit generalizability and reduce statistical power to detect small between-group differences. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 12 weeks, and the long-term sustainability of improvements in cardiopulmonary function, quality of life, and exercise adherence remains uncertain. Third, subgroup analyses were exploratory and not powered to detect interaction effects, and therefore should be interpreted with caution. In addition, adherence was assessed using different methods between groups, which may introduce measurement bias despite the use of standardized definitions. Baseline differences observed in certain SF-36 domains were addressed using statistical adjustment; however, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, the study was not designed to investigate underlying mechanisms, and the observed associations between mHealth support and improved outcomes should be interpreted cautiously. Future multicenter trials with larger samples, longer follow-up, and mechanistic assessments are needed to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial suggests that mHealth-supported delivery of HIIT-based cardiac rehabilitation may lead to greater improvements in cardiopulmonary function and exercise capacity compared with centre-based rehabilitation in patients with stable CHD. These benefits may be partly related to improved exercise adherence. Effects on quality of life were domain-specific, and no additional benefits were observed in anxiety or depressive symptoms. Further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are required to confirm these findings and to evaluate long-term clinical outcomes and sustainability.
