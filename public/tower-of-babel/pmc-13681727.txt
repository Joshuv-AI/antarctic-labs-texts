Spinal subependymomas are rare, benign WHO grade 1 tumors that account for only a small proportion of spinal tumors.1 They are slow-growing lesions that most commonly arise in the cervical spinal cord and generally exhibit favorable long-term outcomes following surgical treatment.2 Because of their rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation, preoperative diagnosis remains challenging, and the definitive diagnosis is frequently established only after histopathological examination.3

Radiologically, spinal subependymomas typically appear as eccentrically located intramedullary tumors with minimal or no contrast enhancement and are therefore most commonly mistaken for other intramedullary tumors, particularly ependymomas and astrocytomas.2–6 An intradural extramedullary tumor-like appearance is exceedingly uncommon and may lead to diagnostic confusion as well as inappropriate intraoperative decision-making. Here, we report a rare case of cervical spinal subependymoma with marked exophytic growth that mimicked an intradural extramedullary tumor on MRI. We also discuss the radiological pitfalls, the impact of anchoring bias during surgery, and the optimal surgical strategy.

Illustrative Case

Clinical History

A 53-year-old man presented to another hospital with right-sided paresthesia. MRI revealed a cervical spinal tumor, and he was referred to our institution for further evaluation and treatment. On admission, the patient complained only of right-sided paresthesia and had no motor weakness or gait disturbance. MRI demonstrated a dorsal tumor at the C1–2 level causing marked compression of the spinal cord. The lesion was isointense to the spinal cord on T1-weighted (T1W) images and slightly hyperintense on T2-weighted (T2W) images (Fig. 1A and B). No gadolinium enhancement was identified (Fig. 1C). The tumor was relatively well demarcated from the normal spinal cord, and a CSF cleft was focally observed between the lesion and the spinal cord (Fig. 1D and E). CT showed no calcification within the lesion (Fig. 1F). Based on these imaging findings, an intradural extramedullary tumor was suspected. Although the absence of contrast enhancement was atypical, schwannoma was considered the most likely diagnosis, and elective tumor resection was performed. FIG. 1.Preoperative images. Surgical Findings

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position. The occipital bone to the C2 spinous process was exposed. A suboccipital craniotomy and partial laminectomy of C1 and the upper portion of C2 were performed, followed by dural opening. The tumor was found beneath an intact arachnoid membrane and extensively covered the dorsal surface of the spinal cord, making it difficult to visualize its entire extent (Fig. 2A and B). Initially, a small portion of the lesion surface was submitted for intraoperative pathological examination. Internal decompression was continued, and once the tumor had become sufficiently mobile, a nerve root was identified emerging from the tumor (Fig. 2C–E). An intraoperative pathological examination suggested a low-grade glioma, which for the first time raised the possibility of an intramedullary origin of the lesion. Gross-total resection was abandoned, and the tumor was removed as much as possible while carefully preserving the interface with the normal spinal cord. As internal decompression progressed, the tumor became increasingly mobile, and continuity between the tumor and the spinal cord was observed at the ventral surface of the tumor (Fig. 2F and G). Histopathological Findings

H&E staining demonstrated scattered clusters of small cells within a fibrillary background accompanied by microcystic changes (Fig. 3A and B). Neither perivascular pseudorosettes nor true ependymal rosettes, characteristic histological features of ependymoma, were observed. Nuclear atypia and mitotic activity were absent. Immunohistochemical analysis showed diffuse positivity for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and the Ki-67 labeling index was approximately 1% (Fig. 3C and D). Stainings for both epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) and SRY-related HMG-box gene 10 (SOX10) were negative (Fig. 3E and F), making extramedullary tumors such as meningioma and schwannoma, which had been suspected preoperatively, less likely. Staining for oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (OLIG2) was also negative (Fig. 3G), making an astrocytic tumor less likely. Based on these findings, the tumor was diagnosed as a spinal subependymoma. FIG. 3.Pathological findings. Postoperative Course

The patient developed right upper extremity weakness immediately after surgery, with a manual muscle test (MMT) grade of 3. The weakness improved to MMT grade 4 on postoperative day 1 and gradually improved with rehabilitation. The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 12 with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 2 and returned to work 2 months after surgery. At the 1-year follow-up, his mRS score had improved to 1 and his muscle strength had recovered to nearly the preoperative level, although right-sided paresthesia persisted. No adjuvant therapy was administered, and follow-up MRI has demonstrated no evidence of tumor progression (Fig. 4). One limitation of the present case is that the extent and volume of residual tumor are difficult to assess accurately because its signal intensity is relatively similar to that of the normal spinal cord, and it shows no contrast enhancement on MRI. We plan to perform follow-up MRI every 6–12 months and carefully monitor the patient for any clinical or radiological changes. FIG. 4.A: Sagittal T2W MR image obtained immediately after surgery. Discussion

Observations

Subependymomas are rare, benign WHO grade 1 tumors that account for less than 1% of all CNS tumors and represent only a small proportion of spinal ependymal tumors.1,7,8 They most commonly arise in the fourth and lateral ventricles, whereas spinal involvement is uncommon and predominantly occurs in the cervical and cervicothoracic regions.3,4 Owing to their indolent growth and nonspecific imaging findings, preoperative diagnosis remains challenging,6 and it is not uncommon for subependymoma to be diagnosed only after resection and subsequent histopathological examination.8

Radiologically, spinal subependymomas typically present as eccentrically located intramedullary tumors that are iso- to hypointense on T1W imaging, hyperintense on T2W imaging, and demonstrate minimal or absent gadolinium enhancement.4,5 As a result, they are often preoperatively mistaken for other intramedullary tumors, particularly ependymomas and astrocytomas.6 Toi et al. proposed that their subpial growth pattern results in a steep fusiform expansion of the spinal cord, termed the “bamboo leaf sign,” which may help distinguish subependymoma from ependymoma and astrocytoma.9 Overall, spinal subependymomas are generally recognized radiologically as intramedullary tumors. However, our case demonstrated none of these characteristic findings and instead closely mimicked an intradural extramedullary tumor. The lesion caused marked ventral displacement of the spinal cord, and an apparent CSF cleft was identified between the tumor and the cord, leading us to suspect schwannoma preoperatively (Fig. 5). Only a few reports have described spinal subependymomas with an exophytic or apparently extramedullary appearance. Do et al. reported a cervical subependymoma that appeared completely extramedullary on MRI and emphasized that subependymoma should remain in the differential diagnosis of spinal masses with an extramedullary appearance.10 Similar to their case, our patient had a dorsal cervical lesion with minimal enhancement and marked exophytic growth. However, our case is unique in that an apparent CSF cleft was identified preoperatively, further strengthening the impression of an intradural extramedullary lesion. We speculate that, rather than following its typical subpial growth pattern, the tumor expanded predominantly toward the subarachnoid space, thereby creating an apparent CSF cleft between the lesion and the spinal cord and mimicking an intradural extramedullary tumor.

Another important lesson from this case is the influence of anchoring bias on intraoperative decision-making. Because preoperative imaging strongly suggested an intradural extramedullary tumor, the intraoperative findings were initially interpreted within this diagnostic framework. Furthermore, because of the large size of the tumor, adequate mobilization and reflection of the lesion were difficult. Consequently, the point of attachment to the spinal cord could not be adequately identified (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the finding of a nerve root emerging from the tumor further reinforced the erroneous impression that the lesion was a schwannoma arising from a nerve root (Fig. 2E). The possibility of an intramedullary origin was not recognized until the intraoperative pathological examination suggested a low-grade glioma. In such a situation, inadvertent manipulation could have resulted in injury to the normal neural tissue. This case highlights that excessive reliance on preoperative imaging may bias intraoperative interpretation and delay recognition of the true pathology, underscoring the importance of maintaining diagnostic flexibility throughout surgery.

The present case also has important implications for surgical management. Subependymomas are benign and extremely slow-growing tumors with a low proliferative potential and a very low risk of recurrence. Several studies have shown favorable long-term outcomes even after subtotal resection.2,4,11 Soleiman et al. reviewed 105 reported cases and demonstrated that postoperative neurological deterioration occurred in more than half of patients undergoing gross-total resection, whereas malignant transformation has never been reported.2 Similarly, Jain et al. reported durable tumor control after subtotal resection in a series of subependymomas.4 Therefore, maximal safe resection rather than aggressive gross-total resection should be considered the preferred surgical strategy. In our patient, gross-total resection was abandoned once an intramedullary origin was suspected, and a small residual tumor was intentionally left to preserve neurological function. Although transient postoperative weakness developed, the patient recovered to nearly his preoperative neurological status and has shown no radiological progression during follow-up. This case highlights the importance of continuously reassessing the diagnosis and surgical strategy during surgery. Once a benign intramedullary tumor such as subependymoma is suspected, preservation of neurological function should take precedence over aggressive gross-total resection.

Lessons

Spinal subependymomas can rarely exhibit marked exophytic growth and mimic intradural extramedullary tumors on MRI. The presence of a CSF cleft does not necessarily indicate an extramedullary origin. Surgeons should remain aware of this possibility and avoid anchoring bias during intraoperative decision-making. Once an intramedullary origin is suspected, maximal safe resection should be prioritized over aggressive tumor removal to preserve neurological function.
