Delayed-Onset C1 Myelopathy after Cervical Laminoplasty
Authors: Yuto Kobayashi, Koji Tamai, Minori Kato, Hiromitsu Toyoda, Akinobu Suzuki, Shinji Takahashi, Yuta Sawada, Masayoshi Iwamae, Yuki Okamura, Masato Uematsu, Hiroshi Taniwaki, Ryo Sasaki, Yuki Kinoshita, Maya Suzuki, Masashi Tsujino, Hiroaki Nakamura, Hidetomi Terai
Published: 2026

Cervical laminoplasty is a motion-preserving procedure for multilevel cervical spondylotic myelopathy with favorable long-term outcomes1). Several delayed postoperative complications after laminoplasty, including progressive kyphosis and reduced cervical range of motion, have been reported2). However, involvement of the craniovertebral junction long after surgery has rarely been recognized3).

Previous studies have suggested that altered cervical biomechanics may contribute to increased mechanical stress at the C1-C2 complex and retro-odontoid pseudotumor formation4,5). In this report, we describe a series of patients in whom symptomatic C1 myelopathy developed in the late postoperative period.

Between January 2018 and December 2024, we retrospectively identified nine patients who underwent surgery for C1 myelopathy more than 1 year after primary cervical laminoplasty at our institution. The initial laminoplasty was performed from C3 to C6 with partial laminotomy at C2 and C7. These cases were identified through a review of surgical records and imaging databases. The mean interval between the initial and subsequent surgeries was approximately 13 years.

Preoperative imaging at the time of the initial laminoplasty was available in four cases. In these cases, no apparent cervical instability or kyphotic alignment was observed. However, congenital C1 hypoplasia, which was defined on the basis of the C3-C2 spinolaminar line test, was already present before primary surgery6). Neurological improvement after the initial laminoplasty was achieved and sustained at the 2-year follow-up (Table 1).

At the time of surgery for C1 myelopathy, patients commonly presented with posterior neck pain, progressive numbness, and impaired hand dexterity. Radiological examination showed reduced cervical range of motion compared with that at the time of the initial laminoplasty in available cases. All patients exhibited congenital C1 hypoplasia. Atlantoaxial instability and severe vertical subluxation (Table 2) were not observed on cervical radiographs, except in one patient7,8). Magnetic resonance imaging consistently revealed enlargement of the retro-odontoid soft tissue (Figure 1A and B). Computed tomography frequently showed pseudotumor calcification (Figure 2A and B), supporting a chronic degenerative process.

Because atlantoaxial instability was not identified on dynamic radiographs in most patients before surgery for C1 myelopathy, posterior fixation was not performed. All patients underwent isolated C1 posterior decompression without fusion. Postoperatively, neurological symptoms were relieved in all cases, particularly gait disturbance and upper extremity numbness. On follow-up imaging, the enlargement of the retro-odontoid soft tissue showed regression at 1 year postoperatively (Figure 1C and D). In no patient did new-onset atlantoaxial instability develop, nor was secondary fixation required during the follow-up period. These outcomes suggest that, in carefully selected patients without radiographic instability, decompression alone may be sufficient to achieve neurological improvement while preserving upper cervical motion.

This case series highlights that symptomatic C1 myelopathy can emerge as a rare but clinically important late phenomenon more than a decade after cervical laminoplasty. In the present series, congenital C1 hypoplasia was not recognized on preoperative imaging at the time of the initial laminoplasty and did not influence the postoperative course. This finding suggests that potential C1 stenosis may be overlooked in patients with cervical myelopathy, consistent with previous reports9). Chronic mechanical stress at the craniovertebral junction, associated with congenital C1 hypoplasia, may contribute to pseudotumor formation even in the absence of radiographic instability. Consistent with this concept, retro-odontoid pseudotumor has been reported to develop even in the absence of atlantoaxial instability, owing to chronic mechanical stress10). Surgeons should be aware of this potential late phenomenon when examining patients with new-onset occipital pain or progressive myelopathic symptoms long after cervical laminoplasty.
