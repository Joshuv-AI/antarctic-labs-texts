Regulatory B cell CCL3 competency promotes disease resolution and oligodendrogenesis in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis
Authors: Andrea Pennati, Xingyi Tang, Catigan Hedican, Amar Yeware, Md Mahfuzur Rahman, Owen A Zeitler, Shala Yuan, Jacques Galipeau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegulatory B cells (Bregs) contribute to immune homeostasis via IL-10–dependent and independent pathways. To dissect additional mediators, we investigated splenic derived GM-CSF/IL-15 fusokine (GIFT15)-induced Bregs in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) using transcriptomics and functional validation. Bulk RNA-seq of splenic Bregs revealed upregulation of Ccl3, GzmB, and Il27 subunits compared to resting B cells. Functional studies showed that CCL3-deficient Bregs failed to suppress disease, whereas GzmB-deficient Bregs retained efficacy, and IL-27 receptor signaling in recipients was dispensable. Flow cytometry demonstrated that CCL3 expression correlated with FoxP3⁺ Treg and Tr1 expansion, along with CD206⁺ macrophage polarization. In the CNS, transient, tissue-dependent increases in oligodendroglial markers O4 and GalC were detected. Collectively, these findings identify CCL3 as a non-redundant effector of Breg-mediated protection, acting primarily through peripheral T-cell and myeloid remodeling, with secondary CNS impacts. These results highlight the translational potential of CCL3-competent, spleen-derived GIFT15 Bregs for therapeutic modulation of autoimmune demyelination.Subject terms: Chemokines, Multiple sclerosis

Introduction

Regulatory B cells (Bregs) are a heterogenous subset of B lymphocytes that preserve immune balance by suppressing inflammatory responses and promoting tolerance1. They arise from multiple B cell lineages and display diverse phenotypes, functions, and suppressive mechanisms2. Naturally occurring Bregs have been described in both mice and humans and include subsets such as CD1dhiCD5+ B10 cells, TIM-1+ Bregs, and transitional Bregs3,4. These populations are primarily defined by their ability to secrete interleukin-10 (IL-10), which inhibits pro-inflammatory T cell responses and promotes regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation5. In autoimmune models such as experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), Bregs mitigate disease through secretion of IL-10 and interleukin-35 (IL-35)6–8. Other cytokines, including IL-27 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), as well as effector molecules such as granzyme B (GzmB) and PD-L1, expand the repertoire of Breg regulatory mechanisms9–14. The secretion of GzmB by Bregs contributes to their immunomodulatory function by inducing apoptosis in pro-inflammatory immune cells, and by reshaping the T cell receptor (TCR) signaling pathways, thereby dampening excessive immune responses and maintaining immune homeostasis11,15–17. Collectively, these mechanisms highlight the importance of Bregs in controlling inflammation and preventing autoimmunity.

Beyond their natural occurrence, Bregs can also be generated ex vivo. Several methods have been described, including stimulation with CD40L, TLR ligands, or cytokine combinations, each conferring distinct phenotypes and functions13,14,18,19. Among these, treatment with the GM-CSF/IL-15 fusokine (GIFT15) has been shown to reprogram splenic B cells into a potent regulatory population20,21. GIFT15-induced Bregs suppress inflammation and promote remyelination in EAE21–23. In adoptive transfer studies, Bregs reduce central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, suppress autoreactive T cells, and polarize CD4+ T cells in the periphery, which then migrate to the CNS and facilitate oligodendrocyte progenitor cell (OPC) activation21–23. Thus, Bregs exert therapeutic effects without directly homing to the CNS, and their capacity to drive remyelination is of particular significance for multiple sclerosis (MS), where regenerative therapies remain limited22.

Transcriptomic profiling of GIFT15-induced Bregs highlighted Ccl3, GzmB, and Il27, together with checkpoint and cytokine-signaling programs. Importantly, IL-27 was demonstrated at the protein level in GIFT15-Bregs in our prior work (secreted heterodimeric IL-27 : p28/EBI3)22 and more recently GzmB. To define functional contribution, we transferred Ccl3⁻/⁻ or GzmB⁻/⁻-Bregs and used Il27ra⁻/⁻ EAE recipients. Loss of CCL3 in donor Bregs abrogated clinical benefit, whereas granzyme B deficiency did not; efficacy was retained in Il27ra⁻/⁻ recipients, indicating that host IL-27 signaling (via IL-27Rα/WSX-1) is dispensable. Collectively, these findings indicate that CCL3 makes a non-redundant contribution to Breg-mediated benefit in EAE, while other effector mechanisms remain operative.

Results

Transcriptomic profiling of mouse B cells and Bregs

Splenic B cells were purified from 8-week-old C57BL/6 female littermates to high purity (>95% CD19⁺; Fig. 1A–C) and either cryobanked immediately or cultured with GIFT15 for 72 h to generate GIFT15-Bregs. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that GIFT15-Bregs expressed B220 while showing low levels of CD138 (Fig. 1D). In addition, GIFT15-Bregs upregulated IL-10 (35.7% vs. control 2%) and CD1d mean fluorescence intensity, rising from 7.4 × 10² in control B cells to 1.4 × 10³ in GIFT15-Bregs, consistent with an immunoregulatory phenotype.

To better define these cells, we performed bulk RNA-seq on purified B cells and GIFT15-Bregs (n = 3 per group) to define transcriptomic differences between the two populations. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation of conventional splenic B cells and Bregs (Fig. 2A), with greater variability among B cells than GIFT15-induced Bregs. Differential expression identified 7104 DEGs (FDR < 0.05; |log₂FC | ≥1), with 3260 up- and 3844 down-regulated in Bregs relative to B cells (Fig. 2B). A volcano plot is shown in Fig. 2C; the top 100 up- and down-regulated genes are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

To further characterize the GIFT15-Breg population, we examined canonical B-cell markers and regulatory genes. Regulatory-associated transcripts were increased (Il10 ~32×; Il27/p28 present only in Bregs; Ebi3 ~ 8.5×; Havcr1/TIM-1 ~1.5×; Lag3 ~158×; Cd274/PD-L1 ~ 1.8×). Canonical markers diverged with increased Cd27 (~2.6×) and decreased Cd38, while immunoglobulin transcripts broadly trended upward—consistent with an activated/memory-like B-cell phenotype that retains B-cell identity rather than a plasmablast program. These genes clustered in the heatmap and rank among the highest log₂ fold changes (Fig. 2D).

We next compared our dataset with the Duboi et al. Breg meta-signature from their meta-analysis24. Using variance-stabilized counts, we computed module scores/ssGSEA and visualized the signature as a heatmap. GIFT15-Bregs showed coordinated up-regulation of signature genes—including Il10, Il27 (p28), Ebi3, Pdcd1lg2 (PD-L2), Cd274 (PD-L1), Havcr1 (TIM-1), Lag3, Irf4, Prdm1, Batf, Ccl22, Ccl3, Ccl4, and Ccr7—relative to conventional B cells, confirming convergence with established Breg programs (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table 1). Notably, Il10 is significantly increased (~32×) despite not falling within the top 100 DEGs by effect size.

Chemokine-related transcripts were among the most strongly differential expressed: Ccl22 (log₂FC ≈ +10.7, FDR ~ 0), Ccl3 (+8.3, FDR ~ 0), Ccl4 (+7.5, FDR ~ × 10⁻²⁴⁷), Ccr7 (+2.4, FDR ~ 7 × 10⁻²⁷⁶), and Ccr4 (+8.3) ranked within the top-100 DEGs, indicating a strong chemokine program compatible with enhanced secretion potential.

At the level of transcriptional control and signaling, GIFT15-Bregs show increased expression of IRF4, PRDM1, and BATF, with relative attenuation of BCL6/PAX5. Gene-set results indicate activity of JAK/STAT (STAT3/STAT1) and NF-κB pathways, aligning with the heightened checkpoint and cytokine transcripts observed. Consistent with these regulatory shifts, functional enrichment highlights immune-signaling programs—including interferon signaling, chemokine-mediated signaling and leukocyte migration, cytokine-mediated and inflammatory responses—and antigen processing and presentation via MHC class II. Concordantly, MHC class II genes are broadly upregulated, supporting increased antigen-presentation capacity in Bregs (Fig. 2E).

Protein-level validation of Granzyme B and CCL3 expression in Bregs confirms transcriptomic findings

We previously reported GIFT15-Bregs secrete IL-10 and IL-2721,22. To validate candidates from the transcriptome, we analyzed paired B cells and GIFT15-Bregs (same starting population) by western blot and chemokine array (Fig. 3A, B). Western blots confirmed GzmB upregulation in GIFT15-Bregs. Conditioned media (n = 3 independent donors) showed IL-16, CCL22, and CCL3/4 signals above background in GIFT15-Bregs, whereas these analytes were at or below the assay’s detection limit in resting B-cell supernatants (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Table 2).

T cell suppression assay

To evaluate the suppressive capacity of C57BL/6, Ccl3-/- or GzmB-/--Bregs, a T cell suppression assay was conducted. The proliferation of T cells was evaluated by labeling the cells with CFSE prior to PHA stimulation and measuring dye dilution as an indicator of replication (Fig. 4A–C). CFSE fluorescence was measured after gating on CD3⁺ T cells to ensure analysis was restricted to the T cell population. At a Bregs-to-splenocytes ratio of 1:10, C57BL/6 and GzmB-/--Bregs were significantly able to decrease T cell proliferation (2.6% and 11.5%, respectively) while Ccl3-/--Bregs partially limited the proliferation of T cells to 27% compared to PHA-activated T cells with a proliferation of 35% (Fig. 4A, B). Complete suppression of proliferation was observed at a 1:1 ratio of Bregs versus splenocytes for C57BL/6 and GzmB-/- -Breg. Moderated suppression was observed with Ccl3-/--Bregs compared to C57BL/6-Bregs (19.5%). Statistical analysis comparing C57BL/6-Bregs with Ccl3-/- and GzmB-/--Bregs revealed a significant difference between Ccl3-/- and C57BL/6-Bregs (Fig. 4C).

Contribution of Bregs–Secreted CCL3, GzmB, and IL-27, to the resolution of EAE

To assess the therapeutic potential of Bregs in EAE, we administered three doses of either Ccl3-/- or GzmB-/--Bregs at one-week intervals, or C57BL/6-Bregs into IL-27 receptor alpha knockout (Il27ra-/-) EAE mice. Treatment began when mice exhibited early signs of EAE, with an initial clinical score of 0.5–1 on a scale of 5. C57BL/6 EAE mice served as control arm.

C57BL/6 EAE mice receiving C57BL/6-Bregs showed a significant and progressive reduction in disease severity, ultimately achieving complete remission of EAE symptoms (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table 3). Similarly, administration of GzmB-/--Bregs resulted in a comparable therapeutic effect, leading to full remission (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table 3). This suggests that GzmB deficiency does not impair the immunomodulatory function of Bregs in the context of EAE.

In contrast, mice treated with Ccl3-/--Bregs failed to show significant improvement in disease symptoms compared to C57BL/6 Bregs-treated mice (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table 3). The lack of EAE amelioration in the absence of CCL3 highlights its potential role in Bregs-mediated immunosuppression. These findings indicate that CCL3 signaling is crucial for the therapeutic efficacy of Bregs in controlling EAE progression.

Since IL-27 is a heterodimeric cytokine, impairing either of its subunits would disrupt additional interleukin signaling pathways, making Il27ra-/- a more specific model for studying IL-27 function (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Table 3)25. Interestingly, Il27ra-/- mice exhibited full recovery from EAE, indicating that IL-27 host responsiveness is dispensable for disease resolution in this model. Luxol Fast Blue histology corroborated the clinical scores: cords from C57BL/6-Breg–treated mice showed uniform myelin preservation, whereas Ccl3⁻/⁻-Breg recipients exhibited multifocal demyelination, indicating that CCL3 is required for Breg-mediated protection of CNS myelin (Fig. 5D).

CCR1 and CCR5 expression in T cells and macrophages supports CCL3-driven immunoregulation

To assess the CCL3 → CCR1/CCR5 axis, we quantified CCR1 and CCR5 on T cells and macrophages from spleen, lymph nodes, and CNS at disease onset of C57BL/6 EAE mice. Both receptors were readily detectable on CD3⁺ T cells and F4/80⁺ myeloid cells, although expression patterns varied by tissue. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that CCR5 was highly expressed on both CD3⁺ T cells and F4/80⁺ macrophages across the spleen, lymph nodes (both draining inguinal/axillary and mesenteric lymph nodes), and CNS. In contrast, CCR1 was detected at lower levels overall, with relatively greater expression in lymph node and CNS compartments compared to spleen (Fig. 6A–C).

No significant differences were detected in the abundance or profile of spinal cord-infiltrating macrophages (CD45high) between C57BL/6 Breg-treated and Ccl3-/--Bregs treated EAE mice over the course of the first month post-transfer (Fig. 8B). Bregs mediated modulation of oligodendrocyte lineage progression in the spinal cords of EAE mice

To evaluate the presence of oligodendrocytes and the distribution of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), flow cytometry was performed on non-hematopoietic (CD45–/low) cell populations. A panel of antibodies targeting key markers across the oligodendrocyte lineage was used to characterize developmental stages (Fig. 9A–C)29. OPCs were isolated using a 40% Percoll gradient centrifugation protocol. During flow cytometric acquisition, sequential gating strategies were applied based on forward and side scatter to eliminate debris and irrelevant events, allowing for enrichment of intact cells based on size and complexity. Singlets and live cells were selected, and CD45–/low gating was used to exclude hematopoietic cells and microglia (Fig. 9A).

Lineage analysis included early progenitor markers (PDGFRα, A2B5) and extended to more differentiated stages, including premyelinating and myelinating oligodendrocytes marked by GALC and O4 (Fig. 9B, C). Within two weeks post-transfer, spinal cords from C57BL/6-Breg recipients showed increased GalC and O4 (p = 0.0002 and p = 0.003), with no consistent differences at other time points (Fig. 9C). We interpret these data as a transient, tissue-dependent rise in late oligodendroglial markers, not definitive evidence of mature oligodendrocytes or remyelination.

Translational insights into CpG-activated Human B cell function: conserved CCL3 and CCL22 secretion across mouse and human systems

To benchmark translational relevance, we modeled human Bregs by stimulating peripheral human B cells with CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (TLR9). This widely used approach reproducibly induces an IL-10–competent, regulatory-like phenotype (see gating/activation in Fig. 10A–D)30. To evaluate whether CCL3 secretion is conserved between species, we conducted a comparative analysis of conditioned media derived from CpG-stimulated human B cells. Conditioned media of human B cells and B-CpG were collected for chemokine profiling, and array analysis revealed that CpG-stimulated human B cells secreted both CCL3 and CCL22 (Fig. 10E, Supplementary Table 4). These data indicate that CCL3/CCL22 production is not Breg-specific but a TLR9-activation output of human B cells.

Discussion

Despite the success of immunomodulatory therapies in reducing relapse rates and slowing disease progression in MS, efforts are still needed to develop a critical unmet need for treatments that directly promote remyelination and repair of damaged neural tissue31. Although preclinical studies have identified several pathways that regulate OPC maturation and remyelination—including Wnt, Notch, and LINGO-1 signaling—translating these insights into effective clinical therapies has proven challenging32. For instance, while the anti-LINGO-1 monoclonal antibody opicinumab showed promise in early-phase trials, it failed to meet efficacy endpoints in later-stage clinical studies33. The development of therapies that enhance oligodendrocyte differentiation, survival, and myelin sheath formation is essential for achieving neuro-restoration and improving long-term outcomes in people living with MS.

This study reveals distinct transcriptional and functional roles for Bregs in modulating neuroinflammatory responses, emphasizing the critical involvement of CCL3 in Bregs-mediated immune regulation and CNS repair. The transcriptomic analysis of splenic B cells and GIFT15-induced Bregs revealed profound molecular differences that underscore the reprogramming of B cells toward an immunoregulatory phenotype. Principal component analysis demonstrated distinct clustering between Bregs and conventional B cells, reflecting significant divergence in gene expression profiles. Interestingly, induced Bregs showed a more homogeneous transcriptional profile compared to B cells, suggesting a defined, stimulus-driven regulatory program. Through transcriptomic profiling of conventional murine B cells and Bregs, we identified over 7104 differentially expressed genes, highlighting a unique immunoregulatory signature in Bregs. These included upregulation of cytokines such as IL-27, which we also detected as a secreted factor from GIFT15-Bregs22. IL-27 is a critical cytokine for the induction of Tr-1 cells and for dampening Th17 differentiation, thereby reinforcing its relevance to Breg-mediated suppression of neuroinflammation34,35. Alongside IL-27, we also identified enrichment of GzmB and chemokine signatures, both of which have been reported in the literature as contributing to immunoregulatory mechanisms, providing further justification for focusing on these pathways in our analyses.

Although IL-10 did not rank among the top 100 most differentially expressed genes, it was nevertheless significantly overexpressed in GIFT15-Bregs compared to conventional B cells. We and others have previously demonstrated that IL-10 is indispensable for the therapeutic function of Bregs in EAE, as IL-10⁻/⁻-Bregs fail to confer disease protection2,21. The transcriptomic enrichment in interferon signaling, chemokine signaling, and MHC class II antigen presentation pathways points to Bregs as active modulators of immune responses rather than passive suppressors. The elevated expression of cytotoxic mediators, particularly GzmB, further underscores the functional plasticity of Bregs and supports the emerging view that Bregs may suppress immune activation through both soluble factors and cytotoxic mechanisms36. Our phenotypic and transcriptomic analyses indicate that GIFT15-Bregs are more consistent with a regulatory phenotype than with plasmablast differentiation. At the transcriptomic level, GIFT15-Bregs upregulated Il10, Il27, Pdcd1lg2, and chemokine transcripts, together with regulatory transcription factors such as Prdm1, Irf4, and Batf, while lacking enrichment of immediate-early activation signatures. Consistent with these data, flow cytometry confirmed preserved canonical B-cell identity (CD19⁺, B220⁺), low CD138 expression, and increased IL-10 and CD1d. Taken together, these data indicate that GIFT15-Bregs represent an activated yet distinct regulatory B-cell program rather than a plasmablast-like expansion. We used resting splenic B cells as the primary baseline to capture the full extent of GIFT15-driven reprogramming.

To test whether our conclusions depend on using resting B cells as the baseline, we re-benchmarked GIFT15-Bregs against activated B-cell references from GSE35998 (LPS ± anti-CD40)6,24. As anticipated, comparison with an activated B-cell control reduces the absolute number of DEGs, but the overall directionality and core transcriptional program are maintained. The Breg-up signature remains dominated by a regulatory/secretory module (e.g., Il10, Pdcd1lg2/PD-L2, Cd5, Prdm1, Irf4, Xbp1) rather than a generic immediate-early activation signature. Consistent with this, only a handful of classic LPS/CD40 early-response genes (FOS, JUN, EGR, NF-κB, DUSP, CD69, ICAM1, SOCS3, etc.) appear among our top differentials, and these are enriched in the B-cell–up group rather than the Breg-up group. Thus, our results are robust to the choice of activated controls and do not rely on resting B cells alone.

Guided by the transcriptome and criteria favoring causal inference, we prioritized three secreted, receptor-defined effectors—CCL3, GzmB, and IL-27—for mechanistic tests. GzmB and IL-27 were previously verified at the protein level in GIFT15-Bregs, and here CCL3 was prominently upregulated with CCR1/CCR5 enriched on likely responder cells (T cells, macrophages), defining a tractable signaling axis; available Ccl3⁻/⁻, GzmB⁻/⁻ donors mice and Il27ra⁻/⁻ recipients mice then enabled direct tests of necessity in vivo. Functionally, C57BL/6 and GzmB-deficient Bregs retained the ability to significantly inhibit T cell proliferation in vitro, while CCL3-deficient Bregs exhibited impaired suppressive capacity, indicating that CCL3 plays a critical and non-redundant role in Breg-mediated T cell regulation. This functional deficit was further reflected in vivo: only C57BL/6 and GzmB-deficient Bregs induced complete remission in EAE, whereas mice treated with Ccl3⁻/⁻-Bregs failed to show clinical improvement. These results indicate that CCL3 is required, yet operates within a broader peripheral program that reshapes the lympho-myeloid repertoire—consistent with recruitment or activation of regulatory cell populations37.

Interestingly, treatment with C57BL/6-Bregs led to a time-dependent increase in both FoxP3⁺ Tregs and Tr-1 cells across peripheral lymphoid organs and the CNS, effects that were significantly diminished in the absence of CCL3. This supports the notion that Bregs shape the adaptive immune response by promoting regulatory T cell expansion, a key mechanism by which immune tolerance is reestablished in autoimmune settings38. Similarly, CCL3-expressing Bregs enhanced the expression of CD206 by macrophages in both peripheral tissues and the CNS, indicative of an alternatively activated, regulatory phenotype. These macrophages are known to support tissue repair and suppress inflammation, suggesting that Breg-derived CCL3 contributes to creating a tolerogenic microenvironment during EAE.

Bregs are associated with transient increases in late oligodendroglial markers (O4/GalC) consistent with indirect pro-repair signaling. In contrast to current therapies that mainly target immune suppression, our Breg-based strategy not only modulates inflammation but also stimulates intrinsic repair processes in the central nervous system22,23. However, the specific Breg-derived factors responsible for driving this regenerative effect remain to be fully identified. Consistent with our prior report that GIFT15-Bregs enhance remyelination by g-ratio electron microscopy23, we now find that loss of CCL3 in donor Bregs abrogates this benefit: Luxol Fast Blue staining of the lumbar spinal cord of EAE C57BL/6 mice shows persistent demyelination in Ccl3⁻/⁻-Breg recipients mice. Together with the immune readouts (Treg/Tr-1 expansion and CD206⁺ macrophage polarization), these data support a model in which Breg-derived CCL3 creates a reparative milieu that secondarily enables oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelin repair.

In addition, our findings raise the possibility that CCL3 contributes to oligodendrocyte lineage progression during EAE resolution. Flow cytometric analysis of spinal cords revealed that mice treated with C57BL/6-Bregs exhibited significantly elevated expression of GalC and O4—markers associated with premyelinating and myelinating oligodendrocytes—within two weeks of adoptive transfer, compared to those receiving Ccl3⁻/⁻-Bregs. Although this effect appeared most evident at week 2 and was less consistent at other time points, it supports a model in which CCL3 influences OPC maturation indirectly. Since Bregs have not been observed in the CNS in our studies22, their effect on oligodendrocyte lineage cells is likely mediated through modulation of the inflammatory milieu or recruitment of supportive immune populations rather than direct interaction with glia.

To evaluate the translational significance of this pathway from a comparative biology perspective, we first considered that CpG stimulation can induce a regulatory-like phenotype in human B cells39,40. Such CpG-induced B cells are characterized by IL-10 secretion and the ability to suppress activated T cells, features that align with a Breg-like function40. Within this context, we observed that these B-CpG cells also secrete CCL3, consistent with prior reports linking CCL3 to Breg biology, including GzmB-related Bregs41. However, our B-CpG cells did not express GzmB, whose induction in Bregs is typically associated with IL-21 stimulation42. This distinction suggests that CpG-driven CCL3 secretion reflects a separate regulatory program from the IL-21–GzmB axis.

These findings underscore the conserved role of CCL3 production in activated B cells and highlight its plausible mechanistic contribution to Breg-mediated immune regulation in humans. While additional work is needed to establish whether CCL3 defines discrete Breg subsets, the presence of CCL3⁺ regulatory-like B cells raises the possibility that such cells could be harnessed as dual-function autologous therapies for multiple sclerosis—capable of both restraining pathological inflammation and fostering endogenous CNS repair.

Methods

Mice and genotyping

Mice were maintained under SPF conditions with ad libitum food and water. For EAE experiments, male and female mice were induced at 8–10 weeks of age. C57BL/6 mice (C57BL/6 J, strain #: 000664), Ccl3-/- mice (B6.129P2-Ccl3tm1Unc/J, strain #: 002687) and Granzyme B (GzmB)-/- mice (B6.129S2-Gzmbtm1Ley/J, strain #: 002248) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory, colonies were bred in-house and genotyped by PCR following Jackson Laboratory protocols. Il27ra⁻/⁻ mice (B6N.129P2-Il27ra^tm1Mak^/J, JAX #018078) were purchased as homozygous animals and used without in-house breeding; genotype for each shipment was verified by the vendor’s PCR certificate. Animals were 8–10 weeks old at induction.

Reagents and antibodies

The following reagents and antibodies were used: MOG35-55 (Sigma Millipore), incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (Difco, BD), H37Ra Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Sigma Millipore), Percoll (GE Healthcare), and Ghost Dye Red 780 (Tonbo Biosciences).

For phenotypic analysis of lymph–myeloid cells and microglia, the following monoclonal antibodies were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions: CD45 (BioLegend, clone 30-F11, BV570), CD11b (BD, clone M1/70, BB700), Ly6C (eBioscience, clone HK1.4, AF700), PD-L1/CD274 (BioLegend, clone 10 F.9G2, BV711), CD206/MMR (BD, clone 19.2, PE), F4/80 (BioLegend, clone BM8, APC).

For chemokine-receptor analysis on T cells and macrophages: CCR1/CD191 (BioLegend, clone S15040E, AF488) and CCR5/CD195 (BioLegend, clone HM-CCR5, PE), with lineage gating as indicated in figure legends.

For oligodendrocyte analysis, the following markers were used per manufacturer guidelines: A2B5 (R&D Systems, clone 105, Alexa Fluor 647), O4 (R&D Systems, Alexa Fluor 405-conjugated antibody), Galactocerebroside/GalC (Sigma-Millipore, clone mGalC, FITC), and PDGFRα/CD140a (Invitrogen, clone APA5, FITC).

For human B-cell activation (B-CpG) phenotyping, we used: CD19 (BioLegend, clone HIB19, APC), CD25/IL-2Rα (BioLegend, clone BC96, PE), CD71/TfR1 (BioLegend, clone CY1G4, FITC), CD73/NT5E (BioLegend, clone AD2, BV421).

In vitro culture assay

Mouse B cells were isolated from the spleens of C57BL/6 or Ccl3-/- or GzmB-/- mice by negative selection (Stem Cell Technologies) and achieved ≥95% purity (CD19⁺)and cultured in R10 medium (RPMI-1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum -FBS (Sigma), 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate (Lonza), 2 mM L-Glutamine (Lonza), 10 mM HEPES (Lonza), 1% Essential Amino Acid (Gibco), 0.1 mM Beta-mercaptoethanol (Gibco) and the addition of 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin) and stimulated with 10 μg/mL recombinant bacterial-derived GIFT15 for 72–96 h22.

Human B cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). PBMCs were isolated from heparinized blood of a healthy human donor by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. Human B cells were isolated from whole PBMCs (Stem Cell Technologies) and cultured in R10 medium and treated with 0.1 μM CpG (ODN2006, Invivogen), in a 5% CO2 incubator for 3–5 days.

Splenocyte isolation and T cell proliferation assay

Mouse splenocytes were isolated by harvesting spleens under sterile conditions and mechanically dissociating them through a 70 µm cell strainer in RPMI-1640 (Corning) medium. The cell suspension was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer to remove erythrocytes, followed by washing and resuspension in complete culture medium. Viable splenocytes were counted using trypan blue exclusion and used for downstream applications.

Mouse splenocytes were labeled with 5 mM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR) in 1 mL of PBS for 15 min at 37 °C, after which labeling was halted by adding an excess of fetal calf serum (FCS). The cells were then washed twice with RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma) and subsequently cultured in of Bregs from C57BL/6, Ccl3-/- and GzmB-/- cells (1:1 to 1:10 splenocytes:Bregs ratio). After 2 days of co-culture, T-cell proliferation was assessed by analyzing CFSE fluorescence intensity using flow cytometry. CD3⁺ T-cell proliferation was further analyzed using anti-mouse CD3 antibodies (Invitrogen), with phytohemagglutinin (PHA)-activated CD3⁺ T cells serving as a reference for proliferation, and unstimulated CD3⁺ T cells (no PHA) as a basal control (proliferation <1%).

B cells were isolated from the spleens of C57BL/6, Ccl3-/- or GzmB-/- mice by negative selection (Stem Cell Technologies) and stimulated with 10 μg/mL recombinant bacterial-derived GIFT15 for 72–96 h.RNA sequencing and primary processing.

Splenic B cells from 8-week-old female C57BL/6 littermates were cultured with GIFT15 (10 µg/mL, 72–96 h) to generate Bregs or left untreated (B cells). For each condition, n = 3 biological replicates were prepared. Following GIFT15 culture (Bregs) or at isolation (B cells), 1 × 10⁶ cells per sample were pelleted and cryopreserved. Total RNA was extracted after thawing using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was measured with Qubit BR reagents; RNA integrity was assessed on an Agilent TapeStation (High Sensitivity RNA ScreenTape). Samples with RIN ≥ 7 proceeded to library prep.

Library preparation and sequencing

Poly-A–selected, strand-specific libraries were prepared with the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit. Final libraries were QC’d on the Agilent TapeStation (D1000 ScreenTape) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 using an S4 300-cycle flow cell (paired-end 150 bp, PE150), yielding ~15 million paired reads per sample.

Computational processing

Raw FASTQ files underwent QC with FastQC. Adapters and low-quality bases were removed with Trim Galore; PCR duplicates were removed per the MedGenome standard pipeline. Cleaned reads were aligned to mouse GRCm39 with STAR. Gene-level read counts were generated with HTSeq. Differential expression used DESeq2 with design ~ group (Bregs vs B cells). Unless stated otherwise, statistical significance was defined as padj < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg), and figures reporting DEG numbers additionally required |log₂FC | ≥1. For visualization (PCA, heatmaps), variance-stabilizing transformed (VST) counts were used.

Exploratory analyses

Exploratory plots and gene-set summaries were produced in iDEP v2.10 (accessed August 2025). Raw counts were normalized by DESeq2’s median-of-ratios method; VST values were used for PCA/heatmaps. When comparing to literature Breg signatures, DESeq2-normalized counts were log1p-transformed, row-z-scored, and displayed as heatmaps. Enrichment analyses were performed using DAVID v2024q4 for GO terms and Graphite Web for pathway context. Previously published RNA-seq data from Dubois et al.24 are available from GEO under accession GSE125901.

Chemokine array

Chemokine array was conducted using concentrated B cells or GIFT15-Bregs-conditioned media and analyzed with the Proteome Profiler Mouse Chemokine Array Kit (R&D Systems, ARY020) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

For human samples, condition media from CpG-stimulated and unstimulated B cells were assessed using Proteome Profiled Human Chemokine Array Kit (R&D Systems, ARY017) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Western blot

Splenic B cells were purified by negative selection (≥95% CD19⁺). Freshly isolated cells were lysed for the B-cell condition; an aliquot was cultured with GIFT15 (10 µg/mL, 72 h) to generate GIFT15-Bregs and then lysed. Cells were extracted in RIPA + protease/phosphatase inhibitors (20 min on ice), cleared (14,000 g, 15 min, 4 °C), and quantified by BCA. 20–30 µg protein/lane was resolved on 10–12% SDS–PAGE, transferred to PVDF (0.45 µm), blocked (5% milk/TBST, 1 h), and probed overnight (4 °C) with anti-granzyme B (mouse-reactive, WB-validated; 1:1000 in 5% BSA/TBST). After HRP-secondary (1:5,000, 1 h, RT), bands were detected by ECL. Membranes were re-probed for GAPDH. Data reflect ≥3 independent experiments.

Human anti–IL-10 ELISA

Anti–IL-10 antibodies were quantified using an in-house sandwich ELISA (R&D format). Briefly, high-binding 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with recombinant human IL-10 (1–2 µg/mL), blocked with 1% BSA in PBS, and incubated with diluted serum or culture supernatant samples. Bound antibodies were detected with HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG, developed with TMB substrate, and read at 450 nm. A purified human anti–IL-10 antibody served as the reference standard, and concentrations were interpolated using a 4-parameter logistic regression.

EAE induction and analysis

We induced and scored EAE as previously outlined43. In brief, EAE was initiated through subcutaneous injection of 50 μg of MOG35-55 (Sigma Millipore) emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant (Difco, BD) containing 5 mg/mL H37Ra Mycobacterium tuberculosis in C57BL/6 or Il27ra-/- mice. Pertussis toxin (Sigma Millipore) was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 100 ng on days 0 and 2. Disease symptoms typically appeared between 11- and 14-days post-immunization (dpi).

The EAE clinical scores were assigned as follows: 0, normal; 1, flaccid tail; 2, hindlimb weakness; 3, flaccid tail with paralysis of one front or hind limb; 4, complete hindlimb paralysis and partial paralysis of a front limb; 5, tetraplegia, moribund state, or death. Mice with a clinical score of ≥0.5 were included. Animals reaching a clinical score of 4 or above were humanely euthanized according to IACUC protocol M006564. Prior to experimental intervention, EAE mice were randomized to ensure each test group had an equal starting average clinical score. All in vivo experiments used randomization and blinded outcome assessment. No ad-hoc exclusions were made; all animals meeting prespecified criteria were analyzed.

Histology (Luxol fast blue)

At the terminal time point, mice were perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Lumbar spinal cords were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS overnight at 4 °C, processed through graded ethanol/xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (8–10 µm) were cut, stained with Luxol Fast Blue (0.1% in 95% ethanol; differentiated in 0.05% lithium carbonate/70% ethanol), and imaged by bright-field microscopy.

Cell Isolation and Flow CytometrySpleens and lymph nodes (LNs), draining (inguinal/axillary) and mesenteric lymph nodes, were harvested postmortem and placed into RPMI medium (Lonza). To generate single-cell suspensions, the tissues were gently passed through a 70 μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences)44. Red blood cells from the spleen suspensions were then lysed using Red Cell Lysis Buffer (Sigma-Aldrich). For the detection of cell surface markers, cells were stained with the different fluorochrome-labeled monoclonal antibodies reported above. For spinal cords, mice were perfused intracardially with ice-cold Dulbecco’s PBS lacking Ca2+ and Mg2+. Spinal cords were collected and immediately homogenized using a plunger in RPMI medium. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 40% Percoll in HBSS (GE Healthcare). The cell suspension was centrifuged at 650 × g for 25 min, with no deceleration. The cells were isolated from the pellet and washed twice with FACS buffer. Single cells were analyzed based on forward and side scatter of light, with further discrimination achieved by using various fluorochromes. To detect cell surface markers, cells were stained in FACS buffer with fluorochrome-labeled monoclonal antibodies and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. Flow cytometry analysis and data acquisition were conducted using an Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and data analysis was performed with FCS Express (DeNovo Software).

Experimental design, statistical analysis and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless noted; for non-parametric analyses, median (IQR) is reported. Given small group sizes (5–6 per arm), we did not assume normality a priori. Normality was assessed with Shapiro–Wilk; when assumptions were met, we used unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests (two groups) or one-/two-way ANOVA with Holm–Šidák post hoc tests (≥3 groups). When normality was not met, we used Mann–Whitney U tests (two groups) or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s multiple-comparisons (≥3 groups). Longitudinal EAE scores were analyzed by two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Geisser–Greenhouse correction; when missing values occurred because of humane endpoints, a mixed-effects model (REML) was used. For time-pointwise post hoc comparisons of clinical scores, Holm–Šidák correction was applied. For single-analyte flow cytometry/ELISA comparisons, we used Mann–Whitney U tests; for multi-marker panels, familywise error was controlled with Holm–Šidák. RNA-seq statistics used DESeq2 with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control as detailed in the RNA-seq section. Significance was set at α = 0.05. Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism (v10). All in vivo experiments were performed with biological replicates (individual mice). Flow cytometry and ELISA experiments included at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise specified. Reproducibility was confirmed by repeating key experiments independently as indicated in the figure legends.

For EAE studies, each treatment group contained 5–6 mice, and each experiment was replicated in three independent cohorts (total n per condition indicated in figure legends). RNA-seq analyses used n = 3 biological replicates per group. Flow cytometry, ELISA, chemokine array, and T-cell suppression assays were performed using 3–5 independent biological replicates unless otherwise specified. Exact n values for each experiment are reported in the corresponding figure legends.

Group sizes of 5–6 mice were chosen from an a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1) based on pilot data and prior studies from our group in EAE treated with GIFT15-Bregs22,23, which consistently show a ~ 1.0 difference in peak clinical score between groups (SD ≈ 0.7–0.9) and ~35–40% reduction in clinical score AUC. For a two-way repeated-measures design (within-subject correlation ρ ≈ 0.5, ε = 1), these parameters correspond to an effect size f ≈ 0.35–0.40; with α = 0.05 and 80% power, n = 6 per group is sufficient. Randomization and blinded outcome assessment were used for all in vivo experiments, and no ad-hoc exclusions were made.
