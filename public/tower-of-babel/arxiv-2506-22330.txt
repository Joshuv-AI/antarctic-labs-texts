Hawaii conjecture through the lens of Cauchy indices
Dmitry Gnatyuk, Mikhail Tyaglov

Abstract
Given a real polynomial p p , we study some properties of real critical points of its logarithmic derivative Q ⁡ [ p ] = ( p ′ / p ) ′ Q[p]=(p^{\prime}/p)^{\prime} using the theory of Cauchy indices. As a by-product we improve the lower bound for the number these points.



Key words.
Hawaii conjecture, Cauchy indices, root location.

AMS subject classification.
26C10, 30C15, 30C10

Dedicated to Alexander Aptekarev on the

occasion of his 70
th
anniversary.

1
Introduction

In the remarkable work
[
2
]
T. Craven, G. Csordas, and W. Smith posed a conjecture stated that for a real polynomial
p
the number of the
real critical point of its logarithmic derivative

Q[p](z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}\dfrac{d}{dz}\left(\dfrac{p^{\prime}(z)}{p(z)}\right)=\dfrac{p(z)p^{\prime\prime}(z)-\left(p^{\prime}(z)\right)^{2}}{\left(p(z)\right)^{2}}.

(1.1)

does not exceed the number of non-real zeroes of
p
:

Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p]\right)\leqslant 2m,

(1.2)

where
2m=Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p)
, and
Z_{\mathbb{R}}(f)
and
Z_{\mathbb{C}}(f)
denote the number of real and non-real zeroes of a function
f
, respectively.

In
[
8
]
the Hawaii conjecture was proved in a rather sophisticated way for polynomials and entire functions of class
\mathcal{L-P}^{*}

(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Definition 1.4]
and references there). The method used in
[
8
]
seemed not to be simplified for polynomials
only. However, it remains interesting to find a more simple proof based on polynomial properties, since once the Hawaii conjecture is true for polynomials,
it is true for some classes of entire functions as well
[
3
]
.
We remind the reader that the proof of the Hawaii conjecture was based on the following inequalities
[
8
, Theorem 3.16]

2m-2m_{1}\leqslant Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p]\right)\leqslant 2m-2m_{1}+Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p^{\prime}]\right)

(1.3)

hold for polynomials and entire functions of class
\mathcal{L-P}^{*}
possessing the so-called property A (see
[
8
, Definition 1.26]
).
The property is rather technical, and functions with such a property seem to be not of interest themselves, out of that proof. However, any real
polynomial or entire function
f
can be replaced by a new function
g
such that
Q[f]=Q[g]
and
g
possesses property A. Precisely,

g(z)=e^{\sigma z}f(z)
, where an appropriate
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
always exists,
[
8
, Theorem 1.28]
. Basing on this fact,
one can reformulate
[
8
, Theorem 3.16]
without any additional properties for arbitrary polynomials and entire functions
of class
\mathcal{L-P}^{*}
(see Theorem
1.2
below).

In what follows, we restrict ourselves by considering only real polynomial. However, some facts can be extended to the class
\mathcal{L-P}^{*}

of entire functions.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a polynomial
p
and a real number
\sigma
, we call the polynomial
p_{\sigma}
defined as

p_{\sigma}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}p^{\prime}(z)+\sigma p(z),

(1.4)

the generalised or Laguerre derivative of
p
. It is clear that
p_{0}

coincides with the ordinary derivative
p^{\prime}
.

For the Laguerre derivative, the following inequality holds (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Problem 192, p. 69]
).

Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p_{\sigma})\leqslant Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p),\qquad\forall\sigma\in\mathbb{R}

(1.5)

where
Z_{\mathbb{C}}(f)
denotes the number of non-real zeroes of the function
f
.

Thus, since
g^{\prime}(z)=e^{\sigma z}f_{\sigma}(z)
, Theorem 3.16 from
[
8
]
can be reformulated for polynomials as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
p
be a real polynomial with
Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p)=2m>0
. Then there exists
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

2m-2m_{\sigma}\leqslant Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p]\right)\leqslant 2m-2m_{\sigma}+Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p_{\sigma}]\right),

where
Q[p_{\sigma}]=\left({p^{\prime}_{\sigma}}/{p_{\sigma}}\right)^{\prime}
, and
2m_{\sigma}=Z_{\mathbb{C}}\left(p_{\sigma}\right)<2m
.

This theorem, in fact, allows us to apply the method of Rolle’s theorem from
[
8
]
for polynomials only.
However, this does not simplify the proof much, because the proof from
[
8
]
for polynomials is the longest
one. The shortest case is the entire functions without maximal and minimal real zeroes, e.g. sine multiplied by a polynomials.

In this work, we present another approach to study the properties and the number of real zeroes of the function
Q[p]
,
the so-called Cauchy indices introduced by A. Cauchy in 1837. In Section
2
, we find the
connection between zeroes
Q[p]
and
Q[p_{\sigma}]
. Section
3
is devoted to
a relation between zeroes of
Q[p]
and
p_{\sigma}
. Here we obtain that, given a real polynomial
p
, there exists only
finitely many complex numbers which are multiple zeroes of the polynomials of the family
p_{\sigma}
(Corollary
3.2
).
Finally, in Section
4
using the Cauchy indices, we establish a generalisation (one direction)
of
[
2
, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1]
. As a by-product we improve the lower bound for the number

Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p]\right)
, cf. Theorem
1.2
.

2
Critical points of logarithmic derivatives of
p
and
p_{\sigma}

We remind that the critical points of the logarithmic derivatives of a given real polynomial
p
are the zeroes of
the function

Q[p](z)=\left(\dfrac{p^{\prime}(z)}{p(z)}\right)^{\prime}=\dfrac{p(z)p^{\prime\prime}(z)-[p^{\prime}(z)]^{2}}{p^{2}(z)}.

Together with the function
Q[p]
, we will study the following polynomial

F[p](z)=p(z)p^{\prime\prime}(z)-[p^{\prime}(z)]^{2},

(2.1)

which is the numerator of the rational function
Q[p]
.

In this section, we establish certain formulæ providing relations between zeroes of the functions

Q[p]
and
Q[p_{\sigma}]
. We start with the following property of zeroes of the polynomial

F[p]
(see Lemma 2.3 in
[
8
]
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
be a zero of
Q[p_{\sigma}]
of multiplicity
r\geqslant 1
such that

p(\lambda)\neq 0
,
p^{\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
,
p_{\sigma}(\lambda)\neq 0
. If
Q[p](\lambda)=0
, then

\lambda
is a zero of
Q[p]
of multiplicity
r+1
.

Proof.

Since
p(\lambda)\neq 0
,
p_{\sigma}(\lambda)\neq 0
, we can deal with
F[p]
and
F[p_{\sigma}]

without loss of generality.

First we find a relationship between
F[p]
,
F[p^{\prime}]
and
F[p_{\sigma}]
. From (
2.1
) we have

\begin{array}[]{c}p^{\prime}(z)F[p^{\prime}](z)=[p^{\prime}(z)]^{2}p^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)-p^{\prime}(z)[p^{\prime\prime}(z)]^{2}=\\
\\
=p(z)p^{\prime\prime}(z)p^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)-p^{\prime}(z)[p^{\prime\prime}(z)]^{2}-p^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)F[p](z)=p^{\prime\prime}(z)F^{\prime}[p](z)-p^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)F[p](z).\end{array}

(2.2)

Now for
F[p_{\sigma}]
one has

F[p_{\sigma}](z)=p_{\sigma}(z)p_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}(z)-[p_{\sigma}^{\prime}(z)]^{2}=F[p^{\prime}](z)+\sigma F^{\prime}[p](z)+\sigma^{2}F[p](z).

From this formula and (
2.2
), we obtain

p^{\prime}(z)F[p_{\sigma}](z)=p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(z)F^{\prime}[p](z)+(\sigma p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(z)-p^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(z))F[p](z).

(2.3)

Let
\lambda
be a zero of
F[p_{\sigma}]
of multiplicity
r
and

F[p](\lambda)=0
. Then
p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
, since
p^{\prime}(\lambda)
would vanish otherwise.
Moreover,
p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)\neq 0
as well. Indeed, from
F[p](\lambda)=0
and

p(\lambda)\neq 0
,
p^{\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
, it follows that

\dfrac{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}=\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}.

(2.4)

If
p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)=0
, then from (
2.4
) we get

-\sigma=\dfrac{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}=\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}

that implies
p_{\sigma}(\lambda)=0
, a contradiction.

Now from formulæ (
2.2
)–(
2.3
)
it follows by induction that

p^{\prime}(\lambda)F^{(j)}[p_{\sigma}](\lambda)=p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)F^{(j+1)}[p](\lambda),\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,r.

(2.5)

Since
\lambda
is a zero of
F[p_{\sigma}](\lambda)
of multiplicity
r
, one has
from (
2.5
) that
F^{(k)}[p](\lambda)=0
,
k=0,1,\ldots,r
, and

F^{(r+1)}[p](\lambda)\neq 0
. Hence,
\lambda
is a zero of
F[p]
of multiplicity exactly
r+1
. But

p(\lambda)\neq 0
by assumption, therefore,
\lambda
is a zero of
Q[p]
of multiplicity
r+1
.
∎

As a consequence of (
2.2
)–(
2.3
) we immediately obtain the following.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
be a zero of
Q[p]
of multiplicity
r\geqslant 2
and
p(\lambda)\neq 0
,
p^{\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
. Then
\lambda

is a zero of
Q[p_{\sigma}]
of multiplicity
r-1
for any
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p_{\sigma}(\lambda)\neq 0
.

3
Relations between zeroes of
Q[p]
and
p_{\sigma}

It turns out that multiple zeroes of
Q[p]
have a relation with multiple zeroes of polynomials
p_{\sigma}
defined in (
1.4
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
p
be a real polynomial, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
p(\lambda)\neq 0
. The number
\lambda
is a zero of the
function
Q[p]
of multiplicity
r\geqslant 1
if and only if there exists
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda

is a zero of the polynomial
p_{\sigma}
of multiplicity
r+1
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\lambda
is a zero of multiplicity
r+1
of
p_{\sigma}
for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Then we have

p_{\sigma}^{(j)}(\lambda)=p^{(j+1)}(\lambda)+\sigma p^{(j)}(\lambda)=0,\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,r,

and

p_{\sigma}^{(r+1)}(\lambda)=p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)+\sigma p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)\neq 0.

Consequently, the following identities hold

\dfrac{p^{(j+1)}(\lambda)}{p^{(j)}(\lambda)}=\dfrac{p^{(j)}(\lambda)}{p^{(j-1)}(\lambda)}=-\sigma,\qquad j=1,\ldots,r,

(3.1)

since
p^{(j)}(\lambda)\neq 0
,
j=0,1,\ldots,r+1
due to
\sigma\neq 0
by assumption.
Now identities (
3.1
) imply

Q\left[p^{(j-1)}\right](\lambda)=0,\qquad j=1,\ldots,r.

(3.2)

Moreover, since
p_{\sigma}^{(r+1)}(\lambda)\neq 0
, one has

\dfrac{p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)}{p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)}\neq-\sigma.

Therefore,

F^{\prime}\left[p^{(r-1)}\right](\lambda)=p^{(r-1)}(\lambda)p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)-p^{(r)}(\lambda)p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)\neq 0,

(3.3)

otherwise, we would have

\dfrac{p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)}{p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)}=\dfrac{p^{(r)}(\lambda)}{p^{(r-1)}(\lambda)}=-\sigma,

a contradiction. Thus, inequality (
3.3
) implies

Q^{\prime}\left[p^{(r-1)}\right](\lambda)=\dfrac{p^{(r-1)}(\lambda)p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)-p^{(r)}(\lambda)p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)}{\left[p^{(r-1)}(\lambda)\right]^{2}}\neq 0.

Consequently,
\lambda
is a simple zero of
Q[p^{(r-1)}]
. Now from (
3.2
) and
Theorem
2.1
it follows that
\lambda
is a zero of
Q[p^{(r-j)}]
of multiplicity
j
,

j=1,\ldots,r
. That is,
\lambda
is a zero of
Q[p]
of multiplicity
r
:

Q^{(j)}\left[p\right](\lambda)=0,\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,r-1,

(3.4)

as required.

If now
\sigma=0
, that is, if
p^{(j)}(\lambda)=0
,
j=1,\ldots,r+1
, and
p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)\neq 0
, then
from (
1.1
) it follows that
\lambda
is a zero of
Q[p]
of multiplicity
r
,
so identities (
3.4
) hold in this case, as well.

Conversely, let
\lambda
be a zero of
Q[p]
of multiplicity
r
, so identities (
3.4
) hold.
Since
p(\lambda)\neq 0
by assumption, these identities can be rewritten as follows

F^{(j)}\left[p\right](\lambda)=0,\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,r-1.

If
p^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
, then from (
1.1
) we have
p^{(j)}(\lambda)=0
,
j=1,\ldots,r+1
,
and
p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)\neq 0
, so the statement of the theorem holds in this case.

Let now
p^{\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
. Then the identity
F[p](\lambda)=0
implies

\dfrac{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}=\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}.

So we can introduce the number

\sigma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}-\dfrac{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}=-\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}.

Thus, we get

p_{\sigma}(\lambda)=p^{\prime}(\lambda)+\sigma p(\lambda)=0,

and

p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)=p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)+\sigma p^{\prime}(\lambda)=0.

Now formula (
2.3
) yields

F^{(j)}[p^{\prime}]=0,\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,r-2.

In particular,

F[p^{\prime}](\lambda)=p^{\prime}(\lambda)p^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda)-\left[p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\right]^{2}=0,

so

\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}=\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}=-\sigma,

and therefore,

p^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)=p^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda)+\sigma p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=0.

Now by induction we obtain the identities

F^{(j)}\left[p^{(k)}\right](\lambda)=0,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,r-1,\ j=0,1,\ldots,r-k-1,

(3.5)

which give

p^{(k+1)}_{\sigma}(\lambda)=p^{(k+2)}(\lambda)+\sigma p^{(k+1)}(\lambda)=0,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,r-1,

so

p^{(r)}_{\sigma}(\lambda)=p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)+\sigma p^{(r)}(\lambda)=0.

Since
\lambda
is a zero of
Q[p]
of multiplicity
r
, it is a simple zero of
F\left[p^{(r-1)}\right]
by (
3.5
),
that is,

F^{\prime}\left[p^{(r-1)}\right](\lambda)=p^{(r-1)}(\lambda)p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)-p^{(r)}(\lambda)p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)\neq 0,

so

\dfrac{p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)}{p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)}\neq\dfrac{p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)}{p^{(r)}(\lambda)}=-\sigma.

Thus,
p^{(r+1)}_{\sigma}(\lambda)=p^{(r+2)}(\lambda)+\sigma p^{(r+1)}(\lambda)\neq 0
, as required.
∎

As a by-product, we obtain the following curious fact.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
p
be a real polynomial with simple zeroes. Then the number of complex points that
can be multiple zeroes of the polynomials of the family
p_{\sigma}

does not exceed
2\deg p-2
, and the points do not depend on
\sigma
. Moreover, the number
of real such points is bounded by
Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p)
.

Proof.

Since
p
has no multiple zeroes, any zero of
F[p](z)
is the zero of
Q[p](z)
. Moreover,
\deg F[p]=2\deg p-2
.
Now by Theorem
3.1
, we obtain that any multiple zero of a polynomial
p_{\sigma}
for
some
\sigma
is a zero of
F[p]
. The second statement of the corollary follows from the inequality (
1.2
) and
Theorem
3.1
.
∎

4
Cauchy indices

In this section, we establish a generalisation (one direction)
of
[
2
, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1]
using the Cauchy indices
whose definition and basic properties we present first.

Consider a real rational function
R
which may have a pole at
\infty
.

Definition 4.1
.

The quantity

\Ind\nolimits_{\omega}(R)\mathop{{:}{=}}\begin{cases}\;+1&\;\text{if}\quad R(\omega-0)<0<R(\omega+0),\\
\;-1&\;\text{if}\quad R(\omega-0)>0>R(\omega+0),\end{cases}

(4.1)

is called the
index
of the function
R
at its

real
pole
\omega
of
odd
order.

We also set

\Ind\nolimits_{\omega}(R)\mathop{{:}{=}}0

(4.2)

if
\omega
is a real pole of the function
R
of
even

order.

Suppose that the function
R
has
m
real poles in total, viz.,

\omega_{1}<\omega_{2}<\dots<\omega_{m}
.

Definition 4.2
.

The quantity

\Ind\nolimits_{a}^{b}(R)\mathop{{:}{=}}\sum\limits_{i\,\colon\,a<\omega_{i}<b}\Ind\nolimits_{\omega_{i}}(R).

(4.3)

is called the
Cauchy index
of the function
R
on the
interval
(a,b)
.

We are primarily interested in the quantity
\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(R)
, the Cauchy
index of
R
on the real line. However, since the function
R

may have a pole at the point
\infty
, it is convenient for us to
consider this pole as
real
. From this point of view, let
us introduce the index at
\infty
following, e.g.,
[
1
,
6
]
.
To do so, we consider the function
R
as a map on the
projective
line

\PR^{1}\mathop{{:}{=}}\mathbb{R}^{1}\cup\{\infty\}
into itself. So, if the
function
R
has a pole at
\infty
, then we let

\Ind_{\infty}(R)\mathop{{:}{=}}\begin{cases}\;+1&\quad\text{if}\quad R(+\infty)<0<R(-\infty),\\
\;-1&\quad\text{if}\quad R(+\infty)>0>R(-\infty),\\
\;\quad\!0&\quad\text{if}\quad\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits R(+\infty)=\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits R(-\infty).\end{cases}

(4.4)

Thus, the generalised Cauchy index of the function
R
on the
projective real line is

\Ind_{\PR}(R)\mathop{{:}{=}}\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(R)+\Ind_{\infty}(R).

(4.5)

Remark 4.3
.

Obviously, if the function
R
has no pole at
\infty
, then the generalised
Cauchy index
\Ind_{\PR}(R)
coincides with the usual Cauchy index
\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(R)
.

Following
[
1
]
, we list a few properties of
generalised Cauchy indices, which will be of use later.
First, note that a polynomial
q(z)=cz^{\nu}+\cdots

(\nu=\deg q)
can be viewed as a rational function with
a single pole at
\infty
, hence

\Ind_{\infty}(q)=\begin{cases}-\mathop{\rm sign}\nolimits c\;&\text{if}\quad\nu\quad\text{is odd},\\
\;\;\;0\;&\text{if}\quad\nu\quad\text{is even}.\\
\end{cases}

(4.6)

The following theorem (see
[
1
,
6
]
and references there)
collects all properties of Cauchy indices that we need.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
R
be a real rational function.

1)

If
d
is a real constant, then
\Ind_{\PR}(d+R)=\Ind_{\PR}(R)
.

2)

If
q
is a real polynomial and
|R(\infty)|<\infty
,
then
\Ind_{\PR}(q+R)=\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(R)+\Ind_{\infty}(q)
.

3)

If
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are real rational functions that have no
real poles in common, then

\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(R_{1}+R_{2})=\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(R_{1})+\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(R_{2}).

4)

\Ind_{\PR}\left(-\dfrac{1}{R}\right)=\Ind_{\PR}(R)
.

Now we are in a position to establish an extension of an important result by T. Craven, G. Csordas
and W. Smith
[
2
, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1]
for polynomials (see also
[
4
, Problem 133]

and
[
5
, Problem 728]
).

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
p
be a real polynomial with
Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p)=2m
. If there exists
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

Q[p_{\sigma}](x)<0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
where it is defined, then

Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p]\right)=2m-2m_{\sigma},

(4.7)

where
2m_{\sigma}=Z_{\mathbb{C}}\left(p_{\sigma}\right)
. Moreover, the real zeroes of
Q[p]
different
from zeroes of
p_{\sigma}
of multiplicity greater than
2
(if any) are simple.

Note that
2m_{\sigma}\leqslant 2m
by (
1.5
).

Proof.

Suppose first that
\sigma\neq 0
, so
\deg p_{\sigma}=\deg p=:n
. By assumption

Q[p_{\sigma}](x)=\dfrac{p_{\sigma}(x)p^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(x)-[p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(x)]^{2}}{p^{2}_{\sigma}(x)}<0

(4.8)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p_{\sigma}(x)\neq 0
.

Consider the function

H(z)=\dfrac{p_{\sigma}(z)}{p(z)}=\dfrac{p^{\prime}(z)}{p(z)}+\sigma.

It is easy to see that

H^{\prime}(z)=Q[p](z)=\dfrac{p(z)p^{\prime\prime}(z)-[p^{\prime}(z)]^{2}}{p^{2}(z)},

(4.9)

and

\dfrac{H^{\prime}(z)}{H(z)}=\dfrac{p(z)p^{\prime\prime}(z)-[p^{\prime}(z)]^{2}}{p(z)p_{\sigma}(z)}=\dfrac{p(z)p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(z)-p^{\prime}(z)p_{\sigma}(z)}{p(z)p_{\sigma}(z)}=\dfrac{p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(z)}{p_{\sigma}(z)}-\dfrac{p^{\prime}(z)}{p(z)}.

(4.10)

Since
\dfrac{H^{\prime}(z)}{H(z)}\to 0
as
|z|\to\infty
, from (
4.5
) we have

\Ind_{\PR}\left(\dfrac{H^{\prime}}{H}\right)=\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\dfrac{H^{\prime}}{H}\right)

(4.11)

Suppose that
p(z)
has
d_{1}
simple real zeroes and
d_{2}
multiple real zeroes:

p(z)=a\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d_{1}}(z-\lambda_{i})\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d_{2}}(z-\mu_{j})^{n_{j}}q(z),

where
a>0
,
n_{j}\geqslant 2
,
j=1,\ldots,d_{2}
, and
q(x)\neq 0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Here
\deg q=2m
. Then

p_{\sigma}(z)=a\sigma\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d_{2}}(z-\mu_{j})^{n_{j}-1}g(z)h(z),

(4.12)

where
g(z)
has only real zeroes by assumption, and
h(x)\neq 0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\deg h=2m_{\sigma}
. So we have

\deg g=n-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d_{2}}(n_{j}-1)-2m_{\sigma}=d_{1}+d_{2}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d_{2}}(n_{j}-1)+2m-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d_{2}}(n_{j}-1)-2m_{\sigma}=d_{1}+d_{2}+2m-2m_{\sigma}.

(4.13)

Let

g(z)=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{l_{1}}(z-t_{k})\prod\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(z-s_{i})^{r_{i}},

(4.14)

where
r_{i}\geqslant 2
,
i=1,\ldots,l_{2}
, and the numbers
t_{k}
and
s_{i}
are real and different from
\mu_{j}
.
Then we have

\dfrac{p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(z)}{p_{\sigma}(z)}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\dfrac{n_{j}-1}{z-\mu_{j}}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{l_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-t_{k}}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}\dfrac{r_{i}}{z-s_{i}}+\dfrac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)},

and

\dfrac{p^{\prime}(z)}{p(z)}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-\lambda_{i}}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\dfrac{n_{j}}{z-\mu_{j}}+\dfrac{q^{\prime}(z)}{q(z)}.

Consequently, from (
4.10
) one gets

\dfrac{H^{\prime}(z)}{H(z)}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{l_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-t_{k}}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}\dfrac{r_{i}}{z-s_{i}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\dfrac{1}{z-\lambda_{i}}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\dfrac{1}{z-\mu_{j}}+\dfrac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}-\dfrac{q^{\prime}(z)}{q(z)}.

(4.15)

The polynomials
h
and
q
have no real zeroes, so their logarithmic derivatives do not contribute to
\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\dfrac{H^{\prime}}{H}\right)
. Thus,
from Theorem
4.4
and from (
4.13
)–(
4.15
) we obtain

\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\dfrac{H^{\prime}}{H}\right)=l_{1}+l_{2}-d_{1}-d_{2}=d_{1}+d_{2}+2m-2m_{\sigma}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1)-d_{1}-d_{2}=2m-2m_{\sigma}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1).

(4.16)

Note that
s_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,l_{2}
, is a zero of
Q[p]
of multiplicity
r_{i}-1
by Theorem
3.1
.

Furthermore, since

-\dfrac{H(z)}{H^{\prime}(z)}=Az^{2}+Bz+C+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{c_{k}}{z^{k}},

it follows that
\Ind_{\infty}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)=0
. Now from Theorem
4.4
, (
4.5
),
and (
4.11
) one has

\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)=\Ind_{\PR}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)=\Ind_{\PR}\dfrac{H^{\prime}}{H}=\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\dfrac{H^{\prime}}{H}=2m-2m_{\sigma}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1).

(4.17)

Note that the numbers
s_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,l_{2}
, are not poles of the function
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
, but they are simple
zeroes of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
.

Let now
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be a pole of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
. Clearly,
p(\lambda)\neq 0
and
p_{\sigma}(\lambda)\neq 0
, and by (
4.10
) we have

F[p](\lambda)=p(\lambda)p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)-[p^{\prime}(\lambda)]^{2}=0.

I.
If
p^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
, then
p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=0
, so
p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)=0
. At the same time,
p^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
, since otherwise, we would
have
p^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)=0
that contradicts (
4.8
). Thus, in this case

F^{\prime}[p](\lambda)=p(\lambda)p^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda)-p^{\prime}(\lambda)p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0,

so
\lambda
is a simple pole of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
(and a simple zero of
Q[p]
), and

-\dfrac{H(z)}{H^{\prime}(z)}=\dfrac{A_{\lambda}}{z-\lambda}+O(1)\qquad\text{as}\quad z\to\lambda,

(4.18)

where by (
4.8
) and (
4.10
), one has

A_{\lambda}=\lim\limits_{z\to\lambda}\dfrac{p(z)p_{\sigma}(z)(z-\lambda)}{p^{\prime}(z)p_{\sigma}(z)-p(z)p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(z)}=\dfrac{p(\lambda)p_{\sigma}(\lambda)}{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)p_{\sigma}(\lambda)-p(\lambda)p^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)}=-\dfrac{p_{\sigma}(\lambda)}{p_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}>0,

(4.19)

since
p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)=0
as we showed above. Thus, (
4.18
)–(
4.19
) imply

\Ind\nolimits_{\lambda}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)=1,

(4.20)

according to (
4.1
).

II.
Suppose now that
p^{\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
. Let us show that this is a simple pole of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
(a simple zero of
Q[p]
), as well.
Indeed, from (
4.10
) we have

F[p](\lambda)=p(\lambda)p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)-[p^{\prime}(\lambda)]^{2}=p(\lambda)p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)-p^{\prime}(\lambda)p_{\sigma}(\lambda)=0,

so

\dfrac{p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)}{p_{\sigma}(\lambda)}=\dfrac{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}.

(4.21)

At the same time, by (
4.8
) and (
4.21
)

\begin{array}[]{c}\dfrac{F^{\prime}[p](\lambda)}{p(\lambda)p_{\sigma}(\lambda)}=\dfrac{p(\lambda)p^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)-p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)p_{\sigma}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)p_{\sigma}(\lambda)}=\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)}{p_{\sigma}(\lambda)}-\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}<\left(\dfrac{p^{\prime}_{\sigma}(\lambda)}{p_{\sigma}(\lambda)}\right)^{2}-\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}=\\
\\
=\left(\dfrac{p^{\prime}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}\right)^{2}-\dfrac{p^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{p(\lambda)}=\dfrac{F[p](\lambda)}{p^{2}(\lambda)}=0.\end{array}

(4.22)

Thus,
\lambda
is a simple pole of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
, and from (
4.22
) we have

-\dfrac{H(z)}{H^{\prime}(z)}=\dfrac{B_{\lambda}}{z-\lambda}+O(1)\qquad\text{as}\quad z\to\lambda,

with

B_{\lambda}=\lim\limits_{z\to\lambda}\dfrac{p(z)p_{\sigma}(z)(z-\lambda)}{-F[p](z)}=-\dfrac{p(\lambda)p_{\sigma}(\lambda)}{F^{\prime}[p](\lambda)}>0,

so (
4.20
) holds.

Thus, we obtained that all the real poles of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
are simple, and the Cauchy index of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
at every
real pole equals
1
. From (
4.17
) it follows now that the function
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
has exactly

2m-2m_{\sigma}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1)

(4.23)

real poles, counting multiplicities. All these poles are real simple zeroes of the function
Q[p]
,
according to (
4.9
)–(
4.10
). Additionally, the function
Q[p]

has real zeroes at the points
s_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,l_{2}
, and the number
s_{i}
is a zero of
Q[p]
of
multiplicity
r_{i}-1
. In particular, it is a multiple zero of
Q[p]
if
r_{i}\geqslant 3
. As we mentioned
above, at the points
s_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,l_{2}
, the function
Q[p]
has exactly

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1)

real zeros, counting multiplicities, which together with (
4.23
)
gives (
4.7
).

Let now
\sigma=0
. Then the formula (
4.7
) can be proved in a similar way.
The only difference is that for
\sigma=0
,
p_{0}=p^{\prime}+0\cdot p=p^{\prime}
, and
\deg p_{0}=n-1
. In this case,
the index of the logarithmic derivative of
H
on the real line is

\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\dfrac{H^{\prime}}{H}\right)=2m-2m_{\sigma}-1-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1),

see (
4.16
), and

-\dfrac{H(z)}{H^{\prime}(z)}=z+c_{0}+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{c_{k}}{z^{k}},

so it follows that
\Ind_{\infty}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)=-1
, and from Theorem
4.4
, (
4.5
),
and (
4.11
) we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)-1=\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)+\Ind_{\infty}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)=\Ind_{\PR}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)=\\
\\
=\Ind_{\PR}\left(\dfrac{H^{\prime}}{H}\right)=\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\dfrac{H^{\prime}}{H}\right)=2m-2m_{\sigma}-1-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1).\end{array}

Therefore,

\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)=2m-2m_{\sigma}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1),

where
r_{i}
and
l_{2}
are defined in (
4.12
) and (
4.14
) with
p_{0}=p^{\prime}
.
Consequently, the numbers
s_{i}
are zeroes of
p^{\prime}
, so if
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a pole of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
, then
p^{\prime}(\lambda)\neq 0
.
In a similar way as above, one can show that every real pole of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
is simple, and the index of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}

at each real pole is
1
. Thus, formula (
4.7
) can be proved in the same way as for the case
\sigma\neq 0
.
∎

As a by-product, from the proof of Theorem
4.5
we easily obtain the following fact.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
p
be a real polynomial with
Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p)=2m
, and let

2m_{\sigma^{*}}=\min\limits_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}}\left(Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p_{\sigma})\right),

where the polynomial
p_{\sigma}
is defined in (
1.4
). Then

Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p]\right)\geqslant 2m-2m_{\sigma^{*}}.

(4.24)

Proof.

Indeed, from the proof of Theorem
4.5
it follows that for
arbitrary
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}

\Ind_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}\right)=2m-2m_{\sigma}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1),

(4.25)

where
r_{i}
are multiplicities of multiple zeroes
s_{i}
of
p_{\sigma}
which are not
zeroes of
p
. The poles of
-\dfrac{H}{H^{\prime}}
are zeroes of
Q[p]
. Moreover, according to
Definition
4.1
, the zeroes of
Q[p]
of even multiplicity do
not contribute to the index (
4.25
). Thus,
the index (
4.25
) is equal to the sum of

distinct
zeroes of
Q[p]
of odd multiplicities with indices
of various signs (if any) at them. Consequently, we have

Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p]\right)\geqslant 2m-Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p_{\sigma})-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1).

(4.26)

Now recall that each zero
s_{i}
of
p_{\sigma}
of multiplicity
r_{i}
is a zero of
Q[p]

of multiplicity
r_{i}-1
,
i=1,\ldots,l_{2}
. Therefore, additionally to the zeroes guaranteed
by the index (
4.25
), the function
Q[p]
has
exactly

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l_{2}}(r_{i}-1)

zeroes, counting multiplicities, at the points
s_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,l_{2}
. Now
from (
4.26
) it follows that

Z_{\mathbb{R}}\left(Q[p]\right)\geqslant 2m-Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p_{\sigma})

for any
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
2m-Z_{\mathbb{C}}(p_{\sigma})\leqslant 2m-2m_{\sigma^{*}}
, the lower bound (
4.24
) is true.
∎
