Computed Tomographic Volumetric Analysis of the Nasal Cavities in Domestic Cats (Felis catus) and Anatomical Implications for Intranasal Drug Delivery
Authors: Montel Charles, Charlier Hippolyte, Paquet Charlène, Jourdan Géraldine, Mogicato Giovanni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo perform a computed tomography (CT)‐based volumetric assessment of the nasal cavities in domestic cats and to evaluate the relationship between nasal cavity volume and selected morphometric variables. Fifty‐six European cats that underwent CT examination of the head were retrospectively included. Three‐dimensional segmentation of the right and left nasal cavities was performed using dedicated software. Total, right, and left nasal cavity volumes were measured. Associations between nasal cavity volume and sex, age, and body weight were assessed using multiple linear regression analysis. Bilateral symmetry was evaluated using paired comparisons. Intra‐observer reliability was determined using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Mean total nasal cavity volume was 6.74 ± 1.79 mL. No significant difference was observed between left (3.38 ± 0.92 mL) and right (3.36 ± 0.91 mL) nasal cavity volumes. Male cats exhibited significantly larger nasal cavity volumes than females (8.3 ± 1.7 mL vs. 5.8 ± 1.1 mL; p < 0.001). No significant association was found between nasal cavity volume and age or body weight after adjustment for sex. Intra‐observer agreement was excellent (ICC = 0.98). CT‐based three‐dimensional segmentation provides reliable volumetric assessment of the feline nasal cavities. Significant sexual dimorphism was identified, whereas laterality, age, and body weight had no significant influence on nasal cavity volume. The limited nasal cavity volume supports the use of low‐volume, concentrated formulations for intranasal drug delivery in cats.Keywords: cat, computed tomography, intranasal administration, morphometry, nasal cavity, volumetry

Introduction

Among emerging administration routes, intranasal (IN) drug delivery has gained interest in veterinary medicine because it offers an effective way to directly reach the central nervous system while partially bypassing the blood–brain barrier, and is often characterized by a faster and more intense clinical response than that observed after intramuscular administration (Bustamante et al. 2024). IN administration has been investigated especially for medications requiring a rapid onset of action, such as alpha‐2 agonists (Bustamante et al. 2024; Hommuang et al. 2023; Kaya and Yanmaz 2025; Liu et al. 2025), benzodiazepines (Freitag et al. 2022; Okur et al. 2023), ketamine (Marjani et al. 2015; Vlerick et al. 2020), and opioids (Cinar and Yanmaz 2024).

The nasal cavity represents a highly specialized anatomical structure in mammals (Barone 1997; Evans and Christensen 2013; Singh 2017). Beyond its respiratory role, it is involved in air filtration, humidification, thermoregulation, and olfaction (Evans and Christensen 2013; Xi et al. 2023). In carnivores, the nasal cavity is characterized by a complex arrangement of conchae and ethmoturbinal structures that markedly increase mucosal surface area within a relatively limited osseous space (Barone 1997; Evans and Christensen 2013). In the cat, this organization is particularly compact, reflecting the small skull size and the functional importance of olfactory processing (Xi et al. 2023).

From a pharmacological perspective, nasal anatomy is of major relevance because the geometry of the cavity may directly influence intranasal drug deposition, mucosal contact time, clearance, and systemic absorption (Pasteur et al. 2024). Limited intranasal cavity volume may restrict administrable doses, whereas turbinal complexity may affect the distribution pattern of liquid droplets or aerosols (Djupesland 2013; Ousaid et al. 2020; Touitou and Illum 2013). Accordingly, quantitative anatomical information is essential for the rational development of feline intranasal administration protocols.

Despite the clinical interest of the intranasal route (Alwali et al. 2026), morphometric data regarding the feline nasal cavity remain scarce. Previous studies have mainly focused on descriptive anatomy (Diaz Martinez et al. 2024), rhinoscopic findings (Diaz Martinez et al. 2024; Elie and Sabo 2006), pathological imaging (Losonsky et al. 1997; Reetz et al. 2006), or acoustic rhinometry (Straszek et al. 2003). Although computed tomography (CT) is widely recognized as the reference imaging modality for detailed evaluation of the nasal cavity in animals (Losonsky et al. 1997; Mogicato et al. 2012; Montel et al. 2026; Reetz et al. 2006), few studies have provided volumetric quantification of the normal feline nasal airspace using three‐dimensional segmentation techniques.

CT‐based volumetric analysis offers several advantages for anatomical investigation. It enables non‐destructive three‐dimensional reconstruction of complex cavities, objective quantification of air‐filled spaces, and evaluation of bilateral symmetry and interindividual variation (Borowska et al. 2024; Craven et al. 2007). Such methods may provide clinically relevant reference values while improving understanding of anatomical constraints associated with IN drug delivery.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to perform a CT‐based volumetric assessment of the nasal cavities in domestic cats and to evaluate the relationship between nasal cavity volume and selected morphometric variables.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This study included 56 domestic cats ( Felis catus ) that underwent CT examination for various clinical indications. Only examinations including the head region with sufficient image quality for post‐processing analysis were included. Cases with severe artefacts, extensive nasal cavity destruction, or major structural asymmetry unrelated to physiological variation were excluded. Bodyweight, sex, age and breed were recorded for each animal.

The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines for the use of anonymized clinical imaging data. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee ‘Sciences et Santé Animale 115—Ecole Nationale Vétérinaire de Toulouse’ with the authorization number APAFIS# 21559‐2019071917392588v3.

CT Imaging Protocol

All CT examinations were performed using a 16‐slice helical CT scanner (Optima CT540, GE Healthcare, USA). The animals were positioned with the mandible resting on the CT table and aligned perpendicularly to the hard palate in the rostrocaudal direction. Dorsoventral and lateral scout views ensured correct alignment. Scanning was conducted from the nostrils to the atlanto‐occipital joint. Images were acquired using a 0.625 mm slice thickness at 0.3 mm intervals. Acquisition parameters were as follows: reconstruction interval: 0.3 mm, tube voltage: 140 kVp, 60 mAs, field of view: 512 × 512, pitch factor: 0.5625. Images were stored in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format and anonymized prior to analysis.

Image Processing and Segmentation

Bone window (CT image reconstruction setting optimized for bone detail) (window level 500 Hounsfield Unit (HU) and window width 2500 HU) images were reviewed for nasal and sinonasal diseases, anomalies, and assessment of image quality.

Segmentation was performed separately for the right and left nasal cavities. The nasal cavities were segmented using 3D Slicer software (www.slicer.org v. 5.10.0 stable release). First, an intensity threshold (between −1024 to 400 HU) was applied to isolate the air‐filled spaces of the nasal cavities. The resulting mask was used to restrict the analysis to the selected regions. Regions of interest corresponding to the nasal cavities were then identified using fiducial markers from the Segment Editor Extra Effects extension (version aa3103b). Segmentation was subsequently refined by manually removing disconnected structures and artefacts that did not belong to the nasal cavities. Finally, the segmented cavities were reconstructed and visualized in three dimensions using the Show 3D function, allowing volumetric measurements and morphometric analyses to be performed. The following variables were recorded: right nasal cavity volume (mL), left nasal cavity volume (mL), total nasal cavity volume (mL).

Reproducibility and Measurement Protocol

To assess measurement reliability, a subset of 10 randomly selected cases was re‐segmented by the same observer after a 2‐week interval. Intra‐observer agreement was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 11.0.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test.

Differences in total nasal cavity volume between male and female cats were evaluated using an unpaired Student's t‐test. Left and right nasal cavity volumes were compared using a paired Student's t‐test.

To investigate the influence of age and body weight on total nasal cavity volume while accounting for potential sex‐related effects, a multiple linear regression analysis was performed with total nasal cavity volume as the dependent variable and sex, age, and body weight as independent variables.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Nasal Cavity Volumes

Table 1 shows the nasal cavity volume values of the 56 cats. The mean left and right nasal cavity volumes were 4.1 ± 0.9 mL and 4.0 ± 0.8 mL in males, compared with 2.9 ± 0.6 mL and 2.8 ± 0.5 mL in females, respectively. The mean total nasal cavity volume was 8.3 ± 1.7 mL in males and 5.8 ± 1.1 mL in females.

Relationship With Morphometric Variables

Total nasal cavity volume was significantly higher in male cats compared with female cats (8.3 ± 1.7 mL vs. 5.8 ± 1.1 mL, respectively; p < 0.001) (Figure 4).

Multiple linear regression analysis demonstrated no significant association between nasal cavity volume and age or body weight after adjustment for sex (p > 0.05 for both variables).

Intra‐Observer Reliability

Intra‐observer agreement for volumetric measurements was excellent, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.98, indicating high repeatability of the segmentation and volume extraction protocol.

Variability and Anatomical Observations

Marked interindividual variability was observed in nasal cavity morphology. Differences in turbinal complexity, nasal passage width, and overall cavity configuration were evident across individuals. Despite this variability, the general anatomical pattern was consistent, with a highly convoluted turbinal system occupying the majority of the nasal cavity space. No gross anatomical abnormalities affecting the nasal cavity architecture were observed in the included population.

Discussion

This study provides quantitative volumetric data regarding the feline nasal cavities using three‐dimensional CT segmentation. To our knowledge, morphometric volumetric assessment of the nasal cavities in cats is still limited in the veterinary literature, despite the increasing clinical and research interest in sinonasal imaging and intranasal therapeutic approaches. The use of CT‐based three‐dimensional segmentation enabled detailed anatomical delineation of the nasal cavities while preserving the complex geometry of the turbinates and meatuses. Such approaches are increasingly used in human medicine for surgical planning, airflow modeling, and intranasal drug delivery studies. In veterinary medicine, these methods remain relatively underdeveloped, particularly in cats.

Direct volumetric data on the feline nasal cavity remain scarce in the veterinary literature. Previous studies have primarily focused on qualitative descriptions of nasal anatomy, acoustic rhinometry, or partial volumetric assessments using casts or imaging‐based approximations (Barone 1997; Diaz Martinez et al. 2024; Elie and Sabo 2006; Evans and Christensen 2013).

Experimental work combining acoustic rhinometry and physical casting has previously demonstrated that in cats, in vivo measurements may underestimate true anatomical volume depending on mucosal status, with reported variability linked to nasal congestion and physiological conditions (Straszek et al. 2003). This highlights the dynamic nature of the nasal cavity and supports the use of CT‐based segmentation as a more anatomically robust approach for structural evaluation (Bakıcı et al. 2020; Smith et al. 2021; Teeling et al. 2020).

In comparison with the dog, which exhibits extreme morphological diversity related to craniofacial conformation, the cat appears more homogeneous, although still subject to measurable variation linked to cranial proportions (Gündemir et al. 2023; Schmidt et al. 2017). In humans, nasal cavity volumes are generally larger in absolute terms but proportionally smaller relative to body size, reflecting different functional evolutionary pressures (Loftus et al. 2016).

Principal Findings

One of the main findings of this study was the significant sexual dimorphism observed in nasal cavity volume. Male cats exhibited significantly larger nasal cavity volumes than females, with a mean total volume approximately 43% greater than that measured in female cats. This result is consistent with the general craniofacial dimorphism described in domestic cats, in which males typically present larger skull dimensions and more robust cranial conformations (Boonsri et al. 2019; Gittleman and Valkenburgh 1997; Pitakarnnop et al. 2017). The observed volumetric difference likely reflects overall differences in skull size rather than specific functional adaptations of the nasal cavities themselves.

Interestingly, despite this marked sex‐related difference, no significant association was identified between nasal cavity volume and body weight after adjustment for sex. This finding suggests that nasal cavity volume may be more strongly influenced by craniofacial morphology and sexual dimorphism than by body mass alone. Similarly, no significant correlation was found between age and nasal cavity volume. The absence of age‐related volumetric variation in the present population may indicate that the nasal cavities reach stable adult dimensions relatively early in life in European cats. However, subtle age‐related changes affecting mucosal thickness, turbinate density, or sinonasal airflow could still occur without substantially modifying global cavity volume (Smith et al. 2021; Teeling et al. 2020). No significant left–right asymmetry was detected, supporting the reliability of segmentation and the general bilateral symmetry of feline nasal chambers—an observation relevant for conditions with unilateral disease.

Anatomical and Functional Implications

The present values provide reference data for anatomical, clinical, and translational studies, including computational fluid dynamics modeling and comparisons across craniofacial types. The feline nasal cavity shows high turbinate complexity within a constrained osseous space, which increases mucosal surface area but limits total airspace volume (Alwali et al. 2026; Bustamante et al. 2024).

The clinical relevance of the present findings should be considered in light of the growing interest in intranasal drug administration in cats. Intranasal delivery is no longer restricted to experimental applications, as sedative and analgesic protocols based on dexmedetomidine, alone or combined with opioids such as morphine or tramadol, have demonstrated effective sedation and anaesthetic‐sparing effects in feline patients (Hommuang et al. 2023). Recent studies have shown that intranasal dexmedetomidine can induce clinically relevant sedation comparable to intramuscular administration while providing a non‐invasive route of drug delivery (Di Franco et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025). However, current dosing regimens are primarily based on pharmacological efficacy and drug concentration rather than on the anatomical capacity of the feline nasal cavity. Consequently, the administration volume may not always be optimized for feline nasal anatomy. The volumetric data generated in the present study therefore provide anatomical information that may assist in the future development of concentrated low‐volume intranasal formulations and delivery devices. Such optimization could improve mucosal deposition while minimizing drug loss through external leakage, swallowing, or drainage into the nasopharynx.

Limitations

Several limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged.

First, this is a retrospective CT‐based study, and imaging was not performed under standardized physiological conditions. Nasal cavity volume may therefore be influenced by transient factors such as mucosal congestion, respiratory cycle, or sedation status at the time of imaging (Bakıcı et al. 2020; Brinkschulte et al. 2013; Teeling et al. 2020).

Second, only adult European cats were included, limiting extrapolation to other breeds with different craniofacial conformations (Schmidt et al. 2017). The sample size, although consistent with previous veterinary morphometric studies, remains limited and may not fully capture breed‐specific variability.

Third, segmentation was performed semi‐manually, which may introduce operator‐dependent variability despite careful standardization. In addition, only global cavity volumes were assessed; separate analysis of specific anatomical regions such as the ethmoidal region, ventral meatus, or frontal recesses was beyond the scope of the present work.

Future studies could investigate breed‐related variations, age‐related changes in young or geriatric populations, and the relationship between nasal cavity morphology and respiratory function. Automated or AI‐assisted segmentation techniques may also improve reproducibility and facilitate large‐scale morphometric analyses (Litjens et al. 2017).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that CT‐based three‐dimensional segmentation allows reliable volumetric assessment of the feline nasal cavities. Significant sexual dimorphism was identified, whereas no influence of age, body weight, or laterality was observed. These characteristics have direct implications for intranasal drug delivery, supporting the need for low‐volume, carefully formulated administration strategies tailored to feline nasal anatomy.
