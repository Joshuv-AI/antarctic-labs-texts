On the distributed resistor-constant phase element transmission line in a reflective bounded domain
Anis Allagui ∗, Enrique H. Balaguera, Chunlei Wang

Abstract
In this work we derive and study the analytical solution of the voltage and current diffusion equation for the case of a finite-length resistor-constant phase element (CPE) transmission line (TL) network that can represent a model for porous electrodes in the absence of any Faradic processes. The energy storage component is considered to be an elemental CPE per unit length of impedance z c ​ ( s ) = 1 / ( c α ​ s α ) z_{c}(s)={1}/{(c_{\alpha}s^{\alpha})} with constant parameters ( c α , α ) (c_{\alpha},\alpha) instead of the ideal capacitor of impedance z ⁡ ( s ) = 1 / ( c ​ s ) z(s)={1}/{(c\,s)} usually assumed in TL modeling. The problem becomes a time-fractional diffusion equation for the voltage that we solve under galvanostatic charging, and derive from it a reduced impedance function of the form z α ( s n ) = s n − α / 2 coth ( s n α / 2 ) z_{\alpha}(s_{n})=s_{n}^{-\alpha/2}\coth({s_{n}^{\alpha/2}}) , where s n = j ​ ω n s_{n}=j\omega_{n} is a normalized frequency. We also derive the system’s step response, and the distribution function of relaxation times associated with it. The analysis can be viewed and used as a support for the fractal finite-length Warburg model.

We recall that in a single pore of an electrified porous electrode filled with electrolyte, the electrolyte’s resistance and the electrolyte-electrode interface double-layer capacitance can be viewed respectively as a distributed resistance and distributed capacitance over the the length of the pore
[
13

. This equivalent ladder-like network model can be represented by cutting up the overall resistance and capacitance, and connecting the building blocks in a TL network (Fig.

[
14

\frac{\partial v(x,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{rc}\frac{\partial^{2}v(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}},\quad 0<x<\infty,\;t\geqslant 0

In a recent contribution by some of the co-authors

This is to account more generally for the anomalous resistive-capacitive behavior observed in both frequency and time-domain data on most porous electrodes used in practical applications (supercapacitors, fuel cells, batteries, etc.). The current on the CPE is given by the fractional-order differential equation:

i_{c}(x,t)=C_{\alpha}\,{}_{0}\text{D}_{t}^{\alpha}v_{c}(x,t)

\alpha

{}_{0}\text{D}_{t}^{\alpha}f(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(m-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{m-\alpha-1}f^{(m)}(\tau)d\tau

Here

,
m-1<\alpha<m
(

\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{z-1}e^{-u}du,\,(\text{Re}(z)>0)

This leads in the frequency domain to the impedance of the CPE being

II
Theory

II.1
Statement of the problem

We consider in this study the time-fractional partial differential equation (PDE) of 1D diffusion over the bounded domain
[0;L]
:

{}_{0}\text{D}_{t}^{\alpha}v(x,t)=\frac{1}{rc_{\alpha}}\frac{\partial^{2}v(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}},\quad 0<x<L,\;t>0

(8)

Furthermore, we consider the following boundary Neumann conditions
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{r_{0}}\left.\frac{\partial v(x,t)}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=i_{0},\quad t>0

(9)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial v(x,t)}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L}

\displaystyle=0,\quad t>0

(10)

where
i_{0}
is a constant current,
and the initial condition:

v(x,t=0)=0,\quad 0<x<L

(11)

Again, this TL model describes the case of galvanostatic charging of a uniform and homogeneous pore in a porous conductive material completely filled with an electrolyte, where the capacitive behavior is represented by a CPE of constant parameters (Fig.
2
).

II.2
Solution by operational method

First we represent the voltage
v(x,t)
as
[
28
]
:

v(x,t)=v_{1}(x,t)+v_{2}(x,t)

(12)

The function
v_{2}(x,t)
is chosen to satisfy the nonhomogeneous boundary conditions given by Eqs.
9
and
10
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial v_{2}(x,t)}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=-r_{0}i_{0},\quad t>0

(13)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial v_{2}(x,t)}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L}

\displaystyle=0,\quad t>0

(14)

which results in
[
28
]
:

v_{2}(x,t)=-r_{0}i_{0}x+\frac{r_{0}i_{0}x^{2}}{2L}

(15)

The problem of
v_{1}(x,t)
is

{}_{0}\text{D}_{t}^{\alpha}v_{1}(x,t)=\frac{1}{rc_{\alpha}}\frac{\partial^{2}v_{1}(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}},\quad 0<x<L,\;t>0

(16)

with the homogeneous boundary conditions

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial v_{1}(x,t)}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=0,\quad t>0

(17)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial v_{1}(x,t)}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L}

\displaystyle=0,\quad t>0

(18)

and the initial condition

v_{1}(x,t=0)=r_{0}i_{0}x-\frac{r_{0}i_{0}x^{2}}{2L},\quad 0<x<L

(19)

We now apply the finite Fourier cosine transform to the problem of
v_{1}(x,t)
with respect to the variable
x
.
The finite Fourier cosine transform is
defined for a function
f(x)
in the interval
[0;L]
as
[
29
,
30
,
31
]

\bar{f}(k)=\mathcal{F}_{c}[{f}(x);k]=\int\limits_{0}^{L}{f}(x)\cos(k\pi x/L)dx

(20)

where
k=1,2,\ldots
. This is derived from the Fourier cosine series for
f(x)
:

f(x)=\frac{a_{0}}{2}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\cos(k\pi x/L)

(21)

Its inverse transform is given by:

f(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{c}[\bar{f}(k);x]=\frac{1}{L}\bar{f}(0)+\frac{2}{L}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\bar{f}(k)\cos(k\pi x/L)

(22)

After taking into account the boundary conditions (Eqs.
17
and
18
), the transformed equation becomes:

{}_{0}\text{D}_{t}^{\alpha}\bar{v}_{1}(k,t)+\frac{(k\pi)^{2}}{rc_{\alpha}L^{2}}\bar{v}_{1}(k,t)=0

(23)

We carry on by taking the Laplace transform
of both sides of Eq.
23
with respect to
t
, which leads to the diffusion equation in the
(k,s)
-domain to be:

\tilde{\bar{v}}_{1}(k,s)=\frac{\bar{g}_{1}(k,0)s^{\alpha-1}}{s^{\alpha}+{(k\pi)^{2}}/{(rc_{\alpha}L^{2})}}

(24)

where:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{1}(k,0)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{c}[v_{1}(x,t=0);k]

(25)

\displaystyle=r_{0}i_{0}L^{2}\frac{-2k\pi+(2+k^{2}\pi^{2})\sin(k\pi)}{2k^{3}\pi^{3}}

(26)

We recall that the Laplace transform is defined for a function
f(t)\;(t>0)
as

\tilde{f}(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f(t);s\}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}f(t)dt,\;\;\text{Re}(s)>0

(27)

and the inverse Laplace transform is defined as:

f(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{\tilde{f}(s);t\}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}e^{st}\tilde{f}(s)ds

(28)

Also, the Laplace transform of the Caputo time-fractional derivative of order
\alpha
is given by:

\mathcal{L}\left[{}_{0}\text{D}_{t}^{\alpha}f(t);s\right]=s^{\alpha}\tilde{f}(s)-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m-1}s^{\alpha-k-1}f^{(k)}(0^{+})

(29)

Now for the inversion steps, we start first by applying the inverse Laplace transform using the Prabhakar formula
[
32
]
:

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{s^{\beta-1}}{s^{\alpha}+a};t\right\}=t^{\alpha-\beta}\text{E}_{\alpha,\alpha-\beta+1}\left(-at^{\alpha}\right)

(30)

where
\text{E}_{a,b}^{c}(z)
is the three-parameter Mittag-Leffler function defined by
[
33
]
:

\text{E}_{a,b}^{c}(z)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(c)_{k}}{\Gamma(ak+b)}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}\quad(a,b,c\in\mathbb{C},\mathrm{Re}({a})>0)

(31)

with
(c)_{k}=c(c+1)\ldots(c+k-1)=\Gamma(c+k)/\Gamma(c)
.
Thus, we obtain:

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{1}(k,t)

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{1}(k,0)\text{E}_{\alpha}\left(-\frac{(k\pi)^{2}t^{\alpha}}{rc_{\alpha}L^{2}}\right)

(32)

Then, by inverse finite Fourier cosine transform,
we obtain the voltage part
v_{1}(x,t)
as the infinite series:

\displaystyle v_{1}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}i_{0}L}{3}

\displaystyle-{2r_{0}i_{0}L}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(k\pi x/L)}{(k\pi)^{2}}\text{E}_{\alpha}\left(-\frac{(k\pi)^{2}t^{\alpha}}{rc_{\alpha}L^{2}}\right)

(33)

Similar results were shown also by Luchko
[
34
,
35
]
using the method of separation of variables.
The total voltage in the TL network is therefore given by:

\displaystyle v(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}i_{0}L}{3}-r_{0}i_{0}x+\frac{r_{0}i_{0}x^{2}}{2L}+\frac{r_{0}i_{0}}{rc_{\alpha}L}\frac{t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}

\displaystyle-{2r_{0}i_{0}L}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(k\pi x/L)}{(k\pi)^{2}}\text{E}_{\alpha}\left(-\frac{(k\pi)^{2}t^{\alpha}}{rc_{\alpha}L^{2}}\right)

(34)

with
[
28
]
:

\frac{r_{0}i_{0}}{rc_{\alpha}L}\frac{t^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}=\frac{1}{2}\,{}_{0}\text{D}_{t}^{-\alpha}\left(\frac{2}{L}\int\limits_{0}^{L}\frac{r_{0}i_{0}}{rc_{\alpha}L}dx\right)

(35)

in which the term in the integrand being

\frac{r_{0}i_{0}}{rc_{\alpha}L}=\frac{1}{rc_{\alpha}}\frac{\partial^{2}v_{2}(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}-{}_{0}\text{D}_{t}^{\alpha}v_{2}(x,t)

(36)

For
\alpha=1
, the voltage reduces to the ideal
RC
-based TL case (some terms are missing in
[
27
]
):

\displaystyle v(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}i_{0}L}{3}-r_{0}i_{0}x+\frac{r_{0}i_{0}x^{2}}{2L}+\frac{r_{0}i_{0}\,t}{rcL}

\displaystyle-{2\,r_{0}i_{0}L}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(k\pi x/L)}{(k\pi)^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(k\pi)^{2}t}{rc_{1}L^{2}}\right)

(37)

We verify using
[
36
]
:

\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\frac{\pi x}{2}+\frac{x^{2}}{4},\quad 0\leqslant x\leqslant 2\pi

(38)

that the initial voltage at
t=0
is indeed zero.

The current
i(x,t)
along the TL can be obtained from the gradient of the voltage via:

i(x,t)=-\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial v(x,t)}{\partial x}

(39)

which gives:

\displaystyle i(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}i_{0}(L-x)}{rL}

\displaystyle-\frac{2r_{0}i_{0}}{r}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(k\pi x/L)}{(k\pi)}\text{E}_{\alpha}\left(-\frac{(k\pi)^{2}t}{rc_{\alpha}L^{2}}\right)

(40)

We verify that for
x=0
and
r=r_{0}
, we retrieve the input current
i_{0}
(boundary condition given by Eq.
9
), and for
x=L
, the current is zero.

II.3
Derivation of impedance function

The impedance of the electrode/electrolyte system is computed from the ratio of the Laplace transform of the voltage by that of the current at the surface
x=0

[
15
,
14
]
, i.e.:

Z_{\text{TL}}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{L}\left[v(x=0,t);s\right]}{i_{0}/s}

(41)

With the use of the Laplace transform formula for an the Mittag-Leffler function (Eq.
30
)
and knowing that
\coth(z)
can be expressed by the series representation:

\coth(z)=\frac{1}{z}+2z\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{z^{2}+(k\pi)^{2}}

(42)

the definition given in Eq.
41
leads to the reduced impedance function:

\frac{Z_{\text{TL}}(s)}{r_{0}L}=\frac{\coth\left(\sqrt{rc_{\alpha}L^{2}s^{\alpha}}\right)}{\sqrt{rc_{\alpha}L^{2}s^{\alpha}}}

(43)

This is clearly a direct generalization of the case of
RC
-based TL network having
\alpha=1

[
1
,
14
]
. Taking the normalized angular frequency
s_{n}=s(rc_{\alpha}L^{2})^{1/\alpha}
, we can rewrite the impedance function in the form:

\frac{Z_{\text{TL}}(s_{n})}{r_{0}L}={{s_{n}^{-\alpha/2}}}{\coth({s_{n}^{\alpha/2}})}

(44)

Furthermore, we can proceed by applying one round of inverse Laplace transform using the Prabhakar formula (Eq.
30
)
or using

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{\mathcal{L}\{v(x=0,t);s\}}{i_{0}/s};t\right\}=\frac{1}{i_{0}}\frac{\partial v(x=0,t)}{\partial t}

(45)

while taking into account the zero initial condition (Eq.
11
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{-1}

\displaystyle\{Z_{\text{TL}}(s);t\}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}t^{\alpha-1}}{rc_{\alpha}L\,\Gamma(\alpha)}+\frac{2r_{0}}{rc_{\alpha}L}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}\text{E}_{\alpha,\alpha}\left(-\frac{(k\pi)^{2}t^{\alpha}}{rc_{\alpha}L^{2}}\right)

(46)

Note that the
m^{th}
(
m\in\mathbb{N}
) derivative of
t^{\beta-1}E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}(at^{\alpha})
is given by
[
37
]
:

\frac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}t^{\beta-1}E_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}(at^{\alpha})=t^{\beta-1-m}E_{\alpha,\beta-m}^{\gamma}(at^{\alpha})

(47)

and

\text{E}_{\alpha,0}(z)=z\text{E}_{\alpha,\alpha}(z)

(48)

Eq.
46
corresponds to the TL system’s
response function commonly obtained in experimental chronopotentiometric experiments.
We verify that for
\alpha=1
, we obtain:

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{Z_{\text{TL}}(s);t\}=\frac{r_{0}}{rc_{1}L}\vartheta_{3}\left[0,\exp\left({-\frac{\pi^{2}t}{r{c_{1}}L^{2}}}\right)\right]

(49)

where
\vartheta_{3}(z,q)
is the Jacobi elliptic theta function defined as
[
38
]
:

\vartheta_{3}(z,q)=1+2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{k^{2}}\cos(2kz)

(50)

By applying another round of inverse Laplace transform to Eq.
46
, we can derive the distribution function of relaxation times
g(\tau)
following the Debye
RC
elemental process, as shown in
[
39
]
. We use the inverse Laplace transform of the Mittag-Leffler function
[
40
]
(formula #92):

x^{1-1/\alpha}\text{E}_{\alpha,\alpha}(-x)=\frac{\sin(\alpha\pi)}{\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{\alpha}e^{-x^{1/\alpha}u}du}{1+2u^{\alpha}\cos(\alpha\pi)+u^{2\alpha}}

(51)

where
x
is replaced with
bs^{\alpha}
and
u
is replaced with
b^{-1/\alpha}v
, which gives:

\displaystyle s^{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle\text{E}_{\alpha,\alpha}(-bs^{\alpha})=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\alpha\pi)}{\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{v^{\alpha}e^{-sv}dv}{b^{1/\alpha+1}+2b^{1/\alpha}v^{\alpha}\cos(\alpha\pi)+b^{1/\alpha-1}v^{2\alpha}}

Then, from Eq.

the following expression for

is derived:

\displaystyle g(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\pi\alpha)\tau^{\alpha-1}}{\pi r{c_{\alpha}}L^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2\,}{b_{k}^{1/\alpha+1}\tau^{2\alpha}+2b_{k}^{1/\alpha}\tau^{\alpha}\cos(\alpha\pi)+b_{k}^{1/\alpha-1}}\right)

(53)

where
b_{k}=(k\pi)^{2}/(rc_{\alpha}L^{2})
and
0<\alpha<1
.
This means that the impedance function of a self-similar

-CPE TL network can be expressed as the integral

\frac{Z_{\text{TL}}(s)}{r_{0}L}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{g(\tau)}{{1+s\tau}}d\tau

using the reduced impedance of an

), with

(Eq.

. Note that

-function
[

]
, knowing that the generalized Mittag-Leffler function can be expressed in terms of the

-function as

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we showed that the modified impedance model
{{{s_{n}^{-\alpha/2}}}\coth({s_{n}^{\alpha/2}})}
obtained empirically by inserting the dispersion coefficient
\alpha
into the classical theory
{{{s_{n}^{-1/2}}}\coth({s_{n}^{1/2}})}
that describes bounded diffusion, can be derived exactly from the (Caputo) time-fractional diffusion equation (Eq.
8
). This corresponds to a homogeneous TL model consisting of distributed
R
s and CPEs of constant parameters. Importantly, we also derived by inverse Laplace transform the system’s response to a step function (Eq.
46
), and by another round of inverse Laplace transform the distribution function of relaxation times of the Debye type (Eq.
53
or
60
). These formulas are provided in terms of the Mittag-Leffler function, which is a special function frequently encountered in fractional calculus. The results of this study can find many practical applications for the fundamental analysis of electrochemical systems in the time and frequency domains, as well as other mass or heat problems obeying Eq.
8
.
