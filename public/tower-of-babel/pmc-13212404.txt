A needle-free bubble injector enabling depth-controlled isotropic and anisotropic perforation using electrically induced bubbles and heat generated by electric discharge
Authors: Yuudai Aokusa, Nobutoshi Ota, Shigeaki Miura, Mayu Nakahigashi, Yuma Minami, Yoko Yamanishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransdermal drug delivery is advantageous in administering therapeutic agents, such as many vaccines and macromolecular drugs that do not remain in the body after oral ingestion. Needle-free injectors have been developed as an alternative to the existing syringes for drug delivery; however, because of the difficulties in controlling the jet flow during perforation, their practical use remains a challenge. Herein, we propose a novel needle-free injector. By controlling the distance between the target and the device, the injector performs (i) isotropic perforation using electrically induced bubbles or (ii) anisotropic perforation using localized heating generated by electric discharge at the device tip. Furthermore, by controlling the applied voltage, the size of the electrically induced bubble and the speed of the microjet generated by bubble collapse can be regulated. As a result, the perforation depth was controlled in the range of 0.7–1.1 mm, which is difficult to achieve using conventional needle-free injectors. These results indicate the potential of the proposed method for precise and minimally invasive transdermal drug delivery.Keywords: Electrically induced bubble, Electric discharge, Needle-free injector, Depth control

Introduction

Transdermal drug delivery, unlike oral administration, has the advantage of avoiding digestion and absorption processes in the body. In recent years, the use of drug therapies employing macromolecules that do not remain in the body after oral ingestion and exhibit low diffusion rates through the skin, as well as other therapies, such as insulin therapy for diabetic patients, has increased. Since their invention in the mid-19th century, syringes with needles have been widely used in transdermal drug delivery (Stachowiak et al. 2009). However, they are associated with several issues, including contamination (Kane et al. 1999; Varmus et al. 2003; Hauri et al. 2004; Vishnu et al. 2012; Abe et al. 2018), needle stick injuries (Kermode 2004; Sahebi et al. 2025; Abdelmalik et al. 2023), painful injections, and needle phobia. To address these issues, technologies, such as microneedle delivery (Wermeling et al. 2008; Gill et al. 2007; Cammarano et al. 2024), iontophoresis (Wu et al. 2007; Mudry et al. 2007), and needle-free injectors, have been developed. Needle-free injectors are generally based on forces, such as Lorentz forces or gas pressure, to propel drugs through the skin. However, because of their small nozzle size, they frequently clog (Tagawa et al. 2013). If the applied force is too low, the jet speed becomes insufficient for drug delivery (Schramm and Mitragotri 2002). Conversely, excessive force can excessively increase jet speed, causing significant damage to the skin and underlying tissues (Barolet and Benohanian 2018). Controlling the delivery depth is difficult, among other issues. Consequently, needle-free injectors have not yet fully replaced traditional needle-based injectors.

In this study, we propose a novel perforation technique featuring isotropic or anisotropic depth-direction variations in the target material by controlling the speed of the microjet generated by the collapse of an electrically induced bubble or the heat generated at its tip, respectively. Initially, we investigate the effects of electrical energy, bubble size, microjet speed, and the configuration of the anode and cathode connections. Subsequently, based on the proposed technique, we conduct perforation experiments to verify its feasibility in biological samples.

Concept

Figure 1 shows the proposed needle-free injector system. This system performs two distinct operations within a single device: (i) Target perforation using a microjet; localized flow generated by the implosion of an electrically induced bubble and the resulting shockwave (Lauterborn and Ohl et al. 1997). (ii) Target perforation using the heat generated at the device tip without bubble formation. The system consists of a bubble injector, which is made of a dielectric capillary, a metal wire electrode as the active electrode, a counter electrode, an ionic solution, and a pulse power supply. It has been reported that when applying a high-frequency voltage (not a pulse voltage) to the bubble injector, an electrically induced bubble is generated; the bubble can perforate commercially available chicken meat (Ichikawa et al. 2018). When applying a pulse voltage to the bubble injector, the electric field is concentrated on its tip, creating a high electric field (Yamashita et al. 2022).

Concepts of needle-free injectors. (a) Schematic of a perforation method based on microjets and shockwaves generated by bubbles (created by applying a pulse voltage) and their subsequent collapse. (b) Schematic of a perforating method based on the heat generated by electric discharge. (c), (d) High-speed camera images of a bubble and a microjet, respectively

Generally, the formation of cavitation bubbles generated by femtosecond lasers is attributed to localized increases in pressure caused by thermoelastic effects (Vogel et al. 2005). Consequently, the formation of the electrically induced bubbles (induced by the high electric field in our system) causes a rapid increase in the local temperature at the device tip due to current flow (Ichikawa et al. 2019). The growth and collapse of such a bubble produces a microjet and a shockwave (Ichikawa et al. 2018; Ma et al. 2025), resulting in target perforation. Alternatively, grounding the bubble injector to the target prevents bubble formation, enabling direct target perforation by the heat at the tip. The proposed method can be used in target perforation without needles. The device is compact and easy to handle; therefore, it can be used in various applications, such as the direct insertion of the bubble injector into the body, for drug delivery.

Methods

Experimental setup

Figure 2 shows a schematic and a photograph of the experimental setup, which consists of a bubble injector, a counter electrode, a pulse power supply (PLT1500, BEX CO., LTD., Japan), a 1 kΩ resistor, a 3-axis manipulator, an oscilloscope (DS5614A, IWATSU ELECTRIC CO., LTD., Japan), a high-speed camera (HPV-X2, SHIMADZU CORPORATION, Japan), a microscope (VHX, KEYENCE CORPORATION, Japan), a white interferometer (WI-5000, KEYENCE CORPORATION, Japan), a 3D Micro X-ray computed tomography (CT) system (CosmoScan FX, Rigaku Holdings Corp., Japan), a 2D Charge-coupled device (CCD) spectrometer (Solid Lambda CCD, Spectra Co-op, Japan), and a light source. The bubble injector was implemented by inserting a 100-µm tungsten wire into a glass capillary with inner (ID) and outer diameters (OD) of 110 μm and 300 μm, respectively, and fixing the capillary so that the end faces of the tip are aligned. A 3-axis manipulator was used to accurately control the position of the bubble injector. 0.9% NaCl was used as an aqueous solution (saline). Experiments were conducted to measure the size of the bubble generated by varying the power supply voltage, the speed of the microjet produced by the bubble collapse; additionally, we measured the area and depth of the perforated holes when the bubble injector was in contact with the target swine skin and at a 25-µm distance.

Needle-free perforating system. (a) Experimental setup. (b) Schematic of the experimental setup. (c) Power supply setup. (d) Bubble injector (glass tube: OD: 300 μm, ID: 110 μm)

Voltage and current evaluation

Initially, we applied a single 10-µs pulse width with a repetition count (set number = 1) of a 600–1100 V voltage at 100-V increments to the bubble injector to investigate the voltage, current, electric energy, and spectrum generated by the bubble formation. Actual voltage was approximately 3–5% smaller than set value of applied voltage. Then, we conducted experimental tests under two conditions: (i) connecting the bubble injector and the counter electrode to the anode and the cathode, respectively; (ii) connecting the bubble injector and the counter electrode to the cathode and the anode, respectively.

Bubble evaluation

Next, we observed the behavior of the bubble from its formation to collapse using a high-speed camera and investigated the changes caused by the applied voltage. We evaluated the behavior of the bubble by calculating its volume from its diameter (which was derived by binarizing the difference between the first frame and each subsequent frame in the video and treating the projected area as a circle) and using the resulting time-series data. The speed of the microjet generated after bubble collapse was calculated by averaging the distance traveled by the microjet per frame over 25 frames (1 frame = 1 µs), starting from the point with the smallest bubble volume.

Perforation evaluation

Finally, we performed of skin perforation on swine by attaching and detaching (22 ± 4 μm, n = 11) the bubble injector on their skin. The perforation depth was measured using a white light interferometer and the hole size was measured using a microscope. CT scan images were used to measure the maximum width and depth of the holes. The hole shape was evaluated by calculating the aspect ratio. Experiments were conducted using two biological replicates with five technical replicates under identical conditions for each biological replicate (total n = 10).

Discussion

This study focused on the development of a needle-free injector. By controlling the distance between the target and the device, the injector performs isotropic or anisotropic perforation for accurate drug delivery to the intended location. For this purpose, we investigated a needle-free bubble injection device that exploits both the electrically induced bubble generated by an applied pulse and the heat generated at the device tip to perforate two types of holes in the target.

Controlling the generated bubble is crucial for the isotropic perforation of targets. For this purpose, we investigated the bubble growth mechanism. The time evolution of the resistance between the bubble injector and the opposing electrode when the injector is connected to the cathode and anode has been presented in Fig. 4. Initially, after applying a voltage, the resistance remains constant, regardless of the bubble injector connection to the anode or cathode (Fig. 4(d); the constant-resistance time decreases as the applied voltage increases (Fig. 4(c). Subsequently, the resistance increases; this is attributed to the bubble generation at the tip of the bubble injector (Figs. 5(b) and (d). The peak resistance values when the injector is connected either to the anode or the cathode increase as the applied voltage decreases (Fig. 4(f); this is attributed to the low applied energy, which impedes ion formation within the bubbles. The subsequent decrease in resistance after reaching its peak value is attributed to the generation of sodium radical species at the anode and sodium radical species, hydrogen radicals, and hydroxyl radicals at the cathode during this phase. When the voltage application was stopped before the resistance peaked, the obtained spectrum could not be confirmed, and the generated bubbles did not expand. This indicates that achieving a significant growth of the generated bubbles requires either a localized high temperature sufficient to generate radical species or the generation of radical species itself (i.e., plasma generation). Furthermore, the difference in the bubble size at the anode and cathode, despite applying equal amounts of electrical energy, is attributed to the generation of hydrogen and hydroxyl radicals at the cathode. Radicals were not generated at the anode because the applied electrical energy was consumed for the generation of radicals in the cathode, resulting in less energy available for bubble growth compared to that in the anode (Fig. 5(e). The increased perforation depth caused by a large bubble volume (Figs. 5 and 6) can be attributed to the energy released during bubble implosion and the pressure increase caused by the increase in the speed of the microjet. Since larger bubbles generate higher microjet velocities (Fig. 5(f)), this results in a greater pressure increase upon collapse, which can explain with the experimentally observed increase in perforation depth (Fig. 6).

To investigate the isotropic perforation mechanism, we conducted experiments using agarose gel; these experiments confirmed that bubbles entered the indentations created by the microjet (Fig. 7) and expanded within the holes, causing the isotropic perforation of the target.

When the bubble injector is grounded, no bubble formation occurs. In this case, the electrode is applied to the target, and perforation is caused by heat; this mechanism is similar to that of an electric scalpel (Gjika et al. 2016). During pulse application, an electric discharge was observed. This is attributed to the initial contact between the bubble injector and the target; applying heat caused the target material to evaporate, creating a space for the discharge to occur. Figure 8 illustrates the anisotropic perforation caused by the electric discharge. Initially, considering that the electrode is in contact with the target, the heat generated by the applied pulse voltage causes tissue evaporation, creating a small cavity, which contains no solution. In this stage, reapplying the pulse voltage induces electric discharge. The resulting discharge is applied uniformly in one direction within the tissue, leading to anisotropic perforation and was observed only after some time after applying the pulse voltage; this is considered the cause of perforation. However, the sample condition used in the present study was not optimal for detailed biological tissue evaluation. Therefore, further investigations using more fresh or living tissues are required to assess thermal damage and other biological effects through histological analysis, cell viability assays, and related methods.

The most significant finding of this study is that the shape and area of the hole can be altered by changing the distance between the bubble injector and the target. For example, for a local drug delivery, perforation is performed by placing the bubble injector in contact with the target. Subsequently, the bubble injector is moved at least 100 μm away from the target to deliver the drug using the flow generated by the microjet (perforation is impossible at distances greater than 100 μm), or the drug can be delivered to the target using topical agents. For drug delivery over a wide area, the distance between the bubble injector and the target should be increased (e.g., 25 μm) to enable isotropic perforation while delivering the drug. Furthermore, the accurate control of the perforation depth requires the accurate control of the distance between the bubble injector and the target. In the experiments, this distance was set using information obtained from a high-speed camera. LCR (inductance, capacitance, and resistance) sensors can be used to accurately set this distance and control the perforation depth without human intervention. The proposed system can be regarded as a method for delivering drugs to the region surrounding the perforated hole. However, the delivery distance may vary depending on the physicochemical properties of the administered drug. Therefore, further investigations are required to clarify the relationship between drug type and delivery performance.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a novel needle-free injector that performs both isotropic and anisotropic perforation methods; additionally, it controls the perforation depth. We proposed two perforation methods: the first exploits physical energy such as shockwaves and microjets generated by the collapse of a bubble; the second exploits Joule heating and electric discharge generated by applied electrical energy. The experimental results showed changes in the bubble size and microjet speed by varying the applied voltage, temporal changes in voltage and current by connecting the bubble injector to either the anode or cathode, and changes in the size of the generated bubbles. These results verified the factors that promote significant bubble growth. We demonstrated that by separating the bubble injector from the target, isotropic perforation is achieved. Additionally, we found that increasing the applied voltage increases the size of the bubbles; as a result, the microjet speed increases, enabling deeper target perforation. Conversely, when the bubble injector is grounded to the target, no bubble formation occurs, and the perforation becomes anisotropic. Furthermore, increasing the applied voltage increases the electrical energy, enabling deeper target perforation. Table 1 shows a comparison between the proposed and existing devices. The setup time for drug injection has been reduced, and the penetration depth from the skin surface to the subcutaneous tissue can be varied, thus addressing the issue associated with the commercially available needle-free syringes. Control of the perforation depth within the dermal layer may enable adjustable invasiveness while avoiding unnecessary perforation into deeper tissues. Furthermore, by freely adjusting its aspect ratio, the proposed device is not restricted by the drug characteristics. In future work, we will measure the amount of drug that can be injected into living subjects and investigate the device conditions to increase the accuracy of perforation depth. For future practical use of the proposed system, precise control of the distance between the device and the skin is essential. This can be achieved by integrating positioning systems or distance-sensing technologies, which would enable stable and automated control of perforation depth.
