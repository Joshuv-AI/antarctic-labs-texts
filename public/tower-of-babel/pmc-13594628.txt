When does IAPA begin? Interpreting corticosteroid dose thresholds in a target trial emulation
Authors: Dilek Kuzukıran Kocataş
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with interest the multicenter study by Xu et al. [1], who used a target trial emulation approach to examine the dose-response relationship between pre-ICU corticosteroid exposure and influenza-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (IAPA) in critically ill patients with influenza. The dose points corresponding to an HR of 1.5 at a cumulative dexamethasone-equivalent dose of 43.4 mg and an HR of 2.0 at 51.4 mg, together with the reported inflection point at 63.7 mg, are particularly noteworthy because these values may readily be interpreted as clinically relevant risk thresholds [1]. However, several issues related to temporality and risk estimation should be considered when interpreting these data-derived dose points.

First, ICU admission was defined as time zero, and proven or probable IAPA diagnosed during follow-up was considered an incident outcome [1]. However, it is not clear from the eligibility criteria whether IAPA that was already present but unrecognized at ICU admission was systematically excluded. This distinction may be important. The median time from ICU admission to IAPA diagnosis was only 3 days (IQR 2–5), and 75.4% of the 191 IAPA cases were diagnosed within the first 5 days [1]. Moreover, the diagnostic workup, including bronchoscopy, bronchoalveolar lavage, galactomannan testing, fungal culture, and chest computed tomography, was not standardized across participating centers and was performed at the discretion of the treating physicians [1]. Given that the diagnosis of IAPA relies on a combination of clinical, radiological, and mycological findings [2], it remains uncertain whether some cases diagnosed early after ICU admission were already present but unrecognized at baseline.

This distinction is particularly relevant in the context of target trial emulation. Appropriate alignment of eligibility, treatment assignment, and the start of follow-up at time zero is a central design principle of this approach [3]. The fact that corticosteroid exposure occurred before ICU admission does not, by itself, establish that unrecognized IAPA was absent at time zero. A potential gap between the timing of diagnosis and the underlying onset of IAPA may therefore complicate the temporal ordering of exposure and outcome, particularly among patients diagnosed shortly after admission. Sensitivity analyses sequentially excluding cases diagnosed within the first 48 and 72 hours after ICU admission could help assess the extent to which the observed dose-response relationship may be influenced by disease that was already present but undetected at baseline.

Second, follow-up ended at IAPA diagnosis, death, or ICU discharge, and the association between corticosteroid exposure and IAPA was estimated using an IPTW-weighted Cox model [1]. Death is a competing event because it precludes subsequent development or diagnosis of IAPA. Although the Cox model provides a valid estimate of the cause-specific hazard when deaths are treated as censoring events, the resulting hazard ratio is not equivalent to a proportional change in the cumulative incidence of IAPA [4]. The issue, therefore, is not the use of the Cox model itself, but the description of the HR = 1.5 and HR = 2.0 dose points as a “50% increase in risk” and a “doubling of risk,” respectively [1]. This distinction becomes clinically relevant if these dose points are interpreted as risk thresholds. Complementary cumulative incidence estimates accounting for death as a competing event could help clarify the clinical meaning of these dose points in terms of the observed probability of IAPA [4].

Finally, the severity-specific dose thresholds also warrant cautious interpretation. The SOFA>7 cutoff was not a prespecified clinical threshold but was derived in the same cohort using the Youden index from the ROC curve for IAPA [1]. This data-derived cutoff was then used to estimate subgroup-specific dose points corresponding to HR = 1.5 at 35.2 mg and HR = 2.0 at 39.5 mg among patients with SOFA>7 [1]. Deriving the subgroup definition and the subsequent dose thresholds from the same dataset may increase the dependence of these estimates on the particular sample studied. Independent validation would therefore be important before these severity-specific values are considered clinically meaningful risk thresholds.

These considerations do not negate the reported association between corticosteroid exposure and IAPA, but they may limit the interpretation of the data-derived dose points as clinical risk thresholds. Sensitivity analyses addressing the timing of early IAPA diagnoses, cumulative incidence estimates accounting for the competing risk of death, and independent validation of severity-specific thresholds would help determine whether these dose points represent reproducible clinical thresholds or exploratory markers of a dose-response association.

Sincerely,

Dilek Kuzukıran Kocataş, MD
