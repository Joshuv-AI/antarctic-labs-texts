A Remote Program for Navigator‐ and Pharmacist‐Led Management of Obesity Medications in Adults: Rationale, Design, and Implementation
Authors: Bryn Falahee, John W Ostrominski, Kavishwar B Wagholikar, Ozan Unlu, David Zelle, Sanjay Kumar, Daniel Gabovitch, Michela Tucci, Austen Smith, Hunter Nichols, Irene G McCarthy, Samar Mushtaque, Tabitha V Rutkowski, Ashley C McCarthy, Heather J Baer, Christopher P Cannon, Naomi D L Fisher, Jorge Plutzky, Caroline M Apovian, Benjamin M Scirica, Alexander J Blood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveRemote interventions leveraging team‐based care may ameliorate persistent gaps in obesity medication implementation, but prospective studies are lacking. In this paper, the design, enrollment, and implementation of a remote intervention for obesity medications are described.MethodsThe Supplementary Anti‐Obesity Medication Integration into a Longitudinal Obesity (SAIL) study is a fully remote program with pharmacist‐ and navigator‐led obesity medication implementation. Individuals with body mass index (BMI)‐defined overweight or obesity receiving a digital lifestyle intervention were recruited for participation in SAIL via a survey. Effectiveness outcomes included obesity medication prescription and body weight change at 6 months. Focus groups were conducted with study staff after program completion to identify implementation barriers and solutions.ResultsBetween June 2023 and September 2024, 200 patients responded to the survey, of which 99 were eligible. Of these, 75 enrolled in SAIL (mean age, 50 ± 12 years; 83% women; mean BMI 36 ± 6 kg/m2). Focus groups identified multiple implementation challenges and opportunities, including medication access, insurance coverage, and patient education. Addressing misinformation, frequent patient engagement, and program flexibility emerged as unique strengths of SAIL.ConclusionsThe final results of SAIL will inform the effectiveness of remote, algorithm‐driven, navigator‐ and pharmacist‐led programs for obesity medication prescription and management.Keywords: digital, implementation, navigators, obesity, pharmacotherapy, remote

Introduction

The substantial and growing impact of overweight and obesity, combined with historically suboptimal implementation of established and effective therapeutic interventions, has brought the need for novel healthcare delivery strategies into sharp focus. Continued reliance on traditional healthcare delivery strategies, such as in‐person clinic visits, risks substantial delays and suboptimal deployment of weight management interventions. In the United States, < 1% of eligible individuals receive lifestyle modification for obesity [1], with similar trends for the implementation of obesity medications and metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) [2, 3].

These challenges are multifactorial, relating to patient and provider awareness, weight bias and stigma, training and educational gaps, cost and inconsistent insurance reimbursement, and time constraints [4]. Indeed, primary care physicians have been estimated to require 27 hours per day to provide all contemporary guideline‐recommended care in the United States, with obesity‐related counseling requiring the greatest single time investment [5]. Further, while the number of physicians with dedicated obesity medicine training is increasing [6], the availability of this expertise remains limited. As such, while primary care and dedicated obesity medicine clinics remain critically important for obesity diagnosis and management, additional pathways are needed.

Digital healthcare delivery approaches leveraging more diverse settings and personnel (e.g., patient navigators and pharmacists) may offer a more scalable, cost‐effective, and inclusive solution for the population‐wide treatment of overweight and obesity. Previously, the PROPS (Partnerships for Reducing Overweight and Obesity with Patient‐Centered Strategies) Study demonstrated the effectiveness of an online lifestyle intervention program plus population health management support on body weight [7, 8]. Other studies have demonstrated similar results [9, 10, 11]. However, while lifestyle interventions are foundational components of multifaceted obesity management approaches, weight reduction is typically modest, long‐term sustainability is limited, and health outcomes are not consistently improved when used in isolation, due in part to counterregulatory physiological changes that impede body weight loss and promote weight recurrence [12, 13, 14]. Therefore, strategies incorporating obesity medications, which address key pathophysiological mechanisms of obesity, are needed [15]. The Accelerator for Clinical Transformation (ACT) team at Brigham and Women's Hospital (BWH; Boston, MA, USA) has previously demonstrated the utility of remote, algorithm‐driven, navigator‐ and pharmacist‐led implementation of medications for the management of hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, type 2 diabetes, and heart failure [16, 17, 18]. Whether similar strategies may enhance weight management is uncertain.

The objective of this paper is to present the rationale, design, baseline characteristics, and implementation experience of the Supplementary Anti‐Obesity Medication Integration into a Longitudinal Obesity Study (SAIL), a fully remote navigator‐ and pharmacist‐led intervention for obesity medications. Key challenges encountered during implementation, with implications for future research efforts leveraging similar strategies, are additionally reviewed.

Methods

Program Design

SAIL was a fully remote quality improvement program designed to enhance the implementation and management of obesity medications. Participants were recruited from the PROPS 2.0 (Partnerships for Reducing Overweight and Obesity with Patient‐Centered Strategies 2.0) patient population (NCT05553925). The design of PROPS 2.0 has been previously described [8]. In brief, PROPS 2.0 is an ongoing, large‐scale program evaluating a digital lifestyle intervention plus coaching within the Mass General Brigham (MGB) healthcare system (Boston, MA, USA). The digital lifestyle intervention employed in PROPS 2.0 is RestoreHealth (Healthfleet Inc.), a 12‐month digital health program/app that pairs an online cognitive behavioral therapy‐based curriculum (including lessons related to nutrition, exercise, sleep, and stress management) with personalized one‐on‐one coaching. RestoreHealth leverages data from multiple interconnected devices, including digital scales and activity trackers.

PROPS 2.0 participants were eligible for SAIL if they had a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2 or ≥ 27 kg/m2 with ≥ 1 weight‐related complication (hypertension, dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea, or polycystic ovary syndrome), and experienced either weight gain at 3 months after PROPS 2.0 enrollment or no weight loss at 6 months after PROPS 2.0 enrollment (Table 1). The intended SAIL enrollment was 200 participants but was re‐scaled to 75 owing to funding‐related limitations. Eligible patients were contacted via phone by a navigator who completed a medication reconciliation and verification of eligibility. Established treating providers were then approached for consent to implement obesity medications according to sequential, stepped titration algorithms (Figures 1 and 2, Supporting Information S1: Figures S1 and S2), which were developed by obesity medicine specialists at the BWH Center for Weight Management and Wellness (CWMW) and deployed by the ACT team. Some (30 of 75) SAIL participants (40%) were additionally offered a video telemedicine visit with a physician. The program was approved by the Mass General Brigham institutional review board with a waiver of written informed consent as the program was intended for quality improvement. All participants provided verbal consent to participate.

Medication Initiation and Titration

Medication titration was overseen by pharmacists practicing under a Collaborative Drug Therapy Management (CDTM) agreement with Mass General Brigham (Boston, MA, USA), which allowed pharmacists to manage obesity medications in accordance with guideline‐based algorithms developed by a team of pharmacists, nurses, and obesity medicine expert clinicians. State legislation provides the legal basis for CDTM agreements, and we have previously described this approach for the remote management of hypertension and other cardiometabolic conditions [19]. In brief, the CDTM employed in SAIL afforded qualified pharmacists the authority to select, initiate, titrate, and monitor obesity medications through direct engagement with program participants under the supervision of physicians.

A manual algorithm and workflow were developed to generate personalized initial medication recommendations (Figures 1 and 2). A navigator was assigned to act as the primary contact point for each participant. All navigators held a bachelor's or master's degree but did not have formal clinical training. However, all navigators received training in obesity and in obesity medications, and were qualified to approach patients and obtain basic demographic and clinical information.

Each algorithm‐derived titration step was passed from the pharmacist to the navigator. The navigator then facilitated medication adjustments by telephone and conducted longitudinal surveillance of weight loss, side effects, and any medication access issues in accordance with approved protocols. This information was relayed back to the pharmacist through the electronic health record (EHR) and patient management platform.

As indicated, the pharmacist signed prescription(s) for new medications or titrations under the CDTM agreement. Medication initiation and titration orders were dictated by the algorithm (Figures 1 and 2, and Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). Titrations proceeded until patients reached the maximum target dose or reported intolerable symptoms. Nutrient‐stimulated hormone (NuSH)‐modulating therapies (including glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists [GLP‐1RA]) with US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for long‐term use were employed as first‐line medications owing to their greater efficacy on body weight reduction. However, while out‐of‐pocket approaches were permitted in SAIL, agents covered by each participant's health insurance were prioritized. Phentermine and phentermine‐containing combination therapies (i.e., phentermine‐topiramate, which are controlled substances in the United States, were not used in the program owing to their unique safety and monitoring considerations.

Follow Up

From the time of medication initiation, patients were contacted for follow‐up at least every 2 weeks for the first 12 weeks, and then 2 weeks after attainment of a maximally tolerated dose. However, more frequent engagement was also permitted depending on the needs of each participant. During follow‐up, navigators assessed body weight, behavior change, and medication side effects. If concerns for medication safety or tolerability were apparent, the medication was discontinued, and an alternative was attempted. In the absence of side effects warranting discontinuation or delay of titration, medications were titrated to the maximum tolerated dose.

Program Completion

Patients graduated from the program 2 weeks after reaching maximum tolerated therapy, when no covered medications were available or accessible, or when transfer of care to the BWH CWMW was requested due to medication shortages or for clinical reasons. In addition to continued participation in the PROPS 2.0 program, all participants were offered care through the BWH CWMW.

Qualitative Evaluation of Program Implementation

At the conclusion of the program, 2 hour‐long focus groups were conducted with purposive sampling of key program members. One focus group was conducted with the program pharmacist, and another was conducted with the 3 navigators who managed all patient interactions throughout the program. Both focus groups were performed shortly following program conclusion and led by a physician team member (JWO). Responses were recorded by another physician team member (BF). Narratives were examined using a rapid qualitative analysis approach, and core themes were extracted by two team members (JWO and BF). Given the small number of focus group members and strong thematic overlap, no formal assessment of consensus or saturation was performed.

Program Outcomes

The primary outcome of SAIL is the proportion of program participants prescribed ≥ 1 obesity medication at any time during the program. Secondary outcomes included (1) mean change (absolute and relative) in body weight between SAIL enrollment and 6 months following program completion and (2) the proportion of participants achieving clinically relevant (≥ 5%) body weight reduction at 6 months. Body weight was assessed using data from linked digital scales and/or EHRs at baseline and 6 months.

Statistical Analysis

Given sample sizes of 75 in the intervention arm and 24 in the control arm, and assuming a 0%–5% prevalence of obesity medication implementation in the control arm, we estimate ≥ 80% power to detect a 25.6% difference in obesity medication implementation. Obesity medication prescriptions were assessed through participant reports and EHR data. Consistent with PROPS 2.0 [8], multiple imputation was performed to account for missing body weight data after 6 months. Between‐group differences in body weight change were initially examined using paired t‐tests and McNemar's test. Multivariable‐adjusted linear and logistic regression models were additionally constructed to ascertain the impact of patient characteristics on these outcomes.

The full analysis set (FAS) included all program participants (intention‐to‐treat), and the per‐protocol set (PPS) included participants who initiated an obesity medication and completed the study protocol. Post‐hoc secondary analyses may be conducted after full data review and will be considered hypothesis‐generating. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Between June 2023 and September 2024, 200 PROPS 2.0 participants responded to the SAIL survey. Of these, 99 were eligible. Of these, 54 enrolled in and completed the remote medication intervention (n = 21 dropped out) and 24 elected to continue with the PROPS 2.0 intervention alone, yielding a final cohort of 75 adults (mean [SD] age, 51 [12] years; 83% women; mean [SD] BMI, 35.6 [6.2] kg/m2) (Table 2). Over the course of the program, data were collected on the number of patient and health system (pharmacy and insurance company) contacts, prior authorizations submitted, and tasks completed by the pharmacist and the three patient navigators overseeing the SAIL program outreach (Table 3).

Qualitative Evaluation of Program Implementation

Exit Survey Responses

At the program's conclusion, a survey was distributed to the 14 participants who reached and maintained the maximum tolerated dose of an obesity medication (program completers), of whom 12 responded. Overall, respondents to the voluntary survey were satisfied with the program at completion and appreciated the remote design. All agreed or strongly agreed that the SAIL program was helpful in advancing weight‐related goals, 8 (67%) reported a preference for a remote weight management program if offered either an in‐person or remote program, and 7 (58%) reported a preference to have in‐person visits for weight management in addition to a remote program (Figure 3). The response to the navigators was positive. Multiple patients mentioned and thanked their navigators by name in the survey comments, noting that their support was crucial to their continuation in SAIL.

Challenges, Opportunities, and Considerations for Future Implementation

Key challenges emerged from the focus group discussions, corresponding to five domains: medication availability and access, medication insurance coverage, education and medication usage, unique patient population considerations, and program adaptability (Figure 4).

Medication Availability and Access

The patient navigators and pharmacists emphasized that shortages of all products at various time points throughout the program posed a significant challenge. In addition, evolving shortages often necessitate switching between products and maintain progress on weight‐related goals. This led to issues concerning the estimation of relative equipotency and tolerability, especially between NuSH‐based therapies. Patients often had differing and unpredictable responses to similar dose changes when switching between products (i.e., from semaglutide to tirzepatide, or vice versa). This difficulty was compounded by the need to revisit plan coverage and re‐establish prior authorization (PA) and copay card processes after restarting or switching between therapies, leading to burdensome levels of additional paperwork and patient frustration. Access issues were occasionally magnified by copay cards becoming unavailable or being discontinued by pharmaceutical companies.

These logistical issues were exacerbated by a high prevalence of unrealistic expectations concerning the availability of injectable obesity medications, possibly related to robust marketing for products that were not actively available in the retail pharmacy setting, requiring frequent patient engagement and education. Issues around availability—especially of NuSH‐based therapies—became more challenging as the program progressed. Over time, the SAIL team found that transparency (i.e., shared uncertainty regarding availability or time to availability) was the most important factor in managing patient expectations.

Navigators also experienced significant challenges in determining availability at local pharmacies. Early in SAIL, before pharmacy outreach was restricted due to excessive time‐demands, program navigators would often call approximately 15 and 20 pharmacies per participant, per day, to inquire about medication availability. Phone calls with pharmacies ranged between 5 and 30 minutes. Most pharmacies had established dynamic waitlists, such that even in‐supply medications were often committed to other individuals. In other cases, pharmacies were amenable to holding medications for 1–2 hours, or sometimes until the next day. However, this was not always a feasible approach given the participants' active work schedules and other obligations. Finally, many pharmacies attempted to give estimates of when they expected to have medications in‐stock, but these timelines were frequently inaccurate.

As these issues became more pervasive, the navigators found that patients often conducted their own research before appointments and were well‐informed regarding the medication shortages. Some participants were able to initiate therapy or maintain adherence by commuting more than 30 minutes away to obtain prescribed medications. However, there was significant variability in the amount of time each participant invested to locate medications. The SAIL team empowered patients to call pharmacies independently; however, the navigators estimated that approximately half of the SAIL participants were unhappy, frustrated, or disappointed when asked to contact pharmacies.

Insurance Coverage

Insurance coverage was heterogeneous across payers and often lacking or insufficient. When obesity medications were not on insurance formularies, navigators would assist participants in locating copay cards for approximately $500 per month. However, many copay cards had restrictions for use and were not interoperable with all forms of insurance (typically requiring commercial insurance).

The SAIL program adapted to these challenges through direct outreach to insurance providers on behalf of program participants, ascertaining coverage directly. However, responses from insurance payer representatives were often unclear or insufficient, with SAIL program personnel feeling “passed around” to different representatives. These outreach efforts were also time‐consuming. Program personnel estimated spending about 30 minutes to 2 hours on the phone with insurance companies prior to reaching someone able to provide the answers they needed. Wait times were also substantially variable across insurance providers.

The prior authorization (PA) process posed significant logistical and workload challenges. The SAIL pharmacist spent between 3 and 90 minutes on each PA. Up to 5 PAs could be required per patient for the same medication when doses were escalated, which became a significant paperwork burden. New PAs were also required with health insurance changes. While the PA process across most insurance providers became more streamlined over the program duration, the PAs completed by the SAIL team had as many as 50 questions, some of which were redundant or confusing. Furthermore, letters of medical necessity and appeals were often suggested by insurance providers as a pathway for participants whose PAs were initially rejected. However, the SAIL program eventually discontinued all appeals given the substantial time required and low success rate (0%). Ultimately, while off‐label medications associated with lower out‐of‐pocket expenses were trialed, insufficient accessibility or affordability of first‐line agents were major drivers of participant drop‐out.

Automated features implemented at BWH during the study period to streamline PAs in the EHR were not straightforward. Electronic PA (ePA) approaches, which submit prior authorizations automatically after entering prescriptions, were variable in their success (∼60%–70%). In approximately one‐third of cases, the automatic PA was either not submitted, or the medication would be released to the pharmacy before the PA was completed, leading to confusion for patients and providers. Further, the ePA interface frequently changed over the study period, requiring frequent re‐training. In the event the ePA was initially unsuccessful, subsequent PAs were submitted outside of the EHR system, which required manual tracking and paper PAs.

Some strategies improved the PA process. For example, it was helpful to have the PA completed prior to sending medications to the participant's pharmacy, and to place the PA number in the EHR‐based electronic ordering forms to pharmacies. To streamline PA‐related processes, SAIL had one pharmacist who performed and monitored all PAs.

Patient Education and Medication Usage

A substantial amount of navigator time was spent on education around medication use and administration. Navigators provided educational video links for patients, texts, emails, and messages over the EHR‐based Patient Gateway. For those initiating NuSH‐based therapies, the navigators reviewed optimal medication storage, the injection procedure, need for gradual escalation over time to a maximally tolerated dose, and anticipated side effects with participants, during remote visits approximately 15–20 minutes in duration. However, even with the educational materials provided and the high level of patient engagement, some participants incorrectly stored or administered medications. For instance, some participants left medications unrefrigerated for prolonged periods, rendering them ineffective. In one instance, confusion relating to pen orientation resulted in injection into the air rather than into the skin. As such, frequent and timely re‐education, coupled with surveillance for issues related to medication administration is needed.

Misconceptions around the therapeutic mechanisms of obesity medications—in many cases associated with the use of social media to conduct research—were also prevalent. Several participants believed that some of the medications acted as “fat burners”, rather than suppressing appetite, reducing gastrointestinal motility, or improving neurohormonal regulation of body weight. These misconceptions occasionally impede the understanding of health benefits, anticipated side effects, and need for long‐term use.

Injectable therapies (GLP‐1RA or tirzepatide) were widely preferred by SAIL program participants, but this was not universal. Two participants, for whom oral medications were not an option for clinical reasons, dropped out of the program because of a desire to avoid injection‐based therapies. However, even if initially hesitant to initiate an injectable medication, participants often became amenable after experiencing the ease of use of the prefilled single‐use pens. The most common concern for those hesitant to proceed with an injectable medication was the potential of needing to fill a syringe on their own for injection.

Unique Patient Population Considerations

As noted above, SAIL enrolled a highly motivated population of adults willing to engage in a technology‐based obesity medication program. The navigators noted that SAIL patients were engaged, well‐informed, and often assertive when barriers prevented access to preferred medications. SAIL navigators found that program participants often had strong preferences regarding available medications, namely for once‐weekly NuSH‐based therapies rather than once‐daily NuSH‐based therapies or non‐NuSH‐based therapies. Specific medication names were often requested due to positive experiences shared by friends or family members.

Emotional themes of frustration and blame (e.g., when medications were not accessible or covered by insurance) were commonly encountered by navigators. Apprehension around injectable therapies was also common, with many participants requesting to defer initiation prior to completing their own research outside the program. To address these challenges, navigators found it useful to return to the original source of motivation to enroll in SAIL and to emphasize the wide‐ranging benefits of effective weight management, including improved cardiometabolic health. In addition, navigators often found that patients had excessively optimistic views (e.g., “miracle drugs”) concerning some NuSH‐based therapies, and were disappointed when their weight reduction was not as rapid or as substantial as hoped. As such, additional time and engagement from the navigators was often required to reframe expectations, acknowledge wide variation in individual‐level trajectories of health and body weight, and emphasize that multiple interventions (e.g., sustained adherence to lifestyle interventions) are often needed to attain health goals.

Some SAIL participants had comorbid conditions requiring additional attention from program team members. For instance, initiation of obesity medications occasionally requires concomitant management of glucose‐lowering therapies. Given the high number of concomitant medications for mental health conditions in SAIL, initiation of naltrexone‐bupropion or topiramate necessitated additional monitoring for mood‐related or behavioral side effects.

Discussion

The SAIL program was designed to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of a novel, scalable, and fully remote navigator‐ and pharmacist‐led obesity medication implementation program in adults with overweight or obesity. The SAIL program identified several unique challenges related to remote weight management pharmacotherapy implementation, with implications for health policy and future programs.

Several features of the enrolled SAIL population are noteworthy. First, consistent with recent randomized clinical trials [20, 21], most of which similarly mandated ≥ 1 unsuccessful lifestyle attempts, most SAIL participants were women, and the mean baseline BMI was 36 kg/m2. Second, despite a relatively young age, more than 75% of participants had 3 or more weight‐related complications, signifying substantial obesity‐related morbidity. Third, the use of obesity‐promoting medications was common and may have contributed to suboptimal weight reduction with the remote lifestyle intervention alone. This highlights the need for remote weight management interventions focusing on de‐prescribing in addition to prescribing. Such capabilities may be best derived from digital programs integrated within local care systems and settings (e.g., primary care). Indeed, most SAIL program participants surveyed preferred a hybrid approach to weight management inclusive of both remote and in‐clinic interfacing. Care coordination may be most relevant for individuals with obesity‐related complications or for those requiring close management of concomitant medications (e.g., glucose‐lowering medications). Moreover, robust connection with local healthcare systems may enhance exposure to the wider multimodality spectrum of contemporary weight management strategies (e.g., exercise physiologists, registered dietician nutritionists, MBS, and emerging endoscopic approaches) and permit timely escalation of therapy when indicated.

While digital technologies have been extensively evaluated for the treatment of overweight and obesity, prior trials and implementation programs have focused almost exclusively on lifestyle interventions. While prior studies and meta‐analyses evaluating digital lifestyle interventions have demonstrated important successes, short‐term weight reduction is typically modest and not sustained even with continued intervention. In recent individual studies and meta‐analyses, digital lifestyle interventions for weight management have been associated with body weight reductions of 1–3 kg [7, 9, 22]. As such, clinically significant (≥ 5%) weight reduction may be elusive for most individuals. In a recent analysis of a digital lifestyle program deployed in the National Health Service of the United Kingdom, fewer than 1 in 5 participants achieved ≥ 5% weight reduction after 2 years [23]. Moreover, these modest benefits have been shown to attenuate substantially over time, consistent with (1) prior trials evaluating traditional in‐person interventions [13, 14]; and (2) the complex pathobiology of obesity that hinders weight reduction and promotes recurrent weight gain [14]. In Project SMART (Social and Mobile Tools for Weight Loss in Overweight and Obese Young Adults), body weight reduction was significantly greater among those enrolled in a digital lifestyle intervention compared with control at 6 and 12 months, but not at 18 and 24 months [24]. These findings underscore the need for studies evaluating remote obesity medication implementation, especially among individuals with greater obesity‐related morbidity. SAIL is well‐positioned to address this critical evidence gap.

The remote design leveraging diverse non‐physician personnel, namely navigators and pharmacists, implemented in SAIL has previously been deployed to enhance the medical management of hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, type 2 diabetes, and heart failure [16, 17, 18]. However, obesity medication implementation is associated with unique (and interrelated) challenges and opportunities spanning domains of patient education, prior authorization burden, medication access and insurance coverage, and program adaptability. These challenges were directly targeted in SAIL. Overall, the SAIL experience emphasizes the importance of sustained educational efforts concerning obesity medication mechanisms, administration, expected side effects, and misinformation. Product shortages and unpredictable insurance coverage necessitate payer transparency and flexible communication pathways to maintain trust. The rapid pace of obesity medication innovation additionally underscores the need for adaptable programs capable of continuous iteration and process improvement.

The SAIL program has important limitations. First, while the nesting of SAIL within a digital lifestyle program provides the unique opportunity to promote guideline‐directed care and evaluate a multimodality digital approach, it preferentially enrolls individuals inclined to receive digital interventions. Second, as SAIL was designed as a pilot implementation study, it did not include a prospective control arm. However, effectiveness endpoints were compared with a population of PROPS 2.0 participants who were eligible for SAIL but declined to participate, consistent with prior studies [17]. Third, while the SAIL program was designed for 200 participants, 75 were ultimately enrolled due to funding‐related constraints, with implications for statistical power and the implementation‐oriented insights that might have been informed from broader enrollment. Fourth, as the PROPS 2.0 study ended 6 months after completion of SAIL enrollment, follow‐up in the primary analysis was limited to 6 months. As this timeline is shorter than clinical trials of obesity medications [20, 21], body weight reduction may be underestimated. Fifth, SAIL was conducted in the context of multiple evolving weight management pharmacotherapy shortages, which impacted SAIL process evolution and obesity medication implementation. Sixth, phentermine and phentermine‐containing compounds, while approved for weight management in the United States, were not used in SAIL. Seventh, SAIL was conducted within a single healthcare system by a team experienced in remote optimization of medical therapy. Hence, further efforts may be needed to ascertain generalizability to other systems and settings. Similarly, SAIL primarily enrolled women and persons with self‐reported White race, which may limit generalizability to men and individuals with more diverse racial and ethnic backgrounds.

Conclusion

The final results of this novel healthcare delivery intervention will inform the feasibility and effectiveness of remote, algorithm‐driven, navigator‐ and pharmacist‐led management of obesity medications. Several key challenges and considerations for future implementation efforts emerged over the course of the program, including medication availability and access issues, medication insurance coverage and prior authorization needs, and the high volume of patient education required for success. Program adaptability emerged as a strength of the SAIL pilot given the changing landscape of therapeutic approaches for overweight and obesity.
