Determining elderly-friendly radiation oncology clinics: a comparative analysis of accessibility and transport, physical environment, and privacy aspects (TROD 013 − 005)
Authors: Meltem Kirli Bolukbas, Evrim Metcalfe, Emine Elif Ozkan, Uğur Selek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiotherapy is one of the main components of cancer treatment. However, its fractionated nature requires patients to make repeated visits to radiation oncology clinics. Considering the global increase in the elderly population and the growing number of older cancer patients, creating elderly-friendly radiation oncology clinics has become a healthcare priority. This study assesses the elderly-friendliness of radiation oncology clinics in Turkey, in terms of accessibility/transportation logistics, physical environment, and privacy considerations.MethodsData were collected via a 48-item survey developed to assess the elderly-friendliness of radiation oncology clinics. The World Health Organization’s Age-friendly Primary Health Care Centers Toolkit was used as the framework for survey development. Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi (Version 2.3.28) and JASP (Version 0.18.3).ResultsA total of 155 physicians from 91 radiation oncology clinics participated in the survey. The median total comfort score was 26 (range:12–31). Clinics in private hospitals had significantly higher comfort scores (median = 28, range:18–31) compared with public (median = 25.5, range:12–31) and university hospitals (median = 26.0, range:18–31) (p = 0.02), while no significant difference was observed between public and university hospitals (p = 0.6). Clinics that were considered comfortable according to the statement “Our clinic is comfortable for geriatric patients” had significantly higher scores regarding privacy (p < 0.001), accessibility/transportation (p = 0.001), physical environment (p = 0.001), signage (p = 0.001), and total comfort score (p < 0.001). ROC analysis showed an AUC of 0.844 for the total score, with an optimal cut-off value of ≥ 26 (sensitivity: 72.97%, specificity: 76.47%, CI: 75.3%-91.1%, p < 0.001). According to the cut-off point of 26, 4 of the 26 private hospitals (15.3%), 15 of the 37 university hospitals (40.5%), and 14 of the 28 public hospitals (50.0%) scored below the threshold.ConclusionThis study aimed to highlight the importance of factors such as accessibility, physical environment, and privacy in determining the comfort of geriatric cancer patients and revealed significant differences among the types of radiation oncology clinics in Turkey. The initial findings of this survey indicate the need for certain adjustments to establish elderly-friendly clinics and ensure inter-clinic standardization, and they provide important insights for implementing the necessary improvements in radiation oncology clinics.Keywords: Radiation oncology, Elderly-friendly hospitals, Geriatric oncology, Accessibility, Privacy

Introduction

Demographic change worldwide has accelerated over the past decade and is expected to further accelerate in the coming years, particularly in developing countries [1, 2]. According to the Global Cancer Observatory, the number of cancer cases globally is projected to rise from 20 million to 32.6 million within the next 20 years, and from 240,000 to 419,000 in Turkey. Importantly, this increase will occur predominantly within the geriatric population. Among individuals aged 65 years and older, cancer cases are expected to increase from 10.6 million to 20.5 million worldwide, and from 110,000 to 250,000 in Turkey [3]. This rapid growth of the elderly population makes the specific needs of older adults more visible in health care. As radiotherapy is an essential component of cancer treatment and is administered one or more times throughout the treatment process, elderly patients are required to visit radiation oncology clinics frequently [4]. This makes it critical to reduce the challenges that older adults face while accessing radiotherapy clinics and during the treatment.

The World Health Organization (WHO) published the Age-Friendly Primary Health Care Centre Toolkit in 2008; however, its practical implementation has remained limited [5, 6]. In the literature, elderly-friendly healthcare studies are mostly discussed within conceptual frameworks and focus primarily on primary-healthcare settings [7–11]. However, no comprehensive study has yet evaluated radiation oncology clinics specifically in terms of elderly-friendly features. Given that radiotherapy is administered in fractions, the difficulties encountered by elderly patients tend to recur repeatedly throughout the treatment process. Therefore, the aim of this study was to comprehensively evaluate radiation oncology clinics in Turkey in terms of their elderly-friendliness, focusing on accessibility/transportation logistics, physical environment, and privacy.

Materials and methods

In this study, the evaluation criteria were derived from the recommendations of the World Health Organization’s Age-friendly Primary Health Care Centers Toolkit [5]. These criteria encompassed key factors influencing the delivery of elderly-friendly services, including accessibility/transportation logistics, physical environment, privacy and appointment management. The item pool was taken from the WHO toolkit and jointly reviewed by the author team, which included at least one senior, board-certified radiation oncologist working in each hospital type (state, university and private), and the questions were formulated to ask mainly about concrete, observable yes/no features (e.g. presence or absence of specific facilities or practices). Based on these criteria, a 48-item questionnaire was developed to assess the level of elderly-friendliness in radiation oncology clinics and was distributed online to practicing radiation oncologists.

The questionnaire was implemented as an online form in which all items were mandatory; therefore, only fully completed surveys were obtained and included in the analysis, and no data imputation was required.

In the analysis, center-based and individual responses were evaluated separately. Since variables such as physical environment, transportation, signage, appointment follow-up, and total score directly reflect the structural characteristics of the centers, only the response of the most senior physician from each center was used for these parameters. In contrast, the privacy sub-dimension, which depends on the physician’s one-to-one communication with patients and personal observations of clinical practice, was evaluated using the individual responses of all physicians. Additionally, to explore the relationship between physicians’ perception of clinical comfort and the center-based scores, participants were asked to indicate their level of agreement with the statement “Our clinic is comfortable for geriatric patients.” A 5-point Likert scale was used (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). For analysis, these responses were dichotomized into two categories: uncomfortable (scores 1–3) and comfortable (scores 4–5).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi (version 2.3.28) and JASP (version 0.18.3) software. Data was first summarized using descriptive statistics. The distribution of numeric variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test (for n < 50) or the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Anderson–Darling tests (for n ≥ 50). For comparisons between two groups of non-normally distributed numerical variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was used; for comparisons among three or more groups, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied, followed by the Dwass–Steel–Critchlow–Fligner post-hoc test for pairwise multiple comparisons when significant differences were detected. For categorical variables, the Pearson Chi-square test was used when assumptions were met; otherwise, the Fisher’s Exact test was applied in small samples, and the Fisher-Freeman-Halton test was used for RxC contingency tables. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of the total score in relation to the statement “Our clinic is comfortable for geriatric patients.” A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Across Turkey, 91 centers and 155 radiation oncologists participated in the study, representing a 65% response rate from 140 radiation oncology clinics. The participating centers were categorized into three groups: public hospitals (n = 28, 30.8%), university hospitals (n = 37, 40.6%), and private centers (n = 26, 28.6%). The number of buildings or blocks was significantly higher in university and public hospitals compared with private hospitals (p < 0.001), whereas the number of floors was greater in private hospitals than in both public and university hospitals (p < 0.001). This suggests that public and university institutions typically consist of low-rise, multi-block campuses, while private institutions are more often multi-storey, single-block (monoblock) buildings. Significant differences were also observed regarding the floor locations of outpatient clinics and treatment devices. In private hospitals, 46.2% of outpatient clinics and 57.7% of treatment devices were located on basement level 2, while these rates were markedly lower in public and university hospitals (p < 0.001). In contrast, in public and university hospitals, outpatient clinics and treatment devices were predominantly located on the ground floor or basement level 1. Additionally, the number of radiation oncologists working in private hospitals was significantly lower than in both public and university hospitals (p < 0.001). Clinics classified as comfortable showed significantly higher sub-dimension scores in privacy (p < 0.001), transportation (p < 0.001), physical environment (p = 0.001), combined physical environment and signage scores (p = 0.001), as well as in total score (27.0 [19.0–31.0] vs. 21.0 [12.0–28.0]; p < 0.001). No difference was observed between the groups in appointment follow-up scores (p = 0.195). In clinics considered comfortable, scores were predominantly above the median cut-off of 26 (73%), whereas in those considered uncomfortable, they were mostly below this threshold (76.5%) (p < 0.001) (Table 8). A ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of the total score to discriminate whether clinics were elderly-friendly. The ROC analysis demonstrated that, with a cut-off point of ≥ 26, sensitivity was 73% and specificity was 76% (AUC = 0.844, CI:75.3–91.1%, p < 0.001). Based on this cut-off point, the vast majority of private hospitals (84.6%) scored above this threshold (p = 0.024). In university hospitals, 59.5% of centers, and in public hospitals, 50% of centers scored ≥ 26.

Discussion

The rapid increase in the global elderly population necessitates substantial transformations in healthcare services [1]. In response, the WHO published the Age-friendly Primary Health Care Centres Toolkit in 2008, highlighting the need for hospitals designed to meet the unique requirements of older adults and to deliver care in accessible and supportive environments [5]. Nevertheless, despite its value, the toolkit has seen limited implementation over the past 15 years [6]. Particularly in treatments such as radiotherapy that require repeated visits, considering the unique needs of elderly cancer patients, it is of critical importance to address the design of elderly-friendly clinics with a comprehensive approach [12]. Within this framework, our study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of radiation oncology clinics based on elderly-friendly criteria and contributes country-specific evidence from Turkey to the global literature.

Various conceptual models and assessment tools have demonstrated that elderly-friendly clinics should be multidimensional, addressing not only the physical environment but also accessibility, communication, care processes, and management policies [7–11, 13, 14]. One of the earliest examples was the establishment of geriatric units in Hong Kong in the mid-1970s, inspired by the UK model [7]. In a concept mapping study with 306 participants, eight fundamental components of the elderly-friendly acute care model were identified: guiding principles, leadership, organizational structures, physical environment, patient-family-centered approaches, aging-sensitive practices, geriatrics staff competence, and interdisciplinary resources/processes [8]. Similarly, in a Hong Kong study involving 28 elderly individuals and 12 healthcare professionals, the concept of an elderly-friendly hospital was defined across 13 domains, including transportation, orientation, physical environment, examination process, pricing, community-based services, referral systems, dissemination of health information, medication management, care standards, feedback mechanisms, communication skills, and general respect/attitude [9]. The “Senior-Friendly Hospital” model in Canada proposed five key elements to enhance healthcare for older adults: organizational support, care processes, emotional–behavioral environment, ethical principles, and physical environment. In an evaluation of 155 adult hospitals in Ontario, 83% reported having ethics consultation and advance care directive policies, yet only 28% had age-specific quality improvement practices, and 34% incorporated elder-friendly physical design resources [10]. More recent conceptual frameworks emphasize that age-friendly health systems should be approached as multidimensional structures, extending beyond the physical setting to include governance, human resources, financing, and service delivery [11]. Some studies, however, have focused on generating applicable criteria for implementation. For example, an Indian study identified 44 feasible indicators for elderly-friendly services [13]. Another recent study introduced a 22-item scale, structured across four dimensions: physical environment, communication and service, clinical processes, and management policies, and validated it among 330 patients [14]. The common consensus across these studies is that the design of elderly-friendly clinics should not be limited to physical conditions alone but should also incorporate a comprehensive geriatric approach. However, the effective implementation of such geriatric approaches primarily depends on ensuring fundamental elements such as the physical environment, accessibility, and privacy. At this point, our study addresses this critical gap by providing detailed, data-driven insights into these fundamental elements within radiation oncology clinics.

Making and following appointments is one of the first and most frequent challenges older patients face when accessing healthcare. Elderly individuals often need to manage multiple appointments, not only for their oncologic treatments but also for various comorbidities [15]. In a study from Iran, none of the hospitals had an elderly-adapted appointment scheduling system; there were no dedicated phone lines or reminder services, and 70% of elderly patients reported difficulty during the appointment process [16]. Liu et al. highlighted that adapting examination and treatment processes to the needs of older adults and reducing waiting times are integral to creating an elderly-friendly hospital [14]. In our study, the highest appointment management scores were observed in private hospitals (p < 0.001). Moreover, the task of arranging the next appointment suitable for the patient was achieved in over 90% of private and university hospitals, compared with 64% in public hospitals (p = 0.011). In Turkey’s public hospitals, appointments are scheduling is only available through the Central Hospital Appointment System via internet or mobile phone with personal account; face to face scheduling at clinic secretariats is not possible. This system creates barriers for older adults who may have many difficulties while dealing with voice-guided menus due to visual or auditory impairments or limited digital literacy. Therefore, the adaptability of practices used in private hospitals should be examined for other hospital types [9].

After scheduling an appointment, elderly patients are required to reach the hospital and access different locations within the facility. In our study, 90% of public and private hospitals were easily accessible by public transport, while this rate was 65% in university hospitals. This difference may be attributed to the fact that university hospitals in Turkey are often located on large campuses outside city centers [17, 18]. Similar patterns have been reported in other countries. In Iran, 80% of hospitals were considered accessible via public transport [16], yet a Hong Kong study found that 60% of elderly patients still needed a companion to reach the hospital [9]. Our findings also indicate that the floor levels of radiation oncology outpatient clinics and treatment devices differ by hospital type. In particular, the concentration of clinics and treatment devices in the lower basement floors of private hospitals may create additional physical barriers and transportation challenges for elderly patients. In Turkey, in-hospital transfer support is available in 96% of private hospitals, whereas this rate remains 70% in university hospitals (p < 0.001). In Iran, voluntary assistance is available in only 4% of hospitals [16], while in Canada, age-friendly hospitals have emphasized the need to organize transfer processes in ways that maintain the independence and safety of frail individuals [10]. In summary, proximity to public transportation, spatial layout of clinics, and in-hospital transfer support should be considered critical factors for ensuring regular and safe access to radiotherapy services for elderly patients.

The physical environment is one of the cornerstones of elderly-friendly hospitals [8, 9]. Woo et al. highlighted that it is often difficult for patients to distinguish between staff categories in clinics and emphasized the importance of waiting areas designed for companions, particularly those accompanying wheelchair users [9]. In our study, the most notable difference in physical infrastructure was related to the presence of an emergency alarm system: while 100% of private hospitals had an alarm system, the rate was 85% in public hospitals and 65% in university hospitals (p = 0.001). Similarly, a study from Iran reported that more than half of the hospitals examined lacked alarm systems in toilets [16]. In the physical environment dimension of Liu et al.’s scale, lighting, non-slip floors, emergency alarm systems, and clear signage were identified as essential elements of elderly-friendly care [14]. In our study, private hospitals were generally advantageous regarding physical characteristics, but this advantage disappeared when physical and signage scores were combined. No significant differences were found between groups in the signage criteria, and compliance rates remained between 75% and 95% across all centers. Ahmadi et al. reported that 88% of hospitals had directional signage in key areas [16], while Woo et al. noted that elderly patients often found signage inconsistent, sometimes adequate but at other times confusing, especially in newer facilities [9]. Although our study did not reveal a clear difference between hospital types regarding physical environment and signage, the results may indicate that these aspects could benefit from evaluation under more standardized criteria consistent with international benchmarks.

Privacy is another critical dimension of elderly-friendly clinics. In our study, the median privacy score was 4 in public hospitals and 5 in both university and private hospitals. While the total privacy score did not differ significantly between hospital types (p = 0.514), the provision of privacy during examination (e.g., using a door or curtain) was reported in 85% of public hospitals compared with 100% in university and private hospitals (p = 0.011). This difference may partly reflect the lower patient load in private hospitals (median = 550 new files annually) compared with public and university hospitals (median = 1000). In daily practice, high patient volumes in public and university hospitals can lead to crowded waiting areas, shared examination spaces and shorter consultations, which make it more difficult to provide visual and acoustic separation for older and frail patients. Private hospitals tend to have fewer physicians overall (median = 2), whereas public (median = 5) and university hospitals (median = 7) have more physicians, but these counts include residents, which may underestimate the actual workload per specialist for public hospitals. Our analysis included only newly registered patients, and did not include the entire follow-up patient population, which is often higher in public hospitals due to the Turkish insurance system: many patients receive radiotherapy free of charge in private centers but later continue follow-up in public institutions, increasing patient density and leading to an additional pressure on privacy. Although public and university hospitals had similar numbers of new patients, the privacy score difference may reflect organizational structure and clinical culture rather than just workload. There are some studies published reporting results in line with our study. In Iran, privacy was reported to be limited in public hospitals due to high patient density and shared spaces [16]. In contrast, in the USA and Canada, privacy and respectful communication are recognized as key indicators of elderly-friendly care [8, 10]. In Korea, easy staff identification and clear, legible signage have been proposed as structural elements supporting both privacy and safety [14, 16, 19]. Alibhai et al. demonstrated that implementing a Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment, which takes 1–1.5 h, not only improves privacy and individualized care but also reduces healthcare costs by saving 7,387 Canadian dollars per patient in government expenditures [20]. Our findings underscore that privacy cannot be underestimated to be caused by patient volume alone; it requires multidimensional structural and organizational strategies. Moreover, ensuring privacy is not only an ethical and humanistic obligation but also a practical necessity for building a sustainable healthcare system.

Clinical comfort can be considered a key indicator of elderly-friendly hospitals. Responses to the final questionnaire item, “Our clinic is comfortable for geriatric patients.” showed a strong parallel with the subscale scores. This finding suggests that clinicians’ global judgements of comfort are not random, but may partially integrate the underlying structural conditions captured by the score. The median total score was 21 in centres where physicians answered, “not comfortable”, compared with 27 in centers where physicians answered “comfortable” (p < 0.001). In addition, 73% (n = 54) of the centers scoring above the cut-off value of 26 rated their clinics as comfortable (p < 0.001). However, because this comfort item is a single, physician-reported measure and does not capture the multiple interacting factors and multidisciplinary perspectives (nurses, physicians, radiation therapists and patients) that shape comfort in real clinical practice, the ROC results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating, and the proposed cut-off of 26 as a preliminary, context-dependent threshold that warrants confirmation in larger, prospectively designed cohorts.

Building on these results, questions arise regarding the adaptability, feasibility applicability of elderly-friendly models in clinical practice. For example, in India, only 44 of the 70 criteria derived from the WHO toolkit were considered practically applicable [13]. In a Delphi study from Korea, only 30 of 67 criteria were judged to be implementable [19]. Similarly, in our study, 36.3% (n = 33) of centers scored below the cut-off point, and scores ≥ 26 were significantly more common in private hospitals compared with public and university hospitals (p = 0.024). These findings emphasize that, beyond developing elderly-friendly clinic models, careful consideration must be given to their feasibility and implementation in daily routine.

This study has several strengths. It is, to our knowledge, the first multicentre analysis to systematically compare state, university and private radiation oncology clinics in terms of their elderly-friendliness, using a structured, WHO-based framework for the care of older adults. The inclusion of different hospital types, the use of clearly defined structural criteria and the linkage of these criteria to a composite score provide a practical starting point for benchmarking and quality improvement in real-world radiotherapy settings. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. The data were collected only from one country, the number of participating centres was modest and the cross-sectional, self-reported design may be subject to recall or reporting bias. Our tool was intentionally designed to focus on objective structural and organisational elements, and the privacy component was planned as a physician-based assessment; therefore, it does not encompass all dimensions of elderly-friendly care, including the level of radiotherapy technology, the availability of specific equipment, detailed communication practices, humanistic and psychosocial support or the lived experience of patients. Future studies should include more participants, follow patients over time, and explicitly incorporate the views of nurses, radiation therapists and patients to build a more comprehensive and multidisciplinary model of elderly-friendly radiation oncology clinics. Such a framework could also enable meaningful comparisons between countries and support international benchmarking of elderly-friendly radiation oncology services.

Conclusion

This study represents the first comprehensive evaluation of the current status of radiation oncology clinics in Turkey in terms of elderly-friendliness. The findings reveal considerable opportunities for improvement in appointment management, accessibility, physical environment, and privacy particularly in public and university hospitals. For treatments that require complex and repeated visits, such as radiotherapy, implementing elderly-friendly clinic designs is crucial to ensure safe, accessible, and high-quality care for older patients.
