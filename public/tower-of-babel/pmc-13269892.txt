Unlocking the ionic transport dynamics modulated by concentration artifacts of Li(Na)-ion batteries via operando optical fiber spectroscopy
Authors: Fu Liu, Cédric Leau, Romain Dugas, Mohammad Farkhondeh, Charles Delacourt, Jean-Marie Tarascon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The performance and lifetime of next-generation rechargeable batteries are limited not only by their materials, but also by our incomplete understanding of how ions move through their complex, porous architectures during operation. Spatial heterogeneities in ion transport—long assumed to be negligible in models—can silently degrade capacity, efficiency, and safety. Here, we present an operando optical platform based on tilted fiber Bragg grating sensors and infrared fiber spectroscopy to resolve lithium-ion and sodium-ion transport dynamics at high perpendicular spatial and temporal resolution. This dual-modality approach complementarily tracks electrolyte refractive index and solvent coordination under comparable electrochemical cycling conditions, revealing previously hidden concentration anomalies at charge and discharge endpoints and during voltage-plateau transitions. These anomalies correlate with mass-transport overpotentials and deviate from predictions of the Doyle–Fuller–Newman model, indicating spatially non-uniform current distributions that challenge the long-standing assumption of reaction homogeneity in battery modeling. The compact, non-invasive, and readily integrable fiber-based strategy offers a pathway toward more durable, efficient, and sustainable energy-storage technologies.

Introduction

To address the growing concerns over climate change and the reliance on fossil carbon-based energy, renewable energy sources have been widely adopted and discussed. Lithium (Li) ion batteries, known for their improved reliability and cost-effectiveness, have become a leading energy-storage technology for applications ranging from portable devices to electric vehicles and grid-load balancing1,2. However, the performance of these batteries is heavily dependent on ionic transport and thermodynamic properties. The dynamics of ion transport gives rise to concentration gradients in the electrolyte, which are caused by the insertion and extraction of Li-ion in the active materials. Solid experimental data with enough spatial and temporal resolution would enable more accurate determination of state-of-the-art transport properties. A number of operando techniques have been employed to shed light on transport phenomena during battery operation. These techniques have greatly advanced our understanding of the dynamics of Li-ion insertion and extraction, a process that has been theoretically explored by Newman3,4. Various methods, including X-ray diffraction (XRD)5–9, transmission electron microscopy (TEM)10, Raman spectroscopy11–13, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy14–16, neutron imaging17–20 and potential measurement between reference electrodes21–25, have been used to quantify Li-ion concentration across the electrodes and separators. Despite these advances, a universal understanding of ionic-transport dynamics remains elusive, with literature reports often presenting contradictory results 5,7,26.

To clarify the underlying science of ion transport during operation, the optical fiber Bragg Gratings (FBGs) sensors have garnered attention for battery monitoring due to their compact size, remote-sensing capabilities, and ease of integration into battery systems without interfering with internal chemistry. These sensors have enabled various applications including monitoring temperature, pressure, and stress, and linking these metrics to battery electrochemical behavior, such as solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation, material phase transitions, and gas generation27–29. However, tracking Li-ion concentration with FBGs remains a challenge, as the sensor is unable to sense the surrounding medium due to the confinement of the sensing signal to the fiber core. To address this limitation, tilted fiber Bragg gratings (TFBGs), which have the same structure as FBGs but with a tilted grating plane, have been widely used for measuring the refractive index of an external medium through the evanescent field of cladding mode resonances30–33. This has led to significant advancements in battery sensing including the detection of electrolyte chemical dynamics34–37. However, the refractive index measured by a TFBG sensor reflects an averaged effect of all species in the electrolyte, hence the ionic concentration is difficult to be decoupled. Encouragingly, operando infrared (IR) fiber spectroscopy, proposed in 202238, has allowed for the identification of chemical species and the observation of electrolyte and additive decomposition during cycling. Together, TFBG sensors and IR fiber spectroscopy offer new opportunities for addressing the challenges of battery sensing. They enable operando optical monitoring of ionic concentrations via both refractive index changes and chemical molecular signatures, respectively.

In this study, we report on the use of TFBG sensors and IR fiber spectroscopy to operando track the dynamics of Li(Na)-ion transport. By measuring refractive index and chemical bond absorption of solvent molecules, we gain insights into ionic concentration changes influenced by physical phenomena, such as migration, diffusion and convection. We show that measured ion concentrations deviate from those simulated with Doyle–Fuller–Newman (DFN) model considering uniform in-plane electrochemical reaction distributions, especially at the end of charge and discharge, as well as during the transition between two potential plateaus. These concentration artifacts are highly correlated to mass-transport overpotential, and the reaction nonuniformities of active material leading to current density nonuniformities may be identified as a possible contributor to such deviation. Ultimately, a parallel-circuit model composed of two branches was introduced to explain these nonuniformities. TFBGs and IR fiber spectroscopy offer a powerful toolkit for operando monitoring key chemical and physical with high temporal and spatial resolution. This approach enables precise, continuous monitoring of battery performance and contributes to the development of more reliable and efficient energy-storage technologies.

Characteristics of TFBG and infrared fiber spectroscopy sensing

Before diving into operando battery sensing, it is useful to first briefly introduce the suitability of TFBG sensors and IR fiber spectroscopy for measuring ion concentration. TFBGs, immersed in an electrolyte (Fig. 1a), are fabricated within the core of commercial single-mode fiber in which coupling occurs between the fundamental core mode and the backward-propagation cladding modes30,31. Operando measurement of ionic transport dynamics within electrolyte

Building on the promising proof-of-concept for measuring Li(Na)-ion concentration in the electrolyte, we investigated the potential of operando chemical dynamics testing by inserting a 2 or 4 mm thick, 12.8 mm internal-diameter polyether ether ketone (PEEK) ring—through which two 1 cm long TFBG sensors can pass—into the middle of a Swagelok cell. The PEEK ring separates the positive and negative electrode (lithium or sodium metal film) to ensure that the fiber sensor does not contact with either electrode (Supplementary Fig. S2a,b), thereby avoiding strain induced by the active- material volume expansion37. Additionally, any thermal effect from the electrolyte background environment was calibrated and compensated, as described in Supplementary Fig. S3. The influence of bias voltage between two stainless steel electrodes at both side of the PEEK ring filled with an electrolyte but without active materials was studied, and the result confirms that the Li-ion concentration was not influenced by bias voltage without an ionic current passing through. (Supplementary Fig. S4). Thus, the combination of TFBG and IR fiber spectroscopy enables the measurement of Li(Na)-ion concentration and mass-transport dynamics during cell cycling.

Based on the aforementioned concept, we first measured the Li-ion concentration deploying double TFBG sensors to track the mass-transport dynamics of Li | |Li symmetric cells. As shown in Fig. 2a, b, the TFBG sensors are positioned at a distance of 0.08 mm from the lithium film surface (the two electrodes are separated by a 2 mm PEEK ring), which is considered sufficiently distant to minimize, though not fully eliminate, interfacial SEI influence. Additionally, given the relatively large electrolyte volume (125 µL) compared with the active material loading (26.5 mg/cm²), the rate of SEI growth is way smaller than the cycling current. Thus the Li(Na)-ion concentration change induced by SEI formation is negligible compared to that arising from ion diffusion and migration. Encouraged by the results mentioned above, we next attempted to establish a quantitative relation between transport dynamic processes and cation concentration in electrode through a Galvanostatic Intermittent Titration Technique (GITT) test. The overall profile of Li(Na)-ion concentration variation (Fig. 3a, b, red curve) closely matches the results obtained in Fig. 2c, d, showing that the Li(Na)-ion concentration change is enhanced at the transition between the potential plateaus or at end of charge and discharge (though this effect is less pronounced with highly concentrated salt in the case of LFP, Supplementary Fig. S8). We focus on the temporal response of the cation concentration to the current pulse (voltage polarization with applied current) and the subsequent rest period (driven by the relaxation of concentration gradients in both the active material and the electrolyte) shown in the insets of Fig. 3c. The fast overpotential decay (iR drop) that gathers contributions such as ohmic and charge-transfer at both electrodes hasn’t been discussed since it is quite stable upon ion transport, and we are heading to the long-time potential decay (mass-transport overpotential) which is the contribution to the cell voltage of the difference in concentration in both electrolyte and active material at the interface between them, as compared to their values at equilibrium. The concentration artifacts match well with the mass-transport overpotential decoupled by the rest period of the GITT test (Fig. 3c, d). Building on the evidence so far, which links Li-ion concentration in the electrolyte to mass-transport overpotential related to active material at the transition between potential plateaus or at the end of charge and discharge, Fig. 4a, c then showcased a meticulous and insightful exploration of Li-ion transport in both electrolyte and positive electrode powder with active materials, such as lithium manganese iron phosphate (LMFP) and lithium manganese oxide (LMO). The variation of Li-ion concentration within the electrolyte near the positive electrode side clearly correlates with the concentration within active material powder, which are both well matched to the mass-transport overpotential shown in Fig. 4b, d (e.g., also decided by the cycling rate shown in Supplementary Fig. S12). In these cases, the Li-ion concentration is enhanced or depressed at the transition between potential plateaus and at the end of charge or discharge. Specifically, for lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC111, Fig. 4f), there is no noticeable peak in the mass-transport overpotential related to the potential plateaus transition, and as expected, there is no fluctuation in Li-ion concentration in this region (Fig. 4e). All above indicates that Li-ion concentration artifacts in the electrolyte due to mass-transport overpotential are likely related to the active material itself (i.e., overpotential due to solid diffusion). Regarding how overpotential contributes to the concentration artifacts, the mass-transport overpotential observed at the end of charge (or discharge) and plateau transitions reflects concentration gradients for Li-ion between the core and the surface of the active material particles. Suppose part of the active material reacted earlier because of a lower resistance for the reaction. When reaction is completed for this part, solid-phase overpotential will increase, which provides the driving force to drive the reaction of the most resistive part. These inhomogeneities can induce abrupt concentration changes in the electrolyte, which are not captured by standard 1D + 1D DFN simulations24,25. To further investigate how the mass-transport overpotential affects the Li(Na)-ion concentration in the separating electrolyte layer, we explore the Li(Na)-ion concentration by increasing the distance between the fiber sensor and the electrode surface. For the Li | |LFP and Na | |NVPF half-cells (Fig. 5a, b), a 4 mm thick PEEK ring was used to separate the positive and negative electrode. As the distance between the positive electrode and the fiber sensor increased from 0.08 mm to 1.01 mm, the phenomenon of mass-transport overpotential related to Li(Na)-ion concentration artifacts in the separating electrolyte layer gradually disappeared (this was triple-checked with an Li | |LMFP half-cell in Supplementary Fig. S13a). In case the TFBG is far enough from the insertion electrode, the Li(Na)-ion concentration stabilized after reaching pseudo-steady state without any concentration artifacts at the transition between potential plateaus and at end of charge or discharge, as confirmed with different positive electrode materials including LFP, LMFP, LMO, and NMC111 (Supplementary Fig. S14). Meanwhile, it is clear that during discharge the variations in concentration on both sides of the electrode surface are significantly suppressed by 83% due to the bigger gap between two electrodes. This asymmetric transport dynamics is attributed to convection, which is related to the higher electrolyte density on top that contributes as a driving force to smear out concentration profiles because of buoyancy. To better illustrate the general case of mass-transport overpotential dependent Li(Na)-ion transport dynamics, we consider the full cells (LTO | | LMO and NVP | | NVPF, Fig. 5c, d), where both the positive and negative electrodes can influence ionic concentration. Using the 4 mm thick PEEK ring just like in Fig. 5a, b, and ensuring that the two fiber sensors are positioned close to the two electrodes (0.08 mm), we observe that the dynamics of Li(Na)-ion transport (concentration) operate independently in terms of concentration artifacts. This result suggests that the 4 mm gap is large enough so that the mass-transport overpotentials from the positive and negative electrode do not interact (Supplementary Fig. S13c, d). However, when the electrode distance is reduced (~0.65 mm) by using a 2 mm thick PEEK ring for the NVP | | NVPF full cell (Supplementary Fig. S13b), both the positive and negative electrode exhibit the similar concentration distortions (nearly symmetric Na-ion concentration change). It indicates that the mass-transport overpotential from the NVPF electrode also affects the concentration signal on the fiber positioned closer to the NVP electrode. Overall, the mass-transport overpotential of the active material only affects the Li(Na)-ion concentration in the electrolyte over limited distances due to the cation concentration at the particle surface.

Building on the previous exploration of mass transport, we decided to further investigate the operando monitoring of ionic transport dynamics considering the influence of the solvent and temperature, which both affect the electrolyte viscosity (Fig. 6). Since increasing temperature has a beneficial effect on mass transport in electrolytes, the operando test at temperatures of 5 °C, 25 °C, and 55 °C have been done, corresponding to a gradual decrease in the viscosity of the solvent (PC)45,46. At higher temperatures, the reduced viscosity leads to less change in Li-ion concentration due to a higher diffusion coefficient at 55 °C, which also results in stronger artifacts at the end of charge and discharge (with more pronounced convection, Supplementary Fig. S15) (Fig. 6a). Conversely, at 5 °C, mass transport slows down and creates a stronger concentration gradient. As a result, the maximum concentration at the end of charge or discharge is smaller than at 25 °C (Fig. 6c). Such trend of temperature-dependent ionic transport is also observed in the Na-ion transport dynamics, supported by Supplementary Fig. S16. On the other hand, when the temperature is held constant at 25 °C, we varied the solvent (Fig. 6b, d) with the basic viscosity trend: ν(DMC) < ν(EC/DMC) < ν(PC)47. This explains why the concentration variation of lithium ions is weakest in the DMC solvent compared to the other two. Meanwhile, when comparing EC/DMC to PC, the use of viscous PC solvent suppresses the convection effect. All the TFBG measurements presented in this work were conducted in binary electrolytes (one salt and one solvent). Though the TFBG response in the ternary electrolyte is consistent with that observed for the single-electrolyte cell, the similar concentration-related artifacts appear at the end of charge and discharge as well as during the transition between the two potential plateaus (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. S17). For electrolytes containing three or more components, the refractive index (TFBG sensor) or Li-ion concentration-based absorption band (IR fiber) vary with the overall composition and can no longer be unambiguously correlated with the Li-ion concentration. This is because the wavelength of TFBG sensor and absorption band can also be modulated by gradients in other solvent components beside Li salt (the refractive index and Li-ion based absorption band of the electrolyte changes with the varying ratio of solvents involved in solvation shown in Supplementary Fig. S18). Discussion

Herein, TFBG sensors and IR fiber spectroscopy, which are cost-effective and easily integrated into batteries during operation, were demonstrated for operando testing of the dynamics of Li(Na)-ion transport inside the electrolyte and positive electrode of Li(Na)-ion batteries. These methods reveal the correlation between Li(Na)-ion concentration and convection (due to density gradient) and electrolyte transport properties (depending on the solvent type and temperature). Additionally, as measured by GITT, it was shown that the local Li(Na)-ion concentration measured in the separating electrolyte and positive electrode in the vicinity of the fiber is influenced by the mass-transport overpotential depending on Li(Na) solid diffusion in the active material and on transport in the electrolyte phase. This is observed, in particular, at the transition between potential (pseudo) plateaus for active materials, such as LMFP, LMO, and NVPF as well as at the end of charge and discharge for all insertion materials investigated.

Despite the promising insights provided by TFBG sensors and IR fiber spectroscopy, a limitation lies in the incomplete explanation of mass transport in the electrolyte-apparent correlation between mass-transport overpotential and concentration artifact effect. These experimental results cannot be reproduced by 1D + 1D DFN simulations that assume uniform surface current density. As seen in Fig. 7, There are several reasons for this. (1) charges/discharges are not experimentally measured following an appropriate manner. There was a relaxation between each charging and discharging event, but unfortunately there was no constant-voltage following each constant-current event, which means that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x$$\end{document}x at EOD and EOC strongly depends on the C-rate (Fig. S12). Hence, it complicates the analysis of the charges/discharges at different C-rates. (2) we assumed a Fickian diffusion coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{s}$$\end{document}Ds deriving from a constant thermodynamic diffusion coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathscr{D}}}}}_{{{{\mathcal{s}}}}}$$\end{document}Ds. It would have been more accurate to derive the Fickian diffusion coefficient from GITT, but in order to do this, GITT measurements are to be performed on a cell setup for which porous-electrode (PE) effects are minimized, because models that we use for GITT analysis ignore PE effects. We analyzed GITT from Li | |LM0.5F0.5P, but the derived Fickian diffusion coefficient is not well confirmed. As for simulation, only four parameters were manually adjusted, namely the rate constant of charge-transfer kinetics at the positive electrode, the thermodynamic diffusion coefficient, and a contact resistance (Table S1). Three hypotheses for such discrepancy were proposed: (1) non-uniformity of the reaction across the electrode depth. As the charge and discharge proceeds, a reaction front moves deeper into the electrode, such as LFP and LTO, increasing the effective diffusion length of the electrolyte, as investigated by modeling by Chen et al21. In our test conditions, however, the experimental parameters are included by default in P2D simulations and given the small electrode thickness of 500 µm compared to the separator thickness of 2000 µm, the simulations do not reproduce the concentration artifacts (Supplementary Fig. S19). Interestingly, based on Chen’s simulation parameters using the P2D (1D + 1D) DFN model, such concentration artifact appears due to the wavefront of Li-ion concentration across the electrode depth (Supplementary Fig. S20). Unlike compounds, such as LFP and LTO, which undergo significant first-order phase transition during Li composition changes with the reaction flux confined to a narrow reaction zone leading to reaction non-uniformity of the reaction48,49, the compounds that exhibit solid-solution behavior upon (dis)charging, such as NMC111 show a uniform reaction rate (Fig. 4e). However, the concentration artifact still exists. Therefore, the non-uniformity of the reaction across electrode depth could not be the only reason for concentration artifacts. (2) nonuniformity of the reaction over the electrode geometric surface due to masking effect by the TFBG. As the fiber partially masks the electrode, reaction would first occur in the unmasked areas and eventually it occurs at surface locations that are shaded with the fiber. In other words, current density is lower than expected in the shaded areas at first, and it is higher than expected toward the end of charge, so that concentration gradients may follow the trend we see experimentally. Such an assumption was investigated using 2D + 1D DFN simulations based on LMFP half-cell that accounts for such a possible masking effect of the TFBG sensor in the cell. In these simulations, the grid on the surface of positive electrode was not considered because the concentration artifacts still happen on negative electrode side without grid. Results of the simulations suggest the TFBG sensor doesn’t induce such a masking effect and simulations based on experimental conditions of the setup do not reproduce the ion-concentration artifacts (Supplementary Fig. S21). To assess the influence of the masking effect experimentally, we etched a 125 μm-diameter optical fiber sensor down to 35 μm to minimize the masking effect in an NVPF half-cell (Supplementary Fig. S22). The Na-ion concentration showed similar peaks with comparable amplitude change to the 125 μm-diameter sensors, suggesting that the masking effect does not account for the concentration artifact. On the other hand, the Li-ion concentration measured by X-ray fluorescence7 and potentials measurement between reference electrodes26 for LFP half-cells without any masking effect still show concentration artifacts at the end of charge or discharge. Therefore, the masking effect shouldn’t be the original reason. (3) electrode mass-transport overpotential effect on the liquid-phase concentration inside electrolyte. To further understand this effect, we tested LMFP active material with different particle sizes (5-10 µm and ~1 µm). The larger particle size (higher overpotential) led to more than two times stronger concentration fluctuations at potential plateaus transition during discharge (Supplementary Figs. S23 and S24), supporting the link between electrolyte concentration and mass-transport overpotential. However, the intrinsic mechanism that connects mass-transport overpotential and ionic concentration in the electrolyte remains unclear. Based on the 1D + 1D DFN simulation validation of Li | |LM0.5F0.5P system considering the cycling rate (Fig. 7), concentrations measured at different distances from positive electrode (Supplementary Fig. S25) and overpotential breakdown, the concentration artifacts observed in experiments are not reproduced whatsoever as well as other positive electrode materials including LMO, NMC111 (Supplementary Fig. S19). A qualitative explanation can be proposed for the observed behavior. At the end of charge or discharge and plateau transition, the concentration gradient of Li⁺ (or Na⁺) ions within both the electrolyte and active material particles becomes strongly in plane non-uniform. This is primarily due to diffusion lag—ions have not had sufficient time to equilibrate—which leads to a pronounced mass-transport overpotential. Leveraging the properties of TFBG sensors and IR fiber spectroscopy, such as multiple resonance peaks, high refractive index sensitivity, ultrafast response, and molecular recognition, they open new avenues for battery sensing. By utilizing ultrafast TFBG pump-probe technology50, the dynamics of electron and ion transport could be separately examined. In combination with IR fiber, it becomes possible to isolate and measure the impact of factors like electrical double layers, SEI formation51, or dendrite growth based on Li-ion concentration52. Together, TFBG sensors and IR fiber spectroscopy offer non-invasive diagnostic capabilities, enabling operando monitoring of chemical, physical, and thermal parameters with satisfactory time and spatial resolution. This approach facilitates the extraction of critical information including state of charge, health assessments, operational guidance, and early failure detection.

Methods

Synthesis of LMFP

LiMn0.5Fe0.5PO4/C-based composite was prepared by a high-temperature solid-state reaction. A mixture of 2 mmol MnC2O4.2H2O (Sigma, 99% purity), 2 mmol FeC2O4.2H2O (Sigma, 99% purity), 2 mmol Li2CO3 (Sigma, 99.9% trace metals basis), 4 mmol NH4H2PO4 (Sigma, 99.999% trace metals basis) was ball-milled with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) in ethanol at 350 r/min for 1 hour. After the evaporation of ethanol, the ball-milled mixture was calcined at 450 °C for 4 hours, followed by calcination at 650 °C for 6 hours under Ar atmosphere. The LiMn0.7Fe0.3PO4 was purchased from HCM CO., LTD.

Preparation of composite electrodes

LFP (Sigma, >97% (XRF)), LTO (Ossila, >99% purity), LMO (Alfa Aesar, 99.9% purity), LMFP, NMC111 (Sigma, 98% purity), NVP (Tiamat, 98% purity), NVPF (Tiamat, 98% purity) and Super P conductive carbon (Sigma, <1 ppm Ni and <5 ppm Fe impurities) were mixed by a mortar with pestle grinding with the ratio 80/10 wt%. A 10 wt% dispersion of poly(tetrafluoroethylene) binder (PTFE, Alfa Aesar) was then added to the positive electrode composite, which was rolled into a smooth film by multiple folds with a glass rod at 25 °C, punched into disks (1.2 cm diameter, 500 µm) with a loading of approximately 26 mg/cm², and dried and stored under vacuum 24 hours at 80 °C.

TFBG sensor fabrication

A 10 mm long TFBG sensor with a 7° internal tilt angle (556.015 nm period) was inscribed in CORNING SMF-28 fiber using the phase-mask technique. The fiber, featuring an 8.2 µm core and 125 µm cladding, underwent hydrogen loading at room temperature and 15.2 MPa pressure for 14 days to enhance photosensitivity. The grating was written with a KrF pulsed excimer laser (PM-848 model, Light Machinery, Inc.), operating at 248 nm with a 100 Hz pulse rate. The laser beam was focused cylindrically along the fiber axis, and a rectangular section was chosen to irradiate the grating region. The phase mask and fiber were rotated simultaneously to inscribe the grating with an expected tilt angle in the core.

Computational details

The transmission spectra simulations were carried out using an analytical method based on a three-layer cylindrical waveguide model33. First, the simulated spectra were calibrated against the experimental spectra in air to verify that the model accurately captured the dispersion characteristics of both the fiber core and cladding, ensuring a good match between the simulated and experimental results. Next, the refractive index of the electrolyte was determined by manually adjusting the refractive index of the surrounding layer (the third layer) in the simulation until it matched the experimental spectra.

Integration of TFBG sensors or IR fiber into modified Swagelok

A PEEK ring (12.8 mm diameter, 2 mm (4 mm) thickness) is used to house a 10 mm-long fiber sensor, with the ring holding 125 µL (250 µL) of electrolyte for immersion and electrochemical tests. This ring is fixed at the center of a 19 mm diameter Swagelok cell, where two fiber sensors or IR fiber can pass through four drilled holes spaced by ~1.8 mm or 3.8 mm. A Li metal foil (provided by Sigma with 99.9% trace metals basis, 0.38 mm thickness, 14 mm diameter, and is prepared directly prior to cell assembly with <0.1 ppm water and <0.1 ppm oxygen in glovebox at 25 °C) is attached to one side of the PEEK ring as the negative electrode, while a steel grid (purchase from Alfa Aesar, 325 mesh woven from 0.036 mm dia wire) on the opposite side holds a Celgard (purchase from Polypore, thickness: 25 μm) separator beneath the positive electrode disk. The solvent PC (99.7% purity, Sigma) was chosen and dried by molecular sieves (2−3 mm, Macklin), and storage in an aluminum bottle at 25 °C in glovebox. The Whatman separator (Cytiva) with 2.7 μm pore size, 0.67 mm thick and 100 ea was chosen. The cells were assembled within an argon-filled glovebox. In our configuration, perpendicular spatial resolution is about 80 µm. it does not exceed that of Raman fiber probes in pouch cells (typically around 23 µm, as reported by Yamanaka et al.43, and 75 µm by Fawdon et al.12. The limitation mainly arises from the Swagelok configuration used in our setup, where the introduction of a grid between the PEEK ring and the negative electrode restricts the achievable resolution. However, the resolution can be improved to a comparable level to that reported by Yamanaka et al.43 by embedding multiple TFBG probes into a Li-pouch cell, where the probe spacing can be more easily controlled.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical performance of the Swagelok cell was evaluated by a BCS-810 or MPG2 potentiostat (Biologic, France) at 25 °C inside a temperature-controlled oven (Memmert, ±0.1 °C).

Thermal calibration of TFBG sensors

The thermal calibration of TFBG sensor was performed in Swagelok cell filled with air or electrolyte without any active material from 15 °C to 40 °C by 5 °C increments and 4 hours step inside temperature-controlled oven (Memmert, ±0.1 °C).

Operando measurement by TFBG sensors and IR fiber

For the operando measurements using the TFBG sensor, optical transmission spectra were captured every minute (with each spectrum taken in 1-minute intervals) using an optical integrator (CTP10, EXFO SOLUTIONS), offering a resolution of 1 pm over the wavelength range of 1500 nm to 1600 nm. The IR fiber spectroscopy operando measurements were performed using a Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (Bruker), equipped with an accessory connection system on the right side for integrating the TAS fiber. This configuration enabled infrared input from 12000 cm⁻¹ to 600 cm⁻¹, with the full spectrum being recorded every 3 minutes.

Operando measurement by XRD

Operando XRD measurements were performed using a D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker) with a Cu Kα X-ray source (λKα1 = 1.54056 Å, λKα2 = 1.54439 Å) and a LynxEye XE detector. The XRD patterns and electrochemical data were simultaneously recorded using a custom-designed airtight cell with a beryllium window, generating a full XRD pattern every 20 minutes.
