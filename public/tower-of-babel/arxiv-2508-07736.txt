Non-Standard Models of Homotopy Type Theory
Nima Rasekh

Abstract
. Homotopy type theory is a modern foundation for mathematics that introduces the univalence axiom and is particularly suitable for the study of homotopical mathematics and its formalization via proof assistants. In order to better comprehend the mathematical implications of homotopy type theory, a variety of models have been constructed and studied. Here a model is understood as a model category with suitable properties implementing the various type theoretical constructors and axioms. A first example is the simplicial model due to Kapulkin–Lumsdaine–Voevodsky [ KL21 ] . By now, many other models have been constructed, due to work of Arndt, Kapulkin, Lumsdaine, Warren and particularly Shulman, culminating in a proof that every Grothendieck ∞ \infty -topos can be obtained as the underlying ∞ \infty -category of a model category that models homotopy type theory [ AK11 , Shu15b , LW15 , LLS20 , Shu19 ] . In this paper, we propose the filter quotient construction as a new method to construct further models of homotopy type theory. Concretely, we prove that with minor assumptions, the filter quotient construction [ Ras25a ] preserves all model categorical properties individually that implement various type theoretical constructors and axioms. On the other hand, the filter quotient construction does not preserve many external properties that are of set theoretical nature, such as cocompleteness, local presentability or cofibrant generation. Combining these, the filter quotient construction preserves models of homotopy type theory and can result in models that have not been considered before and exhibit behaviors that diverge from any of the established models.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
From Homotopy Theory to Homotopy Type Theory

Homotopy theory is a branch of mathematics that studies mathematical structures (topological spaces, chain complexes, categories, spectra, …) up to some chosen notion of equivalence (homotopical equivalences, quasi-isomorphism, categorical equivalence, stable equivalence, …). Historically, it first arose in topology, where topological spaces were considered up to (weak) homotopy equivalences. From these early days, homotopy theory faced a fundamental tension: homotopy equivalences of topological spaces are strictly weaker than bijections of the underlying sets. This means homotopy equivalences are not naturally part of the underlying mathematical foundation. As a result, first steps in algebraic topology and homotopy theory added those equivalences artificially to the structure, for example via localizations or via the construction of homotopy categories
[
Bro65
,
GZ67
]
. This situation suggested the need for pursuing and developing a foundation for mathematics more suitable for homotopical mathematics.

Since the 2010s, a new foundation has been developed, that can properly incorporate homotopical mathematics:
homotopy type theory

[
Uni13
]
. In general, type theories are a foundation for mathematics with a particular focus syntax. This syntactic angle makes it a particularly suitable foundation for formalizing mathematics via proof assistants, such as Rocq or Lean
[
CH86
]
. Homotopy type theory is one such type theory that, via the
univalence axiom
, axiomatically incorporates a notion of equivalence, making it the ideal setting for homotopy theory. As a result, in recent years many homotopical definitions and theorems have been developed in homotopy type theory, what is known as
synthetic homotopy theory
or
synthetic topology
.

As already pointed out, the syntactical nature of type theories makes homotopy type theory also a suitable framework for the formalization of homotopical mathematics via proof assistants. Prominent examples includes the formalization of various results in topology (such as the Blakers-Massey theorem or the computation of homotopy groups) in proof assistants, using
(cubical) Agda
and
Rocq

[
CH86
,
LS13
,
HFFLL16
,
VMA19
,
BLM22
,
LM23
]
.

1.2.
Models of homotopy type theory

While a type theoretical approach is ideal when working with computers and proof assistants, many mathematicians prefer a semantic approach. This has motivated the development of rigorous methods to translate between type theories and type theoretical constructors on the one side (syntax), and categorical structures with categorical universal properties on the other side (semantics). This is known as the
syntax-semantics duality
. From this perspective, for a given type theory we construct categories that are the
models of the type theory
, whereas on the other side, for a given category, we extract the type theory as the
internal language
of the category. As a result there is now an extensive literature establishing precise equivalences between various type theories (such as Martin-Löf type theories, possibly with
\Pi
-types) and various categories (such as locally Cartesian closed categories or topoi)
[
See83
,
See84
,
LS88
,
Hof95
,
CGH14
,
CD14
]
. This effort, in particular, includes a model of Martin-Löf type theory via groupoids, which is the first example where non-trivial equivalences (in this case for groupoids) arise
[
HS98
]
.

Unfortunately, this translation between type theories and categorical models is not straightforward, and involves many technicalities. As a result many technical categorical tools have been introduced that can assist with this translation effort, and give us effective ways to construct categorical models out of type theories. This includes
contextual categories
and
categories with attributes

[
Car86
]
,
display map categories

[
Tay87
]
,
comprehension categories

[
Jac93
]
,
categories with families

[
Dyb96
]
, and
natural models
and
natural pseudo-models

[
Awo18
]
. Moreover, many of these approaches have proven to be equivalent in recent years
[
KL18
,
ALN25
]
. Each one of these different notions is defined as a category along with additional data, that captures the data that comes from the type theory. Using these precise technical tools, many further categorical models of various type theories have been constructed in recent years.

1.3.
Semantics via Model Categories

The development outlined in the previous section suggests a natural solution when trying to better understand homotopy type theory: we pick one of the technical categorical methods for constructing models of type theories and apply it to the specific case of homotopy type theory. Unfortunately, the notion of equivalence that is inherent to homotopy type theory gives it a homotopical flavor, which suggests that any suitable model should also involve a notion of equivalence, making the construction and study of models of homotopy type theory particularly challenging. In the particular case of the groupoid model this issue was addressed “by hand”, as natural equivalences that are equivalent are necessarily equal, naturally restricting the amount of coherences that needed to be addressed
[
HS98
]
. However, for the general case, the required coherences to properly deal with equivalences are not restricted in any way. What is hence necessary is a strict category with a manageable notion of equivalence. Fortunately, here one can benefit from the pioneering work of Quillen.

Quillen originally introduced the notion of a
model category

The combination of these two facts, namely the existence of additional challenges when pursuing models of homotopy type theory and the powerful ability of model categories to capture homotopical data, motivated several figures, such as Voevodsky and Awodey, to pursue an alternative intermediate step when constructing categorical models of type theories: instead of directly constructing a model for homotopy type theory, we first construct a model category, and then extract a model for homotopy type theory from it, using one of the technical categorical models discussed above

1.4.
New Semantics via Filter Quotients

In this work we introduce a new method for constructing models of homotopy type theory via
filter quotient model categories
. This results in models that heretofore had not been constructed and differ in their properties from the established models. The idea of using filters in mathematical logic to construct non-trivial models goes back to major figures such as Skolem
[
Sko34
]
and Łoś
[
Łoś55
]
. Additionally, while not explicitly in the proof, an intuition regarding filter quotients plays an important role in Cohen’s forcing construction, proving the independence of the continuum hypothesis
[
Coh63
]
. This motivated Lawvere and Tierney to introduce topos theory as a categorical foundation for mathematics and to define therein
filter quotients
as a way to construct new models
[
Law64
,
Tie72
]
. These techniques were further refined by Adelman and Johnstone
[
AJ82
]
, and have since become a standard part of modern topos theory
[
Joh77a
,
MLM94
]
. Recently, the filter quotient construction has also been generalized to
\infty
-categories
[
Ras21
]
and model categories
[
Ras25a
]
.

In order to understand how filter quotients help construct models of homotopy type theory, it is instructive to first review relevant aspects of the filter quotient construction. A filter is defined as a certain subset of a poset. Given a model category
\mathcal{M}
and a suitable filter in the poset of subterminal objects in
\mathcal{M}
, denoted
\Phi
, we obtain a new model category
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
, along with a projection functor
P_{\Phi}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}

[
Ras25a
]
. With this result at hand, the major aim of this paper is to state and prove a precise version of the following meta-statement:

(1.1)

The functor
P_{\Phi}
preserves enough model categorical properties, so that the filter quotient model category
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
models the same type theoretic constructors and axioms as
\mathcal{M}
.

Here the word “enough” is important, as the projection functor will certainly not preserve all properties, such as infinite (co)limits and cofibrantly generated model structures, necessitating a careful analysis. We tackle this problem in three steps:

(1)

In
2.12
we summarize all relevant model categorical properties required to model type theoretical constructors via model categories.

(2)

In
Theorem

2.13
we precisely prove which one of the properties are preserved by the projection functor.

(3)

In
Theorem

2.14
we prove that the preservation properties in
Theorem

2.13
suffice to model the same type theoretic constructors.

1.5.
New Models and Their Implications

Establishing this result immediately implies that anything proven in homotopy type theory is valid in more situations than previously anticipated. This includes the examples already mentioned, but also other results, such as the
Hurewicz theorem

[
CS23
]
. Beyond this general observation, this approach has concrete implications regarding our understanding of homotopy type theory.

(1)

We can immediately observe that the universal property of infinite coproducts cannot be expressed in the internal language of homotopy type theory (
Corollary

2.21
).

(2)

We can observe that while all models of homotopy type theory have a natural number object, the size of the set of natural numbers can vary significantly between models, and models of homotopy type theory can have
non-standard natural numbers
(
Corollary

2.22
).

(3)

We can observe the existence of models of homotopy type theory that are well-pointed, meaning generated by the terminal object, but not equivalent to the
\infty
-category of spaces, which was an impossibility with previously known models (
Proposition

2.23
). Motivated by other constructions in logic, such as
[
Pal97
]
, such a model can reasonably be considered a
non-standard model of homotopy type theory
or a
non-standard model for spaces
(
Example

2.24
).

Fundamentally all of these examples are a concrete manifestation of the fact that in certain models a notion of “infinity” internal to homotopy type theory diverges from a notion of “infinity” from the model. This observation is in fact crucial when studying
\infty
-category theory in different foundations (i.e. internal to homotopy type theory and
\infty
-topos theory), which is the aim of
[
Ras25b
]
. See
Remark

2.25
for a detailed discussion.

Remark 2.15

One interesting aspect of

We end this section with some interesting implications, in particular of

2.18
. The binary coproduct type in any model of a type theory will correspond to the universal property of a binary coproduct in category theory. However, we would not expect the universal property of an infinite coproduct to correspond to any type constructor in homotopy type theory. While the previously developed models could not match this intuition, the lack of even countable coproducts in

finally confirms this.

Corollary 2.21
.

The existence of infinite (co)limits is independent of homotopy type theory. In particular, the universal property of infinite (co)limits cannot be articulated in the internal language of homotopy type theory.

Every model of homotopy type theory has a natural number type, which in our models corresponds to the natural number object. Beyond its universal property, a natural number object in a category can also have the property of being
standard

is jointly surjective, where
n
ranges over the external set of natural numbers. We would not expect this property to appear on the type theory side, as even its articulation requires an external perspective. Yet, it is true in every type-theoretic model topos (where the maps
\{n\}
in fact give us a colimit cocone). Again,

2.18
helps address this, as the natural number object therein is not standard (as proven in
[

, Example D.5.1.7]
).

Corollary 2.22
.

Models of homotopy type theory can have standard or non-standard natural number objects.

Type theories are by definition
internally well-pointed
, meaning the function type
1\to X
is just
X
, and this property does translate to every model, where it manifests as the fact that the internal mapping object
X^{1}
is just
X
. However, for models we can also consider
external well-pointedness
. We say a simplicial model category
\mathcal{M}
with cofibrant terminal object
1
is

if the mapping space functor
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(1,-)\colon\mathcal{M}\to s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
is faithful. In the non-homotopical setting this external notion of well-pointedness is in fact one of the defining properties of Lawvere’s
elementary theory of the category of sets (ETCS)

[
Law64
]
, see also
[
MLM94
, Section VI.10]
. Up until now, this property was very restrictive for
\infty
-categorical models of HoTT, as the following result demonstrates.

Proposition 2.23
.

Up to equivalence, there is a unique well-pointed type-theoretic model topos
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

For existence, we simply let
\mathcal{M}=s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Kan}
. Now, let
\mathcal{M}
be a type-theoretic model topos and assume that
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(1,-)\colon\mathcal{M}\to s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
is faithful. We denote by
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}}(1,-)\colon\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}\to\mathscr{S}
the induced functor on underlying
\infty
-categories, where we are following the common convention
\mathscr{S}=\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}(s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Kan})
. Notice
\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}
is a cocomplete
\infty
-category
[
Lur09
, Corollary 4.2.4.8]
. Hence, it suffices to prove
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}}(1,-)
is in fact full. Indeed, in that case
\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}
is a cocomplete sub-
\infty
-category of
\mathscr{S}
, which, by the universal property of
\mathscr{S}
, means it must be equivalent.

By
[
Lur09
, Proposition A.3.7.6]
,
\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}
is a presentable
\infty
-category, meaning, by
[
Lur09
, Corollary 5.5.2.9]
,
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}}(1,-)
has a left adjoint denoted
1\otimes-\colon\mathscr{S}\to\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}
. Now, faithfulness implies that for every object
X
in
\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}
, the counit of the adjunction
1\otimes\mathrm{Map}_{\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}}(1,X)\to X
is
(-1)
-truncated in
\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}
, meaning it is a subobject of
X
. Let
c\colon X\to\Omega
be the map to the subobject classifier, classifying this subobject. Now, by definition, every map
1\to X
factors through
1\otimes\mathrm{Map}_{\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}}(1,X)
, which means that
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}}(1,c)\simeq\mathrm{Map}_{\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}}(1,t)
, where
t\colon X\to\Omega
classifies
\mathrm{id}_{X}
. By the well-pointedness assumption, this implies that
c
and
t
are equivalent subobjects of
X
, meaning
c
is a weak equivalence and so
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}\mathcal{M}}(1,-)
is fully faithful.
∎

This result implies that there is a unique model of homotopy type theory that has small colimits and is well-pointed, namely
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Kan}
. It was already known that dropping the well-pointedness assumption results in many new models (every type theoretic model-topos), however, as of now, it was an open problem whether there are further well-pointed models, even though it would have been expected. With our results at hand, a slight variation of
Example

2.18
helps address this matter.

Example 2.24

(Non-Standard Model of Homotopy Type Theory)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a non-principal ultrafilter on

, meaning it is a maximal non-trivial filter in

. Then

is a model of Martin-Löf type theory with all the structures stated in

2.14
. Moreover,
\prod_{\mathcal{U}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
is externally well-pointed, as

has no non-trivial subobjects, see also
[

, Example 7.2]
for a more detailed discussion. However,
\prod_{\mathcal{U}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
is not equivalent to
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Kan}
, as it does not have infinite coproducts, by

.

Filter quotient constructions with respect to ultra filters have been used throughout the literature to construct non-standard models of set theory, in the sense of Lawvere
[
Law64
]
, as well as models for non-standard analysis
[
Pal97
]
, see
[
Joh77b
, Example 9.45]
for further details. From this perspective

or

.

Remark 2.25
.

At some level the implications of the main theorem, meaning
Corollaries

2.21
and
2.22
and the existence of the model in
Example

2.24
, should be unsurprising to experts and consistent with previous expectations. However, these results do provide a more rigorous understanding of the concept of “infinity” in homotopy type theory, as we shall explain.

On the one side, homotopy type theory inherently carries an internal notion of infinity, given via its natural numbers and various constructions built on top of it. On the other side, any notion of
\infty
-category is inherently infinite (it involves an infinite layer of morphisms and coherences). Hence the second we aim to construct a model of homotopy type theory, we secretly have two notions of infinity that we are working with, one coming from homotopy type theory, and one from our notion of
\infty
-category that we have chosen to construct our models. In many cases these do coincide. Those cases include the locally presentable models, where the existence of small colimits guarantees that any internal notion of “infinity”, such as the natural number object, matches with external notions of “infinity”, in this case meaning the natural number object is given as an infinite coproduct.

However, in this paper, we have constructed new models where these two notions of infinity visibly diverge, resulting in the kind of results stated above. While this divergence might not always matter, it will play a crucial role when trying to analyze inherently infinite notions. A simple example occurs when we try to compare
\infty
-categories internal to homotopy type theory with
\infty
-categories internal to models, at which point the diverging infinities result in a discrepancy. See
[
Ras25b
]
for further details.

3.
Technicalities and Proofs

This section is dedicated to technical definitions and detailed proofs. As explained in
Remark

2.15
, most model categorical properties straightforwardly transfer from a model category to its filter quotient. However, two properties require more care and are considered separately: the construction of higher inductive types (
Section

3.1
) and the construction of univalent universes (
Section

3.2
). Having covered the two challenging situations, we can finally complete the proof in
Section

3.3
.

3.1.
Initial Algebras

In this subsection we prove that filter quotient model categories inherit the construction of higher inductive types via the existence of initial algebras from the original model category. Before we proceed with the proof, we review the original argument by Lumsdaine and Shulman and why it needs to be adjusted
[
LLS20
]
.

Intuitively, for a given monad
\mathbb{T}\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}
, a higher inductive type is a suitably initial
\mathbb{T}
-algebra object in
\mathscr{C}
that is weakly stable under pullbacks. To make this argument precise, the authors proceed with the following three steps.

(1)

First they provide a precise definition of initiality, via weakly stable typal initial
\mathbb{T}
-algebras with representable lifts
[
LLS20
, Definition 12.4, Definition 12.5]
, and observe it has the desired semantic implications, giving us higher inductive types
[
LLS20
, Theorem 12.8]
.

(2)

They then define a notion of
cell monad with parameters

[
LLS20
, Definition 12.9]
in an excellent model category. It is point-wise defined as a (possibly infinite) composition of pushouts along monad cells, which are, in turn, monads freely generated by polynomial endofunctors
[
LLS20
, Definition 11.10]
.

(3)

Finally, the authors prove that if
\mathcal{M}
is an excellent model category, and
\mathbb{T}
is a cell monad with parameters, then
\mathcal{M}
has weakly stable typal initial
\mathbb{T}{-}\mathcal{A}\mathrm{lg}_{\textbf{\text{f}}}
-algebras and representable lifts
[
LLS20
, Theorem 12.13, Theorem 12.14]
.

Unfortunately, in two of these three steps they use the fact that excellent model categories are locally presentable and cofibrantly generated. Indeed, a monad cell is defined as a free monad generated via polynomial functors, which requires local presentability
[
LLS20
, Lemma 11.9]
, cell monads with parameters are defined as transfinite compositions, which also need infinite colimits
[
LLS20
, Definition 11.10]
, and the construction of the typal initial
\mathbb{T}{-}\mathcal{A}\mathrm{lg}_{\textbf{\text{f}}}
-algebra requires the small object argument
[
LLS20
, Theorem 11.13]
. As local presentability and cofibrant generation are not preserved by the filter quotient construction, we cannot use these assumptions anymore. Instead, we need to isolate the relevant property that will be preserved by the filter quotient construction. This motivates focusing on
fibred categories of structures with parameters (FCoSwP)

[
LLS20
, Definition 12.3]
, which is an abstraction employed by the authors to bundle the data of a category of fibrant objects, its category of fibrations and a category of algebras of a monad
[
LLS20
, Lemma 12.11]
. We hence proceed as follows:

(1)

We prove that a fibred category of structures with parameters on a category induces one on its filter quotient (
Proposition

3.19
).

(2)

Moreover, we show that the projection preserves weakly stable typal initial algebras or representable lifts (
Theorem

3.22
).

(3)

Finally, we define cell monads with parameters on a filter quotient category (
Definition

3.29
), and prove its category of algebras has weakly stable typal initial algebras and representable lifts (
Theorem

3.32
)

These results suggests that instead of excellent model categories, we need good model categories that satisfy the following property
IA
.

Definition 3.1

(Precise formulation of
IA
)
.

A model category
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
IA
if there is a class of fibred monads, the
cell monads
, such that for every cell monad with parameters the associated FCoSwP of
\mathbb{T}
-algebras has weakly stable typal initial
\mathbb{T}
-algebras with representable lifts.

From this perspective the main result can be summarized as stating that the filter quotient construction preserves
IA
. See
Theorem

3.32
for a precise statement. The axiomatic treatment of cell monads in this context might be unexpected, however, we do observe that if we start with the example of cell monads in an excellent model category, then its induced notion of cell monad on filter quotient does not in fact lose any example of interest, and in particular every building block, the monad cells (
Proposition

3.41
). Having better understood the required conditions and anticipated results, we now dive into the precise definitions and results.

Let
(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{T})
be a comprehension category, meaning
\mathcal{T}
is a Grothendieck fibration over
\mathscr{C}
with a Cartesian functor to the target projection
\mathscr{C}^{\rightarrow}\to\mathscr{C}
. Recall the following notion introduced in
[
LLS20
, Definition 12.1]
. A
parameter scheme
over
(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{T})
is a finite set
\mathcal{P}
whose elements are either type parameters or term parameters. To every parameter scheme, we associate a fibration,
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{C}
, called the
instantiation of
\mathcal{P}
defined inductively via the elements of
\mathcal{P}
. See
[
LLS20
, Definition 12.1]
for the inductive characterization.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a category. A
fibred category of structures with parameters (FCoSwP)
over
\mathscr{C}
consists of the following data.

(1)

A comprehension category
(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{T})

(2)

A finite set
\mathcal{P}
, called the
parameter scheme
, with elements either
type parameters
or
term parameters
.

(3)

The associated
instantiation of
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{C}
, which is the Grothendieck fibration defined via
\mathcal{P}
.

(4)

A Grothendieck fibration
\mathcal{S}\to\mathscr{C}
along with a faithful isofibration
\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\times_{\mathscr{C}}\mathscr{C}^{\rightarrow}
that is amnestic, meaning the only isomorphism that is mapped to the identity is itself the identity.

Notation 3.3
.

As part of this definition, we adopt the following notational conventions and terminology:

•

For a given object
\Gamma
in
\mathscr{C}
, we use
H
in
\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
to denote an object in the category
\mathcal{T}
over
\Gamma
. We use similar notations for
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})
.

•

For an object
H
in
\mathcal{T}(\Gamma)
, we denote its image in
\mathscr{C}^{\rightarrow}
by
\Gamma.H\to\Gamma
.

•

For an object
(\theta,X\to\Gamma)
in
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\times_{\mathscr{C}}\mathscr{C}^{\rightarrow}
(meaning
\theta
is in
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})(\Gamma)
) we denote an object in the fiber an
\mathcal{S}(\theta)
-structure on
X
.

We now want to show that an FCoSwP over
\mathscr{C}
induces an FCoSwP over
\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
. This requires several definitions and lemmas. First we have the following simple lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{T})
be a comprehension category and
F\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{C}
a discrete fibration. Then
(\mathscr{F},F^{*}\mathcal{T})
is a comprehension category over
\mathscr{F}
and the functor
\mathscr{F}^{*}\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}
preserves and reflects Cartesian morphisms.

Proof.

As
F
is a discrete fibration, the pullback of
\mathscr{C}^{\rightarrow}\to\mathscr{C}
along
F
is precisely
\mathscr{F}^{\rightarrow}\to\mathscr{F}
. Moreover, Grothendieck fibrations and Cartesian functors are evidently pullback stable. Finally, it is a direct observation that a morphism
(f,g)
in
F^{*}\mathcal{T}
is Cartesian if and only if
f
is Cartesian in
\mathcal{T}

[
Bor94
, Proposition 8.1.15]
.
∎

For the next definition, recall that for a comprehension category
(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{T})
, the categories
\mathscr{D}_{1}(\mathcal{T})
(
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{T})
) have objects dependent projections (display maps), and both have morphisms given by pullback squares. Finally, we denote by
\mathcal{T}_{\cong}\to\mathscr{C}
the (faithful) subcategory of
\mathcal{T}
with the same objects and only Cartesian morphisms. For more details see
[
LLS20
, Section 12]
. We need the following observation regarding these definitions.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{T})
be a comprehension category,
F\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{C}
a discrete fibration. Then we have
F^{*}\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{T})\cong\mathscr{D}(F^{*}\mathcal{T})
,
F^{*}\mathscr{D}_{1}(\mathcal{T})\cong\mathscr{D}_{1}(F^{*}\mathcal{T})
,
F^{*}\mathscr{D}_{1,*}(\mathcal{T})\cong\mathscr{D}_{1,*}(F^{*}\mathcal{T})
and
F^{*}\mathcal{T}_{\cong}\cong(F^{*}\mathcal{T})_{\cong}
.

Proof.

Lemma

3.4
implies that the pullback of dependent projections (display maps) along the discrete fibrations
F
are again dependent projection (display maps). This implies the first three isomorphisms. The last part follows from the fact that the pullback preserves and reflects Cartesian morphisms (
Lemma

3.4
).
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T})
be a comprehension category,
\mathcal{P}
a parameter scheme and
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{C}
the associated instantiation. Let
F\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{C}
be a discrete fibration. Then there is a pullback parameter scheme
F^{*}\mathcal{P}
, for which the associated instantiation is precisely
F^{*}\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{F}
.

Proof.

We use the mutually inductive definition in
[
LLS20
, Definition 12.1]
to prove the result similarly via mutual induction.

•

If
\mathcal{P}
is empty, then we take
F^{*}\mathcal{P}
to be empty as well, and we indeed have
F^{*}\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{F}
.

•

Let us assume that
\mathcal{P}
is extended by a type parameter
\alpha\colon\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{T})
. Then
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\langle F^{*}\mathcal{P},F^{*}\alpha\rangle)\to\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\langle F^{*}\mathcal{P}\rangle)
is by definition the pullback of
F^{*}\mathcal{T}_{\cong}\to\mathscr{F}
and hence by pullback gluing, following
Lemma

3.5
, also the pullback of
\mathcal{T}_{\cong}\to\mathscr{C}
. Similarly,
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\langle\mathcal{P},\alpha\rangle)\to\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})
is the pullback of
\mathcal{T}_{\cong}\to\mathscr{C}
, by definition. Hence, by pullback cancellation we get the desired isomorphism
F^{*}\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\langle\mathcal{P},\alpha\rangle)\cong\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\langle F^{*}\mathcal{P},F^{*}\alpha\rangle)
. This means we extend
F^{*}\mathcal{P}
by the type parameter
F^{*}\alpha\colon F^{*}\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{D}(F^{*}\mathcal{T})
, using the fact that
\mathscr{D}(F^{*}\mathcal{T})\cong F^{*}\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{T})
(
Lemma

3.5
), finishing this induction step.

•

Let us assume that
\mathcal{P}
is extended by a term parameter
\alpha\colon\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{T})
,
\beta\colon\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{D}_{1}(\mathcal{T})
. Then, employing an analogous pullback cancellation argument as in the previous step, it follows that
F^{*}\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\llbracket\mathcal{P},\alpha,\beta\rrbracket)\cong\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\llbracket F^{*}\mathcal{P},F^{*}\alpha,F^{*}\beta\rrbracket)
. This means we extend
F^{*}\mathcal{P}
by the term parameter
F^{*}\alpha\colon F^{*}\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{D}(F^{*}\mathcal{T})
and
F^{*}\beta\colon F^{*}\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{D}_{1}(F^{*}\mathcal{T})
, finishing the induction step.∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
F\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{C}
be a discrete fibration, and
(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P}))

Proof.

First of all, Grothendieck fibrations, isofibrations, faithful functors and amnestic functors are evidently pullback stable. Moreover, by

,

is still a comprehension category. Finally, by
Lemma

,
F^{*}\mathcal{P}
is a parameter scheme with instantiation
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(F^{*}\mathcal{P})
. Hence we are done.
∎

We record here the following basic fact about Grothendieck fibrations.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
F\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{C}
be a Grothendieck fibration. If
\mathscr{F}
has a terminal object
\hat{1}
, then
F(\hat{1})
in
\mathscr{C}
is subterminal. Moreover, if an object in
\mathscr{C}
does not admit a morphism to
F(\hat{1})
, meaning it is not in the full subcategory
\mathscr{C}_{F(\hat{1})}
, then its fiber is empty.

Remark 3.10
.

Lemma

3.9
implies that for a Grothendieck fibration

with terminal object
\hat{1}
, we can without loss of generality restrict the codomain to
\mathscr{C}_{F(\hat{1})}
, at which point the image of
\hat{1}
will in fact be terminal. We will henceforth assume that if
\mathscr{F}
has a terminal object, its image is indeed terminal in
\mathscr{C}
.

Lemma 3.14

\mathscr{C}
be a category with with finite products and
\Phi

, where functoriality is given by mapping

is of the form

U
is in
\Phi
and
X
in
\mathscr{C}
, and a morphism from

Definition

2.5

Lemma 3.15

The filtered colimit of Grothendieck fibrations and Cartesian functors is a Grothendieck fibration. Similarly, the filtered colimit of isofibrations is an isofibration.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let

\mathscr{C}
.

(1)

The filter
\Phi
induces a diagram of Grothendieck fibrations
\mathscr{F}_{U}\to\mathscr{C}_{U}
and Cartesian functors, compatible with the functors
P_{V}\colon\mathscr{C}_{U}\to\mathscr{C}_{V}
.

(2)

The resulting colimit of the Grothendieck fibrations
F_{U}
,

\underset{U\in\Phi^{op}}{\mathrm{colim}}F_{U}=F_{\Phi}\colon\mathscr{F}_{\Phi}\to\mathscr{C}_{\Phi},

is a Grothendieck fibration.

(3)

If
\mathscr{F}
has a terminal object
\hat{1}
, then the
\mathscr{F}_{\Phi}
is itself the filter quotient of
\mathscr{F}
.

Proof.

(1)
For a given
V\leq U
in
\Phi
, by
Lemma

3.12
, the functor
P_{V}\colon\mathscr{C}_{U}\to\mathscr{C}_{V}
lifts to a commutative diagram of Cartesian functors
\mathscr{F}_{U}\to\mathscr{F}_{V}
, giving us the desired natural diagram
F_{U}\colon\mathscr{F}_{U}\to\mathscr{C}_{U}
.

(2)
By the previous step,
F_{(-)}
is a natural transformation between the diagrams
\mathscr{F}_{(-)},\mathscr{C}_{(-)}\colon\Phi^{op}\to\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}
. This induces a functor on colimits
\mathscr{F}_{\Phi}\to\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
. By
Lemma

3.15
, this functor is a Grothendieck fibration.

(3)
If
\mathscr{F}
has a terminal object
\hat{1}
, then by
Lemma

3.12
, the filter
\Phi
lifts to a filter on
\mathscr{F}
, also denoted
\Phi
, and, by
Lemma

3.14
, the induced filter quotient coincides with the filtered colimit
\mathscr{F}_{\Phi}
.
∎

The following useful lemma follows directly from the bijection invariance of colimits and
Lemma

3.5
.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{T})
be a comprehension category and
\Phi
a filter of subterminal objects on
\mathscr{C}
. Then we have
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{T})_{\Phi}\cong\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{T}_{\Phi})
,
\mathscr{D}_{1}(\mathcal{T})_{\Phi}\cong\mathscr{D}_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\Phi})
,
\mathscr{D}_{1,*}(\mathcal{T})_{\Phi}\cong\mathscr{D}_{1,*}(\mathcal{T}_{\Phi})
and
(\mathcal{T}_{\cong})_{\Phi}\cong(\mathcal{T}_{\Phi})_{\cong}
.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T})
be a comprehension category,
\mathcal{P}
a parameter scheme and
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})\to\mathscr{C}
the associated instantiation, and let
\Phi
be a filter of subterminal objects on
\mathscr{C}
.
\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}
, defined as the filtered colimit of
\mathcal{P}_{U}
(
Definition

3.8
), is a parameter scheme over
\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
, for which the associated instantiation is precisely given by the filtered colimit
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P})_{\Phi}\to\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

We can precisely repeat the three inductive steps and the arguments therein from the proof of
Lemma

3.6
, this time relying on the isomorphisms from
Lemma

3.17
, instead of
Lemma

3.5
.
∎

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P}))
be an FCoSwP over
\mathscr{C}
such that
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}
have terminal objects, and
\Phi
a filter of subterminal objects. Then
(\mathscr{C}_{\Phi},\mathcal{T}_{\Phi},\mathcal{S}_{\Phi},\mathcal{P}_{\Phi},\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}))
is an FCoSwP over
\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

First, by
Lemma

3.16
, the induced functors on filter quotients
\mathcal{T}_{\Phi}\to\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
,
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi}\to\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
are still Grothendieck fibrations. Moreover, by
Lemma

3.15
,
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi}\to\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P}_{\Phi})\times_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}^{\rightarrow}
is additionally an isofibration. Next, by
Lemma

3.18
,
\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}
is a parameter scheme over
\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
, with instantiation
\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P}_{\Phi})
. Now, the fact that filtered colimits commute with finite limits implies that the filtered colimits preserve monomorphisms. So, filtered colimits preserve faithfulness, meaning
S_{\Phi}\to\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P}_{\Phi})\times_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}^{\rightarrow}
is faithful.

Finally, a morphism
[f]
in the filter quotient
S_{\Phi}
is an isomorphism if there is a
U
in
\Phi
, such that its representative
f
in
S_{U}
is an isomorphism. Now, if the image of
[f]
is the identity, then so is the image of
f\times V
, for some
V
. As, by
Lemma

3.7
, the functor
S_{V}\to\mathcal{I}\mathrm{nst}(\mathcal{P}_{V})\times_{\mathscr{C}_{V}}(\mathscr{C}_{V})^{\rightarrow}
is amnestic, it follows that the image of
f\times V
is the identity, which means the image of
[f]
is the identity. This proves the functor is amnestic, hence we are done.
∎

Having constructed the filter quotient FCoSwP, we now prove it preserves relevant properties of the original FCoSwP. First, we note here the following basic categorical lemma.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
F\colon\mathscr{C}^{op}\to\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
be a representable functor with representing object
C
. Let
L\colon\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{C}
be a functor with right adjoint
R
, then
F\circ L
is also representable, with representing object
R(C)
.

Finally, we can move on to the main result regarding FCoSwP and filter quotients. Here we refer the reader to
[
LLS20
, Definition 12.4]
for the definition of weakly stable typal initial
\mathcal{S}
-algebras and
[

for the definition of representable lifts.

.

Let

(1)
By definition weakly stable typal initial

.

\mathcal{S}_{\Phi}
-algebras if there exists a
U
in

has weakly stable typal initial
\mathcal{S}_{U}
-algebras

(2)

\mathcal{S}
has representable lifts if there exists a
U
in
\Phi
, such that
\mathcal{S}_{U}
has representable lifts.

(3)

in
\Phi

\mathcal{S}_{U}
-algebras and

3.2.
Univalent Universes

In this subsection we prove that the filter quotient construction preserves univalent universes. This requires reviewing some aspects of its semantics, as presented in
[
Shu19
]
. Intuitively a universe in a model category
\mathcal{M}
is a fibrant object
U
in
\mathcal{M}
, such that morphisms
X\to U
are in an appropriate sense equivalent to fibrations
Y\twoheadrightarrow X
, functorial in
X
. Evidently, an object
U
induces a representable functor
\mathcal{M}(-,U)
, so as a first step we need to make the other side functorial.

Definition 3.43
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a finitely complete category.
\mathbb{M}\colon\mathcal{M}^{op}\to\mathscr{G}\mathrm{rpd}
is the pseudo-functor that takes
X
in
\mathcal{M}
to
(\mathcal{M}_{/X})^{\simeq}
, meaning the underlying groupoid of
\mathcal{M}_{/X}
. If
\mathcal{M}
has additionally a model structure, we let
\mathbb{F}\mathrm{ib}\colon\mathcal{M}^{op}\to\mathscr{G}\mathrm{rpd}
be the full sub-functor of
\mathbb{M}
with objects fibrations in
\mathcal{M}
.

It would be unreasonable to expect an actual equivalence of groupoids between
\mathcal{M}(-,U)
and
\mathbb{M}
, as it would imply
\mathbb{M}
takes values in sets. What we need is a more refined notion of equivalence that fits the situation. The solution is to transfer parts of the model structure from
\mathcal{M}
to the category of pseudo-functors
\mathrm{Fun}^{ps}(\mathcal{M}^{op},\mathscr{G}\mathrm{rpd})
, as was originally realized by Shulman
[
Shu19
, Definition 5.1]
.

Definition 3.44
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category and
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}\colon\mathcal{M}^{op}\to\mathscr{G}\mathrm{rpd}
pseudo-functors. A morphism
\alpha\colon\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
is an
acyclic fibration
, if it has the right lifting property with respect to
\mathcal{M}(-,A)\to\mathcal{M}(-,B)
for all cofibrations
A\to B
in
\mathcal{M}
.

We can use
Definition

3.44
to define universes for an even broader class of pseudo-functors, as the one introduced in
Definition

3.43
, namely
locally representable and relatively acyclic notion of fibred structure covering all fibrations (LPaRANoFScaF)
.

Definition 3.45

(
[
Shu19
, Definition 3.1, Definition 3.10, Definition 5.11]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category. A
locally representable and relatively acyclic notion of fibred structure covering all fibrations (LPaRANoFScaF)
consists of the following data and conditions:

•

A pseudo-functor
\mathbb{F}\colon\mathcal{M}^{op}\to\mathscr{G}\mathrm{rpd}

•

A discrete fibrations of pseudo-functors
\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{M}
with small fibers.

•

For every morphism
\mathcal{M}(-,Z)\to\mathbb{M}
the pseudo-pullback along
\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{M}
is representable.

•

The image of
\mathbb{F}
in
\mathbb{M}
, denoted
|\mathbb{F}|
, is precisely
\mathbb{F}\mathrm{ib}
.

•

The resulting morphism
\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}\mathrm{ib}
is an acyclic fibration.

For such an
\mathbb{F}
, intuitively, we would now hope a universe is an object
U
along with a choice of acyclic fibration
\mathcal{M}(-,U)\to\mathbb{F}
. However, this is still unrealistic, as it would imply
U
itself is classified by a map
1\to U
, which results in a situation analogous to Russell’s paradox. Here the solution is straightforward: we need to suitably restrict
\mathbb{F}
to smaller classes of fibrations.

Definition 3.46
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a cofibrantly generated model category. Let
\mathbb{F}
be a LPaRANoFScaF and
\kappa
a regular cardinal. Let
\mathbb{F}^{\kappa}
be defined as the pullback
\mathbb{F}\times_{\mathbb{M}}\mathbb{M}^{\kappa}
, where
\mathbb{M}^{\kappa}
is the full sub-pseudo-functor of
\mathbb{M}
given by
\kappa
-small morphisms.

With all these pieces at hand, Shulman finally proves that for a suitable model category
\mathcal{M}
(in particular it needs to be cofibrantly generated, with underlying category locally presentable) and
\mathbb{F}
a LPaRANoFScaF, there exists a regular cardinal
\lambda
, such that for all regular cardinals
\kappa
larger than
\lambda
, the pseudo-functor
\mathbb{F}^{\kappa}
has a fibrant univalent universe
U_{\kappa}

[
Shu19
, Theorem 5.22]
.

Moving on to our situation, we want to repeat these arguments for filter quotients. Unfortunately, that is not possible, as filter quotients are generally not cofibrantly generated. On the other hand, this machinery is primarily used to generate a tower of universes. Hence, building on work done in
[
Shu19
, Appendix A]
, we will formulate a precise definition of such a tower of universes and show this property is indeed preserved by the filter quotient construction.

Definition 3.47
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category and
\mathbb{F}
a LPaRANoFScaF. A
universe
for
\mathbb{F}
is a choice of object
U
, along with a map of pseudo-functors
\mathcal{M}(-,U)\to\mathbb{F}
, such that the induced map onto the image is an acyclic fibration. An object in
\mathbb{F}
that is in the image of
U
is a fibration
classified
by
U
.

Having defined universes, the only thing missing is univalence. Given a fibration
\tilde{U}\to U
in a Cartesian closed model category
\mathcal{M}
, Shulman constructs the
universal object of equivalences

\mathrm{Eq}(\tilde{U})\to U\times U
, along with a section of the
\Delta\colon U\to U\times U
, denoted
\mathrm{idtoequiv}\colon U\to\mathrm{Eq}(\tilde{U})
. See
[
Shu15a
, Section 4]
for a detailed construction. We can now use this overview to define univalent universes.

Definition 3.48
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category and
\mathbb{F}
a LPaRANoFScaF. A
univalent universe
for
\mathbb{F}
is a universe
U
for
\mathbb{F}
such that the induced map
\mathrm{idtoequiv}\colon U\to\mathrm{Eq}(\tilde{U})
is an equivalence in
\mathcal{M}
, where the fibration
\tilde{U}\to U
is the image of the identity map
\mathrm{id}_{U}\colon U\to U
under the map
\mathcal{M}(-,U)\to\mathbb{F}
.

We now use the theory of pseudo-models, as discussed in
[
Shu19
, Appendix A]
, to develop a theory of universes suitable to our situation. Recall that a natural pseudo-model is a category
\mathcal{M}
with a terminal object and a representable strict discrete fibration
\omega\colon\mathbb{T}\mathrm{m}\to\mathbb{T}\mathrm{y}
in
\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{M}^{op},\mathscr{G}\mathrm{rpd})

[
Shu19
, Definition A.1]
. A
natural model
is a pseudo-natural model such that
\mathbb{T}\mathrm{m}
is discrete
[
Awo18
]
. The
canonical natural pseudo-model
of a model category
\mathcal{M}
is given by
\mathbb{F}\mathrm{ib}\times_{\mathbb{M}}\mathbb{M}_{\bullet}\to\mathbb{F}\mathrm{ib}
, where
\mathbb{M}_{\bullet}\to\mathbb{M}
is the forgetful fibration from pointed objects
[
Shu19
, Example A.5]
. We now use this background to define universes.

Definition 3.49

(Precise formulation of
U
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category.
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
U
if it has the following structure and properties:

•

\mathcal{M}
is locally Cartesian closed.

•

\mathcal{M}
has a LPaRANoFScaF denoted
\mathbb{F}
(
Definition

3.45
).

•

A
level structure

\mathcal{L}

[
Shu19
, Definition A.12]
.

•

For every
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
, a fibrant univalent universe
U_{\alpha}
in
\mathcal{M}
, in the sense of
Definition

3.48
, such that
U_{\alpha}
is strictly closed under
\Sigma
-types,
\Pi
-types, identity types, and binary sum types, in the sense of
[
Shu19
, Definition A.16]
, and containing the empty type, the unit type, the natural numbers type, the spheres types
S^{n}
, as well as other “cell complex” types such as the torus
T^{2}
.

•

The collection of
U_{\alpha}
induce an
\mathcal{L}
-family of strict Tarski universes in the canonical natural pseudo-model of
\mathcal{M}

[
Shu19
, Definition A.14]
that classify all fibrations in
\mathcal{M}
.

Using the ideas from
[
LW15
]
and the work in
[
LLS20
]
, to every natural pseudo-model and choice of family of local universes
\mathcal{V}
, in the sense of
[
Shu19
, Definition A.19]
, we can associate a natural model
\omega_{!}\colon\mathbb{T}\mathrm{m}_{!,\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{T}\mathrm{y}_{!,\mathcal{V}}
which strictly models type theoretical constructors. If
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
U
, then we can associate to the
\mathcal{L}
-family of strict Tarski universes a family of local universes
\mathcal{V}

[
Shu19
, Example A.23]
, resulting in a natural model
\omega_{!,\mathcal{V}}\colon\mathbb{T}\mathrm{m}_{!,\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{T}\mathrm{y}_{!,\mathcal{V}}
, which we call the
associated natural model
. Building on that, by
[
Shu19
, Theorem A.25]
,
U
indeed suffices to model universes, meaning we have the following result.

Corollary 3.50
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category that satisfies
U
. Then the associated natural model
\omega_{!,\mathcal{V}}\colon\mathbb{T}\mathrm{m}_{!,\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{T}\mathrm{y}_{!,\mathcal{V}}
has strictly stable
\Sigma
-types,
\Pi
-types, identity types, and binary sum type, and an
\mathcal{L}
-family of strict Tarski universes strictly closed under all these type constructors, meaning
\mathcal{M}
models arbitrarily large univalent universes.

We now want to prove that
U
is preserved by the filter quotient construction. This requires several technical preliminary constructions and lemmas. Recall that to every pseudo-functor valued in groupoids denoted
\mathbb{F}
, we can associate a Grothendieck fibration
\int_{\mathscr{C}}\mathbb{F}\to\mathscr{C}
, via the Grothendieck construction. See
[
LR20

Proposition 3.54
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a locally Cartesian closed model category,
\mathbb{F}
a LPaRANoFScaF, and
\Phi
a model filter. Then
\mathbb{F}_{\Phi}
, defined via
Definition

3.51
, is also a LPaRANoFScaF.

Proof.

We check the conditions separately, relying on the computations established in
Lemma

3.52
.

•

We need to show the fiber of
\mathbb{F}_{\Phi}\to\mathbb{M}_{\Phi}
is discrete and small. This follows from the fact that the fiber of
\mathbb{F}_{\Phi}\to\mathbb{M}_{\Phi}
over an object
X
is the filtered colimit of the fibers of
\mathbb{F}_{U}\to\mathbb{M}_{U}
, for
U
in
\Phi
. Hence, the results from the fact that
\mathbb{F}
is a LPaRANoFScaF, and discrete small groupoids are closed under filtered colimits.

•

Let
\alpha\colon\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(-,Z)\to\mathbb{M}_{\Phi}
be a natural transformation. By the Yoneda lemma, the data of such a natural transformation is equivalent to a choice of morphism
p\colon W\times U\to Z\times U
, for some
U
in
\Phi
. This means we can lift
\alpha
to a natural transformation
\alpha_{U}\colon\mathcal{M}_{U}(-,Z)\to\mathbb{M}_{U}
, with the property that
\alpha=\mathrm{colim}_{V\leq U}\alpha_{V}
, where
\alpha_{V}\colon\mathcal{M}_{V}(-,Z)\to\mathbb{M}_{V}
is uniquely given by
p\times V
.

Now, we know that filtered colimits commute with pullbacks, and that the filter quotient of a representable functor is representable, by
Lemma

3.52
. Hence, to finish this step, it suffices to prove the pullback of the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}_{V}(-,Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{M}_{V}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{V}}

is representable. However, this is true by assumption, as
\mathbb{F}
is a LPaRANoFScaF.

•

By assumption, the map
\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}\mathrm{ib}
is surjective, and filtered colimits preserve surjectivity. Hence, the map
\mathbb{F}_{\Phi}\to\mathbb{F}\mathrm{ib}_{\Phi}
is also surjective, meaning
|\mathbb{F}_{\Phi}|=\mathbb{F}\mathrm{ib}_{\Phi}
.

•

This follows from applying
Lemma

3.53
to the trivial fibration
\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}\mathrm{ib}
. ∎

Proposition 3.55
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a locally Cartesian closed model category,
\Phi
a model filter.

(1)

P_{\Phi}
preserves universes.

(2)

P_{\Phi}
preserves the fibrancy of the universe.

(3)

P_{\Phi}
preserves the univalence of the universe.

(4)

P_{\Phi}
preserves any type the universe contains, including empty type, unit type, the natural numbers type, the sphere types, and other “cell complex types”.

Proof.

(1)
Let us denote the image of
\mathcal{M}(-,U)\to\mathbb{F}
in
\mathbb{F}
by
|\mathcal{M}(-,U)|
. By assumption,
\mathcal{M}(-,U)\to|\mathcal{M}(-,U)|
is a trivial fibration of pseudo-functors, and so, by
Lemma

3.53
,
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(-,U)\to|\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(-,U)|
is also a trivial fibration, proving that
P_{\Phi}
preserves universes.

(2)

P_{\Phi}
preserves fibrancy, by
Theorem

2.9
.

(3)
Following
[
Shu15a
, Section 4]
(see also
Definition

3.48
), the universal object of equivalences
\mathrm{Eq}(\tilde{U})\to U\times U
is exclusively constructed via the locally Cartesian structure on
\mathcal{M}
, which is preserved by
P_{\Phi}
(
Proposition

2.6
). Hence, if
\mathrm{idtoequiv}\colon U\to\mathrm{Eq}(\tilde{U})
is an equivalence, then it remains so after applying
P_{\Phi}
.

(4)
By
Proposition

2.6
,
P_{\Phi}
preserves the empty type, unit type, the natural numbers type, the spheres types, and other “cell complex types”, as these are finite (co)limits.
∎

Proposition 3.57
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
satisfy
U
and
\Phi
a model filter. If the
\mathcal{L}
-family of pseudo Tarski universes is closed under
\Sigma
-types,
\Pi
-types, identity types, or binary sum types, then so is its image via
P_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

We will consider the case of
\Pi
-types and the other cases follow similarly. By
Theorem

2.9
,
P_{\Phi}
preserves fibrations, meaning it preserves both
\mathbb{T}\mathrm{y}
(whose objects are fibrations) and
\mathbb{T}\mathrm{y}^{\Pi}
(whose objects are pairs of fibrations, as defined in
[
Shu19
, Below Remark A.8]
). Moreover,
P_{\Phi}
preserves surjectivity. Finally, by
Proposition

3.55
,
P_{\Phi}
preserves universes. Hence, applying
P_{\Phi}
to the diagram in
[
Shu19
, Definition A.16]
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}
we obtain the following diagram of functors from
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}^{op}
to groupoids

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(-,(\diamond\centerdot P_{\Phi}U_{\alpha})\triangleleft(\diamond\centerdot P_{\Phi}U_{\beta}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}(-,\diamond\centerdot P_{\Phi}U_{\alpha\vee\beta})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{T}\mathrm{y}^{\Pi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{G}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{T}\mathrm{y}}

Given that the lift is preserved as part of the diagram,
P_{\Phi}
preserves
\mathcal{L}
-families of pseudo Tarski universes closed under
\Pi
-types (which simply means the lift to
\mathbb{G}
exists).
∎

Theorem 3.58
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category,
\Phi
a model filter. Then
P_{\Phi}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
preserves
U
.

Proof.

First,
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is locally Cartesian closed by
Proposition

2.6
. Next, by
Proposition

3.54
,
P_{\Phi}
maps the LPaRANoFScaF
\mathbb{F}
to the LPaRANoFScaF
\mathbb{F}_{\Phi}
. Next, we take the same level structure
\mathcal{L}
given for
\mathcal{M}
. By
Proposition

3.55
,
P_{\Phi}(U_{\alpha})
is a fibrant univalent universe. Moreover, by
Proposition

3.57
, the family of universes
P_{\Phi}(U_{\alpha})
is closed under the same type formers. Finally, by
Proposition

2.6
,
P_{\Phi}
preserves pullback squares, and so in particular the pullbacks involved in the definition of strict Tarski universes. Hence, we are done.
∎
