Campylobacter fetus isolates from both human patients and healthy cattle carry three distinct cytolethal distending toxin (cdt) gene clusters
Authors: Wen WEN, Noritoshi HATANAKA, Srinuan SOMROOP, Sharda Prasad AWASTHI, Atsushi HINENOYA, Shinji YAMASAKI
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCampylobacter fetus is a zoonotic pathogen. Although the precise virulence mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated, cytolethal distending toxin (CDT) is considered as one of the well-characterized virulence factors in Campylobacter. In silico analysis of the genome of C. fetus type strain ATCC27374T indicates that there are three cdt gene clusters, Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III. However, it is not clear whether these clusters are ubiquitously present in C. fetus and their association with diseases in humans and animals. In this study, we have analyzed the distribution and nucleotide sequences of these cdt gene clusters in 137 C. fetus strains isolated from human patients and healthy cattle. MLST and PFGE were also applied to determine clonal relationship between C. fetus strains isolated from patients and cattle. We found all C. fetus strains carry three Cfcdt gene clusters by colony hybridization assay and the strains belonged to 38 different pulsotypes. Whole genome sequencing of 38 C. fetus strains was carried out to determine the entire cdt gene cluster sequences and their sequence type (ST). Among 38 strains, six STs were identified, and each cdt gene cluster showed high similarity (>99%). Interestingly, some of these Cfcdt genes are more similar to the cdt genes of other Campylobacter species than other Cfcdt gene types. Altogether, the results suggest that three Cfcdt gene clusters are highly conserved in C. fetus and the strains belonging to ST-6 may be more pathogenic to human.Keywords: Campylobacter fetus, cytolethal distending toxin (cdt), sequence type (ST), zoonotic pathogen

Campylobacter is one of the major causes of diarrhea worldwide and the foodborne diseases caused by this bacterium has increased in many industrialized countries over the last decade [16, 33]. Among the 32 currently recognized species in the genus Campylobacter, the most prominent species is Campylobacter jejuni, and to a lesser extent C. coli, which are isolated from human diarrhea and domestic animals such as poultry and cattle [19]. Nevertheless, other Campylobacter species, such as C. fetus, C. upsaliensis and C. hyointestinalis have also been reported to be associated with human diseases [16, 28]. C. fetus is a zoonotic pathogen and mostly causes extraintestinal infections in humans and abortions in animals and occasionally it may be responsible for gastroenteritis [34]. Unlike C. jejuni and C. coli, C. fetus is the most detectable pathogen in bacteremia and septicemia with a detection rate of 19–53% [34] and 24–41% [1], respectively, and the fatality rate is 14% [34] which may reach to 25% as reported in France [30]. The pathogenic mechanisms of C. fetus causing extraintestinal infections in humans and abortions in animals have not yet fully understood. Although the surface layer (S-layer) has been reported to assist the persistent survival of C. fetus in the bloodstream and enable their evasion of the host immune system [29], our knowledge regarding how C. fetus causes such kinds of diseases in humans and animals remains limited.

Cytolethal distending toxin (CDT) is a well-characterized Campylobacter toxin and considered to be an important virulence factor. CDT holotoxin is composed of three subunits, CdtA, CdtB, and CdtC, which are encoded by the cdt gene cluster, cdtA, cdtB, and cdtC, respectively. CDT causes cell distention and DNA damage when it acts on target cells, resulting in irreversible cell cycle arrests and eventual cell death [36]. C. jejuni, C. coli and C. fetus are documented to ubiquitously possess a single cdt gene cluster, having a distinct species divergence [2]. However, our recent preliminary in silico analysis using the published whole genome sequences of C. fetus type strain ATCC27374T indicates that there are three cdt gene clusters present in at least 24 C. fetus strains, which are designated Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III in this study. It has been suggested that CDT might promote the invasion of Campylobacter into host cells [3] and help to fight with the host immune response by affecting immune cells [25]. Further, Cagnoli et al. have hypothesized that CDT is an important toxin in C. fetus pathogenesis of bovine embryonic mortality [4]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the possession of these three cdt gene clusters and higher production levels of cdt gene-products due to three copies might be the reason why C. fetus cause more severe infections in humans and abortions in animals than C. jejuni and C. coli do. Our final goal is to understand how Cfcdt gene products of C. fetus are associated with its pathogenesis.

In this study, as a first step to our final goal, we examined the distribution of three cdt gene clusters in 137 C. fetus strains originated from human patients and healthy cattle. Furthermore, the entire cdt gene sequences and sequence type (ST) were determined by whole genome sequencing analysis to understand how much variations are there in each cdt gene cluster in various C. fetus strains and how correlation is there between cdt gene sequences and STs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

A laboratory collection of 137 C. fetus strains was used in this study (Supplementary Table 1). Eighteen C. fetus strains were isolated from blood samples of patients with extraintestinal infections including sepsis and aneurism, and 119 strains were isolated from healthy cattle. C. jejuni strain 81–176 and C. fetus type strain ATCC27374T were used as negative and positive controls, respectively, for the colony hybridization assay. Escherichia coli strain JM109 was used for cloning of Cfcdt genes. Campylobacters were grown on blood agar [blood agar base No.2 (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK) supplemented with 5% (v/v) defibrinated horse blood (Nippon Bio Supp. Center, Tokyo, Japan)] under anaerobic condition (10% CO2, 10% H2, 80% N2) at 37°C for 48 hr. E. coli strains carrying recombinant plasmids were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB; Becton, Dickinson and Co., Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) broth supplemented with 30 μg/mL kanamycin (Nacalai Testque Inc., Kyoto, Japan) at 37°C for 18 hr with shaking.

Cloning of Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III gene clusters in E. coli

Each of the Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III gene cluster was amplified by PCR using genomic DNA of C. fetus strain ATCC27374T as templates and region-specific primer sets, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). Each PCR product was digested with BamHI and XhoI (Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan) followed by ligation into similarly digested vector pET28a (+) (Kmr, Supplementary Table 3). Although pET28a (+) is an expression vector but not cloning vector, to skip one subcloning step pET28a (+) was used for both cloning and expression of the gene and gene products. The ligated product was transformed into the E. coli strain JM109 (Supplementary Table 3) and the desired recombinant clone was selected on L-agar plate containing kanamycin (30 µg/mL). Each recombinant clone carrying the Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II or Cfcdt-III gene cluster was further confirmed by sequencing of the insert DNA. Sequences of the insert DNAs indicated successful cloning of the genomic regions 26,799–28,977, 949,581–951,563 and 951,850–953,915 of the Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III gene clusters in C. fetus strain ATCC27374T(NCTC10842; Accession No. LS483431.1), respectively.

Preparation of template DNAs from recombinant clones

DNA template of the E. coli strain carrying the recombinant clone of Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II or Cfcdt-III gene cluster, namely, E. coli (Cfcdt-I+), E. coli (Cfcdt-II+) or E. coli (Cfcdt-III+), as follows. Each strain was inoculated in 3 mL of LB broth containing 30 μg/mL kanamycin and incubated at 37°C with shaking for 18 hr. An aliquot of each bacterial culture was diluted 10 times with TE buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)] followed by boiling of the mixture for 10 min, centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C and collection of supernatants. The supernatants thus collected were used as DNA templates for the following experiments.

Colony hybridization assay

DNA probes of nine target cdt genes [Cfcdt-I (A, B, C), Cfcdt-II (A, B, C) and Cfcdt-III (A, B, C)] were prepared by PCR using corresponding template DNA of the recombinant E. coli (Cfcdt-I+), E. coli (Cfcdt-II+) and E.coli (Cfcdt-III+) strains and gene specific primer set, respectively, as described in Supplementary Table 4. Each PCR product was purified from agarose gel using the Wizard SV Gel and PCR clean-up system (Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA), and used as a DNA probe in colony hybridization assay. Colony hybridization assay was conducted by using nine 32P-labelled gene probes, i.e., A, B and C genes of each Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III gene clusters, as described previously with some modifications [14, 37]. Briefly, each cdt subunit gene (A, B or C) of Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III (total nine probes) was radiolabeled with 32P-dCTP by the random priming method using Megaprime DNA Labelling System (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Buckinghamshire, UK) and [α-32P]-dCTP (370 MBq/Ml) (PerkinElmer Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). For hybridization experiment, 137 C. fetus strains with C. fetus strain ATCC27374T (used as a positive control) and C. jejuni strain 81–176 (used as a negative control) were grown on a nitrocellulose membrane (Schleicher and Schuell BioScience GmbH, Dassel, Germany) overlaid on a blood agar plate with proper culture conditions as described before for 24 hr. While the recombinant E. coli (Cfcdt-I+), E. coli (Cfcdt-II+) and E. coli (Cfcdt-III+) strains were used as positive controls, the strain JM109 carrying the empty vector pET28a (+) [JM109 (pET28a)] served as a negative control and these strains were similarly grown on nitrocellulose membranes overlaid on L-agar plate containing 30 µg/mL of kanamycin. Bacterial colonies grown on nitrocellulose membranes were lysed, and their genomic DNAs were denatured by in situ alkaline lysis method followed by fixation of DNAs on the membrane by UV cross-linking with a UV cross-linker (CX-2000; UVP LLC, Upland, CA, USA). The processed nitrocellulose membranes were hybridized with the target gene probes under high stringency conditions [37], and radioactivity was visualized by using a biomolecular imager (Model Typhon FLA 7000, GE Healthcare UK Ltd., Chalfont, UK).

Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)

The clonality of the 137 C. fetus strains was assessed by PFGE of SmaI (Takara Bio Inc.) digested genomic DNAs using the Pulse Net USA protocol (https://www.cdc.gov/pulsenet/pathogens/pfge.html). In brief, each C. fetus strain was grown on blood agar (Oxoid) followed by harvesting of bacterial cells from agar plate, suspending them in phosphate-buffered saline [PBS(−); pH 7.4] and embedding the cell suspension into 1% Seakem gold agarose plugs (Lonza Rockland, Inc., ME, USA). Next, bacterial cells in agarose plugs were lysed by lysis buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM EDTA] supplemented with 0.5 mg/mL proteinase K (Sigma) and 1% sarcosine (Sigma) at 54°C for 30 min with shaking (160 rpm). Intact genomic DNA of campylobacters present in agarose plugs was digested with 40 U of SmaI enzyme in T buffer (Takara Bio Inc.) at 25°C for 2 hr followed by PFGE of digested DNA fragments on 1% pulsed-field-certified agarose gel (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA) in 0.5X TBE (45 mM Tris, 45 mM boric acid, 1 mM EDTA) buffer using a CHEF Mapper (Bio-Rad) at 20–400 kb size range for 26 hr 56 min. After electrophoretic run, the agarose gel was stained with ethidium bromide (1 μg/mL) for 30 min and destained with distilled water twice for 15 min each time, and the gel image was captured using a ChemiDoc system (Bio-Rad). The PFGE fingerprints were analyzed with BioNumerics Version 8 (settings: Dice, UPGMA with 0.5% optimization and 0.5% band tolerance) to determine the genetic relationship among the C. fetus strains.

Whole genome sequencing of C. fetus strains

Whole genome sequences of 38 representative C. fetus strains (selected on the basis of their PFGE pulsotypes) were determined by using short-read sequencing technique. Bacterial strains were grown on 5% horse blood agar (Oxoid) as mentioned above. Genomic DNA from each strain was purified using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to manufacturer’s instructions. DNA libraries were prepared using Illumina DNA Pre kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with the IDT for Illumina DNA/RNA UD Indexes SetA essentially as described by the the manufacturer. Bacterial whole genome sequencing was performed in Illumina NextSeq 1,000 platform using the NextSeq 1,000/2,000 P2 kit (Illumina). After sequencing, the reads were de novo assembled into contigs using the CLC Genomics Workbench (QIAGEN). Assembled contigs were subjected to Local BLAST with the sequences of three cdt genes of the reference strain ATCC27374T available in the GenBank (NCTC10842; Accession No. LS483431.1) and subjected to alignment to compare the cdt gene sequences among the reference and the 38 C. fetus representative strains.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) of allelic sequences were extracted from WGS data using the SeqSphere+ (Ridom, Münster, Germany) software and the sequence types (STs) were assigned based on seven housekeeping gene sequences, namely, aspA, glnA, gltA, glyA, pgm, tkt and uncA [31]. The new alleles and profiles generated in this study were submitted to the Campylobacter MLST database (https://pubmlst.org/) and assigned as ST-81.

Nucleotide sequence accession numbers

Nucleotide sequences of three cdt gene clusters in 38 C. fetus strains analyzed in this study have been registered in the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) with the accession numbers LC815639 to LC815752, and LC819631 to LC819633.

RESULTS

Distribution of three cdt gene clusters in C. fetus

To check whether three Cfcdt gene clusters are present in the genomes of 137 C. fetus strains used in this study, a colony hybridization assay using nine cdt gene probes specific to each cdt subunit gene (A, B or C) of the clusters was conducted. In colony hybridization assay, C. jejuni strain 81–176 and E. coli strain JM109 carrying the empty vector pET28a (+) used as negative controls did not react with any of the nine probes, whereas the reference C. fetus strain ATCC27374T used as a positive control reacted with each of the nine probes. Furthermore, the Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II or Cfcdt-III derived A, B and C gene probes hybridized only with the recombinant E. coli (Cfcdt-I+), E. coli (Cfcdt-II+) or E. coli (Cfcdt-III+) strains, which strongly suggests that the gene probes are highly cluster specific or in other words there are no cross-reactions between the gene probes and their target genes. Indeed, all the gene probes hybridized with 137 C. fetus strains used in this study, which indicates that the strains likely carry three types of Cfcdt gene clusters.

PFGE of C. fetus strains

To assess the clonal relationship among 137 strains of C. fetus of human and cattle origin, we performed PFGE of SmaI digested genomes of these strains. Such analysis grouped all these C. fetus strains into 38 pulsotypes based on an 85% similarity (Fig. 1). Among these 38 typed strains, 8 of them were obtained from human and the rest were isolated from cattle. Interestingly, some human and cattle isolates showed very similar pulsotypes (Fig. 1). The representative 38 pulsotyped C. fetus strains were used for further experimentation.

Analysis of three cdt gene clusters of C. fetus

In silico analysis of the Cfcdt gene sequences of the C. fetus reference strain ATCC27374T revealed that the sizes of the Cfcdt-IA, Cfcdt-IB and Cfcdt-IC genes are 822, 801 and 549 bp, respectively, whereas those of Cfcdt-IIA, Cfcdt-IIB, Cfcdt-IIC, Cfcdt-IIIA, Cfcdt-IIIB and Cfcdt-IIIC are 618, 795, 552, 702, 795 and 552 bp, respectively. Whole genome sequencing of 38 representative C. fetus strains revealed that the sizes of Cfcdt-IA (822 bp), Cfcdt-IB (801 bp), Cfcdt-IIC (552 bp), Cfcdt-IIIA (702 bp), and Cfcdt-IIIC (552 bp) genes are identical to that of C. fetus reference strain ATCC27374T. However, the sizes of genes of some of the 38 pulsotyped C. fetus strains showed variations when compared with that of the Cfcdt-IC (549 bp), Cfcdt-IIA (618 bp), Cfcdt-IIB (795 bp), and Cfcdt-IIIB (795 bp) genes of the reference C. fetus strain ATCC27374T. Similarity of the genes among three different Cfcdt gene clusters varied from 33.8 to 45.3% in the case of CfcdtA, 55.1 to 70.3% for CfcdtB and 26.4 to 42.9% for the gene CfcdtC. Interestingly, Cfcdt-IA gene was found to be most similar to that of C. coli cdtA (CccdtA) gene (50.1%) while Cfcdt-IIA and Cfcdt-IIIA genes showed the highest similarity with the C. hyointestinalis cdt-IA (Chcdt-IA) gene (54.4% and 58.4%, respectively) (Table 1A). On the other hand, Cfcdt-IB gene showed the highest similarity with the C. jejuni cdtB (CjcdtB) gene (61.0%) while Cfcdt-IIB and Cfcdt-IIIB genes were most similar to each other (70.3%) and to a lesser degree with the C. hyointestinalis cdt-IB (Chcdt-IB) gene (63.7% and 67.0%, respectively) (Table 1B). Furthermore, Cfcdt-IC gene showed the highest similarity with the C. helveticus cdtC (ChelcdtC) gene (53.8%) while Cfcdt-IIC and Cfcdt-IIIC genes were most similar to C. hyointestinalis cdt-IC (Chcdt-IC) gene (46.6% and 52.8%, respectively) (Table 1C). Mutations such as substitution, insertion or deletion were detected in the Cfcdt gene clusters, resulting in frameshift and truncation of CDT protein in some cases (Table 2, Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 5).

Analysis of MLST

Sequence type (ST) of 38 representative C. fetus strains were also determined by whole-genome sequencing. Thirty-five C. fetus strains were identified to belong in five STs (2, 3, 5, 6 and 20) and remaining three strains were assigned to a novel ST, ST-81 (Supplementary Fig. 1). As shown in Table 2, one strain was assigned to ST-2 (2.6%), 16 to ST-3 (42.1%), three to ST-5 (7.9%), 14 to ST-6 (36.8%), one to ST-20 (2.6%), and three to ST-81 (7.9%). Among them, 8 strains isolated from human were typed to ST-6 (n=7) and ST-20 (n=1), while 30 strains isolated from cattle were typed to ST-2 (n=3), ST-3 (n=16), ST-5 (n=3), ST-6 (n=7) and ST-81 (n=3). It should be noted that ST-6 included both human strains (n=7) and cattle strains (n=7). In addition, the allelic profile of ST-81 showed only one base substitution in the nucleotide sequence of the glyA gene compared to ST-2.

DISCUSSION

CDT is one of the well-characterized virulence factors in Campylobacter and so far, only one cdtABC operon has been identified in C. jejuni and C. coli [2, 17]. On the other hand, C. hyointestinalis, a species in the Campylobacter genus that is closely related to C. fetus [7], has been found to possess three types of cdt genes at the strain-dependent level, some strains have Chcdt-II genes [18], some strains possess both Chcdt-I and Chcdt-II [13, 18], and some strains harbor all Chcdt-I, Chcdt-II and Chcdt-III genes [13; unpublished]. In silico analysis of C. fetus type strain ATCC27374T indicated that there are also three cdt gene clusters in C. fetus. Therefore, in this study, we attempted to examine whether C. fetus ubiquitously possesses three cdt gene clusters by examining 137 C. fetus strains isolated from human patients and healthy cattle. Previously it has been reported that C. fetus genome carries single cdt gene cluster [2], which is confirmed in this study as Cfcdt-I. Moreover, two more different types of cdt gene clusters (Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III) have also been detected in all the C. fetus strains examined in this study, which strongly suggests that three distinct cdt gene clusters might ubiquitously be present in C. fetus.

Whole genome sequence analysis revealed that three Cfcdt gene clusters are present in distinct locations in the C. fetus genome. Cfcdt-I gene cluster (spanning the genomic region 26,799–28,977) has been found to be located separately from other two cdt gene clusters Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III identified in this study (Fig. 2). Interestingly, Cfcdt-II (spanning the region 949,581–951,563) and Cfcdt-III (spanning the region 951,850–953,915) are positioned in an upstream-downstream orientation relative to each other. In addition, the analyzed C. fetus genomes showed identical gap of 287 bp between Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III gene clusters. Furthermore, nucleotide sequence analysis of 38 C. fetus strains examined in this study showed presence of no mobile genetic element within the up or downstream of any of the Cfcdt gene clusters (Supplementary Table 5), which suggests that the cdt genes in C. fetus are conserved virulence genes and might contribute to survival of C. fetus in the host. However, GC contents of Cfcdt-I, Cfcdt-II and Cfcdt-III gene-clusters are 38.0, 38.2 and 36.1%, respectively, while whole genome sequence analysis indicated that the GC content of C. fetus is 33.5%, slightly less than any of the three Cfcdt gene clusters I-III (data not shown). Thus, it may be possible that these Cfcdt gene clusters may be acquired by C. fetus from other bacteria, which needs further confirmation. Moreover, DNA sequences of three Cfcdt gene clusters of all C. fetus strains showed 99% similarities among themselves (Supplementary Table 6) suggesting that the cdt gene clusters present in C. fetus genomes are highly conserved. However, nucleotide sequence similarity among three cdt subunit genes, i.e., A, B and C in C. fetus is not always high. Only Cfcdt-IIB and Cfcdt-IIIB are most similar with each other (Table 2). In other cases, Cfcdt-I to -III subunit genes showed similarity with that of C. helveticus, C. hyointerstinalis, C. jejuni or C. coli (Table 1, Fig. 3). However, phylogenetic analysis using the sequence of 16S rDNA, rpoB, and gyrA genes always indicated closeness of C. fetus to C. hyointestinalis and distances from C. jejuni, C. coli, C. upsaliensis or C. helveticus (Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, several studies suggest that gene evolution in Campylobacter species can be influenced by both horizontal gene transfer and host-specific selective pressures [7, 9, 11]. Specifically, the divergence in virulence genes might be driven by adaptation to different host environments [9], as has been observed in other bacterial pathogens [21]. Therefore, we hypothesize that cdt genes in Campylobacter might have independently evolved from each Campylobacter species under unique selective pressure.

To compare the clonal relationship between C. fetus strains isolated from human and cattle, PFGE and MLST analyses were performed. A total of six STs were identified among 38 strains of C. fetus. Human isolates were mostly, seven out of eight, typed as ST-6, while half of the cattle isolates, 16 out of 30, belonged to ST-3 (Table 2). Distribution of ST between human and cattle strains is significantly different (Fisher’s Exact Test, P<0.05), suggesting that there may be a host-specific pattern in the distribution of certain ST with C. fetus populations. Previous research showed that specific STs in C. jejuni were frequently associated with a particular host species [9]. It should be noted that C. fetus isolated from humans were more frequently documented as ST-6 [5, 6, 8, 10, 15, 20, 27, 31, 35], establishing that ST-6 strain is probably the most important type of C. fetus involved in human infections. Indeed, as shown in Fig. 1, strains belonging to ST-6 isolated from humans and cattle showed very similar PFGE patterns, indicating that C. fetus ST-6 associated with cattle may be the source of infection in humans and they might be more virulent than other STs. Future studies regarding characterization of the biological activity of three CfCDTs are going on and may provide the relation between ST and CfCDTs. Recently, several reports documented the infection of C. fetus by ingesting raw food [12, 23, 24, 26] or contact with livestock directly [22, 32], further confirming that healthy cattle could be a potential source of C. fetus infections in humans, however, it needs further evidence.

To ascertain the variations among three Cfcdt gene clusters in 38 strains, the Cfcdt gene sequences were further analyzed for STs. It was found that several substitutions have occurred in the cdt gene sequences of these strains at the nucleotide sequence level. However, substituted bases and their positions are conserved in the same ST except for some of the ST-3 strains. Furthermore, a single base deletion has been detected in Cfcdt-IC of ST-3 (n=1), Cfcdt-IIA of ST-5 (n=3) and Cfcdt-IIIB of ST-2 (n=1) as well as of ST-81 (n=3). On the other hand, a single base insertion has also been found in the Cfcdt-IIB gene of ST-6 (n=14). The deletion or insertion of nucleotide has also happened at the same locus in C. fetus strains belonging to same ST. Deletion or insertion of bases detected in these Cfcdt genes might result translation of functionally defective truncated subunits, which needs confirmation. Importantly, C. fetus strains belonging to ST-3 (1 strain out of 16) and ST-20 carried three complete sets of Cfcdt gene clusters, whereas strains of ST-2, ST-5, ST-6, ST-81, and one of the ST-3 type have been found to carry different truncated CfCDT subunits, suggesting that there might have a relationship between STs and Cfcdt gene sequences. CdtB is an enzymatically active subunit of the CDT holotoxin [36]. In our study, however, C. fetus strains typed as ST-6 possess a truncated CfCdt-IIB subunit, indicating CfCDT-II may not be toxic to mammalian cells. Further studies are required regarding biological activities and immunological properties of each of the Cfcdt gene products of C. fetus strains to understand clearly about the role of the CDT toxin in pathogenesis in humans as well as in animals.

In summary, this is the first extensive analyses of distribution and nucleotide sequences of three cdt gene clusters carrying C. fetus strains isolated from human patients and healthy cattle. Our analysis further indicates that although the C. fetus strains carry three distinct cdt gene clusters in their genomes, they are not highly similar to each other rather they are closely similar to that of other Campylobacter species. Lastly, our study suggests that the C. fetus ST-6 type might be predominantly associated with human diseases, which warrants further investigations for confirmation.
