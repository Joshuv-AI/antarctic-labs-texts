High-titer antinuclear antibodies are associated with severe immune-related toxicity and exploratory survival outcomes in advanced gastric cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Wanrong Liu, Hui Tang, Ningning Li, Wei Qiu, Xiaoyuan Li, Lin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinicians often screen for autoantibodies before initiating immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in advanced gastric cancer (GC), yet the utility of organ-specific panels remains unclear.ObjectivesTo evaluate the association of baseline antinuclear antibody (ANA) titers and organ-specific autoantibodies with severe immune-related adverse events (irAEs) and clinical outcomes in patients with advanced GC receiving ICIs.DesignSingle-center retrospective cohort study.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 244 patients with advanced GC treated with ICIs. Baseline ANA titers and organ-specific antibodies, including Ro52, thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb), and thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb), were systematically evaluated. Associations with grade 3–4 irAEs were assessed using Firth’s penalized logistic regression. Progression-free survival (PFS) was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods, Cox proportional hazards regression, subgroup analyses, and propensity score matching.ResultsThe prevalence of organ-specific antibodies was low (<7%) and failed to predict toxicity. In contrast, high-titer ANA (≥1:160, 17.6% of patients) predicted grade 3–4 irAEs (OR = 7.98, 95% CI: 3.35–19.5; p < 0.001). Although the high-titer ANA group included a numerically higher proportion of PD-L1-negative tumors, exploratory survival analyses showed longer PFS in patients with high-titer ANA in univariable analysis (p = 0.038), whereas the association was attenuated after multivariable adjustment (adjusted HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.35–1.01, p = 0.056) and remained borderline after propensity score matching (p = 0.049).ConclusionsHigh-titer ANA was associated with an increased risk of severe irAEs in patients with advanced GC receiving ICIs. Its association with longer PFS was exploratory and requires prospective validation. These findings suggest that ANA titers may help baseline toxicity risk stratification, whereas routine screening for low-yield organ-specific autoantibodies may offer limited clinical utility in unselected patients.Keywords: antinuclear antibody, immune-related adverse events, immune checkpoint inhibitors, gastric cancer, autoimmunity

1. Introduction

Advanced gastric cancer (GC) is a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. 1 Recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), represented by PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, have become the standard of care for advanced GC, significantly improving survival outcomes.2,3 However, while ICIs activate anti-tumor immunity, they also disrupt immune tolerance, leading to immune-related adverse events (irAEs). 4 Although high-grade (grade 3–4) irAEs are relatively rare, they can lead to treatment discontinuation, permanent organ damage, or even death. 5 Therefore, identifying patients at high risk for severe toxicity before treatment is clinically critical for developing personalized monitoring and management strategies.

Given the clinical similarities between irAEs and autoimmune diseases, clinicians often empirically screen for a broad range of autoantibodies, including the antinuclear antibody (ANA) spectrum and organ-specific antibodies (e.g., anti-thyroid antibodies), before ICI treatment to predict specific organ toxicities. 6 However, the health economic value and clinical utility of this “broad screening” strategy in gastric cancer remain unclear. On one hand, existing studies on the predictive value of ANA are controversial; some studies only focus on qualitative ANA status (positive/negative) while ignoring that different titers may represent distinct immunological backgrounds.7,8

On the other hand, ICI-induced organ damage (such as thyroiditis) is thought to be primarily mediated by T cells. Its pathogenesis may differ from classical antibody-mediated autoimmune diseases, questioning the necessity of routine organ-specific antibody testing. 9 In clinical practice, extensive autoantibody screening without evidence not only increases medical costs but may also lead to misjudgment of toxicity risks.

To address these clinical uncertainties, we systematically evaluated the clinical utility of baseline broad-spectrum autoantibodies (ANA titers) and organ-specific antibodies (Ro52, TPOAb, TgAb) in a real-world cohort of 244 advanced GC patients treated with ICIs. We aimed to determine which screening strategy more accurately predicts severe toxicity risk and survival outcomes. Our goal is to provide evidence to optimize clinical resource allocation, distinguish “high-risk immune backgrounds” from “low-value routine screening,” and explore the feasibility of a risk-stratified autoantibody testing strategy.

2. Patients and methods

2.1. Study design and patient population

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cohort studies 10 (Supplementary File 1). We enrolled patients with advanced or metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma who received ICI treatment at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) between December 2020 and December 2024. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of PUMCH (Approval No. S-K2098) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature and anonymized data, informed consent was waived.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) Histologically confirmed advanced or metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma; (2) Received at least two cycles of anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 therapy, administered as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy and/or targeted therapy; (3) At least one measurable target lesion per RECIST 1.1 criteria; (4) Available baseline serum ANA and organ-specific autoantibody results (including Ro-52, TPOAb, TgAb) before the first ICI dose.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) Pre-existing autoimmune diseases requiring long-term corticosteroids (>10 mg/day prednisone equivalent); (2) Active infection or severe inflammatory state documented before treatment; (3) ECOG performance status≥2.

2.2. Autoantibody detection and clinical assessment

All serum samples were collected within 7 days prior to the first ICI treatment. ANA titers were measured by indirect immunofluorescence assay (IIF) using HEp-2 cells as the substrate. The ANA profile was detected by line immunoassay, identifying multiple autoantibodies including anti-Ro52, SSA, SSB, dsDNA, and anti-thyroid antibodies. Patients were classified into three groups based on baseline ANA titers: ANA negative, ANA 1:80 (low titer), and ANA ≥1:160 (high titer). The cutoff of ≥1:160 was selected because ANA titers at this level are commonly considered more clinically meaningful in autoimmune serology than low-titer ANA positivity, which may occur in healthy individuals or reflect nonspecific background autoimmunity. In addition, this threshold provided a practical separation between low-titer ANA positivity and a higher-titer autoimmune background for risk-stratified analysis.

Baseline clinical characteristics (age, gender, ECOG score, metastatic sites, PD-L1 CPS, MMR status, etc.) were extracted from electronic medical records.

Treatment-related variables, including ICI treatment category, chemotherapy-containing regimen, targeted therapy use, ICI agent, and treatment line, were extracted and compared across ANA titer groups.

2.3. Definition of clinical outcomes

Tumor response was assessed according to RECIST 1.1. 11 Overall Survival (OS) was defined as the time from the first ICI dose to death from any cause or the last follow-up. Progression-Free Survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the first ICI dose to radiologically confirmed disease progression or death. The median follow-up duration was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method.

Patients were included in the survival analysis if both PFS time and PFS event status were available. Patients without valid survival follow-up information were excluded from survival analyses but retained in baseline and toxicity analyses. To assess potential selection bias, baseline characteristics were compared between patients included in and excluded from the survival analysis. Assessment and management of all irAEs followed the institutional standard of care, based on CTCAE v5.0 and relevant guidelines. This study focused on high-grade irAEs, defined as grade 3 or higher.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using Pearson’s chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test for sparse contingency tables larger than 2 × 2, as appropriate.

Toxicity Prediction Analysis: Given the low incidence of high-grade irAEs (rare events), we used Firth’s penalized logistic regression to reduce bias associated with standard logistic regression in small samples. We analyzed the association between baseline ANA status and high-grade irAEs, calculating odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Because the number of grade 3–4 irAE events was limited, the primary multivariable Firth logistic regression model was kept parsimonious and adjusted for age and ECOG performance status. To assess potential confounding by treatment-related factors, sensitivity analyses were performed by additionally adjusting for chemotherapy-containing ICI regimen, treatment category, and treatment line.

Survival Analysis: Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between ANA titer groups were compared using the Log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate associations with OS and PFS, calculating hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate the robustness of the association between high-titer ANA and PFS, exploratory subgroup analyses were performed according to age (<65 versus ≥65 years), sex, ECOG performance status, liver metastasis, PD-L1 CPS, and chemotherapy-containing ICI regimen. Univariable Cox regression models were used to estimate the association between high-titer ANA and PFS within each subgroup, and interaction terms between high-titer ANA and each subgroup-defining variable were evaluated using Cox regression models. Patients with missing or unknown values for the subgroup-defining variable were excluded from the corresponding subgroup analysis.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using nearest-neighbor matching at a 1:2 ratio with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations. Matching covariates included age, sex, ECOG performance status, liver metastasis, PD-L1 CPS, and MSI/MMR status. Missing or unknown PD-L1 CPS and MSI/MMR values were retained as missingness-indicator categories in the PSM and multivariable Cox regression analyses to preserve sample size; these categories were not interpreted as biological subgroups. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD <0.1 considered good balance.

Interaction analyses were performed to evaluate whether chemotherapy-containing ICI treatment modified the association of high-titer ANA with grade 3–4 irAEs or PFS, and PFS subgroup analyses were performed according to chemotherapy-containing ICI treatment.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics by ANA titer

A total of 244 patients were included. Based on baseline ANA titers, patients were divided into three groups: ANA ≥1:160 (43 cases, 17.6%), ANA 1:80 (72 cases, 29.5%), and ANA negative (129 cases, 52.9%).

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. There were no significant differences in age, gender, primary tumor location, histology, liver metastasis, HER2 status, MSI/MMR status, or ECOG score among the three groups. Notably, Ro52 positivity differed significantly across ANA titer groups (p = 0.023), whereas PD-L1 CPS distribution did not differ significantly across ANA titer groups (p = 0.058), although PD-L1 CPS <1 tumors were numerically more frequent in the high-titer ANA group. Other autoantibodies, including TPOAb and TgAb, showed no significant differences.

Treatment-related variables were additionally compared across ANA titer groups (Supplementary Table S4). Overall, 22 patients received ICI monotherapy, 193 received ICI plus chemotherapy, 26 received ICI plus chemotherapy and targeted therapy, and 3 received ICI plus targeted therapy. The distribution of treatment category did not differ significantly across ANA titer groups (p = 0.798). 3.2. Association between high-titer ANA and high-grade irAEs

The incidence of any-grade irAEs in this cohort was 48.8%, and the incidence of grade 3–4 irAEs was 11.1%.

Using Firth’s penalized logistic regression, we evaluated the relationship between baseline ANA and high-grade irAEs. In univariable analysis, patients with ANA ≥1:160 had a significantly increased risk of grade 3–4 irAEs compared to those with ANA <1:160 (OR = 6.86, 95% CI: 3.00–16.0, p < 0.001).

In multivariable analysis, adjusting for age and ECOG score, ANA ≥1:160 remained associated with grade 3–4 irAEs (adjusted OR = 7.98, 95% CI: 3.35–19.5, p < 0.001). Additionally, patients with ECOG score 1 had a higher risk of high-grade irAEs compared to ECOG 0 (OR = 2.74, 95% CI: 1.04–8.54, p = 0.041), while age was not significantly associated (Table 2 and Figure 1(a)).

In univariable Firth logistic regression analyses, none of the evaluated organ-specific autoantibodies was significantly associated with grade 3–4 irAEs (Ro52: p = 0.935; TPOAb: p = 0.716; TgAb: p = 0.469; Supplementary Table S2).

The organ-specific profiles of grade 3–4 irAEs are summarized in Supplementary Table S5. Among the 27 patients who experienced grade 3–4 irAEs, the most common phenotypes were pneumonitis (n = 6, 22.2%), dermatitis (n = 5, 18.5%), hepatitis (n = 5, 18.5%), colitis (n = 4, 14.8%), cystitis (n = 3, 11.1%), thyroiditis (n = 3, 11.1%), and myocarditis (n = 1, 3.7%). When stratified by ANA status, the overall distribution of grade 3–4 irAE phenotypes did not differ significantly between the ANA ≥1:160 and ANA <1:160 groups (Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test, p = 0.480; Supplementary Table S5). Because of the limited number of events, phenotype-specific statistical comparisons were not performed.

To address potential treatment-related confounding, we performed additional sensitivity analyses incorporating treatment regimen and treatment line (Supplementary Table S8). The association between high-titer ANA and grade 3–4 irAEs remained robust after additional adjustment for chemotherapy-containing ICI regimen (adjusted OR = 7.78, 95% CI: 3.27–19.05, p < 0.001), treatment category (adjusted OR = 8.08, 95% CI: 3.38–19.96, p < 0.001), and treatment line (adjusted OR = 8.17, 95% CI: 3.40–20.30, p < 0.001). Similar results were observed in an exploratory model adjusting for both chemotherapy-containing regimen and treatment line (adjusted OR = 7.76, 95% CI: 3.23–19.23, p < 0.001).

3.3. Relationship between ANA titer and irAE severity

We observed a significant difference in irAE severity distribution across ANA titer groups. The proportion of grade 3–4 irAEs was significantly higher in patients with ANA ≥1:160 compared to the ANA negative and ANA 1:80 groups, suggesting a potential trend toward greater irAE severity across increasing ANA titer categories (Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test, p < 0.001) (Figure 1(b)).

3.4. Association between ANA status and any-grade irAEs

In contrast, baseline ANA status was not significantly associated with the overall incidence of any-grade irAEs. ANA status did not predict the occurrence of any-grade irAEs regardless of whether it was analyzed as a categorical or binary variable (Supplementary Table S1).

3.5. Survival outcomes by ANA titer

Tumor response was evaluable in 238 patients. The overall ORR and DCR were 29.4% and 95.0%, respectively. ORR did not differ significantly across the ANA-negative, ANA 1:80, and ANA ≥1:160 groups (29.1%, 30.9%, and 27.9%, respectively; p = 0.941). DCR was also comparable across groups (93.7%, 97.1%, and 95.3%, respectively; p = 0.674). The distribution of best response categories was not significantly different across ANA titer groups (p = 0.887).

Of the 244 patients, 215 had valid PFS follow-up information and were included in the PFS analysis, whereas 29 were excluded because of incomplete PFS follow-up data. For the OS analysis, 210 patients had available OS follow-up information, whereas 34 were excluded because of incomplete OS follow-up data. Among the 210 patients included in the OS analysis, the median follow-up duration estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method was 7.9 months. These excluded patients were retained in baseline and toxicity analyses. Baseline characteristics were compared between patients included in and excluded from the survival analysis (Supplementary Table S7). Compared with included patients, excluded patients were more frequently male, had poorer ECOG performance status, and were more likely to have liver metastasis. Differences were also observed in PD-L1 CPS among known cases and in MSI/MMR missingness, whereas ANA titer group, treatment category, and treatment line did not differ significantly between included and excluded patients. These findings indicate that potential selection bias related to incomplete follow-up could not be excluded.

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with baseline ANA ≥1:160 had significantly better Progression-Free Survival (PFS) than those with ANA <1:160 (Figure 2(a)). The median PFS was 8.0 months in the ANA ≥1:160 group versus 5.1 months in the ANA <1:160 group (Log-rank p = 0.035). For Overall Survival (OS), the ANA ≥1:160 group showed a trend toward prolonged survival (9.9 months vs. 8.5 months), but the difference was not statistically significant (Log-rank p = 0.163) (Figure 2(b)).

To evaluate the association between ANA titers and PFS after adjustment for key baseline factors, we performed multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression. After adjusting for potential confounders including age, ECOG score, liver metastasis, MSI status, PD-L1 expression, chemotherapy-containing ICI regimen, and treatment line, the association between high-titer ANA and longer PFS remained borderline and did not reach conventional statistical significance (adjusted HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.35–1.01, p = 0.056; Table 3). ECOG performance status remained significantly associated with PFS, whereas PD-L1 CPS, MSI/MMR status, and liver metastasis were not significantly associated with PFS in the multivariable model (Table 3).

We further explored whether the association between high-titer ANA and clinical outcomes differed according to chemotherapy-containing ICI treatment. No significant interaction was observed between high-titer ANA and chemotherapy-containing ICI treatment for grade 3–4 irAEs (p for interaction = 0.364) or PFS (p for interaction = 0.157). In the chemotherapy-containing subgroup, high-titer ANA was associated with longer PFS (HR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.28–0.90, p = 0.020), whereas no significant association was observed in the non-chemotherapy-containing subgroup (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 0.41–4.56, p = 0.609). However, because the interaction test was not statistically significant and the non-chemotherapy-containing subgroup was small (n = 24), these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

3.6. Sensitivity analysis based on propensity score matching

To minimize selection bias and assess the robustness of the association between ANA titers and PFS, we performed 1:2 propensity score matching (PSM). After matching, 124 patients were included (42 high-titer, 82 low-titer). Baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups (SMD < 0.1, Figure S1 and Table S3). Kaplan–Meier analysis of the matched cohort showed a borderline association between high-titer ANA and longer PFS (log-rank p = 0.049, Figure 4). Together with the attenuated multivariable Cox result, this borderline PSM finding supports interpreting the association between high-titer ANA and PFS as exploratory rather than definitive evidence of an independent prognostic or predictive biomarker.

Of the 124 matched patients, 110 patients with complete PFS follow-up information were included in the Kaplan–Meier analysis.

4. Discussion

In this real-world advanced gastric cancer cohort, we systematically compared the clinical value of “broad-spectrum” autoantibodies (ANA titers) versus “organ-specific” antibodies (Ro52, TPOAb, TgAb) in immunotherapy risk stratification. Our findings provide new insights into the clinical utility of “comprehensive screening”: our results indicate that organ-specific antibody panels appeared to have limited predictive value in unselected populations. In contrast, the inexpensive and basic ANA titer (especially ≥1:160) emerged as a potential biomarker for predicting severe toxicity risk and potential therapeutic benefit, whereas its association with longer PFS was attenuated after multivariable adjustment and should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Our findings suggest that the routine use of the evaluated organ-specific autoantibodies for predicting severe irAEs may have limited value in unselected patients with gastric cancer. In this cohort, the positive rates for Ro52, TPOAb, and TgAb were low (<7%) and were not significantly associated with specific organ toxicities (such as myocarditis or thyroiditis) or overall irAE incidence. This “negative result” is biologically plausible. Previous mechanistic studies suggest that ICI-induced organ damage is primarily driven by direct infiltration and destruction of normal tissues by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD8+ T cells), rather than classical antibody-mediated humoral immune responses.9,12 Therefore, specific autoantibodies in baseline serum may merely reflect the patient’s underlying autoimmune background rather than being direct effectors of ICI toxicity. 13 From a health economics perspective, routine testing of these low-yield markers without clinical indications may not be cost-effective. 14

In contrast, ANA titers demonstrated unique predictive value. We found that simple ANA “positivity” (including low titer 1:80) could not effectively discriminate risk; whereas higher titers (≥1:160) were associated with grade 3–4 irAEs. This cutoff is also clinically interpretable. Previous studies have shown that low-titer ANA positivity is relatively common in healthy individuals, whereas ANA positivity at 1:160 occurs in only a small minority of healthy subjects. International laboratory recommendations also commonly use 1:160 as a clinically meaningful screening cutoff for ANA IIF on HEp-2 cells in adults.15,16 This supports the clinical rationale for distinguishing low-titer ANA positivity from high-titer ANA, because low ANA titers may represent nonspecific background autoimmunity, while higher titers are more likely to reflect a clinically relevant autoimmune predisposition. This suggests that ANA in this context is not a binary diagnostic marker, but a continuous variable reflecting the body’s “Immunological Tone.” High-titer ANA may signal a state of systemic immune susceptibility with a lower threshold for immune tolerance. When ICIs release the immune brakes, this susceptibility can easily translate into a fierce “cytokine storm” or multi-organ autoimmune attack, leading to severe toxicity.6,8 We observed a stepwise increase in the proportion of grade 3–4 irAEs across increasing ANA titer categories, although this pattern should be interpreted cautiously given the observational design and limited number of severe events.

We observed longer PFS in the high-titer ANA group in unadjusted analyses, but this association remained borderline after multivariable adjustment incorporating clinically relevant baseline and treatment-related variables. Therefore, the efficacy-related findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Notably, ANA titer was not associated with short-term tumor response endpoints, as ORR and DCR were comparable across ANA titer groups. Therefore, the exploratory association between high-titer ANA and PFS should not be interpreted as evidence that ANA directly predicts objective tumor shrinkage or disease control.

This finding aligns with the classical theory of “Toxicity-Efficacy Coupling”, 17 suggesting that baseline immune activation may underlie both enhanced antitumor responses and increased immune-related events.18,19 High-titer ANA may therefore reflect an immune-activated background associated with immunotherapy responsiveness, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Previous studies have implicated adaptive immune activation and tertiary lymphoid structures in immunotherapy responsiveness.20–23

Based on these findings, ANA titer may warrant further evaluation as a component of baseline toxicity risk assessment. High-titer ANA may identify patients who require closer clinical observation for severe irAEs; however, prospective studies are needed to determine whether ANA-guided monitoring improves clinical outcomes. The evaluated organ-specific autoantibodies showed low positivity rates and limited associations with severe toxicity in this cohort. Therefore, their incremental clinical value and cost-effectiveness as routine screening tests in unselected patients remain uncertain and require further investigation.

Although the high-titer ANA group showed longer PFS in exploratory analyses, this did not translate into a statistically significant OS benefit. The discrepancy between PFS and OS may be influenced by subsequent treatment and post-progression survival; however, detailed post-progression treatment data were not available in the present dataset. Therefore, this explanation remains speculative and has been acknowledged as a limitation. Additionally, the follow-up period was relatively limited; OS benefits may become clearer with longer follow-up.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective analysis, which may introduce selection bias and residual confounding. Second, we only analyzed baseline ANA status and did not assess dynamic changes in ANA titers during treatment. Third, although chemotherapy-containing ICI regimen and treatment line were incorporated into the multivariable PFS model, residual confounding related to heterogeneous treatment regimens and treatment selection cannot be excluded. Fourth, 29 patients were excluded from PFS analyses because of incomplete PFS follow-up information, and these patients differed from the survival-analysis cohort in several baseline characteristics, including sex, ECOG performance status, liver metastasis, PD-L1 CPS among known cases, and MSI/MMR missingness. Therefore, selection bias related to incomplete follow-up cannot be excluded. In addition, detailed post-progression treatment information, including later-line systemic therapy and post-progression survival, was not consistently available; therefore, the potential influence of subsequent therapy on OS could not be assessed. Finally, the association between high-titer ANA and PFS was attenuated after multivariable adjustment and did not reach conventional statistical significance, and the PD-L1 CPS <1 subgroup was small. Thus, the efficacy-related findings, particularly those in PD-L1-negative disease, should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Because several subgroup analyses included small numbers of patients, particularly the PD-L1 CPS <1 and non-chemotherapy-containing subgroups, the findings require validation in larger multicenter cohorts or independent external datasets.

5. Conclusion

In a real-world population of advanced gastric cancer patients treated with immunotherapy, high-titer ANA was associated with severe immune-related adverse events. Its association with longer progression-free survival was exploratory and should not be considered definitive evidence of an independent prognostic or predictive biomarker. Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate the clinical utility of ANA-guided risk stratification.
