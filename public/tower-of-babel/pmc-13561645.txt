Mitochondrial Proteostasis Links Diabetes and Sarcopenia: Cross-Scale Convergence from Experimental Models to Human Multi-Omics
Authors: Shibo Wei, Soyeon Shin, Dongryeol Ryu, Chang-Myung Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenia in type 2 diabetes mellitus is increasingly recognized as a mechanistic consequence of chronic metabolic stress rather than mere age-related comorbidity. This review synthesizes evidence demonstrating how insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, lipotoxicity, and inflammation converge on skeletal muscle mitochondrial proteostasis to drive progressive decline. We evaluate seven pathway modules—mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)/sirtuin (SIRT)-linked regulation, protein import, and the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt)—across an evidence map encompassing basic, clinical, and multi-omics studies. Dynamics and mitophagy represent mechanistically central quality-control nodes; their impairment permits dysfunctional organelle accumulation and promotes atrophic cascades. Direct evidence density, however, remains weighted toward oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial biogenesis. NAD+/SIRT-linked regulation, protein import fidelity, and UPRmt represent mechanistically upstream but comparatively underinvestigated signals. We propose a diabetes-centered framework where mitochondrial proteostasis failure mediates atrophy and reinforces insulin resistance via a self-amplifying feed-forward loop, supported by pathway responsiveness to coherent interventions. Human multi-omics data highlight network-level dysregulation rather than isolated defects, underscoring module-based biomarker strategies. Translationally, exercise remains the mechanistic cornerstone, while pathway-directed adjuncts—NAD+ precursor repletion, mitophagy modulators, and emerging pharmacotherapeutics—are warranted for patients with identifiable module-specific failure patterns.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is increasingly recognized as a condition that accelerates the decline of skeletal muscle mass and function beyond the trajectory expected from aging alone, with direct clinical consequences including frailty, mobility limitation, falls, and metabolic decompensation [1-4]. This cooccurrence is not incidental. In many patients, sarcopenia represents not simply parallel aging biology but a downstream consequence of persistent metabolic stress, operating through mechanisms that are distinct from, although partially overlapping with, those underlying normoglycemic muscle aging. A diabetes-centered mechanistic framework is therefore both timely and clinically warranted.

Clinically, sarcopenia is commonly operationalized using consensus frameworks such as the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) and the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), which emphasize reduced muscle strength, reduced muscle quantity or quality, and impaired physical performance [5,6]. The recently updated AWGS 2025 consensus further shifts the focus toward lifecourse muscle health, extends sarcopenia assessment to middle-aged adults, and simplifies the diagnostic algorithm by defining sarcopenia as concurrent low muscle mass and low muscle strength, while retaining physical performance as an outcome measure rather than a diagnostic criterion [7]. In this review, these criteria are used as a clinical anchor rather than as formal eligibility criteria for reclassifying all included studies. Accordingly, diabetic sarcopenia is discussed as a clinically measurable decline in muscle strength, muscle mass or quality, and physical performance occurring in the context of chronic diabetic metabolic stress. This framing links mechanistic findings from experimental, translational, and multi-omics studies to clinically relevant endpoints, including grip strength, gait speed, chair-stand performance, appendicular skeletal muscle mass, and muscle quality.

Regional evidence is particularly relevant because sarcopenia is common among Asian adults with T2DM and is associated with clinically relevant risk factors. A recent AWGS-2019–based systematic review and meta-analysis reported a substantial prevalence of confirmed sarcopenia among Asian patients with T2DM, supporting the need for regionally appropriate screening and functional assessment [8]. Korean nationwide data linking body-composition change to incident diabetes further support the need to integrate muscle mass, muscle quality, and functional endpoints into diabetes-focused risk assessment [9].

Classical investigations established skeletal muscle mitochondrial dysfunction as a hallmark of insulin resistance and T2DM, characterized by impaired oxidative capacity, altered substrate utilization, and reduced mitochondrial quality [10,11]. In parallel, the sarcopenia literature has identified mitochondrial stress and defective organelle quality control as core determinants of myofiber vulnerability and progressive functional decline [3,12]. Although these fields have historically evolved in parallel, accumulating evidence from studies of diabetic sarcopenia now integrates these perspectives, suggesting that mitochondrial quality-control failure—rather than isolated bioenergetic insufficiency—may represent a central pathogenic process linking chronic diabetic metabolic stress to progressive muscle loss [13-15].

This convergence has important mechanistic implications. Mitochondrial proteostasis—encompassing the coordinated regulation of organelle dynamics, mitophagy, biogenesis, respiratory coupling, and protein import and folding—functions as an active determinant of cellular adaptation rather than a passive downstream consequence of metabolic injury. Disruption of these quality-control systems within the chronic metabolic environment of T2DM promotes the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, amplifies proteolytic signaling, impairs contractile energetics, and increases myofiber susceptibility to atrophic stimuli in a manner not fully accounted for by bioenergetic deficits alone.

Importantly, the relationship between mitochondrial proteostasis impairment and diabetic muscle loss is bidirectional and self-reinforcing. Compromised mitochondrial quality in skeletal muscle exacerbates substrate inflexibility and impairs glucose disposal, thereby intensifying systemic insulin resistance and accelerating disease progression. This establishes a feed-forward loop that necessitates intervention at mechanistically defined nodes. This bidirectional relationship is supported by experimental and clinical evidence, including studies in exercise physiology and metabolic pharmacology demonstrating that targeted modulation of mitochondrial quality-control pathways yields concurrent improvements in muscle phenotype and systemic metabolic parameters, underscoring the translational tractability of these pathways [16,17].

This review is structured as a mechanism-driven and translationally oriented synthesis. Emphasis is placed on causal biology: the disruption of mitochondrial proteostasis by diabetic metabolic stress, downstream effects on muscle atrophy and contractile dysfunction, and identification of biologically plausible therapeutic targets within a diabetes-centered framework. To improve methodological transparency, we constructed a direct evidence-density map based on a module-based scoring of primary original studies relevant to diabetes-related skeletal muscle dysfunction, diabetic sarcopenia, or experimentally relevant muscle models. Reviews, meta-analyses, protocols, editorials, and background articles were excluded from recurrence-density scoring but retained for narrative synthesis where appropriate. Seven mitochondrial proteostasis modules were predefined before scoring: mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)/sirtuin (SIRT)-linked regulation, mitochondrial protein import, and the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt). Each eligible study was scored in a binary manner for each module, with one assigned when a module-specific marker, pathway component, or functional assay was directly measured in skeletal muscle tissue or muscle-derived models, and 0 when the module was not directly assessed. Direct evidence was defined as experimental measurement reported in the Results, rather than mention in the Introduction or Discussion; studies were not scored positively on the basis of general mitochondrial terminology, inferred dysfunction, oxidative stress, ultrastructural abnormalities, or non-muscle systemic biomarkers alone. Recurrence density was calculated as the number of eligible original studies directly assessing each module, rather than as an estimate of effect size or biological importance. Because this was a narrative mechanistic review rather than a systematic review, formal duplicate screening, independent reviewer adjudication, and risk-of-bias assessment were not performed. DIABETES-CENTERED CAUSAL AXIS FOR SARCOPENIA

The metabolic milieu of T2DM imposes sustained and convergent stress on skeletal muscle mitochondria through at least four interacting pressures: hyperglycemia-driven oxidative burden [1], insulin resistance–associated impairment of anabolic and mitochondrial biogenic signaling [2], lipid overload with accumulation of ceramides and diacylglycerols [18,19], and chronic low-grade inflammation mediated by circulating and locally produced cytokines [15,20]. Each of these stressors disrupts distinct components of mitochondrial proteostasis, including fission–fusion kinetics, selective autophagic clearance, protein import fidelity, and respiratory supercomplex integrity, while their concurrent activation within the diabetic muscle environment produces network-level perturbation that exceeds the compensatory capacity of individual quality-control systems [14]. The critical transition occurs when mitochondria shift from adaptive stress remodeling, in which transient activation of quality-control pathways restores organelle integrity, to a maladaptive state characterized by accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, sustained reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and aberrant activation of proteolytic and atrophic signaling pathways, including the ubiquitin-proteasome and autophagy-lysosome systems [15,21]. The consequence extends beyond reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) availability, encompassing a qualitative deterioration in mitochondrial signaling competence that may sensitize myofibers to catabolic programs. The organizational structure of this sequence is illustrated in Fig. 1, which presents convergent upstream stressors, a central proteostasis failure node, and downstream atrophic effectors as an integrated mechanistic axis rather than a linear cascade.

This pathogenic sequence extends beyond the level of the muscle fiber. Deterioration of mitochondrial quality in skeletal muscle impairs substrate oxidative flexibility and reduces insulin-stimulated glucose disposal, thereby amplifying systemic hyperinsulinemia, promoting ectopic lipid accumulation, and accelerating progression of the diabetic phenotype [2,10,11]. The resulting feed-forward architecture—wherein diabetic metabolic stress disrupts mitochondrial proteostasis, and proteostasis failure further reinforces systemic metabolic dysfunction— represents a defining structural feature of Fig. 1 with direct implications for disease staging and therapeutic targeting. This framework indicates that interventions directed at mitochondrial quality control may interrupt disease progression at a nodal point that is upstream of both sarcopenic and glycemic outcomes, rather than acting on an endpoint in isolation.

A potential causal role of mitochondrial proteostasis within this axis, beyond its function as a correlated biomarker, is supported by intervention responsiveness. This interpretation is supported by several intervention-based examples: exercise training in men with T2DM modified skeletal muscle sphingolipid metabolism and mitochondrial quality-control markers; D-pinitol-mediated heat shock protein family A member 1-like (HSPA1L)-Parkin mitophagy regulation and dynaminrelated protein 1 (DRP1)/PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)-linked mitophagy activation by ginsenoside compound K (CK) improved mitochondrial quality-control signaling together with muscle or metabolic phenotypes; and dapagliflozin-based intervention improved skeletal muscle mitochondrial quality control and insulin resistance in experimental T2DM models [13,17,22,23]. This bidirectional responsiveness— deterioration under diabetic stress and restoration under mechanistically coherent intervention—provides evidence consistent with mediation that extends beyond correlational associations and supports a causal framework for the pathwaylevel analysis presented in this review.

CORE MITOCHONDRIAL PROTEOSTASIS MODULES IN DIABETIC SARCOPENIA

Across eligible original studies with direct skeletal muscle or muscle-derived measurements, OXPHOS and mitochondrial biogenesis were the most frequently assessed modules, whereas mitochondrial dynamics showed intermediate recurrence (10/30) and mitophagy was less frequently measured (5/30). This distribution indicates that diabetes-related skeletal muscle mitochondrial research has historically been weighted toward respiratory capacity, electron transport chain (ETC)/OXPHOS content, ATP-linked readouts, and mitochondrial content or biogenic markers. Importantly, lower measurement recurrence should not be interpreted as lower biological relevance. Dynamics and mitophagy remain mechanistically central qualitycontrol nodes because disruption of fission–fusion balance, impairment of the PINK1–Parkin mitophagy axis, and failure of selective mitochondrial turnover are closely linked to dysfunctional organelle accumulation, oxidative stress, and activation of atrophic signaling in diabetic muscle models [13,22,24]. Human translational studies in T2DM similarly link mitochondrial quality-control remodeling in skeletal muscle to impaired insulin action and reduced metabolic flexibility, in agreement with preclinical findings [18,25].

Consistent with this distribution, mitochondrial biogenesis and OXPHOS represented the highest-density measurement layer in the direct evidence map, with recurrence densities of 19/30 and 27/30, respectively. This high recurrence reflects the long-standing emphasis of diabetes-focused muscle studies on mitochondrial content, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α)–mediated transcriptional programs, mitochondrial DNA copy number and proteomic output, and OXPHOS complexes—particularly complexes I and III. These alterations are associated with impaired insulin-stimulated glucose uptake and diminished sub-strate oxidative flexibility [2,26]. In sarcopenic muscle independent of diabetes, similar reductions in mitochondrial renewal capacity compromise contractile resilience and contribute to progressive functional decline [3,12,27]. These findings support an integrated model in which suppression of biogenesis and impairment of OXPHOS do not operate as independent lesions but interact with dynamics and mitophagy failure in a mutually reinforcing manner: impaired clearance increases the burden of dysfunctional organelles, reduced biogenesis limits replacement with functional organelles, and cumulative deterioration of the mitochondrial proteome sustains energetic and redox stress that drives atrophy [14,22,24,28]. Fig. 2 summarizes direct-measurement evidence density across mitochondrial proteostasis modules and identifies areas of both extensive characterization and under-investigation. The figure should therefore be interpreted as a measurement-frequency map rather than a biological priority ranking.

NAD+/SIRT-linked regulatory pathways, mitochondrial protein import, and the UPRmt occupied low recurrence strata within the direct evidence map, with recurrence densities of 4/30, 1/30, and 1/30, respectively. Their quantitative underrepresentation should not be conflated with mechanistic marginality. As shown in Fig. 2, this distribution more likely reflects gaps in current investigation rather than diminished biological relevance. Each of these modules occupies a regulatory position that is upstream of, or orthogonal to, more frequently measured pathways, with the capacity to influence multiple components of the proteostasis network simultaneously. NAD+ availability regulates SIRT deacylase activity, particularly SIRT1 and SIRT3, which exert broad post-translational control over mitochondrial proteome function, mitophagic flux, and biogenic transcription [29-31]. Disruption of NAD+/SIRT signaling under chronic metabolic stress may therefore exert system-wide effects across proteostasis pathways. Mitochondrial protein import, mediated primarily through the translocase of the inner membrane (TIM)/translocase of the outer mem-brane (TOM) translocase complexes, represents a proximal determinant of mitochondrial proteome integrity; disruption of import fidelity could contribute to downstream quality-control failure before overt bioenergetic impairment becomes apparent [32,33]. The UPRmt, as the principal mitochondrial proteotoxic stress-response pathway, similarly functions at a regulatory level that may influence the balance between adaptive and maladaptive responses to mitochondrial stress managed by dynamics, mitophagy, and biogenesis pathways [34,35]. Accordingly, both mitochondrial import fidelity and UPRmt activation represent strong candidates for future human diabetic sarcopenia cohorts incorporating network-level proteostasis profiling. Their systematic investigation constitutes a clear translational gap identified by the current evidence-density framework.

EVIDENCE FROM BASIC SCIENCE: ANIMAL AND CELLULAR MODELS

The mechanistic sequence connecting diabetic metabolic stress to mitochondrial proteostasis failure and subsequent muscle atrophy is most clearly delineated in rodent and cellular models [21,28], where experimental systems permit precise temporal resolution, genetic manipulation, and pathway-level interrogation that are not feasible in human tissue studies. The studies summarized in Table 1 collectively define this mechanistic architecture across diverse experimental platforms and intervention strategies [13-15,20-24,28,29,36-39]. The primary insight arises not from individual models or compounds, but from the consistent convergence observed across them.

Phytochemical agents, such as umbelliferone and ginsenoside CK [21,22], anti-inflammatory interventions targeting celecoxib-sensitive and receptor-interacting serine/threonineprotein kinase 3 (RIPK3)-dependent pathways [14,15], extracellular vesicle–mediated delivery of PINK1-activating signals [24], sodium glucose cotransporter (SGLT) inhibitors including dapagliflozin and tofogliflozin [23,36], and gene- or pathway-focused studies involving ATPase Na+/K+ transporting family member beta 4 (ATP1B4), milk fat globule-EGF factor 8 (MFG-E8), and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling [13,20,28] operate through distinct upstream mechanisms. Despite this diversity, these interventions consistently converge on a shared downstream outcome: restoration of mitophagic flux and mitochondrial quality-control signaling, accompanied by attenuation of muscle wasting phenotypes [22,28].

This convergence provides a level of inferential strength that extends beyond individual studies. When structurally and mechanistically distinct interventions, applied across multiple model systems and diabetic induction paradigms, produce protective effects that align on mitochondrial proteostasis restoration, this pattern supports the interpretation that mitochondrial quality control represents a central vulnerability in diabetic muscle rather than an incidental correlate of intervention-induced metabolic improvement. The organizational structure of this convergence—mapping heterogeneous experimental inputs onto a shared proteostasis rescue output—is summarized in Fig. 3, which presents the evidence as a network-level causal framework rather than a collection of isolated pathways.

A recurring feature across these studies is the close coupling between immunometabolic stress signaling and mitochondrial dysfunction. As demonstrated in models targeting celecoxibsensitive pathways, RIPK3 signaling, and STING-focused model activation [14,15,20] in Table 1, inflammatory mediators— including nuclear factor κB–dependent transcriptional programs, necroptotic stress signaling, and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ-dependent metabolic dysregulation— actively exacerbate mitochondrial impairment. These processes reduce mitophagic capacity, increase oxidative damage to mitochondrial proteins, and activate proteolytic effectors, including the ubiquitin-proteasome and calpain pathways, thereby accelerating myofibrillar degradation. This evidence supports an integrated model in which inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction form a unified immunometabolic stress axis, with reciprocal amplification between components. Therapeutic strategies targeting only one component of this axis are therefore likely to achieve partial and potentially transient effects.

Exercise-related models provide a distinct and mechanistically informative context within the basic science literature. The dapagliflozin-plus-exercise and vibration-intervention models summarized in Table 1 demonstrate that contractile and mechanical stimuli activate mitochondrial quality-control pathways— including miR-378–mediated regulation of mitochondrial dynamics and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)– PGC-1α–driven biogenic transcription—through pathways that pharmacologic agents activate only partially and in isolation [23,37]. Beyond these direct mitochondrial adaptations, increasing attention has focused on systemic mediators induced by exercise as inter-organ signals capable of propagating mitochondrial and metabolic benefits beyond the contracting fiber itself. The Bifidobacterium adolescentis-derived nicotinic acid pathway, as represented in Table 1, provides a microbiome-linked example of how NAD+ precursor availability connects gut-derived metabolite signaling to skeletal muscle mitochondrial function and sarcopenia outcomes, extending the mechanistic scope of exercise-adjacent interventions into the domain of NAD+-linked proteostatic regulation [29]. Among broader exercise-responsive mediators, short-chain fatty acid– linked metabolic axes and circulating exerkines have similarly emerged as biologically plausible candidates for mediating cross-tissue effects relevant to both sarcopenic and diabetic phenotypes [40].

The significance of these findings extends beyond exercise physiology. Identification of molecular mediators induced by exercise and metabolite signaling provides a mechanistic bridge from empirical exercise interventions to the discovery of defined therapeutic targets. This framework supports the development of pharmacological strategies that emulate specific exercise-induced pathways, an approach of particular relevance for patients whose functional limitations or comorbidities restrict the intensity or feasibility of exercise-based interventions.

HUMAN TRANSLATIONAL AND MULTIOMICS EVIDENCE

The translational validity of the mitochondrial proteostasis framework depends on whether comparable pathway-level disturbances can be detected in human skeletal muscle under conditions of T2DM and metabolic dysfunction. The available human literature, although heterogeneous in tissue access and study design, consistently demonstrates a directional signal that aligns with preclinical observations. Reported alterations include mitochondrial dynamics and quality-control programs [2,18], impaired mitophagy and biogenic signaling [11,25,41], and respiratory dysfunction extending beyond isolated bioenergetic deficits [26,42]. Several studies further establish functional relevance by linking the magnitude of mitochondrial quality-control impairment to measurable indices of insulin sensitivity [18,19] and metabolic flexibility [25,43,44], thereby advancing these findings beyond descriptive histological observations to mechanistically informative translational evidence. To enhance the recency and clinical applicability of this translational layer, selected recent human and translational studies published between 2021 and 2026 were summarized in Table 2 according to study population or model, exposure or intervention, outcomes assessed, and main finding [17-19,25, 44-54].

Among the selected studies, convergent evidence reinforces the centrality of ceramide–ETC coupling in human diabetic muscle pathology. Axelrod et al. [18] demonstrated that ectopic lipid accumulation activates mitochondrial fission and quality-control networks early in the development of insulin resistance, indicating that disruptions in mitochondrial dynamics occur upstream rather than as secondary consequenc-es. Diaz-Vegas et al. [19] further characterized an ETC–ceramide– coenzyme Q axis that contributes to skeletal muscle insulin resistance, providing a mechanistic link between lipotoxicity and impaired mitochondrial quality control at the level of respiratory complexes. Together, these studies define a coherent sequence linking ceramide-driven dynamics-to-respiration failure that is detectable in human tissue. Hendlinger et al. [17] extended this framework by demonstrating that exercise training increases skeletal muscle sphingomyelinase activity and modifies mitochondrial quality control in men with T2DM, indicating that ceramide-associated suppression of quality-control pathways is reversible under physiological intervention.

Evidence supporting disruption of mitophagy and proteostatic surveillance has also accumulated in human studies. Dantas et al. [25] demonstrated that deubiquitinating enzymes regulate mitochondrial quality control and insulin sensitivity in skeletal muscle of patients with T2DM, identifying the ubiquitin-editing component of mitophagy as a functionally relevant axis in human disease rather than an exclusively preclinical observation. Mandarino and Willis [43] offered a thermodynamic reappraisal of low mitochondrial content as a driver of insulin resistance in human skeletal muscle, reporting proteostasis-linked findings that situate the human defect within the framework of organelle quality-control collapse rather than stoichiometric bioenergetic loss alone. Critically, Whytock et al. [44] reported a dissociation between insulin resistance and mitochondrial capacity in human skeletal muscle, demonstrating that conventional bioenergetic measures are insufficient proxies for mitochondrial quality-control dysfunction and underscoring the need for proteostasis-specific endpoints.

Recent interventional studies provide evidence that oxidative stress contributes to the amplification of mitochondrial dysfunction in human muscle. Fiorenza et al. [45] demonstrated that reduction of mitochondrial oxidative burden attenuates lipid-induced insulin resistance, supporting a causal role for mitochondria-derived ROS and establishing redox-targeted interventions as a mechanistically coherent therapeutic approach. Complementary non-invasive evidence from Wahidi et al. [46], using dynamic magnetic resonance imaging-based assessment of peripheral oxidative metabolism, confirmed impaired mitochondrial function in T2DM in vivo, providing imaging-level support for the functional correlates observed in biopsy-based studies. Sarabhai et al. [47] further demonstrated that hyperbaric oxygen therapy improves tissue-specific insulin sensitivity and mitochondrial capacity, linking oxygen availability to restoration of mitochondrial function. At the metabolite level, Petersen et al. [48] showed that compartment-specific accumulation of diacylglycerol and C18:0 ceramide within sarcolemmal and mitochondrial fractions regulates muscle insulin sensitivity and responds to weight loss, identifying lipid compartmentalization as a translatable therapeutic target.

The emerging multi-omics evidence base adds a system-level dimension that single-modality approaches cannot provide. Integrative analysis across mitochondrial transcriptomic programs [18], proteostasis-related protein abundance and modification signatures [25,26], and metabolically linked pathway outputs [31] increasingly support a model in which diabetic sarcopenia reflects coordinated network-level proteostasis failure distributed across multiple quality-control modules, rather than a discrete single-lesion disorder amenable to single-target correction. Makhnovskii et al. [49] identified dysregulation of the early postprandial gene response in obese and T2DM muscles, revealing that failure extends beyond baseline into the dynamic transcriptional response to nutrient challenge, a finding relevant to the temporal patterning of quality-control collapse. Shi et al. [55] performed a proteomic analysis of diabetic skeletal muscles and identified 5-oxoprolinase, ATP-hydrolysing (OPLAH) downregulation as a link between oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and impaired glucose uptake, illustrating how proteomic profiling maps novel pathway nodes within the quality-control network. Tan et al. [50] extended this to the metabolomic level, identifying serum metabolomic biomarkers specific to diabetic sarcopenia and supporting the feasibility of minimally invasive biomarker-based module profiling in clinical populations.

The network architecture that emerges from multi-omics integration carries direct and practically important implications for translational study design and clinical application. Interpretive frameworks built around individual biomarkers, such as a single OXPHOS subunit, mitophagic receptor, or inflammatory mediator, are likely to underestimate biological complexity and may misclassify patients whose dominant module failure differs from the selected marker. A more appropriate translational strategy involves module-based profiling that captures the relative contributions of mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy, biogenesis and respiratory coupling, and stress-response and protein import programs within each individual, and aligns intervention selection with the dominant failure pattern. Patients with predominant quality-control impairment— manifested as high ceramide burden, suppressed DRP1/mitochondrial fission protein 1 (FIS1)-mediated fission, or defective PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy—may require different therapeutic approaches than those with primary defects in respiratory coupling or reduced PGC-1α–driven biogenesis, even when overall clinical phenotypes appear similar by conventional metrics [56,57]. This module-priority framework is now methodologically feasible given advances in muscle transcriptomic and proteomic profiling using minimally invasive biopsy techniques [58,59].

These translational prospects should be interpreted in the context of the current limitations of the human evidence base. Lin et al. [51] demonstrated that sleep restriction alters muscle transcriptomic profiles in ways linked to insulin resistance risk, illustrating how non-metabolic systemic perturbations confound the interpretation of quality-control data in cross-sectional studies. Kunz et al. [52] showed that adipose tissue macrophage– driven systemic inflammation is associated with skeletal muscle mitochondrial abnormalities, introducing an additional confounding axis that cannot be resolved directionally in cross-sectional designs. Iatrogenic factors represent another source of complexity: Kuretu et al. [60] and VanDerStad et al. [61] demonstrated that commonly used pharmacologic agents, including SGLT2 inhibitors, independently modify mitochondrial metabolism in insulin-resistant muscles, complicating attribution of observed quality-control defects to the diabetic milieu itself.

More broadly, heterogeneity in biopsy depth, inconsistent endpoint definitions, variability in metabolic phenotyping, and the widespread reliance on cross-sectional or short-term interventional designs collectively limit the precision of mechanistic inference [62]. Cross-sectional data do not allow resolution of temporal sequencing, such as whether disruption of mitochondrial dynamics precedes mitophagy impairment or whether suppression of biogenesis occurs prior to OXPHOS deterioration in human tissue. Consequently, mechanistic confidence in the human literature is stronger for overarching pathway directionality—confirming disruption of quality-control programs consistent with preclinical models—than for precise causal sequencing. This distinction motivates the presentation of the quantitative evidence map in terms of recurrence density rather than pooled efficacy estimates. Ultimately, these limitations underscore the need for longitudinal, mechanistically detailed human studies as a critical next step in translating the mitochondrial proteostasis framework to diabetic sarcopenia [63,64].

THERAPEUTIC IMPLICATIONS FOR DIABETES MANAGEMENT

The mechanistic framework outlined in the preceding sections has direct implications for the design, evaluation, and clinical translation of therapeutic strategies targeting diabetic sarcopenia. If mitochondrial proteostasis failure contributes as a plausible mediator—rather than solely a downstream epiphenomenon— of muscle loss in T2DM, then therapeutic approaches should prioritize restoration of quality-control network function rather than management of end-stage manifestations. This perspective shifts the focus of intervention from body-composition outcomes to pathway-level correction as the primary mechanistic objective for both pharmacologic and lifestyle strategies.

Within this framework, exercise-based therapy remains the foundational component of diabetes-focused intervention strategies, supported by extensive and consistent mechanistic evidence [17,27,40,65]. Contractile activity engages mitochondrial quality-control pathways through multiple convergent mechanisms, including activation of AMPK- and PGC-1α– mediated biogenic transcription [66], stimulation of mitophagic flux via BCL2-interacting protein 3-like (BNIP3L, also known as NIX)- and FUN14 domain containing 1 (FUNDC1)-dependent pathways [67], promotion of fission– fusion dynamics that facilitate removal of damaged organelles [68], and induction of systemic exerkine signaling that extends metabolic benefits across tissues [69,70]. No current pharmacologic intervention replicates this breadth of effects. The translational evidence supporting exercise-induced mitochondrial restoration in diabetic and sarcopenic muscle remains stronger than that for any individual adjunctive therapy. Accordingly, exercise prescription should move beyond general physical activity advice and incorporate structured, progressive, and modality-specific training. Clinically, this includes progressive resistance training targeting major muscle groups to improve strength and muscle quality, combined with moderate- to-vigorous aerobic exercise to enhance oxidative capacity, substrate flexibility, and cardiometabolic risk control. Highintensity interval training may be considered in appropriately selected patients when functional capacity, comorbidity burden, and safety allow, whereas frail or mobility-limited patients may require lower-intensity initiation followed by gradual progression [71-73].

Adjunctive strategies targeting specific components of the quality-control network are biologically justified and increasingly supported by mechanistic data, provided their use is guided by pathway-based rationale. NAD+ precursor supplementation targets the SIRT-dependent regulatory layer that governs post-translational control of mitochondrial proteome function and mitophagic capacity, and depletion of NAD+ under chronic diabetic stress provides a mechanistically coherent rationale for repletion in this population [29-31]. Nutritional and phytochemical interventions with demonstrated effects on mitophagy signaling, mitochondrial dynamics, or UPRmt activation represent additional candidates when aligned with the dominant module of dysfunction in individual patients [21,22, 24,38]. Intervention selection should therefore proceed from mechanistic evidence to therapeutic choice, rather than from availability to retrospective justification, with implications for both trial design and interpretation of heterogeneous outcomes.

The expanding literature on glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors in diabetic sarcopenia requires careful interpretation. Although both classes provide metabolic benefits in T2DM, pharmacotherapy in patients at risk for sarcopenia should be evaluated beyond glycemic control and body-weight reduction alone. In particular, the potential for concurrent lean-mass loss during GLP-1–based therapy has emerged as a clinically relevant concern, especially in older adults or patients with baseline sarcopenia risk [74,75]. Therefore, pharmacotherapy studies in this population should prospectively assess appendicular skeletal muscle mass, grip strength, gait speed, chair-stand performance, physical performance batteries, and muscle quality [5,6]. Mechanistic links between GLP-1 receptor agonists and skeletal muscle mitochondrial function remain under investigation, and evidence linking GLP-1 receptor agonists to sarcopenia-related markers in diabetes remains heterogeneous [16,75]. For SGLT2 inhibitors, available preclinical and experimental evidence suggests possible effects on skeletal muscle mitochondrial quality control and metabolism, whereas direct human skeletal muscle evidence remains limited [23,36,61]. Future trials should integrate standard glycemic outcomes with direct skeletal muscle mitochondrial endpoints—biopsy-based transcriptomic and proteomic profiling, mitochondrial respiratory function measurement, and validated functional readouts of muscle contractile performance—in adequately sized and mechanistically stratified cohorts. Importantly, pharmacologic strategies should be evaluated alongside structured exercise and individualized nutritional support, particularly resistance training, to distinguish metabolically beneficial fat loss from clinically undesirable loss of muscle mass or function [71-73,76].

Translating this framework into clinical practice requires a biomarker strategy that reflects the proposed module architecture of the condition rather than the convenience of individual protein measurements [77]. Single-marker approaches—such as isolated quantification of a mitophagic receptor, a respiratory complex subunit, or a circulating inflammatory mediator— are unlikely to fully capture network-level proteostasis failure. Because such network-level dysfunction may represent a major pathogenic mechanism, single markers used as primary trial endpoints may generate false-negative results when interventions correct adjacent modules or false-positive signals when marker changes are dissociated from functional improvement. A more appropriate translational biomarker panel should integrate mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy markers, respiratory competence indices, NAD+-linked regulatory signals [78], and function-centered clinical outcomes, including grip strength, gait speed, and objective physical performance battery scores [5,6]. These clinical outcomes anchor molecular changes to the contractile and metabolic phenotypes that ultimately determine patient-relevant benefit. Aligning such panels with module-based patient stratification would further enable the identification of subgroups in which specific quality-control failures predominate, making it possible to match intervention selection to individual biological vulnerabilities, rather than applying uniform therapeutic strategies across a mechanistically heterogeneous population [76].

CONCLUSIONS

The mechanistic framework presented in this review is internally coherent and increasingly supported across experimental systems. However, translation into precision clinical application requires a structured research agenda that addresses the gap between associative evidence and causal inference. The principal limitation of the current literature lies not in the absence of mechanistic hypotheses, but in the lack of prospective human studies capable of evaluating these hypotheses with sufficient temporal resolution, pathway specificity, and integration of clinical outcomes. Addressing this limitation requires the establishment of prospective diabetes cohorts with standardized skeletal muscle phenotyping across sites and time points. These cohorts should incorporate detailed mitochondrial module assessments to distinguish among impairments in dynamics and mitophagy, biogenesis, respiratory function, and upstream regulatory systems, including NAD+-related and protein import pathways. Functional outcomes should be measured using instruments sensitive to clinically meaningful changes across the time course of sarcopenia progression. Without such infrastructure, research will continue to rely on cross-sectional data that define associations without resolving causal sequence, and intervention trials will remain focused on endpoints that are too distal from underlying biology to detect pathway-specific effects.

Intervention trials should be designed with mechanistic stratification as a core principle rather than a post-hoc analytical approach. Variability in outcomes across exercise, nutritional, and pharmacologic studies in diabetic muscle likely reflects the inclusion of heterogeneous populations with differing dominant module dysfunctions. Trials that define proteostasis module profiles at baseline—distinguishing impairments in mitophagic clearance, mitochondrial biogenesis, or respiratory coupling—and align interventions accordingly may improve detection of pathway-specific treatment effects. Such an approach would also generate mechanistically interpretable response data to support precision therapeutic strategies. Advances in minimally invasive muscle biopsy techniques and high-resolution transcriptomic and proteomic profiling now make this approach feasible, supporting its adoption as a standard design element in future diabetic sarcopenia trials.

Integrated human multi-omics pipelines represent the analytical infrastructure through which pathway signatures can be connected to treatment responses and functional outcomes in a manner that neither single-modality biomarker studies nor conventional clinical endpoints can achieve independently. The development and prospective validation of such pipelines, combining skeletal muscle transcriptomics, mitochondrial proteomics, metabolomic profiling of NAD+-linked and sub strate oxidation intermediates, and circulating exerkine and inflammatory signatures, would provide the mechanistic resolution necessary to move from population-level treatment recommendations to individually calibrated intervention strategies. Particular investigative priority should be directed toward the expansion of evidence in the current low-density modules of mitochondrial protein import and UPRmt activation, whose quantitative underrepresentation in the existing literature obscures what may be mechanistically upstream, and therefore, causally important in determining whether diabetic muscle mounts an adaptive or maladaptive response to proteostatic stress. Whether impaired import fidelity and insufficient UPRmt induction are early initiating events in the proteostasis failure cascade or context-dependent modifiers whose contribution varies with disease stage and metabolic severity is a question whose answer has direct implications for both biomarker panel design and the identification of therapeutic windows in which quality-control rescue is still mechanistically achievable.

Overall, the evidence synthesized in this review supports a conceptual shift in diabetic sarcopenia, from a clinical comorbidity to a mechanistically defined consequence of chronic metabolic stress affecting mitochondrial proteostasis in skeletal muscle. Convergent findings from experimental models and human studies implicate coordinated disruption of mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, biogenesis, and OXPHOS, with additional regulation through NAD+-dependent signaling, protein import systems, and stress-response pathways. This mechanistic framework provides a basis for advancing from associative studies to hypothesis-driven clinical trials. A translational strategy centered on exercise as the foundational intervention, complemented by pathway-targeted adjunct therapies guided by individual module dysfunction, and supported by multi-omics-based stratification, offers a rational pathway toward precision treatment of diabetic sarcopenia.
