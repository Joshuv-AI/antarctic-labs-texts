On the difference of the intersection power graph and the power graph of a finite group
Sudip Bera, Peter J. Cameron

Abstract
. The two graphs of the title both have vertex set G G . In the intersection power graph, x x and y y are joined if some non-identity element is a power of both; in the power graph, x x and y y joined if one is a power of the other. Thus the power graph is a spanning subgraph of the intersection power graph, and we define the edges of the difference graph to be the difference of these edge sets. In this paper, we give a number of results about the difference graph. We examine groups whose power graph and intersection power graph coincide. In addition, we make some observations on isolated vertices in difference graphs. We study the connectedness and perfectness of difference graph with respect to various properties of the underlying group G G . Furthermore, we investigate the operation of twin reduction on graphs, a technique that yields smaller graphs which may be easier to analyze. Keywords: Intersection power graph; Power graph; Finite group MSC2020: 05C25, 20D10



1.
Introduction

The study of graphs associated with various algebraic structures has attracted
considerable attention over the past two decades. Such investigations are useful in

(1)

characterizing the resulting graphs,

(2)

characterizing algebraic structures that give rise to isomorphic graphs, and

(3)

understanding the interdependence between algebraic structures and their
corresponding graphs.

A wide variety of graphs have been introduced to explore the properties of
algebraic structures through graph-theoretic methods. Notable examples include
the commuting graph
[
9
]
, the generating graph
[
23
]
,
the power graph
[
18
,
26
]
, and the enhanced power graph
[
1
,
5
,
6
]
.
The notion of a power graph was originally introduced in semigroup theory by
Kelarev and Quinn
[
27
]
and the intersection power graph was introduced in
[
4
]
.

All groups considered in this paper will be finite.

We define the power graph and intersection power graph of a group. For
comparison, we also define the enhanced power graph, though we do not study
this graph.

Definition 1.1
.

[
4
]

Let
G
be a group with identity element

.

(1)

The
intersection power graph
of

, denoted by

, is the graph with vertex set

and two distinct non-identity vertices

and

are adjacent if and only if there exists ai
non-identity
element

such that

is a power of both

and

; equivalently,
\langle x\rangle\cap\langle y\rangle\neq\{e\}
. By convention, we take

to be adjacent to all other vertices, where
e
is the identity element of the group
G
.

(2)

The
power graph

\mathcal{P}(G)
of
G
is the graph with vertex set
G

with two vertices
x
and
y
adjacent if and only if one of them is a power of the other.

(3)

The
enhanced power graph

\mathcal{P}_{e}(G)
of

is the graph with
vertex set
G
, in which two vertices
x
and
y
are adjacent if there exists

such that both

and

are powers of

.

Remark 1.1
.

The power graph has received a lot of attention in the literature: see

[
26
]
for a survey. Less attention has been given to the
intersection power graph: see
[
4
]
. It was briefly
considered in
[
12
]
under the name
dual enhanced power graph
.

Clearly, if
x
and
y
are joined in the power graph of
G
, then they are joined in both the intersection power graph and the enhanced power graph; in other
words, the power graph is a spanning subgraph of each of the other two.

This motivates the study of two difference graphs; in one of these, the edges
are those of the enhanced power graph which are not edges of the power graph;
in the other, they are those of the difference power graph which are not
edges of the power graph. The first of these graphs was studied in
[
7
]
,
and will not be considered further here; our purpose is to study the second.

Definition 1.2
.

The difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
of a finite group
G
is the difference of the intersection power graph of
G
and the power graph of
G,
where all isolated vertices are removed.

This graph is the subject of the paper. However, the first question that should
be asked after this definition is the following:

Problem 1.1
.

Given a group
G
, identify the isolated vertices in the difference graph. In
particular, which groups have the property that the difference graph has no
edges?

Where an edgeless graph is called “null graph” or “empty graph”.
We will use the term “undeleted difference graph” if we wish to retain the
isolated vertices. Note that a group

has the property that
\mathcal{D}(G)

has no edges if and only if the intersection power graph and power graph of
G

are equal. We will discuss further the question of isolated vertices in
Section
5
, and the question of when it is
the null graph in Sections
2
and
8
.

Remark 1.2
.

An analogous question, that of determining groups whose power graph and
enhanced power graph are equal, has been solved
[
1
]
. These are
precisely the
EPPO groups
, those in which every element has prime
power order. After preliminary work by Higman and Suzuki, these were
classified by Brandl
[
8
]
in 1981. (A perhaps more accessible version
appears in the survey
[
16
]
.) We hope that the groups for
which the power graph and intersection power graph are equal also form an
interesting class.

Plan of the Paper

•

Under the same hypotheses, we characterize all the isolated vertices of the difference graph

Section

In Section

In Section

2.
When is the difference graph empty?

In this section, we examine groups whose power graph and intersection power
graph are equal, that is, those groups whose difference graph is empty.
We also make some observations on isolated vertices in difference graphs.
We begin with a simple result showing that the difference graph shares a
property with many other graphs on groups.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
H
be a subgroup of
G
. Then the undeleted difference graph of
H
is
the induced subgraph of the undeleted difference graph of
G
on the vertex
set
H
.

This is true because the adjacency of vertices in
H
depends only on elements
of
H
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the class of finite groups whose difference graph is
empty. The next theorem raises the possibility of using induction to classify
the groups in
\mathcal{C}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that either
G
cannot be generated by two elements, or
G
has
trivial center. Then
\mathcal{D}(G)
is null if and only if
\mathcal{D}(H)

is null for every proper subgroup
H
of
G
.

Proof.

Given two elements
x
and
y
, if the subgroup
H=\langle x,y\rangle
is
proper, and
x
and
y
are joined in
\mathcal{D}(G)
if and only if they are
joined in
\mathcal{D}(H)
. So we only have to consider the case when

\langle x,y\rangle=G
. But in this case
\langle x\rangle\cap\langle y\rangle

commutes with
x
and
y
, and so lies in
Z(G)
, and so by assumption is
trivial; so
x
and
y
are not joined in the intersection power graph.
∎

There is a simple sufficient condition for membership in
\mathcal{C}
:

Proposition 2.3
.

(a)

An element of a group
G
whose order is prime is isolated in the
difference graph.

(b)

If every element of
G
has prime order, then
g\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

(a) Suppose that
a
has prime order
p
. If
b
is joined to
a
in the
difference graph, then there exists
c\in\langle a\rangle\cap\langle b\rangle

with
c\neq e
. Thus
\langle a\rangle=\langle c\rangle
, so

a\in\langle b\rangle
, and
a
is joined to
b
in the power graph, a
contradiction.

(b) is clear from (a).
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Groups satisfying condition (b) of this Proposition are EPPO groups, so can
be identified from Brandl’s list. In particular,
A_{5}
is the only simple
group satisfying this condition, since the other simple groups in the list
contain elements of order
4
or
9
. However, there are other simple groups
in
\mathcal{C}
, as we will see.

We conclude this section with a curious connection with the power graph. Recall
that a graph
\Gamma
is a
cograph
if it does not contain the
4
-vertex
path
P_{4}
as induced subgraph.

Proposition 2.5
.

If
\mathcal{D}(G)
is null, then the power graph of
G
is a cograph.

Proof.

We show the contrapositive. Suppose that
\mathcal{P}(G)
is
not a cograph; let
(a,b,c,d)
be an induced
P_{4}
. In the directed power
graph (with an arc
x\to y
if
y
is a power of
x
), the directions must
alternate; for, if
a\to b\to c
, say, then
a\to c
, and
a
and
c
would
be joined in the power graph. Suppose, without loss, that

a\to b\leftarrow c\to d
. Then
b
is a power of both
a
and
c
; and
b\neq e
,
since
b
is not joined to
d
. Thus
a
and
c
are joined in the intersdection
power graph but not in the power graph; so thir difference is not null.
∎

Finite groups whose power graph is a cograph are studied in
[
15
]
, though
these authors do not give a complete classification.

Remark 2.6
.

The converse of this theorem is false: for
G=\mathbb{Z}_{4}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, the power
graph is a cograph, but the difference graph is not null. However, it does
hold in many cases, as we will see.

Let

3.1.
Difference graphs are universal

Theorem 3.8
.

For every finite graph
\Gamma
, there is a finite group
G
such that

\Gamma
is an induced subgraph of
\mathcal{D}(G)
. Moreover,
G
can be
chosen to be cyclic of squarefree order.

The proof depends on two results which may be of independent interest.

Recall that a
Sperner family
is a family
\mathcal{F}
of subsets of
a finite set
X
with the property that, for any two distinct sets

F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have
F_{1}\not\subseteq F_{2}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Any finite graph is an induced subgraph of the intersection graph of a
Sperner family.

Proof

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a finite graph. Take
X=V\cup E
,
and for each
v\in V
, put
F_{v}=\{v\}\cup\{e\in E:v\in e\}
. Then:

•

If
v\neq w
, then
F_{v}\not\subseteq F_{w}
: for
v\in F_{v}\setminus F_{w}
.

•

If
v
is joined to
w
, then
e=\{v,w\}\in F_{v}\cap F_{w}
; otherwise

F_{v}\cap F_{w}=\emptyset
.

So
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{v}:v\in V\}
is a Sperner family and the map
v\mapsto F_{v}

is an isomorphism from
\Gamma
to its intersection graph.

Proposition 3.10
.

The intersection graph of any Sperner family is an induced subgraph
of
\mathcal{D}(G)
, where
G
is a cyclic group of squarefree order.

Proof

Let
(X,\mathcal{F})
be a Sperner family. Choose a prime

p_{x}
for each
x\in X
, so that the chosen primes are distinct. Now let

G=\prod_{x\in X}\mathbb{Z}_{p_{x}},

where
g_{x}
is a generator of the factor
\mathbb{Z}_{p_{x}}
. Then
G
is
a cyclic group of squarefree order. For
each set
F\in\mathcal{F}
, put
g(F)=\prod_{x\in F}g_{x}
. Since
\mathcal{F}

is a Sperner family, none of the elements
g(F)
is a power of another;
and
g(F)
and
g(F^{\prime})
are joined in the difference graph if and only if

F\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

Remark 3.11
.

We could instead choose the groups
G
in the theorem to
be nonabelian simple groups. For the group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
contains a
cyclic subgroup of order
(q-1)/2
. So to embed the cyclic group of order
n

in it, we choose

to be a prime congruent to
1
modulo
2n
; by Dirichlet’s
theorem, there are infinitely many such primes.

4.
When is the difference graph connected?

In this section, we examine groups whose difference graphs are connected. In the connected case, we establish bounds on the diameter of these difference graphs, and in the disconnected case, we make observations concerning their isolated vertices. Our main theorems in this regard are as follows:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group with
\pi(Z(G))\geq 2.
Then the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
is connected if and only if
G\not\cong\mathbb{Z}_{pq}
(with
p,q
primes). Moreover, when connected,

\operatorname{diam}\bigl(\mathcal{D}(G)\bigr)\leq 6.

Let
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
denote the set of all generators of the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, and let
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{D}(G))
denote the set of all isolated vertices in the graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Under the same hypotheses, the set of isolated vertices is

\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{D}(G))=\begin{cases}G,&\text{if }G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{pq},\;p\neq q\text{ primes};\\
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})\cup\{e\},&\text{if }G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{m},\;m\neq pq;\\
\{\,g\in G:o(g)\text{ is prime}\}\cup\{e\},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

As an immediate consequence of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
, we have the following:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\pi(\mathbb{Z}_{m})\geq 2.
Then the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
is empty if and only if
m=pq,
where
p
and
q
are two distinct primes.

We first establish several lemmas concerning the difference graph associated with a finite cyclic group
G
satisfying
\pi(G)\geq 2
. These lemmas will serve as key tools in the proof of our main connectivity results for the difference graph.

4.1.
Cyclic group

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
G
be a cyclic group with
\pi(G)\geq 2.
Then
\mathcal{D}(G)
is disconnected if and only if
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{pq},
where
p
and
q
are two distinct primes. Moreover
\text{diam}(\mathcal{D}(G))\leq 6,
whenever
G\ncong\mathbb{Z}_{pq}.

Let
|G|=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}
with
r\geq 2
, and suppose
G
is a cyclic group. Let
g\in G
be an element that is neither of prime order nor a generator of
G
, and whose order factors as

\mathrm{o}(g)=p_{i_{1}}^{\beta_{i_{1}}}\cdots p_{i_{s}}^{\beta_{i_{s}}},

where each
\beta_{i_{j}}\geq 1
for all
j\in[s]
. We define the
weight
of
g
by

w(g)=\beta_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\beta_{i_{s}}.

Finally, let

X=\{\,g\in G\mid w(g)=2\}.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
X\subseteq G
be the subset of elements with weight
w(g)=2
. Then the vertices in
X
are path-connected in the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
, and the distance between any two such vertices is at most
2
.

Proof.

Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in X
. We will show that there exists a path of length at most 2 between
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
in
\mathcal{D}(G)
. We distinguish two cases:

Case 1:

\mathrm{o}(g_{1})=\mathrm{o}(g_{2})
.

•

If
\mathrm{o}(g_{1})=p_{i}^{2}
for some prime
p_{i}
, then since
\pi(G)\geq 2
, choose another prime
p_{j}\neq p_{i}
and let
g\in X
with
\mathrm{o}(g)=p_{i}p_{j}
. Then
g_{1}\sim g\sim g_{2}
in
\mathcal{D}(G)
.

•

If
\mathrm{o}(g_{1})=\mathrm{o}(g_{2})=p_{i}p_{j}
with
p_{i}\neq p_{j}
, then:

–

If
r\geq 3
, pick a third prime
p_{k}\notin\{p_{i},p_{j}\}
, and choose
g\in X
with
\mathrm{o}(g)=p_{i}p_{k}
. Again,
g_{1}\sim g\sim g_{2}.

–

If
r=2
, then
G
has order
p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}p_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}
with
\alpha_{i}\geq 2
or
\alpha_{j}\geq 2
(otherwise,
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
would be isolated vertices). Assume
\alpha_{i}\geq 2
; choose
g\in X
of order
p_{i}^{2}
. Then
g_{1}\sim g\sim g_{2}.

Case 2:

\mathrm{o}(g_{1})\neq\mathrm{o}(g_{2})
.

Here
\lvert\pi(g_{1})\cap\pi(g_{2})\rvert\leq 1
, since greater intersection would force equality of orders.

•

If
\lvert\pi(g_{1})\cap\pi(g_{2})\rvert=1
, at least one of
g_{1},g_{2}
has order divisible by two distinct primes, so
g_{1}\sim g_{2}
.

•

If
\pi(g_{1})\cap\pi(g_{2})=\emptyset
, then:

–

If

Let

and

6.
Maximal cliques in cyclic groups

Let
G
be the cyclic group of order
n
. Suppose that
\Gamma
is one of the
following graphs on
G
: the power graph, the intersection power graph, or
their difference. If two elements in a cyclic group have the same order,
then each is a power of the other. Hence the graph
\Gamma
has the property
that if a maximal clique contains an element
x
, then it contains every
element of the same order as
x
. Hence a maximal clique in any of these
graphs is defined by a set
S
of divisors of
n
, and consists of all
elements whose order belongs to
S
. Let us denote this set by
C(S)
.
Note that
|C(S)|=\sum\limits_{s\in S}\phi(s)
, where
\phi
is Euler’s function.

Now we have the following:

•

If
\Gamma
is the power graph, then
C(S)
is a clique if and only if

S
is a chain in the lattice of divisors of
n
.

•

If
\Gamma
is the intersection power graph, then
C(S)
is a clique if
and only if
S
is an intersecting family.

•

If
\Gamma
is the difference graph, then
C(S)
is a clique if and only
if
S
is an intersecting Sperner family.

So to find, for example, the clique number of the difference graph, we have
to find which intersecting Sperner family
S
gives the largest value of

C(S)
, according to the above formula.

Remark 6.1
.

De Brruijn
et al.

[
10
]
proved a Sperner-type theorem for Sperner
families in the lattice of divisors of an integer; we require a weighted
version of this result, where the weight of the integer
s
is
\phi(s)
.

Remark 6.2
.

In an arbitrary group, a clique in the power graph is contained in a cyclic
subgroup, so the clique number of the power graph only depends on the orders
of group elements. This is not the case for the intersection power graph or
the difference graph, as the group
Q_{8}
shows.

7.

In this section, we address the following fundamental questions for a finite group

•

When is the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)

?

•

Under what conditions is
\mathcal{D}(G)

?

•

When does
\mathcal{D}(G)
possess an

We present a complete characterization of those finite groups

Theorem 7.1

Let

Then

is perfect if and only if

To establish Theorem

Proposition 7.2

Let

\pi(G)\geq 4
. Then the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
is not perfect.

Proof.

Let
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4}
be four distinct primes dividing
\lvert G\rvert

None of these

are isolated in
\mathcal{D}(G)
, by Theorem
4.2

Notice that

a_{1}\sim a_{2}\sim a_{3}\sim a_{4}\sim a_{5}\sim a_{1}

forms a 5-cycle an induced odd cycle of length 5 in
\mathcal{D}(G)
. However, odd holes (induced odd cycles of length
\geq 5
) cannot appear in perfect graphs by the Strong Perfect Graph Theorem. Hence,
\mathcal{D}(G)
is not perfect.
∎

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
G
be a finite nilpotent group that is not cyclic and satisfies
\pi(G)=3
. Then the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
is not perfect.

Proof.

Let
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
be the distinct primes dividing
\lvert G\rvert
. Since
G
is nilpotent and non-cyclic, there exists at least one prime, say
p_{1}
, for which there are at least three distinct cyclic subgroups of order
p_{1}
. Denote corresponding generators by
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
with
\langle a_{i}\rangle\neq\langle a_{j}\rangle
for
i\neq j
. Pick elements
b
and
c
in
G
with
\operatorname{o}(b)=p_{2}
and
\operatorname{o}(c)=p_{3}
, respectively.

Then one can verify that the vertices

a_{1}b,\quad a_{2}b,\quad a_{1}bc,\quad a_{3}c,\quad a_{1}c

form an induced cycle of length 5 in
\mathcal{D}(G)
. Because induced odd cycles of length at least 5 (odd holes) are forbidden in perfect graphs by the Strong Perfect Graph Theorem, it follows that
\mathcal{D}(G)
cannot be perfect.
∎

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
G
be a cyclic group with
\pi(G)=3
. Then the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
is perfect if and only if

G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}},

where
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are distinct primes.

Proof.

First, assume
G
is cyclic and not of the form
\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}}
; then some prime square
p_{1}^{2}
divides
\lvert G\rvert
. One constructs an induced 5-cycle in
\mathcal{D}(G)
: select elements
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{5}
such that

\mathrm{o}(a_{1})=p_{1}^{2},\quad\mathrm{o}(a_{2})=p_{1}p_{2},\quad\mathrm{o}(a_{3})=p_{2}p_{3},\quad\mathrm{o}(a_{4})=p_{1}^{2}p_{2},\quad\mathrm{o}(a_{5})=p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}.

They form an induced odd cycle of length 5; by the Strong Perfect Graph Theorem, such an odd hole prevents
\mathcal{D}(G)
from being perfect.
Now let
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}}
, where
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are distinct prime numbers. In this case, by Theorem
4.2
, every non‑isolated vertex in the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
has order of the form
p_{i}p_{j}
with
i\neq j
.
Suppose, for contradiction, that
\mathcal{D}(G)
contains an induced odd cycle of length
2k+1
with
k\geq 2
:

a_{1}\sim a_{2}\sim\cdots\sim a_{2k+1}\sim a_{1}.

Without loss of generality, let
\mathrm{o}(a_{1})=p_{1}p_{3}
. Then by Lemma
3.6
,
\mathrm{o}(a_{2})
must be either
p_{2}p_{3}
or
p_{1}p_{2}
.
If
\mathrm{o}(a_{2})=p_{2}p_{3}
, then
\mathrm{o}(a_{3})
is either
p_{1}p_{3}
or
p_{1}p_{2}
.
In the former case,
\mathrm{o}(a_{4})
must be one of
p_{1}p_{2}
or
p_{2}p_{3}
, which makes
a_{4}
adjacent to
a_{1}
(by Lemma
3.7
), contradicting the assumption that the cycle is induced.
Thus, no induced odd cycle of length
\geq 5
can exist in
\mathcal{D}(G)
.
Moreover, the complement graph
\overline{\mathcal{D}(G)}
breaks into three disjoint cliques again precluding any induced odd cycle of length
\geq 5.

∎

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

The proof follows from Proposition
7.2
and Lemmas
7.3
, and
7.4
.
∎

7.1.
Bipartite graph

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group with
\pi(Z(G))\geq 2
. Then the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
is bipartite if and only if

G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}p_{j}}
, where
p_{i},p_{j}
are distinct primes and
\alpha_{i}\geq 1
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a finite group with
\pi(Z(G))\geq 2
. We consider several cases:

Case 1:

G
is neither cyclic nor isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\times Q_{2^{n}}
(with
\gcd(m,2)=1
).
By Lemma
4.9
,
\mathcal{D}(G)
contains a 3-cycle. Hence, it is
not
bipartite (since bipartite graphs cannot contain odd cycles).

Case 2:

G
is cyclic with
\lvert G\rvert=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}
and
r\geq 3
.
Choose three distinct primes
p_{i},p_{j},p_{k}
dividing
\lvert G\rvert
. There exist elements
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in G
with

\mathrm{o}(g_{1})=p_{i}p_{j},\quad\mathrm{o}(g_{2})=p_{j}p_{k},\quad\mathrm{o}(g_{3})=p_{i}p_{k},

which form a triangle in
\mathcal{D}(G)
. Again, this odd cycle implies that
\mathcal{D}(G)
is not bipartite.

Case 3:

\lvert G\rvert=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}
with
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 2
.
Select five elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{5}\in G
with orders:

\mathrm{o}(a_{1})=p_{1}^{2},\quad\mathrm{o}(a_{2})=p_{1}p_{2}^{2},\quad\mathrm{o}(a_{3})=p_{1}^{2}p_{2},\quad\mathrm{o}(a_{4})=p_{2}^{2},\quad\mathrm{o}(a_{5})=p_{1}p_{2}.

These form an induced 5-cycle in
\mathcal{D}(G)
, proving that it is not bipartite.

Case 4:
One exponent
\alpha_{i}=1
. Without loss of generality, let
\alpha_{2}=1
, so

\lvert G\rvert=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2},\quad\alpha_{1}\geq 1.

If
\alpha_{1}=1
, then by Theorem
4.2
,
\mathcal{D}(G)
is an empty graph, hence bipartite.
If
\alpha_{1}>1
, partition

V(\mathcal{D}(G))=V_{1}\cup V_{2},

where

V_{1}=\{g\in G:\mathrm{o}(g)=p_{1}^{t}\},\qquad V_{2}=\{g\in G:\mathrm{o}(g)=p_{1}^{t}p_{2}\},

for
0\leq t\leq\alpha_{1}
. There are no edges within
V_{1}
nor within
V_{2}
, making
\mathcal{D}(G)
bipartite.
∎

Remark 7.6
.

G
is cyclic of prime power order.
Then both the power graph
\mathcal{P}(G)
and the intersection power graph
\mathcal{G}_{I}(G)
are complete. Thus, their difference graph is empty, i.e., bipartite.

7.2.
Eulerian Properties of Difference Graphs

In this section, we characterize all finite groups
G
for which the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
is Eulerian.

Theorem 7.7
.

For any finite group
G
, the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
is Eulerian.

Proof.

To show that
\mathcal{D}(G)
is Eulerian, it suffices by Euler’s theorem to prove that each vertex has even degree and that every component with edges is connected. Since
\mathcal{D}(G)
is derived from group structure, its connectedness is naturally inherited from adjacency definitions; so it remains only to verify that every vertex has an even degree.

Let
a
be any vertex in
\mathcal{D}(G)
. If
a
is isolated (i.e., has degree zero), its degree is trivially even. Otherwise, consider distinct neighbors
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
of
a
outside its own cyclic subgroup
\mathcal{G}_{a}
. By Lemma
3.7
, each neighbor
x_{i}
brings along all vertices in
\mathcal{G}_{x_{i}}
as neighbors of
a
. Since these cyclic subgroups
\mathcal{G}_{x_{i}}
are pairwise disjoint for different
x_{i}
, the degree of
a
is

\deg(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi\bigl(\mathrm{o}(x_{i})\bigr),

where
\phi
is Euler’s totient function.

By Proposition
2.3
, any non-isolated neighbor
x_{i}
must have
\mathrm{o}(x_{i})\geq 3
, so
\phi\bigl(\mathrm{o}(x_{i})\bigr)
is even for each
i
. Hence
\deg(a)
, being a sum of even integers, is itself even.
Consequently, every vertex of
\mathcal{D}(G)
has even degree. This completes the proof that
\mathcal{D}(G)
is Eulerian.
∎

8.
Simple groups

Recall the class
\mathcal{C}
of groups in which the difference graph is null.
In this section we determine the non-abelian simple groups
G
. Since these groups
have trivial center, it follows from Theorem
2.2
that to decide
whether
G\in\mathcal{C}
it suffices to consider whether the proper subgroups
of such a group belong to
\mathcal{C}
. The only such groups are

G=\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
for certain prime powers
q
, though it seems beyomd the
reach of number theory to determine precisely which prime powers occur, or
even if there are finitely or infinitely many.

We deal first with the groups
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
; this requires a lemma.

Lemma 8.1
.

The difference graphs of the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and the dihedral group

D_{2n}
are equal.

Proof.

The elements of
D_{2n}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{n}
all have order
2
, so by
Proposition
2.3
(a), they are isolated, and
hence deleted when we construct the difference graph.
∎

Proposition 8.2
.

The simple group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
has difference graph null if and only if
the following holds: setting
d=\gcd(q-1,2)
, each of
(q-1)/d
and
(q+1)/d
is
either a prime power or the product of two primes.

Proof.

The list of maximal subgroups of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
was given by
Dickson
[
20
, Chapter 22]
and is available in many places, for example Huppert
[
25
, Chapter 2]
. They are among the
following (not all of these are maximal, or even occur in a given group):

(1)

Dihedral groups of order
2(q\pm 1)/d
;

(2)

Semidirect products
V\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{(q-1)/d}
;

(3)

Subgroups
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q_{0})
or
\mathrm{PGL}(2,q_{0})
, where
q
is a power of
q_{0}
;

(4)

A_{4}
,
S_{4}
or
A_{5}
.

We consider these in turn.

(a): The difference graph for these is the same as that of the cyclic group
of order
(q-1)/d
; our earlier results show that it is null if and only if
its order is either a prime power or the product of two primes.

(b) The affine group has trivial center, so we only have to consider the
elementary abelian group of order
q
and the cyclic group of order

(q-1)/d
.

(c) These groups will not concern us since
q_{0}\pm 1
divides
q\pm 1
.

(d) These three groups are easily seen to have null difference graph. (The
groups
A_{4}
and
A_{5}
have all elements of prime order, while the maximal
subgroups of
S_{4}
are
D_{6}
,
D_{8}
and
A_{4}
.)
∎

Remark 8.3
.

The values of
q
for which the difference graph of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
is null are
the same as those for which the power graph of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
is a cograph

[
12
, Proposition 8.7]
. However, number theory does not yet provide the
tools to decide exactly which such groups occur.

To deal with the remaining groups, we use Proposition
2.5
, which shows that their power graphs must be cographs, together with

[
15
, Theorem 1.3]
, which determines the non-abelian simple groups whose
power graphs are cographs: they are certain groups

\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
and
\mathrm{Sz}(q)
together with
\mathrm{PSL}(3,4)
.
So we only need to show that the difference graphs of Suzuki groups and

\mathrm{PSL}(3,4)
are not null, which we do by showing that these groups
contain the quaternion group
Q_{8}
as a subgroup.

Let
q
be an odd power of
2
, with
q>2
. The Suzuki group
\mathrm{Sz}(q)

has Sylow
2
-subgroup
P
of order
q^{2}
; its center is elementary abelian
of order
q
, and the remaining elements all have order
4
. Moreover, the
group has an automorphism of order
q-1
permuting the involutions
transitively
[
24
]
. So each of the
q-1
involutions is the square
of
q
elements of order
4
; in particular, there exist
x
and
y
with

x^{2}=y^{2}=z
(say) and
z^{2}=1
. Thus
\langle x,y\rangle
is a quaternion group.

Finally, consider
\mathrm{PSL}(3,4)
. We will only consider elements of

2
-power order, so we may work in the matrix group
\mathrm{SL}(3,4)
.
Let

X_{a}=\begin{pmatrix}1&a&0\\
0&1&a^{-1}\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}

for each non-zero element
a
in the field of
4
elements. It is easily
checked that
\det(X_{A})=1
and that

X_{a}^{2}=Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Again we have two such matrices
X_{1}
and
X_{\omega}
(where
\omega
is a
cube root of
1
) such that
X_{1}^{2}=X_{\omega}^{2}=Z
, and so a quaternion group.
So we have proved:

Theorem 8.4
.

The only non-abelian finite simple groups whose difference graphs are null
are those in Proposition
8.2
.

9.
Groups with a unique involution

The class of groups with a unique involution has many occurrences in different
areas; for example, it contains the binary polyhedral groups

[
19
, p. 82]
, which feature in the celebrated observation by John
McKay connecting the regular polyhedra with the exceptional root systems
(discussed in
[
13
, pp. 109f]
).

Let
G
be a group containing a unique involution
z
. Then the set
F
of
elements of even order in
G
contains at least half the elements of
G

(since, of each pair
(g,gz)
, at least one belongs to
F
) and carries a
complete graph in the intersection power graph of
G
. Moreover, if
g\notin F
,
then
g
is joined to at least the element
gz
of
F
in the power graph;
and, if the order of
G
has a proper divisor
d
, then
g^{d}z
is an element of

F
joined to
G
in the difference graph of
G
. The difference graphs of at
least some of these groups, for example the binary polyhedral groups, should
repay investigation.

The structure of groups in
G
is known
[
3
]
. We outline the argument.
First, note that the Sylow
2
-subgroups of
G
also have a unnique involution,
and so are cyclic or generalized quaternion. Also,
Z=\langle z\rangle
is a
normal (even central) subgroup of
G
, and
H=G/Z
has cyclic or dihedral Sylow

2
-subgroups. The structure of such groups is determined by Burnside’s
transfer theorem
[
11
, p. 155]
and the Gorenstein–Walter
theorem
[
22
]
. If
O(H)
is the largest odd-order normal subgroup of
H
,
then
H/O(H)
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)

coontaining
\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)
, or
A_{7}
, or a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
H
.
Now a cohomological argument due to Glauberman, described in
[
3
]
, shows
that, for any group
H
with cyclic or dihedral Sylow
2
-subgroups, there is
a group
G
, unique up to isomorphism, which contains a unique involution
z

and has the property that
G/\langle z\rangle
is isomorphic to
H
.

10.
An example

In the paper
[
7
]
, the authors took various small finite simple groups
(with orders less than
10^{4}
), constructed the difference of the power graph
and the enhanced power graph, performed complete twin reduction on this graph,
and inspected the result. For most of the graphs tested, the results were not
too interesting; either the graph was reduced to a single vertex, or it had
a number of isomorphic connected components. For the Mathieu group
M_{11}
,
of order
7920
, an interesting graph was found; it was bipartite semiregular
on
385
vertices with degrees
3
and
4
having diameter and girth
10
and
automorphism group
M_{11}
.

We have performed some similar computations using the difference graph

\mathcal{D}(G)
defined in this paper. The results seem broadly similar.
In particular, for the Mathieu group
M_{11}
, we obtained a graph with

825
vertices. It contains an independent set of size
165
, each of whose
vertices has four neigbours in the induced subgraph on the complement
B
.
This graph on
660
vertices is regular with valency
5
; it has diameter
9
,
girth
3
, and automorphism group
M_{11}
.

Further investigations would be interesting!

11.
Conclusion and open issues

In this paper, we studied the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)
of a finite group
G
.
The investigation was primarily focused on identifying the isolated vertices in
\mathcal{D}(G)
,
in particular, determining which groups yield a difference graph with no edges,
as well as examining the connectedness and perfectness of such graphs.
Several questions arising from this work suggest directions for further research.

For a finite group
G
with
\pi(Z(G))\geq 2
, it was shown that
\mathcal{D}(G)
is connected
and has diameter at most
6
.
However, for groups
G
with
\pi(Z(G))\leq 1,

\mathcal{D}(G)
may or may not be connected.
This naturally leads to the following open problems.

Question 11.1
.

If
\pi(Z(G))\leq 1,
then what can be said about the connectivity of the difference graph
\mathcal{D}(G)?
Can

\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{D}(G))
be greater than
6
?

Question 11.2
.

Complete the classification of isolated vertices of the difference graph.

Question 11.3
.

Complete the classification of finite groups whose difference graph is perfect.

Question 11.4
.

Complete the classification of finite groups whose difference graph is bipartite.
