Artificial intelligence-driven magnetic property prediction and materials discovery for next-generation spintronics
Authors: R. Elilarassi, D. Sivanandakumar, Palaniappan Nagarajan, P. Ilanchezhiyan, V. S. K. Venkatachalapathy, K. Velmurugan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid advancement of spintronic technologies has intensified the demand for magnetic materials with precisely engineered properties, including high spin polarization, large magnetic moments, tunable exchange interactions, and robust thermal stability. However, the simultaneous optimization of these properties remains challenging due to the vast compositional, structural, and interfacial design space governing magnetic systems. In this context, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have emerged as powerful tools for accelerating materials discovery and property prediction. This review provides an overview of recent progress in ML-driven spintronic materials research, focusing on how data-driven models, integrated with high-throughput first-principles calculations and experimental databases, are transforming the prediction of key magnetic properties. Advances in descriptor engineering, spanning compositional, structural, and electronic features, are discussed alongside emerging approaches such as graph neural networks and physics-informed learning. Key material classes, including Heusler alloys, topological spin systems, and two-dimensional magnets, are highlighted in the context of AI-assisted screening and inverse design. The review concludes by outlining future directions toward physics-guided, uncertainty-aware, and autonomous discovery frameworks that may enable closed-loop optimization of next-generation spintronic materials.

Introduction

Spintronics, or spin-based electronics, exploits the spin degree of freedom of electrons in addition to charge, enabling non-volatile, energy-efficient, and ultrafast device functionality beyond conventional charge-based electronics. Through fundamental discoveries such as GMR and TMR and technologies such as magnetic tunnel junctions and spin–orbit torque, spintronics has emerged as a pivotal technology for next-generation memory and logic, including MRAM, and for next-generation neuromorphic and quantum computing architectures.1–7 The application of spin-based technologies has now been extended into quantum computing and spin-based photonics, fueled by advances in manipulating and controlling quantum spin states and coherences.8–12 At the microscopic scale, spintronic properties are governed by the electronic structure. This electronic structure determines exchange interactions, magnetic anisotropy, and spin-dependent transport through d-band filling, orbital hybridization, and spin–orbit coupling. The magnetic and spin properties are strongly sensitive to composition, crystal symmetry, dimensionality, and interfacial properties, which are especially important in industrially relevant materials such as Heusler alloys, 2D magnets, and topological materials.13–17 The recent development of atomically thin magnetic materials and artificially structured heterostructures has shown tunable spin properties in reduced dimensions, enabling miniaturized and multi-functional devices.18–20 However, establishing predictive physically interpretable relationships among chemical composition, electronic structure, and macroscopic magnetic properties remains a major challenge in spintronic material design.

To develop next-generation spintronic devices, candidate materials must exhibit large tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR), high Curie temperature (Tc), large magnetocrystalline anisotropy (Ku), low Gilbert damping, and controllable exchange and chiral interactions,21–25 all within the same material. Simultaneously achieving these properties within a single material platform remains a major challenge, particularly under nanoscale operating conditions. As device dimensions continue to decrease, we need to consider additional constraints such as thermal stability, interfacial spin scattering, and coherence loss.26,27 Experimental approaches and theoretical first-principles calculations, such as DFT, are currently employed to discover and optimize spintronic materials. While the methods provide valuable insights into magnetic properties (e.g., exchange interaction and magnetocrystalline anisotropy), they are computationally expensive for multi-component alloy systems, interfacial materials, and for simulating magnetism at finite temperatures, thereby limiting their applicability for exhaustive exploration of the vast material design space of compositions, structures, thicknesses, and interfaces.28,29 Artificial intelligence, especially machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), offers a powerful alternative framework for learning complex relationships between electronic structure and magnetic properties and for predicting material properties (e.g., Tc, Ku, and spin polarization, etc.), therefore accelerating materials discovery.30,31 Recent advances, including graph neural networks, kernel-based methods, ensemble learning, and automation in conjunction with high-throughput databases, enable inverse materials design for targeted spintronic functionalities and facilitate closed-loop optimization strategies.32–35

Furthermore, integration of chemically and physically interpretable features into machine learning models, such as electronegativity, valence electron concentration, orbital occupancy, bonding types, and related chemical descriptors, is crucial to gain a better understanding of the underlying microscopic magnetism.31,35 This approach allows bridging the gap between entirely data-driven predictions and understanding fundamental physical principles. Even with the recent development of ML-based approaches, some key challenges remain, including limited data availability and diversity, poor generalization across chemical space, and insufficient experimental validation. These models focus on predicting results rather than providing an interpretable description of underlying physics, rendering them unable to extract information on the fundamental mechanisms controlling magnetism. So far, most existing ML models focus either on the interpretability of magnetic mechanisms or on the accurate prediction of specific magnetic properties. A unified and physically interpretable approach that connects electronic structure descriptors and magnetic interactions and predicts spintronic functionalities of materials is currently absent.

In this review, we discuss (i) the basic physical descriptors governing magnetism, (ii) various machine learning techniques used for predicting magnetic properties of materials, (iii) AI-driven prediction of spintronic device-relevant properties, (iv) new classes of materials discovered through AI frameworks, (v) key challenges in AI-driven spintronics research, and (vi) future research directions toward physics-informed and autonomous materials discovery for next-generation spintronic technologies. Spintronic materials discovery is undergoing a transformative shift driven by the integration of quantum physics, materials chemistry, and artificial intelligence, enabling the rational and accelerated design of next-generation electronic, magnetic, and quantum information technologies. The central theme of this review is that the future of spintronic materials discovery lies not in purely data-driven prediction alone, but in the integration of physics-informed artificial intelligence with electronic-structure theory and autonomous materials design strategies.

Principles: electronic-structure foundations and descriptor engineering for AI-driven spintronics

The use of AI techniques to accelerate magnetic materials discovery relies on the fact that magnetic behavior emerges from complex electronic-structure interactions, and understanding these underlying physical mechanisms, together with the descriptors governing spin-dependent phenomena, is essential for reliable predictive modeling. In spintronic systems, phenomena such as magnetic ordering, anisotropy, spin transport, and topological spin textures are governed by intricate electronic correlations across multiple length and energy scales, thereby providing a foundation for descriptor engineering and ML-based materials design.

Electronic-structure origins of magnetism

Magnetism in solids arises from the collective alignment and interaction of electron spins, particularly in atoms with partially filled d- and f-orbitals, and their coupling with the surrounding electronic structure. Magnetic ordering arises from the competition between itinerant and localized electron states through interactions including direct exchange, superexchange, and double exchange.36–38 Stoner's theory provides a first-order approximation of itinerant ferromagnetism by relating exchange splitting to the electronic density of states at the Fermi level; however, real materials often deviate from this approximation because of strong electron correlations, bandwidth effects, and d-state hybridization with other orbitals.39 At a more fundamental level, electron localization and magnetic coupling are governed by d-state occupation and exchange interactions between electron spins, which are influenced by d–d and p–d hybridization together with crystal-field effects.37,40 Spin-dependent interactions coupled to the lattice, such as spin–orbit coupling (SOC), play a central role in governing spintronic phenomena, including magnetocrystalline anisotropy and spin-dependent transport.41,42 These interactions are highly sensitive to composition, symmetry, and dimensionality, thereby playing a crucial role in emerging materials such as Heusler alloys, two-dimensional magnets, and topological systems.43–45 In addition, broken inversion symmetry and interfacial coupling can give rise to chiral exchange interactions, including the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI), which facilitates the formation of non-collinear spin textures such as magnetic skyrmions.46 Accurately capturing these interactions remains challenging because of limitations in existing first-principles approaches, including inaccuracies in exchange–correlation functionals, the treatment of strong electron correlations, and the incorporation of finite-temperature effects and spin fluctuations.39

Since these interactions are inherently nonlinear and strongly coupled, conventional analytical approaches are often insufficient for efficiently exploring large chemical spaces within practical timescales. Accordingly, machine-learning methods have developed as effective alternatives to identify the hidden relationships between electronic-structure features and magnetic properties. Key magnetic and spintronic properties

At the microscopic level, spin-dependent electronic interactions give rise to experimentally observable magnetic and spin-transport properties that govern the functionality of spintronic devices. The Curie temperature (Tc) defines the thermal stability limit of long-range magnetic ordering and is determined by the strength and range of exchange interactions. Accurate prediction of Tc requires incorporation of many-body interactions and thermal spin fluctuations beyond conventional first-principles calculations.48 Magnetocrystalline anisotropy energy (MAE), arising from the interplay between SOC and crystal-field effects, determines magnetic-domain stability and is a critical requirement for spintronic device operation and scaling.41 Systems with broken inversion symmetry support chiral spin textures such as skyrmions, stabilized by the DMI, which represent promising candidates for next-generation memory technologies owing to their low power consumption and high information-storage density.46,49 The formation of magnetic moment and spin polarization in transition-metal systems is primarily governed by d-orbital occupancy and exchange splitting, which influence spin-injection efficiency and magnetoresistive response. In addition to these magnetic characteristics, Berry curvature in momentum space governs charge-to-spin conversion efficiency in materials lacking inversion symmetry and is directly associated with spin Hall conductivity (SHC), a key property in spin–orbit torque devices.50 These magnetic and spintronic properties are highly sensitive to chemical composition, defects, disorder, and competing exchange interactions. Consequently, predicting quantities such as Tc, MAE, magnetic moment, SHC, and DMI has become a major objective of contemporary ML-based studies focused on magnetic-property prediction and spintronic materials discovery.

Physically informed descriptors for magnetism

Effective predictive modeling of material properties requires physically meaningful descriptors capable of capturing the interactions governing material behavior. Such descriptors enable robust and interpretable structure–property relationships and improve the predictive capability of ML models.51 In a broader context, this concept extends naturally to descriptors derived from electronic structure. Element-specific parameters such as atomic number, electronegativity, atomic radius, and valence electron concentration play a crucial role in governing electron distribution, bonding characteristics, and crystal-structure formation. Magnetic descriptors, including d-electron count, exchange splitting, and orbital occupancy, are particularly significant for determining magnetic-moment formation and phase stability, especially in alloy systems such as Heusler compounds.52 Structural descriptors, including lattice symmetry, coordination environment, bond geometry, and strain, strongly modulate electronic band structure through variations in orbital overlap and hybridization. Importantly, strain acts as an important tuning parameter capable of modifying electronic structure and magnetic behavior. Electronic-structure-based descriptors encode both electronic and topological properties, including density of states, bandwidth, band-gap values, band topology, and Berry curvature.53,54 Recent advances in representation learning aim to extract these relationships directly from crystal structures using graph-based neural-network architectures. These approaches utilize local atomic configurations and bonding environments to develop chemically informed and transferable representations across diverse materials systems. Crystal graph convolutional neural networks (CGCNNs) and message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) learn directly from graph-based crystal-structure representations and can automatically identify interpretable structural and bonding features.53,54 The integration of physically informed descriptors with learned representations substantially improves the interpretability and predictive generalization of ML models for magnetic materials design.

Materials informatics and the extended PSPP framework

Materials informatics is a field that integrates processing, structure, properties, and performance. The conventional method for magnetic materials is processing → structure → performance. To construct successful prediction models and incorporate the physical origin of magnetism, the electronic structure must be included as a mediating layer: processing, structure, electronic structure, magnetic characteristics, and device performance.55 Advances in computational resources, experimental approaches, and open datasets have created a huge opportunity to investigate structure–property connections in a variety of chemical spaces.56 Existing datasets often focus on bulk equilibrium properties and do not consider interfaces, defects, disorder, non-equilibrium phases, or multi-scale phenomena. In addition to data scarcity, variation in computational workflows, DFT-specific parameter tuning across databases, limited experimentally verified datasets, and their relatively narrow scope reduce the reliability and transferability of current ML models, particularly for highly correlated magnetic systems and metastable phase search.

Future research on the subject will seek to integrate physical understanding of material processes with the development of prediction models capable of accounting for both multi-scale electronic and spin correlations while remaining resilient and understandable. AI/ML techniques for magnetic property prediction and materials discovery

AI/ML algorithms offer a framework for predicting magnetic behavior using material composition and crystal structure, building on Section 2's electronic-structure description of magnetism, which links magnetic properties to spin-resolved electronic states, hybridization, and exchange interactions. These methods establish quantitative structure-property relationships between chemically and electrically grounded descriptors and macro-scale magnetic response, enabling property prediction and material design.

Unlike first-principles techniques, which require large processing resources to explicitly solve quantum-mechanical equations, machine learning models learn the underlying structure–property connections directly from data. This enables quick exploration of huge chemical regions while preserving reasonable forecasting accuracy. The accuracy and transferability of the predictions, on the other hand, are heavily influenced by how well the physics of electronic structures, magnetic interactions, and chemical descriptors are recorded within the machine learning framework. Regression models and descriptor-based learning

Regression-based models are one of the simplest and most understandable ways to forecast magnetic properties, including Curie temperature (Tc), magnetic moment, and exchange parameters. Early materials-informatics research demonstrated that composition- and structure-derived descriptors can be quantitatively translated to material properties via statistical-learning approaches.58,59 This framework was then reinforced by the addition of chemically and physically informed descriptors, such as d-band filling, valence electron concentration, ionization energy, exchange splitting, and spin polarization, which encode the electronic origins of magnetism.60,61 One significant advantage of regression-based approaches is their interpretability, which enables the identification of major descriptors governing magnetic behavior as well as insight into composition–property relationships. The expressiveness of preset feature spaces, however, limits the predictive potential of these models. Complex magnetic phenomena, such as competitive exchange interactions, spin frustration, and non-collinear magnetism, are difficult to express using low-dimensional descriptors, restricting applicability across chemically different materials.

Ensemble learning: nonlinear structure–property mapping

Ensemble-learning methods, such as Random Forest (RF), gradient boosting, and decision-tree-based algorithms, improve on traditional regression techniques by capturing nonlinear correlations between descriptors and target qualities. These models work especially well on heterogeneous and compositionally complicated datasets with strong nonlinear descriptor-property connections. Over extensive magnetic-materials datasets, high prediction performance has been reported for key magnetic parameters such as Curie temperature, magnetocrystalline anisotropy energy (MAE), exchange interactions, and Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI).60–62 Ensemble-learning methods not only increase predicted accuracy, but they also enable feature-importance analysis, revealing the relative contributions of chemical, structural, and electronic descriptors. Despite these advantages, ensemble techniques are intrinsically interpolative and do not explicitly account for the underlying physics regulating magnetic exchange interactions. Extrapolation capabilities are limited, especially for materials outside the training domain or systems with significant electron correlations, competing magnetic phases, or temperature-dependent spin fluctuations.

Deep learning for electronic-structure representation

Deep learning architectures have been proven to effectively learn hierarchical and multidimensional aspects from electronic structure data, such as density of states (DOS), charge density, and band structure. CNNs have been effectively used to study the link between electronic structure and magnetic properties using spectral and electronic representations.63,64 Unlike descriptor-based approaches, this strategy eliminates the need for human descriptor engineering while capturing emergent and non-local magnetic effects.76,77 Deep neural networks' prediction capabilities were tested for hidden structure–property correlations in orbital hybridization, band topology, and magnetic ordering. However, such methods require large amounts of high-quality data, are frequently opaque, and conventional DL structures do not ensure spin symmetry, conservation laws, rotational invariance, and so on, limiting predictability in extrapolation and strongly correlated systems.

Graph neural networks and structure-aware learning

Graph neural networks offer a significant advancement in materials informatics by storing crystal structures into graphical representations that include atoms as nodes and interatomic bonds as edges. This method captures local coordination contexts, bonding topologies, and chemical interactions that influence magnetic exchange mechanisms.65 CGCNNs and other graph-based architectures have made encouraging predictions for formation energies, electrical characteristics, and magnetic behavior.65–67 Extensions of graph-based learning to magnetic systems have resulted in spin-aware network designs that learn spin interactions, local magnetic surroundings, and exchange channels directly. Magnetic ordering, Curie temperatures, spin polarization, and spin-dependent transport have all been effectively predicted using GNN-based architectures, including message passing neural networks (MPNNs), equivariant GNNs, and SpinGNNs.78–81 GNNs may provide a more physically grounded prediction framework since they directly correlate material structure to magnetic exchange interactions. Nevertheless, they do not naturally account for long-range magnetic exchange interactions, finite temperature variations, or highly correlated behavior. Research into extending these models with spin Hamiltonians, many-body physics, and first-principles constraints is ongoing.69,70

Training strategies, validation, and dataset bias

The robustness and reliability of the predictive capability of ML models are strongly dependent on the training data, protocols, and evaluation strategies. Random cross-validation tends to overestimate predictive performance when a high degree of compositional or structural correlation exists within the dataset, thereby necessitating composition- or structure-based splitting strategies for accurate assessment of model generalizability. Magnetic materials datasets are often scarce and restricted to particular material families (e.g., transition-metal alloys) or narrow regions of the compositional space (e.g., equilibrium bulk phases). Furthermore, variations in the computational methodologies used for data generation, together with the scarcity of experimentally measured magnetic-property values, remain significant bottlenecks in the development of reliable ML models. The limited availability of comprehensive and accurate data, coupled with insufficient experimental validation, further reduces model performance in systems involving high correlation, strongly correlated electron behavior, and metastable phase formation. To address these challenges, recent studies have focused on uncertainty quantification, the development of benchmark datasets for magnetic materials, and explainable AI methods coupled with rigorous validation procedures to guide ML model development.71,82–84 Bayesian frameworks, ensemble-based uncertainty methods, and probabilistic neural networks are being actively explored to provide more reliable confidence estimates and to identify unreliable extrapolative predictions. Experimental verification of model predictions is also becoming a critical component of accelerated materials discovery.

Feature engineering and physics-informed learning

Feature engineering establishes a critical link between electronic-structure physics and machine-learning (ML) models. Traditional descriptors such as electronegativity, atomic radius, valence electron concentration, and atomic number provide a fundamental understanding of chemical trends. However, advanced descriptors additionally account for orbital occupancy, exchange splitting, spin polarization, symmetry, and magnetic exchange pathways. In recent years, considerable emphasis has been placed on physics-informed and hybrid learning approaches, in which prior knowledge, including symmetry constraints, conservation laws, magnetic Hamiltonians, and electronic-structure theory, is incorporated into ML architectures.61,68,72 The integration of chemically meaningful descriptors with learned representations enhances not only the predictive capability and interpretability of ML models but also their transferability across different material systems.85–87 The predictive performance of ML models for magnetic properties strongly depends on the quality of descriptors that capture orbital interactions, exchange mechanisms, and the spin-dependent nature of magnetism. Consequently, physics-informed learning represents an important step toward the development of reliable and physically interpretable ML frameworks for spintronic-material discovery. Large foundation models pretrained on extensive materials databases to learn transferable chemical and structural representations are expected to transform ML-based spintronics research by enabling accurate prediction of magnetic properties, particularly in situations where only limited training data are available. Increasing model complexity often improves predictive capability but simultaneously reduces interpretability, creating a tradeoff between mechanistic understanding and predictive accuracy. Recent advances in materials AI are increasingly moving beyond conventional supervised learning toward multimodal and foundation-model-based frameworks capable of learning transferable representations from large-scale computational and experimental datasets. Self-supervised and transfer-learning approaches are particularly promising for magnetic materials research because they enable knowledge transfer across chemically related systems and improve predictive capability in data-scarce regimes. In parallel, large language model (LLM)-assisted materials discovery is emerging as a new paradigm for automated literature mining, hypothesis generation, workflow optimization, and integration of domain knowledge with materials databases. The combination of multimodal learning, physics-informed AI, and autonomous or self-driving laboratory platforms integrating high-throughput computation, robotic synthesis, and real-time experimental feedback is expected to substantially accelerate closed-loop discovery and optimization of next-generation spintronic materials.73–75

Regression-based methods provide interpretable predictions with relatively low computational cost but often struggle to capture complex nonlinear magnetic interactions. Ensemble-learning approaches improve nonlinear structure–property mapping and predictive accuracy; however, their extrapolation capability remains limited outside the training domain. In contrast, deep-learning architectures, particularly CNNs and GNNs, are highly effective for learning representations directly from electronic structure and interatomic bonding environments, enabling improved prediction of structure-sensitive magnetic phenomena. Nevertheless, these approaches generally require substantially larger datasets and computational resources. Regression and ensemble-learning frameworks remain highly effective for descriptor-driven magnetic-property prediction, particularly in low-data and chemically constrained regimes where interpretability and computational efficiency are important. In contrast, deep-learning and graph-based architectures become increasingly advantageous for systems involving complex exchange interactions, spin textures, and nonlocal electronic correlations. Unlike conventional ML methods that rely heavily on handcrafted descriptors, deep-learning approaches progressively shift toward automated representation learning directly from electronic structure and crystal topology. Physics-informed and hybrid ML frameworks further bridge these approaches by incorporating domain knowledge, symmetry constraints, and electronic-structure principles into data-driven learning, thereby improving both predictive reliability and physical interpretability. Consequently, the optimal ML strategy for magnetic-material discovery depends strongly on the complexity of the target property, the availability of high-quality datasets, and the ability of descriptors or learned representations to capture the underlying magnetic interactions. Collectively, AI/ML-based approaches for magnetic-material discovery can be viewed as a hierarchical framework in which progressively advanced models exhibit an increasing capability to describe subtle magnetic phenomena. Although significant progress has been achieved in modeling long-range spin interactions, finite-temperature effects, and quantum fluctuations in correlated systems, further advances will likely depend on the development of physics-informed hybrid ML frameworks integrating first-principles electronic-structure theory, spin Hamiltonians, quantum many-body physics, multimodal learning, and experimental feedback mechanisms. Such integrated approaches are expected to improve the predictive reliability of magnetic-property models and accelerate the discovery of next-generation spintronic materials.

AI-driven prediction of magnetic properties

Extending the fundamental concepts and machine learning techniques discussed in the previous sections, this section focuses on the AI-driven prediction of magnetic properties in materials relevant to spintronics. Recent advances in artificial intelligence have enabled the extraction of complex structure–property relationships from compositional, structural, and electronic descriptors with significantly improved efficiency and accuracy compared to conventional approaches. Notably, AI and machine learning models are increasingly being employed to predict key magnetic properties, including magnetic moment, Curie temperature, magnetic anisotropy, coercivity, exchange interactions, and spin-dependent transport quantities. These data-driven methodologies, when combined with first-principles insights, provide a powerful framework for rapid and reliable screening of candidate materials across large chemical spaces. As a result, AI-enabled prediction strategies are playing a crucial role in accelerating the identification, optimization, and rational design of next-generation magnetic and spintronic materials with targeted functionalities.

Tunneling magnetoresistance (TMR) & MTJ performance (RA, spin polarization)

Magnetic tunnel junctions (MTJs), which exhibit variable resistance depending on the relative magnetization directions of the ferromagnetic electrodes, are among the most important elements for spintronic applications, such as magnetic random-access memories (MRAMs) and high-sensitivity magnetic sensors.92 In a recent work, Shenghong and his co-workers93 studied spin-dependent tunneling (tunnel magnetoresistance, TMR) in MgAl2O4 (MAO), specifically, disordered MAO as the insulating barrier in a Fe/disordered-MgAl2O4/Fe(001) magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ). They combine first-principles transport calculations (i.e., DFT + tunneling conductance computations) with machine learning techniques, specifically, Bayesian optimization and LASSO regression, to explore a large configurational space of disordered MAO structures and identify which structural motifs maximize the TMR ratio. The schematics for creating a magnetic tunnel junction with a high tunnel magnetoresistance ratio using Bayesian optimization are displayed in Fig. 6. They examined 1728 different structural possibilities for disordered MAO. Within roughly 300 structural evaluations, they converged to optimal (or nearly optimal) options using Bayesian optimization.

Their analysis reveals that a key factor behind high TMR is the in-plane distance between two Al atoms in the MAO barrier, indicating that increasing Al–Al distance tends to increase the majority-spin Δ1 conductance (the coherent tunneling channel), which enhances the TMR. Additionally, they found that among atomic compositions in the barrier plane, a ratio of Al : Mg : vacancy ≈ 2 : 1 : 1 (on the [001] plane) tends to yield higher TMR ratios, suggesting that not just disorder, but controlled atomic arrangement, can be beneficial. The paper argues that this “materials informatics and first-principles transport” approach is an effective way to design high-performance spintronic devices based on MTJs with complex/disordered barriers. These results demonstrate that machine learning combined with first-principles calculations provides an effective strategy for optimizing MTJ performance by identifying key atomic-scale features governing tunneling behavior. However, extending these approaches to diverse material systems requires improved descriptor generalization.

Spin Hall conductivity/effective spin Hall angle (SHA) and charge to spin conversion

The spin Hall effect (SHE) is a fundamental spintronic phenomenon in which an applied charge current generates a transverse spin current due to spin-dependent deflection of electrons. This effect leads to spin accumulation at material edges and plays a central role in spin–orbitronic devices, including spin transistors and spin valves. A material's efficiency in generating spin currents is quantified by its spin Hall conductivity (SHC), making SHC a critical parameter for identifying candidates for high-performance spintronic applications. Although intrinsic SHC can be computed from first principles, large-scale screening is computationally demanding. A notable exception is the high-throughput study,94 which calculated intrinsic SHC for over 20 000 nonmagnetic crystals, identifying 169 materials with SHC exceeding 1000 (ℏ/e) S cm−1. Beyond this work, most studies remain limited to material-specific investigations due to the high computational cost of ab initio calculations.

Recently, a residual Crystal Graph Convolutional Neural Network (Res-CGCNN) was developed for both regression and classification of Spin Hall conductivities (SHCs) using only structural and compositional descriptors.95 The model achieves a mean absolute error (MAE) of 115.4 (ℏ/e) S cm−1 for SHC regression and an ROC-AUC of 0.86 for classification tasks, demonstrating strong predictive capability. Using the trained model, the authors further perform high-throughput screening of previously unexplored structures in the Materials Project (MP) database. Several novel candidate materials are found in this study, most notably compounds with X = P, Si, and those that show anticipated SHC values more than 1000 (ℏ/e) S cm−1. Such results highlight the strength of DL-based crystal graph models in accelerating the discovery of high-SHC spin Hall materials. Fig. 8a shows the distribution of the Res-CGCNN predicted SHCs for various materials, showing a preponderance of cases with SHCs less than 600 (ℏ/e) S cm−1. Fig. 8b shows the Res-CGCNN predicted probability scores for classification and SHCs for regression for selected materials. The strong agreement with DFT confirms that graph-based models effectively capture complex structure–property relationships for SHC, although their interpretability and dependence on training data remain limiting factors.

Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI) in magnetic materials

The Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI)—an antisymmetric exchange interaction arising in systems with broken inversion symmetry governs the formation of chiral magnetic textures such as skyrmions,96,97 vortices,98 bimerons,99 hedgehogs,100 chiral domain walls.101,102 These textures offer strong potential as information carriers for low-power, high-speed spintronic devices.103,104 Since the interfacial DMI depends sensitively on atomic alignment and chemical bonding, first-principles prediction of extrinsic DMI remains highly challenging. Experimental techniques are also often complex and may require iterative refinement between measurement and modeling. Recently, machine-learning (ML) approaches have emerged as powerful tools capable of predicting DMI magnitudes directly from ground-state spin configurations with high accuracy,105,106 offering a promising route to rapid and scalable characterization of chiral magnetic interactions. Machine learning techniques were shown to reliably extract key physical parameters from complex spin configurations in two-dimensional ferromagnets exhibiting Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interactions.107 Using convolutional neural networks trained on Monte Carlo-generated spin textures, the model accurately predicts quantities such as spin chirality, magnetization, temperature, and external magnetic field directly from raw spin data. The approach remains robust even when trained on partial spin components and successfully generalizes to disordered systems and different length scales. A convolutional neural network (CNN) was demonstrated to predict the magnitude of the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI) directly from standard magnetometry datasets, particularly families of first-order reversal curves (FORCs), without requiring specialized measurement setups.108 They generate training data via micromagnetic simulations, creating many synthetic hysteresis loops under varying parameter sets (with different DMI strengths, possibly other parameter variations)—these are their “fingerprints.” (ii) They train the CNN to map these hysteresis/fingerprint curves to the corresponding DMI magnitude (and perhaps other magnetic parameters). (iii) They then test the CNN on (simulated) test data. The model achieves ≈0.096 (train) and ≈0.155 (test) average absolute error (normalized label units) and the authors release the HoyaFORCs dataset and training code. Since many labs have only standard magnetometers (VSM, SQUID, etc.), this approach opens a pathway to estimate DMI, enabling faster screening, feedback, and materials optimization for spintronics applications. Collectively, these studies highlight the potential of machine learning to extract complex magnetic interactions directly from spin configurations and experimental data. Despite promising accuracy, model transferability and dependence on synthetic datasets require further attention.

Prediction of magnetic moment

Discovering materials with large magnetic moments is important for applications such as motors, generators, magnetic refrigeration, and broadly magnetic/spintronic technologies. Also, predicting large magnetic moments (atomic/per-formula-unit/total magnetization) from composition and structure is a high-value problem for spintronics: it lets you screen large chemical/structural spaces for ferromagnets, high-moment compounds, and materials with desired site-resolved moments without running expensive DFT for every candidate. Modern ML (especially graph neural networks) gives the best tradeoff of accuracy and transferability. A large-scale machine-learning framework was developed to identify inorganic compounds from the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD) that are likely to exhibit ferromagnetism with high magnetic moments, while avoiding computationally expensive first-principles calculations for every candidate material.109 They train several machine-learning models on a dataset drawn from the Materials Project (which includes DFT predictions of magnetic moments), specifically: Graph Neural Networks (GNNs): Using crystal-graph representations (atoms as nodes, bonds/neighbor relations as edges), via architectures like Crystal Graph Convolutional Neural Network (CGCNN) and possibly other variants. Random Forest (RF) models: based on a set of ∼100 engineered descriptors capturing chemical composition and crystal structure features (e.g., elemental properties, structural metrics). Applying these trained models to the ICSD database (hundreds of thousands of entries) to predict which known compounds may have large magnetic moments. Their RF-based models achieved performance comparable to GNNs. They report 15 candidate materials (from ICSD) predicted to have large magnetic moments and (per their knowledge) not yet studied experimentally for magnetism, offering targets for further verification. The work demonstrates that ML models (both descriptor-based and graph-based) can significantly accelerate the search for high-moment magnetic materials, though magnetic properties appear intrinsically harder to predict than many other materials' properties. In short, without running a new DFT for every known structure, they trained ML surrogates to find promising high-moment ferromagnetic candidates, thereby making large-scale screening tractable. To rapidly identify promising candidates preceding computationally expensive DFT validation, a machine-learning model trained on data from the Materials Project was employed to predict atomic magnetic moments (magnetization) across a broad range of hypothetical Mn-based compounds.110 The publication includes a density plot comparing ML-predicted magnetic moments per atom with actual values from the database, showing that the model learns the trends well and can approximate magnetic moments across compositions, even though precise numerical values of the predicted magnetic moments for particular newly proposed materials aren't easily accessible in the publicly accessible abstract summary. The projected and DFT-derived magnetic moment values are correlated in this density map, indicating that the ML model successfully captures the distribution of magnetic moments and can select potential compounds with higher predicted moments for additional first-principles validation.

Curie temperature

Ferromagnetic materials undergo a transition to a paramagnetic state at the Curie temperature, which is governed by intricate quantum mechanical interactions such as magnetic anisotropy and spin exchange.111 The Curie temperature is fundamentally governed by exchange interactions arising from d-orbital occupancy and electronic band structure, which are strongly influenced by chemical composition and bonding environment. Reducing the experimental burden of material synthesis, improving the performance and dependability of magnetic devices, and speeding up the design of magnetic materials are all possible with accurate Tc prediction. This is important because high Tc is necessary for practical spintronic and magnetocaloric devices. Although first-principles methods for estimating Tc, such as density functional theory (DFT) in conjunction with effective Hamiltonians, usually the Heisenberg model, have been widely used, they frequently suffer from high computational costs, limited scalability, dependence on structural data, and a strong dependence on the choice of exchange correlation approximation.112,113

Several independent researchers have used machine learning (ML) to forecast magnetic transition temperatures since its emergence. Using ML to calculate Tc greatly reduces the computational cost and expedites the screening of huge compositional spaces when compared to full-scale ab initio computations. Machine-learning-based regression and feature-selection techniques were employed to evaluate and predict the Curie temperature (Tc) of binary alloys composed of 3d transition metals and 4f rare-earth elements.114 The concentration of the rare-earth element was the most prominent of the 28 descriptors employed, and they discovered that model accuracy was maximized when the first 5 to 10 descriptor features were used. Using a small data set of 108 experimental data, the study showed an R2 of 0.96 and an MAE of 41 K. In line with expectations, they also observed a progressive decline in model performance when more descriptors were added. Notably, ML-driven investigation used only the chemical composition of ferromagnetic materials to estimate their Tc values.115 A random-forest regression framework was constructed using composition-derived descriptors from over 2500 chemical compounds. The final model, incorporating 129 input features, yielded a cross-validation R2 of 0.81 and a test-set R2 of 0.87 with an MAE of 57 K. In another comparative study, when trained on 2500 compounds and tested on 3000 more entries, Random Forest fared better than k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), finding iron-rich systems as more affordable options and cobalt-rich materials as having the greatest Tc.116

A large-scale machine-learning workflow was developed to predict the Curie temperature (Tc) of ferromagnetic materials using chemical composition descriptors, based on a comprehensive database containing approximately 35 000 experimentally measured Tc entries solely based on chemical composition (i.e., without structural or crystallographic input).117 They use a gradient-boosted statistical feature-selection (GBFS) pipeline: starting from many candidate descriptors derived from composition (atomic numbers, periodic table positions, electronegativity, fraction of transition metals, d-electron counts, etc.), they prune for redundancy (multicollinearity reduction), then select the most relevant subset via recursive feature elimination. Using these features, they train a regression model (gradient-boosted trees), fine-tuned via Bayesian hyperparameter optimization. In 10-fold cross-validation, they report R2 ≈ 0.92 ± 0.01, MAE ≈ 40.8 ± 1.9 K, and RMSE ≈ 80 ± 5 K. They show that, despite using only composition-based features (no structural parameters, no crystal data), the model often reproduces (qualitatively and to a fair extent quantitatively) known composition-Tc trends, including non-monotonic dependences (e.g., in Mn–Co alloys) and approximate peak Tc compositions in Ni–Fe. Fig. 10 shows the ML-generated magnetic phase diagrams-prediction of Tc as a function of elemental composition. The accurate reproduction of experimental trends highlights the ability of composition-based ML models to capture non-linear Tc behavior, though the absence of explicit structural information may limit predictive generality.

Using a curated dataset of 2500 ferromagnetic compounds, a data-driven machine-learning approach was proposed to predict Tc using two types of elemental descriptor-based features: one based on stoichiometry-weighted descriptors and the other utilizing Graph Neural Networks (GNNs).118 The efficacy of graph-based feature learning was demonstrated by CatBoost's R2 score of 0.91, which was further enhanced by the usage of GNN-based representations. CatBoost trained on the stoichiometry-weighted descriptors achieved an R2 score of 0.87. They also demonstrated that using an uncurated dataset that is easily accessible online is the cause of the CatBoost model's poor R2 score of 0.66. These developments collectively demonstrate how ML, and especially GNNs, allow precise, composition-based predictions of physical properties, decreasing the need for expensive tests and laborious first-principles simulations and speeding up the discovery of new materials. Taken together, it provides a practical framework for rapid screening of high-Curie temperature materials for spintronic and magnetic applications. Consequently, machine learning provides a powerful framework for predicting Curie temperature across large compositional spaces with high accuracy. Nevertheless, the absence of structural and magnetic interaction details can limit predictive reliability in complex systems.

Magneto crystalline anisotropy energy (MAE)

A tiny, relativistic energy (typically meV per atom or less) resulting from spin–orbit coupling (SOC) and modest orbital hybridization is known as MAE (and PMA as the device-relevant out-of-plane component). Magnetic anisotropy arises from spin–orbit coupling and crystal-field interactions, both of which are strongly governed by atomic number and local coordination environments. Accurate prediction typically requires computationally expensive fully relativistic DFT calculations incorporating spin–orbit coupling (SOC), or even beyond-DFT approaches, particularly for large-scale screening involving multiple compositions, strain conditions, defects, or surfaces. By learning structure–property maps from a small number of high-quality computations and then predicting MAE/PMA orders of magnitude faster for huge candidate spaces, machine learning provides a way to speed up discovery. This approach is being actively extended to anisotropy after demonstrating success for related magnetic parameters (Curie temperature, magnetic moment, coercivity proxies). Lee and co-workers119 achieved state-of-the-art precision (e.g., MAE mean absolute error (0.0241 eV per atom) for formation enthalpy) by encoding stoichiometries as weighted element graphs, demonstrating excellent sample efficiency, uncertainty quantification, and transfer learning.

Machine-learning-assisted prediction of magnetocrystalline anisotropy energy (MAE) was applied to intrinsically magnetic two-dimensional materials to minimize the high computational cost of SOC-enabled DFT calculations.120 Using physically significant descriptors taken from C2DB, such as chemical, structural, and magnetic properties that indirectly encode spin–orbit and symmetry effects pertinent to MAE, the authors build supervised machine learning models. Feature-selection scores were evaluated to minimize overfitting and reduce descriptor redundancy, thereby enhancing model robustness and interpretability.

The following multiple regression techniques are benchmarked: support vector machines, XGBoost, random forests, decision trees, LASSO, and linear regression. With low RMSE values of 0.25 meV for MAE(x) and 0.22 meV for MAE(y), linear regression stands out as the most effective method for predicting MAE. These results indicate that MAE in two-dimensional magnetic systems can be effectively described through near-linear relationships with relevant structural and electronic parameters. A gradient-boosted ML model (trained on DFT data) was utilized to predict MAE (and SOC energies) for transition-metal dimers on substrates.121 The model achieves accurate MAE predictions without needing expensive non-collinear SOC-DFT for each candidate. The MAEs predicted by the ML model are compared with DFT-computed values and two baseline models, Multivariate Linear Regression (MLR) and Multivariate Lasso Regression (LASSO), as shown in Fig. 12a and b. By ensuring predictions for every data point and basing each inference on unobserved data, cross-validation reduces the possibility of overfitting. These results reveal that appropriately selected descriptors enable even simple models to achieve high accuracy in MAE prediction, emphasizing the critical role of feature engineering over model complexity.

A synergistic DFT and machine-learning approach was utilized to explore the modulation of magnetic anisotropy energy (MAE) in a ferromagnetic Fe monolayer interfaced with ferroelectric ABO3 perovskite substrates.122 The authors demonstrate that MAE is extremely sensitive to the interfacial atomic structure and ferroelectric polarization direction using first-principles calculations that include spin–orbit coupling. The MAE is observed to substantially correlate with induced magnetic moments on interfacial BO2 layers and oxygen atoms, emphasizing the involvement of interface magnetism, rather than being simply determined by the extent of ferroelectric polarization. Accordingly, these studies demonstrate that machine learning can effectively accelerate MAE prediction, particularly when combined with physically meaningful descriptors. However, accurately capturing relativistic effects remains a key challenge.

Coercivity

Coercivity is a critical figure of merit for high-performance permanent magnets, yet its reliable prediction remains challenging due to its strong dependence on intrinsic magnetic properties and extrinsic microstructural features. In permanent-magnet research, intrinsic magnetic properties (magnetic moment, anisotropy constants, exchange interactions) can be computed by first-principles methods (e.g., DFT), whereas extrinsic properties like coercivity Hc, which depend on microstructure, defects, grain boundaries, etc., are much harder to predict through ab initio calculations. Traditional micromagnetic simulations can estimate coercivity but tend to over-predict by roughly one order of magnitude compared to experiments; such a discrepancy is known (in part) as the “Brown paradox.” To forecast and compare Hc, a variety of prediction methods are used on both datasets, such as machine learning, statistical inference, and micromagnetic modeling (mumax3 program).123,124 Bhandari et al.125 present an accurate machine-learning framework for predicting the coercivity of permanent magnets by integrating physically motivated descriptors with advanced learning algorithms. The model is trained on a curated dataset by merging experimental measurements with first-principles-derived magnetic parameters, capturing the complex, nonlinear relationships between composition, magnetic anisotropy, saturation magnetization, exchange interactions, and microstructural characteristics.

Among the evaluated models, gradient-boosting-based approaches demonstrate superior predictive accuracy and robustness, significantly outperforming conventional empirical models. The measured coercivity could be replicated using different combinations of grain size and inter-grain exchange coupling. A realistic coercivity of 0.40 T was obtained for grains of approximately 30 nm with an inter-grain/intra-grain Aex ratio of 0.58. Hence, the integration of intrinsic and microstructural features demonstrates that ML can realistically predict coercivity, although capturing complex real-world microstructures remains a key challenge. The output predictions were generated by feeding the tuned XGB ML model with the ab initio-calculated values. Furthermore, a hysteresis loop was found using micromagnetic simulation. Additionally, we used the DFT intrinsic parameters listed in Table 3 to calculate the ML (trained with experiment (ML-Exp) and scaled micromagnetic data (ML-mumax3) independently) and scaled micromagnetic simulated Hc for various materials. The proposed approach enables rapid screening and optimization of candidate magnet compositions, providing a data-driven pathway toward the rational design of next-generation permanent magnets with enhanced coercivity and reduced reliance on critical elements. In conclusion, machine learning offers a promising pathway for predicting coercivity by integrating intrinsic and extrinsic material features. Future models must better account for real microstructural complexity to achieve fully predictive capability.

Heusler alloys and half-metals

Heusler alloys constitute one of the most extensively investigated classes of spintronic materials owing to their remarkable compositional tunability, diverse magnetic behavior, and strong correlation between crystal structure and electronic properties. These intermetallic compounds exhibit a broad range of functionalities, including metallicity, semiconducting behavior, superconductivity, topological electronic states, and, most importantly, half-metallic ferromagnetism.130–133 In half-metallic Heuslers, one spin channel behaves metallically while the opposite spin channel exhibits a bandgap, resulting in nearly 100% spin polarization at the Fermi level. Such highly spin-polarized electronic structures make Heusler alloys promising candidates for spin injectors, magnetic tunnel junctions (MTJs), spin-transfer torque (STT), and spin–orbit torque (SOT) devices. All Heusler compounds can generally be classified into half-Heusler (XYZ), full-Heusler X2YZ, inverse Heusler (XA), and quaternary Heusler (XX′YZ) structure types with respect to atomic occupancy and ordering.133,134 Typically, transition metals are expected at X/X′/Y positions and main-group elements at the Z site. Fig. 14 displays the common crystal structures for semi-Heusler, full-Heusler, inverse Heusler, and quaternary Heusler compounds, respectively, showing their different atomic arrangements (ordered/disordered), which contribute to the diversity of their magnetic and electronic behavior.135 Owing to the wide chemical tunability allowed by these lattice types, their electronic band structures, exchange interactions, and spin polarization can be tailored. Specifically, quaternary Heusler alloys are considered one of the most promising candidates for multifunctional spintronic heterostructures and topological spintronic devices due to their flexibility in elemental combinations and structural tunability.

More recently, GNNs and crystal graph convolutional frameworks have improved AI-driven exploration of spintronic Heusler compounds by explicitly encoding crystal topology and local atomic environment in the training phase.136–138 In contrast to descriptors that rely mostly on composition-derived variables, GNN architectures can directly learn structure-dependent representations based on atomic connectivity and symmetries and thereby achieve better transferability across chemically diverse Heusler materials. These methods have successfully predicted formation energies, magnetic ordering, and electronic structures in high-dimensional alloy space, while graph-based deep-learning workflows coupled with autonomous DFT calculations and active learning methods have been reported as new paradigms for “self-driving” materials discovery platforms for spintronic applications.137,138 To highlight the elemental freedom in a quaternary Heusler system, the periodic table of elements suitable for the X/X′, Y, and Z sites in the crystal structure is shown in Fig. 15.139 The wide chemical space in Heusler materials provides significant opportunities to tailor magnetic ordering, crystal symmetry, and electronic structures with desired properties, which are essential to engineer half-metallicity, Curie temperature Tc, magnetocrystalline anisotropy, and spin transport properties.

The electronic and magnetic properties of Heusler alloys arise from exchange splitting and orbital hybridization between transition-metal d and main-group p states,140 with minority-spin gap formation strongly dependent on valence electron concentration, chemical ordering, and lattice symmetry. Consequently, the Slater–Pauling relationship relating total magnetic moment to valence electron concentration has become a dominant design principle for predicting the magnetic behavior of Heusler systems,141 making Heusler alloys particularly attractive for AI-guided materials discovery, as their magnetic properties exhibit straightforward composition–property relationships that can be translated into interpretable physical descriptors. Machine learning (ML) has since revolutionized Heusler alloy discovery, replacing trial-and-error screening with physically guided prediction workflows and enabling exploration of the computationally expensive to explore exhaustively XYZ and XX′YZ high-dimensional compositional spaces far beyond typical density functional theory (DFT)-based screening approaches. ML workflows can predict formation energies, magnetic moments, Curie temperature, band gap, and half-metallicity from feature descriptors such as valence electron concentration, electronegativity difference, lattice parameters, and atomic radius mismatch.142–144 The performance of ML models for Heusler alloys is heavily dependent on the underlying physics of descriptor–property relationships governing magnetism, and since Heusler properties display non-linear dependencies on composition and electronic filling, ensemble tree-based methods such as Random Forest (RF) and gradient boosting typically outperform linear or kernel-based models for large compositional datasets. One of the first widespread ML approaches in this area used Random Forest regression models for the prediction of lattice constants, magnetic moments, and formation energies in full-, half-, inverse-, and quaternary-Heusler alloys.145 Descriptors derived from composition, along with DFT databases, were used to achieve prediction R-values in the range of 0.80–0.94 for all properties examined. Valence electron concentration was shown to be a highly influential descriptor controlling magnetic moment, once again reinforcing the physical validity of the Slater–Pauling framework for AI-driven magnetic material discovery and highlighting that RF models work well for Heusler systems because they can capture non-linear descriptor–composition relationships while remaining interpretable through feature-importance analysis. ML has also made great strides in predicting Heusler alloys that retain strong spin polarization at finite temperatures—vital for functional spintronic applications. Coupling ML with atomistic spin dynamics and Monte Carlo methods enabled the prediction of finite-temperature spin fluctuations, magnon excitations, electron-correlation effects, and disorder-induced spin rotation in Heusler systems, thereby facilitating the evaluation of material performance under realistic operating conditions.146,147 For example, controlled Z-site alloying has been shown to shift the Fermi level deeper into the minority-spin gap, thereby enhancing the thermal stability of half-metallic compounds against spin-flip excitations. This highlights the effectiveness of AI-driven compositional engineering in overcoming the commonly utilized zero-temperature approximation in standard DFT calculations to predict realistic spintronic performance. One major shortcoming in current AI-driven Heusler research is that these models heavily rely on DFT calculations on idealized Heusler structures and do not explicitly incorporate magnetic fluctuations at finite temperatures, electron correlations, or chemical disorder, all of which ultimately reduce experimental spin polarization and magnetic stability.

Another major challenge in the AI-driven discovery of Heusler compounds is that many predicted half-metallic Heuslers are found to be thermodynamically unstable or highly susceptible to antisite disorder, phase segregation, and nonstoichiometric defects during synthesis, leading to substantial reductions in spin polarization and Curie temperature.148,149 This motivates the integration of experimental synthesis knowledge, uncertainty quantification, and disorder awareness into AI algorithms. ML models have also made significant headway in the regression and classification of materials identified through high-throughput exploration of half-Heusler and quaternary Heuslers. Biliska and Winiarski used Support Vector Regression (SVR) to predict lattice parameters, bulk modulus, thermal conductivity, and electronic stability of 18-electron half-Heuslers.150 It was shown that SVR performed very well for chemically constrained and small datasets where descriptor dimensions are limited; however, it proved less robust and scalable than RF or boosting methods for large compositional datasets. More recently, Matsumoto et al. presented interpretable ML frameworks trained on ab initio magnetic interaction data to predict Curie temperatures for various Heusler classes.151 They found that exchange interaction strength, atomic size mismatch, and chemical ordering were all important descriptors for predicting the Curie temperature of a given Heusler alloy.

In the high-Tc regime, ML algorithms such as Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) have been developed to enable high-throughput screening of hundreds of thousands of candidates, such as in the recent study of quaternary XX′YZ Heuslers, which yielded high-Tc ferromagnets with excellent predictive accuracy, as illustrated in Fig. 16. The study used roughly 6500 experimental and computational data points to yield prediction high predictive accuracy and demonstrated highly accurate prediction of the Curie temperature.152 Such workflows allow the screening of nearly 100 000 candidate alloys and the identification of novel ferromagnetic Heusler compounds suitable for MTJ electrodes. Gradient boosting methods provide more accurate rankings and can handle strong non-linear descriptor–composition relationships better than standard RF algorithms and are therefore ideally suited for the chemical diversity found within the vast Heusler compositional space.

To highlight ML-guided Heusler discovery, a high-throughput statistical analysis of ML outcomes was considered. To efficiently screen stable magnetic Heusler compounds, a research group evaluated more than 236 000 prototype structures. The study revealed that the compositional search space was extremely sparse, with only a very small fraction of compounds simultaneously satisfying both thermodynamic stability and magnetic ordering requirements. The obtained results highlighted the importance of physically motivated descriptor-based AI frameworks for targeted spintronic materials discovery.131 This study also highlights a significant issue in AI-driven materials discovery: simply enlarging the dataset is insufficient to accelerate discovery without physics-informed descriptors. Furthermore, optimization-driven ML-assisted frameworks have shown considerable potential for engineering tailored magnetic functionalities. In a recent application, Random Forest regression was coupled with a genetic algorithm to optimize magnetocaloric properties in Ni–Co–Mn–Ti Heusler alloys.153 Consequently, the AI-guided optimization protocol enabled the discovery of a novel composition that demonstrated large magnetization changes associated with martensitic transitions, showcasing the potential of evolutionary ML strategies for multifunctional magnetic materials. Transfer-learning approaches and ML interatomic potentials have likewise played important roles in the rapid prediction of magnetic stability and magnetocrystalline anisotropy in compositionally complex Heusler materials. Although deep-learning and transfer-learning frameworks often achieve superior predictive performance, several studies report reduced physical interpretability compared with interpretable ensemble-learning methods such as RF and gradient boosting. Consequently, an important contradiction within the current literature is that highly accurate models do not always provide physically transparent insights into the mechanisms governing magnetic ordering and half-metallicity. As a result, simpler descriptor-based models remain widely used because they enable the extraction of physically meaningful relationships between electronic structure, valence electron concentration, and magnetic behavior. This trade-off between predictive accuracy and interpretability remains one of the central challenges in AI-driven spintronic materials discovery. Beyond conventional predictive modeling, inverse-design strategies based on generative artificial intelligence are increasingly being explored for targeted spintronic materials discovery.155,156 Variational autoencoders (VAEs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and Bayesian optimization frameworks enable autonomous exploration of chemically complex Heusler compositional spaces toward predefined objectives such as high Curie temperature, robust half-metallicity, low magnetic damping, and enhanced spin Hall conductivity. These approaches significantly reduce the dimensionality of combinatorial alloy optimization and provide an efficient route toward next-generation multifunctional spintronic materials design. Unsupervised learning approaches have also revealed hidden structure–property relationships within large Heusler datasets. In a recent work,138 K-means clustering and Random Forest regression were used to analyze magnetic behavior across 1153 Heusler compounds. Their models achieved strong predictive performance for magnetic moment and saturation magnetization, with R2 values of approximately 0.82–0.85. High-throughput screening of the OQMD database subsequently identified promising magnetic candidates that were later validated using DFT calculations, further demonstrating the reliability of integrated AI-DFT discovery frameworks. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that the future of Heusler alloy discovery will likely depend on hybrid AI frameworks integrating physically interpretable descriptors, graph-based structural learning, finite-temperature magnetic modeling, uncertainty-aware optimization, and autonomous high-throughput experimentation. The combination of explainable machine learning, generative inverse design, and experimentally informed synthesis constraints is expected to substantially accelerate the discovery of robust, scalable, and technologically viable spintronic Heusler materials for next-generation memory, logic, and neuromorphic devices.

Topological materials for skyrmions and spin–orbit devices

Topological materials represent a rapidly emerging class of quantum materials in which electronic wavefunctions possess nontrivial topological invariants protected by crystal symmetry, spin–orbit coupling (SOC), and band inversion mechanisms.157–159 Unlike conventional materials, whose transport properties are governed primarily by band dispersion, topological systems exhibit symmetry-protected surface or edge states, large Berry curvature, spin–momentum locking, and dissipation-resistant transport channels. These unique quantum phenomena provide a powerful foundation for next-generation spintronic technologies, including spin Hall effect (SHE) devices, spin–orbit torque (SOT) switching, magnetic skyrmion memories, and low-power quantum information platforms.157–163 The integration of topology and magnetism is particularly important for spintronic applications because broken time-reversal or inversion symmetry can generate strong Berry curvature and large Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interactions (DMI), both of which are essential for stabilizing chiral spin textures and enabling efficient spin–charge interconversion.160–162 In topological insulator/ferromagnet heterostructures, spin–momentum-locked surface states produce highly efficient SOT generation, enabling current-induced magnetization switching at substantially lower current densities than conventional heavy-metal systems.163 Similarly, Weyl semimetals and magnetic topological phases exhibit exceptionally strong anomalous Hall conductivity and topological transport responses arising from momentum-space Berry curvature singularities. These properties make topological materials promising candidates for THz spintronic devices, skyrmion racetrack memories, and ultrafast low-dissipation logic architectures. Despite their enormous technological potential, discovering topological spintronic materials remains exceptionally challenging because nontrivial topology emerges from the simultaneous interplay of SOC, crystal symmetry, magnetic ordering, electron correlation, and band inversion. Conventional first-principles identification of topological phases requires computationally intensive symmetry analysis, Wannierization, Berry curvature calculations, and full band-structure validation. Consequently, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are increasingly being employed to accelerate topological materials discovery through rapid identification of chemically and structurally favorable candidate compounds before undertaking expensive electronic-structure computations. A major advance in this area was the introduction of chemically interpretable heuristic descriptors capable of predicting topological behavior directly from composition. A “topogivity” framework was developed, in which each element is assigned a numerical topological propensity parameter (τe) quantifying its likelihood of contributing to nontrivial topology.164 Remarkably, this approach predicts topological character using only chemical composition without requiring structural symmetry analysis or wavefunction calculations. Using nested cross-validation, the model achieved approximately 82.7% classification accuracy, demonstrating that topological tendencies can be partially encoded at the elemental level. More importantly, the work established a physically interpretable descriptor-driven paradigm linking heavy-element chemistry, SOC strength, and band inversion probability. Such descriptor-based approaches are computationally efficient and highly scalable, making them particularly valuable for large-scale chemical screening.

Machine learning classification frameworks have subsequently expanded the discovery of magnetic topological materials within chemically constrained spaces. Boulton and Kim developed Decision Tree, Random Forest (RF), and XGBoost classifiers to identify magnetic topological insulators and semimetals within layered AxByCz compounds (A = Ni, V, Co, Eu; B= Bi, Sb; C = Te, Se).165 Their XGBoost model achieved approximately 87.6% accuracy for metal/nonmetal classification, 95.7% accuracy for trivial versus topological insulators, and 84.1% accuracy for trivial versus topological metals. The resulting ML-assisted screening identified several candidate magnetic topological insulators, later validated through density functional theory (DFT) and maximally localized Wannier function calculations. This study also illustrates why gradient-boosting models often outperform simpler RF and support vector machine (SVM) approaches in topological materials discovery. Because topological behavior depends on highly nonlinear interactions among composition, symmetry, and relativistic effects, boosting-based algorithms provide superior handling of complex descriptor coupling and imbalanced datasets.

Multiple ML frameworks, including Random Forest, XGBoost, MODNet, Automatminer, and graph neural networks (GNNs), were systematically benchmarked for binary and multiclass topological classification tasks. Among these approaches, XGBoost achieved approximately 92% binary classification accuracy and ∼85% multiclass accuracy, demonstrating that chemically informed ensemble-learning methods remain highly competitive even relative to more computationally intensive deep-learning architectures. Importantly, interpretable descriptors such as packing efficiency and p-valence electron fraction emerged as dominant predictors governing topological behavior, reinforcing the critical role of physically meaningful descriptors in AI-driven quantum materials design. Although descriptor-based models exhibit strong interpretability and computational efficiency, they often struggle to fully capture subtle symmetry-driven and correlation-induced topological transitions. This limitation has motivated the increasing adoption of graph-based deep-learning frameworks capable of explicitly encoding crystal connectivity, local bonding environments, and relativistic interactions. Researchers developed a graph neural network integrated with persistent homology descriptors to classify topological materials directly from crystal structures.167 By simultaneously incorporating geometric and topological information, their framework achieved approximately 91.4% accuracy and an F1 score of ∼88.5%, outperforming conventional graph-based approaches such as CGCNN. More importantly, the model successfully generalized to previously unseen and out-of-distribution materials, demonstrating the superior transferability of graph-based learning for chemically diverse topological systems. In contrast to RF and SVM models, GNN architectures excel because they preserve explicit structural relationships and symmetry information that are often lost in descriptor-only approaches. However, despite their higher predictive performance, deep-learning models generally exhibit lower interpretability, making it more difficult to extract physically transparent design principles governing topology and magnetism.

Recent developments in inverse design and generative AI have further transformed topological materials discovery from passive screening into proactive materials generation. Researchers introduced a crystal-topology materials transformer (CTMT) framework that combines deep generative models, stability filtering, and lightweight topology predictors to generate entirely new topological compounds.168 Using this end-to-end AI-driven workflow, the authors identified 16 topological semimetals and 4 topological insulators, including several low-symmetry and Kramers–Weyl systems difficult to identify using conventional approaches. Generative frameworks are particularly important because they expand exploration beyond known databases into previously inaccessible compositional spaces. Unlike traditional high-throughput screening, which merely ranks existing compounds, inverse-design approaches actively generate candidate structures optimized for target properties such as large topological band gaps, chiral symmetry, or enhanced Berry curvature. This capability is especially relevant for room-temperature spintronic devices, where large SOC-induced gaps and robust magnetic ordering are essential for practical operation. Quantum-inspired ML approaches have additionally emerged as promising alternatives for capturing complex electronic correlations in topological systems. Xu et al.169 developed a quantum-classical hybrid neural network (QANN) that maps compositional descriptors onto quantum probability amplitudes to capture pairwise elemental interactions neglected in conventional heuristic frameworks. By incorporating complex-valued neural representations, the model preserved physical interpretability while significantly improving predictive performance for topological classification. The framework successfully identified several previously unreported topological compounds subsequently validated through DFT calculations, demonstrating that quantum-inspired AI architectures may provide a physically motivated route toward modeling strongly correlated topological phases. High-throughput ML screening has also significantly accelerated the discovery of two-dimensional (2D) topological materials relevant to nanoscale spintronic devices. Schleder et al. employed ML classifiers trained on large DFT databases to predict nontrivial topology in 2D materials with >90% accuracy.170 Their framework identified 56 nontrivial compounds, including 17 previously unknown topological insulators later confirmed through first-principles calculations. Because 2D topological systems enable efficient electrostatic gating, strain engineering, and interfacial DMI control, they are particularly promising for skyrmion-based devices and low-power SOT applications.

More recently, hybrid AI frameworks integrating large language model (LLM) embeddings with physical descriptors have emerged as a new direction in topological materials discovery. The TXL Fusion framework was developed by integrating composition-based descriptors, crystallographic symmetry information, and large language model (LLM)-generated semantic embeddings to classify topological insulators and semimetals across datasets containing more than 38 000 compounds.171 By integrating chemical heuristics with semantic learning, the framework achieved improved predictive accuracy and generalization relative to descriptor-only approaches. Similarly, it has been demonstrated that simple frequency-based elemental descriptors combined with RF and SVM classifiers can effectively predict Dirac, Weyl, and nodal-line semimetals, with classification accuracies of 82–83%.172 These studies highlight an important emerging trend in AI-assisted quantum materials discovery: relatively simple descriptor-based models often remain highly competitive when chemically meaningful features are carefully engineered. Another breakthrough in descriptor-driven topological discovery, where SISSO (Sure Independence Screening and Sparsifying Operator) framework was used to derive low-dimensional descriptors capable of classifying topological insulators using only elemental properties such as atomic number and electronegativity.173 AI-assisted high-throughput screening of more than four million alloyed tetradymite compositions identified nearly two million potential topological insulators, thereby dramatically expanding the accessible topological materials landscape. Such studies demonstrate that interpretable descriptor-based AI models can dramatically accelerate topological materials exploration while maintaining strong physical transparency.

Machine learning has also accelerated the identification of magnetic Chern insulators and quantum anomalous Hall systems. Recent work demonstrated a supervised neural network framework capable of predicting topological character directly from chemical formula and Hubbard U parameters without explicit symmetry analysis.174 Combined with high-throughput screening of 2D materials databases, the framework identified multiple candidate magnetic Chern insulators exhibiting experimentally accessible band gaps. These systems are particularly attractive for dissipationless spintronic transport and ultralow-power quantum electronics. Despite these remarkable advances, several important limitations remain in AI-driven topological materials discovery. Many existing ML models are trained primarily on zero-temperature DFT datasets that neglect finite-temperature magnetic fluctuations, disorder effects, electron correlation, and synthesis constraints. Consequently, predicted topological phases may not always remain stable under experimentally realistic operating conditions. Furthermore, an important contradiction within the literature is that highly accurate deep-learning frameworks frequently sacrifice interpretability, whereas simpler descriptor-based models provide clearer physical insight but may fail to capture subtle symmetry-protected phenomena. This trade-off between predictive accuracy and physical interpretability remains one of the central challenges in AI-assisted topological spintronics. Collectively, current studies establish several unifying design principles governing topological spintronic materials. First, heavy-element substitution enhances SOC and promotes band inversion. Second, symmetry breaking generates Berry curvature and interfacial DMI essential for skyrmion stabilization and efficient SOT generation. Third, magnetic ordering enables access to quantum anomalous transport phenomena and topological magnetic phases. These physically grounded principles, combined with AI-driven high-throughput discovery frameworks, are rapidly transforming the design of topological materials for next-generation spintronic technologies.

Two-dimensional magnetic materials and van der Waals spintronic systems

Two-dimensional (2D) magnetic materials have emerged as one of the most promising platforms for next-generation spintronics because they combine atomically thin geometry with tunable magnetism, strong spin–orbit coupling (SOC), and interfacial quantum phenomena.175–178 Following the experimental realization of intrinsic ferromagnetism in layered compounds such as CrI3 and Cr2Ge2Te6, the field rapidly expanded toward the discovery of ultrathin magnetic semiconductors, metals, half-metals, and topological magnets suitable for nanoscale spintronic architectures. Unlike bulk magnets, where long-range magnetic ordering is relatively robust, magnetism in 2D systems is highly sensitive to reduced dimensionality, interlayer coupling, lattice symmetry, strain, defects, and substrate interactions. Consequently, discovering stable 2D magnets with high Curie temperature (Tc), large magnetic anisotropy energy (MAE), and efficient spin–charge conversion remains a major challenge for both theory and experiment.

From a physics perspective, long-range magnetic ordering in low-dimensional systems is stabilized through the combined effects of exchange interactions, SOC, and magnetic anisotropy, consistent with the Mermin–Wagner theorem.179–181 Descriptor-driven studies consistently identify orbital occupancy, local coordination environment, crystal-field splitting, and longer-range exchange interactions (J2 and J3) as dominant factors governing magnetic stability and ordering in layered systems. In addition, broken inversion symmetry and strong SOC generate interfacial Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI), enabling chiral spin textures and skyrmionic states that are central to low-power spintronic technologies.179,180 These mechanisms directly connect atomic-scale structure to emergent spin functionality, thereby establishing a natural foundation for AI-assisted materials discovery. AI and machine learning (ML) approaches have become essential for navigating the enormous chemical and structural design space associated with 2D magnetic materials. Traditional density functional theory (DFT)-based searches are computationally demanding because reliable prediction of magnetic ordering, MAE, spin Hall conductivity (SHC), and structural stability requires spin-polarized calculations, often including SOC and finite-temperature corrections. Recent ML-driven discovery pipelines demonstrate how descriptor engineering, graph learning, and active learning can substantially accelerate identification of promising 2D spintronic materials. An interpretable ML framework combining descriptor engineering, SHAP analysis, and online DFT validation was developed to screen more than ∼2400 candidate 2D materials for intrinsic ferromagnetism.185 Their workflow identified ∼615 potential magnetic systems and ultimately predicted ∼60 novel 2D ferromagnetic materials, including Cr2NF2 as a candidate antiferromagnetic ferroelectric multiferroic, Os2Cl4 as a half-metal, and Fe3GeSe2 as a metallic ferromagnet. Importantly, the study demonstrated that orbital occupancy and coordination environment act as dominant descriptors controlling magnetic ordering. By incorporating interpretability analysis, the authors linked ML predictions directly to underlying exchange physics rather than treating the model as a purely empirical black box. Xu and his colleagues186 demonstrated the effectiveness of descriptor-guided screening by investigating 672 transition-metal oxyhalide monolayers. Their ML-assisted framework identified 78 ferromagnetic systems, including 38 materials with predicted Curie temperatures above 200 K, approaching technologically relevant operating regimes. Notably, the study identified second-nearest-neighbor exchange interaction J2 as a dominant descriptor controlling magnetic ordering within this chemical family. This finding illustrates how AI frameworks increasingly function not only as predictive tools but also as mechanisms for extracting physically meaningful magnetic design principles. Graph-based deep learning approaches have proven particularly powerful for 2D materials discovery because magnetic behavior in layered systems strongly depends on local bonding topology and structural connectivity. Recently, graph neural networks (GNNs) together with a Crystal Diffusion Variational Autoencoder were employed to generate and screen more than 11 000 candidate monolayer structures.187 Using ALIGNN-based models trained on ∼1190 magnetic monolayers, the framework achieved a mean absolute error of only 0.039 eV per atom for formation-energy prediction and ∼93% accuracy for magnetic classification. After applying stability and magnetization constraints, DFT validation confirmed 167 stable magnetic monolayers with substantial magnetic moments. The success of GNN-based approaches highlights an important methodological distinction in AI-driven materials discovery. Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) models generally perform well when physically meaningful descriptors such as coordination number, electronegativity, or valence electron count are already known. These models are computationally efficient and highly interpretable, making them suitable for smaller datasets and chemically intuitive screening tasks. However, they often struggle to capture nonlocal interactions, symmetry-dependent magnetic coupling, and complex orbital hybridization effects. In contrast, GNNs directly encode crystal connectivity and atomic environments, allowing them to learn structural and electronic correlations automatically from graph representations. Consequently, GNNs typically outperform descriptor-based models for complex low-symmetry materials and heterostructures, although their reduced interpretability remains a significant limitation. This tradeoff between interpretability and predictive accuracy represents one of the central methodological contradictions in AI-driven spintronic materials discovery. Another important advance is the emergence of active-learning frameworks that iteratively couple ML prediction with targeted first-principles calculations. Active learning was shown to dramatically enhance the discovery of 2D materials with intrinsically high spin Hall conductivity.188 Starting from only 24 chemically diverse systems, the model iteratively refined its training dataset through expected-improvement acquisition strategies and expanded the search space to 41 high-value candidates out of nearly 2000 materials. Remarkably, the best-discovered material exhibited SHC values approximately 23 times larger than the top candidate identified during the initial screening stage. This study demonstrates the efficiency of uncertainty-aware AI frameworks for discovering rare quantum functionalities in extremely large compositional spaces.

AI-driven discovery has also expanded beyond conventional ferromagnets toward magnetic topological materials and flat-band systems. Jinlan Wang et al.189 introduced an active-learning computational framework for screening more than 13 000 candidate 2D magnetic materials for topological phases. By combining ensemble surrogate models with adaptive sampling strategies, the workflow identified 63 promising magnetic topological candidates exhibiting symmetry-protected edge states and nontrivial electronic topology. Particularly notable was the prediction that CoO2 undergoes multiple topological phase transitions under different magnetic configurations, including transitions between insulating and semi-metallic states. These predictions were subsequently validated through DFT calculations, demonstrating the capability of AI-assisted frameworks to discover multifunctional quantum materials with coupled magnetic and topological behavior. Similarly, machine-learning-assisted prediction of flat-band 2D materials was achieved directly from atomic configurations, eliminating the need for computationally expensive precomputed band structures.190 By combining multimodal learning with data-driven flatness metrics, the framework successfully identified kagome-based systems exhibiting strong band flatness and nontrivial topology. These flat-band materials are especially attractive for correlated quantum phases and low-dissipation spintronic transport because enhanced electronic localization can amplify interaction-driven magnetic and topological phenomena. Beyond isolated monolayers, van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures provide an additional degree of freedom through interfacial coupling and proximity effects.191 Engineering stacking order, interlayer spacing, and interface chemistry enables precise control over exchange interactions, DMI, and spin transport. Recent studies report spin Hall conductivities on the order of ∼103–104 (ℏ/e) (Ω cm)−1 and spin–orbit torque efficiencies approaching ∼0.1–0.3 in optimized vdW systems,192,193 placing them in direct competition with conventional heavy-metal spintronic platforms. These advances establish 2D magnets and vdW heterostructures as promising candidates for ultrathin spin filters, voltage-controlled spin transistors, neuromorphic spintronic synapses, and energy-efficient skyrmion devices.

Despite rapid progress, important limitations remain in AI-assisted discovery of 2D magnetic materials. Most existing ML models rely heavily on zero-temperature DFT datasets and therefore inadequately capture finite-temperature magnetic fluctuations, magnon excitations, and substrate-induced disorder. As a result, predicted Curie temperatures and magnetic anisotropies often deviate substantially from experimental measurements. Descriptor-based models offer strong interpretability but may oversimplify complex exchange interactions and many-body effects, whereas deep-learning approaches achieve higher predictive accuracy at the cost of reduced physical transparency. In addition, many current datasets remain chemically imbalanced, favoring structurally stable layered compounds while underrepresenting metastable or strongly correlated systems. Consequently, future progress will require tighter integration of finite-temperature physics, uncertainty quantification, experimental feedback loops, and physically informed graph-learning architectures. Together, these studies demonstrate that AI-driven discovery of 2D magnetic materials is evolving from empirical screening toward physics-informed inverse design. Across diverse material families, several unifying principles consistently emerge: exchange interactions beyond nearest neighbors strongly influence magnetic ordering; SOC and inversion-symmetry breaking govern anisotropy and chiral interactions; and orbital hybridization controls spin polarization and electronic topology. These physically grounded descriptors provide a transferable framework for rational design of atomically thin spintronic materials and quantum heterostructures.

Key challenges in AI-driven magnetic property prediction and spintronic materials discovery

The rapid development of AI-driven approaches for materials discovery and spintronic device design has significantly accelerated predictive capabilities, yet several fundamental and practical barriers continue to limit their full potential. Despite notable progress in machine learning models, graph-based representations, and autonomous workflows, issues such as limited high-quality datasets, weak model interpretability, poor transferability across materials classes, and the high computational cost of multiscale simulations remain unresolved. In addition, bridging the gap between computational predictions and experimental validation continues to be a critical challenge for reliable materials realization. Data limitations and representation of complex magnetic systems

A major limitation in AI-driven spintronic materials discovery arises from the quality and physical realism of available datasets. Unlike conventional materials systems, spintronic materials involve coupled spin, charge, orbital, and lattice interactions governed by relativistic effects, competing exchange mechanisms, and complex magnetic orderings. However, most machine-learning models are trained using high-throughput DFT datasets generated under idealized conditions, typically assuming zero temperature, perfect crystallinity, and simplified collinear magnetic configurations.

As illustrated in Fig. 20a, this creates a substantial gap between computational training environments and experimentally realized magnetic systems, where disorder, defects, finite-temperature spin fluctuations, and non-collinear magnetism strongly influence spintronic behavior. This limitation becomes particularly severe in chemically complex systems. In high-entropy alloys, local chemical disorder and short-range ordering generate heterogeneous magnetic environments that cannot be represented using composition-averaged descriptors. Similarly, correlated oxides exhibit strong electron correlation and competing magnetic phases poorly captured by standard datasets, while in two-dimensional and van der Waals systems, substrate coupling and interfacial reconstruction introduce additional complexity. Despite the rapid expansion of materials databases, experimentally validated datasets for magnetic anisotropy, spin Hall conductivity, and spin–orbit torque efficiency remain limited. Consequently, many reported model accuracies primarily reflect interpolation within known chemical spaces rather than true extrapolative prediction capability.

Limitations of electronic-structure methods and their integration with AI

The predictive capability of AI models is fundamentally constrained by the accuracy of the electronic-structure methods used to generate training data. Although DFT remains the foundation of high-throughput materials discovery, it exhibits well-known limitations in describing strongly correlated systems, non-collinear magnetism, and finite-temperature magnetic behavior. In correlated oxides, semi-local functionals frequently fail to capture Mott physics, whereas accurate prediction of topological band inversion and Berry curvature requires computationally demanding relativistic treatments. These limitations propagate directly into machine-learning workflows, embedding systematic biases into predicted quantities such as Curie temperature, magnetic anisotropy energy, exchange coupling, and spin–orbit coupling strength. As schematically summarized in Fig. 20b, many interactions central to spintronics, including spin–orbit coupling, Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI), and competing exchange mechanisms, occur on energy scales of only a few meV, making predictions highly sensitive to numerical approximations and descriptor selection. Another major limitation is that most AI frameworks are trained on static equilibrium electronic structures, whereas practical spintronic devices operate under non-equilibrium conditions involving spin-current injection, thermal fluctuations, and ultrafast magnetization dynamics. Current AI models, therefore, remain poorly suited for predicting dynamic spintronic behavior. Although beyond-DFT approaches integrated with machine learning can improve physical fidelity, their computational cost limits scalability across large chemical spaces.

Transferability and generalization across diverse material classes

Limited transferability remains a major obstacle in AI-driven spintronic materials discovery. Models trained on specific material families frequently fail when applied to chemically or structurally distinct systems because magnetic behavior depends strongly on local chemistry, symmetry, dimensionality, and electronic topology. Descriptor spaces effective for ordered alloys often become unreliable in disordered or low-dimensional systems. For example, valence electron concentration serves as a useful predictor of magnetic moment in Heusler alloys but loses predictive power in high-entropy alloys, where local chemical fluctuations dominate magnetic interactions. Similarly, descriptors developed for bulk materials frequently fail in two-dimensional systems because reduced dimensionality and interfacial effects fundamentally alter exchange interactions and magnetic anisotropy. As illustrated in Fig. 20c, model performance frequently deteriorates when applied outside the domain of the training dataset, highlighting the difficulty of extrapolative prediction in unexplored chemical spaces. A further challenge is the tradeoff between interpretability and predictive complexity. Descriptor-based methods such as Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) models provide chemically interpretable trends and feature-importance analysis but may fail to capture higher-order structural correlations and symmetry-dependent phenomena. In contrast, graph neural networks (GNNs) and deep-learning architectures generally achieve higher predictive accuracy for complex materials systems while sacrificing interpretability. Balancing physical transparency with predictive generalization, therefore, remains a central challenge for next-generation AI frameworks in spintronics.

Prediction of emergent phenomena and device-relevant properties

Most current AI-driven discovery frameworks focus on predicting intrinsic bulk properties such as magnetic moment, formation energy, or electronic bandgap, whereas practical spintronic functionality is governed by emergent interfacial and transport phenomena. Effects including spin–orbit torque (SOT), Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI), skyrmion stability, and spin transport depend strongly on interfaces, disorder, dimensionality, and non-equilibrium conditions, making them difficult to compute accurately and poorly represented in existing datasets. This creates a critical disconnect between computational discovery and device implementation. For instance, a material predicted to possess high spin polarization may perform poorly in a magnetic tunnel junction due to interfacial scattering and spin-memory loss. Similarly, skyrmion stability depends on subtle competition among exchange interactions, magnetic anisotropy, DMI, and thermal fluctuations that cannot be captured using simplified descriptors alone. Fig. 20d schematically illustrates this multi-scale challenge, where atomistic electronic structure, mesoscale spin dynamics, and device-level transport collectively determine spintronic performance. Current AI models rarely integrate these coupled length and time scales within a unified framework. Consequently, materials identified through high-throughput screening may not exhibit optimal device-level functionality. Bridging this gap will require the integration of first-principles calculations with spin dynamics, charge and spin transport simulations, and device-level modeling frameworks to enable direct prediction of experimentally relevant spintronic performance metrics.

Integration with experiments and autonomous discovery

A final challenge is the limited integration between AI prediction, experimental synthesis, and real-time validation. Although machine-learning models can rapidly identify promising candidate materials, many predicted compounds are metastable, difficult to synthesize, or require highly controlled growth conditions. In spintronic systems, functionality is highly sensitive to disorder, stoichiometry, strain, and interface quality, making experimental realization substantially more difficult than computational prediction. Current discovery pipelines also remain largely one-directional: theoretical predictions are experimentally tested, but experimental outcomes rarely feed back into iterative model refinement. As shown in Fig. 20e, the absence of continuous feedback between computation, synthesis, characterization, and retraining limits the development of adaptive and experimentally informed learning frameworks. Recent advances in active learning, robotic synthesis, and autonomous laboratories provide a pathway toward closed-loop discovery systems in which AI prediction, synthesis, characterization, and retraining occur continuously. Such adaptive workflows are especially important for spintronics because functional behavior emerges from coupled electronic, magnetic, and interfacial phenomena across multiple scales. Developing uncertainty-aware and experimentally adaptive AI platforms will therefore be essential for transforming machine learning from a high-throughput screening tool into a predictive framework for practical spintronic materials and devices.

Future roadmap: AI-driven spintronics

To move beyond descriptive analysis and provide actionable guidance, we propose a unified closed-loop AI workflow for spintronics that enables systematic, scalable, and predictive materials discovery. As illustrated in Fig. 21, this framework integrates data generation, physics-based modeling, uncertainty-aware optimization, and experimental validation into a continuous adaptive cycle, thereby establishing a direct pathway from fundamental materials understanding to device-level realization. The proposed roadmap emphasizes not only accelerated discovery of novel spintronic materials but also the development of reliable, interpretable, and experimentally realizable AI frameworks capable of predicting complex magnetic behavior across multiple length and time scales. The proposed workflow consists of five interconnected stages. It begins with data generation, where high-quality datasets are obtained from first-principles calculations, high-throughput simulations, and experimentally measured magnetic and transport properties. Particular emphasis will be placed on developing FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable) magnetic materials databases containing experimentally validated information on magnetic anisotropy, spin Hall conductivity, spin–orbit torque efficiency, exchange interactions, and finite-temperature magnetic behavior. This is followed by descriptor engineering, where physics-informed, symmetry-aware, uncertainty-sensitive, and structure-dependent features are constructed to capture the underlying magnetic interactions governing spintronic functionality. In future frameworks, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) and interpretable machine-learning approaches are expected to play increasingly important roles in identifying physically meaningful descriptors and uncovering hidden structure–property relationships in complex magnetic systems. In the third stage, AI/ML models, including predictive, graph-based, multimodal, and generative architectures, are developed to learn structure–property relationships across chemically and structurally diverse materials spaces. These models enable both forward prediction and inverse design, allowing targeted optimization of key spintronic properties such as Curie temperature, magnetic anisotropy, spin polarization, spin Hall conductivity, magnetic damping, and spin–orbit coupling strength.

Unified AI-driven workflow and future roadmap for spintronic materials discovery.

Unlike conventional high-throughput screening approaches, inverse-design strategies specify desired functionalities in advance and autonomously search for candidate materials satisfying predefined performance criteria. The integration of physical constraints, including symmetry preservation, thermodynamic stability, synthesizability, and device compatibility, will ensure that AI predictions remain experimentally realistic and technologically relevant. Experimental validation subsequently closes the discovery loop through synthesis, characterization, and iterative retraining, thereby enabling continuous improvement of model accuracy and reliability. Future AI-driven platforms are expected to increasingly incorporate active learning and Bayesian uncertainty quantification to guide adaptive exploration of unexplored chemical spaces and prioritize high-confidence candidate materials for experimental synthesis. Such uncertainty-aware workflows are particularly important in spintronics because functional properties often emerge from subtle competition between exchange interactions, spin–orbit coupling, disorder, and interfacial effects. At the same time, the emergence of autonomous, AI-guided experimental platforms and self-driving laboratories will enable real-time interaction between prediction, synthesis, characterization, and feedback-driven optimization, significantly accelerating the discovery of functional magnetic materials for next-generation spintronic applications.

In parallel, multimodal and multiscale learning approaches will increasingly unify computational and experimental information across different spatial and temporal scales, enabling seamless transitions from electronic structure and atomistic spin interactions to mesoscale spin dynamics and device-level transport behavior. This integration will play a central role in bridging the long-standing gap between materials discovery and technological deployment. In this context, coupling first-principles calculations with spin-dynamics simulations, transport modeling, and device-aware optimization frameworks will facilitate direct prediction of experimentally relevant quantities such as spin-transfer torque efficiency, skyrmion stability, spin diffusion length, and ultrafast magnetization dynamics under realistic operating conditions. Beyond methodological advances, the roadmap also defines a time-dependent developmental trajectory for AI-driven spintronics. In the short term (1–3 years), major efforts will focus on constructing FAIR magnetic datasets, improving descriptor engineering, incorporating explainable AI methodologies, and benchmarking machine-learning models for key spintronic properties. During this phase, improved integration between computational and experimental datasets will also be essential for reducing dataset bias and enhancing model reliability. The mid-term phase (3–7 years) is expected to witness widespread implementation of multimodal data fusion, active-learning-assisted discovery, closed-loop AI-experiment workflows, and accelerated identification of antiferromagnets, topological magnets, skyrmion-hosting materials, and low-dimensional spintronic systems. Significant progress is also anticipated in uncertainty-aware inverse design frameworks capable of optimizing multiple spintronic properties simultaneously while accounting for synthesizability and metastability constraints. In the long term (7–10+ years), the field is anticipated to transition toward fully autonomous discovery ecosystems integrating inverse design, multiscale simulation, device-aware optimization, and self-driving laboratories capable of continuously generating, synthesizing, characterizing, and validating new spintronic materials with minimal human intervention.

Future discovery platforms may further incorporate robotic synthesis, adaptive experiment planning, and autonomous feedback-driven retraining systems that continuously refine predictive accuracy during real-time experimentation. Such autonomous infrastructures could fundamentally transform spintronics from an empirical discovery process into a self-improving and design-driven scientific discipline. Large-scale foundation models and scientific language models may further accelerate this transition by enabling automated extraction of knowledge from scientific literature, materials databases, microscopy data, and multimodal experimental measurements. Coupling these models with generative AI, physics-informed learning frameworks, and autonomous experimentation could enable automated hypothesis generation, intelligent experiment planning, and self-evolving discovery workflows for chemically complex magnetic systems. In addition, future AI frameworks may increasingly combine natural-language reasoning with atomistic simulations and experimental feedback, enabling interactive, human-guided materials design environments for spintronics research. Collectively, the convergence of physics-informed machine learning, explainable AI, generative inverse design, multiscale simulation, uncertainty-aware optimization, and autonomous experimentation is rapidly reshaping the paradigm of spintronic materials discovery. AI is no longer limited to high-throughput screening of candidate compounds; instead, it is evolving into a predictive, adaptive, and experimentally informed design engine capable of linking electronic structure, magnetic interactions, synthesis constraints, and device functionality within a unified framework. Therefore, the present roadmap provides not only a conceptual foundation for future researchers but also a practical strategy for advancing spintronics toward scalable, energy-efficient, robust, and application-oriented technologies that extend beyond the limitations of conventional electronics.

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is increasingly transforming spintronic materials research from a trial-and-error approach to a more predictive and design-oriented framework. The integration of physics-informed machine learning, generative models, active learning, and Bayesian optimization with high-throughput computational screening and experimental datasets has facilitated more efficient exploration of complex magnetic materials spaces and also accelerated the identification of systems with targeted spin-dependent properties. Despite these advances, several limitations remain. These include constraints in data availability and quality, limited model interpretability, and challenges in transferability across chemically and structurally diverse materials systems. Addressing these issues will require the development of robust and standardized data infrastructures, physics-constrained learning architectures, and closer integration between computational predictions and experimental validation. Future progress is expected to emerge from the convergence of AI-driven prediction, autonomous experimentation, and closed-loop optimization strategies. Such integrated frameworks may enable more systematic and efficient materials discovery workflows. In this context, artificial intelligence is likely to play an increasingly central role in spintronic materials design, supporting the rational discovery of functional magnetic materials for memory, sensing, neuromorphic, and quantum applications.
