1
Introduction

We study positive solutions of the parameter depending Dirichlet problem on a unit ball
B
:
|x|<1
in
R^{n}
, modeled on the equation

(1.1)

\Delta u+\lambda\left(u^{p}+u^{q}\right)=0,\;\;\mbox{in $B$},\;\;u=0\;\;\mbox{on $\partial B$},

with real
1<p<q
, and a positive parameter
\lambda
. Such equations arise in astrophysics, see the work of S. Chandrasekhar
[
4
]
, for which he was awarded 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics.
By the classical theorem of B. Gidas, W.-M. Ni and L. Nirenberg positive solutions of (
1.1
) are radially symmetric, so that
u=u(r)
, with
r=|x|
, and hence they satisfy

(1.2)

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{r}u^{\prime}+\lambda\left(u^{p}+u^{q}\right)=0\,,\;u^{\prime}(0)=0,\;\;u(1)=0\,.

We consider both regular and singular solutions at
r=0
(with
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}u(r)=\infty
at singular solutions).
We are interested in super-critical equations with

(1.3)

1\leq p<\frac{n+2}{n-2}<q.

By scaling we can study instead of (
1.2
) the positive solutions of

(1.4)

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{t}u^{\prime}+u^{p}+u^{q}=0\,,\;u^{\prime}(0)=0,\;u(\omega)=0\,,

where
\omega
is the first root, and
\lambda=\omega^{2}
. If no finite
\omega
exists, we refer to the corresponding solution of (
1.4
) as a
ground state
.
Multiple solution curves of (
1.2
) are separated by ground state solutions. Indeed, ”shooting” for (
1.4
), starting with
u(0)>0
, results either with the first zero at some
\omega
(solution of Dirichlet problem (
1.4
)), or
u(r)\rightarrow 0
as
r\rightarrow\infty
(a ground state), while the case of a local minimum
u^{\prime}(\xi)=0
for some
\xi>0
is clearly impossible.

It turns out that super-critical problems have several distinguishing properties, compared with sub-critical problems that were mostly considered until recently.

1.

While in some cases there is exactly one solution curve (e.g., when
p=1
, as is shown below), the number of solution curves may be infinite (as in the case of Lin-Ni equation, as is seen below).

2.

A crucial role in determining the shape of the solution curve is played by the unique singular solution. While singular solutions exist for some sub-critical problems too (when
\frac{n}{n-2}<p<q<\frac{n+2}{n-2}
, see e.g.,
[
11
]
), it appears that they do not play a big role in that case.

3.

Solution curves may have both horizontal and vertical asymptotes. In fact, the Lin-Ni equation has infinitely many horizontal asymptotes, and a vertical asymptote at some
\lambda_{\infty}>0
.

4.

Solution curves often make infinitely many oscillations around a vertical asymptote.

5.

Super-critical equations are very challenging computationally. It turns out that solution curves of (
1.1
) make turns at very large
\lambda
’s, while oscillations around a vertical asymptote occur at very large values of the maximal value
u(0)
. For example, the solution curve for (
1.1
), with
n=3
,
p=4
,
q=7
, has a vertical asymptote at
\lambda_{\infty}\approx e^{11.44}\approx 92967
.

It turns out that a special role is played by the Lin-Ni equation

(1.5)

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{r}u^{\prime}+u^{p}+u^{2p-1}=0\,,\;\;u^{\prime}(0)=0\,,\;u(1)=0\,,

with
\frac{n}{n-2}<p<\frac{n+2}{n-2}
, which implies that
2p-1>\frac{n+2}{n-2}
. It was discovered in Lin-Ni
[
18
]
that this equation has an explicit ground state solution

u(r)=\left(\frac{2p(np-n-2p)}{(np-n-2p)^{2}+p(p-1)^{2}r^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}.

Then I. Flores
[
6
]
discovered that the Lin-Ni equation has infinitely many other ground state solutions, and it is the only equation in the class (
1.1
) with this property.
It turns out that there is a discrete infinite sequence of ground states, which serve to separate an infinite sequence of solutions to the Dirichlet problem. These ground states are of fast decay, and they tend to the Lin-Ni ground state, which is of slow decay. We present computational results on the global bifurcation diagram for the Dirichlet problem, which use some delicate
Mathematica
capabilities. Computing infinitely many solution curves in Figure
3
below involved a tremendous challenge: one needed to go to astronomically high
\lambda
’s, and then zoom in on a small neighborhood of
u(0)=2
.

Our results also suggest that the Lin-Ni equation is very special. We show that the Lin-Ni equation has a singular solution of the form
u(r)=r^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}v(r)
where
v(r)
is an analytic function. For all other equations of type (
1.1
) the results on singular solutions are similar, but
v(r)
is not analytic at
r=0
. We also mention that some kind of reduction of order occurs for the Lin-Ni equation. It was shown in P. Korman
[
14
]
that the Lin-Ni ground state solution of (
1.5
) satisfies the first order equation

(1.6)

u^{\prime}(r)=-Aru^{p},

where
A=\frac{p-1}{np-n-2p}
. (Observe that
A>0
iff
n>\frac{n}{n-2}
, and then
2p-1>\frac{n+2}{n-2}
, the critical exponent.) The Lin-Ni solution (of both (
1.5
) and (
1.6
)) can be written as
u(r)=\left(\frac{An-1}{1+pA^{2}r^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}
.

Our computations show that both the smaller and larger powers in (
1.1
) play an important role in determining the global bifurcation diagram. In Figure
1
we see how the change in the smaller power
p
produces a profound change of the bifurcation diagram.

1.2
),
n=3
,
p=1
,
q=7
.

),
n=3
,
p=4
,
q=7
.

Both analytical and computational results of this paper extend to
p
-Laplacian equations, by proceeding as in
[
16
]
and
[
9
]
, and to non-autonomous equation of the type

\Delta u+\lambda r^{\alpha}\left(u^{p}+u^{q}\right)=0,\;\;\mbox{in $B$},\;\;u=0\;\;\mbox{on $\partial B$},

by using the change of variables in
[
14
]
that removes the
r^{\alpha}
term while preserving the form of the equation and boundary conditions (while changing the dimension
n
).

2
Uniqueness and non-uniqueness of solution curves

In case the nonlinear term
f(u)
changes sign for
u>0
, multiple curves of positive solutions are typical, see e.g., P. Korman
[
12
]
.
If
f(u)>0
for
u>0
, multiple solution curves are also possible, although only for supercritical problems. It appears unclear for which supercritical
f(u)
multiple solution curves occur. We begin with a case when solution curve is unique.

Theorem 2.1

If
p=1
and
q>1
is arbitrary, all positive solutions of (
1.2
) lie on a unique solution curve in the
(\lambda,u(0))
-plane.

Proof:
Let us recall the following well known facts, see e.g., P. Korman
[
12
]
. There is a continuous curve of positive solutions bifurcating from zero at
\lambda=\lambda_{1}
. If this curve extends to
u(0)=\infty
, this solution curve is unique, since
u(0)
is a global parameter uniquely identifying the solution pair
(\lambda,u(r))
. Indeed, all possible values of
u(0)
,
0<u(0)<\infty
are taken up by this curve.

If
u(0)
remains bounded on this curve, then the increasing values of
u(0)
tend to a finite limit, call it
\bar{u}
. Along this curve
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, since it is known that all curves of positive solutions extend globally, see e.g., P. Korman
[
12
]
. Let
v(t)
be the solution of (
1.4
), with
v(0)=\bar{u}
. We claim that
v(t)
is a ground state, i.e., a decreasing solution tending to zero as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Indeed, near
v(t)
we have solutions of (
1.2
) with arbitrarily large
\lambda
, which correspond after rescaling to solutions of

(2.1)

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{t}u^{\prime}+u+u^{q}=0,\;u^{\prime}(0)=0,\;u(\omega)=0

with arbitrarily large
\omega=\sqrt{\lambda}
. If
v(t)
were to vanish at some finite
t
, we would have a contradiction with the continuous dependence of solutions on initial data. We show next that the ground state solution
v(t)
is not possible.

Near the point of bifurcation from zero (which occurs for (
1.4
) at
\lambda=\lambda_{1}
, the principal eigenvalue of the Laplacian on the unit ball, with zero boundary condition) we have small solutions
u(r)
of (
1.2
), which correspond to small solutions
u(t)
of (
1.4
), with
\omega\approx\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
:
Select one such solution, with

(2.2)

u(t)<v(t)\;\;\mbox{ on $(0,\omega)$}.

So that
v(t)
satisfies

v^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{t}v^{\prime}+v+v^{q}=0,\;v^{\prime}(0)=0,\;v(\omega)>0,

or

(2.3)

\left(t^{n-1}v^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+t^{n-1}(v+v^{q})=0,\;v^{\prime}(0)=0,\;v(\omega)>0.

Multiply (
2.3
) by
u
, and subtract the equation (
2.1
) multiplied by
v
, then integrate over
(0,\omega)
. Obtain

-\omega^{n-1}u^{\prime}(\omega)v(\omega)+\int_{0}^{\omega}t^{n-1}uv\left(v^{q-1}-u^{q-1}\right)\,dt=0.

In view of (
2.2
), both terms on the left are positive, a contradiction.

\diamondsuit

Remark
Our computations indicate that the solution curve is still unique for
p>1
, and
p\approx 1
. It is an interesting open question to determine a critical
p>1
at which the solution curve breaks into pieces. The argument above does not work for
p>1
since small solutions of (
1.4
) may have large first roots.

Example

When
n=3
and
q=7
the equation (
1.2
) takes the form

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2}{r}u^{\prime}+\lambda\left(u^{p}+u^{7}\right)=0\,,\;u^{\prime}(0)=0,\;\;u(1)=0\,.

Our numerical computations show that the solution curve is unique for
1\leq p\leq 3.7
, and it breaks into two pieces by
p=3.8
(here
p=4
corresponds to the Lin-Ni equation). As
p
is increased toward
4
, the number of solution curves increases, and it is infinite at
p=4
, see Figure
1
(b).

Proposition 1

In case
1<p<\frac{n+2}{n-2}<q
, there is a number
A>0
so that all positive solutions of (
1.2
) satisfying
u(0)>A
lie on a unique continuous solution curve in the
(\lambda,u(0))
-plane.

Proof:
Multiple curves of positive solutions of (
1.2
) are separated by ground states. We will show later on that ground states with large
u(0)
are not possible. (Solutions of (
1.4
) with large
u(0)
vanish at
\omega\approx\sqrt{\lambda^{*}}
, with
\lambda^{*}
corresponding to the singular solution of (
1.2
).)

\diamondsuit

Let us also recall the following known result, see K. Schmitt
[
22
]
or P. Korman
[
12
]
. It can also be derived from the above propositions, and the fact that solution curves approach a singular solutions at some
\lambda^{*}
as
u(0)\rightarrow\infty
.

Proposition 2

In case
1\leq p<\frac{n+2}{n-2}<q
, there are numbers
0<\bar{\lambda}<\bar{\Lambda}
so that
\bar{\lambda}<\lambda<\bar{\Lambda}
holds for all positive solutions of (
1.2
).

For subcritical problems the situation is much simpler, see e.g., P. Korman
[

]
or T. Ouyang and J. Shi
[
21
]
.

Proposition 3

In case
1<p,q<\frac{n+2}{n-2}
the problem (
1.2
) has a unique positive solution for each
\lambda>0
. These solutions lie on a unique solution curve, which is hyperbola-like, with
u(0)\rightarrow\infty
as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, and

u(0)\rightarrow 0
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
.

3
Singular solution for the power case

In this section we collect the relevant results on the Lane-Emden equation (which arose in astrophysics in the 19-th century)

(3.1)

w^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{r}w^{\prime}+w^{p}=0

that are more or less known, and apply them to the solution curves of (
1.1
). The equation possesses (for
p>\frac{n}{n-2}
) a
singular solution
, which is unbounded at
r=0
. Singular solution plays an important role in determining the shape of solution curves for the Dirichlet problems (
1.1
). We describe the singular solution next.

Define

(3.2)

B=\frac{2}{(p-1)^{2}}\left[-2+(p-1)(n-2)\right].

Observe that
B>0
if and only if
p>\frac{n}{n-2}
. The following lemma is proved by a direct calculation.

Lemma 3.1

Assume that
p>\frac{n}{n-2}
. Then
w_{0}(r)=B^{\frac{1}{p-1}}r^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
is a singular ground state solution of (
3.1
).

Linearization of the equation (
3.1
) around the singular solution
w_{0}(r)
is

z^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{r}z^{\prime}+pw_{0}^{p-1}z=0\,,\;\;z^{\prime}(0)=z(1)=0\,,

which is the same as

(3.3)

z^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{r}z^{\prime}+\frac{pB}{r^{2}}z=0\,,\;\;z^{\prime}(0)=z(1)=0\,.

This is Euler’s equation. Its characteristic equation
s^{2}+(n-2)s+pB=0
has the roots

s=\frac{-(n-2)\pm\sqrt{(n-2)^{2}-4pB}}{2}\,.

The roots are complex, so that
z(t)
is oscillatory, provided that

(3.4)

(n-2)^{2}-4pB<0\,.

To analyze the inequality (
3.4
), we begin with the equal sign:

(3.5)

(n-2)^{2}-4p\frac{2}{(p-1)^{2}}\left[-2+(p-1)(n-2)\right]=0.

Solving this quadratic equation for
p
gives the roots
p_{1},p_{2}
, or two solution curves
p_{1}(n),p_{2}(n)

(3.6)

p_{1}(n)=\frac{n^{2}-8n-8\sqrt{n-1}+4}{n^{2}-12n+20},

(3.7)

p_{2}(n)=\frac{n^{2}-8n+8\sqrt{n-1}+4}{n^{2}-12n+20}.

By an elementary analysis one shows that, see Figure
2
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle p_{2}(n)<0<p_{1}(n)<\frac{n+2}{n-2},\;\;\;\;\mbox{for $2<n<10$}

\displaystyle 1<p_{1}(n)<\frac{n+2}{n-2}<p_{2}(n),\;\;\;\;\mbox{for $n>10$}.

Lemma 3.2

If
p>\frac{n+2}{n-2}
and
2<n\leq 10
, then the inequality (
3.4
) holds.

Proof:
If
2<n<10
, the numerator in (
3.5
) is a quadratic in
p
with the leading coefficient (in
p^{2}
)

(3.9)

(n-2)^{2}-8(n-2)=(n-2)(n-10)<0,

and hence this quadratic is negative when
p
is greater than its larger root. The root
p_{2}(n)
is negative, while the larger root satisfies
0<p_{1}(n)<\frac{n+2}{n-2}
, so that (
3.4
) holds, since
p>\frac{n+2}{n-2}>p_{1}(n)
.

When
n=10
,
\frac{n+2}{n-2}=\frac{3}{2}
,

(n-2)^{2}-4pB=\frac{64-48p}{(p-1)^{2}}<0,

for
p>\frac{3}{2}
.

\diamondsuit

Lemma 3.3

If
p>\frac{n+2}{n-2}
and
n>10
, then the inequality (
3.4
) holds if and only if
p<p_{2}(n)
.

Proof:
The numerator in (
3.5
) is a quadratic in
p
, and the leading coefficient is now positive in view of (
3.9
). This quadratic is negative when
p
is between its roots.

\diamondsuit

Figure 2:
The critical curves

The number
p_{2}(n)
is known as the Joseph-Lundgren exponent, introduced in
[
8
]
. It can be written as

(3.10)

p_{2}(n)=1+\frac{4}{n-2\sqrt{n-1}-4}.

Define
p_{{}_{JL}}=\infty
for
3\leq n\leq 10
and
p_{{}_{JL}}=1+\frac{4}{n-2\sqrt{n-1}-4}
for
n\geq 11
. The following theorem was included in Y. Miyamoto
[
20
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
20
]
)
Assume that
1<p<\frac{n+2}{n-2}<q<p_{{}_{JL}}
. Assume that the singular solution of (
1.1
) occurs at
\lambda=\lambda_{\infty}
. Then the solution curve of (
1.1
) makes infinitely many turns around
\lambda=\lambda_{\infty}
.

Our computations indicate that the solution curve actually has a vertical asymptote at
\lambda=\lambda_{\infty}
, and that the value of
\lambda_{\infty}
is affected drastically by the lower power of
p
.

Let us recall Theorem 4.1 in M.-F. Bidaut-Veron
[
1
]
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
1
]
)
Any solution of the equation (
3.1
) is either of slow decay (when
w(r)\sim cr^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
as
r\rightarrow\infty
), or of fast decay as
r\rightarrow\infty
(when
w(r)\sim cr^{-\frac{1}{n-2}}
as
r\rightarrow\infty
).

Let now
w(r)
be the solution of

(3.11)

w^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{r}w^{\prime}+w^{p}=0\,,\;\;w^{\prime}(0)=0\,,\;w(0)=1.

It is well-known (by Pohozhaev’s identity) that for super-critical
p>\frac{n+2}{n+2}
,
w(r)
remains positive for all
r\in(0,\infty)
, it is decreasing for all
r
, and tends to zero as
r\rightarrow\infty
, i.e.,
w(r)
is a
ground state solution
.

Lemma 3.4

The ground state solution
w(r)
of (
3.11
) is of slow decay.

Proof:
By the Theorem 5.1 part (iv) in M.-F. Bidaut-Veron
[
1
]
the possibility of fast decay is ruled out by the fact that the solution does not oscillate at
r=0
. (I. Flores
[
6
]
has an alternative proof.)

\diamondsuit

The following lemma of S. Chandrasekhar
[
4
]
, which gives the exact form of the slow decay, was used in D.D. Joseph and T.S. Lundgren
[
8
]
and later in C. Budd and J. Norbury
[
3
]
. We present a short proof, based on work of M.-F. Bidaut-Veron
[
1
]
, used in the preceding lemma. We shall use this lemma later to calculate the value of
\lambda_{\infty}
for the problem (
4.6
) below.

Lemma 3.5

For
p>\frac{n+2}{n-2}
, the solution of (
3.11
) satisfies
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}r^{\frac{2}{p-1}}w(r)=B^{\frac{1}{p-1}}
(
B
was defined in (
3.2
)).

Proof:
By Lemma
3.4
,
w(r)
is of slow decay as
r\rightarrow\infty
. Make a change of variables
s=\frac{1}{r}
, and study the solution near
s=0
. Obtain

(3.12)

s^{4}w^{\prime\prime}+(3-n)s^{3}w^{\prime}+w^{p}=0\,.

Make a change of variables

(3.13)

w(s)=s^{\frac{2}{p-1}}v(s)\,.

Upon dividing by
s^{\frac{4p+2}{p-1}}
, the equation (
3.12
) takes the form

(3.14)

v^{\prime\prime}+\frac{c_{1}}{s}v^{\prime}+\frac{1}{s^{2}}g(v)=0\,,

where
c_{1}=\frac{-np+n+3p+1}{p-1}
, and
g(v)=\frac{4p-2n(p-1)}{(p-1)^{2}}v+v^{p}
. By Lemma
3.4
,
v(0)
is finite. From the equation (
3.14
), we see that

(3.15)

g(v(0))=0\,,

since otherwise
v(s)
tends to infinity at
s=0
, which follows by integration of (
3.14
), and contradicts Lemma
3.4
. Solving (
3.15
) one gets
v(0)=B^{\frac{1}{p-1}}
, completing the proof, since
v(0)=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}s^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}w(s)=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}r^{\frac{2}{p-1}}w(r)
.

\diamondsuit

4
The global picture for the Lin-Ni equation

In this section we present surprising computational results on the global solution curves for Dirichlet problem for the Lin-Ni equation

(4.1)

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{r}u^{\prime}+\lambda\left(u^{p}+u^{2p-1}\right)=0\,,\;\;u^{\prime}(0)=0\,,\;u(1)=0\,,

with
\frac{n}{n-2}<p<\frac{n+2}{n-2}
, which implies that
2p-1>\frac{n+2}{n-2}
, and
\lambda
is a positive parameter. At the end of this section we provide an analytical support to our computational results, by studying the linearized problem for (
4.1
).

A simple scaling
argument shows that the value of
u(0)>0
is a global parameter, uniquely
identifying the solution pair
(\lambda,u(r))
of (
4.1
). Therefore we shall study the solution curves
(\lambda,u(0))
. Points on this curve are numerically computed as follows: given any
u(0)=u_{0}>0
, solve the initial value problem

(4.2)

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{n-1}{r}u^{\prime}+u^{p}+u^{2p-1}=0\,,\;\;u(0)=u_{0}\;\;u^{\prime}(0)=0\,,

and let
r>0
be the first root of
u(r)
. Then
\lambda=r^{2}
, as easily follows by scaling, or see e.g., P. Korman
[
12
]
.

We present our computations in case
n=3
and
p=4
. The Mathematica program below is one way of computing the solution curves
(\lambda,u(0))
of (
4.1
). We also have done the computations with higher precision. In most cases the standard double floating point arithmetic seems to be sufficient. Next we present a sample
Mathematica
program (with detailed explanations given in our notebook published in Wolfram Notebook Archive
[
17
]
). The crucial element involves using the WhenEvent option of the NDSolve command to stop integration when the first root of solution is reached.

rep={n->3,p->4}
f[u_]=u^p+u^(2p-1)
dequ=u’’[r]+(n-1)/r*u’[r]+f[u[r]]/.rep
umin=0;
umax=10000000;
nsteps=2000;
delu=(umax-umin)/nsteps;
h=10^-100;
dirichlet={};
For[k=1,k <= nsteps,k++,
u0= umin+k*delu ;
b0=True;
sol1=NDSolveValue[{dequ==0,u[h]==u0,u’[h]==0,
WhenEvent[u[r]== 0,
b0=False;
AppendTo[dirichlet,{r^2,u0}];
"StopIntegration"]},
u,{r,h,1/h}];
If[b0,

Proposition 4

There is a discrete sequence of ground state solutions of Lin-Ni equation (here n=3)

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2}{r}u^{\prime}+u^{4}+u^{7}=0,\;\;u^{\prime}(0)=0.

Proof:
The Lin-Ni solution
