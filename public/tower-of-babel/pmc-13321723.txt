Mental health needs of patients with osteoarthritis based on a preference perspective: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Guiling Liao, JingJing Shang, Yaping Yi, Dongmei Xu, Jiapei Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the characteristics and influencing factors of mental health needs in patients with osteoarthritis (OA) from a preference-based perspective, and to inform the development of tailored service strategies based on their needs and preferences.MethodFrom March to June 2024, 385 patients with OA were recruited from three tertiary hospitals in Changzhou using convenience sampling. Quantitative data were collected using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and a self-developed Mental Health Needs and Preferences Questionnaire, and analyzed using multiple linear regression. Between July and August 2024, a subset of 28 participants was purposively selected from the same sample for semi-structured interviews, and the qualitative data were analyzed thematically.ResultsQuantitative results showed that 56.1% of patients screened positive for anxiety or depressive symptoms. The highest reported need was for disease knowledge (4.21 ± 0.63), followed by symptom control (4.05 ± 0.71), emotion/behavior management (3.85 ± 0.89), and social/occupational functioning (3.42 ± 0.95). Regarding service preferences, patients preferred an orthopedic surgeon-led mode (68.3%), personalized face-to-face consultation (76.4%), pain management content (85.7%), and outpatient support (71.9%). Multiple regression analysis identified six significant influencing factors, including age and education level (P < 0.05). Qualitative findings elaborated on these patterns, revealing four themes: Psychological burden in illness experience, The Foundation of Self-Management: Needs for Health Literacy and Symptom Control, Psychological and Social Functioning Needs, Facilitators Shaping Service Engagement and Preferences. The consistency between quantitative scores and qualitative descriptions reinforced the validity of the identified need domains.ConclusionThis mixed-methods study found a high prevalence of anxiety/depressive symptoms in OA patients and identified mental health needs across four dimensions: disease knowledge, symptom control, emotion/behavior management, and social/occupational functioning. Service preferences favored surgeon-involved, personalized, and pain-focused interventions. A multidisciplinary model integrating orthopedic and mental health professionals should be developed to provide personalized services aligned with patient preferences.Trial registrationThis study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzhou Second People’s Hospital (approval No. [2024]KY003-01). The trial was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number MR-32-25-004771, registered on 28 March 2023).Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14553-8.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), a chronic joint disease characterized by degenerative changes in articular cartilage and hyperplasia of subarticular bone and surrounding bone, affects the knees, hips, hands, and spine, leading to joint dysfunction, stiffness, and pain [1, 2]. As a leading global cause of disability and morbidity, OA has shown a dramatic increase in prevalence over the past decades. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 on trends in the site-specific prevalence of OA, the number of prevalent cases rose by 113.25% between 1990 and 2019, from 247.51 million to 527.81 million [3]. OA not only seriously affects patients’ quality of life, but also imposes a heavy financial and mental burden on individuals, their families, and society [4].

Today, OA ranks as the 14th leading cause of age-standardized years lived with disability (YLDs) worldwide, imposing a significant burden on individuals in the form of pain, restricted mobility, and reduced quality of life [5]. This physical burden is closely intertwined with a substantial psychological one. Patients with OA have been reported to have higher rates of stress perception, depressive mood, and suicidal ideation compared to individuals without OA [6, 7]. The associated psychological symptoms not only have a serious impact on patients’ mental health but can also lead to higher medical costs and service burdens, both by compromising disease management and accelerating the disease process [8]. In developed countries, OA already places a considerable strain on health systems, accounting for approximately 1–2.5% of gross national product (GNP), and the cost of related services is projected to quadruple by 2030 [3].

Although there has been an increase in attention to the mental health status of OA patients in recent years, studies to date have significant limitations. First, most studies have focused on the incidence and severity of negative emotions such as depression and anxiety [6, 9], but there has been a lack of systematic discussion of the characteristics and influencing factors of patients’ mental health needs. In addition, these studies have mainly been quantitative, and qualitative research has rarely been carried out from the perspective of patients’ preferences, with little attention to patients’ subjective mental health needs and service preferences. This gap is critical, as service models that are not aligned with patient preferences are likely to show low uptake and limited effectiveness. The patient-centered care paradigm emphasizes that incorporating patient preferences is essential for shared decision-making, promoting treatment adherence, and ultimately improving health outcomes [10–12].

Therefore, this study uses a mixed research method, from the perspective of preference. It combines quantitative and qualitative research to transcend the limitations of a single research method and gain a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of the mental health needs and service preferences of patients, with the goal of providing more accurate and individualized mental health support for OA patients and promoting the development of better mental health services for them.

Methods

Design

A mixed methods approach, combining quantitative and qualitative methods, was used to comprehensively examine the characteristics and influencing factors of the mental health needs of OA patients. The quantitative portion of this study revealed the current situation and influencing factors of OA patients’ mental health needs, thereby providing data support for meeting their multi-dimensional health needs, while the qualitative portion deeply explored the motivations, emotional experiences, and service preferences of patients expressing their mental health needs. This combination of methods provides a more comprehensive and in-depth understanding of patients’ mental health needs and service preferences, as well as a theoretical basis for formulating suitable ways to meet the mental health needs and service preferences of patients with osteoarthritic diseases.

Quantitative survey

The cross-sectional survey method and the convenience sampling method were used to investigate the mental health needs of OA patients in the orthopedic outpatient clinics of three tertiary A hospitals in Changzhou from March to June 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosed with OA by a specialist physician and disease duration of ≥ 6 months; (2) age ≥ 18 years old; (3) clear consciousness and able to communicate normally; and 4. voluntary participation in this study. The exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of mental illness or cognitive impairment; (2) recent (within three months) major life events or traumatic experiences; and (3) participation in other interventional studies. The minimum required sample size was calculated as 295 based on the principles of sample size calculation for mixed studies [13], the multiple regression formula n ≥ 5k + 50 (where k is the number of independent variables), and an assumed dropout rate of 10%.

The data collectors received standardized training provided by the research team, covering the research objectives, survey methods, informed consent procedures, questionnaire completion, and related matters. After obtaining informed consent, the principal investigator was present to assist patients in completing the questionnaire. Participants were also informed that they might be contacted for a subsequent in-depth interview and provided preliminary consent for this possibility. Contact information was retained with participant consent for potential follow-up interviews. All questionnaires were completed anonymously and collected on site, with an average completion time of approximately 20–25 min. A total of 415 questionnaires were distributed, and 385 were collected, yielding an effective response rate of 92.8%.

The survey tools were as follows. (1) General information questionnaire, compiled by the research team after review of the literature and expert consultation. The final items assessed demographic data (gender, age, marital status, education level, occupation, income, etc.) and disease-related data (course of disease, type of OA, severity, treatment modalities, etc.). See Supplementary File 1 for details. 2) Questionnaire on the mental health needs and preferences of OA patients, the first draft of which was based on a comprehensive literature review and semi-structured expert interviews. Content validity was established through a formal expert panel review process. A panel of eight experts was recruited using purposive sampling, including three orthopedic specialists (with ≥ 10 years of clinical experience), two nursing professors specializing in chronic disease management, two psychologists with expertise in health psychology, and one methodologist experienced in questionnaire development. Each expert independently rated all items on a 4-point relevance scale (1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = quite relevant, 4 = highly relevant) following the methodology described by Polit and Beck. The Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) was calculated as the proportion of experts rating each item as 3 or 4. The Scale-level Content Validity Index using the averaging method (S-CVI/Ave) was computed as the mean of all I-CVIs. Items with I-CVI ≥ 0.78 were retained, while items with I-CVI between 0.70 and 0.78 were revised based on expert feedback. Items with I-CVI < 0.70 were deleted. The results showed that all retained items achieved I-CVI values ranging from 0.78 to 1.00, with an S-CVI/Ave of 0.92, indicating excellent content validity. Additionally, a pilot study was conducted with 30 OA patients (12 males and 18 females; mean age 58.6 ± 12.3 years; disease duration ranging from 8 months to 15 years) recruited from the orthopedic outpatient clinic to assess item clarity, comprehensibility, and response burden. Participants completed the questionnaire and provided verbal feedback through cognitive interviewing techniques. Based on the pilot study results, the following modifications were made: ① five items with ambiguous wording were revised for clarity (e.g., “psychological support” was changed to “emotional counseling and stress management”); ② two items with overlapping content in the symptom management dimension were merged into one; ③ response options for three items were simplified from seven-point to five-point Likert scales to reduce respondent burden; and ④ the questionnaire layout was reformatted to improve readability. The average completion time was reduced from 35 min to 20–25 min after these modifications. The final questionnaire comprises two sections. The first section is the mental health needs dimension (20 items) assessing patients’ needs across four subscales: disease knowledge (5 items), symptom management (5 items), emotion and behavior management (5 items), and social and occupational functions (5 items). Cronbach’s α coefficients were calculated separately for each of the four subscales to assess internal consistency reliability: the disease knowledge subscale (α = 0.76), the symptom management subscale (α = 0.83), the emotion and behavior management subscale (α = 0.87), and the social and occupational functions subscale (α = 0.78). These values ranged from 0.76 to 0.87, all exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70, indicating acceptable to good internal consistency across all four subscales. The higher α values observed for the emotion and behavior management and symptom management subscales are consistent with the greater item homogeneity typically found in affective and behavioral constructs, while the comparatively lower α for the disease knowledge and social and occupational functions subscales reflects the broader conceptual scope of these dimensions. Regarding content validity, the expert panel review yielded I-CVI values ranging from 0.78 to 1.00 across all items in each subscale, and an overall S-CVI/Ave of 0.92, supporting excellent content validity for all four dimensions of the instrument. All 20 items use a uniform five-point Likert scale with consistent anchor points: 1 = not needed at all/strongly disagree, 2 = slightly needed/disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = quite needed/agree, 5 = very much needed/strongly agree. Dimension scores were calculated as the mean of all item scores within each subscale, with higher scores indicating greater needs. The second section is the service preference dimension (12 questions, Q21–Q32), which employs mixed response formats tailored to different aspects of service preferences: (a) Multiple-response questions (allowing multiple selections) are used for: preferred service providers (Q21), service delivery formats (Q23), factors influencing format selection (Q24), factors influencing format selection (Q26), service locations (Q27), integrated service models (Q29), ideal service frequency (Q30), perceived barriers to accessing services (Q31), and overall expectations (Q32); (b) Ranking questions (requiring respondents to order items by importance) are used for: criteria for selecting service providers (Q22, ranking 1–5), prioritization of service content needs (Q25, ranking 1–8), and factors influencing choice of service location (Q28, ranking 1–5); and (c) three-point preference scales (not preferred/neutral/preferred) for evaluating specific service formats (Q23) and locations (Q27). For multiple-choice questions, the percentage of respondents selecting each option was calculated. For ranking questions, weighted scores were computed based on rank positions. For preference scale items, response distributions were reported. Based on the distribution of mental health needs scores in this sample, the following interpretation guidelines were established: scores of 1.00-2.33 indicate low needs, 2.34–3.66 indicate moderate needs, and 3.67-5.00 indicate high needs. However, these cut-off values are exploratory and require further validation in future studies. See Supplementary File 1 for the complete questionnaire and detailed scoring instructions. (3) Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), specifically designed and developed by Zigmond and Snaith in 1983 [14] to measure the level of anxiety and depression in hospital patients. The Chinese version of the HADS scale comprises two subscales, anxiety and depression, each of which has seven items. The total score ranges from 0 to 21 points, with ≥ 8 points taken to indicate that anxiety or depression may be present. This scale has been widely used in the assessment of the psychological status of patients with chronic diseases, and has the characteristics of simplicity, ease of use, objectivity, and reliability. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale is 0.78 [15].

SPSS 26.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Measurement data are expressed here as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), and aggregational data are expressed as cases (n) and percentages (%). The data were analyzed by t-test, χ² test, one-way ANOVA, and multiple linear regression analysis. P < 0.05 was taken to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Qualitative study

From July to August 2024, interview participants were purposively selected from the quantitative sample according to criteria including age, symptom severity, HADS scores, and their scores on the Mental Health Needs and Preferences Questionnaire. Following this, they were contacted by phone for a detailed explanation of the interview’s aims and procedures, noting how their specific experiences would contribute to the study. Written informed consent and a confidentiality agreement were secured from all participants prior to scheduling the interview. The selection criteria were based on the principle of maximum variation sampling [16], and the series of interviews was brought to an end when no new information appeared in the interview results.

The interviews were conducted by research team members who have received formal training in qualitative research methods and possess prior experience in conducting healthcare-related interviews. They took place in a quiet meeting room at the hospital, with each session lasting approximately 30 to 60 min. All interviews were conducted in Mandarin to facilitate open communication. A total of 35 patients were invited for an interview based on the purposive sampling criteria; 28 agreed to participate and completed the interview (labelled P1 to P28), resulting in a response rate of 80%. All interview recordings were transcribed verbatim by a research team members within 24 h after completion. The accuracy of the transcripts was subsequently verified by another member of the research team by checking them against the original recordings.

Based on review of the literature and expert opinions, the interview outline was designed by one of the researchers. This preliminary guide was then reviewed, discussed, and finalized by the broader research team to ensure its relevance and appropriateness. Pre-interviews were conducted before the formal interviews, and the interview guide was correspondingly further revised and improved on this basis. The final interview questions are provided in Supplementary File 2.

The topic analysis method proposed by Braun and Clarke was used to analyze the data through six steps: familiarization with the data, initial coding, finding topics, reviewing the topics, defining and naming the topics, and writing a report [17]. After ensuring the accuracy of the transcripts, two researchers independently coded the interview content using NVivo 12.0 software. To ensure analytical rigor and trustworthiness through investigator triangulation, their initial coding results were compared. Any discrepancies were discussed until a consensus was reached, forming the final thematic framework which was then reviewed by the broader research team. Furthermore, methodological triangulation was explicitly implemented during the mixed-methods analysis phase. We adopted an integrated comparison strategy to systematically juxtapose and interpret the quantitative survey results alongside the emergent qualitative themes, aiming to identify points of complementarity, convergence, or divergence, and thereby achieve a comprehensive understanding of the research problem.

In terms of mental health needs, the highest mean score was for disease knowledge needs (4.21 ± 0.63), followed by the need for symptom control (4.05 ± 0.71), the need for emotion/behavior management (3.85 ± 0.89), and the need for social/occupational functioning (3.42 ± 0.95). The types of disease-related knowledge that patients most wanted to know were disease development and prognosis (4.43 ± 0.52), pain intervention (4.38 ± 0.57), and adverse emotion coping methods (4.16 ± 0.77). In addition, the dimension score of mental health service preference indicated that the patients had strong preferences regarding the form, content, service provider, and service support mode of mental health services. Regarding the type of service, individual one-on-one counseling (76.4%) was the most popular among patients, followed by peer support groups (55.3%) and online health education (51.9%). In terms of service content, the preferred needs were for pain management (85.7%), symptom management (77.2%), and emotional management (73.5%). In terms of service providers, patients most wanted mental health intervention by orthopedic surgeons (68.3%), followed by specialist nurses (52.7%) and psychological counselors (43.1%). In terms of service support methods, hospital outpatient clinics (71.9%) were the most preferred places to obtain services, followed by community health service centers (47.5%) and Internet platforms (44.9%).

Univariate analysis of the influencing factors of mental health needs preferences in OA patients showed that gender, age, education level, disease severity, pain level, disease course, and previous psychological counseling experience were significantly correlated with patients’ mental health needs preferences (P < 0.05). Patients of female gender, younger than 45 years, or having a bachelor’s degree or higher, severe disease, high pain intensity, disease course > 5 years, or previous psychological counseling experience all exhibited higher total scores for mental health needs (see Table 3).

Qualitative findings

The characteristics of the 28 interview participants are presented in Table 5. Of the respondents, 12 were male and 16 were female. Their ages ranged from 36 to 81 years old, with an average of 61.2 ± 11.4 years, including three individuals who were aged <45 years old, eight who were 45 ~ 59 years old, 14 who were 60–74 years old, and three who were 75 years old or older. In terms of education level, six patients had a primary school education or less, 13 had a junior high school/high school education, and nine had junior college/undergraduate degrees. In terms of disease severity, six patients reported mild symptoms, 14 reported moderate, and eight reported severe; while in terms of pain level, four reported mild pain, 13 reported moderate, and 11 reported severe. Integrated findings

The conclusions gleaned from the quantitative and qualitative studies are complementary and compatible. The quantitative portion of this research indicated that patients’ mental health needs and service preferences have commonalities and influencing factors, while the qualitative portion provides an in-depth understanding of how these needs and preferences are shaped. The integrated results are as follows. (1) Multi-dimensional psychological needs: The quantitative results show that the patients’ mental health needs are mainly focused on disease-related knowledge, disease symptom management, negative emotion regulation, and social function recovery. The qualitative results point to the psychological sources of demand, such as persistent pain, functional impairment, and worry about unknowns. (2) Individual service preference: The quantitative and qualitative studies both confirmed that education level, disease severity, and previous psychological counseling experience are factors affecting service preference. Complex factors such as disease cognition, medical experience, and resource accessibility affect the final service choice. (3) Common expectations of integrated services: Both approaches indicated that the patients expect to receive integrated, continuous mental health services, and the qualitative study particularly underscored the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration and personalized services.

Discussion

Principal findings

This mixed-methods study explores the characteristics and influencing factors of the psychological needs of patients with OA from the perspective of preference. The quantitative findings revealed that over half (56.1%) of the 385 participants screened positive for anxiety or depressive symptoms on the HADS, yet their expressed mental health needs were multidimensional, encompassing disease-related knowledge, symptom management, emotional regulation, and social functioning. Multiple regression analysis identified age, education level, disease duration, disease severity, pain intensity, and prior psychological counseling experience as significant predictors of mental health needs. The qualitative findings from 28 in-depth interviews further illuminated how these needs are shaped, revealing that patients’ service engagement is influenced by four key facilitators: Health professional–patient communication and interaction, Interdisciplinary management and care coordination, Patients’ practical preferences, and Access to care and continuity. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of developing integrated, patient-centered mental health services that address not only the clinical symptoms but also the contextual and relational factors influencing patients’ willingness to seek and engage with care.

Mental health status and needs characteristics of patients with OA

The results of the questionnaire survey suggest the presence of anxiety or depressive symptoms in 56.1% of the patients, with 43.6% screening positive for anxiety symptoms and 39.7% for depressive symptoms on the HADS. This finding is generally consistent with the study by Nazarinasab et al. [18] Mental health needs among patients with OA were identified across four dimensions: disease-related knowledge, symptom management, emotion regulation, and social functioning. Among these, the need for disease-related knowledge received the highest score, which is consistent with the findings of Silvana Pérez et al. [19] This study found that patients’ need for disease-related knowledge outweighed their need for emotional support, which may reflect a tendency among Chinese patients to alleviate psychological distress by enhancing their sense of control over the disease when facing a chronic condition. However, one survey [20] has shown that the level of disease-related knowledge and self-behavior management of patients with knee OA in China is relatively low.

The interview results further identified the root causes of these three needs: long-term pain, limited mobility, and worry about future confusion. This is consistent with the study of Werneck et al., [21] who found that chronic pain is related to anxiety and depression, and that long-term pain is not merely a disease-related symptom but is also the source of anxiety, depression, and other negative emotions. Future studies can further examine the question of how to combine disease-related knowledge education and psychological counseling to establish an integrated intervention model suitable for China’s cultural background.

Factors influencing preferences for mental health needs in patients with OA

In this study, multiple regression analysis showed that age, education level, disease course, disease severity, pain, and previous psychological counseling experience were the key factors affecting the mental health needs of the OA patients. Similarly, Qizhen Feng et al. [22] identified age, BMI, quality of life, and social support as significant factors influencing anxiety and depression in patients with knee OA. Taken together, these findings suggest that a range of demographic and clinical variables contribute to the psychological status and service needs of this population. This study also found that patients with chronic diseases who have experience with mental health services are more confident in evaluating the value of such services.

The interview results further confirmed that trust in professional authority, previous medical experience, and service convenience shaped the health demand preferences of OA patients. The interview results further revealed that the mediating effect of patient characteristics such as age, education level, disease course, disease severity, pain level, and previous psychological counseling experience on the formation of their needs is exerted through the influence of the patients’ disease cognition, self-confidence, and resource acquisition ability.

Translating patient preferences into practice

The qualitative findings revealed four key facilitators of service engagement: Health professional–patient communication and interaction, Interdisciplinary management and care coordination, Patients’ practical preferences, and Access to care and continuity.

Communication and interaction. Patients emphasized that communication style, empathy, and feeling validated were more important than providers’ professional titles. This aligns with studies highlighting patient-centered communication as central to chronic care [23]. However, in the Chinese context, where hierarchical patient–provider relationships have traditionally prevailed [24], this explicit desire for empathic interaction may reflect an emerging expectation for more egalitarian care relationships.

Interdisciplinary care and coordination. Patients expressed a clear preference for integrated care models where orthopedic and mental health professionals collaborate, with continuity across disease stages. This echoes Western findings that multidisciplinary approaches improve outcomes in OA [25]. A collaborative model led by orthopedic healthcare providers with support from mental health professionals may be explored to enhance patient acceptance and adherence.

Practical preferences. This study revealed that patients with OA exhibited distinct preferences regarding mental health service providers, formats, content, and modes of support. Patients tended to prefer receiving mental health services from orthopedic surgeons and specialist nurses, favored individualized face-to-face consultations and peer support groups, and prioritized pain management and emotion regulation as key service content. The findings of this study are consistent with those of Geenen et al., [26] who similarly found that rheumatism patients want psychological support from their primary care physician. Qualitative interviews further uncovered what underlies these preferences, including trust in professional authority, the influence of prior healthcare experiences, and considerations of service convenience.

Regarding access to care, patients expressed concerns about geographic accessibility, particularly those living in suburban or rural areas. Such barriers are well-documented in resource-limited settings; a qualitative study by Mntungwana et al. [27] in rural South Africa identified workforce shortages, infrastructure deficits, and socioeconomic challenges as key obstacles to chronic disease management. Similarly, in the Chinese context, geographic disparities and a tiered hospital system present distinct challenges, although the recent growth of internet-based health services offers new opportunities. Patients in this study valued the convenience of community health centers and internet platforms, while recognizing that complex issues still required hospital-based care—a pragmatic approach reflecting patients’ navigation of a healthcare system where digital solutions coexist with traditional structures.

In summary, the facilitators of service engagement identified in this study are broadly consistent with findings from Western settings, particularly regarding the importance of patient-centered communication, multidisciplinary collaboration, and attention to practical preferences. The value patients place on communication and empathy may signal shifting expectations within a historically hierarchical medical culture. Their desire for integrated and continuous care may stem, in part, from the fragmented organization of health services in China. Likewise, their pragmatic approach to access—weighing convenience against perceived quality—appears shaped by geographic disparities and the growing availability of digital health tools. These contextual nuances suggest that service development efforts need to be tailored to the specific realities of China’s healthcare system and patient population.

Implications for clinical practice and service development

This study provides an empirical basis for establishing a mental health service system for patients with OA from a preference perspective, addressing both what is needed and how to provide it. The mixed-methods approach employed here offers a more comprehensive understanding than quantitative methods alone, by not only identifying common patterns and influencing factors through quantitative analysis, but also exploring how these needs arise through qualitative inquiry.

Several implications for clinical practice emerge from these findings. The high prevalence of psychological distress supports integrating routine screening for anxiety and depression into standard OA care. Given patients’ preference for receiving mental health support from orthopedic surgeons and specialist nurses, training programs focused on communication skills and basic mental health assessment are warranted. Patients’ desire for integrated, interdisciplinary care calls for collaborative models that bridge orthopedic and mental health services with continuity across disease stages. Flexible service delivery—including both face-to-face and online formats, as well as accessible community-based care—is also needed to accommodate patients’ practical preferences. Finally, the influence of prior counseling experience on mental health needs suggests that normalizing psychological support as a routine component of chronic disease management may enhance patients’ awareness and confidence in seeking care.

Future research could build on these findings to develop and test preference-informed intervention models that integrate mental health support into routine orthopedic practice, providing empirical evidence for the precise personalization of mental health services for patients with OA.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling from orthopedic clinics in a single city significantly limits the generalizability of the findings. Psychological needs and service preferences are highly influenced by cultural and contextual factors; thus, our results may not be fully representative of the broader OA population in other regions or healthcare settings. Second, the cross-sectional design cannot capture the dynamic nature of preferences or establish causal relationships. Third, although content validity and internal consistency reliability of the self-designed questionnaire were established with acceptable values, exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis was not conducted to assess its structural validity. As this instrument was developed for the purposes of this study and remains preliminary, its factor structure requires further validation in future research.

Conclusions

This mixed-methods study shows that patients with OA have multifaceted mental health needs that go beyond clinically measurable psychological distress. Patients emphasized the importance of empathic communication, interdisciplinary collaboration, flexible service formats, and accessible care settings. These findings suggest that mental health needs extend beyond clinically measurable distress and call for integrated, continuous, and patient-centered approaches.

However, these conclusions should be interpreted in light of methodological limitations, such as the use of a preliminary, self-developed questionnaire and a cross-sectional, single-city sample. Therefore, this study does not offer definitive solutions but points to critical areas for future work, including tool validation and the development of integrated, preference-informed intervention models.
