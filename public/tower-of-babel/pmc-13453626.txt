Modified Angulated Innominate Osteotomy Using Cortical Screw Fixation for Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip: A Technical Note with a Case Series
Authors: Yuta Tsukagoshi, Hiroshi Kamada, Yohei Tomaru, Daisuke Nozawa, Shogo Nakagawa, Shutaro Aiba, Miho Asanuma, Yukino Sekine, Yasuhiro Homma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Salter innominate osteotomy (SIO) has been widely used for the treatment of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) since its original description, with generally favorable clinical outcomes (1). Nevertheless, loss of correction before bone union can compromise the effectiveness of the procedure (2), highlighting the importance of achieving stable fixation at the osteotomy site.

Various modifications of SIO have been proposed to improve stability and promote bone healing (3), (4). Among these, angulated innominate osteotomy without bone grafting, as described by Morita et al., represents an important advancement by eliminating the need for bone graft while maintaining acetabular reorientation (5). Based on its biomechanical concept and reduced surgical invasiveness, we have adopted angulated innominate osteotomy as our preferred technique.

However, despite these theoretical advantages, we encountered cases in which early correction loss occurred after angulated innominate osteotomy during the postoperative period. This clinical experience suggested that the osteotomy design alone may not be sufficient to ensure stability and that the fixation method plays a critical role in maintaining correction until bone union.

Fixation in angulated innominate osteotomy has largely relied on traditional Kirschner wires (K-wires), which may be associated with limited rigidity, migration, and skin-related complications. These considerations prompted us to explore an alternative fixation strategy that could provide stronger initial stability without increasing surgical complexity.

We hypothesized that replacing K-wire fixation with cortical screw fixation would improve stability during the critical bone-healing phase. The purpose of this technical note is to describe a modified angulated innominate osteotomy using 3.5-mm solid cortical screws and to report early clinical and radiographic outcomes in a consecutive case series.

Materials and Methods

Study design and patients

A retrospective review was conducted of a consecutive series of seven patients with DDH who underwent SIO using a modified cortical screw fixation technique. All procedures were performed as isolated pelvic osteotomies. Clinical data collected included age at surgery, operative time, number of screws used, time to full weight-bearing, and perioperative complications.

Radiographic evaluation included the acetabular index (AI), center-edge angle (CEA), acetabular head index (AHI), and the distance-D (D) measured preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, and at 6 months postoperatively. D was defined as lateral displacement of the distal fragment (positive values indicate lateral displacement, and negative values indicate medial displacement) (Figure 1) (6). In this study, we evaluated the reproducibility of the measurement of AI, CEA, AHI, and D. The first author and one coauthor independently measured all parameters for all hips. Measurements by the two observers were compared to assess interobserver reliability. Medical records and radiographs obtained during follow-up were reviewed to determine the presence or absence of correction loss, screw breakage, and implant-related complications. The operative time required for elective implant removal was also recorded. Approval was obtained from the hospital’s institutional review board (approval number H21-362) to perform the retrospective review of medical records and radiographs.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed using a standard anterior approach to the ilium. After exposure of the iliac wing, an angulated innominate osteotomy was carried out according to the previously described technique of Morita et al., allowing anterior and lateral redirection of the acetabulum without the use of bone graft (Figure 2) (5).

Following acetabular reorientation, provisional fixation was achieved using two or three 2.5-mm Kirschner wires inserted from the iliac crest toward the posterior column cortex at the inferior margin of the greater sciatic notch. These Kirschner wires served to maintain the corrected position and to guide definitive fixation.

Each provisional Kirschner wire was then directly exchanged for a 3.5-mm solid cortical screw along the same trajectory without additional drilling. The screws were inserted to engage the posterior column cortex, providing rigid fixation (Figure 3 and 4). Because the diameter of the provisional Kirschner wire closely matched the core diameter of the cortical screw, the exchange was performed smoothly without adding operative steps compared with conventional techniques.

Postoperative management

Postoperatively, patients were immobilized in a hip spica cast for 6 to 7 weeks, after which full weight-bearing was permitted according to radiographic confirmation of early healing. Progression to full weight-bearing was recorded for each patient.

Results

All patients were female. The mean age at surgery was 5.0 years (range: 3.5-6.1 years), and the mean operative time was 90 minutes, which is consistent with previously reported operative times for conventional SIO (5), (7). Two cortical screws were used in four cases, and three screws were used in three cases. Postoperatively, patients were immobilized in a hip spica cast for 6 to 7 weeks, after which full weight-bearing was permitted. Progression to full weight-bearing was achieved at a mean of 6.9 weeks (Table 1).

Discussion

The primary objective of the present technique is to prevent correction loss during the bone-healing phase following SIO. Loss of correction before bone union can compromise the effectiveness of the procedure, and stable fixation during this period is therefore essential. By combining angulated innominate osteotomy without bone grafting (5) and rigid cortical screw fixation, our method provides reliable mechanical stability without increasing surgical complexity.

Angulated innominate osteotomy, as described by Morita et al., represents an important modification of the original Salter technique by eliminating the need for bone grafting while maintaining acetabular reorientation. However, fixation in this modified procedure has continued to rely primarily on Kirschner wires, and concerns regarding stability during the bone-healing period remain. Traditional smooth Kirschner wires are simple and widely used but may be associated with limited rigidity, migration, and skin-related complications (2), (8).

To address these limitations, threaded Kirschner wires have been used by some surgeons to reduce migration and improve fixation stability (9). Although threaded wires may provide improved resistance to backing out compared with smooth wires, the overall rigidity of wire fixation remains inherently limited compared with screw fixation, particularly under early loading conditions. The present technique therefore replaces wire fixation with cortical screw fixation to achieve stronger mechanical stability while preserving the simplicity of the conventional workflow, as provisional Kirschner wires can be directly exchanged for screws along the same trajectory without additional drilling.

Several bioabsorbable fixation strategies have been proposed as alternatives to metallic implants. Kremli (10) reported the use of bioabsorbable rods after SIO, but correction loss occurred in 3 of 18 hips, and these cases were considered surgical failures. More recently, Hedelin et al. (11) described fixation using poly lactic-co-glycolic acid resorbable screws, reporting generally favorable short-term outcomes; however, implant failure was observed following early postoperative trauma. Together, these reports suggest that, despite advances in biomaterials, absorbable implants may remain vulnerable to breakage and correction loss during the critical healing phase. In addition, the radiolucency of absorbable implants may hinder early detection of implant failure, whereas metallic implants allow immediate radiographic assessment of fixation integrity.

Although the present technique demonstrated stable fixation without correction loss in this series, it cannot be concluded that cortical screw fixation is superior to other fixation methods, including K-wire or bioabsorbable fixation, based on the current data. The primary aim of this study was to demonstrate the feasibility and safety of this technique rather than to establish its superiority. In the present study, all patients were treated with a conventional postoperative protocol, including hip spica cast immobilization for approximately 6-7 weeks. Therefore, the potential advantage of rigid fixation in allowing earlier mobilization or omission of casting could not be evaluated. Future studies are needed to determine whether this technique permits a reduction in immobilization period or earlier weight-bearing. These considerations suggest that the present technique may provide a stable fixation construct, which could support modifications of postoperative management. Further investigation is warranted to clarify whether less restrictive postoperative protocols can be safely implemented.

With respect to implant material, a titanium alloy screw was used in the first case of this series; however, its high osseointegration made elective implant removal slightly difficult. From the second case onward, stainless steel cortical screws were used, allowing smooth and uncomplicated implant removal. Thus, the advantages of stainless steel screws include not only low cost and reliable mechanical strength but also favorable characteristics for elective removal.

Although avoidance of implant removal is often cited as an advantage of absorbable implants, this benefit must be balanced against their higher initial cost and potential mechanical limitations. In contrast, stainless steel cortical screws are inexpensive and provide predictable mechanical strength. In our experience, elective implant removal was straightforward and required minimal operative time, making the overall clinical burden acceptable.

One potential concern associated with screw fixation is prominence of the screw head. Compared with bent Kirschner wires that are typically buried subcutaneously, the screw head may be more noticeable beneath the skin. In our series, mild prominence of the screw head was observed in some patients; however, no skin irritation, wound complications, or implant-related symptoms requiring early removal occurred. Although these findings suggest acceptable soft-tissue tolerance, careful attention to screw placement and postoperative observation remains necessary, particularly in patients with thin soft tissue coverage.

Although thin K-wires are traditionally used in younger children, the present case series included patients as young as 3.5 years with stable outcomes. Furthermore, we have successfully applied the same fixation concept in a 1.5-year-old patient who underwent open reduction combined with innominate osteotomy for late-detected DDH (not included in this series), suggesting that this technique can be applied to all patients for whom SIO is indicated.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of cases was small, and the follow-up period was relatively short. Although the maximum follow-up was 4 years, radiographic evaluation at 6 months was considered sufficient to assess the primary advantage of this technique, namely prevention of correction loss before bone union. Second, only radiographic outcomes were evaluated, and clinical or functional outcomes were not assessed. Third, no control group was included for comparison with conventional K-wire or bioabsorbable fixation. Therefore, the relative advantages of this technique over other fixation methods cannot be determined.

In conclusion, modified angulated innominate osteotomy using 3.5-mm solid cortical screw fixation provided stable fixation during the bone-healing period without increasing surgical complexity in this case series. This technique appears to be a feasible and reliable option for fixation in angulated innominate osteotomy. Further studies are required to determine its potential advantages over other fixation methods and to evaluate whether it allows modification of postoperative management.
