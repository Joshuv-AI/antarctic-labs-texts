“Reply: Improving Insight and Application: A Commentary on the Link between Initial Depression and Anxiety and Long‐Term Health Outcomes in Parkinson's Disease Patients”
Authors: Nabila Dahodwala, Daniel Weintraub, Hyunkeun R. Cho, Chelsea Caspell‐Garcia, Roseanne D. Dobkin
Published: 2024

We appreciate the letter by Lin and colleagues 1 in response to our article, “Association of baseline depression and anxiety with longitudinal health outcomes in Parkinson's disease.” 2

They raise several important methodological considerations when critically evaluating this type of clinical research. First, longitudinal observational data can identify relevant associations, but not determine causality. Therefore, we wholeheartedly agree that randomized controlled trials, comprehensive clinical evaluations and systematic longitudinal assessments are needed to help evaluate the true effects of neuropsychiatric complications and their treatment. This will improve the care of individuals with PD who have depression and/or anxiety.

Second, missing data can bias the findings of any research study, and we recognize the challenges associated with long‐term follow‐up and data completeness. The problem of missing data can be partially addressed through statistical techniques (eg, multiple imputation or last observation carried forward [LOCF]), as the authors note. Multiple imputation could indeed provide more efficient estimates, assuming the accuracy of the imputed data. However, the LOCF method may introduce bias into statistical analysis, as it assumes stability in longitudinal outcomes, which is not the case in our study. Other methods to address the bias introduced from missing data include sensitivity analyses and multi‐level models with and without weighting. There are advantages and disadvantages to each approach that have been well described. 3 To robustly manage missing data, we employed the generalized estimation equation (GEE) method, which effectively utilizes all available observations and produces unbiased estimates under the assumption of data being missing completely at random (MCAR). In situations where this assumption does not hold, potential biases could arise. To mitigate this, one can implement the inverse probability‐weighted GEE method, which is a robust approach for managing data that is missing at random (MAR). This method helps ensure the robustness and validity of study results.

Lastly, due to the specific data available and the small size of our analytic sample, we were not able to explore many potential contributors to health outcomes including social determinants of health. On the other hand, the relatively homogeneous cohort of newly diagnosed, untreated patients with PD that we studied allowed us to observe key associations between anxiety, depression and other health outcomes, such as motor scores, disability and time to dopamine replacement therapy initiation, without the need for a larger sample size. In any study, researchers must balance the practicality of study costs, time and participant burden with the desire to collect a comprehensive battery of assessments. Nevertheless, studies with larger, more diverse samples are indeed needed to confirm our findings and expand its generalizability.

In conclusion, we believe that the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) provides a unique, invaluable, free and publicly available resource to investigate health outcomes in Parkinson's disease. More research in this area with different samples and study designs will generate new knowledge about the impact of depression/anxiety and their treatment in PD. This will lead to better understanding of neuropsychiatric disease trajectory, and practical tools to manage the burden of psychiatric symptoms in Parkinson's disease.
