Conversion therapy for biliary tract cancer: redefining survival through selection, response, and resectability
Authors: Van Khanh Nguyen, Hoang Hai Duong, Thanh Tung Lai, Masaki Kaibori
Published: 2026

Historically, systemic therapy has been the dominant management for advanced biliary tract cancer (BTC), but it has offered only a limited benefit in terms of long-term survival (1). The emergence of chemoimmunotherapy combinations has improved outcomes modestly, as reflected in recent phase III trials (2,3). In contrast, the ZSAB-TransGOLP study (4) introduces a new concept: instead of merely intensifying systemic therapy, it leverages it as a transitional step to enable conversion surgery with curative intent. At first glance, the results appear compelling. The GOLP regimen (a combination of tislelizumab, lenvatinib, and GEMOX chemotherapy) achieved high objective response rates, a 63% R0 resection rate, and a median overall survival of 30.8 months in the surgical subgroup, results that compare favorably with historical benchmarks. Previous meta-analyses have suggested that survival may be prolonged in patients with initially unresectable, locally advanced BTC treated with systemic therapy together with aggressive surgical strategies (5). However, apparent improvements relative to historical benchmarks should be interpreted with caution given the differences in patient populations and treatment intent, and these outcomes must be viewed within the context of substantial biological and methodological selection.

A central implication of this study is the evolving definition of therapeutic success for BTC. Traditionally, endpoints such as response rate and progression-free survival have guided evaluation (6). In contrast, surgical outcomes such as the R0 resection rate are increasingly recognized as prognostic indicators of long-term survival (7,8). Importantly, the R0 resection rate should not be interpreted as a direct pharmacologic endpoint of systemic therapy alone, but as a composite outcome influenced by tumor biology, treatment response, patient selection, and surgical decision-making. Within this framework, the radiologic response serves less as a terminal outcome and more as a biological gatekeeper for surgical eligibility. Consequently, overall survival becomes partially determined by the proportion of patients who improve from unresectable disease status to being eligible for curative-intent resection. Importantly, conversion to surgical eligibility does not necessarily guarantee complete oncologic clearance. Despite favorable treatment responses and multidisciplinary reassessment, some patients may ultimately undergo R1 resection because of the infiltrative nature of BTC and the limitations of preoperative assessment. Although these patients do not achieve curative resection, they remain distinct from patients who never become eligible for surgery. While R0 resection remains the optimal oncologic outcome, surgery itself represents only one component within a broader sequence of biological selection, treatment response, and therapeutic decision-making. This transformation reflects not only a shift in treatment goals but also a fundamental redefinition of how survival outcomes are achieved, as results are increasingly shaped by subsequent selection processes rather than by consistent treatment effects across the entire patient population.

These observations highlight an inherent interpretive challenge in conversion strategies: disentangling treatment effect from biological enrichment. Patients who ultimately undergo surgery represent a subgroup characterized by a favorable treatment response, preserved performance status, and likely less aggressive tumor biology. Only patients whose tumors respond sufficiently are advanced to resection eligibility, resulting in a cohort that is biologically distinct from the original population. This introduces unavoidable selection bias and potential immortal time bias, complicating causal interpretation of survival differences between the surgical and non-surgical groups (9). Future studies should employ designs that explicitly account for time-dependent treatment selection, including modeling conversion surgery as a time-dependent exposure in survival analyses. Within this context, surgery should not be viewed as an isolated determinant of outcome but rather as both a marker of favorable biology and a potential mediator of survival benefit. While resection may contribute via macroscopic tumor clearance, the ability to undergo surgery itself reflects underlying disease responsiveness. Accordingly, conversion surgery is an intermediate state within a dynamic treatment trajectory instead of a direct causal driver of prolonged survival.

The study also explores refinements in response assessment using the TDTP-RECIST criteria (4), a volumetric imaging method demonstrating closer correlation with pathological response compared with conventional RECIST v1.1 (10). Although promising, such approaches require careful validation, particularly given the interdependence among interpretation, surgical decision-making, and pathological outcomes in conversion settings. Analyses of biomarkers, including PD-L1 expression and macrophage-related signatures, remain exploratory and highlight the ongoing uncertainty regarding robust predictive biomarkers for identifying patients most likely to benefit from conversion strategies (11,12). From a feasibility standpoint, the GOLP regimen demonstrates an acceptable safety profile, supporting the integration of intensive systemic therapy with surgery in select patients, although generalizability to other populations remains uncertain.

Taken together, the ZSAB-TransGOLP study underscores both the promise and complexity of conversion therapy for BTC, highlighting the importance of trial designs that explicitly account for time-dependent treatment selection when evaluating conversion strategies. Its most important contribution may lie not in establishing the superiority of a specific regimen, but in illustrating how survival outcomes can be shaped by the interplay of response, selection, and resectability, highlighting the need for future studies employing rigorous comparative designs and validated biomarkers to better define this therapeutic paradigm.
