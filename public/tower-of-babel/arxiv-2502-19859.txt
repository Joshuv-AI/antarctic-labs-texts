IL-SOAR : Imitation Learning with Soft Optimistic Actor cRitic
Stefano Viel*, Luca Viano*, Volkan Cevher

Abstract
This paper introduces the SOAR framework for imitation learning. SOAR is an algorithmic template that learns a policy from expert demonstrations with a primal dual style algorithm that alternates cost and policy updates. Within the policy updates, the SOAR framework uses an actor critic method with multiple critics to estimate the critic uncertainty and build an optimistic critic fundamental to drive exploration. When instantiated in the tabular setting, we get a provable algorithm with guarantees that matches the best known results in the desired accuracy parameter ϵ \epsilon . Practically, the SOAR template can boost the performance of any imitation learning algorithm based on Soft Actror Critic (SAC). As an example, we show that SOAR can boost consistently the performance of the following SAC-based imitation learning algorithms: f f -IRL, ML-IRL and CSIL. Overall, thanks to SOAR, the required number of episodes to achieve the same performance is reduced by half. 0 0 0 Project code available at https://github.com/stefanoviel/SOAR-IL/tree/master

Introduction

Several recent state of the art imitation learning (IL) algorithms
[
55
,
115
,
26
,
106
,
102
]
are built on Soft Actor Critic (SAC)
[
28
]
to perform the policy updates.
SAC uses
entropy
regularized policy updates to maintain a strictly positive probability of taking each action. However, this is known to be an inefficient exploration strategy if deployed alone
[
10
]
.

Indeed, several recent theoretical imitation learning achieve performance guarantees by adding exploration bonuses on top of the regularized policy updates, which encourage the learner to visit state-action pairs that have not been visited previously. Unfortunately, such works are only available in the tabular setting
[
85
,
110
]
and in the linear setting
[
103
]
. The design of the exploration bonuses in these works is strictly tight to the tabular or linear structure of the transition dynamics, therefore, these analyses offer little insight on how to design an efficient exploration mechanism using neural network function approximation.

There is, therefore, a lack of a technique that satisfies the following two requirements.

•

It is statistically and computationally efficient in the tabular setting.

•

It can be implemented easily in continuous states and actions problems requiring neural networks function approximation.

In this paper, we present a general template, dubbed Soft Optimistic Actor cRitic Imitation Learning (SOAR-IL) satisfying these requirements.

The main idea is to act according to an
optimistic
critic within the SAC block on which many IL algorithms rely.
Here, optimism means appropriately underestimating the expected cumulative cost incurred by playing a policy in the environment. This principle known as
optimism in the face of uncertainty
has led to several successful algorithms in the bandits community.

While optimism is often achieved using the structure of the problem (tabular, linear, etc.), in this work, we build optimistic estimators using an ensemble technique. That is, multiple estimators for the same quantity are maintained and aggregated to obtain an optimistic estimator.
This technique scales well with deep imitation learning.
To summarize, we have the following contributions.

Theoretical contribution

We show that there exists a computationally efficient algorithm that uses an ensemble based exploration technique that gives access to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
expert trajectories and
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
interactions in a tabular MDP outputs a policy such that its cumulative expected cost is at most
\epsilon
higher than the expert cumulative expected cost with high probability.

Practical Contribution

We apply an ensemble-based exploration technique, SOAR, to boost the performance of deep imitation learning algorithms built on SAC, demonstrating its effectiveness on MuJoCo environments. Specifically, we show that incorporating SOAR consistently boosts the performance of base methods such as Coherent Soft Imitation Learning (CSIL)
[
106
]
, Maximum Likelihood IRL (ML-IRL)
[
115
]
and RKL
[
55
]
. As shown in Figure

Value functions and occupancy measures
We define the state value function at state

for the policy
\pi

V_{c}^{\pi}(s)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum^{\infty}_{h=0}\gamma^{h}c(s_{h},a_{h})|s_{1}=s}\right]

The

Another convenient quantity is the occupancy measure of a policy

Imitation Learning

Theoretical analysis

We need to start with an important remark on the structure of the MDP considered in the proof.

Remark 4.1
.

For technical reasons, in particular for the proof of
Corollary

4.11
, we consider as intermediate step in the proof MDPs where from each state action pairs is possible to observe a transition to only two other possible states.
While this restriction on the dynamics appears to be limiting any MDP can be cast into this form at the cost of a quadratic blow up in the number of states, from
\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|
to
\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|^{2}
. To see this, for a general MDP where from a given state action pair a transition to all possible
\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|
states can be observed
is equivalent to a binarized MDP where this
one layer
transition is represented with a tree of depth at most
\log_{2}\left({\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|}\right)
with binary transitions only. Moreover, the discount factor in the binarized MDP should be set to
\gamma_{\mathrm{bin}}=\gamma^{-\log_{2}\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|}
to maintain the return unchanged.
We consider in this section a binarized MDP with
\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|
states in this section and we squared the number of states in stating
Theorem

3.4
which holds for general MDPs. Moreover, in stating the result for general MDP we also inflated the effective horizon by a factor
\log_{2}\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|
as shown in
Lemma

C.5
.

As mentioned, the proof is decomposed into two main parts: (i) bounding the policy regret
\mathrm{Regret}_{\pi}
and (ii) bounding the cost updates regret
\mathrm{Regret}_{c}
.
In particular, we can prove the two following results.

Theorem 4.2
.

Policy Regret

In a binarized MDP with
\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|
states and discount factor
\gamma
, it holds that with probability
1-3\delta
, for any policy
\pi^{\star}
,
\mathrm{Regret}_{\pi}(K,\pi^{\star})
is upper bounded by

\frac{\log\left|{\mathcal{A}}\right|}{\eta(1-\gamma)}+\frac{\eta K}{(1-\gamma)^{4}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left({\frac{\sqrt{K\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|^{2}\left|{\mathcal{A}}\right|\log(1/\delta)}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}}\right)

and for
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\log\left|{\mathcal{A}}\right|(1-\gamma)^{3}}{K}}
it holds that using the update in (
Min
) or in (
Mean-Std
) it holds that

SOAR for continuous state and actions problems.

In this section, we explain how
Algorithm

1
is instantiated in imitation learning problems with continuous states and action spaces, which therefore requires neural networks to approximate the value function and policy updates.
Since in our analysis for the tabular case, we need to use multiplicative weights/softmax updates, we decided to use SAC, which is an approximation of such updates in the continuous state-action setting.

However, the standard SAC keeps only one network, often called the critic network, to estimate the
Q
values.
On the other hand, we use a pair of them to avoid the excessive overestimation noticed in Double DQN
[
99
]
.
Since it uses only one pair of critics, SAC cannot achieve optimism reliably with high probability.

To fix this issue, we consider multiple critics and we used as an optimistic estimate the mean minus the standard deviation of the ensemble as explained in
Algorithm

5

For any state
s

\left[{0,(1-\gamma)^{-1}}\right]
.

Conclusions and Open Questions

While there has been interest in developing heuristically effective exploration techniques in deep RL, the same is not true for deep IL.
For example, even in the detailed study

the effectiveness of deep exploration techniques is not investigated.
Prior to our work, only few studied the benefits of exploration in imitation learning, mostly in the state-only regime
[

Similarly, the recent work
[
111
]
uses exploration technique in Deep IL but requires solving a complicated non-concave maximization problem.
Our approach is remarkably easier to implement. It achieves convincing empirical results results and enjoys theoretical guarantees.
Moreover, our framework can be expected to be beneficial for any existing or future deep IL algorithm using SAC for policy updates.

Upper bounding the policy regret

See

Proof.

Let us fix a state-action-next state triplet
s,a,s^{\prime}
, a batch index
\ell\in[L]
and an iteration index
k\in[K]
.
Then, we consider the following stochastic estimator for the probability of transitioning to
s^{\prime}
from state
s
taking action
a
.

\displaystyle\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a,s^{\prime})}{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)+2}=\frac{\sum_{\bar{s},\bar{a},\bar{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}_{\ell}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bar{s},\bar{a},\bar{s}^{\prime}=s,a,s^{\prime}}\right\}}}{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)+2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\bar{s},\bar{a},\bar{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}_{\ell}:\bar{s},\bar{a}=s,a}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bar{s}^{\prime}=s^{\prime}}\right\}}}{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)+2}.

Notice that in above estimators the denominator corresponds to the number of visits of the pair
s,a
up to the time
k\in[K]
within the batch
\ell\in[L]
, i.e.
N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)
, increased by
2
for technical reasons. At the numerator instead we have the sum of
N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)
indicators functions which equals one when the state following the state action pair
s,a
is equal to
s^{\prime}
. Each of this indicator is a random variable distributed
according to a Bernoulli random variable with mean
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)
.
At this point, we can use the technique introduced in
[
9
]
. In particular, we will show that

\mathbb{P}\left[{\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a)}\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}~~~\forall s,a,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathrm{children}(s),\forall k\in[K],\forall\ell\in[L].

We distinguish
3
cases:
N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)=0
,
N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)=1
,
N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)\geq 2
.
If
N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)=0
, then, we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)=0\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a).

If
N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)=1
, we distinguish two cases. If
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\geq\frac{1}{3}
, then

\displaystyle\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq\frac{1}{3}\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a).

Otherwise, if
N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)=1
, and
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq\frac{1}{3}
then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{\frac{\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bar{s}^{\prime}=s^{\prime}}\right\}}}{3}\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a)}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bar{s}^{\prime}=s^{\prime}}\right\}}=0}\right]=1-P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\geq\frac{2}{3}\geq\frac{1}{4}.

Finally, for
N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)\geq 2
,we have that for
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\geq 1-\frac{1}{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)}
it holds that

\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq\frac{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)}{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)+2}=1-\frac{2}{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)+2}\leq 1-\frac{1}{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)}\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a),

where the last inequality holds for
N^{k}_{\ell}\geq 2
.
Otherwise, for
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq 1-\frac{1}{N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)}
we can apply
[
9
, Lemma 2]
(adapted from
[
107
, Corollary 1]
) to obtain

\mathbb{P}\left[{\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a)}\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[{\sum_{\bar{s},\bar{a},\bar{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}_{\ell}:\bar{s},\bar{a}=s,a}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bar{s}^{\prime}=s^{\prime}}\right\}}\leq N^{k}_{\ell}(s,a)P(s^{\prime}|s,a)}\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}.

At this point notice that for any positive vector
V\in[0,(1-\gamma)^{-1}]^{\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a)~~\forall s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[{\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})~~\forall s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[{\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\leq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})}\right]

where the inequality holds because of the following implication between events

\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})~~\forall s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)\implies\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\leq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime}).

Moreover, since the estimation at each state
s^{\prime}
is independent we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a)~~\forall s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}\right]=\prod_{s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}\mathbb{P}\left[{\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a)}\right]\geq\left({\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{\left|{\mathrm{children}(s)}\right|}

Then, for any

is concluded by the following chain of inequalities

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left[{\min_{\ell\in[L]}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\geq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[{\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\geq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})~~\forall\ell\in[L]}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell\in[L]}\left({1-\mathbb{P}\left[{\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})\leq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V(s^{\prime})}\right]}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{\ell\in[L]}\left({1-\mathbb{P}\left[{\widehat{P}^{k}_{\ell}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq P(s^{\prime}|s,a)~~\forall s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{children}(s)}\right]}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{\ell\in[L]}\left({1-\frac{1}{4}^{\left|{\mathrm{children}(s)}\right|}}\right)\leq e^{-L\log\left({\frac{4^{\left|{\mathrm{children}(s)}\right|}}{4^{\left|{\mathrm{children}(s)}\right|}-1}}\right)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\eta}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d^{\pi^{k}}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi^{k+1}(a|x)Q_{k}(x,a)+\frac{\eta}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d^{\pi^{k}}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi^{k}(a|x)Q_{k}(x,a)

where the last inequality follows by Jensen’s inequality and convexity of
-\log
.
Hence, we continue the upper bound as follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d^{\pi^{k}}}\left[{\left\|{\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)-\pi^{k+1}(\cdot|x)}\right\|^{2}_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d^{\pi^{k}}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(x,a)\cdot(\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)-\pi^{k+1}(\cdot|x))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta Q_{\max}}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d^{\pi^{k}}}\left[{\left\|{\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)-\pi^{k+1}(\cdot|x)}\right\|_{1}}\right]

Which implies, by Jensen’s inequality and diving both sides by
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d^{\pi^{k}}}\left[{\left\|{\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)-\pi^{k+1}(\cdot|x)}\right\|_{1}}\right]
that

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d^{\pi^{k}}}\left[{\left\|{\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)-\pi^{k+1}(\cdot|x)}\right\|_{1}}\right]\leq\eta Q_{\max}.

∎

Lemma C.4
.

Samuelson’s inequality

Let us consider
L
scalars
\left\{{X_{\ell}}\right\}^{L}_{\ell=1}
and denote the sample mean as
\bar{X}=L^{-1}\sum^{L}_{\ell=1}X_{\ell}

and the empirical standard deviation as
\hat{\sigma}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum^{L}_{\ell=1}(X_{\ell}-\bar{X})^{2}}{L-1}}
, then it holds that

\bar{X}-\sqrt{L-1}\hat{\sigma}\leq X_{\ell}\leq\bar{X}+\sqrt{L-1}\hat{\sigma}~~~\forall~~~\ell\in[L]

Proof.

Let us consider an arbitrary vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
. Then, we have that
\left\|{v}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|{v}\right\|_{2}
. At this point let us consider
v=[X_{1}-\bar{X},\dots,X_{L}-\bar{X}]^{T}
. Moreover,
let us define as
\ell^{\star}
the index such that
\left\|{v}\right\|_{\infty}=\left|{X_{\ell^{\star}}-\bar{X}}\right|
.
Then, we have that for all
\ell\in[L]
,

\left|{X_{\ell}-\bar{X}}\right|\leq\left|{X_{\ell^{\star}}-\bar{X}}\right|\leq\sqrt{\sum^{L}_{\ell=1}(X_{\ell}-\bar{X})^{2}}=\sqrt{L-1}\hat{\sigma}.

Therefore, rewriting the absolute value it holds that

\bar{X}-\sqrt{L-1}\hat{\sigma}\leq X_{\ell}\leq\bar{X}+\sqrt{L-1}\hat{\sigma}

∎

Lemma C.5
.

It holds that

\frac{1}{1-\gamma^{1/\log_{2}\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|}}\leq\frac{\log_{2}{\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|}+2}{1-\gamma}.

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma^{1/\log_{2}\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma^{1/\log_{2}\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|}}

Expert Samples:

D.1
Hyperparameters tuning

To determine how different numbers of neural networks changed the performance, we conducted an ablation study on both the clipping value and the number of neural networks. We performed this analysis for the ML-IRL algorithm on the Ant-V5 environment. We chose this environment since previous experiments showed that its higher complexity led to higher variance in the performance of different algorithms. It was necessary to perform a grid search on the number of neural networks because we noticed that different numbers of neural networks preferred different clipping values.

The results are reported in
Figure

5
. As we observed, in all cases, adding more neural networks leads to better performances. However, this improvement does not increase proportionally with the number of neural networks; in fact, the run with 10 neural networks is outperformed by the one with 4. This led us to select 4 as the fixed value of neural networks, also justified by the much slower training time of the 10-network case.

Figure 5
:
Mean return of ML-IRL Ant-v5 with a different number of neural networks. The grid search for the clipping values was performed over the following values [0.1 0.5 1 5 10 50]. Results are averaged over 3 seeds.

For every environment and algorithm, we performed a grid search over different clipping values. The range of clipping values varied across algorithms. Figure
6
shows the different values used in the search and their impact on performance. The difference in performance across clipping values is small in simpler environments (e.g., Hopper or Walker2d) while it becomes more evident in more complex environments with larger state-action spaces. These plots also show the necessity of the clipping for the exploration bonus. In most environments and algorithms, when a large clipping value is applied, it leads to performance degradation.

Empirically, the Q-network’s standard deviation diverges due to unclipped Q-values. Without value clipping, high Q-values for specific state-action pairs increase the probability of being visited, causing more of these pairs to accumulate in the replay buffer across different rollouts and potentially amplifying the standard deviation across the q-network for the next update. This justifies the necessity of a clipping value on the exploration bonus.
