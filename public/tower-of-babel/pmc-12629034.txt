The epidermal growth factor receptor regulates YAP signalling and promotes diabetic retinal fibrosis by modulating the degradation of SAV1
Authors: Wei Zhang, Xiaopei Zhang, Kexi Chen, Ying Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and Yes-associated protein (YAP) signalling pathways are two intrinsic mechanisms that typically control fibrosis. The intracellular molecules that regulate these two pathways remain unclear. Here, we investigated how EGFR and YAP signalling control retinal Müller cell-related diabetic retinal fibrosis in vivo and in vitro.MethodsHuman retinal Müller cells (MIO-M1) were transfected in vitro with a Flag-tagged EGFR expression plasmid or an EGFR-targeting siRNA. Western blotting and coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) were used to assess the role of EGFR in mediating the interaction between SAV1 and Herc4 and its degradation. In vivo, mice with STZ-induced diabetes received erlotinib (an EGFR inhibitor) intraperitoneally or an intravitreal anti-EGFR antibody. Retinal fibrosis was evaluated via histopathology and YAP signalling analysis.ResultsEGFR inhibited YAP signalling by degrading SAV1 and promoting the interaction between SAV1 and the E3 ligase Herc4. Therefore, EGFR can inhibit the Hippo cascade and promote the nuclear translocation of YAP, thus activating the transcription of downstream Hippo target genes. Pharmacological inhibition of EGFR was found to stimulate the YAP signalling pathway and effectively mitigate retinal fibrosis changes in diabetic mice. Moreover, the intravitreal injection of anti-EGFR antibody markedly inhibited diabetic retinal fibrosis, and initiating this treatment early and administering it multiple times proved to be even more efficacious.ConclusionsThe results of this study demonstrate that EGFR blocks the Hippo pathway by degrading SAV1 in Müller cells. Targeting EGFR effectively mitigated retinal fibrosis in diabetic mice, highlighting its therapeutic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07357-7.Keywords: Epidermal growth factor receptor, Yes-associated protein, Diabetic retinopathy, Retinal fibrosis, SAV1

Introduction

Normal vision depends on a well-organized retinal structure, clear visual axis, and normal functional cell compartment for light transmission. Retinal fibrosis can occur in several retinal diseases, including proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) [1]. Excessive proliferation of retinal fibrous tissue in PDR leads to traction retinal detachment. Chronic or acute retinal injury (such as ischaemia, neurodegeneration, and inflammation) contributes to retinal fibrosis or scarring [2, 3]. Fibrosis changes the structure of the retina, destroys normal cellular connections, and leads to impaired vision [4]. Once retinal fibrosis is established, it is irreversible, and there is currently no effective treatment to prevent it.

Retinal fibrosis is characterized by the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix protein (ECM), which is secreted by transformed retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, vascular endothelial cells, myofibroblast-like cells, microglia, astrocytes, and activated Müller cells [5, 6]. RPE cells and Müller glial cells play key roles in maintaining normal retinal homeostasis [7]. However, under disease conditions, RPE cells can differentiate into myofibroblast-like cells, and Müller cells become gliotic. Several studies have shown that transdifferentiated RPE cells and gliotic Müller cells play key roles in the formation of retinal fibrosis in nAMD, PDR, and PVR [8, 9]. Retinal fibrosis is the main reason for the poor efficacy of intravitreal injection of a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitor to treat neovascular AMD (nAMD), which is characterized by abnormal subretinal vascular growth [10]. Subretinal neovascularization may respond to anti-VEGF therapy, but vision could still worsen due to the development of retinal fibrosis.

The Hippo signalling pathway is an important regulator of homeostasis and organ size in many vertebrates [11, 12]. The core Hippo signalling factors include yes-associated protein (YAP) [13], MOB kinase activator 1A/B (MOB1a/b) [14], Salvador (SAV1) [15], mammalian sterile 20-like 1/2 (MST1/2) [16], large tumour suppressor homologue 1/2 (LATS1/2) [17] and transcription coactivator of PDZ binding motif protein (TAZ) [18]. Notably, the phosphorylation of SAV1 can completely activate LATS1 [19]. Then, LATS1/2 effectively phosphorylates YAP and induces subsequent cytoplasmic sequestration, which subsequently promotes YAP degradation via the proteasomal pathway [20]. A series of intracellular and extracellular signals regulate apoptosis, proliferation, and fibrosis by inhibiting YAP signalling [21].

The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR; also known as ErbB1) gene belongs to the ErbB family and is a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor [22] that is expressed in multiple types of mesenchymal cells, epithelial cells, and neurons [23]. EGFR can activate various downstream pathways through binding with ligands [24]. The classical downstream pathways activated by EGFR include the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway, which regulates cell migration, survival [25], and apoptosis resistance, and the Ras/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, which controls cell proliferation and differentiation [26]. Moreover, EGFR can stimulate the proliferation and growth of fibroblasts, vascular endothelial cells, and keratinocytes [27]. A recent study revealed that EGF can promote the proliferation and migration of RPE cells and increase their survival rate by stimulating the EGFR pathway [28]. Li et al. reported that EGFR activation plays an important role in the progression of PVR [29]. Aharonov et al. reported that EGFR deletion significantly decreased the protein and mRNA levels of YAP in cardiomyocytes [30].

Retinal Müller cells are central to the development of retinal fibrosis, given their pivotal role in preserving retinal homeostasis [31]. Notably, the ability of Müller cells to undergo a phenotypic shift into myofibroblast-like cells is recognized by their contractile properties in the epiretinal membrane. This transformation is marked by the expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), highlighting their contributory role in fibrotic processes [32]. Our previous investigations revealed the critical role of YAP in the activation of retinal Müller cells during the development of diabetic retinal fibrosis, implicating the PI3K/Akt/YAP signalling cascade as a pivotal pathway in retinal Müller cell-mediated profibrotic responses in patients with TGF-β-induced diabetic retinal fibrosis [33]. We further demonstrated that YAP signalling regulated the stiffness-induced stimulation of ARPE-19 cells [34] and that the EGFR-dependent activation of YAP played a key role in the pathogenesis of proliferative vitreoretinopathy [35]. However, the exact role of EGFR in the regulation of retinal Müller cell-induced diabetic retinal fibrosis and the Hippo/YAP phosphorylation cascade remains unclear.

Given the critical role of SAV1—a well-established scaffold protein essential for MST1/2 activation and Hippo signalling [36, 37]—and emerging evidence of EGFR–Hippo crosstalk in fibrosis, we investigated whether EGFR regulates SAV1 stability to modulate Hippo/YAP signalling in Müller cells during diabetic retinal fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Culture and treatment of MIO-M1 human Müller cells

This research adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and conformed to the ARVO’s Statement for Animal Use in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The experiment was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of People’s Hospital of Hotan District and was carried out in accordance with the approved guidelines. The human retinal Müller cell line (MIO-M1) was originally derived from human retinas by Dr. Limb [38] and retains multiple important features of Müller cells in situ [38]. We cultured MIO-M1 cells in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum in 5% CO2 at 37 °C and then passaged the MIO-M1 cells when they reached 80% confluence.

Flag-EGFR and Flag-YAP were obtained from Sigma‒Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Flag-EGFR, Flag-YAP or Flag was separately transfected into MIO-M1 Müller cells to test the overexpression of EGFR or YAP. To inhibit the expression of EGFR or the E3 ligase Herc4, we treated MIO-M1 Müller cells with siRNAs targeting EGFR or Herc4. Nonsilencing siRNA (control-siRNA, Con-siRNA), EGFR-siRNA, and Herc4-siRNA were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (CO). We used Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA) to transiently transfect the indicated plasmids or siRNAs according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To test the proteasomal protein degradation pathway, we treated MIO-M1 Müller cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (20 mM) for 6 h. To test whether EGFR affects the stability of the SAV1 protein, a cycloheximide chase assay was performed on the basis of a published protocol [39]. To investigate whether YAP overexpression could rescue the effects of EGFR knockdown, a sequential transfection assay was performed: MIO-M1 Müller cells were first transfected with EGFR-siRNA or control siRNA for 48 hours, followed by transfection with the Flag-YAP or empty vector plasmid for an additional 24 hours.

Induction of experimental diabetes and treatment

Male C57BL/6 mice between 6 and 8 weeks of age (Charles River Laboratory, China) were housed in the Hotan Animal Center with a 12-hour dark and light cycle. Streptozotocin (STZ; 50 mg/kg; Sigma, USA) was intraperitoneally injected into C57BL/6 mice to establish a diabetic mouse model as previously described [33]. Serum glucose levels were measured 48 hours after STZ injection and every week thereafter. C57BL/6 mice with blood glucose concentrations > 16.6 mmol/L were considered STZ-induced diabetic mice. To disrupt the EGFR signalling pathway, erlotinib (80 mg/kg every other day for 12 weeks; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was intraperitoneally administered following the establishment of the diabetic mouse model. To disrupt the EGFR pathway, we administered an anti-EGFR antibody (#2232; Cell Signaling Technology; Danvers, MA, USA) by intravitreal injection to diabetic mice, following the protocol detailed in a previous study [40]. Briefly, we performed intravitreal injection by using a 30 G Hamilton syringe. We intravitreally injected anti-EGFR antibody (2 µl/10 µg solution in sterile PBS) into the dorsonasal quadrant 1.5 mm from the limbus of the right eye. Control mice were given an intravitreal injection of 2 µl of sterile PBS.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Immunocytochemistry was performed as we have previously described [34]. Briefly, MIO-M1 Müller cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then blocked with 10% foetal bovine serum. After that, we cultured the Müller cells with monoclonal antibodies targeting EGFR, SAV1, or Herc4 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA) overnight. Next, we used secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor (Abcam Biotechnology, CA) to probe and fix the Müller cells with DAPI. We obtained images using a Leica DMI4000B instrument (Olympus Soft Imaging Solutions GmbH, Germany).

Immunohistological and histological measurements

Mouse eyes were incubated in PBS for 2 h at pH 7.4, dehydrated and embedded according to a standard method [6]. For histological measurements, we used Masson’s trichrome staining to measure fibrosis in retinal tissue as described in our previous study [33]. Immunohistochemical analysis was performed on eye slices prepared from the paraffin-embedded retinal tissues. We incubated the eye slices with anti-SAV1 and anti-YAP antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA). Next, the slices were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h, and images were then captured.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from retinal samples by TRIzol reagent. We used the SuperScript First Strand Synthesis System to reverse transcribe cDNA. Next, Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix was used to perform quantitative PCR. The primer sequences are shown in the schematic representation of the EGFR-mediated regulation of YAP signaling in diabetic retinal fibrosis.

Immunoprecipitation

Immunoprecipitation was performed as previously described [41]. Briefly, MIO-M1 Müller cell lysates were treated with a mixture of protease inhibitors and RIPA buffer (0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 1% Triton X-100, pH 7.4). Next, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to remove cell debris. A total of 2 mg of the indicated antibody and 2 mg of protein were used for each reaction. The indicator antibody and normal IgG (as a control) were added and incubated with precleared lysates with rotation at 4 °C overnight. Next, the lysates were incubated with protein A or protein G agarose (Abcam Biotechnology, CA) with rotation for 4 hours. Finally, the beads were washed with RIPA buffer solution 3 times and analysed by Western blotting.

Western blotting

Total protein was obtained from mouse retinal samples and MIO-M1 Müller cells. Cytoplasmic and nuclear extraction reagents (Abcam Biotechnology, CA) were used to isolate cytoplasmic and nuclear extracts from MIO-M1 Müller cells. The protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid assay kit. SDS‒PAGE was used to separate equal amounts of protein. Specific antibodies targeting EGFR, SAV1, YAP, Herc4, ɑ-SMA, CTGF, fibronectin, vimentin, E-cadherin, and collagen I were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (TX). The target proteins were subsequently examined using SuperSignal™ West Pico Plus Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

The data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were evaluated by SPSS 23.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). We used the Mann‒Whitney test to analyse two sets of data. We used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA for statistical comparisons of three groups of data. Statistical differences among multiple groups were assessed via post hoc Tukey’s HSD test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

EGFR interacts with SAV1 and promotes YAP activation

Our previous study revealed that EGFR interacts with YAP in RPE cells, suggesting that EGFR is involved in the regulation of the Hippo pathway [35].

Our results in this study revealed a physical interaction between SAV1 and EGFR in MIO-M1 Müller cells. Specifically, endogenous SAV1 coimmunoprecipitated with endogenous EGFR in MIO-M1 Müller cells (Fig. 1A), whereas endogenous SAV1 coimmunoprecipitated with overexpressed Flag-EGFR in MIO-M1 Müller cells transfected with the Flag-EGFR plasmid (Fig. 1B). Moreover, endogenous EGFR coimmunoprecipitated with SAV1 in Müller cells (Fig. 1C), indicating the interaction between EGFR and SAV1 in Müller cells. In addition, immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed that EGFR and SAV1 were colocalized in the cytoplasm of MIO-M1 Müller cells (Fig. 1D). Next, we examined the regulatory role of EGFR in the Hippo signalling pathway in Müller cells. EGFR overexpression in cells transfected with the Flag-EGFR plasmid led to increased levels of YAP and decreased levels of SAV1 (Fig. 1E). A decrease in EGFR expression led to decreased levels of YAP and increased levels of SAV1, which suggested stimulation of Hippo signalling (Fig. 1E). Moreover, Western blot analysis revealed that EGFR overexpression in cells transfected with the Flag-EGFR plasmid resulted in increased levels of YAP in the nucleus of Müller cells (Fig. 1F). In contrast, a decrease in EGFR expression led to a decrease in YAP levels in the nucleus (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, our results revealed that EGFR overexpression in cells transfected with the Flag-EGFR plasmid increased the mRNA levels of CTGF and CYR61. Conversely, a decrease in EGFR expression blocked the mRNA expression of CTGF and CYR61 (Fig. 1G). Taken together, our findings suggest that EGFR interacts with SAV1 and promotes YAP activation.

EGFR mediates SAV1 proteasomal degradation

We next determined whether gain- or loss-of-function of EGFR affected the transcription of SAV1. The qPCR results revealed that EGFR did not affect the mRNA expression of SAV1 in MIO-M1 Müller cells (Fig. 2A). EGFR overexpression following transfection with the Flag-EGFR plasmid in Müller cells strongly reduced the protein level of endogenous SAV1 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2B). Moreover, the ability of the Flag-EGFR overexpression plasmid to reduce SAV1 levels was blocked by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 2C), indicating that SAV1 degradation by EGFR was regulated in a proteasomal protein-dependent manner. To determine whether EGFR affects the stability of the SAV1 protein, we used cycloheximide chase assays to investigate the turnover rate of endogenous SAV1 in the presence or absence of EGFR. Compared with transfection with the empty vector, transfection with the Flag-EGFR plasmid resulted in a significant decrease in the half-life of SAV1 in Müller cells (Fig. 2D). Conversely, compared with transfection with the empty vector, transfection with the EGFR-siRNA led to a significant increase in the half-life of SAV1 in Müller cells (Fig. 2E). Our findings indicate that EGFR regulates the degradation of SAV1 via the proteasomal protein degradation pathway.

EGFR mediates SAV1 degradation by promoting its binding to Herc4

Because Herc4 is a crucial E3 ligase of SAV1 [42], we investigated whether EGFR degrades SAV1 by promoting its binding to Herc4. We found that Herc4 coimmunoprecipitated with endogenous EGFR (Fig. 3A) and overexpressed Flag-EGFR in MIO-M1 Müller cells transfected with the Flag-EGFR plasmid (Fig. 3B). Moreover, IF staining revealed that EGFR and Herc4 were colocalized in the cytoplasm of Müller cells (Fig. 3C). To investigate whether EGFR can form a tripartite complex with SAV1 and Herc4, we performed a two-step coimmunoprecipitation assay and determined that these three molecules could form a complex (Fig. 3D). EGFR overexpression in response to transfection with the Flag-EGFR plasmid increased the level of SAV1 that was coimmunoprecipitated with the anti-Herc4 antibody in Müller cells, whereas inhibition of EGFR reversed this effect (Fig. 3E and F). In addition, to determine whether the effect of EGFR was dependent on Herc4, we knocked down Herc4 and overexpressed EGFR in Müller cells. Western blot analysis revealed that EGFR overexpression in cells transfected with the Flag-EGFR plasmid increased the level of YAP and reduced the level of SAV1, whereas EGFR overexpression and simultaneous inhibition of Herc4 partially restored the levels of YAP and SAV1 (Fig. 3G). Our findings suggest that EGFR mediates the interaction between Herc4 and SAV1 and promotes the degradation of SAV1.

EGFR inhibition suppresses YAP activation and alleviates diabetic retinal fibrosis

Because EGFR strongly influences the Hippo pathway in Müller cells, we further investigated whether this inhibitory effect of EGFR occurred in diabetic mice. Erlotinib, an inhibitor of EGFR kinase activity, was administered to mice with STZ-induced diabetes (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis revealed significantly greater expression levels of EGFR and YAP in the retinal tissues of mice with STZ-induced diabetes than in those of nondiabetic controls, and these changes were accompanied by the downregulation of SAV1 expression (Fig. 4B). However, EGFR inhibition decreased the levels of YAP and increased the levels of SAV1 (Fig. 4B). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of retinal tissue sections from diabetic mice or nondiabetic controls indicated that inhibition of EGFR expression reversed the DM-induced upregulation of YAP expression and the downregulation of SAV1 expression (Fig. 4C). In addition, diabetic mice treated with an EGFR inhibitor presented increased levels of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and decreased levels of the mesenchymal marker vimentin and the retinal fibrosis markers fibronectin and α-SMA (Fig. 4D). Masson staining showed epiretinal fibrous membranes (black arrow) in diabetic mice. Nevertheless, the administration of an EGFR inhibitor to diabetic mice resulted in diminished development of epiretinal fibrous membranes, suggesting a potential therapeutic effect against fibrosis (Fig. 4E).

To determine whether EGFR regulates retinal fibrosis via Hippo signalling, we assessed the levels of retinal fibrosis markers in MIO-M1 Müller cells. Our findings indicated that EGFR overexpression led to increased levels of α-SMA, fibronectin, and collagen I and reduced levels of E-cadherin (Fig. 4F). Conversely, EGFR inhibition restored the expression of these retinal fibrosis markers (Fig. 4F). We transfected EGFR-siRNA into Müller cells and then overexpressed YAP. Our findings indicated that the overexpression of YAP rescued the reduction in α-SMA and collagen I induced by inhibiting EGFR (Fig. 4G), indicating that EGFR modulates retinal fibrosis via the Hippo signalling pathway.

EGFR blockade inhibits diabetic retinal fibrosis

To elucidate the antifibrotic potential of EGFR inhibition, two therapeutic approaches using an anti-EGFR antibody in a diabetic mouse model were designed to assess both preventive and interventional treatment strategies. First, we initiated anti-EGFR antibody therapy shortly after diabetes induction to evaluate its ability to prevent the onset of retinal fibrosis. The intravitreal injections were administered at the specific time points indicated in Fig. 5A. Our results revealed that the anti-EGFR antibody strongly reduced the expression of EGFR and that low levels of EGFR decreased the levels of α-SMA, CTGF and collagen I (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, Masson staining revealed increased epiretinal fibrous membranes in diabetic retinas (Ctrl group), whereas the administration of an anti-EGFR antibody reduced retinal fibrosis, and earlier treatment resulted in better results (Fig. 5C).

Subsequently, we investigated whether delayed initiation of treatment—starting 6 weeks after diabetes induction—could mitigate the progression of established fibrotic changes, thereby modelling a more clinically relevant therapeutic scenario. As shown in Fig. 5D, we administered an anti-EGFR antibody to diabetic mice 6 weeks after DM induction. Our results indicated that EGFR expression was effectively suppressed and that EGFR inhibition led to reduced levels of α-SMA, collagen I, and CTGF (Fig. 5E). Masson staining revealed that the administration of an anti-EGFR antibody alleviated retinal fibrosis and that multiple treatments led to better results than two treatments did (Fig. 5F). This dual approach allows for the comparison of early versus late intervention, providing comprehensive insight into the timing-dependent efficacy of EGFR blockade in diabetic retinal fibrosis. Taken together, our findings indicate that EGFR blockade can reduce diabetic retinal fibrosis through the Hippo signalling pathway.

Discussion

Our findings reveal a novel molecular mechanism through which EGFR signalling directly antagonizes the Hippo tumour suppressor pathway in diabetic retinal fibrosis, specifically through targeted degradation of the core scaffold protein SAV1 (Fig. 6). We demonstrated that EGFR acts as a critical molecular bridge, enhancing the interaction between SAV1 and its E3 ubiquitin ligase, Herc4, thereby promoting the proteasomal degradation of SAV1 in Müller cells. This degradation effectively dismantles the Hippo kinase cascade, leading to YAP dephosphorylation, nuclear translocation, and the transcriptional upregulation of potent profibrotic mediators such as CTGF and CYR61. This EGFR-Herc4-SAV1-YAP axis represents a significant point of crosstalk between growth factor receptor signalling and the Hippo pathway, which is distinct from regulation by mechanical cues or G-protein coupled receptors [43, 44] Our work positions EGFR not only as a parallel pathway but also as a direct upstream inhibitor of Hippo signalling in this context. This mechanism aligns with and extends observations of EGFR–Hippo interactions in other fibrotic conditions [45, 46], highlighting a potentially conserved pathogenic pathway. The therapeutic potential is underscored by our in vivo results, where both systemic (erlotinib) and localized (intravitreal anti-EGFR antibody) inhibition effectively attenuated retinal fibrosis, with enhanced efficacy observed upon early and repeated treatment. While the crosstalk between EGFR and YAP signalling has been observed in other contexts, such as cancer and renal fibrosis [47, 48], our study revealed a distinct mechanism in diabetic retinal fibrosis. Rather than acting through parallel pathways, we demonstrate that EGFR directly orchestrates the degradation of the core Hippo pathway component SAV1. Crucially, we identified EGFR as a critical molecular scaffold that physically facilitates the interaction between SAV1 and its E3 ubiquitin ligase Herc4 (Fig. 3D–F), thereby promoting the proteasomal degradation of SAV1. This scaffolding function of EGFR represents a novel regulatory layer within the Hippo pathway.

Schematic representation of the EGFR-mediated regulation of YAP signaling in diabetic retinal fibrosis

Aberrant activation of EGFR may contribute to pathological tissue fibrosis during diabetes [49]. Kim et al. demonstrated that EGFR signalling is associated with the Hippo pathway during carcinogenesis [50]. However, the interaction between EGFR and Hippo signalling and its impact on potential treatments for DR are still unknown. Our previous study revealed that EGFR-dependent activation of YAP was closely associated with PVR formation, indicating that targeting YAP and the EGFR pathway could be a promising therapeutic strategy for PVR [35]. Although EGFR signalling has a distinct mitogenic effect on retinal pigment epithelial cells, the results of the present study suggest another important role in gliotic Müller cell EMT and diabetic retinal fibrosis. In DR, Müller cells may be exposed to several growth factors that originate from damaged retinal vascular endothelial cells and promote ECM deposition [51, 52]. EGFR inhibition significantly reduced the expression of the mesenchymal marker vimentin and the retinal fibrosis markers α-SMA and fibronectin but increased the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin. Importantly, the overexpression of YAP abrogated the decrease in α-SMA and collagen I induced by EGFR inhibition (Fig. 4G), indicating that EGFR promoted retinal fibrosis via Hippo/YAP signalling. Zhang et al. also reported that activated gliotic Müller cells express several markers of myofibroblasts, including α-SMA and collagen I [53], which is consistent with our results. In summary, our results suggest that targeting EGFR effectively mitigates retinal fibrosis in diabetic mice, but the molecular mechanism needs further research.

The effect of EGFR on the regulation of YAP levels was recently described [54]. Deletion of EGFR in renal proximal tubule epithelial cells significantly reduced the protein and mRNA levels of YAP. Similarly, in our study, we demonstrated that EGFR changed the protein level of YAP in MIO-M1 Müller cells and in diabetic mice with retinal fibrosis. Our results indicate that the role of EGFR in the regulation of YAP varies among tissues and cells. In the Hippo signalling pathway, SAV1 can interact with and activate MST1 kinase, which is crucial for the complete activation of LATS1 [37]. In the present study, we found that EGFR bound to SAV1 and Herc4 and enhanced their interaction. SAV1, which is a highly conserved protein, was initially identified as a regulator of cytokinesis and mitotic exit in yeast and was subsequently shown to be a potential suppressor gene of carcinoma [55, 56]. SAV1 is specifically degraded by the E3 ligase Herc4, which regulates YAP activation [42]. SAV1/MST kinase phosphorylates and inactivates YAP, and phosphorylated YAP remains in the cytoplasm or is degraded [57]. YAP is a transcription factor whose target genes include CYR61 and CTGF [58]. Our data indicate that EGFR directly interacts with SAV1 in vitro and in vivo and ubiquitously degrades SAV1 in MIO-M1 Müller cells and in diabetic mice with retinal fibrosis. In addition, this study revealed that EGFR promotes SAV1 degradation in a proteasomal protein-dependent manner.

Because Herc4 is the E3 ligase of SAV1 [59], our results showed that EGFR bound to Herc4 and enhanced the interaction between SAV1 and Herc4. The knockdown of EGFR strongly inhibited the interaction between SAV1 and Herc4, indicating that EGFR acts as a bridge for SAV1 to bind with Herc4. Therefore, the degradation of SAV1 induced by EGFR blocked the Hippo cascade and promoted the development of retinal fibrosis and activation in MIO-M1 Müller cells and in diabetic mice with retinal fibrosis. EGFR can increase the YAP-dependent transcription of target genes [60], including CYR61 and CTGF, and promote the progression of retinal fibrosis by reducing the inhibitory component of the Hippo cascade. CTGF is an independent factor that promotes retinal fibrogenesis [61]. In addition to revealing the mechanism by which EGFR inhibits Hippo signalling, we also revealed that pharmacological inhibition of EGFR activates the Hippo cascade and reduces downstream transcription in diabetic mice. In addition, we found that in retinal fibrosis, inhibition of EGFR was related to activation of the Hippo pathway and that the expression of EGFR was negatively correlated with that of SAV1, which confirmed the interaction of the Hippo pathway, EGFR, and retinal fibrosis in vivo. In conclusion, this study provides new insight into the role of EGFR in YAP signalling modulation, indicating that EGFR regulates Hippo signalling via the degradation of SAV1. Moreover, our data indicate that nuclear YAP levels accurately reflected the stimulation of the Hippo pathway (Fig. 1F). Thus, EGFR-regulated YAP nuclear translocation is important in MIO-M1 Müller cells. Our identification of the role of the EGFR-SAV1-YAP axis in diabetic retinal fibrosis may extend to other diseases involving fibrosis and matrix remodelling. Dysregulated EGFR and YAP signalling contributes to cancer stroma and neurodegeneration, as seen in SSH1-driven cholangiocarcinoma [62] and angiogenin-related Parkinson’s disease models [63]. Given the role of EGFR and tissue stiffness in tumour and neural environments, our mechanism—EGFR-mediated SAV1 degradation leading to YAP activation—may represent a common pathway in pathological tissue remodelling. These findings highlight the potential broader therapeutic relevance of targeting EGFR/Hippo signalling across fibroproliferative diseases.

The mechanism of diabetic retinal fibrosis is very complex and involves many factors and signalling pathways. The results of the present study provide new insights into the potential mechanism through which EGFR inhibits Hippo signalling through SAV1 degradation in Müller cells. The newly discovered pathogenic mechanism through which EGFR regulates the Hippo pathway emphasizes the key role of EGFR in the progression of retinal fibrogenesis and can provide a novel strategy for the treatment of diabetic retinal fibrogenesis. We acknowledge that our findings, while mechanistically robust in experimental models, lack direct validation in humans with diabetic retinopathy. Correlating EGFR, SAV1, Herc4, and YAP levels with clinical disease severity would strengthen translational relevance. Future studies are warranted to confirm these targets in patient cohorts. Our study focused on the short-term efficacy and mechanism of EGFR inhibition in diabetic retinal fibrosis. Our findings are supported by a recent study demonstrating that trans-gnetin H induces ROS-dependent Rictor degradation, thereby suppressing mTORC2 signalling and cancer cell viability [64]. This result highlights a potential intersection between metabolic stress, mTOR complexes, and YAP regulation. In our system, EGFR-mediated SAV1 degradation and subsequent YAP activation may be influenced by similar metabolic cues. Future studies should explore whether the mTOR/AMPK pathway, a key sensor of cellular energy status, intersects with the identified PI3K-Akt-YAP axis. AMPK activation or mTOR inhibition under metabolic stress may modulate YAP activity, either via LATS-dependent mechanisms or through direct cross-talk, providing deeper mechanistic insight into the metabolic control of YAP-driven fibrotic responses in the diabetic retina.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, the findings, while robust in in vitro (MIO-M1 cells) and in vivo (diabetic mouse) models, lack validation in human diabetic retinopathy patients; correlations between EGFR, SAV1, Herc4, or YAP levels and clinical disease severity remain unestablished. Second, although EGFR inhibition effectively reduced fibrosis markers during treatment, the long-term durability of these effects and their translation into functional visual recovery (e.g., via ERG) were not evaluated. Third, a comprehensive assessment of potential off-target effects or adverse consequences of targeting the EGFR/YAP pathway—critical for normal tissue homeostasis—was not conducted, despite the use of localized (intravitreal) delivery to minimize systemic exposure. Future studies will require detailed toxicological profiling. Fourth, while the mechanism has been demonstrated in Müller cells and RPE cells [61], its specificity and role in other retinal cell types need further exploration. Finally, we acknowledge that EGFR overexpression via transfection may not fully reflect its physiological upregulation under conditions such as high glucose or hypoxia, which involve complex regulatory mechanisms. This limitation may affect downstream signalling and the broader applicability of our findings. Future studies in more physiologically relevant models are needed to validate these results. These limitations highlight the need for future research to confirm clinical relevance, long-term safety, functional benefits, and pathway dynamics.
