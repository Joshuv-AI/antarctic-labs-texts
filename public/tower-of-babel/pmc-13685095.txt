Formate-driven in situ CO2 recycling for efficient succinic acid bioproduction in engineered Actinobacillus succinogenes
Authors: Mingyang Zhao, Li Wang, Yuanming Ye, Yaqin Sun, Zhilong Xiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuccinic acid (SA) biosynthesis presents a promising sustainable alternative to petrochemical routes, yet industrial potential is often hampered by inefficient CO2 fixation and redox imbalances.ResultsTo systematically address these bottlenecks, a synergistic metabolic engineering strategy was developed in Actinobacillus succinogenes by co-expressing the endogenous gene of formate dehydrogenase (FdoG) and the heterologous gene of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PPC). This design established a self-reinforcing metabolic loop where FdoG-mediated formate oxidation generated in situ CO2 and NADH, which were immediately channeled into the PPC-driven carboxylation pathway. Physiological characterizations, utilizing real-time off-gas analysis, demonstrated that this pathway effectively functions as an endogenous CO2 generator, decoupling production from external mass transfer limitations. Furthermore, carbon flux distribution analysis confirmed the synergistic efficacy, revealing a 12.11% increase in succinate-directed flux and a significant reduction in byproduct accumulation compared to the wild type. To fully exploit this formate-utilization potential, an optimized fed-batch fermentation with pulsed formate feeding was implemented. Consequently, the engineered strain AS-PF achieved a remarkable succinate titer of 96.64 g/L, with a productivity of 1.38 g/ (L·h), corresponding to an overall product yield of 0.78 g/g based on the total consumed mixed carbon source (glucose and formate).ConclusionThis study establishes a sustainable and efficient platform for SA bioproduction by synergistically integrating formate metabolism-driven CO2 recycling with enhanced biosynthetic pathways, thereby advancing the valorization of C1 compounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13068-026-02799-7.

Introduction

Climate instability, evidenced by global warming and erratic weather patterns, is directly linked to anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions [1,2]. With CO2 from fossil fuel combustion accounting for over 70% of these emissions, developing carbon capture and utilization technologies has become a global imperative [3,4]. Microbial biosynthesis has emerged as a sustainable and promising platform for converting renewable carbon resources into value-added chemicals, aligning with goals of carbon neutrality. Among biochemicals, succinic acid (SA) stands out as a pivotal platform compound. Recognized by the U.S. DOE as one of the top 12 value-added platform chemicals, SA is indispensable in the production of biodegradable plastics (e.g., polybutylene succinate, PBS), food additives, and pharmaceuticals [5–7]. While the traditional petrochemical route remains dominant, it is constrained by reliance on non-renewable fossil feedstocks and a significant environmental footprint [8,9]. Consequently, fermentative production of SA using renewable biomass and CO₂ fixation has gained prominence as a green alternative [10]. This biological route is particularly attractive because the reductive TCA (rTCA) pathway inherently fixes CO₂, creating a potentially carbon-negative process [6, 10].

To date, SA biosynthesis has been established in a variety of microbial platforms, encompassing both natural producers (e.g., Actinobacillus succinogenes and Mannheimia succiniciproducens) and engineered non-native hosts, notably Corynebacterium glutamicum and Escherichia coli. For E. coli, tolerance engineering was achieved by introducing a Pseudomonas aeruginosa gene cluster for extracellular polysaccharide synthesis, which bolstered osmotolerance and acid resistance to yield a final SA titer of 70.54 g/L [11]. Conversely, engineering efforts in C. glutamicum prioritized metabolic flux redistribution; a representative study combined the deletion of byproduct pathways (ldh and ackA) with the overexpression of pyc and ppc, alongside the transporter Ncgl0275 to alleviate feedback inhibition, resulting in 64.16 g/L of succinate from corn stover hydrolysate [12]. After replacing native MsMDH with CgMDH from C. glutamicum, succinate titer reached 134.25 g/L, achieving a yield of 1.25 mol/mol glucose and a productivity of 10.33 g/(L·h) [13]. In current strategies, byproduct elimination and flux redirection are combined with ATP-generating pathways, redox-balancing modules, and enhanced transport systems, resulting in SA titers that have surpassed 100 g/L.

A. succinogenes is a natural succinate overproducer characterized by a robust anaerobic central carbon metabolism and remarkable tolerance toward high concentrations of the end product. This intrinsic capability matches or even surpasses the phenotypes achieved by model hosts only after intensive metabolic engineering and adaptive evolution [14,15]. Its ability to efficiently utilize diverse carbon sources, while fixing CO₂ to synthesize succinate at high yields, positions it as a premier candidate for industrial applications [16,17]. Furthermore, A. succinogenes exhibits a multi-faceted tolerance to lignocellulose-derived inhibitors and high osmotic pressure. It robustly withstands furfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural at concentrations up to 10 g/L through active in vivo detoxification [18], tolerates acetate levels up to 38 g/L [19], and maintains high metabolic activity under elevated glucose (> 130 g/L) and succinate (> 55 g/L) [20]. This innate physiological robustness eliminates the need for costly, energy-intensive detoxification steps, establishing this specialist as a uniquely superior chassis for consolidated bioprocessing. Generally, genetic engineering strategies for A. succinogenes are typically divided into two approaches: reinforcing carbon flux toward succinate and eliminating competing byproduct pathways. For instance, overexpressing the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase–malate dehydrogenase–fumarate reductase (PCK–MDH–FUM) pathway yielded strain AS-PMF, which achieved a succinate titer of 50 g/L and a yield of 0.79 g/g glucose [21]. Conversely, deleting the pyruvate formate-lyase gene (pflB) via homologous recombination aimed to block formate formation and redirect carbon flux [22]. While completely eliminating byproducts seems advantageous, it tends to trigger metabolic imbalance by disrupting native carbon and redox equilibrium. Consequently, the strategic utilization of formate, a major fermentation byproduct and an abundant C1 feedstock, emerges as a key solution to this bottleneck. For instance, in Mannheimia succiniciproducens, the heterologous expression of Methylobacterium extorquens FDH2 enabled the strain to productively channel formate into the succinate pathway. This strategy not only supplied additional carbon but also provided essential reducing power, resulting in a succinate titer of 69.83 g/L [23,24]. A heterologous formate dehydrogenase complex coupled the reductive glycine pathway in E. coli facilitated efficient bioconversion of lignin-derived formate [25]. A. succinogenes harbors a native, highly active formate oxidation machinery. Unlike E. coli, which strictly requires the introduction of heterologous formate dehydrogenases, this organism naturally channels formate into reducing power and in situ CO2 [26].

In this study, by elucidating the genetic background of A. succinogenes, we constructed the engineered strain AS-PF, which synergistically couples the overexpression of the endogenous formate dehydrogenase gene (fdoG) with the introduction of the heterologous PEP carboxylase gene (ppc) from E. coli. Crucially, the efficacy of this in situ CO2 recycling strategy was rigorously validated through real-time off-gas monitoring and carbon flux analysis, which confirmed the redirection of metabolic flux from formate toward succinate synthesis. Building on this mechanistic understanding, we implemented a process intensification strategy via pulsed formate feeding. This optimized process achieved a final succinate titer of 96.64 g/L and a productivity of 1.38 g/(L·h), exhibiting an overall product yield of 0.78 g/g based on the total consumed substrates (glucose and formate). An integrated platform for sustainable SA production was established through the synergistic coupling of formate metabolism-driven CO₂ recycling with enhanced biosynthetic pathways, offering a promising route for advancing C1 compound valorization.

Materials and methods

Strain, medium, and cultivation

The bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Table S1. A. succinogenes CICC11014 (wild-type) and its engineered derivatives were used for succinate production, while E. coli DH5α served as the host for plasmid construction. E. coli was cultured in LB medium at 37 °C and 200 rpm.

For A. succinogenes cultivation, both seed and fermentation processes were conducted at 37 °C with agitation at 200 rpm. The seed medium contained (g/L): glucose, 20.0; yeast extract, 6.0; peptone, 2.5; NaH2PO4·H₂O, 9.6; K2HPO4·3H2O, 15.5; NaHCO₃, 10.0; NaCl, 1.0. The fermentation medium consisted of (g/L): glucose, 60.0; yeast extract, 6.0; peptone, 10.0; Na2HPO4, 0.3; NaH2PO4, 1.4; K2HPO4, 1.5; CH₃COONa, 1.4; NaCl, 1.0; MgCl2·6H2O, 0.2; CaCl2·H2O, 0.2; basic magnesium carbonate, 20.0. Chloramphenicol (25 μg/mL) was added to the media for selecting transformants of both A. succinogenes and E. coli when required.

Genome sequencing and annotation

Genomic DNA was extracted from A. succinogenes cells harvested at the mid-logarithmic growth phase using a commercial Bacterial Genome Extraction Kit. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a DS-500 microspectrophotometer. High-quality DNA samples with confirmed integrity (band size > 20 kb on agarose gel) were utilized for library construction and sequencing, which were performed by Shanghai Majorbio Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.

Construction of gene overexpression plasmids

To enhance succinate biosynthesis in the engineered strain, plasmids overexpressing the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase gene (ppc), the formate dehydrogenase gene (fdoG), or both were constructed. The mdh-containing plasmid pMDH was acquired from Addgene (#98503, USA). For the construction of overexpression plasmids, target genes were amplified by PCR with specifically designed primers using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase; the amplified products were then purified and assembled by seamless cloning. The pPPC expression plasmid was constructed as follows. First, the plasmid backbone containing the chloramphenicol resistance gene (which was modified from pMDH to improve screening efficiency) and the mdh promoter was amplified from pMDHCm. Next, the strong PCKA promoter [27] and ppc gene along with a 20-bp homology arm was amplified from A. succinogenes and E. coli genomic DNA using gene-specific primers, respectively. Finally, these fragments were assembled with a Seamless Cloning Kit to generate the final pPPC plasmid.

Electrotransformation of A. succinogenes

Electrocompetent cells of A. succinogenes were prepared for plasmid transformation by electroporation (BTX, USA). First, a frozen stock culture was inoculated (4%, v/v) into seed medium and grown overnight. This culture was then diluted (4%, v/v) into fresh medium and incubated until the mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.4–0.5). The culture was chilled on ice for 10 min, and the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was washed twice with pre-chilled 15% glycerol, with centrifugation performed under the same conditions. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of pre-chilled 15% glycerol, and competent cells were aliquoted (100 μL per aliquot) for storage at −80 °C. For each transformation, 100 μL of competent cells were mixed with 300 ng of plasmid DNA, transferred to a 2 mm electroporation cuvette, and kept on ice for 10 min before pulsing at 2.5 kV. Immediately afterward, 1 mL of seed medium was added to the cuvette, and the cells were allowed to recover for 2–3 h at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. The recovered cells were subsequently plated on seed medium supplemented with chloramphenicol (25 μg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 2–3 days to select for transformants [28].

Fermentation

For serum bottle fermentations, frozen stocks of A. succinogenes were thawed and cultivated in 100 mL of seed medium (in 250-mL flasks) at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 12 h. The seed culture was inoculated at 4% (v/v) inoculum into 100 mL of fermentation medium supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics. Prior to sterilization, all serum bottles were sparged with CO2 for 3 min to establish anaerobic conditions. The fermentations were conducted at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 48 h, with samples collected at 12-h intervals for analysis of biomass, glucose, and organic acid concentrations.

For batch fermentation in a bioreactor, A. succinogenes was initially activated from −80 °C stocks by streaking onto solid seed medium. Single colonies were used to inoculate 100 mL of seed medium, which was incubated anaerobically at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 10 h (primary seed culture). This culture was subsequently transferred at a 5% (v/v) inoculum into fresh seed medium and grown under identical conditions for 12 h (secondary seed culture). The secondary seed culture was then inoculated at a 10% (v/v) inoculum into a 5-L bioreactor containing 2 L of fermentation medium. Prior to inoculation, the bioreactor was sparged with CO₂ (0.1 vvm) for 1 h to establish anaerobic conditions. Fermentation was performed at 37 °C, 200 rpm, and pH 6.5 (maintained by automatic addition of 5 M NaOH) for 48 h under unvented conditions. Samples were taken every 6 h to determine biomass, glucose, and organic acid concentrations.

Fed-batch fermentation employed the same pre-culture procedure as the batch fermentation. During fermentation, pH was maintained at 6.5–6.8 through the addition of NaOH and basic magnesium carbonate. The amount of basic magnesium carbonate added was calculated based on the glucose consumption rate. Glucose concentration was monitored every 2–4 h; when it fell down below 20 g/L, a concentrated glucose solution (800 g/L) was fed via a peristaltic pump to maintain the concentration between 10 and 20 g/L. Additionally, formic acid was supplemented periodically in a batch-wise manner as an auxiliary carbon source.

Analytical methods

Biomass was determined by spectrophotometric measurement of OD600. For metabolite analysis, fermentation broth samples were first centrifuged (12,000 × g, 10 min) to remove cells. The supernatant was diluted appropriately, mixed with an equal volume of trichloromethane, vortexed, and centrifuged again. The resulting aqueous phase was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Metabolite concentrations (glucose, succinate, acetate, and formate) were analyzed by HPLC (Aminex HPX-87H column; 5 mM H2SO4 mobile phase at 0.6 mL/min; column temperature 65 °C; injection volume 20 μL) using external standard methods (R2 > 0.999).

Online monitoring of effluent CO2 was performed using a gas chromatograph (GC7900; Techcomp Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). High-purity nitrogen was employed as the carrier gas with an injection volume of 10 µL. The analysis was conducted at 30 min intervals (2 min analysis time followed by equilibration). Quantification was achieved by comparing sample peak areas with a pure CO₂ standard, and molar concentrations were calculated according to the ideal gas law.

Statistical analysis

All fermentation experiments were conducted in at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Succinate titers, yields, and productivities were compared among different strains using appropriate statistical tests. Two-group comparisons were performed using a two-tailed independent-samples Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons involving three or more groups were conducted via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range post hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. In the figures, error bars represent SD, and asterisks indicate statistically significant differences between specific groups (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results and discussion

Genomic analysis and rational metabolic design

To elucidate the genetic background and identify metabolic engineering targets, the complete genome of A. succinogenes CICC11014 was sequenced and assembled using a hybrid Illumina–PacBio approach. Rigorous quality control, including k-mer analysis and GC-depth distribution assessment, confirmed a high-fidelity assembly with low heterozygosity and no contamination. The final genome consists of a single circular chromosome of 2,319,653 bp with a GC content of 44.92%. Functional annotation against six major databases (NR, Swiss-Prot, Pfam, EggNOG, GO, KEGG) identified 2317 protein-coding genes. Guided by these genomic annotations, a comprehensive metabolic pathway map for succinate synthesis was constructed (Fig. 1B). This reconstruction revealed that A. succinogenes possesses an incomplete TCA cycle and relies exclusively on the reductive branch for succinate production, utilizing phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PCK) as the primary CO2-fixing enzyme. This specialized pathway contrasts with the diversity of other known natural succinate biosynthesis routes [30]. While effective under natural conditions, this pathway presents a kinetic limitation for industrial overproduction: the Km of PPC for HCO3– has been reported to be substantially lower (~ 0.1 μM) than that of PCK (~ 13 μM), and the specific activity of PPC for HCO3– of 250 μmol/(min·g) higher than PCK of 28 μmol/(min·g) [31–34]. Given the high dissolved CO2 bicarbonate concentrations maintained during our high-performance anaerobic fermentations, PPC operates much closer to substrate saturation, providing a significant kinetic advantage for driving the carboxylation flux [35]. Consequently, we reasoned that introducing a high-affinity PPC would more effectively channel carbon flux from the central node toward oxaloacetate, the critical precursor for succinate.

Crucially, genome mining uncovered a native formate dehydrogenase gene (fdoG), which catalyzes the oxidation of formate to CO2 coupled with NADH generation. This discovery highlighted an intrinsic but underutilized capacity for C1 utilization. We hypothesized that fdoG could function as an in situ generator of both reducing power and CO2, thereby addressing the NADH deficit inherent in the reductive TCA cycle. Based on this rationale, a synergistic engineering strategy was designed: co-expressing the heterologous ppc to enhance carboxylation efficiency while overexpressing the endogenous fdoG to drive this pathway with formate-derived NADH and CO2. This design aims to construct a robust cell factory capable of efficient succinate biosynthesis driven by C1 co-metabolism.

WT denotes the wild-type strain expressing pck from a single native copy; therefore, any evaluation of pck in this study is strictly limited to its endogenous chromosomal level. AS-PF and AS-FdoG reached their peak biomass (OD600 of 5.61 and 6.04, respectively) at 12 h and 24 h, whereas AS-PPC and the wild type did not achieve their maximum biomass until 36 h (OD600 of 6.21 and 5.81, respectively). This accelerated growth is hypothesized to be associated with the NADH generated via formate oxidation, which potentially modulates cellular redox balance and energy metabolism [36]. Critically, the fdoG-expressing strains (AS-FdoG and AS-PF) exhibited active formate consumption during the stationary phase, reducing final formate concentrations by 0.87 g/L and 1.64 g/L, respectively, confirming the functional expression of the formate dehydrogenase (Fig. 2B). In terms of product synthesis, the engineered strains demonstrated superior performance despite the lack of pH control. AS-PPC exhibited the highest succinate yield (0.70 g/g) (Fig. 2C). In addition, a significant increase in SA yield was observed in the ppc-expressing strains AS-PF and AS-PPC (P < 0.001 and P = 0.006, respectively), confirming the efficiency of the heterologous carboxylase in carbon fixation. Furthermore, the dual-expression strain AS-PF achieved a 14% increase in succinate titer compared to the control, indicating that the synergistic operation of ppc and fdoG effectively redirects carbon flux toward the target product even under serum bottle conditions.

Physiological analysis of in situ CO2 recycling and metabolic flux redistribution

The reductive TCA (rTCA) pathway is the primary route for succinate synthesis in A. succinogenes, characterized by its high carbon conversion efficiency [37]. In this pathway, the key precursor oxaloacetate (OAA) is synthesized through the carboxylation of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) or pyruvate (PYR) and is subsequently converted to succinic acid via sequential reactions catalyzed by malate dehydrogenase (MDH), fumarase (FUM), and fumarate reductase (FRD). Given this framework, the carboxylation of PEP or PYR serves as the critical node controlling carbon entry. However, a major stoichiometric limitation is the inherent redox imbalance: the synthesis of one succinate molecule consumes two NADH, while glycolysis generates only one [38]. This bottleneck underscores the necessity of supplementing intracellular NADH. Previous studies have validated this metabolic engineering strategy. For instance, redirecting pyruvate flux in S. cerevisiae by knocking out the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier enhanced rTCA flux and succinate production [39]. Among various approaches, a widely applicable strategy is the introduction of a formate dehydrogenase (FDH). FDH oxidizes formate—an industrial byproduct with high reducing power—to CO₂ and NADH, simultaneously providing reducing equivalents and an additional carbon source [40]. The efficacy of this approach is demonstrated by its successful implementation in diverse hosts: expression of FDH in E. coli increased succinate yield by 6% while reducing formate accumulation [41], and heterologous expression in C. glutamicum boosted the succinate yield by approximately 20% under anaerobic conditions [42].

To systematically overcome these stoichiometric limitations in A. succinogenes, we devised a novel strategy to synchronize carbon fixation with redox cofactor supply. Specifically, we constructed the engineered strain AS-PF by synergistically coupling the overexpression of the endogenous formate dehydrogenase gene (fdoG) with the introduction of the heterologous PEP carboxylase gene (ppc) from E. coli. Unlike single-gene modifications, this coupled design establishes a self-reinforcing metabolic loop: the formate dehydrogenase (FdoG) functions as the generator, converting formate into intracellular NADH and CO₂, which are immediately channeled into the PPC-mediated carboxylation pathway for succinate biosynthesis. This mechanism effectively repurposes formate from a waste product into an essential driving force, unlocking the metabolic potential for high-yield succinate production.

The efficacy of this in situ CO2 recycling strategy was validated by combining real-time off-gas analysis with carbon flux distribution analysis. In this study, the off-gas composition was quantitatively determined via gas chromatography (GC). As shown in Fig. 4B, following the cessation of external CO₂ sparging at 1 h, the CO₂ levels in the fermentation of WT declined rapidly. Owing to the comparatively weak FdoG activity in the wild-type strain, formate-derived CO₂ generation is limited, and most of the incorporated CO₂ is therefore supplied exogenously. In contrast, the engineered strain AS-PF exhibited a distinctly slower rate of decline and a higher residual CO₂ level compared to the WT. By the end of the fermentation (48 h), the CO₂ concentration in the AS-PF and WT systems were maintained at approximately 220 mmol/L and 150 mmol/L, respectively. For quantitative comparison, the CO₂ concentration was averaged over the steady‑state phase (12–48 h), during which the profile remained stable, yielding a single representative value for each independent biological replicate (n = 3 per strain). In the engineered strain AS-PF, the off-gas CO₂ concentration was significantly higher than that in the wild-type strain (t4 = −10.846, P < 0.001). This persistent elevation of the AS-PF strain actively generates an endogenous CO₂ stream via formate oxidation. Unlike the WT, which relies solely on the external supply, the FdoG-catalyzed reaction in AS-PF serves as a continuous endogenous replenishment mechanism, elevating local substrate availability for the downstream carboxylation enzymes and driving the thermodynamic equilibrium toward succinate synthesis. In the absence of direct dissolved CO₂ measurements, the extent of this endogenous CO₂ supply advantage remains qualitative. This physiological evidence is strongly corroborated by the carbon flux distribution analysis (Fig. 4A). At the terminal phase (48 h), the flux to formate in AS-PF dropped to a negligible 0.32%, making it the lowest among all investigated strains compared to 1.06% in the WT, while the flux to succinate peaked at 72.63% (P < 0.001). The high sustained off-gas CO₂ levels coupled with minimal formate accumulation provides direct evidence that the engineered AS-PF strain efficiently oxidize formate to CO2. Furthermore, the flux data elucidate why enhancing formate dissimilation alone is insufficient. AS-FdoG (overexpressing fdoG only) reduced formate accumulation but failed to improve succinate titer (55.72%). Instead, a substantial portion of carbon flux was diverted to unclassified byproducts (37.09%), significantly higher than in AS-PF (19.24%, P < 0.001). This indicates a redox-carbon imbalance without the corresponding carboxylation capacity provided by PEP carboxylase (PPC), the excess NADH generated from formate oxidation overflowed into competing pathways rather than the succinate route. This confirms that the synergistic co-expression of ppc is essential to effectively capture the reducing power and CO₂ generated by fdoG.

Finally, the carbon flux distribution dynamics revealed a critical energy trade-off mechanism. While the introduced PPC pathway enhances carbon fixation, it does not generate ATP, unlike the native PEP carboxykinase (PCK) pathway. Carbon flux distribution analysis at the early exponential phase (6 h) provided empirical evidence for this energy burden. AS-PF exhibited the highest carbon flux to acetate (24.15%), exceeding that of the WT (20.22%, P = 0.047). This transient upregulation suggests a metabolic compensation in which cells are forced to direct more carbon toward the acetate kinase pathway to generate essential ATP, thereby counterbalancing the ATP deficit caused by the flux shift from PCK to PPC during rapid growth. Thus, the high succinate titer achieved by AS-PF represents a successfully engineered balance in which the strain utilizes the formate-driven NADH/CO₂ loop to maximize yield (72.63%) while tolerating a necessary ATP-generating bypass via acetate accumulation. This energetic compromise, induced by the deployment of the heterologous PPC bypass, is fundamentally dictated by the unique metabolic architecture of A. succinogenes. Unlike E. coli, where PCK overexpression is frequently required to sustain flux and supplement ATP, A. succinogenes natively maintains a saturated PCK capacity. However, because this native enzyme operates near thermodynamic equilibrium in vivo, further increasing its copy number yields diminishing returns due to a reversibility bottleneck. Introducing a heterologous, irreversible PPC successfully circumvents this constraint. This dual-carboxylase configuration functions as a unidirectional carbon trap that breaks the native thermodynamic equilibrium, aggressively driving the central carbon flux toward succinate.

Process intensification for high-titer succinate production via pulse formate feeding

Although batch fermentation confirmed the physiological advantages of the in situ CO2 recycling strategy, the final succinate titer was constrained by substrate depletion and the limit of initial loading. To unlock the full production potential of AS-PF, an optimized fed-batch fermentation strategy based on mixed carbon sources (glucose and formate) was implemented. Crucially, simply maintaining glucose levels is insufficient to fully exploit the C1-utilization potential of this specific strain. From the perspective of carbon metabolism, the engineered network of AS-PF is uniquely designed for mixotrophy, necessitating a sustained supply of formate as an auxiliary C1 feedstock. This co-utilization strategy ensures that the FdoG-mediated generation of NADH and CO2 is continuously powered, effectively driving the integration of C1 units into the succinate formation. Therefore, we hypothesized that the co-feeding of formate with glucose would create a synergistic effect, redirecting carbon flux toward succinate synthesis and maximizing the efficiency of the coupled FdoG–PPC pathway.

However, a high initial concentration of formate is known to be toxic to cell growth. To mitigate this inhibitory effect while ensuring a sufficient supply for biosynthesis, a pulsed feeding strategy was designed: starting at 16 h (post-exponential phase to minimize growth inhibition), 3 g of formic acid was added to the fermenter at 12–16 h intervals over 70 h process (as indicated by the arrows in Fig. 5). Fermentation was initiated with 60 g/L glucose. To prevent substrate limitation, a concentrated glucose solution (800 g/L) was fed to maintain residual sugar levels between 10 and 20 g/L. Under this optimized fed-batch regime, AS-PF demonstrated robust performance. As shown in Fig. 5A, AS-PF maintained high metabolic activity, consuming 72% of the initial glucose within the first 18 h. The pulsed addition of formate did not significantly retard growth, indicating the success of the timing strategy. Consequently, the optimized fed-batch process by engineered AS-PF achieved a final succinate titer of 96.64 g/L, representing a 26.44% increase over the WT (76.43 g/L). Crucially, the analysis of formate consumption provides direct evidence of the engineered pathway’s efficiency. As shown in Fig. 5, despite a total addition of 12 g of formic acid throughout the fermentation, the residual formate concentration in the AS-PF broth was only 1.18 g/L. In contrast, the WT strain accumulated 3.65 g/L of residual formate despite only 6 g of supplementation. This implies that AS-PF consumed formate at a significantly higher rate. The addition and consumption of 9.64 g of formic acid during fed-batch fermentation increased the succinate titer of the AS-PF strain by 2.89 g/L, reaching 96.64 g/L. This process contributed 210 mmol of NADH to the intracellular pool for redox homeostasis and provided a stoichiometrically equivalent amount of CO₂, thereby facilitating the observed increase in succinate synthesis. This data strongly corroborates our metabolic hypothesis: the co-expressed PPC pathway rapidly consumes the CO₂ and NADH generated from formate oxidation, thereby creating a thermodynamic driving force that shifts the reaction equilibrium toward succinate synthesis. This efficient metabolic coupling prevents formate accumulation and effectively channels the carbon and reducing equivalents into the succinate biosynthetic pathway.

The performance of the AS-PF strain was evaluated in comparison with other representative succinate producers summarized in Table 1. Several engineered E. coli strains have demonstrated excellent yields. For instance, E. coli-W1485 and E. coli-Mgls7-32 achieved yields of 1.78 g/g and 0.87 g/g using a dual-stage process, respectively [43]. Additionally, the engineered yeast Y. lipolytica Hi-SA2 produced 76.62 g/L with a yield to glucose of 0.75 g/g [44]. It is noted that a remarkably high titer of 151.44 g/L with a yield of 1.51 g/g was achieved by A. succinogenes 130Z-ATCC 55618 using cassava root as the substrate [45]. The high production of SA can be attributed to the presence of specific vitamins and minerals in the cassava hydrolysate, which have been demonstrated to stimulate cell proliferation. For A. succinogenes130Z-pMDH and A. succinogenes-ackA-CRISPRi-1006, the enhancement of SA yield can be achieved through the augmentation of carbon flux in synthetic SA pathways (overexpression of malate dehydrogenase) and concomitantly reducing competition from byproduct pathways (the activity of acetate kinase is inhibited), respectively. In the present study, the engineered AS-PF strain, cultivated under anaerobic fed-batch conditions with pulsed formate feeding, achieved a final succinate titer of 96.64 g/L and a productivity of 1.38 g/(L·h), exhibiting an overall product yield of 0.78 g/g based on the total consumed substrates (glucose and formate). In summary, by successfully coupling genetic engineering (overexpressing fdoG and ppc genes) with process engineering (pulsed formate feeding), we have established a highly efficient platform for bio-succinate production that effectively overcomes the stoichiometric redox and carbon limitations of the native host. Conclusion

This study established a highly efficient succinate biomanufacturing platform in Actinobacillus succinogenes by synergistically coupling formate metabolism with the biosynthetic pathway. Specifically, the complete genome sequence of this high-succinate-producing isolate was fully determined, facilitating the reconstruction of a strain-specific genome-scale metabolic model. Through comparative analysis of its central metabolism, the pathways coupling formate oxidation with succinate biosynthesis were systematically elucidated, allowing this native module to be rationally reinforced to substantially enhance succinate production performance. The co-expression of fdoG and ppc effectively transformed formate from a waste byproduct into a strategic donor of NADH and CO₂. Physiological characterizations and carbon flux distribution analysis confirmed that this strategy established a robust in situ CO₂ recycling process. Furthermore, process intensification via pulsed formate feeding fully exploited the strain’s C1-utilization potential, achieving a remarkable succinate titer of 96.64 g/L. This work demonstrates the efficacy of coupling genetic engineering with process optimization, providing a sustainable paradigm for high-value chemical production via carbon-negative pathways.
