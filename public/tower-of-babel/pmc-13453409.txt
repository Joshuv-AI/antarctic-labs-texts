REM Sleep Behavior Disorder Following Focal Brainstem Ischemia: A Case Series with Polysomnographic Characterization
Authors: Jiri Nepozitek, Simona Dostalova, Evzen Ruzicka, Karel Sonka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is characterized by dream-enacting behaviors occurring in the presence of REM sleep without atonia (RWA) and is most commonly associated with synucleinopathies. Structural lesions of the brainstem have been reported as a potential substrate of secondary RBD, yet polysomnographically confirmed cases remain sparsely documented. We report two patients with focal ischemic brainstem lesions and clinically suspected RBD who underwent detailed neurological evaluation, neuroimaging, and video-polysomnography. In both patients, polysomnography demonstrated RWA consistent with RBD. The first patient, a 75-year-old woman, exhibited prominent vocalizations and complex motor behaviors during REM sleep with RWA affecting 91% of REM sleep duration. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed bilateral pontine gliotic changes and a punctate subacute ischemic lesion in the right pontine tegmentum. The second patient, a 41-year-old man with a prior ischemic stroke involving the right medulla oblongata at the ponto-bulbar junction, presented with dream-enacting behaviors and with RWA affecting 41% of REM sleep duration. In both cases, motor manifestations during REM sleep were not attributable to respiratory events. Clinical context and neuroimaging findings supported a structural ischemic etiology. These cases highlight focal brainstem ischemia as a plausible structural substrate of RBD and underscore the importance of detailed polysomnographic and neuroimaging assessment in patients with cerebrovascular disease presenting with dream-enacting behaviors. Focal ischemic brainstem lesions should be considered among the potential contributors to RBD, and their recognition may have important implications for diagnostic evaluation and clinical decision-making.Keywords: REM sleep behavior disorder, REM sleep without atonia, brainstem stroke, polysomnography, ischemic brainstem lesion, secondary RBD

Introduction

Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is defined by dream-enacting behaviors occurring during REM sleep as a result of REM sleep without atonia (RWA) on polysomnography.1 While idiopathic RBD is strongly linked to underlying synucleinopathies,2–4 secondary (symptomatic) RBD has been described in association with focal structural lesions affecting brainstem circuits that generate REM atonia. One of the earliest observations reported vascular-appearing lesions involving dorsal ponto-mesencephalic regions in patients with RBD, suggesting that strategic lesions in the brainstem may be sufficient to disrupt REM-related motor inhibition.5

Evidence suggesting a contributory role of focal ischemia has remained limited to single cases and small series, including patients with discrete pontine ischemic lesions and PSG-confirmed RWA responsive to clonazepam. In a prospective cohort of patients with acute ischemic stroke assessed three months after the event, probable RBD symptoms were more frequent in those with brainstem infarcts, supporting a contribution of brainstem injury to post-stroke RBD; however, polysomnographic confirmation was lacking.6 More recently, quantitative polysomnographic and lesion analyses in brainstem stroke have highlighted that brainstem lesions can modulate REM sleep and REM-related muscle activity in heterogeneous ways, underscoring the importance of detailed polysomnographic characterization and lesion documentation when attributing RBD to focal vascular injury.7

Here, we report two patients with PSG-confirmed RBD following focal ischemic lesions of the brainstem, providing complementary clinical phenotypes and polysomnographic characterization in the context of cerebrovascular brainstem pathology. In contrast to most previously published reports, both cases were characterized by detailed video-PSG and neuroimaging, allowing direct comparison of distinct clinical and polysomnographic phenotypes occurring in the context of strategically located ischemic brainstem lesions.

Case 1

A 75-year-old woman was referred to our sleep center for evaluation of abnormal vocalizations during sleep. Her medical history included essential tremor, with action tremor beginning at childhood age and the formal diagnosis established at 71 years, in addition to type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia. Two years prior to presentation, she underwent a prolonged cardiac surgery (aortic valve replacement and coronary artery bypass grafting) for severe aortic stenosis and coronary artery disease, after which her chronic action tremor worsened.

She was a widow, living alone; collateral history from family members indicated at least a five-year history of abnormal sleep-related vocalizations, including loud screaming and howling. The patient herself was unaware of these behaviors and would not have reported any sleep-related complaints. She denied awareness of abnormal movements during sleep, nocturnal injuries, or falling out of bed. She did not recall dreams associated with the nocturnal vocalizations.

Neurological examination revealed bilateral resting, postural, and kinetic tremor with left-sided predominance, as well as a mild, symmetric hypokinetic–rigid syndrome; these findings were confirmed by a movement disorders specialist. A subtle left-sided peripheral facial nerve paresis was also present.

Dopamine transporter single-photon emission computed tomography (DAT-SPECT) showed normal, symmetric striatal tracer uptake without evidence of reduced presynaptic dopaminergic activity.

Night video-polysomnography demonstrated prominent behavioral manifestations consistent with REM sleep behavior disorder. During REM sleep, the patient exhibited loud vocalizations resembling howling, with a pronounced negative emotional content suggestive of distress, as well as punching of the right upper limb and vigorous defensive and escape-like kicking movements of the lower limbs. Quantitative analysis revealed a severe loss of REM sleep atonia, with RWA present during 91% of REM sleep duration. Representative REM sleep epochs demonstrating RWA are shown in Figure 1. An additional clinically relevant finding was severe obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), with an apnea–hypopnea index of 50.5 events/hour, an oxygen desaturation index of 47.9 events/hour, and a total sleep time with oxygen saturation below 90% (T90) of 47.6%. Importantly, the motor behaviors observed during REM sleep were not temporally associated with respiratory events.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated multiple chronic vascular changes in the cerebral white matter. In the pons, bilateral confluent gliotic lesions were observed, including a small punctate lesion in the right pontine tegmentum with imaging features consistent with a more recent ischemic lesion (Figure 2). The strategically located pontine ischemic lesions were considered a plausible contributing factor to the development of RBD.

The patient was successfully titrated on continuous positive airway pressure therapy for obstructive sleep apnea. Subsequently, clonazepam was initiated at a dose of 0.25 mg at bedtime. The symptomatic effect of treatment on nocturnal vocalizations and motor manifestations could not be reliably assessed, as the patient herself had not perceived the original symptoms as distressing.

Case 2

A 41-year-old man presented to our sleep center for evaluation of abnormal movements during sleep. He had sustained an ischemic stroke six years earlier, involving the medulla oblongata and ponto-bulbar junction. At stroke onset, he presented with vertigo, oculomotor disturbances, mild bulbar symptoms, and a mild left-sided hemiparesis accompanied by hemisensory loss and hemiataxia. He was urgently treated with intravenous thrombolysis with a favorable clinical response. At the time of presentation, residual neurological deficits included an alternating sensory disturbance involving the right side of the face and the left side of the body, as well as mild clumsiness in fine motor movements of the left upper limb. The patient’s medical history was further notable for arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, and a combined thrombophilic condition, including heterozygous factor V Leiden mutation and heterozygous prothrombin (factor II) gene mutation. Follow-up brain MRI performed three years after the stroke demonstrated a chronic ischemic lesion in the right medulla oblongata (Figure 3).

Three years prior to the current evaluation, he had been diagnosed with severe sleep apnea and was treated with adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV), with excellent therapeutic effect. He sought evaluation at our sleep center because of newly developed abnormal nocturnal behaviors during sleep, as reported by his bed partner, who provided a reliable collateral history. They reported recurrent kicking movements of the lower limbs, associated with vivid dream content, such as playing football. Several episodes resulted in minor injuries, including bruises on the limbs. The symptoms had been present for approximately six months.

The patient had been treated with venlafaxine 225 mg/day for approximately two years. However, he did not report any temporal association between the initiation of antidepressant therapy and the onset or progression of his sleep-related symptoms.

Video-polysomnography demonstrated RWA (Figure 1), affecting 41% of REM sleep duration. No prominent complex motor behaviors were observed during the recording; video analysis revealed only subtle trunk and lower limb jerks during REM sleep. Adequate control of obstructive sleep apnea under ASV therapy was confirmed.

Based on the characteristic history of dream-enacting behaviors and the polysomnographic demonstration of REM sleep without atonia, the diagnostic criteria for REM sleep behavior disorder were fulfilled. Given the history of an ischemic lesion involving the brainstem, the condition was considered compatible with structurally mediated RBD in the context of a prior ischemic brainstem lesion.

The patient was treated with melatonin at a dose of 2 mg at bedtime. During follow-up, he reported a marked improvement in RBD symptoms, including a reduction in vivid dreams and an almost complete resolution of reported nocturnal movements.

Discussion

The present case series illustrates RBD emerging in the context of focal ischemic lesions of the brainstem, with polysomnographic confirmation of RWA. While idiopathic RBD is strongly associated with underlying synucleinopathies, these cases support the hypothesis that strategic structural lesions affecting brainstem circuits involved in REM atonia may contribute to the development of RBD. Experimental and clinicopathological studies suggest that REM atonia is generated by a distributed brainstem network involving the sublaterodorsal region of the pons and medullary inhibitory pathways projecting to spinal motoneurons. Disruption of these pathways may lead to loss of REM atonia and the emergence of dream-enacting behaviors. The localization of ischemic lesions in the pontine tegmentum and ponto-bulbar region in our patients is anatomically compatible with disturbance of these REM atonia-generating circuits.8

An important unresolved issue concerns the true prevalence of ischemic–structural RBD following brainstem stroke. Brainstem ischemic stroke is a relatively common clinical condition, yet the literature describing PSG-confirmed RBD in this context remains remarkably sparse.5–7,9,10 Several factors may contribute to this apparent discrepancy. First, patients with brainstem stroke often present with prominent neurological deficits that may overshadow sleep-related symptoms during both acute care and follow-up. Second, RBD symptoms may go unrecognized in patients living alone or in institutional settings, where nocturnal behaviors are either not observed or not systematically reported. Third, even when abnormal nocturnal behaviors are witnessed, limited awareness of RBD among patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers may lead to underreporting and a lack of referral for sleep evaluation. Together, these factors may result in substantial underdiagnosis of post-stroke RBD.

The two cases presented here further demonstrate the heterogeneity of clinical and polysomnographic phenotypes in structurally mediated RBD. One patient exhibited prominent dream-enacting behaviors with marked vocalizations and severe RWA, whereas the other showed a more subtle motor phenotype with less extensive RWA and limited behavioral manifestations on video-polysomnography. This variability highlights that RBD may remain clinically underrecognized in patients with less dramatic nocturnal manifestations.

Differential diagnostic considerations are particularly important in patients with cerebrovascular disease and multiple comorbidities. Both patients had severe sleep apnea, a condition known to produce motor phenomena during sleep that may mimic RBD.11 However, in the first case, REM sleep motor behaviors were not temporally associated with respiratory events, and in the second case, sleep apnea was well controlled under ASV at the time of polysomnography, arguing against respiratory-related pseudo-RBD.

Antidepressant treatment with venlafaxine, a serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor that has been associated with RWA and RBD-like manifestations,12 was present in Case 2. Although no clear temporal relationship was observed between the initiation of antidepressant therapy and the onset of dream-enacting behaviors, the absence of such temporal association does not entirely exclude a potential facilitating role. Nevertheless, the presence of a structurally relevant brainstem lesion provides a plausible alternative substrate for RBD in this case. The favorable clinical response to melatonin is noteworthy; however, given its established symptomatic efficacy in both idiopathic and secondary RBD, treatment response alone does not allow etiological differentiation.

In the first case, the presence of a mild, symmetric parkinsonian syndrome raises the possibility of an underlying neurodegenerative process. Although a negative DAT-SPECT argues against manifest Parkinson’s disease, dopaminergic imaging may remain normal in some patients during the earliest stages of neurodegeneration.13 Therefore, an evolving synucleinopathy cannot be fully excluded. Notably, the patient also demonstrated a more recent pontine ischemic lesion superimposed on chronic vascular changes. The temporal relationship between the progression of clinical symptoms and prior cardiac surgery raises the possibility that a periprocedural ischemic event may have contributed to the development or unmasking of RBD in a brain already affected by chronic vascular pathology.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this report includes only two cases and therefore does not allow conclusions regarding causality or prevalence. Second, underlying neurodegenerative processes cannot be fully excluded, particularly in Case 1. Third, longitudinal follow-up and repeat polysomnographic assessments were not available.

From a clinical perspective, recognizing structurally mediated RBD has important implications. When RBD occurs in the context of focal ischemic brainstem lesions, a structural non-progressive mechanism should be considered alongside the possibility of underlying neurodegenerative disease. This distinction is of practical importance for patient counseling and clinical decision-making. Nevertheless, therapeutic response to established symptomatic treatments such as clonazepam or melatonin appears preserved, as illustrated by both cases.

Conclusion

The present cases emphasize the importance of considering focal brainstem ischemia as a potential contributing factor and structural substrate of RBD and underscore the value of detailed polysomnographic and neuroimaging assessment when RBD emerges in patients with cerebrovascular disease. Clinicians should be aware that RBD may remain underrecognized after brainstem stroke and that structural lesions should be considered alongside neurodegenerative and pharmacological causes in the differential diagnosis of RBD.
