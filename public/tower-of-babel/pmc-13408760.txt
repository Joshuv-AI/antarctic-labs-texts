Ferroptosis-related proteins orchestrate aortic dissection: unveiling novel molecular signatures and therapeutic avenues
Authors: Guohua Cai, Xueying Wu, Rong Guan, Xuemei Qian, Kexin Ren, Xue Guan, Yi Feng, Junquan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAD (Aortic dissection) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency. Initially, through integrated bioinformatics analysis, we screened proteomic data from AD and control samples. By combining this with a ferroptosis-related database, 22 key ferroptosis-associated proteins were identified. Multidimensional analyses (including heatmaps and volcano plots) were used to visualize differences in protein abundance and pathway disruptions. Functional annotation via GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these proteins are involved in critical biological processes such as oxidative stress and inflammation. Validation through Western blot, immunohistochemistry, RT-qPCR, and single-cell RNA sequencing confirmed that these proteins (HO-1, SNCA) are significantly upregulated in AD and exhibit distinct cellular distribution patterns. Single-cell analysis further identified specific cell populations that drive ferroptosis-related events. These findings underscore the role of ferroptosis as a key molecular mechanism in the pathogenesis of AD. By integrating insights from proteomics with cellular-level distribution patterns, this study identified novel therapeutic targets, marking a significant breakthrough in the understanding and potential treatment of this devastating disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54130-x.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cardiology, Cell biology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases

Introduction

AD is an extremely life-threatening cardiovascular disease that poses a severe threat to human health1,2. Its harm is extensive and fatal: patients often experience sudden severe chest or back pain, described as tearing or knife-like, which not only severely impacts quality of life but also triggers a series of critical consequences3. Once an AD ruptures, massive blood loss in a short time can cause patients to quickly go into shock or even sudden death, with an extremely high mortality rate4. Additionally, when the dissection involves branch arteries, it leads to ischemia in corresponding organs: involvement of the coronary arteries can trigger myocardial infarction, involvement of the renal arteries may result in renal failure, involvement of the mesenteric arteries can cause intestinal ischemia and necrosis, and involvement of cerebral arteries can induce cerebral infarction and coma, causing significant damage to multiple organ functions5. In terms of pathological basis, abnormal aortic wall structure and hemodynamic changes play a key role in the development and progression of AD6. A normal aortic wall consists of the intima, media, and adventitia. The media is rich in elastic fibers and smooth muscle, ensuring the aorta has good elasticity and toughness7. However, factors such as hypertension, smoking, cystic medial necrosis of the aorta, hereditary diseases (Marfan syndrome), iatrogenic factors, and pregnancy can all damage the aortic wall structure8–11. In the field of latest research, scientists have made progress in multiple areas. In diagnostic technology, imaging techniques such as multidetector computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance angiography, and transesophageal echocardiography continue to improve, enhancing the accuracy and sensitivity of early diagnosis12–14. Biomarkers such as D-dimer help quickly rule out low-risk AD cases15. In terms of pathogenesis research, understanding of the role of abnormal aortic wall structure and function-including genetic factors, extracellular matrix metabolic disorders, and inflammatory responses-in the development and progression of AD has deepened16,17. Treatment methods are also continuously advancing, with optimized drug therapy regimens, innovative surgical techniques, and expanded application of endovascular interventional therapy18,19.

In recent years, the role of ferroptosis in AD has received increasing attention20–22. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by iron metabolism disorders and lipid peroxidation23–25. Studies have found that tissues from patients with AD exhibit significant iron overload and lipid peroxidation, with severe dysregulation in the expression of multiple redox regulatory hub genes: ferroptosis-driving genes are upregulated, while inhibitory genes are downregulated26,27. In β-aminopropionitrile-induced AD models in mice, the expression of ferroptosis-driving genes is significantly upregulated as aortic degeneration progresses, and lipid peroxidation products increase, this negative effect can be eliminated by specific ferroptosis inhibitors20. This suggests that ferroptosis is involved in the pathological process of AD, being an important factor causing degeneration of the aortic intima and media, disease progression, and death of vascular smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells in the aortic wall, thus providing a new direction for the research, prevention, and treatment of AD.

Materials and methods

Proteome analysis

Control and AD patient samples were collected from the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, with detailed sample information provided in supplementary Table 1. Label-free quantitative proteomic analysis was performed by Shanghai Luming Biotechnology Co., Ltd., with the following detailed workflow:

Protein extraction and preparation

Frozen samples were ground in liquid nitrogen, lysed with lysis buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, and sonicated on ice (80 W, 1 son/1 s off, 3 min total). After centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 10 min at room temperature, the supernatant was collected as total protein extract. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay, and extracts were stored at − 80 °C until further use.

Protein quality control and digestion

Protein extracts (10 µg per sample) were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and visualized with eStain LG protein staining to assess integrity. For qualified samples, 50 µg of protein was reduced with 5 mM DTT at 55 °C for 30 min, alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide in the dark for 15 min, and precipitated with 6 volumes of pre-chilled acetone at − 20 °C overnight. After centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the protein pellet was resuspended in 200 mM TEAB and digested with 1 mg/ml trypsin-TPCK (1:50 enzyme-to-substrate ratio) at 37 °C overnight. Peptides were then labeled with TMTpro reagent (100 mM TEAB buffer, 1 h reaction, quenched with 5% hydroxylamine) and vacuum-dried at − 80 °C.

Peptide fractionation and LC–MS/MS analysis

Dried peptides were reconstituted in 100 mM TEAB and fractionated by high-pH reversed-phase chromatography (Agilent 1100 HPLC, Zorbax Extend-C18 column, 2.1 × 150 mm, 5 µm) using a gradient of 2–90% acetonitrile (PH = 10, ammonia) at 300 µL/min. Fractions eluting between 8 and 60 min were collected, pooled, and vacuum-dried.

Peptides were analyzed by nano-LC–MS/MS on a Thermo Fisher system: samples were loaded onto a PepMap100 trap column (100 µm × 2 cm, C18) at 300 nL/min, separated on a PepMap RSLC analytical column (75 µm × 50 cm, C18) with a gradient of 5–90% acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid) over 75 min, and analyzed in positive ion mode. MS parameters included: full scan resolution 60,000 (m/z 350–1500), AGC target 1e6, max injection time 50 ms; data-dependent MS/MS scans (resolution 30,000, AGC target 2e5, max injection time 45 ms, HCD collision energy 35, dynamic exclusion 60 s).

Data processing and bioinformatics analysis

Raw MS data were processed for database searching (Swissprot database) to obtain protein identification and quantification. Data quality was assessed, and preprocessing was performed with principal component analysis (PCA). Differential proteins were identified by statistical analysis, followed by correlation analysis, Venn analysis, expression clustering, and STEM analysis to explore expression patterns. Functional annotation was conducted using Swissprot, GO, KEGG, NOG, Pfam, and COG/KOG databases28–30. GO enrichment, KEGG pathway enrichment, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis (visualized by Cytoscape), and transcription factor analysis were further performed to characterize the biological functions and regulatory networks of differential proteins.

Sample collection

Human ascending aortic wall tissue samples were collected from 7 patients (4 controls, 3 AAD) at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (April-September 2021). The study was approved by the local ethics committee (No. YJSKY2024-251) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with written informed consent obtained from all participants. The AAD group comprised male patients (33–66 years) with confirmed acute AD (onset ≤ 14 days), while the control group included male patients (51–63 years) undergoing cardiac surgery without aortic disease. Patients with severe organ dysfunction, malignancy, or poor sample quality were excluded. AAD samples were harvested from the intimal tear-adjacent diseased wall, and control samples from normal ascending aortic wall; all samples were rinsed with cold PBS, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. Culture of aortic smooth muscle cells (VSMCs)

Human aortic smooth muscle cells were cultured in Procell Cell Complete Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, Gibco). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. When cells reached 80–90% confluence, they were detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution, passaged at a ratio of 1:3, and seeded into appropriate culture plates for subsequent experiments. Cells from passages 3–6 were used for all experiments to ensure consistent phenotype and viability. For experimental treatments (ferroptosis induction or inhibitor intervention), cells were serum-starved in DMEM containing 0.5% FBS for 12 h prior to treatment, then incubated with the indicated reagents (Ferrostatin-1) for the specified duration.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8 Assay)

VSMCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured overnight for adherence. After the indicated treatments, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h in the dark. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated relative to the control group.

Lipid peroxidation assay (MDA measurement)

MDA kits were purchased from Dojindo Laboratories (Cat No. M496), VSMCs were seeded in 6-well plates and treated as indicated. After treatment, cells were harvested and lysed, and the MDA content was measured using a commercial thiobarbituric acid (TBA) assay kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cell lysates were mixed with TBA reagent, incubated at 95 °C for 40 min, cooled to room temperature, and centrifuged. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm, and MDA concentrations were calculated using a standard curve and normalized to the total protein content of each sample.

Glutathione (GSH) assay

The GSH commercial kit was purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Cat No. A006-2). VSMCs were seeded in 6-well plates and treated as indicated. After treatment, cells were collected and lysed, and the reduced GSH content was measured using a commercial colorimetric or fluorescent assay kit following the instructions. Briefly, cell lysates were reacted with a chromogenic/fluorescent substrate specific for GSH, and the absorbance (at 412 nm for DTNB-based kits) or fluorescence intensity was measured. GSH concentrations were calculated from a standard curve and normalized to the total protein content of each sample.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using a TRIzol reagent (Sigma, American). cDNA reverse transcription, and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was per- formed under the instructions of the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China) and TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China). Relative expression levels were obtained by normalizing to GAPDH. Each reaction was performed in triplicate. The list of primers is presented in Table 2 of the supplementary materials.

Western blot analysis

The extraction of total protein was completed by using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime). Equivalent quantities of proteins were isolated by SDS-PAGE, followed transferred to the PVDF membrane (Millipore). The membranes were sealed with skimmed milk and then were incubated with primary antibodies for HO-1 (10,701–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), SNCA (10,842–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), GAPDH (81,640–5-RR, Proteintech, 1:1000), GPX4 (ab125066, Abcam, 1:1000) and β-actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:1000). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were added to incubate for 1 h at the darkroom. GAPDH and β-actin were as internal control. And the amount of protein was evaluated by the chemiluminescence detection system.

Immunohistochemistry assay

Immunohistochemistry was performed on paraffin-embedded aortic tissue Sects. (4-μm thick) from AD patients and controls. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was conducted by boiling sections in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min, followed by cooling at room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 for 10 min. After rinsing with PBS, sections were incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min to prevent non-specific binding. Primary antibodies against target proteins (HO-1, SNCA; 1:200 dilution in 1% BSA) were applied overnight at 4 °C. Sections were then washed with PBS and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h at 37 °C. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used as the chromogen, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Negative controls were processed by replacing primary antibodies with normal IgG. Images were captured using a light microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed using Image-Pro Plus software, calculating the integrated optical density (IOD) and positive staining area percentage. Three randomly selected fields per section were analyzed, and average values were used for statistical comparison.

Single-Cell data preprocessing and clustering

Single-Cell RNA data (GSE213740) were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Quality control (QC) metrics, including number of genes detected (nFeature_RNA), number of counts of gene detected (nCount_RNA) and percentage of mitochondrial reads (percent.mito), were calculated for each cell based on the following standard: nFeature_RNA > 200, nCount_RNA < 20,000, and percent. mito < 25%. Cells with low gene counts, or high mitochondrial read percentages were filtered out to remove low-quality cells. Doublets were identified and removed using the DoubletFinder R package (v2.0.4)31. Dimensionality reduction was performed by using principal component analysis (PCA), followed by batch effects correction performed by using the Harmony R package (v1.2.1) across multiple samples t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) for visualization32. Cell clustering was carried out using Seurat’s FindVariableFeatures, FindClusters, and FindNeighbors functions identify distinct cell populations. Cell types were manually annotated based on the expression of known cell-type marker genes (ACTA2 for vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMC), COL1A1 for fibroblasts).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0). For proteomic data, after raw data preprocessing, differentially abundant proteins between AD and control groups were screened using the limma package. A threshold of adjusted p-value < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) > 1 was set to identify significant differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess inter-sample variability, and hierarchical clustering for heatmap generation was based on Euclidean distance with complete linkage. For functional enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG), the clusterProfiler package was employed, with statistical significance determined by adjusted p-value < 0.05. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database (version 11.5) and visualized via Cytoscape (version 3.10.2), with hub proteins identified using the CytoHubba plugin. For RT-qPCR, Western blot, IHC data, results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent experiments. Differences between groups were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For single-cell RNA sequencing data, after cell filtering, dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed using the Seurat package. Differential expression of HO-1 across cell populations and groups was identified using the FindMarkers function in Seurat, and tested using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare gene expressions between different groups.

Results

Identification of ferroptosis-associated hub proteins in AD

We performed proteomic sequencing on human samples from control and aortic AD groups to screen differentially expressed proteins, with the aim of exploring ferroptosis-related proteins. By overlapping these proteins with ferroptosis-related genes from the FerrDb database33 (http://www.zhounan.org), we identified 22 overlapping targets. We then conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses and constructed a PPI network to identify hub proteins.To verify their reliability, we validated the expression of these hub proteins at mRNA and protein levels using Western blotting, RT-qPCR and immunohistochemistry. Single-cell analysis was further applied to examine their expression and distribution at single-cell resolution, clarifying their characteristics in different cell populations during AD progression (Fig. 1).

To validate the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins in AD, we performed WB and IHC analyses (Fig. 4). Correspondingly, IHC results displayed more intense staining for SNCA in AD tissues, and quantitative analysis verified the significant up-regulation (p < 0.05). These findings collectively indicate that ferroptosis- related proteins HO-1 and SNCA are up-regulated in AD, suggesting a potential involvement of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of AD.

In vitro functional validation of ferroptosis in VSMCs

To further confirm whether ferroptosis is functionally involved in the pathological progression of AD, we established an Ang II-induced HASMC injury model and intervened with the classical ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1. Western blot results showed that compared with the control group, Ang II treatment significantly downregulated the protein levels of core ferroptosis protective molecules GPX4 in HASMCs; whereas Fer-1 supplementation effectively reversed the inhibitory effect of Ang II and restored the expression of GPX4 (Fig. 5A, B). CCK-8 cell viability assay revealed that Ang II markedly reduced the viability of HASMCs, and Fer-1 intervention could significantly rescue Ang II-induced cell viability decline (Fig. 5C). Ang II stimulation caused significant depletion of intracellular GSH, the core antioxidant substance, and Fer-1 could effectively restore intracellular GSH homeostasis (Fig. 5D). In addition, MDA content was notably elevated in the Ang II group, while Fer-1 treatment significantly suppressed excessive MDA accumulation (Fig. 5E). Collectively, in vitro cellular experiments verified that Ang II can directly induce ferroptosis in HASMCs, manifested as the downregulation of GPX4, decreased cell viability, enhanced lipid peroxidation and GSH consumption. Pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis by Fer-1 can remarkably reverse all the above pathological phenotypes, providing direct functional evidence that ferroptosis participates in the occurrence and development of AD.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to comprehensively elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying AD, with a particular focus on the role of ferroptosis. Through a series of integrated bioinformatic and experimental analyses, we have obtained several key findings.​ Our initial bioinformatic screening identified a set of 22 proteins that are both differentially expressed in AD samples compared to controls and are associated with ferroptosis. These proteins likely play crucial roles in mediating the ferroptosis-related pathological processes in AD. Functional annotation analysis, including GO and KEGG pathway enrichment, revealed that these proteins are involved in a variety of biological processes such as platelet aggregation, inflammatory responses, and several key signaling pathways that are dysregulated in AD. For example, the identified proteins are associated with pathways related to cellular metabolism and redox regulation, which are known to be disrupted in AD pathogenesis.​ At the protein- level validation stage, Western Blot and Immunohistochemistry results demonstrated significant upregulation of key ferroptosis-related proteins HO-1 and SNCA in AD samples. The elevated expression was not only detected at the protein quantity level by WB but also visually confirmed in tissue sections by IHC, indicating their potential importance in the AD-associated ferroptosis process. Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis provided insights into the cellular-level distribution of these proteins. Specific cell populations, such as certain immune cells and vascular smooth muscle cells, were found to have increased expression of SNCA and HO-1 in AD samples, suggesting that these cell types may be the main drivers of ferroptosis - related events in AD.​

Our findings align with some aspects of previous research on AD. For instance, previous studies have also highlighted the importance of oxidative stress and abnormal cell death in AD development34,35. Our identification of ferroptosis-related proteins and their upregulation in AD further supports the notion that dysregulated cell death mechanisms, specifically ferroptosis, contribute to the pathogenesis. However, our study goes a step further by precisely identifying a set of key proteins involved in this process and elucidating their cellular-level distribution, which was not comprehensively addressed in prior work.​ In contrast to some earlier studies that mainly focused on the role of traditional risk factors such as hypertension and genetic connective tissue disorders in AD, our research emphasizes the significance of ferroptosis-related molecular mechanisms36. Although these traditional risk factors are well-established, the molecular events downstream of these factors that lead to AD development are still not fully understood. Our work suggests that ferroptosis may be one of the crucial downstream events triggered by these risk factors, providing a new perspective on the pathophysiology of AD.​ To directly verify the occurrence of ferroptosis in AD-related pathological processes, we performed in vitro experiments in HASMCs using Ang II to simulate pathological stress, together with the ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Fer-1. Results showed that Ang II significantly downregulated GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression, reduced cell viability, elevated MDA levels, and decreased GSH levels, all of which were markedly reversed by Fer-1. These functional rescue experiments provide direct and specific evidence that ferroptosis is activated in AD, rather than being merely a consequence of general oxidative stress or inflammation.

The upregulation of ferroptosis-related proteins such as HO-1 and SNCA in AD may be attributed to multiple mechanisms. HO-1 is a key enzyme in the heme-degradation pathway and is induced in response to oxidative stress. In the context of AD, the chronic mechanical stress on the aortic wall, along with the presence of inflammatory mediators, may lead to increased oxidative stress, thereby upregulating HO-1 expression37. HO-1 has been reported to exert either pro-ferroptotic or cytoprotective effects depending on the cellular context; our data only demonstrate that HO-1 is strongly associated with ferroptosis-related signatures in AD rather than directly driving ferroptosis38. HO-1, in turn, may modulate the cellular redox state and lipid peroxidation, key processes in ferroptosis39.​ SNCA, on the other hand, has been implicated in various neurodegenerative diseases related to abnormal protein aggregation and oxidative stress40,41. In AD, the exact mechanism by which SNCA is upregulated and its role in ferroptosis are still not fully clear. However, it may interact with other proteins in the ferroptosis pathway or directly affect the iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation processes. For example, SNCA may interfere with the normal function of proteins involved in iron storage and transport, leading to iron overload, a characteristic feature of ferroptosis42. Although SNCA is best known in neurodegenerative diseases, its significant upregulation and enrichment at the single-cell level in our study suggest a potential link to vascular ferroptosis, yet its detailed mechanistic role in vascular cells remains to be further explored43. The dysregulation of the identified signaling pathways associated with the 22 ferroptosis-related proteins may also contribute to the development and progression of AD. For example, pathways related to cellular metabolism may be disrupted, leading to abnormal energy production and utilization in aortic cells44. This, combined with the increased ferroptosis, may weaken the aortic wall structure, ultimately predisposing to AD.

Despite the significant insights gained from this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, our study was mainly based on human tissue samples. Although these models provide valuable information, they may not fully recapitulate the complex in-vivo environment of AD. For example, the dynamic changes in hemodynamics and the interactions between different cell types in the aortic wall may be different in a living organism compared to cell culture models. Future studies using in-vivo animal models are needed to validate our findings and further explore the pathophysiological mechanisms of AD.​ Secondly, owing to clinical sample collection constraints, the sample size of this study is relatively small (3 AD patients and 4 controls), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the conclusions. In addition, 2 out of 3 AD patients had a smoking history while none of the controls smoked, and smoking may serve as a confounding factor that affects proteomic and transcriptomic profiles; this imbalance could not be fully adjusted in the analysis due to the limited sample size. Additionally, our study focused only on a subset of ferroptosis-related proteins. There may be other important proteins and molecular pathways involved in AD-associated ferroptosis that were not captured in this study. Expanding the scope of proteomic and genomic analyses in future research could potentially identify additional key players in this process.​ Furthermore, the present findings need to be validated in independent clinical cohorts to confirm their reproducibility before clinical translation.

Our study provides a foundation for future research on AD. One important direction is to further validate the role of the identified ferroptosis-related proteins in in-vivo animal models. Knock-out or over-expression models of these proteins can be used to directly assess their impact on AD development and progression. For example, knocking out the genes encoding HO-1 or SNCA in animal models and observing the effects on aortic wall integrity and the occurrence of AD could provide more conclusive evidence for their roles.​ Another area of future research is to develop targeted therapies against the identified ferroptosis-related proteins or pathways. Given the crucial role of ferroptosis in AD, inhibiting or modulating this process may represent a novel therapeutic strategy. For example, drugs that can specifically inhibit the upregulated ferroptosis-related proteins or block the dysregulated signaling pathways could be developed and tested in pre-clinical models.​ From a clinical management perspective, the identified ferroptosis-related proteins such as HO-1 and SNCA may serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers for early detection of AD, and targeting ferroptosis may provide a new therapeutic strategy for clinical intervention of AD. Furthermore, integrating multi-omics data, including proteomics, genomics, and metabolomics, in future studies will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying AD. This will help to identify additional biomarkers for early diagnosis and prognosis prediction of AD, as well as potential therapeutic targets.​

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study has identified key ferroptosis-related proteins and pathways in AD, providing new insights into the pathophysiology of this life-threatening disease. Although there are limitations, the findings pave the way for future research aimed at developing more effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for AD.​
