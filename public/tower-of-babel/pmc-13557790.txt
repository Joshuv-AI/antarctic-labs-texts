Employee Perceptions of a Clinical Department’s Response to the COVID-19 Pandemic
Authors: Rebecca A. Netson, Joseph Incorvia, Shehnaz Alidina, Hannah C. Brown, Stephanie Miller, S. Kate Bridges, Rachel R. Watson, John G. Meara, Ronald Heald, Amir H. Taghinia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:During the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in the United States in 2020, hospital systems faced significant challenges, including high patient demand, a lack of resources, and the rapidly evolving safety mandates that necessitated new clinical protocols. However, the perceptions of healthcare workers regarding the effectiveness of their departments and management approaches have not been well described.Methods:Thirty semistructured interviews were conducted with healthcare workers in a single clinical department at a pediatric tertiary care hospital in October 2020, 6 months after the issuance of the state-of-emergency order for the pandemic. Thematic analysis was used to identify and differentiate key insights related to both effective and ineffective practice changes that emerged during this period.Results:The transition to remote work during the COVID-19 pandemic was marked by challenges related to technological adaptation and data security, mitigated in part by proactive logistical support, incorporation of department-wide virtual meetings, and a dedicated task force. Changes in healthcare access practices were necessitated by rapidly evolving guidelines, resulting in innovative patient care approaches. Nevertheless, the blurred boundaries between personal and professional lives exacerbated stress and burnout among personnel, necessitating leadership intervention to reaffirm work-time boundaries and promote strategic task prioritization.Conclusions:Instituting consistent channels of communication is instrumental in offering support and disseminating information to employees. Actively maintaining boundaries and prioritizing work-life balance prevents negative impacts on employee mental health. Healthcare entities should invest in strengthening workplace culture and ensuring that these competencies are integrated into practice beyond crisis situations.

Takeaways

Question: How can the experiences of healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic inform departmental improvements?

Findings: Through direct employee interviews, key themes emerged, including employees needing extra support to adapt to remote practices and struggling to maintain work-life balance. These challenges necessitated departmental leadership to maintain consistent communication, enforce and prioritize boundaries, and invest in workplace culture.

Meaning: This study contributes to a deeper understanding of the complex changes faced by healthcare workers and the importance of a comprehensive and supportive approach to managing unprecedented change.

INTRODUCTION

As the highly contagious coronavirus (COVID-19) swept the United States in 2020, hospital systems faced the challenge of serving the overwhelming influx of COVID-19 patients while providing standard care. This flood of patients led to limited resources within hospitals.1 Additionally, federal and state governments instituted occupational safety mandates that led hospital leaders to develop new clinical protocols.2–4

The mental toll of prolonged work hours and the uncertainty of COVID-19 lethality caused an uptick in burnout and the risk of posttraumatic stress disorder among frontline and nonfrontline workers.5,6 The main factors attributable to burnout were the cessation of routine services, redeployment of staff to unfamiliar clinical environments, and the constant risk of complaints of negligence as medical resources and services were placed at their maximum capacity.7

Coinciding with the disruptions to healthcare systems, modifications to the labor market were instituted through remote work. In the aftermath of the pandemic, remote work has become a mainstay, requiring new strategies for organizations to continue collaboration and performance management.8–12 Reviewing departmental responses to the pandemic remains relevant, as many plastic surgery organizations are still recovering from workforce changes and struggling with burnout years later.13–15

This qualitative study examines the perceptions of healthcare workers in a pediatric plastic and oral surgery department of a major tertiary care hospital regarding the COVID-19 departmental response and institutional policy changes. Given the unprecedented nature of the COVID-19 pandemic, capturing employee perspectives provides an opportunity to evaluate the effectiveness of new strategies and identify targets for future improvements.

METHODS

Background

In response to the pandemic and in compliance with federal and state mandates, the Department of Plastic and Oral Surgery (DPOS) at Boston Children’s Hospital introduced modifications to workplace operations supervised by a departmental leadership team of surgeons and administrators. This study was conducted in October 2020 in partnership with Global Health Systems at Harvard Medical School to evaluate employees’ perceptions of these policy modifications.

Study Design

This study was conducted in the DPOS because the authors had visibility into their own departmental response and access to recruit interview participants. We selected a cohort of 30 participants, all involved in the COVID-19 response of the department. The sample included administrators, patient experience representatives (PER), physician assistants, nurses, surgeons, and clinical researchers. Full-time employees within the department from March to October 2020 were eligible to participate, excluding contract employees. Potential participants from each role type were selected using a random integer generator and recruited via email. If they declined to participate, a new person was selected from the same role using the generator.

Participants who did not meet the eligibility criteria were removed from the sample pool (N = 6). The final sample pool consisted of 89 eligible participants: 11 administrators, 22 PERs, 11 physician assistants, 20 nurses, 11 surgeons, and 13 clinical researchers. We aimed to interview at least one-sixth of the employees from each role. The interview protocol was developed in collaboration with the experts in global health systems at Harvard Medical School, exploring themes, including (1) the department’s response to COVID-19; (2) leadership engagement and support; (3) participants’ experiences, including high-level support, work-life balance, motivation, and productivity; (4) organizational culture, including teamwork and values; and (5) lessons learned, including what new processes should be maintained.

Data Collection

We conducted semistructured, one-on-one interviews, lasting approximately 40 minutes. Interviews were conducted via Zoom by 1 of 2 members of the research team (S.A. and J.I.), both with graduate degrees in public health and policy and experience with qualitative research. This study was approved by the Boston Children’s Hospital institutional review board with a waiver of informed consent (IRB-P00042145). All participants provided verbal consent before the interviews, and no interviewees dropped out. The interviews were transcribed, quality-checked, and uploaded to NVIVO V.12 (QSR International, Melbourne, Australia) for coding.

Data Analysis

The audio files and transcriptions were reviewed by 3 researchers. To maintain anonymity, participants were assigned a study identification number and a general role name (eg, “surgeon 1”). Themes were extracted through qualitative content analysis. Researchers S.M. and J.I. reviewed 5 transcripts to develop a preliminary codebook and reached an agreement before coding the remaining 25 transcripts. A third researcher (R.A.N.) validated the categorization of codes by analyzing all 30 transcripts using the final codebook. The coauthors reviewed the patterns identified by the researchers and condensed the findings.

Technological and Logistical Supports

Participants expressed difficulty adjusting to remote work due to the sudden reliance on teleconferencing platforms for communication. This transition led to the migration of hospital-protected data to a virtual warehouse, requiring a new security protocol. Participants reported a lack of prior experience with these technologies and limited availability of technological infrastructure at home. Employees found it difficult to learn the utilities of the new technologies while remaining productive in a rapidly changing environment. Participants agreed that although remote work caused by COVID-19 was unavoidable, the difficulties of the abrupt transition were due to the underuse of existing technologies. As one nurse explained:

I don’t necessarily think that the logistical and technical support has been adequate for people who weren’t set up to work from home… It took a while to get them the support that they needed. And some of them still are really struggling with the technical side of things.

—Nurse 5

Despite these challenges, participants expressed gratitude for the proactive stance of the department in addressing needs by purchasing and distributing laptops within days, rather than waiting for a hospital-wide issuance:

I was in complete awe of the leadership’s fast response to this… Yesterday we were not an organization like this, but on Monday we are… I have been on this team for a long time, and I am not sure I have ever been as proud as I was… for what we accomplished in one business day.

—Administrator 5

Participants said that these decisive actions not only created more time to acclimate to remote work but also expedited the evaluation of the effectiveness of these processes. An additional table of participant quotes further corroborates this. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the qualitative impact of remote work as perceived by participants, grouped by operational areas, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F120.) For instance, an evaluation of the initial call forwarding protocol revealed bottlenecks in queuing that necessitated a switch to a different system. In addition, new virtual visit scheduling platforms were introduced and integrated because the previous system could not keep up with demand.

Communication Strategies

Employees expressed appreciation for leadership efforts to maintain effective communication during the COVID-19 pandemic. In addition to regular department-wide virtual meetings to communicate operational challenges, hospital policies, and culture-building activities, a COVID-19 task force consisting of clinicians and administrators was deployed. It played a crucial role in communicating rapidly changing medical and policy information but also served as a bridge between departmental leadership and staff, providing accessibility for employees who were hesitant to speak out in a wider forum. As expressed by a member of the administrative staff, employees were encouraged to share challenges:

Everyone just had an open-door policy…, “if you have questions, if you have concerns, let us know.” And multiple times in those big department meetings with… 85 people on it, [a department leader would offer], “Email me privately. If no one wants to talk now, then just email me.” Get your concerns out there...

—Administrator 2

Participants revealed that leadership commitment to finding collaborative solutions motivated them to explore virtual alternatives to engagement. For example, a physician assistant mentioned that the transition of in-person clinical learning sessions to a virtual format helped maintain continuity amid the pandemic. Participants agreed that the openness to adopting novel approaches helped alleviate the lack of personal connections due to remote work.

Healthcare Access Practices

Personnel responsible for patient intake faced challenges in adhering to the rapidly changing federal and hospital guidelines for new patient visits due to the high volume of backlogged patients from early in the pandemic. Surgeons and administrators expressed concerns about the impact on the care of patients who were unable to be seen in person due to their canceled elective procedures and the challenges of seeing new patients:

I’ve had to talk to a lot of angry families that were like, “I understand that there’s a pandemic, but my kid needs his teeth out.” … trying to sympathize with families when there’s something so much bigger going on, and our administrative team doing their best with those conversations… to cancel everything from office visits, to surgeries, everything…that was probably [the] most challenging.

—Administrator 2

To ensure the efficient use of hospital room and operating room capacity, department leadership rearranged the assignments of the operating room and clinic block time to prioritize patients with time-sensitive ailments, foregoing the previous standardized distribution scheme. This change required constant communication, accounting for rescheduling and cancelations, such that the hospital-allotted time could be rapidly allocated to available providers and patients:

If I was given [operating room] time that I couldn’t fill, I would let my supervisor know that I don’t need certain dates, so we could divvy it out to someone else… it was that kind [of] teamwork in working these new schedules.

—PER 4

As the department leadership adapted to the mandated guidelines of hospital room availability, there were also significant shifts in how medical practices approached patient care. As practices had to reduce in-person visits, they devised creative methods for triaging, diagnosing, and evaluating patients. In the case of skin lesions, practices requested patients to send smartphone pictures electronically before a telemedicine appointment. This bypassed the limitations of low-resolution videos and provided a clear image of the lesion. Although these changes introduced new workflows for administrative staff, clinicians concurred that the enhancements significantly improved patient access and enabled more accurate diagnoses and treatments, and this practice has continued postpandemic.

Work-life Balance

Although dealing with the uncertainty of the pandemic, participants expressed unrelenting pressure to be productive in their professional lives, leading to burnout. Underscoring this notion was the transition to remote work, blurring boundaries between work and personal time. Forwarding of office phones directly to administrators’ personal phones caused participants to feel an implicit expectation to be constantly responsive to incoming requests, resulting in an unsustainable work-life shift. As one administrator explained:

I don’t ever fully disconnect from work. I check my work email, on like an average weeknight…two or three times that night. I check it on the weekends, which is not healthy. I know you shouldn’t do that. But I have trouble completely shutting off from work.

—Administrator 1

Given consistent reports of overworking, participants reported that colleagues encouraged them to take time off. However, following this advice was not always straightforward, as those providing the encouragement were themselves actively working:

I’d be working [until] 8:00 or 9:00 PM, and so are five of my co-workers. I can see that they’re all online still, too.... Everyone would say to me, “Take time for yourself. Don’t work late.” But they wouldn’t necessarily do that themselves.

—PER 2

Interviewees shared that once concerns about overworking were raised with department leadership, measures were promptly put in place to alleviate the pressure of constant responsiveness. Leaders emphasized that notifications received outside of standard working hours did not necessitate an immediate reply. Additionally, several supervisors proactively scheduled one-on-one meetings with their staff to explore personal accommodations.

DISCUSSION

The COVID-19 pandemic prompted the issuance of new regulatory guidelines for Massachusetts hospitals and healthcare workers on March 17, 2020.16 These changes encompassed 3 elements: deferment of elective surgery to preserve the availability of medical personnel and space, encouragement of telehealth services, and remote work for nonessential personnel.17 Against this backdrop, the present study aimed to ascertain employee experiences with a single clinical department’s endeavors to integrate these changes into its operations within a tertiary care pediatric hospital. The authors aimed to understand the diverse consequences triggered by the mandates and identify strengths and weaknesses of the resultant operational adjustments. Four key themes emerged from employee responses: technological support, communication strategies, healthcare access, and work-life balance. Recognizing the ramifications of these pandemic-induced changes is crucial to improve emergency preparedness and to assist in formulating sustainable, long-term improvements.

Before the pandemic, the use of telehealth was limited.18,19 However, governmental mandates for insurance coverage forced health institutions to reconsider their stance on telehealth, as it emerged as the main viable option. To accommodate this change, healthcare organizations promptly improved or established telehealth services. Yet, challenges associated with the technology itself and limitations of remote visits persisted, thus spurring innovative solutions that addressed these shortcomings.20 In our department, personnel introduced a proactive approach that included the collection of patient images and documents before virtual visits, the revamping of scheduling protocols, and the introduction of integrated technology for online consultations. These advancements in telehealth have proven vital in aiding patient access and throughput, especially in a surgical department where patient assessment is mostly visual.21,22

Despite the swift acquisition and rollout of telehealth technology, lack of prior training impacted efficiency and heightened staff stress. This experience emphasizes the importance of early investment in technology and data security. As exemplified in our department, healthcare organizations should consider proactive adoption of emerging clinical technologies to avoid having to adopt them rapidly under duress, thus compromising patient care.

This study highlighted how work-life balance was significantly impacted during the pandemic, corroborating prior research on its link to increases in mental health concerns among healthcare employees.23 Although telecommunication’s rise was advantageous in many aspects, it blurred the boundaries between personal and professional lives, intensifying healthcare workers’ reported exhaustion. This erosion of boundaries was supported by a 2022 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention report, finding that 93% of healthcare workers reported being stretched too thin, with 82% sharing being emotionally and physically exhausted.2 Given the urgency of the pandemic, the constant availability of employees, enabled by digital technology, became the norm. Further, employee conformity to overworking norms was influenced by colleague work patterns. Given the pervasive implicit expectation of overworking, the explicit and consistent delineation of supervisors in expressing more reasonable expectations for worker availability was a powerful countermeasure. However, this could only be noticed through bidirectional communication, allowing staff to express their concerns and receive the necessary support from management. Fostering an open dialogue culture in the workplace emerged as a crucial strategy for preemptively mitigating feelings of burnout. This not only allows for efficient dissemination of information regarding operational changes but also offers real-time insight into employee sentiments, enabling immediate adjustments.

Our findings can also be interpreted through the lens of the job demands-resources model, which describes that job demands contribute to burnout while job resources help mitigate it.24 Participants described multiple added job demands, such as adhering to changing hospital guidelines, the shift to new technologies, and the pressure to be constantly available. However, the proactive technological support and increased department communication efforts represent crucial resources that buffered stress to enable continued functioning. Situating our themes within this model highlights how organizational support can offset crisis-related strain and sustain workforce well-being and helps transfer findings to other clinical departments beyond DPOS.

LIMITATIONS

Interviews were conducted 6 months after the state of emergency had been ordered by the state of Massachusetts, which may have led to recall bias, as employees may have had time to adapt to the new processes. The authors intended to perform another longitudinal interview within 6 months to a year after initial interviews. However, due to a significant turnover in healthcare workers in our department and nationwide, not enough of the original participants were available, so this study remains focused on the initial COVID-19 response.25 Additionally, participants may have had confounding stressors beyond department policies, and these personal considerations were not granularly investigated. Furthermore, the department was required to adhere to institutional mandates and did not have complete control over its operations. This could limit our findings’ generalizability to other departments that have autonomy within their larger enterprise regarding policy changes. The analysis followed accepted qualitative study methodologies, but researchers’ understanding of participant insights may not encapsulate all employee responses working within the department. Objective indicators such as turnover rates, working hours, and patient volumes were too difficult to track to supplement the qualitative surveys in this study due to remote work and the variation among different roles interviewed (surgeons, administrators, nurses, researchers, PERs).

Furthermore, the unique nature of this pediatric DPOS may have contributed to some of the observations seen herein, thereby limiting generalizability. In a surgical department where appointments are mostly visual, one could argue that the needs of patients forced more rapid adoption of technology (eg, video conference versus telephone; streamlined processing of images sent electronically). The pressure to adapt may not have been as strong in other departments. The DPOS also has a high mix of elective compared with time-sensitive cases, thus allowing more flexibility for cases to be rescheduled. A high proportion of outpatient cases allowed higher throughput and easier scheduling by the hospital because the cases did not strain the resource-intensive parts of the hospital. Overall, the department’s unique patient mix and case acuity resulted in pressures and relief valves that drove adaptation in unique directions that may not be generalizable to other departments in the future.

CONCLUSIONS

Our findings provide important considerations for healthcare organizations as they continue to navigate recovery from the pandemic and seek to build a more sustainable healthcare system for the future. This study contributes to a deeper understanding of the complex and multifaceted challenges faced by healthcare workers during the pandemic and the importance of a comprehensive and supportive approach to crisis management in similar circumstances.
