Abstract
. In light of the diminishing presence of physical third places—informal gathering spaces essential for social connection—this study explores how the social media platform Discord fosters third-place experiences. Drawing on Oldenburg’s conceptual framework, we analyze how Discord’s design elements support the creation of virtual third places that foster both dyadic and community-based relationships. Through 25 semi-structured interviews with active Discord users, we identified 21 design elements aligned with Oldenburg’s third-place characteristics. These elements cluster around four core principles: providing themed spaces for repeated interactions, supporting user autonomy and customization, facilitating mutually engaging activities, and enabling casual, low-pressure interactions. This work contributes to understanding how intentional platform design can cultivate virtual spaces that support meaningful social connections. The findings have implications for designing future social technologies that can help address growing concerns about social isolation in an increasingly digital world.

Related Work

2.1.
“Third Place” in Physical and Virtual Spaces

Oldenburg’s “Third Place”.

Ray Oldenburg
(
Oldenburg and Brissett 1982
;
Oldenburg 1999
)
defines “third places” as inclusive spaces outside the home (first place) and work (second place), where people form relationships through repeated interactions. Examples include cafes and libraries, which exhibit eight key characteristics: 1) “on neutral ground” where people can come and go freely; 2) social “leveling,” making status irrelevant; 3) conversation as the main activity; 4) accessibility and accommodation; 5) regular visitors shaping the ambiance and welcoming newcomers; 6) a “low profile”; 7) a “playful mood”; and 8) a sense of “home away from home.” In the U.S., such spaces are crucial for community development but increasingly scarce
(
Soukup 2006
)
.

Traditional vs. Virtual Third Place.

While Oldenburg emphasizes physical proximity, scholars have explored virtual spaces as digital equivalents
(
Soukup 2006
;
Kendall 2002
;
Foster 2013
;
Markiewicz 2019
)
. Drawing on Goffman’s analogy of social interactions as performances
(
Goffman 2016
)
, Soukup
(
Soukup 2006
)
suggests that a sense of place in virtual spaces can be “self-selected and mutually constructed” through repeated interactions, coalescing around a shared theme or interest. Studies have noted similarities between traditional and online spaces
(
Parks and Roberts 1998
;
Baym 1995
;
Schuler 1996
)
, where computer-mediated communication, like chatrooms
(
Kim and Lim 2015
;
McArthur and White 2016
;
Alcala et al. 2023
)
, fosters playful conversations with emerging regulars. Platforms like Twitch
(
Hamilton et al. 2014
)
, Second Life
(
Erdoğmuş 2009
)
, Facebook, and online games
(
Steinkuehler and Williams 2006
;
Ducheneaut et al. 2007
)
have been proposed as virtual third place. However, these spaces may not fully meet Oldenburg’s criteria given
conversations are not the central focus
, hierarchies exist, and the ambiance of the platform is extravagant rather than home-like.

“Beyond Being There”
(
Hollan and Stornetta 1992

2.2.
Virtual Platforms and the Role of Space

Physical Space Reasoning in Virtual Contexts.

Spatial thinking remains highly relevant in designing virtual contexts, where spatial metaphors like
“virtual architecture,”

“cyberspace,”
and
“gateway”

(
Adams 2010
)
, as well as
“chat rooms”
(
Giese 1998
)
, help conceptualize and navigate digital environments. These metaphors facilitate navigation and sensemaking within virtual spaces
(
Maglio and Matlock 1999
;
Kuhn and Blumenthal 1996
;
Giese 1998
)
. Since people naturally gravitate towards spatial reasoning, such metaphors are advantageous in virtual design, leveraging familiar cognitive processes, enhancing memory, and aligning with sensory perception to reduce user mental effort
(
Kuhn and Blumenthal 1996
)
.

The Role of Space in Relationship Building.

The role of space in relationship building has been debated. Historically, physical proximity was seen as essential for forming social relationships
(
Collins and Blau 1979
)
, but digital technologies led some to argue that space was no longer relevant
(
Rainie 2012
)
. However, Small and Adler’s review
(
Small and Adler 2019
)
, “The Role of Space in the Formation of Social Ties,” shows that physical space remains crucial, particularly in facilitating unplanned encounters and deeper connections. They examine how physical contexts support or hinder tie formation—defined as initiating contact and developing it through repeated interactions—at both micro and meso levels (neighborhood and organizational scales). Drawing from geography, architecture, and urban planning, Small and Adler argue that spatial context influences social ties through three mechanisms: spatial propinquity (
“physical proximity between actors”

1
1

1

Proximity is used literally here, not metaphorically
), spatial composition (
“presence or absence of fixed places that encourage interaction”
), and spatial configuration (
“arrangement of barriers and pathways that segment space”
). One idea we explore in this paper is whether and how (given the significant influence of space reasoning in virtual contexts) spatial reasoning in physical contexts affects relationship-building transfers to digital contexts, too. Given how deeply this spatial thinking affects human reasoning and behaviors, they likely play equally important roles in virtual environments, where spatial design choices may similarly facilitate or inhibit relationship formation.

Space vs. Place.

As Harrison and Dourish
(
Harrison and Dourish 1996
)
articulate, the distinction between space and place is fundamental when examining spatial metaphors. They present space as the structural potential of digital environments, while place emerges when these environments become enriched with cultural norms and shared understandings of appropriate behaviors. Their key principle effectively captures this distinction:
“Space is the opportunity; place is the understood reality.”
While places inherently exist within spaces, the essence of placeness transcends spatial or structural organization—it is fundamentally about how people interact with and within the environment.

This perspective has found considerable support in the academic community. Freeman
(
Freeman and Jacob 2012
)
offers a nuanced view that cyberspace is
“not space”
in the physical sense, but rather a collection of
“place-like”
units—
“distinct,”

“recognizable,”

“intersubjective environments”
with
“spatiotemporal qualities”
and
“bounded”
spaces that can be
“inhabited”
with
“behavioral guidelines and expectations.”
Similarly, Saunders
(
Saunders et al. 2011
)
notes that repeated interactions in virtual environments foster presence and immersion, contributing to the perception of a
“real place.”
Nitsche
(
Nitsche 2009
)
adds that
“identity, self-motivated action, and traces of memory”
are key to creating a sense of
“placeness.”
We maintain the view that
“space”
is a neutral entity with spatial structure, and
“place”
is a bounded space with social rules co-constructed through repeated interactions.

2.3.
Designing for Places in Virtual Spaces

Designing for Place vs. Designing for Space.

Harrison and Dourish
(
Harrison and Dourish 1996
)
observe that virtual platforms frequently employ spatial metaphors to evoke familiar behavioral patterns by replicating physical spatial mechanisms that guide navigation and action. While our behaviors are indeed contextualized within spatial frameworks and often transfer to virtual spaces through physically-inspired affordances, they emphasize a critical distinction:
“in everyday experience and interaction, it is a sense of place, rather than the structure of space, which frames our behavior.”
They further elaborate that
“placeness”
emerges as a
“cultural phenomenon”
through temporal user interactions, specifically through processes of
“adaptation”
and
“appropriation”
—essentially, customization. This suggests that while we can design spaces to facilitate the emergence of appropriate behaviors and placeness, we cannot predefine space as place, as it is ultimately the users who transform spaces into places through their interactions.

Discord’s Spatial Architecture.

From a spatial perspective—(i.e., how the platform is structured and arranged three-dimensionally)—Discord’s architecture closely parallels Slack
(
Slack [n. d.]
)
. Both platforms feature segmented spaces (“Servers” in Discord, “Workspaces” in Slack) represented as icons in the left sidebar, hierarchical channel structures, centralized chat interfaces, and video/audio communication capabilities. However, these similar spatial structures manifest as distinctly different
places
. Slack positions itself as “Where work happens”
(
Slack [n. d.]
)
, while Discord promotes itself as “Group chat that’s all fun & games”
(
Discord [n. d.]
)
. This distinction materializes in their feature priorities: Slack emphasizes productivity-enhancing third-party integrations, while Discord prioritizes audio communication, reflecting its gaming community origins. Their monetization strategies further reflect these different identities—Slack’s premium features center on information access with per-member pricing suited for organizational use, while Discord’s premium offerings focus on individual self-expression without significantly impacting community functionality.

Discord as a Space-Based Social Media.

From a “placeness” standpoint, Discord functions as a social media platform. Research shows its significant presence among teens, identifying Discord as one of their primary social media applications without prompting
(
Kim et al. 2024a
)
, and it regularly appears among the most popular social media platforms for youth
(
Anderson et al. 2023
;
Wikipedia contributors 2024
;
Howarth 2023
)
, reflecting cultural expectations similar to other social media environments. While Discord serves as a space for social connection, its spatial organization differs fundamentally from mainstream social media platforms. Unlike the network-based or commons-based sharing models of platforms like Instagram and TikTok, Discord employs a space-based approach
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, where interactions occur within dedicated spaces such as channels.

This space-based sharing naturally fosters placeness through repeated member interactions. Discord has successfully positioned itself as a third place, aligning with its design objectives to support playful chat for everyone
(
Citron 2020
;
Discord [n. d.]
)
. While virtual third place has traditionally been explored in gaming or three-dimensional space contexts
(
Steinkuehler and Williams 2006
;
Ducheneaut et al. 2007
;
Erdoğmuş 2009
)
, where synchronous occupation and shared activities define the experience, Discord takes a different architectural approach. Rather than leveraging
“relational orientation and reciprocity”
or
“proximity and action”

(
Harrison and Dourish 1996
)
, Discord’s spatial design focuses on
“partitioning”
and
“presence and awareness”
(
Harrison and Dourish 1996
)
without avatar-based embodiment.

Discord’s Approach to Virtual Third Place.

Prior research has largely focused on virtual third places within gaming environments
(
Steinkuehler and Williams 2006
;
Ducheneaut et al. 2007
;
Erdoğmuş 2009
)
, where users engage via embodied avatars in three-dimensional worlds that simulate physical proximity and shared spatial intuitions
(
Harrison and Dourish 1996
)
. In contrast, Discord adopts a two-dimensional architecture centered on
“partitioning”
and
“presence and awareness”

(
Harrison and Dourish 1996
)
. This structural divergence sets Discord apart, positioning it as neither a conventional social media platform nor a traditional virtual third place. Instead, Discord demonstrates how social platforms can be intentionally designed to cultivate a sense of place—specifically a virtual “third place”—departing from the norms of typical social media interfaces.

General Design Considerations for Virtual Third Place.

Virtual “third place” and online relationship-building rely on well-designed social interactions and spatial structures to foster engagement and connection. Key strategies include structuring interactions through roles, shared times, and spaces
(
Ducheneaut et al. 2007
;
Soukup 2006
)
, offering flexible shared focuses for interactions
(
Maglio and Matlock 1999
;
Bruckman and Resnick 1995
)
, creating welcoming atmospheres through “active hosting”
(
Moore et al. 2009
)
, and offering user-generated spaces
(
Ducheneaut et al. 2007
)
. While visual and interactive cues help with social navigation, virtual spaces often require explicit conveyance of social connotations
(
Maglio and Matlock 1999
)
. This is addressed by social translucence—comprising “visibility,” “awareness,” and “accountability”—which enhances users’ presence and promotes natural interactions
(
Erickson and Kellogg 2000
)
. These cues also help maintain appropriate social density, fostering a balanced environment
(
Moore et al. 2009
)
. Well-defined boundaries enhance proximity and community
(
Soukup 2006
)
, involving both physical and epistemological aspects
(
Zhang and Jacob 2013
)
, such as naming themed chatrooms
(
Soukup 2006
)
. Aligning the space’s organization with users’ intuitive navigation
(
Maglio and Matlock 1999
)
strengthens the virtual third place and user experience. While other studies examine elements like moderation and anonymity
(
Bruckman [n. d.]
;
Kraut et al. 2011
;
Kim 2000
)
, this paper focuses on spatial design in virtual third places.

3.
Method

We conducted semi-structured interviews with 25 participants to examine how, if at all, the design of space on social platforms supports remote tie formation.

Of the 74 survey respondents, 55 met the eligibility criteria. We scheduled and conducted interviews with 25 participants on a first-come, first-served basis. The mean age of the participants was 18.0 years (N=25, min=15, max=24, SD=1.97), with 15 (60%) identifying as women, 7 (28%) as men, and 3 (12%) as non-binary. On average, participants had used Discord for 46.3 months, and 80% reported forming friendships beyond casual acquaintances. A detailed list of participant demographics is provided in Table
1
, with additional information in Appendix
A

3.1.
Platform Choice

Discord has recently become one of the dominant social media platforms, with its valuation increasing from $7B in 2020 to $15B in 2021, its monthly active users growing from 56 million in 2019 to 200 million in 2024, and 28% of U.S. teenagers reporting regular use, solidifying its position as one of the top platforms for youth, with many non-gaming communities also adopting Discord. 3.3.
Materials and Procedure

The first and second authors conducted 60-minute semi-structured Zoom interviews with 21 and 4 participants, respectively. Although the division of interviews between the authors could have introduced challenges with consistency, they held frequent meetings and practice sessions to ensure alignment. While the lead author had a more comprehensive understanding of the research, the second author, as a frequent Discord user and gamer, brought valuable insight from both their deeper familiarity with the platform and demographic closeness to the participant group, which may have enhanced rapport.

The interview protocol covered three areas: 1) participants’ experiences with Discord, including their perceptions of its culture, purpose, and daily interactions; 2) the development and nature of relationships formed on the platform; and 3) specific Discord features that facilitate or hinder relationship-building, with comparisons to other platforms (see Appendix
B
for the full interview protocol). We did not introduce the concept of a third place, but when participants made analogies to physical third places, we explored these further while maintaining neutrality. The procedure was approved by our Institutional Review Board (IRB) for both minors (with parental consent) and adults.

Data Analysis

We employed an inductive-deductive analysis approach

)
, using Small and Adler’s
(
Small and Adler 2019
)
framework on physical spatial contexts that influence tie formation. We used this framework as the basis for generating initial codes on virtual space designs relevant to relationship-building. Using these codes, we performed an analysis of 25 interview transcripts, auto-transcribed via Zoom.

Audio recordings were referenced during coding to correct any transcription inaccuracies.

In the first coding phase, all four authors independently open-coded two transcripts line by line, using the initial codes from the deductive framework we developed while remaining open to emerging themes. The first author then refined the codes based on this initial analysis. Following this, the first and second authors coded four additional transcripts, iterating on the codes until saturation was reached. These codes were synthesized and mapped to specific elements from Oldenburg’s framework of eight third-place characteristics

4.1.
On Neutral Ground

Discord’s design fosters a sense of neutrality, offering spaces where interactions are user-driven rather than dictated by algorithms or gate-keeping mechanisms. This section explores how users join communities and interact with open-access channels, highlighting the platform’s emphasis on choice, accessibility, and autonomy.

A. Search and Invite-Based Joining.

Joining communities on Discord relies on direct invites or actively searching (see Figure
1
) for communities of interest, which helps users avoid unsolicited algorithmic suggestions. Participants appreciated this approach, noting how it fosters more intentional and meaningful interactions. P19 explained,
“I get to choose the Discord servers I’m in and the people that are in there. So it makes it easier [to build connections] because I know I’m choosing this group of people… to at least try and start interactions and relationships with.”
In contrast to platforms like Instagram, where algorithmically curated content often exposes users to
“random”
content (P11), Discord requires users to
“meaningfully search out a community online… and join it.”
This process can involve steps like agreeing to community rules, which P11 noted
“helps decrease the level of toxicity.”
P09 compared Instagram’s algorithm-driven approach to a
“quilt”
of mainstream topics, where interactions follow patterns laid out for the user. In contrast, Discord allows users to
“pick which string”
or
“which pattern”
they want to follow, leading to more focused and purposeful interactions.

B. Open-Access Channels.

Discord exemplifies neutral ground through open-access channels—spaces where users can come and go freely, without obligation to engage or formal permissions. These channels significantly lower the barrier to entry or exit, making interactions feel more accessible and less burdensome. The ability to join or leave without needing approval or disrupting others also supports a sense of comfort and autonomy. For example, P04 appreciated Discord’s voice channels, saying
“You can just drop in and drop out of a particular voice channel at any point without taking anybody else’s consent.”
P16 similarly noted,
“You can leave without disturbing the call… , or if you can’t join at the beginning,… you can just join in later.”
This contrasts with platforms like FaceTime, where
“[users] have to individually add [others]”
(P23). On Discord,
“anyone could join that specific call”
(P17). P07 echoed this, stating,
“That’s why I like Discord better than Skype—you don’t have to request to join or ask someone to add you to the call. You just click on the [channel]… and join.”

4.2.
The Third Place is a Leveler

Discord levels the social playing field by allowing users to interact without real-world identity or status constraints. The platform emphasizes self-expression and community involvement over real-world status through pseudonymous identities and role-based systems.

A. Pseudonymous Profiles.

On Discord, users are not required to link their profiles to their real-world identities. Perhaps influenced by the playful culture of Discord, many users predominantly opt for pseudonymous profiles characterized by chosen usernames and avatars, creating an environment that facilitates identity exploration and expression beyond real-world constraints. As P17 describes,
“Yeah, like for example, if someone in real life were to be introverted or keep to themselves, and they seem like a nice person… but then, on Discord, they could be really loud and extroverted, and talk to all these people online, because it’s behind a screen.”
This cultural norm of pseudonymity is further emphasized by P10, who observes:
“There’s no link to who you actually are… like Instagram, people tend to post their face or artwork… things that link back to who they are outside of the little digital world. Discord kind of curates a space where you don’t have to do that.”
This deliberate separation from real-world identity creates opportunities for self-expression, as noted by P04:
“[My favorite thing about Discord is that] I can be anyone I want without other people judging me or knowing who I am.”

B. Contribution-Based Hierarchy.

While pseudonymous profiles diminish the influence of real-world social status, Discord servers maintain a form of internal organization through a role-based system
(
Discord [n. d.]
)
. These roles serve both administrative and social functions, with most being purely identificatory rather than hierarchical. As P08 explains,
“[Roles] just shows up on your profile if you wanted the role. It’s kind of just identification.”
When hierarchical roles do exist, typically in the form of administrators and moderators, they represent the primary—and often only—formal power structure within Discord communities. Notably, these positions of authority are divorced from real-world social status or prestige. Instead, they are server-specific appointments typically granted based on community involvement, tenure, social connections within the server, or voluntary commitment. As P02 explains,
“Usually, the moderators are people that are veterans, like long-time members… and are also really involved in the community.”
While these roles carry significant responsibilities and rights, including rule enforcement and member management, they are generally viewed as service positions rather than status symbols. P25 emphasizes this dynamic:
“It’s just volunteer… so people just apply to us privately. And honestly, most people don’t want to. So we just kind of take what we can get in that regard.”

4.3.
Conversation Is the Main Activity

At its core, Discord thrives on conversation, offering an environment where dialogue is the primary form of engagement. Through its chat-driven design and flexible organization, the platform supports fluid, ongoing interactions that distinguish it from more rigid forms of social media.

A. Conversation-Driven Communication.

Discord distinguishes itself as a chat-centered platform, placing conversation as the primary mode of communication. This contrasts with other social media platforms, where sustained, reciprocal dialogue is less common. P03 shared that while on Instagram, communication feels
“one-way”
because users would
“post, and then you don’t really know if the person on the other end will see it or not,”
whereas on Discord, it is
“just easier for people to get like get engaged”
and it feels
“two-way.”
P15 echoed this, saying,
“It almost feels like social media but just for messaging… whereas on Instagram, messaging feels a little more out of the ordinary.”
Similarly, on Reddit,
“someone asking a question or sharing a picture, and people responding to the OP, but they’re not really talking to each other”
(P25). P25 described TikTok as also
“restricting communication-wise”
, encouraging content creation over interaction. Twitch offers
“just live streaming”
and
“quick chat”
, where
“you can’t form relationships”
(P21). The need to
“make an entire post”
(P04) to interact, as many platforms such as Facebook groups do, experiences there feel very different than those on Discord, where users feel more
“a part of a community”
(P04) by chatting in groups.

B. Conversation Organization.

Discord’s interface offers multiple pathways for organizing conversations, significantly
enhancing
users’ ability to engage in meaningful discussions. Particularly on PC, users can efficiently manage multiple conversations simultaneously. As P19 shared, switching between different servers and message channels is
“really easy,”
allowing them to manage
“2 or 3 conversations at once”
or even
“4 conversations with 2 people”
simultaneously. This multi-conversation capability makes Discord more effective for relationship-deepening interactions compared to platforms like Instagram, where users must constantly
“click in and out”
to manage different conversations (P10).

The platform’s organizational structure mirrors the importance of space segmentation in physical third places. Just as crowded physical spaces can hinder meaningful connections, Discord addresses this challenge by supporting multiple channels within servers. P21 drew an analogy:
“It’s kind of like how a house has multiple rooms. If you’re walking through your house and you see your sister talking on the phone to someone in her room, you’re not going to go in that room. You’re gonna find another room.”
These smaller spaces prevent users from getting
“buried away”
in larger servers (P12) and create more intimate environments for closer conversations.

While Discord’s flexibility in organizing conversations is powerful, it can present challenges. Users often manage numerous servers and channels simultaneously, which can become
“very overwhelming”
for newcomers (P24). To
combat this complexity
, users employ various organizational strategies: pinning important communities, organizing servers into folders based on importance, and using collapsible sections to de-clutter their interface. The platform also offers granular notification controls (see Figure
3
), allowing users to manage their attention effectively. As P02 noted,
“You don’t have to mute the whole server. You could just mute channels or specific things… or people.”
This level of control helps users maintain responsiveness to important messages while preventing notification overload.

4.4.
Accessibility and Accommodation

Discord prioritizes accessibility and inclusivity, offering users a range of features that simplify connections, support diverse public spaces, and accommodate various engagement levels. Its flexibility and cross-platform compatibility make it an inviting space for users from different backgrounds and needs.

A. Low-Barrier Connections.

Discord’s design makes it easy to form connections without needing prior real-world relationships or extensive personal information. Its username-based friend system and shared server contexts allow users to initiate conversations freely, fostering the potential for serendipitous connections similar to those in physical public spaces. Many participants appreciated how Discord servers feel
“more out in the open”
(P21), enabling them to
“shoot message[s] directly without hitting a friend request”
(P04). In contrast, platforms like Facebook require users to navigate
“100 different posts”
,
“filter out”
interests, send a friend request, and
“wait for 2-3 days to connect”
(P04), making connections feel less spontaneous, serendipitous, or casual. P25 noted that Discord allows interactions with a
“big community”
beyond one’s immediate circle, unlike Skype’s more restrictive friend-only model. This openness encourages
“incidental friendships”
(P12), differing from Instagram, where users actively
“seek out… individual people”
. P11 compared Instagram to
“a private party”
where
“you’re trying to get in the party, and you can’t get in the party. So you get sent to the back of the line.”
In contrast, building friendships on Discord is more like being
“in the party, and then you’re talking to somebody who also doesn’t like the party. And it’s like, ‘Hey, you know, let’s just start talking.”’

B. Ecosystem of Diverse Public Spaces.

Discord servers are widely seen as highly accessible, offering public, topic-based communities that attract both gamers and non-gamers alike. The flexible structure of Discord supports a wide range of communities, from gaming to educational or other interest-based groups. P04 highlights this:
“I can find a server… for whatever purpose… there’s something for everything, something for everyone.”
P12 echoed this sentiment:
“In servers that are more inclusive and actually interact with new people, it gets really easy to make friends… with enough time and patience, you can almost always find a community, regardless of the topic.”
Discord’s flexibility supports a wide range of community types and interactions, allowing for diverse, user-driven experiences that contrast with Instagram, where content feels more homogeneous. P09 said that when looking for Love Island-related content on Discord, one could find a community or server
“where we’re going to be recreating Love Island, but through Discord, and find the love of your life…”
, whereas on Instagram there would just be
“a post about it, and it’s going to be mainly ‘Oh my God, this couple got with this couple! They broke up, though they never loved each other.”’

While many platforms allow users to create multiple rooms or groups, these spaces often lack integration into a cohesive whole. For example, P04 compared Facebook groups, which
“don’t have as much categorization capabilities,”
noting that what would be 10 channels in one Discord server becomes
“10 different Facebook groups… I can’t find them under one roof.”
This lack of connected segmentation on other platforms means that even if communities are public, they can feel fragmented, making discovery difficult. Unlike Discord, where everything exists within a cohesive ecosystem, finding related spaces on other apps often requires more effort, as users must navigate between isolated groups or pages rather than seamlessly exploring connected channels within a single server.

C. Tiered Engagement.

One important aspect of accessibility and accommodation is the support for visitors or newcomers (see Figure
5
), which Discord facilitates through features designed for tiered access. When browsing servers, users can peek into spaces without joining, allowing them to get a sense of the environment. Even after joining, they can remain passive observers. This freedom lets users acclimate to unfamiliar environments at their own pace, giving them time to evaluate the community and its members before deciding when, if, and with whom to interact. Participants appreciated this flexibility. P08 contrasted Discord with other platforms where approaching users feel
“less in control”
and
“like a stranger.”
On Discord, however,
“they’re already within the group,”
and users can
“choose to join after observing,”
making it feel like
“you kind of know them a bit.”
P13 valued the
“trial period in the server,”
where they could
“talk to people… without committing to a friendship”
(i.e., sending a friend request). This felt reassuring because, as they (humorously) noted,
“even if it’s anonymous, I kind of know this person’s not a murderer.”
Others valued the
“waiting period for like a week or two”
(P12) to
“lurk”
(P12, P19),
“get the vibe”
(P12), and present themselves more confidently when ready to interact.

D. Cross-Platform Support.

Another element contributing to openness and accessibility is the platform’s
inclusiveness
. Discord’s compatibility across various operating systems and devices makes it feel like a more truly public space in the digital realm. P15 noted Discord is
“easily accessible”
as users can
“access it on multiple devices at once”
. P08 explained that many young users without phones rely on Discord, making it
“convenient for everyone”
(P01). This contrasts with iMessage, which excludes non-Mac users (P07). P09 highlighted that platforms like FaceTime and Instagram are
“so wonky”
for tasks like streaming. P16 shared how Discord
“makes it a lot more accessible”
for their boyfriend with an
“older iPhone”
, allowing them to
“watch YouTube or a movie together”
. P09 also emphasized Discord’s PC accessibility, which benefits gamers who are mostly
“on their PC[s]”
(P03).

4.5.
The Regulars

Discord fosters a sense of community by creating spaces where users frequently encounter familiar faces. Through persistent servers, visibility cues, and custom roles, the platform supports repeated interactions, fostering users’ perception and awareness of these exchanges, which deepens relationships and strengthens social bonds.

A. Persistent Servers.

We find that fixed, immobile virtual spaces—“place-like” spaces—effectively support online relationship building by
providing stable gathering spaces
that facilitate frequent interactions. Unlike most broadcast platforms where interaction points (e.g., posts, comments) are transient, Discord offers stable channels for communication.
3
3

3

Most chat services provide fixed spaces via chat rooms, but they are often not immobile in chat lists, whereas Discord channels’ locations are user-controlled.
One participant (P16) likened this permanence to
“a party that never stops,”
appreciating the ability to
“leave, and… come back,”
knowing
“it’s always gonna be there.”
In contrast, platforms like Instagram felt
“random”
(P11, P12). P12 summarized how consistent communication in fixed spaces fosters community:
“Discord has a much stronger way to make friends… Instagram is more random… but on Discord, you’re in the same channels, see each other regularly… chat, come back a day later, and cross paths again. There’s not nearly that much on Instagram.”

B. Awareness and Visibility.

Another way Discord supports recognizing regulars is through affordances that enhance the visibility and awareness of other users. In virtual spaces, where we lack the social cues and physical presence available in face-to-face interactions, explicit signals are essential to improve social translucence. Discord provides these cues through features that indicate users’ activities (see Figure
6
), such as
“what song [they’re] listening to”
(P21) or
“which game they’re playing and how long”
(P07). These signals help users decide whether to
“not bother”
someone,
“ask questions about the game”
(P21), or
“check in”
(P07). This type of awareness often leads to thoughts about social initiation like,
“‘maybe I’ll go join them”’
(P20). Participants also appreciated the
“friends tab”
that shows
“friends that are active right now”
(P20). Seeing
“the same name over and over”
(P11) gave them a clearer sense of who is
“in-group”
versus
“out-group”
(P11), which often led to the regulars forming
“a small connection”
(P06). Moreover, notifications when someone joins a server were likened to
“opening a door to conversation,”
acting as a casual
“‘Hi!”’
(P05).

C. Custom Roles.

Servers custom roles to highlight regulars or members with similar patterns of engagement, using these signals to encourage repeated interactions even among users who otherwise had little in common. For example, in servers, users
“say a certain message in chat or react to… a bot”
(P21) to receive a role. Such roles created ties with unfamiliar users and facilitated more frequent and natural interactions. P12 likened it to building friendships at school, saying,
“It’s like you’ll see people at, say, high school, where you’re going around, and you see the same people around campus, and you get familiar with their faces, kind of the same way that you would be familiar with the profile picture of someone you keep seeing in your chat rooms.”
P15 used a similar analogy, comparing their experience of getting familiar with members in the same channels as
“having a lot of your daily life overlap”
in a large school. They explained that
“even if… you’ve never met in person, there’s so many ways that you could connect,”
similar to having the
“same teacher.”
This overlap creates relatable experiences and opportunities to help each other, which
“ties you together”
(P15).

4.6.
A Low Profile

Discord’s design encourages a low-pressure environment where users can engage comfortably and authentically. Through ephemeral communication and visual anonymity, the platform fosters casual interactions and authentic connections, free from the social pressures often found in real life or on other platforms.

A. Conversational Flow and Ephemerality.

Discord’s ephemeral communication style creates a low-stakes, casual environment that feels more like a natural gathering space than traditional social media. The fleeting nature of messages encourages users to feel more comfortable and spontaneous. As P19 explains:
“It’s a lot… more casual on Discord… On Facebook groups, if I’m gonna make a post, I have to have something to say… I am contributing something, and then everyone’s responding to me… But on Discord… I’m saying my thing, but then so is the next person, and so is the next person… It is back and forth and doesn’t feel centered on me.”
This dynamic fosters an easy-going, ongoing conversational flow. P04 notes that
“you see all those four or five different people constantly chatting. And you are also part of that group. So it feels more like a… community-building platform.”
The informal, transient nature of these interactions shapes communication style, allowing users to engage more freely. Compared to platforms like Instagram, where interactions feel
“a lot more formal”
and
“less about joking”
(P09), Discord conversations support a fluid, low-profile way of connecting.

B. (Visual) Anonymity.

Anonymity on Discord fosters authentic social connections, especially by removing the appearance-based pressures often found in person and on other platforms. P13 shared,
“I cared way less anonymously on any Discord server… But I can’t in person. When I try making friends, I’m consistently thinking someone’s eyes are on me, or like… people are consistently looking at what I’m doing and making sure they’re with the quote, unquote, right person.”
For those with social anxiety, this anonymity can be especially helpful. P04 humorously shared how it lets them build trust at their own pace, ensuring
“this person won’t kill me”
before sharing more personal details. Discord’s text-based nature also shifts how people connect. As P13 noted,
“you tend to not judge a book by its cover because you don’t know who they are. You don’t know anything about what they look like, what their life’s like, or whatever. So you tend to make friends based on text or just personal interactions.”
P13 further reflected,
“When I started college, everyone seemed super friendly but also super fake. But online, because they were anonymous, they were their true selves. And that’s kind of easy for you to tell right off the bat whether you wanna hang out with this person… or not.”
(B)y minimizing appearance and social expectations, Discord reduces the pressure to perform socially, allowing users to connect over shared interests and fostering a more authentic experience.

4.7.
The Mood is Playful

Discord cultivates a playful atmosphere that encourages lighthearted interactions and community bonding. Through gaming features, bots, and playful visual elements, the platform supports a culture of fun, creativity, and shared enjoyment.

A. Gaming Integration.

Discord’s foundation as a gaming platform shapes its playful atmosphere, as one participant noted,
“Discord’s big thing is being a big community for gamers, I mean, the logo is a game controller”
(P19). The platform integrates various entertainment features, from games to media sharing. As P03 explains,
“Discord has loaded on a bunch of games. You can watch YouTube. You can invite like… Discord bots, which are basically like robots that developers make for Discord to play music.”
Beyond gaming, the platform pioneered features like screen sharing and party games, with P02 noting,
“Discord had screen sharing before… iOS did, for Facetime … Now, even Discord has, … little mini games.”
This gaming-focused environment creates a casual space that P18 likens to a game room:
“it’s kind of its own thing, where it’s kind of just to have fun conversations, and like it’s not as serious … not as structured or… like… professional.”
These features foster relaxed, entertainment-focused interactions that distinguish Discord from more formal social platforms.

B. Bots and Activities.

Bots and integrated Activities (see Figure
4.7
(b)) on Discord provide interactive experiences and collaborative tasks that foster engagement and playfulness within communities. Many participants shared experiences of bonding over Activities on Discord, such as
“playing games”
(P11). Beyond using Discord for communication during external games, users can engage with interactive games offered on the platform, with P06 describing one as
“a really fun game to get to know other people.”
Participants noted that servers often feature
“Question of the Day”
channels, where bots post prompts like
“‘What’s your favorite ice cream flavor?”’
These questions spark conversations, as P25 noted,
“People are gonna talk and be like, ‘Oh, I like that flavor too.”’
Bots also
“promote a lot of activity”
(P05) in the server by initiating discussions. An interesting example was a
“counting bot,”
(P03) where members take turns counting in sequence. P03 described it as
“the most active channel on the server,”
where people enjoy the simple activity, even adding fun twists like using math:
“Instead of saying 19, they might say 20 minus 1, and it still counts.”

C. Playful Visual Reactions.

Discord’s support for GIFs, memes, and custom emotes (see Figure
4.7
(c)) enables rich emotional expression and playful interactions in ways that foster connection. These visual elements help users bond over shared emotions, as P09 describes:
“A lot of kids, they like to send GIFs in after an exam. It’s like ‘Oh, I’m in danger,’ you know, just like that. So it’s just a way to… bond over how hard the class is, and… lowers the tension of class, and it makes it feel a little less lonely that you’re struggling.”
Discord also allows communities to create their own visual language through custom emotes, as P03 notes:
“in our server specifically, you can use specific emojis that we upload. And so we have a Duolingo bird which we name ‘Fear’…, and we have like… clowns… different servers, have different things.”
Users appreciate this flexibility in emotional expression, as P25 notes:
“Sometimes they kind of convey different emotions than the regular like Discord ones… sometimes people make emotes that go together so… if you put them in a certain order, they make a picture or a word or something.”
These playful visual elements help users express themselves more authentically and create shared cultural touchstones within their communities.

4.8.
A Home Away from Home

Discord creates a sense of home through customizable servers, niche subspaces, and contextual profiles. These features enable users to organize their communities, bond over shared interests, and express themselves authentically across different spaces, fostering comfort and belonging.

A. Server Customization.

Virtual community spaces, especially larger ones, thrive on well-designed structures. As P07 explains, Discord offers extensive control over community organization, which makes it feel
“more of like a chatting organization or chatting organizing ‘place.”’
Participants emphasized two key aspects: first, that a well-defined structure is essential for the effective functioning of large, dynamic communities, especially in welcoming newcomers. Second, a well-thought-out structure
signals leaders’ commitment
, showing members their experience is valued, boosting loyalty and engagement. P19 compared joining larger servers to
“walk[ing] into a room with a thousand people I didn’t know… and just having to figure out how to introduce myself to people.”
Participants stressed the importance of keeping servers
“not too messy”
(P06) and using bots to manage spaces, such as those that handle
“tickets”
(P24). Conversely, P19 noted that a lack of organization in larger spaces signaled neglect, discouraging engagement. They shared,
“I’ve seen servers where the only roles are admin and non admin… and those servers typically only have a few text channels like General… and for me, that shows a lack of structure. It’s absolute chaos. I can’t track who’s who. I can’t track the conversation. I don’t think I’ve been in one of those servers longer than 12 hours before I’ve left.”

B. Interest-Based Subspaces.

The ability for users to create subspaces—whether as new servers, channels, or chats—allows them to carve out spaces where they feel truly at home. These spaces, built around shared or niche interests, foster a sense of belonging and connection. P08 highlighted how themed channels can
“giv[e] people things to talk about… ”
as
“ideas or conversation starters”
. P15 shared a similar view, noting that servers
“[tie] us together”
. P11 described niche communities as providing a sense of togetherness that feels
“more authentic”
and
“pure”
because
“your main focus is on that specific topic that you care a lot about”
. Without such mutual interest, P12 imagined that relationship-building would be harder, saying,
“You don’t usually just stumble across someone and say, ‘Hey, do you want to be my friend?’… I think you get a couple of weird looks if you [do that].”

Simply being active in specific Discord servers or channels signal
“some kind of mutual interest… off the bat”
(P19), helping users gauge compatibility. This contrasts with other platforms, where, as P23 noted,
“in messages, I would never know if someone likes music unless I ask them directly.”
This implicit understanding fosters immediate comfort and reassurance that there is common ground (P24). P11 captured this dynamic, likening it to being
“the wallflower at the party… talking to somebody else who’s also a wallflower… And you’re like, ‘Hey, this party kind of sucks.’ ‘Yeah, it does kind of suck.”’
P11 also felt the bond formed through shared experience or sentiment can make Discord feel more like a welcoming, familiar place compared to the broader, less-focused interactions of platforms like Instagram.

These subspaces also allow for flexibility in conversation, enhancing the sense of ease and comfort. P02 explained that while stream chats tend to be reactive, Discord servers enable discussions that stray off-topic while still feeling grounded by a common interest. They compared it to choir socials, where
“we don’t have to be talking about singing, but we’re just united through that… and we just have a good time.”
This dynamic creates an atmosphere where users can relax, connect naturally, and feel at home.

Another benefit of Discord’s structure is its ability to foster diverse cultural spaces. P09 explained that
“Instagram is like one big server, whereas Discord is multiple servers connected together.”
On Instagram’s non-segmented space,
“the loudest people tend to determine what happens”
and
“you cannot change that environment.”
In contrast, Discord allows users to
“ban”
toxic individuals or
“just join a different server with the same topic.”
This flexibility enables the creation of micro-communities with distinct cultures and norms while still being part of the larger platform.

C. Contextual Profiles.

Discord’s flexible profile system lets users shape how they appear and interact across their different communities. As P17 notes:
“You can customize it to your own liking and interests… make it pretty specific.”
Users appreciate being able to maintain distinct personas that reflect their role in each space, with P25 sharing:
“My profile is different… in every server… I have so many different profiles. Those are all me… I think it gives you a lot more personality, and it does stimulate conversation.”
This flexibility accommodates both practical needs, as P05 explains:
“You can change your nickname [for each server]. So because this one is associated with my school, I could attach my real name… so that way people would know who I am,”
and personal expression through playful bios, which P15 observes:
“one says, ‘student academic weapon’ and ‘academic victim’ depending on the hour of the day. So usually a lot of people do funny things.”
The platform further enriches these personalized spaces through integration features, with P11 noting:
“It connects with Spotify. And it also connects with the games you’re playing on PlayStation.”
These customization options allow users to adapt and express themselves authentically within each community, making each server feel like a natural space for different aspects of their identity.

4.9.
The Emergence of Discord as a Third Place Social Media

Ten participants used “place-like” analogies to describe their Discord experiences, highlighting the platform’s ability to create a “virtual third place” atmosphere—casual, playful, accessible, leveling, and comfortable. One participant (P24) even described a server structured like a “house” with various third-place-like spaces. These descriptions often contrasted Discord with platforms like Instagram, which participants felt lacked a sense of space.

Beyond occasional spatial metaphors like “spaces” or “rooms,” participants deliberately used analogies to well-defined virtual “places.” When asked to describe Discord, P11 likened it to a
“town square”
for gathering and branching into niche areas. P20 saw it as a
“concert hall”
with themed rooms, while P18 called it a
“game room”
full of interactive elements. P07 compared it to a
“cafe”
where strangers communicate like friends, and P24 described it as an organized
“house”
with distinct sections. P06 viewed it as an
“activity room”
with clubs for socializing, and P16 compared it to a
“hotel”
with dedicated spaces for discussions and private conversations. These analogies illustrate how Discord creates a “virtual third place,” despite its flat, 2D interface, through its experiential and structural-spatial elements.

4.10.
Relational Benefits of Discord as a Third Place Social Media

Participants emphasized that virtual third places on Discord contributed to relationship-building through two main mechanisms: 1) dyadic relationship-building and 2) community-based relationship-building.

Dyadic Relationship-Building.

Participants reported that focused, public, and fixed third spaces facilitated serendipitous encounters with compatible individuals. Thematic segmentation enabled in-depth conversations on diverse topics, enhancing dyadic relationship-building by increasing the chances of meeting suitable people and strengthening bonds. P22 described making friends through Discord servers focused on
“very niche hobbies”
, noting they
“only knew [them] over Discord”
but now talk regularly and have met in person
“literally dozens and dozens of times”
. P19 shared how segmented fixed spaces such as channels for specific topics like math and books helped them feel more comfortable contributing and forming connections, explaining that
“it would start off as… math help… which then turn into daily texting and… some of the friendships I still have.”
Several participants (P09, P10, P16, P21, P22) even formed romantic relationships with users they met on Discord. These experiences highlight Discord’s ability to foster close connections in a virtual space, reflecting Tidwell’s observation that it is
“not in-person but not impersonal”

(
Tidwell 2023
)
.

Community-Based Relationship-Building.

Participants frequently highlighted the strong sense of community they experienced within “servers,” which functioned as virtual third places centered around specific foci. These spaces fostered a sense of togetherness, lowering barriers to relationship-building. Servers offered conveniences like channel lists that felt like
“a direct line of communication”
and cues like
“seeing another active person,”
helping participants
“see the same people very frequently”
and creating a
“community aspect”
where they felt
“a sense of joy and… belonging,”
making it
“easier to make genuine friends”
(P11). P07 agreed, noting
“once you’re in the community, everybody just wants to make friends… everyone’s friendly.”
P24 appreciated finding people
“that aren’t necessarily very easy to find… in person”
through niche communities. P01 described how their network
“just basically grew… friends of friends of friends”
and how they met a close friend through this network, adding that they are
“still friends… today… which is crazy”
after six years.

5.
Discussion

5.1.
From Space to Virtual Third Place: Design and Social Construction

Our analysis revealed 21 distinct design elements across eight third place characteristics that collectively contribute to creating a virtual third place atmosphere. These elements appear to be guided by four underlying design principles:

(1)

Providing Themed Spaces for Repeated Interactions.
Through designs like the diverse ecosystem of public spaces, interest-based subspaces, persistent servers, and custom roles, Discord creates dedicated spaces that encourage regular, meaningful interactions. These spaces serve as anchors for community formation and relationship development.

(2)

Supporting Autonomy and Customization.
Discord empowers users through search and invite-based joining, open-access channels, flexible conversation organization, server customization, contextual profiles, and technical accessibility. This autonomy allows users to shape their experience and environment, essential for developing a sense of place.

(3)

Facilitating Mutually Engaging Activities.
Designs like conversation-driven communication, gaming integration, Bots and Activities, and awareness/visibility tools create opportunities for shared experiences and natural interactions. These activities serve as catalysts for relationship formation and community building.

(4)

Enabling Casual Interactions.
Through pseudonymous profiles, low-barrier connections, contribution-based hierarchy, tiered engagement, conversational flow and ephemerality, visual anonymity, and playful visual reactions, Discord fosters a relaxed, low-pressure environment conducive to organic social connections.

Importantly, these design principles do not operate in isolation but work together synergistically to support the emergence of place-like experiences. As Harrison and Dourish’s framework
(
Harrison and Dourish 1996
)
suggests, while these design elements create the potential for place-making, the actual sense of place emerges through active user participation and appropriation over time. The platform’s design can only create favorable conditions—users ultimately transform these spaces into meaningful places through their interactions and shared experiences.

This highlights the importance of minimal restrictions and extensive customization options in Discord’s design. Since place-making stems from users’ adaptation and appropriation of spaces, providing flexibility allows communities to shape their environments to meet their specific needs. Even identical structural spaces can evolve into distinctly different “places” based on how communities choose to utilize and customize them.

5.2.
Discord’s Space-Based Design: An Alternative Approach to Social Media

While platforms like Reddit, SecondLife, Slack, and Gather share similar aims of creating virtual third places or structural similarities with Discord, our participants naturally gravitated toward comparing Discord with mainstream social media platforms like Instagram. This tendency reflects the participants’ perception of Discord as a social media platform and society’s established mental model of such platforms. This suggests that Discord could offer an alternative approach to social platform design, especially in cultivating third-place characteristics that support community and relationship building.

Social media have moved from space-based models to network-based (like Instagram) and now commons-based (like TikTok) approaches
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. While no single model is inherently superior—each serves different purposes, leading many users to maintain multiple platform presences—Discord’s space-based nature offers distinct benefits that the youth seek online:

•

The ability to engage in casual, organic conversations without the pressure of creating formal posts

•

Opportunities for community-based interactions and belonging

•

Freedom from appearance-based pressures and profile curation expectations

•

Support for serendipitous and gradual friendship development through persistent spaces

•

Access to (niche) communities with like-minded individuals or those with shared interests

•

An overall casual, playful atmosphere

These characteristics are often absent or difficult to achieve on network-based platforms like Instagram, where interactions can feel more formal, curated, and less conducive to organic relationship development. Discord’s focus on casual conversation, playful engagement, and cross-platform support has also established it as an effective “hangout” space, compared to gaming platforms or business-oriented tools like Slack.

While loneliness and isolation have reached epidemic levels
(
Office of the Surgeon General (2023a) OSG
)
, existing social media platforms have not effectively addressed these challenges. Current policy discussions and academic research primarily focus on restricting youth access to platforms like TikTok and addressing mental health concerns
(
Office of the Surgeon General (2023b) OSG
)
, rather than investigating new approaches to foster meaningful online connections
(
Kim et al. 2024b
)
. This reactive stance, while important for protecting users, may overlook opportunities to develop more supportive social technologies.

5.3.
Relationship-Building in Virtual Spaces

Our examination of Discord reveals key design elements that support the development of place-based experiences and community in online environments. By synthesizing these findings into a taxonomy of designs, we provide a framework for crafting virtual spaces that not only connect people but cultivate lasting relationships.

Discord’s ability to function as a third place, despite its digital nature, facilitates both intimate friendships and broader community-building. This demonstrates the potential for well-designed virtual spaces to help address the decline of physical gathering places and growing concerns about social isolation in the digital age. Our findings also highlight how many current social platforms lack “placeness,” often reducing interactions to fleeting exchanges rather than fostering meaningful connections.

As we navigate an increasingly online future, understanding how to create supportive virtual spaces becomes essential for individual and societal well-being. The lesson is clear: the architecture and cultures within our virtual spaces profoundly shape the architecture and cultures of our relationships. The digital realm, often criticized for its potential to isolate, can—when mindfully designed—enhance rather than detract from our fundamental human need for connection.

5.4.
Limitations and Future Work

Our study has several limitations. First, we only focused on Discord as a virtual third place, but other platforms may offer different or more effective implementations. Another key limitation is our incomplete examination of the potential risks associated with Discord’s design, which may harm social connections rather than foster them. For example, the platform’s high accessibility and lack of robust safeguards have contributed to serious issues such as catfishing and grooming, particularly affecting youth
(
Jargon 2019
;
Kelly 2022
)
. Finally, our interviews were conducted with people who actively use Discord and have prior experience forming friendships on the platform. This may introduce bias, particularly in comparisons between Discord and other social media platforms.

The opportunity exists to explore how these design principles might extend to emerging technologies like Social Virtual Reality (VR), though current accessibility limitations constrain its potential as a widespread virtual third place in the near term. Additionally, as discussed in Section
2
, Discord does not leverage all possible “features of space”—elements like “relational orientation and reciprocity” and “proximity and action” remain unexplored, as the platform lacks embodied avatars or open-world navigation. Further, future social media platforms may evolve into 3D environments like Animal Crossing
(
ani [n. d.]
)
, where users build “villages” and interact in more spatially oriented ways. This suggests opportunities for future work to investigate how incorporating such elements might expand our taxonomy for supporting virtual third places.

6.
Conclusion

Our study demonstrates how Discord’s intentional design facilitates third-place experiences on social media, fostering both dyadic and community-based relationship-building. By aligning with Oldenburg’s third-place characteristics, Discord offers a space that promotes casual, playful interactions, reduces social hierarchies, and supports organic, meaningful connections. As physical third places continue to diminish, the potential for platforms like Discord to mitigate social isolation and cultivate community becomes increasingly significant. The design of social media that incorporates more place-like environments may offer a pathway to better societal well-being in an increasingly digital world. Future research should explore how these design principles can extend to other emerging technologies and address potential risks associated with virtual third places.
