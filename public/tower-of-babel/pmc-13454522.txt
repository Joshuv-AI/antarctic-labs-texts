Numerical and experimental investigations on the rock-cutting mechanism of cylindrical PDC cutters and the influence of key parameters on cutting efficiency
Authors: Dongyang Xue, Xingsong Liao, Haijun Zhang, Hua Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolycrystalline diamond compact (PDC) bits often encounter challenges such as stick–slip, cutter breakage, and low rate of penetration (ROP) when drilling through complex formations with interlayered soft and hard rocks. Cylindrical PDC cutters have demonstrated promising rock-cutting performance in hard formations, offering a new approach for drilling hard and brittle strata. However, the underlying mechanical mechanisms still need to be thoroughly elucidated. In this study, the rock-breaking mechanism of a single cylindrical PDC cutter is systematically investigated by combining theoretical analysis, finite element (FE) simulation, and laboratory experiments. First, a theoretical model for the cutting force is derived based on Evans’ cutting theory, clarifying its mechanical essence. Subsequently, an elastoplastic damage FE model based on the Drucker-Prager criterion is established. This model quantitatively analyzes the effects of the cutter back rake angle (5°-25°) and the rock static elastic modulus (10GPa-50GPa) on the von Mises equivalent stress, equivalent plastic strain (PEEQ), and mechanical specific energy (MSE) during the rock fragmentation process. The results show that the Mises stress increases logarithmically with the increase of the post-cutting angle and the elastic modulus, while the PEEQ strain shows an exponential decreasing trend. The MSE increases with both the back rake angle and elastic modulus; the rock-breaking efficiency is highest at a 5° back rake angle and lowest at 25°. The reliability of the simulation results is confirmed through cross-validation between micro-PDC bit laboratory experiments and numerical simulations. Based on the quantitative results, a small back rake angle of 5° is recommended for hard rocks (elastic modulus > 30GPa) to reduce energy consumption, while a 10°-15° back rake angle is suggested for soft to medium-hard formations to balance efficiency with chip removal. Introduction

Polycrystalline diamond compact (PDC) bits are widely used in petroleum drilling. In recent years, with the improvement in cutter quality and variety, PDC bits are no longer limited to soft formations but can also drill through hard interlayers and long hard-rock intervals. To enhance the cutting efficiency of PDC bits and hybrid bits, it is necessary to investigate the interaction mechanism between a single PDC cutter and rock. Many researchers have conducted extensive studies on rock fragmentation through experiments, theoretical analyses, and numerical simulations.

In terms of rock-breaking experiments, Evans1 pioneered the study of the PDC bit rock-breaking mechanism, elucidating the mechanical nature of coal cutting by blades and pointing out that tensile stress leads to cutting failure. Rånman2 simplified Evans’ rock cutting model and proposed a model that describes rock cutting by wedge-shaped cutters. Geoffroy and Minh3 investigated the rock-breaking behavior of a single PDC cutter under normal force and rotary cutting conditions, establishing relationships among normal force, cutting angle, and rotational displacement.

In theoretical research, the cutting load of PDC cutters has always been a focus. Evans1 first proposed a specific expression for the cutting force using the maximum tensile stress theory. Yilmaz et al.4 argued that using only a shear failure model or a tensile failure model alone yields inaccurate cutting force predictions, and they employed multiple linear regression to compute the cutting force. Hareland et al.5 analyzed the forces acting on a PDC cutter during indentation and cutting, derived the contact pressure between a single PDC cutter and rock using contact mechanics, and evaluated the cutting efficiency of PDC bits. Huang et al.6 obtained a complete stress distribution model during rock cutting based on solid mechanics and fracture mechanics,however, due to the complexity of the mathematical formulas, they opted for numerical methods. Chen et al.7 analyzed the tensile-shear rock-breaking mechanism of PDC cutters and the distribution characteristics of cutting loads, and derived theoretical formulas for the cutting load based on the energy balance principle.

In numerical simulations, Pryhorovska et al.8, Han et al.9, and Shao et al.10 used finite element (FE) simulation to model the drilling process of PDC bits. Sun et al.11 and Chen et al.12 conducted rock-breaking simulations of PDC bits based on the discrete element method (DEM), investigating the distribution of torque and weight on bit (WOB) on individual cutters during dynamic rock breaking. Zhang et al.13 and Li et al.14 studied the mechanism of improving PDC bit rock-breaking efficiency through multi-dimensional impact, establishing impact-cutting rock-breaking analysis models and revealing rock fragmentation mechanisms under one-dimensional and multi-dimensional impacts.

Recently, several studies have further advanced the understanding of cylindrical PDC cutters and rock-breaking efficiency. Liu et al.15 conducted a comparative study on the rock-breaking performance of cylindrical and conventional PDC cutters in granite, demonstrating that cylindrical cutters exhibit higher impact resistance and more uniform stress distribution. Wang et al.16 investigated the impact resistance and rock-breaking characteristics of cylindrical PDC cutters, highlighting their advantages in hard rock drilling. Zhang et al.17 performed a numerical investigation of the effect of rock heterogeneity on PDC cutter performance, emphasizing the need to consider lithological variations. Li et al.18 developed a coupled finite element-discrete element approach for modeling rock cutting under confining pressure. Al-Abduljabbar et al.19 applied specific energy concepts to optimize PDC bit performance in heterogeneous formations. Chen et al.20 provided insights into rock cutting mechanics under confining pressure using finite element modeling. Perez et al.21 quantified the influence of back rake angle on PDC cutter rock cutting efficiency. Wu et al.22 investigated the combined effect of back rake angle and depth of cut on PDC cutter performance. Ahmed et al.23 employed machine learning to predict mechanical specific energy for PDC bits in heterogeneous formations. Zhao et al.24 applied a phase-field modeling approach to study fracture evolution in rock cutting with PDC cutters. Kim et al.25 performed numerical analysis of pick shape and rock properties on cutting performance. Chen26 studied the rock-breaking mechanism of PDC bit cutters in volcanic rock formations. Jin et al.27 numerically investigated the rock-breaking performance of special-shaped PDC cutter combinations. These recent works underscore the growing interest in optimizing PDC cutter design for complex formations.

In summary, current research mainly focuses on rock-breaking efficiency and bit wear in homogeneous formations. This study aims to address the gaps in existing research by systematically investigating, through FE simulation, the quantitative influence of the back rake angle of a cylindrical PDC cutter and the static elastic modulus of the rock on stress, strain, and mechanical specific energy (MSE) during rock fragmentation, and by revealing the underlying mechanical mechanisms beyond mere macroscopic observations. Concurrently, cross-validation with laboratory experiments is conducted to provide a theoretical basis and actionable engineering guidance for the refined design of PDC bits in complex formations.

It is noteworthy that, to focus on the isolated effects of the core parameters (back rake angle and elastic modulus), this study incorporates a series of reasonable simplifications and assumptions in the modeling process. (1) Rock homogeneity assumption: actual formations are heterogeneous; however, this study simplifies the rock as a homogeneous, continuous medium to clearly reveal the mechanism of the core parameters, establishing a baseline for subsequent studies that incorporate lithological variations. (2) Cutter rigidity assumption: actual cutters experience wear; however, this study models the cutter as a rigid body to exclude interference from cutter deformation and focus purely on the rock-breaking mechanics. Cutter wear and life prediction require separate studies using specialized fatigue and wear models. While these simplifications limit the model’s direct applicability in extremely heterogeneous formations, they ensure the scientific clarity of this investigation.

Methodology

This study adopts a methodology combining theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and experimental verification, thereby strengthening the intrinsic connections among these parts. First, a cutting force model is derived through theoretical analysis. This model serves as a theoretical benchmark for extracting and validating the cutting force in simulations and justifies the selection of the Drucker-Prager criterion, which describes the behavior of hard and brittle rocks, as the constitutive model. Second, based on the theoretical analysis, an FE model for single PDC cutter rock fragmentation is established. Single-factor analyses are conducted to investigate the influence of the back rake angle and static elastic modulus on the rock-breaking response. Subsequently, micro-PDC bit laboratory experiments are performed to "cross-validate" the simulation model using the core indicator of "MSE," thereby ensuring its reliability. Finally, based on the simulation data, the mechanical mechanisms are explored in depth, and specific engineering optimization recommendations are proposed. Force analysis of the cutting cutter and theoretical model validation

PDC bit is mainly composed of a polycrystalline diamond composite sheet of the drill head and the front end of the cutter. During rock breaking, the bit weight is transferred to the bit to make the bit move downward. At the same time, the diamond composite sheet with the characteristics of self-sharpness, high hardness and wear resistance directly cuts the rock like a scraper. Since the compressive strength of the rock under confining pressure in the formation is greater than the shear strength, when cutting the rock, the torque transmitted by the bit makes the diamond composite sheet always maintain a sharp state to cut into the rock. When the rock is stressed beyond its shear strength, it is shear broken by the PDC bit cutter.

PDC bit is an integral bit arranged by many cutters. PDC bit is directly involved in rock breaking work during drilling. The overall force of the bit during drilling is mainly synthesized by the force received by all the cutters, and the force of the cutters depends on its distribution space position on the bit. Therefore, the overall force of PDC bit is more complicated. For a single cutter, the force state reflects the rock breaking ability and failure form of the cutter, and the cutter is mainly affected by axial force, tangential force and lateral force when working. The state of the cutter in the normal cutting rock breaking, during cutting, the cutting area of the cutter is closely related to the structural back rake angle and side angle of the cutter, and is also affected by the depth of cutting. Rock strength criterion and damage theory

The rock deformation occurs first under the action of external force, with the continuous increase of external force, the deformation of the rock will also increase, when the external force reaches or exceeds a certain limit, the rock will occur some form of damage. Compared with ordinary materials, the complexity of mineral composition and structural configuration of rocks makes the deformation properties of rocks much more complicated. It is very important to select a suitable rock constitutive model for numerical simulation of rock damage by external load. The Drucker-Prager criterion not only considers the effect of the intermediate principal stress, but also the effect of the average stress. This model requires fewer input parameters and can well describe the yield behavior of rocks under stress. FE modeling of single PDC cutter rock fragmentation

The FE model establishment process mainly includes: constructing the geometric models of the single cutter and rock, model assembly and meshing, material assignment, constraint of the cutter (bit) via a load reference point, surface‑to‑surface contact setup, rock boundary constraints, and loading of WOB/rotational speed on the bit (reference point). The bit continuously rotates and penetrates. According to the Drucker‑Prager criterion, elements are deleted when the rock reaches the yield limit. The reference point records historical outputs such as cutting force and ROP throughout the calculation process.

Meshing

In FE analysis, mesh density significantly affects computational accuracy and speed. Therefore, when meshing, both accuracy and solution time must be considered. If the mesh is too fine, the computation will be time‑consuming and require substantial storage. Conversely, if the mesh is too coarse, the failure and detachment of rock may not be visible in post‑processing, or the detachment may be unsatisfactory, leading to errors in the output curves. Therefore, the part of the rock geometry to be cut is partitioned. According to the size of the rock chips, the fine mesh region is set to 16 elements across the cutting path, while the coarse mesh region is set to 8 elements. C3D8R hexahedral elements are used, which ensure accuracy while improving computational efficiency. Enhanced hourglass control is employed to mitigate excessive element distortion. Mesh sensitivity analysis

To select an appropriate mesh size, a mesh sensitivity analysis was performed. Keeping the geometric models and material properties of the bit and rock unchanged, the fine mesh region size was set to 8, 12, 16, 24, and 32mm. Numerical simulations were conducted, and the rock‑breaking MSE was extracted. Not only was the convergence of MSE monitored, but the peak cutting force and the distribution of the stress concentration zone at the cutter tip were also compared for different mesh sizes. The mesh convergence criterion was defined as a relative error in MSE less than 5% between two consecutive mesh refinements. The results are shown in Figs. 9, 10 and Table 2. For the refined mesh region, a larger mesh size corresponds to a sparser mesh, consistent with the physical definition of mesh density.

Cross-validation between laboratory experiments and numerical simulation

This section aims to rigorously and quantitatively validate the single‑cutter simulation model using the core indicator of MSE derived from micro‑PDC bit experiments and to clarify the mechanical equivalence between them.

Equivalence analysis between experiment and simulation

A micro‑PDC bit can be considered a symmetrical combination of two PDC cutters; its rock‑breaking process is the superposition of the forces and fragmentation effects of individual cutters. Therefore, by comparing the MSE response from micro‑drilling experiments with that from single‑cutter simulations under identical rock conditions, the reliability of the single‑cutter simulation model can be indirectly validated. The two are not directly equivalent but are comparable in terms of the macroscopic statistical law of energy dissipation. The cross‑validation in this study is based on this principle of equivalence.

Laboratory experiment and numerical simulation setup

Laboratory micro‑drilling experiments were conducted on arkose sandstone samples using a rock drilling tester (Fig. 12). The arkose sandstone samples had the following basic properties: porosity of 12.5% and a confining pressure of 5MPa applied during the experiment to simulate in-situ stress conditions. The weight on bit (WOB) was set to 0.5kN, and the rotational speed was set to 55r/min. Torque and ROP were recorded, and the MSE was calculated. A corresponding FE model of the micro‑PDC bit was established, employing the exact same rock constitutive model (Drucker‑Prager) and material parameters as the single‑cutter model. Numerical modeling of rock fragmentation by a micro-PDC bit

Micro-drilling experiments were conducted on feldspar sandstone samples obtained from coring using a rock drilling tester. Boundary condition loading

In the model, the drill bit is defined as an integral rigid body component, while the rock mass is treated as a continuous, homogeneous, and isotropic medium. Deformation and wear of the bit during the rock-breaking process are not considered. It is assumed that rock fragments are promptly evacuated from the borehole, meaning that failed rock elements are immediately deleted upon reaching the failure criterion.

The motion of the bit is defined as a combination of displacement along the Z-axis and rotation about the Z-axis, with a rotational speed of 60 r/min. The rock is meshed using 8-node reduced-integration hexahedral elements (C3D8R) with hourglass control, focusing on the region to be fragmented by the bit. The interaction between the rock and the bit is modeled using surface-to-surface contact, and self-contact is also considered. Displacement constraints in the X, Y, and Z directions are applied to the bottom surface of the rock, as illustrated in Fig. 17.

Simulation results and mechanism analysis of single PDC cutter rock fragmentation

The visual contours obtained from FE simulations include core physical quantities such as stress, strain, displacement, temperature, and coupled field parameters. Among these, stress and strain are the most important physical quantities in visual contours. The von Mises equivalent stress is based on the von Mises yield criterion, which converts a complex multiaxial stress state into an equivalent uniaxial tensile/compressive stress for determining whether a material enters the plastic stage. This parameter is suitable for isotropic materials and can intuitively identify stress concentration zones in a structure. The equivalent plastic strain (PEEQ) reflects the irreversible plastic deformation of a material throughout the loading process by accumulating plastic strain. Its value is always non‑negative, and a value greater than zero indicates that plastic deformation has occurred. This parameter is a key indicator for determining material failure (e.g., fracture).

The results show that the Mises stress increases exponentially with the back rake angle (Fig. 24) and exponentially with the elastic modulus (Fig. 25). The underlying mechanisms are:

① Back rake angle effect: A larger back rake angle reduces the contact area between the cutter’s rake face and the rock, concentrating the same cutting load near the cutter tip. This creates a higher stress gradient, leading to a local increase in Mises stress.

② Elastic modulus effect: Rocks with a higher elastic modulus possess greater stiffness and resistance to deformation. Under the same cutting displacement, a larger force is required to induce yielding, resulting in higher stress concentration in the contact zone. While this high stress concentration facilitates fracture initiation in hard rocks, it may also accelerate cutter tip wear.

Comparative analysis with conventional planar PDC cutters

To highlight the advantage of cylindrical PDC cutters in hard rock, a comparative simulation was performed under identical conditions (back rake angle 5°, rock elastic modulus 40GPa) using a conventional planar PDC cutter. All other simulation parameters were kept exactly the same as those in the cylindrical cutter simulations, including cutting speed (3mm/s), rotational speed (60r/min), mesh size (16mm), contact properties (friction coefficient 0.3, hard contact), material constitutive model (Drucker-Prager criterion), and solver settings (step time 10 s, mass scaling 10). The results show that the MSE for the cylindrical cutter (approximately 21.35GPa) is significantly lower than that for the planar cutter (approximately 28.70GPa). Furthermore, the von Mises stress distribution for the cylindrical cutter is more uniform, avoiding the severe stress singularity at the tip of the planar cutter. This quantitative comparison confirms that, through their unique geometry, cylindrical cutters optimize stress distribution, enabling more efficient volumetric rock fragmentation in hard formations and demonstrating their potential as an alternative to conventional cutter designs.

Discussion

The simulation results presented in Section "Simulation results and mechanism analysis of single PDC cutter rock fragmentation" reveal quantitative relationships between the back rake angle, rock elastic modulus, and key rock-breaking responses (von Mises stress, PEEQ strain, and MSE). In this section, these findings are compared with those reported in the literature to contextualize the contributions of this study.

First, the positive correlation between back rake angle and von Mises stress observed in this study (exponential increase from 0.563GPa at 5° to 0.625GPa at 25°) is consistent with the experimental results of Perez et al.21, who reported that increasing the back rake angle from 5° to 20° intensified stress concentration near the cutter tip. Similarly, Wu et al.22 found that a larger back rake angle reduces the contact area, leading to higher local stresses. However, previous studies primarily focused on qualitative trends, whereas this study provides a specific exponential fitting model (R2 = 0.8969) that can be used for engineering estimation.

Second, the decreasing trend of PEEQ strain with increasing back rake angle (from 0.4402 at 5° to 0.4227 at 25°) and with increasing elastic modulus (from 0.4404 at 10GPa to 0.4142 at 50GPa) indicates a transition from shear-dominated to compression-dominated failure. This mechanistic interpretation aligns with the discrete element simulations of Zhang et al.17, who observed that brittle rocks exhibit limited plastic deformation before fracture. However, the exponential decay function fitted in this study (R2 > 0.97) provide a more precise quantitative description than previously available.

Third, the monotonic increase of MSE with back rake angle and elastic modulus (e.g., from 21.55GPa at 5°/10GPa to 39.06GPa at 25°/50GPa) corroborates the findings of Al-Abduljabbar et al.19 and Ahmed et al.23, who demonstrated that higher rock strength and steeper cutter angles increase specific energy consumption. Notably, the MSE values obtained in this study are within the range reported by Hareland et al.5 for hard rock cutting (20GPa-45GPa). A novel contribution of this work is the establishment of regression models (exponential or power-law) for MSE as a function of elastic modulus for each back rake angle (Table 8), enabling rapid field estimation.

Fourth, the comparative analysis between cylindrical and conventional planar PDC cutters (Section "Comparative analysis with conventional planar PDC cutters") shows that at a 5° back rake angle and 40GPa rock modulus, the cylindrical cutter reduces MSE by approximately 25% (21.35GPa vs. 28.70GPa). This advantage is even more pronounced than the 15%-20% improvement reported by Liu et al.15 for granite, possibly due to differences in rock type and cutting parameters. The more uniform stress distribution observed for the cylindrical cutter supports the argument of Wang et al.16 that cylindrical geometry mitigates stress singularities and delays cutter fatigue failure.

Finally, the cross-validation between micro-PDC bit experiments and simulations (Section "Cross‑validation between laboratory experiments and numerical simulation") confirms the reliability of the FE model, with MSE predictions falling within the experimental distribution. This level of validation is often missing in similar numerical studies (e.g.,20,25), strengthening the confidence in the quantitative conclusions presented herein.

In summary, while the overall trends observed in this study agree with previous literature, the key novelties include: (1) quantitative fitting functions (exponential, power-law, logarithmic) for stress, strain, and MSE as functions of back rake angle and elastic modulus; (2) specific engineering recommendations for selecting back rake angles based on rock elastic modulus thresholds; (3) a validated FE model that can serve as a basis for further multi-variable optimization (e.g., depth of cut, rock heterogeneity, cutter wear).

Limitations and future work

This study, through controlled variable analysis, systematically reveals the quantitative influence of back rake angle and rock elastic modulus on rock‑breaking efficiency, providing a theoretical basis for the optimal design of PDC bits. However, it is essential to acknowledge the limitations inherent in any numerical simulation study, as defining these boundaries is crucial for the correct understanding and application of the conclusions.

First, this study treated the depth of cut as a constant parameter (the 3mm/s ROP corresponds to a fixed depth of cut per revolution) and did not systematically analyze it as an independent variable. In actual drilling engineering, the depth of cut is a dynamic parameter resulting from the combined effects of WOB, rotational speed, rock drillability, and bit design. It directly influences cutter forces, chip thickness, rock‑breaking energy, and borehole cleaning. Treating the depth of cut as a constant, while helpful for isolating the pure effects of back rake angle and elastic modulus, limits the model’s applicability in predicting rock‑breaking responses under variable depth‑of‑cut conditions (e.g., WOB fluctuations, vibration drilling). Future research should incorporate the depth of cut as a key variable in the model, explore its coupling mechanism with the back rake angle, and develop a dynamic rock‑breaking efficiency prediction model that more closely reflects actual drilling conditions.

Second, as noted in the introduction, this study assumed the rock to be a homogeneous, continuous medium and ignored cutter wear. These simplifications provided a clear path to revealing the core mechanical mechanisms. However, actual formations exhibit heterogeneity, anisotropy, and pore fluids, and cutters undergo progressive wear during service. Therefore, future work will focus on:

Introducing multiple variables: Conduct full factorial numerical experiments incorporating the depth of cut, rock heterogeneity (e.g., bedding planes, fractures), and confining pressure as primary variables.
