Endoscopic submucosal dissection for a colorectal tumor with coexisting muscle-retracting sign and underlying diverticulum: the first reported case
Authors: Taiji Yoshimoto, Daisuke Matsuyama, Ayaka Nakanishi, Kei Nakatani, Kosuke Nagakura, Hiroo Ishii, Hiroshi Takihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) for colorectal lesions becomes particularly challenging in the presence of the muscle-retracting sign (MRS), and the risk of perforation is further increased when a diverticulum underlies the lesion. We report a case of ESD for a 25-mm Paris type 0–Is tumor in the sigmoid colon surrounded by multiple diverticula. During ESD, a broad MRS obscured the appropriate submucosal plane. Careful dissection revealed a diverticulum directly beneath the lesion, indicating a focal defect of the muscularis propria. Subsequently, an intraoperative perforation occurred but was immediately detected and successfully closed using endoscopic clips, allowing completion of en bloc resection. Postoperative computed tomography demonstrated minimal free air, and the patient recovered uneventfully with conservative management. This case highlights that even in an extremely high-risk situation involving both an MRS and an underlying diverticulum, careful dissection and prompt endoscopic closure may avoid surgery and achieve curative resection.Keywords: muscle-retracting sign, submucosal fibrosis, diverticulum, colorectal endoscopic submucosal dissection

Introduction

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is an established treatment for large colorectal neoplasms, allowing en bloc resection and precise histopathological assessment. However, colorectal ESD remains technically demanding, particularly in the presence of severe submucosal fibrosis. The muscle-retracting sign (MRS), characterized by triangular retraction of the muscularis propria toward the lesion, is considered to result from submucosal invasive cancer or submucosal fibrosis associated with chronic inflammation, and is recognized as a predictor of technical difficulty and increased perforation risk during colorectal ESD [1–5]. Lesions located near or involving colonic diverticula present additional challenges because diverticula lack the muscularis propria, rendering the colonic wall structurally vulnerable to perforation [6–9].

Although MRS and diverticulum-associated lesions have been described separately, reports describing their coexistence within the same colorectal lesion are lacking. Here, we report a case of sigmoid colon ESD in which broad MRS and an underlying diverticulum coexisted, resulting in intraoperative perforation that was successfully managed endoscopically.

Case report

A man in his 60s underwent colonoscopy after a positive fecal immunochemical test. Colonoscopy revealed a protruded lesion (Paris type 0–Is) measuring approximately 25-mm in diameter in the sigmoid colon (Fig. 1a). Multiple diverticula were scattered throughout the surrounding sigmoid colon (Fig. 1b), raising suspicion of a diverticulum underlying the lesion. ESD was performed to achieve en bloc resection. During dissection, a broad MRS was observed over a wide area, making identification of the appropriate dissection plane difficult (Fig. 1c). Because of marked fibrotic traction, dissection was performed immediately above the muscularis propria. A diverticulum beneath the lesion was subsequently exposed, revealing a localized defect of the muscularis propria and representing the perforation site (Fig. 1d). An intraoperative perforation subsequently occurred but was immediately recognized. The defect was promptly closed using endoscopic clips, allowing completion of ESD with en bloc resection (Fig. 1e and f). Postoperative computed tomography demonstrated only minimal free air, and the patient recovered uneventfully with conservative management. Histopathology revealed moderately to well-differentiated adenocarcinoma arising in an adenoma without submucosal invasion, with negative vertical and horizontal margins and no lymphovascular invasion (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Our case illustrates an exceptionally high-risk situation in colorectal ESD, in which two independent perforation risk factors—MRS and an underlying diverticulum—were present simultaneously. MRS reflects submucosal invasion or severe submucosal fibrosis, which retracts the muscularis propria toward the lesion, narrowing the safe dissection plane and substantially increasing the risk of perforation [1–5]. Protruded (Paris type 0–Is) lesions are sometimes associated with extensive fibrosis and possible MRS, particularly in the setting of chronic inflammatory changes. Conversely, diverticula lack the muscularis propria, rendering the colonic wall structurally fragile. Even meticulous submucosal dissection may result in perforation when a diverticulum is involved [6–9]. The presence of multiple diverticula surrounding a lesion should alert endoscopists to the possibility of an underlying diverticulum, even if it is not clearly visible before dissection.

When both conditions coexist, the safety margin becomes extremely limited. In lesions suspected of overlying a diverticulum, preserving a thin layer of submucosa over the diverticular area may help prevent complete exposure of the defect. However, in this case, broad MRS necessitated dissection immediately above the muscularis propria, making preservation of submucosal tissue over the diverticulum impossible. This led to exposure of the diverticular defect and subsequent perforation. Although discontinuation of ESD may be considered when an MRS is encountered during the procedure, we decided to continue ESD because we considered that the positive MRS was caused by chronic inflammatory fibrosis rather than deep tumor invasion, the patient’s vital signs remained stable throughout the procedure, and the exposed diverticular defect, which represented the perforation site, was successfully closed with endoscopic clips.

Importantly, immediate recognition of the perforation and prompt endoscopic closure enabled successful completion of ESD. The presence of only minimal postoperative free air suggests that early closure effectively limited contamination.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous reports have specifically described colorectal ESD in a lesion simultaneously exhibiting broad MRS and an underlying diverticulum. This case highlights that when multiple diverticula surround a protruded lesion and broad MRS is encountered, the safe dissection plane may be narrow. Adequate preparation for immediate closure, including ready availability of endoscopic clips, is essential before proceeding with deep dissection.

Conclusion

The coexistence of a muscle-retracting sign and an underlying diverticulum represents a particularly high-risk scenario during colorectal ESD. However, even if intraoperative perforation occurs, prompt recognition and immediate endoscopic closure can allow safe completion of the procedure and may help avoid surgical intervention.
