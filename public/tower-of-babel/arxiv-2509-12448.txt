A computational method for type I error rate control in power-maximizing response-adaptive randomization
Stef Baas

Abstract
Maximizing statistical power in experimental design often involves imbalanced treatment allocation, but several challenges hinder its practical adoption: (1) the misconception that equal allocation always maximizes power, (2) when only targeting maximum power, more than half the participants may be expected to obtain inferior treatment, and (3) response-adaptive randomization (RAR) targeting maximum statistical power may inflate type I error rates substantially. Recent work identified issue (3) and proposed a novel allocation procedure combined with the asymptotic score test. Instead, the current research focuses on finite-sample guarantees. First, we analyze the power for traditional power-maximizing RAR procedures under exact tests, including a novel generalization of Boschloo’s test. Second, we evaluate constrained Markov decision process (CMDP) RAR procedures under exact tests. These procedures target maximum average power under constraints on pointwise and average type I error rates, with averages taken across the parametric space. A combination of the unconditional exact test and the CMDP procedure protecting allocations to the superior arm gives the best performance, providing substantial power gains over equal allocation while allocating more participants in expectation to the superior treatment. Future research could focus on the randomization test, in which CMDP procedures exhibited lower power compared to other examined RAR procedures. † † Abbreviations : RAR: response-adaptive randomization, CMDP: constrained Markov decision process, CMDP-P: CMDP maximizing power, CMDP-BP: CMDP maximizing power constraining patient benefit, DBCD: doubly adaptive biased coin design.

Introduction

A two-armed randomized controlled trial evaluates a potential causal effect between administering an intervention (instead of a control treatment) to trial participants and an outcome variable of interest.
Randomized controlled trials traditionally keep the target proportion of participants randomized to each treatment fixed during the trial.
In this setting, the inferential properties of standard statistical tests are often well understood, while such designs can take less effort to implement in practice in comparison to alternatives.

A common misconception in the literature is that two-armed randomized controlled trials with a fixed equal proportion of participants allocated to each treatment (i.e., equal allocation) lead to maximal statistical power
(
Pin et al. 2025a
)
. This statement does not hold in general;
Azriel et al. 2012
showed that equal allocation optimizes power for binary outcomes, in one (the Pitman) asymptotic testing framework, whereas unbalanced allocation is optimal in another (the Bahadur) asymptotic testing framework.

Neyman 1934
showed that the treatment group sizes minimizing the variance of the difference in means – hence maximizing power of the Wald test in the normal outcomes model and by a normal approximation in many other statistical models – are proportional to the standard deviations of the respective outcomes.

Pin et al. 2024
showed that when changing the measure of interest, e.g., from the mean difference to the log odds ratio, the optimal proportion is almost never an equal proportion, while its functional form differs substantially from the optimal proportion for the mean difference.

The allocation proportions maximizing power often depend on unknown model parameters.
To this end, several procedures have been proposed in the literature to learn and target the optimal allocation proportion.
These procedures can be classified as
response-adaptive randomization
(RAR) procedures, which sequentially adjust the allocation probabilities of participants to treatments based on the trial history of observed participant outcomes and
treatment allocations.
Power-maximizing RAR procedures are RAR procedures that maximize a metric related to statistical power.
Frequentist examples of power-maximizing RAR procedures are sequential estimation procedures targeting the proportion of
Neyman 1934
based on the doubly adaptive biased coin design
(
Eisele 1994
, DBCD,)
, the efficient randomized design
(
Hu et al. 2009
)
, or the mass-weighted urn design introduced in
Zhao 2015
.

Giovagnoli 2017
provided an overview and extension of Bayesian optimal designs for binary responses and showed that the allocated proportion under the
A
-optimal Bayesian RAR procedure (using the allocation probability that minimizes a utility function describing the variance of the treatment effect estimator) converges to the proportion in
Neyman 1934
, and is hence a power-maximizing RAR procedure.

An often overlooked aspect in the literature of power-maximizing RAR procedures is the finite-sample type I error rate.
Baldi Antognini et al. 2018
indicated for general RAR procedures that, although asymptotically the type I error rate is controlled by the classical Wald test, its inferential properties are compromised for smaller sample sizes.
Pin et al. 2025c
showed that for a specific RAR procedure targeting the proportions in
Neyman 1934
, the type I error rate can become as high as 82.2% for a trial size of 50 participants.
Such a degree of type I error rate inflation is detrimental from a regulatory perspective, as type I error rate control is a minimal and first-line requirement for any clinical trial.
To control the type I error rate inflation,
Pin et al. 2025c
derived two novel optimal allocation proportions based on more robust score tests instead of the (unpooled) Wald test. They showed through simulations that these novel target proportions led to a better type I error rate control.

In the current paper, we evaluate approaches with finite-sample guarantees for two-arm clinical trials with binary outcomes. In particular, we investigate two broad classes of alternative solutions to control the type I error rate for power-maximizing RAR procedures.
First, we investigate the use of alternative statistical tests. As part of this strategy, we consider conditional and unconditional exact tests for (power-maximizing) RAR designs.
Exact tests are statistical tests ensuring a (finite-sample) type I error rate bounded by a nominal significance level. Conditional exact tests provide this guarantee for several different reference sets (e.g., all sets of trial realizations with a given total number of successes), while unconditional tests only provide this guarantee when the reference set just contains all trial realizations.
We construct exact tests through the network algorithm introduced for randomization tests in
Mehta et al. 1988
and first applied to construct exact tests (based on the population model) for RAR procedures in
Wei et al. 1990
. We use the implementation of the network algorithm in
Baas et al. 2025b
, shown to be computationally tractable for trials with up to
1,000
participants, where previously the computational limit was around 100 participants.
As part of this strategy, we develop and introduce a novel generalization of Boschloo’s exact test
(
Boschloo 1970
)
for data collected using an RAR procedure. This exact test increases the unconditional power of the conditional exact test, which was often shown to already yield highest power out of all comparators for RAR designs in
Baas et al. 2025b
(where only conditional and unconditional exact tests were considered).

Our second solution approach is to consider the
constrained Markov decision process
(CMDP) approach introduced in
Baas et al. 2025a
to construct novel (average) power-maximizing RAR procedures that control the type I error rate both pointwise and on average. Such RAR procedures are novel, as
Baas et al. 2025a
only considered CMDP procedures maximizing expected treatment outcomes under constraints on the average power and average type I error rate. In this work, we combine this approach with the exact testing strategy to balance power and type I error trade-offs.
It is hypothesized that the use of exact tests for power-maximizing CMDP designs leads to a lower degree of power deflation than when they are applied to traditional power-maximizing procedures. This is because the CMDP designs ensure that the type I error rate will already be close to the nominal level under the asymptotic Wald test, hence the performance will be similar under exact tests.
While
Baas et al. 2025c
show that tests optimizing an unweighted average power measure can provide almost uniform power gains over standard tests in equal allocation designs, we note that, similar to the approach in
Baas et al. 2025c
, informative priors (or weighted averages) can be used to tune the performance of the test to the specific trial parameters deemed realistic for a given trial application.

The paper is structured as follows:
Section 2
introduces the considered clinical trial model, exact statistical tests (including our generalized version of Boschloo’s test in
Section 2.2.2
), and the operating characteristics.
Section 3
lists the (power-maximizing) RAR procedures considered in the paper.

Section 4
provides a numerical comparison of the considered RAR procedures and exact tests, as well as results from the randomization test.
Section 5
concludes the paper and provides topics of future research.

Exact tests for a two-arm response-adaptive design with binary outcomes

2.1
Model: Two-arm response-adaptive design with binary outcomes

In this section, we describe the mathematical model underlying our numerical results.
Consider a clinical trial comparing two treatments with unknown outcome distributions: a control (C) treatment and a developmental (D, i.e., experimental) treatment. Participants are enrolled sequentially and assigned to one of the treatments using a
response-adaptive randomization
(RAR) procedure. This procedure updates the allocation probabilities based on the current trial history. After each participant is assigned a treatment, a binary outcome –sampled from a Bernoulli distribution– is observed and incorporated into the trial history before assigning the next participant.

We now formalize this trial description. Let
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}=(\theta_{\text{C}},\theta_{\text{D}})\in(0,1)^{2}

be a tuple containing (unknown) success probabilities. Throughout this paper, the same ordering convention (i.e., first C then D) will be used when forming tuples from variables for the control and developmental treatment.
For a fixed trial size of
n\in\mathbb{N}
participants, define
\bm{Y}_{a}=(Y_{a,1},Y_{a,1},\dots,Y_{a,n})

to be a sequence of independent Bernoulli random variables for each treatment arm
a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}
, hence, independently,
\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(Y_{a,t}=1)=\theta_{a}
for
a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}
. Each
Y_{a,t}
denotes a potential outcome for trial participant
t
under treatment
a
.
The set of possible trial histories of treatment allocations and outcomes is defined as
\mathcal{H}=\bigcup_{t=0}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{t}
where
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{()\}
only contains the empty tuple and

\mathcal{H}_{t}=\{(a_{1},y_{1},a_{2},y_{2},\dots,a_{t},y_{t}):y_{w}\in\{0,1\},\,a_{w}\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\},\;\forall w\leq t\}

denotes the set of potential trial histories up to participant
t.

An RAR procedure
\pi:\mathcal{H}\mapsto[0,1]
is a mapping, taking as input the available trial history and producing as output the probability that the next participant is allocated to treatment C. It is assumed that the trial is fully sequential, i.e., trial participants are allocated to treatment one after another, and the corresponding treatment outcome for each participant is observed before the next participant needs to be allocated. Letting
\bm{H}_{0}=()
, we recursively define the trial history as

\bm{H}_{t}=(A_{1},Y_{A_{1},1},A_{2},Y_{A_{2},2},\dots,A_{t},Y_{A_{t},t})
, where the distribution of each (random)
allocation
A_{t}\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}
is defined according to
\mathbb{P}(A_{t}=\text{C}\mid\bm{H}_{t-1})=\pi(\bm{H}_{t-1})
for
t=1,\dots,n
. We denote the probability distribution over trial histories under parameter vector
\mathbf{\theta}
and RAR procedure
\pi
by
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}
.

Let
S_{a,t}
denote the (random) number of successes and
N_{a,t}
denote the (random) number of allocations for treatment arm
a
after allocating participant
t
, i.e., for all
a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\},\;t\in\{1,\dots,n\}

S_{a,t}=\sum_{u=1}^{t}Y_{a,u}\mathbb{I}(A_{u}=a),\quad N_{a,t}=\sum_{u=1}^{t}\mathbb{I}(A_{u}=a),

where
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function.
We now introduce a variable representing the trial
state,
\bm{X}_{t}=(\bm{S}_{t},\bm{N}_{t})
. which summarizes the trial information through the successes and allocations for each arm up to allocating participant
t
.
Letting
\bm{X}_{0}=((0,0),(0,0))
be the initial state, the state space for
\bm{X}=(\bm{X}_{t})_{t=0}^{n}
equals
\mathcal{X}=\cup_{t=0}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{t}
where for all
t
we define:

\mathcal{X}_{t}=\{((s^{\prime}_{\text{C}},s^{\prime}_{\text{D}}),(n^{\prime}_{\text{C}},n^{\prime}_{\text{D}})):\bm{s}^{\prime},\bm{n}^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,t\}^{2},\,\bm{s}^{\prime}\leq\bm{n},\;\textstyle\sum_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}n_{a}=t\}.

By, e.g.,
Yi 2013
, it follows that
for all states
\bm{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}_{t}

\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}(\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t})=g_{t}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{t})\prod_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}\theta_{a}^{s_{a}(\bm{x}_{t})}(1-\theta_{a})^{n_{a}(\bm{x}_{t})-s_{a}(\bm{x}_{t})},

(1)

where
n_{a}
,
s_{a}
record the treatment group sizes and successes encoded in
\bm{x}_{t}
. The coefficients
g_{t}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{t})
, representing the sum of allocation path probabilities leading up to a state
\bm{x}_{t}
, can be calculated using a network algorithm
(
Mehta et al. 1988
)
. An important thing of note is that network algorithms usually compute the probabilities (
1
), whereas
Baas et al. 2025b
uses it to compute the parameter-free component
g_{t}^{\pi}
.

Baas et al. 2025b
additionally provided a computationally efficient algorithm to compute
g_{t}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{t})
for trial sizes up to
1,000
participants.

2.2
Asymptotic and exact Wald tests

We consider conditional and unconditional exact tests for testing

H_{0}:\theta_{\text{C}}=\theta_{\text{D}}\text{ versus }H_{1}:\theta_{\text{C}}\neq\theta_{\text{D}}

using the Wald statistic
\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})
which, assuming
\min_{a}n_{a}(\bm{X}_{n})>0
, is defined as:

\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})=\begin{cases}\frac{\hat{\theta}_{\text{D}}(\bm{X}_{n})-\hat{\theta}_{\text{C}}(\bm{X}_{n})}{\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\theta}_{\text{C}}(\bm{X}_{n})(1-\hat{\theta}_{\text{C}}(\bm{X}_{n}))}{{n}_{\text{C}}(\bm{X}_{n})}+\frac{\hat{\theta}_{\text{D}}(\bm{X}_{n})(1-\hat{\theta}_{\text{D}}(\bm{X}_{n}))}{{n}_{\text{D}}(\bm{X}_{n})}}},&\text{if~$\exists a\;\text{s.t.}\;\hat{\theta}_{a}(\bm{X}_{n})\in(0,1),$}\\
\text{sign}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{D}}(\bm{X}_{n})-\hat{\theta}_{\text{C}}(\bm{X}_{n}))\cdot\infty,&\text{else},\end{cases}

(2)

where
\hat{\theta}_{a}(\bm{X}_{n})=s_{a}(\bm{X}_{n})/{n}_{a}(\bm{X}_{n})
for all
a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}
and we use the convention
0\cdot\infty=0
.

Yi and Wang 2011
showed that, under several specific conditions for the RAR procedure, the restricted maximum likelihood estimator under
H_{0}
exists and satisfies a central limit theorem, in which case T
(\bm{X}_{n})
converges to a standard normal random variable. This result asymptotically justifies the two-sided asymptotic Wald test that rejects
H_{0}
when
|\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})|\geq z_{1-\alpha/2}
for
z_{1-\alpha/2}
the inverse cumulative standard normal distribution function
at
1-\alpha/2

and a nominal significance level
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

Remark 1

(Burn-in period)
.

In (
2
) it is assumed that
\min_{a}n_{a}(\bm{X}_{n})>0
, and, in the current paper, this is enforced through a burn-in period in which
b
participants are allocated treatment in a way such
that
N_{\text{C},2b}=N_{\text{D},2b}=b.

Several allocation methods can be used to ensure a balanced assignment of participants to treatment arms during the burn-in period. In practice, this choice is particularly important when the burn-in length is small, as it is then crucial to minimize the probability of treatment imbalances.
Commonly used procedures in this setting include the truncated binomial design, big stick design, permuted block design, and the random allocation rule, each with its own benefits and challenges
(
Berger et al. 2021
)
.
Despite the practical relevance of choosing an appropriate burn-in allocation method, it is important to note that, for our evaluation, the specific procedure used does not impact our results.
Under the model of
Section 2
, the distribution of
\bm{X}_{n}
is invariant to the specific allocation procedure used during the burn-in phase, as long as balanced treatment group sizes are achieved. Hence, we will not make an assumption on the procedure used to enforce balanced allocation during the burn-in period.

2.2.1
Conditional and unconditional exact tests

This section describes conditional and unconditional exact tests for clinical trials employing an RAR procedure. Such tests were, to the best of our knowledge, first considered in
Wei et al. 1990
. The conditional exact test we consider is not based on the treatment group sizes and hence coincides with the test suggested by a reviewer of
Begg 1990
.

We first describe the conditional exact test for RAR designs based on total successes.
For equal allocation, the conditional exact test reduces to Fisher’s exact test
(
Fisher 1934
)
.
In this test, the nuisance parameter
\theta=\theta_{\text{C}}=\theta_{\text{D}}
under the null hypothesis is eliminated by conditioning on the total sum of successes in the trial
s(\bm{X}_{n})=\sum_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}s_{a}(\bm{X}_{n})
.
Following, e.g.,
Baas et al. 2025b
, for
\mathbf{\theta}
such that
\theta_{\text{C}}=\theta_{\text{D}}
:

\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}(\bm{X}_{n}=\bm{x}_{n}\mid s(\bm{X}_{n})=s^{\prime})=\binom{n}{s^{\prime}}^{-1}g_{{n}}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{n})\cdot\mathbb{I}(s(\bm{x}_{n})=s^{\prime}),

(3)

as the right-hand side above does not depend on
\mathbf{\theta}
, one can calculate the left and right conditional two-sided critical values (both of level
\alpha/2
) based on
\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})
given
s(\bm{X}_{n})=s^{\prime}
, denoted
\bar{c}(s^{\prime})
and
\underaccent{\bar}{c}(s^{\prime})
, respectively. The resulting test is conditionally exact for each total sum of successes, i.e., for all possible total sums of successes
s^{\prime}
we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})\notin(\underaccent{\bar}{c}(s^{\prime}),\bar{c}(s^{\prime}))\mid s(\bm{X}_{n})=s^{\prime})\leq\alpha.

Next, we consider the unconditional exact test for RAR designs, extending Barnard’s unconditional exact test
(
Barnard 1945
)
. In comparison to the conditional exact test, this test only uses two critical values, ensuring a maximum unconditional type I error rate below the nominal significance level
\alpha
, i.e., it determines the upper critical value
\bar{c}
such that

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}\in[0,1]^{2},\\
\theta_{\text{C}}=\theta_{\text{D}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\bm{x}_{n}:\,\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}_{n})\geq\bar{c}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}(\bm{X}_{n}=\bm{x}_{n})\leq\alpha/2.

The lower critical value
\underaccent{\bar}{c}
of the unconditional exact test is defined similarly using the left tail of the distribution.
An efficient algorithm to compute
\bar{c}
and
\underaccent{\bar}{c}
with a desired precision is provided in
Baas et al. 2025b
. The resulting test is unconditionally exact, meaning
\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}(\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})\notin(\underaccent{\bar}{c},\bar{c}))\leq\alpha
for all
\mathbf{\theta}
such that
\theta_{\text{C}}=\theta_{\text{D}}
, but not necessarily conditionally exact based on the total sum of successes, i.e., it may be that for some
s^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{\theta}
such that
\theta_{\text{C}}=\theta_{\text{D}}
we have
\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}(\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})\notin(\underaccent{\bar}{c},\bar{c})\mid s(\bm{X}_{n})=s^{\prime})>\alpha
.

2.2.2
Generalized Boschloo test

The generalized version of Boschloo’s test
uses, in the vein of
(
Boschloo 1970
)
, the p-values corresponding to the conditional exact Wald test as test statistics in the unconditional exact test to raise the unconditional maximum type I error rate to
\alpha
.
Unlike Boschloo’s test, the generalized version of Boschloo’s test depends on a test statistic, as we only condition on the sum of successes. Following (
3
), the conditional p-value for the Wald test based on the sum of total successes is given by

\mathrm{T}_{\text{GB}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n}:s(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n})=s(\bm{x}_{n}),\\
|\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n})|\geq|\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}_{n})|\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{s(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n})}^{-1}g^{\pi}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n}).

In the generalized version of Boschloo’s test, these p-values are used as a test statistic in the one-sided unconditional exact test, where we only reject if
\mathrm{T}_{\text{GB}}
(i.e., the p-value) is low.

Let
\varphi(((s^{\prime}_{\text{C}},s^{\prime}_{\text{D}}),(n^{\prime}_{\text{C}},n^{\prime}_{\text{D}}))=((s^{\prime}_{\text{D}},s^{\prime}_{\text{C}}),(n^{\prime}_{\text{D}},n^{\prime}_{\text{C}}))
swap the successes and treatment group sizes per arm for
all
((s^{\prime}_{\text{C}},s^{\prime}_{\text{D}}),(n^{\prime}_{\text{C}},n^{\prime}_{\text{D}}))\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
.
Let
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
be a antisymmetric test statistic if-and-only-if
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}(\varphi(\bm{x}_{n}))=-\mathrm{T}^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{n})
for
all
\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, it can be verified that the Wald statistic
\mathrm{T}
as defined in (
2
) satisfies this property.
Let
\pi
be a symmetric RAR procedure
when
g^{\pi}_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})=g^{\pi}_{n}(\varphi(\bm{x}_{n}))
for all
\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
.

The next lemma provides conditions under which the generalized version of Boschloo’s test is uniformly more powerful (unconditionally) than the conditional exact test.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\underaccent{\bar}{c}_{\mathrm{GB}}
be the (left) unconditional exact critical value for significance level
\alpha
based on
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{GB}}
.
If
\pi
is a symmetric RAR procedure, then:

\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})\geq\bar{c}(s(\bm{X}_{n}))\text{ or }\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})\leq\underaccent{\bar}{c}(s(\bm{X}_{n}))\implies\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{GB}}(\bm{X}_{n})\leq\underaccent{\bar}{c}_{\mathrm{GB}}.

Proof.

We first show that the conditional Wald test coincides with the test rejecting when T
{}_{\text{GB}}\leq\alpha
(which holds if
\pi
is symmetric). As
s(\bm{x}_{n})=s(\varphi(\bm{x}_{n}))
, we have that for every total sum of successes
s^{\prime}
, by the properties of
\mathrm{T}
and
\pi
:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}_{n}:s(\bm{x}_{n})=s^{\prime},\\
\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}_{n})\geq c\end{subarray}}g_{{n}}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}_{n}:s(\bm{x}_{n})=s^{\prime},\\
-\mathrm{T}(\varphi(\bm{x}_{n}))\geq c\end{subarray}}g_{{n}}^{\pi}(\varphi(\bm{x}_{n}))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n}:s(\bm{x}_{n}^{\prime})=s^{\prime},\\
-\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n})\geq c\end{subarray}}g_{{n}}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{n}^{\prime}).

Hence
\underaccent{\bar}{c}(s^{\prime})=-\bar{c}(s^{\prime})
as both critical values are based on a level
\alpha/2
, hence the conditional exact test rejects if-and-only-if
|\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}_{n})|\geq\bar{c}(s(\bm{x}_{n})),
i.e.,

\displaystyle|\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}_{n})|\geq\bar{c}(s(\bm{x}_{n}))\iff\mathrm{T}_{\text{GB}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n}:s(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n})=s(\bm{x}_{n}),\\
|\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n})|\geq|\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}_{n})|\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{s(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n})}^{-1}g^{\pi}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{n})\leq\alpha.

As the test rejecting when
\mathrm{T}_{\text{GB}}\leq\alpha
equals the conditional exact test, we have
\underaccent{\bar}{c}_{\text{GB}}\geq\alpha
and hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})\geq\bar{c}(s(\bm{X}_{n}))\text{ or }\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})\leq\underaccent{\bar}{c}(s(\bm{X}_{n})\iff

\displaystyle|\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})|\geq\bar{c}(s(\bm{X}_{n}))

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}_{\text{GB}}(\bm{X}_{n})\leq\underaccent{\bar}{c}_{\mathrm{GB}}.

∎

2.3
Operating characteristics and their exact calculation

The likelihood (
1
) allows for the calculation of exact operating characteristics, such as the power or type I error rate.
Any operating characteristic can be written as
\mathbb{E}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}[f(\bm{X}_{n})]
for a function
f:\mathcal{X}_{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
.
The operating characteristics considered in the current paper will be:

•

Rejection rate (type I error rate or power)
:

This operating characteristic is calculated as
\mathbb{E}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}[f(\bm{X}_{n})]
where

f(\bm{x}_{n})=\mathbb{I}\Big(\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}_{n})\leq\underaccent{\bar}{c}(\bm{x}_{n})\text{ or }\mathrm{T}(\bm{x}_{n})\geq\bar{c}(\bm{x}_{n})\Big)

for a pair of critical values
\underaccent{\bar}{c},\bar{c}
, which may depend on
\bm{x}_{n}
through a summary of
\bm{x}_{n}
(e.g., the total sum of successes in the conditional exact test).
If
\mathbf{\theta}
satisfies
H_{0}
, the rejection rate equals the type I error rate, whereas the rejection rate equals statistical power when
\theta_{\text{C}}\neq\theta_{\text{D}}
. For a nominal significance level
\alpha
, it is desired to have the type I error rate bounded by
\alpha
, while higher power is better.

•

Patient benefit (expected
proportion of allocations on the superior arm)
:

This operating characteristic equals

\left[\sum_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}[n_{a}(\bm{X}_{n})/n]\mathbb{I}(\theta_{a}=\max_{a}\theta_{a})\right]-1/2\mathbb{I}(\theta_{\text{C}}=\theta_{\text{D}}).

The patient benefit represents the proportion of participants on the superior arm. Other measures, such as the expected number of successes
\mathbb{E}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}[s(\bm{X}_{n})]
could also be used to denote patient benefit, but an advantage of using the proportion is that we should aim to have a patient benefit higher than an absolute value of
50\%
.

We have from the likelihood (
1
) that

\mathbb{E}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}[f(\bm{X}_{n})]=\sum_{\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}f(\bm{x}_{n})g^{\pi}_{n}(\bm{x}_{n})\prod_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}\theta_{a}^{s_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})}(1-\theta_{a})^{n_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})-s_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})},

(4)

which can be written as
(\bm{f}\circ\bm{g}_{n}^{\pi})^{\top}\bm{p}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}
with
\circ
the Hadamard product and
\bm{p}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}
containing the product-terms in the above expression. Hence, we can
store
\bm{f}\circ\bm{g}_{n}^{\pi}
and then only have to take the inner product with
\bm{p}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}
for different vectors
\mathbf{\theta}
when we want to calculate (
4
) for different parameters
\mathbf{\theta}
.

3
Response-adaptive randomization procedures

In this section, we list the power-maximizing RAR procedures considered in this paper, which are RAR procedures that maximize a metric related to power.
We will first list response-adaptive procedures known from the literature (including modifications to protect patient benefit), after which we introduce our considered novel power-maximizing
constrained Markov decision process
(CMDP) procedures.

3.1
Response-adaptive randomization procedures known from the literature

•

DBCD Neyman allocation
:

Under Neyman allocation
(
Neyman 1934
)
the expected proportion of participants randomized to the control group equals

\displaystyle\rho(\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$})=\frac{\sqrt{\theta_{\text{C}}(1-\theta_{\text{C}})}}{\sqrt{\theta_{\text{C}}(1-\theta_{\text{C}})}+\sqrt{\theta_{\text{D}}(1-\theta_{\text{D}})}}.

(5)

As indicated in
Hu and Rosenberger 2003
;
Rosenberger and Hu 2004
, the allocation proportion (
5
) maximizes the (asymptotic) power function for the asymptotic Wald test described at the start of
Section 2.2
.
The parameters
\mathbf{\theta}
are unknown, and in this paper the proportion (
5
) is targeted through a
doubly adaptive biased coin design

(
Eisele 1994
;
Hu and Rosenberger 2006
, DBCD,)
, based on the sequential estimation procedure for
\theta_{a}
in Example 2 of
Hu et al. 2009
, which is a flexible targeting procedure which, in comparison to, e.g., efficient randomized-adaptive designs
(
Hu et al. 2009
)
, satisfies the property that the randomization probability to control converges almost surely to (
5
), resulting in stability for large samples. This power-maximizing RAR procedure is referred to as DBCD Neyman allocation. Examples of other RAR procedures to target optimal proportions, such as
(
5
)

are the efficient randomized design
(
Hu et al. 2009
)
and the mass-weighted urn design
(
Zhao 2015
)
.

•

Tempered DBCD Neyman allocation
:
Let
\pi_{\text{NA}}(\bm{x}_{t})
be the probability of randomizing a participant to the control group based on a state
\bm{x}_{t}
under DBCD Neyman allocation.
Depending on
\bm{x}_{t}
it can be the case that
\pi_{\text{NA}}(\bm{x}_{t})>0.5
while
\tilde{\theta}_{\text{C}}(\bm{x}_{t})<\tilde{\theta}_{\text{D}}(\bm{x}_{t})
or
\pi_{\text{NA}}(\bm{x}_{t})<0.5
while
\tilde{\theta}_{\text{C}}(\bm{x}_{t})>\tilde{\theta}_{\text{D}}(\bm{x}_{t})
, i.e., the probability of allocating to the inferior treatment (based on the estimates
\tilde{\theta}_{a}(\bm{x}_{t})
) is higher than 50%. In
Zhang and Rosenberger 2005
, this problem was identified in a different setting, where it was suggested to let the target allocation proportion be
50\%
in this case, i.e., in the tempered DBCD Neyman allocation procedure, we have

\pi_{\text{TNA}}(\bm{x}_{t})=\begin{cases}\pi_{\text{NA}}(\bm{x}_{t}),\quad&\text{if~$\pi_{\text{NA}}(\bm{x}_{t})>0.5\text{ and }\tilde{\theta}_{\text{C}}(\bm{x}_{t})>\tilde{\theta}_{\text{D}}(\bm{x}_{t})$}\text{ or }\\
&\text{if~$\pi_{\text{NA}}(\bm{x}_{t})<0.5\text{ and }\tilde{\theta}_{\text{D}}(\bm{x}_{t})>\tilde{\theta}_{\text{C}}(\bm{x}_{t})$,}\\
1/2,&\text{else.}\end{cases}

The tempered DBCD Neyman allocation procedure is hence a power-maximizing RAR procedure under the constraint that the allocation probability does not lead to more participants being allocated inferior treatment on average.

•

Equal allocation
: In case of equal allocation, assuming
n
is even, we have:

g_{n}^{\pi_{\text{EA}}}(\bm{x}_{n})=\binom{n_{\text{C}}(\bm{x}_{n})}{s_{\text{C}}(\bm{x}_{n})}\binom{n_{\text{D}}(\bm{x}_{n})}{s_{\text{D}}(\bm{x}_{n})}\mathbb{I}(n_{\text{C}}(\bm{x}_{n})/n=n_{\text{D}}(\bm{x}_{n})/n=1/2),

i.e., the function
g_{n}^{\pi_{\text{EA}}}
concentrates on states where the proportion of participants allocated to either treatment group is 1/2, and equals the product of the number of possible outcome paths length
n/2
having
s_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})
successes for each arm
a
. Similar as for the burn-in period, the actual procedure
\pi_{\text{EA}}
corresponding to
g^{\pi_{\text{EA}}}
is irrelevant for the analysis at hand (as we only look at the distribution over final states), and can, e.g, correspond to a truncated binomial, big stick, or permuted block design
(
Berger et al. 2021
)
.
As shown in
Azriel et al. 2012
, equal allocation is a power-maximizing randomization procedure under the Pitman asymptotic testing framework.

•

Bayesian RAR
: We consider a Bayesian RAR design following
Thompson 1933
;
Thall and Wathen 2007
.
For a prior probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
on
[0,1]^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle{\pi}_{\text{B}}(\bm{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{Q}(\theta_{\text{C}}\geq\theta_{\text{D}}\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t})^{t/(2n)}}{\mathbb{Q}(\theta_{\text{C}}\geq\theta_{\text{D}}\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t})^{t/(2n)}+\mathbb{Q}(\theta_{\text{C}}<\theta_{\text{D}}\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t})^{t/(2n)}}.

Hence, the probability
\pi_{\text{B}}(\bm{x}_{n})
equals the power-tuned posterior probability that the control treatment is better, based on the prior distribution
\mathbb{Q}
(usually the uniform distribution for
\mathbf{\theta}
). This randomization procedure targets high expected treatment outcomes. The probabilities
\mathbb{Q}(\theta_{\text{C}}\geq\theta_{\text{D}}\mid\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t})
can be calculated efficiently based on Algorithm 1 in
Kaddaj et al. 2025
.
Bayesian RAR is not a power-maximizing RAR procedure, and is included as a comparator RAR procedure as it is commonly applied in RAR trials
(
Pin et al. 2025b
)
.

3.2
Constrained Markov decision process procedures

For a prior probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
for
\mathbf{\theta}
with support
[0,1]^{2}
, let a Bayesian average operating characteristic equal
\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}[f(\bm{X}_{n})]\mathbb{Q}(d\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$})
.
The goal of the
constrained Markov decision process
(CMDP) approach
(
Baas et al. 2025a
)
is to find an RAR procedure that sequentially randomizes participants to treatment to maximize a Bayesian average operating characteristic, while ensuring control of other Bayesian average operating characteristics. For a fixed maximum randomized allocation rate
p\in[0.5,1]
the randomization probabilities
\pi(\bm{x}_{t})
are restricted to the set
\{1-p,1/2,p\}
, e.g., when
p=0.95
participants can only be assigned to a treatment with probability
5\%
,
50\%
or
95\%
.
In
Baas et al. 2025a
, CMDP procedures are found through an optimization approach that uses a cutting plane algorithm applying backward recursion in every iteration.

The CMDP procedures constructed in this paper are novel as
Baas et al. 2025a
(i) only considered maximizing expected treatment outcomes and did not consider maximizing power, (ii) did not have point-wise constraints (i.e., constraints for specific parameter values), and (iii) used an uncorrected version of Fisher’s exact test to analyze the data, whereas in this paper exact Wald tests are constructed based on CMDP procedures.

The novel
CMDP-P
(CMDP-power) procedure targets maximum Bayesian average power (hence it is a power-maximizing RAR procedure), while the constraints of the procedure relate to type I error rate control. A CMDP-P procedure is an approximately optimal policy
(found through the procedure in
Baas et al. 2025a
, Section 3.3)
for the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{\pi}{\max}\,\mathbb{P}_{1}^{\pi}\left(|\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})|\geq z_{1-\alpha/2}\right)

(6a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{0}(|\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})|\geq z_{1-\alpha/2})\leq\alpha^{\prime},

(6b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}_{0}}(|\mathrm{T}(\bm{X}_{n})|\geq z_{1-\alpha/2})\leq\alpha^{\prime\prime},\quad\forall\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}_{0}\in\hat{\Theta}_{0}.

(6c)

In the above,
\hat{\Theta}_{0}\subset\{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}_{0}\in[0,1]^{2}:\theta_{0,\text{C}}=\theta_{0,\text{D}}\}=:\Theta_{0}
has finite cardinality and, following
Equation 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{1}(\bm{X}_{n}=\bm{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\hskip-8.53581pt\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}(\bm{X}_{n}=\bm{x}_{n})d\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}=g_{n}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{n})\prod_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}\hskip-8.53581pt\text{B}(\tilde{s}_{a}(\bm{x}_{n}),\tilde{f}_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})),

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{0}(\bm{X}_{n}=\bm{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{P}_{(\theta,\theta)}^{\pi}(\bm{X}_{n}=\bm{x}_{n})d\theta=g_{n}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{n})\text{B}(\tilde{s}(\bm{x}_{n}),\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{n})),

(8)

where
\tilde{s}_{a}=s_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})+1,\tilde{f}_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})=n_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})-s_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})+1
equal one plus the number of treatment successes and failures in arm
a
,
\tilde{s}(\bm{x}_{n})=1+\sum_{a}s_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})
,
\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{n})=1+\sum_{a}f_{a}(\bm{x}_{n})
equal one plus the total amount of successes and failures, and
B
denotes the beta function.

The objective function in (
6a
) equals the Bayesian average power of the Wald test under the uniform prior
\mathbb{Q}
on
[0,1]^{2}
, which assumes all success rates to be equally likely a priori.
The measure represents power as the null hypothesis
H_{0}
has zero measure under the integral in (
7
). The constraint (
6b
) enforces the Bayesian average type I error rate over the set
\Theta_{0}
, assuming a uniform prior, to be less than
\alpha^{\prime}
, where it is recommended to set
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
. The constraint (
6c
) ensures point-wise type I error rate control for all
points
\theta_{0}\in\hat{\Theta}_{0},
where it is necessary that
\alpha^{\prime\prime}\leq\alpha.

A novelty in the current paper, not considered in
Baas et al. 2025a
, is that the set of RAR procedures
\pi
that we optimize over is restricted to RAR procedures with a burn-in period
b.
This is enforced through a restriction of the action space for the first
2b
decision epochs.
We note that in practical situations the priors used in (
6
) as well as the values in
\hat{\Theta}_{0}
(used in (
6c
))
may also be based on historical information about the treatment success rates.

In order to protect patient benefit in the CMDP framework, we define the
CMDP-BP
(CMDP-benefit-and-power) procedure.
The CMDP-BP procedure has the same objective and constraints as the CMDP-P procedure (and is hence a power-maximizing RAR procedure), with the additional constraint that the average patient benefit should at least be higher than 0.5 for several subsets of the parameter range.
Choosing lower and upper interval
endpoints
\ell_{a,\sigma}\leq u_{a,\sigma}\in[0,1]
for all
a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\},\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that we
have
[\ell_{\text{C},\sigma},u_{\text{C},\sigma}]\cap[\ell_{\text{D},\sigma},u_{\text{D},\sigma}]=\emptyset
,
defining
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\sigma}(\bm{X}_{n}=\bm{x}_{n})
as the Bayesian average probability of observing
\bm{x}_{n}
under an uniform prior
over
[\ell_{\text{C},\sigma},u_{\text{C},\sigma}]\times[\ell_{\text{D},\sigma},u_{\text{D},\sigma}]
,
and letting
F_{\text{B}(\tilde{s},\tilde{f})}
denote the cumulative Beta distribution function for parameters
\tilde{s},\tilde{f}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\sigma}(\bm{X}_{n}=\bm{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\ell_{\text{D},\sigma}}^{u_{\text{D},\sigma}}\int_{\ell_{\text{C},\sigma}}^{u_{\text{C},\sigma}}\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}}^{\pi}(\bm{X}_{n}=\bm{x}_{n})d\theta_{\text{C}}d\theta_{\text{D}}}{\prod_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}(u_{a,\sigma}-\ell_{a,\sigma})}

\displaystyle=g_{n}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{n})\prod_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}\frac{F_{\text{B}(\tilde{s}_{a}(\bm{x}_{n}),\tilde{f}_{a}(\bm{x}_{n}))}(u_{a,\sigma})-F_{\text{B}(\tilde{s}_{a}(\bm{x}_{n}),\tilde{f}_{a}(\bm{x}_{n}))}(\ell_{a,\sigma})}{(u_{a,\sigma}-\ell_{a,\sigma})/\text{B}(\tilde{s}_{a}(\bm{x}_{n}),\tilde{f}_{a}(\bm{x}_{n}))},

A CMDP-BP procedure is an approximately optimal policy for the optimization problem, obtained by adding the following constraint to (
6
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\sigma}[N_{\arg\max_{a\in\{\text{C},\text{D}\}}\theta_{a}}/n]\geq 1/2,\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{S}.

Note that the restriction
[\ell_{\text{C},\sigma},u_{\text{C},\sigma}]\cap[\ell_{\text{D},\sigma},u_{\text{D},\sigma}]=\emptyset
enforces that one of the arms always has a higher success rate in every rectangle considered.

4.1
Response-adaptive randomization targeting optimal proportions

We first describe the results for (more traditional) RAR procedures based on targeting an optimal proportion (either in terms of power or expected outcomes), which are the DBCD Neyman allocation, tempered DBCD Neyman allocation, and Bayesian RAR procedures.

4.1.1
DBCD Neyman allocation: Type I error rates

The top row of
Figure 1
shows type I error rate profiles under the different tests for DBCD Neyman allocation (
Section 3.1
). The asymptotic Wald test shows substantial type I error rate inflation under DBCD Neyman allocation for null success rates close to the endpoints of the parameter range, reaching values around 15% for both considered trial sizes.
Figure 3
shows a potential cause for this behavior: the variance in the allocation proportions is higher under large and low success rates, causing high variance of the test statistic under the null hypothesis. This could be explained by the higher sensitivity of the target proportion to small estimated treatment effects for these parameters, combined with an increased variance through employing a RAR procedure. For instance, for
\hat{\theta}_{\text{C}}=0.50,\;\hat{\theta}_{\text{D}}=0.52
we have
\rho(\hat{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}})=0.50
whereas for
\hat{\theta}_{\text{C}}=0.97,\;\hat{\theta}_{\text{D}}=0.99
we have
\rho(\hat{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\theta}$}})=0.63
.

The unconditional exact test, which adjusts the critical values based on the type I error rate function for the asymptotic test, shows a similar type I error rate profile shape as the asymptotic test and is conservative for null success rates around 50%. On the contrary, the conditional exact and the generalized version of Boschloo’s test (henceforth referred to simply as Boschloo’s test) show a less conservative type I error rate profile, which is often close to the significance level, especially for the larger trial horizon
n=250.
A similar result for the conditional exact test was found for RAR procedures targeting high expected treatment outcomes in
Baas et al. 2025b
.

4.1.2
DBCD Neyman allocation: Power

The middle row of

that

,
while each point on the respective curves starting from an open marker corresponds to a parameter vector such that

. When
\theta_{\text{D}}
becomes larger than the point
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}
corresponding to a new open marker, a new curve starts, and the visibility of the old curve is decreased.

As expected, the asymptotic Wald test has higher power under DBCD Neyman allocation than under equal allocation. The unconditional exact test, on the other hand, has roughly uniformly lower power than the unconditional exact test under equal allocation. This brings the added value of using DBCD Neyman allocation to optimize power into question, as the unconditional exact test is often the most powerful exact test under equal allocation
(
Mehrotra et al. 2003
, see also

. The conditional exact test often shows higher power under DBCD Neyman allocation compared to equal allocation, which is likely due to the conservativeness of the conditional exact test (i.e., Fisher’s exact test) for equal allocation. In contrast, Boschloo’s test shows a slightly worse performance in terms of power for DBCD Neyman allocation when compared to equal allocation.

5
Conclusion and discussion

We have investigated solutions for type I error rate inflation under power-maximizing
response-adaptive randomization
(RAR) procedures, which is a problem recently identified in
Pin et al. 2025c
for RAR procedures targeting maximum statistical power. This problem is of particular concern for the use of RAR designs in confirmatory trial settings. In this paper, we focused on two-arm clinical trials with binary outcomes.

As a first step, exact tests such as the conditional exact test (based on total successes) and unconditional exact test, as well as a novel generalized version of Boschloo’s test, were considered in combination with the
doubly adaptive biased coin design
(DBCD) Neyman allocation. This idea alone did not lead to a satisfying result, as the unconditional exact test, which out of all considered exact tests almost uniformly provided highest power under equal allocation, showed a very conservative type I error rate profile under DBCD Neyman allocation, and low power as a result. This is mainly due to the type I error rate profile for the asymptotic Wald test under DBCD Neyman allocation being very peaked. An improvement was found in terms of power for the conditional exact test, but this is somewhat of an empty victory for DBCD Neyman allocation as the conditional exact test for equal allocation coincides with Fisher’s exact test, which is known to be very conservative. For instance, power improvements for the conditional exact test were even (often) seen for the Bayesian RAR procedure solely targeting high expected treatment outcomes.
For DBCD Neyman allocation and Bayesian RAR procedures, our newly developed generalized version of Boschloo’s test often showed highest power.

A second considered step is to directly incorporate the type I error rate constraints when maximizing power by the RAR procedure through the
constrained Markov decision process
(CMDP) framework considered in
Baas et al. 2025a
.
This approach yields uniformly higher power for the unconditional exact Wald test than under the equal allocation design for a trial size of 50 participants, whereas for a trial size of 250 participants, the power gains are smaller and less uniform.
A benefit of the CMDP procedure is that it can be used for any test, not only the Wald test
(e.g.,
Baas et al. 2025a
, considered Fisher’s exact test instead)
. Another benefit is that the extension to multiple treatment arms might be more readily available.

We also considered versions of DBCD Neyman allocation and CMDP
procedures that aimed to protect the expected proportion of participants allocated to the superior arm at a minimum of 50%. As expected, these procedures led to a uniform increase in expected treatment outcomes compared to their non-protected counterparts, but curiously, they also showed comparable or higher power for most considered parameters.

The evaluation in this paper shows that CMDP procedures show higher power than equal allocation for the unconditional exact Wald test under a trial size of 50 participants. For a trial size of 250 participants, the power gains under CMDP were smaller; however, patient benefit values under the CMDP procedure, maximizing power and constraining patient benefit, are often higher than under equal allocation. Hence, we suggest using this design in small to moderate trial sizes when the model of
Section 2
can reasonably be assumed.
In case the model in
Section 2
cannot be assumed, one might use a randomization-based test, and our results show that the CMDP procedures showed lowest power under this test, while DBCD Neyman allocation and equal allocation showed higher power. As tempered DBCD Neyman allocation showed high power values as well as uniform gains in patient benefit over DBCD Neyman allocation and equal allocation, we suggest using tempered DBCD Neyman allocation when using a randomization-based test.

While we aimed for a general comparison of RAR procedures in this paper, a number of specific choices were made, listed at the start of
Section 4
, and these choices could warrant further investigation in specific trial settings. For instance,
Pin et al. 2025c
showed that the type I error rate inflation under DBCD Neyman allocation is exacerbated when decreasing the burn-in value. The best choice of the burn-in length may depend on (a priori beliefs surrounding) the specific trial parameters at hand, and could possibly be determined using the evaluation procedure described in
Tang et al. 2025
. One further thing of note is that we mainly focused on the Wald test for simple differences in this paper. We note that our evaluation can also be directly applied to other treatment effect formulations (e.g., the odds ratio) by changing the DBCD Neyman allocation procedure as explained in
Pin et al. 2024
, and by reformulating the CMDP procedures using the Wald test for the treatment effect formulation of interest. Our conclusions likely do not carry over to multi-arm trials, e.g., in multi-arm settings with fixed control allocation proportions, the type I error rate inflation is likely not as substantial as in the two-arm setting, leaving more room for power gains.

As Algorithm 1 in
Baas et al. 2025a
does not solve the CMDP problem exactly (but often yields a procedure having the desired properties) and can have a relatively long computation time, future research could focus on alternative and more computationally tractable solution methods for the CMDP problem. Another potential future research topic is obtaining exact instead of (Bayesian) average guarantees for CMDP procedures, which could help provide uniform power improvements, even for higher trial sizes.
Future research could also investigate improving the performance of CMDP procedures for randomization-based tests.
An additional topic of future research is modeling the test outcomes as decisions made by the CMDP problem, in an approach similar to the one in
Baas et al. 2025c
, thereby removing the dependence on a specific test known from the literature and improving the average power gains of the CMDP-P procedure.
Lastly, the curves showing the differences in expected proportion of participants allocated to the superior arm for the CMDP procedures did not show an interpretable pattern, and future research could investigate the asymptotic allocation proportions for CMDP procedures. To conclude, we found that the protected CMDP procedure often outperforms equal allocation under the unconditional exact test, while also showing benefits in terms of expected treatment outcomes, especially for small trial sizes (e.g.,
50
participants). For larger trial sizes (around
250
), the power gains are smaller, but several promising future research directions could lead to CMDP procedures outperforming equal allocation in such settings.

Appendix C
Rejection rates for randomization test

Table 7
shows simulation-based unconditional type I error rate and power for the randomization-based Wald test
(
Simon and Simon 2011
)
for several trial sizes and parameter values.
The rejection rates were obtained by sampling a trial realisation from the specific trial design and parameter values, and then re-randomizing the allocations according to the specific designs to determine the critical value of the Wald statistic given the simulated sequence of outcome variables in the trial. The number of simulations was set to 1,000, and the
uncertainty surrounding the estimated rejection rates is indicated by a 95% confidence interval based on a normal approximation. Highest and lowest power values (determined by overlapping confidence intervals) are indicated by overlined bold (green) and underlined bold (red) values, respectively.

As the randomization-based Wald test depends on the way trial participants are sequentially randomized to treatment, an assumption had to be made about the randomization procedure for equal allocation. For this comparison, we use a permuted block design with blocks of 10 for equal allocation and with blocks of size 12 for the burn-in period. We note here that the permuted block design is not the only choice of randomization procedure for equal allocation, and other randomization methods might lead to a lower degree of predictability
(
Berger et al. 2021
)
.

The randomization-based Wald test is an exact test by default; hence, any type I error rate inflation past the 5% level in
Table 7
is due to simulation noise. The randomization-based Wald test shows a relative performance of the designs that looks quite different from the comparison in
Table 1
. Mainly, the CMDP-EP and CMDP-P procedures often give the worst performance in terms of power, while curiously, the tempered DBCD Neyman allocation procedure often gives the best performance in terms of power, followed by the DBCD Neyman allocation and equal allocation procedures. An explanation could be that the CMDP procedures have allocation probabilities in the set
\{0.05,0.5,0.95\}
. Hence, while the CMDP procedures are not deterministic, their paths might concentrate probabilistically, leading to low power under the randomization test.
Instead, DBCD Neyman allocation procedures have a larger range of allocation probabilities, and hence, the aforementioned clustering of allocation paths might not occur for such procedures. The low power for randomization tests under aggressive and close to deterministic RA procedures was already recognized in
Villar et al. 2018
. Comparing the maximum power values under the randomization-based Wald test with the power values in
Table 1
, the randomization-based test gives lower power for
n=50
and small or large control success rates, while roughly giving a similar performance for control success rates closer to
0.5
. For
n=250
, the randomization-based Wald test often gives comparable maximum power to the maximum values in
Table 1
.
