Post‐Radiation Dental Care Access Among Head and Neck Cancer Survivors: A Safety Net Perspective
Authors: Armita Norouzi, Kennedy Johnson, Kevin Avelar, Alyssa Civantos, Alonn Ilan, Megan L Durr, Yogin Patel, Alan Paciorek, Li Zhang, Yasmin Eltawil, Yousef Alqasieer, Kwang Kim, Jymie Graham, Patrick Ha, Jason Chan, Sue S Yom, Lori Ross, Su Li, Shauna Brodie, Mary Jue Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective(s)This study describes dental care access among individuals with head and neck cancer (HNC) at Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital (ZSFG), a large, public, safety‐net hospital that serves a substantial underserved population.MethodsWe identified a convenience sample of participants ≥ 18 years of age through review of routine outpatient HNC surveillance visits. Surveys were translated to Spanish and Simplified Chinese by certified medical interpreters. We used descriptive statistics to summarize findings and logistic regression to analyze predictors of dental care access, represented with odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p‐values. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05.ResultsAmong 41 participants, the majority were male (63%) with a median age of 59 years [interquartile range (IQR) 48–64]. The most represented race/ethnicity was Asian (37%). 63% reported seeing a dentist only annually or less frequently both before and after cancer diagnosis. Prescription fluoride product use was reported by 15% of participants (n = 6). On univariate analysis, a non‐English primary language was significantly associated with lower odds of dental visits at least twice a year pre‐diagnosis (OR 0.19, 95% CI 0.05–0.78; p = 0.021). Participants who reported challenges finding a dentist had lower odds of dental visits at least twice a year post‐diagnosis (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.04–0.77; p = 0.022).ConclusionOur findings suggest that HNC survivors in low‐resource settings may experience limited access to routine dental care. Targeted interventions to support access to dental care in higher‐risk populations may improve adherence to guideline‐recommended care at all stages of cancer treatment.Level of Evidence3Keywords: cancer survivorship, dental care access, dental oncology, head and neck cancer, radiation therapy, safety‐net hospital

Introduction

Surgical resection and radiation therapy (RT), often in conjunction with chemotherapy and more recently immunotherapy, have emerged as the mainstay of head and neck cancer (HNC) treatment [1, 2]. Though a first‐line tool for cancer treatment, RT is associated with a spectrum of oral complications, including mucositis (98%–100% of patients), trismus (17%–44%), xerostomia (57%–78%), dental caries (~49%), and osteoradionecrosis (ORN) (10%–18%) [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. These sequelae of treatment are common and can have devastating effects on patients' daily functioning and quality of life [11]. Therefore, multiple guidelines, including from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), recommend early referral to a dental specialist in the multidisciplinary management of oral health prior to the initiation of RT [12, 13, 14].

The importance of dental care in patients with HNC extends beyond the prevention and management of treatment‐related oral toxicities. Prior studies have demonstrated a significant association between poor oral health indicators and survival among patients with HNC, highlighting the role of oral health in comprehensive cancer care [15, 16]. However, access to dental care in the United States has proven challenging for many patients with HNC, with multiple studies citing the financial burden of evaluation and treatment in the setting of inadequate dental insurance coverage as a leading driver [17]. Several studies examining the completion of pre‐RT dental evaluations in patients with HNC have shown that 33%–81% of patients do not receive all recommended components of dental evaluation and treatment before RT initiation [18, 19]. Though some studies report relatively high rates of pre‐RT dental evaluation, those studies have been largely conducted in higher‐income, college‐educated, and White populations [20].

Barriers to dental care may be particularly pronounced among underserved populations, who already experience disparities in HNC treatment and outcomes. Numerous investigations have demonstrated that underserved populations are more likely to experience inferior HNC treatment outcomes [21, 22, 23]. Specifically, racial minorities, patients in low socioeconomic (SES) brackets, and those with Medicaid or no insurance coverage are more likely to experience delays to post‐operative RT [24]. These challenges occur within a broader context of limited dental care access in the United States. Approximately 57 million Americans were estimated to live in a dental health professional shortage area in 2024 [25]. According to the US Department of Health and Human Services, a significant fraction of the United States population lacks dental coverage, with 29% of all adults and 62% of older adults reported as uninsured [26]. In addition, a 2020 policy brief by the UCLA Center for Health Policy Research found that low‐income adults in California received less timely dental care, and those with Medicaid coverage were less likely to have visited a dentist in the prior 6 months compared to those with private coverage [27].

Despite these well‐documented barriers, little is known about dental care access among underserved patients with HNC receiving treatment in safety‐net healthcare settings. To address this gap, we sought to characterize dental care access among low‐income patients with HNC treated at Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital (ZSFG), a large public safety‐net hospital serving a medically underserved population. Specifically, we evaluate access to dental care before and after diagnosis and treatment, patient‐reported barriers to dental care, oral hygiene practices, and receipt of preventive measures such as prescription‐strength fluoride among patients with HNC at ZSFG.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Following approval from the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) and ZSFG Institutional Review Boards, we conducted a cohort study of adult patients (≥ 18 years) receiving care at an outpatient otolaryngology clinic between October 2024 and November 2025.

Study Setting

ZSFG is a public, safety‐net hospital and trauma center that serves a highly diverse patient population across a spectrum of linguistic, cultural, and socioeconomic backgrounds. Approximately 80% of ZSFG patients are publicly insured through Medicaid and Medicare with roughly 8% reported as uninsured [28]. ZSFG's population includes Latine/x (42%), Asian (20%), White (16%), and Black (13%) patients. Around 40% of ZSFG patients report a primary language other than English [29]. Therefore, our study at ZSFG is uniquely positioned to examine disparities in dental care access among patients with HNC within a safety‐net healthcare system.

Study Participant Eligibility and Recruitment

Eligibility criteria included patients ≥ 18 years of age with a history of HNC treated with RT who had at least one natural tooth. Individuals who were completely edentulous were excluded. Eligible participants were identified through review of otolaryngology head and neck cancer surveillance clinic schedules prior to routine follow‐up visits. Study personnel then performed manual chart review to confirm a history of HNC and prior treatment with RT, based on documentation contained in clinic notes, treatment records, and other clinical documentation within the electronic medical record. For clarity, RT for patients receiving HNC care at ZSFG is typically delivered at UCSF, while other components of care continue at ZSFG. Consents were created at an elementary reading level provided in the patient's primary language via pre‐translated materials or with the use of a telephone or in‐person certified medical interpreter. Interested participants who provided informed consent were enrolled in person or remotely. Participants were compensated for their time and participation with a $25 USD gift card.

Dental Care Access Survey Development

The dental care access survey was created by the study team to collect information on demographics, HNC behavioral risk factors, dental visit frequency before and after HNC diagnosis, oral hygiene practices, presence of oral symptoms, and patient‐reported barriers to dental care and administered at one clinic visit (Supporting Information 1). Participants completed the survey either electronically on a clinic tablet or on paper immediately before or after their regularly scheduled HNC surveillance visit. Survey items were informed by relevant literature and refined through review by clinical experts on the study team.

The survey included questions regarding dental care utilization, oral hygiene practices, oral symptoms, receipt of a pre‐radiation dental evaluation, use of fluoride products, and knowledge of radiation‐related oral health risks. Representative survey items included: “How often did you see a dentist prior to your cancer diagnosis?”, “How often have you seen a dentist since your cancer diagnosis?”, “Did you have a dental evaluation before your first radiation treatment?”, and “Were you told about fluoride treatment after radiation?” Participants were also asked about barriers to obtaining dental care, including lack of dental insurance coverage, cost, lack of time, fear or dislike of the dentist, embarrassment, lack of perceived importance of dental care, and challenges finding a dentist. Survey items included dichotomous response options (yes/no/I don't know), as well as multiple‐choice and multi‐select formats. Formal psychometric validation beyond expert review was not conducted. To facilitate participation among patients with limited English proficiency, the survey was translated into Spanish and Simplified Chinese by certified medical interpreters at ZSFG. Certified medical interpreters were also available to assist participants during the consent and survey process when needed.

Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

Our primary outcomes of interest were patient‐reported oral hygiene practices and dental care access, which we assessed by dental visit frequency before and after HNC diagnosis using a five‐category frequency scale (“Never,” “Once a year,” “Twice a year,” “More than twice a year,” or “I don't know”). We also evaluated patient‐reported fluoride varnish receipt, use of prescription‐strength fluoride toothpaste, and patient recall of receiving a pre‐RT dental evaluation. Participant‐reported hygiene practices were assessed via the study questionnaire (Supporting Information 1).

In accordance with NCCN‐recommended guidelines, dental visit frequency was dichotomized as high frequency (≥ 2 visits/year) versus low frequency (≤ 1 visit/year) for regression analyses. Participants who selected “I don't know” were grouped with the ≤ 1visit/year category, as uncertainty about dental visit frequency was thought to reflect irregular or non‐routine dental care utilization and to minimize upward bias in estimates of regular care. To assess the robustness of this classification, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding participants who selected “I don't know” for dental visit frequency (Supporting Information 2).

We utilized descriptive statistics to summarize patient‐reported demographics, dental care practices, and patient‐reported barriers to care (Tables 1 and 2). Stable housing was defined as renting or owning a residence, while unstable housing was defined as any non‐permanent living arrangement, including staying with someone temporarily, shelter‐based housing, medical respite, or living in a vehicle. We used heatmaps to depict the frequency of reported barriers to dental care among participants with high‐ versus low‐dental visit frequency, before and after HNC diagnosis (Figure 1). To formally test whether barriers were related to dental visit frequency, we fit logistic regression models with high‐ versus low‐visit frequency as the outcome and all reported barriers together as predictors. We then used likelihood ratio tests to assess whether the set of barriers, considered jointly, was associated with dental visit frequency. For post‐diagnosis analyses, models were additionally adjusted for pre‐diagnosis dental visit frequency to account for baseline care patterns.

Results

Participant Demographics, Oncologic History, and HNC Behavioral Risk Factors

Sixty‐six patients were approached for participation in the study, 43 consented and enrolled, and 41 ultimately completed the survey, yielding an overall completion rate of 62%. Although not all patients who declined participation provided a reason, lack of time was the most cited reason for both declining participation and not completing the survey (n = 4), followed by having established dental care (n = 3), feeling overwhelmed by cancer treatment (n = 2), and low concern for dental health (n = 2).

Among 41 participants (Table 1), the majority were male (n = 26, 63%) with a median age of 59 years (interquartile range [IQR] 48–64). The most represented race/ethnicity was Asian (n = 15, 37%), followed by Black or African American (n = 8, 20%) and Hispanic (n = 8, 20%). Just over half of participants reported a primary language other than English (n = 21, 51%), including 11 (27%) Cantonese‐ or Mandarin‐speakers and 6 (15%) Spanish‐speakers. Most participants had not completed college (n = 31, 76%), and 15% reported being unstably housed. In terms of HNC behavioral risk factors, 5 (12%) participants reported currently smoking, none reported current chewing tobacco use, and only 1 (2%) participant reported heavy alcohol consumption, defined as > 4 drinks/day or > 14 drinks/week (> 3 drinks/day in females).

Access to Dental Care, Oral Hygiene Practices, and Oral Symptoms

Among the 41 participants, few participants (n = 15, 37%) reported dental visits at least twice a year both before and after cancer diagnosis (Table 2). Among participants with low baseline dental visit frequency (n = 26), 8 (31%) improved to high frequency post‐diagnosis. However, there was no significant difference in dental visit frequency prior to versus after cancer diagnosis, with fair agreement between pre‐ and post‐diagnosis dental visit frequency (Cohen's κ = 0.245). A total of 23 participants (56%) reported a different frequency of visits after their HNC diagnosis compared to before, with no evidence that increases (11) or decreases (12) were more common than the other (Stuart‐Maxwell p = 0.405) (Supporting Information 3). 46% and 27% of participants recalled being informed of the importance of dental care and preventive fluoride, respectively. Only 15% of participants (n = 16) reported either prescription fluoride toothpaste use or fluoride varnish receipt. In total, 27% reported use of any fluoride product, with only one participant reporting both modalities. No participants endorsed fluoride tray use.

While 73% described brushing their teeth at least twice daily, about half (n = 20, 49%) reported flossing at least once daily. Further, 61% of participants endorsed xerostomia, and only 13% reported use of products for dry mouth. Similarly, the majority of participants (n = 34, 83%) reported missing teeth while only 37% endorsed use of prosthodontics. Regarding other oral symptoms, 18 (45%) reported tooth sensitivity, 14 participants (35%) reported broken or painful teeth, 10 (26%) reported difficulty swallowing, 9 (23%) reported mouth sores, 7 (18%) reported dental infection, 3 (8%) reported oral fungal infection, and 3 (8%) reported trismus. The most commonly reported adjunctive oral care product was alcohol‐based mouthwash (n = 11, 29%) followed by hydrogen peroxide (n = 8, 21%), with only 5% (n = 2) endorsing chlorhexidine gluconate use (brand name Peridex).

Patient‐Reported Barriers and Predictors to Dental Care

The most frequently reported barrier to dental care was cost (n = 26, 63%), followed by lack of insurance coverage (n = 20, 49%), challenges with finding a dentist (n = 19, 46%), lack of importance of dental care (n = 9, 22%), lack of time (n = 8, 20%), fear of the dentist (n = 7, 18%), and embarrassment (n = 5, 12%). Of the participants who reported cost as a barrier to dental care, 53% reported at least twice‐yearly dental visits both before and after cancer diagnosis (Figure 1). Among those reporting difficulty finding a dentist, only 27% reported seeing a dentist at least twice per year prior to cancer diagnosis, decreasing to 20% after diagnosis. Barriers as a group were not associated with dental visit frequency prior to diagnosis but were significantly associated with dental visit frequency following diagnosis (likelihood ratio test, p = 0.26 and p = 0.01, respectively) (Figure 1).

Given the modest sample size and small cell counts for several subgroup analyses, results from regression models should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. Effect estimates may be unstable, and confidence intervals were often wide. On univariate analysis, a non‐English primary language was significantly associated with lower odds of visiting the dentist at least twice a year prior to cancer diagnosis (OR 0.19, 95% CI 0.05–0.78; p = 0.021) (Figure 2A). Participants reporting challenges finding a dentist had lower odds of at least twice annual dental visits after cancer diagnosis (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.04–0.77; p = 0.022) (Figure 3B). Higher education was significantly associated with greater odds of reporting fluoride varnish receipt (OR 3.18, 95% CI 1.13–8.98; p = 0.029) and higher odds of recalling pre‐RT dental evaluation (OR 3.19, 95% CI 1.04–9.76; p = 0.042) (Figures 2C and Supporting Information 4, respectively). Hispanic ethnicity was associated with higher odds of prescription fluoride toothpaste use (OR 15.50, 95% CI 2.12–113.54; p = 0.007), although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of events and wide confidence intervals (Figure 2D). In absolute terms, prescription fluoride toothpaste use was reported by 50% of Hispanic participants (4/8, 95% CI 0.16–0.84) compared with 6% of non‐Hispanic participants (2/33, 95% CI 0.01–0.20) (Fisher's exact p = 0.009). Sensitivity analyses excluding participants who selected “I don't know” yielded similar results, with no meaningful changes in the direction or statistical significance of associations (Supporting Information 3).

Discussion

In this study, a substantial proportion of patients reported inadequate access to dental care. Over half of participants were non‐English speaking, and a primary language other than English was significantly associated with lower dental visit frequency prior to HNC diagnosis. While this association did not reach statistical significance after diagnosis, there is a suggestion of a potential ongoing disparity. In addition to poor access to dental care, only 15% of individuals indicated receipt of prescription fluoride toothpaste or fluoride varnish despite current NCCN guidelines recommending either 1.1% fluoride gel/tray use daily or fluoride varnish application every three months [13]. Higher education was significantly associated with patient‐reported receipt of fluoride varnish and receipt of a pre‐RT dental evaluation. Pre‐RT dental evaluation is widely recommended and considered standard practice for head and neck RT at academic and high‐volume centers, including UCSF. Therefore, the observed findings may reflect limitations in patient recall or awareness rather than true differences in receipt of care. Finally, although guidelines recommend alcohol‐free mouthwash for managing xerostomia and preventing caries, our cohort more frequently reported using agents that may worsen dry mouth and oral mucositis, suggesting potential gaps in oral health counseling.

Prior studies of dental care access have largely involved populations with lower rates of public insurance, higher median income, and higher education attainment than our cohort. Dor et al. reported a significant increase in twice‐yearly dental visits among patients with HNC following treatment from 70% to 84%, whereas our publicly insured population did not demonstrate a significant change [20]. Similarly, Threet et al. found that most patients recalled receiving oral health counseling and endorsed high rates of regular dental visits, which contrasts with the much lower recall and visit frequency observed in our cohort [30]. These differences may reflect disparities in education, health literacy, and insurance status. Supporting this, Watson et al. demonstrated that publicly insured patients, while less than 10% of their sample, had poorer baseline oral health and higher rates of ORN, highlighting the vulnerability of publicly insured populations to inadequate dental care and post‐RT complications [31].

Although disparities in dental care access among low‐income and publicly insured populations have been previously described, our study contributes several novel observations. First, we characterize dental care access specifically among HNC survivors receiving care within a public safety‐net system, a population that remains underrepresented in the literature. Second, beyond documenting dental utilization, we evaluated patient‐reported barriers, oral hygiene practices, preventive fluoride use, and recall of oral health counseling. Notably, despite a high prevalence of xerostomia and other oral symptoms, relatively few participants reported receiving fluoride‐based preventive interventions or using products intended to mitigate treatment‐related oral complications. We also observed low recall of counseling regarding dental care and fluoride use following RT. To our knowledge, these patient‐reported measures of preventive fluoride utilization, oral health counseling recall, and oral hygiene practices have not been well described among HNC survivors receiving care within a public safety‐net healthcare system. Together, these findings suggest that barriers to optimal oral health in this population may extend beyond insurance coverage and access alone and may also involve patient education, care coordination, and survivorship support.

Although exploratory, our findings suggest that patient‐reported dental care access may remain suboptimal among HNC survivors receiving care in safety‐net settings. Social drivers of health such as income, educational attainment level, insurance coverage, and primary language may contribute to differences in the ability of patients to access and receive dental care both before and after RT. Prior studies suggest that financial burden is a major barrier to dental care, and public insurance alone may not fully mitigate disparities [17]. For example, low‐income patients with Denti‐Cal have been reported to have fewer regular dental visits than higher‐income counterparts [27]. State‐level variability in Medicaid dental benefits has been associated with differences in rates of preventive care, with more comprehensive coverage linked to higher utilization [32]. Gaps in access may also be related to the limited number of dental providers who accept Medicaid, which has been attributed in part to lower reimbursement rates [33]. Policy interventions that expand and standardize Medicaid dental benefits, increase Denti‐Cal reimbursement, and offer provider incentives may help improve dental care access for underserved HNC survivor populations.

Limitations

This study has multiple limitations. Given our study's relatively small sample size, the precision of several statistical estimates was limited, particularly for subgroup analyses with small cell counts. Consequently, observed associations should be interpreted cautiously, as some effect estimates may be unstable and confidence intervals were often wide. Larger, prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings and guide strategies to improve adherence to recommended oral care. Accordingly, the regression analyses presented in this study should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. Multivariable analyses were not presented due to sparse cell counts across multiple predictors, as such models were unstable and prone to overfitting; future studies with a larger sample size may be more powered for multivariable analysis. Furthermore, the use of a convenience sample introduces the potential for selection bias, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader HNC populations, particularly in settings with different sociodemographic or health system characteristics. Moreover, responses may be influenced by recall bias as outcomes and behaviors were assessed using self‐reported survey measures. Finally, recruitment in the otolaryngology clinic while in surveillance may introduce social desirability bias and increase the risk of overreporting favorable oral hygiene practices and health behaviors.

Future Directions

Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter studies with larger but equally diverse HNC cohorts to validate the findings observed in this study and characterize how access to dental care may evolve over the cancer care continuum. Moreover, incorporating patient‐reported measures with objective clinical endpoints, such as caries burden, fluoride utilization, and osteoradionecrosis incidence, may allow for further risk stratification. Lastly, embedding targeted interventions into these studies, including care coordination between oncology, otolaryngology, and dental services and patient navigation, may enable evaluation of any clinically meaningful impact on oral health outcomes.

Conclusion

Routine dental care is essential to the survivorship of patients with HNC treated with RT. Despite its importance, survivors in low‐resource settings may have limited access to dental care. In this exploratory study, our findings suggest that social determinants of health, including primary language and educational attainment, may influence access to dental care and receipt of preventive dental interventions. HNC survivors in low‐resource settings may therefore be at increased risk of oral complications related to radiation therapy. Targeted, multidisciplinary interventions to support dental care access in higher‐risk populations may improve adherence to guideline‐recommended care throughout the cancer care continuum and reduce survivorship disparities.
