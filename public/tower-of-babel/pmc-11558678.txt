Elucidating Antibiotic Permeation through the Escherichia coli Outer Membrane: Insights from Molecular Dynamics
Authors: Javad Deylami, Shu Sin Chng, Ee Hou Yong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Antibiotic resistance represents a critical public health threat, with an increasing number of Gram-negative pathogens demonstrating resistance to a broad range of clinical drugs. A primary challenge in enhancing antibiotic efficacy is overcoming the robust barrier presented by the bacterial outer membrane. Our research addresses a longstanding question: What is the rate of antibiotic permeation across the outer membrane (OM) of Gram-negative bacteria? Utilizing molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, we assess the passive permeability profiles of four commercially available antibiotics—gentamicin, novobiocin, rifampicin, and tetracycline across an asymmetric atomistic model of the Escherichia coli (E. coli) OM, employing the inhomogeneous solubility-diffusion model. Our examination of the interactions between these drugs and their environmental context during OM permeation reveals that extended hydrogen bond formation and drug–cation interactions significantly hinder the energetics of passive permeation, notably affecting novobiocin. Our MD simulations corroborate well with experimental data and reveal new implications of solvation on drug permeability, overall advancing the possible use of computational prediction of membrane permeability in future antibiotic discovery.

Introduction

Infectious diseases represent a substantial threat to humanity, owing to their rapid spread and mutation within populations, thereby posing severe risks to global public health and the economy.1−3 Despite advancements in modern healthcare, bacterial and viral infectious diseases still present a significant mortality risk. Recent analyses based on statistical models reveal that in 2019, around 4.95 million deaths were associated with bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR), with 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to this issue.4 According to a 2019 report by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the USA, over 2.8 million individuals are affected by antibiotic-resistant infections annually, leading to at least 35,000 deaths each year.5 Projections indicate that by 2050, AMR could be responsible for the annual deaths of 10 million people worldwide.6 Additionally, a 2017 study highlights the economic challenges in antibiotic development, noting that the average cost of developing an antibiotic is approximately US$ 1.5, whereas the average revenue from antibiotic sales is only around US$ 46 million annually.7,8 In this context, computational approaches for investigating permeation mechanisms and the physicochemical properties of chemical fragments have emerged as promising strategies to reduce costs in antibiotic development.

Gram-negative bacteria, a major class of pathogens causing severe diseases, are increasingly resistant to most available antibiotics.9 The resilience of Gram-negative bacteria largely arises from their outer membrane (OM), which provides protection against harsh environments. The OM is an asymmetric membrane, comprising of phospholipids (PLs) in the inner leaflet and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) molecules in the outer leaflet.10−15 LPS molecules, comprising lipid A, core oligosaccharide, and O antigen, are negatively charged and densely packed in the outer leaflet, forming a highly impermeable barrier, resulting in a low passive diffusion of antibiotics across the OM bilayer.16−18 However, this barrier is not absolute; cells often express channels or porins in the OM for nutrient uptake, providing hydrophilic pathways for the entry of certain small-molecular-weight drugs.19,20 In this regard, multidrug efflux pumps actively expel a broad range of antibiotics from cells and contribute to the overall antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacteria. Generally, the susceptibility of different bacterial species to various antibiotic classes varies significantly due to differences in OM composition and structure as well as the diversity of efflux pumps across species. The complexity of the OM barrier hinders our comprehensive understanding of the physicochemical aspects of drug permeation. It is still unclear why certain antibiotics can penetrate the OM while others cannot, a factor that hampers the rational design of new drugs to combat bacterial infections.

Experimental methods focused on assessing drug efficacy in inhibiting growth are crucial to understanding antibiotic permeation and cellular entry. It is empirically understood that antibiotics capable of crossing the OM are generally small (e.g., less than 600 Da in Escherichia coli) and polar, enabling them to pass through general diffusion porins.21−24 Nevertheless, the complexity of the Gram-negative OM complicates straightforward deductions about the physicochemical properties of various antibiotics. In this context, the direct measurement of drug accumulation in cells can provide valuable insights. Recently, Richter et al. determined drug concentrations in E. coli cells experimentally, uncovering new criteria for selecting or designing potent antibiotics against Gram-negative pathogens. They found that besides size and polarity, factors like high rigidity, low globularity, and the presence of amines contribute to increased cellular accumulation and, consequently, greater drug efficacy.25 An added complexity is that some antibiotics are substrates for efflux pumps, complicating the discernment of whether a drug is failing to enter the cell or is being efficiently expelled. A notable study employed efflux pump mutants and engineered large pores in the OM to differentiate the roles of the OM barrier and efflux pumps in drug efficacy, as gauged by the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). This study resulted in the categorization of existing drugs into groups based on whether the OM barrier and efflux pumps independently or synergistically hinder drug accumulation in cells.26 While this innovative approach effectively differentiates the actions of various antibiotics, it primarily reveals permeability through hydrophilic pathways in the OM (like general porins or engineered pores) and does not directly evaluate permeation across the hydrophobic, asymmetric OM bilayer.

In recent years, numerous molecular dynamics (MD) studies employing atomistic and coarse-grained simulations have been conducted to explore the permeation mechanisms of various solutes through symmetric and asymmetric membranes.27−44 The potential of mean force (PMF), representing the change in free energy of a solute along a specified reaction coordinate,45 is generated through umbrella sampling.46 This data is used to calculate the permeation coefficient across the membrane, employing either the inhomogeneous solubility-diffusion model (ISDM)32,47−49 or a recent kinetic model.50 Significantly, Carpenter et al. employed the ISDM to compute the permeability of different drug-like molecules at the all-atom level, a method subsequently validated in vitro through the parallel artificial membrane permeability assay.51,52 While this research was limited to solute permeability across a symmetric bilayer, it effectively demonstrated the precision and effectiveness of MD simulations. Subsequently, Carpenter et al. investigated the permeation of several common and small solutes, such as benzene and ethanol, across an asymmetric E. coli OM model containing LPS with a truncated core, albeit using atomistic simulations.53 This research marked an important step in employing asymmetric membranes to ascertain the physicochemical principles of solute permeation.

However, accurately measuring the PMF and permeation coefficient of real antibiotics across the OM, particularly at the all-atomistic level, remains challenging due to the larger size and complexity of these molecules. In our study, we build on the similar methodologies established by Carpenter et al.,53 applying these approaches to the more complex and clinically relevant antibiotics. While we utilize the existing ISDM framework, the significance of our study lies in the subject of the simulations, specifically, the investigation of how antibiotics such as gentamicin, novobiocin, rifampicin (also known by the abbreviated name rifampin in the literature), and tetracycline permeate through the OM. Our simulations reveal that the marked destabilization of novobiocin within the LPS core and lipid A regions may arise from continuous solvation processes as these molecules penetrate the hydrophobic bilayer. This observation highlights previously unexplored consequences of solvation on drug permeability, suggesting that the solvation dynamics of antibiotics within the OM can profoundly influence their ability to cross this barrier. Specifically, it suggests that the process of solvation, whereby solvent molecules interact with and stabilize the drug molecules, can either facilitate or impede their journey through the membrane, depending on the nature and extent of these interactions. This insight underscores the complexity of drug permeation and the need to consider solvation effects in the development of antibiotics with optimal permeability properties. By accounting for protonation changes during permeation, in accordance with the Henderson–Hasselbalch (HH) equation, we enable straightforward and direct comparisons with the experimental efficacy of these antibiotics, thereby significantly enhancing the potential of computational predictions to inform the membrane permeability and aid in the discovery of new antibiotics.

Methods

MD Simulations

The simulation system is an all-atomistic charge-neutralized membrane consisting of around 14,000 atoms. The inner leaflet composition is similar to E. coli bacteria,54,55 consisting of 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine, 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoglycerol, and tetramyristoyl cardiolipin (TMCL1) PLs at a ratio of 15:5:1, while the outer leaflet has 7 LPS molecules of the E. coli K12 strain without O antigens. LPSs without O antigens are known to be rough and are more susceptible to hydrophobic antibiotics.56,57 The system contains a total of 2729 water molecules. The membrane system contains 150 mM of KCl and the LPS molecules are neutralized by Na+ cations. The membrane topology was generated using the CHARMM-GUI membrane builder.58−60 To obtain the structure of drugs, the CGenFF program61 was employed to create GROMACS topology files for each drug. The simulation system was modeled using the CHARMM36m force field62 in conjunction with the TIP3P water model.63

All MD simulations and analysis of trajectories were performed using the GROMACS 5.1.2 package64 with an integration step of 2 fs and hydrogen bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm.65 The van der Waals interactions cutoffs were set to 1.4 nm66 and the particle mesh Ewald scheme67 was carried out to calculate all electrostatic interactions. The Verlet grid cutoff scheme68 was applied for neighbor searching, using an update frequency of 20 and a cutoff distance of 1.4 nm for short-range neighbors. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three directions.

First, the systems were energy-minimized using a steepest descent algorithm.69 After that, NVT and NPT equilibrium simulations were performed with a total time of 110 ns, in accordance with CHARMM-GUI protocols.60 During six sequential steps of equilibration simulations, various restraints were applied to the water, LPS, and PL molecules, based on the protocols of CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder, and the restraint forces were gradually reduced during the equilibration process.58−60 During the equilibration simulations, the temperature was kept at 310 K using a Berendsen thermostat with a time constant of 1.0 ps.70 The pressure was maintained at 1.0 bar by semi-isotropic application of a Berendsen barostat, with a time constant of 5.0 ps.70,71

PMF Simulations

The membrane normal z was chosen as the reaction coordinate, and the four drugs were pulled across the OM along the reaction coordinate (from z = −5 nm to z = 5 nm) at a rate of 0.005 nm/ps, and the center of mass of drug molecules restrained harmonically with a force constant of 1000 kJ mol–1 nm–2. The trajectory of pulling simulation was used to extract initial structures for umbrella sampling and calculate the PMF for the four drug molecules in neutral form.46,72,73

In order to obtain PMF profiles, every PMF window was run for 20 ns to allow equilibration, followed by 100 ns of simulations with the drug restrained in the z dimension by a harmonic force constant of 1500 kJ mol–1 nm–2. During PMF simulations, the temperature was maintained at 310 K using a Nose–Hoover thermostat71,74 with a time constant of 1.0 ps; the pressure was maintained at 1.0 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat semi-isotropically, with a time constant of 5.0 ps and compressibility of 4.5 × 10–5 bar–1.75

On average, one window for every ∼1.25 Å of the membrane was used (at least 80 windows in total) for the complete space of 10 nm, spanning the whole membrane and the bulk water regions on either side of it. This ensures sufficient overlap of histograms for all four drugs (see Supporting Information Appendix, Section 1). The free energy of the transfer profile of each drug was constructed using the gmx wham tool of the weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM) in GROMACS.76,77 The first 50 ns of each umbrella sampling window was discarded for equilibration. In the event of insufficient sampling, we carried out additional windows or longer simulations to ensure convergence of each PMF. Finally, all PMF profiles are normalized to zero in bulk water phases, i.e., z ≤ – 4 nm and z ≥ 4 nm. The P2 order parameter of gentamicin, novobiocin, tetracycline, and rifampicin as it is steered along the membrane can be found in Supporting Information Appendix, Section S5.50,78

Distance-Dependent Hydrogen Bonds

The average number of hydrogen bond formation was calculated by counting the hydrogen bonds using GMX hbond64 implementation for every PMF window, using the default criteria: distance <0.35 nm and angle of hydrogen donor–acceptor <30°. The membrane center, which is the region within the OM void of any water molecules after equilibration simulations, is found to be in the range of the reaction coordinate given by z ∈ [0.25, 1.25] nm (Figure 1).

The average number of contacts between the drugs and the OM compartments was calculated using GMX mindist64 implementation for every PMF window, i.e., the distance between two groups being less than 0.5 nm.

Results

Free Energy Profiles Reveal That Novobiocin Is Destabilized in the LPS Layer in an Asymmetric OM Model

To understand the passive permeation characteristics of the four drugs as they pass through the asymmetric E. coli OM, we calculate the equilibrium free energies of each drug in its neutral form G(z) along the reaction coordinate z, which is chosen as the normal to the OM. The free energy profiles of the four drugs are distinctly asymmetric as shown in Figure 2.

PMF as a function of the distance (z) along the normal of the E. coli OM for four drugs in neutral form. The PMF profile of every drug has been constructed from at least 100 ns/window MD simulation. The PMF profiles are distinctly asymmetrical: rifampicin, gentamicin, and tetracycline partition favorably, while novobiocin experiences substantial energetic barriers.

As these drugs proceed into the lipid A headgroup region, favorable energetics can be observed with substantial stabilization in the middle of each leaflet. In these PMF profiles, the trend of stabilization is interrupted by a local maximum at the junction of the two leaflets (z ∼ 1 nm). Our results suggest that these three drugs generally partition favorably into the hydrophobic core of the bilayer.

The PMF profile of novobiocin is distinctively different. It experiences a sharp energy barrier upon entry into the LPS layer. The rise for novobiocin begins at the lipid A headgroup. As the drug enters the lipid A and PL hydrophobic tail region, the free energy decreases, suggesting better partitioning in the membrane. Novobiocin experiences energetic barriers in the LPS core and lipid A headgroup region but favorable energetics in the lipid A tail region and PL tail area. We conclude that the asymmetric OM bilayer presents a series of unfavorable energetic barriers for novobiocin, with the highest destabilization in the lipid A headgroup.

Destabilization of Novobiocin in the LPS Core and Lipid A Regions May be due to Sustained Solvation as They Break into the Hydrophobic Bilayer

To understand the basis of why novobiocin is destabilized in the LPS core and lipid A regions, we examined various types of interactions between the drugs and its surroundings as they permeate through the OM. Specifically, we quantified the number of polar contacts, i.e., the number of hydrogen bonds (H bonds) formed between drugs and LPS, PL, and water molecules, and the ion–dipole interactions between drugs and Na+ ions as shown in Figure 3 (see Supporting Information Appendix, Section 1.2). On average, each drug initially forms 1–3 H bonds with the LPS core sugars. Expectedly, as it transits the asymmetric bilayer, each drug begins to lose H bonds with LPS and gains 1–2 H bonds with PL headgroups. All four drugs also form a significant number of H bonds with water molecules at the membrane–water boundary, ranging from 8 to 12 on average. The number of H bonds with water molecules remains relatively constant within the LPS core region but drops significantly once the drug enters the membrane core (z ∼ 1 nm). As the drug moves toward the PL head group area, the availability of water molecules increases and correspondingly, the number of H bonds formed increases as well. Interestingly, we observed differences in the water H-bond profiles between novobiocin and rifampicin/gentamicin/tetracycline, in a manner that correlates with the PMF curves. For rifampicin, gentamicin, and tetracycline, the number of H bonds with water starts to fall at the lipid A headgroup region (z ∼ – 1 nm), while for novobiocin, the H-bond profiles drop only at the lipid A tail area (z ∼ 0 nm). Novobiocin has a large number of hydrogen bonds with water in the lipid A headgroup region, where we would expect the drugs to already begin to penetrate the hydrophobic region of the bilayer. This is where the highest destabilization of these drugs is detected, implying that the delay in breaking H bonds with water for novobiocin, compared to the other three drugs, may contribute to destabilization as these drugs enter the acyl tail regions. The number of intramolecular H bonds for all drugs is generally low except for rifampicin, which forms about 2–3 throughout the bilayer.

Novobiocin Is Least Permeable across the OM in Neutral Forms

We next computed permeation rates of the drugs in neutral form using the ISDM equation. To do that, we required information about position-dependent diffusion coefficients D(z), which can be calculated for each window of umbrella sampling using eq 3. We find that the diffusion coefficients for the four drugs in our asymmetric bilayer are approximately 0.5 × 10–10 m2/s, which is about ∼5 times smaller than that observed in an earlier study on a symmetric bilayer.50 Overall, the diffusion profiles of all four drugs have a relatively symmetric pattern, in contrast to the PMF profiles (see Supporting Information Appendix, Section 1.3).

Based on the PMF and D(z) data, permeability coefficients for each drug in neutral form, Pm0, are calculated and tabulated in Table 1. Unsurprisingly, the drug that is least permeable across the OM is novobiocin (given its destabilizing PMF profile), with an almost 4 orders of magnitude difference between the most permeable (i.e., tetracycline) and least permeable (i.e., novobiocin) drugs. Consistent with the idea that delay in desolvation of H bonds and Na+ contacts are important factors to consider, the number of H bonds with water or Na+ when the drugs are in the center of the membrane exhibited strong inverse correlations with the permeability coefficients with correlation coefficients R2 = 0.77 and 0.69, respectively, as depicted in Figure 5A,B. The Pearson correlation coefficients between the permeability coefficients of the drugs and various types of interactions in the membrane center (z ∈ [0.25, 1.25] nm) are summarized in Figure 5C. We note that while the number of H bonds between the drug and water molecules shows a strong correlation with permeability, this parameter is more appropriately understood as an explanatory factor for why permeability barriers are high. Rather than serving as a stand-alone predictive descriptor for permeability prior to MD, it highlights key interactions that contribute to passive permeation, which can be further explored through simulations or experimental validation in virtual screening efforts. Discussion

One of the primary challenges in passive permeation through the OM of Gram-negative bacteria is the formidable barrier posed by the lipid asymmetry of the bilayer. To elucidate the passive permeability of drugs across the OM, we conducted all-atomistic simulations using an asymmetric OM model that includes complete LPS structures with core oligosaccharides. This study marks a significant advancement as we have, for the first time, successfully characterized the energetic profiles of four clinically important antibiotics as they traverse the all-atomistic asymmetric OM. Our simulations, which spanned a cumulative time of approximately 48 μs (12 μs per drug), provided a meaningful equilibrium sampling of real drug molecules as they permeated the bilayer. While longer simulations would ideally yield more comprehensive insights,42 our study was limited to this duration due to computational resource constraints, making our simulations still computationally intensive yet manageable within our capabilities.97,98 Utilizing PMF calculations and incorporating position-dependent diffusion coefficients, we determined permeability coefficients using the ISDM, a framework extensively validated in various computational and experimental studies.31,32,50,52,87

The process of desolvation, which involves the removal of coordinating ions and hydration shells, is widely recognized as a critical step for polar solutes to penetrate and permeate hydrophobic lipid bilayers. However, the extent to which solvation influences membrane permeation has not always been clear. It is important to note that while the center of the membrane is largely devoid of water molecules after equilibration, some drug molecules may still form hydrogen bonds with water molecules before they reach this hydrophobic region. However, no additional H bonding with water molecules occurs once the drugs penetrate the membrane core. This further clarifies that drug–water interactions are restricted to the initial stages of permeation before the drugs enter the membrane’s hydrophobic center. Our study provides clear evidence of the significant impact of solvation on the permeability of novobiocin, in contrast to gentamicin, rifampicin, and tetracycline. Notably, novobiocin maintained substantial hydrogen bonds with water molecules and contacts with Na+ ions even as they approached the center of the membrane. This delayed desolvation process not only adversely affected the energetics and, consequently, the permeability of these drugs across the OM but also highlighted previously unappreciated factors that may influence the desolvation process. Although further investigation is needed, we speculate that the differences in desolvation between these groups of antibiotics might indicate that novobiocin forms stronger hydrogen bonds and ion–dipole interactions.

The significance of ionization in drug permeation is well-established; most drugs contain functional groups that can lose or gain protons at different pH levels, resulting in various charged states.93,95,99−102 The likelihood of a drug’s functional group being (de)protonated depends on its ionization constant (pKa) and the pH of the surrounding medium, as described by the H–H equation. The effects of (de)protonation can reduce the population of neutral species capable of permeating the membrane. Assuming the validity of the pH-partition hypothesis, which states that only the neutral/zwitterionic form of an ionizable molecule contributes to passive permeation, the pH-dependent effective permeability coefficient (Pmeff) is simply the neutral permeability (Pm0) scaled by f, the fraction of the neutral/zwitterionic state over all possible states.93 The conversion formula for determining Pmeff takes into account the number of acidic and basic ionization sites in the drug to accurately represent the proportion of charged versus neutral states for each ionizable group in the molecule.

The calculated pH-dependent log Pmeff values for all four antibiotics, as shown in Figure 6, generally indicate a significant decrease in permeability for each drug after accounting for ionization effects (see Methods for more details). According to previous research by Yue et al.,101 these corrective formulas may overestimate the impact of protonation, suggesting that the actual permeability likely lies somewhere between Pmeff and Pm0. In general, the permeability coefficients of the four antibiotics enable us to make meaningful comparisons and rationalize the existing MIC data of these drugs against E. coli. Krishnamoorthy et al.26 measured MIC values (reproduced in Table 2) for these antibiotics in E. coli strains with either an intact OM or one expressing an artificial large pore, thus distinguishing drug efficacy based on their ability to permeate the OM. For comparison, a recent assay on POPC liposomes inferred the effective permeability coefficient, Pmeff, of drugs from changes in fluorescence during permeation, finding that the log Pmeff of rifampicin, tetracycline, and gentamicin are −4.27, −5.86, and −7.60, respectively.95

The permeability of antibiotics across the bacterial OM is a critical factor in their efficacy against Gram-negative bacteria, such as E. coli. To evaluate this, MIC values are often used, representing the lowest concentration of an antibiotic that inhibits visible bacterial growth. Specifically, in this study, we reference the relative MIC (RMIC), defined as the ratio of the MIC value in the absence of pore expression to the MIC value with pore expression (RMIC = MICno pore/MICwith pore; see Table 2).26 This ratio provides insights into the impact of OM porins on drug permeability. Drugs with lower RMIC values are generally considered to have better permeation through the OM, as the presence of pores facilitates their entry into bacterial cells. Furthermore, among drugs with similar RMIC values, those with lower MIC values in the absence of pore expression are expected to exhibit higher permeation rates. This correlation occurs because a lower MIC value typically indicates a higher drug efficacy against the bacterium, which can often be attributed to better penetration through the bacterial OM. In the context of this study, the MIC technique, when combined with the comparison of relative MIC values, offers valuable insights into the permeability characteristics of antibiotics and their potential effectiveness against Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli. This approach underscores the importance of considering both the structural characteristics of the OM and the physicochemical properties of antibiotics in the design and evaluation of new drugs.

Our calculations revealed that novobiocin, which is a large antibiotic (>600 Da), is highly impermeable across the E. coli OM, as indicated by their effective permeability coefficients. This is consistent with their general inability to inhibit the growth of E. coli with an intact OM (high MICno pore = 64 μg/mL). For novobiocin, which is hydrophobic, the presence of a large pore does not facilitate its crossing of the OM (MICwith pore = 32 μg/mL). Therefore, the main route for novobiocin to enter E. coli cells is via passage through the OM bilayer, albeit inefficiently. Gentamicin, a small and hydrophilic drug, inhibits the growth of wild-type (WT) E. coli effectively, regardless of the presence of large hydrophilic pores, implying high permeability. Given that it predominantly exists in a multiple charged state with an extremely low permeability, it clearly does not permeate across the OM bilayer itself but is sufficiently small to pass through general diffusion porins like OmpC/F.

Both rifampicin and tetracycline are permeable across the E. coli OM. Tetracycline, the second most permeable drug based on simulation results, is small and very hydrophilic and is known to pass through general diffusion porins like OmpF/C, resulting in low MIC (high efficacy) against WT E. coli. Rifampicin presents an interesting case. Despite being the largest of the four drugs, our findings suggest that its permeation rate is actually the best. This unexpected ability to partition into the OM bilayer might explain why the experimental MIC of rifampicin is lower than the corresponding values for novobiocin. Although adding a large pore allows more rifampicin to enter the cell (lowering the MIC by 16-fold), the notion that such a large antibiotic can directly permeate the OM bilayer efficiently is noteworthy.

Generally speaking, our in silico screening approach, which directly reports on OM permeability, may be more applicable for understanding and rationalizing the efficacy of large drug candidates with molecular weights beyond the cutoff sizes of diffusion porins, in some ways outperforming screens based solely on classical molecular descriptors. In this study, we employed an atomistic force field to investigate the permeation event of antibiotic molecules along the E. coli OM. Although the all-atom modeling approach provides a precise and detailed picture of molecular mechanisms, it is computationally expensive and limits the number of small molecules that can be examined. Employing a coarse-grained force field in future work may not only expand our understanding of the permeation process to a broader range of chemical fragments but also increase the predictive capability for antibiotic-candidate compounds, which is crucial from a drug design perspective.

Conclusions

Evaluating drug permeability across the OM of Gram-negative bacteria is critical for understanding and improving antibiotic efficacy. Traditionally, the size cutoff for antibiotics capable of diffusing through OM porins has been a primary benchmark for distinguishing effective drugs. However, our study challenges the simplicity of this criterion by demonstrating that MD simulations of antibiotics permeating an asymmetric OM bilayer provide a more nuanced and precise evaluation of drug permeability.

Our PMF calculations revealed significant variability in the free energy barriers encountered by different antibiotics, with novobiocin exhibiting particularly high energetic barriers due to delayed desolvation processes. The interaction analysis underscored the importance of hydrogen bonds and sodium ion (Na+) contacts in modulating the permeability of these drugs. Specifically, the persistence of these interactions in the membrane’s core regions contributed to the decreased permeability observed for novobiocin, highlighting the critical role of solvation dynamics in drug permeation beyond just molecular size. This study not only provides a detailed mechanistic understanding of how antibiotics traverse the complex OM but also offers a validated computational framework that can be extended to other Gram-negative bacterial contexts.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that detailed computational studies, such as those employing PMF calculations and interaction analyses, are indispensable for advancing our understanding of antibiotic permeability. This work provides a foundation for more informed drug design strategies that consider the intricate balance of interactions within the OM, potentially leading to the development of more effective antibiotics capable of overcoming bacterial resistance.
