Nibedita Mondal, Sandeep Kumar Mondal, Nayantara Gupta

Abstract
The detection of very high-energy gamma-rays from M87 can provide crucial insights into particle acceleration and radiation mechanisms in jets. The recent observations by the Large High Altitude Air Shower Observatory (LHAASO) detector extend the energy range of TeV gamma-ray astronomy, and also the variability study to the TeV energy domain. We have modelled the low state and flare state multi-wavelength spectral energy distributions of M87 within a time-dependent framework. In our model, the low state gamma-ray flux results from the emissions from the sub-parsec and the kilo-parsec scale jets of M87, whereas the flare state gamma-ray flux is mainly produced in the sub-parsec scale jet. We have shown that the spectral and temporal features of the TeV gamma-ray spectrum of M87 are consistent with this two-zone model, where the contribution from the sub-parsec scale jet significantly increases during the flare state.

I
Introduction

M87 is a misaligned BL Lac
(
Reimer et al. 2004
)
, precisely misaligned high-energy-peaked BL Lac (HBL)
(
Bai & Lee 2001
)
, a giant elliptical E0 galaxy close to the center of the Virgo Cluster having a parsec-scale jet, a one-sided nonthermal kilo-parsec-scale jet
(
Hada et al. 2024
)
, and a low-luminosity active galactic nucleus (LLAGN;
Ho 1999
). M87 is also known as 4FGL J1230.8 + 1223, NGC 4486, or 3C274, which is commonly categorised as a Fanaroff-Riley I radio galaxy (FR-I RDG;
Fanaroff & Riley 1974
) located at redshift z = 0.00428
(
Cappellari et al. 2011
)

with Right Ascension (R.A) = 187.7059° or 12h 30m 49.42s and Declination (Decl.) = 12.3911° or
+12^{\circ}23\arcmin 28\farcs 0
.

Since M87 is the only source that has been traced from the supermassive black hole (SMBH) shadow (about 0.005 pc) to the outer jet (
\sim
25  kpc), exhibiting a relativistic jet, it is considered the best-researched laboratory for studying SMBH physics, accretion, and radio-loud AGN properties.

Very-high-energy (VHE,
0.1\,\sim\,100~\mathrm{TeV}
) gamma-rays are messengers of cosmic ray acceleration sites. The origin of this VHE emission of jetted AGNs like M87 is still elusive. The High Energy Gamma Ray Astronomy (HEGRA) reported the first VHE gamma-ray emission from M87 in 1998–1999
(
Aharonian et al. 2003
)
. Furthermore, the next-generation high-energy instruments such as High Energy Stereoscopic System (H.E.S.S.;
Aharonian et al. 2006
), Very Energetic Radiation Imaging Telescope Array System (VERITAS;
Acciari et al. 2008
), Major Atmospheric Gamma Imaging Cherenkov (MAGIC;
Albert et al. 2008
), and
Fermi
Large Area Telescope (
Fermi
-LAT;
Abdo et al. 2009
) also reported gamma ray emission. Additionally, High Altitude Water Cherenkov Observatory (HAWC) recorded long-term VHE emission from this source at a weak statistical significance (3.6
\sigma
)
(
Albert et al. 2021
)
. According to previous studies, M87 not only exhibits variations in its spectrum and flux in flaring states but also displays complex behaviour in non-flaring states at gamma-ray frequencies
(
Ait Benkhali et al. 2019
)
. The following is a brief history of M87 in different activity phases.

Four significant VHE flares have been detected in 2005, 2008, 2010, and 2018
(
Aharonian et al. 2006
;
Albert et al. 2008
;
Abramowski et al. 2012
;
Algaba et al. 2024
)
in several bands, exhibiting one-day variability. LHAASO Water Cherenkov Detector Array (LHAASO-WCDA) detected VHE flux state
(
Cao et al. 2024
)
in 2022 in an 8-day time bin. Apart from these, the source has been seen in the quiescent state, with a high flux state occasionally appearing for very short time scales.

At around 16 Mpc from Earth, it is one of the nearest radio galaxies, and has been identified as a VHE
\gamma
-ray emitter in the TeV range. During long-term monitoring from 2021 to 2024, LHAASO observed M87 with a statistical significance of
\sim 9\sigma
, and the observed energy spectrum extended up to
20\,\mathrm{TeV}

(
Cao et al. 2024
)
. The HAWC reported the emission from M87 over approximately 7.5 years (from 2015 to 2022) in the energy range of 16 to 26 TeV
(
Alfaro et al. 2025
)
.

M87 exhibits brightness across the entire electromagnetic spectrum, which makes it an excellent candidate for broadband multi-wavelength observations. Multi-wavelength studies at different epochs reported emission across spatial scales ranging from the event horizon to the kilo-parsec (kpc) scale jet, and on time scales from days to years, but due to insufficient data in different time domains, the underlying physics of spatial and temporal variations remains unclear
(
Hada et al. 2024
)
. AGN jets have been considered to be one of the main sources of high-energy gamma-ray emission in the universe, but it is still unknown how particles are accelerated over short timescales and how the physical parameters of the jets vary with time. In this work, we have studied the jet emission from M87 with data analysis and modelling of the spectral energy distributions to address the key aspects of jet and accretion physics, in particular, the emission mechanisms and regions in the high-energy regime, in light of the gamma-ray data recently reported by the LHAASO observatory.

II.1.1
Fermi
-LAT Data Analysis

The
Fermi
Gamma-ray Space Telescope (FGST) is a space-based high-energy gamma-ray observatory. It carries two instruments: the Large Area Telescope (LAT), which serves as the primary instrument, and the Gamma-ray Burst Monitor (GBM). The LAT is an imaging, pair-conversion, wide-field-of-view telescope which can detect gamma-rays in the energy range from 20 MeV to beyond 300 GeV, with a field of view of 2.7 sr at 1 GeV and above
(
Atwood et al. 2009
)
. It scans the entire sky every three hours and has been in continuous operation since August 2008.

For our work, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of
Fermi
-LAT gamma-ray Pass 8 data for M87, using data collected from the Fermi Science Support Center (FSSC) server
(
Fermi Science Support Center 2025
)
, covering more than 16.5 years (4
th
August 2008 to 6
th
February 2025). The data were extracted from a circular region of interest (ROI) with a radius of 30
∘
, centered on M87, covering the energy range 100 MeV–300 GeV.

The dataset was processed using Fermipy (v1.0.1;
Wood et al. 2017
), an open-source Python package for
Fermi
-LAT data analysis. A 15
{}^{\circ}\times
15
∘
region was defined in the configuration file, and only photons with energies between 100 MeV and 300 GeV were included, while events with zenith angles above 90
∘
were excluded to reduce contamination from the Earth’s limb. For event selection, the ‘P8R3 SOURCE’ class (evclass=128) was used, as recommended for analyses with relatively small regions of interest (ROIs) less than 25
∘

(
Bruel et al. 2018
)
. The evtype parameter was set to 3 to include all event types, covering both the front and back sections of the tracker. Data quality cuts were applied using

\texttt{DATA\_QUAL}>0\ \&\&\ \texttt{LAT\_CONFIG}==1

to ensure that only high-quality data collected under standard LAT science operations were included.

For source modelling, the
Fermi
-LAT Fourth Source Catalogue Data Release 4 (4FGL-DR4; gll_psc_v35.fits;
Ballet et al. 2024
) was included, with the Galactic diffuse emission modelled using the gll_iem_v07 template
(
Acero et al. 2016
)
and the isotropic extragalactic background modelled with iso_p8r3_source_v2_v1.txt. Within the region of interest (ROI), the parameters of 4FGL J1230.8+1223, together with the isotropic (isodiff) and Galactic diffuse (galdiff) components, were left free. The normalisation (norm) of the diffuse components was allowed to vary, while for 4FGL J1230.8+1223, represented by a log-parabola, the norm, spectral index (alpha), and curvature (beta) were freed. The fit was performed using
gta.fit()
with the NEWMINUIT optimiser, iterating until the fit quality reached 3 to obtain the best-fit model. Following Fermipy documentation
(
Fermipy Collaboration 2016
)
, the
Fermi
-LAT gamma-ray light curve of M87 was then extracted in different time bins, as described in Sec :
III
.

LHAASO monitored M87 from MJD 59281-60385
(
Cao et al. 2024
)
. Within this period, MJD 59607-59615 is labelled as VHE flaring state or flare state. So we consider the phase between MJD 59281-59607 as ‘Preflare’ ( low-state 1), denoted as LS1 & the phase between MJD 59615-60385 as ‘Postflare’ ( low-state 2), denoted as LS2. This has also been summarised in the Table

Fermi
-LAT gamma-ray spectral energy distribution (SED) for these three phases, using

The details about the three

-LAT gamma-ray SEDs have been discussed in Sec :

We followed the standard data reduction procedure
2
2

2

https://www.swift.ac.uk/analysis/index.php
to prepare the raw data, i.e., Level 1 data for analysis, using the software package, HEASoft
3
3

3

https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/software/heasoft/
.
Xrtpipeline
(v0.13.5) has been used to generate the cleaned event files corresponding to each observation. During the analysis, the calibration file (CALDB; version 20240522) and other standard screening criteria have been applied to the cleaned data. We have used the photon count (PC) mode data for our analysis. For non-piled-up spectra, we consider a circular source region between 32
′′
– 40
′′
, with the source at the center. In case of the piled-up spectra, we carried out the standard procedure for pile-up correction
4
4

4

https://www.swift.ac.uk/analysis/xrt/pileup.php
and an annular source region centred on the source has been considered. In both cases, the background was extracted from a circular region located far from the source of interest, in an area with minimal contamination from other sources, and with a radius twice that of the source region. Using the
xselect
tool, we defined the source and background regions and extracted the corresponding spectrum files for each observation. Subsequently, auxiliary response files (ARFs) were generated using the
xrtmkarf
tool. The spectrum file was then grouped with its corresponding response matrix ( which could be found using the
quzcif
) and the ARF file using the
grppha
tool.
Thereafter,
addspec
and
mathpha
were used to generate the combined source and background spectra, respectively. In this work, we produced combined source and background spectra for the two phases, i.e., LS1 and LS2. As mentioned earlier, the ‘Flare’ phase does not have any simultaneous Swift observation. LS1 comprises 11 observations acquired between March 19, 2021, and Jan 11, 2022, and LS2 consists of 26 observations from March 12, 2022, to April 23, 2023.

In the next step, we again used
grppha
to group the combined source and background files of each phase and generate the final spectrum file, which was then used in
xspec
for further analysis.

The spectra of the two phases were modelled using the
xspec
package (
Arnaud 1996
; Version 12.14.1). We found that both Swift-XRT spectra are best described by the
mekal + (TBabs*powerlaw)
model, providing a better fit than
mekal + (TBabs*logpar)
or
mekal + (TBabs*plec)
. The best-fit parameters were derived using chi-square statistics. Table
3
summarises the free and fixed parameter values of the best-fit model. Other than these parameters, the normalisation factors for both the mekal and powerlaw components are left free. Finally, we extracted the unabsorbed spectra corresponding to LS1 and LS2, which were then used in constructing the multi-wavelength spectral energy distributions (MWSEDs) of M87 for these two phases.

Archival Data

In this work, we have used the archival data from Large High Altitude Air Shower Observatory (LHAASO), Monitoring of Jets in Active Galactic Nuclei with VLBA Experiments (MOJAVE), Markarian Multiwavelength Data (MMDC), and Space Science Data Centre (SSDC).

II.2.1
LHAASO Data

The LHAASO
(
Cao 2010
)
is a next-generation ground-based facility in Daocheng, China, designed to investigate the origin of cosmic rays and the mechanisms of VHE gamma-ray production over an extensive energy range from
\sim
100 GeV to 1 EeV. It consists of three major detector systems: the Water Cherenkov Detector Array (WCDA;
Li et al. 2014
), optimised for gamma-ray observations in the 100 GeV–30 TeV range; the Kilometer Square Array (KM2A), operating in the 30 TeV–1 PeV range for precise cosmic-ray composition studies
(
Cui et al. 2014
;

Statistically, no flux-enhanced state was detected during the LHAASO monitored period, i.e., MJD 59281–60385. There is a possibility that the flux may have increased slightly compared to the average gamma-ray flux between 2014 and 2019; however, this period falls outside the focus of our study.

Typically, after identifying flux enhancements in a light curve with larger time bins, we analyse the light curve with shorter binning to reveal possible sub-structures more clearly
(
Mondal et al. 2021

Cao et al. 2024

1(b)

Fermi
-LAT light curve using 7-day binning (Fig.
1(c)
). The flux variation appears highly random, and the flux errors are very large. Using the Bayesian block method, we did not find any GeV flaring during the VHE flare (grey-shaded region), and the data quality is not feasible for further analysis in smaller time bins
(
Mondal et al. 2023
)
. Finally, we analysed the
\sim
60-day light curve in 8-day bins (Fig.
1(d)
), as done in
Cao et al. 2024
also. Only one data point falls within the VHE flare period (grey-shaded region), with TS
>
25. Because only a single data point is available for this period and flux uncertainties increase when using smaller time bins, it is not possible to reliably determine whether
Fermi
-LAT detected a flare coincident with the VHE event observed by LHAASO in 2022. Given this limitation, any claim of a flare state would be statistically unsupported. Reduced flux uncertainties would have allowed finer temporal binning and a more detailed examination of the light curve for potential flux enhancements. Therefore, the current
Fermi
-LAT data does not provide statistically significant evidence for a GeV flaring event coincident with the VHE flare of M87.

We consider a homogeneous spherical emission region of radius
R
having a uniform magnetic field
B
inside it, moving through a relativistic jet of bulk Lorentz factor
\Gamma_{b}
(corresponding to Doppler factor
\delta
). This region contains relativistic electrons and protons; the relativistic electrons lose energy via synchrotron radiation and inverse-Compton (IC) processes, viz., as discussed in
Tomar et al. 2021
. We are considering a leptonic model where the energy loss of protons is insignificant compared to that of electrons.

As discussed before in sec :
IV
, from the maximum likelihood analysis of
Fermi
-LAT data, a log-parabolic distribution of gamma-rays was found to best fit the data in both cases, i.e. for flare and low state. A log-parabolic photon spectrum can be produced from the radiative loss of a log-parabolic electron spectrum
(
Massaro et al. 2004
)
. Therefore, we have used a log-parabolic spectrum for the injected electrons in the emission region to explain the MWSED of M87, given by the following expression:

Q(E)=L_{0}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{-\alpha-\beta\log_{10}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)},

(3)

where
Q(E)
is the log-parabolic distribution,
L_{0}
is the normalisation constant,
E_{0}
is the scaling factor or pivot energy (set to 1 TeV in our modelling and kept fixed),
\alpha
is the spectral index, and
\beta
is the curvature index.

We use ‘GAMERA’
(
Hahn 2016
)
, an open-source code to model the MWSEDs of M87, available on GitHub
10
10

10

http://libgamera.github.io/GAMERA/docs/main_page.html
. It solves the time-dependent transport equation (Eqn.
4
) and propagates the particle spectrum N(E, t) for an injected spectrum to calculate the synchrotron and IC emissions, including the Klein–
Nishina effect.

\frac{\partial N(E,t)}{\partial t}=Q(E,t)-\frac{\partial}{\partial E}(b(E,t)N(E,t))-\frac{N(E,t)}{\tau\textsubscript{esc}(E,t)}

(4)

Q(E,t) is the injected electron spectrum and b(E,t) corresponds to the energy loss rate by synchrotron and IC, and can be defined as
(
\frac{dE}{dt}
)
. In the last term, the escape time scale is assumed to be independent of energy and time,
\tau\textsubscript{esc}=\eta_{esc}\frac{R}{c}
, where
\eta_{esc}
denotes the coefficient of escape time.

The code, subsequently, calculates the synchrotron and synchrotron self-Compton (SSC) emissions, which are Doppler boosted by a factor of
\delta^{4}

(
Dermer & Menon 2009
)
in the observer’s frame due to relativistic beaming, where,
\delta=[\Gamma_{b}(1-\beta_{j}cos\theta)]^{-1}
is the Doppler factor,
\Gamma_{b}
is the bulk Lorentz factor,
\beta_{j}
is the intrinsic speed of the jet frame and
\theta
is the viewing angle of the jet with respect to the line of sight of the observer.

We have assumed two emission regions, one in the sub-parsec scale jet and another in the kilo-parsec scale jet of M87. The light curve of the VHE flare data recorded by the LHAASO collaboration
(
Cao et al. 2024
)
suggests a minimum variability timescale of one day, which constrains the size of the emission region. In our model, the VHE flare happened
from the sub-parsec scale jet; however, the kiloparsec scale jet gives a dominant contribution to VHE gamma-ray data in the low state.
In the sub-parsec scale jet, the gamma-rays are produced by the SSC mechanism, whereas in the kilo-parsec scale jet, they are mostly emitted by the external Compton mechanism (EC). We have assumed the values of the physical parameters of the jets, for example, the magnetic field
(
Tomar et al. 2021
)
and the Lorentz factor (
Walker et al. 2018
,
Wang & Zhou 2009
), following the earlier studies on the jets of M87.

In the EC mechanism, the external radiation fields, e.g., CMB, starlight, and the dust emitted photons, are isotropic with respect to the AGN frame. The starlight and dust energy density of the host galaxy has been used from
Stawarz et al. 2003
to calculate the EC emission from the kilo-parsec jet of M87. The high-energy gamma-rays produced in external Compton emission are Doppler-boosted by the factor of
\delta^{6}
/
\Gamma^{2}
in the observer’s frame
(
Dermer & Menon 2009
)
.

The parameters listed in the Table
7
and Table
8
are adjusted to fit the simulated spectral energy distributions to the data points.

(5)

U^{\prime}_{e}=\frac{1}{V}\times\int_{E_{min}}^{E_{max}}Q(E)EdE

(6)

U^{\prime}_{B}=\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}

(7)

According to
Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration 2019

\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{M_{BH}}{M_{\odot}}\right)=9.81\pm 0.05

VI.1
Results from Data Analysis

Based on LHAASO observations between 2021 and 2024,
Cao et al. 2024
conducted a comprehensive study of the different activity phases of M87. In this study, the LHAASO gamma-ray light curve was divided into three segments, consisting of two low states on either side of an 8-day flaring episode, which occurred between MJD 59607 and 59615. They also mentioned the possibility of a GeV gamma-ray flare in
Fermi
-LAT at the same period of the LHAASO VHE flare.

In this work, we focused on the same three-year period covered by the LHAASO observations and searched for multi-wavelength data of M87 corresponding to this time period. Data obtained within the time range of each phase or state were treated as simultaneous data, which we used to construct the MWSEDs of the respective states. To improve the data coverage in the MWSED, additional non-simultaneous observations were also incorporated, providing a more comprehensive picture of both the low and flare states. We found that this source was monitored by various telescopes across multiple wavebands, including
Fermi
-LAT, Swift, LHAASO, MOJAVE, HESS, VERITAS, and MAGIC (details of all simultaneous and non-simultaneous observations are provided in Sec :
II
).

We analysed
Fermi
-LAT and Swift data, and the other data (simultaneous or non-simultaneous) have been compiled, to construct the MWSED of low state and flare state, as shown in Figs.
2(a)
and
2(b)
.

The
Fermi
-LAT data analysis has two objectives: (i) light curve analysis to investigate a potential GeV flare coinciding with the VHE flare from M87 in January 2022, and (ii) gamma-ray SED analysis.

For the light curve analysis, we started by analysing 16.5 years of
Fermi
-LAT gamma-ray data (Fig.
1(a)
). Through this analysis, we aimed to identify possible gamma-ray flux enhancements between 2021 and 2024 (MJD 59281–60385; yellow shaded region in Fig.
1(a)
). A detailed analysis of this period was then carried out, with the gamma-ray light curve, and the observations were binned in the same way as done by
Cao et al. 2024
. Typically, long-term gamma-ray light curve analyses are carried out using relatively large time bins. Then the Bayesian block method is applied to identify any flux enhancements (commonly referred to as ‘structures’ or ‘sub-structures’). The detected flux enhancements are then examined by zooming into the corresponding intervals. The light curve, within these intervals, is re-analysed using shorter time bins. As the time-bin size decreases, the structures become more prominent, while flux uncertainties also increase. This process continues until the flux uncertainties become sufficiently large, and further reduction of the bin size is no longer feasible (see
Mondal et al. 2021
). But here, the 3-year-long 90-day binned
Fermi
-LAT light curve (Fig.
1(b)
) already shows large flux uncertainties even when the data bin size is large ( 90 days). Further, smaller time-binning would increase the flux uncertainties, as can be seen in Fig.
1(c)
, where we analysed the same three-year data in 7-day binning. The flux uncertainties are so large that further smaller binning is not feasible
(
Mondal et al. 2023
)
. Both of the plots are marked by a vertical grey strip, which denotes the 8-day VHE flaring period. As can be seen, there is still no strong signature of GeV flux. Subsequently, we examined the 2-month-long
Fermi
-LAT gamma-ray light curve in 8-day binning (Fig.
1(d)
). Only one data point lies within the VHE flaring period with TS = 25.7, i.e.,
\gtrsim
5
\mathrm{\sigma}
detection significance. Even with more than 5
\mathrm{\sigma}
detection significance, just based on the presence of one data point, it cannot be certainly claimed that a GeV flare may have occurred during the same period of the VHE flaring. Moreover, due to the increase in the flux uncertainty, further smaller binning is also not feasible to verify the presence of additional data points, and therefore, with the available observations, it appears less likely that a GeV flare occurred during this period.

Subsequently, the
Fermi
-LAT SED was analysed for the period from 2021 to 2024. We specifically followed the time intervals listed in Table
1
. We considered both the power-law and log-parabola models to fit the gamma-ray SED. Through maximum likelihood analysis, we found that the log-parabola spectrum provides the best-fit model for all the phases (see Table
6
). All the fitted parameters are listed in the same table.

VI.2
Results from Multi-wavelength Modelling

The LS1 and LS2 states were combined and labelled as the low state in this work. We modelled the MWSED of this state, with the SSC emission from the sub-parsec scale jet. This model successfully explains the
Fermi
-LAT MeV–GeV observations as the
Fermi
-LAT data points are covered by the SSC bump. However, the SSC emission from the sub-parsec scale jet is inadequate to account for the LHAASO-WCDA and LHAASO-KM2A data at higher energies (Fig.
3
), hence, we needed another emission region to explain the VHE gamma-ray emission. We considered the contribution from the kiloparsec-scale jet of M87 for this purpose. At the kiloparsec-scale jet, the EC emission is significantly higher compared to the SSC emission, as the emission region has a radius of the order of
10^{21}
cm. The external photon fields, such as starlight, thermal dust emission, and the cosmic microwave background (CMB), acted as targets for IC scattering. The corresponding energy densities of the external photon fields were adopted from
Stawarz et al. 2003
. The total emission from the kiloparsec-scale jet consists of the following components: synchrotron, SSC, IC/Starlight, IC/CMB, and IC/Dust. As shown in Fig.
3
, IC/Starlight dominates and largely accounts for the observed TeV flux; the contribution from IC/CMB is lower than the IC/Starlight; and the contribution from IC/Dust is lower than the IC/CMB. The solid black curve in Fig.
3
represents the total SED combining the sub-parsec and kiloparsec jets. The hardening of the VHE gamma-ray spectrum has been attributed to the EC emission from the kiloparsec-scale jet. The solid black curve passes through the first LHAASO-WCDA data point and the upper error bars of the subsequent LHAASO-WCDA data points. VII
Discussion and Conclusion

Several emission models have been suggested previously to explain the MWSED of the M87 jet. Leptohadronic model has been used in the following papers
Reimer et al. 2004
,
Fraija & Marinelli 2016
, and
Boughelilba et al. 2022
. In this case, the fitting of the SED involves three emission mechanisms, which dominate in three different energy bands: a photohadronic emission in the VHE gamma-ray band, an inverse Compton emission in the energy range of X-rays to GeV gamma-rays, and a synchrotron emission in the radio to X-ray energy band.
Lucchini et al. 2019
explained the radio to X-ray emission by a multizone model, which yields a lower gamma-ray flux than the measured flux. Two distinct one-zone leptonic models were employed in
EHT MWL Science Working Group et al. 2021
, but they failed to simultaneously represent the high and low energy components of the SED of M87.
Alfaro et al. 2022
has shown that the one-zone SSC model, along with the photo-hadronic model, can explain the MWSED covering radio to VHE gamma-ray data. They included H.E.S.S., MAGIC, and HAWC data in their SED modelling. It is evident from their work that a separate emission model is needed to explain the VHE data of the M87
jet. The VHE gamma-ray data observed by the LHAASO collaboration show spectral hardening near 20 TeV, which has been explained by
Xue et al. 2025
with the
\pi^{0}
decay gamma-rays produced in pp interactions, and also with the two-zone SSC model.

Ait Benkhali et al. 2019
argued that the one-zone SSC model is challenged by a potential excess over the standard power-law model at around 10 GeV during the low-state of M87, which suggests the need for a second radiation component. The X-ray emissions from the knots in the kilo-parsec scale of M87 have been studied earlier by reanalysing the archival data from
Chandra
observations
(
Sun et al. 2018
)
.
Sun et al. 2018
modelled radio to X-ray data from the knots of the kilo-parsec scale jet of M87 with synchrotron emission of relativistic electrons. They noted that for some knots, a second component is needed to explain the high-energy data.
Tomar et al. 2021
demonstrated that synchrotron and SSC emission from relativistic electrons in a sub-parsec scale jet can account for the observed multi-wavelength data from M87 up to 8 GeV. In our work, we used a two-zone leptonic model to fit the broadband SED of M87; the two zones are located at the sub-parsec and the kilo-parsec scale jets, respectively. In the low-state, the
Fermi
-LAT MeV–GeV data points are satisfactorily covered by the SSC emission from the sub-parsec scale jet. LHAASO collaboration measured the emission of VHE gamma-rays with a flux level of
10^{-12}~\mathrm{erg}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}~\mathrm{s}^{-1}

(
Cao et al. 2024
)
during this state. It was noted in their work that the low-state VHE emission has a higher possibility of coming from large-scale structures, such as the bright knots HST-1, knot A in the kiloparsec-scale jet. We have shown that the LHAASO data points are well fitted by the VHE gamma-ray spectrum produced in EC emission of the ultra-relativistic electrons accelerated at the kiloparsec-scale jet of M87. This model can explain the hardening in the VHE gamma-ray spectrum near 20 TeV.

LHAASO collaboration
(
Cao et al. 2024
)
also reported a flare in their VHE gamma-ray data, which lasted for 8 days in 2022. They observed that this flare rose fast (
\tau_{\rm d}^{\rm rise}=1.05\pm 0.49
days) and decayed at a slower rate (
\tau_{\rm d}^{\rm decay}=2.17\pm 0.58
days), and mentioned that its dayscale variability indicates that the VHE emission originates from a compact emission region that is only a few Schwarzschild radius from the centre of the supermassive black hole, with a radius of
R\sim 2.7\times 10^{15}\delta\,{\rm cm}
(doppler factor
\delta{\lesssim 3}
). This implies that the acceleration and emission process of non-thermal particles may occur close to the central black hole. In earlier studies, the underlying physical mechanisms of such flares have been speculated.
According to recent high-resolution general relativistic magnetohydrodynamic simulations, flares close to the black hole event horizon may be powered by episodic magnetic reconnection events (
Ripperda et al. 2020
,
Hakobyan et al. 2023
). Another possible argument (
Barkov et al. 2010
,
Aharonian et al. 2017
) is that an efficient production of VHE photons through hadronic processes may result from interactions between a relativistic magnetised outflow near the jet formation zone and a star that was partially tidally disrupted by the contact with the SMBH. In our work, the SSC emission by the relativistic electrons in the sub-parsec scale jet has been used to model the flare-state MWSED of M87 within a time-dependent framework; the GeV-TeV gamma-ray data are well explained by this model. The size of the emission region used in our model is comparable to that mentioned in the paper by the LHAASO collaboration
(
Cao et al. 2024
)
.

In conclusion, our two-zone leptonic model explains the flare and low state data of LHAASO, the hardening in the LHAASO data in the low state, and the variability time in the flare state. Future observational data from LHAASO-WCDA and LHAASO-KM2A would be useful to understand more about the hardening in the spectrum of M87, as well as the measurement of the variability time from the gamma-ray light curve would be helpful to explore more about the location and size of the emission region.
