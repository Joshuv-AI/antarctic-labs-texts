Atrial fibrillation identification in patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease: a machine learning model based on immune-inflammatory markers
Authors: Yao Zhang, JiaXin Qi, Xia Chen, Lin Pang, Zhangwei Shi, Bowen Wei, Cheng Lian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global prevalence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is continuously rising, making it a significant public health concern. Recent studies indicate that NAFLD is independently associated with an increased risk of atrial fibrillation (AF), potentially mediated by chronic inflammation and immune responses. However, there is currently a lack of AF risk identification tools specifically for the NAFLD population. This study aimed to identify AF and model its association with immune-inflammatory markers in NAFLD patients.MethodsThis study enrolled 723 patients with ultrasound-confirmed NAFLD (AF group: n = 203, non-AF group: n = 520). Clinical data were collected, and 10 immune-inflammatory markers (including Systemic Immune-inflammation Index (SII), Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI), Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR), Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR), Monocyte-to-HDL Cholesterol Ratio (MHR), etc.) were calculated. Feature selection was performed using univariate logistic regression, LASSO, and the Boruta algorithm. Ten machine learning algorithms were employed to construct models, optimized via 10-fold cross-validation. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to interpret the model, and the best-performing model was ultimately deployed as an online web calculator.ResultsMultivariate analysis identified SII, NLR, PLR, MHR, and SIRI as independent predictors of AF (all P < 0.05). In the test set, the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model demonstrated the best predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.848 (95% CI: 0.785–0.911). Accuracy, specificity, precision, and F1-score were 0.847, 0.910, 0.745, and 0.713, respectively. SHAP analysis revealed age, PLR, and creatinine as the most influential predictive variables. Based on the final model, we developed a user-friendly online risk calculator.ConclusionThis study constructed and validated a machine learning model integrating multiple immune inflammatory markers for effectively identifying the risk of atrial fibrillation in patients with NAFLD. The SVM model exhibited excellent discriminatory ability and clinical applicability. The developed web tool aids in the identification of high-risk individuals and offers new strategies for preventive interventions against AF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02025-8.Keywords: Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Atrial fibrillation, Immune-inflammatory markers, Machine learning, Association modeling

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has become a growing global public health challenge, imposing a substantial burden on affected individuals and healthcare systems [1]. Globally, the total number of NAFLD patients is estimated to exceed the sum of those with obesity (approximately 650 million) and type 2 diabetes (approximately 400 million) [2]. As a multisystem diseases, the profound impact of NAFLD on cardiovascular health extends beyond significantly increasing the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD); its role as a potential driver of arrhythmias, such as atrial fibrillation (AF), is increasingly recognized [3, 4]. Notably, existing evidence supports that NAFLD is closely associated with AF recurrence and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, independent of traditional cardiometabolic comorbidities like obesity, diabetes, and hypertension [5, 6].

AF, the most common sustained arrhythmia in clinical practice, has garnered increasing attention due to its rising prevalence and incidence [7]. The occurrence of AF cannot be fully explained by factors (known as the age, genetic predisposition, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes), Therefore, it is extremely urgent to identify new triggers [8]. NAFLD emerging as a novel independent risk factor for AF, has gained widespread interest in the past few years. Mechanistic studies reveal that multiple metabolic dysregulations, chronic low-grade inflammation, and oxidative stress induced by NAFLD constitute the critical pathophysiological substrate linking NAFLD to the rising risk of AF [9]. Immune-inflammatory biomarkers, as a novel type of indicator that can directly represent the inflammatory process of the body. They not only reflect the intensity of inflammatory responses but also reveal levels of oxidative stress and underlying metabolic imbalance. In individuals with NAFLD [10–12], these biomarkers provide a critical window for the early identification of AF risk and for implementing targeted interventions. Multiple studies have confirmed that immune-inflammatory biomarkers such as the Systemic Immune-inflammation Index (SII), Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI), Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR), Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR), and Monocyte-to-HDL Cholesterol Ratio (MHR) are significantly associated with cardiovascular events, including AF [13–15].

However, existing AF risk assessment tools and prediction models focus predominantly on the general population and traditional risk factors, with limited research on AF risk prediction specifically for the distinctive NAFLD population. Furthermore, traditional modeling methods have certain limitations in handling complex, high-dimensional data [16, 17]. In contrast, machine learning methods can not only efficiently process multi-dimensional and unstructured clinical data, but also enhance the predictive performance and robustness of the model through techniques such as ensemble learning and feature importance assessment. More importantly, the model interpretation techniques developed in recent years (such as SHAP) have made machine learning models no longer “black boxes”, but rather capable of providing transparent and interpretable predictive basis for clinical decision-making. Therefore, developing a machine learning model capable of effectively integrating these immune-inflammatory biomarkers is urgently needed and holds significant importance for improving the accuracy, personalization, and biological interpretability of AF risk assessment in NAFLD patients.

This study aims to develop and validate a machine learning model based on multiple immune inflammatory markers for identifying the risk of atrial fibrillation in patients with NAFLD, and to enhance the interpretability of the model by using the SHAP method, thereby providing an efficient, transparent and easy-to-apply decision support tool for clinical practice.

Methods

This cross-sectional study enrolled patients from the Xi’an No.3 Hospital between December 2022 and December 2024. All participants were diagnosed with NAFLD via abdominal ultrasonography. Patients with significant alcohol consumption (men ≥ 30 g/day, women ≥ 20 g/day) were strictly excluded. Abdominal ultrasound examinations were performed by certified sonographers who had intermediate or higher qualifications and extensive experience. AF was diagnosed based on standard 12-lead electrocardiogram or Holter monitoring recordings during hospitalization; patients with a prior confirmed history of AF were also included in the AF group.

Exclusion criteria included: 1.acute infection or previous use of glucocorticoids/immunosuppressants; 2. history of other cardiac diseases: including rheumatic heart disease, structural heart disease, and other cardiac conditions clearly leading to arrhythmias (e.g., sick sinus syndrome, pre-excitation syndrome, etc.); 3. other liver diseases: hepatic steatosis or liver injury due to other identifiable causes, including autoimmune liver disease, viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, drug-induced liver injury; 4. lack of clinical data. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Hospital of Xi’an. Informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the use of retrospective anonymized data analysis. Ultimately, 723 NAFLD patients were included.

Baseline data were extracted from the Hospital Information System (HIS), including demographic characteristics (age, sex), clinical history (smoking history, hypertension [HT], diabetes [DM], coronary heart disease [CHD]), and anthropometric measurements (height, weight). Laboratory data were obtained from the first fasting venous blood test after admission, covering ALT, AST, Scr, TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, and complete blood count parameters (PLT, LYM, MON, NEU). Inflammatory biomarkers were calculated as follows: SII = Plt × NEU/LYM; SIRI = NEU × MON/LYM; NLR = NEU/LYM; PLR = PLT/LYM; PNR = PLT/NEU; NHR = NEU/HDL-C; MHR = MON/HDL-C; PHR = PLT/HDL-C; LHR = LYM/HDL-C; NMR = NEU/MON.

Variable selection

Feature selection in machine learning analysis is carried out using three methods, including univariate logical analysis, Boruta algorithm and LASSO regression. To ensure a robust and concise final feature set, we adopted a strict screening strategy: only when a variable is simultaneously selected by all three methods is it reserved for model development. Specifically, a variable must meet the following criteria: (1) It must be statistically significant in univariate logistic regression (P < 0.05). (2) It has non-zero coefficients in the LASSO regression model. (3) Confirmed as an “important” feature by the Boruta algorithm.

Univariate logistic regression is a common statistical method that screens features by calculating the P-value for each single variable; variables with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant in this study.

The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm is a linear model method for regression analysis that incorporates variable selection and regularization. LASSO adds a regularization term to the loss function to constrain the size of regression coefficients, automatically selecting important features and ignoring unimportant ones, thereby simplifying the model and reducing overfitting [18].

The Boruta algorithm is a wrapper method for feature selection. Its core idea is to compare original features with artificial features (shadow features) to determine the importance of the original features, thereby screening out features truly related to the target variable [19].

Model development and validation

A stratified sampling strategy was used to randomly divide the dataset into training and test sets at a ratio of 7:3. Ultimately, the training set included 507 patients (AF: n = 143; non-AF: n = 364), and the test set included 216 patients (AF: n = 60; non-AF: n = 156). To ensure reproducibility, a fixed random seed (seed = 123) was set for data splitting and model training. The following 10 machine learning models were employed for AF risk identification: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Neural Network (NNET), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), AdaBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost.

All machine learning models are developed using raw, unscaled data. Hyperparameters for all models were fixed after grid search (Table S1). Models were fitted on the training set based on 10 rounds of 10-fold cross-validation, with the folds being stratified by the AF outcome to maintain the same class distribution in each fold as in the overall training set. To address the class imbalance (AF prevalence: 28.1%), we incorporated class-weighted learning during the training of all models. Specifically, we utilized the ‘balanced’ mode, which automatically adjusts the weight of each class to be inversely proportional to its frequency in the data.

Model performance was evaluated using metrics including the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score. Calibration curves were plotted to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the net benefit across different thresholds. The best model was selected based on performance in both the training and test sets.

Model interpretation and application

SHAP is a method for interpreting the output of machine learning models and quantifying the contribution of each feature to the model. Based on the concept of Shapley values in game theory, complex black box models (such as Random Forest, XGBoost, and deep learning models) are decomposed into the “contribution values” of individual features, providing local and global interpretability.This study was ultimately analyzed based on the test set.

The final SVM model was deployed as an online calculator using the R package ‘shiny’. The application, hosted on the ShinyApps. io platform, features a sidebar interface for users to input the predictor variables. Upon submission, the server-side logic validates the inputs, processes them through the pre-trained model, and returns the computed AF risk probability. The tool’s output was verified against the original model to ensure consistency and fidelity.

Statistical analysis

We excluded cases with missing clinical variables, and there were no missing values among the 723 included subjects. All potential outliers were clinically reasonable, so these outliers were retained in the analysis to maintain the integrity of the real data and avoid bias. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the t-test. Continuous variables with skewed distribution are expressed as median with interquartile range and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables are expressed as percentages and compared between groups using the chi-square test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to identify independent risk factors for AF. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to evaluate the dose-response relationship between immune-inflammatory biomarkers and AF. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess the predictive value of immune-inflammatory biomarkers for AF. A detailed study design flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. A P-value < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.0) using the following packages: caret (for model training and cross-validation), glmnet (for LASSO and logistic regression), kernlab (for SVM), xgboost, lightgbm, catboost (for gradient boosting models), SHAPforxgboost (for SHAP analysis), and shiny (for web calculator development).

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 723 subjects were included, comprising 520 in the non-AF group and 203 in the AF group. As shown in Table 1, compared with NAFLD patients without atrial fibrillation, patients in the atrial fibrillation group present a more risky profile of clinical features. Specifically, patients in the atrial fibrillation group were significantly older (median age: 68.0 years vs. 56.0 years, P < 0.001), and the proportion of males was higher (65.5% vs. 57.3%, P = 0.043). In terms of comorbidities, the prevalence of hypertension (70.9% vs. 49.6%) and coronary heart disease (59.6% vs. 39.0%) was significantly higher among patients with atrial fibrillation (both P < 0.001). In addition, laboratory indicators also suggest that patients in the atrial fibrillation group have poorer renal function (higher serum creatinine), an unhealthy lipid profile (lower TC, HDL-C, and LDL-C), and a more significant systemic inflammatory state (higher inflammatory indicators such as SII, NLR, and PLR). However, there were no significant differences in BMI, smoking history, prevalence of diabetes and platelet count between the two groups.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis

We investigated the independent impact of immune-inflammatory biomarkers on AF risk in the entire cohort. Univariate logistic regression analysis showed that all 10 immune-inflammatory markers were significantly associated with AF risk (all P < 0.05) (Table 2). After adjusting for sex, age, BMI, smoking history, hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease, multivariate analysis identified SII (OR = 1.001, 95% CI: 1.001–1.002, P < 0.001), NLR (OR = 1.301, 95% CI: 1.140–1.483, P < 0.001), PLR (OR = 1.010, 95% CI: 1.006–1.014, P < 0.001), MHR (OR = 2.458, 95% CI: 1.196–5.052, P = 0.014), and SIRI (OR = 1.322, 95% CI: 1.083–1.614, P = 0.006) as independent related factor of AF. MHR showed the strongest association (OR = 2.458). Among the secondary markers, NHR (OR = 1.162, 95% CI: 1.047–1.290, P = 0.005) and PHR (OR = 1.005, 95% CI: 1.001–1.008, P = 0.004) remained statistically significant, while LHR (P = 0.138) and PNR (P = 0.272) lost significance in the multivariate model.

Dose-response relationship

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were used to further explore the correlation between immune-inflammatory biomarkers and AF (Fig. 2). After adjusting for confounders, the results showed: SII, NLR, NHR, and MHR exhibited a linear positive correlation with AF risk (P for overall < 0.05; P for nonlinear > 0.05); whereas SIRI, PLR, and PHR showed a nonlinear correlation with AF risk (P for overall < 0.05; P for nonlinear < 0.05). Furthermore, no correlation was found between NMR or PNR and AF risk (P for overall > 0.05).

Model development and performance comparison

We performed 10 rounds of 10-fold cross validation and built 10 machine learning models for AF risk association. The results are shown in Figs. 6; Table 4. The SVM model performed the best in the test set (AUC = 0.848, 95% CI: 0.785–0.911), followed by the Logistic model (AUC = 0.845, 95% CI: (0.779–0.911) and the GBM model (AUC = 0.845, 95% CI: 0.783–0.906). The AUC values of different models were compared using the DELONG test (Table S7). Although there was no statistically significant difference in the AUC values between the SVM model and the Logistic model (P > 0.05), The accuracy, specificity, precision and F1 score of the SVM model were the highest (0.847, 0.910, 0.745 and 0.713 respectively), and the sensitivity was moderate. It can be seen from the DCA curve that the comprehensive performance of the SVM model is the best (Table S8). It can be seen from Fig. 6b; Table 4 that the SVM model also has a good degree of correction. Overall, the SVM model has the best correlation with the risk of atrial fibrillation in patients with NAFLD.

Model interpretation

For clinical application, machine learning models cannot be used directly without interpretation. Therefore, we used the SHAP framework to interpret the “black box” mechanism of the model, including global and local explanations. The SHAP summary bar plot (Fig. 7a) ranked the feature importance within the model, showing that age, PLR, and creatinine were the three most important features. The SHAP beeswarm plot (Fig. 7b) visually displayed the strength and direction of each feature’s effect on the model; for example, higher age, PLR levels, and creatinine levels, and lower total cholesterol levels, were associated with greater AF risk. Additionally, SHAP dependence plots (Fig. 7d) explained the correlation between each feature and the SHAP value. A SHAP value greater than zero indicates a positive predictive value for the model, suggesting a higher AF risk, while a value less than zero indicates a negative predictive value, pushing the outcome towards a non-AF outcome. Figure 7c showcases the local interpretation of the SHAP model, illustrating the contribution values of various features for an individual sample. For instance, in one AF patient, age (69 years), Scr (74 µmol/L), TC (3.62 mmol/L), and positive CHD history contributed 0.267, 0.205, 0.185, and 0.144, respectively, collectively shifting the results towards AF.

Discussion

NAFLD has become the most common chronic liver disease worldwide, affecting one-third of the adult population [21]. In recent years, its clinical focus has expanded from purely hepatic complications to extrahepatic systemic diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases [22]. AF, as the most common arrhythmia, has a complex pathogenesis that cannot be fully explained by traditional risk factors such as age, hypertension, and diabetes [23]. Recent population cohort studies suggest a significant association between NAFLD and AF independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors (HR ≈ 1.5–2.0) [24, 25], indicating that NAFLD itself can serve as an “emerging risk marker” for AF. Although chronic low-grade inflammation is considered a crucial link between NAFLD and AF, it is difficult to assess precisely in clinical practice. Inflammatory biomarkers, as novel composite indicators of inflammation, are not only easily accessible but have also been found to be closely associated with increased hepatic steatosis and numerous inflammation-related diseases [26–29]. Therefore, exploring the association role of inflammatory biomarkers in the risk of NAFLD-related AF has significant clinical significance.

This study systematically developed and validated a machine learning model for identifying AF in NAFLD patients based on multiple immune inflammatory biomarkers. Our research found that among various inflammatory markers, SII, NLR, PLR, MHR, and SIRI were identified as independent related of AF risk in NAFLD patients. Furthermore, in the subsequent machine learning modeling, the SVM model demonstrated the best overall performance. SHAP analysis revealed age, PLR, and creatinine as key features. Finally, we translated the results into a user-friendly online web calculator for clinical use. These findings provide a new and reliable tool for cardiovascular risk stratification and early identification of AF in NAFLD patients.

The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms linking NAFLD and AF are currently thought to involve chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation, which play key roles in promoting structural and electrophysiological changes in the atria [30–32]. Chronic low-grade inflammation induced by NAFLD is largely cytokine-mediated. Excessive TNF-α activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, promoting the upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators leading to liver inflammation and steatosis; IL-6 activates the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, leading to upregulated transcription of acute-phase reactants, amplifying hepatic inflammation. These surging inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α) activate cardiac fibroblasts, leading to atrial remodeling and electrical instability, thereby contributing to AF occurrence [33–35]. Secondly, in NAFLD, hepatic fat excess leads to increased ROS production, inducing cellular damage that causes cell injury and inflammation within atrial tissue, subsequently triggering atrial fibrosis and exacerbating AF episodes while increasing the risk of thromboembolic events [36].

In clinical studies, inflammation has also been found to be associated with AF occurrence. Zhang et al. found that SII was an independent predictor of AF recurrence after catheter ablation in hypertensive patients (HR 1.13), with superior predictive performance compared to CRP and hsCRP [37]. A recent retrospective cohort study found PLR to be an independent predictor of long-term AF recurrence following ablation after adjusting for the APPLE score [38]. In a follow-up cohort of 5.88 million person-years, Yang et al. showed that the HR for developing AF significantly increased with rising neutrophil count, monocyte count, and NLR [15]. Moreover, a study investigating the association between complete blood count parameters and atrial fibrillation identified NLR, PLR, LMR, SII, MPV/PLT, and MHR as potential early predictors of AF risk [39]. However, the majority of these studies have been conducted in general populations, with limited data available for specific groups like individuals with NAFLD. Our study found that multiple immune-inflammatory markers (e.g., SII, NLR, PLR, MHR) were significantly and independently associated with AF risk in NAFLD patients, consistent with the growing evidence in this field. Particularly noteworthy, MHR demonstrated the strongest association effect (adjusted OR = 2.458), consolidating our team’s previous findings [40]. The MHR functions as a biomarker with a dual character, reflecting both inflammation (monocytes) and lipid metabolism (HDL-C) [41]. On one hand, monocytes represent an active innate immune state, they can be activated by NAFLD-related hepatogenic inflammatory signals and migrate to cardiac tissue, promoting atrial fibrosis and electrical remodeling through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β [42]. On the other hand, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) normally possesses anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions; under inflammatory conditions, HDL transforms into dysfunctional HDL, further exacerbating disturbances in the atrial microenvironment [43, 44]. Therefore, an elevated MHR reflects not only an enhanced pro-inflammatory state but also a weakened anti-inflammatory protective mechanism, a dual pathological process that may be key to its prominent performance in predicting AF risk. Additionally, this study found that PLR had relatively strong predictive power for AF in NAFLD patients (AUC = 0.69), indicating that platelet- and neutrophil-mediated inflammatory pathways remain significant in NAFLD-related AF; these cellular components may further exacerbate atrial dysfunction by promoting microthrombosis and oxidative stress [45].

Although this study focused on the association value of novel immune-inflammatory markers, both the Boruta algorithm and SHAP analysis consistently indicated that age remains the most powerful predictor of AF risk in NAFLD patients. This aligns with previous perspectives [46]. More importantly, after adjusting for this significant confounder, a range of immune-inflammatory markers (e.g., PLR, MHR) still provided independent, significant additional association value. This suggests that among NAFLD patients of the same age, individual inflammatory status is a key factor driving heterogeneity in AF risk.

Although this study employed a retrospective cross-sectional design, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria ensured the homogeneity of the study population and data reliability. For variable processing, a triple feature selection strategy (univariate logistic analysis, Boruta algorithm, and LASSO regression) was used, combining the advantages of filter, wrapper, and embedded methods. Univariate logistic analysis preliminarily screened statistically significant variables (P < 0.05); LASSO regression compressed variable coefficients through L1 regularization, automatically selecting important features and avoiding overfitting [47]; the Boruta algorithm further verified feature importance by creating shadow features for comparison with original features [48]. This multi-step screening strategy ensured the robustness and clinical relevance of the finally selected variables. The eight finally determined predictors (Age; CHD; ALT; Scr; TC; HDL; PLR; MHR) cover multiple dimensions including demographics, cardiometabolic indicators, liver and kidney function, and inflammatory markers, reflecting the complex pathophysiology of the NAFLD-AF association.

Compared to traditional statistical models, machine learning algorithms can better integrate complex biomarkers. This study systematically compared 10 mainstream machine learning algorithms (LR, SVM, GBM, NNET, RF, XGB, KNN, Adaboost, LightGBM, CatBoost), optimized hyperparameters via grid search, and employed 10 rounds of 10-fold cross-validation to ensure model stability. This comprehensive model comparison strategy avoided algorithm selection bias. The results indicated that the SVM model performed best in the test set (AUC = 0.848), likely due to SVM’s advantages in handling high-dimensional data and nonlinear problems, suitable for the complex multivariate relationships in this study. However, machine learning models are often seen as “black boxes,” limiting their clinical adoption. This study enhanced model interpretability through the SHAP framework. SHAP analysis explained the model’s decision-making mechanism from both global feature importance (e.g., age, PLR, creatinine as the top three important features) and local individual prediction levels, revealing the direction and strength of each feature’s impact on AF risk (e.g., higher age, PLR, and creatinine levels associated with greater AF risk). This interpretation method for the “black box” model significantly improved its clinical credibility and usability. Finally, this study translated the predictive model into a web-based clinical calculator, facilitating the rapid translation of research findings into clinical practice. This tool allows clinicians to input 8 routinely available clinical parameters to obtain an individualized AF risk, greatly enhancing the model’s practicality and accessibility.

This study systematically evaluated the performance of various traditional machine learning algorithms in identifying AF risk in NAFLD patients based on immune-inflammatory markers. Although the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model demonstrated the best predictive performance and clinical applicability, we must acknowledge certain methodological limitations of our current work. Firstly, our benchmark tests did not include recently developed ensemble hybrid architectures from the bioinformatics field, such as BitterEN and ANNprob-ACPs [49, 50]. These models, which integrate classical machine learning with deep learning networks, have shown potential for surpassing the capabilities of single-model paradigms in extracting complex biological signals. Secondly, regarding feature selection, while our triple screening strategy ensured variable robustness, it did not fully leverage hybrid feature ranking techniques (e.g., the framework employed by MLAFP-XN [51]). Such techniques, by integrating statistical indicators with neural network-derived relevance scores, could further enhance feature discriminative power and biological interpretability. Finally, we recognize that clinical biomarker data contain implicit temporal or combinatorial patterns, sharing analytical logic with biological sequences like RNA. Future work could analyze longitudinal inflammatory markers in patients by drawing on strategies like the position-specific pattern recognition used in tools such as DHUpredET [52].

This study has limitations. Firstly, the single-center, retrospective design of this study may introduce selection bias. Our institution is a tertiary hospital and may attract patients with more serious comorbidities or complex conditions. This can explain why the prevalence of atrial fibrillation is relatively high in our NAFLD cohort and may limit the promotion of our model in the community or primary care population. In the future, external validation is needed in prospective cocohort studies in different regions and at different levels of medical centers to confirm its generalization ability. Secondly, the cross-sectional design makes it difficult to establish causal inferences; Future prospective cohort studies need to verify the temporal relationship between these inflammatory markers and the risk of atrial fibrillation. Thirdly, although demographic and laboratory test variables are included, residual confounding factors may cause deviations in our results. Finally, although machine learning models have demonstrated good predictive performance, their practicality in actual clinical Settings needs further verification.

Finally, we transformed the predictive model into a web-based clinical calculator, which facilitated the rapid conversion of research results into clinical practice. It is worth noting that this online calculator is only for research and to assist clinical assessment purposes and should not be used as an independent diagnostic tool. Clinical decisions should always be based on a comprehensive assessment by qualified healthcare professionals.

Conclusion

In summary, this study developed and validated an AF risk association model for NAFLD patients by integrating multiple immune-inflammatory markers and machine learning algorithms. SII, NLR, PLR, MHR, and SIRI were identified as independent correlation of AF risk in NAFLD patients, with the SVM model demonstrating optimal performance. The provided risk association tool can be used to identify high-risk NAFLD patients, enabling early screening and intervention for AF. The generalizability of these results requires further confirmation in multi-center, prospective studies.
