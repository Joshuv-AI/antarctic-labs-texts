The cultural adaption and validation of the Self-Regulated Learning Perception Scale (SRLPS) and the development of its short version in Chinese medical students
Authors: Chunyu Xin, Xuemei Cui, Yuxin Song, Ruixue Zhang, Ning Ding, Deliang Wen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelf-regulated learning (SRL) is a pivotal skill in the digital era, closely correlating to academic performance and professional development, particularly within medicine, where rapid advancements in knowledge and technology demand continuous learning. The Self-Regulated Learning Perception Scale (SRLPS), validated for reliability and validity, is a critical tool for assessing SRL among medical students. However, its application in Chinese medical education remains largely unexplored. This study aims to adapt the SRLPS for Chinese medical students and to develop a concise version to reduce participant burden and enhance the scale’s practicality and generalizability.MethodsTwo stages of surveys were conducted. In Stage 1, SRLPS was translated into Chinese through forward translation, back translation, and review and then was administered to 339 medical students. To assess the internal reliability and construct validity of the Chinese version of SRLPS, we calculated Cronbach’s α values and conducted Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), respectively. According to psychometric performance, some items were removed to create a short version of SRLPS (SRLPS-S). In Stage 2, the SRLPS-S was administered to 736 medical students to verify its reliability and construct validity using Cronbach’s α and CFA. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to examine the associations between self-regulated learning perceptions and personal and household characteristics, including gender, maternal education level, and annual household income.ResultsThe Chinese SRLPS demonstrated acceptable internal consistency reliability and construct validity, although the elevated Cronbach’s α indicated possible item redundancy. The SRLPS-S exhibited satisfactory internal consistency reliability and construct validity, confirming the four-domain structure of the original SRLPS. Wilcoxon Rank-sum test showed students’ self-regulated learning positively linked to higher maternal education levels, higher annual household income, and male gender.ConclusionThe Chinese SRLPS and its short version are reliable and valid for assessing self-regulated learning. These scales can assist educators in identifying students who might require additional support in their learning strategies and can also be used for personal development. The short version is recommended for its enhanced psychometric properties and greater cost-efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06354-6.

Introduction

In the era of digital technology, learners encounter increasingly cognitive-complicate and knowledge-concentrated tasks frequently [1]. Nevertheless, with the abundance of educational resources accessible in the dynamic digital realm [2], learners now have more chances to expand their knowledge beyond the confines of conventional classrooms. Meanwhile, learners are required to possess sophisticated skills [3] and develop strategies for learning independently [4, 5], namely self-regulated learning behaviors. It has been well demonstrated that these behaviors could enhance academic performance [6, 7] and potentially boost career prospects [8]; thus, they are regarded as one crucial survival skill in the 21st century.

The concept of self-regulated learning was first introduced at the 1986 annual meeting of the American Educational Research Association [9]. It was defined as students’ active participation in their learning, including their metacognitive, motivational, and behavioral processes [10]. Self-regulation was further argued as a combination of learners’ behavior, self-awareness, and motivational beliefs before, during, and after their learning [11, 12]. Through the social cognitive model lens, Zimmerman viewed self-regulated learning as a proactive process of setting goals, selecting and deploying strategies, and monitoring effectiveness to acquire academic skills [13]. Research has shown that self-regulated learners are likelier to reach their potential [10] and achieve better academic performance [6, 7] than traditional learning methods. Moreover, self-regulated learning can be more effective in acquiring knowledge, skills, and attitudes [14], and these learning outcomes could be maintained longer [15].

The importance of physicians engaging in SRL is evident in medicine because patient care must meet high standards in such a fast-changing field [16]. Medical education thus aims to foster SRL in students to adapt to changes and enhance their medical and clinical skills [6], making self-regulated learning an essential skill for medical students. Previous studies found that medical students with sophisticated self-regulated learning skills tend to be highly motivated and confident in themselves, which leads to better academic performance [17]. They typically tried various deep learning strategic approaches and used those proven effective [18], and could explore concepts at a deeper level and achieve better academic performance [19]. Furthermore, self-regulated learners may use strategic thinking and actions when learning clinical skills. As a result, self-regulated learning skills could be a significant predictor of clinical skills development [20]. In addition, self-regulated learning skills can help medical students realistically self-assess their cognition and behaviors and become lifelong learners [18]. In this way, medical students could adapt to persistently developing patient care models and expanding medical knowledge, stay updated with the leading edge in this professional field [18, 21], and satisfy the demanding requirements of medical services when practicing in the future [14].

Several scales assess self-regulated learning, such as the Learning and Study Strategies Inventory (LASSI) [22, 23] and the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ) [24, 25], with Chinese versions available. Despite this, this study chose to culturally adapt the SRLPS developed by Turan [26], mainly for the following reasons. Firstly, this scale is designed using a conceptual framework like Zimmerman’s [26, 27], with a clear structure that includes four domains: Motivation and Action for Learning, Planning and Goal Setting, Strategy Use and Assessment, and Lack of Self-Directedness. Secondly, with 41 items, the SRLPS is more concise than scales containing over 80 items, capturing the essence of self-regulated learning cost-efficiently. Finally and importantly, the SRLPS has consistently shown sound psychometric properties, including internal consistency reliability and construct validity, among medical student populations across various countries, such as Turkey [26–28], Brazil [29], Pakistan [30], and Portugal [31, 32]. However, some studies have reported Cronbach’s α values exceeding 0.90 for the SRLPS, suggesting potential redundancy among the items. Lengthy scales can impact respondents’ willingness to complete questionnaires, potentially leading to low response rates and missing data, which can compromise survey quality and limit the scale’s utility [33, 34]. Therefore, further item reduction may be necessary to enhance its practicality and prevalence.

To satisfy the increasing demand for theoretical and empirical research on self-regulated learning behavior [35, 36], the current study aimed to cross-culturally translate and adapt SRLPS into Chinese and verify psychometric properties in Chinese medical students. For broader adoption and reasonable use of the SRLPS, the study aimed to shorten the scale while maintaining its reliability and validity. Overall, this study will focus on (1) the validation of the Chinese version of SRLPS in Chinese medical students and (2) the development and validation of the SRLPS-S in Chinese.

Methods

Survey design

The study can be divided into two stages. In Stage 1, SRLPS was adapted into Chinese, and its psychometric properties were tested based on a survey. Furthermore, the SRLPS-S was developed. In Stage 2, the reliability and validity of the SRLPS-S were verified based on another survey. The associations between self-regulated learning behaviors and individual characteristics were analyzed.

Stage 1

Study instrument

The SRLPS developed by Turan et al. contains four domains, i.e., Motivation and action to learning (7 items), Planning and goal setting (8 items), Strategies for learning and assessment (19 items), and Lack of self-directedness (7 items) [26]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for each domain, reported as 0.88, 0.91, 0.83, and 0.76, respectively [26], demonstrate solid internal consistency reliability of SRLPS. Items of Motivation and action to learning, Planning and goal setting, and Strategies for learning and assessment are scored from 1 (Disagree Strongly) to 5 (Agree Strongly). The numerical sequence corresponding to the score was inverted in the dimension Lack of self-directedness, with 1 indicating Agree Strongly and 5 indicating Disagree Strongly. The total score ranges from 41 to 205, with a higher score indicating better self-regulated learning skills.

Translation of the SRLPS

A three-step linguistic validation procedure employed included forward translation, back translation, and review [37]. Firstly, the SRLPS was independently translated into Chinese by N.D. and one colleague from China Medical University. All translators and authors reconciled the discrepancies in meaning and expressions in a consensus meeting, which yielded an initial version. Secondly, back translation was independently performed by a Chinese Canadian student and an English teacher (R.Z.) from China Medical University, who were unaware of the original English version of SRLPS. Finally, an expert committee, including all researchers and translators, performed the reconciliation review. The backward translation was compared with the original scale, contradictions were discussed, and discrepancies were resolved until two versions were confirmed identical. Then twenty medical students were recruited to complete the scale in Chinese and then asked to check cultural relevance and to point out any ambiguities or misunderstandings. According to their feedback, the Chinese version of SRLPS was finalized (see Appendix 1). The entire process is depicted in Fig. 1.

Participants and procedure

To test the reliability and validity of SRLPS, in September 2019, we randomly invited third-year undergraduate medical students at China Medical University to participate in our first-round survey. The rationale for choosing third-year students is as follows: As Chinese third-year medical students begin to delve deeper into their specialized fields and participate in clinical clerkships [38], the increasing academic load propels them to become proficient self-regulated learners, capable of effectively managing their cognition, motivation, and behavior throughout the demanding academic journey [39]. Consequently, students at this stage may display a range of self-regulated learning behaviors, which makes them an ideal group for validating the scale’s reliability and validity. Eligible participants were third-year medical students at China Medical University who enrolled in 2017. Students were excluded if they were part-time, transferred from a different major, failed a year, or took time off during medical school.

The survey was administrated through Wenjuanxing (Wenjuanxing Tech Co. Ltd, Changsha, China), a local Chinese commercial online survey platform. This platform’s flexibility allows participants to respond to the survey via either computers or mobile devices, thereby offering convenience and reducing the potential for self-selection bias due to disparities in technical equipment. Furthermore, the platform’s support has significantly minimized missing values in our survey data, ensuring the high quality and reliability of the survey. Besides SRLPS, the questionnaire also collected participants’ gender information (complete questionnaires in Appendix 2).

Development of the short version of SRLPS

We shortened the items based on psychometric properties as well as content criteria. Items with the following criteria were considered to remove: (1) corrected item-total correlation (CITC) value less than 0.30 and an increase in Cronbach’s α value when deleted; (2) high modification index value suggesting a strong correlation with other items. The scale’s construct remained unchanged, including the number and meaning of the domains. The authors carefully selected to ensure that each domain was adequately covered and had a reasonable number of items.

Stage 2

Study instrument

All participants filled out the SRLPS-S and provided individual characteristics, including gender, maternal education level (junior high school or below; high school; university; or master’s degree or above), and annual household income (less than 10,000 CNY; 10,001 to 20,000 CNY; 20,001 to 50,000 CNY; 50,001 to 100,000 CNY; or more than 100,000 CNY), see Appendix 2.

Previous studies found that socioeconomic status (SES) was associated with children’s learning behaviors, with wealthier families typically investing more money and time in their children’s education [40, 41], and among the core dimensions of SES - occupation, income, and education - maternal education emerged as the strongest predictor, significantly influencing other family resources that are strongly correlated with children’s future development [42]. In China, mothers are predominant in child-rearing, and children perceive high maternal parenting attributes [43]. Therefore, we included the confounding factors of self-regulated learning, including maternal education level, maternal occupation, and household income. It is hypothesized that a higher level of maternal education and higher household income may correlate with an increased level of children’s SRL.

Participants and procedure

To examine the psychometric properties of SRLPS-S, the second-round survey was implemented in November 2019. All full-time third-year undergraduate medical students at China Medical University were asked to participate in this study voluntarily.

Statistical analysis

Reliability. Cronbach’s α was calculated to assess the internal consistency of SRLPS and SRLPS-S and their domains. An α value of more than 0.70 was considered acceptable [44].

Validity. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to test the SRLPS and SRLPS-S’ factor structure. Goodness-of-fit indices reported in this research include Chi-square to the degrees of freedom (χ2/df), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). The model was considered acceptable if the valve of χ2/df was less than 5.00 [45], CFI and TLI were greater than 0.90, and RMSEA and SRMR were less than 0.08 [46].

Frequencies and percentages were used to describe the categorical variables, while means and standard deviation (SD) were computed to describe continuous variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the correlations between SRLPS and SRLPS-S and their domains. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to identify the differences in the SRLPS scores, which were probably caused by demographic characteristics, including gender, maternal education level, and household income. Statistical analysis was performed using Stata (version 15.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX), and a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

The ethical approvals of the study were obtained from the China Medical University Ethics Committee (2017075). The surveys were voluntary, and each participant signed an informed consent. All information collected in the study was guaranteed to be used only for scientific research and kept confidential.

Stage 2

Demographics

As shown in Table 1, of 969 third-year medical students, 736 consented to participate in this study and filled out questionnaires, resulting in a response rate of 75.95%. Of the total respondents, 58.56% (N = 431) were female. Respondents were evenly distributed among the four levels of maternal education, and most (41.98%) reported annual household incomes ranging from 50,001 to 100,000 CNY, whereas 8.15% reported incomes below 10,000 Chinese yuan.

Validation of SRLPS-S

The Cronbach’s α value was 0.89 for the total scale and 0.85, 0.85, 0.92, and 0.93 for the four domains, respectively (see Table 2). All the values above fell in the acceptable range and showed good reliability of the short version scale.

The path diagram of SRLPS-S is presented in Fig. 3b. Consistent with the results of Stage 1, standardized factor loadings of all items showed significant and high values varying from 0.69 to 0.92. On verifying the goodness-of-fit of the SRLPS-S, χ2/df was 2.78, CFI was 0.97, TLI was 0.97, RMSEA was 0.05, and SRMR was 0.04 (see Table 3). All the index values met the required standard, indicating the stability of the construct.

Connection with demographics

Table 6 presented differences in Chinese medical students’ perception of self-regulated learning by demographic characteristics. Males’ overall perception was significantly higher than females’ (p < 0.01). In the domains of Motivation and action to learning and Lack of self-directedness, males had significantly higher scores than females. In contrast, in the domains Planning and goal setting and Strategies for learning and assessment, the gender difference is not significant. The maternal educational level might lead to a significant difference in overall perception and its first three domains, i.e., Motivation and action to learning, Planning and goal setting, and Strategies for learning and assessment. Annual household income was also associated with students’ perceptions. However, the significance is only marginal (p < 0.05). Medical students with the lowest annual household income scored the lowest in the perception of Planning and goal setting. In contrast, those whose annual household income was higher than 50,000 CNY scored the highest, and the difference is statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study attempted to translate and validate the SRLPS in Chinese and further developed its short version. The results verified acceptable psychometric properties of SRLPS and SRLPS-S, demonstrating their potential to be applied in future research on evaluating self-regulating learning perception in Chinese medical students.

Despite the acceptable reliability and validity of SRLPS in Chinese medical students, the development of its short version is still rational. Firstly, for researchers, a short scale could help release the tension caused by limited research budgets. In the face of increasingly fierce competition for research funding, researchers must budget and spend every penny wisely. When developing a study protocol, striking a balance between valid measures and the length of questionnaires can be a common issue. Short but reliable and valid scales mean financial efficiency and are usually preferred [47]. Secondly, lengthy scales could burden survey participants, possibly deterring them from completing the questionnaire, thereby reducing the effective response rate and survey quality [48]. Thirdly, scales with redundant items could have an extremely high Cronbach’s α. It was argued that an α value over 0.90 might imply a need to shorten the scale [49], and in some studies, the criteria were raised to 0.95 [50]. Cronbach’s α for SRLPS is valued at 0.95 and ranges between 0.90 and 0.96 for four domains, which are very close to the threshold proposed. After shortening the scale by deleting 22 items, the range of Cronbach’s α value changed to between 0.85 and 0.93, indicating that the short version is still reasonably reliable although much briefer.

Pearson correlation coefficients between each domain were also calculated to demonstrate structural consistency between SRLPS and SRLPS-S. The study found that using either SRLPS or SRLPS-S, the correlation coefficients between any two of the first three domains (Motivation and Action to Learning, Planning and Goal Setting, and Strategies for Learning and Assessment) were high. However, there were only weak correlations between these three domains and the final domain, Lack of Self-Directedness. This might be explained by the difference between self-regulated learning and a similar terminology - self-directed learning. Self-regulated learning refers to using cognitive and metacognitive strategies to achieve intended learning [51] and usually takes place in an academic environment. The first three domains cover almost all the aspects of regulated learning mentioned above, thus highly correlated with each other. In contrast, self-directed learning emphasizes students’ autonomy in the learning process beyond what the curriculum or teacher instructs, often outside of the classroom [52]. With self-regulated learning, teachers impose the learning goal, while students with self-directed learning are allowed more freedom in designing the learning environment [52]. In this way, it is likely that some learners are skillful in learning regulation but lack self-directedness. If learning goals formulated by teachers do not match learners’ goals, the effect of self-directedness on learning outcomes might be limited [53]. For example, as one empirical research demonstrated, medical students’ perception of benefiting from problem-based learning was weakly associated with Lack of self-directedness but strongly with all the other three domains of SRLPS [27].

The role of family socioeconomic status in influencing self-regulated learning abilities is crucial, as suggested by the family capital theory. Previous studies have demonstrated that students with better maternal education backgrounds or higher average annual household incomes tend to have higher self-regulated learning abilities, including greater learning motivation, better learning environments, and improved self-adjustment skills [54]. In this study, maternal education level and annual household income significantly correlated with students’ ability to self-regulate their learning. Mothers usually play a vital role in raising children [43] and significantly influence children’s learning behaviors and strategies [55]. Better-educated mothers tend to be involved earlier in children’s education [56], provide more helpful assistance in the learning process [54], and spend more time actively developing children’s talents and skills [57]. Students from wealthier families are more likely to have a better educational environment [58], which includes more accessible access to technological resources and home learning experiences, which might help children form learning strategies and foster self-regulated learning skills.

This study found that male students tended to perform better in self-regulated learning skills, although previous studies have not consistently shown the relationship between gender and self-regulated learning. Some suggested a strong link between males and high self-regulated learning skills [54], while others have not noticed any differences[59]. The need for more research to explore the underlying mechanisms to explain this gender difference is urgent and essential.

Limitations and future directions

This study only involved third-year medical students from one institution, which undermines the generalization of our research conclusions. Chinese medical students usually start to gradually specialize and participate in clinical clerkships in the third grade, during which the workload and difficulty of courses increase [38]. As a result, third-year medical students’ self-regulated learning behaviors are related to their academic performance and future careers as physicians, so they need much attention. Additionally, it is worth noting that by 2019, 105 medical schools, accounting for 75.5% of institutions in China, had been accredited for clinical medicine by the Working Committee for the Accreditation of Medical Education (WCAME) of the Ministry of Education (MoE), suggesting homogeneity in medical education across Chinese medical schools [60]. Nevertheless, in future research, it is crucial to further enhance the sampling framework by incorporating more medical institutions and students from various grades to validate the psychometric properties of SRLPS and SRLPS-S and gain a deeper understanding of the heterogeneity of medical students’ self-regulated learning behaviors.

Although this study comprised two rounds of surveys, the design nature is still cross-sectional, meaning that the test-retest reliability cannot be verified. Since test-retest reliability could inform the rational use of one scale in occasions needing multiple evaluations of single participants, it is particularly vital for intervention studies on self-regulated learning. Thus, a longitudinal study is necessary to monitor students’ self-regulated learning perception over time and confirm the test-retest reliability of SRLPS.

Another limitation is that the scales’ criterion validity was not confirmed in this study, although the study examined some confounding individual factors of self-regulated learning, such as maternal education level and household income. Future studies could benefit from further demonstrating the connection of SRLPS or SRLPS-S scores with other self-regulated learning measurements or even students’ academic performance.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the SRLPS and its short version showed sound reliability and validity, indicating their reasonable use for assessing SRL in a Chinese sample. The scales could be used for educational assessments to identify students needing support for learning behaviors, self-detection, and improvement. The SRLPS-S might be the preferred option in research due to its superior psychometric properties and cost efficiency enhanced by its more concise length.
