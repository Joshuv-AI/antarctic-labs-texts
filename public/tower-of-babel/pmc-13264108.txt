Prenatal cigarette exposure induces offspring hyperactivity and affective alterations via gut –brain axis metabolic dysregulation and region-specific neuronal hyperactivation
Authors: Tian-Lei Zhang, Wen Qiu, Xin-Yu Shi, Yu-Han Gou, Yan-Ran Shi, Yuan-Jie Bi, Yue Wang, Hai-dong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prenatal cigarette exposure (PCE) is a major preventable risk factor for neurodevelopmental disorders such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), but the underlying mechanisms extending beyond direct developmental neurotoxicity remain poorly defined. This study investigated the long-term integrative effects of PCE on offspring behavior, neuronal activation, and gut metabolome using an established mouse model. Pregnant C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to whole-body cigarette smoke or control air exposure from pre-mating until birth. Compared to controls, PCE offspring exhibited a transient reduction in early postnatal weight gain, followed by a robust hyperactive phenotype in adolescence and adulthood, accompanied by increased despair-like behavior without deficits in social interaction. These behavioral alterations were associated with region-specific neuronal hyperactivation, characterized by a significant increase in c-Fos–positive cells in the paraventricular area (PVA) and basal ganglia (BG) — regions implicated in stress integration and motor regulation, respectively, while no significant changes were detected in the medial prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, basolateral amygdala, or nucleus accumbens. Furthermore, PCE induced selective microglial activation in the PVA, accompanied by impaired intestinal barrier integrity as evidenced by reduced colonic Claudin-5 expression. Untargeted fecal metabolomics revealed a persistent reprogramming of gut metabolic pathways, including glycerophospholipid metabolism, phosphatidylinositol signaling, and arachidonic acid metabolism. Together, these findings demonstrate that prenatal cigarette exposure induces enduring behavioral and metabolic abnormalities that correlate with selective neuroimmune and neuronal alterations, highlighting region-specific gut–brain axis correlates of PCE-induced neurobehavioral alterations.

Introduction

Maternal smoking during pregnancy and lactation is a well-recognized environmental risk factor for neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) in offspring, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (Thapar et al., 2003; Linnet et al., 2003; David, 2013). Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that maternal exposure to secondhand smoke before and during pregnancy may increase the risk of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) in offspring (Thapar et al., 2003; Langley et al., 2012), and that both maternal and paternal smoking during pregnancy are associated with increased ADHD risk in offspring, suggesting potential intrauterine mechanisms beyond genetic confounding (Langley et al., 2012).Notably, the prevalence of low birth weight following PCE and the increased risk of ASD have been well documented, underscoring the public health significance of this exposure (Gustavson et al., 2017; Thapar et al., 2009; Arafat and Minică, 2018). Nicotine, the primary psychoactive component in cigarette smoke, can cross both the placental barrier and the blood-brain barrier (Dwyer et al., 2008; Slotkin, 2004). By disrupting cholinergic signaling pathways and dopamine reward pathways, it directly impairs the brain development processes of fetuses and neonates (Slotkin, 2004; Bailey et al., 2012). However, the precise biological mechanisms by which prenatal cigarette exposure programs offspring brain development and induces long-term behavioral abnormalities remain incompletely elucidated.

Traditional research has focused on the direct effects of nicotine on the developing brain. Although previous studies have emphasized widespread neurotransmitter alterations following prenatal nicotine exposure (Dwyer et al., 2008; Slotkin, 2004), whether prenatal cigarette exposure induces persistent and region-specific alterations in neuronal activity patterns during adolescence and adulthood remains insufficiently understood (Ernst et al., 2001; da Rocha et al., 2025; Shaw et al., 2007). Immediate early genes such as c-Fos provide a sensitive readout of neuronal activation at the regional level, enabling the identification of selectively vulnerable neural substrates rather than assuming global circuit dysfunction (Dickstein et al., 2006a; Kovács, 2008; Morgan and Curran, 1991; Lin et al., 2021). Therefore, investigating c-Fos expression in specific brain regions of offspring exposed to prenatal cigarette smoke—either under basal conditions or following behavioral tests in adulthood—is of great significance for understanding intrinsic excitability changes in their neural circuits. The brain regions analyzed in this study (medial prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, basolateral amygdala, nucleus accumbens, paraventricular area, and basal ganglia) were selected based on their established roles in motor regulation, stress integration, and ADHD-related circuit dysfunction(Dickstein et al., 2006b; Castellanos and Proal, 2012; Samea et al., 2019).

Beyond its direct effects on the central nervous system, prenatal cigarette exposure may indirectly impact offspring neurodevelopment via gut-brain axis pathways (Dinan and Cryan, 2017; Mayer et al., 2014). Gut microbes and their metabolites can transmit signals to the brain through immune, neuroendocrine, and vagal routes (Cryan and Dinan, 2012; Morais et al., 2021), with the potential to modulate neuroinflammation and neural homeostasis (Braniste et al., 2014; Erny et al., 2015; Perry and Holmes, 2014; Paolicelli et al., 2011). Environmental stressors during pregnancy, including toxin exposure, have been associated with disruption of offspring gut microbiota and subsequent behavioral abnormalities in preclinical models (Ratsika et al., 2024; Krautkramer et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2023; Miyake et al., 2021).

In human neurodevelopmental disorders, individuals with ADHD exhibit aberrant microbial profiles and alterations in nicotinamide metabolism (Lu et al., 2025), and fecal transplantation from ADHD donors into rodents has been shown to induce hyperactivity and attentional instability (Lu et al., 2025). Complementarily, symptom-specific profiling reveals that short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing taxa (e.g., Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis) are depleted across ADHD dimensional presentations, and restoration of these taxa in FMT mice ameliorates both hyperactivity and inattention (Wang et al.). Clinically, fecal acetic and propionic acid concentrations correlate inversely with inattention and hyperactive/impulsive severity scores in children (Boonchooduang et al.), while distinct bacterial genera (e.g., Ruminococcaceae UCG_004) track inattention symptoms specifically (Boonchooduang et al.). Mechanistically, microbial metabolites may influence these dimensional phenotypes through microglia-mediated pathways that preferentially target subcortical and prefrontal circuits (Cao et al., 2025), with gut-derived signals showing regional selectivity for stress-responsive brain areas (Cao et al., 2025; Barki et al., 2022). Although systematic evidence remains limited on how prenatal cigarette exposure specifically alters the adult offspring gut metabolome and related neurobehavioral phenotypes (da Rocha et al., 2025; Zare et al., 2025), randomized trial data indicate that perinatal probiotic supplementation can improve postpartum mood outcomes (Slykerman et al., 2017), suggesting that early-life microbial interventions may offer a feasible strategy to modify neurodevelopmental trajectories affected by prenatal exposures.

Furthermore, environmental exposure in early life is often accompanied by alterations in growth and development trajectories. Reports have indicated that prenatal cigarette exposure may be associated with low birth weight or preterm birth in offspring (Barker, 1986; Barker et al., 1989; Thapar and Rutter, 2009), and such changes in developmental trajectories may themselves serve as early warning indicators for future neurobehavioral problems. Therefore, integrating growth and development parameters with neurobehavioral and molecular indicators for analysis can provide a more comprehensive perspective on the overall effects of prenatal exposure.

Against this background, the present study aimed to systematically investigate the comprehensive long-term effects of prenatal cigarette exposure on offspring using an animal model. We tested the hypothesis that prenatal cigarette exposure alters the postnatal growth trajectory of offspring and induces hyperactivity and affective changes in adolescence and adulthood, and that these behavioral abnormalities correlate with sustained increases in neuronal activity in specific brain regions (as assessed by c-Fos expression) and significant dysregulation of the fecal metabolome. To address this hypothesis, we monitored offspring weight changes during early postnatal life, conducted behavioral tests (open field test, elevated plus maze, tail suspension test, and three-chamber social interaction test) during adolescence, used immunofluorescence staining to detect c-Fos expression and microglial status in predefined brain regions, assessed intestinal barrier integrity via Claudin-5 staining, and performed untargeted metabolomics to analyze the fecal metabolomic profile of adult offspring By integrating multi-dimensional data on behavior, neural activity, and gut metabolism, this study seeks to provide new insights into the correlates of hyperactivity and affective alterations in offspring induced by prenatal cigarette exposure—particularly by clarifying the potential roles of neuronal activity and the gut-brain axis in this process.

Materials and methods

Animals and prenatal cigarette exposure

Animals and housing

This investigation was conducted in accordance with the welfare guidelines for laboratory animals and received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Shandong First Medical University (Approval No. W202512190940). C57BL/6J female and male mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from Shandong Pengyue Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Jinan, China). Upon arrival, all mice were allowed to acclimate for one week under standard specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Animals were housed in a temperature-controlled (22 ± 1 °C) and humidity-controlled (50 ± 10%) environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 a.m.). Food and water were provided ad libitum throughout the experiment.

Prenatal cigarette exposure protocol

A prenatal cigarette exposure (PCE) model was established in this study using a whole-body cigarette smoke exposure paradigm as previously described with modifications (Janbazacyabar et al., 2021; Gonzalez-Padilla et al., 2024). Briefly, twelve age-matched female mice were randomly assigned into two groups: the control group (CON, n = 6) and the prenatal cigarette exposure group (PCE, n = 6). The randomization was performed using a random number table to ensure unbiased group allocation.

PCE Regimen: One week prior to mating, female mice in the PCE group were subjected to whole-body cigarette smoke exposure. The exposure was conducted in a custom-made, ventilated exposure chamber (dimensions: 40 cm × 30 cm × 25 cm, YU YAN Instruments, China) twice daily (at 9:00 a.m. and 3:00 p.m.). In each session, three commercial cigarettes (Hong Tashan, containing 12 mg tar and 1 mg nicotine per cigarette) were ignited and allowed to combust completely. The smoke was pumped into the chamber, and the exposure duration per session was approximately 15-20 min, or until the cigarettes were fully consumed. This regimen was designed to simulate moderate to heavy smoking in humans. Control mice were placed in an identical chamber for the same duration but were exposed to fresh, filtered air.

Mating and Continued Exposure: After one week of acclimation and exposure, female mice were housed with sexually mature male mice in a 1:1 ratio. Pregnancy was confirmed by the presence of a vaginal plug checked every morning between 8:00 and 9:00 a.m.; the day a vaginal plug was detected was designated as gestational day 0.5 (G0.5). Dams exhibiting vaginal plugs were subsequently singly housed to facilitate individual monitoring. The cigarette smoke exposure protocol continued throughout the entire gestation period until the day of parturition.

Postnatal Care and Weaning: Pregnant dams were allowed to deliver and raise their offspring naturally. The average number of offspring per dam was 6.0 ± 1.6 (mean ± SD) in the control group (n = 7 dams) and 7.2 ± 2.3 in the PCE group (n = 9 dams). The latency from mating to delivery (pregnancy duration) was 28.5 ± 12.5 days for control dams and 29.6 ± 12.7 days for PCE dams. Neither measure differed significantly between groups (offspring number: p = 0.26; latency: p = 0.87, unpaired t-test), indicating that the cigarette smoke exposure did not affect maternal fertility or litter size (Supplementary Table S1). Litters were culled to 4-6 pups per dam on postnatal day 1 (PND1) to standardize nutrition and maternal care. Pups remained with their biological mothers until weaning at PND21. After weaning, offspring were group-housed by sex and litter, with a maximum of 4-5 mice per cage. All offspring, regardless of maternal exposure, were maintained on a standard chow diet and were not subjected to any further direct smoke exposure. For all subsequent experiments, a maximum of two male and two female offspring from each litter were used for each test to avoid litter effects, in accordance with established recommendations (Zorrilla, 1997; Percie Du Sert et al., 2020).

Monitoring of offspring body weight

The body weight of all offspring was monitored longitudinally to assess the impact of prenatal cigarette exposure on physical development. Pups were weighed on the day of birth (PND0) and subsequently at key developmental time points: PND4, PND7, PND14, and PND21 (weaning). After weaning, body weight was recorded weekly until adulthood (PND90). A precision electronic balance (ZOGGI, China) was used for all measurements. Data were analyzed separately for males and females.

Behavioral testing of adolescent and adult offspring

Behavioral assessments were conducted on two cohorts of offspring: one during adolescence (starting at PND45) and one in adulthood (starting at PND90). All behavioral tests were performed during the light phase (between 9:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m.) in a dedicated, sound-attenuated behavioral testing room. Illumination intensity during testing was 50 ± 5 lux as measured at the center of the apparatus The testing apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol and allowed to dry completely between trials for each animal to eliminate olfactory cues. All sessions were recorded by a ceiling-mounted camera and analyzed using the SMART video tracking system (Version 3.0, Panlab S.L., Spain). Mice were habituated to the testing room for 30 min before each test.

Open field test

The open field test was used to assess locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior in a novel environment, as previously described (Prut and Belzung, 2003). The apparatus consisted of a square white Plexiglas arena (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm). Before testing, mice were habituated to the testing room for 30 min. Each mouse was gently placed in the center of the arena and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. The entire session was recorded using an ANY-maze video tracking system (Stoelting Co., USA). The following parameters were analyzed:-Total distance traveled (cm): a measure of general locomotor activity.-Distance traveled in corners: the arena floor was divided into 16 equal squares; the four corner squares were defined as the corner zone.-Center zone time: the central 20 cm × 20 cm area was defined as the center zone; reduced time in the center indicates increased anxiety-like behavior.

Elevated plus maze test

Anxiety-like behavior was further evaluated using the elevated plus maze, as previously described (Walf and Frye, 2007). The apparatus was made of black Plexiglas and consisted of two open arms (30 cm × 5 cm) and two enclosed arms (30 cm × 5 cm × 15 cm) extending from a central platform (5 cm × 5 cm). The entire maze was elevated 50 cm above the floor. Before testing, mice were habituated to the testing room for 30 min. Each mouse was placed on the central platform, facing an open arm, and allowed to explore the maze for 10 min. Tail suspension test

Depression-like behavior and behavioral despair were assessed using the tail suspension test (Cryan et al., 2005). Mice were securely suspended by the tail from a metal rod using adhesive tape (the tip of the tail was placed approximately 50 cm above the floor). The test session lasted for 6 min. The entire session was recorded, and the SMART system was used to measure the total duration of immobility during the final 4 min of the test. Immobility was defined as the absence of any limb or body movement, except those necessary for respiration. A longer immobility time is interpreted as increased despair-like behavior.

Three-chamber social interaction test

Sociability and social novelty recognition were evaluated using the three-chamber social interaction test, as previously described (Moy et al., 2004). The apparatus consisted of three interconnected chambers (center chamber: 45 cm × 10 cm × 25 cm; left/right chambers: 10 cm × 10 cm × 30 cm) with small openings allowing free access. The dividers between chambers contained grilles for olfactory and limited tactile interaction. Before testing, mice were habituated to the testing room for 30 min. The test comprised three phases, each lasting 10 min:(1)Habituation: The test mouse was placed in the center chamber and allowed to explore all three empty chambers.(2)Sociability: An unfamiliar conspecific mouse (stranger 1) was placed in a wire mesh cup in the left chamber; an identical empty cup was placed in the right chamber. The test mouse was placed in the center chamber and allowed to explore freely. The social interaction index was calculated as (time with social cup − time with empty cup)/(time with social cup + time with empty cup).(3)Social novelty recognition: A second unfamiliar conspecific mouse (stranger 2) was placed in the previously empty cup (right chamber), while stranger 1 remained in the left cup. The social preference index was calculated as time with stranger 2/(time with stranger 1 + time with stranger 2) × 100%.

Immunofluorescence staining and quantification

Tissue preparation and sectioning

A subset of mice from the adult cohort (PND90) was used for immunofluorescence analysis. Approximately 90 min after the completion of the final behavioral test (to allow for optimal c-Fos protein expression), mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and subsequently sacrificed. They were perfused intracardially with 0.9% saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The brain and colon tissues were rapidly dissected, postfixed in 4% PFA solution at 4 °C overnight, then cryoprotected by immersion in 30% sucrose solution until they sank to the bottom. Coronal brain sections (40 μm thick) and colon cross sections (10 μm thick) were obtained using a cryostat (Leica CM1950, Germany) and mounted onto glass slides for storage at −20 °C. Brain sections were collected at the following bregma coordinates according to the Paxinos and Franklin mouse brain atlas: medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC, +1.77 to +1.97 mm), nucleus accumbens (NAc, +1.33 to +1.69 mm), basal ganglia (BG, +0.25 to +0.61 mm), paraventricular area (PVA, −0.23 to −0.47 mm), basolateral amygdala (BLA, −0.95 to −1.07 mm), and hippocampal CA regions (CA, −1.55 to −1.79 mm).

Immunofluorescence staining

Sections were permeabilized with PBST (0.1 M PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100) for 30 min at room temperature, then blocked with PBST containing 5% normal goat serum for 1 h to block nonspecific staining. For immunofluorescence staining, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against c-Fos (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology), Iba1 (1:500, Affinity Biosciences) and Claudin-5 (1:500, Affinity Biosciences) diluted in blocking solution at 4 °C overnight. On the next day, sections were rinsed three times with PBS (5 min each) and incubated with corresponding fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000, Proteintech) at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. After three additional PBS washes (5 min each), sections were stained with DAPI (YuanYe Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.) for 5 min to label cell nuclei. Finally, slides were sealed with anti-fade mounting medium. Images were acquired using a laser scanning microscope (Pannoram MIDI Digital Scanner, Japan) and analyzed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). For microglial morphological analysis, soma size and process length were measured using ImageJ's skeleton analysis plugin (Young and Morrison, 2018). Activation status was defined by increased Iba1 intensity and decreased process complexity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0, USA). Data are presented as individual data points overlaid with mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene's test. Comparisons between two independent groups were analyzed using two tailed independent Student's t-tests. For analyses involving three or more groups, one way or two-way ANOVA was employed, with Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test applied for multiple comparisons.

Litter Effect Control: To account for potential litter effects, the "litter" was initially considered as a random factor in a linear mixed model. However, as no more than two pups per sex per litter were used for any given endpoint, and the results from the mixed model did not differ from the standard ANOVA, the latter is reported for simplicity, in line with common practice in the field. Sex was initially included as a factor in all analyses; no significant sex × treatment interactions were detected (p > 0.05 for all), thus data were collapsed across sex for final presentation. Sex-stratified data are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. Due to the lack of sex-specific effects in behavioral measures and the limited sample size for downstream assays, all subsequent experiments (including c-Fos immunofluorescence, Iba1 staining, Claudin-5 analysis, and untargeted metabolomics) were performed on a mixed-sex cohort with balanced representation (approximately 50% male and 50% female per group), and data were analyzed without sex stratification unless otherwise specified. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Sample preparation and extraction

Fecal samples were collected from adult offspring (PND90), with six biological replicates per group, totaling 12 samples. After collection, samples stored at-80 °C refrigerator were thawed on ice. A 400 μL solution (Methanol: Water = 7:3, V/V) containing internal standard was added into 20 mg sample, and vortexed for 3 min. The sample was sonicated in an ice bath for 10 min and vortexed for 1 min, and then placed at −20 °C for 30 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min (4 °C). The sediment was removed, then the supernatant was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 3 min (4 °C). A 200 μL aliquot of supernatant was transferred for LC-MS analysis.

HPLC conditions

All samples were for two LC/MS methods. One aliquot was analyzed using positive ion conditions and was eluted from T3 column (Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 Column 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm ∗100 mm) using 0.1 % formic acid in water as solvent A and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile as solvent B in the following gradient: 5 to 20 % in 2 min, increased to 60 % in the following 3 min, increased to 99 % in 1 min and held for 1.5 min, then come back to 5 % mobile phase B within 0.1 min, held for 2.4 min. The analytical conditions were as follows, column temperature, 40 °C; flow rate, 0.4 mL/min; injection volume, 4 μL; Another aliquot was using negative ion conditions and was the same as the elution gradient of positive mode (Chen et al., 2009).

MS conditions (QE)

All the methods alternated between full scan MS and data dependent MSn scans using dynamic exclusion. MS analyses were carried out using electrospray ionization in the positive ion mode and negative ion mode using full scan analysis over m/z 75-1000 at 35000 resolutions. Additional MS settings are: ion spray voltage, 3.5 KV or 3.2 KV in positive or negative modes, respectively; Sheath gas (Arb), 30; Aux gas, 5; Ion transfer tube temperature, 320 °C; Vaporizer temperature, 300 °C; Collision energy, 30,40,50 V; Signal Intensity Threshold, 1 × 106 cps; Top N vs Top speed, 10; Exclusion duration, 3s.

Untargeted metabolomics data analysis

PCA

Unsupervised PCA (principal component analysis) was performed by statistics function prcomp within R (www.r-project.org). The data were unit variance scaled before unsupervised PCA (Thévenot et al., 2015; Shaffer, 2002).

Hierarchical cluster analysis and pearson correlation coefficients

The HCA (hierarchical cluster analysis) results of samples and metabolites were presented as heatmaps with dendrograms, while Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) between samples were calculated by the cor function in R and presented as only heatmaps. Both HCA and PCC were carried out by R package ComplexHeatmap. For HCA, normalized signal intensities of metabolites (unit variance scaling) are visualized as a color spectrum.

Differential metabolites selected

For two-group analysis, differential metabolites were determined by VIP (VIP >1) and P-value (P-value <0.05, Student's t-test) (Thévenot et al., 2015). VIP values were extracted from OPLS-DA results, which also contain score plots and permutation plots, generated using R package MetaboAnalystR. The data were log-transformed (log2) and mean-centered before OPLS-DA (Thévenot et al., 2015). In order to avoid overfitting, a permutation test (200 permutations) was performed.

KEGG annotation and enrichment analysis

Identified metabolites were annotated using KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), annotated metabolites were then mapped to KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html)(Viant et al., 2017; Kanehisa and Goto, 2000). Pathway significance was defined as p < 0.05 after Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

Prenatal cigarette exposure induces a transient early-life growth deficit and programs hyperactivity and despair-like behavior in adolescent offspring

We first assessed the impact of prenatal cigarette exposure (PCE) on postnatal growth and subsequent behavioral phenotypes of the offspring (Fig. 1A). Postnatal survival rates at 24 h were comparable between PCE and control litters, indicating that the exposure regimen was not acutely lethal (Fig. 1B). Neither the average number of offspring per dam nor the latency to pregnancy differed significantly between groups (p > 0.05, see Supplementary Table S1). Monitoring of body weight from birth through to adulthood revealed that PCE offspring exhibited a significant growth deficit during the early postnatal period. Significant differences between groups were observed on postnatal day (PND) 4 (p < 0.05), PND 6 (p < 0.01), and PND 7 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). The most pronounced growth restriction occurred between PND 7 and PND 14, with PCE offspring showing significantly lower body weights at PND 14 (p < 0.05) and PND 23 (p < 0.001) compared to controls. This potential anxiogenic effect of PCE was further investigated in the elevated plus maze. We observed a non-significant trend toward a reduction in the number of entries into the open arms in PCE offspring, while other parameters, such as time spent in the open arms, were unchanged (Fig. 2D). To assess behavioral despair, a correlate of depression-like behavior in rodents, we performed the tail suspension test. Strikingly, PCE offspring exhibited a significant increase in immobility time compared to controls (Fig. 2E), suggesting an increased state of behavioral despair. In contrast, PCE did not affect social behavior, as measured by the three-chamber social interaction test, with no differences observed in sociability or preference for social novelty between groups (Fig. 2F).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that prenatal cigarette exposure induces a transient early-life growth delay and has enduring programming effects on offspring behavior, culminating in a distinct profile of adolescent hyperactivity, increased thigmotaxis (suggestive of anxiety-like behavior), and despair-like behavior, without affecting fundamental social interaction.

Prenatal cigarette exposure induces region-specific neuronal hyperactivation in adolescent offspring

Given the behavioral hyperactivity observed in PCE offspring, we next examined whether prenatal cigarette exposure induced alterations in neuronal activity within brain regions implicated in motor regulation, emotional processing, and stress responsiveness. Neuronal activation was assessed using c-Fos immunofluorescence staining in adolescent offspring (PND45). c-Fos expression was analyzed under basal conditions without acute behavioral stimulation to capture persistent changes in intrinsic neuronal tone.

Quantitative analysis revealed a region-specific pattern of neuronal activation, rather than a global increase across all examined brain areas. Specifically, a significant increase in c-Fos–positive cells was detected in the paraventricular area (PVA) and basal ganglia (BG) of PCE offspring compared with controls (Fig. 3A and B). To determine whether region-specific neuronal activation was accompanied by neuroimmune alterations, we examined microglial status using Iba1 immunofluorescence staining in the same brain regions.

Consistent with the c-Fos findings, PCE offspring exhibited a significant increase in Iba1-positive microglial density in the PVA compared with controls (Fig. 4A–C). In contrast, no significant differences in Iba1 immunoreactivity were detected in the basal ganglia, despite the presence of increased neuronal activation in this region (Fig. 4B–D). This dissociation suggests that neuronal activation and microglial responses are not obligatorily coupled across all brain regions, and that microglial activation may exhibit higher regional specificity.

To assess whether prenatal cigarette exposure also affected peripheral barrier function, we examined the expression of the tight junction protein Claudin-5 in the colonic epithelium. Claudin-5 is a key component of tight junctions that maintains intestinal epithelial barrier integrity; its reduction is associated with increased paracellular permeability (Sayed et al., 2024; Uzun et al., 2026; Morita et al., 1999). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated a robust reduction in Claudin-5 expression along the colonic mucosal layer in PCE offspring compared with controls (Fig. 4E). Quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis revealed a significant decrease in Claudin-5 levels in the PCE group (Fig. 4F), suggesting compromised intestinal epithelial integrity.

Together, these findings demonstrate that prenatal cigarette exposure is associated with selective neuroimmune changes within stress-associated brain regions and concurrent alterations in intestinal epithelial integrity, supporting a model in which region-specific central immune changes coexist with peripheral gut barrier alterations.

Prenatal cigarette exposure programs persistent alterations in the adult fecal metabolome

In light of the concurrent neuroimmune changes and intestinal barrier alterations observed in PCE offspring, we next examined whether these central and peripheral changes were accompanied by long-lasting alterations in gut-derived metabolic signals. We conducted an untargeted metabolomic analysis of fecal samples collected from adult offspring (PND >90), a time point at which early behavioral abnormalities had stabilized but systemic metabolic reprogramming may persist.

We first performed a principal component analysis (PCA) to gain an overview of the global metabolic profiles. The PCA score plot revealed a clear separation between the metabolic phenotypes of the Control and PCE groups along principal component 1 (PC1), which accounted for the largest proportion of variance in the dataset (Fig. 5A). To refine our understanding of the group separation and identify the specific metabolites driving this discrimination, we employed Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA). The OPLS-DA model yielded a clear separation between groups (Fig. 5B), with high explanatory power (R2Y = 0.995). Model validation using permutation testing (200 permutations) confirmed that group separation was not due to random chance (0/200 permutations for R2Y, p < 0.005; Supplementary Fig. S2). The Q2 value of the original model was 0.439; permutation testing showed that 18 out of 200 permuted models yielded Q2 values higher than the original (p = 0.09), indicating limited predictive power, which we acknowledge as a limitation (see Discussion).

We next identified individual differentially abundant metabolites by combining variable importance in projection (VIP >1.0) from the OPLS-DA model with univariate statistical significance (p < 0.05). The resulting volcano plot illustrates a substantial number of metabolites being significantly upregulated (red) or downregulated (green) in the PCE group compared to controls (Fig. 5C). A total of 161 differential metabolites were identified, including 85 upregulated and 76 downregulated metabolites.

To elucidate the potential biological relevance of these metabolic alterations, we performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. The most significantly impacted pathways included glycerophospholipid metabolism (ko00564, P = 5.20 × 10−11, 37 differential metabolites), phosphatidylinositol signaling system (ko04070, P = 4.45 × 10−8, 16 differential metabolites), and inositol phosphate metabolism (ko00562, P = 4.56 × 10−7, 15 differential metabolites) (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Table S2).

Additionally, pathways related to lipid signaling and synaptic function were significantly enriched, including arachidonic acid metabolism (ko00590, P = 0.00070, 16 differential metabolites), retrograde endocannabinoid signaling (ko04723, P = 0.00035, 18 differential metabolites), and long-term depression (ko04730, P = 5.46 × 10−7, 14 differential metabolites). The glycerophospholipid and arachidonic acid metabolic pathways are of particular interest as they generate bioactive lipid mediators (e.g., prostaglandins, endocannabinoids) that regulate neuroinflammation and synaptic plasticity.

Furthermore, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (ko00592, P = 0.00016, 15 differential metabolites), linoleic acid metabolism (ko00591, P = 0.00133, 14 differential metabolites), and glycerolipid metabolism (ko00561, P = 2.34 × 10−5, 21 differential metabolites) were also significantly altered, collectively indicating widespread disruption of lipid metabolic networks.

In summary, our metabolomics data demonstrate that prenatal cigarette exposure is associated with persistent and multifaceted alterations in the adult fecal metabolome, with predominant effects on glycerophospholipid, inositol phosphate, and arachidonic acid metabolic pathways. These gut-derived metabolic disturbances may contribute to the neurobehavioral alterations observed in PCE offspring.

Discussion

The detrimental effects of maternal cigarette smoking on offspring health are a significant public health concern, with epidemiological studies consistently linking prenatal cigarette exposure (PCE) to an increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, including ADHD (Langley et al., 2012; Thapar and Rutter, 2009). While these associations are clear, the underlying biological mechanisms, particularly those involving the gut-brain axis, remain incompletely understood (Morais et al., 2021; Mayer et al., 2022) In this study, we found that PCE in mice is associated with three interrelated outcomes transient early-life growth restriction, adolescent hyperactivity and despair-like behavior accompanied by region-specific neuronal activation, and persistent reprogramming of the adult gut metabolome. These correlational findings suggest that the developmental origins of PCE-associated behavioral changes may involve lasting alterations in both central neural circuits and peripheral metabolic systems (Interino et al., 2025).

It is important to recognize that the behavioral phenotype observed—hyperactivity, increased thigmotaxis, and despair-like behavior—does not fully recapitulate the core features of ADHD, which additionally include attention deficits and impulsivity that were not assessed here. Consequently, while the hyperactivity domain overlaps with ADHD-like behavior, the present findings are more conservatively interpreted as PCE-induced alterations in locomotor and affective domains.

Our c-Fos mapping revealed that PCE is associated with increased neuronal activity within subcortical regions such as the PVA and basal ganglia, a pattern that aligns with neuroimaging meta-analyses in children and adolescents with ADHD demonstrating predominant alterations in subcortical circuits, particularly within the basal ganglia (Dickstein et al., 2006a; Castellanos and Proal, 2012; Samea et al., 2019) Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether the observed increase in basal c-Fos expression reflects altered circuit function relevant to behavior, as c-Fos was measured under resting conditions without a specific behavioral challenge.

A dissociation emerged in neuroimmune responses: microglial activation was observed in the PVA but not in the basal ganglia, indicating a partial dissociation between neuronal activity and neuroimmune responses. Such regional divergence has been reported in other neurodevelopmental models and may reflect differences in local microenvironmental sensitivity to early-life insults (Erny et al., 2015; Perry and Holmes, 2014; Paolicelli et al., 2011). Concurrently, reduced intestinal Claudin-5 expression suggests compromised epithelial barrier integrity(Braniste et al., 2014), although this inference rests on a single tight junction protein without functional permeability assays.

Beyond neural alterations, our study reveals a persistent reorganization of the gut metabolome in adulthood following PCE, as evidenced by the clear separation of global metabolic profiles and the extensive disruption of glycerophospholipid and arachidonic acid metabolic pathways. These pathways generate diverse bioactive lipid mediators, including pro-inflammatory eicosanoids (e.g., prostaglandins, leukotrienes), pro-resolving lipid mediators, and endocannabinoids (e.g., AEA, 2-AG), which collectively modulate immune responses and neuroinflammatory tone. Endocannabinoids and certain specialized pro-resolving mediators exert neuroprotective effects by promoting microglial M2 polarization and suppressing excessive neuroinflammation (Tanaka et al., 2020), whereas uncontrolled arachidonic acid conversion via COX-2 can exacerbate inflammatory responses (Tanaka et al., 2017). In the intestine, arachidonic acid metabolites participate in epithelial barrier regulation in a context-dependent manner (Ren et al., 2025). The concurrent reduction in colonic tight junction proteins, including Claudin-5, is consistent with compromised intestinal barrier integrity.

Several methodological constraints warrant acknowledgment. The metabolomic analysis was constrained by a small sample size (n = 6 per group) and the use of supervised OPLS-DA with VIP-based feature selection, which is sensitive to overfitting in small datasets. Although permutation testing was performed, an independent replication cohort is needed to validate these findings.

A pivotal unresolved question concerns the causal linkage between central and peripheral findings. We propose that the gut-brain axis represents a candidate pathway linking PCE to long-term neurobehavioral outcomes. Yet, the present data are purely correlational and do not establish causality. PCE-induced alterations in the maternal environment may program early gut microbial colonization and metabolic capacity in the offspring, resulting in persistently altered gut metabolic output. These gut-derived signals could potentially access the brain through immune, endocrine, and neural pathways, including vagal and humoral routes (Cryan and Dinan, 2012; Braniste et al., 2014; Ratsika et al., 2024; Chi et al., 2017). Rather than inducing widespread cortical dysfunction, such signaling may selectively bias the maturation and reactivity of stress-sensitive and motor-related subcortical circuits.

Experimental approaches targeting the microbiome offer promising avenues for establishing causality. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from PCE offspring into germ-free or antibiotic-treated recipients could test whether gut dysbiosis alone influences stress-responsive neural systems and behavior. Conversely, transplantation of control microbiota into PCE offspring may reveal the therapeutic potential of microbial reprogramming, as demonstrated in other neurodevelopmental exposure model (Hsiao et al., 2013). Complementarily, targeted metabolite-based interventions—such as supplementation with short-chain fatty acids or specific amino acids (e.g., glutamine) during defined developmental windows—may help determine whether restoring gut metabolic balance can normalize neuroimmune status and behavioral outcomes. Butyrate, a histone deacetylase inhibitor, has been shown to modulate neuroinflammation and synaptic plasticity in multiple experimental models (Wei et al., 2023; Silva et al., 2020), making it a candidate for future mechanistic studies. Critically, our data cannot determine whether the observed neuroimmune and metabolic changes are causally linked; future studies must include direct manipulations of the gut-brain axis to establish causality.

In conclusion, this correlational study identifies a persistent gut-brain metabolic signature associated with PCE, characterized by region-specific neuronal and microglial changes, intestinal barrier alterations, and long-term reprogramming of the gut metabolome. By integrating behavioral, neuroimmune, and metabolic evidence, our findings support a model in which early-life cigarette exposure correlates with changes in stress- and motor-regulatory systems that may involve gut-brain axis dysregulation. Dissecting this axis using microbial and metabolic interventions will be essential for establishing causality and for developing strategies to mitigate the long-term neurodevelopmental risks associated with PCE.
