Stability of the 1d swarmalator model in the continuum limit
Kevin O’Keeffe

Abstract
We study the 1d swarmalator model in the continuum limit. We examine the stability of its collective states which have compact support: synchrony, where the swarmalators lie in two sync dots (zero dimensional support), and the phase wave, where the swarmalators line up in a ring with uniformly spaced positions and phases (one dimensional support). The compact support imposes analytic difficulties that occur in many other swarmalator models and thus is blocking progress in the field. We show how to overcome this difficulty, deriving the two states’ stability spectra exactly.

Synchronization is a classic topic in nonlinear science. Ordinarily, the focus is on the oscillators’ phase dynamics; their spatial degrees of freedom are either ignored entirely, as in mean field models, or are dynamically inactive, as in lattice models where the oscillators are pinned at fixed locations in space. By contrast, a new wave of work considers oscillators which are mobile, swarming through space as they synchronize in time. One barrier in analyzing so-called swarmalators is that their steady state patterns are often rings, annuli or disks; linearizing around states like these which have compact support leads to eigenvalue equations with generalized functions (delta functions and their relatives) whose analysis is non-standard. We present a case study of the 1d swarmalator model where such eigenvalue equations can be solved exactly. Specifically, we compute the stability spectrum of the model’s sync and phase wave states in the continuum limit. These states are 1d analogues of several real world swarmalators states, such as vortex arrays of sperm

and sync crystals of Janus particles

They may also be useful in other parts of nonlinear science where compactly supported patterns arise, such as bacterial aggregation

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,1}=-nK,\hskip 14.22636ptn>2\;\;n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

(72)

which echo our previous findings.

First modes
. Integrating
\int E\cos\xi d\xi,\int E\sin\xi d\xi
yields equations of form

\displaystyle\frac{d_{0}\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{0}K}{4}-\frac{e_{1}J}{2}+\frac{e_{3}J}{2}

(73)

\displaystyle\frac{d_{1}\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d_{1}K}{2}+\frac{d_{3}K}{2}-\frac{e_{0}J}{4}-e_{2}J+\dots

(74)

\displaystyle\frac{d_{2}\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle=d_{2}(-K)-\frac{3e_{3}J}{2}

(75)

\displaystyle\frac{d_{3}\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3d_{3}K}{2}+\dots

(76)

\displaystyle\frac{e_{0}\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{1}J}{2}-\frac{d_{3}J}{2}+\frac{e_{0}K}{4}

(77)

\displaystyle\frac{e_{1}\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{0}J}{4}+d_{2}J-\frac{e_{1}K}{2}+\frac{e_{3}K}{2}+\dots

(78)

\displaystyle\frac{e_{2}\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3d_{3}J}{2}-e_{2}K+\dots

(79)

\displaystyle\frac{e_{3}\lambda}{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3e_{3}K}{2}

(80)

where the
\dots
denote terms with
d_{i>3},e_{i>3}
. The solutions for
c_{i},d_{i},e_{i}
are rather long and unimportant for stability concerns, so we don’t display them (see
[

]
). The solutions for
\lambda
are however clean

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,0}=0

(82)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,1}=-nK,\hskip 14.22636ptn>2,\;\;n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

(83)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,2}=\frac{1}{4}\Big(-K\pm\sqrt{9K^{2}-8J^{2}}\Big)

(84)

These are our final answers. The
\lambda_{\infty,0},\lambda_{\infty,2}

exactly (Eq. (

Similar considerations as for the warm up cases show there is no continuous spectrum.

This completes our analysis of the phase wave.

II.4
Stability of phase wave for
K_{j}
coupling

Phase waves occur in a generalized 1d swarmalator model where the uniform couplings have been replaced with random quantities like so

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}J_{j}^{\prime}\sin(x_{j}-x_{i})\cos(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})

(85)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}K_{j}^{\prime}\sin(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})\cos(x_{j}-x_{i})

(86)

The effect is to make the swarmalator interactions non-reciprocal, which arises commonly in biology. The stability of the phase wave is unknown; finite-
N
analysis were unsuccessful. Our continuum analysis can however handle this case.

The main difference is the density now depends on the random couplings,
\rho(\xi,\eta,J,K,t)
. The interpretation is that
\rho(\xi,\eta,J,K,t)d\xi d\eta dKdJ
gives the fraction of swarmalators with phases between
(\xi,\eta)
and
(\xi+d\xi,\eta+d\eta)
and couplings between
(J,K)
and
(J+dJ,K+dK)
at time
t
. The velocities become

\displaystyle v_{\xi}=-r_{K}\sin\xi-s_{J}\sin\eta

(87)

\displaystyle v_{\eta}=-r_{J}\sin\xi-s_{K}\sin\eta

(88)

Notice the new order parameter are now coupling-weighted

\displaystyle r_{(K,J)}

\displaystyle=\int(J,K)e^{i\xi}\rho(\xi,\eta,J,K)g(J)h(K)d\xi d\eta dJdK

(89)

\displaystyle s_{(K,J)}

\displaystyle=\int(J,K)e^{i\eta}\rho(\xi,\eta,J,K)g(J)h(K)d\xi d\eta dJdK

(90)

where we use
(K,J)
notation to denote either
J
or
K
. The
g(J),h(K)
are the densities of
J,K
. Plugging in the
\rho=\rho_{0}+\epsilon\rho_{1}
ansatz yields

\displaystyle v_{\xi}=-\mu_{J}\sin\eta+\epsilon\Big(r_{K,1}\sin(\phi_{K,1}-\xi)+s_{J,1}\sin(\psi_{J,1}-\eta)\Big)

(91)

\displaystyle v_{\eta}=-\mu_{K}\sin\eta+\epsilon\Big(r_{J,1}\sin(\phi_{J,1}-\xi)+s_{K,1}\sin(\psi_{K,1}-\eta)\Big)

(92)

We see the difference is the appearance of
mean
couplings
\mu_{J},\mu_{K}
in the unperturbed piece of the velocity. The discrete spectrum becomes

\displaystyle\lambda b=

\displaystyle\mu_{K}b\cos\eta+\sin\eta\left(\mu_{K}b_{\eta}+\mu_{J}b_{\xi}\right)-\frac{s_{K,1}}{2\pi}\sin\phi_{K,1}\delta^{\prime}(\eta)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big(r_{K,1}\delta(\eta)\cos(\xi-\phi_{K,1})+r_{J,1}\sin(\xi-\phi_{1,J})\delta^{\prime}(\eta)\Big)

(93)

where, recall,
b=b(\xi,\eta,K,J)
. The ansatz is the same as before

\displaystyle b(\xi,\eta,J,K)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big(a_{0}(\eta,K,J)+a_{1}(\eta,K,J)\cos\xi

(94)

\displaystyle+b_{1}(\eta,K,J)\sin\xi\Big)

(95)

Zeroth mode
. Projecting onto the zeroth mode yields

\displaystyle\lambda a_{0}(\eta,K,J)=

\displaystyle\mu_{K}a_{0}\cos\eta+\mu_{K}a_{0,\eta}\sin\eta-\frac{s_{K,1}}{2\pi}\sin\phi_{K,1}\delta^{\prime}(\eta)

(96)

We use the same ansatz for
a_{0}(\eta,J,K)=\sum_{n=1}c_{n}\delta^{(n)}(\eta)
. Importantly, the
c_{n}
do
not
depend on the
(J,K)
. The expression for the order parameter becomes

\displaystyle s_{k,1}

\displaystyle=\int Ke^{i\eta}b(\xi,\eta,J,K)g(J)h(K)d\xi d\eta dJdK

(97)

\displaystyle=\int Ke^{i\eta}a_{0}(\eta,J,K)g(J)h(K)d\eta dJdK

(98)

\displaystyle=\int K(-ic_{1}+c_{2}+ic_{3}+c_{4}+\dots)g(J)h(K)dJdK

(99)

\displaystyle=\mu_{K}(-ic_{1}+c_{2}+ic_{3}+c_{4}+\dots)

(100)

Plugging this expression for
s_{K,1}
along with the ansatz for
a_{0}(\eta)
into the Eq. (
96
) and matching coefficients of the delta functions leads to a set of simultaneous equations like before. The solution is

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,0}=0

(101)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,1}=-n\mu_{K},\hskip 14.22636ptn>2,\;\;n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

(102)

First modes
. The procedure is the same here so we just quote the result

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,0}=0

(103)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,1}=-\frac{n}{2}\mu_{K},\hskip 14.22636ptn>2,\;\;n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

(104)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,2}=\frac{1}{4}\Big(-\mu_{K}\pm\sqrt{9\mu_{K}^{2}-8\mu_{J}^{2}}\Big)

(105)

Aside from the new divisor of
2
in
\lambda_{\infty,1}
, the only difference between the
K_{j}
and
K
coupling is that the
(K,J)
get replaced by their mean values. This confirms the conjecture made in
[
17
]
.

Notice that these stability results hold for
any
distributions
g(J),h(K)
. This is quite surprising. The only features of these densities that influence the dynamics are their mean values.

II.5
Stability of sync states in continuum limit

Finally, we close with an analysis of the sync state defined by
\xi_{i}=C_{1}
and
\eta_{i}=C_{2}
for all

. We set
C_{1},C_{2}=0
without loss of generality. Then the density we perturb around is

\displaystyle\rho(\xi,\eta)=\delta(\xi)\delta(\eta)

(106)

The density and velocity fields decompose as

\displaystyle\rho(\xi,\eta)=\delta(\xi)\delta(\eta)+\epsilon\eta(\xi,\eta,t)

(107)

\displaystyle v_{\xi}=-K\sin\xi-J\sin\eta+\epsilon(Kr_{1}\sin(\phi_{1}-\xi)+Js_{1}\sin(\psi_{1}-\eta))

(108)

\displaystyle v_{\eta}=-J\sin\xi-K\sin\eta+\epsilon(Jr_{1}\sin(\phi_{1}-\xi)+Ks_{1}\sin(\psi_{1}-\eta))

Subbing this into the continuity equation and expanding gives

\displaystyle\lambda b(\xi,\eta)=

\displaystyle b_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)(J\sin\xi+K\sin\eta)

\displaystyle+b_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)(K\sin\xi+J\sin\eta)

\displaystyle+K(\cos\xi+\cos\eta)b(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle+r(K\delta(\eta)\delta^{\prime}(X)\sin(\xi-\phi_{1})

\displaystyle+J\delta(\xi)\sin(\xi-\phi_{1})\delta^{\prime}(\eta)

\displaystyle+K\delta(\xi)\delta(\eta)\cos(\phi_{1}))

\displaystyle+s(K\delta(\xi)\delta^{\prime}(Y)\sin(\eta-\psi_{1})

\displaystyle+J\delta(\eta)\delta^{\prime}(X)\sin(\eta-\psi_{1})

\displaystyle+K\delta(\xi)\delta(\eta)\cos(\psi_{1})).

The ansatz

\displaystyle b(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0,j=0\\
(i,j)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\delta^{i}(\xi)\delta^{(j)}(\eta)

eventually yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,0}=0

(112)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,1}=-n(J+K)\hskip 14.22636ptn>2,\;\;n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty,2}=-m(J-K)\hskip 14.22636ptm>2,\;\;m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

Appendix

We walk through a simple example of a linear operator
with
a continuous spectrum (recall that all the examples in the main text did not have one). Our goal is to give some beginning intuition for how they work. For a proper introduction, see Beck’s excellent notes on the linear stability of PDEs.
[
52
]

Consider the linear operator

\displaystyle L_{1}b(\omega)

\displaystyle=i\omega b(\omega)+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{b(\nu)}{\nu^{2}+\lambda^{2}}g(\nu)dy

(115)

where
g(\nu)
is a probability density. This operator arises when analyzing the stability of the incoherent state in the Kuramoto model
[
39
]
. The definition of the continuous spectrum is the set of
\lambda
for which the operator
L_{1}-\lambda I
is
not
surjective. The standard move is to consider the equation

\displaystyle(i\omega-\lambda)b(\omega)+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{b(\nu)}{\nu^{2}+\lambda^{2}}g(\nu)dy=f(\omega)

(116)

Surjectivity means that for every
f(\omega)
on the right-hand side, there exists at least one function
b(\omega)
on the left-hand side that satisfies the equation. The question is: for what values of
\lambda
does this property break down? Consider
\lambda=i\omega
. The leads to the equation

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{b(\nu)}{\nu^{2}+\lambda^{2}}g(\nu)dy=f(\omega)

(117)

Here, no matter what
b(\omega)
we insert into the (definite) integral, a constant comes out. This implies that the operator
L_{1}-\lambda I
no longer distinguishes between different inputs
b(\omega)
, meaning it is no longer surjective. Therefore,
\lambda=i\omega
belongs to the continuous spectrum, as it fails to produce unique outputs for every input function
b(\omega)
.
