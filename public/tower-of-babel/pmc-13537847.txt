Streetview greenspace types and dementia risk in older adults: the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis
Authors: Marcia Pescador Jimenez, Liliana Paloma Rojas-Saunero, Cameron J Reimer, Esra Suel, Pi-I Debby Lin, Perry Hystad, Francine Grodstein, Ana V Diez-Roux, Brent Coull, Peter James
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Greenspace has been associated with lower dementia risk, but most studies have used satellite-derived measures that cannot distinguish vegetation types. We used streetview (SV)-based metrics of greenspace from an on-the-ground perspective to examine greenspace types that may be relevant for dementia risk. We followed 6761 participants from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, 2000-2018. Using deep learning algorithms applied to SV images, we measured %trees, %grass, %other green (plants/flowers/fields) in the 500 m area around participants’ residences. We examined the controlled direct effect of greenspace with dementia accounting for death as a censoring event, using a weighted Kaplan–Meier survival estimator, with the product of exposure weights and inverse-probability-of-censoring by death. We ran models separately per greenspace type. Mean baseline age was 62 years (SD, 10). Participants exposed to lowest levels of %trees were more likely to have a household income < $24 999. We observed 585 (8.7%) dementia cases and 1437 (21.3%) deaths over follow-up. We observed a direct effect of high exposure to %grass on 19-year dementia risk had death been prevented of −4.57 (95% CI, -8.15, −0.40) percentage points. No associations were observed for %trees (−0.07; 95% CI, −2.29 to 2.11) and %other green (−0.03, 95% CI, −1.59 to 1.48). Higher percentage of grass may reduce dementia risk.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease-related dementias (AD/ADRD) affect an estimated 4 to 5 million older adults in the US1 and the prevalence is projected to surpass 13 million by 2050.2 Racial/ethnic groups, including Black and Latino adults, have an elevated risk for AD/ADRD, as well as comorbidities that can contribute to accelerated cognitive decline.3 Incidence of AD/ADRD is 40%-100% higher among Black adults compared with White adults,4 while Latino adults tend to be younger at the time of diagnosis than White adults.5

Greenspace, or natural vegetation, may benefit cognition,6,7 potentially lowering risk of neurodegenerative diseases, and providing a novel intervention to reduce AD/ADRD risk.8 Literature suggests that greenspace may benefit cognition by reducing exposures to air pollution, noise, and extreme temperature, relieving mental stress, restoring attention, and promoting physical activity and social interaction.9 However, research on greenspace and AD/ADRD in racially diverse populations remains limited.10 This reflects the paucity of data to examine whether greenspace associations vary by racial/ethnic identity, which limits evidence to inform future interventions.11

Further, in existing literature, greenspace is typically measured with satellite-based indices that are nonspecific and provide no information on vegetation type (eg, trees), or whether the vegetation is within view at the street level. So far, no prospective studies have been able to evaluate the relationship between streetview (SV)-level measures of specific greenspace features and AD/ADRD, largely because of difficulties in estimating these measures across large populations.

Finally, in the field of dementia, a methodological challenge arises when individuals at risk of dementia may die of other causes prior to its onset.12 This may yield counterintuitive results, where exposures that are known to be protective for mortality risk sometimes seem harmful for the risk of dementia. Because greenspace has been associated with reduced mortality rates,13 it is possible that participants with higher exposure to greenspace will have lower mortality rates, and thus may seem to have a higher risk of dementia (Figure S1). To account for death as a competing event, we capture the effect of greenspace exposure on dementia through a hypothetical setting in which somehow death could be eliminated.12

This study addresses gaps in the literature by examining novel datasets, such as SV images to generate refined estimates of greenspace exposure (eg, trees, grass) from a ground-based viewshed. We quantified associations between long-term exposure to SV-based metrics of residential greenspace and dementia incidence within a racially diverse cohort, accounting for competing events. We hypothesized that participants who had a higher exposure to specific greenspace types, such as trees, and did not die throughout the study period, would have lower incident dementia compared to those with lower exposure.

Methods

Study population

The Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), started in 2000-2002, initially recruited 6814 men and women 45-84 years of age free of clinically recognized cardiovascular disease from 6 US sites: Baltimore, Maryland; Chicago, Illinois; Forsyth County, North Carolina; Los Angeles County, California; New York, New York; and St. Paul, Minnesota.14 Participants were sampled to be racially/ethnically diverse within each city. All participants underwent questionnaire administration, and physical and clinical examinations at baseline and at follow-up visits through 2018 (Figure S2). Ongoing periodic follow-ups of the participants by phone call are used to maintain connection, to update contact information, and to ascertain medical events between examinations. In the present study, we used follow-up and events data through 2018. MESA was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at all participating institutions, and participants gave written informed consent.

Outcome: dementia case ascertainment

Dementia case ascertainment was defined using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes from hospitalizations and death certificates.15 Every 9-12 months, telephone interviews were conducted to inquire about interim hospital admissions and deaths. Hospital discharge diagnosis codes were obtained, and medical records were reviewed by 2 physicians. We included events identified annually between 2000 and 2018 (Figure S2). Methodology of identification has been validated and used in prior MESA publications.16 Dementia was depicted by a significant decline in cognitive function, not accounted for by secondary conditions (eg, malignancy or substance use) or other mental disorders (eg, schizophrenia). Incident cases of dementia were assigned to the midpoint between their previous examination-free of these ailments and current examination dates. Further information is available in Methods A of supplementary material.

Exposure: residential exposure to greenspace

We used deep learning segmentation algorithms applied to nationwide SV imagery from 2007 (the first date SV images were available) to 2018. First, we created a nationwide 100 m street network grid for all core based statistical areas. For each grid point location and each year, we downloaded 4 images: north, south, east and west. These high-resolution images capture the visual domain along all public streets in the US and provide a ground-level perspective on greenspace as individuals experience it. We used the pyramid scene parsing network (PSPNet) model trained on the ADE20K dataset17 to derive SV imagery-based measures of greenspace. This model segments out each pixel into one of 150 different classes. For this analysis, we focused on specific greenspace types, such as trees, grass, plants, flowers, and fields, within each image. The output for each image was the percentage of pixels of greenspace types. Then, the percentages were averaged across the 4 images to create greenspace percentages within view at each grid point. We created annual 100 m rasters from grid points and linked each residential address over follow-up to SV-derived greenspace metrics from 2007 to 2018 (updated annually). Addresses from 2000 to 2006 were assigned 2007 SV-data. We used focal statistics to create annual rasters where each pixel represents the average of all pixels from the original nation-wide 100 m raster in a 500 m buffer (primary exposure) and in a 1000 m buffer.

The key exposure metrics that we examined were %trees, %grass, %other green (plants/flowers/fields), and %all green (all vegetation combined). We followed the accumulation of risk model,18 to illustrate longitudinal associations between exposure to greenspace and AD/ADRD risk. Specifically, we used the chain of risk model to test whether individuals repeatedly exposed to the advantage of greenspace exposure would have a lower risk of dementia in the life course. Thus, we parametrized our exposure as time-varying cumulative means, defined as the mean across all years from 2000 to each follow-up examination. Following previous research on high exposure to greenspace and mental health,19 high exposure was defined as third tertile vs not, for each greenspace type. There were 48 participants who had missing greenspace data and were dropped from the sample (Figure S3).

Covariates

We adjusted for covariates (assessed at baseline unless otherwise indicated) that are associated with living in areas with greenspace and have been associated with AD/ADRD, but were unlikely to result from either.20 These included age, sex (male/female), self-reported race/ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Latino, Chinese-American), and individual socioeconomic factors, including education level (completed high school/GED or less, some college/technical school/associate degree, bachelor’s/graduate degree), and time-varying household income in USD (≤ 24 999, 25 000-49 999, 50 000-74 999, ≥ 75 000). Race and ethnicity were conceptualized as social constructs that capture a range of systemically driven exposures linked to inequality.21 Metabolic syndrome was defined per the Third Adult Treatment Panel of the National Cholesterol Education Program (ATP III) modified criteria.22,23 Self-rated health categories included poor/fair/good/very good/excellent. We additionally adjusted for time-varying neighborhood socioeconomic status (NSES). NSES was based on a principal component analysis of census-tract measures obtained from the U.S. Census 2000, and the American Community Survey (ACS) 2005-2009, and ACS 2007-2011.24 Higher NSES score indicates better NSES (range: −2.5, 4.7). A small fraction of missingness was present for baseline covariates (< 5% for all except NSES, details in Figure S3), hot-deck imputation was used to replace missing values. To account for further potential confounding (vs mediation) we used baseline air pollution, physical activity, hypertension and depressive symptoms.25,26 Particulate matter < 2.5 μm in diameter (PM2.5) was assessed using predictive models informed by monitors that collected 2-week samples over a 4-year period at participants’ residences.27 Total minutes of moderate and vigorous physical activity were summed, then multiplied by metabolic equivalent (MET) levels to obtain MET-minutes per week.28 Hypertension was self-reported diagnosis (yes/no), and depressive symptoms were assessed with the 20-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale.29

Death and time at death

We assessed death occurring between baseline interviews in 2000 through 2018. In the event of a participant’s death, family members or representatives reported the event through follow-up calls. Lastly, periodic searches were conducted in the National Death Index (NDI) and obituaries to identify participants who had lost contact with the study. Death assessment has been validated in prior studies in this cohort.30

Statistical analysis

We conducted descriptive analyses of individual-level variables by %trees exposure at baseline. We examined Spearman’s rank correlations between all SV-based exposures, NSES, and population density. Due to the high correlation between SV-based %trees (here on refered to as %trees) and %all green, we only present %trees in the main results, and %all green in the Supplementary material.

Our estimand of interest is the controlled direct effect of high exposure vs low (third tertile vs not) green space on dementia incidence under a hypothetical intervention that eliminates death during follow-up (ie, treating death as a censoring event).12 This can be interpreted as the joint effect of green space and an intervention that prevents death throughout the study period. Alternatively, the total effect of green space on dementia is conceptualized as both the direct effect of greenspace and dementia, and the indirect effect of greenspace on dementia mediated by death (Figure S1). Given that prior literature suggests greenspace or causes of greenspace (ie, NSES) affect death,13 the total effect for greenspace may appear to increase dementia risk simply because it helps people live longer, giving them more time to develop dementia.31,32

We used inverse probability treatment weights (IPTW) to account for confounding of the association between greenness and dementia so that participants who had high exposure to greenspace and those who did not were comparable in all their characteristics. For each year of follow-up, each participant’s IPTW was the inverse of their probability of high exposure to greenspace for that year, based on a pooled-logistic regression model conditional on baseline covariates (sex, race, education level, and MESA site), and time varying covariates (income, NSES).

In addition, to satisfy the independent censoring assumption between death and dementia, we used inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) to account for censoring due to death (ie, account for differences between people who remained under follow-up and those who were censored due to death). For each year of follow-up, each person’s IPCW was the inverse of their cumulative probability of survival in each year prior to that year, conditional on measured shared causes of death and dementia. For an individual who has died by time t, the year-t censoring weight is 0. We used a pooled logistic regression model to calculate the probability of death each year for each participant based on baseline covariates (baseline age, sex, race, education level, MESA site, metabolic syndrome, self-rated health) and time-varying covariates (time on study with cubic splines, income, NSES).

After estimating the weights, we used a weighted version of the Kaplan–Meier estimator extended to time-varying exposures, including the product of IPTW and IPCW, to estimate the cumulative probability (ie, risk) of dementia over 19 years of follow-up for those with high vs low exposure to each greenness exposure. Based on the risk of dementia for both exposure levels, we calculated risk ratios and risk differences at 19 years of follow-up. About 95% CIs were calculated based on percentiles from 500 bootstrapped samples. Further details on the analysis for competing events can be found in Methods B of supplementary material. Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1. Code to run all analyses and reproduce our results is available on GitHub.

As secondary analysis, we estimated the total effect of high greenspace exposure vs low with dementia risk, which can be influenced by how greenspace may reduce the risk of death. For estimation, we used the IPTW described above on a weighted version of the Aalen–Johansen estimator to obtain the risk of dementia at each year of follow-up accounting for the probability of surviving over follow-up, treating death as a competing event. We estimated the risk ratio and risk difference at 19 years of follow-up as in the main analysis. Second, we re-run the main analysis and total effect estimates changing the greenspace exposure buffer to 100 and 1000 m to investigate immediate and walkable area around the residence as potential relevant contexts.

Effect modification

Previous work has shown that the beneficial association of greenspace with AD/ADRD risk might be more pronounced in vulnerable populations (eg, racial and ethnic minorities or participants living in neighborhoods with low SES).33 We examined whether the association of greenspace with dementia is modified by: (1) race and ethnicity, (2) NSES (tertiles: low, medium, high), and (3) Census tract population density (number per square kilometers; tertiles: low, medium, high) as a measure of urbanicity. Following Ward et al.34 on how to assess health disparities, we examined the distribution of greenspace types across effect modifiers. Further, we present the number of participants with and without the outcome in each stratum of the effect modifier. We tested the interaction between each of the hypothesized effect modifiers and greenspace type in the model including the product of IPTW and IPCW.

Sensitivity analysis

To address potential temporal misalignment—given that street-level greenspace observed in 2007 may not accurately represent conditions in 2000-2002—we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricting the follow-up period to 2007-2018. To examine beneficial associations at very high levels of specific % green space, we conducted a sensitivity analysis defining high exposure as fourth quartile vs not. We further analyzed participants who did not move during follow-up (n = 4177). Finally, to address further potential confounding, we added sensitivity analysis adjusting for air pollution, physical activity, hypertension, and depressive symptoms.

Results

A total of 1882 (27.8%) Non-Hispanic Black, 803 (11.9%) Chinese-American, 1488 (22.0%) Latino, and 2588 (38.3%) Non-Hispanic White participants were included in the study (Table 1). The overall mean age at baseline was 62.2 (SD, 10.2). Participants who lived in areas with low levels of %trees at baseline were more likely to be Latino or Chinese-American, have a high school degree or less, have a household income lower than $24 999, and have lower NSES (Table 1).

%Trees was highly correlated with %grass (r = 0.69, Figure S4). %Trees was negatively correlated with population density (r = −0.39; Figure S4). Participants who lived in areas with low levels of %trees were more likely to be living in Los Angeles, CA, or New York, NY.

In total, 585 (8.7%) of MESA participants included in the analytical sample developed dementia and 1437 (21.3%) died over 19 years of follow-up. The mean time to dementia was 10.9 years (mean age, 83; SD, 7.7), and the mean time to death was 10.6 years (mean age, 79; SD, 9.9). A total of 384 (8.5%) of participants with low %trees exposure developed dementia, compared to 201 (9.1%) of participants in the high %trees exposure. Similarly, participants in the low exposure group were more likely to have died (995, 22.1%) compared to participants in the high exposure group (442, 19.6%; Table 1). Participants who were excluded from the analytic sample were more likely to be White and have an income higher than 75 K (Table S1).

Overall, we observed a protective association of %grass with dementia risk, had death been fully prevented during the study period. Participants with high%grass exposure had 4.57 (95% CI, −8.15, −0.40) percentage points lower risk of dementia compared to those with low exposure (Table 2; Figure S5). In contrast, we did not observe associations for %trees or %other green (−0.07, 95% CI, −2.29 to 2.11; and −0.03, 95% CI, −1.59 to 1.48, respectively). Results were similar on the risk ratio scale. In secondary analysis, where the association may be influenced by the impact that greenspace has on reduced risk of mortality (ie, total effects), we observed that results for all 3 greenness exposures shifted towards the null, both in the absolute and relative scales.

Estimates of the effect of greenspace on dementia risk had death been fully prevented during the study period, at a 100 m buffer attenuated for %grass (RD: −0.22 (−3.08, 2.19)) while results for %other green showed a protective association (RD: −2.27 (−3.88, −0.87); Table S2). All estimates of the association of greenspace with dementia risk, that can be influenced by how greenspace affects death, shifted towards the null as in main analysis.

In sensitivity analysis, when we restricted the follow-up period to 2007-2018, we observed a suggested protective association of %grass with dementia risk, had death been fully prevented during the study period (−2.93, 95% CI, −7.31 to 0.58; Table S3), albeit the CI now includes the null potentially due to a smaller sample size and follow-up period. When defining high exposure as fourth quartile vs not, we observed a larger estimate for the direct effect of high exposure to %grass on 19-year dementia risk had death been prevented, but with a wider confidence intervalCI (−6.18, 95% CI, −9.63 to 2.46; Table S4). When restricting to participants who did not move, we observed an attenuated estimate for the direct effect of high exposure to %grass on 19-year dementia risk (−2.00, 95% CI, −3.07 to 0.02; Table S5). Results further adjusting for air pollution, physical activity, hypertension, and depressive symptoms remained robust for the direct effect of high exposure to %grass on 19-year dementia risk (−4.68, 95% CI, −8.72 to −0.62; Table S6).

Following Ward et al.,34 we examined the distribution of greenspace types across all effect modifiers of interest. We observed that White participants had the highest exposure for all greenspace types, while Latinos had on average the lowest exposure (Figure S6). The distribution of dementia cases showed that 264 (10%) White, 165 (9%) Black, 116 (8%) Latino, and 40 (5%) Chinese-American participants developed dementia. The distribution across NSES tertiles suggested that neighborhoods with high NSES had the highest mean exposure to %trees, but lowest mean exposure to %grass (Figure S7). Finally, the distribution across population density tertiles suggested that neighborhoods with high population density had the lowest mean exposure to all types of greenspace (Figure S8). We did not observe evidence of effect modification for the association between types of greenspace and dementia by any of the effect modifiers, as statistical power was limited by sample size.

Discussion

In this study of 6761 participants in the MESA cohort, we used deep learning algorithms applied to SV imagery to generate refined estimates of residential exposure to greenspace from a ground-based viewshed from 2000 to 2018. We found a protective association for %grass, and to a lesser extent of %other green, with risk of dementia, had death been prevented over follow-up. Thus, after accounting for the association of greenspace with mortality, higher exposure to %grass was associated with reduced risk of dementia.

To our knowledge, this is the first study that examines the association between %grass and dementia risk; thus, we refer to prior literature on mental health outcomes. The robust protective association between high vs low grass exposure with dementia is in line with a recent study of 33 490 participants from the Nurses’ Health Study II where higher exposure to grass was associated with lower incident depression.35 Our results on grass are also in line with a previous study in Vancouver, Canada that observed an association between higher exposure to grass-herb vegetation and reduced odds of reported mental health disorders.36 More generally, the beneficial aspects of greenness have been observed in a previous study of the Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS) (n = 2141; mean age = 75.3 years; 95% White) which found that residential greenspace was associated with lower rate of incident dementia.37 However, while the CHS study was not able to differentiate between greenspace types that could be singled out as potential interventions, our study sheds light into grass as a potential driver of the beneficial associations on dementia risk. Potential mechanisms for grass to decrease dementia risk include decreasing stress; air pollution, and extreme temperatures; and offering a setting for physical activity and social interactions.6 The negative correlation between % grass and population density, along with how % grass is distributed across population density tertiles, suggested that high-density areas could benefit from increasing grass coverage. These results shed light on the functionality of urban planning to maximize health benefits, and greater understanding of the determinants of urban health for policy makers. Future urban greening programs, with a focus on grass as a specific feature of greenspace that may improve public health will facilitate usage and the benefits accrued from urban greenspace.38

Importantly, the protective association between specific greenspace types and dementia only emerged when we estimated the controlled direct effect of greenspace had death been fully prevented over follow-up. Although this estimand is debatable, considering that greenspace exposure was associated with mortality in previous studies13 as also evidenced in these data, a total effect account for death as competing event shifts all results towards the null Previous literature on greenspace and dementia risk has mostly overlooked the importance of accounting for the association with death. Our results speak to the importance of future studies choosing a research question and the corresponding statistical method in the context of competing events.12

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, dementia classification was ascertained using ICD codes. The clinical assessment underlying the assignment of dementia ICD codes is not standardized. Literature has shown that dementia from medical records is less likely to capture dementia in minoritized groups and those who have less interactions with the healthcare system.39 This means that using ICD codes to identify cases may yield an unrepresentative sample. In our sample, White participants were substantially more likely to have received a diagnosis across all dementia types, followed by Black participants (Table S7). In contrast, patterns by income were less consistent; however, individuals in the highest income category were more frequently diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, and cognitive impairment (Table S7). These disparities suggest that our findings may be biased towards the null for non-White participants, as well as for lower-income groups with respect to certain dementia subtypes. Related, dementia was treated as a single outcome despite substantial etiologic heterogeneity (including Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, and vascular dementia), each with potentially different risk factors, etiology, and pathophysiology. Given the distribution of mixed dementias in our sample, unfortunately we did not have statistical power for analysis by dementia type. Second, we relied on residential addresses to assess exposure, which might not represent where participants spend most of their time or how they interact with greenspace. Related, our analysis might be biased by exposure misclassification,40 although our results remained consistent across different buffer sizes. Our SV metrics can only capture greeenspace types near the streets where images were captured. We therefore miss information on backyards, and other settings where people might be exposed to greenspace. We are also limited by the frequency of SV images; they represent a snapshot in time of how streets looked when they were captured. However, it is unlikely that any differential bias occurs due to this exposure measurement error. Our findings may be influenced by residential selection bias or residual confounding. However, our results remained consistent in analysis among non-movers, and analysis further adjusting for air pollution, physical activity, hypertension, and depressive symptoms.

This study adds to the literature in several ways. First, the majority of prior studies have focused on predominantly White samples, but our analysis used data from a racially-diverse cohort of US adults. Covariate data were well-characterized, which likely enabled adequate control for potential confounding. The measurement of numerous covariates and health outcomes, along with a large sample size and a 19-year follow-up, provided the ability to examine competing events. Finally, we used deep learning algorithms applied to over 350 million SV images, allowing to identify types of greenspace most relevant to health and illustrating translation to prevention strategies. These results contribute to our understanding of how greenspace impacts dementia by adding specific information on the types of greenspace that might drive cognitive health.
