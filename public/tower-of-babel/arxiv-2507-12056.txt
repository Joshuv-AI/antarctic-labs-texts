Selective decoupling in multi-level quantum systems by the SU(2) sign anomaly.
Giorgio Anfuso, Giulia Piccitto, Vittorio Romano, Elisabetta Paladino, Giuseppe A. Falci

Abstract
We investigate dynamical decoupling operated by 2 ​ π 2\pi -pulses in a two-level subspaces of a multilevel system showing that it may leads to selective decoupling. This provides a flexible strategy for decoupling transitions in a quantum network, when control to directly address them is not available which can be use to control internode interaction or actively suppress decoherence.



keywords
dynamical decoupling, qutrits, Magnus expansion

1
Introduction

Finding strategies to mitigate the effects of noise is a key issue in quantum technologies. Among these, dynamical decoupling (DD) has attracted a great deal of interest in several platforms
Paladino et al. 2014
;
Viola and Lloyd 1998
;
Vitanov 2015
;
Stassi and Nori 2018
;
D’Arrigo et al. 2024
;
D’Arrigo et al. 2014
;
Falci et al. 2004
;
D’Arrigo et al. 2005
. It is based on the idea that a sequence of pulses can revert the sign of operators in the Hamiltonian that are “orthogonal” (in the Hilbert-Schmidt sense) to the generators of the pulses. A given component of the Hamiltonian can be effectively attenuated by shining sequences of short equidistant pulses that reverse its sign at alternating intervals
complete cancellation being achieved in the limit of infinitely large pulse rate. More efficient sequences achieve substantial cancelation at lower pulse rates
Paladino et al. 2014
such as the Uhrig sequence
Uhrig 2008
which reduces decoherene due to low-frequency ”pure dephasing” noise affecting a two-level system.

Even though DD has been widely investigated for two-level systems, the growing interest in the application of qudits to quantum technologies requires the extension of these studies to the domain of multilevel systems.
Extending the geometrical framework, a possible strategy can be identified at least ideally by arguing that interactions that are off-diagonal in the ”logic” basis of the eigenstates of the uncoupled system can be asymptotically mitigated through sequences of
\pi
-pulses with the corresponding diagonal generator.
In particular, this approach could allow for the decoupling of interactions involving specific transitions. To this end, control Hamiltonians oriented in specific directions must be available, which is often not the case in real quantum hardware.

To address this issue, we propose to exploit the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
sign quantum anomaly by using
2\pi
pulses on a transition different from the one we wish to decouple. This approach offers two advantages: (i) selectivity, canceling one interaction while preserving the other, and (ii) enhanced control flexibility, since the
2\pi
pulse can be implemented by any two-level operator acting on the operating transition. Consequently, protocols based on the sign of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
significantly broaden the toolbox for dynamical decoupling in multilevel systems.

The paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
2
we introduce the model. The results are discussed in Sec.
3
. In Sec.
4
we draw the conclusions.

The Magnus expansion
Magnus 1954
is a mathematical tool for solving differential equations of the form
Y^{\prime}=A(t)Y
, being
Y
a
k-
dimensional vector and
A(t)
a time-dependent linear operator. This method is particularly useful when
[A(t_{1}),A(t_{2})]\neq 0
, for
t_{1},t_{2}
belonging to the integration window.
The formal solution reads
Y(t)=T\exp\big(\int_{0}^{t}A(s)ds\big)Y_{0}
, being
Y_{0}=Y(t_{0})
the initial condition.
The Magnus expansion expresses this formal solution by an asymptotic series
Y(t)=\exp\big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Omega_{k}(t)\big)Y_{0}
, with
\Omega_{k}(t)
some defined matrices. For our convenience we report the first three terms of the series
Blanes et al. 2009
:

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}A(t_{1})dt_{1},\quad\Omega_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}[A(t_{1}),A(t_{2})]dt_{1}dt_{2},

(3)

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}(t)=\frac{1}{6}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\Big([A(t_{1}),[A(t_{2}),A(t_{3})]+[A(t_{3}),[A(t_{2}),A(t_{1})]\Big)dt_{1}dt_{2}dt_{3}.

If
A(t)
is a piecewise constant matrix, the Magnus expansion can be cast to a generalized Beker-Campbell-Housdorf formula (see Section 2.8 of
Blanes et al. 2009
).

3
Results and discussion

We start the analysis by rewriting Eq.
2
as

U^{n}(T_{f})=e^{-iH\tau_{n}}e^{-iH_{R}\tau_{n-1}}\dots e^{-iH_{R}\tau_{1}}e^{-iH\tau_{0}}=:Te^{-i\int_{0}^{T_{f}}dt\mathcal{H}(t)},

(4)

which defines

\mathcal{H}(t)\Big|_{t\in I_{i}}=\begin{cases}H\quad\ \ i\ \ \text{is even};\\
H_{R}\quad i\ \ \text{is odd}.\end{cases}

(5)

Therefore, we can use the results of the previous section to evaluate
H_{\text{eff}}
at the various orders of the Magnus expansion, by letting
A(t)=-i\mathcal{H}(t)
.
The main objective is that of finding, order by order, the best decoupling sequence making
H_{\text{eff}}
close to the target
H_{T}
at least for the couplings that we want to cancel selectively.
In general, for a
n
-pulses sequence we have to determine
n+1
time intervals, thus we need
n+1
equations. For
n=2
a we need 3 equations: one is the constraint Eq
1
, the first and the second order of the Magnus expansion in Eq.
3
. Starting with the first order of the effective Hamiltonian. Because of the piecewise structure of
\mathcal{H}
it simply reads

H_{\text{eff}}^{1}=\frac{1}{T_{f}}\int_{0}^{T_{f}}\mathcal{H}(t)dt=\Bigg[H\sum_{j\ \text{even}}^{n}\delta_{j}+H_{R}\sum_{j\ \text{odd}}^{n}\delta_{j}\Bigg],

(6)

where we have posed
H_{\text{eff}}^{1}=\frac{i}{T_{f}}\Omega_{1}(T_{f})
.
We notice that this terms exactly reduces to the target Hamiltonian if we introduce the constraint

\quad\sum_{j\ \text{even}}^{n}\delta_{j}=\sum_{j\ \text{odd}}^{n}\delta_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\quad.

(7)

This means that in order to preserve the target Hamiltonian we require that all the following terms of the Magnus expansion are vanishing.
Looking at second order from Eq
3
we can compute

H_{\text{eff}}^{2}=\frac{-i}{T_{f}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T_{f}}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}[\mathcal{H}(t_{1}),\mathcal{H}(t_{2})]dt_{2}dt_{1}\ ,

(8)

Once again exploiting the structure of
\mathcal{H}
, decomposing the integration into all the possible intervals and observing that

\bigg[\mathcal{H}(t_{1}),\mathcal{H}(t_{2})\bigg]_{{}^{t_{1}\in I_{j}}_{t_{2}\in I_{k}}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0&\ \ \text{if }\ \ (-1)^{j}=(-1)^{k}\ \ ;\\
(-1)^{j}[H,H_{R}]&\ \ \text{otherwise }\ \ ,\end{array}\right.

(9)

after some computation one can find that

H_{\text{eff}}^{2}=\frac{iT_{f}}{2}[H,H_{R}]\sum_{j=1}^{n/2}\left[\delta_{2j-1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\delta_{2l}\right)-\delta_{2j}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\delta_{2l+1}\right)\right],

(10)

Before proceeding, let us evaluate the latter term for the two cases of
n=2
and
n=4
pulses. We obtain

H_{\text{eff}}^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{iT_{f}}{2}\left[H_{R},H\right]\delta_{1}(\delta_{0}-\delta_{2})&\text{if}\quad n=2\\
\\
\dfrac{iT_{f}}{2}\left[H_{R},H\right]\left[(\delta_{1}+\delta_{3})(\delta_{4}-\delta_{0})+\delta_{2}(\delta_{1}-\delta_{3})\right]&\text{if}\quad n=4\\
\end{array}\right.

(11)

We recognize that Eq.
1
, Eq.
7
, Eq.
10
offer the minimal set of equations, giving only exact solution in the
n=2
case, which is
\delta_{0}=\delta_{2}=1/4
and
\delta_{1}=1/2
.
However, this set of equations is not enough to determine a unique solution for
n>2
.
For finding the optimal solutions for
n=4
pulses we need to take into account some higher order. We evaluated the third order contribution of Magnus expansion, resulting in:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}H_{\text{eff}}^{3}=\dfrac{-i}{T_{f}}\Biggl\{&\left[H,\left[H_{R},H\right]\right]&\Biggl[-\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\delta_{1}+\delta_{3}\Bigr)\Bigl(\delta_{2}^{2}+\delta_{4}^{2}+\delta_{0}^{2}-4\delta_{4}\delta_{0}\Bigr)+\\
&&\\
&&+\delta_{1}\delta_{2}\Bigl(2\delta_{0}-\delta_{4}\Bigr)+\delta_{2}\delta_{3}\Bigl(2\delta_{4}-\delta_{0}\Bigr)\Biggr]\\
&&\\
+&\left[H_{R},\left[H_{R},H\right]\right]&\Biggl[\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\delta_{1}+\delta_{3}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigl(\delta_{0}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{4}\Bigr)-3\delta_{1}\delta_{2}\delta_{3}\Biggr]\Biggr\}\end{array}\ .

(12)

This equation gives in principle two different contributions to the set of equations, one for each different nested commutator, providing exactly the
n+1
equations needed to find a unique solution to the optimization problem. Unluckily, by solving this system, we do not find any real (i.e. physical) solution, suggesting that the proposed sequence does not allow to reproduce exactly the target Hamiltonian.
However, some interesting results can be obtained by releasing some constraint on the third order.
In fact, if we require the vanishing of the unwanted couplings only, we obtain a class of solutions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\delta_{0}&=\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{r(\delta_{1})}{8\delta_{1}}-\frac{\delta_{1}}{2},\\
&\\
\delta_{2}&=\frac{1}{8(1-2\delta_{1})\delta_{1}\mp 4r(\delta_{1})},\\
&\\
\delta_{3}&=\frac{1}{2}-\delta_{1},\\
&\\
\delta_{4}&=\frac{r(\delta_{1})\mp(1-4\delta_{1}^{2})}{4-8\delta_{1}},\\
\end{array}\right.

(13)

with
r(\delta_{1})=\sqrt{16\delta_{1}^{4}-16\delta_{1}^{3}+2\delta_{1}}
.
By imposing the
\delta_{i}
to be real (i.e. the argument of the square root to be positive) and positive we obtain that
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}<\delta_{1}<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}
.
Interestingly, we find that among the possible sequences, the Uhrig one, characterized by
\delta_{i}=\sin^{2}\Big(\frac{(i+1)\pi}{2n+2}\Big)-\sin^{2}\Big(\frac{i\pi}{2n+2}\Big)
, is suitable for canceling the coupling in the subspace
\{\ket{e},\ket{f}\}
.

4
Conclusion

In this work we have discussed a protocol for selective dynamical decoupling in multilevel systems, by leveraging on the sign anomaly of the
SU(N-1)
subalgebra of a
N
level system. We stress that this approach provide a general framework for multilevel systems that is particularly suitable from the experimental perspective.
To investigate the robusteness of the method, we minimized subsequent terms of the Magnus expansion of the evolution operator, to find the optimal sequence to cancel the unwanted couplings. The discussion carried in the paper has put the attention on a 3LS, in order to set the toy model for further numerical analysis. However, is important to mention that the results are very general and directly apply to many physical instances, as the decoupling of subsystems of an ultrastrongly coupled architecture
Falci et al. 2017
;
Falci et al. 2019
;
Giannelli
et al. 2024
, once the correct pulse operators are identified.
We find that it is not possible to find a sequence that reproduce exactly the target Hamiltonian, but we can engineer some sequences (as the Uhrig one) that gives the desired results up to order
T_{f}^{2}
, being
T_{f}
the total evolution time.

An interesting question is that of understanding the extension of this work for an increasing number of pulses, to eventually find some expression to the continuous limit.
In fact, a natural extension is that of reproducing the same results by substituting the instantaneous pulses with some more experimentally achievable continuous control.
Finally, we stress that this approach can be generalized also to control problems and can be exploited for Hamiltonian engineering.
