High-throughput mesoscopic optical imaging data processing and parsing using differential-guided filtered neural networks
Authors: Hong Zhang, Zhikang Lu, Peicong Gong, Shilong Zhang, Xiaoquan Yang, Xiangning Li, Zhao Feng, Anan Li, Chi Xiao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
High-throughput mesoscopic optical imaging technology has tremendously boosted the efficiency of procuring massive mesoscopic datasets from mouse brains. Constrained by the imaging field of view, the image strips obtained by such technologies typically require further processing, such as cross-sectional stitching, artifact removal, and signal area cropping, to meet the requirements of subsequent analyse. However, obtaining a batch of raw array mouse brain data at a resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.65 \times 0.65 \times 3 \,\upmu \hbox {m}^{3}$$\end{document}0.65×0.65×3μm3 can reach 220TB, and the cropping of the outer contour areas in the disjointed processing still relies on manual visual observation, which consumes substantial computational resources and labor costs. In this paper, we design an efficient deep differential guided filtering module (DDGF) by fusing multi-scale iterative differential guided filtering with deep learning, which effectively refines image details while mitigating background noise. Subsequently, by amalgamating DDGF with deep learning network, we propose a lightweight deep differential guided filtering segmentation network (DDGF-SegNet), which demonstrates robust performance on our dataset, achieving Dice of 0.92, Precision of 0.98, Recall of 0.91, and Jaccard index of 0.86. Building on the segmentation, we utilize connectivity analysis for ascertaining three-dimensional spatial orientation of each brain within the array. Furthermore, we streamline the entire processing workflow by developing an automated pipeline optimized for cluster-based message passing interface(MPI) parallel computation, which reduces the processing time for a mouse brain dataset to a mere 1.1 h, enhancing manual efficiency by 25 times and overall data processing efficiency by 2.4 times, paving the way for enhancing the efficiency of big data processing and parsing for high-throughput mesoscopic optical imaging techniques.

Introduction

As the most complex organ, the brain harbors an intricate network of trillions of neurons, glial cells, and other cellular constituents with sophisticated interconnections.This diversity underscores the distinct functional states of various cell types across different brain regions [1]. Consequently, obtaining three-dimensional anatomical data of the entire organ at the mesoscale level is pivotal for a holistic grasp of the cellular organization and the underlying pathological mechanisms [2]. In 2023, the introduction of the high-throughput array fluorescent micro-optical sectioning tomography (array-fMOST) system marked a significant advancement. This technology enables rapid data acquisition with superior imaging quality, and facilitates comparative analyses of multiple samples under uniform conditions [3]. The advent of high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies, spearheaded by array-fMOST, has substantially augmented the efficiency of procuring mesoscale datasets of the mouse brain.

Taking array-fMOST as an example, this imaging technique can achieve a voxel resolution of 0.65 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}× 0.65 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times 3 \,\upmu \hbox {m}^{3}$$\end{document}×3μm3 and obtain complete coronal images of 32 mouse brain specimens within 15 days [3]. This method significantly enhances the throughput of acquiring raw imaging datasets of mouse brains but also introduces numerous challenges. First, the data volume generated is extraordinarily large. For instance, the raw image data obtained from a batch of mouse brain arrays can reach 220 TB. This not only imposes extremely high demands on storage and computational resources but also makes data processing exceedingly complex. Second, raw image data usually requires multiple processing steps, including slice stitching, artifact removal, and cropping of signal regions. These steps are not only time-consuming but also prone to errors. Traditional image processing methods often struggle to handle such high-resolution and large-volume data, and multiple read-write operations on such vast datasets waste substantial memory resources, increasing processing time and complexity. Moreover, manually inspecting and verifying the position of each mouse brain and segmenting its contours is highly time-intensive and labor-intensive. Researchers need a reliable automated algorithm to improve efficiency [4].

However, due to variations in samples, imaging system differences, and scattering effects, the grayscale intensity of different mouse brains in a single array image varies significantly, making it extremely difficult to set an appropriate grayscale threshold to distinguish mouse brain samples, noise, and background. In such cases, traditional image enhancement methods may alter the original structural information and intensity of the images, affecting the accuracy of subsequent analyses. Finally, determining the three-dimensional spatial location of each mouse brain poses another challenge. The segmentation results of two-dimensional images may contain noise and abnormal regions, and these errors can impact the extraction of cropping boxes for further analyses. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a robust algorithm that can accurately determine the three-dimensional position of each mouse brain based on the segmentation results and crop it for subsequent analyses and processing. Based on this, it is essential to develop an automated workflow for decoupling individual mouse brain images from high-throughput array mouse brain data. This workflow must comprehensively consider various factors, such as image quality and background noise, to ensure the accuracy and robustness of its key algorithms. In recent years, deep learning network models have demonstrated exceptional performance and accuracy across multiple fields due to their powerful representational capabilities, which enable them to automatically extract abstract features from images. Among these models, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have proven to be the most successful methods for image analysis. CNNs can extract local information from images and integrate it into global information for classification or segmentation. They excel in handling complex image scenarios, such as mixed noise, low contrast, and low resolution [5–12], showing great potential in segmenting and identifying the outer contours of individual mouse brains.

Concurrently, applying image enhancement techniques to imaging data is beneficial for improving the quality and discernibility of the data, making it more conducive to observation or further analytical processing. However, common image enhancement algorithms, such as histogram equalization-based image enhancement algorithms [13], multi-scale alternating switch operator-based image enhancement algorithms [14], and Gamma correction-based image enhancement algorithms [15], all have the potential to alter the original structural information and intensity of the images. For arrayed mouse brains, an excellent image enhancement algorithm should be able to significantly improve the clarity of image edges, spots, and other textural details while preserving a large amount of original foundational information. In 2010, He et al. [16] introduced the concept of the Guided Filter (GF), which can reduce image blur and noise through guided image information, while maintaining edge information without blurring, commonly used for tasks such as image denoising, image enhancement, and image smoothing. Lang et al. [17] proposed a low-light image enhancement method based on guided filtering and multi-scale differential fusion, which enhances contrast and preserves image details by iteratively applying differential operations on filtered images obtained from guided filters at different scales, with good generalization capabilities, making it effective for enhancing other modality images.

In this paper, we construct a deep differential guided filter module (DDGF), inspired by the traditional multi-scale guided filter iterative differential approach for capturing image details. This module, with its adaptive variable parameters, is capable of flexibly learning a richer set of features, effectively enhancing the details of arrayed mouse brain images while simultaneously eliminating background noise. Building upon this, we integrate the module with a lightweight deep learning network [18] to design a deep differential guided filtering segmentation network (DDGF-SegNet) for arrayed mouse brains that is both light-weight and highly precise. With the support of ample imaging data, we tested our segmentation algorithm on an arrayed mouse brain dataset, achieving a mean Dice coefficient of 0.92, mean Precision of 0.98, mean Recall of 0.91, and a mean Jaccard index of 0.86. Subsequently, based on the segmentation outcomes, we further employed a minimum bounding rectangle calculation algorithm from connectivity analysis to simply and effectively determine the three-dimensional spatial extents of each mouse brain. In addition to the aforementioned, this paper addresses the characteristics of the original imaging datasets from high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies, exemplified by array-fMOST, by establishing a comprehensive processing pipeline, as depicted in Fig. 1. This pipeline integrates various image processing algorithms in an organic manner, automating and unifying the entire processing workflow. It eliminates the need for repetitive image input/output of large-volume intermediate data, thereby saving the labor costs associated with data processing. In a cluster environment, we have accelerated the processing scheme using message passing interface(MPI) parallel technology. With 16 nodes, each equipped with 256 G of memory, the average time required to process a set of mouse brain mesoscale imaging datasets is reduced to just 1.1 h. Methods

To better accommodate the subsequent signal analyses requirements (such as cell counting, neuron reconstruction, etc.) of the original images generated by high-throughput mesoscopic optical imaging technologies spearheaded by array-fMOST, we has developed an automated method for segmenting and identifying the positional parameters of mouse brain arrays. Building upon this, and in conjunction with currently established image processing methods such as section reconstruction, artifact removal, downsampling, we have further established an efficient and systematic automated high-throughput processing pipeline, as depicted in Fig. 1.

In the automated high-throughput processing pipeline for high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies, the cross-section reconstruction module (Fig. 1(a)) employs a redundancy elimination stitching algorithm, enabling the fusion of redundant image segments. We have independently designed the array mouse brain segmentation model module (Fig. 1(b)) and the array mouse brain crop frame recognition algorithm module (Fig. 1(c)). For the strip artifacts removal module (Fig. 1(d)), we utilize a moving median filter to eliminate the grayscale jump values in the grayscale mean curve of the image rows and columns.

In accordance with the data flow that transitions from the original strip assembly to the arrayed whole image, and subsequently to the individual mouse brain image, this approach has replaced the manual observation and cropping of each mouse brain. We have also integrated all necessary processing steps into a cohesive workflow. The entire procedure requires only a single round of image reading and writing, thus avoiding repetitive input/output operations on large volumes of data, which also increases the level of automation. Furthermore, by integrating MPI, this paper enables the entire processing scheme to run in parallel with multi-threading on high-performance computing clusters, greatly improving the efficiency of high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging data processing and parsing.

Cross-section reconstruction

Representative of high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies, array-fMOST performs strip imaging on a wide range of samples, setting an overlap area of approximately 16 pixels between adjacent strips to prevent signal loss at the imaging edges. To reconstruct a complete field of view, it is necessary to stitch the original strip data and eliminate the imaging redundancy between strips. During image fusion, we employed a fade-in and fade-out redundancy stitching algorithm [19], primarily aimed at achieving a natural smooth transition in grayscale between two images, especially in their overlapping regions. The key to this strategy is to multiply the pixel values located in the overlapping area by corresponding weights and then sum them to generate a new image, enabling a smooth transition in the overlapping area of the two stitched images. In this process, all pixel grayscale information is utilized, with each pixel being assigned an appropriate weight so that the sum of the weights multiplied at corresponding positions in the two images equals one. Additionally, it is noteworthy that pixels closer to their own position have a larger self-weight, while the weight of the opposite image is correspondingly smaller. Ultimately, through this method, this paper has achieved a continuous and seamless grayscale fusion between images.

The arrayed mouse brain segmentation model DDGF-SegNet is composed of an enhancement module and a lightweight deep learning network. In this paper, we initially designed the DDGF module as the enhancement component, capable of effectively enhancing the details of the arrayed mouse brain images while simultaneously removing background noise. A lightweight deep learning network

The lightweight deep learning network consists of an encoder and a decoder, forming a deep convolutional neural network with skip connections, which has demonstrated its powerful capabilities in biomedical image processing tasks [23]. The encoder consists of a series of convolutional layers and max pooling layers; the convolutional layers are utilized to extract features from the images, while the max pooling layers reduce the image resolution, preserving the most salient features. This part can be regarded as a conventional convolutional neural network. The decoder also includes a series of upsampling layers and convolutional layers; the upsampling layers serve to restore the feature maps from the encoder to the original resolution, and the convolutional layers transform the feature maps into pixel-level segmentation results.

In the encoding module, each convolutional layer is composed of two consecutive 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×3 convolutional kernels followed by a PReLU activation function. Subsequently, a 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×2 max pooling layer is employed after each convolutional layer to downsample the image resolution. Within the decoding module, an upsampling block consists of a nearest neighbor upsampling layer, a concatenation function, and a double convolutional layer, utilizing BatchNorm2d and PReLU activation functions. The network initially performs two convolutions to alter the channel dimensions, followed by multiple iterations of upsampling, concatenation, and double convolution operations to restore the image to its original size. Additionally, skip connections are incorporated between the encoder and decoder to integrate the high-level feature maps from the encoder with the low-level feature maps in the decoder, thereby enhancing segmentation performance and mitigating over-smoothness. The entire network employs PReLU activation functions, with the exception of the final layer, which uses a Sigmoid activation to scale the output to an appropriate range for computing cross-entropy loss. This network performs only three rounds of up- or down-sampling operations, significantly reducing the network’s parameter count while maintaining segmentation accuracy.

Crop each single mouse brain and strip artifacts removal

After obtaining the coordinates of the arrayed mouse brain frames, each mouse brain is cropped according to the frame coordinates, followed by the removal of strip-like artifacts. Due to various optical aberrations, the strip imaging strategy is affected by the luminance attenuation effect of being brighter in the center and darker on both sides within a single field of view. The large image after stitching will produce strip-like artifacts, and the edge luminance attenuation not only affects the visual experience of the observer but also has a serious impact on subsequent data analysis. Therefore, it is necessary to further process the stitched image to remove strip-like artifacts.

There are numerous established methods for ribbon artifact removal, for instance, Escande et al. [25] regarded the elimination of stripes as a restoration issue and proposed the Variational Stochastic Noise Removal (VSNR) method. Additionally, Liang et al. [26] introduced a Multi-Directional Stripe Removal (MDSR) method, which applies Fast Fourier Transform in the non-subsampled contourlet transform domain to reduce stripe components. However, these methods have a heavy computational load and are not suitable for real-time large-scale data processing. In this paper, we adopt the Moving Median Filter proposed by Ding et al. [27] to remove the grayscale jump values in the image row and column mean curves, effectively compensating for the illumination decay at the edges of the ribbons and facilitating easy computation.

This ingeniously transforms the two-dimensional image artifact removal problem into a one-dimensional denoising problem. Subsequently, a moving median filter is applied to remove the local extrema of the grayscale mean curve, yielding a new mean curve \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ms(y)$$\end{document}ms(y). Implementation details

The deep learning segmentation model proposed in this paper was trained and tested on a standalone computer with the following specifications: an Intel® CoreTM i9-10940X CPU @ 3.30GHz (12 Cores), 256GB DDR4 (3200MHz) RAM, and an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 (24GB) GPU. The code was executed in Python 3.7, implemented based on their open-source repositories available on GitHub. The testing of our proposed processing pipeline for arrayed data was completed on the high-performance computing platform at the HUST-Suzhou Institute for Brainsmatics, utilizing 16 computational nodes. Each node is configured with: Intel® Xeon® Gold 6248 CPU @ 2.50GHz (20 Cores) and 256GB DDR4 (3200MHz) RAM.

We trained the model using the prepared array of mouse brain training and validation datasets. The batch size was set to 6, with a total of 300 training epochs. Discussion

In this study, we have established an automated high-throughput processing pipeline with a rational data flow, designed for high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies represented by array-fMOST, and based on cluster MPI parallel computing. This pipeline highlights its advantages over previous fragmented data processing operation schemes, alleviating the contradiction between rapid data acquisition and slow processing. It integrates all necessary image processing operations into a cohesive workflow, automating and unifying the entire processing procedure, thus avoiding the repetitive image input/output of large-volume intermediate data and reducing the labor costs associated with data processing. This approach surpasses the time-consuming and labor-intensive characteristics of the original data processing schemes and is applicable to high-throughput, high-resolution whole-brain imaging of various cellular type samples in mouse, significantly enhancing the data processing and parsing efficiency of high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies. Combined with high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies such as array-fMOST, the automated high-throughput processing pipeline can provide a reliable method for obtaining complete high-dimensional information from intact biological samples.

Deep learning has recently achieved unprecedented progress in a variety of visual tasks, including image classification, semantic segmentation [39], and medical image analysis [40]. Building upon this, we have developed a DDGF-SegNet, which is a lightweight deep learning network for automated, high-precision segmentation and recognition of arrayed mouse brain data. This model employs the DDGF module, designed in-house, to effectively enhance the details of arrayed mouse brain images and filter out background noise. To the best of our knowledge, the DDGF is the first convolutional module dedicated to integrating the principles of multi-scale iterative differential guided filtering into the field of deep learning, providing a efficient image enhancement technique for the processing and parsing of data in high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies.

The core advantage of this technology lies not only in its efficient processing of arrayed mouse brain data but also in its strong scalability, making it adaptable to the needs of other high-throughput imaging datasets. In particular, for scenarios with similar imaging patterns (such as continuous slices, high resolution, non-overlapping sample arrays, and optical imaging), this algorithm demonstrates broad application potential. Its modular workflow, lightweight segmentation network, deep differential guided filtering (DDGF) noise processing capabilities, and automated cropping box identification function allow it to flexibly address medical imaging tasks for different tissues or organs, while maintaining adaptability to high-throughput imaging data and efficient utilization of computational resources.

The modular workflow design, in particular, provides the algorithm with flexible adaptability, allowing specific modules to be independently optimized according to the needs of different high-throughput datasets. For example, if the imaging data does not require stitching, it can be directly input into the segmentation module, which can be adjusted through parameter settings (such as regularization coefficients) to accommodate the characteristics and requirements of different medical data. For instance, for high-throughput imaging data with more noise, the regularization strength can be increased to suppress background noise. The cropping box identification module can adjust the structuring element shape according to the data morphology. The target region shapes vary significantly across different imaging datasets, and adjusting the size and shape of the structuring element allows the cropping box identification module to adapt to different medical imaging scenarios. For example, using circular structuring elements can better handle regular boundaries, making it suitable for segmenting circular organs. Using elliptical or rectangular structuring elements can match the aspect ratio of the target region, making it suitable for segmenting elongated targets. For complex boundaries, custom-shaped or dynamically generated structuring elements can be used to adapt to curved or irregular forms. This modular design not only enhances the algorithm’s versatility but also facilitates its rapid deployment in other high-throughput medical imaging tasks. Compared to traditional methods, its transferability across different imaging datasets with similar features benefits from the high adaptability of deep learning networks. By fine-tuning model parameters, it can quickly adapt to the segmentation tasks of new datasets.

Although the automated high-throughput processing pipeline proposed in this paper significantly improves efficiency and accuracy, each module still faces certain limitations and challenges in practical applications, requiring further optimization in future work. In the slice reconstruction module, the existing stitching algorithms are mainly based on gray-level smoothing of overlapping regions, which may introduce edge blurring or artifacts in areas with extreme gray-level variations (such as high-contrast transition regions). In the segmentation module, while multi-scale differential guided filtering (DDGF) effectively enhances the balance between noise removal and edge preservation, its performance is sensitive to filter parameters and regularization coefficients. Different experimental datasets may require additional parameter tuning. Furthermore, deep learning models rely heavily on high-quality annotated data, which presents a challenge in scenarios where data annotation resources are limited. Consider developing semi-supervised learning methods to reduce the workload and errors associated with manual annotation. In the cropping box identification module, morphological operations (such as dilation and erosion) are highly sensitive to the boundary quality of binary images. If the binary segmentation results contain small noise points or discontinuous boundaries, these operations may further amplify the errors. Additionally, the parameters of morphological operations (such as structuring element size) need to be optimized for different datasets; otherwise, boundary detection results may become biased. In the stripe artifact removal module, the existing median filtering algorithm performs limitedly when dealing with high-density, irregular stripes, and residual artifacts may interfere with subsequent image processing tasks.

To address these challenges, future work can focus on developing adaptive parameter adjustment mechanisms, introducing semi-supervised learning methods, combining cropping box optimization strategies with morphological priors, and exploring frequency-domain analysis and multi-scale methods to further improve the robustness and generalizability of the algorithm, thereby optimizing the overall performance of the processing pipeline.

Conclusion

In this study, we integrated the concept of guided filtering with image differencing to design the DDGF module within the realm of deep learning. This module, when combined with a lightweight deep learning network, has facilitated the development of a lightweight convolutional neural network-based segmentation algorithm DDGF-SegNet for arrayed mouse brains, enabling accurate segmentation with a reduced number of parameters. Subsequently, leveraging the characteristics of the data post-segmentation, we have established a simple and effective method to ascertain the specific spatial extents of each mouse brain. Furthermore, by optimizing the overall processing workflow, we have designed an automated high-throughput processing pipeline with a rational data flow, based on cluster MPI parallel computing, for high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies represented by array-fMOST. The average time required to process a dataset of mouse brain data is merely 1.1 h. This scheme circumvents the repetitive image input/output of large-volume intermediate data, effectively addressing the time-consuming and labor-intensive issue associated with processing the original imaging data of arrayed mouse brains. The manual efficiency has been enhanced by 25 times, and the overall data processing efficiency has been increased by 2.4 times, offering new opportunities for enhancing the efficiency of data processing and parsing in high-throughput mesoscale optical imaging technologies.
