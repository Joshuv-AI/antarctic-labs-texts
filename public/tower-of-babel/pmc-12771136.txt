Distant recurrence from thymoma biopsy seeding: a case report
Authors: Anita Paiva, Carlos S Pinto, Susana Guimarães, Andrea V Arrossi, Ritsuko Komaki, Paolo Mendogni, Pedro Fernandes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough some evidence shows an increase in its incidence, epithelial thymic tumors that comprise thymomas, thymic neuroendocrine tumors and thymic carcinomas, are rare (ranging from 2.5–2.8 per million). Thymomas, as presented in this paper, although slow growing lesions are malignant tumors, as the potential to invade surrounding structures and metastasize has been proven. In this case report, we present another feature of these neoplasms, recurrence. Although local and regional are far more frequent, distant can also occur. We present a case of late extra-thoracic recurrence associated with seeding from a preoperative biopsy, a very rare, but real scenario, that needs to be taken into account when deciding the need for invasive staging.Case DescriptionWe present a case of a 75-year-old woman who was sent for evaluation of painful palpable extra-thoracic nodules, 8 years after a type A thymoma excision. After image reconstruction, surgical exploration and detailed clinical history and record examination, it was considered as recurrence associated with seeding from preoperative biopsy. She had been followed with regular computed thoracic tomography imaging, with no signs of recurrence during the 5-year period, at the moment of oncological follow-up discharge.ConclusionsThis case is a statement for the changes that have been made in diagnosis, treatment and follow-up of epithelial thymic tumors in the last years. Nowadays, the biopsy is not proposed when a resectable epithelial tumor of the mediastinum is the probable diagnosis, as the case presented. We believe that a longer follow-up for these patients is needed as they can recur later than the initially considered 5-year period, and the absence of surgical excision of the biopsy tract or in the presence of a complex biopsy (involving, for example, pleural puncture), should possibly be considered a pseudo-R0.Keywords: Thymoma, recurrence, seeding, biopsy, case report

Highlight box

Key findings

• New insights in diagnosis, treatment and follow-up have resulted from case reports like this, for example, reducing the possible risk of biopsy seeding, by reducing the indications for pre-surgical biopsy. Another is extending follow-up beyond the 5-year interval to identify late recurrence.

What is known and what is new?

• Literature has shown that thymoma seeding is possible, and some patients have recurred after the follow-up period.

• It is a natural change in the guidelines reducing the indications for pre-operative biopsy, if thymoma is suspected, and increasing follow-up for some patients with risk factors, such as big lesions, previous biopsy, some types of thymoma (associated with higher rates of recurrence) or residual disease after surgery.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Expert guidelines should be followed regarding diagnosis and follow-up.

• Some special care must be taken in patients in whom biopsy was done as a part of the initial diagnosis, for example, longer follow-up.

Introduction

Thymomas are primary tumors of thymic epithelial cells that are classified according to the cell morphology and degree of accompanying thymic lymphocytes in five main types: A, AB, B1, B2 and B3. Metastases are uncommon and most frequently involve intrathoracic organs (local or regional) such as diaphragm, pleura or pericardium (1). Biopsy seeding is possible, and it usually happens in the first years of follow-up, but rare cases are found in the literature.

Rationale

While the diagnosis is based on pathologic examination, pre-operative percutaneous biopsy of the prevascular mediastinum lesions is discouraged by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) when thymoma is strongly suspected by the clinical and radiological features and the lesion appears resectable. This guideline is based on the potential risk of tumor seeding in the needle tract due to violation of the tumor’s capsule, which could result in thoracic wall and pleural metastases (2), which can even occur several years after the diagnostic procedure (3,4).

Knowledge gap

Complete surgical resection is the best oncological outcome predictor (2). Even after complete surgical resection, local and regional thymoma recurrences are not uncommon. On the other hand, cases of extra-thoracic/distant are rare. Even today, besides major evolution in staging, surgical treatment and follow-up, it is not possible to identify patients who will recur and when. We believe that this case report makes a statement about biopsy risks, specially in cases where a resectable prevascular mediastinal lesion is present, and about the duration of follow-up, when a possible pseudo-R0 excision is made, as the biopsy tract was not excised during surgery. The role of radiation of the biopsy tract, in cases like this, is yet to be discussed as the number of cases is very low and the indications for radiation have become even more specific.

Objective

We present a case of thymoma with chest wall seeding from the transthoracic biopsy that manifested with thoracic wall metastases 8 years after surgical resection of a thymoma. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://med.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/med-25-27/rc).

Case presentation

The patient was a 67-year-old woman who initially presented with cough that motivated a thoracic X-ray that revealed a bulky mass in the superior and prevascular mediastinum measuring 11 cm, with no apparent invasion of major structures (Figure 1). Although the suspicion for thymoma was high, the patient underwent a transthoracic ultrasound-guided biopsy via an anterior approach, which yielded a diagnosis of type A thymoma, before presentation to the surgical team. She was anti-acetylcholine receptor antibody negative. As it had diagnosis, no germ cell tumor markers were tested. We hypothesize, as it is not written in the clinical information, that the biopsy was made with the assumption that it was not resectable for its size. A subsequent median sternotomy with total thymectomy was performed, with a histological R0 excision. The patient continued under surveillance for 5 years with thoracic computed tomography (CT) scan (6/6 months initially, yearly till the 5th year) with no evidence of recurrence. Eight years after the initial surgical resection, and 3 years after losing follow-up, at the age of 75 years, the patient presented with painful hard subcutaneous nodules on the left hemithorax. A CT scan of the thorax revealed several nodules in the left hemithorax ranging from 12 to 25 mm involving the skin and deeper structures till the intercostal space (Figures 2,3). A biopsy of the most superficial and painful lesion confirmed the diagnosis of thymoma recurrence. Imaging comparison of the possible trajectory of the needle during biopsy procedure before the first surgery (based on the information provided by the patient, as no image of the procedure is present) and the current location of the lesions revealed that they matched a possible needle tract in the thoracic wall, consistent with probable tumor seeding. Surgical excision with radical intention was performed. An incision was made in the left second intercostal space (Figure 4). The lesions in the skin, subcutaneous tissue and pectoralis (major and minor) muscles were identified and removed as well as the one bulging into the intercostal space, which was taken out with bone, for negative margins. Histopathological evaluation of all nodules confirmed the diagnosis of metastases from the thymoma surgically excised 8 years ago. The patient is well and has no sign of recurrence, 2 years after the last surgical excision.

Discussion

Local late recurrence of stage I thymomas is well known and there are even cases described in the literature of local or regional recurrence decades after surgical excision (3-5). Thymomas were divided by the World Health Organization according to their typical histologic appearance in five main types, A, AB, B1, B2 and B3, that have different prognosis after surgical excision. The types A, AB, and B1 have an excellent overall survival rate of more than 90% to 95% at 10 years. Five-year survival for types B2 and B3 is 75%, 70%, respectively, related to their disease extent at diagnosis and its recurrence rate (6). This case illustrates that the histologic classification of thymomas, as we know it, is not a good predictor of clinical outcome. Seeding of the tumor in the needle tract after biopsy is very rare and there are only a few cases described in the literature. Tumor seeding usually occurs early in the follow-up, and not several years later, like in this case. Reviewing the case, no clinical and imaging signs of extra-thoracic recurrence were present during the 5-year follow-up. The biopsy technique used, in this case by echography, probably associated with a prolonged procedure time and a higher number of biopsy attempts (there is no procedural images or detailed reports were available), could have played a role in the risk of seeding. Based on the clinical history, imaging findings and surgical recurrence excision procedure, we believe that this was the cause of the extra-thoracic/distant recurrence. Most of the thymoma typical mimickers, such as epithelial spindle cell tumors of thymic origin (sarcomatoid thymic carcinoma and spindle cell carcinoid tumor), mesenchymal neoplasms (synovial sarcoma, and dedifferentiated liposarcoma) should be taken in account in the diagnosis of a prevascular mediastinal mass. In the case of the recurrence, the most probable differential diagnosis of extra-thoracic nodules are –neoplasm, such as metastatic (skin) or local invasion (breast cancer), infectious, such as skin dissemination of tuberculosis in an immunocompromised patient (after total thymectomy) or inflammatory, such as granulomatous skin lesions. One might argue, if a biopsy could be spared in this late recurrence, but metastatic extra-thoracic disease from thymic epithelial tumor was not the first suspected diagnosis, and an effort was made in the second surgery do excise the biopsy site. We wonder if the patient had undergone radiation after the first surgery, taking into account the biopsy tract, if this would have happened. Today, there is no indications for this kind of treatment consolidation in this setting, but, probably, we should take in account during oncological meeting decision, the description of the biopsy, as some might experience the same outcome as this one.

Conclusions

As NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines propose, well-defined prevascular mediastinal mass in the thymic bed with negative tumor markers in the absence of other adenopathy or continuity with the thyroid (7), that are suspected as resectable should not undergo biopsy and surgery should be the primary diagnostic and treatment procedure. We believe that in special cases such as in a difficult biopsy, in lesions whose anatomy increases the risk of seeding (pleural spaces crossing) or in patients with higher risk of recurrence (immunosuppression status) a change in follow-up should be made to increase the possibility of early detection of recurrence. We believe that even when an R0 surgery is attained, one should give special attention to the biopsy site/tract, as the absence of its excision can be considered a pseudo-R0.

Comments

Radiation Oncologist, by Dr. Ritsuko Komaki

This is a very rare but interesting case. My comment for this case is that post-operative radiation therapy (PORT) is not indicated at the time of original surgery and after recurrence. Originally this case was type A thymoma and was completely resected. The recurrence of this patient experienced is neither distant metastasis nor local recurrence as the imaging study comparing the trajectory of the needle during biopsy procedure previous to the resection and the current lesions revealed that the location of the lesions matched the needle tract in the thoracic wall, consistent with tumor seeding. This recurrence is associated with the needle tract seeding which has been documented with other tumors. There is a case report of needle implantation after transthoracic needle biopsy. This case had unresectable thymoma treated by chemotherapy with an excellent response. The needle tract recurrence developed within 4 months after the completion of chemotherapy (5). My final comment of this case is that original needle biopsy should have been skipped and the en-block surgery was needed to avoid implants or seeding along the needle tract. Also, the patients with thymoma need to be followed beyond 10 years with CT/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan annually since most patients do not manifest any symptoms from early recurrence that can be treated with curative intent.

Surgeon, by Dr. Paolo Mendogni

The case presented by Dr. Pinto is very interesting and covers an issue that deserves further studies and more in-depth analysis. In fact, the no-biopsy policy is usually followed in the high-volume and experienced centers in which there is a careful multidisciplinary evaluation of almost every single prevascular mediastinal tumor case. On the other hand, sometimes, patients arrive from other centers with a histologic diagnosis made by percutaneous (or even thoracoscopic) biopsy. This could increase the risk of tract seeding (if percutaneous biopsy performed) or pleural seeding (if video-assisted thoracic surgery is performed) even after several years. In such cases the treatment of the recurrent disease could be difficult, as in the case reported (with a multifocal spread in several organs), or in case of multiple pleural lesions, requiring often pleurectomy/decortication and/or hyperthermic intrathoracic chemotherapy (HITHOC). Moreover, in several cases of recurrent pleural (and sometimes tract seeding) disease, surgery doesn’t reach a complete resection, but often is achievable a R1 or R2 resection, with other treatments needed (i.e., HITHOC, radiant therapies or chemotherapies). I think that the percentage of cases of tract seedings is underestimated in the literature and would deserve more attention, even in multicenter worldwide studies.

Pathology, by Dr. Andrea V Arrossi

Histologically, the mass in the initial thymectomy and the subsequent excision of the chest wall lesions were similar and consisted of lobules of spindle cells with monotonous oval to elongated nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli and scant cytoplasm. The cells were arranged in short and long fascicles and sheets with numerous rosettes and hemangiopericytoma-like growth (Figures 5,6). Provided immunohistochemical stains confirmed the epithelial thymic immunophenotype of the tumor cells (immunoreactive to cytokeratin 19 and p40) and highlighted several small T-lymphocytes (CD5 positive) scattered throughout, few of them TdT positive (less than 10%). The findings are in keeping with a thymoma, WHO type A. These thymomas are usually considered indolent and have been associated with excellent survival and lower recurrence and metastatic rates than the rest of thymomas (AB, B1, B2, B3). Local recurrences and metastases occur. As with other types of thymomas, more aggressive behavior is difficult to predict for thymomas type A, and the extent of the disease, staging, and surgical margins status remain the main determinants of clinical outcome. Recurrences and metastasis have been documented with “classic” type A thymomas, in some instances associated with marked cellular atypia or necrosis. Though the resection margins had been “negative”, the margin status was pseudo-R0, given that the tissues through which the percutaneous biopsy was performed had not been removed, leaving the biopsy tract seeding as the potential source of recurrence.

Histological examination of the excised recurrence (hematoxylin and eosin staining, ×2 and ×20): multinodular neoplasm, partially delineated by fibrosis and exhibiting fibrous septation. The tumor displays a fascicular, sheet-like, and focally rosette-like architectural pattern. It is composed predominantly of spindle to ovoid cells with scant eosinophilic cytoplasm and irregular, vesicular nuclei. Lymphocyte-rich areas constitute less than 10% of the neoplasm. The mitotic index is 1 mitosis per 10 high-power fields.

(A) CD5 immunohistochemistry, at ×2 showing lobulated thymic epithelial tumor nodules separated by fibrous septa. CD5 immunostaining highlights patchy membranous positivity in neoplastic epithelial cells, supporting a thymic origin. The lymphocyte-rich background remains CD5-negative, providing contrast with the epithelial component. (B) CK19 immunohistochemistry, at ×2 showing well-demarcated lobules of thymic epithelial tumor cells separated by fibrous septa. It has diffuse strong cytoplasmic positivity throughout the neoplastic epithelial component, consistent with an epithelial-rich thymoma. Surrounding non-epithelial tissues remain unstained, providing clear delineation of tumor boundaries. (C) Hematoxylin and eosin staining, at ×2 demonstrating lobulated architecture composed of epithelial tumor cells admixed with variable numbers of immature lymphocytes. Fibrous septa divide the tumor into discrete lobules, with no evidence of capsular invasion at this magnification. The epithelial cells show bland, oval nuclei with fine chromatin, consistent with a low-grade thymic epithelial neoplasm. (D) Hematoxylin and eosin staining, at ×20 showing solid nests and sheets of neoplastic thymic epithelial cells with interspersed immature lymphocytes. The epithelial cells exhibit round to oval nuclei with fine chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli. Fibrous septa partially subdivide the tumor, and no overt capsular or peritumoral invasion is evident at this magnification, consistent with a low-grade thymic epithelial neoplasm. (E) p40 immunohistochemistry, at ×2 image demonstrating strong nuclear p40 expression within the neoplastic thymic epithelial cells, confirming epithelial differentiation. Immature lymphocytes in the background are appropriately negative, creating a clear contrast between epithelial and non-epithelial components. (F) TdT immunohistochemistry, at ×20 showing numerous immature lymphocytes with strong nuclear TdT positivity distributed throughout the tumor. The neoplastic thymic epithelial cells are negative, creating a characteristic biphasic pattern. This staining highlights the abundance of cortical-type thymocytes, a feature supportive of a lymphocyte-rich thymoma.
