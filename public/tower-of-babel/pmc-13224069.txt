ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveUnderstanding risk factors for chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression during adolescence is essential to improve outcomes for these individuals as adults. Individuals aged between 1 and less than 18 years with 2 or more estimated glomerular filtration rate measurements between 30 and <90 mL/min/1.73 m2 separated by ≥90 days without an intervening estimated glomerular filtration rate ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2 were included.ExposureCumulative SBP load was defined using area under and above the SBP curve (ie, time and magnitude) and time-only approaches using the 50th, 75th, and 90th SBP percentiles.OutcomesTime to KRT (long-term dialysis initiation or kidney transplantation) or death were ascertained via linkage with the US Renal Data System.Analytical ApproachCox proportional hazards models were used to investigate the relationship between blood pressure (BP) control and the composite of KRT or death.ResultsThe cohort included 2,585 individuals with a median follow-up of 7.45 years (IQR, 6.05-9.20), among whom 4.6% (n = 118) met the KRT or death outcome between ages 18 and 30 years. Cumulative SBP load derived from electronic health record data can inform risk of adverse long-term kidney outcomes.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by irreversible loss of kidney function resulting in excess morbidity, mortality, and health care expenditure.1 Advanced CKD (stages 3-5) occurs in 0.36% of individuals aged 12-17 years in the United States and is most commonly the result of congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract and glomerular disease.2 Alterations in growth, health development, and cognition make CKD management during adolescence challenging for patients, caregivers, and health care providers.3 Understanding risk factors for CKD progression in the adolescent age group is essential to improve outcomes for these individuals as adults.4

CKD is commonly accompanied by elevated blood pressure (BP).5,6 However, the relationship between cumulative BP load during adolescence and subsequent progression to kidney failure has not been well studied.7,8 Although treatment of hypertension can slow loss of kidney function and reduce cardiovascular risk in children with CKD,9, 10, 11 there is a lack of real-world data to inform target BP thresholds and characterize short- and long-term risk. The objective of the current study was to retrospectively analyze electronic health records (EHRs) of children with CKD from 14 academic medical centers in the Preserving Kidney Function in Children with Chronic Kidney Disease (PRESERVE) study to understand the effect of cumulative systolic blood pressure (SBP) load during adolescence on time to kidney replacement therapy or death during young adulthood.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

Using National Patient-Centered Clinical Research Network (PCORnet) infrastructure, we conducted a multicenter EHR-based retrospective cohort study to examine the association between SBP control in adolescents with CKD (aged 14 to less than 18 years) and risk of KRT or death in young adulthood (ages 18-30 years) (Fig 1). EHR data were analyzed from 14 academic medical institutions within 5 participating clinical research networks that were part of the PRESERVE study12 (Item S1).Figure 1Study design. Study design. Abbreviations: CKD, chronic kidney disease; KRT, kidney replacement therapy.

EHR data from January 1, 2009 to December 31, 2020 were harmonized to the PCORnet Common Data Model (v6.0), extracted in February 2024, and linked with the United States Renal Data System (USRDS) registry2 to obtain information on outcome events (kidney transplant, dialysis initiation, or death). Data from the EHRs included patient diagnoses, clinical measurements, procedures, and vital signs from health care encounters.

Cohort Formation

A detailed description of participant inclusion for the PRESERVE study has been previously reported by Denburg et al,12 with selection criteria outlined in Table S1. Cohort formation for the present analysis is presented in Fig S1. Individuals between the ages of 1 and less than 18 years with 2 or more estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) measurements between 30 and <90 mL/min/1.73 m2 separated by ≥90 days without an intervening eGFR value ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2 were included. They were also required to have 3 or more valid SBP measurements over 2 years at different ages during the baseline BP assessment window (ie, ages 14, 15, and 16; 15, 16, and 17; 14, 15, and 17; or 14, 16, and 17 years). BP measurements were obtained exclusively from outpatient visits, and participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) SBP ≥300 mm Hg; (2) diastolic BP ≥160 mm Hg; (3) SBP less than diastolic BP within a paired measurement; or (4) any BP measurement obtained on the day before, the day of, or the day after anesthesia exposure. If an individual had multiple measurements recorded on the same day, the mean value was used. Individuals with history of long-term dialysis or kidney transplantation before age 18 were excluded.

Variable Definitions

Exposure: BP Control

As established methods for assessing BP control using observational cohort data are lacking, our primary analysis used a measure representing cumulative BP load13,14 to estimate each participant’s level of SBP control between ages 14 and less than 18 years (Fig 2). To calculate age-, sex-, and height-specific SBP percentile values, we compared our observed SBP values to normative BP data from the 2017 Clinical Practice Guideline for Screening and Management of High Blood Pressure in Children and Adolescents.15 Next, a cumulative SBP load >90th SBP percentile was calculated using an area under the curve (AUC) approach accounting for both the duration and magnitude of SBP elevation. Linear interpolation between observed SBP measurements was used to create a continuous SBP curve, and the area under the SBP curve >90th percentile for age, sex, and height was calculated. The AUC was normalized for the duration of each participant’s baseline BP assessment window to account for varying observation intervals. A higher area value, with units of percentile point × time (pp×time), indicates poorer SBP control, reflecting a longer duration and/or greater degree of elevated BP than the SBP percentile threshold.Figure 2Illustration of the systolic blood pressure (SBP) assessment methodology for a sample participant. For descriptive analyses, participants with an AUC of 0 were defined as having well-controlled SBP. Moderate and poorly controlled BP groups were defined among the remaining participants using the median AUC value as a cut point (ie, less than median AUC considered moderately controlled SBP and greater than or equal to median AUC considered poorly controlled SBP). Secondary measures of cumulative SBP load above the 75th and 50th percentiles were similarly calculated and dichotomized into BP control groups at the median time.

As an additional secondary exposure, we also calculated a duration-based measure of BP control. This was defined as the proportion of time between ages 14 and less than 18 years SBP was ≥90th percentile.15 Duration-based measures were also calculated using the 75th and 50th SBP percentiles and dichotomized into BP control groups at the median time.

To quantify the potential benefit of SBP control below the target SBP percentile threshold, we defined a third exposure metric, the cumulative SBP load <90th percentile. This was calculated as the area above the SBP curve (AAC) and below the 90th percentile, normalized to the duration of the baseline BP assessment window. A higher AAC indicates better BP control. Similar AAC measures were calculated using the 75th and 50th SBP percentiles.

Model Covariates

Model covariates were assessed before or at the time of a participant’s first BP measurement during the baseline BP assessment window. Demographic variables extracted from the EHR included sex assigned at birth and race/ethnicity. If a participant identified as Hispanic, their race/ethnicity was classified as Hispanic. For non-Hispanic participants, race was categorized as Black/African American, White, Asian/Pacific Islander, or Other. Insurance status at the last visit before the beginning of the baseline BP assessment window was classified using payer type codes from the PCORnet Common Data Model (commercial/self-pay, Medicaid/ State Children’s Health Insurance Program, and other).

Antihypertensive medication prescription, proteinuria, CKD etiology, and eGFR were assessed using EHR data from the 12 months before the first BP measurement in the baseline BP assessment window. When multiple eGFR measurements were available, the most recent measurement to the start of the BP assessment window was used. Antihypertensive medication exposure was determined by a prescription with start date within the 12-month lookback window for an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, angiotensin receptor blocker, β-blocker, or calcium channel blocker. Proteinuria was captured using urine dipstick measurement, because urinary protein-creatinine ratios were not available because of data quality limitations. Urine dipstick values of 2+, 3+, or 4+ were categorized as positive while values of 1+, trace, or missing were treated as negative, with 1+ considered negative to minimize false positives from concentrated urine specimens.

CKD etiology was categorized hierarchically as malignancy-related, glomerular, or nonglomerular disease/non–malignancy-related. Malignancy-related CKD was defined by the presence of chemotherapeutic medications or a cancer diagnosis code, regardless of timing. Glomerular disease was defined, in the absence of any malignancy codes, as the presence of at least 2 glomerular disease diagnosis codes occurring on different dates or a glomerular disease diagnosis code and a nontransplant-related kidney biopsy procedure code. All remaining cases of CKD were classified as nonglomerular/nonmalignancy.

eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease in Children Study “under 25” (U25) equation based on serum creatinine, age, sex, and height.16 Health care utilization was quantified using the total number of BP measurements in the baseline BP assessment window and the number of hospitalizations lasting >1 day duration in the 12 months before the first BP measurement in the baseline BP assessment window. Nonkidney-related medical complexity was assessed over a 3-year period before the start of the BP assessment period using the Pediatric Medical Complexity Algorithm.17 This approach classifies International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification codes into 18 different body systems and categorizes individuals into either complex chronic disease, noncomplex chronic disease, or no chronic disease.

Outcome: KRT or Death

Outcome follow-up began at each participant’s 18th birthday and ended at KRT event, death, time of the last health care encounter, or on December 31, 2020, whichever came first. KRT events (long-term dialysis initiation or kidney transplantation) were ascertained via linkage between PRESERVE study participants and the USRDS. PRESERVE participant information was linked to the USRDS database by an honest broker using name, date of birth, and social security number, with the latter provided only when available and permitted by the participating institution. The linked USRDS data included information on kidney failure etiology, date of dialysis initiation, dialysis modality, transplant information, causes of death, and medical comorbid conditions.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics for demographic and clinical variables were generated for the full sample and separately for each BP control group (well-controlled, moderately controlled, poorly controlled). Kaplan–Meier curves were estimated for time to the composite outcome of KRT or death across the BP control groups. Cox proportional hazards models were used to investigate the relationship between BP control and the composite of KRT or death. Each SBP control measure was modeled as a continuous predictor in separate models. Primary analyses evaluated cumulative SBP load >90th percentile. Secondary analyses evaluated the alternative SBP metrics (percent time and cumulative SBP load <90th percentile) and alternative percentile thresholds (75th and 50th percentiles).

All models were adjusted for sex, race/ethnicity, CKD etiology, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade use, hospitalizations, medical complexity, eGFR, and the number of BP measurements. RAAS blockade use was included as a covariate because its antiproteinuric effects, independent of its BP-lowering effects, may influence CKD progression and thus confound the relationship between BP control and outcomes. Other antihypertensive medications were not adjusted for. We also accounted for the potential correlations between patients within institutions using robust sandwich-type standard errors.

For each model, we examined Martingale residuals to assess the linearity of the association between BP control and the KRT or death outcome. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests (using P value <0.05) and plots against time for each model. Given the presence of these violations in multiple models, we included an interaction between time intervals and BP control to estimate piecewise hazard ratios. The follow-up intervals were specified as follows: ages 18 to less than 19 years (0-1 years of follow-up), 19 to less than 20 years (1-2 years of follow-up), 20 to less than 23 years (2-5 years of follow-up), and 23-30 years (5-12 years of follow-up).

Results

The final analytic cohort included 2,585 individuals with median follow-up of 7.45 years (IQR: 6.05-9.20), among whom 4.6% (n = 118) experienced a KRT or death endpoint between ages 18-30 years. Among these individuals, 42 underwent dialysis only, 29 received a kidney transplant only, and 47 began long-term dialysis before kidney transplantation. Compared with those who did not progress to KRT, those with a KRT event were more likely to have an underlying glomerular etiology for their kidney disease (51% vs 15%), proteinuria (85% vs 39%), and antihypertensive medication prescriptions (Table S3).

Based on cumulative SBP load (ie, AUC) >90th SBP percentile, 655 participants had well-controlled BP, 964 patients had moderately controlled BP (less than median of 1.37 pp×time), and the remaining 966 patients had poorly controlled BP (≥1.37 pp×time) (Table 1). There was a greater proportion of female participants across all BP control groups. There were a higher proportion of African American participants (29%) and lower proportion of White participants (52%) with poor BP control than well and moderately controlled BP. There was also a higher proportion of participants with Medicaid/State Children’s Health Insurance Program participation among those with poor BP control. BP control did not differ by eGFR or non–kidney-related medical complexity. Antihypertensive medications were prescribed in 57% of the full cohort and were most commonly RAAS blockers (47%) and calcium channel blockers (25%). In a fully adjusted Cox model, cumulative SBP load >90th percentile between ages 14 and 18 was significantly associated with an increased hazard of KRT or death. Based on evaluation of Martingale residuals, the association was linear across AUC levels. Each pp×time increase in cumulative SBP load >90th percentile was associated with a 1.36 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.17-1.58) times higher hazard of KRT or death at age 18-19. Hazard ratios for ages 19-20, 20-23, and 23-30 years were 1.18 (95% CI, 0.96-1.46), 1.13 (95% CI, 0.99-1.29), and 1.03 (95% CI, 0.81-1.30), respectively (Fig 4a, Table S4). A similar decreasing pattern over time was observed for the 75th and 50th percentile SBP thresholds. The effect of improved BP control on the hazard of KRT or death between ages 19 and 30 years was not statistically significant (Fig 4c, Table S6). C-statistics ranged from 0.889 to 0.895 across all primary and secondary models (Table S7).

Discussion

Hypertension is a modifiable risk factor for progression of CKD in childhood and the development of target end-organ damage, such as left ventricular hypertrophy.9,11 Yet, hypertension may go under- or untreated.5 We found that real-world BP measurements can inform risk of adverse long-term kidney outcomes and define clinically meaningful target thresholds. We used multicenter EHR data to characterize the risk of KRT among children with CKD. We focused on SBP control during the ages of 14.0-17.9 years when adolescents experience significant physiologic18 and socioemotional changes that place them at risk for CKD progression. We found that worse BP control was associated with a markedly increased risk of kidney failure necessitating KRT in young adulthood and that this risk tapers over time. Risk increased linearly with increasing uncontrolled SBP. This implies that large increases in the amount of time below the SBP threshold could substantially reduce risk of kidney failure. Using both area (ie, time and magnitude) and time-only based methods, we observed that improved BP control was associated with a lower risk of kidney failure. Such data may be used to encourage health care providers to address uncontrolled BP and counsel patients and caregivers regarding the benefits of improved medication adherence, closer BP monitoring, or dietary interventions, etc. Future work on other factors influencing BP control, including social determinants of health, are also warranted to understand high-risk groups for potential policy interventions.

Clinical trials in both pediatric and adult CKD populations support intensified BP lowering to delay progression of CKD and KRT (eg, SPRINT19). The ESCAPE trial (n = 385) demonstrated a renoprotective benefit of 24-hour mean arterial pressure reduction to <50th percentile in children with both glomerular disorders and hypoplasia-dysplasia.11 We similarly found a protective benefit for BP control below the SBP thresholds we examined (50th, 75th, and 90th). Moreover, we found greater benefits as the magnitude and/or duration increase of values below these SBP percentile thresholds. As opposed to the protocolized and controlled setting of a clinical trial, these findings support the dynamic nature of real-world BP management, especially in adolescents. For example, even for an individual with fluctuations above and below a given BP threshold, a significant reduction in risk can be achieved by sustained efforts over time.

Clinical practice guidelines recommend that 24-hour mean arterial pressure in children with CKD be controlled to <50th percentile for age, sex, and height or SBP to <90th percentile using manual auscultatory office BP measurement.9 Although ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) may better characterize BP control and predict left ventricular hypertrophy in children and adolescents than in-office BP measurements, ABPM is not always readily available or feasible for repeated assessments.20 For example, cumulative BP loads have been associated with incident CKD in adults with normal kidney function and no history of hypertension or antihypertensive medication use, highlighting the prognostic value of methods to assess BP load, such as ABPM, over time.13

The adolescent years are characterized by poor health outcomes among those with CKD, such as high rates of graft rejection among those having undergone kidney transplantation.21 There is also a scarcity of evidence to inform an optimal approach to supporting adolescent patients with CKD with the social, developmental, psychological, and health-related obstacles they experience during the transition from pediatric to adult care.22 Although overall mortality during follow-up in our analysis was low (<1%), it is well known that adults with childhood-onset kidney failure have high rates of mortality, predominantly related to cardiovascular disease.23,24 Consequently, our findings have important implications for health care providers and families navigating the transition from pediatric to adult care. First, poor BP control during late adolescence should be recognized as a strong risk factor for disease progression, with steps taken to ensure that BP is well controlled over time using assessment by ABPM, coordination and communication with primary care providers regarding BP goals, or rigorous home monitoring, when feasible. Providers transitioning care of an adolescent with CKD should communicate a thorough assessment of BP control as part of a comprehensive transition plan. Similarly, those individuals with a history of poor BP control as adolescents should be considered high risk for cardiovascular mortality.10

The principal strength of the study is its size, geographic and racial diversity, duration of follow-up, use of real-world clinical data, and rigorous ascertainment of the outcome event using linked USRDS data. Much of the hypertension literature separates adults from children. Our data helps to inform the gap in large-scale clinical research that spans the pediatric and adult divide. Study limitations include potential misclassification of BP related to white coat or masked hypertension. Future studies incorporating ABPM data could inform the direction of bias from these phenomena. Adherence to prescribed antihypertensive medications was not assessed. Medical management that may have occurred outside of the study sites was also not captured. In the United States, most children with CKD are cared for by pediatric nephrologists at tertiary care referral centers. External validity to settings where primary care providers may be managing CKD or to populations with limited access to care may be limited. Last, the cumulative SBP load (AUC) and AAC methods used to define BP control did not capture potential BP fluctuations above and below a given BP percentile within an individual. The clinical implications of such fluctuations should be explored in future analyses.

In summary, using multicenter EHR data, we demonstrated that among adolescents with CKD, worse BP control is associated with a markedly increased risk of kidney failure in young adulthood. Substantial risk reduction can be achieved with even moderate improvements in BP control. Clinicians and patients should enhance efforts to address this modifiable risk factor for kidney failure.
