Reconstruction of a chronic extensor digitorum communis tendon rupture using a bridge tendon graft
Authors: Shunpei Hama, Masataka Yasuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere were no reports of chronic extensor digitorum communis (EDC) III tendon rupture in zone 5. We report a case of chronic EDC III tendon rupture in zone 5 reconstructed with a bridge tendon graft using palmaris longus tendon. End-to-end tendon repair seemed difficult for chronic EDC tendon rupture, and it was feared that direct repair would result in residual extension contracture of the finger. A 47-year-old right-handed male underwent a bridge tendon graft using the palmaris longus tendon for a chronic EDC III tendon rupture in zone 5 six weeks after the injury. Six months after the surgery, the middle finger extension was almost full, and the flexion range of motion was full.Key Words: chronic extensor rupture, extensor digitorum communis, bridge tendon graft

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic extensor tendon ruptures are typically treated with end-to-end suturing. However, there have been no previous reports of chronic traumatic extensor tendon ruptures. In mallet finger injuries, an extensor tendon rupture that persists for more than four weeks is considered chronic.1 While primary repair is preferred for acute tendon ruptures, alternative methods—such as bridge tendon grafts, tendon transfers, or tendon reconstruction—are required if surgery is delayed due to late diagnosis.2 We report a case of a chronic rupture of the extensor digitorum communis (EDC) tendon to the third digit (EDC III) in zone 5, which was reconstructed using a bridge tendon graft harvested from the palmaris longus tendon.

CASE REPORT

A 47-year-old right-handed male, employed as an antiques dealer, sustained a laceration to the dorsal aspect of his left third metacarpophalangeal joint at work. He presented to the emergency department the same day, where the wound was cleaned and closed using Steri-Strips (3M, Two Harbors, MN, USA). At that time, the medical record indicated that active extension of the middle finger was preserved. However, three weeks later, he visited a local clinic due to an inability to extend the middle finger and was subsequently referred to Baba Memorial Hospital. His past medical history included hyperuricemia. Flexion range of motion was full, but middle finger extension was limited to −15 degrees, compared to 10 degrees on the unaffected side (Figure 1A). Four weeks had passed since the injury, making direct end-to-end repair less feasible. Therefore, we planned a bridge tendon graft between the proximal and distal ends of the ruptured EDC III tendon.

Surgery was performed under brachial plexus block six weeks after the injury. A dorsal incision was made over the third metacarpophalangeal joint. Intraoperative findings revealed an EDC III tendon rupture distal to the intertendinous connections, with a gap of approximately 10 mm (Figure 1B). Scar tissue was excised, and the EDC III tendon was dissected both proximally and distally. As anticipated, end-to-end repair was not feasible. A palmaris longus tendon graft was harvested for use as a bridge. The proximal EDC III tendon was used as the power source, as it demonstrated adequate passive distraction amplitude. We performed two interlacing sutures distally and three proximally (Figure 1C). The final tension was adjusted by passively flexing and extending the wrist using the dynamic tenodesis effect. The appropriate tension was determined based on the finger extension and flexion cascades, with the greatest extension observed in the index finger and the least in the little finger, and the greatest flexion in the little finger and the least in the index finger. Postoperatively, the wrist was immobilized with a palmar cast in slight extension. The middle finger was buddy-taped to the ring finger. Active range-of-motion exercises began immediately. The cast was removed at three weeks. At six months postoperatively, grip strength was 41 kg on the right hand and 32 kg on the left. Extension of the middle finger was nearly full (Figure 1D), and flexion range of motion was also fully restored. There was no evidence of graft displacement during finger flexion.

DISCUSSION

Six weeks had elapsed since the initial injury, meeting the criteria for a chronic extensor tendon rupture, according to the definition for mallet finger.1 Given the gap between the tendon ends and the risk of residual extension lag following direct repair, we opted for a bridge tendon graft using the palmaris longus tendon. The patient showed excellent clinical recovery at six months. To our knowledge, no prior cases of chronic EDC rupture in zone 5 have been reported. We suspect that an incomplete EDC III tendon rupture at the time of injury progressed to a complete rupture. In chronic Achilles tendon and extensor hallucis longus ruptures, satisfactory outcomes have been reported following reconstruction using scar tissue interposition.2 In this case, we used a palmaris longus tendon graft instead of scar tissue, as it remains a reliable option in hand surgery. To move the metacarpophalangeal joint from 30 degrees of hyperextension to 90 degrees of flexion, 17 mm of tendon excursion is required.3 The active contraction amplitude is estimated as 80% of the passive distraction amplitude3:

active amplitude = passive amplitude × 0.8.

Thus, at least 11 mm of passive distraction was needed for the proximal tendon stump to serve effectively as the motor. In this case, the rupture was relatively recent and located distal to the intertendinous connections, so we anticipated minimal retraction of the proximal stump and used it without directly measuring its amplitude. However, measuring the passive distraction could have provided more certainty in surgical planning. In conclusion, a bridge tendon graft using the palmaris longus tendon was successfully performed for a chronic EDC III rupture in zone 5. The functional outcome was excellent.
