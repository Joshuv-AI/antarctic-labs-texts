Isoform–paralog specificity and tissue-dependent vulnerabilities in neurological disorders
Authors: Karina Hernandez-Quijada, Kenneth Y Kwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this Outlook, Hernandez-Quijada and Kwan discuss a study in this issue of Genes & Development by Lee et al. that shows that selective interactions between Capicua isoforms and ATXN1 paralogs underlie the tissue and phenotypic diversity of their dysfunction. This selectivity may extend to other disease-relevant isoform/paralog pairs that could potentially be leveraged for improved therapeutic targeting in neurological disease.Keywords: ATXN1, ATXN1L, CIC, Capicua, isoform, neurodegeneration, neurodevelopment, paralog, protein complex

Many neurological disorders are characterized by selective regional vulnerability, a puzzling phenomenon in which mutations in broadly expressed causal genes lead to disruptions that are confined to specific brain regions or neural cell types (Fu et al. 2018; Kampmann 2024). Notable examples include Huntington disease, in which an expanded HTT gene, which is ubiquitously expressed, causes selective degeneration in medium spiny neurons of the striatum (Sieradzan and Mann 2001). Several causal genes of spinocerebellar ataxias are also broadly expressed but lead to selective degeneration of Purkinje cells in the cerebellum (Hekman and Gomez 2015). Here, Lee et al. (2026) address the paradox of selective vulnerability by dissecting the interactions between distinct isoforms of the transcriptional repressor Capicua (CIC) and paralogs of Ataxin1 (ATXN1) (Fig. 1).

ATXN1-L (green) preferentially complexes with CIC-S (cyan; ATXN1L–CIC-S). Mouse models of ATXN1 paralogs and CIC isoforms show remarkable isoform–paralog specificity in phenotypes. In spinocerebellar ataxia type 1 (SCA1), polyglutamine expansion in ATXN1 increases its binding affinity for CIC, a transcriptional repressor, leading to hyperrepression of CIC target genes (Fryer et al. 2011; Coffin et al. 2023). Both ATXN1 and its paralog, ATXN1L, bind CIC. Despite their shared interaction with CIC, loss-of-function phenotypes for Atxn1 and Atxn1l diverge. Atxn1 deletion causes hippocampal learning and behavioral deficits (Matilla et al. 1998), whereas Atxn1l deletion causes hydrocephalus, lung defects, and perinatal lethality (Lee et al. 2011). Based on their observation that two isoforms of CIC (CIC-S and CIC-L) show distinct spatiotemporal gene expression patterns, Lee et al. (2026) hypothesize that CIC isoforms could confer regional specificity by selectively interacting with ATXN1 or ATXN1L in a tissue-dependent manner.

Gene isoforms arising from alternative promoter usage (as in the case of CIC-S and CIC-L), exon usage, or polyadenylation can produce structurally distinct proteins from the same gene, contributing significantly to functional diversity. Despite advances in genetic tools, the study of gene isoforms and their potential contribution to disease remains a challenge. Lee et al. (2026) meticulously generated and analyzed isoform-specific Cic knockout mouse models to address this important question. Using these new models, the investigators uncovered strikingly divergent functions for CIC-S and CIC-L. Cic-S deletion leads to hydrocephalus, emaciation, lethargy, impaired lung alveolarization, and significant preweaning lethality. In contrast, Cic-L deletion does not impact growth or survival but causes neurobehavioral deficits (including hyperactivity, motor learning deficits, and learning impairments in fear conditioning) that are not observed in the Cic-S knockout mice that survive past weaning. Most notably, these divergent Cic phenotypes correlate with Atxn1 and Atxn1l phenotypes with remarkable isoform–paralog specificity. Cic-S knockout phenotypes closely resemble those previously reported in Atxn1l knockout mice (e.g., impaired lung alveolarization, hydrocephalus, and lethality) (Lee et al. 2011). In parallel, Cic-L deletion phenocopies Atxn1 deletion (e.g., reduced anxiety, hyperactivity, impaired motor coordination, and learning and memory deficits).

ATXN1 and ATXN1L interact with CIC to mediate gene repression (Lam et al. 2006; Lee et al. 2011). To study potential isoform-specific transcriptomic consequences, Lee et al. (2026) performed RNA-seq on Cic-S and Cic-L knockouts. Intriguingly, the transcriptomic phenotypes mirror the cellular and behavioral phenotypes in isoform–paralog specificity. In the developing lung, differential gene expression in Cic-S knockout shows significant and directionally congruent overlaps with Atxn1l knockout (Lee et al. 2011). Correspondingly, in the adult cortex, transcriptomic changes in Cic-L knockout correlate with Atxn1 knockout.

These phenotypic and transcriptomic alignments support a model in which Cic isoforms confer the distinct functions of Atxn1/Atxn1l paralogs. To assess the molecular underpinnings of this possibility, Lee et al. (2026) profiled ATXN1–CIC complex formation by size exclusion chromatography. They found that ATXN1 and ATXN1L both form large CIC-containing complexes in the wild-type cortex, but the stability of these complexes is CIC isoform-dependent. Cic-S deletion destabilizes large complex formation dramatically for ATXN1L but only moderately for ATXN1. In contrast, Cic-L deletion disrupts ATXN1 large complex formation more severely. These differences in stability dependence suggest preferential binding of ATXN1L to CIC-S and ATXN1 to CIC-L, which is further corroborated by study of a W-to-A mutation in CIC in the shared ATXN1 binding domain. Remarkably, W37A in CIC-S abolishes ATXN1 but not ATXN1L binding. Conversely, the corresponding W946A in CIC-L disrupts ATXN1L binding but not ATXN1 binding. The investigators further found that the unique N terminus of CIC-L is sufficient to support ATXN1–CIC-L interaction.

Together, the effective use of isoform-specific CIC models and comprehensive biochemical characterizations provide mechanistic support for the hypothesis that functional divergence in CIC isoforms and isoform–paralog-specific complex formation underpin the divergent phenotypes in Atxn1 and Atxn1l knockout mice. Importantly, this work provides a molecular explanation for the selective effects of ATXN1 loss of function on hippocampal function (Matilla et al. 1998). Future investigations may be aimed at unveiling the mechanism restricting the toxicity of expanded ATXN1 to the cerebellum, including whether the CIC-S isoform, which is highly expressed in the cerebellum, mediates the effects of ATXN1 gain of function in SCA1 and whether tissue-dependent genome occupancy of the ATXN1–CIC complex also contributes.

The present study by Lee et al. (2026) provides compelling evidence that gene isoforms and paralogs can interact, with underappreciated specificity, to create new combinations of complexes in tissue-dependent and developmental stage-dependent ways. This possibility is conceptually distinct from previously hypothesized contributors to selective vulnerability in neurological diseases, including the exceptional metabolic demands and limited regenerative capacity of the brain. This framework may more broadly apply to other disease-relevant proteins that have multiple isoforms or paralogs. An important implication of this work is that studying gene-level expression alone may obscure potentially important isoform contributions to neurological disorders. Furthermore, the functional specificity conferred by gene isoforms may not only illuminate disease etiology and regional vulnerability but also inform isoform-specific therapeutics potentially with improved treatment specificity.
