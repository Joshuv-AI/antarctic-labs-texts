Alteration in cerebrospinal fluid flow based on the neurological prognosis of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients
Authors: So-Young Jeon, Yeonho You, Changshin Kang, Jung Soo Park, Wonjoon Jeong, Hong Joon Ahn, Jin Hong Min, Yong Nam In, In Ho Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluated alterations in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow based on the neurological prognosis of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) patients. This prospective observational study was conducted from May 2023 to June 2024. Stroke distance was measured using magnetic resonance imaging flowmetry immediately and at 72 h after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), with negative values indicating caudocranial direction. The caudocranial direction of CSF flow was observed in 17 (56.7%) patients immediately after ROSC, and in 20 (66.7%) patients at 72 h after ROSC. There was no significant difference in the occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow immediately after ROSC between the groups with good and poor neurological prognosis [6 (50.0%) vs. 11 (61.1%); p = 0.55]. However, the occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow at 72 h after ROSC was significantly higher in the group with poor neurological prognosis compared to the group with good neurological prognosis [3 (25.0%) vs. 17 (94.4%); p < 0.001]. This study demonstrated that the occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow at 72 h after ROSC was significantly higher in the group with poor neurological prognosis compared to the group with good neurological prognosis.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Medical research

Introduction

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) not only protects the brain from external impacts but also plays an essential role in maintaining homeostasis by regulating ions and metabolites, and removing waste products from around the brain, all of which are vital for sustaining life1. CSF is predominantly secreted by the highly vascularized epithelial cells of the choroid plexus, and the secretion is influenced by hydrostatic and oncotic2. Typically, CSF flows from the lateral ventricle to the third and fourth ventricle and is drained into venous circulation via arachnoid granulation3–5. However, in some conditions such as increased intracranial pressure (ICP) and idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus, CSF flows form fourth ventricle to the lateral ventricle, defined as the caudocranial direction of CSF flow6.

Cerebral ischemic-reperfusion injury occurring after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) depletes cellular energy and leads to blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption and cell apoptosis due to free radicals, reactive oxygen species, and other inflammatory factors7–10. In OHCA patients, an increase in CSF albumin levels resulting from BBB disruption is known to worsen neurological prognosis11,12. A previous study on OHCA patients observed that lower levels of carbon dioxide partial pressure measured in the CSF were associated with poor neurological outcomes, presumably due to CSF flow impairment13.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) flowmetry is a non-invasive method for assessing CSF flow and is useful in identifying changes in CSF flow and treatment effects in patients with elevated ICP14. Various parameters of CSF flow are measured using MRI flowmetry. These parameters can be obtained at the ampulla of cerebral aqueduct using MRI flowmetry, and progression of some diseases can be evaluated through these parameters15.

In OHCA patients with poor neurological prognosis, changes in these parameters are expected due to high ICP and BBB disruption [9.13]. However, to our knowledge, there have been no studies investigating whether there is an alteration in CSF flow of OHCA patients treated with target temperature management (TTM). Therefore, we evaluated alterations in CSF flow using MRI flowmetry based on the neurological prognosis of OHCA patients who underwent TTM.

Methods

Manage approval and consent

The Institutional Review Board of the Chungnam National University Medical Center (CNUH IRB202210059004) approved this study, and all procedures and protocols were implemented in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference of Harmonization and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent and approval for research on human medical ethics were obtained from the patient’s next of kin.

Study design and patients

This prospective observation study was conducted from May 2023 to June 2024 at a single center. Primary end point was the observed alteration in CSF flow based on the neurological prognosis of OHCA patients who underwent TTM. This study included OHCA patients treated with TTM whose Glasgow coma scale (GCS) was 8 or less after ROSC. Neurological prognosis of the patients was evaluated six months after ROSC using face-to-face interviews or structured telephone interviews, through the Glasgow-Pittsburgh cerebral performance categories (CPC) scale. A good neurological prognosis was defined as CPC 1 and 2, while a poor neurological prognosis was defined as CPC 3–5. Face-to-face interviews or structured telephone interviews were conducted by an emergency physician who fully understood the protocol (Supplementary Material). The exclusion criteria were as follows; (1) < 18 years of age, (2) traumatic cardiac arrest or interrupted TTM (due to hemodynamic instability), (3) ineligible for TTM (i.e., intracranial hemorrhage, active bleeding, known terminal illness, or poor pre-arrest neurological status), (4) no next of kin to consent to this study, and (5) refusal of further treatment by the next of kin.

Data collection

The data were collected from the electrical medical record database and included: age, sex, presence of a witness at the time of collapse, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), initial monitored rhythm, etiology of cardiac arrest, time from collapse to CPR (no flow time), time from CPR to ROSC (low flow time), Charlson comorbidity index, GCS evaluated immediately after ROSC, and CPC at 6 months after ROSC.

TTM protocol

TTM was administered using cooling devices (Arctic Sun ® Energy Transfer Pads TM, Medivance Corp., Louisville, USA). The target temperature was maintained with 33 °C for 24 h and then rewarmed to 37 °C at a rate of 0.25 °C/h16. The target temperature was monitored using esophageal and bladder temperature probes. Midazolam (0.05 mg/kg intravenous bolus, followed by a titrated intravenous continuous infusion at a dose between 0.05 and 0.2 mg/kg/h) and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg intravenous bolus, followed with an infusion of up to 0.5 mg/kg/h) were administered for sedation and control of shivering. All patients were monitored using a continuous amplitude-integrated electroencephalogram (aEEG; Olympic Medical CFM 6000; Natus Inc., Seattle, WA). Antiepileptic drugs were administered (levetiracetam: loading dose, 2 g bolus intravenously; maintenance dose, 1 g bolus twice daily, intravenously) when evidence of electrographic seizure was present or a clinical diagnosis of seizure was made. Fluid resuscitation or vasopressors were administered as necessary to maintain mean arterial pressure (MAP) between 65 and 100 mmHg17.

Magnetic resonance imaging protocol and analysis

The first MRI flowmetry was performed immediately after ROSC, and the second MRI flowmetry was performed at 72 h after ROSC using a 3 T scanner (Achieva 3 T; Philips Medical System, The Netherlands) on all patients included in the study. The time required for MRI flowmetry was 20 min, with the first MRI flowmetry performed just before TTM initiation and the second conducted immediately after TTM completion. During MRI scanning, hemodynamic monitoring was applied using an intraoperative MRI-compatible patient monitoring system (Veris 8600 MR, MEDRAD Inc., Indianola, PA USA). Patients included in this study underwent MRI flowmetry using an MRI-compatible ventilator (Hamilton MR1, Hamilton Medical AG, Bonaduz, Switzerland). To maintain hemodynamic stability, patients requiring catecholamine support received continuous IV infusion using an IV flow regulator (Olefusion®, DRB Healthcare Corp., Busan, South Korea) during MRI scanning. The images were analyzed with the “Q-flow” software (Philips Healthcare). A radiologist measured the MRI flowmetry parameters as follows; peak systolic velocity (PSV), peak diastolic velocity (PDV), maximum velocity (Vmax), forward flow volume (FFV), backward flow volume (BFV), stroke volume (SV), stroke distance, and maximum flow (flowmax)14,15. Vmax at the aqueduct was represented as the average of PDV and PSV. FFV was the volume that flowed in the craniocaudal direction through the aqueduct during one cardiac cycle, while BFV was the volume that flowed in the caudocranial direction through the aqueduct during one cardiac cycle. SV was represented as the average of FFV and BFV. Stroke distance represented the distance traveled by CSF in one cardiac cycle, with positive values indicating craniocaudal direction and negative values indicating caudocranial direction. Flowmax was calculated by multiplying the cross-sectional area of the aqueduct by Vmax15,18.

Statistical analysis

We planned to enrol 30 patients based on the inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria, as there were no previous studies reporting on the CSF dynamics in OHCA patients. Continuous variables were presented as median with interquartile range or as mean and standard deviation, depending on their normal distribution. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. We used Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to compare PSV, PDV, Vmax, FFV, BFV, SV, stroke distance, and flowmax measured immediately after ROSC with those measured at 72 h after ROSC. Comparisons between groups with good and poor neurological prognosis were conducted using the chi-square test, the Fisher exact test, the Mann–Whitney U test, or independent t-test, depending on their normal distribution. We used PASW/SPSS software, version 18 (IBM Inc., Chicago, USA) for all statistical analyses, and results were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

All 30 patients included in this study survived to discharge. Of the 30 patients enrolled, 11(36.7%), 1(3.3%), 2(6.7%), 8(26.7%) and 8(26.7%) patients had CPC scores of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively. The majority of OHCA patients exhibited a non-shockable rhythm at the time of discovery, with hypoxia being the most common cause of arrest (Table 1). Among the 30 enrolled patients, the MAP was 84.00(75.00 ~ 94.00), the pH was 7.38(7.32 ~ 7.43), and the arterial CO2 was 39.00(36.00 ~ 44.00) (Fig. 1).

Subgroup analysis of MRI flowmetry parameters based on the neurological prognosis

The caudocranial direction of CSF flow was observed in 17 (56.7%) patients immediately after ROSC, and in 20 (66.7%) patients at 72 h after ROSC (Fig. 2). Immediately after ROSC, there was no difference in stroke distance between groups with good and poor neurological prognosis. However, at 72 h after ROSC, the stroke distance was lower in the group with poor neurological prognosis compared to the group with good neurological prognosis (Table 3). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in the occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow immediately after ROSC between groups with good and poor neurological prognosis [6 (50.0%) vs. 11 (61.1%); p = 0.55]. However, the occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow at 72 h after ROSC was significantly higher in the group with poor neurological prognosis compared to the group with good neurological prognosis [3 (25.0%) vs. 17 (94.4%); p < 0.001], (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, it was observed that the volume and velocity of CSF flow increased at 72 h after ROSC compared to immediately after ROSC, although there was no difference in stroke distance. Additionally, the occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow at 72 h after ROSC was significantly higher in the group with poor neurological prognosis compared to the group with good neurological prognosis. Among the 13 patients who showed craniocaudal direction of CSF flow immediately after ROSC, 7 (53.8%) exhibited caudocranial direction of CSF flow at 72 h after ROSC, and all 7 of these patients demonstrated a poor neurological prognosis. Conversely, among the 17 patients who showed caudocranial direction of CSF flow immediately after ROSC, 4 (23.5%) exhibited craniocaudal direction of CSF flow at 72 h after ROSC, and three of these patients demonstrated a good neurological prognosis. However, due to the small number of patients, making definitive conclusions regarding the occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow at 72 h after ROSC between patients with poor and good neurological prognoses remains challenging.

CSF is generated at a rate of 0.3 to 0.4 ml/min, and CSF flow is influenced by cardiac pulsation and pulmonary respiration. During the early systolic phase, the pressure in the ventricle is high, while the pressure measured at the base of the spinal canal is low, resulting in craniocaudal CSF flow. In the mid-systolic phase, as spinal canal pressure gradually increases but remains lower ventricular pressure, craniocaudal CSF flow continues. During the late systolic phase, spinal canal pressure exceeds ventricular pressure, causing CSF flow to reverse to a caudocranial direction, which persists through the diastolic phase19,20. In this study, stroke distance was observed to be -0.07(-0.17 ~ 0.05) immediately after ROSC and -0.07(-0.18 ~ 0.03) at 72 h after ROSC. These findings indicated instances of caudocranial CSF flow in OHCA patients.

Plasma is transported across the capillary endothelium into the choroidal interstitial space through an oncotic pressure gradient. It then moves from the choroidal epithelium to the ventricular space via active transport4,21,22. The subdural space is situated between the inner layer of the dura and the arachnoid. Excess fluid in this space can accumulate due to various reasons. These causes can be broadly classified into two entities: subdural hygroma, which results from a vacuum effect on CSF due to brain atrophy without increased ICP, and external hydrocephalus, which occurs due to CSF flow impairment with enlargement of the subarachnoid spaces concurrent with raised ICP23,24. Furthermore, in patients with elevated ICP, alteration in CSF flow have been observed6. This latter process can be associated with a tear in the arachnoid membrane, which can be relieved using external CSF drainage25,26. A previous study noted that although ICP elevation was not significant in OHCA patients, it increased in patients with poor neurological outcomes27. In OHCA patients, brain compliance decreases due to brain edema and elevated ICP28. When brain compliance decreases, the systolic and diastolic pressures transmitted to the ventricles increase, leading to a hyperdynamic flow of CSF through the aqueduct29. Normally, the Vmax through the aqueduct does not exceed 5.2 cm/sec30. In this study, the Vmax measured at 72 h after ROSC was 5.26 (2.76–9.28), and particularly in patients with poor neurological prognosis, it was 5.73 (3.07–11.08), suggesting that hyperdynamic CSF flow due to decreased brain compliance is occurring in OHCA patients with poor neurological prognosis. Additionally, the occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow (negative stroke distance) at 72 h after ROSC was significantly higher in the group with poor neurological prognosis compared to the group with good neurological prognosis. The changes in CSF flow observed in OHCA patients in this study are speculated to be due to an oncotic pressure gradient caused by BBB disruption following ischemic-reperfusion injury. However, further studies are needed to prove the results. Although the number of patients included in this study is small, the observed changes in CSF flow using MRI flowmetry in OHCA patients will provide a basis for strategies aimed at improving neurological outcomes in these patients.

TTM affects the production and clearance of CSF, which is related to the reduction of ICP. For every 1 °C decrease in core body temperature between 31 and 41 °C, CSF production decreases by 11%. Additionally, TTM reduces CSF outflow resistance, facilitating CSF clearance31,32. However, since CSF pressure, which is closely related to CSF flow, is influenced by external factors such as arterial pressure, venous pressure, respiration, and shivering, further studies are required to fully understand the impact of TTM on CSF flow33. In this study, all enrolled patients underwent TTM at 33 °C, so the influence of TTM on the study results might be minimal.

There are several limitations to this study. First, it was conducted at a single center with a small sample size. However, a post hoc sample size calculation indicated that the occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow at 72 h after ROSC was 17 (94.4%) in the group with poor neurological prognosis and 3 (25.0%) in the group with good neurological prognosis, and 28 patients were required to achieve a power of 0.95 at a significant level of 0.05. Second, patients were evaluated based on brain MRI, so there was no continuous data available to monitor changes in CSF flow. Third, in this study, midazolam was administered to OHCA patients that could affect CSF flow34. However, since it was given to all enrolled patients, it is unlikely to have significantly influenced the study results. Finally, the study did not assess ICP or BBB disruption after ROSC. Future studies should aim to address these limitations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that caudocranial direction of CSF flow was observed in OHCA patients immediately and at 72 h after ROSC. The occurrence of caudocranial CSF flow at 72 h after ROSC was significantly higher in the group with poor neurological prognosis compared to the group with good neurological prognosis.
