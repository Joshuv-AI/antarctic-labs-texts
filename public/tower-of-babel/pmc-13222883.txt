Hybrid CNN-decision tree framework for efficient transmission line fault detection and classification: an XAI-based approach
Authors: Anish Kumar Biswas, Md Faysal Ahamed, Fariya Bintay Shafi, M Murugappan, Muhammad E H Chowdhury, Rajeswaran Nagalingam, T Samraj Lawrence
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate, fast, and interpretable fault identification on electrical transmission lines is essential for maintaining power system stability and reducing outage durations. In this study, we propose a hybrid 1D convolutional neural network–Decision Tree (1D-CNN–DT) for transmission line fault detection and classification, in which the 1D-CNN acts solely as a feature extractor. During this process, the Decision Tree performs the final, interpretable classification. By preserving decision transparency and achieving high diagnostic accuracy, the proposed architecture differs from conventional end-to-end deep learning models. In MATLAB/Simulink, four distinct transmission line scenarios were simulated to evaluate the framework under realistic operating conditions. These scenarios included short lines, long distributed lines, source-end faults, and load-end faults. We developed a large, balanced dataset of three-phase voltage and current measurements per unit, covering standard operation and ten types of faults. According to the proposed model, fault detection accuracies were 99.89%, 99.94%, 99.94%, and 99.97%, and fault classification accuracies were 99.93%, 99.58%, 99.44%, and 99.86% across the four transmission line configurations. In addition to its high accuracy, the hybrid framework demonstrated significantly lower computational complexity and shorter training and inference times than conventional ANN- and LSTM-based approaches, without requiring manual signal transformations. The SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) are integrated to enhance trust and practical usability, providing both global and instance-level interpretability that reveals how voltages and currents contribute to individual faults. According to the results, a hybrid architecture that combines deep learning and explainable AI offers reliable, efficient, and transparent real-time transmission line monitoring and protection.Keywords: Transmission line, Convolutional neural network, Fault detection, MATLAB simulink, Explainable AI(XAI), SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP), Fault classificationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With rapid industrialization and urbanization, there is an ever-increasing demand for electrical power in the current generation. Owing to the constant need for energy, the number of power-producing units has increased, which has led to the development of a complex power system (PSN)1. A conventional power supply network (PSN) comprises three primary components: energy production, distribution, and transmission. Transmission lines are part of the critical infrastructure we’ve come to rely on in our modern societies. They transport electricity from generation to distribution. Even a brief power outage can cause significant inconvenience, financial costs, and even death. The loss of electrical power impacts not only businesses and office buildings but also essential public institutions such as fire, police, and the military; hospitals, clinics, and healthcare entities. The reliable and continued functioning of these systems is vital to the daily functioning of industrial, commercial, transportation, and domestic activities2. Any disruption of the electrical grid could result in substantial financial loss and negative social impacts. Power losses in transmission systems can result from various reasons, such as faults in insulation, foreign objects, accidents or natural disasters, human intervention, or interference by animals3. These interruptions often cause short circuits, destabilization of the electric system, and consequently, significant energy losses4. For the effective operation of electrical infrastructure, the identification as well as localization of electrical transmission line faults is essential. Considering the dynamics, range, and interrelated nature of components in an electrical power system, disturbances or component disruptions are inevitable5. Typically, these problems are observed by locating point measurements of voltage and current. Therefore, for the electrical system to continuously provide atmospheric service, the fault origin must be accurately and immediately identified during a transmission line emergency power outage6. In the absence of a system imbalance due to insulation failures or contact between live wires, the system becomes unbalanced only at the point of a fault to earth, and the fault current is a short-circuit problem of the line. A short circuit is an abnormal connection between two points of different potential, resulting from an accident or by design, and differs from non-fault current in having extremely low impedance7. These are the most common and damaging fault conditions that cause an excessive current to flow through the transmission line equipment. If these disturbances last for several seconds, there will be significant damage to the equipment. Short circuits may occur from both inside and outside. Short-circuit regimes are divided into two categories: symmetrical and unsymmetrical. A concurrent short circuit across all three phases produces a symmetrical fault with no special conditions8. Three-phase faults are (LLL) exceptional cases of symmetrical faults. The problem is asymmetrically more common in a transmission system. All values of currents and phase shifts can be so varied as to model an asymmetrical fault generating the out-of-balance condition9. The unbalanced faults are divided into three cases. A significant quantity of power is dissipated due to short-circuit faults. To ensure stability, promptly determining the nature of the issue and its precise location is essential. To prevent power loss, the ineffective portion must be cut away from the healthy portion to ensure a continuous power supply. Traditionally, relays and circuit breakers, or circuitry, circulate these functions. Not all situations, however, allow for sufficient response times to minimize the impact of disruptions. Detection and location capabilities: Providing these capabilities improves the reliability of electrical transmission systems. Therefore, for line segment isolation, system stability, and safety, accurate and timely fault detection (FD) along with fault classification (FC) are essential. Upon reviewing the relative literature, it is found that the labelled data for different fault cases in transmission lines is very little. While training data is required to perform fast classification of multiple failure scenarios, a labeled dataset preprocessing is desired. To accommodate the significant problem in this research stream, this research has designed the simulation-based data set by precisely labeling each faulted occurrence and short circuit fault using MATLAB/Simulink.

Related work

Power system malfunctions can cause equipment damage, system instability, and power outages. Timely and precise problem diagnosis and classification are essential to the dependability and safety of power systems13–16. Different algorithms, such CNN and ANN, have been widely used recently for fault classification and detection. Tong et al.17 created a CNN model for identifying along with characterizing transient faults in electrical power transmission lines, achieving 0.9994 for area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and 98.24% which is average classification accuracy. Similar to this, Lee et al.18 acquired 98.00% accuracy utilizing CNN to identify the four categories of faulty cell sensors and communications data. Different researchers implemented artificial neural networks to detect faults; Abdullah19, for instance, used discrete wavelet transform along with ANN in conjunction for detection of transmission line faults with an accuracy of 98%. Capsule networks and other more intricate structures have been developed. Fahim et al.'s CNSF framework20 obtained 99.72% which is an average accuracy over eleven fault classes and four transfer learning settings. The undecimated discrete wavelet transform & the Chebyshev neural network was used by Vyas et al.21 to classify ten fault types with a 98.69% accuracy rate. Several machine learning algorithms have also been used in addition to neural network-based techniques. Using extreme learning machines, Chen et al.22 created fault classification models based on Gaussian transmission and summation wavelet. Zhang et al.23 used SVM along with LSTM in order to detect transmission line faults, and the accuracy was 97.7%. An integrated machine learning approach for obtaining features that employs support vector machine classification as well as wavelet packet modelling, was presented by Ray et al.24. Majd et al.25 used k-nearest neighbors to obtain 98.6% accuracy for ten fault types. To improve power system dependability by early failure identification,26 introduced a fault diagnosis method for power transformers that uses data translation techniques and dissolved gas analysis. To achieve a predicted accuracy of 90.61% six machine learning algorithms were refined. In the meantime, LSTM network-based fault detection and classification approach along with a discrete wavelet transform (DWT) approach for DC transmission lines is provided. With the use of a specific relay system and Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter optimization, the method achieves an accuracy of 99.04%27. Real-time RFID data measurement combined with a hybrid CNN-LSTM model improves microgrid fault detection and rotor angle stability with a 94.93% classification accuracy28. A PMU-based ensemble learning framework combining DT, RF, and K-NN achieved 99.88% classification accuracy across six transmission line fault scenarios, enhancing interpretability and fault diagnosis reliability through explainable AI29. An interpretable IoT-based sensor fault detection framework (XAI-LCS) using XGBoost and explainable AI techniques, which achieved 99.8% validation accuracy across four fault types including bias and drift, while also addressing data imbalance for reliable industrial deployment30. To enhance transparency in fault prediction, a Random Forest-based model integrated with XAI methods like SHAP and LIME was developed by Indrayani et al.31, enabling interpretable fault analysis on real-world Indonesian distribution network data for informed maintenance strategies. Jang et al.32 proposed an explainable fault diagnosis framework by integrating SHAP with adversarial autoencoders to accurately detect and interpret both single and multiple faults in chemical process systems, thereby improving transparency without compromising diagnostic performance. Sairam et al.33 proposed an edge-deployable eXplainable Fault Detection System (XFDS) for PV panels that integrates an irradiance-based physical model with an XGBoost classifier and LIME-based XAI, achieving accurate incipient fault detection and transparent explanations for field technicians. Recent work proposed a DNN-based fault diagnosis model combined with Shapley-value-based integrated gradients, achieving high interpretability for individual predictions in complex process systems34 Despite recent advancements in transmission line fault diagnosis, many existing approaches remain computationally expensive, time-consuming, and overly complex in architecture. These methods often rely on deep learning models such as LSTMs or capsule networks, which, while powerful, lack interpretability and are unsuitable for real-time deployment in resource-constrained environments. Additionally, most prior studies depend on manual feature extraction techniques like the discrete wavelet transform (DWT), further increasing complexity and engineering effort. To bridge this gap, the present study introduces a novel hybrid framework that integrates a 1D Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN) for automatic feature extraction with an interpretable Decision Tree classifier for final fault classification. This architecture not only eliminates the need for manual signal transformation but also ensures lightweight, fast, and transparent decision-making. Extensive fault scenarios including 10 distinct fault types across four realistic transmission line configurations are simulated in MATLAB/Simulink to ensure robustness and generalizability. Moreover, by incorporating SHAP-based explainable AI (XAI), the model addresses the critical black-box limitation of prior approaches, enabling both global and local interpretability. In doing so, this research fills a critical void in the literature by delivering an efficient, interpretable, and scalable fault detection and classification system optimized for real-world power grid applications. Suggested framework for fault identification

To identify faults, this research introduces a novel approach, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Four distinct transmission line scenarios were simulated in MATLAB Simulink, yielding per-unit voltage and current data for both normal and fault states across 10 fault categories. Faulty data were collected under fault conditions, while non-faulty data were randomly collected. A training set and a testing set were created from the data, and the input features were standardized using StandardScaler. A hybrid model for feature extraction has been developed that integrates a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) with a decision tree for classification. The hybrid model was trained because of fault detection along with classification for four transmission line scenarios. The structure was compared with the top-performing models with respect to classification performance, dataset size, and computational complexity. The ensuing subsections provide a detailed explanation of each phase.

Simulation of model and generation of data

Figures 3, 4, 5 and 6 are the graphical representation of the simulation models for the four transmission line cases, and the details of their components. Both short-distance and long-distance transmission lines are modelled using these models, wherein energy is generated in one location and transferred to another. In practical scenario, loads consist of a combination of capacitive (C), inductive (L), along with resistive (R) components and are often (for the sake of simplicity) represented by a single equivalent load. This study looked at four standard TL configurations. TL-1 was composed of one generation unit and one RLC load for distributed high transmission lines with fault at the load end, while TL-2 was composed of one generation unit and one RLC load for short transmission lines. Three RLC loads and two generating units made up the TL-3 case for distributed high transmission lines with faults at load end, and Three RLC loads, and two generating units made up the TL-4 case for distributed high transmission lines with faults at source end. The transmission line for long distances was 200 km long.

Standardization

Standardization processes the values of features or attributes such that the mean is zero and the standard deviation equals one, but the information and the relations between the elements remain unchanged. This guarantees that every feature makes an equal contribution to the model and removes scale differences, especially if features have very different ranges. Standardization is appropriate for algorithms that are sensitive to feature magnitudes, such as deep learning and machine learning models. In this study, we have used standard-scaler standardization, which standardizes the data using the formula in Eq. (1). Hybrid 1D-CNN and decision tree

This study presents a hybrid model for extracting features that integrate one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) along with decision tree for fault detection (FD) and classification (FC), which enhances the efficiency and robustness of the learning process. CNN removes the need for manual feature engineering by automating the feature extraction process. Three convolutional layers with 32 filters make up the architecture, which uses a hierarchical approach to extract features from the current and voltage values. The first layer will take 960 input units, the second layer will reduce the dimensionality to 200, and the last layer will reduce it to 10, with kernel size 2 used at all 3 stages. Each layer uses the ReLU activation function to add non-linearity, which enables the network to learn intricate patterns from the input characteristics (the three-phase per-unit current and voltage values). The convolutional layers can detect complicated patterns in the voltage and current signals by encoding low-level and high-level features. The convolutional layers are designed to learn not only spatial but also temporal dependencies of the data which is vital for fault detection tasks. To reduce computational complexity, MaxPooling1D layers with pool size 2 shrink the spatial dimensions of feature maps while keeping just the most significant features. The extracted features are converted to a one-dimensional vector and then fed into two fully connected layers (MLP), with 32 neurons each. CNN model employs the Adam optimizer for model training with a learning rate of 0.0001, the retrieved characteristics are utilized to train the decision tree classifier, which executes the final fault classification. After feature extraction by the 1D-CNN, a decision tree classifier is used for fault detection (FD) and fault classification (FC). A decision tree model is trained on features extracted by the CNN and evaluated by tenfold cross-validation for robustness. StandardScaler normalizes features to have uniform scaling before training decision tree for every fold, accuracy, confusion matrix, classification report, and ROC AUC are calculated. Mean accuracy and standard deviation between folds are reported, which constitute a complete evaluation of model performance. ROC curve is produced for each class value to illustrate the trade-off between the true positive and true negative ratios, reflecting the decision tree’s efficacy in differentiating fault types. The hybrid framework has the advantage of automated feature extraction, lower computational overhead, and shorter processing times compared with the more complex architectures. The combination of the hierarchical feature learning of CNN and the simplicity of decision tree leads to an efficient and interpretable model. Assessment criteria

The models were evaluated using a number of widely used statistical measures, including F1-score, recall, accuracy, and precision. The ratio of accurately classified examples across all classes to the total number of instances is known as accuracy as in Eq. (17):

Really faulty, not faulty, not faulty but forecasted as faulty, and faulty but predicted as not faulty are the classes denoted by true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN), respectively. According to Eq. (18), the precision (P) is the ratio of true positives (TP) to all positive forms. It is the percentage of correctly predicted erroneous data to the total number of incorrect data.

Recall (R) is the number of correct predictions that a model makes divided by the number of actual cases.

The harmonic mean of precision and recall is employed to determine the F1-score.

The proposed model’s performance was evaluated using the ROC-AUC metric, which is a metric that quantifies a model’s capacity to differentiate between classes. The proposed model’s robustness and efficacy in fault detection and transmission line classification are demonstrated by the metrics in conjunction with training and testing time and other parameters.

Analysis and results

Fault classification

The proposed hybrid model was rigorously evaluated using fault data generated from four distinct synthetic transmission line (TL) configurations. The confusion matrices corresponding to TL-1, TL-2, TL-3, and TL-4 are illustrated in Fig. 10, while the detailed performance metrics, such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score are summarized in Tables 4, 5, 6 and 7. The model achieved exceptionally high classification accuracies across all scenarios: 99.93% for TL-1, 99.58% for TL-2, 99.44% for TL-3, and 99.86% for TL-4. These results demonstrate the strong generalization capability of the proposed 1D CNN-Decision Tree framework in detecting and classifying diverse transmission line faults. Importantly, the model maintained high performance regardless of the underlying TL configuration, indicating that its effectiveness is not sensitive to structural variations in the power system. To further validate the robustness of the framework, ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curves for each TL setup are presented in Fig. 11. The model achieved an AUC (Area Under the Curve) of 100% across all fault types in TL-1 and TL-4, and nearly perfect AUC values for TL-2 and TL-3, confirming its high discriminative power. Additionally, to assess generalizability on external data, the model was tested using publicly available fault datasets from Jamil et al.42, hosted on Kaggle. The performance on this external dataset is visualized through the confusion matrix in Fig. 14, reaffirming the adaptability and reliability of the proposed method beyond the simulated environments.

Analysis of computational complexity and time

In Table 12, the computational complexity and processing time of the proposed 1D CNN-Decision Tree model are compared with state-of-the-art deep learning approaches. With only 15.5 K trainable parameters and 0.9 MB model weight size, the proposed model reduces training time (6 min) and inference time (1.3 ms), much more rapidly than conventional CNNs (780 K parameters, 24 min training time, 4.1 MB size) and LSTM-based models (CNN-LSTM 350 K, LSTM-DWT 400 K). This efficiency stems from two key aspects: (i) the CNN component’s ability to automatically learn hierarchical features from raw voltage and current signals without extensive manual feature engineering, and (ii) the Decision Tree’s extremely low computational cost for final classification. As a result, the hybrid model is both lightweight (requiring only 0.9 GB of GPU memory) and computationally efficient (2.5 MFLOPs), making it ideal for real-time or resource-constrained applications. Compared to conventional architectures such as CNN or LSTM variants, which involve high memory usage and longer execution due to multiple convolutional or recurrent layers and dense weight matrices, the proposed model performs similarly in terms of classification accuracy while being 1.8-4.7 times faster in inference and 4-5 times faster in training. As a result, the proposed 1D CNN-Decision Tree architecture achieves an optimal balance between anmodel performance, interpretability, and computational resources.

Explainable AI and model interpretability

Explainable AI methodologies utilizing SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were employed to perform a comprehensive interpretability analysis of the hybrid model’s decision-making process. The SHAP framework effectively bridges the interpretability gap between the automatic feature extraction capabilities of the convolutional neural network (CNN) and the transparent decision-making process of the decision tree classifier, ensuring end-to-end explainability for critical power system applications.

Background selection strategy for balanced explanations

The accuracy and fairness of SHAP explanations heavily rely on the selection of a representative background dataset. In the proposed multiclass fault detection framework, a balanced background dataset was constructed to ensure that SHAP values faithfully represent the model’s behavior across all classes, including both faulty and non-faulty instances. For the fault classification problem, the background dataset was formed by randomly sampling an equal number of instances (500 samples) from each of the 11 classes (10 fault types and 1 no-fault class), resulting in a total of 5550 background instances. This balanced selection prevents class imbalance from skewing expected value calculations and ensures fair representation in the SHAP computation. For the fault-detection scenario, which involves distinguishing between fault and non-fault conditions, a separate balanced dataset was used. A total of 1000 samples were selected, comprising 500 instances from the fault class and 500 instances from the no-fault class. This balanced approach ensures that both classes are equally represented in the background dataset, thereby preventing any bias and accurately reflecting the true decision boundary between fault and no-fault conditions.

Fault classification interpretability

The SHAP bar plot provides model interpretability by quantifying each feature’s contribution to the fault classification decisions. With a mean SHAP value of 0.6, voltage_phase_c is the most discriminative feature, followed by current_phase_a and current_phase_b, which have respective mean SHAP values of roughly 0.55 and 0.52 (Fig. 15). Interestingly, voltage measurements consistently outperform current measurements in distinguishing fault types, which align well with power system theory where voltage deviations typically serve as primary fault indicators. This explainability analysis strengthens the practical applicability of the CNN-Decision Tree approach by demonstrating clear patterns in feature importance.

SHAP bar plot for fault classification.

By illustrating how distinct factors help shift the model’s prediction from the baseline expectation E[f(X)] = 0.091 to the final prediction f(x), SHAP waterfall charts offer instance-level explanations. Each plot demonstrates the additive nature of feature contributions, where positive values (red bars) push the prediction toward the target class while negative values (blue bars) push it away. As shown in Fig. 16, for the 8th instance no-fault case (NF), we observe that current_phase_b exhibits the strongest positive contribution (+ 0.32), followed by voltage_phase_b (+ 0.29), effectively driving the model’s confidence toward correct classification. Interestingly, the ACG fault case shows voltage_phase_c (-0.07) as the dominant feature, highlighting how different fault types activate distinct feature importance patterns, which demonstrates model’s ability to learn fault-specific electrical signatures rather than relying on generic patterns. Strong insights into the model’s decision-making process across various fault scenarios are revealed by the waterfall analysis. While the AB fault case demonstrates predominantly negative contributions from voltage measurements, particularly voltage_phase_b (-0.06), the BG fault case shows a contrasting pattern where voltage_phase_b provides strong negative evidence (-0.24) but current_phase_c contributes positively (+ 0.11).

Fault detection interpretability

The SHAP waterfall plots are shown in Fig. 17. provide comprehensive instance-level explanations for the hybrid CNN-Decision Tree model’s binary fault detection decisions across four representative transmission line scenarios, demonstrating the proposed model’s sophisticated ability to distinguish between normal operating conditions and fault states through transparent feature contribution analysis that enables understanding the specific electrical patterns driving each detection decision with unprecedented clarity and interpretability. The 0th instance waterfall plot reveals a definitive no-fault detection where the model’s base value of 0.5 is systematically reduced through negative SHAP contributions from all electrical measurements, with current_phase_c providing the strongest negative contribution (−0.13), followed by voltage_phase_a (−0.12) and voltage_phase_b ( − 0.08), ultimately driving the final prediction toward the no-fault category with high confidence, indicating that all electrical parameters exhibit characteristics consistent with normal transmission line operation where voltage levels remain stable within acceptable ranges and current flows follow expected patterns without any anomalous deviations that would suggest system disturbances.

SHAP waterfall plot for fault detection of (a) 0th instance, (b) 8th instance, (c) 24th instance, (d) 38th instance.

In stark contrast, the 8th instance waterfall plot demonstrates the first fault detection scenario where positive SHAP contributions from key electrical features systematically drive the prediction above the critical 0.5 threshold toward fault classification, with voltage_phase_a providing a significant positive contribution (+ 0.4) while current_phase_a adds substantial supporting evidence (+ 0.17) for fault conditions, illustrating how the proposed model has learned to recognize characteristic voltage deviations and current anomalies that collectively indicate transmission line disturbances requiring immediate attention from automated protection systems. The 24th instance analysis reveals another distinct fault detection case where current_phase_c emerges as the dominant positive contributor (+ 0.33) to fault detection, accompanied by voltage_phase_a (+ 0.24) and various current measurements that provide additional corroborating evidence, demonstrating the model’s remarkable ability to identify fault signatures across different phase combinations and electrical parameter variations that characterize the diverse spectrum of fault commonly encountered in complex transmission line operations. The 38th instance waterfall plot presents the most pronounced fault detection scenario where current_phase_b provides the strongest positive contribution (+ 0.3) to fault detection, followed by voltage_phase_b (+ 0.22) and supporting contributions from current measurements, indicating a severe fault condition where multiple electrical parameters exhibit significant deviations from normal operating ranges that necessitate immediate protective action to prevent catastrophic equipment damage and widespread system instability. These comprehensive waterfall visualizations collectively demonstrate the hybrid model’s sophisticated understanding of transmission line electrical behavior, where the clear distinction between the 0th instance showing exclusively negative SHAP contributions and the fault instances displaying predominantly positive contributions validates the model’s learned ability to recognize authentic fault signatures while effectively avoiding false alarms during normal operating conditions, thereby ensuring reliable protection system performance.

Conclusion

This study proposes a novel hybrid 1D CNN-Decision Tree framework for intelligent fault detection and classification in power transmission lines. A 1D-CNN was used solely for automatic feature extraction from voltage and current signals, while a Decision Tree classifier provided interpretable results. We tested the model on four realistic transmission line configurations simulated in MATLAB/Simulink, and we achieved exceptional fault detection and classification accuracies (up to 99.97% and 99.93%, respectively). Furthermore, the proposed model is more computationally efficient than state-of-the-art deep learning approaches. Only 15.5 K trainable parameters require just 6 min of training time, 0.9 GB GPU memory, and 1.3 ms for inference, making it well-suited to real-time and resource-constrained environments. Compared to traditional CNN, LSTM, and transformer-based methods, which require 10X to 50X more resources, this has a notable advantage. In addition, the model achieves a low MFLOPs score of 2.5, as well as a compact model weight of 0.9 MB, which reinforces its lightweight design. Moreover, SHAP-based explainable AI was integrated for visualizing and interpreting feature influence, allowing operators to make transparent decisions. To improve generalizability, a large, diverse dataset with 22,000 samples of 11 classes was generated, and multiple real-world TL scenarios were simulated. Despite the framework’s effectiveness in controlled settings, real-world deployment may be impacted by noise and uncertainty. It is anticipated that future work will focus on robust analysis, real-time testing, and extending the model for fault localization. Overall, the proposed interpretable and efficient framework presents a promising direction for enhancing intelligent power system protection.
