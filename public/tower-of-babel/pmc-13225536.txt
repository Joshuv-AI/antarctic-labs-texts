Association between the development of sepsis and the BAR index in patients with acute pancreatitis: A retrospective investigation based on MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Jinhai Wang, Fuhong Zheng, Lei Zhu, Haibo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis, as a severe complication of acute pancreatitis (AP), needs to be identified and treated as early as possible. The blood urea nitrogen-to-albumin ratio (BAR) index has shown good predictive ability in many studies. However, its effectiveness in predicting AP complicated by sepsis has not been further verified. Therefore, this study mainly discusses the correlation between BAR and the risk of sepsis, aiming to provide a simple and early warning tool for sepsis risk stratification in AP patients. This retrospective cohort study analyzed the data of 779 AP patients from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care database 3.1. Critically ill participants admitted between 2008 and 2022 at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston were included. According to the Sepsis 3.0 diagnostic criteria, the AP patients included in the study were further divided into a sepsis group and a non-sepsis group. The Boruta algorithm, combined with the extreme gradient boosting model, was used to screen variables beneficial to the outcome. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to evaluate the relationship between the BAR index and the occurrence of sepsis after admission. The results showed that the BAR index was significantly associated with the occurrence of sepsis, and the risk of sepsis increased with the increase in the BAR index (all P values for trend < .001). Subgroup analysis showed that the BAR index was positively correlated with the probability of sepsis in patients of different ages, genders, and those with hypertension and diabetes (all P for interaction > .05). The area under the curve of the combined use of the BAR index and clinical indicators was 0.704 (0.667–0.741), which was significantly higher than that of using the BAR index alone, 0.611 (0.572–0.651), with a statistically significant difference (P < .001). In conclusion, the study results suggest that the BAR index may help identify which AP patients are at a higher risk of developing sepsis.

1. Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common and severe gastrointestinal disorder that requires emergency hospitalization. The global incidence of AP is estimated to range from 15 to 30 cases per 100,000 people annually.[1] Although many patients recover with conservative management, approximately 20% to 30% progress to severe AP (SAP), which carries a significantly higher risk of complications, including systemic inflammatory response syndrome, organ failure, and sepsis.[2] Sepsis, a life-threateningcondition triggered by infection, is one of the most critical complications of AP and is strongly associated with increased mortality rates, prolonged hospital stays, and long-term morbidity. Studies have shown that 30% to 40% of AP patients develop sepsis, further exacerbating the disease course and leading to poorer outcomes.[3] Therefore, early identification of AP patients at high risk for sepsis is crucial for improving prognosis and guiding timely clinical interventions. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum albumin (Alb) are 2 commonly used biomarkers in clinical practice to evaluate a patient’s renal function and nutritional status.[4,5] Both BUN and Alb levels provide valuable insights into the body’s metabolic and inflammatory states. Recently, the blood urea nitrogen-to-albumin ratio (BAR), a simple and easily calculable index, has gained attention as a promising prognostic biomarker in critically ill patients.[4–6] The BAR index integrates renal function and nutritional status, 2 critical factors in assessing the severity of various diseases. It has been shown to correlate with adverse outcomes in conditions such as community-acquired pneumonia[7] and gastrointestinal bleeding,[8] highlighting its potential for clinical use. AP is often accompanied by significant inflammatory responses, fluid imbalances, and multiorgan dysfunction, which can lead to an increased risk of infection and sepsis. For example, traditional inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) are commonly used for sepsis detection, but they do not always reflect the comprehensive physiological changes occurring in critically ill AP patients.[9] In contrast, BAR offers a more holistic approach, reflecting not only the patient’s inflammatory status but also the impact on renal and nutritional reserves. Given the complex interplay of these factors in the progression of AP, BAR may offer a more reliable and straightforward marker for predicting sepsis risk. However, while the predictive power of BAR has been demonstrated in other diseases, its specific application to sepsis in AP patients has not been systematically studied. This study aims to fill this gap by exploring the association between BAR and the risk of sepsis in AP and to provide a new objective basis for the clinical early identification of high-risk patients and the optimization of treatment decisions.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source

All data required for this retrospective study were obtained from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database.[10] All personal data were deidentified to ensure patient privacy. Ethical approval and the requirement for informed consent were waived. The first author (Jinhai Wang) completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative course and passed both the “Data or Specimens Only Research” and “Conflicts of Interest” examinations (ID: 13521079) to obtain authorization to access the database. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

2.2. Study population

According to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnostic codes for AP (ICD-9 code: 5770; ICD-10 code: K85.0), patients with AP were selected from the MIMIC-IV database. A total of 1204 patients with AP were included. The main exclusion criteria were patients who were not admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for the first time (n = 212) and patients with missing data on Alb, BUN, complete blood count, or biochemical test results (n = 213). Finally, a total of 779 patients were included in the analysis. According to the Sepsis 3.0 diagnostic criteria, the patients were then divided into septic and non-septic groups. A flowchart illustrating the patient inclusion and exclusion process is shown in Figure 1.

2.3. Clinical data collection

Data extraction was performed using the Decisionlinnc1.071 software (Hangzhou Yontong Information Technology Co., Ltd., https://www.statsape.com). Patient characteristics, including age, sex, body weight, and ethnicity, were collected. The extracted complications and comorbidities mainly include hypertension, diabetes, and acute kidney injury (AKI). Laboratory indicators include white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), red blood cell distribution width, hemoglobin, platelet count, anion gap, bicarbonate, calcium, chloride, potassium, creatinine, glucose, Alb, BUN (urea-nitrogen), sodium, lactate, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2), partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), prothrombin time, partial activated thromboplastin time, and international normalized ratio. All laboratory data and disease diagnosis scores were collected within 24 hours of admission.

Utilizing the formula (BUN mg/dL)/(albumin g/dL), the BAR index was computed.[11] Variables with more than 30% missing values were removed, and for the remaining variables with missing values, the multiple imputation method was used to fill them.[12]

2.4. Feature selection

Before investigating the association between the BAR index and incident sepsis in AP patients, we employed a machine learning framework to systematically rank candidate predictors based on existing literature. Feature selection was performed in 2 stages. First, the Boruta algorithm was used to distinguish informative variables from noise. Boruta works by creating permuted copies, or “shadow features,” of each original feature. Variables whose Z-scores consistently exceed the maximum Z-score of any shadow across random forest iterations are retained, while those that do not surpass this threshold are discarded.[13] Second, an extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model was trained on the retained features. To further assess feature importance, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values were computed, quantifying each variable’s contribution to the predicted probability of sepsis. SHAP transforms high-dimensional interactions into additive importance scores, significantly improving the interpretability of the model and reducing its “black-box” nature.[14] This approach provides clinicians with an intuitive, visual summary of the factors driving model predictions (Fig. 2).

2.5. Statistical analysis

DecisionLinnc (version 1.071) software was used to perform statistical analysis in this study.[15] Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviation or medians with interquartile ranges based on distribution and analyzed using the Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher exact test.

Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were evaluated using proportional hazards regression models (Cox regression models). There were 3 models built. Model 1 did not adjust for covariates. Model 2 included sex and age as variables. Model 3 further adjusted for additional relevant variables selected for their importance using the Boruta algorithm, including age, sex, weight, BAR, WBC, calcium, and bilirubin. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the consistency of the BAR index across subgroups. The 4-node restricted cubic spline curve was applied to verify the nonlinear relationship between BAR and the risk of sepsis. The predictive value of BAR was further evaluated through the receiver operating characteristic curve, and the area under the curve (AUC) values of different models were compared to assess the predictive effect of the models.

3. Results

3.1. Patient characteristics

A total of 779 experimental subjects were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database. This population included 459 males (58.92%), 325 patients with hypertension (41.72%), 255 patients with diabetes (32.73%), and 390 patients with AKI (50.06%). They were divided into a sepsis group (310 cases) and a non-sepsisgroup (469 cases). There were significant differences between the 2 groups in hemoglobin, RBC, WBC, Alb, calcium, lactate, prothrombin time, ALT, AST, bilirubin, creatinine, and BUN (P < .05). The sepsis patient group had higher levels of WBC, lactate, AST, ALT, bilirubin, creatinine, CAR (ratio of creatinine to albumin), and BAR, and lower levels of calcium. In Table 2, multivariate regression analysis showed that when BAR was grouped by quartiles and compared (Q1 ≤ 2.90, 2.90 < Q2 ≤ 5.71, 5.71 < Q3 ≤ 9.36, 9.36 < Q4 ≤ 20.74), the risk of sepsis in patients in the fourth quartile (Q4) was significantly higher than that in patients in the first quartile (Q1) and lower than that in patients in the second quartile (Q2). Multivariate regression analysis also showed that the odds ratio of the Model 3 BAR index was 1.0191, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.0005 to 1.0381, indicating that the risk of sepsis in patients with AP increased significantly with the increase in BAR (all trend P values < .001; Table 2). The nonlinear relationship between BAR and the risk of sepsis in patients with AP was confirmed by restricted cubic splines (Fig. 3). 3.4. Assessment of the predictive accuracy of the BAR index

The AUC for the BAR index alone was 0.611 (95% CI: 0.572–0.651), with a best threshold of 0.376. However, when the BAR index was combined with other clinical factors selected through the Boruta algorithm, the predictive performance improved significantly, with the AUC increasing to 0.704 (95% CI: 0.667–0.741). This indicates that the combined model enhanced diagnostic accuracy and reliability. A statistically significant difference in AUC values between the 2 models was observed, as confirmed by the DeLong test (Z = −3.362, P < .001), indicating that the composite model incorporating clinical markers was superior to the BAR index alone (Fig. 4).

4. Discussion

This study explored and analyzed the relationship between the BAR and the incidence of sepsis in patients with AP. Several previous studies have shown that the BAR index has an extraordinary potential role in predicting the risk of sepsis.[4,5,16,17] This retrospective study, from different perspectives, mainly revealed a significant correlation between the BAR index and the incidence of sepsis in patients with AP. As the BAR index increases, the risk of sepsis in patients with AP significantly increases.

Previous studies have shown that BUN is a reliable prognostic indicator for sepsis. Meanwhile, Alb has an obvious negative correlation with the occurrence and development of sepsis.[18] However, during the occurrence and development of the disease, there are multiple factors influencing BUN and Alb. BUN is affected by some factors related to the patient’s condition. BUN is one of the products of protein metabolism in the liver.[6] BUN in the blood is filtered out by the glomeruli and reabsorbed by them. In patients with sepsis, due to the consumption of the disease, the rate of protein catabolism is significantly increased, and they are often prone to complications with AKI. Therefore, the baseline BUN level in sepsis patients is elevated.[4,5,19] Sepsis affects hemodynamics, leading to insufficient effective circulating blood volume, microcirculatory disorders, and inadequate renal perfusion. This can further deteriorate renal function, increase the production of BUN, and reduce its excretion.[19] Existing relevant studies have shown that the silent infiltration of inflammatory mediators and immune cells, as well as abnormal regulation of nitric oxide synthase, can further reduce the blood flow in the renal parenchyma in sepsis-induced AKI, thereby inducing and exacerbating microcirculatory disorders.[20] BUN is affected by the patient’s own condition.[18] From the perspective of Alb, the malnutrition state caused by disease consumption may reduce the plasma Alb level. Similarly, the systemic inflammatory response also has the same effect.[21,22] The degree of change in serum Alb represents the severity of the disease to a certain extent and can serve as an additional risk-stratifying biomarker for disease occurrence, death, and prognosis in some aspects.[23,24] A sharp decline in serum Alb levels after infection usually indicates a poor prognosis. In addition, diseases such as liver dysfunction and kidney damage can reduce serum Alb levels.[23,24] Therefore, as a clinical indicator that is easy to detect, convenient to obtain, and has obvious biological significance, Alb is often used to diagnose and evaluate sepsis and its prognosis. Meanwhile, composite indicators based on Alb ratios have been derived.[25,26]

The pathophysiological mechanism behind the BAR index, reflecting the poor prognosis of the disease, remains unclear and requires further research and verification. In evaluating the poor prognosis and severity of diseases, it has more valuable clinical significance than BUN or ALB.[27] BAR is simple to calculate, easy to detect, relatively objective, and can better reflect practical clinical problems. The BAR level is closely related to the effective circulating blood volume, which can provide more accurate clinical guidance for blood volume management in patients with sepsis.[28] Some retrospective studies have explored the application value of the BAR index in other diseases: Ryu et al demonstrated that BAR is a useful prognostic factor for aspiration pneumonia.[29] Fang et al also reported similar results in a prognostic study of patients with critical acute pulmonary embolism.[30] Prognostic studies on BAR and other diseases, such as chronic heart failure[28] and Escherichia coli bacteremia,[31] have also been reported. The possible mechanisms by which a high BAR predicts the occurrence of sepsis and poor prognosis can be explained by the levels of BUN and Alb. A high BAR indicates a high BUN concentration and a low Alb level. Low renal perfusion and hypoalbuminemia often occur in critically ill patients, leading to elevated BUN and BAR levels. This will help clinicians understand the implications of an elevated BAR level and assess the prognosis of patients.

Research on clinical biomarkers and their application in assessing patient prognosis and disease risk has been topics of discussion in recent years. Based on previous studies, this research mainly considered and verified the correlation between clinical nutrition, metabolic imbalance, and the occurrence of sepsis in patients with AP. Notably, this study elaborated on the special role of BAR in the onset of sepsis in patients with AP. The relationship between BUN, Alb, and sepsis has been preliminarily verified in previous studies. The application of a new clinical composite index generated by combining the 2 in predicting sepsis in patients with AP provides a new perspective for clinical practice. When used in combination with clinical indicators, the AUC of the BAR index for predicting sepsis is significantly increased to 0.704 (95% CI: 0.667–0.741) compared with single markers (Fig. 4), indicating the superiority of composite metabolic indicators in predicting diseases. Biomarkers are different from disease scores (such as Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score, Ranson score system, and Acute Pancreatitis Score) because they are more time-sensitive, objective, and applicable.[32] Early identification of high-risk patients can enable earlier implementation of disease prevention and control in clinical work, aiming to reduce the incidence and mortality of sepsis.

Variable screening is crucial for building predictive models. Previous studies mostly used univariate logistic regression or least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression to select variables and build prediction models.[33] With the advancement of machine learning algorithms, there is increasing attention on using machine learning for feature selection in clinical practice.[34] In this study, the Boruta algorithm (random forest feature selection, XGBoost method) was successfully applied in this experiment for variable screening. The Boruta algorithm can systematically evaluate the importance of each variable, thereby significantly improving the accuracy, stability, and interpretability of the model while reducing the risk of overfitting.[13] On this basis, the importance of features selected by the XGBoost model was subsequently visualized using SHAP values. SHAP values, derived from cooperative game theory, assign precise contribution values to each feature, providing a more detailed analysis of feature impacts. This enables even complex models to be understood by clinical practitioners.[14] Through the above machine learning methods, this study revealed that the clinical indicators (WBC, RBC, creatinine, calcium, bilirubin, and urea nitrogen) were identified in assessing the onset of sepsis in patients with AP. This enables us to have a more in-depth understanding of the occurrence and development mechanisms of AP and sepsis, and also provides treatment guidance for clinical work.

Therefore, to further enhance the accuracy of BAR prediction, the following strategies are proposed. First, improve the analysis process: BAR is calculated based on blood biochemical test results from different detection platforms. Affected by experiments, there may be slight errors. It is recommended to conduct the test within 24 hours after admission for AP. At the same time, set unified standards for reference of detection indicators to reduce systematic errors in BAR calculation and improve the consistency of prediction results among different centers. Second, optimize the prediction model by combining multiple indicators: As a single indicator, BAR has unique convenience in clinical applications, but its prediction performance still needs to be improved. BAR can be further combined with traditional inflammatory markers (such as CRP and PCT), organ function scores (such as Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score and Bedside Index for Severity in Acute Pancreatitis score), and clinical imaging features to construct a precise prediction model and improve the early screening ability for patients with AP complicated by sepsis. Third, multicenter prospective external validation: This experiment is a retrospective study based on a single public database. Multicenter prospective cohort studies are needed for further validation to clarify the optimal cutoff value of BAR in different populations and improve its adaptability to different populations. Although this study provides compelling data on the relationship between the BAR index and the likelihood of sepsis in patients with AP, there are still some limitations. First, serum Alb or BUN values may change over time. This study only included the initial BAR values and did not monitor their dynamic changes, even though these values might more accurately predict the prognosis of sepsis. Second, due to missing data, many variables extracted from the database were discarded. These variables included serum CRP, PCT levels, and some other inflammation-related biomarkers, which could have helped researchers clarify the mechanism of sepsis mortality. Third, for patients with multiple ICU admissions, only those with the first ICU admission were included in the analysis, which may lead to selection bias. Additionally, BUN levels may be affected by diet. However, due to the limitations of the MIMIC-IV database, we did not obtain the dietary information of the participants. Finally, this study is a retrospective single-center study. During the experiment, rigorous statistical methods were applied as much as possible to reduce the bias caused by confounding factors. However, there are still some confounding factors that are difficult to control. A prospective multicenter study needs to be carried out to verify the value of BAR in screening for sepsis risk in patients with AP and to explore further its mechanism.

5. Conclusion

In adult patients with AP, an elevated BAR at ICU admission may be associated with the risk of developing sepsis. We found that the BAR index was positively correlated with the occurrence of sepsis in patients with AP. Early BAR at ICU admission is an independent risk factor affecting prognosis. When used in combination with other clinical indicators and disease scores, it can further improve the accuracy of predicting the prognosis of sepsis, providing new ideas for the early warning and intervention of sepsis in patients with AP.
