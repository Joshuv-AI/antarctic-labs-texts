Mitochondria-targeted nanozyme hydrogel remodels the microenvironment to enhance bone regeneration in diabetes-associated periodontitis
Authors: Jingyu Yan, Chenying Cui, Lihong Zhou, Yurong Xu, Kaifang Zhang, Kun Liu, Yajuan Gong, Jian Zhou, Xiuping Wu, Bing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes-associated periodontitis is a multifactorial disease driven by metabolic disorders and chronic inflammation, featuring mitochondrial dysfunction, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), and impaired bone metabolism that collectively impair tissue regeneration. To modulate this pathological microenvironment, we developed an intelligent therapeutic system integrating antioxidation, mitochondrial targeting, and regeneration. The Mn-Zn-tannic acid@SS31 (MZT@S) nanozyme was encapsulated within a PLGA-PEG-PLGA (PPP) thermosensitive hydrogel to obtain MZT@S/PPP. MZT@S exhibits superoxide dismutase-like and catalase-like activities, which enable efficient elimination of ROS. In addition, the mitochondria targeting peptide SS31 enhances mitochondrial localization of the nanozyme and thereby promotes mitochondria-derived ROS scavenging and restores mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. The injectable hydrogel allows rapid in situ gelation and provides sustained local release. The MZT@S/PPP hydrogel restores mitochondrial function, promotes osteogenesis and angiogenesis, and induces macrophage M2 polarization, thereby re-establishing immune homeostasis. In vivo, the system suppresses alveolar bone loss and enhances new bone formation in diabetes-associated periodontitis, leading to a markedly improved inflammatory and regenerative microenvironment. Introduction

Diabetes-associated periodontitis is a highly prevalent chronic inflammatory disease characterized by progressive loss of periodontal supporting tissues, including sustained resorption and structural destruction of alveolar bone [1]. As representative conditions at the intersection of systemic metabolic disorders and localized oral pathology, diabetes and periodontitis exhibit a bidirectional relationship [2]. The hyperglycemic microenvironment not only exacerbates inflammatory injury in periodontal tissues but also markedly impairs their intrinsic capacity for repair and regeneration. Consequently, this pathological interplay poses a serious threat to oral health and significantly diminishes patients’ quality of life [3].

At the pathological level, diabetes-associated periodontitis is a chronic disorder driven by multiple interconnected factors, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, immune dysregulation and impaired bone metabolism [4, 5]. As the primary intracellular source of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondria continuously overproduce mitochondria-derived ROS (mtROS), thereby exacerbating oxidative stress [6]. Excessive mtROS accumulation damages mitochondrial DNA and membrane integrity, suppresses oxidative phosphorylation and disrupts ATP synthesis [7, 8]. This process further triggers positive feedback-mediated mitochondrial impairment, ultimately leading to cellular senescence, apoptosis and reduced regenerative capacity [9]. In addition, oxidative stress disrupts the immune microenvironment by skewing macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, enhancing the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, and suppressing M2 polarization [10, 11]. Concurrently, ROS disturb bone metabolic homeostasis by promoting osteoclast activation and inhibiting the expression of osteogenic genes, thereby accelerating alveolar bone resorption and structural deterioration [12, 13].

Current therapeutic strategies for diabetes-associated periodontitis primarily involve mechanical periodontal debridement, antimicrobial therapy, and experimental or adjunctive antioxidant interventions [14]. However, these approaches have inherent limitations. While debridement and antimicrobial treatments can control local inflammation, they are insufficient to reverse oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Antioxidant-based interventions often exhibit single-mode activity, lack targeted delivery, and show limited retention within periodontal tissues, collectively resulting in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes [15]. Therefore, the development of a multifunctional therapeutic platform capable of simultaneously scavenging ROS, restoring mitochondrial function, and re-establishing immune homeostasis holds significant clinical value for effectively mitigating the progression of diabetes-associated periodontitis and promoting tissue and bone regeneration [16, 17].

In this context, metal-polyphenol networks (MPNs) have attracted considerable attention in tissue repair and antioxidant therapy due to their excellent biocompatibility, tunable structures, and diverse biological functions [18]. Among natural polyphenols, tannic acid (TA) serves as a representative building block with strong metal-chelating capacity, radical scavenging ability, and inherent anti-inflammatory properties [19]. By introducing biologically active metal ions such as manganese (Mn) and zinc (Zn), this enables the construction of composite materials with enzyme-mimicking activities. The resulting Mn-Zn-TA (MZT) complexes exhibit superoxide dismutase (SOD)- and catalase (CAT)-like activities and synergistically modulate immune inflammation while promoting osteogenesis. Upon cellular internalization, nanoparticles are often sequestered within lysosomes, where the acidic, enzyme-rich environment can cause structural degradation, limiting their therapeutic efficacy within critical organelles such as mitochondria [20].

Given the central role of mitochondria in the generation and regulation of ROS, the development of intelligent nanozyme systems capable of mitochondrial targeting has emerged as an essential strategy for achieving precise antioxidant modulation [21]. SS31 is a small mitochondrial-targeting peptide bearing positive charges that selectively accumulates within the inner mitochondrial membrane. It binds to cardiolipin and prevents its aberrant interaction with cytochrome c, thereby stabilizing the electron transport chain, reducing mtROS leakage, and enhancing mitochondrial function [22, 23]. Based on this mechanism, we constructed an SS31-modified MZT nanomaterial (Mn-Zn-TA@SS31, abbreviated as MZT@S), which enables targeted scavenging of mtROS, restoration of oxidative phosphorylation, and subsequent recovery of cellular metabolic homeostasis. This strategy ultimately enhances cellular functionality and facilitates tissue regeneration.

Notably, diabetes-associated periodontitis is often accompanied by irregularly shaped, deep periodontal pockets. The local inflammatory microenvironment features increased viscosity and excessive exudate, which makes it difficult for conventional drugs or nanomaterials to effectively penetrate the lesion and achieve adequate retention [24, 25]. To address this challenge, we employed a thermosensitive hydrogel based on the PLGA-PEG-PLGA (PPP) triblock copolymer as a smart delivery carrier [26]. This hydrogel remains a free-flowing sol at low temperature, enabling minimally invasive injection into periodontal pockets, and undergoes rapid sol-gel transition at body temperature. The in situ formed gel conforms closely to the lesion site, provides mechanical protection, and enables sustained release of therapeutic components. As a result, it significantly prolongs local residence time and improves therapeutic efficacy [27, 28].

Based on this design, we embedded the mitochondria-targeted MZT@S nanozyme into the PPP thermosensitive hydrogel to construct an injectable and in situ forming multifunctional delivery system, referred to as MZT@S/PLGA-PEG-PLGA (MZT@S/PPP). This platform provides mechanical support and prolonged release through the hydrogel matrix, while simultaneously leveraging the mitochondrial targeting capability of SS31 and the enzyme-mimicking activities of MZT. Within the inflammatory microenvironment, MZT@S/PPP enables precise scavenging of mtROS, restoration of mitochondrial function, reestablishment of immune homeostasis and enhancement of periodontal tissue regeneration, thereby addressing the multifaceted pathological features of diabetes-associated periodontitis. To evaluate the therapeutic potential of this system, we comprehensively characterized the physicochemical properties of the MZT@S nanozyme and the MZT@S/PPP hydrogel, employed RNA sequencing to elucidate the cellular regulatory mechanisms of MZT@S and performed both in vitro and in vivo studies to assess its regenerative efficacy in diabetes-associated periodontitis models (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Synthesis and characterization of MZT and MZT@S

MZT was synthesized through metal-polyphenol coordination driven self-assembly. Briefly, manganese chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2·4H2O) and zinc chloride (ZnCl2) were used as metal precursors to prepare a mixed Mn2+/ Zn2+ solution (10 mM total metal concentration, molar ratio 1:2). The resulting metal solution was mixed with an equal volume of TA (5 mg/mL) and stirred at 40℃ under a nitrogen atmosphere for 2 h. The pH was maintained at 8.5 with 0.1 M NaOH. The products were collected by centrifugation, washed with ultrapure water, sonicated briefly, sterilized through a 0.22 μm filter, and stored at 4℃ in the dark.

SS31 peptide was introduced onto the surface of MZT via a 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide/N-hydroxysuccinimide (EDC/NHS) -assisted surface modification process to confer mitochondrial-targeting capability. SS31 ((D-Arg)-2’,6’-dimethyltyrosine-Lys-Phe-NH2, purity 98.23%) was obtained from QYAOBIO. Briefly, MZT was dispersed in ultrapure water, followed by the addition of EDC (10 mM) and NHS (5 mM). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min to facilitate surface activation. Subsequently, SS31 was added at a peptide-to-carrier mass ratio of 5%, and the reaction mixture was stirred at 4℃ for 12 h. The obtained product (denoted as MZT@S) was collected by centrifugation and washed thoroughly with ultrapure water to remove any unbound peptide.

The morphology and elemental distribution of MZT and MZT@S were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was used to verify metal-polyphenol coordination and peptide conjugation. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements were performed to determine hydrodynamic size and surface charge. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to assess surface elemental composition and chemical states.

Evaluation of enzyme-like activities and antioxidant capacity of MZT@S

The SOD-like activity of MZT@S was evaluated using the xanthine and xanthine oxidase system, in which the generated superoxide oxidizes hydroxylamine to nitrite with an absorbance peak at 550 nm. MZT@S at various concentrations was added to the reaction mixture, and the scavenging rate was calculated according to the manufacturer’s instructions (SOD Assay Kit, mlbio, China). CAT-like activity was assessed by monitoring the decomposition of H2O2. Samples were mixed with PBS containing 20 mM H2O2 at pH 7.4 and incubated at 37 °C, and dissolved oxygen was recorded in real time using a dissolved oxygen electrode (JPB 607 A, LeiCi, China). The O2 generation rate was used to determine CAT-like catalytic efficiency. Hydroxyl (•OH) radical scavenging was analyzed by electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy using DMPO as a spin trapping agent. •OH radicals were produced in a Fenton system containing 10 mM H2O2 and 1 mM FeSO4, and ESR spectra were recorded after adding MZT@S at different concentrations to evaluate the suppression of the characteristic DMPO/•OH quartet signals.

The total antioxidant capacity of MZT@S was evaluated using ABTS•+ and DPPH• scavenging assays (mlbio, China). ABTS•+ shows a characteristic absorbance at 734 nm, and DPPH• shows a maximum absorbance at 515 nm. Evaluation of cellular biocompatibility

Based on preliminary concentration screening (10–200 µg/mL), the optimal treatment concentration for MZT and MZT@S was determined to be 50 µg/mL. At this concentration, the materials exhibited significant antioxidant and proliferation-promoting effects without affecting cell viability. All subsequent cell experiments were performed using this concentration.

Bone marrow derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with the materials for 1, 3, and 5 days. Cell viability was quantified using the CCK-8 assay by measuring absorbance at 450 nm after a 2 h incubation. For live/dead assay, cells were exposed to the materials for 24 h, stained with Calcein-AM and PI for 30 min, and imaged by fluorescence microscopy to visualize viable and nonviable cells.

Evaluation of intracellular ROS scavenging and mitochondrial function

Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the DCFH-DA probe. After cell attachment, an oxidative stress model was induced by exposing cells to 200 µM H2O2 for 6 h, followed by incubation with the materials for 24 h. The cells were then washed with PBS and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA at 37℃ for 30 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were acquired and analyzed to determine ROS levels. Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using JC-1 staining. After 24 h of treatment, cells were incubated with JC-1 working solution at 37℃ for 30 min, washed with PBS, and imaged to calculate the red-to-green fluorescence ratio. MtROS were examined using the MitoSOX probe. Cells were incubated with 5 µM MitoSOX at 37℃ for 30 min after 24 h of treatment, washed, and imaged to evaluate mtROS generation. Cellular ATP content was quantified using an ATP assay kit following the manufacturer’s protocol.

In vitro osteogenic differentiation assay

To evaluate the osteogenic effects of MZT and MZT@S under oxidative stress, BMSCs were exposed to 200 µM H2O2 for 6 h, after which the medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium. Cells were cultured for 7 and 14 days. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining was performed on day 7 and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining on day 14 following the respective kit protocols. Mineralization was quantified by dissolving ARS-stained deposits in 10% cetylpyridinium chloride and measuring absorbance at 562 nm.

In vitro osteoclast differentiation assay

To investigate the impact of oxidative stress on osteoclast differentiation, RAW264.7 cells were exposed to 200 µM H2O2 for 6 h, after which the medium was replaced and 50 ng/mL RANKL was added to induce differentiation. TRAP staining was performed on day 7, and TRAP-positive multinucleated cells containing three or more nuclei were quantified. TRAP activity was further determined by measuring absorbance at 405 nm using the pNPP substrate. On day 7, rhodamine-phalloidin was used to visualize F-actin structures, followed by DAPI nuclear staining. Fluorescence imaging was conducted to assess osteoclast maturation, with the formation of intact F-actin rings considered a key morphological indicator.

Angiogenesis assay

HUVECs were used to evaluate angiogenesis and migration. For the tube formation assay, Matrigel was added to 96-well plates and allowed to polymerize at 37℃. HUVECs were then seeded and cultured for 6 h, after which tube structures were imaged and quantified using ImageJ. Cell migration was assessed using Transwell chambers. After 24 h of incubation, cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed, and migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and quantified under an inverted microscope.

Macrophage polarization regulation assay

RAW 264.7 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (200 ng/mL) for 6 h and then co-incubated with the materials for 24 h. After treatment, cells were collected, washed, and resuspended in flow cytometry buffer. Anti-F4/80-FITC, CD86-PE, and CD206-APC antibodies were added and incubated at 4℃ for 30 min in the dark. Following PBS washing, flow cytometry was performed. Data were analyzed using FlowJo. Cell debris and doublets were excluded by FSC/SSC gating, and F4/80+ cells were identified as macrophages. The proportions of CD86+ and CD206+ cells were quantified to assess material-mediated modulation of M1/M2 polarization.

Assessment of anti-aging effects

To induce cellular senescence, cells were exposed to 200 µM H2O2 for 6 h after adhesion, followed by replacement with fresh medium according to the experimental groups. Senescent cells were detected using the senescence β-galactosidase staining kit (Beyotime). After fixation, cells were incubated at 37℃ (non-CO2) for the recommended time, and the proportion of blue-stained cells was quantified under a microscope.

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the EdU cell proliferation detection kit. Cells were incubated with EdU for 2 h, fixed, permeabilized, and stained. Fluorescence images were acquired, and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was determined.

DNA damage was assessed by γ-H2AX immunostaining. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked, then incubated with γ-H2AX primary antibody for 1 h and a fluorescent secondary antibody for 30 min. γ-H2AX foci were visualized under a fluorescence microscope, and nuclear foci intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

IF staining was performed to evaluate the expression of specific proteins in different cell types. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4℃ with the corresponding primary antibodies. After washing, Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Cytoskeleton was stained with rhodamine-phalloidin when required, followed by DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Fluorescence images were acquired to analyze protein expression.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

mRNA expression was analyzed by RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol, and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit with GAPDH as the internal reference. Six biological replicates were included per group. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to the control. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1-S6.

Synthesis of PPP and MZT@S/PPP

The PPP triblock copolymer was synthesized via ring-opening polymerization using PEG (Mn = 1000) as the initiator and D, L-lactic acid (D, L-LA) and glycolide (GA) as monomers in a molar ratio of 8:1. Polymerization was catalyzed by Sn(Oct)2 at 130℃ for 24 h. The product was purified and freeze-dried to obtain PPP.

To prepare the hydrogel, PPP was dissolved in PBS (pH 7.4) at 15 wt% and stirred for 12 h. MZT@S suspension (25 mg/mL) was then added at 0.5% (w/w) and gently stirred to form the MZT@S/PPP hydrogel.

Rheological characterization

The rheological properties of the hydrogels were characterized using a rheometer. Temperature sweep from 20℃ to 50℃ was performed to determine the sol-gel transition. Shear rate sweep at 25℃ from 1 to 100 s− 1 was conducted to assess shear-thinning behavior. Strain (0.1–100%) and frequency (0.1–100 rad/s) sweeps were carried out at 37℃ to evaluate structural stability and dynamic mechanical properties.

Establishment and treatment of the diabetes-associated periodontitis model

Eight-week-old male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanxi Medical University (KQDW-2024-004). Type 2 diabetes mellitus was established by a high-fat/high-sugar diet for 4 weeks combined with a low-dose streptozotocin injection (30 mg/kg). Persistent hyperglycemia was confirmed by fasting and random blood glucose measurements, followed by oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and an insulin tolerance test (ITT) to verify impaired glucose tolerance and insulin resistance. Rats meeting the diagnostic criteria were classified as diabetic and maintained for an additional week to allow stabilization of blood glucose levels.

Periodontitis was established by placing a ligature around the maxillary second molar and applying a suspension of live Porphyromonas gingivalis (1 × 108 CFU/mL) to the gingival sulcus for 3 weeks. After model establishment, rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6). The Healthy group received no treatment, and the Periodontitis group served as the disease control. The MZT@S group received weekly local injections of MZT@S suspension (25 mg/mL) at the alveolar bone defect. The MZT@S/PPP group was administered the MZT@S/PPP thermosensitive hydrogel, followed by gentle massage to ensure uniform distribution and in situ gelation. All treatments were performed once per week for 12 weeks. Control groups received an equivalent volume of PBS.

At the study endpoint, rats were euthanized, and the maxillary second molars with surrounding periodontal tissues were collected. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and analyzed by micro-computed tomography to determine the cementoenamel junction (CEJ)-alveolar bone crest (ABC) distance and bone parameters including bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). Tissues were then decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to evaluate periodontal morphology, and Masson’s trichrome staining was performed to assess collagen deposition.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to detect TNF-α and IL-6, and IF staining was used to assess CD206, CD31, and OCN. For IHC, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB were applied, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. For IF, fluorophore-labeled secondary antibodies were used, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. All images were captured under consistent exposure conditions and analyzed using ImageJ. CD206 expression was quantified by the CD206+/DAPI ratio, CD31 expression by microvessel density, and OCN by relative fluorescence intensity. TNF-α and IL-6 were reported as the percentage of positive staining area. Image analyses were performed independently by two blinded investigators.

Statistical analysis

All experiments included at least three independent samples. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. For experiments involving two independent variables or repeated measurements over time, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. Significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.0001 (****).

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of MZT@S

In this study, a metal-polyphenol coordination-driven self-assembly strategy was employed to fabricate TA-based nanoparticles coordinated with Mn2+ and Zn2+ ions (MZT) [29]. TA contains abundant pyrogallol and galloyl groups with multidentate chelating capability (Fig. S1), enabling rapid complexation with metal ions to form stable MPNs. When Mn2+ and Zn2+ are simultaneously introduced into an aqueous TA solution, multivalent coordination interactions occur between the metal ions and the phenolic hydroxyl groups, thereby inducing the formation of a three-dimensional nanostructure (Fig. 2a).

Under alkaline or oxidative conditions, the pyrogallol groups of TA undergo deprotonation followed by oxidation to generate semiquinone radicals and quinone intermediates, thereby initiating oxidative coupling reactions and enhancing network stability [30]. During this process, Mn2+ can be partially oxidized to Mn3+. Mn3+ exhibits redox catalytic activity that accelerates the oxidative polymerization of TA, promoting the formation of quinone structures and intermolecular crosslinking among polyphenols. Although Zn2+ does not possess redox activity, it functions as a Lewis acid that forms stable coordination interactions with the phenoxide and carboxyl groups of TA [31]. These interactions contribute to regulating particle size distribution, improving structural stability, and controlling network crosslinking density. The cooperative effects of Mn3+-mediated oxidative polymerization and Zn2+-driven coordination stabilization collectively facilitate the efficient assembly of MZT nanoparticles with uniform size and high structural integrity [32].

To further confer mitochondrial-targeting capability, SS31 (Fig. S2) was immobilized onto the MZT surface through an EDC/NHS-assisted surface modification strategy, yielding MZT@S nanoparticles (Fig. 2a). Considering that TA-based metal-phenolic networks predominantly consist of polyphenolic hydroxyl groups and coordination bonds, the interfacial chemistry between SS31 and MZT is likely governed by a combination of surface-assisted coupling processes and multiple noncovalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions between the cationic residues and the negatively charged MPN framework, as well as possible coordination effects. Such multi-point interfacial interactions contribute to stable peptide retention on the nanoparticle surface under physiological conditions.

TEM revealed that MZT exhibited an irregular wrinkled sheet-like morphology with non-uniform thickness and no observable lattice fringes (Fig. 2b). This structural feature is consistent with the intrinsic characteristics of metal-polyphenol coordination networks, which typically lack long-range order due to rapid precipitation and multivalent coordination interactions during their formation. The sheet-like morphology may also arise from stacking or shear effects introduced during the drying process [33]. After surface modification with SS31, the resulting MZT@S sample displayed no noticeable change in overall morphology, and no distinct coating layer was observed (Fig. 2b). This is likely attributable to the small molecular size and sparse surface distribution of SS31, as well as the ultrathin peptide layer, which makes direct visualization challenging under conventional TEM imaging conditions [34]. EDS confirmed the presence of Mn, Zn, C and O in the MZT nanoparticles. In contrast, the MZT@S sample exhibited a pronounced increase in the nitrogen signal, providing indirect evidence for the successful conjugation of SS31. SEM further revealed sheet-like or flocculent stacked structures with relatively rough surfaces (Fig. 2c), suggesting a high specific surface area that may facilitate subsequent biological interactions.

FT-IR analysis showed that TA exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at approximately 1700 cm− 1 corresponding to the C = O stretching vibration, along with a broad band in the 3200–3500 cm− 1 region attributed to O-H stretching, reflecting the abundance of phenolic hydroxyl groups in TA. In comparison, the O-H stretching band of MZT in the 3200–3500 cm− 1 region became significantly narrower and exhibited a slight shift, indicating coordination interactions between the phenolic hydroxyl groups and Mn2+/Zn2+ ions. In addition, the absorption peak near 1700 cm− 1 assigned to the C = O stretching of ester groups showed a slight shift and reduced intensity in MZT, likely due to changes in the electronic environment induced by metal coordination that alter the vibrational characteristics of adjacent carbonyl groups. For MZT@S, a pronounced enhancement in the absorption band within the 1600–1700 cm− 1 region was observed, corresponding to the C = O stretching vibration of peptide amide groups, which is characteristic of proteins and peptides. Moreover, increased absorption attributed to C-N stretching vibrations appeared in the 1200–1400 cm− 1 region, further confirming the successful conjugation of SS31 onto the nanoparticle surface (Fig. 2d).

XPS of MZT revealed characteristic peaks corresponding to Mn 2p, Zn 2p, C 1s, and O 1s. In the spectrum of MZT@S, a pronounced increase in the intensity of the N 1s peak was observed, providing additional evidence for the successful incorporation of SS31 (Fig. 2e).

DLS analysis showed a slight increase in the average diameter of MZT@S compared with unmodified MZT, which can be attributed to the formation of a surface peptide layer following SS31 conjugation (Fig. S3). Zeta potential measurements further demonstrated that SS31 carried a positive charge of + 14.37 ± 1.71 mV, whereas MZT exhibited a negative charge of -27.29 ± 4.53 mV. After SS31 modification, the surface potential of MZT@S shifted to -14.36 ± 3.19 mV (Fig. 2f). This substantial shift in surface charge confirms the successful grafting of the positively charged SS31 peptide onto the negatively charged MZT surface [22].

Taken together, the morphological features and surface chemical signatures consistently demonstrate the effective conjugation of SS31 onto MZT, leading to the formation of the MZT@S nanoparticles. This establishes a solid material basis for subsequent biological functional evaluations.

Evaluation of ROS-scavenging and antioxidant activities of MZT@S

Given the essential role of nanozymes in mitigating oxidative stress, we systematically evaluated the ROS-scavenging capacity and overall antioxidant activity of MZT@S.

The superoxide anion (O2•⁻) scavenging activity was assessed using the hydroxylamine oxidation method. In this system, xanthine oxidase generates O2•⁻, which subsequently oxidizes hydroxylamine to nitrite, producing a characteristic absorbance at 550 nm [35]. As the concentration of MZT@S increased from 10 to 30 µg/mL, the absorbance at 550 nm decreased markedly (Fig. 2g), corresponding to an inhibition efficiency that increased from 38.82% to 82.86% (Fig. 2h). These results demonstrate a clear dose-dependent scavenging effect of MZT@S toward O2•⁻. The catalytic decomposition of H2O2 by MZT@S was further evaluated to determine its CAT-like activity (Fig. 2i). At concentrations of 3, 5, and 10 µg/mL, the amount of released O2 increased significantly with higher MZT@S doses, indicating that MZT@S effectively catalyzes the breakdown of H2O2 to generate O2. This activity suggests potential for alleviating hypoxic conditions within pathological microenvironments [36]. The •OH radical scavenging performance was evaluated using ESR spectroscopy with DMPO as the spin-trapping agent. In the Fenton reaction system, •OH radicals produce a characteristic quartet ESR signal (Fig. 2j) [37, 38]. As the concentration of MZT@S increased from 50 to 150 µg/mL, the intensity of the ESR signal progressively decreased, indicating a potent and concentration-dependent •OH scavenging capability.

To further evaluate the comprehensive antioxidant capacity of MZT@S, ABTS•+ and DPPH• scavenging assays were performed. In the ABTS•+ system, the ABTS•+ exhibits a characteristic absorption peak at 734 nm [39]. As the concentration of MZT@S increased, the absorbance at 734 nm decreased markedly (Fig. 2k), corresponding to a scavenging efficiency ranging from 35.73% to 80.29% (Fig. 2l), indicating a clear concentration-dependent scavenging effect. In the DPPH• scavenging assay, the stable DPPH• shows a characteristic absorption peak at 515 nm [40]. Upon addition of MZT@S, the absorbance at 515 nm progressively decreased (Fig. 2m), while the calculated scavenging rate increased from 39.8% to 87.1% (Fig. 2n), further demonstrating a distinct concentration-dependent trend.

Taken together, MZT@S effectively scavenged O2•⁻, H2O2, and •OH by mimicking SOD and CAT activities, while simultaneously generating O2. Moreover, MZT@S exhibited clear concentration-dependent antioxidant activity in both the ABTS•+ and DPPH• radical systems. These findings provide solid experimental evidence supporting the potential of MZT@S nanozymes to alleviate oxidative stress and regulate inflammatory microenvironments for biomedical applications.

In vitro biocompatibility of MZT@S

To systematically assess the cytocompatibility of MZT@S, a live/dead assay was performed to evaluate its potential cytotoxicity. After 24 h of co-incubation, the majority of cells displayed green fluorescence, whereas red fluorescent signals were virtually absent (Fig. 3a), indicating that both MZT and MZT@S induced minimal cytotoxicity and maintained normal cellular viability. To further examine their effects on cell proliferation, CCK-8 assays were conducted on days 1, 3, and 5. As shown in Fig. 3b, c, cell viability increased progressively in all groups over time, and no significant difference was found between the MZT@S group and the Control group. These results confirm that MZT@S does not impair cell proliferation and exhibits favorable in vitro biocompatibility.

Exploration of the biological functions of MZT@S

To systematically evaluate the regulatory effects of MZT@S on cellular function under oxidative stress, RNA sequencing was performed on BMSCs treated with H2O2 alone and in combination with MZT@S. The results revealed that MZT@S induced significant remodeling, with 4,148 genes upregulated and 2,091 genes downregulated (Fig. 3d), indicating widespread modulation of gene expression.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed that MZT@S significantly modulates a broad range of biological processes and signaling pathways associated with inflammation, immune regulation, oxidative stress response, angiogenesis, osteogenesis, cell migration, and cellular senescence (Fig. 3f, i). Regarding inflammatory and immune regulation, anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4 were markedly upregulated, whereas pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 were downregulated, suggesting that MZT@S may suppress inflammatory activation and help restore immune homeostasis (Fig. 3e, g). Within angiogenesis- and osteogenesis-related pathways, genes involved in VEGF and BMP-2 signaling were notably upregulated, indicating that MZT@S may enhance both vascular formation and osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 3e, g). In addition, genes associated with oxidative stress response and cellular fate including pathways related to antioxidant defense, cell migration, and cell adhesion, were favorably regulated, supporting the notion that MZT@S can promote tissue regeneration by ameliorating the oxidative stress microenvironment.

To further validate these trends, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed (Fig. 3j). The results showed that the MZT@S group exhibited significant negative enrichment in pathways associated with impaired tissue repair, including inflammation amplification, cellular senescence, and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation. In particular, the downregulation of pathways related to inflammatory amplification and immune hyperactivation, such as the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (hsa04620), NOD-like receptor signaling pathway (hsa04621), and NIK/NF-κB signaling (GO:0038061), indicates that MZT@S effectively suppresses the sustained activation of pro-inflammatory signaling. Moreover, the negative enrichment of the collagen degradation pathway (R-HSA-1442490) suggests a reduction in matrix metalloproteinase activity, thereby mitigating ECM degradation and helping preserve tissue structural integrity.

In summary, it suppresses inflammation amplification and restores immune homeostasis, enhances antioxidant defenses to mitigate oxidative cellular damage, promotes the activation of angiogenesis- and osteogenesis-related signaling pathways, and preserves ECM integrity while improving the microenvironment for cell migration. Together, these findings reveal that MZT@S enhances cellular function and regenerative potential through an integrated regulatory network involving anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, angiogenic, and osteogenic responses. This provides a strong molecular basis for its application in tissue repair and regenerative medicine. Based on these results, we further validated the key biological effects of MZT@S at the cellular level.

We next investigated whether SS31 confers mitochondrial targeting capability to MZT@S at the cellular level. MZT@S was fluorescently labeled with FITC, and its intracellular localization was examined, with mitochondria stained using MitoTracker Red. As shown in Fig. 3h, MZT@S was efficiently internalized by cells and exhibited a pronounced spatial overlap with mitochondrial signals, yielding a clear yellow fluorescence in the merged images. In contrast, MZT displayed a diffuse cytoplasmic distribution with minimal mitochondrial co-localization. Together, these results provide direct evidence that SS31 modification effectively mediates the mitochondrial targeting of MZT@S.

Antioxidant capacity and mitochondrial protection of MZT@S

Excessive accumulation of ROS induces oxidative stress, which consequently impairs the intracellular antioxidant defense system and disrupts mitochondrial structure and function (Fig. 4a) [41]. Although treatment with MZT partially reduced ROS accumulation, the levels remained higher than those in the Blank group. In contrast, MZT@S treatment resulted in a substantial reduction in ROS levels, approaching those observed in the Blank group (Fig. 4d), indicating that MZT@S effectively scavenges intracellular ROS and alleviates oxidative stress. To further assess the cellular consequences of elevated ROS levels, cytoskeletal morphology was examined (Fig. 4b). Cells in the Control group exhibited pronounced shrinkage and cytoskeletal disorganization, consistent with ROS-induced structural damage. Notably, cells treated with MZT@S displayed clear morphological restoration, characterized by uniform spreading and an intact cytoskeletal architecture. These findings demonstrate that MZT@S not only mitigates oxidative stress by reducing ROS accumulation but also preserves cellular structural integrity and supports morphological recovery [42].

MitoSOX staining was used to quantify mtROS (Fig. 4g, h). The Control group showed significantly elevated mtROS levels. MZT reduced ROS to some extent, while MZT@S further and significantly decreased mtROS accumulation. This enhanced effect is closely associated with the mitochondrial-targeting property of MZT@S. The SS31 peptide preferentially localizes to the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it scavenges mitochondrial-derived ROS by inhibiting lipid peroxidation, thereby preventing the propagation of oxidative damage signals [43]. ATP measurement (Fig. 4k) demonstrated a substantial reduction in ATP content in the Control group. MZT treatment partially restored ATP production, whereas MZT@S nearly reinstated ATP levels to those of the Blank group. Together, these results indicate that MZT@S more effectively preserves mitochondrial function than MZT alone. By rapidly eliminating mtROS during the early stages of oxidative stress, MZT@S prevents membrane potential collapse, thereby sustaining ATP synthesis.

In summary, MZT can partially alleviate H2O2-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial injury. However, the mitochondrial targeting capability conferred by SS31 markedly enhances the efficiency of MZT@S in scavenging mtROS, restoring membrane potential, maintaining ATP production, and protecting the cytoskeleton. This substantially strengthens the cellular antioxidant defense and preserves overall structural integrity. These findings indicate that MZT@S provides not only morphological and functional protection but also regulates oxidative stress at the molecular level, underscoring its strong potential for applications in oxidative stress-related diseases.

To evaluate osteoclast differentiation, we first performed TRAP staining and activity assays. Therefore, MZT@S not only promotes osteogenesis under antioxidant conditions but also significantly suppresses osteoclast differentiation and function, exhibiting superior performance compared to MZT alone and providing a solid foundation for bone tissue repair.

Preparation and characterization of the MZT@S/PPP thermosensitive hydrogel

To adapt to the complex pathological microenvironment of diabetes-associated periodontitis and achieve efficient therapeutic delivery, we combined MZT@S with PPP thermosensitive hydrogel to construct the MZT@S/PPP hydrogel (Fig. 9a, S12). This strategy not only preserves the functional advantages of MZT@S but also leverages the thermosensitive properties of PPP to enable sustained release and precise delivery. As shown in Fig. 9c, the hydrogel remains in a free-flowing sol state at room temperature (25℃), allowing easy injection. When the temperature approaches physiological levels (approximately 37℃), the sol-to-gel transition occurs rapidly, forming a stable gel capable of maintaining its structure even upon inversion (Fig. 9d). These results indicate that the hydrogel undergoes rapid and stable in situ gelation at physiological temperature and exhibits excellent injectability and tissue adaptability [53].

(c, d) Macroscopic appearance of the hydrogel at room temperature (25℃) and near-physiological temperature (approximately 37℃). (e) Injectability of the hydrogel at room temperature. (f) Temperature sweep analysis showing the sol-gel transition behavior of the hydrogel. (g) Shear-rate sweep analysis evaluating the shear-thinning behavior of the hydrogel at 25℃. SEM images (Fig. 9b) show that the MZT@S/PPP hydrogel exhibits an irregular and porous network structure with pore sizes on the order of several tens of micrometers. The nanoparticles are uniformly distributed across the surface, which facilitates drug release as well as the exchange of nutrients and metabolic waste [54]. The injectability of the hydrogel for localized periodontal drug delivery was subsequently evaluated. The results indicate that the hydrogel can be smoothly and continuously extruded through a conventional medical syringe (Fig. 9e), enabling precise filling of irregular periodontal pockets or complex tissue defects [55].

To systematically evaluate the rheological properties and local drug-delivery adaptability of the MZT@S/PPP thermosensitive hydrogel, rheological analyses were performed. In the temperature-sweep test (Fig. 9f), the loss modulus (G′′) remained consistently higher than the storage modulus (G′) within the temperature range of 20–35℃, indicating that the system predominantly exhibited viscous behavior and a sol-like state. As the temperature increased, G′ and G″ intersected and subsequently rose sharply, with G′ exceeding G″, demonstrating the completion of the sol-to-gel transition. This transition is likely attributed to enhanced hydrophobic interactions among the thermosensitive segments and the increased density of physical crosslinking points at elevated temperatures, promoting the formation of a three-dimensional network (Fig. 9a). Shear-rate scanning (Fig. 9g) revealed that both samples exhibited a pronounced decrease in viscosity at high shear rates, demonstrating typical shear-thinning behavior that facilitates reduced injection resistance. Strain-sweep analysis (Fig. 9h) showed that at 37℃, within the low-to-moderate strain range, G′ remained higher than G″ with minimal variation, indicating that the gel exhibited elastic, solid-like characteristics under small deformations and maintained a stable three-dimensional network. When strain exceeded approximately 10%, both moduli dropped rapidly, reflecting progressive network disruption. Frequency-sweep results (Fig. 9i) demonstrated that at 37℃, G′ exceeded G″ across the full frequency range, with G′ showing a slight increase with frequency, suggesting good dynamic stability. Moreover, both G′ and G″ of MZT@S/PPP were higher than those of PPP throughout the entire range, confirming its superior mechanical properties and structural robustness. Overall, MZT@S/PPP integrates thermosensitive gelation, shear-thinning characteristics, excellent network stability, and strong dynamic mechanical performance, providing a reliable material foundation for injectable delivery, in situ fixation, and long-term support in periodontal tissue repair [56].

To evaluate the drug-release performance of MZT@S/PPP, swelling, degradation, and release experiments were conducted at 37 °C in both PBS (pH 7.4) and a simulated diabetes-associated periodontitis microenvironment (pH 6.2 + 25 mM glucose). The swelling profile (Fig. 9j) showed that under acidic, high-glucose conditions, the hydrogel exhibited a markedly higher swelling ratio than under neutral conditions. The hydrogel volume increased rapidly within 0–12 h and reached equilibrium at approximately 20 h. This behavior can be attributed to the elevated glucose concentration, which increases the osmotic pressure of the surrounding medium and competes with PEG chains for hydrogen-bonding sites, thereby promoting water infiltration and inducing rapid expansion of the network pore size. In addition, the acidic environment accelerates the hydrolysis of PLGA ester bonds, generating more carboxyl and hydroxyl groups that enhance hydrophilicity and further sustain the high swelling ratio. The increased swelling not only facilitates rapid early-stage drug release but also provides a physical pathway for sustained release at later stages. The degradation behavior (Fig. 9k) further demonstrates the influence of environmental conditions on hydrogel stability. Under pH 6.2 + glucose conditions, the degradation of the hydrogel was significantly accelerated. The acidic medium enhances PLGA hydrolysis, leading to progressive disruption of network crosslinking points and subsequent structural loosening. Meanwhile, the high-glucose environment alters hydrogen bonding and osmotic pressure, facilitating water penetration and further accelerating ester-bond hydrolysis, ultimately increasing the overall degradation rate [53].

In the previous swelling-degradation experiments, the acidic and high-glucose environment was shown to accelerate network swelling and PLGA hydrolysis. To quantitatively evaluate this microenvironment-responsive release behavior, standard curves for two model drugs were first established. As shown in Fig. S13, FITC-SS31 exhibited a linear fluorescence response within the concentration range, with a regression equation of y = 183.83x − 2.2361 (R2 = 0.9962). Similarly, MZT showed a strong correlation between absorbance and concentration, with a regression equation of y = 0.00208x − 0.00268 (R2 = 0.9947) (Fig. S14). The excellent linearity of both calibration curves provides a reliable basis for accurately calculating cumulative release rates under different pH/glucose conditions. Under both conditions, the release curves of FITC-SS31 (Fig. 9l) and MZT (Fig. 9m) displayed an initial burst release followed by a gradual stabilization. In the pH 6.2 + 25 mM glucose environment, the release rates of both FITC-SS31 and MZT were significantly faster than those observed in pH 7.4, and the final cumulative release amounts were markedly higher. This behavior can be attributed to the accelerated hydrolysis of the PPP thermosensitive hydrogel under acidic conditions, coupled with glucose-induced alterations in osmotic pressure and network structure, which promote swelling and enhance diffusion. Notably, within the first 48 h, FITC-SS31 showed a slightly higher release rate than MZT. This is primarily due to its smaller molecular weight and positive charge, which confer a higher diffusion coefficient during the initial swelling phase. A minor contribution from weak non-covalent interactions may also influence its early release. Following this initial stage, the release profiles of both components gradually converged, reflecting the stability and synchronized release characteristics of the covalently linked MZT@S structure. This coordinated release ensures the sustained availability and functional persistence of the mitochondrial-targeting peptide within the pathological microenvironment.

Overall, the MZT@S/PPP thermosensitive hydrogel integrates excellent injectability, in situ gelation behavior, and responsiveness to pathological cues. This system undergoes accelerated swelling and degradation under the acidic and high-glucose conditions associated with diabetes-related periodontitis, and exhibits dynamically drug release and robust mechanical stability. MZT@S/PPP is expected to function as an intelligent local drug delivery platform with strong environmental adaptability and sustained therapeutic potential, providing new strategies and a material basis for bone regeneration and periodontal tissue repair in diabetes-associated disorders.

Repairing diabetes-associated periodontitis with MZT@S/PPP hydrogel

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the MZT@S/PPP thermosensitive hydrogel in repairing diabetes-associated periodontitis in vivo, a diabetes-associated periodontitis model was established in SD rats. All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the regulations for the administration of experimental animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanxi Medical University (ethical Approval No. KQDW-2024-004). Following high-fat/high-sugar diet feeding combined with low-dose streptozotocin administration, rats developed typical features of type 2 diabetes mellitus. Compared with Healthy group, type 2 diabetes mellitus rats exhibited reduced activity, lethargic behavior, and rough, dull fur, indicating impaired general condition associated with chronic metabolic dysfunction. In the OGTT, type 2 diabetes mellitus rats showed significantly elevated fasting blood glucose levels (Fig. S15). After glucose loading, the glucose peak was higher and hyperglycemia persisted, with blood glucose levels failing to return to baseline at 120 min, indicating impaired glucose tolerance. In the ITT, type 2 diabetes mellitus rats displayed a markedly attenuated early glucose-lowering response to insulin (Fig. S16). Blood glucose levels declined slowly and plateaued at approximately 60–90 min, reflecting impaired glucose clearance and insulin resistance.

After one week of stable hyperglycemia, a ligature was firmly placed around the neck of the upper second molars to promote plaque accumulation. Porphyromonas gingivalis (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 200 µL) was subsequently injected into the gingival sulcus to accelerate the onset of periodontitis. After three weeks of ligature placement and bacterial inoculation, the rats exhibited typical features of periodontitis, including gingival redness and swelling. Successful establishment of the model was further confirmed by a significant reduction in alveolar bone height, as assessed by micro-CT imaging. Once the model was confirmed, the pre-prepared MZT@S/PPP thermosensitive hydrogel was injected into the alveolar bone defect area, followed by gentle massage of the gingiva to ensure uniform distribution and in situ gelation of the material (Fig. 10a).

After three months of treatment, the rats were euthanized, and periodontal tissues together with related samples were collected for further analysis. To ensure consistency and comparability of measurements, all micro-CT images were acquired under identical scanning angles and magnifications, with the teeth positioned in the same apical plane. As shown in the 3D reconstruction images (Fig. 10b), the Healthy group displayed smooth, continuous alveolar bone margins with an intact bone structure. In contrast, the Periodontitis group exhibited pronounced alveolar bone resorption and a flattened bone crest, indicative of severe structural destruction. The MZT@S group exhibited a certain degree of attenuation in alveolar bone loss. Notably, the MZT@S/PPP group exhibited minimal root exposure, and the alveolar bone morphology and architecture were nearly restored to normal, with a substantial recovery of bone volume. These findings demonstrate that the MZT@S/PPP thermosensitive hydrogel, through the sustained release of MZT@S, effectively promotes bone regeneration and repair in diabetes-associated periodontitis.

To further quantitatively assess the therapeutic effects of the MZT@S/PPP thermosensitive hydrogel on bone defects in diabetes-associated periodontitis, key bone microarchitectural parameters were systematically evaluated. The distance from the CEJ to the ABC was first measured as an indicator of alveolar bone resorption (Fig. 10c). The Periodontitis group showed a markedly increased CEJ-ABC distance compared with the Healthy group, indicating severe alveolar bone loss. In contrast, the CEJ-ABC distance in the MZT@S/PPP group was significantly smaller than that in both the Periodontitis and MZT@S groups, suggesting that this composite system effectively suppresses alveolar bone resorption, preserves bone crest height, and promotes bone regeneration. Further microstructural analysis revealed that the Periodontitis group exhibited significantly reduced BV/TV (Fig. 10d), BMD (Fig. 10e), Tb.Th (Fig. 10f), and Tb.N (Fig. 10g), along with markedly increased Tb.Sp (Fig. 10h), compared with the Healthy group. By contrast, the MZT@S/PPP group displayed substantially higher BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, and BMD, while Tb.Sp was significantly reduced. These results collectively demonstrate that MZT@S/PPP not only markedly inhibits diabetes-associated periodontitis induced bone resorption but also enables reconstruction of bone volume and remodeling of trabecular architecture during long-term treatment, highlighting its strong therapeutic potential for repairing bone defects under diabetic periodontal conditions.

Histological evaluations were conducted after 3 months of intervention to assess periodontal repair. H&E staining (Fig. 10i) revealed intact periodontal architecture in the Healthy group, whereas the Periodontitis group exhibited severe tissue destruction, including disrupted periodontal ligament organization, extensive inflammatory infiltration, and marked alveolar bone loss. The MZT@S group showed reduced inflammation and partial structural restoration. Notably, the MZT@S/PPP group demonstrated the most pronounced regeneration, characterized by a continuous gingival epithelium, well-aligned periodontal ligament fibers, restored alveolar bone margins, and tight integration between new periodontal ligament and cementum. Masson trichrome staining (Fig. 10j) further confirmed these results. The Healthy group displayed dense, orderly collagen fibers, while the Periodontitis group showed fragmented and disorganized collagen. Collagen deposition improved in the MZT@S group, and the MZT@S/PPP group exhibited abundant newly formed, well-organized collagen tightly integrated with the alveolar bone surface, indicating substantial enhancement of collagen reconstruction and periodontal tissue regeneration.

To further assess the regulatory effects of MZT@S/PPP on bone formation, IF staining of OCN was performed. In the Healthy group, OCN was evenly distributed at the alveolar bone-periodontal ligament interface, whereas its expression was markedly reduced in the Periodontitis group, indicating impaired osteogenesis. MZT@S treatment partially restored OCN levels, while MZT@S/PPP showed the strongest and most widespread OCN signal in newly formed bone and periosteum, suggesting enhanced osteoblast differentiation and mineralization (Fig. 11a, b). Overall, these results suggest that MZT@S/PPP effectively restores osteogenic activity and promotes the orderly reconstruction of the bone matrix.

To further elucidate the immune-regulatory effects of MZT@S/PPP, IF staining for CD206 and IHC staining for TNF-α and IL-6 were performed. CD206-positive M2 macrophages were markedly reduced in the Periodontitis group. MZT@S treatment partially restored M2 polarization, whereas the MZT@S/PPP group exhibited the strongest and most uniformly distributed CD206 signal. Quantitative analysis confirmed that the CD206+/DAPI (%) ratio was highest in the MZT@S/PPP group, suggesting stable and sustained immune regulation (Fig. 11c, d). TNF-α (Fig. 11e, f) and IL-6 (Fig. 11g, h) signals were weak in the Healthy group but significantly elevated and widely distributed in the Periodontitis group, reflecting active inflammation and osteoclast activity. MZT@S treatment reduced their expression, while MZT@S/PPP showed the weakest signals, comparable to healthy tissues, indicating superior anti-inflammatory effects. Collectively, these results demonstrate that MZT@S/PPP not only restores osteogenic activity but also establishes a stable immune microenvironment by promoting M2 polarization and suppressing inflammatory cascades, thereby facilitating periodontal tissue repair and bone regeneration.

Regarding angiogenesis, CD31 immunostaining (Fig. 11i) revealed an intact and uniformly distributed capillary network in the Healthy group, whereas the Periodontitis group exhibited reduced vessel density and disrupted vascular architecture, indicating impaired blood supply. MZT@S treatment partially restored vascularization, as evidenced by increased CD31-positive signals. The MZT@S/PPP group showed the strongest CD31 signal, with higher capillary density and a continuous, well-organized vascular network (Fig. 11j). These results suggest that MZT@S/PPP effectively restores endothelial function and promotes angiogenesis and vessel stabilization through combined antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and hydrogel scaffold effects, ensuring sufficient and sustained blood supply to support tissue regeneration.

Throughout the 12-week treatment period, fasting blood glucose levels were monitored on Days 1, 14, 28, 56, and 84 to assess glycemic status across experimental groups (Fig. S17). The Healthy group consistently exhibited normoglycemia, whereas all other groups maintained stable hyperglycemia, confirming the persistence of the diabetic state throughout the study. Notably, no significant differences in blood glucose levels were detected among the Periodontitis, MZT@S, and MZT@S/PPP groups at any time point, indicating that local administration of MZT@S or MZT@S/PPP did not influence systemic glycemic control. Accordingly, the observed therapeutic effects on periodontal inflammation and alveolar bone regeneration were independent of changes in blood glucose levels and can be attributed to the localized biological activity of the treatments.

In addition, the in vivo biosafety of the MZT@S/PPP hydrogel was evaluated by histological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney). H&E staining revealed no observable structural damage, inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, or fibrosis in any of the examined organs. The overall tissue architecture was comparable to that of the Periodontitis group, indicating that the hydrogel treatment did not induce additional systemic toxicity or organ injury (Fig. S18). These findings suggest favorable biocompatibility and systemic safety, supporting its potential for long-term local application in the oral cavity.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the MZT@S/PPP hydrogel exhibits pronounced therapeutic advantages in repairing periodontal defects under diabetic conditions. MZT@S effectively scavenges excessive ROS, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and inflammatory injury in the hyperglycemic microenvironment and restoring mitochondrial function and osteogenic activity in osteoblasts. Stabilization of the inflammatory microenvironment subsequently supports the reactivation of bone matrix secretion and mineralization. Meanwhile, the three-dimensional porous network of the PPP hydrogel provides physical support for cellular adhesion, migration, and vascular ingrowth, while maintaining sustained local release of MZT@S to ensure long-term therapeutic efficacy. The synergistic effects of inflammation reduction, immune regulation, angiogenesis promotion, and osteogenesis enhancement collectively accelerate bone regeneration and periodontal tissue reconstruction. Overall, the regenerative outcome of MZT@S/PPP is markedly superior to that of MZT@S alone.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed an intelligent thermosensitive hydrogel system (MZT@S/PPP) that integrates mitochondrial-targeted antioxidation, immunomodulation, and regenerative functions to reconstruct the diabetic microenvironment and promote bone regeneration. The MZT@S nanozyme exhibited SOD- and CAT-like catalytic activities, efficiently scavenging ROS and alleviating oxidative stress. Incorporation of the mitochondrial-targeting peptide SS31 endowed the system with precise subcellular localization and enhanced mtROS clearance, thereby restoring mitochondrial bioenergetics. The PPP hydrogel functioned as an injectable, sustained-release scaffold that maintained prolonged local therapeutic activity. Through synergistic actions, this platform restored redox homeostasis, alleviated cellular senescence, promoted macrophage M2 polarization, and enhanced osteogenic and angiogenic processes, collectively reconstructing the inflammatory and metabolic microenvironment. In vivo, MZT@S/PPP effectively suppressed alveolar bone resorption and stimulated new bone formation in a diabetes-associated periodontitis model. Despite these encouraging results, this study is limited by its reliance on a rat model, which does not fully recapitulate the pathological complexity of human diabetes-associated periodontitis. Further evaluation in large-animal models and additional preclinical studies are required to assess long-term efficacy, biosafety, and translational potential. Overall, this multifunctional hydrogel represents a promising strategy for the precise treatment of diabetes-associated periodontitis.
