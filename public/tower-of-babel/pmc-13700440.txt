Interdisciplinary collaboration between healthcare professionals and criminal investigation authorities in responses to sexual abuse in Portugal
Authors: Nuno Coelho, João Gregório
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEffective responses to sexual abuse require coordinated collaboration between healthcare and justice professionals. However, interdisciplinary collaboration across these sectors remains poorly understood. This study examined collaboration between physicians, nurses, and criminal investigators involved in sexual abuse response in Portugal, using Bronstein’s Model of Interdisciplinary Collaboration as a conceptual framework.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among healthcare professionals and criminal investigators working in services involved in sexual abuse response. The questionnaire measured five dimensions of interdisciplinary collaboration: interdependencfe, newly created professional activities, flexibility, collective ownership of objectives, and reflection on the collaborative process. Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always). Descriptive statistics and group comparisons were used to examine differences between professional groups and by years of professional experience.ResultsA total of 297 participants from healthcare and justice sectors completed the survey. Criminal investigators consistently reported higher levels of collaboration across several dimensions of the model, while physicians reported the lowest levels, with nurses occupying an intermediate position. The largest sectoral differences were observed in communication and information exchange, perceptions of professional equality, and indicators of innovation and organizational coordination. In contrast, victim/family engagement showed similarly low endorsement across groups. Less experienced criminal investigators reported higher collaboration scores across several items, particularly those related to innovation, openness, and shared responsibility, whereas more experienced investigators reported perceptions closer to those of healthcare professionals.ConclusionThe findings suggest that collaboration between health and justice professionals is shaped by organizational structures and professional roles. Strengthening coordination may require clearer communication procedures, shared protocols, and institutional spaces for joint reflection and learning to support consistent interdisciplinary practice in sexual abuse response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40352-026-00441-0.Keywords: Interdisciplinary collaboration, Sexual abuse, Healthcare professionals, Criminal investigation, Health and justice, Interprofessional practice, Portugal

Background

Child sexual abuse (CSA) is recognized as a major public health concern worldwide due to its high prevalence and profound consequences for physical, psychological, and social well-being (UNICEF; IRC, 2023). The World Health Organization defines child sexual abuse as the involvement of a child in sexual activity that he or she does not fully comprehend, is unable to give informed consent to, or is developmentally unprepared for (WHO, 2020). Recent global estimates suggest that approximately one in five women and one in seven men report having experienced some form of sexual abuse during childhood, although substantial underreporting indicates that the true prevalence is likely higher (Coelho et al., 2023). Child sexual abuse has been consistently associated with a wide range of adverse outcomes, including depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, substance misuse, suicidal behaviors, sexual and reproductive health problems, and increased risk of revictimization across the life course. Beyond its impact on affected children and their families, child sexual abuse imposes significant social and economic burdens on healthcare systems and justice institutions (Piolanti et al., 2025).

Children who experience sexual abuse and their families often interact with multiple services simultaneously, including healthcare and criminal justice institutions (Coelho et al., 2021; Piolanti et al., 2025). Healthcare professionals are responsible for the identification, clinical assessment, treatment, and safeguarding of children and adolescents who may have experienced sexual abuse, while criminal investigation authorities are tasked with investigating suspected offences and supporting judicial proceedings (Csorba, R., Atas Elfrink, Z., & Tsikouras, P., 2024). Because children depend on caregivers to access and navigate these services, effective responses to child sexual abuse require coordinated collaboration between healthcare and justice professionals to ensure child protection, family support, and access to justice (Moylan et al., 2017).

Among the various professionals involved in responses to child sexual abuse, physicians, nurses, and criminal investigators play particularly important and complementary roles. Because these professionals frequently interact throughout the clinical, forensic, and judicial stages of case management, the quality of collaboration between them may influence both child protection outcomes and the effectiveness of investigative processes.

In this context, interdisciplinary collaboration has become a central component of effective responses to child sexual abuse, contributing to improved communication, more comprehensive assessments, reduced service fragmentation, and better support for children and families throughout investigative and therapeutic processes (Rawlinson et al., 2021a, 2021b; Reeves et al., 2017; WHO, 2003, 2024). However, achieving effective collaboration remains challenging, as professionals often work within different organizational structures, professional cultures, legal mandates, and systems of accountability.

In response to limitations associated with fragmented institutional responses, several countries have developed multidisciplinary and interprofessional models aimed at improving outcomes for children affected by abuse and violence. Examples include Sexual Assault Response Teams (SARTs), Sexual Assault Referral Centres (SARCs), and multidisciplinary child advocacy models, all of which emphasize coordinated action among healthcare professionals, law enforcement agencies, forensic specialists, social workers, and other relevant stakeholders (Adams & Hulton, 2018; Greeson & Campbell, 2013; Herbert & Bromfield, 2019; Moylan & Lindhorst, 2015). Evidence suggests that the effectiveness of these approaches depends not only on the existence of formal protocols but also on the quality of professional relationships and communication between participating agencies (Coelho et al., 2021; Luce H, Schrager S, Gilchrist V., 2010).

Despite growing recognition of the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration, empirical evidence regarding collaborative practices between physicians, nurses, and criminal investigators remains limited, particularly in the context of child sexual abuse (Coelho et al., 2023). Available evidence suggests that collaborative relationships may be influenced by professional cultures, organizational hierarchies, role expectations, and implicit power dynamics, which can either facilitate or hinder effective interdisciplinary practice (D’amour et al., 2005; Reeves et al., 2017). Understanding how professionals perceive and experience collaboration is therefore essential for strengthening coordinated responses and ensuring that services effectively meet the needs of children and families affected by sexual abuse.

Portuguese context

In Portugal, responses to child sexual abuse involve coordinated action across healthcare, forensic, child protection, and criminal justice systems. Under Portuguese law, crimes involving sexual freedom and sexual self-determination, including the sexual abuse of minors, fall under the exclusive jurisdiction of the Polícia Judiciária (PJ), the national criminal investigation police force responsible for investigating serious crimes and conducting criminal investigations related to sexual offences (LOIC, 2008).

Several institutional initiatives have sought to strengthen responses to child sexual abuse, including efforts by the National Institute of Legal Medicine and Forensic Sciences (INMLCF), the national body responsible for medico-legal and forensic examinations (DL n.o 53, de 16 de Junho, 2021); the Polícia Judiciária (PJ); and the National Commission for the Promotion of the Rights and Protection of Children and Young People (CNDPCJ), which coordinates policies and actions aimed at promoting children's rights and protection (CNDPCJ, 2010, 2022, 2024; DGS, 2022).

Formal procedures for addressing sexual abuse within healthcare settings have been established within the Portuguese National Health Service (Serviço Nacional de Saúde – SNS), although awareness and implementation remain uneven across institutions (Coelho et al., 2021). Emergency departments are often the first point of contact for children and families seeking assistance. Depending on local resources and institutional arrangements, cases may involve emergency physicians, pediatricians, nurses, forensic physicians, social workers, and criminal investigation authorities. When forensic physicians are unavailable, hospital teams may be responsible for conducting examinations and collecting biological evidence in accordance with legal and forensic protocols (Magalhães T, Vieira DN, 2011; Vieira P., 2016).

Despite these developments, important challenges remain. Previous studies have identified gaps in service coordination, inconsistencies in clinical and forensic procedures, limited referral pathways, and barriers to communication between healthcare professionals and criminal investigation authorities (Coelho et al., 2021; Vasconcelos et al., 2011). Difficulties in information sharing and coordination may compromise both continuity of care and the effectiveness of investigative processes.

Recent national data highlight the importance of addressing these challenges. Sexual offences against minors continue to represent a significant proportion of crimes involving children and adolescents in Portugal, while reported cases of rape and other sexual offences have increased in recent years (Instituto Nacional de Estatística, 2025). At the same time, available evidence suggests that only a minority of cases identified within healthcare settings are formally reported to criminal investigation authorities (Vasconcelos et al., 2011).

Despite the central role of interdisciplinary collaboration in effective responses to child sexual abuse, empirical research examining collaboration between healthcare professionals and criminal investigation authorities in Portugal remains scarce. The present study addresses this gap by examining collaborative practices between physicians, nurses, and criminal investigators involved in child sexual abuse responses in Portugal.

To do so, this study was grounded in Bronstein’s Model of Interdisciplinary Collaboration (Bronstein, 2002), which conceptualizes collaboration as a multidimensional process emerging through interactions between professionals working across institutional boundaries. The model proposes five interrelated dimensions: interdependence, newly created professional activities, flexibility, collective ownership of goals, and reflection on the collaborative process. A detailed description of the dimensions and their corresponding questionnaire items is provided in Table S1 (Supplementary Materials).

These dimensions capture both structural and relational aspects of interdisciplinary work and provide a useful framework for examining collaboration between healthcare and criminal investigation professionals in responses to child sexual abuse.

Previous research suggests that perceptions of interdisciplinary collaboration may vary according to professional role and organizational context (Rawlinson et al., 2021a, 2021b). Criminal investigators routinely work within multidisciplinary investigative structures and depend on information exchange with external agencies, which may contribute to stronger perceptions of collaborative practice (Herbert, James Leslie & Bromfield, Leah, 2019). In contrast, healthcare professionals often work within organizational environments characterized by strong professional autonomy and specialized role expectations, which may create challenges for collaboration across institutional sectors (D’amour et al., 2005; Hall, 2005).

Evidence from the interprofessional collaboration literature further suggests that nurses tend to report more positive attitudes toward collaborative practice than physicians (Gregoriou et al., 2025). Nurses frequently assume responsibilities related to care coordination, interprofessional communication, and continuity of care across healthcare settings. These functions place them at the intersection of multiple professional interactions and may foster stronger engagement in collaborative practices than is observed in other healthcare professions (Hall, 2005; Reeves et al., 2017; WHO, 2010).

Perceptions of collaboration may also vary according to professional experience. Less experienced professionals are often trained within educational models that place greater emphasis on teamwork and interprofessional practice and may therefore hold more positive views of collaborative work. In contrast, more experienced professionals may have encountered organizational and interagency barriers that lead to more critical evaluations of collaboration. Understanding how professionals perceive interdisciplinary collaboration has important practical implications. Identifying factors that facilitate or hinder collaboration may help inform professional training, communication procedures, referral pathways, and interagency protocols designed to improve coordinated responses to sexual abuse and strengthen support for victims and their families.

Based on this theoretical framework and existing empirical evidence, the study tested the following hypotheses: (H1) criminal investigators report higher levels of interdisciplinary collaboration than healthcare professionals (physicians and nurses) across the five dimensions of the model; (H2) nurses report higher levels of interdisciplinary collaboration than physicians; (H3) less experienced professionals report higher levels of perceived interdisciplinary collaboration than their more experienced counterparts.

To test these hypotheses, a cross-sectional quantitative design was employed, using an adapted version of Bronstein’s Index of Interdisciplinary Collaboration administered to physicians, nurses, and criminal investigators involved in responses to child sexual abuse in Portugal.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional quantitative study was conducted to examine interdisciplinary collaboration between physicians, nurses, and criminal investigators involved in responses to sexual abuse in Portugal.

Data collection instrument

Although several international instruments exist to assess interdisciplinary collaboration, the literature reveals a lack of validated tools specifically addressing collaboration between healthcare professionals and criminal investigators in the context of sexual abuse (Coelho et al., 2023). To address this gap, the present study adapted Bronstein’s Index of Interdisciplinary Collaboration (IIC) to the Portuguese context and to the professional groups under investigation.

The adaptation process followed established methodological guidelines for cross-cultural translation and instrument adaptation (Beaton et al., 2000; Cruchinho et al., 2024). First, the original IIC was independently translated into Portuguese by two bilingual translators with no prior contact with one another and no access to the original instrument during the translation process. This approach ensured linguistic independence and reduced bias in terminology selection. Next, a multidisciplinary panel composed of nine professionals — three physicians, three nurses, and three criminal investigators—was convened. Participants were nominated by their respective supervisors based on professional experience and familiarity with interdisciplinary work. This panel conducted a comparative analysis of the translated versions, resolving discrepancies and selecting the most appropriate wording for each item to ensure semantic equivalence, terminological precision, and conceptual fidelity to the original instrument.

In the final stage, the same professionals completed the preliminary version of the questionnaire and provided structured feedback regarding clarity of instructions, comprehensibility of items, relevance to professional practice, and time required for completion. Minor linguistic adjustments were made based on this feedback to enhance clarity and feasibility.

The internal consistency of the adapted version of Bronstein's Interdisciplinary Collaboration Index (IIC) was assessed using Cronbach's alpha, considering the responses of healthcare professionals (physicians and nurses). The complete scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = 0.93). The Cronbach's alpha values calculated if individual items were excluded ranged from 0.93 to 0.94, indicating that no single item negatively affected the reliability of the instrument.

It was also evaluated for criminal investigators. The complete scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α ≈ 0.90). Cronbach's alpha values, if individual items were excluded, ranged from 0.89 to 0.91, indicating that no item negatively affected the reliability of the instrument within this professional group. These results support the use of the adapted IIC to assess interdisciplinary collaboration between criminal investigators and health professionals involved in responses to sexual abuse.

Questionnaire structure

The final questionnaire comprised four sections. The introductory section presented the study objectives, encouraged full participation, provided researcher contact information, and included an informed consent statement ensuring anonymity and confidentiality. The sociodemographic section contained six closed-ended questions addressing gender, age, educational attainment, professional group, years of professional experience, and prior contact with cases of sexual abuse. The third section consisted of the Interdisciplinary Collaboration Scale (see Table S1 in the Supplementary Materials), comprising 43 items adapted from Bronstein’s Index of Interdisciplinary Collaboration and measured using a six-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (“Don’t know/No answer”) to 5 (“Always”). The scale assessed five dimensions of interdisciplinary collaboration: interdependence (14 items), newly created professional activities (6 items), flexibility (5 items), collective ownership of goals (8 items), and reflection on the collaborative process (10 items). The final section expressed gratitude for participants’ collaboration.

Setting and sampling

Given the nature of the study and the difficulty of obtaining random samples from the target populations, convenience sampling was employed. The target population included physicians and nurses working in public hospitals within the Portuguese National Health Service (SNS) located in the geographic area covered by the Lisbon and Tagus Valley Directorate of the Polícia Judiciária (Diretoria de Lisboa e Vale do Tejo – DLVT). This geographic area comprises nine hospitals with emergency departments. Of these, three hospitals agreed to participate in the study and provided access to healthcare professionals for recruitment.

The study was restricted to the geographic area covered by the Lisbon and Tagus Valley Directorate (DLVT) to ensure institutional coherence between healthcare services and criminal investigation authorities. Although the questionnaire was administered online, limiting recruitment to this area ensured that participants belonged to the same interagency response network and were therefore more likely to share collaborative experiences within comparable organizational, legal, and referral structures. Restricting the study to a single PJ directorate also reduced variability associated with regional differences in institutional practices and intersectoral coordination.

Healthcare professionals were recruited from General Emergency Departments and Pediatric Emergency Departments, as these units are most likely to encounter survivors of sexual abuse. Participating institutions included three Local Health Units (Unidades Locais de Saúde) within the Lisbon metropolitan and surrounding regions.

Criminal investigators included all investigators assigned to the Second Section of the Lisbon and Tagus Valley Directorate of the Polícia Judiciária (DLVT), a specialized unit responsible for investigating sexual offences involving adults and minors. These investigators frequently interact with healthcare professionals during the forensic and judicial stages of case management.

After institutional authorization was obtained, the questionnaire was distributed electronically via a secure Google Forms link. The link was sent to department heads in the hospitals that accepted to participate (medical and nursing leadership) and to the Criminal Investigation Coordinator in the police sector. Each supervisory body was responsible for forwarding the questionnaire to eligible professionals, ensuring that the researchers had no direct contact with participants. The estimated average completion time for the questionnaire was approximately 15 min.

For healthcare professionals, the questionnaire was available between March 8, 2024, and January 23, 2025. This extended collection period reflected delays associated with institutional authorization processes across multiple health units. A total of 274 responses were obtained from healthcare professionals recruited from the three participating hospitals. Of these, four respondents declined participation after reviewing the informed consent form. For criminal investigators, the questionnaire was available between February 22, 2024, and May 29, 2025. All eligible investigators (N = 27) completed the questionnaire, corresponding to a 100% response rate within this professional group.

Ethical considerations

Authorization to conduct the study was obtained from the Boards of Directors of all participating hospitals and from the National Directorate of the Polícia Judiciária. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the School of Health Sciences and Technologies of Lusófona University (Opinion No. 13–23). Participation was voluntary, and respondents could withdraw or revoke consent at any time without consequences. The research team had no direct access to participant identities, and all responses were collected anonymously.

Data analysis

Survey data were exported to Microsoft Excel and subsequently coded for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample. Comparative analyses were conducted to examine differences between professional groups. Responses coded as “Don’t know/No answer” were treated as missing values and excluded from the calculation of dimension scores.

Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for continuous variables due to non-normal distributions. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using RStudio (version 2026.01.0 + 392).

Results

Sample characteristics

The final sample comprised 297 participants, including 270 healthcare professionals (physicians and nurses) and 27 criminal investigators (Table 1). Nurses represented the largest professional group (74%), followed by physicians (17%) and criminal investigators (9%). Overall, 70% of participants were female, and the mean age of the sample was 40 years (SD = 9). Most respondents held a bachelor’s degree (72%), followed by a master’s degree (27%). Nearly half of the participants (47%) reported 16 or more years of professional experience. Slightly more than half of the sample (57%) reported having encountered cases of sexual abuse during their professional practice. All criminal investigation professionals had encountered cases of sexual abuse. Regarding gender, 57% were female and 43% male, a distribution that differs from national workforce statistics for the Polícia Judiciária, which indicate a predominance of male professionals.

Interdisciplinary collaboration scores

Analyzing only the subset of respondents who reported prior contact with sexual crime cases, significant differences were observed between professional groups across all five dimensions of Bronstein’s Model of Interdisciplinary Collaboration (Fig. 1). Criminal investigators (PJ Inspectors) reported the highest mean scores in all dimensions, followed by nurses, with physicians consistently reporting the lowest scores.

Dimension scores by professional category (Nurses, Physicians, PJ Inspectors). Bars show mean scores, for each dimension computed only among respondents who reported prior contact with sexual crime cases. Kruskal–Wallis test p-value for differences across the three professional groups

A clear gradient across professional groups was observed in all dimensions of the collaboration index. Physicians reported the lowest scores, nurses intermediate values, and criminal investigators the highest. Consistent with hypothesis H2, nurses scored higher than physicians across all five dimensions, suggesting stronger perceived engagement in interdisciplinary collaboration. Taken together, these findings provide support for the study hypotheses. Hypothesis 1 was supported, as criminal investigators consistently reported higher levels of interdisciplinary collaboration than healthcare professionals across the dimensions examined. Hypothesis 2 was also supported, with nurses reporting higher collaboration scores than physicians. Hypothesis 3 received partial support, as less experienced criminal investigators generally reported more positive perceptions of collaboration than their more experienced counterparts, whereas this pattern was not consistently observed among healthcare professionals.

Across Bronstein’s five dimensions, a similar pattern is evident at the item level. In most items, criminal investigators reported higher scores than healthcare professionals, indicating stronger perceptions of collaborative practice. This trend is particularly pronounced in the dimensions of interdependence, newly created professional activities, collective ownership of goals, and reflection on the collaborative process. By contrast, healthcare professionals, especially physicians, show lower scores and greater variability. Differences are less marked in the flexibility dimension, suggesting greater convergence between groups in perceptions related to the adaptation of professional roles during collaborative work.

These overall patterns provide the context for the item-level differences illustrated in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Differences between Justice and Health professionals were examined across the five dimensions using the distribution of responses across categories (Table S2). Figures 2–6 show the percentage of respondents in each professional group who reported that the specific factor was present “always” or “almost always” (scores of 5 or 4 on the Likert scale). Because the response scale ranges from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), higher proportions indicate that a given collaborative practice is perceived as occurring more frequently. Conversely, low proportions indicate that the practice is perceived as rarely occurring. The interpretation of the figures therefore focuses on the relative distance between the response profiles of the professional groups: when the lines are close, perceptions are similar; when they diverge, the groups report different frequencies in the occurrence of that collaborative practice. This visualization facilitates comparison of response patterns across professional groups and dimensions of the collaboration model.

Interdependence

Figure 2 presents the distribution of responses for the Interdependence dimension. Several items show comparable response profiles between Justice and Health professionals, particularly those related to operational collaboration, such as Teamwork, Role Clarity, and Support, where both groups report relatively high levels of agreement.

However, marked sectoral differences emerge in several items. The most pronounced divergence occurs in Communication, where 82.6% of criminal investigators report frequent communication with healthcare professionals compared with 22.7% of health professionals (p < 0.001). A similar pattern is observed in Expertise, with investigators more often reporting reliance on the expertise of healthcare professionals (78.3% vs 29.9%; p < 0.001). Differences are also evident in Equality, where investigators more frequently report that health professionals treat them as professional equals (86.4% vs 47.6%; p = 0.001). A smaller but statistically significant difference is observed in Informality, indicating that informal communication channels are reported slightly more often by investigators (8.7% vs 0%; p = 0.019).

Within the justice sector, most items show limited variation by experience. However, less experienced criminal investigators report higher scores in Equality and Cooperation, suggesting that early-career investigators more frequently perceive collaborative interactions with healthcare professionals as reciprocal and integrated into their professional responsibilities.

Newly created professional activities

Figure 3 presents the response distribution for the Newly Created Professional Activities dimension. Across several items, Justice and Health professionals show broadly similar perceptions regarding the outcomes of interdisciplinary collaboration.

Nevertheless, statistically significant sectoral differences are observed in Innovation, Protocols, and Coalitions. In Innovation, all criminal investigators report that innovative programs arise from interdisciplinary teamwork (100%), compared with 75.2% of healthcare professionals (p = 0.012). Differences are also evident in Protocols, where investigators more frequently report that organizational protocols reflect cooperation between sectors (82.6% vs 56.6%; p = 0.021). In Coalitions, investigators less frequently report the creation of collaborative structures such as working groups or committees than healthcare professionals (8.7% vs 33.7%; p = 0.021), suggesting differing perceptions regarding the institutionalization of interdisciplinary collaboration. The closest alignment between sectors is observed in Synergy, where both groups report high agreement that collaboration produces outcomes that would not be achieved individually (87.0% vs 80.6%; p = 0.566).

Within the justice sector, a consistent experience gradient is visible. Less experienced investigators report higher levels of agreement across several items, particularly those related to Innovation and Protocols, whereas more experienced investigators display response patterns closer to those of healthcare professionals, suggesting a convergence of perceptions with increasing professional experience.

Flexibility

Figure 4 presents the response distribution for the Flexibility dimension. Overall, Justice and Health professionals show relatively similar response profiles across several items, including Flexibility and Autonomy, where proportions of agreement are comparable between groups. However, statistically significant differences emerge in Rigidity and Collaboration. In Rigidity, healthcare professionals more frequently report that criminal investigators and health professionals follow strict professional roles (75.0% vs 45.0%; p = 0.025). In contrast, investigators more frequently report working with healthcare professionals in different ways depending on the situation (Collaboration: 65.2% vs 22.3%; p < 0.001), suggesting that investigators perceive greater situational adaptability in collaborative practice.

Within the justice sector, an experience-related pattern is again visible. Less experienced criminal investigators report higher levels of agreement across several items, particularly those related to autonomy and procedural adjustment during collaboration. More experienced investigators display response profiles closer to those of healthcare professionals, indicating convergence in perceptions with professional experience. Within the healthcare sector, a similar experience-related pattern emerges among physicians. More experienced physicians more frequently report strict adherence to formal professional roles, whereas less experienced physicians more often report willingness to assume tasks outside their formal responsibilities and to sacrifice some degree of autonomy to support joint problem solving, suggesting a more flexible view of interdisciplinary collaboration among early-career physicians.

Collective ownership of objectives

Figure 5 presents the response distribution for the Collective Ownership of Objectives dimension. Across most items, Justice and Health professionals report broadly similar levels of agreement regarding shared responsibility in collaborative work. A statistically significant difference between sectors is observed in Openness, which refers to whether collaborative interactions occur in a climate that allows disagreement and diverse perspectives. Criminal investigators report substantially higher agreement than healthcare professionals (85.7% vs 46.1%; p = 0.001).

The closest alignment between sectors is observed in Victim/Family Engagement, where agreement levels remain relatively low in both groups (31.2% vs 52.9%; p = 0.159), indicating that the involvement of victims and families in interdisciplinary processes is perceived as occurring infrequently. Within the justice sector, less experienced investigators report higher levels of agreement across several items, including Commitment, Joint Decisions, Conflict Resolution, and Openness, suggesting stronger perceptions of collaborative engagement with health professionals. More experienced investigators again display response patterns closer to those of healthcare professionals, indicating convergence of perceptions with increasing professional experience. Within the healthcare sector, physicians report lower agreement than nurses in Implementation, which refers to the joint implementation of plans or protocols toward shared objectives, suggesting that nurses more frequently perceive participation in the operational implementation of interdisciplinary plans involving criminal investigators.

Reflection on the process

Figure 6 presents the response distribution for the Reflection on the Collaborative Process dimension. Several items reveal substantial sectoral differences in perceptions of reflective and evaluative collaborative practices. The largest divergence is observed in Information, where 83.3% of criminal investigators report that healthcare professionals share information objectively with investigators, compared with 23.1% of healthcare professionals (p < 0.001). Differences are also observed in Climate, where investigators more frequently report that professionals attempt to create a positive working climate (78.3% vs 50.9%; p = 0.021), and in Optimism, where investigators express greater confidence in collaborative problem-solving capacity (91.3% vs 67.6%; p = 0.024). In contrast, healthcare professionals more frequently report mediation practices in collaborative situations (Mediation: 19.8% vs 0%; p = 0.025).

Several items show strong convergence between sectors, particularly those related to Role Negotiation, Strategies, and Evaluation, where both groups report low levels of agreement, indicating that these reflective practices are perceived as occurring infrequently.

Within the justice sector, less experienced investigators again tend to report higher levels of agreement across several items, particularly those related to information exchange and collaborative interaction with healthcare professionals.

“Don’t know/No answer” responses

An analysis of “Don’t know/No answer” responses revealed a clear asymmetry between professional groups. Physicians exhibited the highest proportion of such responses across most dimensions, followed by nurses, whereas criminal investigation professionals showed the lowest proportion. This pattern was particularly evident in the dimensions of interdependence, collective ownership of goals, and flexibility. The higher prevalence of “Don’t know/No answer” responses among healthcare professionals indicates lower reported familiarity with collaborative processes involving criminal investigation authorities. In contrast, criminal investigators reported fewer “Don’t know/No answer” responses across the examined dimensions.

Discussion

This study examined interdisciplinary collaboration between physicians, nurses, and criminal investigators involved in responses to sexual abuse in Portugal, using Bronstein’s Model of Interdisciplinary Collaboration (Bronstein, 2002) as the guiding framework. This study addresses an important gap in the literature, as empirical evidence examining collaboration between healthcare professionals and criminal investigation authorities in responses to sexual abuse remains limited, particularly within the Portuguese context. By exploring how different professional groups perceive interdisciplinary collaboration, the study contributes to a better understanding of the organizational and professional factors that may facilitate or hinder coordinated responses to sexual abuse. Although previous studies have examined interprofessional collaboration within healthcare settings and multidisciplinary responses to child abuse, few studies have directly compared the perceptions of physicians, nurses, and criminal investigators involved in sexual abuse response. To our knowledge, this is the first Portuguese study to examine interdisciplinary collaboration across health and justice sectors using Bronstein’s Model of Interdisciplinary Collaboration. These findings are relevant not only for understanding collaboration between professional groups but also for informing organizational strategies, professional training, communication procedures, and interagency coordination aimed at strengthening responses to sexual abuse.

Three findings are particularly important for understanding interdisciplinary collaboration between healthcare professionals and criminal investigators in responses to sexual abuse.

First, the more favorable perceptions reported by criminal investigators may reflect the organizational characteristics of criminal investigation work. Investigators routinely operate within multidisciplinary teams and depend on collaboration with external agencies, making interprofessional interaction a central component of their daily activities. The multidisciplinary nature of criminal investigation work within the Polícia Judiciária may further contribute to these findings. Criminal investigations frequently involve collaboration with professionals from different backgrounds, including forensic physicians, psychologists, laboratory specialists, legal professionals, and other investigative units (Herbert & Bromfield, 2021). Such routine exposure to multidisciplinary work may strengthen collaborative competencies and contribute to more positive perceptions of interprofessional collaboration (Bronstein, 2002; Reeves et al., 2017).

Second, nurses generally reported more positive perceptions of collaboration than physicians. This finding is consistent with previous research suggesting that nurses frequently assume coordinating roles that require communication across professional and organizational boundaries(Karam et al., 2026). Their involvement in care coordination and continuity of care may provide greater opportunities to engage in collaborative activities and to recognize their value in practice (Hall, 2005; Reeves et al., 2017; WHO, 2010).

Third, less experienced criminal investigators reported more favorable perceptions of collaboration than their more experienced counterparts. One possible explanation is that recent professional training increasingly emphasizes teamwork, interagency cooperation, and collaborative problem-solving. More experienced professionals may evaluate collaboration more critically due to prolonged exposure to organizational barriers and practical constraints encountered throughout their careers (Rawlinson et al., 2021a, 2021b).

Together, these findings indicate that cross-sector collaboration in sexual abuse response is not simply “high vs low,” but structured—and made visible—through different organizational routines and professional logics, consistent with interprofessional collaboration scholarship emphasizing the role of power relations, role clarity, and communication infrastructure (D’amour et al., 2005).

These findings can be interpreted through the combined lens of Bronstein’s model of interdisciplinary collaboration and organizational theory. Bronstein emphasizes that effective collaboration depends on interdependence, shared goals, and reflective practices embedded in professional routines (Bronstein, 2002). The higher collaboration scores reported by criminal investigators may reflect organizational environments closer to Mintzberg’s concept of professional adhocracies, characterized by flexible coordination and horizontal communication (Mintzberg, H., 1993). In contrast, healthcare settings often operate within more hierarchical structures, which may limit the visibility and operationalization of interdisciplinary collaboration across institutional boundaries. This lens helps explain why criminal investigators report substantially higher Communication and Information exchange than healthcare professionals: in investigative settings, cross-sector contact is often directly tied to case progression (eg, evidence interpretation, timelines), whereas healthcare settings may experience contact with justice professionals as episodic, procedurally constrained, or mediated through documentation and formal requests rather than routine interaction. The result is a perceptual asymmetry: one sector experiences “contact” as central to core work; the other experiences it as peripheral to clinical workflow. This is consistent with broader interprofessional and interagency literature identifying poor communication, unclear roles, and lack of structured interfaces as recurring barriers to collaboration (Rawlinson et al., 2021a, 2021b).

The strongest divergence appears within the Interdependence dimension, particularly in communication-related items. Criminal investigators report substantially higher levels of frequent communication with healthcare professionals than healthcare professionals report with investigators. Similar asymmetries appear in perceptions of reliance on expertise and professional equality (Coelho et al., 2023).

Bronstein (2002) identifies interdependence as the core mechanism through which interdisciplinary collaboration becomes operational. The magnitude of the communication gap observed here suggests that collaboration may be experienced asymmetrically across sectors. Investigators appear to perceive collaboration as more frequent and reciprocal than healthcare professionals do. This asymmetry aligns with literature showing that interprofessional collaboration in healthcare is often shaped by entrenched professional hierarchies and role segmentation (D’amour, D., & Oandasan, I., 2005). Medical practice in particular has historically been characterized by strong professional autonomy and vertical authority structures, which may limit horizontal, cross-sectoral interaction. The finding that investigators more frequently report being treated as professional equals further reflects differences in professional status perceptions across sectors. Research on interprofessional power dynamics consistently shows that collaboration is influenced by perceived status hierarchies, with medicine often occupying a dominant position within health systems. When collaboration extends beyond healthcare into justice settings, these hierarchies may become even more complex.

In the dimension of Newly Created Professional Activities, Criminal investigators reported higher endorsement of Innovation (100% vs 75.2%) and Protocols (82.6% vs 56.6%), suggesting they more frequently perceive collaboration as producing new ways of working and formalized cooperative procedures. This interpretation is compatible with Bronstein’s emphasis that collaboration can generate new joint activities and role arrangements. Yet the pattern for Coalitions reverses: healthcare professionals more often report coalitions/committees emerging from interdisciplinary work (33.7% vs 8.7%). Rather than contradicting investigator-reported innovation, this suggests that the two sectors may be noticing different “forms” of collaborative infrastructure: (i) criminal investigators may experience collaboration primarily as case-based coordination and procedural adaptation, not as standing committees; (ii) Healthcare professionals may be more exposed to formal committee structures, even if these coalitions are largely internal or do not translate into frequent intersectoral contact. This distinction is well documented in the sexual assault and child protection coordination literature. Sexual Assault Response Team (SART) and multidisciplinary team models emphasize that sustainable coordination depends on both case-level coordination and community/agency-level structures (i.e. protocol development, regular meetings, and shared systems), yet the presence of a structure does not guarantee consistent cross-sector practice (Moylan et al., 2017).

From an organizational perspective, investigative environments may resemble Mintzberg’s (1993) concept of professional adhocracies, where coordination is achieved through mutual adjustment rather than rigid standardization. By contrast, healthcare organizations often rely on standardized protocols, professional jurisdictional boundaries, and bureaucratic governance mechanisms (Mintzberg, 1993; Reeves et al., 2017). While such structures are essential for clinical safety, they may constrain the visible emergence of interdisciplinary innovations across institutional boundaries. Interestingly, healthcare professionals report higher levels of coalition or committee development than investigators, suggesting that formal structures may exist in health systems but are not necessarily perceived as innovative by investigators. This distinction highlights that formalization does not automatically translate into perceived collaborative transformation.

The Flexibility dimension reveals a nuanced pattern. While overall differences are smaller than in other domains, two items stand out: investigators more frequently report situational adaptability in collaborative work, whereas healthcare professionals, particularly physicians, more frequently report strict adherence to formal professional roles. This pattern reflects well-documented tensions between professional autonomy and collaborative permeability. Medical autonomy, central to professional identity in medicine, may inadvertently limit role flexibility in interdisciplinary settings (D’amour, D., & Oandasan, I., 2005). Nurses, by contrast, often operate within more relational and coordination-oriented roles, which may explain their consistently higher collaboration scores relative to physicians. This aligns with prior interprofessional research showing that nurses frequently function as boundary spanners in complex care environments (Reeves et al., 2017) This pattern is consistent with healthcare interprofessional literature showing that professional identity concerns, role protection, and hierarchical relations can limit role permeability. Hall’s analysis of professional cultures also emphasizes that professions develop distinct values, problem-solving styles, and “languages,” reinforced through training and socialization, which can impede collaborative adaptability when roles and expectations clash (Hall, 2005).

In the dimension of Collective Ownership of Goals, overall differences between sectors are smaller, with one key exception: criminal investigators report significantly higher levels of openness in collaborative interactions. Openness to disagreement and alternative perspectives is central to Bronstein’s model, as it enables joint problem solving and shared goal construction. The lower healthcare endorsement of openness may reflect hierarchical norms, time pressure, and risk management cultures that can discourage dissent, particularly across institutional boundaries.

The relatively low and comparable scores in victim/family engagement across both sectors are notable. This suggests that, despite policy emphasis on victim-centered approaches in sexual abuse response, interdisciplinary planning involving victims and families may remain underdeveloped in practice. Similar implementation gaps have been identified in integrated child protection and forensic health systems (Rawlinson et al., 2021a, 2021b).

In the Reflection on the Collaborative Process dimension, differences are again pronounced, particularly in information exchange and perceptions of collaborative climate. Investigators report substantially higher levels of objective information sharing and optimism regarding collaborative capacity. Simultaneously, both sectors reported very low agreement for more formalized reflective routines (eg, Strategies, Role Negotiation, Inclusion, Evaluation), suggesting that structured cross-sector learning (eg., regular joint review, planned improvement discussions, explicit negotiation of roles) may be limited. This aligns with broader evidence that interprofessional collaboration often falters when it is not supported by recurring organizational routines (meetings, checklists, structured rounds, facilitated action planning), even though such interventions can improve professional practice and care processes in some settings (Reeves et al., 2017). Healthcare professionals report higher levels of mediation practices, but overall reflective practices such as evaluation and role negotiation remain low across both sectors. Bronstein (2002) emphasizes reflection as a mechanism for sustaining collaboration over time. The low levels of reported role negotiation and evaluation in both groups suggest that reflective processes may not be fully institutionalized. Healthcare systems often engage in reflection reactively (e.g., following adverse events), rather than as routine interdisciplinary practice (Reeves et al., 2017). Investigative contexts, particularly in high-stakes criminal cases, may incorporate more systematic debriefing procedures.

The higher levels of “Don’t know/No answer” responses among healthcare professionals further suggest that interdisciplinary collaboration with criminal investigators may be less structurally visible within health organizations. Collaboration may occur episodically or be concentrated among specific roles (e.g., forensic nurses), rather than embedded across clinical routines. Criminal investigators, by contrast, may experience cross-sector collaboration as more central to case progression. Taken together, these patterns suggest that interdisciplinary collaboration may be more structurally embedded and visible within criminal investigation settings than within healthcare environments. Criminal investigation teams appear to operate within less hierarchical, more flexible organizational forms that resemble Mintzberg’s concept of professional adhocracies, characterized by decentralized decision-making, horizontal communication, and adaptive coordination (Mintzberg, H., 1993). Such configurations are conducive to collaboration, innovation, and reflective practice. Healthcare organizations, by contrast, remain largely hierarchical, with rigid professional boundaries that may impede effective intersectoral collaboration.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the use of convenience sampling limits the generalizability of the results. Although the sample reflects professionals working in key services involved in sexual abuse response, it may not be representative of all healthcare or criminal investigation professionals in Portugal.

In addition, it was not possible to calculate a response rate for healthcare professionals. The questionnaire was distributed through hospital leadership structures, and the research team did not have access to the number of eligible professionals who received the invitation. Consequently, the denominator required to estimate a response rate was unavailable.

The representativeness of the sample may also have been affected by several characteristics of the study design. The study was restricted to professionals working within the geographic area covered by the Lisbon and Tagus Valley Directorate of the Polícia Judiciária (DLVT), which may limit the transferability of the findings to other regions of Portugal. In addition, the professional groups were unevenly represented, with nurses substantially outnumbering physicians and criminal investigators. The sample also included a predominance of female participants. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings and their generalizability.

Second, data collection occurred during an atypical period marked by recurrent closures and reorganization of pediatric emergency services within the Portuguese National Health Service. These structural disruptions, driven by workforce shortages and organizational instability, may have affected participation rates and professionals’ perceptions of collaboration.

Third, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of response bias, including social desirability and recall bias. Participants’ perceptions of collaboration may not fully correspond to actual collaborative practices.

Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. While differences between professional groups were identified, longitudinal research would be necessary to examine how collaborative practices evolve over time and in response to organizational interventions such as joint training or protocol implementation.

Conclusion

This study identifies systematic differences in how interdisciplinary collaboration is perceived by healthcare professionals and criminal investigators involved in responses to sexual abuse in Portugal. Across the five dimensions of Bronstein’s collaboration model, criminal investigators generally report stronger perceptions of collaborative practice, while healthcare professionals, particularly physicians, report lower levels of interaction in several domains. These differences suggest that collaboration is experienced within distinct organizational contexts, with investigative environments appearing to support more visible coordination, adaptive interaction, and collective reflection than healthcare settings.

The findings indicate that strengthening justice–health collaboration requires more than reaffirming the importance of interprofessional cooperation. Rather, it requires institutional mechanisms that make collaboration routine, visible, and reciprocal across sectors. In particular, clearer procedures for information exchange, shared operational protocols, and regular spaces for joint work and reflection, such as multidisciplinary case reviews or structured debriefings, may help reduce the perception gaps identified in this study. The relatively low endorsement of victim and family participation further suggests that survivor-centered collaboration is not yet consistently embedded in interdisciplinary planning.

Overall, improving responses to sexual abuse will depend on strengthening the structural interfaces between health and justice systems. Embedding collaboration into training, protocols, and organizational routines may help sustain coordinated practices over time and ensure that interdisciplinary collaboration becomes an operational component of professional practice rather than an aspirational principle.
