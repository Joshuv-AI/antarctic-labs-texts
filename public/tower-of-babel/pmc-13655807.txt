Treatment patterns and prognostic factors of radiation-associated esophageal fistula in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Han-Yu Si, Qiu-Ying An, Hong-Shan Ji, Ya-Jing Wang, Ya-Ge Jia, Yu-Hao Su, Meng-Lu Liu, Jin Yan, Zi-Han Zhang, Chan-Jun Zhen, Wen-Wen Bai, Rui Zhang, Li-Yuan Fu, Yu-Zhi Song, Ping Zhang, Zhi-Guo Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost previous studies have focused on exploring the formation mechanism of esophageal fistula (EF) and predicting its risk of occurrence. However, few studies have reported the treatment and prognosis of EF after radiotherapy (RT) in patients with esophageal cancer (EC). This study aimed to explore treatment patterns and prognostic factors of radiation-associated esophageal fistula (RAEF) in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC).MethodsA total of 91 EF patients were retrospectively enrolled from January 2016 to July 2023. Based on the fistula type, 39 patients presented with tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF) and 52 with esophagomediastinal fistula (EMF). Survival analyses utilized the Kaplan-Meier method. Multivariable Cox regression analysis was employed to ascertain prognosis.ResultsBy the last follow-up date on June 30, 2025, all patients had died. The cohort had a median post-fistula survival time of 3.1 months, and their 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month survival rates were 51.6%, 18.7%, and 5.5%, respectively. All patients received nutritional support therapy, including nasal feeding (n=61), esophageal stenting (n=10), gastrostomy/jejunostomy (n=12), and simple fluid infusion therapy (n=8). Gastrostomy/jejunostomy was associated with the highest post-fistula survival rates (3-month: 83.3%, 6-month: 41.7%, 12-month: 25.0%). In TEF, esophageal stenting provided comparable 3-month post-fistula survival rates to gastrostomy/jejunostomy (71.4% vs. 75.0%). Among 21 patients who had EF during primary RT, those completing the planned RT after receiving nutritional support therapy achieved superior fistula closure rates compared to treatment discontinuation (77.8% vs. 25.0%). Multivariable Cox analysis identified reirradiation, simple fluid infusion therapy, persistent fistula, and hemoglobin <120 g/L as independent unfavorable prognostic factors.ConclusionsGastrostomy/jejunostomy is associated with improved survival in patients with RAEF. Esophageal stenting represents a viable short-term palliative option for TEF patients. Completing planned RT may promote fistula healing for EF during primary RT. Hemoglobin level serves as an accessible prognostic biomarker.Keywords: Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), radiotherapy (RT), esophageal fistula (EF), prognostic factors, treatment patterns

Highlight box

Key findings

• We show that gastrostomy/jejunostomy is associated with improved survival in patients with radiation-associated esophageal fistula (RAEF). Additionally, esophageal stenting represents a viable short-term palliative option for tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF) patients, and hemoglobin level serves as an accessible prognostic biomarker.

What is known and what is new?

• Nutritional support therapy is commonly employed for patients with esophageal fistula (EF) in clinical practice. However, the optimal regimen and therapeutic options for different types of EF still lack clear recommendations.

• Our findings are significant because we focused on this selected population in radiation-specific etiology, excluding patients with disease progression or untreated EF. Furthermore, we analyzed characteristics of different treatments.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings provide a reference for selecting nutritional support strategies in patients with RAEF and suggest that routine hematological parameters may serve as prognostic indicators.

• Whether esophageal cancer patients with EF are suitable for radiotherapy (RT) requires further investigation.

Introduction

In China, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) accounts for more than 90% of all esophageal cancer (EC) (1,2). Esophageal fistula (EF) is a devastating complication of EC, and its formation is predominantly attributed to tumor invasion into adjacent tissues and organs, including the pleura, pericardium, trachea, bronchi, and aorta. Additionally, while chemoradiotherapy (CRT) effectively kills tumor cells, it may also cause rapid tumor regression without adequate time for normal tissue repair, leading to the development of EF (3-5). Some studies report that the incidence of EF in EC patients after CRT ranges from 5.8% to 30.1% (6-9). EF is frequently accompanied by life-threatening complications such as severe pulmonary infection, massive hemorrhage, and cardiorespiratory failure. Thus, the prognosis for patients who develop EF is extremely poor, with a median post-fistula survival time of 2 to 4 months, and most patients die within 6 months (10,11). Although EF has attracted much attention, most previous studies focused on exploring the formation mechanism of EF and predicting the risk of its occurrence (7-10). Some studies on EF have included confounding variables like disease progression and untreated EC (11-15), thereby limiting their applicability in radiation-specific etiology. Furthermore, there are few studies reporting the treatment and prognosis of EF after radiotherapy (RT) in patients with EC, and the specific treatment patterns have not been clearly specified in National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines. Nutritional support therapy is commonly employed for patients with EF in clinical practice, including nasal feeding, esophageal stenting, gastrostomy/jejunostomy, and simple fluid infusion therapy (13). However, the optimal regimen and therapeutic options for different types of EF still lack clear recommendations. Particularly, RT after the development of EF remains controversial (14-16). By focusing exclusively on this selected population, this study aims to analyze the therapeutic efficacy of different treatment modalities and identify prognostic factors in patients with radiation-associated esophageal fistula (RAEF). We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1519/rc).

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively collected clinical data on ESCC patients who developed RAEF at The Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University between January 2016 and July 2023. Inclusion criteria: (I) age ≥18 years, (II) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score ≤2, (III) pathological diagnosis of ESCC, (IV) stage II–IV according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (8th edition) before RT, (V) patients who received treatment based on RT of the primary esophageal lesion, (VI) EF was diagnosed by endoscopy, computed tomography (CT), or X-ray esophagography. Exclusion criteria: (I) patients with prior or concomitant malignancy, (II) fistula development prior to RT or attributed to locoregional progression, (III) underwent EC surgery, (IV) EF attributable to other causes, such as medical injury or trauma, (V) significant gaps or loss in follow-up medical records. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Human Ethics Committee of The Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (No. 2025KS107). The need for informed consent was waived by the same ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Data collection and diagnostic criteria

The collected clinical data mainly included age, sex, tumor length, tumor thickness, cTNM stage, tumor location, fistula location, type of EF, hematological parameters, body mass index (BMI), and treatment. Hematological parameters and BMI were collected within two weeks prior to the diagnosis of EF.

The diagnostic criteria for EF are as follows (17,18): (I) esophagography shows the contrast agent in the esophagus entering the trachea/bronchi or mediastinum; (II) CT shows that gas can be seen around the esophagus and in the mediastinum. There is a gas fistula between the esophagus and the adjacent mediastinum, and sometimes effusion can be seen in the pleural cavity; (III) the fistula of the esophageal wall can be seen. Follow-up

Follow-up examinations included esophagography, chest/abdominal CT, and/or positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET/CT). The post-fistula survival time was calculated from diagnosis of EF to death. The median post-fistula survival time was defined as the survival time corresponding to a survival rate of 50% from the diagnosis of EF.

Statistical analysis

The Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to ascertain prognostic factors. The post-fistula survival time was estimated by Kaplan-Meier curves, which were compared using the log-rank test. The optimal cut-off values of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were generated using surv_cutpoint of the survminer R package. All statistical analyses were performed in R software (version 4.2.1), with a two-sided P value <0.05 defining statistical significance.

Results

Clinical data of patients

A total of 91 EF patients met inclusion criteria, comprising 67 males (73.6%) and 24 females (26.4%), with a median age of 63 years (range, 37–85 years). The median length of the esophageal tumor was 6.0 cm (range, 3.2–12.0 cm), and the median tumor thickness was 15.0 mm (range, 6.7–36.0 mm). Anatomically, 39 patients (42.9%) presented with tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF) and 52 (57.1%) with esophagomediastinal fistula (EMF). The distribution of fistula locations was as follows: upper thoracic in 29 patients (31.9%), middle thoracic in 53 patients (58.2%), and lower thoracic in 9 patients (9.9%). The optimal cut-off values of NLR and PLR were 9.6 and 413.9, respectively. Other clinical data are listed in Table 1.

Survival of all patients

By the last follow-up date on June 30, 2025, all patients had died. The median post-fistula survival time was 3.1 months, and their 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year survival rates were 51.6%, 18.7%, and 5.5%, respectively.

Post-fistula RT

RAEF patients were categorized into four groups: fistula post-primary RT (n=51), fistula during primary RT (n=21), fistula post-reirradiation (n=13), and fistula during reirradiation (n=6). In our cohort of 91 patients with EF, the total radiation dose ranged from 5.4 to 70.0 Gy (5 days/week, 1.8–2.25 Gy/day) for patients who developed fistula during or after primary RT, and from 79.8 to 120.0 Gy (5 days/week, 1.8–2.0 Gy/day) for those who developed fistula during or after reirradiation. All patients underwent intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT). Fifty-nine patients received CRT, while 32 received RT alone. The chemotherapy regimens were primarily taxane- or fluorouracil-based, combined with platinum agents. Among the 51 patients who developed EF after completing primary RT, the median time from the end of primary RT to EF diagnosis was 3.1 months (range, 0.1–14.0 months), with 48 cases (94.1%) occurring within 6 months after RT completion. Among the 13 patients who developed EF after reirradiation, the median time from the end of reirradiation to EF diagnosis was 3.0 months (range, 0.4–5.9 months). The detailed characteristics of these 21 patients are presented in Table S2. The median post-fistula survival time was 6.6 months for patients who continued RT and 3.5 months for those who discontinued RT. The 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year survival rates were 100.0%, 55.6%, 22.2% vs. 58.3%, 25.0%, 8.3%, respectively (Figure 3D, P=0.34). Among the 21 patients developing EF during primary RT, completion of the planned RT after receiving nutritional support therapy achieved fistula closure in 77.8% (7 of 9 patients) vs. 25.0% (3 of 12) in those discontinuing treatment (Figure 4). Notably, patients were categorized by pre-fistula nutritional status. Better nutritional status was defined as meeting at least two of the criteria: hemoglobin ≥120 g/L, albumin ≥30 g/L, and BMI ≥18.5 kg/m2. Among 13 patients with better nutritional status, 8 (61.5%) had fistula closure, while among remaining 8 patients with worse nutritional status, only 2 (25%) had fistula closure.

Discussion

The management of EF after RT for EC remains a critical and unresolved challenge. Although nutritional support continues to be the cornerstone of treatment, the applicable population and efficacy of different nutritional support methods have not yet been conclusively determined. Earlier studies have indicated (13,19) that proactive interventions such as gastrostomy, nasal feeding, and esophageal stenting may be superior to conservative fluid infusion therapy alone, particularly with the benefits of gastrostomy being most evident. Xu et al. conducted an analysis of 98 EC patients with EF. Compared with simple fluid infusion therapy, gastrostomy was associated with a 59% reduction in mortality risk (19). Another retrospective cohort study involving a relatively large sample of 352 patients also demonstrated that for EC patients with EF, the median post-fistula survival time for those receiving gastrostomy, nasal feeding, esophageal stenting, and simple fluid infusion therapy was 3.0, 2.2, 2.7, and 1.0 months, respectively. Gastrostomy has shown potential superiority over esophageal stenting and nasal feeding (13). Our study also analyzed the patterns of nutritional support. In contrast, previous studies enrolled EF patients with confounding factors (EF resulting from locoregional progression and untreated EC). This study exclusively enrolled patients with RAEF, thereby providing direct evidence for treatment in this specific population. Despite differences in the study cohort, our findings presented similar outcomes: patients receiving gastrostomy/jejunostomy exhibited post-fistula survival rates of 83.3% at 3 months and 25.0% at 1 year, whereas the simple fluid infusion therapy group showed only a 25.0% survival rate at 3 months, and all patients died within 6 months. Therefore, for patients with RAEF in EC, those who receive gastrostomy/jejunostomy have a survival advantage, which may be associated with reduced stimulation of the fistula and ensured nutritional intake.

Although esophageal stenting can quickly alleviate symptoms by sealing the fistula, preventing infection, and improving oral intake, some scholars contend that it is particularly advantageous in TEF (20-22). Nevertheless, for EC patients who have undergone CRT, the insertion of an esophageal stent is more prone to cause life-threatening complications. Over time, this risk may manifest as chest pain, new perforations, hemorrhage, stent migration, and tracheal compression, all of which can be fatal (23-25). In our TEF subgroup analysis, we also observed that esophageal stenting had certain advantages in short-term efficacy, whereas its impact on long-term survival was limited. Patients who received esophageal stenting and gastrostomy/jejunostomy exhibited similar 3-month post-fistula survival rates (71.4% vs. 75.0%). However, the 6-month post-fistula survival rates dropped significantly to 0% in esophageal stenting, compared to 17.1% in gastrostomy/jejunostomy. As Russo et al. pointed out, esophageal stenting may be particularly appropriate for patients with a short life expectancy and limited treatment options (26). Overall, although esophageal stenting provides short-term benefits for patients with radiation-associated TEF, the higher incidence of fatal complications necessitates caution in its recommendation. In the future, we will further focus on identifying populations most likely to benefit from the use of esophageal stenting.

In addition to nutritional support, CRT or RT after EF is a treatment strategy that has been widely discussed in recent years. Nonetheless, its application remains controversial as the presence of a fistula was believed to be a relative contraindication to CRT or RT. Recent studies have shown that CRT or RT for patients with EF can prolong survival time and improve the fistula closure rates (14,16,27,28). Nevertheless, there are relatively few research data on EF patients during RT. Wang et al. conducted an analysis of EF patients during RT and found that subsequent CRT achieved a fistula closure rate of 67.9%. Moreover, patients who continued RT showed significantly prolonged survival compared to those who discontinued RT (2-year overall survival rate: 32.7% vs. 6.8%, P<0.01) (9). Our findings are in accord with these studies, indicating that subsequent RT for EF patients during RT achieved a fistula closure rate of 77.8%. Furthermore, patients who continued RT showed a trend toward better survival compared to those who discontinued RT (median post-fistula survival time: 6.6 vs. 3.5 months). These observations support the hypothesis that RT induces soft tissue swelling in the esophageal wall, which temporarily occludes the fistula, thereby limiting the spread of infection and improving survival time (29).

Although studies have demonstrated that EC patients with EF may benefit from RT, clear criteria for identifying patients eligible for subsequent RT remain lacking. Therefore, this study further analyzed the association between pre-fistula nutritional status and fistula closure. Among patients with better nutritional status, 61.5% achieved fistula closure, but only 25% of those with worse nutritional status did. EF patients with better nutrition demonstrate superior tissue repair capacity. We speculate that patients who have better pre-fistula nutritional status will have a higher rate of fistula closure after subsequent RT. However, whether nutritional status can be used as an indicator for subsequent RT for EF patients during the RT period still needs to be explored with a larger sample size in the future.

Multivariate Cox analysis identified reirradiation, persistent fistula, hemoglobin <120 g/L, and simple fluid infusion therapy as unfavorable prognostic factors for RAEF. Despite being a vital treatment for locally recurrent EC, reirradiation is challenging due to the legacy of initial radiation damage (vascular and tissue damage, rigid fibrotic walls, and poor blood supply). This impaired healing capacity makes the fistula difficult to close after reirradiation and is linked to an extremely poor survival rate (30-33). Additionally, the poorer prognosis may be exacerbated by the inherently high malignancy of locally recurrent EC. Apart from reirradiation factors, nutritional indicators also significantly influence prognosis. The study by Guan et al. (11) revealed that the hemoglobin level was strongly related to the survival time after EF. Hemoglobin level serves as an important indicator reflecting the physiological function and nutritional status. A retrospective study on ESCC showed that anemia is correlated with reduced overall survival (34). Low hemoglobin levels may impair the repair of normal tissue and lead to tumor hypoxia. Consequently, the resulting immune microenvironment becomes increasingly hostile to tissue repair and may exacerbate tumor aggressiveness (35,36). Although albumin was not identified as a prognostic factor in the multivariable Cox analysis of our study, Dai et al. (37) demonstrated that higher albumin levels significantly improve post-fistula survival time. This may be related to the role of albumin in promoting wound healing and alleviating oxidative damage (38,39). Therefore, as important indicators of physiological and nutritional status, nutritional indicators should be closely monitored for their dynamic trends in clinical practice to assist in prognostic assessment. These accessible biomarkers should inform clinical decision-making regarding treatment modalities.

This study had several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study that inevitably introduced confounding factors. Second, the analysis of RAEF lacked detailed RT parameters, such as RT target volume. Third, the nutritional treatment methods for EMF have not been thoroughly analyzed. There are significant differences in the number of EMF cases receiving different treatments, making it difficult to obtain reliable conclusions. Despite these limitations, our study describes treatment patterns and prognostic factors of RAEF in ESCC, a topic rarely reported previously. In the future, further large-scale studies should be conducted.

Conclusions

Gastrostomy/jejunostomy is associated with improved survival in patients with RAEF. Esophageal stenting represents a viable short-term palliative option for TEF patients. Completing planned RT may promote fistula healing for EF during primary RT. Hemoglobin level serves as an accessible prognostic biomarker.
