Acute-on-chronic liver failure–associated CD45+ EPCs reduced survival via promoting fatty acid β-oxidation in vascular endothelial cells
Authors: Xing Li, Nan Zhang, Xiu-Qing Pang, Hai-Shi Wu, Cheng-You Yu, Ze-Xuan Huang, Yan-Fang Xing, Zhi-Liang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) is associated with high mortality and multi-organ damage in chronic liver disease. This study investigated the role of CD45+ erythroid progenitor cells (EPCs) in ACLF progression and underlying mechanisms. CD45+ EPCs from 22 patients with hepatitis B virus-related hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) were analyzed by flow cytometry. Immunofluorescence was used to visualize cell distribution in liver tissue. Mitochondrial function assays and RNA sequencing were performed on isolated CD45+ EPCs. In vitro co-culture of CD45+ EPCs with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) was used. ACLF and chronic liver injury mouse models were used to explore pathological mechanisms, and adoptive transfer experiments evaluated survival and organ injury. CD45+ EPCs were elevated in the circulation and liver of ACLF mice, and adoptive transfer of these EPCs reduced survival in recipients. Among patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer Staging stage D HCC, those with decompensated liver function (Child–Pugh grade C) had significantly higher circulating CD45+ EPCs than those with compensated function (Child–Pugh grade A). EPCs were predominantly localized near vascular endothelial cells in both patients with ACLF and mouse models. CD45+ EPCs from patients displayed mitochondrial retention and ferroptosis. In vitro, ACLF plasma with CD45+ EPCs induced HUVEC death via ferroptotic products, primarily malondialdehyde, which activated FAO. CD45+ EPCs accumulated in the liver, spleen, and brain and correlated with hepatic and cerebral injury. Pharmacological FAO inhibition improved ACLF mice survival. These findings identify CD45+ EPCs as key mediators of ACLF progression via mitochondrial dysfunction and FAO-mediated endothelial injury, highlighting their therapeutic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06234-w.

Introduction

Decompensation of liver disease can lead to the loss of treatment opportunities for many other conditions, such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1, 2], liver metastases [3], and many other diseases [4, 5]. Notably, liver function is the most critical factor in HCC staging [1, 6]. Patients with progressive decompensated liver disease are considered to be in the terminal stage regardless of tumor burden [7]. The major causes of decompensated liver disease [8–11] include drug-induced liver injury, hepatitis, liver malignancy, and autoimmune liver diseases. Despite the clinical use of various hepatoprotective drugs, the progression of decompensated liver function varies widely among individuals and remains difficult to predict. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying the deterioration of decompensated liver function is of great clinical value.

Anemia is a common complication in patients with decompensated liver disease. Since 2013, studies have reported the pathological accumulation of erythroid progenitor cells (EPCs) in the circulation of patients with anemia and tumors and have investigated their immunosuppressive functions [12, 13]. Our previous studies demonstrated that impaired erythroid cell development plays an important pathological role in various liver diseases [2, 14, 15]. In addition, large numbers of CD71+ EPCs accumulate in the circulation of patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and HCC [13, 16], suggesting a pathological role for abnormal extramedullary hematopoiesis [17]. Under these conditions, EPCs derived from the differentiation of megakaryocyte–erythroid progenitor cells fail to mature into erythrocytes in the circulation [18, 19]. Moreover, HCC-associated CD45+ EPCs exhibit morphological and surface marker characteristics similar to those of granulocytes and monocytes, leading to their designation as erythroid-differentiated myeloid cells (EDMCs) [13]. Beyond their immunosuppressive effects, we have previously demonstrated that EPCs impair dendritic [2] and endothelial [14] cells. However, the role of CD45+ EPCs in the progression of decompensated liver disease has not been investigated.

Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) is a severe complication in patients with chronic liver disease and is associated with high short-term mortality [10, 20]. Our previous study showed that circulating CD45+ EPCs predicted survival in patients with ACLF [21]. However, the causal relationship between CD45+ EPCs and ACLF mortality remains unclear. In the present study, we investigated the role of CD45+ EPCs in ACLF progression and explored the underlying mechanisms using mouse models of ACLF, which provide an optimal platform to study disease progression [22].

Materials and methods

Study participants

Between January 2021 and December 2024, 25 patients with CHB and 25 patients with hepatitis B virus–related ACLF (HBV-ACLF) from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (SYSU) were enrolled in this study to investigate circulating CD45+ EPCs. CHB was diagnosed according to the Guideline for the Prevention and Treatment of Chronic Hepatitis B [23] and is defined as a chronic inflammatory liver disease caused by persistent HBV infection. HBV-ACLF was diagnosed based on the Chinese Group on the Study of Severe Hepatitis B–Acute-on-Chronic Liver Failure criteria [24]. Between January 2023 and December 2024, 22 patients with hepatitis B virus-related HCC were also enrolled. All patients had HCC staged Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer Staging (BCLC) C/D and had not received any anticancer treatment. This study was approved by the Clinical Ethics Review Board of the Third Affiliated Hospital of SYSU, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants at admission.

Mice

Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 10% CCl4 (5 µL/g, twice weekly) for 8 weeks to establish the chronic liver injury (CLI) model, followed by a single dose of 50% CCl4 (5 µL/g) and cecal ligation and puncture surgery to induce ACLF.

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; Procell, Wuhan, China; CP-H082) were maintained in HUVEC-specific complete medium (Procell) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All cell lines were authenticated by the supplier three months prior to the study based on viability and morphology. Cells were not cultured for longer than two months.

RNA sequencing data analysis

CD45+CD235+CD71+ cells from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of three patients with HBV-ACLF and three with CHB were sorted using a FACSAria III cell sorter (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), achieving > 95% purity. RNA sequencing was performed on the BGISEQ-500 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China) with 50-bp single-end reads (~ 20 million reads/sample). Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human genome using RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization (version 1.2.12), and gene-level counts and transcripts per kilobase million values were quantified. Samples with ≥ 80% aligned reads were included in the analysis. DESeq2 was used to assess differential expression between experimental groups. Overall changes were considered significant at p < 0.05, with an additional fold change (FC) (|log2FC| > 1) applied to generate the final gene set.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA was extracted using the Multisource Total RNA Miniprep Kit (Axygen, Union City, CA, USA). RNA purity and concentration were assessed by measuring the absorbance at 260/280 nm using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qRT-PCR was performed using commercially available primers (Table S2) and SFYBR Premix Ex Taq II (DRR081; Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China). The reaction mixture contained 3 mL cDNA (equivalent to 50 ng RNA), 0.5 mM forward and reverse primers, and 10 mM SYBR Green Master Mix (Abm, Canada), adjusted to a total volume of 20 mL. Amplification was performed at 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min, followed by melting curve profiling to confirm specificity. Target gene expression levels were normalized to 18 S rRNA using the comparative cycle threshold (Ct) method (2−ΔΔCt). Fluorescence for each cycle was analyzed using the ABI Prism 7000 sequence detection system (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Results are reported as relative expression, normalized to that of GAPDH (CST, Danvers, MA, USA) as the housekeeping gene, and expressed in arbitrary units.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect CD45+Ter119+CD71+ erythroid cells in formalin-fixed mouse tissues, and the number of these cells was quantified in both the CLI and ACLF mouse models. The following antibodies were used: anti-CD45 and donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (red), anti-Ter119 and donkey anti-rat Alexa Fluor 647 (yellow), anti-CD71 and donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (green), and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, dihydrochloride (DAPI; blue). The antibodies and reagents used in the experiments are listed in Table S1.

Flow cytometry

CD45+CD235+CD71+ and CD45−CD235+CD71+ cells were sorted with a purity > 95%, yielding at least 1 × 106 cells per sample. Flow cytometry was performed as previously described [2]. Analysis was conducted using the CytoFLEX LX software (Beckman Coulter, USA). Histograms and geometric mean fluorescence intensity values were obtained using FlowJo software.

Patient follow-up and statistical analysis

Patients were followed for at least three months or until death. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the first day of treatment to death. Variables between groups were compared using the χ2 test (or Fisher’s exact test, when appropriate) and the t-test or nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. OS was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were assessed with the log-rank test. For in vitro experiments, statistical analyses were conducted using unpaired t-tests. Correlations between parameters were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation or logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, and all p-values were two-sided. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), R (version 3.6.0), and SPSS Statistics (version 26.0).

Results

ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs promote mortality in HCC and ACLF

CD45+ EPCs in the liver (Fig. 1A) and circulation (Fig. 1B) of the ACLF mouse model were significantly higher than those in the CLI model with compensatory liver function. Adoptive transfer of CD45+ EPCs from the spleen and circulation of ACLF mice accelerated death in both ACLF (Fig. 1C) and HCC (Fig. 1D) mouse models. Among patients with BCLC stage D HCC, those with decompensated liver function (Child–Pugh grade C; n = 11) had significantly higher circulating CD45+ EPCs than those with compensated liver function (Child–Pugh grade A; n = 11) (Fig. 1E). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that EPCs were predominantly localized near vascular endothelial cells in both patients with ACLF and ACLF mice (Fig. 1F and Fig. S1), further supporting potential interactions between CD45+ EPCs and vascular endothelial cells.

ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs exhibit mitochondrial retention and damage

A previous study demonstrated that CD45+ EPCs display a reduced capacity to differentiate into mature erythrocytes, with mitochondrial loss being a necessary step for erythroid maturation [25]. MitoTracker staining and transmission electron microscopy revealed that the number of mitochondria in CD45+ EPCs from the circulation of patients with ACLF and those with decompensated HCC was higher than that in patients with CHB and compensated HCC (Fig. 2A-C). However, electron microscopy revealed that the mitochondria showed significant shrinkage (Fig. 2D), with the proportion of normal mitochondria markedly decreased (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, JC-1 assays showed a significant increase in the JC-1 monomer ratio (Fig. 2F-H), suggesting a reduced ability of mitochondria in ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs to maintain normal membrane potential. Overall, ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs exhibited mitochondrial retention damage, which may contribute to the deterioration of liver function in patients with decompensated liver disease.

ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs exhibit EDMC features and ferroptosis

CD45+ EPCs in patients with ACLF expressed myeloid cell markers. The levels of CD11b, CD14, CD15, and CD16 were significantly increased in ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs compared to those in patients with CHB (Fig. 3A and Fig. S2). Giemsa staining also showed that CD45+ EPCs in patients with ACLF resembled the morphology of granulocytes or monocytes (Fig. 3B, C). Consistently, CD45+ EPCs from ACLF mice also exhibited morphological characteristics of granulocytes or monocytes (Fig. 3D). We previously observed maturation defects in CD45+ EPCs, although the underlying mechanisms were not investigated. In this study, ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs and CD45+ EPCs from patients with CHB were collected for RNA sequencing, which revealed alterations in lipid metabolism (Fig. 3E and Figs. S3 and S4). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted to examine pathways related to various cell death modalities, including necrosis, pyroptosis, apoptosis, ferroptosis, cuproptosis, and autophagy. The analysis revealed activation of the ferroptosis pathway (Fig. 3F). The mRNA expression of ferroptosis-related molecules CHAC1, PTGS2, SLC7A11, and ACSL4 was upregulated, whereas RGS4 was downregulated (Fig. 3G). CD45+ EPCs isolated from the circulation of ACLF mice and from the bone marrow of healthy mice were cultured overnight. Trypan blue staining showed that ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs exhibited a lower survival rate than those from bone marrow (Fig. 3H). Treatment with ferroptosis inhibitors reduced CD45+ EPC death (Fig. 3I), whereas apoptosis inhibitors, necrosis inhibitors, oxidative stress inhibitors, and copper chelators had no effect. In conditioned media from ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs, lipid peroxidation products, such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), as well as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as high mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1)—but not adenosine triphosphate (ATP)—were elevated compared with media from CHB-associated CD45+ EPCs (Fig. S5).

ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs suppressed HUVEC proliferation by activating fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO)

Previous studies have reported that EPCs and MDSCs can injure endothelial cells [14, 26]. In this study, we found that ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs alone were not effective in suppressing HUVEC proliferation (Fig. 4A). However, when combined with plasma from patients with ACLF, ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs significantly inhibited HUVEC proliferation (Fig. 4B). Previous reports have also shown that plasma levels of extramitochondrial FAO products—such as fatty acylcarnitine and free fatty acids—are significantly elevated in patients with ACLF [27]. Consistently, we found enriched free fatty acids in ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs and in their plasma-induced HUVECs (Fig. 4C). Therefore, we speculated that ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs activate FAO in HUVECs, potentially leading to metabolic reprogramming. ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs and their plasma-induced HUVECs showed increased FAO activity (Fig. 4D), as well as elevated activity of acyl-CoA synthetase and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) (Fig. 4E). We speculated that large amounts of free fatty acids enter induced HUVECs, are converted into fatty acyl-CoA, and subsequently enter the mitochondria to activate FAO. In addition, enriched lipoyl carnitine directly enters the mitochondria through carnitine lipoylcarnitine translocase to drive the initiation of FAO. Inhibition of CPT-1a by etomoxir and that of 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase by trimetazidine both significantly reduced the ability of ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs combined with their plasma to induce HUVEC death in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4F). Activation of FAO may lead to metabolic dysregulation in HUVECs. Acyl-CoA overproduction inhibited hexokinase, thereby blocking the production of glucose-6-phosphate (Fig. 4G). Glucose phosphorylation is the first step in the pentose phosphate pathway. Hexokinase inhibition might result in a critical reduction in ribose and NADPH production via the pentose phosphate pathway, potentially increasing the sensitivity of cells to oxidative stress–related death. However, ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs and their plasma did not alter the NADP/NADPH ratio in HUVECs (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these findings suggest that ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs combined with their plasma activate FAO in HUVECs, leading to HUVEC death.

ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs activate FAO in HUVECs via MDA

The role of ferroptosis in ACLF-associated CD45+ EPC–mediated activation of HUVECs was investigated. CHB-associated CD45+ EPCs were treated with ferroptosis inducers (erastin and RSL3) and then co-cultured with HUVECs, while PBS-treated CHB-associated CD45+ EPCs served as controls. Proliferation of HUVECs was inhibited when co-cultured with erastin- and RSL3-treated CD45+ EPCs in the presence of ACLF plasma (Fig. 4H). Consistently, levels of free fatty acids and the activities of acyl-CoA synthetase and CPT1 in induced HUVECs were elevated following co-culture with ferroptosis inducer–pretreated CD45+ EPCs (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, pretreatment of ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs with the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 reduced their suppressive effect on HUVEC proliferation (Fig. 4J). These HUVECs showed decreased levels of free fatty acids and reduced activities of acyl-CoA synthetase and CPT1 compared with those co-cultured with PBS-pretreated ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs (Fig. 4K). These findings suggest that ferroptosis in ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs activates FAO in HUVECs. To further investigate the mechanism, MDA-, 4-HNE-, and DAMPs-treated CHB-associated CD45+ EPCs were co-cultured with HUVECs. Conditioned media from ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs were used as a positive control. The proliferation of HUVECs was inhibited when co-cultured with MDA-treated CD45+ EPCs in the presence of ACLF plasma (Fig. 4L). Consistent with this, levels of free fatty acids and the activities of acyl-CoA synthetase and CPT1 were elevated (Fig. 4M). In addition, FAO inhibitors alleviated the suppression of HUVEC proliferation by MDA and ACLF-conditioned media (Fig. 4N).

ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs promote mortality

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs accumulated in multiple organs, including the brain, liver, and spleen (Fig. 5A, B), and their abundance was positively correlated with the extent of brain and liver injury (Fig. 5C, D). Moreover, adoptive transfer of ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs increased their accumulation in multiple organs (Fig. 6A, B) and exacerbated multiorgan injury in the ACLF mouse model (Fig. 6C). In vivo treatment with etomoxir and trimetazidine improved OS induced by the transfer of ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

ACLF is a complex clinical syndrome characterized by the acute deterioration of liver function in patients with preexisting chronic liver disease [10, 20]. A critical aspect of ACLF is the dysfunction of vascular endothelial cells (VECs), which play a significant role in disease progression and severity [28]. Our previous studies showed that tumor-associated CD71+ EPCs impaired VECs and lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), thereby promoting tumor metastasis [14, 29]. CD45+CD71+ EPCs enhanced VEC migration via TGF-β and compromised tube formation through fibrinogen beta chain [14]. In contrast, CD45−CD71+EPCs promoted the proliferation of LECs and induced a hybrid epithelial–mesenchymal state in LECs [29]. In this study, we reported for the first time that ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs induce VEC death via activation of FAO, thereby contributing to multiple organ injury. Consistent with the disease-specific roles and mechanisms of MDSCs [30], the pathologic effects of CD45+ EPCs are also disease-specific. This role may be specific to ACLF, as ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs alone could not induce VEC death in the absence of serum from ACLF patients, which is rich in extramitochondrial components of FAO. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to identify ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs and elucidate the role in inducing VEC death through FAO activation.

Using serum from patients with ACLF, we demonstrated that ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs induce VEC death via the activation of FAO. Under physiological conditions, VECs rely primarily on glycolysis as their energy source and preferentially undergo aerobic glycolysis (the “Warburg effect”), even in the presence of oxygen [31]. This metabolic mode enables rapid ATP production, supporting endothelial cell functions, such as migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis [32, 33]. However, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation is also crucial for their function. Studies have demonstrated that inhibition of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, particularly complex V, disrupts the nitric oxide-dependent vascular relaxation of endothelial cells [34]. ATP generated by mitochondria plays a specific role in maintaining vascular tone [34]. FAO is essential for endothelial cell migration and vascular formation, particularly by contributing to nucleotide synthesis and epigenetic modifications [35]. Glutamine metabolism is involved in angiogenesis by supporting the tricarboxylic acid cycle and biosynthetic pathways. Endothelial cells in the tumor microenvironment enhance glutamine metabolism to sustain abnormal angiogenesis [36]. In this study, we found that activation of FAO induced by CD45+ EPCs in combination with serum from patients with ACLF may trigger VEC death.

ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs utilize free fatty acids and acylcarnitines from plasma and tissue fluid to activate FAO in VECs, leading to their metabolic reprogramming. Glucose phosphorylation is the initiating step of the pentose phosphate pathway, and inhibition of hexokinase results in a marked reduction of ribose and NADPH, products of the pentose phosphate pathway, which may contribute to oxidative stress–related cell death. However, our study showed no significant decrease in NADPH levels in VECs induced by ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs. Therefore, we speculated that the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex transports H+ generated by FAO to the outer mitochondrial membrane and provides reducing equivalents to the cytoplasm through hydrogen donation by coenzyme Q, thereby preventing oxidative stress–related cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and autophagy. When hexokinase is inhibited, VECs may rely on compensatory metabolic reprogramming—including activation of glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis from amino acids and pyruvate, and activation of galactose metabolism—to offset the deficiency of glucose phosphorylation products and ribose. Furthermore, FAO activation may impair pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and disrupt the tricarboxylic acid cycle. We hypothesize that FAO-induced metabolic reprogramming is a key driver of VEC death. The specific type of cell death, however, remains to be clarified.

Anemia is a significant complication of ACLF, affecting patient outcomes and complicating clinical management [37]. Understanding the multifactorial nature of anemia in ACLF is essential for developing effective therapeutic strategies and improving the prognosis of patients with ACLF. The accumulation of CD71+ EPCs in circulation has been reported in various conditions, including anemia [16], CHB [2], HCC [14], and in neonates [38, 39]. The pathological roles of CD71+ EPCs in circulation are disease-specific and include immune suppression [15, 40] and the induction of epithelial–mesenchymal transition in endothelial cells [41]. Therapeutic approaches targeting erythrocyte development and maturation, particularly those that promote the maturation of circulating EPCs, may improve the prognosis of ACLF.

Current animal models (such as CCl4- and cecal ligation-induced liver failure models) do not fully replicate the extramedullary hematopoietic microenvironment observed in patients with HBV-ACLF. However, the role of ferroptosis in the development of CD45+ EPCs remains unclear. Further studies focusing on ferroptosis are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. In addition, metabolomic analyses of HUVECs after co-culture are required to confirm the accumulation of specific FAO intermediates. Moreover, the spatial proximity between CD45+ EPCs and vascular endothelial cells was validated only through hematoxylin–eosin staining and immunofluorescence. Whether endothelial cell injury arises from direct cellular interactions with EPCs or from their secretory profiles warrants further investigation. Future experiments should focus on protein–protein interaction detection and interaction intensity analyses for further verification. The mechanisms underlying FAO activation–induced HUVEC death also remain to be clarified. Additionally, the animal experiments examining the effects of FAO inhibitors in ACLF mice receiving adoptive transfer of CD45+ EPCs were limited to a 7-d observation period. Future studies should include larger sample sizes and assess pathological changes at multiple time points to delineate the dynamic evolution of CD45+ EPCs in vivo. Finally, the clinical utility of circulating EPCs as prognostic markers for ACLF requires validation through multicenter clinical studies with large cohorts.

Conclusion

In summary, we identified elevated levels of CD45+ EPCs in the circulation of patients with ACLF. ACLF-associated CD45+ EPCs exhibited features consistent with EDMCs, underwent ferroptosis, induced HUVEC death via the activation of FAO by MDA, and contributed to reduced survival in ACLF.
