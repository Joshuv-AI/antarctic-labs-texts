Modeling the dynamics of access to healthy food. The case of Cleveland, Ohio
Authors: John Pastor Ansah, Robinson Salazar Rua, Atharva Patki, Owusua Yamoah, Sanjay Rajagopalan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Food insecurity in Cleveland, Ohio, reflects structural inequities that shape urban food systems in the United States and beyond. Historical legacies of racial segregation, chronic underinvestment in marginalized neighborhoods, and persistent wage stagnation have contributed to uneven access to affordable, nutritious food. Understanding these disparities requires a systemic perspective that captures the dynamic interactions among economic, social, and market forces shaping food access and consumption.This study employs a system dynamics modeling approach, developed through a Group Model Building (GMB) process with low-income African American residents, to explore the drivers of access to healthy food and assess the long-term effects of policy scenarios. Simulation results suggest a substantial increase in households with limited access to nutritious food, driven primarily by declining affordability and rising food costs. Within the model, these trends are associated with greater availability of unhealthy food retailers, increased reliance on food banks and pantries, and continued closure of full-service grocery stores, contributing to a worsening food environment.The findings suggest that narrowly targeted interventions may have limited long-term effects unless combined with broader strategies addressing household purchasing power and local food retail conditions. Comprehensive, multisectoral policies that combine income-growth strategies, market incentives, and food-system interventions may yield greater improvements in modeled outcomes. These results should be interpreted as exploratory, model-based insights to inform policy discussions rather than predictions of actual policy effects.

Introduction

Food insecurity remains one of the most pressing public health challenges globally, affecting millions of individuals and families and shaping health outcomes across the life course (Gundersen & Ziliak, 2015). Food insecurity is commonly defined as the lack of consistent access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food necessary for an active and healthy life (Coleman-Jensen et al., 2022). Beyond the immediate implications for nutrition, food insecurity has far-reaching consequences for physical health, economic stability, and social well-being. Individuals experiencing food insecurity face elevated risks of chronic conditions such as obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, contributing to reinforcing cycles of poverty and poor health (Seligman & Schillinger, 2010). These dynamics are particularly pronounced in urban settings, where socio-economic inequalities, historical disinvestment, and structural barriers to food access often converge (Walker et al., 2010).

In the United States, food insecurity continues to disproportionately affect low-income and minority populations. National estimates indicate that approximately 10.2% of U.S. households experienced food insecurity in 2021, with substantially higher rates among Black and Hispanic households (Coleman-Jensen et al., 2022). Urban centers frequently exhibit the most visible manifestations of these disparities. Cleveland, Ohio, illustrates the complex interaction between economic disadvantage and food access. With a poverty rate exceeding 30%, more than double the national average, Cleveland ranks among the poorest large cities in the United States (Walker et al., 2010). At the same time, substantial portions of the population experience limited access to healthy food due to the spatial distribution of food retail outlets, the decline of full-service grocery stores in some neighborhoods, and the growing presence of convenience stores and other outlets offering predominantly unhealthy food options (Beaulac et al., 2009).

Cleveland therefore provides an analytically useful context for examining the structural dynamics of urban food insecurity. The city reflects many of the conditions observed in post-industrial urban areas across the United States, including concentrated poverty, neighborhood disinvestment, and uneven food retail landscapes. Historical patterns of segregation, economic restructuring, and policy decisions have contributed to spatial inequalities in access to resources, including healthy food (Walker et al., 2012). As a result, nutritious food is often less accessible and less affordable in disadvantaged neighborhoods. These inequities disproportionately affect vulnerable populations—including children, older adults, and individuals with chronic health conditions—who are particularly sensitive to the health consequences of poor diet quality (Walker et al., 2010).

Although a substantial body of research has documented associations between poverty, food retail environments, and diet-related health outcomes, much of the existing literature examines these factors independently. Comparatively less attention has been devoted to understanding how economic conditions, food retail markets, and emergency food systems interact dynamically over time to shape food access. Food environments evolve through feedback processes linking household income, food affordability, retail market viability, and reliance on emergency food assistance.

Addressing these interconnected dynamics requires analytical approaches capable of capturing system complexity and feedback processes. System dynamics modeling provides a useful framework for examining how social, economic, and environmental factors interact over time to influence population outcomes (Hirsch et al., 2007). By representing feedback loops, delays, and nonlinear relationships, system dynamics models allow researchers to explore how changes in one part of a system may propagate through other components and influence long-term outcomes. In the context of food systems, this approach enables examination of how factors such as income levels, food prices, retail market viability, government assistance programs, and health outcomes interact and evolve over time (Hirsch et al., 2007). This study contributes to literature by developing a system dynamics model that explicitly represents the interacting feedback mechanisms shaping access to healthy food. The model captures key relationships among household income, food affordability, grocery store viability, the expansion of unhealthy food outlets, and reliance on emergency food infrastructure. While the model is parameterized using data from Cleveland, Ohio, the underlying structure reflects mechanisms common to many urban food systems characterized by economic inequality and uneven food retail environments. The resulting framework provides a tool for exploring how structural economic conditions and food system dynamics interact to influence long-term patterns of food access and for examining potential policy interventions aimed at improving food security.

Background

Cleveland provides a compelling case study for examining the structural dynamics of food access and health inequities. Like many post-industrial U.S. cities, Cleveland faces persistent socioeconomic challenges alongside substantial public health disparities. The city’s food environment is characterized by pronounced inequalities in access to nutritious food, with low-income and minority neighborhoods disproportionately affected. Many of these communities have historically been classified as food deserts—areas with limited access to affordable, healthy food retail outlets. In such environments, residents often rely on convenience stores, dollar stores, and fast-food establishments that primarily offer calorie-dense but nutrient-poor foods (Walker et al., 2010). These food environment constraints contribute to unhealthy dietary patterns and are associated with elevated rates of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, reinforcing cycles of health disadvantage (Seligman & Schillinger, 2010).

The relationship between food access and health outcomes is further shaped by broader social determinants of health that influence household decision-making and resource availability. Households with limited financial resources frequently face difficult trade-offs between affordability and nutritional quality, often prioritizing lower-cost foods to meet immediate consumption needs (Gundersen et al., 2011). These decisions may be reinforced by perceived extrinsic mortality risks—situations in which individuals believe that life outcomes are strongly determined by forces beyond their control—which can reduce incentives to invest in long-term health behaviors (Pepper et al., 2014). Structural barriers such as transportation limitations may also restrict access to full-service grocery stores that offer fresh produce, even when households possess the financial resources to purchase healthier foods (Coleman-Jensen et al., 2022). Together, these factors illustrate how economic constraints, behavioral responses, and built-environment conditions interact to shape food access in disadvantaged communities.

Food access is also closely connected to broader patterns of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) risk, including hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease (Hammond & Dubé, 2012). Poor diet quality represents a major upstream determinant of these conditions, linking food environments to long-term population health outcomes. Although CKM outcomes are not explicitly modeled in the present study, access to healthy food represents a critical structural driver of dietary behavior and thus an important upstream determinant of cardiometabolic health. By examining how economic conditions and food system dynamics influence access to healthy food, this study contributes to a growing body of research aimed at understanding the structural drivers of cardiometabolic health disparities.

Methods

This study employs a system dynamics (SD) modeling approach (Ansah et al., 2025; Cavana & Mares, 2004; Homer & Hirsch, 2006; Meadows, 2008; Sterman, 2000; Trochim et al., 2006) to analyze the factors influencing food systems and access to healthy food in Cleveland, Ohio. The model was developed based on insights from a Group Model Building (GMB) workshop, initially designed to examine the onset and management of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors among low-income African Americans. A qualitative system dynamics model was developed during the workshop to represent multiple interacting sectors that contribute to CVD risk factors. For this study, we extracted and refined the food sector from that qualitative system dynamics model to develop a quantitative system dynamics model of the feedback mechanisms influencing access to healthy food. Insights generated during the group model-building workshops—including stakeholder narratives, facilitated discussions, and participatory causal loop diagramming—were used to identify key variables, feedback relationships, and system boundaries that informed the structure of the quantitative system dynamics model. This study builds on prior work in participatory and community-based system dynamics by integrating stakeholder-informed system mapping with long-term simulation of food access dynamics in an urban food system.

The GMB approach facilitated stakeholder participation in developing the quantitative system dynamics model (Vennix et al., 1996; Richardson & Andersen, 1995; Rouwette et al., 2002; Hovmand, 2014). The research team from Case Western Reserve University and University Hospitals Medical Center conducted two full-day workshops in Cleveland on November 18, 2023, and February 17, 2024, engaging 20 stakeholders, including community leaders, healthcare providers, residents, and researchers. These sessions focused on identifying the primary drivers of access to healthy food, developing behavior-over-time graphs, and validating the qualitative system dynamics model. Stakeholders also contributed insights into potential interventions to improve access to healthy food. Ethical approval for this study, including the stakeholder engagement activities, was obtained from the University Hospitals Cleveland Institutional Review Board (IRB). All participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and participants received remuneration for their time and contributions to the workshops.

Model structure and assumptions

The simulation model conceptualizes Cleveland’s population as two primary stocks: (1) the population with adequate access to healthy food and (2) the population with limited access to healthy food. These two stocks capture the fundamental dynamics of food access in the city and allow the model to represent transitions between food security and insecurity over time. The population with adequate access to healthy food is defined as individuals or households that can consistently obtain nutritious, affordable, and culturally appropriate food without significant barriers. In contrast, the population with limited access to healthy food refers to individuals or households who regularly face barriers to accessing nutritious, affordable, and culturally appropriate food. The size of each stock is influenced by demographic processes, including births, deaths, and net migration, as well as behavioral and structural transitions between the two states. Specifically, the stock of people with limited access to healthy food increases through births into food-insecure households, net migration of populations facing limited access, and transitions from adequate to limited access. It decreases due to deaths in this population and transitions into the adequate-access category. Conversely, the stock of those with adequate access increases through births into food-secure households, net in-migration, and transitions from limited to adequate access, while decreasing through deaths and transitions to limited access. Birth and death flows are determined by their respective crude rates and total population, while net migration is calibrated using estimated net migration rates. Transitions between the two access states are central to the model structure and are influenced by several key determinants. Movement from limited to adequate access is driven by a reference transition rate that is modified by factors such as the quality of the food environment, the level of nutrition education, and access to healthy food through food banks and pantries. Similarly, transitions from adequate to limited access are influenced by a reference rate and the state of the food environment. Improving the food environment both accelerates the transition from limited to adequate access and slows the reverse transition.

Food Environment Dynamics: The food environment is represented in the model as a stock that captures the broader context shaping food access. It reflects the combined influence of food access, the availability of unhealthy food, and population preferences for healthy food. Improvements in food access and shifts in preferences toward healthier food options enhance the food environment, whereas increased availability of unhealthy food exerts a negative effect. Food access is itself a function of three primary factors: food affordability, transportation access, and the average distance to healthy food outlets. As affordability and transportation improve, food access increases; however, a greater distance to healthy food negatively impacts access.

Food Affordability and Income Dynamics: Food affordability, measured as a stock, is defined as the proportion of household income spent on food, a commonly used indicator of food’s economic accessibility (Lee et al., 2013). Food affordability was operationalized as the proportion of households spending ≤12% of their income on food. This threshold was used as a modeling reference point reflecting typical food expenditure shares in high-income countries (approximately 7–12% of disposable income), rather than as a formal definition of food insecurity (FAO et al., 2023; Hirvonen et al., 2020; World Bank, 2019). The model calculates food affordability by capturing the interaction between two key components: the cost of healthy food and average household income. The cost of healthy food increases with inflation and decreases when food prices fall. Cost estimates are based on USDA data for a household size of 2.4 persons, representing an average family. Average household income is modeled as a stock that grows through wage increases and Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefits for low-income families. The income distribution is disaggregated into twelve income groups (Supplementary file S1: income distribution) using data specific to Cleveland’s population, allowing the model to capture income heterogeneity and its influence on food security.

Unhealthy Food Availability and Food Bank Dynamics: The model posits that declining food affordability increases the availability of unhealthy food, represented as a stock that responds to changes in affordability and the prevalence of food banks and pantries. In the model, limited access to healthy food is represented as an aggregate stock that captures the proportion of households facing barriers to obtaining nutritious food. This indicator reflects multiple dimensions of food access rather than representing a single component. The aggregated formulation was adopted to maintain model tractability while capturing the overall system-level dynamics influencing food access. Although the model explicitly represents mechanisms related to food affordability and retail availability, other dimensions of food access—such as cultural food preferences and neighborhood-level heterogeneity—are not explicitly modeled and are implicitly represented within this composite measure. As food becomes less affordable, the number of food pantries is expected to increase to meet rising demand for emergency food assistance. However, the model hypothesizes that this growth can have unintended consequences for the retail food landscape. An increase in free food distribution may reduce customer traffic at grocery stores that sell healthy food, leading to financial strain and eventual closures. As grocery stores close, the average distance to healthy food outlets increases, further undermining food access and deepening food insecurity. This dynamic illustrates a reinforcing feedback loop in which declining affordability contributes to the proliferation of food pantries, which in turn undermines grocery store viability, leading to greater spatial barriers to healthy food access.

Grocery Store Dynamics: Grocery stores are modeled as a stock with an outflow due to closures. Closure rates are determined by average customer volume, food affordability, and the number of food banks and pantries in the area. Customer volume is calculated based on total population, the number of grocery stores, and the proportion of the population that shops at dollar stores or online retailers. As affordability declines and food bank use rises, grocery store closures become more likely. These closures feed back into the system by increasing the average distance to healthy food, thereby reducing food access and further deteriorating the food environment.

Workshop participants included community stakeholders involved in food access initiatives, public health practitioners, and representatives from organizations working within the local food system. While participants did not formally represent grocery store corporations, many had direct experience working with food retailers, food banks, and community food access programs, and these perspectives informed discussions of grocery store dynamics and food retail conditions during the group model-building sessions.

Dual Role of Food Banks and Pantries: The model explicitly incorporates both the positive and negative roles of food banks and pantries in the local food system. On one hand, these services increase access to healthy food for food-insecure households and deliver nutritional education that facilitates transitions from limited to adequate access. On the other hand, their growth — particularly if not accompanied by parallel support for retail food infrastructure — can accelerate grocery store closures and contribute to the spatial expansion of food deserts. The net impact of food banks and pantries on the food system depends on the balance between these opposing forces. When adequately funded and strategically integrated into a broader food system plan, they can enhance access and support transitions to food security. However, if they primarily operate as reactive measures to reduce affordability without structural income and market interventions, they risk perpetuating the very dynamics they aim to alleviate.

Access to healthy food model.

Some parameters (e.g., initial food access, reference nutrition education, the fraction of healthy food from food banks, and the effectiveness of nutrition education) were initialized using reference values that represent baseline system conditions. Such normalization is common in system dynamics models when variables represent conceptual constructs or when empirical estimates are unavailable (Sterman, 2000).

Model validation and calibration: The structure behavior test (Barlas & Kanar, 1999) was used to validate the model. During the structure test, the model structure was presented to researchers working with food systems in the US and in Cleveland to verify the model’s assumptions regarding causal relationships, feedback structure, and level aggregation. It is important to emphasize that the model’s initial structure was developed with community stakeholders during a group model-building workshop. The food access model presented herein is consistent with available evidence on US food systems. Thus, the model is grounded in current knowledge and available evidence on access to healthy food in Cleveland, Ohio. For the behavior test, the simulated behavior of key variables (i.e., population with limited access to healthy food, population with adequate access to healthy food, food environment, and availability of unhealthy food) was compared with available data. The result suggests that the simulated outcomes compare favorably with the actual data, demonstrating that the model performs credibly for the visual fit test.

Table 1 shows the parameters calibrated among the list of model inputs. We used the automated calibration function in Vensim (a system dynamics software package) for our calibration (Ventana Systems, Inc, 2022). First, we identified the outcome variables to match the available historical data. The variables with available historical data were population with limited access to healthy food, population with adequate access to healthy food, food environment, and availability of unhealthy food. We identified model parameters with unknown values and their plausible ranges. The parameters we varied and their range are provided in Table 1. Lastly, we allowed the Vensim software’s global optimization algorithm to adjust the unknown parameters to automatically match the specified historical data. Supplementary Material S3 compares simulated outcomes with available empirical food system indicators and reports model goodness-of-fit using Theil inequality statistics. The sensitivity analysis varied ±25% of the following parameters: reference transition from adequate to limited food, reference transition from limited to adequate food, net migration rate, crude birth rate, initial average income, and fraction of SNAP beneficiaries enrolled in double-up program. These parameters were selected because they directly influence the principal model outcomes through three central mechanisms: population movement between states with adequate and limited access, demographic change, and household purchasing power. The two transition parameters determine the rate at which households move between adequate and limited access to healthy food, while net migration and crude birth rate influence the size and composition of the modeled population over time. Initial average income was included because food affordability is a central driver of the model’s simulated food access dynamics, and SNAP double-up enrollment was included because it represents the primary uptake assumption for one of the modeled food assistance interventions. Together, these parameters capture uncertainty in the main pathways most directly linked to the model’s core outcomes and policy scenarios.

Other structurally important parameters, including the elasticity of food affordability with respect to food banks, the adjustment time for food affordability, the effect of food banks and pantries on grocery store closures, the effectiveness of nutrition education, and assumptions regarding the fraction of healthy food obtained through food banks, were not included in the primary sensitivity analysis. These parameters were treated as structural or reference assumptions because empirical estimates were limited and because the present analysis focused on uncertainty in the most direct population-flow, demographic, income, and intervention-uptake parameters. We recognize, however, that these parameters may influence simulated outcomes, particularly those related to food bank expansion, grocery store closures, and the food environment index. Therefore, the uncertainty intervals should be interpreted as reflecting uncertainty in the selected parameter set rather than the full range of structural uncertainty in the model. Future work could extend the sensitivity analysis to include these additional parameters as more empirical evidence becomes available.

Intervention evaluation

The intervention scenarios modeled in this study represent stylized policy levers rather than detailed operational implementations of specific programs. In system dynamics modeling, simplified policy representations are commonly used to explore how changes in key structural drivers may influence system behavior over time. Accordingly, the modeled interventions—including income changes, SNAP double-up programs, food bank funding, and combined policy scenarios—are designed to represent directional shifts in underlying system conditions rather than precise programmatic rollouts. The assumed implementation year reflects a scenario start point used to examine potential system responses, rather than an assumption of instantaneous real-world policy adoption. These stylized scenarios allow the model to explore potential long-term dynamics while acknowledging that real-world implementation would involve gradual changes, institutional constraints, and behavioral responses not explicitly represented in the model. The chosen time horizon (2015—2045) was intended to allow sufficient time for structural dynamics in the food system to unfold, as these processes typically evolve gradually over extended periods.

Business-as-usual: This scenario assumes that current trends in access to healthy food remain unchanged over the simulation time, although future policies may affect these parameters. This hypothetical experiment is unlikely in an ever-changing food environment, but it is included as a reference point for evaluating alternative interventions.

Income intervention: Under the income scenario, we explored the impact of a substantial and moderate increase in average family income on access to healthy food. The substantial change in average family income assumed a skewed income distribution towards the high-income bracket, while the moderate increase assumed a normal income distribution. Appendix A shows the distribution of income used under the income scenarios compared to the business-as-usual. Under this scenario, we assumed that the average family income distribution would improve due to educational and economic policies implemented at the federal and state levels. Given the structural nature of these policies, such interventions are likely to impact income distribution with a significant time lag; however, in the model, income distribution was assumed to change in 2026.

Healthy food incentive intervention: This intervention is administered by the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA), the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), and many state and local governments. This initiative provides grants, low-interest loans, and tax incentives to support grocery stores, farmers’ markets, and food retailers in underserved communities. It also encourages public-private partnerships to improve food access and economic development in food deserts. Under this intervention, we assumed that grants and low-interest loans would be provided to grocery stores in at-risk communities to reduce grocery store closures.

Supplementary Nutritional Assistance Program (SNAP) and double-up intervention: SNAP provides financial assistance for low-income households to buy groceries. This intervention aims to match SNAP benefits when used to purchase fresh fruits and vegetables at farmers’ markets and participating grocery stores. Under this intervention, we assumed that 60% of SNAP recipients use this initiative to increase the quantity of healthy food the household can buy using their SNAP benefits.

Food Banks/pantries intervention: The intervention includes the Emergency Food Assistance Program (TEFAP) a federal program that provides food and administrative support to food banks and pantries, and many state and local initiatives to fund food banks/pantries. It helps supplement the diets of low-income Americans by distributing USDA-purchased food or using Community Development Block Grants to fund food pantries. In the simulation model, this intervention is implemented by doubling funding for food banks/pantries to ensure that households relying on them consistently receive healthy food.

Comprehensive intervention: This intervention simultaneously implements income, SNAP double-up, grocery store incentives, and food bank/pantries funding interventions to evaluate the combined impact on access to healthy food.

Results

To aid interpretation, outcomes are reported using different scales depending on the modeled construct. Food affordability is reported as the percentage of households spending 12% or less of their income on food. Limited access to healthy food is reported as the percentage of residents with limited access. Grocery stores and food banks/pantries are reported as counts. The food environment and unhealthy food availability are dimensionless model indices, with higher food environment values representing more favorable conditions and higher unhealthy food availability values representing greater availability of unhealthy food outlets.

Business-as-usual: Under current trends, the model projects substantial deterioration in food affordability and the overall food environment in Cleveland (Fig. 2). The proportion of households spending ≤12% of income on food—a proxy for affordability—is projected to decline from 27.4% (95% uncertainty interval [UI] 20.0–35.4) in 2025 to 15.6% (10.1–23.6) by 2045. Correspondingly, Cleveland’s food environment index declines by approximately 53%, from 6.57 (95% UI 5.98–6.73) to 3.08 (1.84–4.88). Over the same period, the prevalence of residents with limited access to healthy food increased from 7.3% (95% UI 7.1–7.9) to 17.7% (10.6–27.4). These trends coincide with increasing availability of unhealthy food outlets (48.5% to 61.7%), declining grocery stores (21 to 8), and growing reliance on emergency food infrastructure, with food banks and pantries increasing from 151 (95% UI 150–152) to 273 (259–289).Fig. 2Projected food system outcomes under alternative intervention scenarios, 2014–2045.

Income-based interventions: Income growth scenarios substantially improve projected food access outcomes. Under a substantial income increase, food affordability in 2045 rises to 46.1% (95% UI 38.0–56.5), a 195% improvement relative to business-as-usual, while a moderate income increase raises affordability to 23.9% (95% UI 15.7–34.5), a 53% improvement. These gains translate into broader improvements in the food environment. The food environment index increases to 8.81 (95% UI 5.48–13.61) under the substantial income scenario and to 4.41 (95% UI 2.65–7.05) under the moderate scenario—186% and 43% higher than business-as-usual, respectively. Correspondingly, the prevalence of limited access to healthy food declines to 6.1% (95% UI 3.5–10.0) and 12.6% (95% UI 7.2–20.1), representing reductions of 66% and 29%. These improvements are accompanied by structural changes in the retail food environment. The availability of unhealthy food outlets declines to 38.8% under the substantial income scenario and to 55.0% under the moderate scenario (vs 61.7% under business-as-usual). Grocery stores increase from 8 to 10 and 9, respectively, while reliance on emergency food infrastructure declines modestly, with food banks and pantries decreasing to 249 and 263.

Healthy Food Incentives: Retail-focused healthy food policies primarily affect the food retail structure rather than affordability. Food affordability in 2045 remains similar to the base-case projection at 14.7% (95% UI 9.9–20.2), while the food environment index increases modestly to 3.88 (95% UI 2.37–6.01), representing a 26% improvement relative to business-as-usual. These improvements are driven by the expansion of healthy food retail. Grocery stores increase substantially to 21 (95% UI 18–24), compared with 8 under business-as-usual, while the availability of unhealthy food outlets declines modestly to 57.6% (95% UI 53.0–61.7). The prevalence of limited access declines to 13.4% (95% UI 7.9–21.3), and the number of food banks and pantries decreases to 183 (95% UI 165–203).

Food Assistance Interventions: SNAP Double-up and Food Bank Funding: Food assistance interventions produce limited structural improvements relative to the business-as-usual trajectory. Under the SNAP Double Up scenario, food affordability remains at 15.6% (95% UI 10.1–23.6), the food environment index remains at 3.08 (95% UI 1.84–4.88), and the prevalence of limited food access remains at 17.7% (95% UI 10.6–27.4). The retail food environment is largely unchanged, with unhealthy food outlets at 61.7%, grocery stores at 8 (95% UI 7–10), and food banks and pantries at 273 (95% UI 259–289). Increasing food bank funding modestly reduces limited food access to 14.6% (95% UI 8.3–23.6) but does not improve underlying food system conditions. Food affordability remains unchanged at 15.6% (95% UI 10.1–23.6), while the food environment index declines slightly to 2.83 (95% UI 1.68–4.49). Structural indicators continue to deteriorate, with unhealthy food outlets increasing to 64.1% (95% UI 58.2%–69.5%), grocery stores declining to 5 (95% UI 4–7), and food banks and pantries increasing to 279 (95% UI 263–297).

Comprehensive Intervention: The combined intervention scenario produces the largest improvements across all modeled outcomes. Food affordability increases to 44.7% (95% UI 36.1–52.5) by 2045, representing a 187% improvement relative to business-as-usual. The food environment index increases to 10.51 (95% UI 6.60–15.98), a 242% improvement relative to the base case. These improvements substantially reduce food access inequities. The prevalence of limited access to healthy food declines to 3.1% (95% UI 1.7–5.2), representing an 82% reduction relative to the base case. At the same time, unhealthy food outlets decline to 37.5% (95% UI 31.6–43.2), grocery stores increase to 20 (95% UI 16–24), and food banks and pantries decline to 170 (95% UI 154–187). Discussion

The simulation results suggest a concerning potential trajectory for Cleveland’s food system, highlighting how structural economic conditions, market dynamics, and policy environments may interact to shape food access outcomes over time. Under the modeled status quo scenario, the number of households with limited access to healthy food is projected to increase substantially, driven primarily by declining food affordability. As food becomes less affordable within the model simulations, unhealthy food retailers are projected to become more prevalent, reliance on food banks and pantries increases, and the continued closure of grocery stores offering nutritious options contributes to the expansion of food deserts. Together, these simulated dynamics suggest a potential worsening of Cleveland’s food landscape with implications for health equity, neighborhood vitality, and long-term socioeconomic well-being.

A central driver of this projected deterioration in the model is persistently low household income relative to rising food costs. Although the United States is a high-income nation, Cleveland’s median household income remains well below national and state averages, with nearly half of households earning $40,000 or less annually and two-thirds earning less than $60,000. These income constraints may force many households to allocate a relatively large share of their income to food expenditures, potentially increasing vulnerability to food insecurity. Within the simulation structure, reinforcing feedback loops (R1 and R2) (see Table 2) illustrate how worsening affordability may reduce access to nutritious food, which in turn can degrade the local food environment and potentially contribute to adverse health outcomes. The model also illustrates how declining food affordability may influence the food system through additional feedback processes. Feedback loop R3 (see Table 22) suggests that worsening affordability may increase reliance on food banks and pantries while simultaneously undermining the financial viability of grocery stores serving low-income communities. However, this relationship may be localized to Cleveland’s food retail environment and should be tested empirically in other urban contexts or explored in future model extensions. As stores close and the average distance to healthy food increases, access to nutritious foods may become further constrained. Although feedback loops R4 and R5 suggest that emergency food services and nutrition support programs may partially mitigate these dynamics by improving short-term food access, the simulation indicates that such interventions alone may be insufficient to reverse the broader structural processes driving the modeled trends.

Several insights emerge from these simulations that may be relevant to policy discussions. First, the model suggests that food affordability and access could worsen under current trends, with a substantial proportion of Cleveland households projected to experience barriers to obtaining nutritious food by 2045. Importantly, these simulated trends appear to reflect not only the distribution of food retailers but also broader structural factors shaping household purchasing power and neighborhood food environments.

Second, within the model scenarios examined, income-related interventions appear to have the greatest potential to improve food affordability and access. Substantial increases in household income within the simulation are associated with improvements in food affordability, reductions in the density of unhealthy food retailers, greater stability in grocery store presence, and improvements in the modeled food environment. These findings suggest that economic conditions—such as wages, employment opportunities, and income supports—may play an important role in shaping food system outcomes and could be an important component of broader food security strategies.

Third, the simulation results indicate that targeted food-sector interventions alone may be insufficient to substantially alter long-term trends in food insecurity. Programs such as SNAP double-up initiatives, food bank funding, and grocery store incentives appear to provide meaningful short-term improvements in food access within the model, but they do not fully address the affordability constraints that drive many of the reinforcing feedback processes. As such, these interventions may help slow the growth of food insecurity rather than fundamentally alter the trajectory projected under the status quo.

Finally, the simulation experiments suggest that multi-component strategies may have the greatest potential to influence long-term system behavior. Policies that combine income improvements with food assistance programs, incentives for maintaining grocery store presence, and investments in community food infrastructure are associated with the largest reductions in limited access to healthy food within the model simulations. These results suggest that coordinated action across economic, social, and food system domains may be important for addressing complex drivers of food insecurity.

Overall, the findings highlight that food insecurity in Cleveland appears to be shaped by a set of interconnected structural and economic processes. While downstream support programs play an important role in alleviating immediate needs, the simulation suggests that broader structural factors—particularly income constraints—may be critical in shaping long-term food system dynamics. Addressing food insecurity may therefore require strategies that combine economic policies to improve household purchasing power with targeted efforts to strengthen local food environments.

As with all simulation studies, these findings should be interpreted as scenario-based insights rather than causal estimates. The model is intended to explore potential system dynamics and interactions between economic conditions, food environments, and food access, rather than to generate precise forecasts of real-world outcomes. Accordingly, the intervention scenarios should be understood as stylized policy experiments illustrating potential system responses, rather than predictions of the exact effects or feasibility of specific policy programs. In practice, policy implementation over such time horizons would also be shaped by political cycles, fiscal constraints, and institutional changes, which are not explicitly modeled here but are important considerations when interpreting the long-run scenarios.

Policy implications

The findings of this study suggest that policy discussions about food insecurity and limited access to healthy food may benefit from moving beyond isolated programmatic responses toward more integrated strategies that address the structural drivers of food affordability and access (Gundersen & Ziliak, 2015; Loopstra & Tarasuk, 2013). In particular, the simulation highlights the potential importance of economic conditions in shaping outcomes in food systems. Policies that influence household income—such as wage growth, workforce development programs, social protection policies, or income support programs—may therefore play an important role in broader food security strategies (FAO et al., 2023; Gundersen & Ziliak, 2015).

Income-based interventions could take several forms, including unconditional transfers, conditional income supports tied to employment or program participation, and redistributive policies implemented through taxation or social protection systems (Banerjee et al., 2019; Bastagli et al., 2016). Unconditional income transfers may be particularly relevant in contexts of structural deprivation because they provide households with flexibility to allocate resources according to their immediate needs, including food purchases (Bastagli et al., 2016). Evidence from several real-world income transfer programs in the United States suggests potential benefits of such approaches. For example, the Alaska Permanent Fund dividend provides annual unconditional payments to residents and has been associated with reductions in poverty and improvements in household financial stability (Goldsmith, 2012; Jones & Marinescu, 2018). Similarly, research on tribal casino revenue disbursements has documented improvements in child well-being, educational attainment, and long-term health outcomes following unconditional income transfers to households (Akee et al., 2010; Costello et al., 2003. More recent municipal guaranteed-income pilots, including the minimum-income program in Gary, Indiana, suggest that modest, unconditional payments may improve financial stability and reduce food insecurity among participating households (West et al., 2021).

The design of income interventions may also influence their effectiveness. Conditional programs, such as work-linked income supplements or targeted food assistance programs, can help direct resources to specific populations but may introduce administrative barriers that limit access (Currie & Gahvari, 2008). In contrast, unconditional transfers may reduce administrative complexity and potentially address what has been described as “extrinsic mortality risk,” whereby individuals experiencing structural deprivation prioritize immediate survival needs over long-term health investments (Nettle et al., 2017; Pepper & Nettle, 2014). By reducing financial uncertainty and improving perceived future security, such policies may enable households to make more consistent investments in health-promoting behaviors, such as purchasing nutritious foods (Banerjee et al., 2019).

At the same time, the simulation results suggest that income improvements alone may not fully resolve food access challenges if structural conditions within local food retail environments remain unchanged. For example, increased reliance on emergency food infrastructure without parallel efforts to sustain full-service grocery retailers could unintentionally weaken local food markets (Ver Ploeg et al., 2009; Walker et al., 2010). Declining population, persistent poverty, and the proliferation of dollar stores and emergency food services may reduce the customer base needed to sustain grocery stores that offer nutritious foods, contributing to further store closures and expanding food deserts (Walker et al., 2010; USDA ERS, 2019). Policymakers may therefore wish to consider strategies that help attract and sustain grocery retailers in underserved areas, including financial incentives, zoning reforms, transportation investments, and community ownership models (Giang et al., 2008).

More broadly, the model highlights the importance of multi-sector coordination. The reinforcing processes influencing food insecurity—including labor market conditions, food prices, retail dynamics, and health outcomes—extend beyond the food sector itself (FAO et al., 2023; Gundersen & Ziliak, 2015). Policy responses may benefit from coordinated action across multiple domains, including labor markets, housing policy, transportation systems, social protection programs, and public health initiatives (FAO et al., 2023). Policies that reduce income inequality and strengthen household purchasing power may also help stabilize the broader economic conditions shaping neighborhood food environments (Loopstra & Tarasuk, 2013).

From a public health perspective, these dynamics may have important implications. Diet quality is strongly associated with the development of chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease (Afshin et al., 2019; Mozaffarian et al., 2018). If trends toward declining food affordability persist, increases in food insecurity could contribute to a greater burden of diet-related chronic diseases, potentially widening existing health inequities in cities such as Cleveland (Gundersen & Ziliak, 2015; Seligman & Schillinger, 2010).

Overall, the simulation findings suggest that integrated policy strategies may have greater potential to influence food system outcomes than isolated interventions (FAO et al., 2023). Within the scenarios examined, approaches that combine income improvements, continued food assistance, incentives for grocery store retention, and investments in community food infrastructure produced the largest improvements in food access in the model. Such integrated strategies may help address both the economic and structural factors shaping food insecurity and could represent a promising direction for future policy development. However, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory, model-based insights rather than direct evidence of real-world policy effects. Although income-related interventions appear influential in the model scenarios, their implementation is subject to political, fiscal, and temporal constraints, and their feasibility and effects may vary across contexts (Bastagli et al., 2016). In addition, it is also important to recognize the critical role that food banks and pantries play in addressing immediate food needs and supporting community resilience (Feeding America, 2023). The purpose of the model is not to diminish the importance of these institutions but to explore how broader structural conditions may influence long-term patterns of food access.

The model represents populations at an aggregate level to capture system-level dynamics, and limited access to healthy food is therefore represented as a binary stock rather than being disaggregated across demographic or neighborhood subgroups. Although community stakeholders—including African American residents from affected neighborhoods—participated in the model development process, the current model structure does not explicitly represent subgroup heterogeneity. Future work could extend the model to incorporate racial, socioeconomic, gender, age, or neighborhood-level differences through disaggregated or spatial modeling approaches.

One promising direction for advancing this work is integrating microsimulation approaches to examine the distributional impacts of policy interventions. While the present system dynamics model captures population-level feedback processes linking income, food affordability, and food system dynamics, microsimulation models can represent household-level heterogeneity and evaluate how policies such as cash transfers, wage changes, or taxation reforms may affect different socioeconomic groups (Li & O’Donoghue, 2013). Combining system dynamics with microsimulation could therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of both the structural dynamics and distributional consequences of policies aimed at reducing food insecurity.
