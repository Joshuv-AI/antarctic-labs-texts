Is dancing with care a possible solution to problematic smartphone use among middle-aged and older adults? A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yicheng Xu, Lin Yuan, Jia Xu, Chun Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global rise of problematic smartphone use has resulted in economic, health, and cognitive losses among middle-aged and older adults. Studies on the influencing factors of problematic smartphone use in this population have been conducted from an individual perspective; few have explored the role of social interaction activities. We investigated the association of Dancing with Care (DWC) with problematic smartphone use among middle-aged and older adults in China. DWC is promoted as a group-based social interaction activity to enhance social integration in communities. It is famous for its flexible participation formation as a dancing activity based on people’s interests and emotions. Specifically, we analyzed whether it may contribute to low problematic smartphone use in this population.MethodsWe surveyed 998 residents aged 45–89 years from 10 urban and rural communities each in two cities in China from April to May 2024. DWC team attachment, problematic smartphone use, loneliness, socioeconomic status, and other variables were assessed. A moderated mediation model was used to analyze whether DWC team attachment lessened participants’ problematic smartphone use and whether an urban–rural difference existed. We also examined whether loneliness mediated the effect of DWC team attachment on problematic smartphone use.ResultsDWC team attachment was negatively associated with participants’ problematic smartphone use. Loneliness mediates the association between DWC team attachment and older participants’ problematic smartphone use. Moreover, urban and rural residence moderated the association between DWC team attachment and problematic smartphone use.ConclusionDancing with Care team attachment partially corresponded to lower problematic smartphone use and less loneliness. This effect varied between rural and urban areas, as urban participants had weaker social networks, higher information literacy, and stronger smartphone use habits compared with rural participants. This study highlights the association between a social interaction activity and problematic smartphone use among middle-aged and older adults.

Background

Problematic smartphone use (PSU) among middle-aged and older adults has emerged in many countries, including China [1–3], owing to the widespread adoption of smartphones and the Internet [4]. According to the “54th Statistical Report on the Development of China’s Internet,” the proportion of Internet users aged 50 years and older increased to 33.3%, which exceeded 366 million [5].Smartphone penetration rate is rapidly increasing among middle-aged and older adults [6]. Having a smartphone and knowing how to use it effectively implies being up to date with the latest advances, such as online consultations, health monitoring, and engaging in the digital social world [7–9].

Studies have examined problems owing to PSU among middle-aged and older adults, and reported economic losses, health damage, emotional and psychological problems, and lower quality of life [4, 10, 11]. In particular, loneliness and the desire to escape from real-life problems, such as health issues and financial pressures, are significant contributors [12, 13]. Research has also argued that social factors, such as a lack of social interaction or social exclusion [14, 15], are associated with PSU in middle-aged and older adults [4, 16]. In addition, studies propose that technical factors from smartphone designers push users toward PSU [17], as algorithms in smartphone apps provide personalized recommendations based on their targeted interests and habits [11, 18, 19]. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the association between daily social interaction activities and PSU is lacking and requires urgent exploration. Therefore, we sought to fill this gap via an investigation of how a popular social interaction activity, “Dancing with Care” (DWC), impacts PSU and how this effect differs between rural and urban participants, especially among middle-aged and older adults.

DWC is promoted as a social interaction activity to enhance social integration in communities [20]. Furthermore, it is famous for its flexible participation formation as a dancing activity based on people’s interests and emotions. All DWC participants are taught to dance, share dancing experiences, and interact with each other [21, 22]. Similar to other social interaction activities, participants in DWC are invited to join dance events to broaden their social network [23]. However, unlike other social interaction activities, non-dance activities, such as shopping, traveling, caring for children, organizing parties, sharing informal family care activities, or mutual help from DWC peers, may also be organized based on any DWC team member’s needs [20]. Therefore, DWC participants experience strong team attachment, which may contribute to their social inclusion.

We argue that including DWC, particularly its team attachment factor, is essential for analyzing social interaction [20, 24, 25]; a set of PSU-related problems pertain to social function, such as loneliness [16, 26]. Our findings regarding urban–rural differences can provide a new perspective for understanding the role of social interaction in different social environments. Our results have important implications for practice, such as encouraging communities and nursing homes to alleviate the risk of excessive smartphone use by encouraging people to join DWC [20, 23], which enhances social connections and can lead to lower loneliness.

Literature review and hypotheses

DWC team attachment and PSU

The Attachment Theory posits that secure attachment promotes health and happiness by satisfying social connection and communication needs [27, 28]. Joining teams is a good way for individuals lacking social contact [29–31] to fulfill their needs. DWC, a popular social interaction activity with strong team attachment traditions in China, facilitates social interaction and interpersonal connections among middle-aged and older adults [20]. Participating individuals can better meet their interpersonal social needs through daily interactions and mutual assistance [32]. Thus, we assume that DWC participants may have lower PSU as their needs for social and interpersonal connections are fulfilled through DWC team attachment, rather than the overuse of smartphones. Therefore, those with higher DWC team attachment may not need to overuse smartphones to satisfy their social interaction needs, leading to a lower probability of experiencing PSU.

Since the activity is free of charge, signing up implies that participants find it enjoyable [20]. People join DWC as a team based on common interests, similar habits, and daily living patterns, which leads to engagement in activities rather than isolation, idle behavior, or fixation on their smartphone screens [23]. Those with DWC team attachment are willing to perform exercise activities with peers and DWC mates despite using smartphones [25]. Consequently, they are less prone to developing PSU. In addition, they are more likely to actively participate in various activities, such as group dancing, shopping with DWC mates, and cooking together, thereby enriching their lives and obtaining a sense of purpose [20]. Hence, DWC participants objectively shorten the amount of time spent on smartphones, which can diminish the likelihood of experiencing PSU [33, 34].

From a learning perspective, those with stronger DWC team attachment may have high enthusiasm and willingness to communicate with their teammates [35, 36]. We posit that a high probability of these individuals are inclined toward learning information technology skills and techniques from their teammates, which can contribute to enhancing their information literacy and lowering PSU [23]. In addition, neuroscience studies have revealed that dancing exercises can decelerate the cognitive aging process [37–39]. Hence, DWC activities may also enhance participants’ cognitive abilities, enabling better self-control and avoiding PSU [40].

Therefore, based on the literature, we propose that DWC team attachment would be significantly and negatively associated with middle-aged and older adults’ PSU, and those with higher DWC team attachment would have lower PSU levels, as shown in Fig. 1. Our first hypothesis is as follows:

Mediating role of loneliness between DWC team attachment and PSU

We further analyzed the extent to which loneliness plays a role in the association between DWC team attachment and PSU in middle-aged and older adults. Studies have suggested that team attachment is linked to better social connections, and social support contributes to a sense of belonging [41–43]. This can effectively alleviate loneliness, encourage individuals to overcome solitary feelings, and sustain their perception of being engaged in the community [44, 45]. Furthermore, those with strong team attachment have more opportunities to communicate with peers and establish or enhance their social network [46]. Therefore, middle-aged and older adults with a strong sense of team attachment may have lower loneliness [31, 47]. Research suggests that those with strong team attachment, such as DWC team attachment, exercise more actively with teammates and support each other [23, 48]. Furthermore, DWC peers may share meals, assist with young grandchildren, and share daily household tasks [23]. They may also engage in volunteer services and contribute to their community’s development in their spare time [20]. Such mutual support and volunteer activities can contribute to lower loneliness [49, 50]. Therefore, DWC team attachment may significantly impact middle-aged and older adults’ loneliness.

Loneliness, a subjective emotional experience stemming from defects or deficiencies in interpersonal relationships, is an unpleasant experience with long-lasting adverse effects on mental health [51]. It can result in negative social or avoidance behavior [52, 53]. People experiencing profound loneliness exhibit a diminished inclination to establish social connections or engage with society [54, 55]. Dearth of social activities and real-life interactions is a prevalent reality among those experiencing relatively intense loneliness [56, 57]. However, studies argue that such people may seek social contact through the virtual realm, as they harbor a fear of genuine social interactions yet yearn for social connectivity owing to their solitary state [58, 59]. Therefore, accessing social interactions via the virtual world, such as utilizing smartphones for online engagement, may emerge as their preferred option [13, 60]. In accordance with the compensatory Internet Use Theory [61], people with strong loneliness are more inclined to use their smartphones for extended periods compared with those with minimal loneliness, which could be associated with a higher likelihood of developing PSU.

In addition, people with high levels of loneliness are less likely to be supervised or receive positive guidance from others to caution against excessive smartphone use, considering their limited social contact and connections with peers [6, 58]. Family members might also have insufficient time to provide guidance, particularly for those with a smartphone overuse problem [5]. Hence, many individuals may not even recognize that they are entangled in the difficulties associated with PSU. Therefore, we posit that people with a strong sense of loneliness are more likely to experience high levels of PSU. While feeling lonely is often painful, having no one to confide in and tangible physical pain to communicate to a physician is also damaging [55]. Most lonely people are unsure of how to express their distress or feel ashamed of articulating their problems [52]. Close family members may sometimes fail to comprehend the dire circumstances these individuals are experiencing [62]. Consequently, they are more likely to overuse their smartphones in a compensatory manner to escape real problems, pain, and reality; thus, they are more susceptible to engaging in PSU, as shown in Fig. 1. Based on the above analysis, we proposed the second hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: Loneliness would mediate the association between DWC team attachment and PSU in middle-aged and older adults.

Moderating role of rural-urban residence

Rural-urban disparities are evident in China [63, 64]. People residing in different regions have varying degrees of social inclusion and lifestyles, which leads to distinct social communication patterns and activity participation [65, 66]. Smartphone usage has gained popularity owing to social welfare policy implementation [67], as smart apps can facilitate the use of social insurance policies, such as making medical care insurance and pension insurance contributions and booking an online doctor’s appointment [58]. Consequently, their prevalence has increased in both rural and urban areas [68]. The rapid development of high technology has pushed users to access, accept, get used to, and even overuse smartphones, which can lead to PSU [69].

We sought to explore whether any difference exists between rural and urban residents regarding the association between DWC team attachment and PSU. Rural residents exhibit higher familiarity with people in their social network owing to geographical proximity. In contrast, urban residents tend to have a lower degree of familiarity, as posited by the theory of stranger society [22, 66]. Familiarity within rural communities diminishes the relationship of interpersonal connections among DWC team members [70], leading to a relatively weaker influence of team attachment on PSU compared with their urban counterparts.

Logically, people in the rural community should have stronger ties and more frequent social connections besides being DWC team members. Therefore, DWC team attachment should play a weaker role in suggesting change to their use of smartphone use. However, people in the urban community experience stronger social isolation than that in rural community. Limited community ties and relevant community activities lead DWC team activity as significant moment for building social ties and exchange ideas. Since mutual support from DWC team members is particularly crucial for those in urban communities [23], DWC team attachment may have a stronger significant association to PSU in urban than rural communities.

Conversely, in rural communities, overall information literacy is relatively low [71], making it challenging for people to obtain professional support from DWC team members [63]. In fact, they may even acquire undesirable smartphone usage habits from their peers. However, urban residents have higher information literacy owing to their stronger educational background or work experiences [72]. Hence, they may be reasonably adept at using smartphones and willing to share their experiences with DWC team members [20]. Therefore, we argue that DWC team attachment may be differently associated PSU for rural and urban participants as shown in Fig. 1 and posit the third hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: The rural-urban factor would moderate the association of DWC team attachment with PSU; this effect is stronger and weaker for middle-aged and older adults in urban and rural communities, respectively.

Methods

Participants and procedures

This survey was conducted from April to May 2024 in Hefei and Wuhu, in the Yangtze River Delta region of China. A district or county was randomly selected from the two cities, along with five urban and rural communities each. Hence, 10 urban and rural communities each were selected. Well-trained university students conducted the questionnaire surveys, and 80 and 40 questionnaires were distributed in each urban and rural community, respectively; this ratio was determined based on China’s urbanization rate. Until the end of 2023, the urbanization rate of China’s permanent population was 66.16%, and questionnaires distributed in urban communities accounted for 66.67%.

Well-trained university student investigators, with support from community staff and supervisors, recruited participants from the DWC team who met the inclusion criteria: (1) not younger than 45 years, (2) had continuously participated in activities in the DWC team for the past six months, (3) lived in the community for at least nine months in the past year, (4) owned and used a smartphone for over a year, (5) able to communicate, and (6) willing to participate after they understood the research aims. We also applied two exclusion criteria during the survey process: (i) participants diagnosed with severe mental disorders or severe communication or hearing impairments that hindered normal interaction and (ii) when the participant’s spouse, children, or other family members strongly objected to their participation. The questionnaire was administered face-to-face, and most participants completed it themselves. Some participants were unable to complete the questionnaire (owing to unclear sight, limited education, etc.). Hence, the investigators orally explained the items and completed the questionnaire based on their responses. After the questionnaire was completed, all participants received a canvas bag worth approximately US$1.

Of the 1,200 questionnaires distributed, we received 1,039 questionnaires, which yielded a response rate of 86.58%. After 45 invalid questionnaires were excluded, we obtained 998 valid questionnaires, which resulted in a valid response rate of 83.17%. Criteria for considering invalid questionnaires were when questionnaires exhibited one or more of the following characteristics: (i) participants provided responses for < 50% of the items, (ii) a trend response pattern was observed, i.e., the same option was selected for 10 consecutive items, or (iii) participants failed the attention check item. For example, if participants were instructed to select “strongly disagree” for a specific item but chose another option instead.

Among the participants, there were 754 women (75.55%) and 244 men (24.45%). Participants’ age ranged from 45 to 89 years, with an average of 61.94 years (standard deviation = 7.48 years). Furthermore, 629 (63.09%) and 368 (36.91%) had a junior high school education or higher and primary school education or lower, respectively. In addition, 909 (91.08%) were married, and 355 (35.71%) reported one or more chronic diseases when surveyed.

Measurement tools

DWC team attachment

Six items were derived from the “depend” and “closeness” dimensions of Collins and Read’s scale [29]. Items were adjusted to make them suitable to measure DWC team attachment; five experts revised the items based on further discussion and focused group interviews outcomes. Item examples included: “I find it relatively easy to get close to others as I am engaged in the DWC team” and “I know that my DWC team peers will be there when I need them.” Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (very inconsistent) to 5 (very consistent). We conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to examine the DWC team attachment scale based on the data obtained from the main survey. Results demonstrated good structural validity, with the fit indices as χ²/df = 4.385, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 0.993, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) = 0.985, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.058, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) = 0.014. Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.901. Mean of the six items was used as an indicator of participants’ DWC team attachment. The higher the score, the higher the DWC team attachment.

Loneliness

Referring to the practice of the International Social Survey Programme, loneliness was measured based on three items of the UCLA Loneliness Scale (Version 3) [73]. Specifically, participants rated the frequency of their experience as “lack of companionship,” “being left out,” and “being isolated” in the past four weeks. Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). Their average score was used as an indicator of loneliness. The higher the score, the stronger the loneliness. Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.871.

PSU

We used five items from the Smartphone Addiction Scale to measure PSU [74]. Slight adjustments were made according to the participants’ living situations based on the experts’ suggestions. Example items included “Feeling pain in the wrists or at the back of the neck while using a smartphone” and “Feeling impatient and fretful when I am not holding my smartphone.” Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). We conducted a CFA to examine the structural validity of PSU scale based on the data obtained. Results demonstrated good model fit, with the following indices as χ²/df = 2.704, CFI = 0.998, TLI = 0.992, RMSEA = 0.041, and SRMR = 0.010. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.842. The mean of all items was used as an indicator of participants’ PSU. The higher the score, the higher the degree of PSU.

Other variables

A binary variable was used to measure participants’ rural-urban differences, where urban = 1 and rural = 0, all respondents had lived in urban and rural communities for at least nine months during the past 12 months. Other variables included gender (1 = man, 0 = woman); age; educational level (1 = junior high school and above, 0 = primary school and below); marital status (1 = married, 0 = other); self-rated health status on a 7-point Likert scale, where the higher the score, the higher the health level; and objective health status to assess whether participants had one or more chronic diseases as a proxy indicator (1 = yes, 0 = no). Socioeconomic status was also assessed via Griskevicius et al.’s scale [75]. It contained six items rated on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.863. The mean of all items was used as an indicator of socioeconomic status. The higher the score, the higher the socioeconomic status.

Statistical analysis

Questionnaire data were entered into an Excel document and imported into SPSS version 25.0 and Mplus version 8.11 after cross-checking. The bootstrap method used 10,000 repeated samplings, and significance level was set to 0.05. Common method bias was evaluated before data analysis [76]. First, a descriptive analysis was conducted on the core variables, with indicators that included the mean, standard deviation, and correlation coefficient among the variables. Second, the direct effect of DWC team attachment on PSU was assessed. Third, we analyzed whether loneliness played a mediating role in the association between DWC team attachment and PSU [77, 78]. Fourth, whether the rural-urban variable moderated the association between DWC team attachment and PSU was also analyzed [79]. Fifth, the constructed model’s robustness was assessed [80]. For analysis of the mediation and moderation effects, we performed mean-centered processing on all continuous variables, which included core variables, such as DWC, PUS, loneliness, and control variables, such SES, age, and self-reported health [81]. In addition, all control variables, such as gender, age, educational level, and health status, were added to the constructed models, unless otherwise specified. Furthermore, we conducted comparative analyses between the model results without control variables and those including them to assess the robustness of our core findings in the final stage of data analysis according to recommendations from methodological experts [76, 82].

Results

Common method bias test

Use of a cross-sectional design entails the risk of common method bias [83]. Hence, we used Harman’s single-factor test to assess the risk [84]. Results revealed that the factor with the largest eigenvalue extracted via exploratory factor analysis could only explain 25.078% of the total variance, below the 40% recommended by experts [85]. Therefore, our data did not have serious common method bias problems.

Simple mediation effect test

Before the simple mediation effect test, we examined whether participants’ DWC team attachment had a direct association with their PSU. Results revealed that DWC team attachment was significantly and negatively associated with PSU, B = −0.122, p <.001, 95% confidence interval (CI) obtained by 10,000 bootstrap sampling calculations = [−0.193, −0.046]. Notably, the results presented here, as well as those that follow, are based on continuously scaled variables that were mean centered throughout this study. Hence, participants with higher DWC team attachment had a lower PSU level. Thus, Hypothesis 1 was confirmed.

A simple mediation effect model was constructed with DWC team attachment, loneliness, and PSU as the independent, mediating, and outcome variables, respectively (Table 2). DWC team attachment was negatively associated with loneliness, while loneliness had a significant positive effect on PSU. Bootstrap results revealed that DWC team attachment affected PSU through loneliness, and its 95% CI [−0.081, −0.029]. Hence, loneliness played a mediating role between DWC team attachment and PSU, which confirmed Hypothesis 2.

Assessment of moderated effect

We examined whether the rural-urban variable moderated the association with DWC team attachment on PSU and whether the mediating effect of DWC team attachment on PSU through loneliness was significant when the moderating variable was included (Table 3). Results revealed that the cross-product term of DWC team attachment and rural-urban residence significantly affected PSU, B = −0.173, p =.016. The 95% CI of the interaction term [−0.314, −0.037], which indicated that the rural-urban variable moderated the association with DWC team attachment on PSU. We conducted simple slopes analyses to clearly present the moderating effect of the rural-urban variable [86]. For urban participants, DWC team attachment was significantly and negatively associated with PSU, B = −0.131, p =.005, 95% CI [−0.220, −0.037]. However, the effect of DWC team attachment on PSU was not significant for those in rural areas, B = 0.042, p =.465, 95% CI [−0.066, 0.162]. An indicator was constructed to represent the difference in the effect of DWC team attachment on PSU for participants who lived in different regions. Results revealed that the 95% CI [−0.314, −0.037]. Therefore, a significant difference was observed in the effect of DWC team attachment on PSU under the two living conditions; the rural-urban variable moderated the association with DWC team attachment on PSU. For participants who lived in cities, DWC team attachment was significantly and negatively associated with PSU, while this effect was not significant for those who lived in rural communities. In addition, when the moderating rural-urban variable was included, the mediating effect of DWC team attachment on PSU through loneliness was still significant, B = −0.050, p <.001, 95% CI [−0.079, −0.028].

Robustness test

According to expert recommendations [80, 87], we analyzed whether the main results remained consistent when the control variables, such as gender, age, educational level, marital status, objective and subjective health status, and SES, were not included. Results revealed that the direct effect of DWC team attachment on PSU was still significant without the control variables, B = −0.174, p <.001, 95% CI [−0.249, −0.100]. In the simple mediation effect model, the effect of DWC team attachment on PSU through loneliness was still significant, B = −0.071, p <.001, 95% CI [−0.103, −0.045]. After rural-urban residence was added as the moderating variable, the cross-product term of DWC team attachment and rural-urban residence still had a significant effect on PSU without control variables, B = −0.197, p =.007, 95% CI [−0.257, −0.073]; for participants in cities and rural areas, the association between DWC team attachment and PSU was significantly negative, B = −0.167, p <.001, 95% CI [−0.257, −0.073] and insignificant, B = 0.030, p =.607, 95% CI [−0.086, 0.147], respectively. These results were consistent with those of previous studies that contained control variables. Hence, our results have strong robustness [80, 88].

Discussion

Our findings on DWC team attachment, loneliness, PSU, and rural-urban differences have extensive practical implications which contribute to a comprehensive and in-depth understanding of the social world and promote technological development.

First, our study revealed that DWC team attachment is significantly associated with diminished PSU, which indicates that encouraging engagement in social team activities could enhance social connections and be associated with lower loneliness. Consequently, this will facilitate healthier and more rational use of smartphones. Therefore, communities, nursing homes, and similar institutions should organize additional team activities tailored to participants, such as interest groups and volunteer services. Furthermore, we introduced the aspect of the association between DWC team attachment and participants’ PSU behavior, which enriches the theoretical connotation of the sociology of aging and the technology acceptance model. It underscores the crucial role that social interactions play in technology use and offers new perspectives for future research [89, 90].

We contend that uncovering participants’ challenges and needs related to their smartphone use is imperative. This necessitates adhering to the principles of technological ethics, ensuring that the design, development, and use of technology are consistent with users’ interests and needs, and respect their privacy and autonomy. However, in the pursuit of promoting digital integration, a balance is required between people’s digital rights and obligations [91]. This requires collaborative efforts from the government, enterprises, and all sectors of society to provide people with a safe, convenient, and easy-to-use digital environment to promote healthy and scientific social behavior for smartphone use.

Second, we argue that as a mediating factor, lowering loneliness is crucial to improving participants’ overall mental health. This necessitates that all sectors attend to their material needs and prioritize psychological well-being. Therefore, support should be provided to facilitate the establishment and maintenance of good social relationships via social opportunities, psychological counseling, and other relevant services [7]. Communities inevitably involve engagement in social behavior. Promoting lifelong development and a learning paradigm is essential for people to actively have a healthy social life. We argue that middle-aged and older adults should be guided to appropriately utilize their smartphones throughout their life course, including smartphone use training and online courses to promote their overall development and social participation.

The government should fully consider social needs when formulating policies and build a more inclusive and supportive social environment for middle-aged and older adults. This can be achieved through the provision of diverse community activities and establishment of social platforms tailored to this demographic, which can contribute towards lower loneliness and promote social participation and digital integration [13]. Furthermore, designing smart technologies better aligned with the needs of middle-aged and older adults is imperative. Technology developers should consider this population’s cognitive characteristics, usage habits, and emotional needs when designing smart products. They should develop easy-to-use and safe smart technologies that also prevent technology addiction. Technology developers should explore ways to integrate social functions more closely into smart technologies, such as promoting interactions between family members and establishing social circles for middle-aged and older adults through smart devices, to meet their social needs.

Third, this study revealed disparities in the association between DWC team attachment and PSU among rural and urban participants, which underscores the importance of developing differentiated intervention strategies for people in different regions. In cities, lowering loneliness through a combination of online and offline social activities should be the focus. Conversely, in rural areas, greater attention may be required to enhance information literacy and smartphone usage skills to enable middle-aged and older adults to better integrate into the digital society [22, 64]. This study provides a novel understanding of how social activities and personal experiences interact to influence acceptance and use of technology. Logically, DWC team attachment should be more effective in rural communities since rural community has stronger tie than urban community. However, our findings suggest a lower effective association between DWC team attachment and PSU as high familiarity within rural communities diminishes the relationship of interpersonal connections among DWC team members. Furthermore, relatively low information literacy makes it challenging for rural people to obtain professional support from DWC team members. This has profound implications for the development of a further inclusive and diverse digital society that caters to the needs of middle-aged and older adults. Hence, promoting balanced urban and rural development necessitates increased government investment in rural areas, improving information literacy, bridging the rural-urban digital divide, and achieving digital equity for middle-aged and older adults.

Fourth, we advocate for the need to enhance participants’ well-being. This study emphasizes the role of DWC team attachment in the association between low loneliness and low PSU. This indicates that in an aging society, building a diversified social network is essential for improving well-being. Such diversification extends beyond traditional family and community ties and encompasses social teams formed based on shared interests, activities, or values. A diversified social network can offer enhanced emotional support, facilitate information exchange, and foster a sense of belonging, which can ultimately boost life satisfaction and happiness. Furthermore, addressing the imbalance in well-being in urban and rural areas is critical. Disparities association between DWC team attachment and PSU between urban and rural participants mirror the underlying imbalances. In cities, participants may be more prone to feelings of loneliness and consequently rely more on their smartphones for social interaction. Conversely, in rural areas, close-knit traditional community networks and lower levels of information literacy may diminish dependence on smartphones. Consequently, considering rural-urban differences is essential when formulating social welfare policies. Strengthening rural community development and improving information literacy can promote more balanced well-being for middle-aged and older adults across both urban and rural settings.

Fifth, this study unveils the learning potential and needs of the middle-aged and older adult population regarding smartphone usage and presents an opportunity to explore the synergies between traditional and digital cultures. Guiding individuals to actively participate in digital activities, such as online learning and digital art creation, can enrich their later life and promote social activeness and inclusiveness. Further, the association between DWC team attachment and participants’ PSU is important for intergenerational communication. Joint participation in social team activities between young people and middle-aged and older adults can enhance mutual understanding and respect across generations.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered in future research. First, owing to the limitations of the research conditions, we used a cross-sectional design. Hence, our results could not provide direct evidence of causal relationships. Future studies should consider using follow-up research data or quasi-experimental or experimental research to clearly establish the relationship between DWC team attachment and PSU. Second, our participants were mainly middle-aged and older adults with relatively low educational levels, then short-form scales were used to improve the effectiveness of the questionnaire. Future research may use conventional scales. Third, we found that DWC team attachment corresponded to lower level of PSU use and lowered loneliness. However, we cannot exclude the possibility of other potential mechanisms, which should be explored in future research. Fourth, although some demographic variables were controlled, other potential confounders, such as personality traits and prior smartphone use habits, were not considered. Future research should include other variables as control or even independent variables. Fifth, we used data collected in China, where DWC is a popular social activity for middle-aged and older adults; therefore, results should be generalized with caution. DWC was developed from Guangchang Wu, with its development in the perspective of compensating the achievement of “Chinese-style” care system [92], based on its Confucian cultural background. It clearly enhances social inclusion and social communication in Chinese society, which has also garnered academic attraction from global scholars with research outcomes [93–95]. However, the limited generalizability is also clear and direct when we examine Chinese samples to analyze DWC. Future research should consider examining whether and how DWC activities and team attachment change the behavioral habits of middle-aged and older adults or their health condition in a broad cultural context. Sixth, our participants had a gender imbalance issue, which may decrease the generalizability of this research outcomes. Actually, more women participated in the DWC teams than men due to family carer role and interests in dancing activities. Future research could include further discussion on gender balance with DWC analysis.

Conclusion

This study bridges this gap by examining the influence of social interaction, particularly through the DWC model, on PSU in middle-aged and older adults. It also analyzes whether this effect differs in urban and rural areas. This study highlights the need for further exploration of the mechanisms through which social interaction impacts PSU and the development of targeted intervention strategies to address this issue considering rural-urban disparities.
