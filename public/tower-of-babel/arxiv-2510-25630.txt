Average Rank of Elliptic Curves Over Function Fields
ırmak Balçık

Abstract
. Let q q be a prime with q ≥ 5 q\geq 5 . We show that the average rank of elliptic curves over a function field 𝔽 q ​ ( t ) \mathbb{F}_{q}(t) , when ordered by naive height, is bounded above by 25 / 14 ≈ 1.8 25/14\approx 1.8 . Our result improves the previous upper bound of 2.3 2.3 proven by Brumer in [ Bru92 ] . The upper bound obtained is less than 2 2 , which shows that a positive proportion of elliptic curves has either rank 0 0 or 1 1 . The proof adapts the work of Young, which shows in [ You06 ] that under the assumption of the General Riemann Hypothesis for L L -functions of elliptic curves, the average rank for the family of elliptic curves over the rational numbers is bounded above by 25 / 14 ≈ 1.8 25/14\approx 1.8 .



1.
Introduction

Let
E
be an elliptic curve over a global field
K
. The Mordell-Weil theorem asserts that the group of
K
-rational points
E(K)
of
E
forms a finitely generated group
E(K)\cong E(K)_{\text{tor}}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{r}
where
E(K)_{\text{tor}}
is the finite torsion subgroup and
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
is the rank. The study of ranks of elliptic curves has become a central topic in number theory. It remains nevertheless mysterious. For example, it is not known if the rank of elliptic curves over a given number field is bounded. On the other hand, over function fields, the rank is known to be unbounded due Ulmer
[
Ulm02
]
. To get a better understanding of ranks of elliptic curves in general, one can hope to determine the average rank of elliptic curves. The average rank for the family of elliptic curves over functions fields was first bounded by Brumer
[
Bru92
]
, who gave an upper bound of
2.3
. In this paper, we obtain an upper bound less than
2
, which particularly shows that a positive proportion of elliptic curves has either rank
0
or
1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{P}^{1}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
where
q\geq 5
is a prime. Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)=\mathbb{F}_{q}(\mathcal{C})
be the function field of the curve
\mathcal{C}
. Each elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
has a model

E_{A,B}:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B

where
A,B
are in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
satisfying
-16(4A^{3}+27B^{2})\neq 0
. Define the canonical (naive)
height
of the elliptic curve as follows; let
E^{\prime}
be any elliptic curve isomorphic to
E
of the form
E^{\prime}_{C,D}:y^{2}=x^{3}+Cx+D
where
C,D\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t],
then

h(E):=\inf_{E^{\prime}\cong E}\Big(\max\{3\deg C,2\deg D\}\Big).

For ease of notation, we introduce

\mathcal{D}(d)=\{E_{A,B}:\deg A=\big\lfloor\frac{d}{3}\big\rfloor,\ \deg B=\big\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\big\rfloor\}.

Write
\rk(E)
for the Mordell-Weil rank of
E(\mathbb{F}_{q}(t))
.
Our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.0.1
.

Let
q\geq 5
be a prime. Then,

\limsup_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{D}(d)}\sum_{E\in\mathcal{D}(d)}\rk(E)\leq\frac{25}{14}.

With a little bit more effort one can relate our set
\mathcal{D}(d)
to the more customary,

\{0<\deg A\leq\big\lfloor\frac{d}{3}\big\rfloor,\ 0<\deg B\leq\big\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\big\rfloor\}.

Notice that for
d
growing to infinity, the relative density of the two sets differ only by
\leq C/q
with
C
an absolute constant. With a little bit more effort one can also restrict the summation to minimal models. We avoid these restrictions as they make the proof somewhat more complicated, but for little gain, in our view.

Brumer in
[
Bru92
]
also gave an upper bound of
2.3
for the average rank of elliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}
under the assumption of the General Riemann Hypothesis for elliptic curve
L
-functions. Under the same conditions, his work was later improved to
2
by Heath-Brown
[
Hea04
]
, and then to
25/14\approx 1.8
by Young in
[
You06
]
. The author is inspired by the work of Young
[
You06
]
and proves the analog of his result in the context of the function field. In the algebraic spectrum of similar studies, Bhargava and Shankar
[
BS15
]
remarkably proved an unconditional upper bound of
1.5
for the average rank of elliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}
.

2.
Notation and Background

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field whose cardinality
q
is a prime with
q\geq 5.
We denote by
\mathcal{H}
the set of polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and by
\mathcal{M}
the set of monic polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
. And by
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\mathcal{M}_{n}
the set of polynomials of degree
n
and the set of monic polynomials of degree
n
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t],
respectively. The norm of a nonzero polynomial
F\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
is defined as
|F|=q^{\deg F}
and
|F|=0
for
F=0
.

2.1.
L
-functions attached to Dirichlet Characters

Let
h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
be a monic polynomial. Then a
Dirichlet character
of modulus
h
is a homomorphism

\chi:(\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/h\mathbb{F}_{q}[t])^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}.

For any multiple
mh
of
h,

\chi
induces a homomorphism
(\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/mh\mathbb{F}_{q}[t])^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}

We call a character
\chi
of modulus
h
primitive if it cannot be induced from a modulus of lower degree and refer to
h
as the conductor of
\chi
. We can evaluate a Dirichlet character
\chi
of
conductor
h
at an element
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
by

\chi(g)=\begin{cases}\chi(g\ (\mathrm{mod}\ h))&\text{if}\ h\ \text{and}\ g\ \text{are coprime,}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

The principal Dirichlet character
\chi_{0}
of modulus
h
is defined by the property that
\chi_{0}(g)=1
if
h,g
are coprime and
\chi_{0}(g)=0
otherwise.

To any Dirichlet character
\chi
, we attach a Dirichlet
L
-function of a complex variable
u
by

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\chi(f)u^{\deg f}.

The
L
-function has Euler product

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\prod_{P}(1-\chi(P)u^{\deg P})^{-1}

where the product runs over all monic prime polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
. Let
n\geq\deg h
be an integer. If
\chi
is non-principal, it is easy to show that

\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\chi(f)=0

and thus
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)
is in fact a polynomial of degree at most
\deg h-1.
If
\chi(ag)=\chi(g)
for all
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times},
then
\chi
is said to be
even
.

2.2.
Quadratic characters

The quadratic character
\Big(\frac{\cdot}{P}\Big)
for any prime
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
is defined by

\Big(\frac{f}{P}\Big)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ f\ \text{is a non-zero square}\bmod P\\
-1&\text{if}\ f\ \text{is a non-square}\bmod P\\
0&\text{if}\ P|f\end{cases}

The above definition can be multiplicatively extended to
\Big(\frac{\cdot}{D}\Big)
for any non-zero monic
D\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and we write
\chi_{D}
for the quadratic character
\Big(\frac{\cdot}{D}\Big)
. The reciprocity law asserts that if
A,B\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
are relatively prime, non-zero monic polynomials, then

\Big(\frac{A}{B}\Big)=(-1)^{\frac{q-1}{2}\deg A\deg B}\Big(\frac{B}{A}\Big).

This relation continues to hold if
A,B
are not coprime as both sides vanish.

2.3.
Exponential Function

We recall the exponential function introduced by Hayes in
[
Hay66
]
. Note that the elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}((\frac{1}{t}))
can be expressed as Laurent series. Specifically, each
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((\frac{1}{t}))
can be written uniquely as

(1)

\displaystyle a=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{i}\Big(\frac{1}{t}\Big)^{i}

where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that all but finitely many
a_{i}
with
i\geq 0
are non-zero. There is a valuation defined by

\nu(a)=\text{smallest}\ i\ \text{such that}\ a_{i}\neq 0.

For
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((\frac{1}{t})),
the exponential function is given by

e(a)=e^{2\pi ia_{1}/q}

where
a_{1}
is the coefficient of
\frac{1}{t}
in (
1
). We recall from
[
Hay66
]
that
e(a+b)=e(a)e(b)
for
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((\frac{1}{t})).
For
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
we have
e(A)=1.
If
A,B,H\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
are such that
A\equiv B\pmod{H},
then
e(A/H)=e(B/H).

2.4.
Gauss sum

The generalized Gauss sum is defined by

G(u,\chi)=\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}\chi(V)e\Big(\frac{uV}{f}\Big).

For our later work, we require knowledge of
G(u,\chi_{P})
for a non-zero monic prime
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
. The next lemma allows us to compute
G(u,\chi_{P})
which is due Gauss.

Lemma 2.4.1
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
a monic prime polynomial. Then

\displaystyle G(V,\chi_{P})=|P|^{1/2}\Big(\frac{V}{P}\Big).

Proof.

It follows from
[
Flo16
, Lemma 3.2]
.
∎

3.
Poisson Summation Formula

For
F
a general periodic function
\pmod{f}
, define the Fourier transform of
F
as

\widehat{F}(u;f)=\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}F(V)e\Big(\frac{uV}{f}\Big).

The following Poisson summation formula holds.

Lemma 3.0.1
.

Let
F
be a periodic function
\pmod{f}
such that
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
is a polynomial of degree
n
and let
m
be a positive integer. Then

(2)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{g\in\mathcal{M}_{m}}F(g)=\frac{q^{m}}{|f|}\sum\limits_{\deg V\leq n-m-1}\widehat{F}(V;f)e\Big(\frac{-Vx^{m}}{f}\Big).

If
V=0
then
\deg V=-\infty
by convention and so the above equality holds.

Proof.

For any polynomial
g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
, we have the following equality using the definition of the Fourier transform,

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|f|}\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}\widehat{F}(-V;f)e\Big(\frac{Vg}{f}\Big)=\frac{1}{|f|}\sum\limits_{u\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}F(u)\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}e\Big(\frac{V(g-u)}{f}\Big).

If
u\neq g,
then
\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}e\Big(\frac{V(g-u)}{f}\Big)=0,
since we can find a polynomial
h
such that
e\Big(\frac{h(g-u)}{f}\Big)\neq 1
and then

e\Big(\frac{h(g-u)}{f}\Big)\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}e\Big(\frac{V(g-u)}{f}\Big)=\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}e\Big(\frac{(V+h)(g-u)}{f}\Big)=\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}e\Big(\frac{V(g-u)}{f}\Big).

Hence the only non-zero term on the right hand side of (
3
) is determined by
u=g.
Therefore, we obtain

(4)

\displaystyle F(g)=\frac{1}{|f|}\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}\widehat{F}(-V;f)e\Big(\frac{Vg}{f}\Big).

Each
g\in\mathcal{M}_{m}
can be written as
g=x^{m}+u
with
\deg u\leq m-1.
Using (
4
), we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{g\in\mathcal{M}_{m}}F(g)=\frac{1}{|f|}\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}\widehat{F}(-V;f)e\Big(\frac{Vx^{m}}{f}\Big)\sum\limits_{\deg u\leq m-1}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big)=\mathcal{S}_{1}+\mathcal{S}_{2}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|f|}\sum\limits_{\deg V\leq n-m-1}\widehat{F}(-V;f)e\Big(\frac{Vx^{m}}{f}\Big)\sum\limits_{\deg u\leq m-1}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|f|}\sum\limits_{n-m\leq\deg V\leq n-1}\widehat{F}(-V;f)e\Big(\frac{Vx^{m}}{f}\Big)\sum\limits_{\deg u\leq m-1}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big).

We first evaluate
\mathcal{S}_{1}
. When
\deg V\leq n-m-1
and
\deg u\leq m-1
, then
e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big)=1.
Since there are
q^{m}
polynomials of degree
\leq m-1
, it follows under
V\mapsto-V
that

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}=\frac{q^{m}}{|f|}\sum\limits_{\deg V\leq n-m-1}\widehat{F}(V;f)e\Big(\frac{-Vx^{m}}{f}\Big).

Now we will show that
\mathcal{S}_{2}=0.
We re-write

\sum\limits_{\deg u\leq m-1}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big)=1+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{m-1}\sum\limits_{c=1}^{q-1}\sum\limits_{u\in c\mathcal{M}_{i}}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big).

Notice that if
i\leq n-2-\deg V
, then
e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big)=1.
If
i\geq n-\deg V
, then it follows from
[
Hay66
, Lemma 3.7]
that
\sum\limits_{u\in c\mathcal{M}_{i}}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big)=0.
Since
n-1-\deg V\leq m-1
in
\mathcal{S}_{2},
combining all these above

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\deg u\leq m-1}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big)

\displaystyle=1+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-2-\deg V}\sum\limits_{c=1}^{q-1}q^{i}+\sum\limits_{c=1}^{q-1}\sum\limits_{u\in c\mathcal{M}_{n-1-\deg V}}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big)

\displaystyle=1+(q-1)\frac{q^{n-1-\deg V}-1}{q-1}-q^{n-1-\deg V}

\displaystyle=0

Hence
\mathcal{S}_{2}=0
. Combining this with
(
4
)
and
\eqref{firstsummand},
completes the proof of
(
2
)
.
∎

Corollary 3.0.2
.

Let
F
be a periodic function
\pmod{f}
such that
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
is a polynomial of degree
n
and let
m
be a positive integer. Then

(6)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{g\in\mathcal{H}_{m}}F(g)=\frac{q^{m}}{|f|}\Big(\sum\limits_{\deg V=n-m-1}-\widehat{F}(V;f)+\sum\limits_{\deg V<n-m-1}(q-1)\widehat{F}(V;f)\Big).

If
V=0
then
\deg V=-\infty
by convention and so the above equality holds.

Proof.

Each
g\in\mathcal{H}_{m}
can be written as
g=ax^{m}+u
where
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and
\deg u\leq m-1.
Using (
4
) above,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{g\in\mathcal{H}_{m}}F(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|f|}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}\Big(\sum\limits_{V\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)}\widehat{F}(-V;f)e\Big(\frac{Vax^{m}}{f}\Big)\Big)\sum\limits_{\deg u\leq m-1}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{1}+\mathcal{S}_{2}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|f|}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}\Big(\sum\limits_{\deg V\leq n-m-1}\widehat{F}(-V;f)e\Big(\frac{Vax^{m}}{f}\Big)\Big)\sum\limits_{\deg u\leq m-1}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|f|}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}\Big(\sum\limits_{n-m\leq\deg V\leq n-1}\widehat{F}(-V;f)e\Big(\frac{Vax^{m}}{f}\Big)\Big)\sum\limits_{\deg u\leq m-1}e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big).

With an argument similar to the proof of Lemma
2
, we see that
\mathcal{S}_{2}=0
. It remains to evaluate
\mathcal{S}_{1}
. When
\deg V\leq n-m-1
and
\deg u\leq m-1
, then
e\Big(\frac{Vu}{f}\Big)=1.
Since there are
q^{m}
polynomials of degree
\leq m-1
, it follows under
V\mapsto-V
that

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}=\frac{q^{m}}{|f|}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}\Big(\sum\limits_{\deg V\leq n-m-1}\widehat{F}(V;f)e\Big(\frac{-Vax^{m}}{f}\Big)\Big).

Notice that if
\deg V<n-m-1
, then
e\Big(\frac{-Vax^{m}}{f}\Big)=1
. If
\deg V=n-m-1
, then
\sum\limits_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}e\Big(\frac{-Vax^{m}}{f}\Big)=-1
and hence

\mathcal{S}_{1}=\frac{q^{m}}{|f|}\Big(\sum\limits_{\deg V=n-m-1}-\widehat{F}(V;f)+\sum\limits_{\deg V<n-m-1}(q-1)\widehat{F}(V;f)\Big).

which completes the proof.
∎

4.
The Main Estimate

For a non-principal character
\chi
with conductor
h
, the Dirichlet
L
-function associated to
\chi
is defined by

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\chi(f)u^{\deg f}=\prod_{P}(1-\chi(P)u^{\deg P})^{-1}

where the summation is over all monic polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and the product is over all monic prime polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
. We will need the following result to bound the size of
L
-functions. Note that the result holds for characters of any order.

Lemma 4.0.1
.

Let
\chi
be a primitive character of conductor
h
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
. Then, for
|u|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
and for all
\varepsilon>0
,

|\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)|\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon\deg h}.

In fact, if
\chi
is a character of period
h
, then

|\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)|\ll_{\varepsilon}(16q^{\varepsilon})^{\deg h}.

Proof.

This is the Lindelöf hypothesis in function fields. See
[
Buc+18
, Theorem 20]
for the proof of the first part of the statement.

To prove the second part, assume that
\chi
is a character of period
h
. Let
\chi=\chi_{0}\chi_{1}
where
\chi_{0}
is the principal character
\ (\mathrm{mod}\ h)
and
\chi_{1}
is a primitive character
\ (\mathrm{mod}\ f)
with
f|h
. Since we have

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\prod_{P}(1-\chi(P)u^{\deg P})^{-1}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{1})=\prod_{P}(1-\chi_{1}(P)u^{\deg P})^{-1}

and
\chi_{0}(P)=1
unless
P|h
, then
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)
differs from
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{1})
only on the primes
P
with
P|h
. Therefore,

\frac{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{1})}=\prod_{P|h}\frac{1-\chi_{1}(P)u^{\deg P}}{1-\chi(P)u^{\deg P}}.

Applying the first part to
\chi_{1}
allows us to set a bound

|\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)|=|\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{1})|\Big|\frac{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{1})}\Big|\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon\deg f}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\Big)^{-2\deg h}

Since
\deg f\leq\deg h
and
(1-1/\sqrt{q})^{-1}\leq 4
, the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 4.0.2
.

Let
W\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
be a polynomial of degree
l
and
\chi
a non-principal character
\ (\mathrm{mod}\ W)
. Then

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\end{subarray}}\chi(V)\Big|\ll_{\varepsilon}16^{l}q^{\frac{k}{2}+1+\varepsilon l}\ \ \ \ \text{for any $\varepsilon>0$}.

Proof.

For any
V\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
, there is a unique monic polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
and non-zero
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
V=af
. Therefore

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{V\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\chi(V)=\sum_{a\neq 0}\chi(a)\sum_{V\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}\chi(V).

Using Perron’s formula we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{V\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}\chi(V)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{|u|=r}\frac{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}{u^{k}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u}

where we integrate along a circle with radius
r=q^{-1/2}
. By Lemma
4.0.1
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{|u|=r}\frac{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}{u^{k}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u}\Big|\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{(16q^{\varepsilon})^{l}}{2\pi}\oint\limits_{|u|=r}\Big|\frac{1}{u^{k}}\Big|\Big|\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u}\Big|\ll_{\varepsilon}16^{l}q^{\frac{k}{2}+\varepsilon l}

Then it follows from (
8
) that

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\end{subarray}}\chi(V)\Big|\ll_{\varepsilon}16^{l}q^{\frac{k}{2}+1+\varepsilon l}

for any
\varepsilon>0
, as desired.
∎

Before proceeding further, note that

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)
has a “trivial” zero at
u=1
when
\chi
is even. Thus

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=(1-u)^{\lambda}\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi),\ \ \ \lambda=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ \chi\ \text{is odd}\\
1&\text{if}\ \chi\ \text{is even}\\
\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi)
is a polynomial of degree

\delta=\deg h-1-\lambda
.

Lemma 4.0.3
.

Given
W\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
of degree
l
and a non-principal character
\chi\ (\mathrm{mod}\ W)
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\ \text{prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(P)}{|P|^{1/2}}\Big|\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon l}\ \ \ \ \ \text{for any $\varepsilon>0$}.

Proof.

The Riemann Hypothesis for curves over function fields
[
Wei48
]
asserts that all zeroes of
\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi)
are on the circle
|u|=q^{-1/2}
. Thus, we may express

\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi)=\det(1-u\sqrt{q}\Theta)

for a
\delta\times\delta
unitary matrix
\Theta
. By taking a logarithmic derivative of the identity

\det(1-u\sqrt{q}\Theta)=(1-u)^{-\lambda}\prod_{P}(1-\chi(P)u^{\deg P})^{-1}

which comes from writing
\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi)=(1-u)^{-\lambda}\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)
, we find

-\tr(\Theta^{n})=\frac{\lambda}{q^{n/2}}+\frac{1}{q^{n/2}}\sum\limits_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\Lambda(f)\chi(f)

where
\Lambda
is the von Mangoldt function. It then follows that

\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\ \text{prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(P)}{|P|^{1/2}}\Big|\ll\Big|\frac{\tr(\Theta^{n})-\lambda/q^{n/2}}{n}\Big|\ll\frac{\deg h}{n}\ll\frac{l}{n}\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon l}

for any
\varepsilon>0
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 4.0.4
.

Let
n,k,l
be positive integers. Let
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Then

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\ \text{prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|P|^{1/2}}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{M}_{k}\\

5.
Averaging rank over function fields

5.1.
\mathcal{L}
-function associated to elliptic curves

The
L
-function of
E/\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
is defined analogously as for
E/\mathbb{Q}.
More precisely, let
P
be a prime of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
i.e.
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
is a monic irreducible polynomial or
P=P_{\infty}.
If
P
is a prime of good reduction, then the reduction of
E
(which we also denote by
E
) is an elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{P}=\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/(P)\simeq\mathbb{F}_{q^{\text{deg}P}}
(where
\mathbb{F}_{\infty}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
since the prime at infinity has degree 1), and
Let
N_{P}
be the number of points in the projective coordinates over
\mathbb{F}_{P}
, i.e.

N_{P}=|\{\mathcal{O}\}\cup\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{P}^{2}:y^{2}-(x^{3}+Ax+B)\equiv 0\bmod P\}|.

Let
a_{P}=q^{\deg P}+1-N_{P}
and then

\#E(\mathbb{F}_{P})=q^{\deg P}+1-a_{P},\ a_{P}=\alpha_{P}+\overline{\alpha}_{P},\ |\alpha_{P}|=\sqrt{q^{\deg P}}.

Let

\mathcal{L}_{P}(E,u)=1-a_{P}u+q^{\deg P}u^{2}=(1-\alpha_{P}u)(1-\overline{\alpha}_{P}u)

be the
L
-function of
E/\mathbb{F}_{P}.
If
P
is a prime of bad reduction, we define

\mathcal{L}_{P}(E,u)=(1-a_{P}u)

where
a_{P}=0,1,-1
depending on the type of bad reduction (additive, split multiplicative and non-split multiplicative respectively). Let
N_{E}
be the conductor of
E
which is the product of the primes of bad reduction with the appropriate powers. Let
M_{E}
(respectively
A_{E}
) be the product of multiplicative (respectively additive) primes of
E
.
Then
N_{E}=M_{E}A_{E}^{2}
.

The
L
-
function
of
E
is

\mathcal{L}(E,u)=\prod_{P\nmid N_{E}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_{P}(E,u^{\deg P})}\prod_{P|N_{E}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_{P}(E,u^{\deg P})}.

It is proven by Weil that
\mathcal{L}(E,u)
is a polynomial of degree
N=\deg N_{E}-4=\deg(M_{E})+2\deg(A_{E})-4,
setting
u=q^{-s}
it satisfies a functional equation
s\xleftrightarrow{}2-s,
and its zeros lie on Re
s=1
for elliptic curves in
\mathcal{D}(d)
for any
d
. More precisely,

\mathcal{L}(E,u)=\mathcal{L}(E,q^{-s})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-\mu_{i}q^{-s})

where each
\mu_{i}
is an algebraic integer of absolute value
q
in every complex embedding, moreover
|\mu_{i}|=q
.

In order to prove the main proposition, we need auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma 5.1.1
.

Let
N=\deg N_{E}-4
. We have

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{\mu_{i}}{q}\Big)^{n}=-\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum\limits_{k|n}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\nmid N_{E}\\
\deg P=n/k\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha_{P}^{k}+\overline{\alpha}_{P}^{k}}{k}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P|N_{E}\\
\deg P=n/k\end{subarray}}\frac{a_{P}^{k}}{k}\Big).

Proof.

For any given elliptic curve
E
, we have the following equality

\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-\mu_{i}u)=\prod_{P\nmid N_{E}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_{P}(E,u^{\deg P})}\prod_{P|N_{E}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_{P}(E,u^{\deg P})}.

By change of variable
u=\frac{z}{q}
with
|z|<1
and taking the
\log
of both sides,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\log\Big(1-\frac{\mu_{i}}{q}z\Big)=\sum_{P\nmid N_{E}}-\log\Big(\mathcal{L}_{P}\Big(E,\Big(\frac{z}{q}\Big)^{\deg P}\Big)\Big)+\sum_{P|N_{E}}-\log\Big(\mathcal{L}_{P}\Big(E,\Big(\frac{z}{q}\Big)^{\deg P}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\nmid N_{E}}\Big(-\log\Big(1-\frac{\alpha_{P}}{q^{\deg P}}z^{\deg P}\Big)-\log\Big(1-\frac{\overline{\alpha}_{P}}{q^{\deg P}}z^{\deg P}\Big)\Big)+\sum_{P|N_{E}}-\log\Big(1-\frac{a_{P}}{q^{\deg P}}z^{\deg P}\Big)

Taylor expanding in
z
yields

-\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(\frac{\mu_{i}}{q}z)^{n}}{n}=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{P\nmid N_{E}}\frac{\Big(\frac{\alpha_{P}^{n}+\overline{\alpha}_{P}^{n}}{q^{n\deg P}}\Big)z^{n\deg P}}{n}+\sum_{P|N_{E}}\frac{(\frac{a_{P}^{n}}{q^{n\deg P}})z^{n\deg P}}{n}\Big).

Equating the
n
-th coefficients, we get the desired identity.
∎

Simplifying Lemma
5.1.1
we get the following.

Lemma 5.1.2

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{\mu_{i}}{q}\Big)^{n}=\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\nmid N_{E}\\
\deg P=n\end{subarray}}\Big(\alpha_{P}+\overline{\alpha_{P}}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}+O(q^{-n/100}).

Note that this lemma implies that the sum over primes is bounded by
\deg N_{E}-4.

Proof.

This follows from the previous Lemma upon noticing that the sum

\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\nmid N_{E}\\
\deg P=n\end{subarray}}(\alpha_{P}+\overline{\alpha_{P}})

corresponds to terms with
k=1
in the previous Lemma (the contribution of the terms with

is absorbed into
O(q^{-n/100})
). The terms with

contribute a total of
\ll q^{-n/100}
. It remains to note that, the contribution of the terms with

is

\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\nmid N_{E}\\
\deg P=n/2\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha_{P}^{2}+\overline{\alpha_{P}}^{2}}{2}=-\frac{1}{2}+O(q^{-n/100}).

This follows from
[
MS22
]
[Lemma 2.5(3)] and
[
MS22
]
[(2.7)] with

and

∎

Let

F(A,B;P)=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{P}}\Big(\frac{x^{3}+Ax+B}{P}\Big),
then

\widehat{F}(\alpha,\beta;P)=\sum\limits_{A\ (\mathrm{mod}\ P)}\sum\limits_{B\ (\mathrm{mod}\ P)}F(A,B;P)e\Big(\frac{\alpha A}{P}\Big)e\Big(\frac{\beta B}{P}\Big).

Lemma 5.1.4
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
be a monic prime polynomial and
\overline{\beta}
be defined by
\beta\overline{\beta}\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ P)
if
(\beta,P)=1
and

. Then we have

\widehat{F}(\alpha,\beta;P)=|P|^{3/2}\Big(\frac{\beta}{P}\Big)e\Big(\frac{-\alpha^{3}\overline{\beta}^{2}}{P}\Big).

Notice that if
P|\beta
the sum is zero, consistent with the above formula.

Proof.

By definition,

\widehat{F}(\alpha,\beta;P)=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{P}}\sum\limits_{A\ (\mathrm{mod}\ P)}\sum\limits_{B\ (\mathrm{mod}\ P)}\Big(\frac{x^{3}+Ax+B}{P}\Big)e\Big(\frac{\alpha A+\beta B}{P}\Big).

After the change of variables
B\mapsto B-x^{3}-Ax
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\alpha,\beta;P)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{P}}e\Big(\frac{-x^{3}\beta}{P}\Big)\sum\limits_{A\ (\mathrm{mod}\ P)}e\Big(\frac{A(\alpha-x\beta)}{P}\Big)\sum\limits_{B\ (\mathrm{mod}\ P)}\Big(\frac{B}{P}\Big)e\Big(\frac{\beta B}{P}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{P}}e\Big(\frac{-x^{3}\beta}{P}\Big)\sum\limits_{A\ (\mathrm{mod}\ P)}e\Big(\frac{A(\alpha-x\beta)}{P}\Big)G(\beta,\chi_{P})

\displaystyle=|P|^{3/2}\Big(\frac{\beta}{P}\Big)e\Big(\frac{-\alpha^{3}\overline{\beta}^{2}}{P}\Big)\ \ \ \text{by Lemma \ref{GaussLemma}. }

∎

We will now begin the proof of the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1.5
.

Let
\delta>0
be given. For

we have

(16)

\displaystyle\sum_{E\in\mathcal{D}(d)}\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\nmid N_{E}\\

Proof.

Let

and

.
For any

is given by

Let
