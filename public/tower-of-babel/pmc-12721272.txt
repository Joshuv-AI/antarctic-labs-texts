Red blood cell mass as a predictor of transfusion risk of packed red blood cells in cardiac surgery patients requiring cardiopulmonary bypass
Authors: Jennette Hansen, Niaman Nazir, George Zorn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•RBC mass is a physiologically meaningful predictor of transfusion in cardiac surgery.•Lower RBC mass (<2 L) significantly increases odds of transfusion, even with “normal” hemoglobin.•Female patients and those with low BMI are overrepresented in the low RBC mass category.•RBC mass outperforms traditional predictors such as BMI or hemoglobin alone.•RBC mass is simple to calculate and can be integrated into preoperative EMR-based workflows.

Introduction

Transfusion during cardiac surgery has been consistently associated with increased morbidity and mortality, including risks of infection, immunologic reactions, prolonged hospitalization, and even long-term adverse outcomes [1,2]. Despite advances in blood conservation and surgical techniques, the need for red blood cell (RBC) transfusion remains common in patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) procedures. Identifying patients at higher risk of transfusion preoperatively could allow for targeted optimization strategies, potentially reducing transfusion rates and improving outcomes.

Anemia has long been recognized as a major predictor of postoperative RBC transfusion in cardiac surgery patients [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Traditionally, hemoglobin concentration or hematocrit levels are used to assess anemia. However, these measures are concentration-based and may not fully capture the patient's true oxygen-carrying capacity. Furthermore, perioperative factors such as hemodilution during CPB can further complicate the interpretation of these values. Therefore, there is a growing interest in more comprehensive and physiologically meaningful metrics for predicting transfusion risk.

Body mass index (BMI) has also emerged as a significant factor influencing transfusion likelihood. Several studies have identified low BMI as an independent risk factor for RBC transfusion [[3], [4], [5], [6],8]. Low BMI may correlate with smaller blood volumes, limited physiologic reserve, and potentially poorer nutritional status, all of which could contribute to higher transfusion needs. Interestingly, some studies suggest obesity might confer a protective effect against transfusion, though this appears to be limited to patients with BMI in the overweight and Class I obesity range (BMI25-35) [9,10]. In contrast, others have found that patients with BMI >30 may actually have increased transfusion risk, although differences in BMI between transfused and non-transfused patients were often not statistically significant [7]. These conflicting findings indicate that BMI alone may not be sufficient to predict transfusion risk accurately.

In addition to BMI, female sex has been repeatedly identified as a consistent risk factor for RBC transfusion in cardiac surgery [1,[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Women have smaller blood volumes and lower baseline hemoglobin levels, which may predispose them to transfusion.

Given these limitations of individual predictors such as anemia, BMI, or sex alone, there is a critical need for a more integrative metric that encapsulates multiple physiologic variables influencing transfusion risk. Red blood cell mass (RBC mass) represents such a measure. RBC mass combines information about hematocrit, patient weight, and sex into a single quantitative value that reflects the total circulating red blood cell volume in the body. Unlike hematocrit or hemoglobin concentration, RBC mass accounts for both the concentration and volume components, potentially offering a more physiologically relevant measure of oxygen-carrying capacity.

This study aims to evaluate the utility of RBC mass as a superior predictor of RBC transfusion risk in cardiac surgery patients undergoing CPB. We hypothesize that RBC mass will demonstrate stronger associations with transfusion requirements than traditional predictors like hematocrit or BMI alone. Furthermore, we explore the distribution of RBC mass across patient demographics and its potential clinical application in preoperative risk stratification and optimization.

By identifying patients with low RBC mass preoperatively, clinicians may better target interventions such as anemia correction, iron supplementation, or intraoperative blood conservation techniques. Ultimately, this could lead to reduced transfusion rates, fewer complications, and improved surgical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a large Midwest tertiary care academic medical center, known for its high volume of adult cardiac surgeries, including coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valve repair and replacement, and complex combined procedures. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained prior to data collection, ensuring compliance with ethical standards and patient privacy regulations. The study period spanned one full calendar year from January 1 to December 31, 2024, allowing for comprehensive data capture of all eligible cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) cases within that timeframe.

Patient selection

All adult patients who underwent cardiac surgery requiring CPB during the study period were initially considered for inclusion. To reduce heterogeneity and confounding variables that could disproportionately affect transfusion requirements independent of RBC mass, several exclusion criteria were applied. Patients undergoing procedures with inherently high transfusion risk were excluded, including those with aortic dissection repair, left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation, orthotopic heart transplantation, surgeries requiring circulatory arrest, double valve replacement and/or repair, redo sternotomy, and those requiring urgent return to the operating room within the same admission (“bring-back”). These exclusions ensured that the cohort was more homogeneous with respect to baseline transfusion risk associated with standard CPB procedures.

After applying these criteria, a total of 463 patients met the inclusion criteria and formed the final study cohort.

Data collection

Patient demographic, clinical, laboratory, and perioperative data were extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR) and institutional cardiac surgery database. Variables collected included age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), preoperative hematocrit and hemoglobin values, serum creatinine, cardiopulmonary bypass duration, surgical urgency (elective vs. urgent/emergent), and perioperative medications such as aspirin use within five days of surgery.

Transfusion data were meticulously recorded, noting the incidence of packed red blood cell (pRBC) transfusions administered intraoperatively and postoperatively during the index admission. Use of other blood products such as platelets, fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and cryoprecipitate was also documented.

Calculation of red blood cell mass

The primary variable of interest, red blood cell (RBC) mass, was calculated using a formula integrating patient-specific estimated blood volume and hematocrit value. Estimated blood volume (EBV) was derived based on sex-specific anthropometric standards: 70 mL/kg for men and 65 mL/kg for women, consistent with published literature [11]. RBC mass was then calculated as the product of EBV and hematocrit, reflecting the absolute circulating red blood cell volume in milliliters (L):RBCmass(L)=EstimatedBloodVolume(L)×Hematocrit%

For example, a male patient weighing 70 kg with a hematocrit of 40 % (0.40) would have an estimated blood volume of 4900 mL (70 kg × 70 mL/kg) and RBC mass of 1960 mL (4900 mL × 0.40). The value was then converted to liters.

Variable categorization

To facilitate meaningful statistical comparisons, RBC mass values were divided into three clinically relevant categories based on distribution and transfusion incidence: low (1–2 liters), moderate (2–3 liters), and high (>3 liters). This stratification was chosen to maximize discrimination between patients with varying transfusion risk.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) as appropriate.

Comparisons between transfused and non-transfused groups were made using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Student’s t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare mean differences across the three RBC mass groups.

Logistic regression modeling was performed to evaluate the association between RBC mass category and the likelihood of red blood cell transfusion, adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, BMI, and CPB time. The lowest RBC mass category (1–2 L) was used as the reference group to estimate odds ratios (OR) and 95 % confidence intervals (CI).

All tests were two-sided, and p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that red blood cell (RBC) mass is a strong and independent predictor of packed red blood cell transfusion in patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass. Patients with a lower RBC mass, particularly in the 1–2 L range, had significantly higher odds of requiring red blood cell transfusion compared to those in the 3 + L group. Importantly, this association remained statistically significant, suggesting that RBC mass may be a more individualized and physiologically meaningful metric for transfusion risk stratification than hematocrit or BMI alone, allowing for a quantitative way of assessing transfusion risk.

While hemoglobin concentration and hematocrit are widely used to assess anemia, they are concentration-based measures and can be misleading in the setting of acute volume shifts or chronic physiologic adaptation. Red blood cell (RBC) mass, by contrast, represents the absolute quantity of circulating red blood cells, and therefore better reflects the body’s actual oxygen-carrying capacity. This metric may be particularly relevant in the perioperative cardiac surgery setting, where hemodilution during cardiopulmonary bypass can reduce red cell concentration to anemic levels, disproportionately affecting smaller patients with lower baseline RBC mass.

RBC mass integrates both blood volume and hematocrit, providing a more physiologically grounded estimate of oxygen delivery reserve. Patients with low blood volume and low hematocrit, such as small-framed individuals or those with malnutrition, may have deceptively “normal” hemoglobin values while remaining at high risk for tissue hypoxia and transfusion under stress. Using RBC mass accounts for both dimensions, while improving prediction and individualized risk assessment.

Consistent with previous literature, our findings reaffirm that female sex, older age, lower BMI, lower preoperative hemoglobin, non-elective surgical status, and longer cardiopulmonary bypass time are associated with increased transfusion rates [1,3,4]. However, unlike these isolated variables, RBC mass integrates hematocrit, sex, and body size into a single composite value, potentially offering a more integrated assessment of oxygen-carrying capacity and reserve.

Our institution’s overall transfusion rate was 22 %, notably lower than the 30–40 % rates commonly reported in adult cardiac surgery populations, which often vary based on surgical complexity and institutional practices [1,2]. This relatively low rate may reflect the success of our blood conservation strategies, patient selection criteria, and perioperative anemia management protocols. It may also strengthen the internal validity of our findings, as transfusions in this cohort were more likely to represent true physiologic necessity rather than variability in practice. However, this must be interpreted in the context of our exclusion criteria, which removed patients undergoing higher-risk procedures or “bring-backs”, who would have been more likely to require transfusion.

The observation that patients in the lowest RBC mass category (1–2 L) represented only 25.5 % of the total study population, yet accounted for 54.9 % of all transfusions, is particularly striking. These patients had 18.7 times greater odds of transfusion compared to those with RBC mass over 3 L. This pattern was especially prominent among female patients, who were disproportionately represented in the 1–2 L group, aligning with previous studies reporting higher transfusion rates in women [3,5,6]. These findings emphasize the importance of sex-specific risk stratification. Female patients, who may be overlooked for preoperative anemia correction due to seemingly ‘normal’ lab values, could benefit from targeted RBC mass assessment. Tailoring interventions based on sex specific physiologic profiles may reduce transfusion disparities and improve outcomes.

Further insight is provided by Table 2, which shows that patients in the 2–3 L RBC mass group had an average preoperative hemoglobin of 13.8 g/dL, while those in the >3 L group had an average of 14.5 g/dL. Despite both values being within normal limits, patients in the 2–3 L group had a 4-fold increased risk of transfusion compared to those in the >3 L group. This suggests that even in the absence of anemia by hemoglobin standards, patients with lower RBC mass may have reduced physiologic reserve, necessitating closer scrutiny during preoperative planning and consideration for intraoperative blood conservation strategies.

In addition, a third of the patients transfused and in the 1–2 L and 2–3 L RBC mass group received only 1 unit of red blood cells as resuscitation. One could argue that blood conservation and/or anemia optimization may prevent these patients from requiring a transfusion. In the 1–2 L RBC mass group and transfused, 62.5 % percent received either 1 or 2 units of red blood cells for resuscitation. This subgroup is likely anemic and, if targeted, could prevent transfusion of 1 unit and possibly 2 units of red blood cells, depending on the degree of anemia preoperatively and the weight of the patient.

RBC mass can be readily estimated preoperatively using standard anthropometric data (weight, height, sex) and laboratory values (hematocrit), enabling automated calculation within electronic medical record (EMR) systems. By embedding this metric into existing preoperative assessment tools or decision support modules, clinicians could be alerted to patients with low RBC mass, potentially identifying those who would benefit from targeted interventions such as iron supplementation, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, or blood conservation protocols. For example, preoperative assessment clinics could incorporate RBC mass thresholds into decision support tools to prompt hematology consults or initiate iron therapy.

Additionally, RBC mass could be incorporated into cardiac surgery checklists or anemia management pathways. For example, during pre-anesthesia clinic evaluations, patients with RBC mass below a defined threshold (e.g., <2.5 L) could be flagged for optimization or flagged for intraoperative cell salvage planning. In high-throughput surgical centers, this stratification could help conserve blood bank resources and reduce transfusion-related complications.

This study highlights the potential clinical utility of including RBC mass in preoperative risk stratification. In particular, patients with RBC mass <3 L, regardless of whether their hemoglobin is technically normal, should prompt further evaluation for treatable causes of low red blood cell mass. Those found to be deficient in iron, B12, or folate should be optimized as part of the preoperative preparation. Intraoperative blood conservation strategies should also be considered in this population. While urgent or emergent surgeries may not allow sufficient time for optimization, elective cases provide an opportunity to address these issues in advance.

From a systems perspective, integrating RBC mass into anesthetic and surgical planning could reduce unnecessary transfusions, lower blood product utilization, and ultimately improve postoperative outcomes. Given the rising emphasis on value-based care and blood management, this metric could support institutional protocols aimed at reducing transfusion-related complications. While this study was not designed to establish a specific cutoff value, stratifying patients into RBC mass tertiles or quartiles may inform future guidelines or algorithm development, especially when combined with dynamic intraoperative variables.

Prospective studies are needed to validate RBC mass as a predictive tool and to determine optimal threshold values across various surgical populations. Multicenter studies with diverse patient demographics may help establish reference ranges and guide clinical decision-making. Additionally, integrating RBC mass into machine learning algorithms or clinical calculators could allow for real-time risk stratification and individualized transfusion planning.

Limitations

This study was limited by its retrospective design and relatively small sample size from a single academic institution, which may impact the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, while RBC mass was calculated using established anthropometric formulas, its accuracy diminishes in individuals with higher BMI, as adipose tissue is less vascular and may overestimate blood volume [12]. Furthermore, the absence of standardized institutional transfusion algorithms introduces potential variability in transfusion decision-making, which could influence the observed associations.

Conclusion

RBC mass is a strong and potentially superior predictor of transfusion risk in cardiac surgery patients requiring cardiopulmonary bypass. By integrating key physiologic variables into a single metric, RBC mass provides a quantitative means of assessing transfusion risk by consolidating factors already known to influence the likelihood of transfusion. Prospective validation of these findings and integration into clinical risk models could enhance preoperative planning and reduce unnecessary transfusions. As healthcare systems increasingly prioritize blood management and patient-specific optimization, RBC mass represents a promising tool for aligning clinical practice with individualized care. Its routine incorporation into preoperative pathways could serve as a cornerstone in enhancing surgical safety and resource stewardship.
