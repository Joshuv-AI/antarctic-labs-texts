Pilot scale experimental and numerical study of LNAPL pump and treat and transport in subsurface soil
Authors: Amir Alamooti, Stéfan Colombano, Fabien Lion, Behshad Koohbor, Maxime Cochennec, Jacques Deparis, Clément Zornig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLight nonaqueous-phase liquids (LNAPLs) are major organic contaminants in subsurface environment that accumulate above the water table after spills. Understanding their migration in the subsurface is crucial for effective remediation. This study investigates LNAPL transfer in sand tank experiments and simulations, focusing on spill, redistribution due to water table fluctuations, and pumping-based remediation. Experiments were performed in a large 3D sand-filled tank (5.2 × 3.6 × 3.6 m³) equipped with injection, pumping, and piezometric wells, as well as time domain reflectrometer (TDR) probes to monitor phase distribution and permittivity changes. The experimental results revealed key LNAPL migration patterns: an initial localized hump formed above the water table after a spill, which later spread into a flatter layer. Water table fluctuations caused residual LNAPL entrapment in the middle zone. Pumping created cones of depression near wells, while TDR probes detected permittivity changes linked to saturation shifts during water table cycles. To simulate multiphase flow, we used the continuity equation together with generalized Darcy’s law, and applied the Stone I model to account for three-phase flow behavior. Experimental data, especially from piezometric wells, validated the numerical model, which effectively captured water-LNAPL and LNAPL-air interfaces. A mixing model for relative permittivity was also integrated, successfully reproducing TDR-observed trends across different tank regions. Overall, this work demonstrates that combining large-scale experiments with validated three-phase numerical simulations provides a reliable framework for predicting LNAPL behavior and optimizing remediation strategies at contaminated sites.Keywords: Light non-aqueous phase liquid (LNAPL), Contaminant transport, Time domain reflectrometer (TDR), Three-phase flow in porous media, Water table fluctuations, Pilot scale, RemediationSubject terms: Environmental impact, Hydrology, Civil engineering

Introduction

Light Non-Aqueous Phase Liquids (LNAPLs), which are lighter than water and have very low water solubility, pose a hazard to the groundwater quality and its remediation remain a significant challenge. These immiscible fluids are often associated with petroleum hydrocarbons containing hundreds of components that pose environmental risks and can severely impact long-term groundwater quality1. When released, free-phase LNAPL can migrate through unsaturated zone until reaching the water table and in contact with saturated zone after the fluctuation of water table and LNAPL pumping, potentially leaving behind entrapped or residual LNAPL2. The behavior of LNAPL at the water table is further complicated by natural fluctuations in the water level. Field observations have documented more complex scenarios, particularly during alternating wet and dry seasons, where water table fluctuations cause LNAPL to migrate to lower parts of the aquifer3–6. When the water table rises, LNAPL becomes entrapped in the saturated zone through capillary forces, a phenomenon known as LNAPL entrapment. To mimic the water table fluctuations, the successive drainage and imbibition experiments at different scale can be performed.

Active remediation and recovery of LNAPLs using multiphase extraction methods (remediation technology based on the extraction of groundwater, and NAPL and soil vapor from the subsurface to reduce or eliminate a source of contamination) aim to reduce the source mass, thereby minimizing its footprint, mass transfer, and associated risks to other environmental compartments such as groundwater, soil, and the vadose zone air. LNAPL mass can be recovered in the liquid phase through pump-and-treat methods, a widely used physical extraction-based remediation technique for contaminated groundwater7,8. During pumping, a cone of depression often forms near the NAPL-water interface, influencing subsurface flow dynamics9–11. It is important to properly characterize the cone of depression during pumping the contaminant to optimize the conditions for minimizing the residual LNAPL in subsurface.

To implement effective remediation measures at contaminated sites, it is essential to understand contaminant transport mechanisms, including contaminant migration in the unsaturated zone, the effects of pumping near saturated zone, and the impact of water table fluctuations. Several two-dimensional (2D) laboratory experiments have been conducted to simulate NAPL propagation in the vadose zone12–16. Numerical simulators have also been developed to model NAPL flow and transport in saturated and unsaturated porous media12–14,17–20. However, simulating LNAPL flow in heterogeneous porous media remains a complex task due to factors such as hysteresis, viscous fingering, and the empirical nature of three-phase relative permeability relationships2,20–22.

Høst-Madsen and Høgh Jensen12 conducted 2D experiments (sand-filled thin tank) and numerical simulations to study the migration of LNAPL in both the saturated and unsaturated zones. Using imaging techniques in the laboratory and black oil reservoir modeling, they successfully reproduced the migration front, highlighting the influence of the capillary fringe on the development of the LNAPL lens.

Kechavarzi et al.14 conducted 2D experiments to study LNAPL infiltration through variably saturated zones, quantifying pressures in both wetting and non-wetting phases. Their findings highlighted the importance of non-wetting fluid entrapment during wetting fluid infiltration stages. Wipfler et al.16 utilized similar 2D experiments in a sand-filled chamber to qualitatively investigate LNAPL infiltration through inclined layers of unsaturated sand. Numerical simulations of three-phase flow in these experiments demonstrated good agreement with experimentally observed LNAPL contours. Discrepancies between simulation and experiment were attributed to stronger capillary forces in the simulations and the neglect of non-drainable residual LNAPL.

Atteia et al.18 using simplified modeling approach analyzed the effects of vertical LNAPL layer movement and residual saturations on floating LNAPL thickness in a well. They assessed the influence of groundwater table fluctuations under varying degrees of LNAPL layer confinement. Kacem et al.13 investigated LNAPL infiltration through saturated and unsaturated zones using 2D sandbox experiments. They validated their experimental results through three-phase flow modeling based on Parker’s model, which allowed them to predict water saturation within the 2D cell and reproduce contaminant infiltration across the unsaturated, capillary, and saturated zones. However, they noted slight discrepancies between their numerical and experimental results, attributing them to the model’s neglect of hysteresis. Parker’s model assumes that fluid flow in reservoirs can be represented by a bundle of capillary tubes, with the flow path being proportional to the square of the mean hydraulic radius of the pores occupied by the fluid phase22,23.

Koohbor et al.20 used 2D experiments to evaluate the effects of water table fluctuations on LNAPL deposition in highly permeable media. They complemented their experimental work with three-phase flow numerical modeling, using the Stone I model, which they validated with their experimental results. The Stone I model is based on channel flow theory, which posits that only one fluid can be mobile within any flow channel at a given time22,24. In this framework, the wetting phase occupies the smallest pores, the non-wetting phase fills the largest pores, and the intermediate phase resides in pores of intermediate size. Consequently, at equal water saturations, fluid distributions in a water-oil system are expected to be identical to those in a water-oil-gas system, provided the direction of change in water saturation is the same in both scenarios. Koohbor et al.20 also employed a Time Domain Reflectometer (TDR) to measure bulk permittivity at various locations within their 2D setup. These bulk permittivity values were then translated into the saturations of the three phases and incorporated into their numerical simulations. TDR is an electromagnetic geophysical device that can determine fluid content, composition, and certain characteristics of porous media25. The bulk permittivity of the multiphase (solid and fluids) system can then be inversely translated into fluid saturation levels.

Despite substantial progress, important gaps remain. Much of the literature on LNAPL behavior relies on laboratory-scale or 2D systems, with limited transferability to field-relevant 3D geometries and operational conditions (e.g., water-table cycling and pump-and-treat) and without integrated dielectric monitoring. Pilot- or field-scale studies that do exist often emphasize case results or DNAPL systems, and only rarely couple controlled 3D pilot experiments with validated three-phase (air–water–LNAPL) flow simulations; TDR-based permittivity monitoring has likewise been applied mainly at lab scale20,26–29. Here, we bridge these gaps by (i) conducting large 3D pilot-scale experiments spanning spill, water-table fluctuations, and pump-and-treat; (ii) validating a three-phase numerical framework against pilot-scale observations; and (iii) integrating distributed TDR measurements to interpret phase redistribution and recovery efficiency under realistic interventions.

The study begins by detailing the experimental setup and protocols for investigating LNAPL transport in the subsurface. This is followed by an overview of the governing equations and mathematical framework for three-phase flow in porous media. Experimental and numerical results for phase distribution under different interventions are then compared and analyzed. Finally, relative permittivity data from TDR probes and numerical simulations are evaluated across different regions. This work lays the foundation for improved site characterization, sustainable groundwater management, and the evaluation of long-term remediation techniques.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

The experiments were conducted at the PluriMetric Pilot (PMP) facility located at the BRGM site in Orléans, France (see Fig. 1a). The PMP is a large-scale experimental setup, measuring 11 m in length, 3.6 m in width, and 4 m in height (Fig. 1a). This facility can be subdivided into 2, 3, or 4 independent compartments using partitions. For this study, the left compartment was used, measuring 5.2 m in length, 3.6 m in width, and 4 m in height. Before filling the tank with sand packs, all necessary monitoring (piezometer), injection, and pumping wells, along with TDR, were installed (Fig. 1b). Each piezometer well was 114 mm in external diameter, screened from − 4 m to -1 m depth, with 0.5 mm wide slots. To create a finer filter pack around the wells, PVC pipes with an external diameter of 200 mm and a length of 1 m were placed around each piezometer well. Approximately 0.8 m of surrounding sand was first packed around the PVC pipes, followed by filling the annular space with 0.6–0.8 mm calibrated sand. The PVC pipes were then manually lifted by 0.6 m to allow continued placement of surrounding sand. The PMP was progressively filled with around 125 t of calibrated sand (0.7–1.3 mm grain size), delivered in one-ton bags. This sand was added in successive 50 cm layers, without additional mechanical compaction, as it was self-compacting. The height of the sand from the bottom was 3.6 m. The permeability and porosity of the sand were measured to be approximately 285 ± 10 × 10–12 m2 and 0.35 ± 0.01, respectively. The PMP setup included 8 injection wells, 3 central pumping wells, and 12 monitoring wells (piezometers), as illustrated in Fig. 2. To simulate a real LNAPL spill, a perforated zone containing multiple injection tubes (0.4 m ×1 m) was designated for LNAPL injection between wells P1 and P2. The pilot facility was equipped with two independent injection manifolds (P1–P2 and P2–P3), but for the present experiments only the left-hand manifold (P1–P2) was used. The manifold consisted of 3 tubes (on each side), spaced 0.10 m apart and perforated along their lower halves, ensuring uniform diesel distribution. TDR probes were spatially distributed throughout the PMP to monitor bulk permittivity at various locations (Fig. 2). At each coordinate point (x, y), TDR probes were positioned at depths of 0.6, 1.2, 1.8, 2.4, and 3 m below the sand surface. For these experiments, diesel was used as the LNAPL phase and tap water as the water phase. The physical properties of these phases are given in Table 1. Densities were measured at ambient conditions using a 100 mL pycnometer, while viscosities were determined with a rotational rheometer (Haake Mars 60, Thermo Scientific) using a shallow-angle (1°) cone-plate geometry. Water density corresponds to tap water used in Orléans (France), with a measured value of 998 kg/m³ at ambient conditions. These values were used in the numerical modelling and are reported in Table 1. While Engelmann et al.30 demonstrated robust laboratory DNAPL source zone experiments and Lari et al.31 developed digital twin approaches for NSZD at field sites, the present study complements these by employing a controlled 3D pilot tank to reproduce LNAPL release, water table cycling, and recovery.

Experimental protocols

Figure 3 illustrates side view schematic of consequences of experimental interventions performed at PMP on x-z central plane, and illustrates the average conditions. Figure 3a shows the initial experimental setup, with the PMP fully packed with sand prior to water injection. For the flow experiments, water was initially injected into the PMP through the injection wells (I1, I4, I5, and I8) located on both sides of the tank at a rate of 2000 L/h. The water level was raised to a depth of 2.21 m (steady-state condition), corresponding to a water thickness of 1.39 m from the tank’s bottom (Fig. 3b,c). The depth of the water/air, LNAPL/air and water/LNAPL interfaces were measured using an interface probe. In this study, the term ‘unsaturated zone’ refers to the sand layer above the artificially established water table. Although the water level was created by injection from the bottom rather than natural infiltration, the upper layer contained residual water due to capillary rise, resulting in sub-saturated conditions (< 5% water saturation).

In the next step, diesel was introduced into the tank through infiltration perforated lines situated between wells P1 and P2 at an injection rate of 80 L/h. Diesel injection was conducted during daylight hours (typically between 9 a.m. and 5 p.m.) and paused at night. This process continued over multiple days until the diesel layer reached a thickness of 0.68 m above the water level (steady-state condition) with the total volume of injected LNAPL of 5.6 m3 (Fig. 3d,e). An interface probe (SOLINST) was used to measure air/LNAPL and LNAPL/water interface depths in monitoring wells. It employs conductivity electrodes and an optical sensor to distinguish water and hydrocarbons with 1 mm accuracy.

Subsequently, water injection was performed through the injection wells I1, I4, I5, and I8 at a depth of 3.1 m. Water was injected at a combined rate of 4 × 240 L/h for 6 h, raising the water level to a depth of 1.38 m, corresponding to a water thickness of 2.22 m from the bottom. Next, water was pumped out from well P2 at a depth of 3.1 m with a pumping rate of 700 L/h for 11 h, lowering the water level to a depth of 2.38 m, equivalent to a water thickness of 1.22 m from the bottom. The water level was then raised again to a depth of 1.4 m, corresponding to a water thickness of 2.15 m from the bottom (Fig. 3f–k). This process has been performed several times through experiments (imbibition-drainage) and the water-diesel level before the diesel pumping test was reached to height 1.39 m. In the final step, a pumping test was conducted at wells P1 and P2. Diesel was pumped out from a depth of 1.15 m at a combined rate of 2 × 50 L/h over 9 d, during daytime hours (typically between 9 a.m. and 5 p.m.) (Fig. 3l,m).

In our implementation, we assume (i) quasi-static conditions at TDR frequencies, (ii) negligible interfacial polarization compared to the strong contrast between water and non-conductive phases, (iii) volumetric saturation closure (), and (iv) a unit path-length factor (no probe-specific fitting). Numerical model

The conceptual setup for the pilot-scale experiments and corresponding numerical simulations is shown in Figs. 3, 4 and 5. The domain is considered homogeneous, isotropic, and isothermal, with negligible capillary hysteresis and without mass transfer reactions such as volatilization or dissolution, given the short time frame of each experiment. Diffusion and biodegradation were not included. The model domain corresponds to the physical dimensions of the pilot tank, discretized into a three-dimensional grid that ensures a compromise between spatial accuracy and computational cost. The sequence of operations consisted of four main stages: (i) mesh generation and initialization of the domain under hydrostatic conditions; (ii) establishment of a fully saturated zone by bottom-up water injection, ensuring stable boundary conditions; (iii) controlled LNAPL infiltration at the soil surface until quasi-steady distribution; and (iv) dynamic drainage and imbibition phases achieved by lowering and raising the water table to simulate natural groundwater fluctuation. The upper boundary was defined as constant atmospheric pressure, while the lateral and bottom boundaries were considered impermeable.

Meshing used for the full-domain simulation of the water table fluctuation case.

The system of partial differential equations and constitutive relationships governing three-phase flow in this study (Eqs. (1)–(15)) were discretized and solved using the conforming finite element simulator in COMSOL Multiphysics® (hereafter referred to as COMSOL). COMSOL’s MUMPS solver was employed with quadratic interpolation for pressure and linear interpolation for phase transport. The software has previously been utilized by the authors for modeling two-45 and three-phase29 flow in porous media and has proven effective for simulating coupled flow and transport processes at both small field scales and laboratory experimental setups. However, its application to three-phase flow in porous media remains relatively uncommon. In the present work, the Stone I model was implemented to describe the three-phase interactions, and the resulting simulations were validated and briefly discussed.

The computational domain was discretized into 110,335 tetrahedral elements for the full-domain simulation of the water table fluctuation case, shown in Fig. 4. The boundary conditions applied in various flow scenarios are illustrated in Fig. 5. During the spill stage, the water level was maintained constant, and consequently, the numerical simulation was performed exclusively within the unsaturated zone (Fig. 5a).

Results and discussions

Phases distributions in different interventions

After saturating the bottom of the tank with water, three experimental procedures were performed in the PMP: (1) LNAPL spill, (2) water table fluctuations, and (3) LNAPL pumping.

The LNAPL spill occurred on the top of the system after the primary water injection into the bottom part of the tank. At the start of the spill, the water level, measured through piezometric wells, was approximately 1.42 m. This water level was maintained constant throughout the experiments, and therefore, the numerical simulation was performed only in the unsaturated zone. A three-phase zone exists near the water table due to the capillary fringe; however, given the high permeability of the porous medium, its effect is considered negligible for the sake of numerical simplicity.

The LNAPL spill was performed in several steps, adhering to environmental protocols. The spill occurred during daylight hours (usually from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.) and resumed the next day as needed. Results from piezometric wells and simulation outcomes are shown in Fig. 6. These results indicate that the distribution of diesel floating on the water table initially forms a semi-elliptical shape. Twelve hours after the primary injection, a hump is observed above the water table near the injection zone in the horizontal plane, and LNAPL has not yet spread to the other sides of the tank. After reinjection and redistribution during the stoppage period, 12 h after the secondary injection, the LNAPL spreads more extensively across the x-y plane and forms a larger interface with the water table. This is attributed to the continued injection and the effect of gravity, which causes the LNAPL to redistribute over time. The comparison of experimental and simulated results in Fig. 6 shows good correlation, illustrating the evolution of LNAPL distribution over time and the impact of injection dynamics on its spatial behavior. The mean squared error between the numerical and experimental results is 0.002 and 0.0001 for the LNAPL-air interface at the end of 12 h after stop of primary and secondary injection. The experimental process continued until the thickness of diesel above the water table reached approximately 0.68 m.

Following the diesel spill, water table fluctuations were introduced, as described in the Materials and Methods section. The initial LNAPL layer thickness height and water-LNAPL level in this process were measured at approximately 1.39 m and 0.68 m respectively and were used as the initial value for numerical simulations. At the end of imbibition and drainage processes the heights water-LNAPL levels were measured around 2.15 m and 1.52 m. The water-LNAPL and LNAPL-air interfaces from numerical simulation results were extracted at So =0.5. Figure 8 shows a good agreement between experimental measurements from piezometric wells and numerical simulations, capturing the evolution of the water-diesel and diesel-air interfaces at the end of the imbibition and drainage cycles. The mean squared error between numerical and experimental results is 0.01 and 0.0025 for the LNAPL-air and LNAPL-water interfaces at the end of imbibition, and 0.0016 and 0.0001, respectively, at the end of drainage. The results indicate that at the end of the imbibition phase, the LNAPL layer thickness reduces to approximately 0.6 m. This reduction is attributed to the trapping of residual LNAPL in the middle zone (in this study, the ‘middle zone’ refers to the interval located between the fully water-saturated bottom and the air-saturated top of the PMP tank) due to the rising water level. Conversely, by the end of the drainage phase, the thickness increases to around 0.7 m, mainly due to the reconnection of residual LNAPL ganglia with the receding LNAPL layer. When the water table rises, water displaces LNAPL in pore throats, but capillary forces trap disconnected LNAPL ganglia within pore bodies. This hysteretic behavior results in a reduction of the mobile LNAPL layer thickness above the water Tables22,23. Upon drainage, part of the trapped LNAPL reconnects with the free-phase layer, though a fraction remains immobilized as residual saturation. This pilot-scale system was intentionally designed with an artificial water table created by bottom-up injection to ensure better control of hydraulic conditions and monitoring accuracy.

Comparison of relative permeability measured by TDR probes and simulation results

TDR probes were installed at various locations in the PMP to measure relative permittivity, providing insight into phase saturation. According to Eq. 17, if the saturation of one phase is known, the saturation of the other two can be estimated based on their relative permittivity37. In the top and middle zones, the system consists only of air and LNAPL. Since both phases are electrically non-conductive, the measured relative permittivity values remain low and very similar, which makes it difficult to distinguish between them. For instance, the relative permittivity typically ranges from 3 to 5 for fully air- and LNAPL-saturated zones, making it difficult to extract accurate saturation data from TDR probes, especially considering measurement uncertainties20. On the other hand, when water, an electrically conductive phase, is present, the differences in relative permittivity between the phases become more pronounced, allowing for more reliable estimations.

The saturation of each phase for each time-step of the simulation is translated into its relative permittivity and using Eq. (17) the bulk permittivity at the cell level of the position of a specific TDR is calculated. The calculated value, then is compared against the measured relative permittivity during the experiment. Using the relative permittivity values provided in Table 3, we compared TDR probe measurements with numerical simulations in different zones of the system during interventions involving water table fluctuations and LNAPL pumping. The measured values were averaged at specific depths (1.2 m, 1.8 m, and 2.4 m), where multiple TDR probes were placed, and their readings were averaged to obtain representative values for each level. The comparison of experimental and numerical results is presented in Fig. 11.

Comparison of relative permittivity values obtained by experiments and numerical simulations in three different depths during (a) water table fluctuations, (b) pumping LNAPL.

For the water table fluctuation experiment (Fig. 11a), relative permittivity remained nearly constant at the top and bottom levels. At the bottom (initially water-saturated), this is expected since no significant phase change occurred. At the top (initially air-saturated), although LNAPL invaded the air-filled space, the relative permittivity showed little variation due to the similar values for air and LNAPL. In the middle region (initially LNAPL-saturated), the relative permittivity was around 4. As water infiltrated this region, permittivity increased to approximately 13 and remained stable, indicating water retention at residual LNAPL saturation. Once the water table receded and LNAPL re-invaded the middle zone, the permittivity decreased accordingly. The average absolute relative errors between the experimental and simulation relative permittivity results for water table fluctuations are around 0.04, 0.11, 0.12 for depths of 2.4 m, 1.8 m and 1.2 m. The increase in the value of permittivity is due to the increase of the water saturation as the main polarizing fluid phase. The relative permittivity of water is considerably higher than that of air and that of LNAPL and through the imbibition process, as the water saturation increases so does the bulk relative permittivity. This increase is observed sharper due to the hysteresis effects of imbibition than that of those in the drainage. In addition, during the drainage phase, the residual water saturation trapped in pores, leads to a slightly higher value of residual permittivity than those observed before the drainage process.

For the LNAPL pumping experiment, the measured relative permittivity was around 14 at the water-saturated bottom level, while both the middle and top levels exhibited low values due to the presence of non-conductive phases (LNAPL and air) (Fig. 11b). The results confirm that TDR probes are highly effective for detecting phase saturation when an electrically conductive phase (e.g., water) is present. However, in a multiphase system composed only of two electrically non-conductive phases, distinguishing between the phases remains challenging. In particular, while the experimental measurements at the top and middle levels showed similar relative permittivity values due to the difficulty of distinguishing between air and LNAPL, the simulation produced a slight difference. This distinction arises because the model assigns separate electrical permittivity to each phase ((-) = 1.05, (-) = 2.7), thereby reproducing a theoretical contrast that is difficult to detect experimentally. In practice, the measured values are affected by soil composition heterogeneity, humidity in the air phase, and the precision limits of TDR probes, all of which contribute to overlapping permittivity values in these zones. Consequently, this discrepancy can be interpreted as an expected limitation of the experimental method. Overall, the consistency between experimental and numerical results is stronger in regions where water is present, owing to the much higher dielectric contrast with air and LNAPL. The average absolute relative errors between the experimental and simulation relative permittivity results for water LNAPL pumping are around 0.03, 0.25, 0.24 for depths of 2.4 m, 1.8 m and 1.2 m.

Perspectives for future field applications

The results are promising and demonstrate potential for further investigations. However, several assumptions and limitations need to be addressed in future work. In this study, to maintain a controlled system, the effects of heterogeneity in both geological structure and soil composition were not considered. However, at the field scale, geological heterogeneity, such as barriers, fractures, and multilayer formations, adds complexity to multiphase flow processes, influencing saturation behavior across different layers and altering flow mechanisms in fractures as well as the sharp changes in entry pressure between the fractures and the matrix38.

In real contaminated sites, additional components such as organic matter and clay are present, which may contribute to contaminant attenuation and significantly impact its transport dynamics in porous media39. Also the dissolution of LNAPL phase in water even at low values can affect the transport dynamics.

A thorough understanding of flow and transport mechanisms during contaminant migration is crucial for the effective testing and implementation of remediation strategies. While pump-and-treat methods have been widely used for soil remediation, the use of non-Newtonian fluids, such as polymers, for contaminant displacement can enhance recovery efficiency40. To numerically simulate the dynamics of multiphase flow during polymer injection, three-phase flow modeling must be coupled with transport equations for the polymer. Additionally, the non-Newtonian behavior of polymer solutions must be incorporated into numerical models41,42.

Furthermore, optimizing key parameters, such as the placement and depth of both injection and recovery wells at contaminated sites, is essential for improving remediation efficiency43,44. Integrating remediation strategies with non-invasive geophysical methods, such as Electrical Resistivity Tomography and Induced Polarization, holds significant potential for assessing NAPL-contaminated sites and monitoring remediation progress during and after treatment36,45,46.

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive investigation of LNAPL migration and redistribution in variably saturated (Sw ≈ 1 below the water table and Sw ≈ 0 above) subsurface zones through experimental analysis and numerical modeling. The large-scale PluriMetric Pilot (PMP) tank (5.2 × 3.6 × 3.6 m) allowed for controlled diesel injection, displacement, and recovery, with TDR probes providing valuable insights into phase saturation and distribution. Numerical simulations incorporating the Stone I and van Genuchten-Mualem models effectively reproduced experimental trends.

The results highlight key mechanisms governing LNAPL behavior, including initial spill dynamics, redistribution driven by water table fluctuations, and response to LNAPL pumping. Experimental observations in the PMP, supported by numerical modeling, revealed that LNAPL initially accumulates as a localized hump before spreading along the water table. Water table fluctuations induce partial entrapment in the middle zone, where imbibition reduces mobile LNAPL thickness, while drainage promotes partial reconnection of trapped phases. LNAPL pumping experiments demonstrated the formation of cones of depression around extraction wells, illustrating both the efficiency and limitations of LNAPL recovery.

Numerical simulations successfully captured LNAPL saturation trends, the evolution of water-LNAPL and LNAPL-air interfaces, and variations in relative permittivity measured by TDR probes. The findings demonstrate that TDR probes provide sensitive, spatially distributed indicators of phase redistribution particularly when water is present, thus supporting the suitability of the adopted dielectric mixing model under our pilot-scale conditions.

These findings advance the understanding of LNAPL transport processes and provide valuable guidance for optimizing remediation strategies, including well placement, pumping rates, and monitoring approaches. Future research should examine the influence of heterogeneity, soil layering, and alternative remediation techniques to enhance predictive models and improve LNAPL recovery efficiency in complex subsurface environments.
