Abstract




A rounding and clustering-based exact algorithm for the p-center problem

Abstract

The
p
-center problem consists in selecting
p
facilities from a set of possible sites and allocating a set of clients to them in such a way that the maximum distance between a client and the facility to which it is allocated is minimized. This paper proposes a new scalable exact solution algorithm based on client clustering and an iterative distance rounding procedure. The client clustering enables to initialize and update a subset of clients for which the
p
-center problem is iteratively solved. The rounding drastically reduces the number of distinct distances considered at each iteration. Our algorithm is tested on 396 benchmark instances with up to 1.9 million clients and facilities. We outperform the two state-of-the-art exact methods considered when
p
is not very small (i.e.,
p>5
).

Keywords:
discrete location;
p
-center problem; clustering algorithm; distance rounding

1
Introduction

The
p
-center problem

(PCP)
is a fundamental problem in location science (
[
Laporte et al., 2019
]
). It consists of choosing
p
facility sites to minimize the maximum distance from a client to its nearest open facility. The
p
open facilities are called
centers
. Formally the problem is defined as follows. We consider a set of
N
clients, a set of
M
potential facility sites to open, and their corresponding index set
\mathcal{N}=\{1,...,N\}
and
\mathcal{M}=\{1,...,M\}
. Let
d_{ij}
be the distance between client
i
and site
j
, and
p
be an integer. The objective is to find a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{M}
such that
|S|\leq p
and the value
z=\displaystyle\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\;(\displaystyle\min_{j\in S}\;d_{ij})
is minimized. This distance
z
is called the
radius
of solution
S
.

The
p
-center is a NP-hard problem (
[
Kariv and Hakimi, 1979
]
) and is applied in various contexts, including facility location, emergency service planning, telecommunications network design, healthcare planning, and supply chain management. It allows to decide the best places for centers, such as warehouses or hospitals, to minimize distances or response times (
[
Çalık et al., 2019
]
). There are a variety of exact and approximate methods for this classical problem and its variants.

In this paper, we focus on the exact solution of large-scale instances. We combine existing ideas from the literature and introduce original ones in order to design an efficient solution algorithm.

Since the optimal radius is equal to one of the distances
d_{ij}
, the first idea consists in considering a subset of the distances and deducing bounds from the result. This idea already appears in the binary search algorithm introduced in
[
Minieka, 1970
]
and
[
Daskin, 1995
]
, where for a chosen distance
\delta
one can solve a set-covering problem to deduce whether the optimal radius is more than
\delta
or not. This allows to iteratively divide the number of possible distances by two. This idea was for example extended by
[
Calik and Tansel, 2013
]
by only considering a subset of distances. We extend this idea by iteratively rounding down the distances with increasing precision, thus allowing us to drastically divide the number of distances at each iteration.

It is important to stress that for large-scale instances, the computation of
\{d_{ij}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N},j\in\mathcal{M}}
is prohibitive both in terms of time and memory.
To tackle this problem, the second idea that we leverage is to only consider a subset of clients
\mathcal{R}
, subsequently called
representatives
. The optimal solution of the
(PCP)
when only considering the clients
\mathcal{R}
enables to both obtain a lower bound and an upper bound on the optimal radius. This idea has already been used in
[
Chen and Handler, 1987
]
and
[
Chen and Chen, 2009
]
to start with an initial set of representatives and keep increasing it until the lower bound and the upper bound coincide. It was also recently used and improved by
[
Contardo et al., 2019
]
to develop one of the most effective methods of solving large-scale instances.
Since computing all the distances is not possible for large instances, we assume in the following that the instances are metric and that we are given as an input the coordinates of the clients and sites instead of their distances. This for example ensures that we are able to compute distances between pairs of clients or the barycenter of a set of clients.

Contributions

We introduce a new exact method for the
(PCP)
which combines the two aforementioned ideas of considering a subset of distances and clients. Our main contributions are the following:

1.

We perform a more and more precise rounding of the distances which both enables to quickly provide strong bounds on the optimal solution value and to drastically reduce the number of distinct distances considered at any step.

2.

We use a clustering method to
initialize the subset
\mathcal{R}
of representatives of the clients. We additionally take advantage of the partition obtained by introducing the concept of cluster quadrants which enables us to update set
\mathcal{R}
by only adding a limited number of additional representatives.

3.

We design an efficient procedure to update the list of sites which are dominated. Depending on whether the representatives set
\mathcal{R}
is updated or the bounds on the optimal solution are improved, this strategy can significantly reduce the number of sites to compare.

4.

We conduct an extensive computational analysis where we first assess the impact of different original features of our method. Then, we compare our algorithm to two state-of-the art methods (
[
Contardo et al., 2019
,
Gaar and Sinnl, 2022
]
), and we show that our method outperforms the other two methods when
p
is not very small (i.e., greater than
5
). We consider substantially harder instances than those previously used to evaluate these two exact methods by increasing the maximal value of
p
from
30
to
200
and the maximal number of clients and sites from one million to
1.9
million, all methods being run on the same computer.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews the related literature. Section
3
presents our exact solution method. Illustrations and extensive computational results are presented in Sections
4
and
5
. Finally, some conclusions are drawn in Section
6
.

Notations

A solution of the
p
-center problem is represented by a vector
y\in\{0,1\}^{M}
such that
y_{j}
is equal to
1
if and only if site
j\in\mathcal{M}
is opened in solution
y
. The set of opened sites in solution
y
is denoted by
S(y)\subset\mathcal{M}
. For a given subset of clients
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
,
rad_{\mathcal{R}}(y)
corresponds to the radius of
y
when only the clients in
\mathcal{R}
are considered (i.e.,
rad_{\mathcal{R}}(y)=\displaystyle\max_{i\in\mathcal{R}}\min_{j\in S(y)}d_{ij}
). Thus, the radius of solution
y
is
rad_{\mathcal{N}}(y)
. The
allocation distance
of a client
i\in\mathcal{N}
in solution
y
is
\displaystyle\min_{j\in S(y)}d_{ij}
. A client is said to be
covered within distance

d\in\mathbb{R}
by solution
y
if its allocation distance is at most
d
. Let
K
be the number of distinct distances in
\{d_{ij}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N},j\in\mathcal{M}}
and let
\mathcal{D}=\{D^{1},...,D^{K}\}
be these distances in increasing order.

For a given
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, let
d^{\alpha}_{ij}
be distance
d_{ij}
rounded down to its nearest multiple of
10^{\alpha}
. We denote by
(PCP_{\alpha})
the
p
-center problem in which distances
d^{\alpha}_{ij}
are considered instead of
d_{ij}
. Let
rad^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{R}}(y)
be the radius of
y
for clients
\mathcal{R}
in
(PCP_{\alpha})
(i.e.,
rad^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{R}}(y)=\displaystyle\max_{i\in\mathcal{R}}\min_{j\in S(y)}d^{\alpha}_{ij}
).

Literature review

Several MILP formulations of the
p
-center problem have been presented and discussed in the literature. The first and classical MILP formulation for the
(PCP)
was proposed in
[
Daskin, 1996
]
that consider integer variables to represent the location and allocation decisions, and one real variable to represent the radius.
[
Elloumi et al., 2004
]
introduce an alternative formulation which relaxation bound dominates that of
[
Daskin, 1996
]
. Other formulations are presented in
[
Calik and Tansel, 2013
]
,
[
Ales and Elloumi, 2018
]
, and
[
Gaar and Sinnl, 2022
]
. We refer the reader to
[
Durán Mateluna, 2024
]
for an extensive computational comparison of these formulations. The direct solution of any of these formulation in a standard solver fails at optimally solving instances with several thousands of clients and sites in reasonable time.

A key idea to solve the
p
-center was introduced by
[
Minieka, 1970
]
and presented in
[
Daskin, 1995
]
. For a given distance
D^{k}\in\mathcal{D}
, determining if the optimal radius of the
(PCP)
is at most equal to
D^{k}
can be achieved through the solution of a minimum-cardinality set cover problem denoted by
(SC_{k})
. In
(SC_{k})
, one constraint is associated to each client
i\in\mathcal{N}
. This constraint imposes that at least one of the sites
\{j\in\mathcal{M}\penalty\ |\penalty\ d_{ij}\leq D^{k}\}
must be opened. Consequently, if there exists a solution with at most
p
open sites, then the optimal solution value of
(PCP)
is at most
D^{k}
. From this observation,
[
Minieka, 1970
]
proposes to solve the
(PCP)
by incrementing
k
until
(SC_{k})
leads to a solution of value at most
p
. As presented in Algorithm
1
, this can be improved by solving the set cover problems through a binary search on
\mathcal{D}
(
[
Garfinkel et al., 1977
]
). To reduce the size of
\mathcal{D}
, an initial solution is additionally computed at Step
1
.

[
Daskin, 1996
]
also considered a binary search approach, but solved maximum coverage problems instead of set cover problems.
[
Al-Khedhairi, 2005
]
propose implementation enhancements that reduce the number of iterations.
[
Elloumi et al., 2004
]
improved Algorithm
1
by first relaxing the integrity of the set cover variables in Step
1
. This enables to quickly reach a very strong lower bound called
LB^{*}
. Different heuristics are also introduced to obtain an upper bound called
UB^{*}
. These bounds significantly reduce the possible values of the optimal radius index in
\mathcal{D}
and thus the number of binary search iterations.

To speed up the resolution of the set covers within the binary search, the authors also identified the non-dominated sites.

Definition 1
(
[

A site

We can observe that with this definition, two different sites cannot dominate each other.

Proposition 1

[

There exists an optimal solution of

From this proposition, we deduce that solving
(PCP_{\mathcal{R}})

(PCP_{\mathcal{R}})

If the optimality is not reached (i.e.,

), the set of considered clients
\mathcal{R}
is increased. This step can have a significant impact on the solution time. Indeed, on the one hand, adding too much clients will slow down the solution of the
(PCP)
at each subsequent iteration. On the other hand, adding to few or non-relevant clients may increase the number of remaining iterations required to reach an optimal solution.

To add a relevant set of clients, the authors first solve maximal coverage problems heuristically to generate a set of alternative solutions
Y=\{y^{1},...,y^{S}\}

y^{h}
which enables to maximize the number of clients in
\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{R}
covered within distance

(i.e., the optimal radius of
(PCP_{\mathcal{R}})
). Moreover, to ensure that

it is imposed in these problems that all clients in
\mathcal{R}
must be covered within distance

by

. Since the exact solution of such problems would be too time consuming, the authors solved them through a local search heuristic.
In this heuristic, a small set of clients

is first randomly selected. Solution
y^{h}
, which is initially equal to
\hat{y}
, is then iteratively modified by:

1.

randomly replacing a given number of sites in
y^{h}
; and then

2.

iteratively applying the site replacement that maximizes the number of clients in
C
covered within distance
LB
until a local minima is reached.

If solution
y^{h}
is the best currently known solution of the maximum coverage problem, it is added to
Y
. Moreover, if
y^{h}
enables to cover all clients in set
C
within distance
LB
, new clients are added to
C
. The algorithm stops when the size of
Y
remains the same for a given number of consecutive iterations. Finally, set
\mathcal{R}
is updated through a completion heuristic that identifies a subset of clients of
\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{R}
that together would lead to an increase of the radius for all the solutions
\{\hat{y},y^{1},...,y^{S}\}
(Step
2
).

We now present a new efficient exact algorithm based on a clustering of the client that takes also advantage of an iterative distance rounding procedure.

3
An exact rounding and clustering-based algorithm for (PCP)

This section is dedicated to the presentation of our exact solution method. A high-level view of it is described in Algorithm
3
. We start by the clustering phase that partitions the set of clients into a given number
k\geq p
of clusters. Then, the subset
\mathcal{R}
of representatives is initialized with the clusters medoids. The lower bound
LB
is initialized
0
and a feasible solution
y
is chosen using
\mathcal{R}
to initialize the upper bound
UB
. These initialization steps
3
–
3
are detailed in Subsection
3.1
.

Then in the remaining steps
3
–
3
, we perform our iterative rounding. The decreasing parameter
\alpha
gives the precision of the rounding. It is initialized from the number of digits in
UB
. At each iteration, the crucial step is the exact solution of problem
(PCP_{\alpha})
, i.e., the
p
-center problem with the rounding precision
10^{\alpha}
. This is detailed in Algorithm
4
and in Subsection
3.2
. At each iteration of this algorithm, the set of representatives
\mathcal{R}
is enlarged by adding, within each cluster, uncovered clients for different
(PCP)
solutions. These solutions include the current solution as well as additional ones obtained by local search. We also introduce the notion of cluster quadrants in order to efficiently select the clients added to
\mathcal{R}
. The update of the subset of clients
\mathcal{R}
is detailed in Section
3.3
. Another important aspect of our method is the elimination of dominated sites for the set of representatives (see Definition
1
) within the solution procedure of each rounded problem
(PCP_{\alpha})
. We put a focus on our novel ideas on this aspect in Subsection
3.4
.

We now present our new algorithm and its main features in Subsection
3.1
to
3.4
.

3.2
Exact solution of the
p
-center problem for the rounding precision
10^{\alpha}

The bottleneck of Algorithm
3
is the solution of the
(PCP)
subproblems through binary search (Step
2
). Since the number of binary search iteration is in
\mathcal{O}(log(|\mathcal{D}|))
, we reduce the size of
\mathcal{D}
by iteratively considering an increasingly precise rounding of the distances.

More precisely, for a given rounding factor
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, each distance
d_{ij}
is rounded down to the nearest multiple of
10^{\alpha}
.

Definition 2
(Distance rounding)

For a given client
i\in\mathcal{N}
and a given site
j\in\mathcal{M}
, together with a lower bound
LB
and an upper bound
UB
on the optimal value of
(PCP)

d^{\alpha}_{ij}\triangleq\min\left(\max\left(LB,10^{\alpha}\left\lfloor\frac{d_{ij}}{10^{\alpha}}\right\rfloor\right),\penalty\ UB+1\right).

The distance
d^{\alpha}_{ij}
is imposed to be in the interval
[LB,UB+1]
in order to further reduce
|\mathcal{D}|
.

The rounding factor
\alpha
is initially equal to the number of digits in the radius of a heuristic solution
y
minus one (Step
3
). It is then decremented at each iteration thus enabling the
(PCP)
to be solved with a more precise rounding at the next iteration (Step
3
).

At Step
4
, we use function
solvePCP
α
to compute an optimal solution of the
(PCP)
problem
(PCP_{\alpha})
in which each distance
d_{ij}
is replaced by
d^{\alpha}_{ij}
. Note that this function could be replaced by any
(PCP)
solution method (e.g. Algorithm
2
) provided that the distances can be rounded down whenever they are computed. Function
solvePCP
α
takes as an input both clients sets
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{N}
. It considers
\mathcal{R}
as the initial set of clients and update it by adding clients from
\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{R}
until an optimal solution
\hat{y}
of
(PCP_{\alpha})
is obtained.

The following property ensures that optimally solving
(PCP_{\alpha})
enables to obtain a lower bound on the optimal radius of the original problem (Step
3
).

Property 1

For any solution
\hat{y}
,
rad_{\mathcal{N}}^{\alpha}(\hat{y})\leq rad_{\mathcal{N}}(\hat{y})
.

Proof.
Since
d^{\alpha}_{(ij)}\leq d_{ij}
for all
ij\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{M}
, then
\displaystyle rad_{\mathcal{N}}^{\alpha}=\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\min_{j\in S(\hat{y})}d^{\alpha}_{ij}\leq\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\min_{j\in S(\hat{y})}d_{ij}=rad_{\mathcal{N}}(\hat{y})
.

\square

After obtaining the lower bound
rad^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{N}}(\hat{y})
, we
compute the radius of solution
\hat{y}
without rounding down the distances (Step
3
) which constitutes an upper bound. If it is currently best known, we update
UB
and
\hat{y}
(Step
3
). Note that since the radius of
\hat{y}
on the rounded down distances is equal to
LB
, the new value of
UB
is at most
LB+10^{\alpha}
. Thus, throughout its iterations, this algorithm provides an increasingly tight guarantee on the quality of its bounds. Moreover, at each iteration, there can only be at most
10
distinct distances
d^{\alpha}_{ij}
.

Note that we only cluster the clients once at Step
3
of Algorithm
3
. Since the clients added to
\mathcal{R}
within function
solvePCP
α
are likely to be relevant for the subsequent iterations, they remain in
\mathcal{R}
until the end of the algorithm.

3.3
Updating set
\mathcal{R}
through the clusters quadrants and local search

The choice of the clients added to
\mathcal{R}
at each iteration has a crucial impact on our algorithm. Indeed, increasing
\mathcal{R}
will tend to slow down the solution of the
(PCP)
which would be an incentive not to add too many clients. However, adding too few clients will make the lower bound
LB

To find a relevant compromise, we can first observe that only adding to
\mathcal{R}
clients which are covered within
LB
in the current solution
y
cannot lead to an improvement of
LB
at the next iteration (since the same solution
y
would still lead to a radius of value
LB
). Thus, we focus on the clients of

which are not covered in
y
within distance
LB
. Let
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(y)
be this set of clients.

Since the

\mathcal{R}
.
For each cluster, we want to add clients which are as far away from their medoid as possible (so that they are more likely to increase the radius) and which are also as far away from each other as possible (to make it harder to cover them without covering other points in the cluster). Consequently, as represented in Figure

\mathcal{R}

which is the furthest away from the representative of the cluster, if any. For example, in Figure

, three clients are removed from two of the clusters and only one client is removed for the leftmost cluster. We apply this process to all solutions
\hat{y},y^{1},...,y^{S}

3.4
Improving the computation of the sites dominations

In Algorithm
4
, whenever the lower bound
LB
or the upper bound
UB
is improved, each distance
d^{\alpha}_{ij}
is updated according to Definition
2
. This enables to both reduce the number of distances considered in the binary search algorithm and potentially increase the number of dominated sites.

To reduce the computation time, we first observe that between two consecutive iterations, the set of dominated sites
J_{\mathcal{R}}
only needs to be updated if either:

1.

one of the bounds
LB
and/or
UB
has been improved;

2.

the number of considered clients
\mathcal{R}
has been increased.

Moreover, in both cases, we do not need to test the domination of all possible couples of sites. More precisely, when the first case occurs, we only need to check if the non-dominated sites become dominated. Indeed, improving the bounds reduce the number of distinct distances, thus a dominated site remains dominated. Furthermore, we do not need to check if a non-dominated site is dominated by a dominated site.

In the second case, we only need to check if dominated sites become non-dominated. Indeed, adding clients to
\mathcal{R}
cannot make a non-dominated site dominated
1
1

1

Except in the case where two sites had the same distance to all clients before
\mathcal{R}
was updated.
. To improve the computation of the dominances in that case, we define the vector
dom\in\mathbb{N}^{M}
such that
dom_{j}
is equal to
0
if site
j
is not dominated and to the lowest index of a site that dominates
j
otherwise. If
dom_{j}\neq 0
, we can avoid testing if site
j
is dominated by sites
\{1,...,dom_{j}-1\}
. Indeed, if all these sites were not dominating
j
before the addition of the clients, they can not dominate it after either.

First, in Section

[

and the branch-and-cut from
[

5.1
Features comparison of our method

We first compare the performances of our method in four different configurations:

1.

All features activated
: we use Algorithm
3
to solve the
(PCP)
which calls Algorithm
4
at each iteration to solve
(PCP_{\alpha})
.

2.

Without the local search
: we do not use the local search algorithm at step
4
of Algorithm
4
. Consequently, at step
4

is considered to add clients to
\mathcal{R}
instead of

3.

Without dominations

4

4.

Without rounding the distances

The results are summarized in Figure

As a consequence, in the next section we do not compute the dominations for these instances. Finally, we can see that removing the local search tends to slow down the resolution but enables to improve the number of instances solved to optimality. Since our objective is to improve the solution of hard instances, we keep this feature in the following.

6
Conclusions

We introduce a new exact method to solve the
p
-center problem based on an iterative rounding of the distances which greatly limits the number of possible value for the optimal solution at each iteration. Our experimental analysis highlights that this new feature is by far the one that most improves the performances of our algorithm and allows it to scale up. Moreover, our method includes a clustering of the clients which enables to efficiently initialize the subset of representatives
\mathcal{R}
. We also introduce the notion of cluster quadrants which enables to update
\mathcal{R}
by adding to it relevant clients which are not close to one another and not too many either. Finally, we also efficiently manage the computation of the site dominations and limit the number of MILP subproblems to solve by considering their linear relaxation as often as possible. Through extensive experiments, we compare our approaches to the iterative algorithm from
[
Contardo et al., 2019
]
and the branch-and-cut from
[
Gaar and Sinnl, 2022
]
. As soon as parameter
p
is greater than
5
, our method significantly outperforms these two state-of-the-art algorithms in terms of resolution time, final gap and number of instances solved to optimality. In the future we will try to better handle site dominations of the largest instances and to speed up the update of the subset of clients.
