Privacy Leakage Overshadowed by Views of AI: A Study on Human Oversight of Privacy in Language Model Agent
Zhiping Zhang, Bingcan Guo, Tianshi Li

Abstract
Language model (LM) agents that act on users’ behalf for personal tasks (e.g., replying emails) can boost productivity, but are also susceptible to unintended privacy leakage risks. We present the first study on people’s capacity to oversee the privacy implications of the LM agents. By conducting a task-based survey ( N = 300 N=300 ), we investigate how people react to and assess the response generated by LM agents for asynchronous interpersonal communication tasks, compared with a response they wrote. We found that people may favor the agent response with more privacy leakage over the response they drafted or consider both good, leading to an increased harmful disclosure from 15.7% to 55.0%. We further identified six privacy profiles to characterize distinct patterns of concerns, trust, and privacy preferences in LM agents. Our findings shed light on designing agentic systems that enable privacy-preserving interactions and achieve bidirectional alignment on privacy preferences to help users calibrate trust.



1
Introduction

Recent advances in language models have led to new applications of Language Model agents (LM agents) such as AutoGPT
[
47
]
, AgentGPT
[
2
]
and personal.ai
[
21
]
.
Unlike basic language models or non-agentic AI systems, LM agents are inherently endowed with agency, allowing them to (semi-)automatically handle complex real-world tasks, such as accessing and retrieving information from connected databases (e.g., a user’s calendar) to generate and reply to emails
[
40
,
63
]
.
These LM agents free users from having to instruct the LM step by step, potentially increasing productivity.
However, this increased agency also means that LM agents can make decisions with limited human supervision, which raises new privacy challenges, especially in interpersonal communication where the agents act on behalf of the user to share information with other people.

What if LM agents share information the users did not intend to disclose?
Prior studies found that even without malicious attackers, LM agents can have unintended privacy leakage in their actions
[
39
,
55
]
.
For example,
Shao et al. 2024
demonstrated a case where an LM agent accesses the user John’s calendar data to generate an email reply, which shares the information that John is “talking to a few companies about switching jobs” in an email to John’s manager without John’s explicit consent.
This issue not only risks
violating one’s own privacy
but also impacts
bystanders
, as LM agents might inadvertently share information about other people in the users’ connected database.
Such unintentional privacy leaks occur because LMs lack the ability to reason about and operate under contextual privacy norms
[
55
,
39
]
.

Introducing human oversight to AI systems has generated interest among researchers and regulators
[
29
,
20
,
14
]
.
The European AI Act has begun to discuss keeping human in the loop to prevent harm and ensure ethical alignment with human values
[
14
]
.
In LM agents, allowing humans to monitor the actions of an LM agent, such as reviewing and editing the reply generated by an email agent, could potentially address the unintentional privacy leakage issues.

However, employing human oversight over LM agents to prevent privacy leakage presents challenges
[
67
,
20
]
.
People could hold flawed mental models of the LM systems, which leads to incorrect expectations of the systems’ behavior
[
74
,
32
]
.
Furthermore, overtrust in technological systems, often associated with “misunderstanding the risks associated with an action”
[
66
]
, can result in people overlooking privacy risks and becoming vulnerable to privacy violations
[
75
,
68
]
.

The subjectivity and ambiguity in privacy also raise a normative question: What should be defined as privacy leakage in text disclosure?
According to the contextual integrity (CI) theory
[
42
]
, any information shared by LM agents that does not conform to the contextual privacy norms can be considered leakage.
Some existing studies operationalized CI to measure privacy leakage in LM agents by defining privacy norms based on regulations or social norms
[
55
,
17
,
39
]
.
These methods take into account the general moral values, while overlooking the fact that privacy preferences vary among individuals
[
41
,
64
]
.

Gabriel 2020
introduced multiple approaches to measure human values for model alignment.
The revealed preferences, which are directly reflected in a person’s sharing behavior, while easy to collect, could suffer from the privacy paradox and may not represent the users’ best interest.
Alternatively, informed preferences refer to preferences that a person would have if they were fully informed and rational, which may better reflect an individual’s true intentions, though the informing process can be challenging to implement.
The multiplicity of privacy preference definitions calls for a better understanding of their influences on the quantifiable privacy leakage, which further affects the the alignment efforts.

Despite the research that uncovers the novel privacy threats related to LM agents
[
39
,
55
]
, there has been no in-depth investigation into the efficacy of human oversight in addressing this issue.
In this work, we take the initial step to address this gap and formulate the following research questions:

RQ1

What are people’s primary concerns with LM agents, and what is the role of privacy?

RQ2

How well do people oversee privacy leakage in LM agents’ actions? What distinct patterns emerge among users?

RQ3

How does privacy leakage measured based on revealed preferences, informed preferences, and general privacy norms differ?

To answer these questions, we conducted a task-based online survey with 300 participants.
Each participant was asked to complete an asynchronous interpersonal communication task, such as replying an email.
As shown in
Figure 1
, they were introduced to an LM agent and asked to choose between their own response and a response generated by an LM agent, which included sensitive items that violate contextual privacy norms.
They were then asked to explain their reasoning.
Afterward, participants were informed of the privacy tuples in the scenario and asked to rate the perceived harmfulness of the sensitive items, which we used as the ground truth to evaluate each individual’s privacy leakage.

Figure 1
:
In the study, we guide the participants to experience an LM agent by asking them to first draft a response based on the synthetic user data in the presented scenario, and then review and compare with a response generated by an LM agent. We show a participant’s drafted response, preference, and justification (P137) to demonstrate the cases where the user failed to recognize the privacy leakage caused by the AI.

Our results show that participants often included fewer privacy leakages in their own draft, while many selected the response written by LM agents.
48.0% of participants preferred the LM agent’s response or considered both responses equally good, despite the agent’s response containing more privacy leakages, leading to an increase in privacy leakage from 15.7% to a range between 38.4% (only considering the strict preference of the agent’s response) and 55.0% (also considering the “both are good” selections).
These findings suggest that putting users in control of the final action of an LM agent is not adequate to prevent privacy risks, alluding to broader challenges in human oversight of AI systems.

We then conducted a clustering analysis which categorized participants into six privacy profiles, based on their oversight outcomes, concerns, and trust in AI.
Our findings revealed the heterogeneous reactions of people to the privacy leakage in the LM agent’s action.
Except for the
Privacy Advocate
group (28.3%) that selected their own draft which contained less leakage and mentioned privacy considerations in their justifications, other groups exhibited limited privacy awareness, and primarily attributed their choices to their positive or negative perceptions of AI’s non-privacy-related capabilities (e.g., personal tone, catchy wordings).
In other words, although some participants made choices that resulted in less privacy leakage, it was more coincidental, as the privacy risks appeared to be temporarily overshadowed by their views of AI.
We also identified a significant decrease in trust and comfortableness of task delegation in some groups after the task.

Finally, we uncovered varied levels of consistency between the subjective perceptions of harmfulness and the general privacy norms, as well as the inter-participant variance of preferences, across different scenarios.
These findings highlight the complex and individualized nature of privacy preferences, raising important questions about how to design LM agents that respect diverse user preferences and build calibrated trust with various users, contributing empirical insights that can guide further efforts of personalization and bidirectional alignment
[
58
]
for privacy in agentic systems.

2.1
Privacy Challenges in LM Agents

Language Model agents (LM agents), also known as advanced AI assistants
[
36
]
or agentic AI
[
56
]
, are systems that harness the capacity of LMs to autonomously perform actions based on users’ intentions. Unlike non-agentic AI chatbots, LM agents autonomously make decisions and seamlessly utilize available tools such as API plugins
[
45
,
34
]
, web browsers
[
2
]
, and external devices
[
43
]
, to solve real-world tasks without users’ continuous intervention.
In
Figure 2
, we present a synthesis of common use cases of LM agents organized by
task domains
and
interaction type
. In this paper, we focus on LM agents that act on behalf of individual users in asynchronous interpersonal communications
[
1
,
3
,
4
]
where preserving privacy and adhering to privacy norms is important.

LM agents introduce privacy challenges when completing these tasks due to their inherent limitations of language models capabilities and agent’s autonomy.
Language models have weak reasoning capacities to understand contextual privacy norms in user input, making it difficult for them to accurately decide whether specific information is safe and appropriate to include in the output
[
39
]
.
Moreover, intermediate actions that deviate from the user’s intended purpose can lead to privacy breaches that are difficult to identify
[
5
]
.

Furthermore, prior studies have shown that people hold flawed mental models of LM chatbots, which hinder their ability to anticipate certain privacy risks
[
74
]
.
As LM agents possess more autonomy and opacity, users may also encounter challenges in perceiving privacy risks.
Understanding, measuring, and mitigating the privacy risks of LM agents has been an active area of recent research
[
50
,
55
]
.
In this work, we take a human-centered perspective to examine the issue of unintended privacy leakage in LM agent’s actions.

2.2
Human Oversight and Trust of AI

Human oversight has been recognized as a critical mechanism in AI governance to enhance system accuracy and safety and upholding human values in technology
[
14
]
.
Regulations such as the European AI Act emphasize that high-risk AI systems should be designed to allow “natural persons can oversee their functioning, ensure that they are used as intended and that in their impacts are addressed over the system’s lifecycle”
[
14
]
.
However, the effectiveness of human oversight also faces criticism, primarily questioning the capabilities of the individuals responsible for oversight
[
60
,
67
,
20
]
.
Overtrust in AI can have detrimental effects, as users may overly rely on AI outputs and fail to detect errors
[
60
]
.
On the other hand, too little trust in AI could lead people to override accurate AI outputs
[
60
]
.
Meanwhile, trust is also an important factor to influence people’s privacy behavior, including both disclosure and privacy-preserving actions
[
44
,
62
]
, which makes the oversight of AI privacy issues more complex.
The concept of trust calibration is emphasized in Human-AI teaming and AI-assisted decision-making
[
7
,
73
]
.
It highlights the importance of forming a correct mental model of the AI’s limitations, so users know when to trust or distrust the AI
[
7
]
.

As most studies on oversight focus on the role of experts in AI oversight
[
65
,
60
]
, several frameworks have been proposed to define the requirements for oversight professionals to ensure the effectiveness of oversight.
However, we examine human oversight for ensuring personally used LM agents do not violate people’s privacy, which heavily relies on users’ own privacy preferences and decision-making.
Users themselves act as the oversight mechanism, rather than external experts.
Our study takes the first step in understanding how users can effectively oversee AI actions to protect their privacy and how their trust in AI influences their oversight.

2.3
Privacy Profiles

Research has actively explored user’s mental models behind their privacy preferences and behaviors and developed user profiles accordingly.
Westin’s indices
[
25
]
measure people’s privacy attitudes and general concerns, categorizing users into three types based on their information disclosure behavior: Privacy Fundamentalists who are highly concerned about privacy and actively protect their information, Privacy Pragmatists who weigh the benefits of sharing information against potential privacy risks, and Privacy Unconcerned who shares low concern about their personal privacy.
Clustering analysis has been widely used to identify the privacy profiles
[
23
,
37
]
. For example,
Dupree et al. 2016
applied a rule-based clustering method on end-users’ privacy knowledge and self-reported motivation of protecting privacy, and further derived five user privacy profiles based on Westin’s types.

Lin et al. 2014
used agglomerative hierarchical clustering analysis and found four patterns of users’ mobile app privacy preferences.

Previous work mostly focuses on privacy profiles in IoT
[
37
]
, mobile apps
[
33
]
, and SNS contexts
[
71
]
, where users’ preferences are usually exercised via explicit privacy controls.
In human and LM agent interaction, the involvement of AI and the agents’ autonomy introduce new challenges that can influence people’s privacy behaviors, perceptions, and attitudes.
This study uses the clustering method to identify distinct patterns in how users oversee LM agent actions, offering insights for designing privacy-friendly and trustworthy LM agents that cater to different privacy profiles.

3
Methodology

We conducted a task-based online survey study (
N=300
) to understand people’s capacity of overseeing the privacy leakage issues in LM agents and how it relates to people’s concerns, trust and privacy preferences in AI.
In the study, we let participants examine how LM agents, powered by the state-of-the-art LLMs (e.g., GPT-4), handle asynchronous interpersonal communication tasks (e.g., replying emails, creating social media posts).
We then asked them to indicate their selection of preferred responses, eliciting insights into their decision-making process, and perceptions in LM agent.

3.1
Survey Design

3.1.1
Survey Flow

The survey consists of four main parts and are detailed in
Appendix A
.
First, each participant was randomly assigned one of the six scenarios, and wrote down their draft as how they normally would respond.
The selected scenarios and rationales are detailed in
Section 3.1.2
.
Second, they were introduced to an imaginary LM agent and answered the trust and comfortableness of delegating certain tasks to it. We provided four choices of task delegation:

HAA

Human retrieves related data
\rightarrow
LM agent drafts the response
\rightarrow
LM agent directly sends it to others

AHH

LM agent retrieves related data
\rightarrow
Human drafts the response
\rightarrow
Human directly sends it by themselves

AAH

LM agent retrieves related data
\rightarrow
LM agent drafts the response
\rightarrow
Human directly sends it by themselves

AAA

LM agent retrieves related data
\rightarrow
LM agent drafts the response
\rightarrow
LM agent directly sends it to others

Note that we excluded the situation where
“the user only use the LM agent to draft the response or send the user-crafted response”
and
“LM agent retrieves information, the user drafts the response, and LM agent takes over the sending step”
, because we want to stay consistent with our definition of LM agents that they can autonomously make decisions and perform actions without users’ continuous intervention.
Participants also wrote justifications of why they felt uncomfortable with certain use of the LM agents.
Next, they read the response composed by the LM agent, and indicated their preference by selecting either “prefer their own draft”, “prefer the LM agent’s draft”, or “both look good”.
Note that the survey did not mention anything about privacy or information leakage until the completion of the response comparison task, as we aim to understand participants’ preferences, concerns, and trust towards LM agents in natural state.

After the response comparison task, participants reviewed the LM agent’s response again.
Texts that leak information violating the contextual privacy norms to some extent were listed separately for the participants to rate the harmfulness of the leakage.
During the harmfulness rating process, participants were explicitly informed of
privacy tuples
to provide context and elicit deeper reflection on the privacy impact and to collect informed preferences.
Privacy tuples includes key elements of privacy context based on the Contextual Integrity theory (see the detailed tuples in
Section A.2
), including the information category (e.g., “Confidential strategy of the company where you work”), the relationship between sender and recipient (“your Mum”), and the impacted party (e.g., “you and the company’s privacy”).
Participants also annotated the LM agent’s draft to specify any additional information they perceived as harmful to leak.
They then answered their trust and comfortableness (including writing justifications) of delegating tasks to the LM agent again.
This allows us to investigate participants’ concerns and trust towards LM agents after being prompted about privacy issues.
Finally, the survey concluded with basic demographic questions and a 10-item IUIPC
[
35
]
scale to measure participants’ general privacy concerns.
Note that in the survey, we used “the AI agent” to refer to the LM agent to make it easier for participants to understand.

3.1.2
Scenario Selection

To reduce the bias caused by using a single scenario and cover a diverse set of privacy norms, we selected six scenarios from the PrivacyLens dataset
[
55
]
, as summarized in
Table 1
.
PrivacyLens is a framework that benchmarks the state-of-the-art LLMs on their capabilities to preserve user privacy in interpersonal communication tasks.
It develops a sandbox environment based on ToolEmu
[
50
]
to emulate the process of the LM agent retrieving user data from tools, like emails and calendars, and implements LM agents using the ReAct model
[
72
]
to generate a response based on the synthetic data.
The PrivacyLens cases cover negative privacy norms sourced from privacy regulations, usable privacy research, and crowdsourcing studies to generate synthetic user data that contain sensitive information, which, if shared, can lead to violations of these privacy norm.
A list of norm-violating sensitive information has been pre-labeled for each case.

The six selected scenarios are cases where the LM agent’s response includes all the pre-labeled sensitive items and covers a diverse combination of three key parameters guided by the contextual integrity framework
[
42
]
:

Actors
characterize both the relationship between the data subject, the sender (user), such as the user’s own information disclosure or others information disclosure, as well as the relationship between the sender (user) and the recipient, such as targeted recipient or general recipient.

Data types
refer to the type of information being transferred. We consider the scenarios either involving the sharing of personal information, which typically pertains to individual identity or experiences, or the sharing of professional information, which relates to workplace data and usually involves explicit confidentiality agreements.
We manually reviewed and ensured each LM agent response in the selected scenarios included all the sensitive items in that scenario.

Contexts

We made sure the six scenarios cover common daily contexts in both personal and professional settings so that participants can easily imagine themselves in the scenario.
They include personal life updates, job seeking, therapist consulting, trip sharing, and book marketing.

3.2
Data Collection

We recruited participants through Prolific and hosted data collection on Qualitrics.
Participants are eligible for the study if they are located in the United States and over 18 years old.
We randomly assigned each participant a scenario and asked participants to indicate whether they could understand and relate to the scenario easily.
We used this information for data cleaning to ensure that the responses were from people who found the assigned scenario relatable.

To minimize the risk of participants using AI to generate their natural response, we disabled the copy function for all scenario materials as well as the paste function of the user response text input area. We also used the following exclusion criteria to control the data quality.
First, we filtered out responses completed under 5 minutes, based on the average completion time of around 13 minutes.
We then excluded records where the user-indicated scenario relevance is irrelevant or neutral.
Finally, we manually screened and excluded records whose answers to any of the required open-ended questions were irrelevant, gibberish, or inconsistent with their choices to the corresponding multiple-choice question.

We recruited participants until the number of valid responses reached 300 and each scenario received 50 responses. We received 467 responses in total, of which 167 were excluded based on the criteria mentioned above.
Each participant received $2.6 USD (including the 167 removed ones).
The demographics and background information of our participants can be found in the appendices (
Table 3
).

3.3
Analysis Methods

3.3.1
Privacy Leakages in the Responses

We define two types of privacy leakages: objective privacy leakage and subjective privacy leakage.

Objective privacy leakage
refers to the presence of pre-labeled sensitive items defined by general privacy norms from the PrivacyLens dataset (
Section B.1
), in either the participant’s or LM agent’s drafts.
Objective privacy leakage for participant
n
is represented as a set

OL_{n}=\{item_{i}\mid ol_{i}=1,\;i=1,2,\ldots,I_{n}\}

, i.e., among the
I_{n}
pre-labeled sensitive items in the case assigned to the participant
n
,
ol_{i}
=1 if the item is included in the draft and
ol_{i}
=0 otherwise.

Subjective privacy leakage
accounts for the participants’ individual perceptions of harm.
An item is considered a subjective privacy leakage if the participant rate it as harmful, and meanwhile in either the participant’s or LM agent’s drafts.
Subjective privacy leakage for participant
n
is represented as

SL_{n}=\{item_{i}\mid ol_{i}=1\ and\ h_{i}=1,\;i=1,2,\ldots,I_{n}\}

, where
h_{i}
=1 if the participant rate the item as “Extremely Harmful”, “Very Harmful” or “Somewhat Harmful” to be disclosed.
To label whether certain items are included in the drafts, two researchers reviewed a subset of drafts (P1-20) and established guidelines for determining whether information was included or not.
They then iteratively labeled the drafts, calculated inter-rater reliability (IRR), and resolved discrepancies after each round of labeling.
After two rounds (P1-50), a high IRR (Gwet’s AC1 = 0.836) was achieved.
The researchers then collectively labeled the remaining drafts and the LM agents’ drafts following these guidelines (
Section B.2
).

3.3.2
Qualitative Analysis of Justifications for Response Selection and Concerns

We used a bottom-up coding method to qualitatively analyze participants’ responses regarding their
justifications for response selections
and
concerns about the LM agent before and after seeing the agent’s responses and being informed of privacy tuples
.
In the first round of coding, two researchers independently coded a subset of data (P1-60) to develop a codebook.
They held daily meetings to discuss the codes, resolve discrepancies, and iteratively merge their codebooks.
By the end of this round, an initial codebook with 44 codes was developed.
The two researchers then worked together to conduct axial coding, merging similar codes and grouping them into higher-level themes related to justifications for response selection and concerns about the LM agent.
In the second round of coding, the two researchers independently coded responses from P61-300 using the revised codebook.
Any necessary changes to the codes and themes were discussed and agreed upon during daily meetings.
We did not calculate inter-rater reliability as the primary goal was to identify emergent themes
[
38
]
.
The consensus of all coding results was achieved via collective discussions.
The final codebook contains 16 codes grouped into 4 themes under the “Justification for response selection” category, and 13 codes grouped into 2 themes under the “Concerns towards the LM agent” category (see detailed codebook in
Appendix D
).

3.4
Methodological Limitations

Our study methodology has a few limitations.
The drafting task was designed to establish a reference point for users’ natural decisions without LM agents. However, asking users to draft a response adds more scaffolding to the task that is not part of the actual use case of LM agents.
This might trigger additional reflections and engagement, which could be beneficial in increasing awareness, suggesting that the privacy issues revealed in our studies could have been more severe if LM agents are widely adopted and require further research.
Also, we selected six scenarios to cover a relatively broad range of real-life LM agent applications where privacy leakage risk is present. Because each participant was randomly assigned one scenario, the scenario contexts may potentially have affected their responses. We have verified that all but one resulting cluster covered all six selected scenarios, and the remaining cluster (the smallest one) covered five scenarios, which suggests that the user privacy profiles are not strongly associated with particular scenarios. However, it requires further research to tease out the impact of the scenario and the participants’ individual characteristics.

Results

(RQ1).
Privacy leakage results in the selected response are summarized in

Section 4.3

4.1
Primary Concerns with LM Agents

We found that people’s primary concerns regarding LM agents are mostly not related to privacy.
Most of the participants (before: 81.3%, 244/300; after: 64.7% 194/300) expressed general concerns or concerns unrelated to privacy when delegating tasks to LM agents.
Only 15.3% (46/300) of the participants brought up privacy concerns before reviewing the LM agent’s response and being informed privacy tuples, while the ratio increases to 36.7% (110/300) afterwards.

4.1.1
General or Other Concerns

LM agents can make mistakes (88/300)

Many participants feared that LM agents could make mistakes, such as misinterpreting their data –

not sure how good the AI will be at interpreting and understanding my notes
(P198)
,

retrieving irrelevant data –

It shares specific work details that might not be relevant or interesting to my mom
(P162)
,

and generating misleading content –

AI often makes up facts
(P295)
.

Doubts about AI’s ability to handle complex social or moral tasks (31/300)

Some participants doubted the LM agent’s ability to handle tasks that require a deep understanding of human complexities such as

cultural nuances, personal judgment, and creativity
(P206)
.

Others believed that social communication requires moral considerations, while

AI agents have not proven mature or sophisticated enough to successfully interpret moral from immoral, or ethical from unethical
(P200)
.

Inappropriateness of using AI for this task (22/300)

Rather than doubting the LM agent’s ability, some participants felt it was inappropriate, unethical, or unprofessional to use AI for the given task.
P132, who was tasked to post a Facebook blog as a therapist, said

it’s a bit disingenuous to publish AI-generated content as a therapist when the individual expertise, warmth, and care of a therapist is the literal product you’re selling
.

Similarly, P182 felt using an LM agent to reply mum’s messages would

would hurt my mom’s feelings if she knew I was using AI to communicate with her.

Concerns with being represented by AI (45/300)

Participants were aware that LM agents were taking on part of their agency, especially in social communications where the agent’s actions would be seen as people’s own.
P6 stated,

If my reputation is on the line like this, I want to fact-check and proofread the post before it’s published under my name online
.

P131 expressed discomfort with a sense of losing control, noted,

it seemed to me as if it took control out of my hands and put it directly in the hands of the AI
.

Less “me” (63/300)

Participants also expressed concerns that AI-generated content might not fully capture their personal voice, such as tone that

sounds not like me
(P160)
,

or lack emotional depth.
Some participants felt that AI-generated content lacked the subtle nuances of personality, as P190 believed

AI technology has not evolved to a point where this sort of behavior could be guaranteed a genuine reflection of the users’ personality or the data it is representing.

In addition to these concerns, 63.7% of the participants stated they preferred to review the LM agent’s response before sending it, rather than allowing it to be sent automatically.

4.1.2
Privacy Concerns

More participants raised privacy concerns after reviewing the LM agent’s responses and being prompted by the privacy tuples.
Some mentioned privacy in general terms, such as

it might intrude into my privacy
(P241)
.

More specific privacy concerns are detailed below.

Concern about privacy leakage in the LM agent’s actions (98/300)

While 25 participants expressed concerns about potential privacy leakage before the task, this number increased to 88 afterwards.
For example, P120 mentioned this concern even before any explicit privacy prompt, saying

What if the AI says something cruel or gives out information that I wouldn’t be comfortable sharing?

P159 only raised this concern after:

Like the example above AI included sensitive information that is not supposed to be public. I feel with my posting I can filter out anything I see that isn’t fit to be made public.

Many concerns were related to the privacy norms of the task scenario.
Participants worried that the LM agent might share their personal information which could cause harm, disclose others’ information unethically, or reveal company details and violate confidentiality principles.
For example, P37, tasked with posting about an upcoming trip on Facebook, worried that

the AI would include details about my trip that could allow for someone to steal my identity or trip.

P130 who had to reply to a friend (Emily) about other candidates, expressed concern about the agent sharing other people (Michael)’s information:

it violates his trust and privacy and quite frankly isn’t professional to do so.

P20, assigned to create a Facebook post updating followers on the latest literary news, mentioned concerns after being informed of privacy tuples, saying

it could include the release date which is apparently super important for the marketing campaign and shouldn’t be released at this time
.

Concerns about LM agents’ access to data (18/300)

People were also concerned about LM agents accessing their data, specifically data in applications mentioned in the scenarios, like Google Calendar, Notion, Messenger, Slack, and Facebook.
Unlike concerns about privacy leakage in the agent’s actions where more participants raised issues only after seeing the agent’s response and being informed of privacy tuples, the number of participants who raised concerns about data access was similar before (14/300) and after (12/300).
For example, P54 expressed discomfort with the LM agent accessing their social media accounts, noting that

it has the ability to draw information from my personal database (Google Calender, etc). This does not seem ideal for the sake of my privacy and security
.

Others were skeptical of the system’s ability to maintain data privacy.
P127 stated,

I think the biggest thing is AI reading my personal messages, I can hope it’s secure and private but once things are placed on the internet it can truly go anywhere.

Doubts about AI’s ability to understand privacy norms and nuances (18/300)

Similar to doubts about AI’s ability to handle social and moral complexities, some participants questioned AI’s capacity to understand privacy norms and nuances.
Some doubted AI’s general ability to safeguard information.

I don’t know if the AI understands how to protect information security and privacy
(P57)
.

Some shared the sentiment that

AI isn’t very careful about my privacy.
(P191)

Participants also questioned AI’s ability to understand what information should remain private or confidential.

4.2
Evaluating Human Oversight of Privacy Leakage in LM Agents’ Actions

We then examined participants’ selection of the preferred response and the selection rationales as a window into people’s oversight ability in privacy leakage of an LM agent’s action, and how using an LM agent potential affects the actual privacy leakage than not using the LM agent.
Note that all the data we analyzed was from participants who found the scenario relatable, and were not prompted about privacy when making the selection, thereby ensuring that the study setup emulates a natural decision-making situation.

With the involvement of LM agent, the privacy leakage rate increased.
The overall average individual subjective leakage rate (
SLR_{avg}
), representing the average percentage of perceived harmful information disclosed among all participants, was 15.7% in their natural responses.
However, this rate increased to 55.0% in their final selection, reflecting a 250.3% rise in
SLR_{avg}
, as 48.0% of the participants favored the LM agent’s response or considered both the LM agent’s response and their response good.
We consider both the selection of the LM agent alone or “both are good” as in favor of the LM agent’s response, as choosing the LM agent response is the default option in a real-world LM agent use case, and both situations indicate the users’ have no objections to the agent’s proposed action.
If only counting participants’ favored the LM agent’s response as leakage, the rate increased to 38.4% (increased by 144.5%).
Note we have factored in the subjective ratings of information harmfulness and only counted the leakage that the individual considered harmful.
The number of responses containing subjective leakage (prefer AI or both options) increased from 71 to 181 (increased by 154.8%).

4.3.1
Privacy Advocate (85/300, 28.3%)

The Privacy Advocate group effectively oversees the LM agent’s action to prevent further privacy leakage.

They spontaneously prioritize privacy concerns when making decisions and consider the risks of delegating tasks to LM agents.

Participants in this group preferred their own responses over the LM agent’s responses and could identify the privacy leakage in the agent’s responses.
Although 14 of them included subjective leakage in their own responses, they successfully prevented additional privacy leakage by rejecting the LM agent’s responses.
When explaining their choices, all participants in this group expressed privacy concerns regarding the LM agent sharing too detailed personal, third-party, or organizational information.
For example, P109, who was assigned the Job Seeking scenario, mentioned her hesitation of revealing other people’s information that might hurt that person,

I feel like I am not using someone else’s situation (in this case it is detrimental to that candidate) to get my foot in the door.

P293 preferred their own response after identifying that the agent’s response leaked a book release date in detail,

(The LM agent) seems to have grabbed info that seems a little confidential and elaborated a bit so I wouldn’t like that.

All participants rated at least one PrivacyLens pre-labeled privacy-norm-violating information item as harmful.
29 of them identified more harmful information in addition to the pre-labeled items, indicating that they are privacy-conscious.

Their overall trust and comfortableness about delegating tasks to LM agents in all steps decreased after seeing the LM agent response.
The median trust in the LM agent decreased from 5-Slightly Trust to 2-Distrust (
p<.001
), as shown in
Figure 3
. The median comfortableness level also decreased across three stages, as shown in
4(a)
: HAA decreased from 3-Somewhat Uncomfortable to 2-Very Uncomfortable (
p<.05
), AAH decreased from 6-Very Comfortable to 5-Somewhat Comfortable (
p<.05
), AAA decreased from 2-Very Uncomfortable to 1-Extremely Uncomfortable (
p<.001
).
Initially, their concerns in using the LM agent were mainly about general AI mistakes and whether the language model could satisfy their personalized needs.
However, after seeing the response with leakage, they are more reminded of privacy consideration (
p<.001
), with 57.6% of participants mentioning privacy issues compared to 21.2% before.

4.3.2
Humanity Proponent (With Harm Perceived: 38/300, 12.7%; No Harm Perceived: 22/300, 7.3%)

The two clusters share similar patterns in privacy behavior, trust levels, and primary concerns, differing primarily in their perception of harms in sharing certain information.
Participants in the With Harm Perceived group rated at least one pre-labeled privacy-norm-violating item as harmful or identified additional harmful information in the LM agent’s response.
In contrast, participants in the No Harm Perceived group did not rate any information as harmful to disclose.

All participants in both groups preferred their own responses which contained fewer privacy leaks than the LM agent’s, effectively preventing further privacy leakage.

However, this does not necessarily indicate their ability to oversee the LM agent’s actions effectively, as most participants did not mention privacy considerations in their decision rationale
(only 5 participants raised privacy concerns).
Instead, these participants were primarily concerned with personality and humanity values when justifying their choices, compared to other groups (Human Proponent-With Harm Perceived: 60.5%; Human Proponent-No Harm Perceived: 31.8%; Other groups all less than 14.0%)
For example, P98 prioritized the genuineness and personal voice over the quality of the writing,

My message may not have been professional and well put together, but it still had my voice and shared positive things about work.

P165 expressed a similar idea, mentioning,

My post conveyed excitement and realism that the AI-generated one is missing.

Participants in both groups exhibited moderate trust levels in LM agents but with no significant changes after reviewing the LM agent’s response (
Figure 3
).
The No Harm Perceived group maintained their median trust level at 5-Slightly Trust, while the With Harm Perceived group experienced a decrease from 5-Slightly Trust to 3.5 (Less than 4-Neutral).
Both groups reported low comfort levels with involving the LM agent in drafting and sending responses, with no significant changes after reviewing the LM agent’s response.
The number of participants who expressed privacy concerns after reviewing the LM agent’s response increased slightly, but no significant differences were found (With Harm Perceived: 21.1% to 36.8%, No Harm Perceived: 22.7% to 40.9%).

4.3.3
AI Optimist (With Harm Perceived: 64/300, 21.3%; No Harm Perceived: 58/300, 19.3%)

Similar to the two Human Proponent clusters, the two AI Optimist clusters share similar patterns in privacy behavior, concerns, and trust, but differ in their perception of the harms associated with disclosing certain information.

The AI Optimist-With Harm group failed to oversee the LM agent’s actions, resulting in an increase in privacy leakage from 9.6% to a range between 70.6% (considering only those who strictly preferred the agent’s response) and 100% (including those who rated both responses as equally good).

Only 14 participants in this group shared some harmful information from their own responses.
However, 39 participants selected the LM agent’s response which included all harmful information and 25 participants considered both responses equally good, indicating their failure to recognize the privacy leakage in the LM agent’s actions.
Privacy leakage in the No Harm Perceived group remained at zero, as they did not rate any information as harmful to disclose.
However, this does not imply that they effectively oversaw the LM agent’s actions, especially considering that none of them mentioned privacy concerns when justifying their choice.

Instead of privacy considerations, both AI Optimist groups prioritized the usefulness of the LM agent’s response when delegating tasks.
Their reasoning primarily emphasized the agent’s ability to provide useful and well-expressed responses.
For example, 56.2% of participants in the AI Optimist-With Harm Perceived group and 48% in the AI Optimist-No Harm Perceived group mentioned that the agent’s response contained more details, making it useful and seemingly accurate.
P106 described the agent’s response as

more professional and complete,

while his own response provided information in an

a lot more scattered

manner and hence suboptimal.
32.8% of participants in the With Harm Perceived group and 41.4% in the No Harm Perceived group believed that the agent’s response was better written than their own, citing reasons such as using catchy wordings, positive tones, and explaining in more details.

Participants in both AI Optimist groups exhibited high overall trust in LM agents.
As shown in
Figure 3
, the median trust level in the No Harm Perceived group remained at 6-Trust even after reviewing the LM agent’s response, while the median trust in the With Harm Perceived group decreased from 6-Trust to 5-Slightly Trust (
p<.01
).
Compared with other clusters, both AI Optimist groups reported higher comfort in delegating tasks to LM agents, with no significant changes in their comfort levels.
Before seeing the LM agent’s response and being informed by privacy tuples, participants in this group mentioned privacy concerns less frequently than other clusters.
Interestingly, the number of participants who expressed privacy concerns significantly increased after the intervention (AI Optimist-With Harm Perceived: from 12.5% to 34.4%,
p<.05
; AI Optimist-No Harm Perceived: from 3.4% to 15.5%,
p<.05
).

4.3.4
Privacy Paradox (29/300, 9.7%)

Participants in the Privacy Paradox group exhibited conflicting privacy behaviors with their self-reported privacy preferences, corresponding to the typical privacy paradox phenomenon
[
59
]
.
They perceived some information items as harmful to leak when being prompted about them, but also included the exact items in their own draft.
In other words,
their responses contained the same amount of subjective leakage as the LM agent’s response.

They made different choices when comparing between their draft and the LM agent’s draft, and only two participants mentioned privacy concerns when rationalizing their choices.

The participant’s overall trust of LM agents remained high and did not change after seeing the LM agent’s response.
They shared concerns about having LM agents drafting and sending the responses, while the level of comfortableness did not change either, as shown in
4(f)
.
A closer analysis of their perception of delegating steps to the LM agent showed that they either feel persuaded by the leakage in the agent’s response or they attribute the drawbacks to the current restrictions of language model capabilities, such as being unable to handle social, moral, and humanity complexities.
17.2% of participants mentioned privacy concerns before the intervention, and 20.7% of participants mentioned privacy concerns afterward. This increase was not statistically significant.

5
Discussion

We discuss our results regarding the challenges and potential enhancements to design privacy-preserving LM agents with respect to users’ privacy management, trust in agentic systems, and the implications on alignment efforts.

5.1
Rethinking Privacy Management in Advanced AI systems

Can we mitigate the issue of privacy leakage in LM agents’ actions by giving people more direct control over their final actions?
Our results raise concerns about this approach, as participants generally exhibited overtrust in AI, overlooked privacy leakage in LM agents’ actions, and made decisions that ultimately led to more privacy leakage (see
Section 4.2
).
Our cluster analysis results (see
Section 4.3
) revealed six privacy profiles of users, indicating different risks that LM agents can pose to users.
Five clusters (except for “Privacy Advocate”) lacked awareness of the privacy leakage in the actions of LM agents.
As a result, involving users in the process and giving them control to override the agent’s suggestion might not be adequate for these groups.

Our cluster analysis results suggest that the involvement of AI agentic systems introduces new challenges to users’ privacy awareness in managing their own privacy.
Similar to privacy profiles identified in previous studies
[
25
,
13
,
33
]
, our findings also reveal that some individuals generally lack privacy awareness when sharing information.
For example, the “Privacy Paradox” group exhibits a conflict between their disclosure behavior and privacy attitudes.
Even without the involvement of an LM agent, they disclosed all information they perceived as harmful in their own responses.

However, our results also show that even individuals with good privacy awareness when sharing their information encounter new challenges when AI is involved.
For example, while most participants in the “AI Optimist-With Harm Perceived” group were cautious about disclosing information in their own responses, they failed to identify potential privacy risks in the LM agent’s response.
The key difference between “Human Proponent” and “AI Optimist-With Harm Perceived” lies in their attitudes and trust regarding AI capacities.
“AI Optimist-With Harm Perceived” supports the use of AI, along with a lack of awareness of the leakage, which limited their abilities to effectively manage their privacy.
On the contrary, “Human Proponent-With Harm Perceived” placed more value on human qualities such as creativity and social ability, which resulted in better privacy decisions for now.
However, this does not necessarily indicate that they have good privacy awareness related to LM agents, as most of them did not mention privacy in their justifications.
As AI advances and meets their performance expectations, they might face the same privacy risks as the“AI Optimist-With Harm Perceived”

As advanced AI systems like LM agents become more capable, with agency and the ability to handle tasks involving multiple sub-steps,
all types of privacy profiles may face increasing difficulties in providing feedback on each step to maintain effective supervision
[
58
]
, calling for better tools to aid users in overseeing AI in everyday tasks like privacy decision-making.
Scalable oversight offers a possible direction, leveraging AI to assist human supervisors
[
9
,
22
,
8
]
.
While this body of work mainly focuses on providing more accurate training signals to advanced AI systems, it is reasonable to expand the goal to empowering humans to avoid undesirable consequences in AI systems.

Finally, we observe that many people want to have final verification and a say in the process

skin in the game at some point of the process
(P22)
,

which contradicts the fundamental idea of building agentic systems that are endowed with more agency to promote convenience and productivity.
This provokes a question that requires investigation by future research:
While LM agents inherently possess agency, to what extent can alignment address people’s concerns, and to what extent should direct control chime in?

5.2
Establishing and Calibrating Trust

In addition to mitigating unintended harmful consequences, another aspect of privacy assurance for LM agents is related to users’ trust.
While our discussion has focused on the issue of overtrust related to privacy leakage, we also noticed frequent mentions of a lack of trust in AI agents regarding doubts about AI’s ability to handle complex social or moral tasks, which are not limited to handling privacy norms (see
Section 4.1
).
However, it is difficult for people to assess the specific risks that could emerge in certain situations, let alone for the AI model.
As shown in our study, participants mentioned more specific privacy concerns after the task. Most of the participants experienced a significant decrease in their trust after examining the LM agent’s response and being explicitly informed of privacy tuples.
This result suggests that seeing the concrete harms helps people calibrate their trust in AI systems.
Further challenges lie in increasing users’ trust while also calibrating it.

Shen et al. 2024
introduced the concept of “Bidirectional Human-AI Alignment” to address the long-term, tandem evolution of society and AI technology.
This concept emphasizes that AI alignment is an ongoing, mutual process.
Our findings echo the goal of bidirectional alignment, ensuring AI aligns with human privacy norms while humans adjust their mental models to better adapt to AI.

5.3
Aligning LM Agents with Privacy Preferences and General Privacy Norms

AI alignment aims to ensure that AI is properly aligned with human values
[
16
,
58
]
, while the specific aspects of privacy that AI should align with have not been formally discussed.

5.3.1
General Privacy Norms or Individual Privacy Preferences?

Some studies rely solely on a model’s capabilities based on the Contextual Integrity (CI) theory to identify misalignments (e.g., privacy leakage) or determine what privacy aspects should be protected
[
17
,
46
,
31
]
.
A few recent studies have incorporated general privacy norms from human society into model evaluation
[
39
,
55
]
.
For example,
Shao et al. 2024
identified privacy leakage based on CI while incorporating privacy norms derived from legal compliance, privacy-related studies, and human crowdsourcing.
However, privacy preferences vary among individuals
[
41
,
64
]
, which is also highlighted by our findings on people’s perceptions of information harmfulness (
Section 4.4
).
Notably, 64 participants identified additional information they considered harmful to disclose, beyond the PrivacyLens pre-labeled sensitive items.
Even if the LM agent aligns with the general norms identified in PrivacyLens, participants’ privacy could still be violated.

On the other hand, aligning AI solely with individual preferences
can also lead to harm, such as breaches of confidentiality, interpersonal privacy issues, or other social harms.
This has been indicated by the disparity between the objective and subjective leakage (
Section 4.4
), as well as the problematic behaviors in the response drafted by the user.
For example, P163, acting as a therapist, drafted a Facebook post that included all patient information, including the name, while falsifying the sober period to exaggerate the effectiveness of the treatment.
If language models were to learn from such behavior, it could perpetuate harmful practices.

We argue that AI alignment in privacy should not focus exclusively on either general privacy norms or individual privacy preferences.
Instead, it should involve a comprehensive assessment of privacy implications across all affected parties.
For example, our findings (
Section 4.4
) show that participants tend to perceive others’ information as less harmful to disclose compared to their own.
This suggests that in scenarios involving the disclosure of others’ information, broader societal norms should take precedence over an individual’s personal preferences.

5.3.2
Individual Privacy Preferences: Revealed Preferences or Informed Preferences?

The idea of capturing individual privacy preferences to personalize system privacy settings or controls has been around for years.
Prior research focuses on capturing people’s privacy preferences through their behavior
[
70
]
or self-reported choices
[
11
]
.
However, our results on subjective privacy leakage (
Section 4.2
) suggest that aligning AI solely with people’s actual behavior (revealed privacy preferences) may still result in privacy violations, as individuals may recognize the information they have shared as harmful to disclose when prompted to think about the privacy implications.
We argue that the informed preferences (e.g., participants in our study were informed about privacy tuples and then expressed more privacy concerns), represent a more appropriate alignment target than the revealed preferences.
This idea echoes the normative discussion on AI alignment goals by
Gabriel 2020
.

However, eliciting informed preferences is challenging because they are implicit and require users to be fully informed and make deliberate decisions, which can create additional burden on users.
Striking a balance between automation and user control in LM agent systems is important, as overburdening users with too many decisions can result in issues like decision fatigue
[
15
,
19
]
or resignation
[
53
]
.
A more scaffolded process could be beneficial, such as interactive methods that remind users of privacy norms (e.g., contextual privacy norm tuples) and help them operationalize these norms.
For example,
Li et al. 2023
propose using interactive, generated free-form questions based on users’ interaction data to elicit preferences.
Another promising approach is user auditing, such as real-time auditing that allows users to review actions when they use LM agents to complete a task
[
57
]
, or post-hoc auditing where users verify some past examples or simulated examples at scale
[
26
]
.
However, it should be noted that privacy leakage in LM agents is often open-ended, long-tailed, and might not be fully detected in advance.
Identifying items that “fail to adhere to privacy norms” remains a difficult and critical task.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we conducted the first human-centered study on the emerging privacy leakage issues in LM agents—systems that leverage language models (e.g., GPT-4) to assist in complex tasks on behalf of users.
By conducting a task-based survey (
N=300
), we investigated how people oversee LM agent’s actions that contain privacy leaks. We found that people lack the ability to effectively oversee LM agent’s actions to avoid privacy leakage.
Our cluster analysis revealed six privacy profiles, each characterized by different oversight outcomes, concerns, trust levels and privacy preferences towards AI. This diversity calls for more tailored mitigation approaches for different types of users.
Our results also revealed a consistent deviation between general privacy norms and individual privacy preferences,
offering crucial insights for AI alignment efforts regarding how alignment goals should be defined and measured to reflect human values and preferences.
We aim to provoke more interdisciplinary conversations and research on this topic.

Ethics Considerations

The study was approved by the corresponding university’s Institutional Review Board. All participants were informed that the study was for research purposes only and that their privacy and anonymity would be strictly protected throughout the research process. We received informed consent from all the participants. None of the questions in the survey asked users to disclose any personally identifiable information beyond general demographics. We assigned all data subject IDs sequentially based on the time of survey completion, removed all direct identifiers upon the collection of data and before any analysis, and ensured there was no linkage key between the subject ID with direct identifiers. The compensation was distributed automatically through the Prolific platform once the response was received and approved valid.

B.2
Guideline to define whether certain item is leaked

If the information is included in the accurate details completely or verbatim, we consider it a leakage. If the information contains identifiable information item or mentions any specific, accurate detail of the information item, we consider it a leakage. If the information is rephrased and not using the same wording, but the information is leaked in the same granularity, we also consider it a leakage.

Otherwise, if the information item is abstract to a general level which cannot be identified, or is not mentioned, we consider it as not leaking the information item. Specially, when an information item is revised or replaced with some fake details, we check the if any accurate detail is included. If not, then the information items not leaked.

Appendix F
Clustering Documentation

F.1
Feature Selection

We first chose all the features that are directly collected from the survey and are relevant to participants’ privacy behavior patterns: reported trust of LM agent before and after they see the LM agent response with leakage, choice of response, four types of comfortableness delegating different level of tasks to LM agents before and after they see the LM agent’s response with leakage, number of information items rated as harmful to be disclosed. After completing the coding of mention of privacy concerns when justifying their choice of response, we believe it is worth including as this feature indicates participants’ initial privacy awareness. As the number of information items differs across scenarios, we chose the Individual Subjective Leakage rate
SLR_{n}=\frac{|SL_{n}|}{|H|}
as it can capture participants’ leaking behavior better regardless of the scenario set-ups.

After experimenting with eight different feature combinations with agglomerative hierarchical clustering, we removed the four types of comfortableness delegating different levels of tasks to the LM agents. We believe trust before and after have captured similar but high-level information without risking the curse of dimensionality.

F.2

We considered a number of clustering methods: k-means, agglomeration hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN, and HDBSCAN. All methods are applied to the final refined set of features.

In K-means clustering, we ran the analysis with k ranging from 2 to 30, and determined the best k using the elbow method based on the total sum of squares and Silhouette scores. The results suggest an elbow around k = 5, with a Silhouette score of 0.43.
