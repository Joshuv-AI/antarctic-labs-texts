Eddy current loss behavior of MS/Fe3O4 nanocomposites and M-doped Fe3O4 nanoparticles (M = Mn, Zn) analyzed through dielectric and magnetic studies
Authors: Pari Soltanpour, Rahim Naderali, Khosro Mabhouti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The critical parameter of eddy current change plays a crucial role in analyzing the characteristics and defects of materials used in industry. Following the successful co-precipitation synthesis of magnetite (Fe3O4) nanoparticles (NPs), this study employed X-ray diffraction to analyze the sample’s structure and phases. Morphological FESEM testing revealed diameters of 9 nm, 18 nm, 14 nm, 12 nm, and 20 nm for the pure Fe3O4, Zn-doped Fe3O4, Mn-doped Fe3O4, ZnS/Fe3O4, and MnS/Fe3O4 nanoparticles, respectively. Moreover, dielectric and eddy current investigations of the synthesized samples were conducted at ambient temperature over a frequency range of 10−3 to 106 Hz. The data analysis shows that the resistive and capacitive properties of the samples are primarily attributed to processes associated with grains and grain boundaries. Additionally, across all samples, a decrease in frequency resulted in increased capacitance and dielectric parameters. The variation of dielectric properties and AC conductivity with frequency indicates that the dispersion is mainly due to Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization and charge hopping between Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions at octahedral sites. Frequency-dependent magnetic parameters and electric modulus were observed in all samples. The analysis of magnetic parameters μ′ and μ″, along with eddy currents, suggests that Mn and Zn doping, as well as ZnS and MnS nanoparticles, enhance these parameters compared to their effects on dielectric properties. Manganese sulfide had the greatest impact on eddy currents, while pure magnetite displayed the lowest eddy current and the highest loss tangent. Examination of M-H hysteresis loops revealed that the saturation magnetization (Ms) of multi-domain iron oxide magnetite increases with zinc doping. Conversely, manganese doping and the presence of manganese sulfide and zinc sulfide nanoparticles lead to a decrease in saturation magnetization. The hysteresis loop data also allowed for the extraction of other magnetic properties, including the anisotropy constant (K), anisotropy field (Hk), and magnetic moment (nB).

Introduction

Eddy currents arise from several factors, including magnetic field penetration, surface currents, and the current penetration depth within materials1–3. Researchers focus on eddy currents because of the advantages offered by the eddy current parameter. This parameter can be a decisive factor in determining the application domain of materials across various fields, including Non-Destructive Testing, Manufacturing Quality Control, Material Characterization, Electrical Conductivity testing, Corrosion Detection, Medical Imaging, Composite Material, Heat Treatment Monitoring, Crack Detection in Welds, and Rail Inspection4–7. For instance, eddy currents play a crucial role in Non-Destructive Testing to identify surface and subsurface defects in metallic structures. This approach is commonly used in industries such as aerospace and manufacturing8. Additionally, eddy currents are utilized to evaluate material properties, including electrical conductivity, magnetic permeability, and microstructural changes in materials9. Various materials, especially iron metal oxides such as magnetite (Fe3O4) and various magnetic/semiconductor phases, continue to be essential in the fields of electronics, energy, and sensing technologies due to several key properties. Therefore, it is essential to investigate and re-evaluate their magnetic and electrical parameters and derive quantities such as their magnetic losses and eddy currents4,10. These materials are used as essential components in oxide-based transistors and gas sensors11. Furthermore, their ferromagnetic and magnetoelectric properties make them attractive for advanced magnetic transistor memories and applications in high-frequency bands12,13. Iron oxides also act as catalysts in energy processes and are used in lithium-ion batteries, fuel cells, and energy storage systems14,15. Consequently, if these devices have an oxidation potential, the electrical properties of iron oxides become important, and the study of these properties, in addition to high frequencies, will be important in environments with a wide range of frequencies.

Moreover, due to the unique behaviors of nanomaterials and their emergence as optimal candidates in materials research and fabrication processes, this work explores the magnetic properties of nano-iron oxide particles at low frequencies ranging from 10–3 to 106 Hz to extract changes in eddy currents and magnetic losses. Additionally, the composite fabrication and doping of Fe3O4 hybrid nanomaterials with other materials can lead to enhanced properties and new applications across various fields16,17. To the best of our knowledge, most previous works have investigated the effect of doping on the structure of iron oxide or studied iron oxide compounds in the form of core–shell18. Furthermore, the electrical properties of ZnS/Fe3O4 and MnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs have not been comprehensively examined. Besides, there is a shortage of reports systematically describing the dielectric and magnetic characteristics of undoped Fe3O4, Mn-doped Fe3O4, Zn-doped Fe3O4, MnS/Fe3O4 composite, and ZnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs19,20. Hence, this study investigates the magnetic properties of these samples to inspect changes in iron oxide eddy currents influenced by zinc and manganese doping, as well as the formation of doped element sulfide composites. Synthesizing and characterizing the studied materials can contribute to a deeper understanding of their behavior and potential applications. The co-precipitation method, chosen for its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, low synthesis temperature, and production of uniformly sized nanoparticles, is an effective approach for nanoparticle synthesis with controlled particle size21–23. Additionally, several techniques have been documented for assessing eddy currents, and the methods used for their calculation depend on the frequency spectrum24. In the microwave domain, the waveguide method can measure eddy currents by illuminating the material’s surface with an electromagnetic wave8. These currents, however, were measurable in the low-frequency range (1 Hz–several kHz) and also at high frequencies25,26. At microwave or lower frequencies, it’s crucial to use a method that allows for the extraction of dielectric and magnetic parameters.

However, in the present study, in addition to the doping effect, considering that sulfidation can affect the electrical properties of materials and improve their applications in electronic industries such as the production of LEDs, optical sensors, lithium-ion batteries, etc., in the present work, to investigate the effect of doping and sulfidation on various properties of magnetite nanoparticles, zinc sulfide/iron oxide and manganese sulfide/iron oxide composite NPs were synthesized by the co-precipitation method alongside the doped samples. An attempt was also made to interpret the graphs using the same interpretation as previously studied works. But in the field of methodology, considering that the usual method of calculating the eddy current parameter is to use eddy current sensors, in the present work, by simplifying and using the impedance spectroscopy method after studying the electrical properties, the eddy current parameter was finally extracted by applying known mathematical relations, and using the values of impedance, electrical permittivity and permeability, in the frequency range of 10–3 to 106 Hz. Alongside this analysis, vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) can provide an alternative perspective on magnetic performance and complement the study of magnetic parameters. Furthermore, from a structural and morphological standpoint, parameters such as particle size and crystal structure are reported, with efforts made to establish a logical correlation between structural parameters and magnetic results.

Experimental section

Reagents

Using a consistent chemical co-precipitation method and the same synthesis conditions, the samples in this study were produced from Iron (II) chloride tetrahydrate, Iron (III) chloride hexahydrate, Manganese (II) nitrate tetrahydrate, Zinc nitrate hexahydrate, Sodium sulfide nonahydrate, Sodium hydroxide, ethanol, and double-distilled water.

Preparation of pure, Mn and Zn-doped Fe3O4 NPs

Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles doped with manganese or zinc were synthesized via the co-precipitation method1,2. Briefly, stoichiometric amounts of FeCl₂·4H₂O (0.1 M) and FeCl₃·6H₂O (0.2 M) were dissolved in 100 mL of double-distilled water under magnetic stirring at 600 rpm and heated to 80 °C to prevent Fe2+ oxidation. After stirring for 2 h, an aqueous solution containing Mn(NO₃)₂·4H₂O or Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O, with a molar metal to iron ratio M: Fe = 0.15:2.85 (corresponding to 5 wt% dopant), was added27,28. Subsequently, 2.0 M sodium hydroxide solution was added dropwise to adjust the pH to 9–11. This solution was chosen to optimize magnetite nucleation while minimizing oxidation to non-magnetic phases, such as hematite29, resulting in the immediate precipitation of Fe₃O₄: M (M = Mn or Zn). The black precipitate was magnetically separated, washed repeatedly with double-distilled water and ethanol until the pH of the supernatant reached 6.5, and then dried in an oven at 100 °C for 3 h. The final product was ground to a fine powder. The average separation efficiency was 83%.

Preparation of MnS/Fe3O4 and ZnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs

The MnS/ Fe3O4 and ZnS/ Fe3O4 nanocomposites were prepared using a two-step coprecipitation-sulfidation process. First, FeCl₂·4H₂O (0.1 M) and FeCl₃·6H₂O (0.2 M) were dissolved in 100 mL of double-distilled water and stirred vigorously (600 rpm) for 1 h at 80 °C. Then, the pH of the solution was carefully adjusted to 9–11 by the gradual addition of 2.0 M NaOH. This pH range was chosen based on initial optimization to ensure controlled nucleation of Fe3O4 while preserving Mn2+/Zn2+ ions in solution for subsequent synthesis27–29. The mixture was stirred for another 2 h at 80 °C. Then, an aqueous solution of Mn(NO₃)₂·4H₂O or Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O was added, and stirring was continued for 1 h at the same temperature. After that, an aqueous solution of Na₂S·9H₂O (0.2 M) was added dropwise over 15 min under continuous stirring (600 rpm) to initiate sulfidation. The reaction was maintained at 80 °C for 1 h to ensure the complete formation of MnS or ZnS phases30,31. The resulting black precipitate was collected using an external magnet, washed with water and ethanol to pH ≈ 6.5, and dried at 80 °C for 5 h. The final nanocomposite powders were obtained after gentle grinding. The average yield of the nanocomposites was 85%. Measurements

The synthesized samples’ crystal structure and size were analyzed using high-resolution XRD (Philips PW 1730, Cu-Kα radiation, λ = 0.154056 nm), with 2θ ranging from 10 to 80°. FE-SEM (MIRA3, TESCAN, and the Czech Republic) was used to characterize the surface morphology of all the synthesized samples. Utilizing a highly advanced frequency response analyzer (μ Autolab/ Fra2), the impedance measurements were conducted. The measurements covered 10–3 Hz to 106 Hz, with a resolution of 3 μV, an accuracy of ± 0.2%, and used NOVA Control software. To characterize the electrical properties, the synthesized powders were uniaxially compacted into a disk-shaped form with a diameter of 10.5 mm and a thickness of 0.7 mm under a uniaxial pressure of 40 GPa. The resulting pellets were then sandwiched between two high-purity copper foils to form a copper/nanoparticle/copper parallel-plate capacitor configuration. Impedance spectroscopy measurements were subsequently conducted using an Autolab/FRA2 device over a frequency range of 10–3 to 106 Hz, employing a voltage amplitude of 10 mV at the open-circuit potential (OCP). The magnetic feature of the specimens was sustained by a Vibrating Sample Magnetometer (VSM-MDKB) at room temperature at a field of 1.4 T.

Results and discussion

Structure analysis

As depicted in Fig. 1a, using XRD pattern analysis, the crystallographic phase of all the prepared samples has a cubic crystalline structure of magnetite. In pure, doped, and composite magnetite NPs, peaks at 30.33°, 35.67°, 43.29°, 53.68°, 57.25°, and 62.91° are indexed to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) planes of the Fe3O4 (iron oxide) face-centered cubic structure. The resulting pattern aligns with the reference pattern for Fe3O4 (JCPDS card number 01-075-0449). XRD patterns for magnetite and doped samples lacked secondary peaks, suggesting no impurities, secondary phases (like iron oxide), or elemental segregation at grain/cluster boundaries. The MnS/Fe3O4 and ZnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs showed evidence of phase formation characteristic of manganese and zinc structures.

Diffraction peaks in the MnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs, matching the (100), (002), (101), (103), and (212) planes of hexagonal MnS (JCPDS 040-1289), were observed. Besides, confirmation of the hexagonal ZnS phase within the ZnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs structure is provided by Bragg diffraction peaks indexed to JCPDS card number 01-072-0016. Furthermore, compared to the Fe3O4 sample, the peak centers of the other samples have shifted towards higher angles (Fig. 1b). XRD peak shifts can be caused by variations in dopant ionic radii, instrument effects, temperature, defects, and lattice strain from doping-induced lattice parameter changes, and structural alterations that occur during the composite preparation process32–35.

Williamson–Hall (W–H) method

In addition to Scherer’s method, the Williamson–Hall (W–H) relation offers an alternative approach for determining the crystallite sizes and micro-strains of synthesized samples. This method addresses X-ray peak broadening resulting from both the finite size of coherent scattering domains and internal stress within the material. The Williamson–Hall (W–H) method analyzes line broadening by independently considering size-induced and strain-induced peak broadening, acknowledging the dependence of peak width on 2θ40. In powder X-ray diffraction, strain-induced peak broadening arises from disorders, crystal imperfections, and lattice distortions introduced during the synthesis process. The data presented in Tables 1 and 2 indicate that the incorporation of Mn into the Fe3O4 structure, as well as the presence of ZnS nanoparticles (NPs) in the ZnS/Fe3O4 composite, results in a reduction of dislocation density and stacking faults (SF) compared to Fe3O4 NPs. The observed decrease in the SF parameter in these samples is consistent with a reduction in disorder and a decline in crystallinity, as evidenced by the decreased peak width and intensity in the XRD analysis. Furthermore, the presence of MnS NPs in the MnS/Fe3O4 composite sample leads to a reduction in dislocation density and SF relative to Fe3O4 NPs. Consequently, the MnS/Fe3O4 sample exhibits higher intensity peaks with less broadening, suggesting a larger crystallite size and increased crystallinity. In contrast, the higher SF and δ values observed for Zn-doped Fe3O4 NPs, compared to the other samples, confirm reduced crystallinity, smaller crystallite size, and an increased presence of defects, as inferred from the larger peak width and lower peak intensity43,44. Furthermore, according to the data in Table 4, the strain in some nanoparticles decreases or, in some cases, increases with increasing particle size. Strain in nanoparticles can be influenced by the manufacturing method, surface behavior, and the mechanisms of slow or fast growth. In general, in nanoparticles, as particle size increases, the surface area becomes relatively smaller and the surface strain decreases, but the internal or interfacial strain may change due to structural constraints, compositional inhomogeneities, or the presence of structural defects45.

Furthermore, Table 1 indicates a reduction in the lattice parameter of doped and composite NPs compared to Fe3O4 NPs. This decrease in doped samples may be attributed to cation distribution. Upon doping Fe3O4, the dopant ion typically substitutes sites proportionally to its ionic radius46. While the ionic radius of Mn2+ (0.81 Å) is larger than that of Fe2+ (0.77 Å), Mn doping in Fe3O4 NPs can increase the lattice parameter, expanding the unit cell. Similarly, Zn2+ ions in Zn-doped Fe3O4 NPs preferentially occupy tetrahedral sites, possessing a larger ionic radius (0.74 Å) than Fe2+ ions (0.645 Å) in the A sublattice; consequently, the lattice constant in Zn-doped Fe3O4 can exceed that of Fe3O4. However, the observed reduction in lattice parameters may stem from cation distribution between the A and B sites, influencing the inversion parameter and indicating the percentage of doped ions in the octahedral (B) sites47,48. Accordingly, alterations in cation distribution or migration of cations between interstitial sites correlate with variations in the lattice constant48,49. Moreover, the decreased lattice parameter of the iron oxide phase in the composites may be ascribed to alterations in structural order induced by the presence of secondary phases, namely MnS and ZnS.

Dielectric studies (via the impedance spectroscopy method)

Utilizing a μ Autolab /FRA2 device, impedance data were collected at room temperature across frequencies ranging from 10 to 105 Hz to analyze the dielectric properties of pure Fe3O4 NPs, manganese and zinc-doped Fe3O4 NPs, and MnS/Fe3O4 and ZnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs. Impedance spectroscopy is a key method for studying the electrical properties of nanoparticles, as it enables the separation of grain impedance from other sources, such as electrode and grain boundary impedances. One of the significant factors affecting the impedance properties of ferrites is the microstructure effect. The impedance measurement gives us information about the resistive and reactive components of a material. This pattern indicates that the samples are frequency independent in the high-frequency regime, which can be attributed to the important role of grain boundaries in the Fe3O4 structure. The impact of grains is more pronounced at high frequencies; conversely, grain boundaries exert a more substantial effect at low frequencies. In ferrite structures, such as magnetite, the degree of space-charge polarization shows a strong dependence on the applied frequency range. At high frequencies, space-charge carriers show greater mobility, which is attributed to a decrease in potential barriers and a concomitant increase in free mobility. This phenomenon results in a reduction in impedance, indicating high conductivity of the material at higher frequencies within the grains or conductive-electroactive regions, which is a consequence of the high mobility of charge carriers53. Conversely, at lower frequencies, a decrease in conductivity is observed at the grain boundaries or resistive-electroactive regions. Furthermore, an elevated dipole moment is attributed to several factors, including ions, ionized oxygen vacancies, surface defects, and oxygen antisite defects. Modulation of these factors can alter the cross-correlation barrier between distinct metal ions, thereby influencing the impedance characteristics of the sample54. As depicted in Fig. 8, manganese-containing samples exhibit higher resistance at low frequencies. The electrical resistance of materials mainly results from the scattering of charge carriers caused by structural inhomogeneities, impurities, and phonons. Doping iron oxide with Mn or Zn affects strain forces, electron distribution, and energy levels. Due to differing electronic configurations of Mn and Zn relative to iron, Mn, with a half-filled electron level (3d5 4s2), can create stronger exchange interactions with Fe (3d6 4s2) compared to Zn (3d10 4s2), which has a filled electron level. These differences can significantly alter the electric field and energy levels, increasing scattering. Generally, manganese metal, especially in ferrite phases, boosts scattering and resistance. Zinc also raises resistance in the iron oxide structure, but the extent depends on zinc content and phase structure55,56.

Furthermore, the elevated manganese carrier density results in greater charge accumulation due to increased electric dipoles at grain boundaries in the MnS/ Fe3O4 NPs, leading to an increased capacitive component, as shown in Fig. 8c. The increased carrier density also enhances the material’s response speed to frequency variations, resulting in improved frequency behavior57,58. The observed increase in impedance at low frequencies for all samples suggests the potential utility of these ferrites as dielectrics in low-frequency devices.

A loss peak in dielectric materials arises, as explained by Debye relaxation theory, when the applied electric field frequency aligns with the hopping frequency, a characteristic of space charge polarization74. Additionally, in agreement with Iwauchi75, a significant correlation exists between the conduction mechanism and the dielectric properties of ferrites. Electron displacement toward the applied field, because of electron exchange between ferrous (Fe2⁺) and ferric (Fe3⁺) ions in octahedral sites, causes polarization. Defect dipoles and electron hopping are the main contributors to dielectric loss in ferrites. Electron hopping noticeably affects this loss, especially at low frequencies; its impact decreases with increasing frequency, leading to a reduction of dielectric loss at higher frequencies. All the graphs show significant dispersion as frequency changes, which is a fundamental characteristic of ferrites. Electrical conductivity in ferrites primarily results from electron hopping between ions of an element that has multiple valence states and occupies crystallographically equivalent lattice sites randomly80. The application of an alternating current (AC) electric field accelerates electron hopping, resulting in higher AC conductivity. Also, the rate of electron hopping is directly proportional to the frequency of the applied electric field. The incorporation of Mn into Fe3O4 results in a reduction of the Hc value, as evidenced by the hysteresis loop and data presented in Table 8. This decrease is likely attributable to the increase in crystallite size observed in Mn-doped Fe3O4 relative to its un-doped counterpart. Notably, the enlargement of crystallite size reduces the spin disorder within the Fe3O4 surface layer. As the average particle size approaches a critical single-domain threshold in Fe3O4 particles, a transition to a multi-domain magnetic structure occurs, facilitating domain wall motion. Consequently, an augmented number of grain boundaries is observed, coupled with a diminished boundary volume104,105. This phenomenon weakens the domain wall pinning effect arising from the low volume fraction at the grain boundary, thus contributing to the decreased coercive field in Mn-doped Fe3O4 NPs. Conversely, Zn-doping of Fe3O4 leads to an increased Hc value, potentially stemming from a reduction in crystallite size. Furthermore, in composite samples, the proximity of sulfide nanoparticles to iron oxide nanoparticles can modify the shape, surface characteristics, size, and magnetic-crystalline anisotropy, ultimately influencing the Hc value. The parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M}_{s}$$\end{document}, Hc, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M}_{r}$$\end{document} are valuable indicators from an application perspective. The hysteresis loop area corresponds to the magnetic hysteresis energy, or hysteresis loss, which governs the magnetization reversal of the material. Manipulation of the hysteresis loop area can be achieved through adjustments to grain size, magnetic induction, and magnetic retentivity, all of which are influenced by the nanostructure’s composition. In the Mn-doped magnetite sample, a reduction in the loop area is observed relative to other samples. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and FE-SEM analyses reveal an increase in crystallite and grain size within the Mn-doped Fe3O4, resulting in entrapped pores and subsequent intra-grain porosity. This porosity contributes to a decrease in magnetic and induction, thereby diminishing the loop area. This reduction in loop area is advantageous for minimizing hysteresis loss in soft magnetic ferrites, with implications for applications in computer memory chips, microwave devices, transformers, and related technologies106,107.

Conclusion

In summary, the results demonstrate that surface current testing can be an effective method for studying and characterizing the electrical, magnetic, and structural properties of materials. To achieve this, the co-precipitation technique was used to produce iron oxide nanoparticles incorporating manganese and zinc ions, along with MnS and ZnS nanoparticles. Analysis reveals that the synthesized materials possess a crystalline structure, with average crystallite dimensions ranging from 8.20 to 10.60 nm. Moreover, data demonstrate that higher frequencies correlate with lower dielectric parameters and capacitance, a consequence of the different trap characteristics at these frequencies. Moreover, at low frequencies, the capacitive method indicates smaller values for Mn-doped Fe3O4 compared to the other samples. Besides, magnetic experiments conducted at room temperature revealed the ferromagnetic nature of the samples. Furthermore, at low frequencies, results demonstrate that the magnetic permeability of the Mn-doped Fe3O4 and MnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs exhibits higher values compared to other nanopowders due to their high magnetization. Additionally, the surface current diagrams indicated that Fe3O4 NPs doped with manganese and zinc, as well as MnS/Fe3O4 and ZnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs, increased eddy currents and reduced the magnetic loss of pure Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The highest values of these currents were associated with the zinc sulfide composite sample. Magnetic measurements showed that all samples have ferromagnetic properties, characterized by a multi-domain structure. In addition, with the substitution of Mn and Zn ions in the magnetite structure, as well as with the formation of MnS/Fe3O4 and ZnS/Fe3O4 composite NPs, a change in Ms and Hc values ​​is observed, which can be attributed to the change in crystallite size. The findings of this study revealed that the structural and magnetic properties of magnetite (Fe3O4) nanoparticles can be easily altered by the presence of metal cations. According to our findings, the Fe3O4 NPs and composite NPs exhibit considerable potential for application in electromagnetic devices.
