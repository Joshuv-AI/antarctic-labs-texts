Patterns and predictors of racial/ethnic disparities in HIV care continuum in the Southern USA: protocol for a population-based cohort study
Authors: Xueying Yang, Jiajia Zhang, Bankole Olatosi, Zhenlong Li, Sharon Weissman, Xiaoming Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHealth disparities exist at every step of the HIV care continuum (HCC) among racial/ethnic minority population. Such racial/ethnic disparities may have significantly delayed the progress in HCC in the Southern US states that are strongly represented among geographic focus areas in the 2019 federal initiative titled ‘Ending the HIV Epidemic: A Plan for America’. However, limited efforts have been made to quantify the long-term spatiotemporal variations of HCC disparities and their contributing factors over time, particularly in the context of COVID-19 pandemic. This project aims to identify the spatiotemporal patterns of racial disparities of each HCC outcome and then determine the contribution of contextual features for temporal change of disparities in HCC.Methods and analysisThis cohort study will use statewide HIV cohort data in South Carolina, including all people living with HIV (PLWH) who were diagnosed with HIV in 2005–2020. The healthcare encounter data will be extracted from longitudinal EHR from six state agencies and then linked to aggregated county-level community and social structural-level data (eg, structural racism, COVID-19 pandemic) from multiple publicly available data sources. The South Carolina Revenue of Fiscal and Affairs will serve as the honest broker to link the patient-level and county-level information. We will first quantify the HCC-related disparities by creating a county-level racial/ethnic disparity index (RDI) for each key HCC outcomes (eg, HIV testing, timely diagnosis), examine the temporal patterns of each RDI over time and then using geographical weighted lasso model examine which contextual factors have significant impacts on the change of county-level RDI from 2005 to 2020.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of South Carolina (Pro00121718) as a Non-Human Subject study. The study’s findings will be published in peer-reviewed journals and disseminated at national and international conferences and through social media.

Introduction

The HIV care continuum (HCC), a compelling framework to describe the current clinical goals for managing HIV infection, tracks the progression of people living with HIV (PLWH) from diagnosis, through linkage to care, retention in care and ultimately to the goal of viral suppression.1 Unfortunately, health disparities exist at every step of the continuum among racial/ethnic minority population. According to 2018 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) report, African American and Latino PLWH are less likely to be linked to HIV care (77.1% vs 82.6%), to receive any care (63% vs 70%), to be retained in care (48% vs 52%) and to achieve viral suppression (51% vs 63%) compared with their white counterparts.2 3 Such racial/ethnic disparities may have significantly delayed the progress in HCC in the Southern US states that are strongly represented among geographic focus areas in the 2019 federal initiative titled Ending the HIV Epidemic: A Plan for America (EtHE).4

It has been reported that individual-level strategies to improve health and ameliorate health disparities have been insufficient. Recognition of the impact of upstream social and contextual factors on health outcomes is also critical to improve population health. The HCC-related health disparities among racial, ethnic, sexual and gender minority communities in the South may be rooted in their social and historical contexts. Many groups that are experiencing HCC disparities are also experiencing multiple structural vulnerabilities (eg, poverty, rurality, residential segregation, healthcare provider shortages, unequal access to HIV prevention and treatment services, low health literacy and HIV stigma), which has been aggravated during COVID-19 pandemic.5 These contextual features likely contribute substantially to the persistence of marked racial/ethnic disparities in HCC and vary by geographical area. However, limited efforts have been made to quantify the long-term evolving spatiotemporal variations of HCC disparities and their contributing factors over time. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic has possibly amplified the disparities and altered their trajectory of the impact on HCCs (eg, from attenuating to exacerbating trend).

Since 2017, we have established a statewide HIV cohort, which consists of 12 203 PLWH, who were diagnosed with HIV in 2005–2020 in South Carolina (SC), one of the seven states targeted by EtHE because of a high rural HIV burden.6 The ongoing research extracted and linked longitudinal EHR data from six state agencies. To address the gaps in HCC-related health disparity research, we will leverage the exiting data and link these data to aggregated county-level community and social structural level data (eg, structural racism, COVID-19 pandemic) from multiple publicly available data sources. Beyond the scope of original project, we will first quantify the HCC-related disparities by creating a county-level racial/ethnic disparity index (RDI) for each key HCC outcomes (eg, HIV testing, timely diagnosis and viral suppression), examine the temporal patterns of each RDI over time and develop optimal prediction models to identify the predictors of the temporal change of RDI. The RDI will be operationalised as the percentage of one HCC outcome (eg, % diagnosis rate, % linkage to care) among a group of racial/ethnic majority (eg, White) divided by the percentage of the same HCC outcome among group(s) of racial/ethnic minority (eg, black)’ . With the integration of multilevel data from multiple sources and advanced data analytics, the current study has the following specific aims:

Aim 1

Examine the spatiotemporal patterns of RDI of each HCC outcome (eg, exacerbating or attenuating trend) and identify socially disadvantaged areas in SC where racial disparities are persistent, widening or reducing and also examine the pattern changes in different phases of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Aim 2

Determine the contribution of contextual features (eg, social determinants of health [SDoH], structural racism, COVID-19 pandemic, and unequal healthcare access) for temporal change of RDI in HCC using integrated data from multiple sources via the multivariable generalised regression with lasso penalty.

The proposed research is innovative and significant because identifying and reducing racial/ethnic disparities in HCC outcomes is essential to achieve the EtHE goals in SC and other Southern US states.7 The data on the geographic variations of racial/ethnic disparities across HCC will (1) help state health departments and community-based AIDS service organisations to identify the geographic “hotspot” areas with widen disparities; (2) inform health department to try to attenuate the contextual drivers of poor HCC, such as implementing policies to reduce poverty, provide job opportunities, and ensure equitable housing and neighbourhood conditions; (3) address patients’ social needs as part of healthcare delivery, and diversify the healthcare work force to more closely reflect the racial composition of the patient population; and (4) facilitate researchers to devise more precise, targeted intervention strategies and management for socially disadvantaged areas to achieve the health equality and improve HIV outcomes.

Methods and analysis

Conceptual framework

This is a population-based longitudinal cohort study. Given the multifactorial perspective of health disparities of HCC among racial/ethnic population, we propose a conceptual framework to guide our proposed research (figure 1). Adapted from the conceptual approaches to the study of health disparities,8 this framework depicts the influence of SDoH, structural racism, unequal healthcare access and unanticipated care interruptions due to COVID-19 pandemic on HCC health disparities among racial/ethnic populations. All these multilevel factors will disproportionately impede the access to HIV care in different racial/ethnic groups and ultimately lead to racial/ethnic disparities in HCC outcomes. Guided by the conceptual framework, we will adopt multiple data sources and multifaceted approaches to obtain a comprehensive view of the contextual drivers of the quantified spatiotemporal variations of racial/ethnic disparities on HCC outcomes across time.

Data sources

Data sources for patient-level variables

The patient-level variables will be used to calculate the aggregated county-level HCC outcomes, with patients having the same county address at HIV diagnosis categorised into the same county. The following data sources will be used to extract patient-level variables for the analysis. The individual variables include date of HIV diagnosis, age, sexual and gender minority status, race/ethnicity, risk exposure (eg, men who have sex with men [MSM], transgender, drug user), date of CD4 test, CD4 count, antiretroviral therapy, date of viral load test and absolute value of viral load, which will be used to define the HCC outcomes (timely HIV diagnosis, linkage to care, receipt of any care, retention in care and viral suppression).

SC DHEC-Enhanced HIV/AIDS Reporting System

Monitoring stage of disease at time of diagnosis, linkage to HIV medical care, retention in HIV medical care and viral suppression is dependent on complete reporting of HIV-related laboratory results (including CD4 counts and viral load results) to HIV surveillance programmes and CDC. The confidential name-based reporting of HIV/AIDS in SC began in 1986, leading to the creation of the SC DHEC-Enhanced HIV/AIDS Reporting System (eHARS).9 10 eHARS is a surveillance system developed by the CDC and is designed to efficiently track population-based HIV/AIDS data. SC eHARS is a laboratory-based reporting system to which all statewide CD4 and VL tests have been reported since 1 January 2004, as mandated by the Code of Laws of SC (1976) Section 44-29-10: Regulation 61–20.9 10 The data quality of the SC eHARS exceeds CDC’s minimum standards for HIV/AIDS reporting across states, based on reporting timeliness (95% of cases reported within 6 months of a diagnosis) and completeness.9 10 We have retrieved 12 203 PLWH who were diagnosed with HIV between 2005 and 2020.

Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program Data Report

Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program Data Report is an annual report that captures data regarding the services provided by all Ryan White-funded entities. Providers report on all clients who received services eligible for Ryan White parts A, B, C or D funding, regardless of the actual funding source used for payment. In SC, the part B contractors complete the forms for submission to SC DHEC, which in turn submits the complete data to HRSA.

SC Revenue and Fiscal Affairs Office

Since 1996, SC state law has mandated that the SC Revenue and Fiscal Affairs Office (RFA) receives reports on all diagnoses in the Uniform Billing form (UB-92) format from all emergency departments, hospital inpatient facilities, ambulatory-care facilities and outpatient surgery facilities in SC.10 All general acute care hospitals and specialised hospitals must also provide inpatient and financial information to the SC RFA. Other health institutions mandated to provide similar outpatient data include all hospital-based and freestanding ambulatory surgical facilities, hospital emergency rooms and any healthcare facility providing radiation therapy, cardiac catherisation, lithotripsy and positron emission therapy. The SC RFA Data Warehouse is extensive and includes a wide array of data sources (figure 2).

American Community Survey

The American Community Survey (ACS) is the nationwide survey from a sample of the population in the USA and Puerto Rico.11 The ACS collects information such as age, race, income, education and other socioeconomic/demographic data. All ACS data are survey estimates, and each estimate has a margin of error published by the US Census Bureau. ACS estimates are period estimates that describe the average of characteristics of the population and housing over a period of data collection.

Area Health Resources File

Area Health Resources File (AHRF) is a public data set from HRSA that contains county-level files in eight domains: Healthcare Professions, Health Facilities, Population Characteristics, Economics, Health Professions Training, Hospital Utilization, Hospital Expenditures and Environment. AHRF was designed to be used by policymakers, researchers and others interested in the nation’s healthcare delivery systems and factors that may impact health status and healthcare in the US.

Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System

Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System is a CDC-funded state-based system of surveys that collect information on health-risk behaviours, preventive health practices and healthcare access. It is key source of tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and cancer screening data at the county level.

County Health Rankings and Roadmaps Programme

This programme is a collaboration between the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation and the University of Wisconsin Population Health Institute. It measures vital health factors, including high school graduation rates; obesity; smoking; unemployment; access to healthy foods, air and water quality; and income inequality at the county level.

COVID-19 severity and social-distancing policy data

CDC provides data for nationwide COVID-19 cases confirmed since 11 March 2020 and county-level social distancing policies (eg, emergency declaration, stay at home order, etc) and telehealth services expansion, and each corresponding date of enaction.12

AIDSVu

Created in 2009,13 AIDSVu is a public resource for visualising HIV surveillance data and other population-based information relevant to HIV prevention, care, policy and impact assessment. AIDSVu develops visualisations, maps and downloadable data sets using results from HIV surveillance systems, other population-based sources of information (eg, US census and national probability surveys) and other data developed specifically for display and dissemination through the website. Over 10 years, AIDSVu.org has used an expanded number of data sources and data are available at the county level in 48 US states (including SC).

SC Department of Alcohol and Other Drug Abuse Services

The SC Department of Alcohol and Other Drug Abuse Services (DAODAS) is the SC government agency charged with ensuring quality services to prevent or reduce the negative consequences of substance use and additions. DAODAS coordinates a statewide system of local substance abuse agencies that provide prevention, treatment and recovery services and the DAODAS data comprise of information of county-level opioid crisis and alcohol consumption.

Gilead COMPASS Initiative

The Gilead COMPASS Initiative team developed maps and infographics to mapping existing HIV service deserts and develop data-informed recommendations for areas in which new or expanded capacity will have the most impact on HIV prevention and care under the Emory University Coordinating Center. They developed a directory of organisations that are providing mental health and substance abuse services to PLWH or LGBT populations in the South which will generate data regarding provision of these important services.

Data linkage and integration

We have a legal contract with SC RFA that has served as the honest broker for the linkage of all identifiable data and remove all the identifiable information from the linked data before releasing it to the research team. In the existing project, various data resources are integrated through the following steps. First, we used eHARS from SC DHEC to develop HIV Cohort and obtain their history of HIV infection and treatment, and laboratory results over time. Second, the data with the identifiable link from eHARS were sent to RFA. Using the identifiable link, RFA linked all individual variables (eg, clinical diagnosis, mental health data). The RFA uses deidentified system-generated ID number to ensure confidentiality but allows the study to conduct data mining at both the individual and aggregated levels. County-level variables will be extracted from multiple public database sources with the Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS) as the identification of each county. Then, individual-level data and county-level variables will be linked by FIPS code and calendar year. If a certain data set does not go back to 2005, we will use the data from the nearest year to impute the data in missing years. For county-level missing data, we will use the average value from the neighbouring counties to impute the data in this county. For data security, only the deidentified data set is released to investigators for analysis. RFA has agreed to follow the established procedure to update data through 2022. After the receipt of deidentified data from RFA, our research team will assess the data quality related to missing, aberrant or extreme values.

Definition and description of key study variables

HCC outcomes

Based on CDC’s definition,3 we will define each stage of HCC outcomes with the following definitions: (1) prediagnosis indicators. The county-level HIV diagnosis rate will be derived from SC DHEC (with the denominator as PLWH (including both people whose infection has been diagnosed and those who are infected but don’t know it)) and the county-level pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) rate (eg, PrEP-to-need ratio) will be retrieved from AIDSVu; (2) timely HIV diagnosis. Timely HIV diagnosis will be defined as not having AIDS at the time of initial HIV diagnosis or not being diagnosed with AIDS within 12 months of initial diagnosis; (3) linkage to care. Linkage to HIV medical care was measured by documentation of at least one CD4 or viral load test performed ≤1 month or ≤3 months after diagnosis; (4) retention in care. For retention in continuous HIV medical care, it refers to: ‘Cases with 2 or more CD4 or viral load test results at least 3 months apart during a 12-month period’. We also will include the alternate (additional) analysis ‘Received any care’, which is defined as: ‘Cases with at least one CD4 or VL test result during a 12-month period’ and (5) viral suppression. Viral suppression is defined as: ‘the number of persons diagnosed with HIV and alive at the end of calendar year AND had a viral load ≤200 copies/mL at the most recent viral load test’. Each HCC outcome will be dichotomised into binary variables at each calendar year. Then the individual result of each HCC outcome (eg, linked or not linked to care, retained or not retained in care) will be aggregated to calculate county-level percentage of patients conforming to each stage of HCC outcomes stratified by races (eg, percentage of African Americans who linked to care).

Social and contextual variables

Given the long follow-up time (~15 years), we anticipate that there will be changes of the social and contextual determinants to some extent, particularly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. As listed in table 1, the variables include: (1) SDoH. The measures include the socioeconomic status (eg, poverty rate, unemployment rate, per capital income and educational attainment), household composition and disability (eg, percentages of persons aged ≥65 years or ≤17 years, civilian non-institutionalised population with disability), racial/ethnic minority status and language (eg, percentages of individuals who are members of racial/ethnic groups) and housing type and transportation (eg, percentages of housing in structures with 10 or more units, mobile homes, households without vehicles). (2) Structural racism. As an important determinant and distal causes that shapes the conditions that put individuals ‘at risk of risk’, our study defined segregation using indices reflecting five distinct axes of measurement: evenness, exposure, concentration, centralisation and clustering. The consequences of segregation entail the concentration of poverty and the solidification of racial disparities in economic and educational opportunities. Considered a marker of inequality, it is possible that patterns of segregation may provide additional insight into the greater burden of disease among racial/ethnic minorities in the USA. (4) Unanticipated HIV service disruptions due to COVID-19 pandemic. The COVID-19 pandemic might have aggravated the already significantly delayed progress in HCC in the Southern States. We will include the COVID-19 prevalence in each county. The mitigation measures of the potential HIV service interruptions, such as telehealth expansion services, virtual medical visits utilising telephone calls or online video conferencing services, will also be considered into the analysis. (5) Healthcare access data. Health facility data for the whole of SC will be obtained from SC DHEC, which contains health-side and environment-side information of healthcare facilities licensed by SC DHEC. The driving distance to HIV treatment services will also be retrieved.

Analytic procedures

Calculation of racial/ethnic disparities in HCC

Racial/ethnic disparity in each outcome of HCC and change in such disparity between 2005 and 2020 will be estimated at county level. To quantify the HCC-related disparities, we will create a county-level RDI for each of the key HCC outcomes (ie, timely diagnosis, linkage to care, retention in care and viral suppression). The RDI will be operationalised as the percentage of one HCC outcome (eg, % linkage to care) among group of racial/ethnic majority (eg, White) divided by the percentage of the same HCC outcome among the other group(s) of racial/ethnic minority (eg, Black). For example, the black/white disparity in linkage to care will be defined as the percentage of linkage to care among Black PLWH divided by percentage of linkage to care among White PLWH in each county. When RDI equals 1, it means there are no disparities, otherwise, it means a disparity exists between the two races of interest (eg, White vs Black). The HIV cohort data will be updated on a regular basis, which would allow us to capture the change of RDI before and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

The dynamic patterns of racial/ethnic disparities in HCC outcomes (aim number 1)

Data management

We will calculate the percentage of each HCC outcome with respect to race for each county at each calendar year. Of note, to avoid the very small number in certain county, we will apply the 3-year moving average of RDI calculation at each county level.

Spatial temporal pattern visualisation

The geospatial variation of the 3-year moving average of RDI will be examined by spatial analysis through mapping RDI overtime using ArcGIS V.10.2. Heatmap will be used to visualise the spatial and temporal trend of RDI across the 46 counties in SC between 2005 and 2020.

Spatial temporal pattern analysis

The longitudinal RDI index across the county will be modelled by the spatial temporal model where the spatial correlation is modelled via the random effects following the multivariate normal distribution. The temporal trend will be modelled via the linear, quadratic or B spline, where the best relation will be selected based on Akaike information criterion (AIC) and used to define the dynamic change of the RDI index over time. The dynamic trend will be displayed visually and then we will define the counties with significant positive change over time as ‘Counties with Consistent Widening Disparities’; significant V (U) shape as ‘Counties with Widening Disparities’; significant negative change as ‘Counties with Consistent Reduced Disparities’ and (∩) shape as ‘Counties with Reduced Disparities’; other patterns will be defined as ‘Unstable Trend’. The counties with the same pattern will be clustered, which will be visualised using ArcGIS to identify geographic ‘hotspot’ counties, which could serve as the role example of areas with diminishing racial disparities or areas with widening disparities we need to pay attention to. We will also consider other racial disparity measures, such as absolute differences and gini coefficients.

Geographical weighted lasso model (aim number 2)

The primary outcome is pattern of RDI in HCC outcomes. The secondary outcomes include percentages of each HCC outcome overall and with respect to each race group, aiming to examine whether there are some risk factors having contradicted role for different groups, which are masked in the RDI analysis. We will take advantage of the multiple data sources and will explore contextual predictors of RDI. We will apply the geographically weighted regression (GWR) to examine which contextual factors have significant impacts on the change of county-level RDI from 2005 to 2020. For the temporal alignment, we will make sure all the county-level predictors are retrieved in a time window prior to the outcome events occurrence. The geographical correlation will be considered similarly as aim 1. The high dimensional issue in the model fitting process will be accounted by the penalised form of GWR, called the ‘geographically weighted lasso’ (GWL).14 GWL adds a constraint on the magnitude of the estimated regression coefficients to limit the effects of explanatory variable correlation and performs local model selection by potentially shrinking some of the estimated regression coefficients to zero in some counties. Specific attention will be given to the COVID-19 impact in the model building via (1) adding the interaction terms with different phases of COVID-19 pandemic in analysis and (2) adding the subset analysis, such as the interaction term by HIV risk group (ie, MSM vs heterosexual). The goodness of fit of all models will be evaluated via the AIC or BIC criteria. The results will be reported by the OR for multinomial structure and estimated coefficient for linear structure.

Clinical implication and public health impact

The integration of multiple data sources provides us a cost-effective way to confirm some existing risk factors and explore/find some unknown (hidden) or intersecting contextual factors contributing to the racial/ethnic disparities and its patterns of HCC. The outputs from these analyses will (1) assist health department to identify ‘Counties With Widening Disparities’, which could serve as the target counties for collective efforts to create and maintain opportunities/policies that facilitate health and its determinants at local community level (eg, ensure equitable neighbourhood conditions); (2) ‘Counties With Consistent Reduced Disparities’ could inform healthcare providers to collect protective factors, which could be used to reduce the health disparities in other disadvantaged areas. In addition, the research findings would also inform the public and policymakers to invest more about the nature and extent of racial inequalities in health and to enhance individual and community capacity and build public empathy and political will to effectively address racial/ethnic disparities in HIV outcomes.

Data collinearity and missingness

We are expecting to have missing data in both EHR domains and publicly available data sources. Depending on the missing rate, we plan to either remove or impute the variables (eg, 3-year moving average) as appropriate. Specifically, with the small missing rate, we will impute the missing value. For the variable with the large missing, we will record the missing rate, discuss the variable with domain experts and conduct the analysis with complete entry with moderate missing and exclude the variable when the missing is very large. Additionally, for the potential misclassification issues, we will carry out sensitivity analyses of all this work to assess the extent to which misclassification at certain levels could have distorted the overall research findings. We will use the variance inflation factor (VIF) to check the data collinearity of different covariates. If VIF >10, we will remove one of the highly correlated variables, combine them into a single variable, or use a dimensionality reduction technique to reduce the number of variables while remaining most of the information.

Ethics and dissemination

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at the University of South Carolina (Pro00121718) as a Non-Human Subject study on 6 October 2022. We will publish the findings in peer-reviewed scientific journals and present the study findings at national and international professional conferences and through appropriate social media outlets. We will capitalise on social media and professional networks that can increase the reach and accessibility of findings, such as open access publication, webinars, files and videos available on websites and publicly available channels (eg, YouTube), to increase visibility and impact of the scientific publications and presentations. The dissemination efforts of this project will extend beyond the scientific arena and also target our stakeholders in healthcare system and policymakers in the USA at local (SC DHEC, Prisma Health) and national levels (CDC) through various policy forums, policy papers, and special presentations.
