Introduction

Bradycardia, renal failure, atrioventricular (AV)-nodal blockade, shock, and hyperkalemia (BRASH) syndrome represents a distinctive but frequently overlooked iatrogenic complication occurring when negative chronotropic agents—predominantly beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers—accumulate to toxic concentrations in patients with renal impairment.1 The syndrome comprises 5 key features: bradycardia, renal failure, AV nodal blockade, shock, and hyperkalemia. Although described in the critical care literature, BRASH remains substantially underrecognized in routine clinical practice, with findings frequently misattributed to primary myocardial disease, sepsis, or decompensated heart failure.2,3

BRASH syndrome’s pathophysiology is best understood as pharmacodynamic synergy rather than a uniformly pharmacokinetic process: some implicated agents (eg, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, digoxin, certain renally-cleared beta-blockers) genuinely accumulate in renal impairment, but even a beta-blocker whose own pharmacokinetics are unaffected by renal failure can precipitate BRASH when its ordinary, expected effect on the AV node is potentiated by the hyperkalemia and acidosis that accompany renal failure. The clinical consequences reflect the widespread distribution of beta-adrenergic receptors across multiple organ systems. Bradycardia and AV blockade develop through direct effects on nodal tissue, resulting in profound reduction in cardiac output and shock. Hyperkalemia develops through impaired renal excretion and reduced cellular uptake into skeletal muscle (mediated by beta-2 receptors). A clinically important associated feature, when present, is severe hypoglycemia refractory to standard dextrose administration, caused by blockade of hepatic beta-2 receptors and impairment of gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis—the 2 primary hepatic glucose production pathways. Although not included in the BRASH acronym itself, refractory hypoglycemia in the appropriate clinical context can serve as a diagnostically suggestive finding pointing toward beta-blocker toxicity as the underlying driver.1,4

Recent case reports have highlighted the prevalence of BRASH and the diagnostic challenges in recognizing it in the context of acute critical illness. BRASH typically develops in hospitalized patients already suffering from myocarditis, sepsis, acute coronary syndrome, or other life-threatening conditions—diseases that provide compelling alternative explanations for shock and metabolic derangement, thereby anchoring clinical reasoning away from the consideration of drug-induced toxicity.3,5

Case report

A 55-year-old woman with asthma, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease stage 3 (baseline creatinine 2.0 mg/dL) presented with a 2-week history of dyspnea, productive cough, palpitations, and lower extremity edema. The patient smoked 2–3 cigarettes per day and reported intermittent cocaine use. On presentation, she was tachycardic (144 beats per minute) with electrocardiography showing atrial flutter with rapid ventricular response (Figure 1). Chest imaging showed pulmonary edema and cardiomegaly; computed tomography angiogram of the chest excluded pulmonary embolism but revealed right ventricular dysfunction with mild right pleural effusion and ascites. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severely reduced ejection fraction (22%), global wall motion abnormality, large left ventricular apical thrombus, moderate mitral regurgitation, and elevated pulmonary artery pressure (46 mm Hg). Initial management included rate-control agents (diltiazem and metoprolol), intravenous diuretics, and anticoagulation with enoxaparin and warfarin, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and remdesivir. Immediate management included cessation of all negative chronotropic agents, initiation of vasopressor support, and urgent hemodialysis for metabolic correction.1,2 Glucose monitoring was intensified with point-of-care testing every 15 minutes; dextrose and glucagon infusions were continued. Head computed tomography was negative. On hospital day 6, the patient suffered cardiac arrest with pulseless electrical activity during hemodialysis. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved within 3 minutes.

Repeat echocardiography revealed worsening intracardiac thrombus burden: left ventricular ejection fraction of 15% with a V-shaped, mobile thrombus (2.25 × 2.0 cm), a fixed LV mid-septal thrombus (1.52 cm), and a mobile right ventricular thrombus (2.92 × 0.58 cm). Bilateral lower extremity ultrasound excluded deep venous thrombosis. Hypercoagulable workup was significant for low antithrombin III, factor V, protein C, and protein S levels (Table 3). However, the patient developed complications of the progressive thrombus burden, found to have an acute ischemic stroke with frontal lobe involvement consistent with cardioembolic etiology. After stabilization and weaning from vasopressors, metoprolol was cautiously reintroduced at low doses with close monitoring (Figure 4). Discussion

BRASH syndrome, at its core, is a pharmacodynamic catastrophe rather than a pharmacokinetic one, often presenting as cardiogenic shock—and in this patient, its development was neither sudden nor arbitrary. Metoprolol undergoes predominantly hepatic oxidative metabolism via CYP2D6, and its systemic availability and half-life do not differ to a clinically significant degree in patients with renal failure compared with normal subjects. The drug’s prescribing information explicitly states that no dose reduction is needed in chronic renal failure.6,7 Therefore, the mechanism of BRASH in this patient did not require pharmacokinetic drug accumulation. Rather, it was a pharmacodynamic phenomenon: hyperkalemia and AV nodal blockade acted synergistically, each potentiating the other at levels that would individually have been tolerable.1 Progressive acute kidney injury (creatinine rising from 2.8 mg/dL on admission to 4.5 mg/dL by the time BRASH was recognized) impaired renal potassium excretion, whereas metoprolol—uptitrated over the following days for persistent tachycardia—simultaneously suppressed beta-2-mediated cellular potassium uptake into skeletal muscle and depressed AV nodal conduction through beta-1 blockade. The result was a vicious cycle: rising potassium further depressed nodal conduction already blunted by metoprolol, producing bradycardia disproportionate to either insult alone. This synergistic mechanism—not drug accumulation—is the defining pathophysiology of BRASH, as described by Farkas et al.1

The clinical expression of this synergy followed a logical sequence. Beta-1 adrenergic receptors on sinoatrial and AV nodal tissue govern chronotropic and dromotropic function; with advancing beta-blockade, spontaneous nodal depolarization slows, and AV conduction velocity falls.1,8 In this patient, despite ongoing atrial flutter—which might have been expected to sustain some ventricular rate through persistent atrial impulses—the combined effect of metoprolol and progressive hyperkalemia had blunted AV conduction so severely that the ventricular response fell below 30 beats per minute by day 5. What made atropine resistance diagnostically decisive was not its negativity but its specificity: atropine works by blocking muscarinic cholinergic tone, thereby lifting parasympathetic inhibition from the AV node. Its complete failure to affect heart rate indicated that the bradycardia was not mediated by excess vagal drive—it was the direct consequence of pharmacological blockade of the adrenergic receptors governing nodal function, a mechanism atropine cannot reverse. In the syndrome of BRASH, atropine resistance is not a dead end; it is an arrow pointing to the diagnosis.

An important consideration in this case is the potential confounding role of amiodarone, of which the patient received 3 doses of 400 mg (1200 mg total) on hospital day 4, one day before the BRASH episode. However, several lines of evidence argue against amiodarone as the primary driver of the acute decompensation. First, although the total daily dose fell within the standard oral loading range of 800–1600 mg/day, it was administered for a single day rather than the 1–3 weeks typically required for oral amiodarone to achieve its electrophysiologic and antiarrhythmic effect; the drug’s prescribing information states that effects on abnormal rhythms are “not seen before 2–3 days and usually require 1–3 weeks, even when a loading dose is used.”9 Mitchell et al10 demonstrated in serial electrophysiology studies that AV nodal effects (prolongation of Wenckebach cycle length) were maximal only after approximately 2 weeks of sustained loading, not within 24 hours. Second, the temporal profile implicates metoprolol: the BRASH episode occurred 3 days after metoprolol was uptitrated, coinciding with progressive renal deterioration and rising potassium—the classic BRASH trigger. Third, the rhythm evolution argues against amiodarone: 2 electrocardiograms obtained 3 hours apart during the bradycardic episode showed sinus bradycardia progressing to junctional bradycardia, a pattern of deteriorating rather than stabilizing nodal function, inconsistent with a successful pharmacologic conversion. Fourth, the clinical response confirmed the BRASH mechanism: bradycardia and hemodynamic instability resolved with hyperkalemia correction, volume resuscitation, and beta-blocker cessation, rather than requiring the prolonged course expected with amiodarone toxicity given its weeks-long elimination half-life. Shah et al’s systematic scoping review confirmed that beta-blockers, not amiodarone, are the predominant AV nodal blocking agent in 83.3% of BRASH cases.2 Although amiodarone’s class II and class IV properties may have been a minor additive factor, the pharmacokinetic, temporal, rhythm-evolution, and clinical-response evidence together support metoprolol as the primary driver of BRASH physiology in this patient.1,2

The hemodynamic consequences were predictable: with heart rate below 30 beats per minute and stroke volume already depressed by myocarditis (ejection fraction 22%), cardiac output was catastrophically reduced, whereas beta-1 blockade at the cardiomyocyte level further suppressed contractility through the adenylyl cyclase–cAMP axis.1,5 Sympathetic compensatory mechanisms were equally silenced, explaining why vasopressor support with norepinephrine and dopamine—agents that restore adrenergic tone through pathways independent of the blocked beta-receptor—was indispensable. Hyperkalemia was compounded by a dual mechanism: anuric renal failure eliminated the primary excretory route for potassium, whereas beta-2 blockade on skeletal muscle simultaneously impaired cellular potassium uptake via Na+/K+-ATPase, creating a positive feedback loop with the bradycardia.1,4,11 Hypoglycemia, though not part of the BRASH acronym, served as a critical diagnostic pivot. Beta-2 blockade silences both hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis; unlike the substrate-depletion hypoglycemia of septic or hypovolemic shock, which responds to dextrose, the defect in BRASH is enzymatic—the glucose production machinery is pharmacologically arrested.1,4,5 When dextrose and glucagon both fail to restore euglycemia, the pattern points specifically to beta-blocker toxicity as the underlying driver.

3 converging cardiomyopathic processes amplified the hemodynamic compromise: COVID-19-related myocarditis (direct viral cytopathy, immune-mediated inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction producing a hypercoagulable state reflected in the D-dimer of 6943 ng/mL),12, 13, 14 concurrent cocaine-mediated myocardial injury (coronary vasoconstriction, direct myocytotoxicity, and platelet activation),15 and tachycardia-mediated remodeling from sustained atrial flutter.16 The extensive biventricular thrombus burden—V-shaped LV apical thrombus (2.25 × 2.0 cm), fixed LV mid-septal thrombus (1.52 cm), and mobile right ventricular thrombus (2.92 × 0.58 cm)—was the predictable sequela of profoundly reduced cardiac output on a prothrombotic substrate, and the subsequent acute ischemic stroke with frontal lobe involvement was consistent with cardioembolic embolization.17,18

Once BRASH was recognized, the interventions mapped directly to the pathophysiology: cessation of metoprolol, vasopressor support to restore adrenergic tone, and urgent hemodialysis for metabolic correction.1,2 The recovery of glycemic control following metoprolol cessation—occurring before any meaningful improvement in cardiac function—confirmed that hypoglycemia had been driven by pharmacological blockade rather than the hemodynamic state alone, providing prospective validation that BRASH was a distinct and reversible component of the patient’s derangement. The subsequent cautious reintroduction of metoprolol at low doses underscores that the goal is not permanent abandonment of rate control, but precision dosing calibrated to the evolving renal and electrolyte trajectory. Current nephrology guidance emphasizes dose adjustment for renally-cleared beta-blockers (atenolol, nadolol, and sotalol), but metoprolol’s prescribing information states no dose reduction is needed in renal failure.7,19,20 This case illustrates that even a hepatically cleared AV nodal blocker can precipitate BRASH when its ordinary effect is potentiated by hyperkalemia. The lesson is that any AV nodal blocker’s effective potency should be reassessed continuously against the patient’s evolving electrolyte and acid-base status, with a low threshold to favor alternative agents when hyperkalemia or acidosis is present or actively evolving.

Conclusion

BRASH syndrome, although uncommon, represents a potentially fatal iatrogenic complication that develops from the pharmacodynamic synergy between AV nodal blockade and the hyperkalemia and acidosis of renal failure—a mechanism that does not require drug accumulation and can, therefore, occur even with hepatically cleared agents such as metoprolol. This case demonstrates that prompt recognition—triggered by the combination of bradycardia refractory to atropine, shock unresponsive to fluids, and refractory hypoglycemia in a patient receiving AV nodal blocking agents with deteriorating renal function—coupled with rapid institution of appropriate management (drug cessation, vasopressor support, and hemodialysis for metabolic correction) can be lifesaving. Clinicians must maintain heightened clinical suspicion for BRASH in any hospitalized patient with renal disease receiving negative chronotropic agents, and should continuously reassess the effective potency of these agents against the patient’s evolving electrolyte and acid-base status. The willingness to pivot diagnostic reasoning even in the presence of overwhelming alternative pathology is essential for recognizing and managing this iatrogenic syndrome.
