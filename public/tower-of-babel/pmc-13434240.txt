MIR1255A regulates pathways critical for brain development, risk genes for depression and neurodevelopmental disorders
Authors: Yu Feng, Karen G Wigg, Cathy L Barr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple lines of evidence implicate microRNAs (miRNAs) in major depressive disorder (MDD), including the location of miRNAs genes (MIRs) within genomic regions identified by genome wide association studies (GWAS). To investigate the role of MIRs in the genetic risk for MDD, we identified all associated single nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs) that were expression quantitative loci (eQTLs) for MIRs in brain tissues. Our analyses identified four MIRs having associated eQTLs in the credible SNP set (variants near a genetic association signal predicted to include causal variants). Based on pathway analyses of the predicted targets, we selected MIR1255A for functional studies and overexpressed it in human neural precursor cells (NPCs) to identify target genes in neural cells. Transcriptome analyses of the transfected cells identified 343 differentially expressed (DE) genes at an adjusted p (FDR) <0.05 and an additional 276 DE genes at adjusted p < 0.1 (total 619 genes). Of these, 14 genes were supported as genetic risk genes by fine mapping by GWAS (representation factor 1.8, p < 0.025), with additional genes implicated in risk from biological studies (altered expression or serum levels in individuals with MDD, animal models). Notably, 157 DE genes were implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders (representation factor 1.5, p < 3.31E-07). Gene set enrichment analyses of DE genes revealed the top categories as neurogenesis, generation of neurons, regulation of cell differentiation and neuron development. These findings support MIR1255A as regulator of processes critical for neurodevelopment and a contributor to genetic risk for depression.Subject terms: Stem cells, Comparative genomics

Introduction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNAs that play a crucial role in regulating gene expression, and the genes encoding miRNAs (MIRs) have been implicated in a range of brain and psychiatric disorders. Evidence linking miRNAs to brain disease comes from brain post-mortem tissue studies, analyses of other tissues (such as blood and cerebral spinal fluid), animal models, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [1–5]. A notable example is the MIR137 gene initially identified in GWAS for schizophrenia with subsequently overexpression studies identifying alterations in expression of genes contributing to neuronal differentiation and genes identified as GWAS significant risk loci for schizophrenia [5].

The relationship to psychiatric disorders is not surprising, given that miRNAs are integral to fundamental processes in brain development and function. These include neuron and glial cell type determination, cell proliferation, differentiation, neuron migration, synapse development, neurogenesis in fetal and adult brain, and the formation of neuronal circuits [6–8]. Furthermore, miRNAs are key gene regulators in the response to environmental stressors, influencing cellular responses to stress [1, 4, 7, 9, 10]. Stressful life events and the response to stressors are major risk factors for psychiatric disorders, particularly major depressive disorders (MDD) [11, 12], and miRNAs are likely to play a significant role in these processes.

MIRs have specifically been implicated in MDD by findings of altered expression in blood, post-mortem brain samples and animal models [4, 7, 13–15]. MiRNAs are documented regulators of pathways involved in depression, including neurotransmitter regulation, hyperactivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, as well as cell signaling and immune system pathways dysregulated in depression [13, 15]. Genetic studies have further implicated specific MIRs by virtue of the location of the genes within associated regions from GWAS as well as associated alleles correlated with expression (quantitative trait loci (eQTLs)) [3, 16].

In this study we employed bioinformatic approaches to identify MIR genes as potential risk genes for MDD based on correlation of expression of MIR genes and significantly associated and credible single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) identified in the 3 largest GWAS for MDD [17–19]. Credible SNP sets are sets of variants near a genetic association signal that are predicted, with a specific probability, to include the causal variant for that region. The eQTLs for MIRs were identified in datasets for small RNA from brain tissues that capture expression of mature RNAs and their host genes [16, 20–22] (GTEx small RNA eQTLs v10, https://www.gtexportal.org/home/downloads/adult-gtex/qtl). Based on the predicted gene targets of the miRNAs, one of the candidates, MIR1255A, was then selected and tested in functional studies by overexpression in human neural precursor cells (NPCs) to identify gene targets and pathways in neural cells. Results support MIR1255A as regulator of processes critical for neurodevelopment and a contributor to genetic risk for depression.

Materials and methods

Identification of MDD significant SNPs and eQTLs for MIRs

Data from the three largest available GWAS studies of MDD were used for the selection of candidate MIRs [17–19]. Results from fine mapping and credible SNP sets were obtained from those papers.

Thus far, there have been limited resources that specifically quantified the expression of small RNAs for brain tissues [16, 20–22] (GTEx small RNA eQTLs v10, https://www.gtexportal.org/home/downloads/adult-gtex/qtl). These include adult (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/downloads/adult-gtex/qtl) [20, 21] and fetal brain datasets [16, 22]. Most of the MIR eQTL datasets used for our analyses were specifically selected for small RNAs [16, 20–22]. For GTEx we used data from both RNA-seq (MIR containing host LINC genes, eQLT q < 0.1) and the small RNA-seq resource (GTEx small RNA eQTLs v10, https://www.gtexportal.org/home/downloads/adult-gtex/qtl, eQTL q < 0.1). All datasets reported only cis-eQTLs. Significant SNPs for MDD and SNPs in the credible SNP sets were matched for MIR eQTLs.

MIR target gene predictions

Gene targets and pathways of hsa-mir-1255A were predicted with miRDB (https://mirdb.org/mirdb/index.html) [23], Target Scan 7.2 (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_72/) [24], mirDIP version: 5.3.0.2 and MiRPathDB v2.0 (https://ophid.utoronto.ca/mirDIP/) [25].

Ethics approval

Human neural precursor cells were derived from H9 Embryonic Stem (ES) cells. The H9 ES line is approved for research use by the Canadian Institutes of Health Stem Cell Oversight Committee (SCOC) (https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/54006.html) and the NIH (https://stemcells.nih.gov/registry/eligible-to-use-lines). The protocol was approved by the SCOC and the University of Health Network Research Ethics Board (19-664). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Overexpression studies

Vectors expressing the MIR gene under control of the Cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter were obtained from Origene (Rockville, MD, Human MicroRNA Expression Plasmid MI0006389, SC400075). The control expression vector was identical to the MIR vector but contained no MIR gene (Origene CAT#: PCMVMIR). Vectors contained the Neomycin resistance gene to allow for antibiotic enrichment of transfected cells.

Cell lines were tested for mycoplasma using the EZ-PCR™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit from FroggaBio (Concord ON, Cat. #20-700-20). H9 cell colonies were plated on Matrigel-coated plates (Corning) and maintained in mTSeR medium (StemCell Technologies, Seattle, WA, USA) until 70% confluent. The colonies were then removed from the plate and cultured in suspension as embryoid bodies (EBs) for 2 weeks using NPC media (DMEM/F12 Thermo Fisher, Gibco 10565-018, 1X N2 Thermo Fisher, Gibco 17504-044, 1X B27 without Vitamin A, Thermo Fisher, Gibco 17502-048 and noggin) for generation of forebrain progenitors [26, 27]. The EBs were then plated on Poly-L-Ornithine (10 μg/ml, Sigma cat# P3655)/laminin (5μg/ml, Mouse, Invitrogen, Life Technologies, Cat # 23017-015) (PLOL) coated plates and maintained in NPC medium with FGF2 (Fibroblast Growth Factor Basic Recombinant Human Stemgent cat #03-0002 (50ug)), and 1ug/ml Laminin). The neural rosettes that emerged after 3–4 days were manually selected, dissociated with accutase (StemCell Technologies, Catalog # 07920) by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. Cells were transferred to a 15 ml conical tube with 10 mls of PBS, pelleted and resuspended in NBF media and plated. The NPC lines were then expanded and cryopreserved.

NPCs were transfected with the vectors using the Lonza nucleofector system (Ottawa ON, Amaxa Mouse Neural Stem Cell Nucleofector Kit, Cat# VPG-1004, program A-033). Transfection efficiency was monitored using expression of tGFP cloned within the vector. To increase the number of cells with the plasmid, transfected cells were enriched using Neomycin antibiotic selection for 24 h after transfection to allow expression.

RNA was extracted 72 h post transfection using the mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) [28]. Expression of the miRNA was checked by PCR to confirm overexpression before the mRNA was sent for sequencing. Due to the small size of the MIR, we used a stem-loop design for PCR amplification using the following primers: stem-loop reverse transcriptase primer 5′- GTC GTA TCC AGT GCA GGG TCC GAG GTA TTC GCA CTG GAT ACG ACA ATC TA-3′, Forward primer

5′-TGT GAT TAA ATG TCA AGA CAG GAT GAG CAA AGA A-3′, and Reverse primer

5′-CCA GTG CAG GGT CCG AGG TA-3′. Nucleotides were added at the 5′-end in the forward primer to obtain a visible band on the agarose gel.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) analyses

The design used three independent replicates for transfection of the miRNA vector and control vector. Sequencing was on the Illumina Novaseq X (150 bp pair-ended reads) with 88–126 million reads passing filters and 42–60 million mapped reads. Library preparation and sequencing was performed at the Hospital for Sick Children Centre for Applied Genomics. For RNA-Seq, Fastq files were preprocessed with fastp version 0.23.1 [29]. RNA-Seq raw reads were aligned to the GRCh37 genome using STAR version 2.7.9a [30] and transcription levels counted based on hg19 annotations using HTseq-count (HTSeq/0.11.0) [31]. Ambiguous reads were not counted. Differentially expressed genes were called using DESeq2 [32]. No covariates were included in the RNA-seq analysis. To assess the reproducibility of the RNA-seq libraries, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated based on log2(CPM + 1) values for all pairs of samples. All samples showed high correlation (r > 0.9, p < 0.01).

Gene set overlap of the differentially expressed genes was analyzed with GSEA (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/annotate.jsp) [33] using the following gene sets; KEGG_Legacy, KEGG_Medicus, Wikipathways, Reactome, GO Biological process, GO cellular component and GO molecular function. Enrichment in synaptic cellular components and biological processes was determined using SynGO v1.2 (https://www.syngoportal.org/) [34].

Results

Significant and credible SNPs were identified from three large GWAS studies of MDD [17–19]. SNPs were matched for brain eQTLs from datasets specific for small RNAs [16, 20–22] (GTEx small RNA eQTLs v10, https://www.gtexportal.org/home/downloads/adult-gtex/qtl). Current GWAS for MDD [17–19] did not annotate SNPs for eQLTs for small RNAs, thus, any relationships would be missed. For the most part, miRNAs are poorly captured in standard eQTL studies because sequencing libraries are size selected to remove small RNAs. For some genes, eQTLs are captured for the larger host gene. For example, in GTEx v10 brain tissue samples, eQTLs are captured for 57 LINC MIR host genes (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/downloads/adult-gtex/qtl). We searched the GTEx database for brain capturing eQTLs for the host genes, although the expression level of the host RNA may not associate with the expression of the processed, functional miRNA.

For the GTEx data, we used eQLTs from all available brain regions (Amygdala, Anterior cingulate cortex (BA24), Caudate, Cerebellar Hemisphere, Cerebellum, Cortex, Frontal Cortex (BA9), Hippocampus, Hypothalamus, Nucleus accumbens, Putamen, Substantia nigra). Associated SNPs that had nominal or significant evidence as a brain eQTL for the MIRs were identified for 19 of these. For 4 genes, the associated eQTLs were in the credible SNP set (MIR1255A (hsa-miR-1255a) [18, 19], MIR4528 (hsa-miR-4528) [19], MIR1908 (hsa-miR-1908-5p) [19], MIR5695 (hsa-miR-5695) [19].

The primate specific MIR gene, MIR1255A, was then the focus of further investigation based on two eQTLs reported in fetal brain [16], rs6845263 and rs865020 (eQTL p-value = 0.006 for both SNPs). The beta for rs6845263 was 0.360 and 0.3550 for rs865020 indicating increased expression with the alternative allele (C and A respectively). These are the non-effect alleles reported for MDD. This indicates that the effect alleles are associated with reduced expression of hsa-mir-1255a.

These two SNPs were GWAS significant in multiple MDD studies [17–19] (rs6845263 lead and index SNP p-value 4.875E-08, OR 1.015, beta 0.0154, S.E. 0.003 [17], 1.14E-08 OR 1.0157 [19], p-value 6.29E-09 [18]; rs865020 (lead SNP p value 9.78E-09 [19], p-value 4.86E-09 [18]. We note that the samples overlap for the 3 studies with each successive study increasing the sample size. The two SNPs also had the highest posterior inclusion probability (PIP) in the credible SNP set (SuSiE rs865020 PIP = 0.525 in European ancestry analyses, 0.767 in Diverse ancestry analyses [18]. PIP is defined as the probability for a SNP to be one of the causal SNPs in the region. The rs6845263 SNP was also reported as significant in an independent GWAS for insomnia (p-value 1E-8) which is significantly genetically correlated with MDD [35]. This data supports this locus and SNPs in depression related traits.

Both SNPs are located 5’of the PPP3CA (protein phosphatase 3 catalytic subunit alpha) gene transcription start site. MIR1255A is in the first intron of PPP3CA and transcribed in the same direction, suggesting co-regulation with PPP3CA. However, MIR1255A has ubiquitous expression with low levels of expression (~1 Transcripts per million, TPM) found in almost all tissues profiled in GTEx (https://gtexportal.org/home/), whereas PPP3CA shows much higher expression in brain (~150 TPM) compared to other tissues (~30–40 TPM) suggesting tissue specific independent regulation (transcriptional or post-transcriptional). Co-expression between the MIR and host gene can be cell type dependent [36, 37].

PPP3CA is part of the calcineurin complex, a serine/threonine phosphatase activated by increased intracellular calcium concentrations that directly links calcium signaling to protein phosphorylation [38]. The targets of calcineurin are diverse and include transcription factors, mitochondrial proteins, microtubules, and receptors including glutamate receptors [38]. Neither of the SNPs in the credible SNP set are eQTLs for PPP3CA in brain tissues in PsychENCODE [39] or for any tissue in GTEx, however these SNPs are eQTLs in both databases for an uncharacterized non-coding gene located 5’ of PPP3CA, FLJ20021.

Given that the significant SNPs in the credible SNP set are eQTLs for MIR1255A but not PPP3CA supports the study of MIR1255A as a possible contributor to MDD at this locus. Intronic miRNAs are often co-expressed with their host gene with evidence of synergistic as well as antagonistic relationships [36, 37, 40–42]. The host and miRNA are often found to regulate the same biological pathways, or the miRNA targets a downstream effector of the host [37, 40, 43, 44]. The miRNA- host gene interactions are a specific and evolutionarily conserved autoregulatory mechanism [44]. Thus, altered expression of MIR1255A may impact expression of PPP3CA. However, if hsa-mir-1255a directly regulates PPP3CA, it would be expected that the SNPs would be eQTLs for both genes. Another possibility is that hsa-mir-1255a impacts PPP3CA by altering translation. We queried protein QTL (pQTL) databases (pQTL-Atlas (https://pqtl.whu.edu.cn/home), the ADSP FunGen-AD xQTL data portal with integrated brain pQTLs from ROSMAP, Mount Sinai Brain Bank (MSBB), and the Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center (https://xqtl.niagads.org/)) for evidence that associated SNPs are pQTLs. No pQTLs were identified for PPP3CA. Further, the associated SNPs were not pQTLs for any protein. We note that a relationship in tissues/cells not sampled by those resources cannot be ruled out.

According to mirDIP analyses, PPP3CA is a predicted target of hsa-miR-1255a with a very high score class (top 1%)(https://ophid.utoronto.ca/mirDIP/index.jsp#r). However, this prediction was not supported by TargetScan 8.0 or miRDB (supplementary Tables S1–3).

Using MirPathDB the top 5 pathways of predicted targets of hsa-mir-1255a were synapse, anatomical structure morphogenesis, nervous system development, cell junction, and cellular localization (Supplementary Table S4). Based on these predictions, MIR1255A was chosen for functional study. Given the high false positive rates from prediction programs (close to 50%) [45], we sought to validate the predicted gene targets of hsa-mir-1255a, by transfecting a plasmid expressing the MIR1255A gene under the CMV promoter and identifying differential expressed genes by RNA-seq (Mapping statistics in Supplementary Table S5). Overexpression of MIR1255A in the transfected cells compared to control vectors was confirmed by PCR using a stem loop design before RNA-Seq.

Overexpression of MIR1255A resulted in differentially expression of 343 genes at an adjusted p value (False Discovery Rate (FDR)) < 0.05 and an additional 276 differentially expressed genes at an adjusted p < 0.1 (total 619)(Supplementary Table S6, Volcano Plot Supplementary Fig. S1). Our data did not show significant differences in expression of the host gene PPP3CA. We included all differentially expressed genes with FDR < 0.1 in Supplementary Table S6, regardless of the fold change because we did not expect dramatic changes in individual genes. Even complete loss of a MIR gene rarely results in a dramatic phenotype and here we increased expression [46]. MiRNAs generally work to fine tune expression with perturbations of miRNA expression resulting only in small changes to each of the gene targets at the mRNA and protein levels [47]. The weak changes over all the targets, however, are thought to function cumulatively at the systems level [48]. Indeed, we observed significant enrichment of over 100 gene sets for DE genes FDR < 0.05 (top 100 shown in Supplementary Table S7). Gene set enrichment identified the top 5 categories as neurogenesis, generation of neurons, regulation of cell differentiation, positive regulation of developmental process and neuron development (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Table S7). The significant pathways for synapse, nervous system development, generation of neurons, neurogenesis, among other neural pathways, supports the identified pathways from predicted gene targets. For synaptic gene enrichment, we used SynGO, a knowledge base of expert-curated annotation of synaptic biology [34]. Analyses of the DE genes conditioned on brain-expressed genes as the background, identified significant evidence for enrichment of genes in multiple components and processes of the synapse, particularly the synaptic cleft and post-synaptic structures (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S8).

DE genes conditioned on brain-expressed genes as the background, identified significant evidence for enrichment of genes in multiple components and processes of the synapse, particularly the synaptic cleft and postsynapse.

We checked to see if the DE genes were predicted hsa-mir-1255A targets using three datasets, TargetScan 8.0, miRDB v22.1 and mirDIP version 5.3.0.2. In total, 143 of the DE genes (23%) were predicted targets by one or more prediction programs (Supplementary Table S6). We also checked to see if there was any evidence for experimentally validated interactions of hsa-mir-1255a with the DE genes using TarBase, a resource for curated interactions [49]. For this database, raw datasets produced by interactome mapping methods were uniformly analyzed to maintain consistently high-quality standards, (https://dianalab.e-ce.uth.gr/tarbasev9). Twenty-five of the DE genes have experimental evidence for physical interaction with hsa-mir-1255a supporting these genes as direct targets (Supplementary Table S6). The datasets for interactions with hsa-mir-1255a were from 4 publications [50–53] based on experiments, using 4 different cell types (HeLa, 22RV1, LNCAP, HK2), all of which are tumorigenic. Currently there are no published data for interactions for hsa-mir-1255a in neural cells.

Given that the MIR1255A genomic region is implicated in MDD, we determined if there was evidence implicating the DE genes in genetic risk for MDD using genes identified by fine mapping from the largest available MDD GWAS study [18]. This identified 14 genes that were DE (Supplementary Table S6) of the 225 genes supported by fine mapping (background 17,902 expressed genes in the NPCs, significant evidence for enrichment, representation factor 1.8, p < 0.025). Some of the genes in the DE gene list were supported as depression risk genes from biological studies with evidence for altered expression or serum levels in individuals with depression as well as animal models for depressive behaviour (e.g. NEGR1, PER1, S100B, PDE4B) [54–57]. We note that the p-adj values for NEGR1 and PER1 were only significant at <0.1 (6.15E-02 and 9.98E-02 respectively).

Given that the SNP rs6845263 is GWAS significant for insomnia, we examined the overlap between genes designated as high confidence genes in GWAS for insomnia [35]. Twenty four of 571 insomnia high confidence genes were DE (Supplementary Table S6) but this was not significant (Representation factor: 1.2, p < 0.189). Five of the insomnia genes were also MDD fine mapped genes. Interestingly, 14 of the DE genes have a role in controlling circadian rhythms (PER1, TIMELESS, CSNK1D, CREB1, ZFHX3, JUN, JUND, FOS, FOSB, IER2, IER5, DDIT4, TFRC, TFAM). This suggests the possibility that dysregulation by altered hsa-mir-1255a expression may contribute to the risk for insomnia and sleep disruption in MDD by altering circadian rhythm gene expression.

Noticeably, some of the MDD genes had previously been identified as risk genes for neurodevelopmental disorders including autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Based on this observation, we examined the entire list of DE genes to see if they were previously identified as risk for ASD using the SFARI database (https://gene.sfari.org/database/human-gene/) and for neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD) using the SysNDD databases (https://sysndd.dbmr.unibe.ch/). The SysNDD database is a curated database for genes identified in developmental delay, intellectual disability and ASD. Genes in the SFARI database with a high confidence score, strong candidate or syndromic were used in the analyses. For the NDD database, genes with limited or refuted evidence were not included in the analyses. In total 2,526 ASD and NDD genes were included in the analyses. The background gene list was all genes expressed in the NPCs (n = 17,902). We identified 152 of the 919 DE genes were implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD) (Supplementary Table S6) indicating significant enrichment in the DE gene group for neurodevelopmental genes (representation factor representation factor: 1.7, p < 1.44E-12).

Among the DE genes were genes related to the neurobiology of Alzheimers disease including APOE, LRP1, LRP6, PLD3, CNTNAP2, A2M, CTSB and SORL1 [58–60]. Currently there is no known relationship between the MIR1255A locus and neurodegenerative disease.

Also of note is the reduced expression of the breast cancer risk gene, BRCA2. Higher expression of hsa-miR-1255a in exosomes and original cell samples from breast cancer and breast cancer cell lines compared to control cells, was previously associated with poor overall breast cancer survival. Further, hsa-miR-1255a was suggested as a novel therapeutic target for breast cancer by the authors [61]. Interestingly, BRCA2 has also been identified as an ASD risk gene (https://gene.sfari.org/). BRCA2 is a predicted target by TargetScan v8 suggesting BRCA2 is a direct target of hsa-miR-1255a and further research on this relationship is warranted.

Discussion

MiRNAs have been proposed as potential biomarkers for MDD as well as indicators of treatment response [2, 4, 5, 7, 13, 14, 62–65]. The ability of miRNAs and extracellular vesicles containing miRNAs, to cross the brain blood barrier, be detected in blood, and exhibit relative stability has fueled interest in miRNAs as biomarkers for MDD [7, 10]. Additionally, altered miRNA expression profiles have been documented in response to various treatments, including cognitive behaviour therapy, electroconvulsive therapy, and antidepressant treatments [7, 63, 64, 66], leading to investigations into the potential of miRNA profiles for predicting therapeutic response. Furthermore, the manipulation of miRNA expression—via mimics and inhibitors—has been proposed as a treatment for psychiatric disorders [4, 67, 68]. Thus, identifying MIR risk genes as well as their target genes and pathways, particularly in neural cells, is an important step in moving forward to therapeutics.

In our study, differentially expressed genes in NPCs overexpressing MIR1255A revealed significant alterations in expression of 14 genes of the 225 genes supported by fine mapping for MDD [18]. Notably, four genes—NEGR1, PER1, S100B, and PDE4B are supported by biological data linking the gene to MDD. Although as noted above, the p-adj values for NEGR1 and PER1 were only significant at <0.1. Further molecular studies are necessary to determine how differential expression alters risk.

Our findings support a role of MIR1255A in genetic risk within the GWAS region independent of expression changes of PPP3CA. It is estimated that 20% of miRNAs directly target their host gene, thereby fine-tuning the host gene’s expression and related pathways [37, 40]. Although PPP3CA, was a predicted to be a target of hsa-miR-1255a by mirDIP version: 5.3.0.1, it was not identified as a target but other prediction tools. Further, the SNPs that were eQTLs for hsa-mir-1225a, were not eQTLs for PPP3CA. We found no significant changes in expression of PPP3CA, which suggests that at least in this cell type, hsa-miR-1255a does not directly regulate PPP3CA. However, the two genes may function to coordinate gene expression within the same pathways, and the relationship warrants further exploration.

The altered expression of neurodevelopmental risk genes and pathways is particularly intriguing, given previous data indicating links between disrupted neurodevelopment and depression [69]. This support is gleamed from evidence for higher prevalence of depression among individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders [70–72] and significant genetic correlation between depression and neurodevelopmental disorders including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and ASD (e.g. ADHD (rg = 0.424), ASD (rg = 0.52)) [73–75]. This relationship appears to be especially pronounced for early-onset depression, which is associated with an increased genetic risk for neurodevelopmental disorders—specifically ADHD and traits like pragmatic language difficulties and social communication challenges [76].

A limitation of our study is the reliance on a single cell type. NPCs are a critical cell type in brain development responsible for the development of all neurons and glia [77], and thus an obvious choice for the functional studies, However hsa-mir-1255A, likely influences gene networks in multiple neural cell types. Future studies examining other types of neurons and glia cells are essential for a more comprehensive understanding of miRNA regulation in MDD.

In this study, we focused on a single candidate miRNA implicated from genetic studies; however, miRNAs are known to function coordinately to regulate gene expression [46, 78]. Thus, it is crucial to investigate the networks of miRNAs in MDD. Key candidates include other MIRs within the same associated region (e.g. MIR8066 in the PPP3CA gene). Other genes identified from genome scans as well as those miRNAs previously reported to be dysregulated in MDD are candidates for investigation.
