Experimental and simulation study of advanced injection strategies in an ammonia-diesel dual‑fuel engine for performance and combustion evaluation
Authors: Kislay Kishore, P. Pradeep, Mayank Mittal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study focuses on evaluating split-injection strategies in an ammonia–diesel dual-fuel (ADDF) engine at 1800 rpm under medium load conditions, a load range of particular interest in stationary engine operation, with the broader goal of improving combustion stability and efficiency when operating near a 50% ammonia energy contribution. Medium-load ADDF operation, particularly in small compression ignition (CI) engines, remains sparsely explored, and the influence of diesel split injections on combustion phasing and efficiency is not well understood. In this study, a twin-cylinder common rail direct injection (CRDI) diesel engine was operated in ADDF mode at 1800 rpm and medium load, with ammonia energy share of approximately 50%. A systematic experimental investigation was conducted by varying pilot injection ratio (PIR) up to 50% and pilot injection timing from 46 to 66 crank angle degrees before top dead center (CAD BTDC), while maintaining a fixed main injection timing. The experiments were complemented with computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations using CONVERGE v3.0 to analyze in-cylinder combustion behavior. Results show that an optimized split-injection strategy (PIR of 20% and pilot timing of 60 CAD BTDC) significantly improves combustion efficiency (~ 95%) and enhances brake thermal efficiency under ADDF operation. Further increase in PIR leads to efficiency deterioration due to unfavorable combustion phasing. The pressure and heat release characteristics reveal a shift toward low-temperature combustion with reduced peak pressure compared to diesel-only operation, attributed to the higher specific heat capacity of ammonia. The optimized split strategy advances CA50, reduces combustion duration, and minimizes negative work, thereby improving overall combustion performance. OH-radicals from simulation corroborate a hierarchy of high-temperature reaction intensity: diesel-only > ADDF split > ADDF single. Stability remained acceptable with controlled COV of IMEP under 3%. This study provides an integrated experimental-CFD evaluations of split diesel injection for a medium-load ADDF engine, and identifies an optimized pilot-main injection strategy capable of enhancing ammonia utilization while mitigating efficiency penalties. These findings offer actionable guidance for ADDF operation in power generation sector and other medium-load CI applications.

Introduction

Researchers are increasingly prioritizing the shift from conventional energy sources to cleaner options to help curb global warming while meeting the rising worldwide energy demand. Within this context, the automotive sector stands out as one of the largest consumers of energy and is responsible for almost 25% of global carbon di-oxide (CO2) emissions1. To address this challenge, the adoption of carbon-free fuels and vehicle electrification are considered effective pathways to reduce carbon-based emissions2. However, complete electrification is not expected to be feasible for heavy-duty and marine transport sectors, while stationary power generation systems continue to rely on combustion-based technologies, due to limitations of current battery technology in terms of energy density, weight, and cost3,4. Hence, a better carbon-free alternative fuel is required to power these sectors5. Hydrogen is often viewed as a promising alternative; however, current storage and transportation technologies are not yet mature enough to enable its safe and widespread use in engines6. With the present technology, the direct use of hydrogen in engines remains difficult, as the storage and utilization technologies still need to be further matured before becoming a practical option.

In this regard, ammonia (NH3) emerges as a viable alternative fuel. Ammonia is a carbon-free fuel and also serves as an efficient hydrogen carrier7. The interest in studying the combustion behaviour of ammonia such as flame propagation, ignition delay, and combustion mechanism has existed since the 1950s8. It offers several advantages over hydrogen, including higher energy density, a mature supply chain, and comparatively safer handling9. Ammonia’s key advantage is that it does not produce CO2 during combustion, making it an ideal candidate for achieving carbon-neutrality. Despite these benefits, the use of ammonia in compression ignition (CI) engines is limited by its low flame speed and poor auto-ignition characteristics10. Nevertheless, these challenges can be addressed by utilizing ammonia in dual-fuel mode with diesel as a pilot fuel. This approach not only enables the effective use of ammonia in CI engines but also contributes to a reduction in carbon-based emissions, as well as thermal nitrogen oxides (NOx) and soot emissions11.

Life cycle assessment tests conducted with green ammonia-powered vehicles recorded a 56% reduction in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions compared to diesel-powered vehicles12. However, the use of ammonia with diesel resulted in an increased ignition delay and reduced thermal efficiency13. Ammonia–diesel dual-fuel (ADDF) operation, has shown the capability to reduce CO₂ emissions while maintaining similar power output14. Since ammonia has low flame speed and high ignition energy requirements, the ammonia combustion leads to unburnt ammonia slip11. Even with nitrogen in fuel, NOx emissions were found to be lower than diesel operation for up to 40% diesel substitution with ammonia14. Similar studies with up to 30% ammonia substitution reported the occurrence of N2O (nitrous oxide) emissions, along with unburned ammonia and reduced thermal efficiency15. Furthermore, double injection of the pilot fuel in ADDF mode was found to reduce ammonia slip, but at the cost of increased N₂O emissions16. It was also observed that increasing the NH3 share at a constant start of diesel injection (SODI) led to an increase in N2O emissions. However, advancing the SODI was found to reduce GHG emissions by 12% compared to the diesel-only mode17.

Niki et al.18 investigated the effect of advancing diesel injection timing in ADDF combustion and observed an increase in brake thermal efficiency (BTE). However, when the diesel injection timing exceeded beyond 40 crank angle degrees before top dead center (CAD BTDC), higher HC, CO, and NOx emissions were reported due to fuel wall impingement. In general, advancing pilot fuel injection in dual-fuel mode enhances mixture preparation and promotes stable combustion19, whereas retarding the injection increases fuel stratification, thereby prolonging the combustion duration20. Researchers have also examined the effect of split pilot fuel injection on combustion and emission parameters of ADDF engines. Niki et al.21 reported that the split injection strategy improved combustion efficiency from 90.7% (single injection) to 94.3%, while also reducing N2O and NH3 emissions. Similarly, Yousefi et al.22 demonstrated that split injection increased flame speed and thermal efficiency due to improved mixing, and unburned NH3 emissions were reduced by 83.5% compared to single injection. Xu et al23 analyzed the combined effects of diesel injection timing, pressure, and quantity along with ammonia injection timing. The study showed improved combustion efficiency and faster flame propagation with an optimum pilot diesel quantity. Advanced ammonia injection resulted in better mixture formation but also increased ammonia slip, whereas retarded ammonia injection improved combustion efficiency due to stratification in locally rich zones. Increasing pilot injection pressure enhanced combustion efficiency, but beyond a certain limit, deterioration was observed. Advancing pilot injection timing also increased power output but led to excess NOx emissions.

From these studies, it can be inferred that ammonia in ADDF mode can be effectively utilized with split diesel fuel injection. However, it is also noted that research related to medium-load operation is limited. Since most genset engines operate at low to medium load for the majority of their runtime24,25, further studies focusing on improving engine efficiency at medium load would be highly impactful.

To understand the underlying mechanisms such as species formation and distribution, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) models are widely used to simulate ADDF combustion under various operating conditions. Yousefi et al.22 conducted experiments with an ADDF engine and validated their results using CONVERGE CFD simulations. They employed the SAGE combustion model for simulations and constructed a reaction mechanism by integrating two established mechanisms for ammonia and diesel combustion, adopted from Otomo et al.26 and Frassoldati et al.27, comprising 32 species with 213 reactions and 98 species with 895 reactions, respectively. A fixed embedding grid scaling method was adopted, with a base grid size of 3.0 mm for the engine cylinder and 1.5 mm for intake and exhaust ports. For mesh refinement, adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) was applied. Their models showed good agreement with experimental results for heat release rate (HRR), pressure vs. crank angle (P-θ) diagrams. They reported that the use of split injection significantly improves indicated thermal efficiency, achieving values up to ~ 39.7% (BMEP ~ 8.1 bar) in a heavy-duty engine platform. Furthermore, split injection was shown to substantially reduce unburned ammonia emissions (by up to ~ 83.5% compared to single diesel injection operation) and lower greenhouse gas emissions through reduction in N₂O formation. Altinkurt et al.1 explored ADDF combustion and validated their results using CONVERGE 3.0. They employed the SAGE combustion model with a reaction mechanism consisting of 76 species and 464 reactions. A 6 mm grid size with AMR was applied in regions of high velocity and temperature gradients. Spray breakup and atomization were modelled using a four-level fixed embedding technique. Their CFD results also correlated well with experiments. Xu et al.23 studied ADDF combustion for marine engine applications using both 1D and 3D models. For 3D simulations, CONVERGE 3.0 was used along with the SAGE combustion model and a reaction mechanism based on a combination of chemical kinetics and GRI-Mech 3.0.

From the above studies, it is evident that most CFD investigations of ADDF combustion have been carried out on marine engines, while studies on small and medium-sized engines remain limited. Further research in this area could provide deeper insights, particularly regarding split injection characteristics. Although CFD is a powerful tool for simulating combustion phenomena, it is both time-consuming and costly, especially when studying minor variations in experimental conditions. To address this, 0-D thermodynamic models can serve as a complementary approach during the initial stages of research. These models can provide a simpler, faster, and more cost-effective understanding of complex combustion processes, reducing the need of CFD simulation while still offering valuable insights. Chandra et al.28 developed a thermodynamic-based model for SI engines with the objective of understanding several in-cylinder phenomena such as mixture composition, gas exchange, compression and expansion processes, and wall heat transfer. To achieve a better assessment of engine performance, a full-cycle simulation was carried out by incorporating three integrated sub-models. Their results demonstrated a close agreement between simulation and experimental data. From this conclusion, it is evident that a well-developed thermodynamic model can significantly reduce the cost and time required during the initial stages of experiments, by minimizing the number of test conditions to be considered.

From the reviewed literature, it is clear that three major research gaps remain unaddressed: (i) very limited experimental evidence exists on ADDF combustion at medium load conditions, particularly for small and medium-sized CI engines; (ii) the influence of split diesel injection strategies on mixture preparation, combustion phasing, and efficiency near 50% AES has not been systematically evaluated; and (iii) no integrated framework combining experiments, and CFD simulation has been reported for ADDF engines in this size range. Therefore, this work aims to fill these gaps by experimentally evaluating a split-injection strategy for a twin-cylinder ADDF engine at medium load, supported by detailed CFD analysis using CONVERGE. The novelty of this study lies in identifying an optimized pilot-main diesel split for stable and efficient ADDF operation at substantial ammonia substitution, and in providing insights from experiments, detailed simulations, and thermodynamic modelling.

Experimental setup and operating conditions

The experimental investigations utilized a twin-cylinder, four-stroke common rail direct injection (CRDI) diesel engine platform with natural aspiration, featuring a total displacement of 909 cc achieved through an 83 mm bore and an 84 mm stroke29. Each cylinder employed four-valve actuation, coupled with a 14.05 cm connecting rod and an 18.5:1 compression ratio. The diesel fuel injection system comprised of a second-generation Bosch common rail arrangement with solenoid-actuated injectors, utilizing SAC-type nozzles containing seven 0.138 mm orifices arranged in a 152° spray cone configuration. Figure 1a depicts the image of actual experimental setup and Fig. 1b shows the actual gas supply line with mass flow controllers and pressure gauge. Figure 2 presents the schematic of the experimental setup used for experiments. For dual-fuel operation with ammonia, the base configuration was enhanced through integration of a dedicated gaseous fuel supply system into the intake manifold. This modification included an ammonia storage cylinder, two-stage pressure regulator, anti-corrosive mass flow control, and a mixing chamber to ensure homogeneous mixture formation. Engine control unit involved a NIRA i7RS/1 hardware for real-time control adjustment. Combustion diagnostics employed a Kistler 6052C piezoelectric transducer mounted in the cylinder head, with pressure signals conditioned through a charge amplifier and recorded at 0.1 CAD intervals via NI DAQ hardware. Load control was maintained using an eddy current dynamometer, while intake airflow quantification utilized an orifice-plate flow meter upstream of a surge tank. For 120 consecutive engine cycles, in-cylinder pressure data with a resolution of 0.1 CAD was captured. The absolute pressure within the intake manifold was measured using a piezo-resistive pressure transducer (model number 4007C by Kistler) coupled with the charge amplifier. Temperature readings in the intake and exhaust manifolds were obtained using thermocouple-based sensors. The required ammonia gas flow rate was maintained using a pressure gauge (model number ALICAT PS series) and a mass flow controller (model number ALICAT MCRS series). This was done with the aid of Flow Vision MX software. Readers may refer to Hameed et al.30 and Kishore et al.31 for measurement ranges and accuracies of the instruments used. For analysis, data was gathered in steady-state conditions during experiment. The properties of ammonia and diesel differ significantly and are illustrated in Table 1. Additionally, the minimum ignition energy for ammonia is 8–10 mJ, which is considerably higher than diesel’s 0.2–0.3 mJ32,33. The flammability limit for ammonia is between 15 and 28% by volume, while diesel’s flammability limit is between 1 and 6%34. Experiments were performed at 1800 RPM, where a 4-pole generator produces 60 Hz, making it suitable for electricity generation with a dual-fuel engine. The engine operated at a medium load of 24 Nm (BMEP 3.318 bar), with diesel injected at a common rail pressure of 300 bar. The operating conditions, summarized in Table 2, were carefully designed to investigate the combustion characteristics of the ammonia-diesel dual-fuel (ADDF) engine. The current study extends the authors’ earlier work, where the effects of varying ammonia energy share (AES) and diesel injection timings (DIT) were explored to determine suitable operating conditions in ADDF engines31,35. Based on those findings, an AES of around 47% and the main injection timing of 36 CAD BTDC were adopted for all the ADDF cases here, as this level was shown to provide stable combustion and consistent engine performance. For dual-fuel operation, the ammonia flow rate was fixed at 40 SLPM and regulated via PC-based control of the mass flow controller. These controlled variations allow for a comprehensive analysis of how split-injection strategies can enhance ammonia utilization while ensuring reliable and efficient combustion in ADDF engines. CFD simulation and numerical modelling methodology

The commercial CFD program CONVERGE v3.0 was used for numerical simulations. Only a basic synopsis of the models and sub-models is provided here, as the specifics have already been documented in the authors’ earlier work31. The chemistry calculations employed the transient chemical kinetics solver SAGE, which was integrated into the CFD model. Chemical kinetic mechanisms for ammonia and diesel, consisting of 69 species and 389 reactions, were adopted from Xu et al.40. The RNG k-ε turbulence model was applied to simulate turbulent flow within the combustion chamber, and the Kelvin–Helmholtz Rayleigh–Taylor (KH-RT) model was used to capture diesel droplet atomization and breakup. The RNG k–ε turbulence model was used due to its widespread validation and consistent performance in closed-cycle diesel and dual-fuel engine simulations41. This RANS model is commonly adopted in CONVERGE-based ADDF studies and provides a good compromise between computational efficiency and the ability to capture spray-induced turbulence and low-temperature heat-release behaviour42. The chemical kinetics mechanism employed is a merged ammonia–diesel surrogate mechanism developed from two extensively validated mechanisms (Otomo et al.26; Frassoldati et al.27), ensuring reliable prediction of ignition delay, major species, and HRR characteristics for the ADDF operating regime. Figure 3a depicts an isometric view of the computational domain of the studied engine. The base grid size of 4 mm was selected after performing a grid independence study. To achieve the required mesh resolution in critical zones, fixed embedding was applied to refine the base grid according to predefined embed scales assigned to specific boundaries and regions. Figure 3b illustrates how the grid was dynamically refined using AMR and fixed embedding approaches. This improved simulation accuracy as the computation went on. To reduce the computational time and expense, closed-cycle simulations (IVC to EVO) were conducted for the engine model43. At the IVC condition, the cylinder region was initialized with a premixed ammonia-air charge whose composition and temperature were directly extracted from the experimentally measured ammonia mass, air mass, and mixture temperature for each operating condition. An additional 3% of the trapped mass was included to account for residual gases typically present at intake valve closure44. Diesel fuel was introduced through direct injection using the same pilot and main injection timings and injected quantities measured during the experiments. This initialization procedure ensures that the simulation accurately reflects the in-cylinder mixture state and injection behaviour observed in the physical engine.Fig. 3(a) Isometric view of the computational domain; (b) Side view at the BDC position showing meshing and AMR.

A zero-dimensional thermodynamic analysis was applied to the measured in-cylinder pressure data to estimate the engine’s heat-release and combustion characteristics. The net heat release rate was obtained using the first-law heat-release formulation along with the engine’s geometric parameters for volume and surface area. Convective heat-loss effects were incorporated using the Hohenberg correlation (Eqs. 7 and 8)28. The cumulative gross heat release was then calculated and compared with the total fuel energy to determine the combustion efficiency.

Results and discussion

Effect of split injection on performance and combustion characteristics of ADDF engine

Figure 5 illustrates the BTE characteristics of an ADDF combustion compared to diesel-only operation. In diesel-only mode, employing a single injection at 23.4 CAD BTDC, the engine achieves a BTE of 26%, as denoted by the red square marker. Conversely, the ADDF operation, represented by the blue line, demonstrates a variable BTE as a function of pilot injection ratio (PIR).Fig. 5Experimental brake thermal efficiency showing effect of PIR and varying pilot injection timings.

Experimental brake thermal efficiency showing effect of PIR and varying pilot injection timings.

This trend was analyzed under fixed injection timings for both pilot (60 CAD BTDC) and main (36 CAD BTDC) fuel injections. The BTE for the ADDF operation initially increases from 24.3% at 0% PIR (representing the main (single) injection at 36 CAD BTDC) to a peak of approximately 25% at the PIR of 20%. This initial improvement suggests that a small pilot injection of diesel enhances the overall combustion process by improving the flame propagation and stability of the ammonia-air mixture. However, as the PIR increases beyond 20%, the BTE progressively declines, reaching its lowest value of 21% at 50% PIR. This decline is attributed to several factors, including the increase in pilot fuel quantity, which leads to a reduction in in-cylinder temperature because of the higher latent heat requirement of the diesel pilot. Figure 6a illustrates the combustion efficiency as a function of PIR. It is evident that the combustion efficiency resembles the BTE trend, peaking at approximately 95% at 20% PIR. This confirms that the decreased thermal efficiency is a direct consequence of incomplete combustion of the fuel–air mixture. Further analysis, as shown in the supplementary graph (see Fig. 5), reveals the impact of varying the pilot injection timing (magenta line) while keeping the PIR fixed at 20%. The BTE reaches a maximum of approximately 25% at a pilot timing of 60 CAD BTDC. Deviations from this optimal timing, either earlier (66 CAD BTDC) or later (46 CAD BTDC), resulted in a significant drop in BTE and an increase in convective heat transfer.Fig. 6Combustion efficiency calculated from 0-D thermodynamic modelling using Hohenberg correlation at (a) varying PIR (b) varying pilot injection timing.

Combustion efficiency calculated from 0-D thermodynamic modelling using Hohenberg correlation at (a) varying PIR (b) varying pilot injection timing.

Figure 6b shows how combustion efficiency is affected by pilot injection timing. The result validates that a diesel pilot timing of 60 CAD BTDC facilitates the most effective and complete combustion of the ADDF mixture. While the BTE of ADDF combustion strategy is generally lower than that of diesel-only operation, the results demonstrate that it can be significantly improved through careful calibration. The optimal configuration, identified at 20% PIR and a pilot injection timing of 60 CAD BTDC, maximizes the BTE. Figure 7a presents the brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) and brake specific energy consumption (BSEC) characteristics (Fig. 7b), which are inversely proportional to the previously discussed BTE. As shown in Fig. 7a, the BSFC for the ADDF operation initially decreases to a minimum of 550 g/kW-hr at a 20% PIR, before progressively increasing with higher PIRs. Similarly, Fig. 7b shows that the BSEC follows a concurring trend, reaching its lowest value of 14.75 MJ/kW-hr at the same 20% PIR. Both BSFC and BSEC values are higher than the conventional diesel operation with a BSFC of 250 g/kW-hr and a BSEC of 13.8 MJ/kW-hr.Fig. 7Experimental results showing (a) BSFC (b) BSEC at all operating conditions.

Experimental results showing (a) BSFC (b) BSEC at all operating conditions.

The experimental in-cylinder pressure plot for both diesel-only and ADDF operations is demonstrated in Fig. 8a. During the compression stroke, the reduction in in-cylinder pressure observed with the addition of ammonia is mainly due to the increased specific heat capacity of the ammonia-air mixture, consequently reducing the in-cylinder temperature and thus resulting in lower peak cylinder pressure compared to diesel-only operation. In diesel-only operation, the peak cylinder pressure reaches 102 bar. Under ADDF operation with PIR of 10–50% (pilot at 60 CAD BTDC and main at 36 CAD BTDC), the peak pressure is consistently lower, ranging from 95 to 98 bar. When pilot injection timing was varied from the highest BTE point (PIR:20%; pilot & main: 60 &36 CAD BTDC), the peak pressure increased. Either advancing or retarding the pilot timing resulted in earlier SOC and CA50 occurring before TDC, as shown in Fig. 9b and c. Advancing it from 46 to 50 and 54 CAD BTDC, SOC, and CA50 occurred earlier, resulting in an increase in peak pressure as it is advanced. When it is further advanced to 60 CAD BTDC, the SOC is delayed with CA50 occurring after TDC, which reduces the peak pressure and shifts the pressure curve towards the expansion stroke. Further advancing this to 66 CAD BTDC again shifts the CA50 occurring before TDC, which resulted in increased negative work and also a reduction in BTE. Thus, pilot injection timing of 60 CAD BTDC was the most suitable at the PIR of 20% and main injection timing of 36 CAD BTDC. Figure 8b presents the heat release rate (HRR) traces for all cases. For the diesel-only operation, the HRR exhibits the characteristic behaviour of conventional diesel combustion, showing a dominant premixed heat-release phase followed by the diffusion-controlled combustion. In contrast, all ADDF conditions display a distinct two-stage heat-release behaviour typical of low-temperature combustion at high ammonia substitution. The first peak corresponds to the low-temperature reaction stage initiated by the pilot-injected diesel interacting with the ammonia-air mixture, while the second peak represents the onset of high-temperature main combustion. The magnitude and timing of both stages shift depending on the pilot fraction and injection timing, consistent with the pressure trends observed in Fig. 8a.Fig. 8Experimental results showing (a) in-cylinder pressure plot for both diesel-only and ADDF operation (b) heat release rate for both diesel-only and ADDF operation.Fig. 9(a) Combustion duration (CA10-CA90) (b) Start of combustion (c) CA50 at varying PIR and pilot injection timings.

The combustion duration (CD) was defined as the time taken in crank angle degrees from CA10 (10% of CHR) to CA90 (90% of CHR) and was depicted in Fig. 9a. Under ADDF mode, CD tends to decrease relative to diesel-only, but BTE is nonetheless lower for ADDF. The reason is that even with delayed SOC and CA10 in ADDF, the overall burn duration from SOC to CA90 surpasses the diesel-only case. The best BTE point in ADDF case shown shorter CD across all the PIRs explored. This is because 20% PIR raises in‑cylinder reactivity and temperature, advancing CA50 and shortening ignition delay. Pilot timing primarily controls mixture preparation. Advancing or retarding it moves SOC away from TDC and shifts the CA50.

Figure 10 shows CFD Simulation results showing OH mass fraction contours at CA10 and TDC of compression. Diesel-only generates the most extensive OH radical zones, while ADDF with split injection produces more OH than ADDF with a main (single) injection. The OH mass fraction trends align with the heat-release behaviour, where stronger OH formation correlates with higher premixed HRR peaks45. Contours at CA10 and at TDC of compression indicate widespread OH formation in the diesel-only case, implying more simultaneous high‑temperature reaction zones throughout the chamber46. Between the ADDF strategies, split injection yields larger OH‑rich regions than the single‑injection ADDF, consistent with enhanced mixture preparation and early high‑temperature chemistry from the pilot‑main setting47.Fig. 10CFD Simulation results showing OH mass fraction contours for the three cases at CA10 and TDC of compression.

Figure 11 shows crank‑angle traces of integrated OH mass. The peak is highest for diesel-only, followed by ADDF‑split and then ADDF‑single, consistent with the relative intensity and extent of high‑temperature reaction zones observed in the contours. Thus, diesel-only creates richer, more localized high‑temperature zones that rapidly produce OH and a pronounced premixed HRR spike. In ADDF, a proper pilot‑main split setting improves mixture reactivity and premixing relative to a main (single) injection, raising OH formation and elevating the HRR peak, while still remaining below the diesel-only peak due to dilution and altered kinetics inherent to dual‑fuel operation47.Fig. 11Simulation results showing crank‑angle traces of integrated OH mass.

Figure 12a and b shows that IMEP remains essentially unchanged across all experiments because speed and load were held constant, whereas COV of IMEP was slightly higher for ADDF than diesel-only yet remains within accepted stability limits (well under the common 3% threshold)48,49. ADDF operation relies on a small diesel pilot to ignite a lean, premixed high‑octane charge; variability in local equivalence ratio of premixed charge can increase cycle-to-cycle variation. Maintaining COV of IMEP below 3% is a common control target in engine calibration, reinforcing that all tested conditions operated in a stable regime49.Fig. 12Experimental IMEP and COV of IMEP at (a) varying PIR, (b) varying pilot injection timings.

Both ADDF-split and ADDF-single injection cases exhibited a sharper, slightly higher in-cylinder temperature peak compared to the diesel-only case, as shown in Fig. 13b. These trends align with the previously observed OH/HRR characteristics and the higher combustion efficiency at the best BTE split-injection point. Previous OH contours and traces revealed a clear hierarchy in high-temperature reaction intensity: diesel-only exhibited the highest levels, followed by ADDF-split, and then ADDF-single. Conclusions

The study concludes that a split-injection strategy in an ADDF engine can sustain stable operation at roughly a 50% ammonia energy share while improving combustion completeness and phasing compared with a single-injection ADDF baseline, although with lower BTE than diesel-only case. The optimal setting observed is a 20% pilot injection ratio with pilot timing of 60 CAD BTDC and main injection at 36 CAD BTDC, which maximizes combustion efficiency within the ADDF combustion cases while keeping stability within accepted limits (COV of IMEP < 3%). This mirrors premixed HRR peaks and supports OH radicals as a robust indicator of heat-release strength.IMEP remains essentially constant across conditions due to fixed speed and load, while COV of IMEP is modestly higher in ADDF yet stays under common stability targets (3%), matching industry references that use 3% as a practical threshold for acceptable combustion stability.

Overall, this study contributes novel and actionable evidence that split diesel injection is a highly effective lever to enhance ammonia utilization in medium-load ADDF engines. These findings reinforce the split-injection strategy as a primary path to enhance ammonia utilization in compression-ignition engines for power generation applications. In addition, blending a fraction of hydrogen with the ammonia–diesel mixture could accelerate ignition chemistry, broaden stable lean operation, and further enhance combustion stability and efficiency, suggesting that the combined use of split-injection optimization and hydrogen enrichment represents a promising avenue for future work.
