High intensity in interspinous ligaments: a diagnostic sign of lumbar instability and back pain for degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis
Authors: Jiang Jiang, Min Chen, Ding-an Huang, Jun-Jie Luo, Jian-bang Han, Ming Hu, Ying-feng Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the clinical significance of high intensity signals in interspinous ligaments at the affected segment in degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS), as well as to determine the most effective diagnostic modalities for evaluating segmental instability.MethodsThis study reviewed a consecutive series of patients with L4/5 DLS between July 2023 and December 2023. The enrolled patients were divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of high-intensity signals in interspinous ligaments: the higher group (Group H), and the non-higher group (Group NH). Translational and angular motion was determined using flexion and extension (FE) radiographs or a sitting lumbar lateral radiograph with a supine sagittal MR image (combined, S-MR). The five-repetition sit-to-stand test (5R-STS) was employed to evaluate patients’ objective functional impairment (OFI).ResultsOverall, 73 patients were enrolled in this study, and there were 22 (30.1%) patients in group H and 51(69.9%) patients in group NH, with an average age of 60.3 ± 8.1 years. The patients in Group H exhibited significantly longer 5R-STS times and serious OFI compared to those in Group NH. Compared to Group NH, Group H exhibited significantly higher SP in the sitting position (21.8% vs 16.7%; P < 0.001*), while no significant differences were observed in the upright, flexion, extension, and supine MRI positions (all P values > 0.05). In Group H, “instability” was recognized in 77.3% of patients using S-MR versus 40.9% patients using FE (P < 0.001); While in Group NH, no significant difference was observed in the incidence of “instability” between FE and s-MR (37.3% vs. 31.4%, P = 0.53). Overall, a significantly higher incidence of instability was found in Group H compared to Group NH (77.3% vs .37.3%, P < 0 .001*).ConclusionsDLS with a high intensity within the interspinous ligaments is a distinct subgroup associated with segmental instability, the combination of 5R-STS and S-MR should be regarded as the most clinically relevant approach for assessing OFI and lumbar instability.

Introduction

Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS) refers to a condition characterized by displacement of a vertebral body relative to the adjacent inferior vertebral body, primarily attributed to degenerative changes, occurring in the absence of any defects in the pars interarticularis [1, 2]. This pathology is commonly observed in middle-aged and elderly females and typically manifests at the L4/L5 segment [3]. Surgical intervention is generally advised for patients who do not exhibit improvement with conservative treatment [4]. However, there has been ongoing debate regarding the management of DLS due to the use of various surgical treatment approaches, including decompression, fusion with or without instrumentation, and interbody fusion [2]. In clinical practice, determining the surgical strategy primarily relies on assessing back and/or leg pain, functional disability, compromised health-related quality of life, and corresponding radiological imaging findings.

DLS is a class of heterogeneous diseases that have various subtypes with different radiographic features in both the sagittal and coronal planes [5, 6]. Sagittally, the basic radiographic alterations involve anterior slippage of vertebrae, kyphotic angulation of the disc space, and a reduction in lumbar lordosis [7]. In the coronal plane, the radiographic changes occurred at the slippage segment, commonly manifesting as scoliotic disc wedging and/or lateral listhesis [5]. Additionally, different types of treatment modalities have been proposed for DLS with different radiographic manifestations. For instance, DLS with kyphotic angulation at the slippage segment represents a distinct subgroup linked to segmental instability [2]. In such cases, posterolateral fusion alone may not suffice, and interbody fusion has been proposed as an additional intervention [4, 8]. Similarly, patients with scoliotic disc wedging and/or lateral listhesis at the affected segment constitute a distinct subgroup characterized by coronal malalignment, loss of overall lumbar lordosis, and severe back pain [9]. The utilization of the asymmetrical TLIF procedure enables the restoration of both the coronal and sagittal lumbar profiles, and the attainment of satisfactory outcomes [4]. Thus, the recognition of radiographic characteristics associated with DLS is highly advantageous in determining individualized treatment modalities.

Segmental instability is a crucial factor in the decision-making process regarding surgical interventions for DLS, primarily due to concerns surrounding the potential iatrogenic destabilization of the affected segment [2]. Consequently, various radiographic features have been documented and identified as indicators of lumbar instability in DLS, including kyphotic angulation at the site of slippage, facet joint effusion, and the presence of high-intensity zones within intervertebral discs [7, 10, 11]. Lumbar motions is intimately associated with the functional form of spinal segmental: active, passive, and neural control. Dysfunction in any one of these subsystems can result in an imbalance in the others, ultimately leading to pain and a diminished quality of life. In our clinical observations, we have identified a distinct group of patients with DLS who exhibit a notable high-intensity zone within the interspinous ligaments. This particular subset of patients consistently reports experiencing low back pain whenever there is a active change in their body positions from sit to stand or stand to sit [12]. In the spinal function unit, the intervertebral discs, facet joints, and ligaments collectively contribute to spinal stability [13]. In cases of degenerative lumbar disorders, a high-intensity zone in the interspinous ligaments may be considered indicative of ligament ruptures and spinal instability [14, 15]. However, assessing lumbar stability should rely on individual radiographic manifestations, yet there is limited comprehensive data concerning segmental stability in patients with DLS presenting with a high-intensity zone in their interspinous ligaments. Therefore, this study was conducted to examine and assess segmental stability and motion characteristics from a radiographic perspective for patients with DLS displaying a high-intensity zone in their interspinous ligaments.

Materials and methods

Patients

After obtaining approval from the institutional ethics committee, we conducted a retrospective review of patient records at our institution for individuals who underwent surgery for DLS from July 2023 to December 2023.The age was range from 49 to 85 years, with the average age of 60.3 ± 8.1 years, all of them received conservative treatment. Surgical decision-making is strictly based on patients symptoms and radiological outcomes. Basically, the surgical indications were as follows: (1) failed conservative treatment and (2) progressive neurologic deficits, such as progressive motor weakness or cauda equine syndrome.The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) involvement limited to a single segment at the L4/L5 level; (2) presence of low-grade lumbar spondylolisthesist (Meyerding’s grade I-II); (3) availability of complete radiological examinations including sitting, upright, flexion/extension radiographs, and supine MRI images; and (4) performance of laminectomy and interspinous ligament excision due to lumbar spinal stenosis. Patients meeting any of the following exclusion criteria were excluded from the study: (1) presence of spondylolisthesis at any other level in the lumbar spine; (2) grade III or higher spondylolisthesis at L4/L5; (3) history of lumbar spine fracture; or (4) prior lumbar spine surgery.

Baseline characteristics evolution

Baseline characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), employment status, and patient-reported outcomes, were extracted from medical records. Patient-reported outcomes were assessed using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and the visual analog scale (VAS) for back pain and leg pain.

Pain characteristics and objective functional impairment (OFI) evaluation

Pain characteristics and the five-repetition sit-to-stand test (5R-STS) were used to assess OFI [3]. The pain characteristics were assessed by evaluating the following activities: pain experienced while bending over to tie shoes, climbing stairs, rising from a chair, walking a certain distance, standing for an extended period of time, and engaging in daily physical activities [9, 15]. Participants were instructed to wear lightweight attire and comfortable flat footwear for the 5R-STS evaluation [3]. They were directed to sit on an armless chair with their back straight against a wall, crossing their arms over their chest and keeping their feet flat throughout the test. To achieve one successful repetition, they had to fully rise and return to a sitting position without using their hands or arms. If they couldn’t complete the initial maneuver or needed assistance, the test was stopped. Upon success, participants were instructed to stand up and sit down as quickly as possible for five repetitions after hearing “go”. The experimenter timed these five repetitions from start until completion, which served as the participant’s score. If completion of the test took longer than 30 s, it was recorded as 30 s. According to the participants’ 5R-STS duration in seconds, those with a completion time exceeding 10.4 s were deemed to exhibit OFI.

Radiographic evaluation

The X-rays and MRI scans were obtained from the radiology department in our hospital as part of the routine clinical workup for lumbar spondylolisthesis. All basic radiographic parameters were each measured twice by two senior spine surgeons, and the mean values were utilized for statistical analysis. The basic radiographic parameters on X-rays and MRI scans included slip percentage and slip angle: (1) slip percentage refers to the ratio of the slip distance to the length of the upper endplate of L5, while (2) slip angle refers to the angle between the lower L4 endplate and the upper endplate of L5.

Definition and measurement of instability

As described in previous studies, two common methods were used to evaluate potential instability: (1) flexion and extension (FE) radiographs and (2) a sitting lumbar lateral radiograph (S) and a supine sagittal MR image (MR) (combined as S-MR). The translational motion was measured by the sagittal translation distance of the slipped vertebra from either FE or S-MR. The measurement of angular motion was also conducted by calculating the difference in intervertebral angles between FE and S-MR [3, 16].

The presence of segmental instability was determined when translational motion between two positions exceeded or equaled 8%, or when intervertebral angular rotation between two positions was greater than or equal to 10°. In terms of lumbar spine angulation measurements, a positive angle indicates lordosis, while a negative angle indicates kyphosis [2].

Histological staining

The human interspinous ligament tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by embedding in paraffin. The samples were subsequently sectioned into 5-μm thick sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Masson’s trichrome, and Alcian blue according to established protocols. In this study, four patients with high-intensity signals and four patients without such signals underwent the removal of the interspinous ligament. These patients received total laminectomy due to the severity of their spinal stenosis.

Study group

The presence of a higher T2-weight signal within the interspinous ligaments is defined as indicative of high intensity sign. The enrolled patients were stratified into two groups based on the presence or absence of high-intensity signals in their interspinous ligaments: Group H, comprising patients with high-intensity signals, and Group NH, comprising those without high-intensity signals.

Statistical analysis

Two primary analyses were conducted. Firstly, a comparison was made between Group H and Group NH regarding their baseline characteristics. Secondly, segmental mobility and the ability to identify instability using S-MR were compared to FE. All data were analyzed using SPSS version 17.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The paired t-test was employed to analyze intra-group differences determined by S-MR and FE. The independent samples t-test or Chi-square test was used to analyze differences between Group H and Group NH for categorical variables. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of the ability to identify instability using S-MR or FE

The criterion for lumbar instability was defined as translational motion ≥ 8%. In Group H, spinal instability was identified in 77.3% (17 out of 22) of patients using the S-MR, while only 40.9% (9 out of 22) were identified using FE. Consequently, FE missed the identification of instability in approximately 36.4% (8 out of 22) of patients detected by S-MR. In Group NH, there was no significant difference in the ability to identify instability between S-MR and FE (37.3% vs. 31.4%, P = 0.53). When an angular motion criterion ≥ 10° was applied, a higher percentage of patients with instability was recognized by S-MR compared to FE in Group H (63.6% vs. 27.3%, respectively, P < 0.001*); however, no significant difference was observed between S-MR and FE in Group NH (35.3% vs. 29.4%, respectively, P = 0.52) (Table 4). Overall, a significantly higher incidence of instability was found in Group H compared to Group NH (77.3% vs. 37.3%, P < 0.001*) (Fig. 3). In Group H, histological examination revealed various degenerative changes in the interspinous ligament, including reduction, fragmentation, and necrosis of the fiber bundles, as well as metaplasia and formation of cavities in fibrocartilage. Discussion

The objectives of surgical interventions for DLS primarily include pain alleviation, functional enhancement, and improvement in health-related quality of life [4]. When determining whether surgery is necessary for degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis, several factors come into play. Firstly, the severity of symptoms and their impact on daily life must be assessed. This includes evaluating the level of pain experienced by the patient, as well as any limitations in mobility or activities. Additionally, imaging studies such as X-rays, CT and MRI scans are crucial in understanding the extent of vertebral slippage and identifying any associated spinal instability or nerve compression. These findings help guide surgical decision-making by providing valuable information about potential risks and benefits [5, 17]. Furthermore, non-surgical treatment options should be explored before considering surgery. Physical therapy, medication management, and lifestyle modifications may provide relief for some patients with mild to moderate symptoms without requiring invasive procedures. Ultimately, surgical intervention may be recommended if conservative treatments fail to alleviate symptoms or if there are signs of progressive neurological deficits. Therefore, it is imperative to accurately evaluate pain levels, functional impairments, and the impact on health-related quality of life [17, 18]. This basic information forms the foundation for making informed decisions regarding surgical interventions. In recent years, patients’ basic information and the evaluation of objective functional impairment have undergone significant and necessary changes, shifting from the subjective assessment of the treating physician to a more patient-centered approach [19]. Specifically, the five-repetition motorized treadmill test (MTT), Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, 6-min walk test (6MWT), pain characteristics assessment, and 5R-STS were employed to evaluate objective functional impairment in spinal pathology [18, 20, 21]. In our previous studies, we found that the presence of a positive 5R-STS sign in DLS patients indicates lumbar instability and OFI [3].

The findings of the current study indicate that patients exhibiting high intensity in the interspinous ligaments at the involved segment constitute a distinct subgroup in terms of preoperative patient-reported outcomes and the evaluation of OFI. Compared to Group NH, Group H exhibited more severe back and leg pain as well as a higher degree of OFI. The patients in Group H demonstrated significantly prolonged durations during the 5R-STS compared to those in Group NH. Furthermore, individuals in Group H reported experiencing more severe pain during physical activities such as bending over to tie shoes, climbing stairs, and rising from a chair. The symptoms and OFI in these patients were notably exacerbated during motion, particularly in the movement of the lower lumbar spine. DLS is a heterogeneous disease with a wide range of pain characteristics and severity, as well as predisposing factors [2, 9]. According to an extensive systematic review of the literature and our previous findings, the presence of segmental instability in DLS may play a crucial role in initiating pain [4].

As the positions in radiographic examinations are either too complicated to standardize or hard to perform for most patients with DLS, lateral radiographs at extension, and flexion positions are currently seen as the gold standard for detecting instability in DLS. However, the conventional flexion and extension radiographs failed to effectively identify lumbar instability in patients exhibiting high intensity within the interspinous ligaments. A comprehensive interpretation of the radiographic findings in conjunction with the patient’s clinical manifestations is crucial to optimizing treatment outcomes. In a study conducted by Wang et al. [9], the researchers identified a subgroup of patients with DLS who exhibited a lateral translation and/or lateral angulation in the coronal plane. The presence of lumbar lateral instability further exacerbated patient-reported outcomes and OFI with positional changes, such as exiting a vehicle, rising from a seated position, and ascending stairs [19]. In such cases, posterolateral fusion alone may not be sufficient to address the condition adequately [1]; therefore, interbody fusion has been proposed as an additional intervention. Similarly, Chen et al. demonstrated that DLS with a kyphotic configuration at the involved level represents a distinct subgroup associated with segmental instability and worse OFI [2]. Furthermore, surgical outcomes were found to improve significantly in such cases following surgery. The findings of these studies indicate that specific radiographic manifestations are useful in guiding the decision-making process for surgical strategies.

The interspinous ligaments play a pivotal role in maintaining spinal stability, transmitting loads, and absorbing shock [15]. The presence of high intensity in the interspinous ligaments indicates reputational and dynamic instability in patients with acute spinal trauma [22]. However, there is limited information available regarding the radiographic evaluation of interspinous ligaments and segmental instability in DLS. Degenerative changes occur in various anatomical components of spinal function units, including the intervertebral discs, facet joints, and spinal ligaments [1]. Biomechanical and histopathological studies have demonstrated that degeneration leads to alterations in the physiological properties of interspinous ligaments [15, 22]. In our study, histological examination of the interspinous ligaments revealed a range of degenerative changes in Group H, including reduction, fragmentation, and necrotization of the fiber bundle. Additionally, metaplasia and cavity formation were observed in fibrocartilage. These fibrous bands function as crucial shock absorbers that cushion the spine during physical activity and prevent excessive bending or straightening. Their inherent elasticity and stiffness enable the spine to gracefully flex while maintaining stability, thereby facilitating a seamless execution of a broad spectrum of motions. The higher signal intensity observed in DLS suggested a diminished elasticity and strength within the interspinous ligaments, pointing towards pathological alterations. The findings from these observations provide evidence supporting the insufficiency of high intensity interspinous ligaments in effectively maintaining stability during movement in DLS. However, the optimal radiographic imaging modality for assessing segmental stability in these patients remains uncertain.

The evaluation methods for lumbar instability should be personalized for DLS patients with diverse imaging manifestations [16]. For example, in patients presenting with kyphotic angulation at the affected segment, the combination of an upright lateral radiograph with a supine sagittal MR image has been demonstrated to be superior to traditional flexion–extension imaging in accurately measuring translational motion and identifying instability [2, 4]. For patients with local coronal imbalance and lateral instability, the utilization of upright left and right bending radiographs is recommended [9]. In our previous investigation, we utilized the 5R-STS to assess OFI in patients diagnosed with DLS [3]. Our findings revealed that DLS patients exhibiting a positive outcome on the 5R-STS represent a distinct subgroup closely associated with lumbar instability. The combined utilization of S-MR as an imaging modality demonstrates significant advantages in accurately identifying segmental instability. The findings from our previous studies suggest that the assessment of OFI and the utilization of S-MR for evaluating lumbar instability in these patients can offer valuable assistance.

The maximum forward olisthesis in Group H was observed in the sitting position, while the minimum olisthesis was observed in the supine position. Our findings align with previous research, indicating that conventional FE radiographs lack efficacy in diagnosing lumbar instability in DLS patients who exhibit objective functional impairments [3]. Severe low back pain and OFI may hinder patient compliance during examinations, leading to reduced mobility on FE radiographs [2, 23]. Sitting is a standard weight-bearing posture, and reduced tension in the paraspinous muscles during sitting may alleviate abnormal stress and relieve low back pain [16]. Additionally, the increased diameter of the spinal canal and neural foramen in this position may help alleviate clinical symptoms associated with neurogenic claudication in DLS patients [24]. Therefore, compared to the traditional standing flexion position, adopting a normal sitting posture has the potential to reveal the maximum forward olisthesis. Our results demonstrate a significantly higher slip percentage (SP) on the sitting radiograph compared to both the upright standing radiograph (21.8 ± 4.3 vs. 18.3 ± 3.1; p < 0.001*) and the standing flexion radiograph (21.8 ± 4.3 vs. 19.5 ± 4.5; p < 0.001*). Radiographs obtained in the seated position provide a more effective assessment of lumbar segmental motion in DLS patients with OFI and high intensity within the interspinous ligaments.

The presence of kyphotic angulation at the involved segment in the sitting position, rather than angular motion, in patients with high intensity suggests greater efficacy in indicating segmental instability [3, 4]. The use of abnormal angular motion as a diagnostic tool for identifying pathological segmental mobility is relatively ineffective in DLS. Reduced angular motion may be attributed to degenerative changes in the intervertebral space and facet joints, which can hinder adequate sagittal angulation [25]. In our study, the findings revealed significantly lower angular motion in Group H compared to Group NH (5.4 ± 2.7 vs. 7.3 ± 4.1, P < 0.05*), while demonstrating greater kyphotic angulation (-5.3 ± 3.6 vs. -1.4 ± 4.5, P < 0.001*). From a biomechanical perspective, kyphotic angulation at the involved segment suggests ‘vertical instability,’ which cannot be measured using FE radiographs.

A positive performance on the 5R-STS serves as an indicator of OFI in DLS, particularly with high intensity in the interspinous ligaments. Our findings provide direct radiographic evidence to clarify their relationship. The instability criterion for Group H was defined as translational motion ≥ 8% or angular motion ≥ 10°. Among the participants in this group, 77.3% (17 out of 22) were identified as having lumbar instability based on S-MR imaging. Our study uncovers potential factors contributing to the increased occurrence of severe low back pain and OFI triggered by activities such as performing the 5R-STS or engaging in daily activities like bending down to tie shoes, ascending stairs, and rising from a seated position. Moreover, our findings have significant clinical implications for offering comprehensive counseling in preoperative assessments for patients with DLS. The surgical benefit is generally greater for patients who present with a higher degree of OFI and segmental instability at the affected spinal level [4, 17, 19]. Therefore, in our clinical practice, it is common for us to prescribe a combination of 5R-STS and S-MR for patients who exhibit high intensity in the interspinous ligaments.

Future research could address several limitations in this study. Firstly, the sample size of patients with DLS was relatively small and limited to a single center, which may reduce the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, accurate interpretation of outcomes from the 5R-STS relies on clear instructions provided by the examiner and patient compliance, which could introduce variability in test results. Third, our study primary focus is on degenerative conditions rather than traumatic induced high-intensity signals within the interspinous ligaments.

In conclusion, high-intensity signals within the interspinous ligaments observed on MR imaging in DLS should be considered a key indicator of instability, significantly affecting patient-reported outcomes and OFI. Based on our findings, the combined use of 5R-STS and S-MR presents the most clinically relevant approach for assessing these factors, as it effectively identifies both OFI and lumbar instability.
