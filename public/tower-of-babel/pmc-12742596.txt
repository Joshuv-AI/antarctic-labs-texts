Scabies as a Neglected Tropical Disease: A Comprehensive Review of Pathogenesis, Epidemiology, Clinical Manifestations, Diagnosis and Treatment
Authors: Ansar Ahmad Paray, Mahesh Chandra, Imtiyaz Wani, Manju Singh, Amandeep kaur, Irshad Ahmad Najar, Nadeem Ahmad Paray, Mudasir Manzoor Dar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Scabies is a highly contagious skin infestation caused by the ectoparasitic mite Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, affecting over 200 million individuals worldwide at any given time. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recognized this infection as a neglected tropical disease (NTD) since 2017. Scabies mainly affects underdeveloped, overcrowded, and resource-limited communities, particularly in tropical and subtropical areas. Intense pruritus that gets worse at night and a distinctive rash with papules, burrows, and secondary bacterial infections from scratching are the clinical manifestations produced. Despite its pervasiveness, many national health agendas fail to adequately address it, misdiagnose it, and underreport it. Its persistence is further exacerbated by social stigma, a lack of awareness, and ineffective diagnosis and treatment. This review article highlights the disease’s epidemiological significance, clinical manifestations, diagnostic capabilities, calls for enhanced surveillance, integrated control initiatives, and more funding for research.

Introduction

Scabies is the most common neglected tropical disease (NTD) with cutaneous manifestations, disproportionately affecting low-income populations living in overcrowded areas [1]. Scabies is a highly contagious skin infestation caused by the parasitic mite Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis. It is characterized by intense itching, particularly at night, and a distinctive rash consisting of small, red, raised bumps and burrows in the skin. Scabies is transmitted by direct skin-to-skin contact or indirectly by contact with contaminated material (fomites) [2]. As per the WHO, at least 200 million people worldwide suffer from scabies at any one time. The average prevalence is estimated to be 5% to 10% in children from low-income countries [3]. Scabies has been recognized as a human affliction for thousands of years, with descriptions dating back to ancient civilizations. The term “scabies” is derived from the Latin word “scabere,” meaning “to scratch.” The etiological agent, S. scabiei, was first identified in 1687 by Italian physician Giovanni Cosimo Bonomo, marking a significant milestone in understanding the disease’s etiology [4]. Scabies is a global health issue, with at least 455 million new cases worldwide every year. In 2017, the WHO added scabies to a list of NTDs and gave a call for effective management and control of the disease [5,6].

The management of scabies has greatly improved in recent years due to a better understanding of how the disease is spread and the creation of efficient treatment options. Even with these advancements, scabies remains a serious public health concern, particularly in areas where it is endemic and in environments with limited resources. The purpose of this review is to investigate the prevalence of scabies on a national and international scale, analyze current management approaches, evaluate their efficacy, and investigate infection prevention techniques.

History

Scabies has been recognized in humans since ancient times, with archaeological evidence from Egypt and the Middle East suggesting its presence as early as 494 BCE [7]. In the 4th century BCE, Aristotle described parasites that “escape from little pimples if they are pricked,” a likely reference to scabies mites [8]. The Arab physician Ibn Zuhr is credited with providing one of the earliest clinical descriptions of the mite responsible for scabies. Roman medical writer Aulus Cornelius Celsus (25 BCE–50 CE) was the first to name the condition “scabies” and describe its characteristic symptoms. A pivotal advancement came in 1687 when Italian physician Giovanni Cosimo Bonomo identified S. scabiei as the causative organism, making scabies one of the first human diseases to have a known parasitic origin [8]. Scabies has also had historical relevance beyond medicine; during World War II, for example, Anne Frank was reportedly denied transfer from Auschwitz due to a scabies infection, highlighting its broader impact on human lives [9].

Etiological Organism

Scabies is caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei (S. scabiei). This mite was previously classified as Acarus scabiei within the genus Acarus. It was later reclassified into the genus Sarcoptes, which is part of the superfamily Sarcoptoidea and family Sarcoptidae (Table 1). S. scabiei has various variants that infect humans and other mammals, including S. scabiei var. hominis (humans), S. scabiei var. canis (dogs), S. scabiei var. cuniculi (rabbits), and S. scabiei var. vulpes (red fox). Most experts consider Sarcoptes to be a single species with different varieties adapted to specific hosts [10]. The males of this species measure 210–290 μm, while the females measure 300–500 μm in length. The eggs, which are oval-shaped, measure 150–200 μm [11].

Brief Morphology of S. scabiei var. hominis

S. scabiei var. hominis is a microscopic, obligate human ectoparasite and belongs to the family Sarcoptidae under the order Acarina (mites). The adult parasite is round to oval in shape and dorsoventrally flattened, allowing it to move more easily and burrow beneath the surface the outermost layers of human skin. Females are larger than males in size (approximately 0.30–0.45 mm in length and 0.25–0.35 mm in width) [14]. The body of the mite is unsegmented and has two main regions: the gnathosoma (mouthparts) and the idiosoma (main body), which bears the legs and other appendages [15].

S. scabiei var. hominis has a transversely striated cuticle that is covered in scales and short, triangular spines. The dorsal surface exhibits bristle-like structures called setae, which vary in length and help identify the species [16]. The mite has four pairs of short legs, two of which are oriented forward and two of which are oriented backward. Terminal suckers are present on the first two pairs of legs in females and all four pairs in males, which facilitate host attachment and movement, while some pairs in males also assist in mating [17].

Chelicerae and pedipalps, which are adapted to burrow and feed on host epidermal cells and tissue fluids, are members of the gnathosoma. In order to lay oval-shaped eggs that are between 0.10 and 0.15 mm long, adult females dig serpiginous tunnels into the stratum corneum of the skin [16]. The mite’s pathogenesis and survival in human hosts depend heavily on these morphological adaptations.

Life Cycle of S. scabiei [15,18,19]

S. scabiei var. hominis has a direct life cycle entirely on the human host with five developmental stages: egg, larva, protonymph, tritonymph, and adult. The cycle is completed within 10–17 days under optimal conditions. The various stages in the life cycle are as follows:

1. Egg Stage: Female mites burrow into the stratum corneum (outer layer of the skin) and lay 2–3 eggs per day, up to a total of 10–25 eggs during their lifespan (4–6 weeks on the host). Eggs are oval (~0.1–0.15 mm) and hatch in 3–4 days.

2. Larval Stage: The hatched larvae have six legs and migrate to the skin surface through the burrow. They create short molting pouches (surface burrows), feed on keratin, and molt in 3–4 days.

3. Nymphal Stages: After the first molt, larvae develop into protonymphs, then into tritonymphs (eight-legged). Each molt takes approximately 2–3 days.

4. Adult Stage: Following the final molt, mites become sexually mature adults. Males often roam the skin to find females in burrows to mate. After mating, males die, and fertilized females re-initiate the cycle by burrowing and laying eggs.

5. Survival outside the host: Outside the host, mites can survive 24–36 hours under normal conditions, but up to 3–4 days in cool and humid environments (optimal survival at 10–15°C and 75% humidity).

Mode of Transmission of Scabies

Scabies, caused by the mite S. scabiei var. hominis, is primarily transmitted through prolonged, direct skin-to-skin contact, often requiring 15–20 minutes. This mode of transmission is common among household members, sexual partners, and individuals in crowded living conditions such as care homes, prisons, and refugee camps. Brief or casual contact, like handshakes, typically does not result in transmission. Transmission through contaminated fomites such as bedding is rare, although it is more likely in cases of crusted scabies, where the mite burden is extremely high and even short-term contact or exposure to shed skin scales can result in infestation. Sexual contact is a notable transmission route, particularly in younger and middle-aged adults, and scabies frequently coexists with HIV, with reported prevalence up to 18.6% [20]. Experimental studies suggest that transmission from contaminated clothing or bedding is uncommon, and hygiene practices like frequent bathing do not significantly reduce risk. Scabies transmission is especially high in communal and institutional settings, including prisons, refugee centers, nursing homes, and childcare facilities. Zoonotic transmission from animals is rare; transmission of other variants of S. scabiei from animals to humans is rare, with the most common transmission occurring from dogs [11,20].

Risk Factors

Regarding scabies risk factors, numerous studies have demonstrated statistically significant correlations between scabies and family sizes. According to studies, people who have more than five family members are more likely to contract scabies. It had also been reported that households with six to 10 people had a 1.4-fold higher risk of contracting scabies than those with fewer than five family members. This could be because larger families are more crowded than smaller ones, which leads to more people sharing beds, clothes, and other items. Sleeping with a person infected with scabies is another risk factor. Additionally, flooding has been identified as a significant risk factor for scabies infestations [21].

In hospital settings, low economic status, poor hygiene practices, patients in long-term care facilities, and frequent patient contact without adequate personal protective equipment have been reported as risk factors. Individuals exposed to others with itching symptoms, those with more severe illness, and patients with multiple catheters were found to be at higher risk of scabies infection [22].

Epidemiology and Global Burden

As per the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, scabies affects approximately 206.6 million persons worldwide at any given time, with an annual incidence reaching 622.5 million new cases [23]. The epidemiological data for various world regions showed prevalence estimates ranging from 0.2% to 71%, with the highest prevalence in the Pacific region and Latin America. Among countries with the highest rates, the top 10 were Indonesia, China, Timor-Leste, Vanuatu, Fiji, Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, Vietnam, and the Seychelles [24,25].

A meta-analysis done, including seventy studies, reported a pooled prevalence of 11.9%. They also reported contact history with household members with itch, lack of soap, bed sharing, sharing of clothes, infrequent bathing, presence of pets, and being male as risk factors with Odds Ratio of 11.3, 3.41, 2.64, 2.52, 2.13, 1.76, and 1.19, respectively [26]. In India, the incidence ranges from 13% to 59% in rural and urban areas [26]. According to the 2015 Global Burden of Disease Study, scabies was responsible for 0.21% of disability adjusted life years [24].

A systematic literature review conducted in 2022, which examined 43 population-based studies from October 2014 to March 2022, highlighted strikingly high prevalence rates in specific populations, such as 71.0% in the general population of Ghana and 76.9% among children in an Indonesian boarding school [27]. Additionally, a separate systematic review analyzed data from approximately 200 000 individuals across 72 studies in different parts of India, reporting a prevalence range from 2% to 32%, with a weighted average prevalence of 12%. These findings reflect significant regional variations and underscore the ongoing public health burden posed by scabies, particularly in low- and middle-income settings [28].

Pathogenesis of Scabies

The pathogenesis, which results in severe pruritus and skin lesions, includes immune responses, mechanical damage, and secondary bacterial infections.

Mite Infestation and Mechanical Damage

The fertilized female mite burrows into the stratum corneum, creating serpiginous tunnels where she lays eggs. This burrowing causes mechanical disruption of the epidermis and initiates localized inflammation. The mite secretes salivary proteins and proteolytic enzymes that degrade host tissues, facilitating penetration and feeding on skin keratin and serum components. Early lesions may include erythematous papules, vesicles, and burrow marks, especially in web spaces, axillae, groin, and buttocks. Detection of the host’s IgG antibody in freshly separated mites’ midgut is clear evidence that mites consume the serum of the host [10].

Immunopathogenesis of Scabies

The host immune response plays a central role in the pathogenesis of scabies and involves both innate and adaptive immunity components. When mites burrow into the skin, mite releases enzymes and antigens that trigger inflammation and itching. The pathogenesis involves both direct tissue damage and complex immune modulation that allows the mites to evade host defenses. The innate immune system responds first through keratinocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells releasing cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Adaptive immunity then follows, with marked differences between the two clinical forms: ordinary scabies (OS) and crusted scabies (CS). OS is dominated by a protective Th1/CD8⁺ T-cell response that restricts mite numbers, while CS shows an exaggerated Th2/Th17 response with high IgE and IgG production and poor mite control. Overexpression of TGF-β in CS promotes Treg and Th17 activity, contributing to immunosuppression and heavy infestation. Delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction is primarily responsible for the hallmark symptom of intense pruritus, which typically develops 2 to 6 weeks after the initial infestation. In crusted (Norwegian) scabies, a profound immune dysregulation, especially impaired cell-mediated immunity, leads to hyperinfestation with millions of mites. This severe form is commonly observed in immunocompromised individuals, the elderly, or those with neurological conditions [29].

Secondary Bacterial Infection

Scratching and parasite-mediated suppression of the local immune system can predispose individuals to secondary bacterial infections, most frequently involving Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A streptococci) and Staphylococcus aureus, which may result in conditions such as impetigo, cellulitis, or post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. These bacterial agents causing secondary bacterial infection have the potential to develop antibiotic resistance and lead to severe, unnoticed morbidity [30].

Clinical Manifestations of Scabies

Scabies presents with a range of clinical manifestations influenced by factors such as the patient’s age, immune status, comorbidities, mite burden, mode of transmission, and the duration before diagnosis is made.

The condition’s hallmark skin symptoms include burrows and red bumps in typical areas, as well as severe itching that gets worse at night. Scabies may present differently in older adults, immunocompromised individuals, or infants, displaying atypical skin features and less itching. Clinical presentation varies based on the host’s immune status, age, and duration of infestation [31].

Classic Scabies

This is the most common form and has the following features [32].

Intense Pruritus: This is the hallmark symptom and is typically worse at night due to intense immune response and increased mite activity in warmer environments. The itch is a hypersensitivity reaction to mite antigens (eggs, feces, and saliva).

Burrows: These are the thin, serpiginous, grayish-white or skin-colored lines on the skin, measuring 5–15 mm in length, representing the tunnels created by female mites. Common sites include the finger web spaces, wrists, elbows, axillae, waistline, buttocks, and genital area.

Papular and Vesicular Lesions: Erythematous papules, vesicles, or nodules may be present. Lesions are often excoriated due to scratching and may become secondarily infected. Lesions tend to be bilaterally symmetrical and appear in areas with thin stratum corneum, such as the interdigital web spaces, flexor aspects of the wrists, elbows, axillae, periumbilical area, genitalia (penis, scrotum), buttocks and thighs and areola and inframammary areas in females.

Crusted (Norwegian) Scabies

This is a rare form of scabies that presents with more severe symptoms than those of classic scabies. Large crusted lesions, thick hyperkeratosis, and a very high concentration of mites, often numbering in the thousands to millions are its defining features. CS is most commonly reported in immunocompromised, malnourished, and disabled persons, as well as in patients receiving systemic or strong topical glucocorticoids, organ transplant recipients, people infected with HIV or HTLV-1, and patients with hematologic malignancies. CS has a high mortality rate and can lead to a number of complications, including cellulitis, sepsis, and secondary impetigo, if diagnosis is delayed. It is highly contagious and has the potential to cause an outbreak due to its hyper-infestation, besides immunocompromised patients CS can also be seen in immunocompetent patients [33].

Nodular Scabies

This is a less common manifestation of scabies, characterized by firm, erythematous, pruritic nodules, often involving the genitalia, especially on the scrotum, penis, axillae, and groin. Nodules may persist for weeks or months, representing a hypersensitivity reaction even after successful treatment [34].

Bullous Scabies

This is an uncommon and often underrecognized variant that presents with tense or flaccid blisters mimicking conditions like bullous pemphigoid. This usually affects older adults or immunocompromised individuals; the bullae frequently involve the trunk and limbs and may be accompanied by pruritic papules or burrows. Diagnosis is frequently delayed due to overlap with other blistering disorders, but dermoscopic evaluation and histopathological evidence, such as subepidermal splits with eosinophil-rich infiltrates and, occasionally, linear IgG deposition on direct immunofluorescence, are instrumental in distinguishing bullous scabies from autoimmune blistering diseases [35,36].

Nail Scabies

This is usually seen in cases of CS, which manifests as subungual hyperkeratosis, onychodystrophy, discoloration, and onycholysis. While most reports involve immunocompromised or institutionalized patients, occasional occurrences have been noted in healthy children and adults. The proposed mechanisms include mite infestation under the nails due to scratching or chronic pruritus, leading to auto-inoculation. Diagnosis relies on direct microscopy or dermoscopy of nail clippings or scrapings [37].

Age-related Presentations

Scabies in Infants and Children

In infants and young children, scabies often presents with more severe and widespread symptoms compared to adults. Common clinical features include extensive rashes, irritability, poor feeding, and failure to thrive. Lesions in this age group may be more persistent and appear as papules, vesicles, pustules, or nodules. Unlike in adults, the face, neck, scalp, palms, and soles are frequently involved. In older children, the condition typically manifests as erythematous papules, burrows, and inflammatory nodules, often with secondary excoriation due to intense itching. However, in infants under one year, pruritus may be minimal or unnoticed. Less commonly, nodular or bullous forms of scabies may occur, and children under two years may develop vesicular, papulovesicular, or papulopustular eruptions [38].

Scabies in Aged People

In elderly individuals, scabies may present with atypical clinical features. Although the inflammatory response tends to be diminished in this age group, pruritus often remains a prominent symptom. Involvement of the scalp and face is more common in the elderly, similar to patterns observed in infants, children, and immunocompromised individuals. Older adults are also at increased risk of developing crusted scabies, a more severe and highly contagious form of the disease. This susceptibility is often attributed to age-related factors such as altered immune responses, poor nutritional status, cognitive decline, and reduced ability to maintain personal hygiene [39].

Diagnosis

The International Alliance for the Control of Scabies (IACS) has recognized the harmonization of diagnostic procedures as a critical component in the formulation of a comprehensive global strategy for scabies control. Given the wide variability in clinical presentation, overlapping symptoms with other dermatologic conditions, and the limited availability of skilled personnel or laboratory tools in many endemic regions, consistent and standardized diagnostic criteria are essential for accurate case identification and surveillance. In response to these challenges, the IACS developed a consensus-based diagnostic framework aimed at improving the reliability and comparability of scabies diagnoses across diverse clinical and epidemiological settings (Table 2). This framework categorizes cases into three diagnostic levels: Confirmed scabies, clinical scabies, and suspected scabies based on the presence of mites (via microscopy or dermoscopy), typical lesion distribution, clinical signs, and patient history.

Recent Advances in Laboratory Diagnosis of Scabies

Before starting a specific treatment, confirming the diagnosis by accurate and rapid identification is critical. The diagnosis of scabies may be assisted by invasive and noninvasive tools; however, the history and examination findings are usually adequate to confirm the clinical suspicion. Over the past few decades, various laboratory-based methods have been introduced for scabies diagnosis, including direct microscopy and histopathological examination. In recent years, advancements such as serological assays, dermoscopy, and molecular diagnostic techniques have also been developed. The diagnosis of scabies is challenging and potentially time-consuming, and the disease can exhibit different clinical behaviors as well as responses to treatment [43].

Direct Microscopy

Direct microscopy of skin scrapings is the traditional criterion standard, enabling visualization of mites, eggs, or scybala using 10% KOH or saline preparations. Scabies mites can be seen easily at low power along with other structures, including ova and scybala (feces), and more detailed images can be obtained on high power [44].

Histopathology

Skin scrapings or biopsy specimens are promptly fixed in 10% formal saline or formalin, followed by paraffin embedding. Thin sections, typically 3 to 5 µm thick, are prepared using a rotary microtome and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for microscopic examination. Although histopathology is among the most accurate diagnostic methods for scabies, it is labor-intensive and time-consuming. This reveals the mite parts, the immature forms, and/or the scybala within tissue sections [42].

Videodermatoscopy (VD)

With a magnification of up to ×1000, VD is a high-resolution, non-invasive imaging method that allows for quick and thorough in vivo skin observation. It is especially helpful for identifying ectoparasites like S. scabiei because it improves the visibility of superficial and subcorneal structures. Compared to traditional dermoscopy, VD provides a clearer identification of the mite, its burrows, eggs, and fecal pellets (scybala). Additionally, it makes it easier to store digital images for documentation and follow-up. VD is a promising tool in clinical and epidemiological settings because of its rapid and non-invasive ability to examine large skin areas. It can help with early diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and outbreak control, especially in institutional settings [45].

Dermoscopy

A non-invasive, quick, and affordable diagnostic method that is being used more and more to diagnose scabies. The most widely used handheld dermoscope has a magnification of 10x to 20x. It offers a major benefit over conventional microscopy in that it enables the visualization of the mite and its related structures in real time. The “delta-wing jet” sign, sometimes referred to as the “jet with contrail” or the “triangle sign,” is the most distinctive dermoscopic finding in scabies. This is the mite’s head, which is a triangular, dark brown structure at the end of a whitish, curvilinear line (the burrow). The mite’s mouthparts and front legs are represented by the triangular structure, and the tunnel it makes inside the stratum corneum is represented by the trailing line. Dermoscopy offers a sensitivity ranging between 91% and 94%, and a specificity of 86%–89%, according to recent studies [46].

In vivo Reflectance Confocal Microscopy (RCM)

An emerging non-invasive imaging technique that produces horizontal greyscale images of the skin at various depths, allowing the visualization of epidermis and superficial dermis at cellular resolution, facilitating the diagnosis of several cutaneous disorders, including skin cancers and inflammatory skin diseases. RCM has been employed in the diagnosis of scabies, proving its ability to demonstrate the mite within the epidermis, where the adult female dig burrows and spawn [47].

Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT)

A non-invasive imaging modality that utilizes low-coherence light to generate high-resolution, cross-sectional images of the skin in real time. Although its use in scabies diagnosis is still emerging, OCT allows for visualization of intraepidermal burrows and occasionally the mite itself, without the need for skin excision [48].

Burrow Ink Test (BIT)

A simple bedside technique in which a dark ink (usually from a fountain pen) is rubbed over suspected lesions and then wiped off with alcohol. Ink retained in the burrows highlights their linear or serpiginous paths, aiding visual identification of classic scabies features. BIT is useful in resource-limited settings but lacks sensitivity in mild or atypical cases [49].

Adhesive Tape Test

A simple, non-invasive diagnostic method used to detect S. scabiei mites, their eggs, or fecal pellets directly from the skin surface. This is done by applying a piece of transparent adhesive tape onto a suspicious skin lesion, typically a papule or burrow, then removing it and transferring the tape onto a microscope slide for examination. Studies have demonstrated that while the test’s sensitivity is moderate, approximately 68% to 70%, its specificity and positive predictive value are very high, meaning a positive result effectively confirms scabies infestation. Although less sensitive than dermoscopy, its high specificity and simplicity render it a valuable screening tool, particularly in environments lacking advanced diagnostic equipment [50].

Serological Tests in Scabies

Serological assays aim to detect host antibodies (IgG or IgE) against S. scabiei antigens.

• IgE-based DELFIA using rSar s 14.3 antigen: A dissociation-enhanced lanthanide fluorescent immunoassay (DELFIA) measuring specific IgE to recombinant S. scabiei allergen rSar s 14.3 had shown exceptional performance in humans, with 100% sensitivity and 93.8% specificity, demonstrating potential for highly accurate serological diagnosis [51].

• IgG ELISA using recombinant chitinase-like proteins (rSsCLP5, rSsCLP12): In animal models, such as in rabbits, indirect ELISA targeting IgG against rSsCLP5 and rSsCLP12 antigens had achieved approximately 93–100% sensitivity and 90–98% specificity, reflecting strong serodiagnostic potential; though not yet validated in humans, these findings support further development of antigen-specific assays [52].

• Recombinant cofilin IgG ELISA: A study had reported that an assay using recombinant cofilin protein showed high sensitivity and specificity in rabbits, with no cross-reactivity, demonstrating the feasibility of IgG-targeted serological testing [53].

• Alpha-enolase IgG ELISA: Another study in rabbits using alphaenolase antigen achieved similar diagnostic accuracy with strong sensitivity and specificity, reinforcing the value of targeting specific mite proteins and the importance of ELISA optimization [54].

• IgE/IgG ELISAs targeting allergens (tropomyosin, paramyosin): Controlled studies reported IgE-based assays with up to 98% sensitivity and 90–100% specificity (eg, Sspara2, Sspara3), though IgG assays showed more variable performance (40–96%) [55].

Modern Molecular Techniques for Scabies Diagnosis

Advanced molecular diagnostics have significantly improved scabies detection, especially in cases where traditional methods fall short. Conventional and nested PCR, targeting mitochondrial genes, like cox1 or ribosomal 16S, offer high specificity and enhanced sensitivity. In one study based on 63 patients, nested PCR achieved 100% sensitivity, detecting cases missed by microscopy, highlighting its utility as an early screening [56]. Another study using dry swab sampling revealed 37.9% sensitivity comparable to expert skin scrapings and strong specificity (100%), making it suitable for outbreak surveillance [57]. Molecular assays targeting high-copy repetitive genomic sequences (via probe-based or real-time PCR) have further improved detection thresholds, with high specificity and adaptability to both human and animal samples. Isothermal amplification technologies, such as loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), offer rapid, equipment-free alternatives ideal for low-resource settings. A LAMP assay targeting the ITS-2 gene demonstrated 100% sensitivity and 92.3% specificity in animal samples, suggesting strong potential for field application [58].

Treatment of Scabies

Permethrin 5% cream, oral ivermectin, and benzyl benzoate 25% lotion are the recommended treatments for scabies. Alternative treatments include malathion 0.5% aqueous lotion, ivermectin 1% lotion, and sulfur 6-33% cream, ointment, or lotion (Table 3). Topical scabicide and oral ivermectin are required for CS. Mass treatment of large populations with endemic disease can be performed with a single dose of ivermectin (200 micrograms/kg of bodyweight). Partner management needs a look-back period of 2 months. Screening for other STIs is recommended. Patients and close contacts should avoid sexual contact until completion of treatment and should strictly observe personal hygiene rules when living in crowded spaces. Written information should be provided to suspected cases [59].

Preventive Measures

Scabies prevention requires a multi-tiered approach involving individual behavior, household sanitation, institutional protocols, and population-level interventions.

One of the most effective preventive strategies is the simultaneous treatment of the infected individual and all close contacts, including asymptomatic household members. This is critical because the S. scabiei mite spreads primarily through prolonged skin-to-skin contact, and reinfestation is common if contacts remain untreated [14,63].

• Environmental decontamination is another important measure. Mites can survive for 24–36 hours off the human host; therefore, washing all clothing, bed linens, and towels used by the infected person in hot water (at least 60°C) and drying them at high heat is recommended. Items that cannot be washed should be sealed in plastic bags for at least 72 hours. This practice significantly reduces the chance of indirect transmission through fomites [16,64].

• In institutional settings such as nursing homes, prisons, schools, and shelters—where overcrowding and frequent close contact are common—routine skin examinations, early case detection, and contact tracing are vital. Suspected or confirmed cases should be isolated, and healthcare workers or caregivers must use protective clothing like gloves and gowns. Regular surveillance and prompt reporting to public health authorities are also crucial during outbreaks to implement mass screening and treatment strategies [65].

• On a community or population level, particularly in endemic regions or during outbreaks, mass drug administration (MDA) has proven highly effective. This typically involves administering oral ivermectin (200 μg/kg) to the entire population, with topical permethrin used for those in whom ivermectin is contraindicated (eg, children under 5, pregnant women). MDA programs in places like the Solomon Islands and Ethiopia have reduced scabies prevalence by more than 80% and significantly lowered the rate of secondary bacterial infections such as impetigo [66,67].

• Improving living conditions also plays a preventive role. Mites thrive in crowded, poorly ventilated, and humid environments. Ensuring good ventilation, exposure to sunlight, and keeping sleeping areas dry can help reduce mite survival in the environment. Regular changing and washing of bedding and clothes should be emphasized, especially in rural or resource-limited settings [68].

• Finally, public education and behavioral change communication are foundational. People must be informed about how scabies is transmitted, the importance of hygiene, avoiding close contact with infected individuals, and not sharing clothing or bedding. However, while knowledge is important, it does not always translate into practice; therefore, culturally sensitive educational campaigns and community involvement are essential to ensure sustained behavior change [69].

Conclusion

Scabies continues to be a serious but frequently disregarded public health concern, especially in areas with limited resources and high population density. Scabies is still misdiagnosed, underreported, and not adequately treated in many health systems, even though it affects more than 200 million people worldwide and is listed by the WHO as an NTD. The clinical impact, which is characterized by severe itching, distinctive skin lesions, and possible secondary infections, exacerbates the morbidity at the individual and community levels. Effective control is further hampered by social stigma, low awareness, and deficiencies in the infrastructure for diagnosis and treatment. In order to lessen the burden of this avoidable and curable illness, this review emphasizes the critical need for enhanced surveillance, better diagnostic techniques, community education, and integrated public health interventions, as well as ongoing research and funding.
