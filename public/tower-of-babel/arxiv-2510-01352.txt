The noncommutative KP hierarchy and its solution via descent algebra Journal: …
Gordon Blower, Simon J.A. Malham

Abstract
We give the solution to the complete noncommutative Kadomtsev–Petviashvili (KP) hierarchy. We achieve this via direct linearisation which involves the Gelfand–Levitan–Marchenko (GLM) equation. This is a linear integral equation in which the scattering data satisfies the linearised KP hierarchy. The solution to the GLM equation is then shown to coincide with the solution to the noncommutative KP hierarchy. We achieve this using two approaches. In the first approach we use the standard Sato–Wilson dressing transformation. In the second approach, which was pioneered by Pöppe [ 83 ] , we assume the scattering data is semi-additive and by direct substitution, we show that the solution to the GLM equation satisfies the infinite set of field equations representing the noncommutative KP hierarchy. This approach relies on the augmented pre-Pöppe algebra. This is a representative algebra that underlies the field equations representing the hierarchy. It is nonassociative and isomorphic to a descent algebra equipped with a grafting product. While we perform computations in the nonassociative descent algebra, the final result which establishes the solution to the complete hierarchy, resides in the natural associative subalgebra. The advantages of this second approach are that it is constructive, explicit, highlights the underlying combinatorial structures within the hierarchy, and reveals the mechanisms underlying the solution procedure.

Introduction

Our objective is to solve the complete noncommutative Kadomtsev–Petviashvili (KP) hierarchy by direct linearisation.
In this solution approach, we solve a linear integral equation known as the Gelfand–Levitan–Marchenko (GLM) equation.
The coefficients of the linear integral equation, or scattering data, are assumed to satisfy the linearised form of the KP hierarchy equations.
The solution to this linear integral equation are then shown to satisfy the noncommutative KP hierarchy equations.
This method is called direct linearisation as the solution to the noncommutative KP hierarchy equations are obtained by successively solving two linear problems.
First, the linearised hierarchy equations are solved. Second, with that solution as coefficients, the linear integral equation is solved.
We thus demonstrate that the noncommutative KP hierarchy is integrable in the sense that the solution can be reconstructed from a pair of linear equations.

We achieve this via two approaches. The first approach uses the standard Sato–Wilson dressing transformation.
The second approach follows Pöppe
[
83
]
, who pioneered a “head-on” direct linearisation approach for the KP equation.
We assume the scattering data is semi-additive, and gain a more explicit solution to the hierarchy.
This means that the first order linearised equation in the KP hierarchy is automatically satisfied; we naturally assume the scattering data satisfies the higher order linearised equations as well.
As a result, the solution to the GLM equation and its derivatives satisfy the pre-Pöppe product.
We also show that ‘higher’ dependent variables in the hierarchy,
see equations (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) just below,
are given by a simple formula in terms of the first dependent variable; thus we only need to focus on the latter.
Underlying the pre-Pöppe product is the property that the kernels of certain linear combinations of products of the solution to the GLM equation and its derivatives,
correspond to the product of a pair of kernels.
These represent the natural noncommutative products of terms in the noncommutative KP hierarchy itself.
We construct the pre-Pöppe algebra based on the pre-Pöppe product.
The natural setting for the Sato formulation in this context, is the pre-Pöppe algebra equipped with natural, closed, left and right actions, which generates a bimodule structure.
We augment the pre-Pöppe product to mimic these actions and thus construct the ‘abstract’ augmented pre-Pöppe algebra that is isomorphic to the bimodule structure.
However, the augmented pre-Pöppe algebra is nonassociative, reflecting that the left and right actions are distinct.
The original pre-Pöppe algebra, which is associative, is a natural subalgebra of the augmented pre-Pöppe algebra.
While we perform computations in the nonassociative augmented pre-Pöppe algebra, at any point, we can map back to the bimodule structure and its associated actions,
that are closed in the associative pre-Pöppe algebra.
In fact, we abstract the problem further, and show that the augmented pre-Pöppe algebra is isomorphic to a descent algebra, equipped with a grafting product.
We perform our computations in the descent algebra context, and, consequently show that the solution to the GLM equation satisfies the noncommutative KP hierarchy.
From our perspective, the advantages of this second “head-on” Pöppe approach to direct linearisation of the noncommutative KP hierarchy, are that it:
(i) Establishes explicit formulae at all stages for all the fields and coefficients involved;
(ii) Highlights the underlying, and hitherto hidden, algebraic combinatorial structures present in the hierarchy; and
(iii) Reveals the structures and mechanisms underlying the solution procedure that could, in principle, be adapted, extended and abstracted,
to establish the direct linearisation of more general integrable hierarchies.

We now outline the direct linearisation procedure for the noncommutative KP hierarchy more precisely.
The goal is to solve all the equations in the hierarchy.
To solve the infinite set of nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) constituting the noncommutative KP hierarchy,
we solve the following two linear problems in succession:

(1)
Linearised PDE:
First, we solve the linearised form of the KP equations for
p=p(z,\zeta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},\ldots)

Our goal herein, is to show that

There may be weaker classes of solutions that are not included here, for example, the classes of solutions for the Korteweg–de Vries equation considered by Grudsky and Rybkin

However, the solution form we generate via either of the direct linearisation approaches considered herein, is both constructive and practical.
Indeed, for example, we can numerically generate solutions to the noncommutative KP hierarchy straightforwardly as follows.
We can evaluate the solution

and substitute the inverse Fourier transform of the solution into the GLM equation (

This generates the solution to noncommutative KP hierarchy at any time

See Blower and Malham
[

For convenience for the reader, we summarise the main combinatorial algebras and structures that we require herein. These are the:
(a)
Sato algebra:
This the algebra of pseudo-differential operators, with composition as product.
This algebra provides the context for generating the noncommutative KP hierarchy;
(b)
Pre-Pöppe algebra:
This is the algebra of smooth kernels of Hilbert–Schmidt operators on the half line.
The product in this algebra is the pre-Pöppe product based on semi-additive operators, as described above;
(c)
Action bimodule:
This is a bimodule constructed by equipping the pre-Pöppe algebra with some specific actions that arise in the Sato formulation of the noncommutative KP hierarchy.
It represents the natural context for the Sato formulation in the Pöppe approach;
(d)
Augmented pre-Pöppe algebra:

This is an abstract version of the pre-Pöppe algebra where we augment the pre-Pöppe product to include some basic products that naturally mimic the actions in the bimodule.
It is naturally nonassociative and isomorphic to the action bimodule. The pre-Pöppe algebra is a natural subalgebra;
(e)
Descent algebra:
This is the algebra of descents, equipped with the so-called grafting product.
There are two main representations of the descent algebra that we utilise herein, these are the:
(i)
Standard representation:
We record the descents associated with the standard encoding of planar binary trees, equivalencing by the sets of trees with the same descent sets; and
(ii)
Left-glue-right representation:
This is an isomorphic representation that reveals that the descent algebra and augmented pre-Pöppe algebra are isomorphic.
Hereafter we use ‘
\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}
’ as shorthand for ‘left-glue-right’.
The descent algebra is usually associated with the Solomon Descent Algebra, which is associated with permutations and/or shuffles as well as the algebra of quasi-symmetric functions
[
63
,
31
]
.

We now summarise what is new in this paper. In the noncommutative case, we establish:

(1)

The connection between the Sato formulation for the KP hierarchy via a gauge transformation, and, the GLM equation with semi-additive scattering data;

(2)

An augmented pre-Pöppe algebra. This is the underlying algebra for the Sato formulation in the context of direct linearisation. It is nonassociative;

(3)

The isomorphism between the augmented pre-Pöppe algebra and a descent algebra equipped with a grafting product;

(4)

That the Sato coefficients have a simple explicit expansion formula in descents;

(5)

That the complete KP hierarchy is integrable via direct linearisation, by using (1)–(4).

There are also many applications of noncommutative integrable systems, for example, boomeron, trappon and simulton solutions in noncommutative nonlinear optics;
see Calogero and Degasperis
[

]
, Degasperis and Lombardo
[
20
]
.
Lastly, multi-soliton and
\tau
-function solutions to the noncommutative KP hierarchy can be characterised in terms of quasi-determinants,
see: Etingof, Gelfand and Retakh
[

,
29
]
; Gilson and Nimmo
[

]
; Hamanaka
[

]

.

Our paper is structured as follows.
In Section
2
we introduce the pre-Pöppe algebra that underlies Pöppe’s approach to direct linearisation of the KP hierarchy via semi-additive scattering data.
We give some background material demonstrating the connection between the Lax, Zakharov–Shabat and Sato formulations for the noncommutative KP hierarchy in Section
3
,
and introduce the Sato–Wilson dressing transformation approach to direct linearisation.
We then establish the connection between the solution to the GLM equation in Pöppe’s approach and the Sato formulation for the noncommutative KP hierarchy in Section
4
.
We also establish direct linearisation of the noncommutative KP hierarchy via the Sato–Wilson dressing transformation approach, that generates the solution fields to the whole hierarchy.
Section
5
outlines the explicit formulae for the Sato coefficients from which the noncommutative KP hierarchy equations are constructed in the context
of Pöppe’s direct linearisation approach.
The goal of Section
6
is to explain how we make the step, from the Sato formulation of the noncommutative KP hierarchy,
to the nonassociative noncommutative augmented pre-Pöppe algebra, which we use to solve the hierarchy by direct linearisation.
In Section
7
, we review the algebra of planar binary rooted trees. This is a more general nonassociative algebra than we need.
However, the concepts we introduce in that context, such as the grafting of trees at their roots, are the ones we naturally induce on the

\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}
-algebra, which we discuss in Section
8
, and the descent algebra, which we discuss in Section
9
.
The latter two algebras, and the augmented pre-Pöppe algebra, are all isomorphic.
The
\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}
-algebra is a natural representation for the pre-Pöppe algebra. The descent algebra is a useful abstract representation.
We introduce the degrafting operator in Section
10
and establish and explicit formula for the weight character of a descent in Section
11
.
These are both crucial for establishing the simple explicit expansion formula for the Sato coefficients that we provide in Section
12
.
Finally in Section
13
we show that the solution to the GLM equation with semi-additive scattering data that satisfies the linearised equations,
solves the corresponding noncommutative KP hierarchy equations.
Lastly, in Section
14
we discuss possible future research directions.

2
Pre-Pöppe algebra

Herein we introduce the pre-Pöppe algebra.
Let
\mathbb{V}\coloneqq L^{2}((-\infty,0];\mathbb{R}^{m})
denote the space of square-integrable
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued functions on
(-\infty,0]
,
and
\mathfrak{J}_{2}=\mathfrak{J}_{2}(\mathbb{V})
denote the space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators on
\mathbb{V}
.
Any Hilbert–Schmidt valued operator
G
on
\mathbb{V}
generates a unique kernel function
g\in L^{2}((-\infty,0]^{\times 2};\mathbb{R}^{m\times m})

such that for all
\phi\in\mathbb{V}
and
z\in(-\infty,0]
,

\bigl(G\phi\bigr)(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}g(z,\zeta)\,\phi(\zeta)\,{\mathrm{d}}\zeta.

Conversely, any
g\in L^{2}((-\infty,0]^{\times 2};\mathbb{R}^{m\times m})
generates a Hilbert–Schmidt valued operator
G
on
\mathbb{V}
,
with
\|G\|_{\mathfrak{J}_{2}(\mathbb{V})}=\|g\|_{L^{2}((-\infty,0]^{\times 2};\mathbb{R}^{m\times m})}
. See, for example, Simon
[
94
]
.

Definition 1
(Left and right derivatives)

Consider a Hilbert–Schmidt valued operator
G
on
\mathbb{V}
, with a continuously differentiable kernel
g=g(z,\zeta)
.
Further, assume the partial derivatives,
{\partial}_{z}g
and
{\partial}_{\zeta}g
of
g
, are square-integrable on
(-\infty,0]^{2}
.
We define the left
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}
and right
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}
partial derivatives of
G
, respectively,
to be the Hilbert–Schmidt valued operators corresponding to the respective kernels
{\partial}_{z}g
and
{\partial}_{\zeta}g
.

Remark 1

Note that for any pair of Hilbert–Schmidt valued operators,
G
and
\hat{G}
, with continuously differentiable kernels, we have (naturally
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}
and
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}
commute),

{\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}(G\hat{G})=({\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}G)\hat{G}\quad\text{and}\quad{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}(G\hat{G})=G({\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}\hat{G}).

(13)

Definition 2
(Semi-additive operator)

A Hilbert–Schmidt operator
P
on
\mathbb{V}
with corresponding square-integrable kernel
p
is
semi-additive with parameters

x
and
\boldsymbol{t}

if its operation for any square-integrable function
\phi\in\mathbb{V}
, with
z\in(-\infty,0]
, is,

\bigl(P\phi\bigr)(z;x,\boldsymbol{t})=\int_{-\infty}^{0}p(z+x,\zeta+x;\boldsymbol{t})\,\phi(\zeta)\,{\mathrm{d}}\zeta.

Note that for any such semi-additive operator, we have

{\partial}_{x}P=({\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}+{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}})P.

Remark 2

The parameter
\boldsymbol{t}
is an infinite collection of parameters
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{2},t_{3},\ldots)
.
As will become apparent, we usually denote
t_{1}=x
.
For the Hilbert–Schmidt valued semi-additive operator
P
with kernel
p=p(z+x,\zeta+x;\boldsymbol{t})
,
we consider
z
and
\zeta
to be the primary kernel variables, while
x
and
\boldsymbol{t}
are additional parameters.

Recall the bracket ‘
[\![\cdot]\!]
’ notation in (
10
) for Hilbert–Schmidt valued kernels from the Introduction.
For any Hilbert–Schmidt operator
G
, we denote the kernel
g=g(z,\zeta)
of
G
by
[\![G]\!]=[\![G]\!](z,\zeta)
, i.e.
[\![G]\!]\coloneqq g
.
Crucial to our results herein is the pre-Pöppe product.

Lemma 1
(Pre-Pöppe product)

Assume that
P
and
\hat{P}
are semi-additive Hilbert–Schmidt operators with parameters
x
and
\boldsymbol{t}
, and continuously differentiable kernels.
Further assume that
G
and
\hat{G}
are Hilbert–Schmidt operators with continuous kernels. Then we have the following pre-Pöppe product rule:

[\![G(P_{0,1}\hat{P}\!+\!P\hat{P}_{1,0})\hat{G}]\!](z,\zeta;x,\boldsymbol{t})\\
=[\![GP]\!](z,0;x,\boldsymbol{t})\,[\![\hat{P}\hat{G}]\!](0,\zeta;x,\boldsymbol{t}).

This result is straightforwardly established using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

We now consider the GLM equation (
9
), i.e. the linear integral equation
P=G({\mathrm{id}}-P)
.
Suppose we set,

V\coloneqq({\mathrm{id}}-P)^{-1}.

(14)

Then the solution
G
to the GLM equation is given by,

G=PV.

(15)

Existence and uniqueness of such a solution is given, provided
P
is a Hilbert–Schmidt operator and
\mathrm{det}_{2}({\mathrm{id}}-P)\neq 0
,
where
\mathrm{det}_{2}
is Carleman’s determinant.
For more details, see Blower and Malham
[
10
, Lemma 7]
.
Herein, we assume the operator
P
is semi-additive with parameters
x
and
\boldsymbol{t}
.
We are concerned the kernels
[\![G]\!]
and
[\![V]\!]
and their left
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}
, right
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}
, and
{\partial}_{x}
partial derivatives.
Note that by partial fractions, we have,

V\equiv{\mathrm{id}}+PV\equiv{\mathrm{id}}+VP,

(16)

and thus
{\partial}_{x}
derivatives of
[\![G]\!]
and
[\![V]\!]
are identical.
Let
\mathcal{C}(n)
denote the set of all compositions of
n
.
Hereafter we use the notation,
P_{\hat{a}}\coloneqq{\partial}_{x}^{a}P
.
We assume
P
is continuously differentiable to any order we require, and its partial derivatives at that order are Hilbert–Schmidt valued.
By successively differentiating
V
with respect to
x
, and using the partial fraction formulae (
16
), and then applying the
kernel bracket operator ‘
[\![\,\cdot\,]\!]
’, we observe,

{\partial}_{x}^{n}[\![V]\!]=\sum\chi(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})\cdot[\![VP_{\hat{a}_{1}}V\cdots VP_{\hat{a}_{k}}V]\!].

(17)

The sum is over all compositions
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\in\mathcal{C}(n)
, and,

\chi(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k})\coloneqq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}C_{a_{\ell}}^{a_{\ell}+\cdots+a_{k}}.

Here
C_{\ell}^{n}
is the binomial coefficient
n
choose
\ell
. We now lean on the fact that
P
is semi-additive.
We observe that, by the chain rule, we have that,
P_{\hat{a}}=({\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}+{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}})^{a}P
for any
a\in\mathbb{N}
.
Hence
P_{\hat{a}}
is represented by,

P_{\hat{a}}=\sum_{k=0}^{a}C_{k}^{a}\,{\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}^{k}{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}^{a-k}P.

(18)

The terms in the sum are indexed by pairs
(k,a-k)
of left and right derivatives, respectively.
We can replace each of the terms
P_{\hat{a}_{1}}
, …,
P_{\hat{a}_{k}}
in (
17
) by a Leibniz sum of the form (
18
),
which involves pairs of left and right derivatives of the form
P_{a,b}
.
Hence we can regard the base monomials in (
17
) to be of the form (
11
).
The following formulation of the pre-Pöppe product formula in Lemma
1
is crucial to our construction of the pre-Pöppe algebra.

Lemma 2

For arbitrary Hilbert–Schmidt operators
F
and
\hat{F}
and a semi-additive Hilbert–Schmidt operator
P

with parameters
x,y
and a smooth kernel, and for any
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, we have,

\displaystyle[\![FP_{a,b}V]\!]\,[\![VP_{c,d}\hat{F}]\!]=

\displaystyle\;[\![F({\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}P_{a,b})VP_{c,d}\hat{F}]\!]

\displaystyle\;+[\![FP_{a,b}V({\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}P_{c,d})\hat{F}]\!]

\displaystyle\;+[\![FP_{a,b}VP_{\hat{1}}VP_{c,d}\hat{F}]\!].

Remark 3

Note that the cases
FP_{a,b}={\mathrm{id}}
and
P_{c,d}\hat{F}={\mathrm{id}}
are naturally precluded as
F
,
P
and
\hat{F}
are all assumed to be Hilbert–Schmidt valued.
In particular, the possibility of a pre- or postfactor being ‘
[\![V]\!]
’, is also precluded.

The result of Lemma
2
is straightforwardly established by systematically combining the partial fraction formulae (
16
)
with the pre-Pöppe product rule in Lemma
1
.

Definition 3
(Pre-Pöppe algebra)

We call the real matrix algebra of monomials of the form (
11
), equipped with the pre-Pöppe product in Lemma
2
, the
pre-Pöppe algebra
.
The monomial
[\![V]\!]
is
excluded
from the algebra.

Remark 4

We do not require the pre-Pöppe algebra to be unital. And though ‘
[\![V]\!]
’ is excluded from the algbera, the monomials
[\![VP_{1,0}V]\!]
and
[\![VP_{0,1}V]\!]
are included.

Remark 5

Some linear combinations of monomials of the form (
11
) generate multi-factor products. Such multi-factor products are necessarily of the form,

[\![\cdot]\!](z,0;x,\boldsymbol{t})[\![\cdot]\!](0,0;x,\boldsymbol{t})\cdots[\![\cdot]\!](0,0;x,\boldsymbol{t})[\![\cdot]\!](0,\zeta;x,\boldsymbol{t}),

which hereafter, we represent by
[\![\cdot]\!][\![\cdot]\!]\cdots[\![\cdot]\!][\![\cdot]\!]
.
Further, though we have some leeway to leave
z
and
\zeta
‘free’, strictly speaking, we suppose the monomials (
11
) in the pre-Pöppe algebra are all evaluated at
z=\zeta=0
.
The pre-Pöppe algebra remains consistent.

3
Lax, Zakharov–Shabat and Sato formulations

We outline the Lax, Zakharov–Shabat and Sato formulations for the noncommutative KP hierarchy. Modulo some constraints outlined, these are all essentially equivalent.
We also introduce the Sato–Wilson dressing transformation and demonstrate direct linearisation of the noncommutative KP hierarchy using this approach.
Much of the material herein is based on, or derived from, Kodama
[
48
, Ch. 2]
and Pöppe and Sattinger
[
84
]
.

Let us begin with the Lax formulation. Suppose
L
is the pseudo-differential operator defined by,

L\coloneqq{\partial}+u_{1}{\partial}^{-1}+u_{2}{\partial}^{-2}+\cdots,

(19)

where
u=u_{i}(x,\boldsymbol{t})
, with
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and where
\boldsymbol{t}
represents the set of variables
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},\cdots)
, and
{\partial}\coloneqq{\partial}_{x}
.
The operator
{\partial}^{-1}
represents the formal inverse of the operator
{\partial}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, the usual Leibniz rule applies:

{\partial}^{n}u=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}C^{n}_{k}({\partial}_{x}^{k}u){\partial}^{n-k}.

For example, we have
{\partial}u={\partial}_{x}u+u{\partial}
and also that
{\partial}^{-1}u=u{\partial}^{-1}-u_{x}{\partial}^{-2}+u_{xx}{\partial}^{-3}-\cdots
, and so forth.

Definition 4
(Lax formulation)

With
B_{n}\coloneqq(L^{n})_{+}
, the
Lax formulation
of the noncommutative KP hierarchy is given by the infinite set of equations for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

{\partial}_{t_{n}}L=[B_{n},L].

(20)

Here,
(L^{n})_{+}
represents the polynomial part of
L^{n}
so that
(L^{n})_{+}
is a pure differential operator, and
[B_{n},L]\coloneqq B_{n}L-LB_{n}
is the standard commutator for operators.

The case
n=1
in the definition above generates the equations,
{\partial}_{t_{1}}u_{k}={\partial}_{x}u_{k}
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus, as mentioned in the Introduction, we identify
t_{1}
and
x
.
The cases
n=2,3,\ldots
and so forth generate a coupled infinite set of evolution equations for the
u_{k}
in
t_{n}
.

Definition 5
(Zakharov–Shabat formulation)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the pure differential operators in (
3
).
The Zakharov–Shabat formulation of the noncommutative KP hierarchy is then given by the following system of equations for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
:
{\partial}_{t_{n}}B_{m}-{\partial}_{t_{m}}B_{n}=[B_{n},B_{m}]
.

Remark 6

We note:
(i) An equivalent Zakharov–Shabat formulation is,
[{\partial}_{t_{n}}-B_{n},{\partial}_{t_{m}}-B_{m}]=0
;
(ii) If
B_{n}\coloneqq(L^{n})_{+}
, then a consequence of the Zakharov–Shabat formulation is that the flows defined by the Lax formulation (
20
),
commute for any
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
:
{\partial}_{t_{n}}{\partial}_{t_{m}}L={\partial}_{t_{m}}{\partial}_{t_{n}}L
;
(iii) Again, if
B_{n}\coloneqq(L^{n})_{+}
, then the Zakharov–Shabat formulation directly follows from the Lax formulation, see for example Kodama
[
48
, Th. 2.1]
; and
(iv) That the Lax formulation follows from the Zakharov–Shabat formulation “is true up to a triangular change of time variables”; quoting from Krichever and Zabrodin
[
53
]
.

Example 1

In the Zakharov–Shabat formulation in Definition
5
, if we take
n=2
and
m=3
in which
B_{2}\coloneqq{\partial}_{x}^{2}+b_{2,0}
and
B_{3}\coloneqq{\partial}_{x}^{3}+b_{3,1}{\partial}_{x}+b_{3,0}
,
then with
t_{2}
identified as
y
, and
t_{3}
identified as
t
, we eventually obtain the noncommutative KP equation (
7
).

Let us now outline the Sato–Wilson
dressing transformation
.
It is possible to gauge transform
L
into
{\partial}_{x}
.
Indeed, we can transform,
L\mapsto{\partial}_{x}=W^{-1}LW
, where
W
is the Sato–Wilson operator (
1
).
The coefficients
u_{i}
of
L
are related to the coefficients
w_{k}
of
W
via the relation
LW=W{\partial}_{x}
.
Thus for example,
u_{1}={\partial}_{x}w_{1}
,
u_{2}={\partial}_{x}w_{2}+u_{1}w_{1}
, and so forth.
In particular, we can consider the
w_{k}
variables to be the
primary variables
that determine the noncommutative KP hierarchy.
With this realisation in hand, we give the Sato formulation of the noncommutative KP hierarchy as follows.

Definition 6
(Sato formulation)

The Sato formulation of the noncommutative KP hierarchy is given as follows.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the system of equations,

{\partial}_{t_{n}}W=B_{n}W-W{\partial}_{x}^{n},

(21)

where the operator
B_{n}
is given by
B_{n}=(W{\partial}_{x}^{n}W^{-1})_{+}
.

Remark 7

We can also take the operators
B_{n}
to have the form (
3
), as we did in the Introduction.

We quote the following result from Kodama
[
48
, Th. 2.2]
.

Theorem 3

If the Sato–Wilson operator
W
satisfies the Sato equation (
21
), then the operator
L=W{\partial}W^{-1}
satisfies the Lax equation (
20
)
for the noncommutative KP hierarchy, and the operators
B_{n}=(W{\partial}^{n}W^{-1})_{+}=(L^{n})_{+}
satisfy the Zakharov–Shabat equations in Definition
5
.

For convenience, we set:

A_{n}^{\ast}\coloneqq{\partial}_{t_{n}}-{\partial}_{x}^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{n}\coloneqq{\partial}_{t_{n}}-B_{n}.

Then we also have the following.

Lemma 4

The Sato equations (
21
), are equivalent to the statement,

WA_{n}^{\ast}W^{-1}=A_{n}.

Proof 1

We observe that,
WA_{n}^{\ast}=A_{n}W
, is equivalent to,
W{\partial}_{t_{n}}-W{\partial}_{x}^{n}={\partial}_{t_{n}}W-B_{n}W
, which, via the Sato equations (
21
), is equivalent to
W{\partial}_{t_{n}}-W{\partial}_{x}^{n}=-W{\partial}_{x}^{n}
.
Since
W{\partial}_{t_{n}}=0
, the result follows.
∎

Let us now consider the more classical formulation of the dressing transform, see Pöppe and Sattinger
[
84
]
.
We define the Volterra integral operators
K_{\pm}
as follows:

\displaystyle\bigl(K_{+}\psi\bigr)(x)\coloneqq\int_{x}^{\infty}\hat{K}_{+}(x,\xi)\psi(\xi)\,{\mathrm{d}}\xi,

\displaystyle\bigl(K_{-}\psi\bigr)(x)\coloneqq\int_{-\infty}^{x}\hat{K}_{-}(x,\xi)\psi(\xi)\,{\mathrm{d}}\xi,

where the
\hat{K}_{\pm}
represent the corresponding kernels, with
\hat{K}_{+}(x,\xi)=0
if
\xi<x
and
\hat{K}_{-}(x,\xi)=0
if
\xi>x
.

Suppose
that ‘
{\mathrm{id}}+K_{-}
’ and ‘
{\mathrm{id}}+K_{+}
’ both
dress
the operator
A_{n}^{\ast}
to
A_{n}
, i.e. we have,

A_{n}({\mathrm{id}}+K_{\pm})=({\mathrm{id}}+K_{\pm})A_{n}^{\ast}.

(22)

In other words, both
{\mathrm{id}}+K_{+}
and
{\mathrm{id}}+K_{-}
are representatives of
W
.

Definition 7
(Scattering operator)

We define the
scattering operator

P
as the operator given by,

{\mathrm{id}}-P\coloneqq({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})^{-1}({\mathrm{id}}+K_{+}).

(23)

Theorem 5
(Base equations)

The dressing equations (
22
) hold if and only if,

[{\mathrm{id}}-P,A_{n}^{\ast}]=0.

(24)

Proof 2

We follow Pöppe and Sattinger
[
84
]
.
First we demonstrate the base equations (
24
) imply the dressing equations (
22
).
We observe that,

\displaystyle({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})A_{n}^{\ast}

\displaystyle({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})A_{n}^{\ast}({\mathrm{id}}-P)({\mathrm{id}}+K_{+})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})({\mathrm{id}}-P)A_{n}^{\ast}({\mathrm{id}}+K_{+})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;({\mathrm{id}}+K_{+})A_{n}^{\ast}({\mathrm{id}}+K_{+})^{-1},

giving the result. Now we demonstrate that the dressing equations (
22
) imply the base equations (
24
).
We observe that
({\mathrm{id}}-P)A_{n}^{\ast}-A_{n}^{\ast}({\mathrm{id}}-P)
equals,

\displaystyle\;({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})^{-1}({\mathrm{id}}+K_{+})A_{n}^{\ast}-A_{n}^{\ast}({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})^{-1}({\mathrm{id}}+K_{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})^{-1}A_{n}({\mathrm{id}}+K_{+})-({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})^{-1}A_{n}({\mathrm{id}}+K_{+}),

giving the zero operator and thus the second result.
∎

Corollary 6
(Base equations equivalent)

The base equations (
24
), i.e.
[{\mathrm{id}}-P,A_{n}^{\ast}]=0
, correspond precisely to the base equations (
8
), i.e.
{\partial}_{t_{n}}p={\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}^{n}p-(-1)^{n}{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}^{n}p
.

Proof 3

We observe from Theorem
5
, that the dressing equations (
22
) for
G^{\prime}\coloneqq K_{-}
hold if and only if,

\displaystyle[{\mathrm{id}}-P,A_{n}^{\ast}]=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle A_{n}^{\ast}-PA_{n}^{\ast}=A_{n}^{\ast}-A_{n}^{\ast}P

\displaystyle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle P({\partial}_{t_{n}}-{\partial}_{x}^{n})=({\partial}_{t_{n}}-{\partial}_{x}^{n})P

\displaystyle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t_{n}}P={\partial}_{x}^{n}P-P{\partial}_{x}^{n}

\displaystyle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t_{n}}P=\bigl({\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}^{n}P-(-1)^{n}{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}^{n}P\bigr).

Here for an operator
P
with kernel
p=p(x,\xi)
, we have used the notation
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}p={\partial}_{x}p
and
{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}p={\partial}_{\xi}p
.
∎

Remark 8

The latter form of the base equations, namely (
8
), are the base equations considered by Pöppe and Sattinger
[
84
, eq. 2.4]
and Pöppe
[
83
, eq. 3.6]
.

Lemma 7
(GLM equation)

The operator
G^{\prime}\coloneqq K_{-}
satisfies the Gelfand–Levitan–Marchenko (GLM) equation,

P=G^{\prime}({\mathrm{id}}-P).

(25)

Proof 4

By definition, the scattering operator satisfies,

\displaystyle({\mathrm{id}}+K_{-})({\mathrm{id}}-P)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{id}}+K_{+}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle K_{-}-K_{-}P-P

\displaystyle=K_{+}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle K_{-}-K_{-}P

\displaystyle=P.

In the last step we used that
\hat{K}_{+}(x,\xi)=0
when
\xi<x
.
∎

Recall our choice of ‘
{\mathrm{id}}+K_{-}
’ in (
22
) as one representative for the gauge transformation
W
.
In other words, an example operator
W
in the gauge transformation,
L\mapsto{\partial}_{x}=W^{-1}LW
, is
W={\mathrm{id}}+K_{-}={\mathrm{id}}+G^{\prime}
.
If we combine this fact with Lemma
4
, Corollary
6
and Lemma
7
, we establish the following.

Theorem 8
(Direct linearisation)

Suppose
G^{\prime}
is the solution to the GLM equation (
25
), i.e.
P=G^{\prime}({\mathrm{id}}-P)
, where the kernel
p
, of the operator
P
, satisfies the linearised equations (
8
).
Then the coefficients
w_{k}=w_{k}(x,\boldsymbol{t})
of
W={\mathrm{id}}+K_{-}={\mathrm{id}}+G^{\prime}
, satisfy the Sato system (
21
), and thus the noncommutative KP hierarchy.

Remark 9

If we assume that
P
is semi-additive, then there is a straightforward procedure for extracting the coefficients
w_{k}
from the solution to the GLM equation,
G^{\prime}
.
See Theorem
12
in Section
4
.

Remark 10

For the same scattering data
P
satisfying the linearised equations (
8
), the GLM equation (
25
) for
G^{\prime}
and the GLM equation (
9
) for
G
are equivalent.
We explore this connection in detail in Section
4
.
The direct linearisation formulation in (

To see that this is the case, the most edifying route requires us to consider several equivalent representations of the descent algebra;

Indeed the first representation is that involving planar binary rooted trees in which at each grade, we equivalence specific subsets of elements.
In fact, we consider the algebra of planar rooted trees equipped with the grafting product, which is a natural algebra representation for a nonassociative noncommutative algebraic context.
The equivalencing operation on the algebra of planar binary rooted trees is meant to model the fact that, in our context, there is some redundancy in the tree representation.

This representation reveals the connection between the redundancy-free nonassociative algebra and the augmented pre-Pöppe algebra.
The third representation we use is that of the descent algebra itself, equipped, as mentioned, with the grafting product.
This is the natural product induced from the algebra of planar binary rooted trees after equivalencing.
We also introduce a degrafting operator which we use to succinctly encode the expressions involved.

8
The ‘left-glue-right’ algebra

The augmented pre-Pöppe product is nonassociative. However, there is some redundancy in the planar binary rooted tree representation/encoding.
The source of the nonassociativity present in algebra of operator kernel monomials ‘
[\![VP_{a_{1},b_{1}}V\cdots VP_{a_{n},b_{n}}V]\!]
’ generated by the augmented pre-Pöppe product,
is due to left or right factors involving ‘
[\![V]\!]
’ and that pre- or postmultiplication by such factors generates different counts of left and right derivatives in the resulting kernel monomials.
Products involving factors of the form outlined in Lemma
2
for the pre-Pöppe product are associative.
Thus some parenthesised products involving ‘
[\![V]\!]
’, though they are parametrised by different trees, generate the same linear combination of kernel monomials of the form ‘
[\![VP_{a_{1},b_{1}}V\cdots VP_{a_{n},b_{n}}V]\!]
’.
In other words, the source of this redundancy is due to the fact that several different parenthesised products can
generate the same linear combinations of operator kernel monomials with the exact same number of left and right derivatives on the appropriate
P
operators in each monomial contained therein.
For example, consider the three planar binary rooted trees in Table
2
parametrised by the word-codings ‘
1243
’, ‘
1342

These respectively correspond to the parenthesised products as follows,

\displaystyle[\![V]\!]\cdot\Bigl([\![V]\!]\cdot\bigl([\![V]\!]^{2}\cdot[\![V]\!]\bigr)\Bigr),

\displaystyle[\![V]\!]\cdot\Bigl(\bigl([\![V]\!]\cdot[\![V]\!]^{2}\bigr)\cdot[\![V]\!]\Bigr),

\displaystyle\Bigl([\![V]\!]\cdot\bigl([\![V]\!]\cdot[\![V]\!]^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)\cdot[\![V]\!].

It is straightforward to check that, if we use the augmented pre-Pöppe product, each of these parenthesised products generates the same linear combination of kernel monomials.
More precisely, the result is,

\displaystyle[\![V({\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}P_{\hat{1}})V]\!]+[\![V({\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}P_{\hat{1}})V({\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}P_{\hat{1}})V]\!]

\displaystyle\;+[\![VP_{\hat{1}}V({\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}{\partial}_{\mathfrak{r}}P_{\hat{1}})V]\!]+[\![VP_{\hat{1}}VP_{\hat{1}}V({\partial}_{\mathfrak{l}}P_{\hat{1}})V]\!]

\mathfrak{g}

(\mathfrak{r}+\mathfrak{g})

\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}

Definition 20
(Grafting product)

For any two descents,
(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})_{n}

\displaystyle(a_{1}\cdots

\displaystyle\bigl(a_{1}\cdots a_{k}n(b_{1}+n+1)\cdots(b_{\ell}+n+1)\bigr)_{n+m+1}.

Then a root-graft occurs precisely at that point—this would correspond to an insertion of a factor

right factor descent, but with all the descent positions shifted by ‘

This is because we must account for the insertion of both the left factor descent and the additional insertion of the single descent ‘
n

This product is much more easily interpreted in the binary representation for descents as we see presently.
For the moment, for a useful illustrative example, the reader may refer to the product in Example

As mentioned we thoroughly explore these cases presently.

Remark 23
(Descents and compositions)

There is a one-to-one correspondence between descents and compositons. As we have seen, there is a natural one-to-one map between descents of a given grade and length and binary numbers.
In turn there is a natural one-to-one map between binary numbers and compositions, indeed between compositions of
n
of length
k
and binary numbers involving
n

k

This is standard map. For example in the case of binary numbers of length
n

k

Given such a binary number, suppose we identify at the positions of the ones, the corresponding
k

n
of length

That this gives the one-to-one mapping between any such binary number and compositions of
n
of length

Definition 23
(Descent weight)

The weight
\omega\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}
of any descent is defined as follows. By convention we set
\omega\circ\emptyset_{0}\coloneqq 1
and
start with
\omega\circ\emptyset_{1}\coloneqq C^{0}_{0}
. Then for any descent
\mathfrak{d}\in\mathbb{D}
of length
k
, we define,

\omega\circ\mathfrak{d}\coloneqq\omega\circ(\emptyset_{0}\star\mathfrak{d}_{1})+\omega\circ(\mathfrak{d}_{2}\star\mathfrak{d}_{3})+\cdots+\omega\circ(\mathfrak{d}_{k}\star\mathfrak{d}_{k+1}),

where
\emptyset_{0}\star\mathfrak{d}_{1}
, …,
\mathfrak{d}_{k}\star\mathfrak{d}_{k+1}
are all the possible descent pairs, which when grafted together, generate
\mathfrak{d}
.
For any such pair, we can compute the weight of, say
\mathfrak{d}_{1}\star\mathfrak{d}_{2}
, via,

\omega\circ(\mathfrak{d}_{1}\star\mathfrak{d}_{2})\coloneqq C^{\|\mathfrak{d}_{1}\|+\|\mathfrak{d}_{2}\|}_{\|\mathfrak{d}_{1}\|}(\omega\circ\mathfrak{d}_{1})\,(\omega\circ\mathfrak{d}_{2}).

(46)

Remark 26

We emphasise that: (1) We have naturally extended the weight functional linearly;
(2) The decomposition of the descent
\mathfrak{d}
of length

, into the sum of pairs of descents that when grafted together generate
\mathfrak{d}
, is
degrafting
.
See Section

where we consider this operation in detail. Many basic examples of computing weights of descents can also be found therein;
(3) That the weight of the grafting product of two descents,
\mathfrak{d}_{1}\star\mathfrak{d}_{2}
, is given by (
46
), is based on the corresponding formula
for the grafting of two trees, as outlined in Definition
12
;
(4) We can also think of the weight of a descent, as the sum of all the weight characters associated with the corresponding individual planar binary rooted trees, with that descent.

\displaystyle\;-C_{0}^{n-4}b_{n-4}\star\mathfrak{p}_{0},

\displaystyle b_{n-k}=

\displaystyle\;-C^{n}_{k-1}\mathfrak{p}_{k-1}-\sum_{\ell=2}^{k-1}C^{n-\ell}_{k-1-\ell}b_{n-\ell}\star\mathfrak{p}_{k-1-\ell}.

The ‘
\star
’ product represents the augmented pre-Pöppe product.

If, in these equations, we set
\mathfrak{q}_{n}\coloneqq\mathfrak{p}_{n-1}

\displaystyle b_{n-2}=

\displaystyle\;-\gamma_{n}(2)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{2},

\displaystyle b_{n-3}=

\displaystyle\;-\gamma_{n}(3)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{3}+\gamma_{n}(21)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{2}\star\mathfrak{q}_{1},

\displaystyle b_{n-4}=

\displaystyle\;-\gamma_{n}(4)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{4}+\gamma_{n}(31)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{3}\star\mathfrak{q}_{1}+\gamma_{n}(22)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{2}\star\mathfrak{q}_{2}

\displaystyle\;-\gamma_{n}(211)\cdot(\mathfrak{q}_{2}\star\mathfrak{q}_{1})\star\mathfrak{q}_{1},

\displaystyle b_{n-5}=

\displaystyle\;-\gamma_{n}(5)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{5}+\gamma_{n}(41)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{4}\star\mathfrak{q}_{1}+\gamma_{n}(32)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{3}\star\mathfrak{q}_{2}

\displaystyle\;+\gamma_{n}(23)\cdot\mathfrak{q}_{2}\star\mathfrak{q}_{3}-\gamma_{n}(311)\cdot(\mathfrak{q}_{3}\star\mathfrak{q}_{1})\star\mathfrak{q}_{1}

\displaystyle\;-\gamma_{n}(221)\cdot(\mathfrak{q}_{2}\star\mathfrak{q}_{2})\star\mathfrak{q}_{1}-\gamma_{n}(212)\cdot(\mathfrak{q}_{2}\star\mathfrak{q}_{1})\star\mathfrak{q}_{2}

\displaystyle\;+\gamma_{n}(2111)\cdot((\mathfrak{q}_{2}\star\mathfrak{q}_{1})\star\mathfrak{q}_{1})\star\mathfrak{q}_{1}.

For each coefficient, if we consider the pullback of the map,
c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{\ell}\mapsto\gamma_{n}(c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{\ell})\cdot(\mathfrak{q}_{c_{1}}\star\cdots)\star\mathfrak{q}_{c_{\ell}}
,
we find,
b_{n-2}\to-2
,
b_{n-3}\to-3+21
,
b_{n-4}\to-4+31+22-211
, and,

b_{n-5}\to-5+41+32+23-311-221-212+2111.

It thus appears that each
b_{n-k}
is parametrised by the linear combinations of the compositions of
k
, except for those compositions starting with a ‘
1
’,
and the sign of each term is determined by the length of the composition.
Indeed, let
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\ast}
denote the set of compositions of
k
that do not start with a ‘
1
’. Then we have the following result.

Theorem 22
(Sato coefficients)

The
k^{\mathrm{th}}
coefficient in the Sato formulation of the noncommutative hierarchy equations,
b_{n-k}
, is given by,

b_{n-k}=\sum_{c=c_{1}\cdots c_{\ell}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\ast}}(-1)^{\ell}\gamma_{n}(c)\cdot(\mathfrak{q}_{c_{1}}\star\cdots)\star\mathfrak{q}_{c_{\ell}}.

The sum is over all compositions
c=c_{1}\cdots c_{\ell}
in
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\ast}
, and the product is computed left to right starting with
\mathfrak{q}_{c_{1}}\star\mathfrak{q}_{c_{2}}
.

Proof 11

The result follows by induction. Consider the expression for
b_{n-k}
given in Proposition
21
, now with
\mathfrak{p}_{\kappa}
replaced by
\mathfrak{q}_{\kappa+1}
.
The result is,

\displaystyle b_{n-k}=

\displaystyle\;-C^{n}_{k-1}\mathfrak{q}_{k}-C^{n-2}_{k-3}b_{n-2}\star\mathfrak{q}_{k-2}-C^{n-3}_{k-4}b_{n-3}\star\mathfrak{q}_{k-3}

\displaystyle\;-\cdots-C_{0}^{n-k+1}b_{n-k+1}\star\mathfrak{q}_{1}.

Consider the collection of compositions in
\mathcal{C}^{\ast}_{k-1}
. Postattach a ‘
1
’ to each such composition therein.
Now consider the collection of compositions in
\mathcal{C}^{\ast}_{k-2}
. Postattach a ‘
2
’ to each such composition.
Continue this process until the compositions in
\mathcal{C}^{\ast}_{2}=\{2\}
is postattached by ‘
k-2
’.
It is straightforward to establish that the union of these sets together with
\{k\}
itself is
\mathcal{C}^{\ast}_{k}
.
That the binomial coefficients mirror this construction and generate the
\gamma_{n}
-coefficients is also straightforward, and similarly for the signs of the terms.
This establishes the result.
∎

The explicit form for the noncommutative KP equation of order
n
in the hierarchy thus follows immediately. Indeed we have the following.

Corollary 23
(KP equation)

The
n^{\mathrm{th}}
equation in the noncommutative KP hierarchy is given explicitly by,

b_{-1}=\sum_{c=c_{1}\cdots c_{\ell}\in\mathcal{C}_{n+1}^{\ast}}(-1)^{\ell}\gamma_{n}(c)\cdot(\mathfrak{q}_{c_{1}}\star\cdots)\star\mathfrak{q}_{c_{\ell}}.

The sum is over all compositions
c=c_{1}\cdots c_{\ell}
in
\mathcal{C}_{n+1}^{\ast}
, and, again, the product is computed left to right.

Corollary
23
explicitly outlines all the terms present in the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
equation in the noncommutative KP hierarchy.

10
Degrafting

Herein we develop the degrafting operator.
As we see, the degrafting operator is very natural in the context of the noncommutative KP hierarchy and in our pursuit of the solution to the whole hierarchy.
Recall, our main objective is to solve the whole hierarchy by direct linearisation.

To begin, consider the noncommutative equations given in Proposition
21
.
Recall from Example
20
, the expansion sums for
\mathfrak{p}_{0}

\mathfrak{p}_{1}

\mathfrak{p}_{2}

Definition 25
(Sato weight map)

We define the

Definition 26
(Degrafting)

We define the
degrafting operator

\Delta\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}\otimes\mathbb{D}
by,

\Delta(\mathfrak{d})\coloneqq\sum_{\mathfrak{d}_{1},\mathfrak{d}_{2}\in\mathbb{D}}\langle\mathfrak{d}_{1}\star\mathfrak{d}_{2},\mathfrak{d}\rangle\,\mathfrak{d}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{d}_{2},

where
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
is the inner product on
\mathbb{R}\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{\star}
which is one when both arguments match and zero otherwise.
We set
\Delta(\emptyset_{0})\coloneqq 0
.

The effect of
\Delta
on a descent
\mathfrak{d}
is to enumerate and sum all the possible descents
\mathfrak{d}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{d}_{2}
such that
\mathfrak{d}_{1}\star\mathfrak{d}_{2}=\mathfrak{d}
.

Remark 27

The observant reader will recognise that our definition for
\Delta
is analogous to a standard construct: a coproduct. See Reutenauer
[
86
]
and Section
14

Example 21

With reference to Example

11
Weight formula

Herein we establish a general formula for the weight character of a general descent, i.e. for
\omega\circ(d_{1}\cdots d_{\ell})_{n}
.
This is crucial for the proof of our main result, Theorem
27
.
Recall Definition
23
for the descent weight
\omega
.
Since any descent
\mathfrak{d}
can be constructed from any of the possible ways of factoring it into two descents, we can compute the weight
\omega\circ\mathfrak{d}

associated with any descent
\mathfrak{d}
as follows. We degraft
\mathfrak{d}
and then compute the weight of each of the factorised terms using Definition
23
.
We thus compute,

\omega\circ\mathfrak{d}=\omega\circ\bigl(\star\circ({\mathrm{id}}\otimes{\mathrm{id}})\circ\Delta\bigr)\circ\mathfrak{d}.

(55)

The degrafting operation,
\Delta
, splits
\mathfrak{d}
into the sum of all possible pairs of factors generating
\mathfrak{d}
.
The operator
{\mathrm{id}}\otimes{\mathrm{id}}
, applies the identity to both factors.
The grafting operation ‘
\star
’ grafts all those pairs of factors together—with a slight abuse of notation,
we suppose
\star\circ(\mathfrak{d}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{d}_{2})=\mathfrak{d}_{1}\star\mathfrak{d}_{2}
.
Then we can apply the weight operator
\omega
, utilising the property given in Definition
23
.
A convenient shorthand notation for the inner triple of operations is,

{\mathrm{id}}^{\star 2}\coloneqq\star\circ({\mathrm{id}}\otimes{\mathrm{id}})\circ\Delta.

The following set of binomial coefficient identities are crucial to the proof of the weight formula.
They are established by straightforward computation.

Lemma 24
(Binomial identities)

We have the following three binomial identities, the sum identity,

\displaystyle C_{0}^{n-d-1}+C_{1}^{n-d}+C_{2}^{n-d+1}

\displaystyle+\cdots+C_{d}^{n-1}\equiv C_{d}^{n},

(56a)

and the product identities,

\displaystyle C^{n}_{d}+C^{n}_{d-1}

\displaystyle\equiv C^{n+1}_{d}

(56b)

\displaystyle C^{n}_{d_{1}}C^{n-d_{1}}_{d_{2}-d_{1}}

\displaystyle\equiv C^{n}_{d_{2}}C^{d_{2}}_{d_{1}}.

(56c)

We also frequently use the following property.

Definition 27
(DT-invariance)

We say that a set of binomial coefficient terms, involving the components
d_{1},\ldots,d_{\ell}
and
n
of a generic descent, are
DT-invariant
, if the terms are invariant to the transformation,

d_{k}\to d_{k}-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad n\to n-1,

(57)

for
k=1,\ldots,\ell
. DT-invariance is short for unit
decrease transformation invariance
.

Theorem 25
(Weight formula)

The weight character associated with any descent
(d_{1}d_{2}\cdots d_{\ell})_{n}
is given by,

\omega\circ(d_{1}d_{2}\cdots d_{\ell})_{n}=(C^{n}_{d_{1}}-1)\ast(C^{n}_{d_{2}}-1)\ast\cdots\ast(C^{n}_{d_{\ell}}-1),

\omega\circ(d_{1}d_{2}\cdots d_{\ell})_{n}=\sum(-1)^{m+\ell}C_{d_{\alpha_{1}}}^{n}\ast C_{d_{\alpha_{2}}}^{n}\ast\cdots\ast C_{d_{\alpha_{m}}}^{n},

where the sum is over all ordered subsets
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\}
of
\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
with
m\leqslant\ell
.

\{\emptyset_{2},1_{2}\}
by tagging onto the end of each descent therein the new possible descent position at grade
3
, to get
\{2_{3},12_{3}\}
.
The union of the results of these two processes is
\{\emptyset_{3},1_{3},2_{3},12_{3}\}
, which represents all the descents of grade
3
. “Stretching” and then “appending”
the descent set
\{\emptyset_{3},1_{3},2_{3},12_{3}\}
, then generates the descent set
\{\emptyset_{4},1_{4},2_{4},12_{4},3_{4},13_{4},23_{4},123_{4}\}
which represents all the descents of grade
4
, and so forth.
Let us now establish this generation procedure in the
\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}
-algebra context. Recall that
\{\emptyset_{2},1_{2}\}
have the
\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}
-codes,

\{(\mathfrak{l}+\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{r}+\mathfrak{g})\}.

See, for example, Table
1
.
Recall from Section
8
that all
\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}
-codes consist of factors of
\mathfrak{g}
,
(\mathfrak{l}+\mathfrak{g})
,
(\mathfrak{l}+\mathfrak{g}+\mathfrak{r})
and
(\mathfrak{r}+\mathfrak{g})
,
and there are some natural juxtaposition constraints on such factors. In particular, all
\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}
-codes either end in the factor
\mathfrak{g}
or the factor
(\mathfrak{r}+\mathfrak{g})
.
This is apparent from the
\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}
-grafting product in Definition
16
; and the knowledge that, as representatives, all planar binary rooted trees are generated by grafting.

Definition 30
(Stretching and appending)

Given a generic
\mathfrak{l}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{r}
-code
\mathfrak{d}
which we can always decompose into the form
\mathfrak{d}=\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}\mathfrak{c}
,
where
\mathfrak{c}
is the single rightmost factor such that, either
\mathfrak{c}=\mathfrak{g}
or
\mathfrak{c}=(\mathfrak{r}+\mathfrak{g})
.
Then the
stretch
procedure of
\mathfrak{d}
is given by,

\mathfrak{d}\mapsto\begin{cases}\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{l}+\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{g},&~\text{if}\quad\mathfrak{c}=\mathfrak{g},\\
\mathfrak{d}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{l}+\mathfrak{g}+\mathfrak{r})\mathfrak{g},&~\text{if}\quad\mathfrak{c}=(\mathfrak{r}+\mathfrak{g}),\end{cases}

while the
append
procedure is given by,
\mathfrak{d}\mapsto\mathfrak{d}(\mathfrak{r}+\mathfrak{g})
.

With formal “stretching” and “appending” procedures in hand, we have the following result.

14
Discussion

There are many further aspects and future directions of interest to explore.
Some examples are as follows.

(1)
Noncommutative modified KP hierarchy:
For the noncommutative modified KP hierarchy, the GLM equation has the form
P=G({\mathrm{id}}-Q)
, with
Q\coloneqq P^{2}
.
Many more details can be found in Blower and Malham
[
10
]
.
Therein we establish the Miura transformation between the noncommutative KP equation and a lifted noncommutative modified KP system.
It would be of interest to adapt the methods herein to establish the integrability of the complete noncommutative modified KP hierarchy (lifted version) by direct linearisation,
either via the Miura transformation, or more directly via the modified hierarchy equations.

(2)
Reductive hierarchies:
The noncommutative KP hierarchy reduces to many well-known hierarchies under various symmetry assumptions.
The noncommutative KP hierarchy reduces to the noncommutative Boussinesq hierarchy, and, via a separate symmetry assumption, to the noncommutative KdV hierarchy.
The noncommutative Boussinesq hierarchy is related to the noncommutative Kaup–Kupershmidt and Sawada–Kotera hierarchies; see for example,
Foursov and Moreno Maza
[
32
]
, Hone and Wang
[
45
]
and Kang, Liu, Olver and Qu
[
47
]
.
These hierarchies are in turn related to the Camassa–Holm and Degasperis–Procesi hierarchies via Liouville transformations and so forth.
It would be of interest to understand the consequences, of the direct linearisation solution ansatz and the classes of solutions we have considered herein, for solutions to these reduced hierarchies.

(3)
Numerical simulations:
As mentioned in the introduction, in Blower and Malham
[
10
]
, we generated solutions to the noncommutative KP equation by numerically solving the direct linearisation problem.
The solution to the linearised equations (
8
) can be analytically advanced to at anytime
t>0
in Fourier space.
Then the discrete inverse Fourier transform (via FFT) can be substituted as the scattering data into the GLM equation (
9
).
This in turn can be solved as a numerical linear algebra problem to generate the solution to the noncommutative KP equation at that time
t>0
.
See Blower and Malham
[
10
]
for the precise details, which include simulations involving three-wave interactions to the noncommutative KP equations.
A natural next step is to extend this procedure to higher hierarchy members.

(4)
Quasi-determinant solutions:
It is well-known that solutions to the noncommutative KP hierarchy can be expressed in terms of quasi-determinants,
see Etingof, Gelfand, Retakh
[
28
,
29
]
and Gilson and Nimmo
[
37
]
.
Of interest here, would be to establish the explicit relation between the solution ansatz we have used herein and these quasi-determinant solution formulations.
We have established a quasi-trace interpretation for the solutions herein, see Blower and Malham
[
10
]
.
Investigating this quasi-trace solution form and its connection to quasi-determinant formulations is another interesting future investigation.

(5)
Fredholm Grassmannian flows:
It is well-known that the solution to the KP hierarchy can be interpreted as a flow on a Fredholm Grassmann manifold.
See Sato
[
89
,
90
]
, Segal and Wilson
[
93
]
, and Mulase
[
70
]
.
Further, all possible KP multi-soliton interactions can be parametrised within the context of finite dimensional Grassmannians. This classification was established by Kodama
[
48
]
.
From our perspective herein, for a given semi-additive scattering operator
P
satisfying the linearised KP equations (
8
),
the solution
G
to the GLM equation (
9
) naturally generates a solution flow on a given coordinate patch of a Fredholm Grassmannian.
The coordinate patch is represented by the pair
({\mathrm{id}},G)
,
which parametrises the time-evolving
envelope
or
subspace
of dispersive field solutions represented by the pair
({\mathrm{id}}-P,P)
; see Blower and Malham
[
9
]
.
Also see Doikou, Malham and Stylianidis
[
22
]
, Doikou
et al.

[
23
,
24
]
, Malham
[
59
]
and Blower and Malham
[
10
]
, as well as Beck
et al.

[
5
,
6
]
.
There are many directions of interest to pursue for solutions to the noncommutative KP hierarchy within the Fredholm Grassmannian context, for example:
(a) Flows with singularities could be interpreted as flows for which, locally, the coordinate patch
({\mathrm{id}},G)
is a poor representative patch.
We could easily continue such flows as non-singular flows in another coordinate patch, for example, the pair
(G^{\prime},{\mathrm{id}})
;
(b) Could we extend, improve or view known solution regularity results in this Fredholm Grassmannian context,
for example along the lines of the results of Grudsky and Rybkin
[
38
]
—see item (7) below;
(c) Can we construct a quasi-determinantal bundle associated with the Fredholm Grassmannian and connect that to the quasi-determinant solutions
established for the noncommutative KP equation by Gilson and Nimmo
[
37
]
, and/or a suitably defined
\tau
-function?

(6)
Fay identities:
Connecting our results herein to the algebraic and/or differential Fay identities, and theta functions,
is very much of interest, though definitively a longer term project.
See, for example, Dimakis and Müller–Hoissen
[
21
]
and Takasaki
[
97
]
.

(7)
Analytical generalisations:
The fact that we restrict ourselves herein to Hilbert–Schmidt valued operators might be too restrictive.
It would be of interest to see if we could relax the class of operators we use throughout to bounded linear operators with sufficiently differentiable kernels, for example.
Weakening the classes of solutions for which solutions to integrable systems can be established, has seen a recent resurgence of interest, see for example Grudsky and Rybkin
[
38
]
.

(8)
Algebra generalisations:
The following generalisations are very much of interest:
(a)
General fields:
The results we have established herein naturally generalise to the complex field
\mathbb{C}
.
However they may also generalise to algebra structures in which we replace these fields by quaternions or more general rings.
(b)
Lifted associative algebra:
For any nonassociative algebra, such as the descent algebra
\mathbb{R}\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{\star}
we considered herein, we can generate, via left and right linear maps,
the
associative enveloping algebra
or
multiplication algebra
which is the subassocciative algebra of the algebra of endomorphisms on
\mathbb{R}\langle\mathbb{D}\rangle_{\star}
.
See, for example, Schafer
[
91
]
. Can we lift the descent algebra in this way to simplify our proof?
(c)
Degrafting coalgebra:
The degrafting operation
\Delta
introduced in Section
10
and utilised extensively thereafter, is a natural candidate for a coproduct,
and thus establishing a coalgebra equipped with a degrafting co-product.
Coassociativity of
\Delta
appears to be the main obstruction, but perhaps a more general perspective might help remove this obstacle and enable us to establish a coalgebra based on
\Delta
;
(d)
Hopf algebra:
If a degrafting coalgebra can be established, then it is only natural to look for a Hopf algebra structure.
