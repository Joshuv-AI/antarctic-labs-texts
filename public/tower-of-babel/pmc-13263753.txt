A systematic review and meta-analysis of microsurgery and radiosurgery outcomes in low-grade cerebral arteriovenous malformations: Obliteration rates, morbidity, and mortality
Authors: Setyo Widi Nugroho, Nadya Zaragita, Aryandhito Widhi Nugroho, Fabianto Santoso, Sayyid Abdil Hakam Perkasa, Adhitya Syahada, Theresia Meiske Laura Siscawati Wayne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLow-grade cerebral arteriovenous malformations (LGCAVMs) are treated with microsurgery according to Spetzler-Martin (SM) grade. Radiosurgery is an emerging treatment for LGCAVMs. Existing comparative evidence is mostly from retrospective, non-randomized cohorts, limiting direct comparison between treatments.Research questionThis study compared the outcomes of microsurgery and radiosurgery for LGCAVMs.Material and Methods: This systematic review and meta-analysis followed PRISMA guidelines. Article search was conducted until December 13, 2023, in PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. Articles on the outcomes of SM I-II CAVMs treated with radiosurgery and microsurgery were included. Methodological quality assessment was conducted using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsThis review of 22 studies involving 3259 LGCAVM cases found microsurgery (56.1%) had 93.5% cumulative complete obliteration rate and 1.8% post-treatment haemorrhage compared to 77.2% and 6.6%, respectively, in radiosurgery group (43.9%). Obliteration after radiosurgery was observed after two years. Meta-analysis of five studies comparing both treatments showed that microsurgery yielded higher risk ratio (RR) for complete obliteration of 1.62 [95% CI 1.33, 1.97] but higher RR for morbidity of 10.26 (95% CI 2.99; 35.25). Radiosurgery is beneficial for SM grade II AVMs when microsurgery is risky.Discussion and conclusionMicrosurgery remains associated with higher immediate complete obliteration for LGCAVMs. For selected SM grade II CAVMs (deep-seated, deep drainage, or eloquent areas), radiosurgery is an important alternative when microsurgical risk is high, with complete obliteration observed at two years of follow-up. Given the retrospective, non-randomized evidence, and treatment-selection bias, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Introduction

Cerebral arteriovenous malformation (CAVM) is an abnormal connection of brain blood vessels marked by direct shunts from arteries to veins and the presence of a nidus (Barbosa et al., 2019). The prevalence of this disease is 0.05% among the population (Morris et al., 2009). CAVM symptoms include seizure, focal neurological deficits, and occurrence of rupture, although in some cases of CAVMs, they remain asymptomatic (Barbosa et al., 2019). Rupture occurs in 1-3% of cases each year, which may lead to morbidity and mortality (Stapf et al.).

Classifications and scoring systems have been developed to simplify decision-making for AVM treatment. Spetzler-Martin (SM) grading system, the most commonly used system, classifies CAVMs into five grades based on the nidus size (score 1-3), location (score 0-1), and venous drainage (score 0-1) (Conger et al., 2015a). This grading system was simplified into three groups by Spetzler and Ponce. Grade A consisted of SM grade I and II CAVMs, grade B consisted of SM grade III CAVMs, and grade C consisted of SM grades IV and V CAVMs. Combinations of the existing SM grades were made because of the similar outcomes of each pair (Unnithan, 2020).

There are various choices for CAVM treatment in current practice. For low-grade AVM (LGCAVMs), consisting of SM grade I and grade II CAVMs, microsurgery is recommended due to its high obliteration rate, low complication rate, and low re-rupture rate (Potts et al., 2015). However, in cases where surgical intervention cannot be performed, radiosurgery has been considered an alternative (Conger et al., 2015a). Additionally, in grade II LGCAVMs in eloquent locations or LGCAVMs with deep venous drainage, microsurgery carries a higher risk of neurological deficits.

Over the years, the development of radiosurgery procedures has proven beneficial for various diseases (in this manuscript, “radiosurgery” is used throughout to refer to single-session stereotactic radiosurgery [SRS], including gamma knife, CyberKnife, and LINAC-based platforms; technology-specific terms are used only when distinguishing between platforms in the primary data. Similarly, “post-treatment haemorrhage” is used throughout to refer to any new intracranial bleed attributable to the treated CAVM during follow-up, encompassing “rupture,” “post-treatment rupture,” and “haemorrhage” as used by the primary studies. This noninvasive option has been reported to achieve high obliteration rates with low morbidity in deep-seated, small to medium-sized, or residual CAVMs (Dumont et al., 2018). In the clinical practice, radiosurgery can be offered to LGCAVM patients who cannot tolerate or refuse surgical treatment (Conger et al., 2015b).

Despite its potential, radiosurgery has not been established as an equivalent first-line treatment to microsurgery in current grading-based algorithms. Prior institutional series and narrative reviews have compared the two modalities in heterogeneous ways, often within single center, mixing all Spetzler-Martin (SM) grades, or using different outcome definitions. To our knowledge, a contemporary systematic review that pools comparative obliteration, morbidity, and mortality data specifically for LGCAVM is lacking. A combined analysis of SM grade I and grade II CAVMs is justified because the Spetzler-Ponce grade A classification groups them together on the basis of broadly similar surgical outcomes, and existing institutional series commonly report them as a single low-risk operative group; nonetheless, the heterogeneity of the grade II subgroup (medium size, deep venous drainage, or eloquent location) is recognised and is addressed in our subanalysis and discussion. In real-world practice, the choice between microsurgery and radiosurgery is not a simple head-to-head comparison of equivalent interventions but depends on eloquence, deep venous drainage, nidus size, rupture status, patient age, and institutional expertise. Against this background, the present review aimed to report the obliteration rate, morbidity, and mortality of microsurgery and radiosurgery for LGCAVM, and to identify subgroups in which one modality may offer a more favorable risk-benefit profile than the other.

Methods

Study search

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. Articles were searched in PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library from the date of inception until December 13, 2023. The same core concept terms were used across databases and were adapted to the syntax of each platform; Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were applied in PubMed and mapped to equivalent controlled vocabulary or free-text terms in Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to combine concepts. The two search strings used were “(“low grade AVM" OR "low grade arteriovenous malformation") AND (“gamma knife" OR "radiosurgery" OR "resection" OR "surgery")” and “(“AVM grade I-II" OR "arteriovenous malformation grade I-II") AND (“gamma knife" OR "radiosurgery" OR "resection” OR “surgery”).” Articles that included both radiosurgery and microsurgery for LGCAVM were included in this meta-analysis. The two search strings shown above were the core concept combinations; the full database-specific syntax (including mapped MeSH and Emtree terms and field tags used in PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane, and Google Scholar) is provided as Supplementary Material. Reference lists of included articles and relevant recent reviews were also hand-searched to identify additional eligible studies. No grey literature or conference abstracts were included, and no restrictions were placed on publication date.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) articles containing LGCAVM treated with radiosurgery or microsurgery, (2) no radiosurgery as a pre-surgical treatment, and (3) treatment outcomes (deficits, rupture, modified Rankin Scale, and obliteration rate). When a study reported CAVMs of mixed Spetzler-Martin grades, it was included only when outcome data specific to SM grade I and II patients could be unambiguously extracted from the published tables, figures, or text; studies that reported pooled outcomes across all SM grades without disaggregation were excluded. Both adult and pediatric cohorts were eligible; study population (adult, pediatric, or mixed) was recorded, and this variable was considered in the discussion of heterogeneity. No restrictions were placed on publication date or country. Studies excluded were (1) studies in which analysis of specific SM grade I and SM grade II outcomes were not shown or could not be separated from higher SM grade lesions, (2) embolization as a single treatment, and (3) non-English reviews, articles, or case reports.

Data extraction and assessment

Reviewers NZ, AS, and TW screened the titles, abstracts, and full-text articles that met the research questions and the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Articles were screened by their titles and abstracts. Full-text articles were reviewed in detail, because many abstracts report AVM findings across all Spetzler-Martin grades, and the LGCAVM subgroup data therefore had to be extracted manually. When the number of LGCAVM patients or their outcomes could not be unambiguously isolated from the overall dataset, the study was excluded. For each included study, two reviewers independently extracted the data; disagreements were resolved by discussion and, where necessary, adjudication by the senior author (SWN). Methodological quality of all included observational cohort studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS); a prognostic-focused critical appraisal was applied in addition for the subset of studies that reported time-to-obliteration as a prognostic outcome. Data analysis was performed by NZ, AWN, FS, and SH. All appraisals were conducted by NZ, FS, SH, and SWN.

Data analysis

The primary outcome was complete AVM obliteration, defined as absence of the nidus on post-treatment digital subtraction angiography (DSA) or, when not available, on magnetic ressonance imaging (MRI) based evaluation. Secondary outcomes were post-treatment haemorrhage (any new intracranial bleed attributed to the treated AVM during follow-up), treatment-related morbidity (defined a priori as any new persistent neurological deficit attributable to treatment, including cranial nerve, motor, sensory, language, or visual field deficits, or radiation-induced neurological injury), treatment-related mortality, and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at last follow-up. Because primary studies used heterogeneous wording for morbidity, reported events were mapped during extraction to these predefined categories; events that could not be categorised under this scheme were not counted. The term “radiosurgery” is used to refer single-session stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) such as gamma knife radiosurgery (GKRS), CyberKnife, and LINAC. Post-treatment haemorrhage is used to refer any new bleeding after LGCAVM treatment during the follow-up period, which also covers “rupture”, “post-treatment rupture”, and “haemorrhage”.

Data including patient demographics, obliteration rate, mRS (before and after treatment), rupture events before treatment, morbidity, and mortality in each study were collected, stratified, and analyzed. A meta-analysis (forest plot) was performed using SPSS ver. 29 on studies including paired data from microsurgery and radiosurgery groups. Pooled effect sizes were reported as risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Given anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity (differing follow-up, institutions, and baseline lesion characteristics), a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model was used. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed with the I2 statistic, with values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% taken as low, moderate, and substantial heterogeneity, respectively. Where an arm reported zero events, a standard continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to each cell. Because only five studies provided head-to-head data, formal assessment of publication bias with a funnel plot or Egger's test was not performed, since such tests are underpowered with fewer than ten studies; this is acknowledged explicitly as a limitation. Pre-planned sensitivity analyses by study design and by population (adult vs. pediatric) were precluded by the small number of head-to-head studies and are also acknowledged as a limitation. Quality assessment was conducted using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.

Search results

The authors collected 407 articles, of which 310 were from PubMed, 12 from Scopus, three from the Cochrane Library, and 82 from Google Scholar. Duplicate articles, irrelevant titles, unavailable full-text articles, incomplete SM grade data, and non-English articles were excluded. Radiosurgery was performed in 1430 patients (43.9%), which showed a variable range of complete obliteration in each study (40-93%) (Table 3). Eleven of the 12 studies had a median follow-up duration of >2 years. Cumulative complete obliteration reached 77.2% (1104 of 1430 patients), with a post-treatment rupture incidence of 6.6% (84 of 1278 patients) (Supplementary Table 4). The cumulative mortality rate was 1.4% (19 of 1403 patients) (Supplementary Table 5). These cumulative estimates must be interpreted with caution because follow-up duration varied widely across the included studies (from short surgical follow-up to several years of radiosurgical follow-up). Microsurgery produces an immediate angiographic result, whereas radiosurgery has an obliteration latency of within the range of 2-5 years; simple pooling of cumulative obliteration percentages therefore favours microsurgery mechanically rather than biologically. Seventeen studies reported the use of preoperative embolization for LGCAVM (875 patients). Preoperative embolization was reported in 9 microsurgery studies (497 patients; 15%), 4 radiosurgery studies (135 patients; 4%), and 4 studies involving both microsurgery and radiosurgery patients (243 patients; 7%). In total, 26.8% of the treated patients underwent preoperative embolization (875 out of 3259 patients).

Subanalysis

A subanalysis was performed for SM grade II AVMs because of the heterogeneity of SM grade II components (either medium-sized nidus, deep venous drainage, or located in the eloquent area). Eight studies reported 75-100% complete obliteration after microsurgery. Only three studies showed 40-70% of complete obliteration in SM grade II AVMs that underwent radiosurgery (follow up > 25 months).

Nine studies provided data on pretreatment mRS, in which 76.4% of the patients had mRS of 0-2 (1307 of 1709 patients). Post-radiosurgery mRS were reported in 4 studies (327 patients), in which 91.7% had mRS 0-2 (follow-up >36 months). Post-microsurgery mRS were reported in 12 studies with mRS 0-2 achieved in 87.7% of patients who underwent microsurgery (1451 out of 1655 patients). These three mRS datasets (pretreatment in 9 studies, post-radiosurgery in 4 studies, post-microsurgery in 12 studies) involve different study sets with different baseline patient characteristics, different follow-up intervals, and different imaging schedules, and cannot be treated as directly comparable. The numerical difference between 91.7% (post-radiosurgery) and 87.7% (post-microsurgery) should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of functional superiority of one treatment over the other.

Meta-analysis was performed to compare the obliteration rate and morbidity rate of microsurgery and radiosurgery. Authors found five studies by Nataf et al., Stein et al., Wang et al., Gami et al., and Dumot et al. (Table 4) which included both microsurgery and radiosurgery. Only these five studies were eligible for direct meta-analytic comparison because they were the only studies in which both microsurgery and radiosurgery were performed in the same LGCAVM cohort, reported under consistent outcome definitions, and therefore comparable without cross-study unit-of-analysis errors. The remaining 17 studies reported outcomes for only one modality and were summarised descriptively. We recognise that even these five studies differ in baseline lesion characteristics and in treatment-allocation rules, and that the resulting pooled estimates remain vulnerable to treatment-selection bias; this is addressed further in the Discussion and Limitations. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the quality of these studies. The obliteration rate risk ratio was 1.62 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.33, 1.97) in the microsurgery group, which indicates moderate heterogeneity but with a statistically significant result (p < 0.001). However, microsurgery was associated with a higher risk of postoperative morbidity and mortality, with a risk ratio of 10.26 (95% CI 2.99; 35.25) and was statistically significant (p=<0.001). In the present meta-analysis, morbidity and mortality were reported together because most of the five head-to-head studies reported a combined safety endpoint rather than disaggregated counts. Discussion

Ruptured vs unruptured AVMs

The rupture risk of AVMs ranges from 2 to 4% per year. The likelihood of rupture was greater in deep-seated AVMs, which tend to be larger than lobar AVMs. In addition, deep-seated AVMs tend to be associated with the presence of deep venous drainage (Dumont et al., 2018).

The risk of re-rupture increases to 6-15% within the first year after the initial rupture. The surgical outcomes for ruptured and unruptured AVMs differ, with ruptured AVMs having significantly worse outcomes. Rupture events would influence the neurologic status upon arrival to the hospital, and eventually influence mRS(Cenzato et al., 2018). Post-treatment mRS, despite the presence of a rupture event, was favorable in both the microsurgery and radiosurgery groups (87.7% vs 91.7%).

Obliteration rate

LGCAVM (SM I-II) are considered a low-surgical-risk group for which microsurgery is recommended. Despite microsurgery having been proven to offer a high obliteration rate, with low morbidity and mortality rates, radiosurgery has been known as an alternative. In cases where microsurgery cannot be conducted, radiosurgery can be offered due to its high obliteration rate and low morbidity rate (Conger et al., 2015a).

The complete obliteration rates in the microsurgery and radiosurgery groups were 93.5% and 77.2%, respectively, indicating that microsurgery was superior. The complete obliteration rate of microsurgery reported by Nataf et al. was 94%-100% compared to 69-96% in radiosurgery group (Nataf et al., 2007). The lower complete obliteration rate in the radiosurgery group may have been influenced by the length of follow-up. This is supported by Kano et al. who reported that obliteration rates after SRS procedures were 58%, 87%, 90%, and 95% at 3, 4, 5, and 10 years, respectively, as determined by MRI study or angiography (Kano et al., 2012). Dumont et al. stated that a follow-up period of 5-10 years would be needed to evaluate the benefit of radiosurgery (Dumont et al., 2018). Other factors contributing to the likelihood of obliteration after radiosurgery include younger patient age, smaller AVM volume, smaller isodose surface volume, higher radiation dose, and having only one major draining vein (Conger et al., 2015b; Ding et al., 2014). Ding et al. reported that the 3- and 5-year obliteration rates of LGCAVM with deep venous drainage were 50% and 69% versus 39 and 65% for superficial venous drainage, respectively (p = 0.130). This finding indicates no significant difference in the obliteration rate between each type of draining vein (Ding et al., 2014).

Despite the mentioned findings, based on the forest plot analysis of five studies that included both radiosurgery and microsurgery treatments, microsurgery was only slightly more effective in offering complete obliteration with a risk ratio of 1.62 [95% CI 1.33, 1.97, I2 = 55%] (Fig. 3). The authors concluded that although complete obliteration is more likely to be achieved through microsurgery, radiosurgery offers similar results. Additionally, previous studies have suggested that a longer follow-up duration results in a higher obliteration rate. These comparisons are, however, substantially vulnerable to treatment-selection bias; in almost all of the included retrospective series, the choice of microsurgery versus radiosurgery was made by the treating team on the basis of lesion anatomy, eloquence, deep venous drainage, patient age, comorbidities, and institutional expertise. Lesions referred for radiosurgery were therefore systematically different from those referred for microsurgery (often smaller, deeper, more eloquent, or in older or higher-risk patients), and at least part of the observed difference in obliteration and in morbidity probably reflects this prior selection rather than a pure treatment effect. For this reason, the pooled estimates presented here should be read as hypothesis-generating observations, not as randomised evidence of the superiority of either modality.Fig. 3Forest plot of obliteration rate outcome.

Grading system and potential bias

SM grading was proposed in 1986 to assess the risk of microsurgical resection of CAVMs because surgery was deemed the gold standard for CAVMs at that time. The purpose of this grading system is to predict the risk of morbidity and mortality associated with microsurgical treatment. This grading system comprises several variables, including the CAVM nidus size, venous drainage pattern, and CAVM location eloquence. (Spetzler) Typically, SM grade I and II CAVMs can undergo surgery, whereas higher grades may require more than one type of treatment. Another adjunctive grading system, SM supplementary grading, also helps to stratify surgical risks (Lawton et al., 2010).

Despite the surgical risk stratification value of the SM grading, factors related to radiosurgery are not well appraised. Due to the different nature of how both treatments work, treatment complications differ in microsurgery and radiosurgery. (Spetzler) The SM grading system does not include AVM volume and specific location, which are critical for determining the indications for radiosurgery (Pollock and Flickinger, 2002).

To assess the outcomes of radiosurgery for AVMs, Pollock and Flickinger developed a radiosurgery-based AVM score (RBAS) that includes volume, age, and nidus location. This scoring was defined as AVM score = (0.1)(AVM volume in cm3) + (0.02)(patient age in years) + (0.3)(location of the lesion: frontal, temporal = 0; parietal, occipital, intraventricular, corpus callosum, cerebellar = 1; basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem = 2). In this study, theSM grade did not correlate with excellent patient outcomes (Pollock and Flickinger, 2002). In 2008, Pollock et al. created a modified RBAS, in which the location was divided as a two-tiered variable. The formula was defined as AVM score = (0.1)(volume, mL) + (0.02)(age, years) + (0.5)(location, hemispheric/corpus callosum/cerebellar = 0; basal ganglia/thalamus/brainstem = 1). With this scoring system, smaller results would represent better obliteration rates and lower mRS(Pollock and Flickinger, 2008).

In the articles included in the analysis, SM grading was used in both the microsurgery and radiosurgery arms; none of the included studies used RBAS or modified RBAS (mRBAS) for patient selection or outcome stratification. The mRBAS is discussed here not as an element of the present pooled analysis, but to illustrate how future comparative studies could stratify radiosurgery candidates more accurately than SM grade alone, which was designed to predict microsurgical risk. We therefore recommend that future prospective or registry-based comparisons of LGCAVM treatments record both SM grade and mRBAS at baseline, so that like-for-like comparisons between modalities become possible.

Preoperative embolization

Preoperative embolization was done in 17 out of 22 studies. Although most studies included preoperative embolization, only 26.8% of patients received this additional treatment. Two studies did not mention any data regarding preoperative embolization. There is a tendency that preoperative embolization before microsurgery was done more frequently compared to radiosurgery. The embolization aims to reduce the blood flow to the AVM, minimize operative blood loss, and thus facilitate AVM resection (Wang et al., 2020). Other than blood loss, there are no added neurological benefits by doing preoperative embolization before microsurgery. There was no difference in the postoperative haemorrhage incidence between patients who received preoperative embolization and those who did not (Catapano et al., 2022).

Unlike in microsurgery, the role of preoperative embolization in radiosurgery for LGCAVM is still questionable (Catapano et al., 2022). Embolization results in neovascularization, which cannot be overlooked during radiosurgery planning. Miyachi et al. reported that preradiosurgery embolization was associated with lower obliteration rate due to blurred nidus margin, causing targeting error, and recanalization due to hemodynamic remodeling (Miyachi et al., 2017). Radiosurgery can be performed as a standalone procedure without the need for preoperative embolization. Moreover, it is less invasive compared to embolization and/or microsurgery, and it requires just one treatment session to achieve the desired obliteration. Preoperative or pre-radiosurgical embolization is, however, a potentially important confounder of the pooled estimates presented here. Across the 22 included studies, 26.8% of patients (875 of 3259) received adjunctive embolization, and its distribution was uneven: embolization was used more frequently before microsurgery than before radiosurgery. Because embolization can reduce intraoperative blood loss and facilitate microsurgical resection, but can also create nidal neovascularization and blur the radiosurgical target (potentially lowering radiosurgery obliteration rates), differential use of embolization between the two arms may have independently inflated the apparent benefit of microsurgery in our pooled comparisons. The primary studies did not provide sufficient patient-level data to adjust for embolization exposure, and we were therefore unable to incorporate it as a covariate in the meta-analysis. This is an important confounder to address in future comparative studies.

Morbidity and mortality

Arterial aneurysms are found in 2.7-16.7% of AVM patients, and their presence increases the risk of rupture in AVMs in general. The annual risk of AVMs with arterial aneurysms is 7% compared to 1.7% in patients without arterial aneurysms (Jha et al., 2016).

Microsurgery significantly reduced the rupture incidence after treatment (Gami et al., 2021). In cases treated with radiosurgery, haemorrhage risks were also reduced by 54% at latency period and by 88% after achieving obliteration (Maruyama et al., 2005). Even so, patients with LGCAVM who undergo radiosurgery are still at risk during the interval between treatment and obliteration. Rupture occurs within the first three years after treatment (Zabel et al., 2007; Peciu-Florianu et al., 2020). The presence of aneurysms proximal to the AVM results in a higher rupture rate after radiosurgery. The rates of annual bleeding risk, however, decrease each year from 3.7%, 0.3%, and 0.2% at 1, 1-5 years, and 5-10 years after treatment (Kano et al., 2012). Zabel-du Bois et al. explained that rupture is associated with obliteration status, which is more likely in cases with no or partial obliteration status. In addition to aneurysms, rupture occurs more often in AVMs involving multiple feeders (Zabel et al., 2007).

SM grade I AVMs are homogenous, unlike SM grade II AVMs, which consist of different subtypes that may result in different outcomes. Deep venous drainage in SM grade II AVMs would have worse outcomes than medium-sized SM grade II AVMs and is more likely to result in permanent new deficits (Hung et al., 2017). AVMs with deep venous drainage or eloquent location have been known to be the least favorable lesions because of the potential surgical complications that result in neurological deficits (Graffeo et al., 2020). Thus, despite the current recommendations to perform microsurgery for SM grade II AVMs, deep venous drainage should be considered when choosing between microsurgery and radiosurgery. Therefore, radiosurgery is recommended as the primary therapy for deep and/or eloquent SM grade II AVMs (Dumont et al., 2018; Gami et al., 2021; Sasaki et al., 1998).

The mortality rate was higher in the radiosurgery group than in the microsurgery group. Kano et al. reported that a mortality rate of 2.8% occurred during the latency period after radiosurgery treatment (Kano et al., 2012). Maruyama et al., however, reported that radiosurgery significantly decreases the risk of haemorrhage in patients with cerebral AVMs through thickening of intima, which begins three months after radiosurgery (Maruyama et al., 2005). Based on three studies presenting morbidity and mortality data, microsurgery was 10.26 times (95% CI 2.99; 35.25 with I2 = 40%) more likely to result in morbidity and mortality than radiosurgery (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Forest plot of morbidity and mortality outcome.

Follow up radiologic evaluation

The follow-up periods for microsurgery and radiosurgery differed. Surgery offers immediate obliteration rate and elimination of haemorrhage risks while radiosurgery would take longer follow-up to show complete obliteration (Dumont et al., 2018; Zabel et al., 2007; Peciu-Florianu et al., 2020; Blamek et al., 2011). After radiosurgery, progressive intimal thickening, thrombosis of blood vessels, and complete occlusion of the lumen require 2-5 years to occur (Dumont et al., 2018). The best diagnostic method to assess AVM is angiography, which in nature is more invasive than MRI-MRA. However, a study stated that when an AVM has been identified in T2 MRI sequence, the angiography shows similar result (Kano et al., 2012). Thus, angiography may not be necessary. In the collected studies, the diagnostic methods and timing were variable.

Limitation

Unlike SM grade I, the heterogeneous SM grade II component, especially deep venous drainage and eloquent areas, may affect the treatment outcome for both treatment methods. Microsurgery for medium-sized AVMs would have less surgical risk than for those with deep venous drainage or in the eloquent area. Thus, radiosurgery may benefit SM grade II AVMs located in deep or eloquent areas or with deep venous drainage. The analysis of SM grade II AVMs, including each component and its outcomes, is necessary to determine the best treatment option.

The endpoint of follow-up duration from radiosurgery to complete obliteration is yet to be defined. Studies have shown that a longer follow-up duration would result in a higher obliteration rate (Kano et al., 2012). Although follow-up is recommended for at least 2 years, studies have shown that a follow-up period of >2 years showed a better complete obliteration rate (Kano et al., 2012). Thus, a longer follow-up period is needed to compare radiosurgery and microsurgery. In this study, follow-up duration varies from 12 to 70.8 months. Overall, the heterogeneous data in this study limits the scope of our meta-analysis.

The terminology for morbidity in each study was unclear. Morbidity is characterized by the emergence of new neurological impairments and rupture incidents, though these are not consistently described across all studies. In addition, the number of patients treated at each center was heterogeneous, resulting in a wide range of complete obliteration rates. Some studies do not report the mRS score, either before or after treatment. The authors recommend that the mRS score of radiosurgery and microsurgery, before and after treatment, should be elaborated in future studies. Several additional limitations should be acknowledged explicitly. First, all included studies were observational; no randomised trial has directly compared microsurgery with radiosurgery for LGCAVMs, and the present meta-analytic estimates therefore cannot be interpreted as unbiased treatment effects. Second, treatment allocation in every series reflected center-level expertise and local referral patterns, introducing a center-level expertise bias for which we could not adjust. Third, imaging endpoints were inconsistent across studies: obliteration was confirmed by digital subtraction angiography in some cohorts and by MR angiography alone in others, and the timing of confirmatory imaging varied. Fourth, follow-up intervals were heterogeneous, which systematically favours microsurgery in cumulative comparisons because radiosurgery obliteration is delayed. Fifth, lesion-level anatomical detail (exact nidus size, eloquence mapping, precise draining-vein anatomy, associated aneurysms, prior embolization) was reported inconsistently, and we were therefore unable to perform patient-level adjustment for these determinants of both treatment choice and outcome. Sixth, as noted in the Methods, we could not perform a funnel plot, Egger's test, or planned sensitivity analyses by study design or by adult versus pediatric population, because only five studies provided head-to-head data and these tests are underpowered below that threshold. Seventh, the pooled morbidity-mortality endpoint is a composite and should not be read as an effect estimate for either outcome individually. Taken together, these limitations mean that the present findings should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and should inform the design of future prospective, registry-based, or randomised comparisons rather than be used to guide individual treatment decisions in isolation.

Conclusion

Microsurgery and radiosurgery have both been used to treat Spetzler-Martin (SM) grade I and II CAVMs or LGCAVMs. For LGCAVMs taken as a whole, microsurgery remains associated with a higher immediate complete obliteration rate and with shorter time to definitive angiographic cure, but with a higher pooled estimate of treatment-related morbidity than radiosurgery in the five head-to-head studies available for meta-analysis. Radiosurgery achieves a lower but clinically meaningful obliteration rate over a longer latency period (typically 2-5 years), with post-treatment haemorrhage concentrated in the first few years after treatment. On the basis of the present retrospective, non-randomized evidence, we therefore cannot conclude that radiosurgery is superior to microsurgery for LGCAVMs overall. For the distinct subgroup of selected SM grade II AVMs that are deep-seated, have deep venous drainage, or are located in eloquent regions, radiosurgery remains an important alternative to microsurgery when the anticipated surgical risk is high, and adjunctive scoring systems such as the modified RBAS may help identify patients in whom radiosurgery is likely to achieve favorable obliteration and functional outcomes. This subgroup-level observation is hypothesis-generating, not established by randomised evidence, and the generalised LGCAVM population and this selected high-surgical-risk SM grade II subgroup should not be conflated. Prospective, ideally randomised, comparative studies with standardised outcome definitions and prespecified anatomical stratification are needed before firmer treatment recommendations can be made.
