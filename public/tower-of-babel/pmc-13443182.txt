Engineering turbulence resilience in Bessel-Vortex beams through partial coherence and topological charge pairing
Authors: Jalil Jafari Dashkasan, Rasoul Aalipour, Mohammad Yeganeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEngineering optical resilience against atmospheric turbulence is essential for robust free-space photonic technologies. Here, we demonstrate that dual partially coherent Bessel-Vortex beams—with engineered topological charge pairing—exhibit unprecedented control over turbulence-induced scintillation beyond what is achievable with single beams or partial coherence alone. Using a spatial light modulator, we generate single and dual partially coherent Bessel-Vortex beams and propagate them through a laboratory turbulence chamber calibrated to Kolmogorov statistics () over a 0.15 m path, corresponding to a 1 km atmospheric propagation with equivalent turbulence strength . A key technical innovation of this work is the simultaneous encoding of both the Bessel-Vortex phase profile and the Kolmogorov-distributed random phase onto a single hologram displayed on the SLM—enabling real-time generation of partially coherent structured beams without additional optical components. We find that dual-beam configurations with co-signed topological charges show monotonically increasing resilience with charge difference, whereas counter-signed pairs display a pronounced non-monotonic response with minimal resistance at Δm = 8. Most strikingly, beams with equal-magnitude opposite charges (|m₁| = |m₂|) exhibit monotonic degradation in resilience up to order 14—a behavior absent in single-vortex systems. These results establish dual topological charge pairing as a previously unexplored design parameter for turbulence-resilient optical systems, with direct implications for free-space optical communication, quantum information transfer, and high-precision optical manipulation.Keywords: Atmospheric turbulence, Free-space optical communication, Partial coherence, Dual Bessel-Vortex beams, Scintillation indexSubject terms: Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

In free-space optical communications, astronomical imaging, optical lidar, and laser beam propagation through the atmosphere, the effects of atmospheric turbulence on optical beam propagation are of considerable concern1,2. These effects distort the beam profile and degrade the focusing quality of the beam. Furthermore, turbulence can disrupt the information transmitted by the beam. Atmospheric turbulence can be defined as random spatial and temporal fluctuations in the refractive index, caused by temperature variations. Such conditions cause the optical beam to undergo distortion, intensity fluctuations (scintillation), and lateral displacement, known as beam wander, during atmospheric propagation3–8. To correct wavefront distortions, adaptive optics is commonly employed9,10. This approach improves the performance of optical systems or mitigates wavefront aberrations. However, it requires highly complex and precise optical and electronic control systems, making it a costly solution. Various models have been developed to characterize the effects of atmospheric turbulence, including the Tatarski, von Kármán, and Greenwood models11,12. Among these, Kolmogorov’s cascade theory stands out as one of the most significant and widely applied frameworks in turbulence research. Among the various metrics used to quantify atmospheric turbulence strength, the Fried parameter stands as a pivotal and widely used indicator. The Fried parameter (denoted ), also referred to as the atmospheric coherence length, is a critical metric in optical propagation through turbulent media. It is defined as the diameter of a circular patch on a wavefront over which the root‑mean‑square (RMS) phase deviation induced by atmospheric turbulence does not exceed one radian. Within this coherence cell, the wavefront can be considered approximately planar, and phase variations remain predictable13,14.

Another important parameter for measuring the turbulence intensity is the scintillation index. It quantifies the intensity fluctuations of light caused by atmospheric turbulence, reflecting the temporal and spatial variations in signal strength due to refractive index irregularities in the atmosphere15. The scintillation index is typically used to assess the effects of turbulence on non‑diffracting beams, such as Laguerre–Gaussian or Bessel-Vortex beams. With increasing atmospheric turbulence, the scintillation index—and consequently the intensity fluctuations—become larger, leading to a reduction in the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

One approach to mitigating the effects of atmospheric turbulence on beam propagation is to use beams that are less susceptible to such turbulence. This method is more cost-effective and less complex than adaptive optics systems. Studies over recent decades have shown that partially coherent Gaussian beams are less sensitive to atmospheric turbulence than fully coherent Gaussian beams16–19. The role of spatial coherence in shaping optical field properties has also been demonstrated in diffractive optics. For instance, Farahiyan and Aalipour recently showed that tailoring spatial coherence can significantly enhance the depth of focus in sinusoidal linear Fresnel zone plates—a finding that underscores the broader utility of coherence engineering beyond turbulence mitigation20. Moreover, vortex beams, such as Bessel-Vortex beams, exhibit reduced susceptibility to turbulence compared to Gaussian beams during propagation through turbulent atmospheres21–28. Partially coherent light has been extensively studied as an effective means for mitigating turbulence-induced degradation owing to its intrinsic statistical averaging properties, with comprehensive theoretical foundations and applications summarized in recent reviews29. Building on this framework, partially coherent structured beams have been successfully employed in diverse areas such as robust optical sensing, turbulence-resistant image transmission, multidimensional optical encryption, and emerging optical computing architectures, where reduced sensitivity to phase fluctuations and scintillation plays a crucial role30–35. Moreover, recent studies have addressed turbulence-induced scintillation and its supprcoherence$0ession through coherence tailoring and phase-structure engineering of structured beams36,37. While these works primarily focus on optimizing spatial coherence or phase modulation independently, the present study introduces a distinct and complementary strategy by jointly engineering partial coherence and topological charge pairing in Dual Bessel–Vortex Beams (DBVB). This approach reveals how the relative sign and combination of topological charges provide an additional degree of freedom for controlling turbulence resilience, extending beyond what is achievable through partial coherence or structured phase design alone. This resilience was recently quantified for zero-order Bessel–Gaussian beams under dynamic Kolmogorov turbulence simulated via a rotating pseudo-random phase plate (PRPP); however, that study was limited to single-component beams and did not explore the interference-enhanced robustness achievable through dual-topological configurations—a strategy uniquely demonstrated in the present work15. Recent studies have demonstrated that dual vortex beams, generated by the coaxial superposition of two vortex beams with distinct topological charges, possess unique properties and offer significant potential for advanced research applications, such as propagation in turbulent atmospheres38–40.

Recent studies have demonstrated that, beyond tailoring spatial coherence alone, cross-phase engineering provides a powerful approach for enhancing the robustness of partially coherent structured beams against atmospheric turbulence. For instance, robust measurement of orbital angular momentum (OAM) in partially coherent vortex beams has been achieved even under strong amplitude and phase perturbations, highlighting the role of statistical averaging in suppressing turbulence-induced distortions41. Furthermore, cross-phase modulation has been employed to generate partially coherent persistent hollow beams, enabling the preservation of beam structure and energy distribution in turbulent environments42. In addition, single-shot, intensity-only techniques have been developed for measuring high-order OAM in partially coherent vortex beams, significantly reducing sensitivity to phase distortions and scintillation effects43. These works collectively demonstrate that phase-structure engineering and coherence control can jointly improve turbulence resilience. In contrast to these approaches, the present work explores a complementary and previously less-studied mechanism by investigating how topological charge pairing in Dual Partially Coherent Bessel-Vortex Beams modifies turbulence robustness beyond what is achievable through partial coherence or cross-phase engineering alone.

Recent studies by Lekshmi and Narayanamurthy have extended the understanding of turbulence resilience to partially coherent vortex beams, demonstrating that higher topological charge and reduced spatial coherence can suppress scintillation under dynamic Kolmogorov turbulence simulated via a rotating pseudo-random phase plate (PRPP)44. While their work established the advantage of single-component vortex beams in turbulence mitigation, it does not explore the interference-driven robustness of dual-beam configurations. In contrast, our approach leverages coaxially superimposed dual partially coherent Bessel-Vortex beams with independently tailored topological charges—a strategy that unlocks non-monotonic resilience profiles unattainable with single beams. Furthermore, our optical turbulence generator (OTG), based on convective mixing of hot and cold air, emulates a broadband Kolmogorov spectrum (up to 10 kHz), offering a more realistic representation of atmospheric dynamics compared to the spatially static phase statistics of PRPP-based simulators. These advances, alongside recent demonstrations of robustness in complex structured beams such as radial carpet modes45,46, highlight a growing consensus that turbulence resilience is governed not only by coherence and topology, but by the fine spatial design of the optical field—a principle directly exploited in our dual-topological engineering framework.

This paper experimentally investigates the effects of atmospheric turbulence on partially coherent Bessel-Vortex (PCBV) beams and dual Bessel-Vortex beams. To generate partially coherent beams, the Kolmogorov energy‑cascade theory was employed. In the experimental setup, PCBV beams are produced by illuminating a Gaussian laser beam onto a digital hologram. To simulate atmospheric turbulence in the laboratory, an optical turbulence generator (OTG) was used, operating on the principle of mixing hot and cold air and adhering to Kolmogorov’s energy-cascade theory38.

Experimental and simulation works

To simulate real-world turbulent atmospheric conditions in a laboratory environment, an OTG (featuring dimensions of 600 mm × 400 mm × 200 mm) was designed and constructed13. This OTG operates by combining hot and cold air flows, enabling the generation of controlled turbulence profiles for experimental studies. The active region of the OTG is 100 mm long, while the total beam propagation length inside the OTG is 400 mm. The laboratory-built OTG exhibits a statistical spectrum that aligns with the Kolmogorov turbulence model, characterized by the atmospheric coherence length.

The turbulence intensity can be controlled by adjusting parameters such as the temperature difference and fan speed. The cutoff frequency range of the OTG has been empirically determined to be approximately 10 kHz. The OTG was characterized using an experimental arrangement similar to the setup illustrated in Fig. 1. A He–Ne laser beam (wavelength 632 nm, power 2 mW) was first expanded by a lens L1 (f = 25 mm) and then collimated by a lens L2 (f = 100 mm) before being directed toward the OTG. The expanded beam (diameter 5 mm) then passed through the turbulence chamber integrated into the setup. A CCD camera (acA1300-60gmNIR, Basler) was positioned at the focal plane of lens L3 (f = 100 mm) to capture the intensity of the distorted field. To avoid saturation of the CCD sensor, a neutral density (ND) filter was used. The distance from L2 to OTG is 20 cm, and the distance from OTG to L3 is 15 cm. Image data were recorded continuously for 15 s at a frame rate of 160 fps for each applied temperature difference.

The observed scintillation suppression behaviors can be understood through the interplay of interference effects, phase singularity distribution, and coherence-function topology in dual partially coherent Bessel-Vortex beams.

For dual-beam configurations with co-signed topological charges (m₁, m₂ > 0 or m₁, m₂ < 0), constructive interference between the two vortex components enhances energy concentration in the central lobe. This leads to a more robust intensity profile that is less susceptible to turbulence-induced scintillation. As the charge difference |Δm| increases, the constructive overlap becomes more pronounced, resulting in monotonically increasing turbulence resilience. This behavior is consistent with the findings of Refs.28,29, where co-signed vortex pairs demonstrated enhanced stability in turbulent media.

In contrast, counter-signed topological charges (m₁ > 0, m₂ < 0 or vice versa) produce destructive interference that creates a characteristic cross-shaped intensity pattern with four intensity lobes. The minimum resilience observed at Δm = 8 corresponds to the configuration where phase singularity density is maximized, creating more vulnerable points for turbulence-induced perturbations. Beyond Δm = 8, the interference pattern stabilizes, leading to a recovery in resilience. This non-monotonic behavior has not been previously reported in the literature and represents a novel finding of this work.

Most strikingly, beams with equal-magnitude opposite charges (|m₁| = |m₂|) exhibit complete phase cancellation along the symmetry axis, creating a deep intensity null. As the charge magnitude increases, this null becomes more pronounced and extends radially. Consequently, this configuration displays monotonic degradation in turbulence resilience up to order 14—a behavior absent in single-vortex systems. This anomalous degradation can be attributed to the increasing spatial extent of the phase singularity, which enhances sensitivity to random phase perturbations introduced by atmospheric turbulence.

The partial coherence of the source further modifies these interference effects through statistical averaging. The coherence-function topology influences the stability of phase singularities under turbulence, with reduced spatial coherence providing additional robustness by decorrelating turbulence-induced phase fluctuations across the beam profile. This combined effect of topological charge pairing and partial coherence engineering offers a powerful approach for designing turbulence-resilient optical systems beyond what is achievable through either mechanism alone.

Conclusion

This work introduces two key contributions: first, a novel holographic method for simultaneously encoding Bessel-Vortex phase profiles and Kolmogorov-distributed random phases onto a single SLM display, enabling efficient generation of partially coherent structured light; and second, the discovery that topological charge pairing in dual-beam configurations provides an additional degree of freedom for turbulence mitigation beyond partial coherence alone.

Specifically, we demonstrate that turbulence resilience in structured light can be engineered by co-optimizing spatial partial coherence and topological charge pairing. While the turbulence-mitigating role of partial coherence has been well established in prior literature, the key finding of this work is that the relative sign and magnitude of topological charges in dual partially coherent Bessel-Vortex beams critically influence scintillation suppression. Dual-beam configurations with co-signed charges exhibit monotonically increasing resilience with charge difference, whereas counter-signed pairs display a non-monotonic response with a minimum at Δm = 8. Most strikingly, beams with equal-magnitude opposite charges (|m₁| = |m₂|) exhibit monotonic degradation in resilience up to order 14—a behavior absent in single-vortex systems.

These results establish that topological charge pairing serves as a powerful and previously unexplored design parameter for turbulence-resilient optical systems. The experimental framework presented here, based on a broadband optical turbulence generator and dynamic SLM-based beam shaping, offers a practical pathway for advancing free-space optical communication, quantum information protocols, and precision optical manipulation in turbulent environments.
