Single-cell integration analysis reveals molecular signatures of the tumor microenvironment in early-onset colorectal cancer
Authors: Ting Peng, Qimin Zhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer (CRC), defined as cases diagnosed in individuals under 50, is rising globally. However, its molecular and immune characteristics remain poorly understood. In this study, we analyze single-cell RNA sequencing data from 168 CRC patients, aged 22 to 91, to investigate differences between early-onset and standard-onset CRC. We find a reduced proportion of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, a higher burden of copy number variations, and decreased tumor-immune interactions in early-onset CRC. Additionally, immune signatures unique to early-onset CRC are associated with differential responses to immunotherapy, underscoring the need for tailored therapeutic strategies for this group of patients. These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular and immune landscape of early-onset CRC, emphasizing the importance of developing targeted prevention and treatment strategies. Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Cancer microenvironment

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most prevalent cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide1–3. In recent decades, there has been a concerning global increase in the incidence of early-onset CRC, defined as cases diagnosed in individuals under 504–7. Early-onset CRC now accounts for approximately 10% of all new CRC diagnoses, and it is projected that by the 2030s, one-third of all CRC cases will occur in individuals under 503. Although the precise etiology underlying the rising prevalence of early-onset CRC remains elusive, factors such as early-life exposures, diet, alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity are believed to contribute to this growing trend8–10.

The rising incidence of early-onset CRC has underscored the urgent need to establish effective therapeutic strategies for young adults diagnosed with this disease9,11,12. While survival rates for CRC have improved in older populations due to advances in early detection and treatment, early-onset CRC often presents with distinct biological characteristics that complicate diagnosis and treatment. Studies have shown that early-onset CRC is often diagnosed at more advanced tumor-node-metastasis stages, tends to exhibit poorer histological differentiation, and is more commonly located in the left colon compared to standard-onset CRC13–15. Despite these clinical differences, efforts to identify specific somatic mutations unique to early-onset CRC have yielded limited success13,16–18. Moreover, the molecular and immune characteristics of early-onset CRC remain inadequately understood, creating a critical gap in both basic research and clinical application.

Addressing this gap is essential for developing tailored prevention and treatment strategies for early-onset CRC. Recent advances in single-cell technologies, particularly single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), have revolutionized our ability to characterize the heterogeneity of tumor microenvironments and transcriptional profiles in CRC19–27. The accumulation of scRNA-seq datasets in CRC research over the past few years has facilitated the aggregation of data from populations that include early-onset patients, enabling comprehensive investigation into the transcriptional profiles and tumor microenvironment features of early-onset CRC.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of scRNA-seq data from 168 CRC patients aged 22–91. By comparing different age groups, we identified several key differences in early-onset CRC, including a reduction in the proportion of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells and a higher burden of copy number variations in tumor cells. Notably, we also observed a significant decline in tumor-immune interactions and immune activity in early-onset CRC, suggesting distinct immune evasion mechanisms. Moreover, we demonstrated that immune signatures specific to early-onset CRC are associated with differential responses to immunotherapy, indicating that early-onset CRC may require distinct therapeutic strategies from those used for standard-onset cancers.

Results

A single-cell atlas of CRC across different age groups

To decipher the landscape of the tumor microenvironment in early-onset CRC, we analyzed six publicly available scRNA-seq datasets comprising 168 tumor samples from CRC patients aged 22–9119–24 (Supplementary Data1, 2; Fig. 1A, B). The samples were categorized into four age categories: G1 (<40), G2 (40–60), G3 (60–80), and G4 (>80), with the G1 group, consisting of patients under 40, representing the early-onset CRC group (Fig. 1A, C, Figs. S1A, B). The threshold of 40 was chosen based on the increasing incidence and mortality of early-onset CRC, particularly among adolescents and young adults aged 15–3928–31.

A total of 560,238 cells were initially obtained, of which 554,930 cells passed quality control filtering and were used for further analysis. Harmony32 was employed to correct for batch effects and integrate the datasets. Unsupervised graph-based clustering identified nine major cell types based on established marker genes: B cells (CD79A), plasma cells (JCHAIN), endothelial cells (PECAM1), epithelial cells (EPCAM), myeloid cells (CD14), NK/T cells (CD3D), fibroblasts (DCN), pericytes (RGS5), and MKI67-positive (MKI67+) cells (MKI67) (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1C, E).

These nine cell types were present in nearly all samples. To examine potential age-related changes in cell composition, we performed a comparative analysis of each cell type. This analysis revealed significant differences in the abundance of plasma and myeloid cells (Fig. 1E). Specifically, we observed a decrease in the proportion of plasma cells and an increase in the proportion of myeloid cells with aging (Fig. 1F, G). This finding is consistent with previous studies that reported an obvious increase in the proportion of myeloid cells in the immune system with aging33–35, while the frequency of plasma cells in both bone marrow and peripheral blood decreases with age36,37. Additionally, deconvolution analysis of bulk RNA-seq data from colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) samples in the TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) database also demonstrated an increase in the proportion of myeloid cells with aging (Fig. 1H and Fig. S1F). These results demonstrate a decreased proportion of myeloid cells in the tumor microenvironment of early-onset CRC.

In summary, we have constructed a comprehensive scRNA-seq atlas of CRC across different age groups, providing a valuable resource for investigating the molecular characteristics of early-onset CRC.

Increased copy number burden in tumor cells of early-onset CRC

Next, we sought to investigate the genomic heterogeneity in CRC cells across different age groups. Chromosomal copy number variations (CNVs) in each cancer cell were identified using inferCNV38 (Fig. 2A and Fig. S2A). We observed that the absolute bias scores of copy number were highest in the G1 group and lowest in the G4 group, which was consistent across both microsatellite stability (MSS) and microsatellite instability (MSI) samples (Fig. 2B and Fig. S2B), indicating a higher CNV burden in early-onset CRC. To further validate this observation, we classified 425 COAD samples from the TCGA dataset into four groups based on age at initial pathologic diagnosis (Fig. S2C). Consistent with our findings, the G1 group exhibited higher absolute CNV scores (Fig. S2D).

We then examined the proportion of cells with copy number gains and losses for each gene exhibiting CNVs. In the G1 group, the majority of genes were either amplified or deleted in more than 10% of cancer cells, whereas in the other age groups, most genes showed a CNV proportion below 10% (Fig. 2C). Additionally, we ranked the 168 CRC samples based on the mean proportion of cells with CNVs and categorized them into high- and low-CNV burden groups (Fig. S2E). Over 50% of samples in the G1 group were classified as high-CNV burden, while less than 35% of samples in the other groups fell into the high-CNV burden category (Fig. 2D). This further confirms the higher CNV burden in early-onset CRC.

While the majority of genes with CNVs showed the highest proportion of cells with CNVs in the G1 group, we identified a subset of genes with the highest CNV proportion in the G4 group (Fig. S2F). These genes with copy number gains were located on chromosomes 1, 16, 19, and 21, while genes with copy number losses were predominantly found on chromosome 6 (Fig. 2E).

In conclusion, our analysis of CNVs in CRC across different age groups demonstrates that early-onset CRC is associated with a significantly higher CNV burden, underscoring the genomic heterogeneity that characterizes this group of patients.

Decreased tumor-immune cell interactions in early-onset CRC

To investigate the transcriptional heterogeneity within CRC cells, we extracted all epithelial cells and performed unsupervised clustering, which identified six distinct tumor subtypes (Fig. 3A). Five of these subtypes were defined by the expression of specific markers, including MYC, ADH1C, EMP1, MKI67, and MUC2 (Fig. S3A). Since no specific marker was identified for the sixth subtype, we labeled it as “Other epithelial cells” (Other_Epi). The proportions of these six subtypes were comparable across different age groups (Fig. S3B).

To explore the potential molecular drivers behind the distinct tumor subtypes, we employed single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC)39 to identify the transcriptional networks governing each subtype. As expected, each tumor subtype was associated with specific transcription factors (TFs), including MECOM and NFIB for ADH1C+_Epi; TEAD3 and BACH1 for EMP1+_Epi; BRCA1 and RAD21 for MKI67+_Epi; MYC and SOX4 for MYC+_Epi; SMAD4 and FOXA3 for MUC2+_Epi, TCF7 and ZNF362 for Other_Epi (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, the regulon activity of MYC was lowest in the G1 group, while the regulon activity of BRCA1 was highest in this group (Fig. 3C), suggesting differential TF activity within the tumor cells of early-onset CRC.

We next sought to evaluate the cell-cell communication levels between tumor subtypes and other cell types within the tumor ecosystem. Notably, the Other_Epi subset exhibited weaker communication strength both within the tumor cells and with other cell populations (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, distinct differences were observed in the communication strength between tumor subtypes and other cell types across age groups (Fig. S3C, D). Specifically, interactions between cancer cells and immune cells (including myeloid and NK/T cells) were weakest in the G1 group (Fig. 3E and Fig. S3E), suggesting reduced tumor-immune interactions in early-onset CRC.

To explore the potential molecular basis underlying the reduced tumor-immune interactions in the G1 group, we examined the expression of ligands in epithelial cells across age groups. We found that most ligands, such as CEACAM1, CEACAM5, and CD99, were downregulated in the G1 group (Fig. 3F and Fig. S3F). A similar analysis using COAD samples from the TCGA dataset also revealed a lower ligand expression in early-onset CRC patients, although the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. S3G). These results indicate that the downregulation of ligands in tumor cells is linked to the reduced tumor-immune interactions observed in early-onset CRC.

Immune cell heterogeneity of early-onset CRC

Since we observed that the interactions between tumor cells and immune cells (including myeloid and NK/T cells) were reduced in the G1 group, we started to explore the molecular characteristics of immune cells within this group. We separately extracted NK/T and myeloid cells and performed unsupervised clustering, which revealed 15 NK/T subsets and 11 myeloid subsets (Fig. 4A–D). The proportions of all NK/T subtypes were similar across different age groups, except for a decrease in the IL7R+_CD4 subset in the G4 group (Fig. 4E). However, the myeloid subsets exhibited substantial proportional changes across age groups (Fig. 4F). Specifically, the proportion of macrophage subsets increased with age, while the proportion of dendritic cells (DC) decreased with age (Fig. 4G).

Reduced immune activity in immune cells of early-onset CRC

Next, we conducted differential expression analysis to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the G1 group and the other age groups for both NK/T and myeloid subsets. We identified G1-upregulated (G1-up) and G1-downregulated (G1-down) DEGs within each immune subset (Fig. 5A and Fig. S4A). Interestingly, G1-down DEGs in both NK/T and myeloid subsets were enriched in pathways associated with immune functions, such as cell killing and myeloid leukocyte activation (Fig. 5B and Fig. S4B), indicating that immune activity in early-onset CRC is suppressed. Further analysis revealed that the expression levels of G1-down DEGs in immune cells increased with age, while the expression levels of G1-up DEGs decreased with age (Fig. 5C, Fig. S4C, D).

Additionally, we noticed that several T cell exhaustion markers, such as HAVCR2 and LAG3, were downregulated in the G1 group (Fig. 5B, D). We hypothesized that the downregulation of immune function-related genes might impact the efficacy of immunotherapy. To test this hypothesis, we compared the expression of G1-up and G1-down DEGs in NK/T and myeloid cells from response (R) and non-response (NR) CRC patients who received immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) treatment19,22,24. We found that the expression levels of G1-down DEGs were significantly higher in the response group, while the expression levels of G1-up DEGs were significantly higher in the non-response group (Fig. 5E and Fig. S4E), indicating a possible link between the reduced immune activity in early-onset CRC and diminished response to immunotherapy.

Collectively, these findings provide evidence for a reduction in immune activity in early-onset CRC, which could have significant implications for the effectiveness of immunotherapies in this patient group.

Discussion

The development of tailored treatment strategies for early-onset CRC requires a comprehensive understanding of its molecular features. In this study, we provide an in-depth analysis of scRNA-seq data from 168 CRC patients, aged 22–91. Our findings highlight several critical distinctions between early-onset and standard-onset CRC, shedding light on the molecular and immune landscape of these patient groups.

One of the key findings in our analysis is the reduced proportion of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in early-onset CRC. Myeloid cells play pivotal roles in the tumor microenvironment, influencing both immune response and tumor progression40,41. The diminished presence of these cells in early-onset CRC suggests that the immune landscape may differ fundamentally between early- and standard-onset CRC. This observation is consistent with previous reports that suggest age-related changes in the immune system33–35, which may contribute to altered tumor immune dynamics. However, further studies are necessary to elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying the reduced myeloid infiltration in early-onset CRC.

Our investigation into the genomic and transcriptional landscape of tumor cells revealed an increased burden of CNVs in early-onset CRC. This heightened genomic instability may contribute to the aggressive nature of early-onset CRC, which is often diagnosed at a more advanced stage42–44. CNVs have been associated with tumor heterogeneity and resistance to conventional therapies45,46, and their increased presence in early-onset patients may provide insights into the underlying genetic mechanisms driving the onset of CRC at a younger age.

Another notable finding from our study is the decline in tumor-immune interactions and immune activity in early-onset CRC. This reduction in immune activity may partially explain the distinct immune profile observed in these patients. The impairment of immune surveillance is a well-known factor in tumor progression47,48, and its heightened presence in early-onset CRC could contribute to the more aggressive clinical course observed in these individuals. These findings underscore the need for tailored therapeutic strategies that address the unique immune challenges posed by early-onset CRC.

A particularly clinically relevant insight from our study is the identification of immune signatures specific to early-onset CRC that are associated with differential responses to immunotherapy. While a subset of standard-onset CRC patients with high-level microsatellite instability (MSI-H) benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors, early-onset CRC appears to harbor a distinct immune landscape that could affect its responsiveness to such therapies. This suggests that early-onset CRC may require different immunotherapy strategies or more personalized treatment regimens. As immunotherapy continues to revolutionize cancer treatment, these findings emphasize the importance of considering age-related factors in the development of treatment protocols for CRC.

Our study provides novel insights into the molecular and immune characteristics of early-onset CRC. The reduced infiltration of myeloid cells, increased genomic instability, and altered immune landscape in early-onset CRC patients highlight the need for more targeted research to better understand the biological underpinnings of this disease. Furthermore, the distinct immune signatures identified in our study suggest that early-onset CRC may require tailored therapeutic strategies, particularly in the context of immunotherapy. Future studies should focus on validating these findings and exploring potential therapeutic approaches to improve outcomes for early-onset CRC patients.

Methods

Experimental model and study participant details

This study did not involve any new human participants, animal subjects, or experimental models. All datasets used in this study are publicly available and are listed in Supplementary Data 1. Samples obtained from each dataset are described in Supplementary Data 2. Details regarding data acquisition and processing are provided in the method details.

scRNA-seq analysis

The read count matrices were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus and Synapse databases, as listed in Supplementary Data 1, and were processed using the R package Seurat (version 4.1.1)49. Cells with low quality (defined as having fewer than 400 genes, fewer than 1000 unique molecular identifiers, or more than 30% of read counts corresponding to mitochondrial genes) were excluded. The count matrix was then log-normalized and scaled to a total of 10,000. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the top 2000 most variable genes. The top 30 principal components derived from the PCA were used to correct for batch effects between different samples using Harmony (version 1.1.0)32. Finally, we calculated a UMAP for data visualization with the top 30 dimensions resulted from PCA analysis and calculated clusters using the k-nearest neighbors method with a resolution parameter set to 0.3.

Evaluation and comparison of cell subset abundance

Samples containing more than 50 cells were included in the abundance comparison analysis. A two-sided Student’s t-test was used to compare the abundance of cell subsets between samples from different age groups.

Deconvolution analysis of TCGA dataset

Deconvolution analysis was performed on COAD samples from the TCGA dataset using the BayesPrism R package (version 2.2.2)50, following the instructions provided in the package vignette (https://github.com/Danko-Lab/BayesPrism). As recommended, we excluded ribosomal genes, mitochondrial genes, and genes located on the X and Y chromosomes to minimize noise and enhance computational efficiency. Deconvolution was carried out using protein-coding genes in the reference to reduce batch effects and further improve analysis speed.

Copy number analysis

Copy number analysis was conducted using the R package inferCNV (version 1.24.0)38 with default parameters. In brief, 2000 non-malignant cells, including B cells and T cells, were randomly selected as a normal reference. Then, combining the reference cells with epithelial cells for CNV inference and hierarchical clustering as previously described. The bias score of copy number is defined as the difference between the CNV score calculated by inferCNV and 1. To categorize samples based on CNV burden, we first calculated the mean bias score for each sample. The mean score across all samples was then used as a cutoff to classify the samples into high-CNV and low-CNV burden categories.

Transcription factor analysis

To identify the dominant transcription factors in different tumor subtypes, we employed the SCENIC package (version 1.3.1)39 in R. First, we inferred potential targets for each transcription factor based on co-expression patterns identified by GENIE351. Next, potential direct binding targets were selected through DNA motif analysis and compared with the reference motif database from RcisTarget (version 1.20.0). The regulon activity for each cell was assessed using the AUCell package (version 1.24.0).

Cell–cell communication analysis

To conduct a comprehensive analysis of interactions among each cell type, we utilized the CellChat package (version 2.1.1)52 to perform cell-cell communication analysis. The ligand and receptor genes expressed by each cell type were mapped to a manually curated reference communication network, and the communication probability for each pathway was inferred based on gene expression. We then performed statistical analysis to evaluate communication probabilities between tumor subtypes and other non-tumor cells, as well as communication between different tumor subtypes.

Differential expression and enrichment analyses

Differential expression analysis was performed using the “FindMarkers” function in Seurat, applying Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to genes expressed in more than 25% of the cells in at least one of the compared groups. Genes with a p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2(fold change) >0.25 were considered differentially expressed. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.6.0)53 to identify overrepresented Gene Ontology (GO) terms. Pathways with a p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The quantitative and statistical analyses are described thoroughly in the relevant sections of the method details. Explicit p-values and sample sizes are embedded in the figures or figure legends, and are described in the main text and within the supplementary files.

Visualization

Most figures were generated using the R packages Seurat (version 4.1.1), ggplot2 (version 3.5.0)54, and ggpubr (version 0.4.0). Heatmaps were created using the R package ComplexHeatmap (version 2.14.0)55. All box plots display the top and bottom quartiles, with horizontal lines representing the median. The whiskers indicate the data range within 1.5 times the interquartile range above the third quartile and below the first quartile.
