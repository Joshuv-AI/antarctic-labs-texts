Factors influencing the time to COVID-19 antigen tests negative conversion among students at a Chinese university: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Wenjin Liu, You Jin, Yongyan Wu, Zhenni Xiao, Yan Xu, Zhaohong Du, Jianmei Xiao, Zhipeng Yan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) can cause repeated infections. China experienced an outbreak of COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019) after the Chinese government changed its COVID-19 policy in December 2022. However, little is known about the influencing factors, such as a history of previous infection and vaccine dose, and whether these factors affect the time at which the antigen test results become negative for SARS-CoV-2. Boarding colleges and universities are prone to outbreaks caused by COVID-19. Therefore, we investigated the factors influencing the time of COVID-19 antigen tests conversion at one university from April to June 2023.MethodsThis study included college students from one university in Guangzhou who tested positive for the COVID-19 antigen and collected information such as sex, history of previous COVID-19 infection, vaccination dose, symptom onset date, and antigen tests conversion date for retrospective analysis. Chi-square tests or t-tests were used to compare differences between groups.ResultsA total of 255 college students were included in this study. In addition to fever, the most common symptoms were sore throat, nasal symptoms and fatigue. The average time to antigen tests negative conversion of patients with their first infection was 6.12 ± 1.83 days, and that of patients with their second infection was 4.70 ± 1.43 days. The difference between the groups was statistically significant (P < 0.001). The average time to antigen tests negative conversion was 6.21 ± 1.92 days in patients with more than 3 symptoms except fever, which was significantly greater than that in patients with 0–1 (5.54 ± 1.79 days) or 2–3 symptoms (5.45 ± 1.78 days) (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in time to antigen tests negative conversion according to sex, health observation location or vaccination dose (P > 0.05).ConclusionIn college students, a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection as well as the number of symptoms were significantly associated with the time to COVID-19 antigen tests conversion.Keywords: COVID-19, Symptom, Time to antigen tests conversion, University student, Retrospective study

Background

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection spread throughout almost all parts of the world for more than four years, causing hundreds of millions of infections and many reinfections due to the mutation of SARS-CoV-2, especially the Omicron variant, for which previous infection and vaccination were not protective [1]. During the Omicron period, nearly 60% of infected individuals experienced additional infection within 8 months after their last antigen exposure [1].

Following the initial outbreak of COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019), the Chinese government implemented strict control measures and a dynamic zero-COVID policy beginning in December 2021, aiming to prevent imported cases from abroad as well as sporadic outbreaks at home. When a confirmed COVID-19 case was identified, a complete blockade of the building and community was quickly enforced [2]. Other measures included medical isolation of infected patients as well as their close contacts, regular nucleic acid screening, electronic health certificates for access to public places and public transport, and quarantine for several days for individuals entering the country [3]. However, owing to the high transmissibility and low pathogenicity of the Omicron variant, the Chinese government announced on 7 December 2022 that this strict policy would no longer be implemented, the quarantine policy would be relaxed and mandatory COVID-19 nucleic acid testing would be stopped [4]. During the next two months, i.e., December 2022 and January 2023, the Omicron BF.7 strain spread rapidly in China. For example, in Beijing, the cumulative infection rate reached 92.3% by January 31, 2023 [5]. Like in many other countries around the world, in China, another wave of SARS-CoV-2 infections peaked in April 2023, during which time as many as 860 million people were estimated to be infected with the Omicron XBB subvariant, accounting for 61% of China's total population [6]. This occurred because immune protection from previous infections or vaccinations wanes significantly within a few months [7].

College and university students are more susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection. University students living in residence halls have nearly 3 times greater odds of testing seropositive for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies than university students not living in dormitories [8], and staying in the same room with an infected individual increases the risk of infection [9]. Additionally, crowded classrooms can also favour the spread of the virus [10]. Unlike in European countries or the United States, the vast majority of university students in China live in on-campus dormitories rather than in off-campus dormitories. In addition, most of the dormitory rooms in Chinese universities are occupied by four or more students. As a result, Chinese university students are more susceptible to the spread of SARS-CoV-2. Therefore, in the technical plan for the prevention and control of SARS-CoV-2 infection in universities, secondary and primary schools, and childcare institutions jointly issued by the Ministry of Education and National Health Commission of China, it was proposed that students with mild SARS-CoV-2 infection living on campuses be cared for and treated at college and university health stations or at home [11] and should not live in the dormitory until the COVID-19 antigen test result was negative.

During the outbreak of SARS-CoV-2 in China from April to June 2023, an increasing number of people were infected with the virus, and a significant number of them were infected for the second time. However, it is not entirely clear how several factors, such as previous SARS-CoV-2 infection, affect the recovery time of patients with COVID-19. Previous studies in the United States have shown that the duration of the second infection is shorter than that of the first infection [12], but there are no such studies in China. Here, we retrospectively analysed the factors influencing the time of antigen tests conversion in college students with SARS-CoV-2 infection at one university to help with the management of COVID-19.

Methods

A retrospective analysis was performed on students who were febrile and tested positive for the COVID-19 antigen in the outpatient department of a university in Guangzhou, a city located in southern China, from April to June 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria of the “Diagnosis and Treatment Plan for COVID-19 (the Tenth Trial Edition)” [13] issued by the National Health Commission and the State Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine. (2) Data collection is complete. Patients who are not students, as well as patients with incomplete information, will be excluded. This study was reviewed by the Science Ethics Committee of Guangzhou University (Guangda Scientific Ethics [2023] No. 100). Informed consent from participants was waived by the Science Ethics Committee of Guangzhou University because of the retrospective nature and anonymous information of the study.

Information, including sex, date of onset and antigen test negative conversion, symptoms, previous vaccination doses, health observation location (school health station or home health observation), and history of SARS-CoV-2 infection, was collected. Antigen samples and tests were conducted by trained doctors or nurses for students with fever when they first visited the doctor. Students who tested positive for antigens needed to stay at the school health station or go home for health observation. Antigen-positive students who stay at health stations were tested daily by doctors or nurses after their body temperature is normal, and students who were under home health observation would be tested for antigen sampling and testing under the online guidance of medical staff. If the antigen tests were negative for two consecutive days and the interval between the two tests was more than 24 h, it was allowed to be released from health observation. The time of the patient's antigen tests negative conversion was calculated by subtracting the date of symptom onset from the date of the first negative antigen test conversion. The COVID-19 antigen detection kits used in this study, which detect nucleocapsid antigens, were produced by Guangzhou Wondfo Biotech Co., Ltd., with a colloidal gold test [14].

SPSS 20.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Numerical data are expressed as n (%), and the chi-square test was used to compare differences between groups. The normally distributed data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (x ± s), and a t-test was used to compare the differences between groups.

Results

Subject characteristics

A total of 276 febrile patients with positive COVID-19 antigen test results were eligible in the study, and 21 patients were excluded, of whom 2 were not students and 19 provided incomplete information (Fig. 1). Overall, 255 students were included, with an average age of 20.89 ± 1.93 years, including 126 males (49.4%) and 129 females (50.6%). A total of 197 students (77.3%) were observed at school health stations, and 58 students (22.7%) were observed at home. A total of 169 (66.3%) patients were infected with SARS-CoV-2 for the first time, whereas the other 86 (33.7%) were infected with the virus for the second time. Among these students, 4 (1.6%) received one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine, 37 (14.5%) received two doses, 199 patients (78.0%) received three doses, and 15 patients (5.9%) received four doses. Symptom frequency

Among these 255 patients, in addition to fever, sore throat (137/255, 53.7%) was the most common symptom, followed by nasal symptoms, such as runny nose or nasal congestion (120/255, 47.1%), and fatigue (102/255, 40.0%). In the group infected for the first time, the top three symptoms were consistent with those of the overall population: sore throat (99/169, 58.6%), nasal symptoms (72/169, 42.6%), and fatigue (71/169, 42.0%). Among the groups with secondary infection, the four most common symptoms were nasal symptoms (48/86, 55.8%), sore throat (38/86, 44.2%), fatigue (31/86, 36.0%) and cough (31/86, 36.0%). The main symptoms in these patients were respiratory symptoms such as sore throat, nasal symptoms such as runny nose or nasal congestion, and cough, as well as systemic symptoms such as fatigue and body aches, while the proportions of patients with gastrointestinal symptoms, hyposmia, eye symptoms, and rash were low (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Factors influencing antigen tests conversion time

The antigen tests conversion time of all the antigen-positive students was 5.64 ± 1.83 days. The presence or absence of a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection and the number of symptoms were factors influencing the time to antigen tests conversion. The average time to antigen tests conversion was 6.12 ± 1.83 days for the students who were infected with SARS-CoV-2 for the first time and 4.70 ± 1.43 days for the patients who were infected for the second time, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.001). In patients with more than 3 symptoms except fever, the average time to antigen tests conversion was 6.21 ± 1.92 days, which was significantly greater than that in patients with 0–1 or 2–3 symptoms (P = 0.038). There was no statistically significant difference in the effects of sex, health observation location or the number of vaccination doses on the time of antigen tests conversion (P > 0.05). Discussion

The WHO (World Health Organization) declared COVID-19 a “public health emergency” in January 2020 and subsequently declared it a global pandemic in March 2020 [15]. Although many people have already been infected with the virus and have been vaccinated, there are still individual repeat infections and waves of infections in some countries or regions due to continuous mutation of the virus and a decrease in antibody titres over time [7]. SARS-CoV-2 can spread rapidly on college campuses, especially when new variants emerge, and outbreaks on college campuses can spread rapidly to the surrounding community [16]. Unlike universities in Western countries, the vast majority of students in Chinese universities live on campus rather than off campus, and the density of dormitories in Chinese universities is relatively high. As a result, the spread of COVID-19 on Chinese university campuses is likely to affect many people. Therefore, it is necessary to understand the characteristics of COVID-19 patients on Chinese campuses.

Our study revealed that, in addition to fever, sore throat, nasal symptoms, fatigue, and cough were the most common symptoms in patients infected with the Omicron variant of SARS-CoV-2. The results of a systematic review of 31 studies revealed that the main symptoms of the Omicron variant were high fever, cough, conjunctivitis, sore throat, nasal symptoms, fatigue, body aches and so on [17]. Similarly, a cross-sectional study with 64,515 patients conducted in China revealed that the main symptoms caused by the Omicron variant were upper respiratory symptoms, such as throat discomfort, hoarseness, and nasal congestion. In addition, other systemic symptoms, including fever, headache and fatigue, were also common [18]. After the Omicron variant became the dominant strain, the proportion of people with hyposmia decreased significantly compared with that before [19]. Taken together, the symptoms caused by the Omicron variant are mainly upper respiratory symptoms and systemic symptoms. We found no association between the number of symptoms and factors such as the number of vaccination doses. A previous study suggested that patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 after vaccination have significantly more severe symptoms than those infected who have not been vaccinated [20] and that patients who have received a booster vaccine have a higher frequency of upper respiratory tract symptoms during Omicron waves [21]. This may be because vaccinated people can quickly develop antibodies in the early stages of the disease and maintain an immune response for a longer period of time after a breakthrough infection [20]. Overall, vaccination does not reduce the severity of COVID-19.

In this study, we found that the number of SARS-CoV-2 infections was significantly associated with the COVID-19 antigen tests conversion time, whereas sex, health observation location and the number of vaccination doses had no statistically significant effect on the conversion time. One study in the United States reported that the average nucleic acid conversion time was 7.2 (6.8, 7.5) days for the first infection and 4.9 (4.5, 5.3) days for the second infection [12]. These two durations are longer than those reported in this study, which may be caused by the lower sensitivity of the antigen tests taken by the patients in this study, which might not have the power to diagnose SARS-CoV-2 infection in the very early and late phases [22]. The viral load in patients infected for the first time is also significantly greater than that in those infected for the second time [12]. At present, there is a lack of relevant studies on the number of infections and the time of antigen tests conversion in China.

We found that the number of symptoms also affected the time to antigen tests negativity. There is no consensus on whether clinical symptoms are associated with the timing of viral clearance. A Korean study of 89 COVID-19 patients revealed that asymptomatic COVID-19 patients experienced a significantly faster decline in viral load than symptomatic patients did [23]. A possible cause is that symptomatic individuals have significantly higher viral loads than asymptomatic individuals do [24]. Clinical studies of COVID-19 drugs suggested that the time to clinical improvement in the low-viral-load group was significantly shorter than that in the high-viral-load group [25]. However, some studies took a different view: the presence or absence of symptoms was not related to the time to viral clearance [26]. More research may be needed to clarify the relationship between symptoms and viral loads and the time to negative conversion.

We did not observe associations between the number of vaccine doses and antigen tests conversion time. Some studies have consistently concluded that vaccinated people have a significantly shorter time to virus clearance after infection with the Omicron variant than unvaccinated people do [20, 27]. However, in vaccinated populations, an increasing number of doses does not appear to shorten the time to viral clearance. One study suggested that booster vaccination led to lower antibody titres and longer clearance times for BA.1-infected patients. Individuals who receive booster vaccinations may have a less effective immune response, which increases infection risk and reduces the viral RNA clearance rate [28]. The results of this study revealed that there was no significant association between the number of COVID-19 vaccine doses and the time to obtain negative COVID-19 antigen test results.

This study has certain limitations. First, we included a group of college students aged 17–30 years. More research is needed in other age groups. Second, owing to the conditions of the university hospital, we are unable to perform viral titre tests for patients. Finally, we did not perform dynamic follow-up for changes in clinical symptoms. However, this study revealed the characteristics and factors influencing the antigen tests conversion time of SARS-CoV-2 infection in Chinese college students, which may have an impact on isolation policies, testing recommendations and clinical guidelines.

Conclusion

This study revealed that, in college students, a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection as well as the number of symptoms were significantly associated with the COVID-19 antigen tests conversion time.
