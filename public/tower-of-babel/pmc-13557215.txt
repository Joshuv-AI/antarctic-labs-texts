Affective Predictions in Chronic Pain: Differential Associations With Pain Interference and Intensity
Authors: Tal Ivancovsky, Salvatore Cajiao, Juan R Castaño‐Asins, Antonio Montes‐Pérez, Eva Cortés‐Velasco, Juan V Luciano, Lorena Chanes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundExpectations have an important impact on emotion‐pain interactions. Previous studies have primarily focused on the influence of pain‐related expectations on perceived pain intensity. However, expectations regarding negative‐valence information more broadly may impact perception beyond pain and be altered when pain becomes chronic. The current study aimed to investigate whether patients with chronic pain exhibit alterations in social–emotional expectations beyond pain perception.MethodsThirty‐five adults with chronic pain, recruited at the Pain Unit of Hospital del Mar in Barcelona (three excluded prior to analysis; final n = 32) and 35 age‐ and gender‐matched healthy controls performed a social perception task designed to elicit expectations about facial expressions and assess their impact on social evaluation. Facial expressions were either stereotypically congruent with the evoked emotion (matched trials) or stereotypically associated with a different emotion category (non‐matched trials). Expectations (predictability ratings) and social evaluation (likability ratings) were measured.ResultsSelective alterations were observed for pain interference and intensity. Pain interference was associated with reduced differentiation of predictability ratings between matched and non‐matched trials, suggesting less stereotypical emotion expectations. In contrast, pain intensity (and chronic pain condition itself) was associated with a stronger relationship between predictability and likability ratings, suggesting a greater reliance on predictability during social evaluation.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that chronic pain may be associated with alterations in social–emotional expectations and in their relationship with social evaluation, potentially influencing not only pain perception but also affective and interpersonal functioning.Significance StatementThis study suggests that chronic pain is associated with alterations in social–emotional expectations beyond nociception. Individuals with chronic pain showed a stronger relationship between predictability and likability ratings. In contrast, higher pain interference was associated with smaller differences in predictability ratings between stereotypically matched and non‐matched emotional cues. These associations may help illuminate cognitive–emotional factors linked to the emotional and interpersonal difficulties commonly observed in chronic pain.Keywords: chronic pain, emotional processing, facial expressions, predictive processing, social evaluation

Introduction

Pain is a complex, multidimensional experience involving dynamic interactions between sensory, cognitive, and emotional processes (Bushnell et al. 2013; Ploner et al. 2017). Previous studies suggest that expectations modulate pain perception, with anticipated pain intensity influencing both subjective experience and neural responses (Atlas and Wager 2014; Freeman et al. 2015; Lui et al. 2010).

Predictive processing theories increasingly conceptualize pain as a form of perceptual inference, wherein prior expectations shape the interpretation of incoming sensory input at multiple hierarchical levels, ranging from low‐level sensory predictions to higher‐level, context‐dependent expectations (Büchel et al. 2014; Geuter et al. 2017; Wiech 2016). According to this framework, the brain integrates top‐down predictions with bottom‐up nociceptive signals to minimize prediction error and generate a coherent perception (Arnal and Giraud 2012; Ploner et al. 2017; Song et al. 2021). In chronic pain, however, the predictive system may become maladaptive, relying on outdated priors while failing to incorporate new sensory input (Castejón et al. 2024; Eckert et al. 2022; Hechler et al. 2016; Moseley and Vlaeyen 2015), contributing to the persistence of pain and broader interference in cognitive and affective functioning (Barrett 2017; Karoly 2021; Paulus et al. 2019).

Previous studies have focused on how pain‐related expectations impact pain perception (Atlas and Wager 2014; Freeman et al. 2015; Lui et al. 2010). However, negative expectations more generally may exert similar effects extending beyond pain (Amaro‐Díaz et al. 2022; Wieser et al. 2014). Chronic pain is associated with increased threat perception, negative affect, and social withdrawal (Baliki and Apkarian 2015; Karayannis et al. 2019; Poublan‐Couzardot and Talmi 2024). Furthermore, chronic pain is often characterized by a sustained allostatic load, wherein the brain allocates disproportionate resources to managing the chronic pain state, potentially leaving fewer available resources for executive and social processing (Kiverstein et al. 2022; Lunde and Sieberg 2020; McEwen and Wingfield 2010). However, whether predictive processing alterations extend beyond pain perception to emotionally salient social stimuli remains unexplored.

Moreover, distinctions between pain intensity, i.e., the subjective magnitude of pain, and pain interference, i.e., the impact of pain on daily functioning, in this context, have been overlooked. Importantly, pain interference is more closely linked to psychological distress and cognitive biases than pain intensity (Castaño‐Asins et al. 2023; Hearn et al. 2025; Jensen et al. 2016; Sanabria‐Mazo et al. 2023; Thong et al. 2018), indicating that these dimensions may have partially differential underlying mechanisms. To address this research gap, the present study aimed to investigate whether and how context‐based expectations may differ in the social–emotional domain in patients with chronic pain, from a predictive processing perspective. We employed a validated paradigm (Chanes et al. 2018; Draganov et al. 2023; Ramos‐Grille et al. 2022; Robles et al. 2024) in which participants read short emotion‐evoking scenarios followed by facial expressions that either matched or mismatched the emotion evoked by the scenario. Participants rated the predictability of each facial expression (i.e., the perceived contrast between anticipated and observed facial expressions) as well as its likability. We assessed pain intensity and interference using standardized questionnaires to explore their associations with participants' performance. We hypothesized that, relative to healthy controls, patients with chronic pain would show smaller differences in predictability ratings between matched and non‐matched emotional cues, and a stronger reliance on predictability during social evaluation.

Methods

Ethics Statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital del Mar [2023/11045/I] and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Thirty‐five adults diagnosed with chronic pain (more specifically, chronic back pain), who were outpatients at the Pain Unit of Hospital del Mar, a major public hospital in Barcelona, participated in the study. Inclusion criteria were as follows: diagnosis of chronic back pain; aged ≥ 18 years; native Spanish speaker (or bilingual Catalan/Spanish); no dementia or intellectual disability; and no severe psychiatric comorbidity in acute state. The group consisted of 19 females and 16 males aged 49.7 ± 9.6 (mean ± SD). A control group (n = 35) was also recruited, involving overall healthy individuals, with the following inclusion criteria: report of no current or history of mental or serious physical disease; aged ≥ 18 years; native Spanish speaker (or bilingual Catalan/Spanish). Control individuals were recruited from the general population to match the patients on gender and age (±5 years). Selection of controls was based solely on those criteria, and any candidate meeting the inclusion criteria was not excluded based on any other variable. The group consisted of 19 females and 16 males aged 50.1 ± 9.6 (mean ± SD). Prior to data analysis, three participants diagnosed with chronic pain were excluded from the sample: one participant did not comply with task instructions and two more were excluded due to tiredness and inability to complete the scales, resulting in 32 participants in the final sample (n = 32). Thus, the group of participants with chronic pain consisted of 18 females and 14 males aged 49.7 ± 9.4 (mean ± SD). This resulted in a final analysed sample of 32 participants with chronic pain and 35 controls (total N = 67).

Materials and Procedure

The session was performed at the Pain Unit of Hospital del Mar. Upon arrival, participants were provided with an information sheet and a consent form, which was completed and signed. All participants received a study code, under which study data was collected. General information was obtained from all participants, which included gender, age, and handedness, as well as information regarding sleep quality prior to the assessment and vision (normal or corrected‐to‐normal). Then, participants were asked to perform a predictive processing and social perception task on a computer. The task's approximate duration was ~45 min. Afterwards, participants were asked to fill out a series of self‐reported questionnaires (the duration was between 30 and 45 min) including Brief Pain Inventory (BPI; Daut et al. 1983) and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS; Huskisson 1974). Additional questionnaires were collected as part of a broader assessment battery but were not analysed in the current study because they were not directly related to the hypotheses tested. Participants completed a computerized version of all scales using Qualtrics XM. The patients' diagnoses and age at the time of diagnosis were collected from their medical records.

Predictive processing and social perception task (Chanes et al. 2018). Participants completed 3 practice trials followed by 45 experimental trials on a computer. Instructions and stimuli were presented using E‐Prime 3 (Psychology Software Tools Inc., Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Every trial (Figure 1) started with a fixation screen (4 s) and was followed by a photograph of a neutral facial expression of a target person (Face 1; 5 s). Next, a written short story (scenario) about the target person was displayed for 20 s. The story was designed to evoke one of three emotion categories: fear, sadness, or happiness. The 45 experimental trials included 15 scenarios for each of the three emotions (i.e., 15 fear scenarios, 15 happiness scenarios, and 15 sadness scenarios). Participants were asked to imagine how the target person would look in that scenario while reading. A photograph of the target person was then presented (Face 2; 5 s) exhibiting one of three possible stereotypical facial expressions (happy, fearful, or sad). Therefore, the displayed facial expression could either ‘match’ the evoked emotion (e.g., sadness scenario followed by a stereotypical expression of sadness; matched condition: 9 trials per emotion category) or ‘not match’ the evoked emotion (e.g., sadness scenario followed by a stereotypical expression of fear or happiness; non‐matched condition: 6 trials per emotion category). The order of the trials, pairing of target identities with scenarios, and which scenarios were matched or non‐matched were all pseudorandomized within each individual. Participants were asked to rate how similar the target person looked compared to what they had imagined on a scale from 1 = ‘not at all similar’ to 4 = ‘very similar’. Then, they rated how likable the person was on scale from 1 = ‘very unlikable’ to 4 = ‘very likable’. Colour photographs with a resolution of 400 × 600 pixels from the Interdisciplinary Affective Science Laboratory were used. 1 For each of the 48 trials a different target person (identity) was displayed (3 practice trials: 2 females, 1 male; 45 experimental trials: 28 females, 17 males).

Sequence of screens on a sample experimental trial. After a fixation screen (4 s), a neutral facial expression was presented (5 s). Then, a scenario (short text) describing a situation experienced by the target person was displayed, evoking either fear, happiness, or sadness (20 s). Next, another picture of the target person was displayed (5 s), this time exhibiting a stereotypical facial expression for fear, happiness, or sadness. In 60% of the trials, the facial expression matched the emotion evoked by the scenario (matched trials), and in 40% of the trials it did not match (e.g., stereotypical sad facial expression after a happy scenario; non‐matched trials). Afterwards, two self‐reported rating scales (predictability and likability) were displayed sequentially.

The scenarios (used in Ramos‐Grille et al. 2022) sampled from the four quadrants of the affective circumplex. Positive valence was represented by scenarios evoking happiness, while negative valence was represented by scenarios evoking sadness and fear. All emotion categories (happiness, fear, sadness) included scenarios with high and low arousal.

Brief Pain Inventory (BPI; Daut et al. 1983; Spanish version used: Badia et al. 2003). This inventory assesses pain severity and its impact on daily functioning. It consists of two subscales: Pain Severity Subscale (BPI‐PS, 4 items), measuring pain intensity, and Pain Interference Subscale (BPI‐IS, 7 items), measuring how pain interferes in various life domains such as general activity, mood, and sleep. Each item is rated on a 0–10 Likert scale (0 = ‘no pain’/‘does not interfere’ to 10 = ‘Pain as bad as you can imagine’/‘Completely interferes’).

Visual Analogue Scale (VAS; Huskisson 1974; Spanish version used: Escalona‐Marfil et al. 2020). This tool assesses subjective pain intensity. It consists of a 10‐cm horizontal line, ranging from ‘No pain’ (0 cm) to ‘Worst imaginable pain’ (10 cm). Participants are asked to indicate their current pain level by marking a point along the line.

Data Analysis

Non‐aggregated data from individual trials were analysed using hierarchical linear modelling (HLM 7.0; Scientific Software International Inc., IL, USA), to avoid aggregation across trials in the social perception task. All hierarchical models were estimated on the full experimental dataset, comprising 3015 experimental trials from 67 participants (45 trials per participant); practice trials were used solely for task familiarization and were excluded a priori, resulting in no trial‐level exclusions due to missingness. A continuous sampling model with participants treated as a random factor, and a restricted maximum likelihood method of estimation for model parameters (Raudenbush and Bryk 2002) was used. Continuous trial‐level predictors (e.g., predictability ratings) were person‐mean centred. In contrast, subject‐level variables (e.g., pain intensity and pain interference scores), which do not vary across trials within participants, were grand‐mean centred. Dummy‐coded categorical predictors (e.g., match condition) were entered uncentred, following standard recommendations (Enders and Tofighi 2007). The outcome variables (i.e., predictability and likability ratings) were treated as continuous to avoid data aggregation (Meteyard and Davies 2020). All models included random intercepts to account for between‐participant differences in baseline responding. Random slopes for trial‐level predictors were omitted in cases where they led to non‐convergence (after more than 99 iterations), while random intercepts were retained (Barr et al. 2013; Gelman and Hill 2007). The final random‐effects structure for each model is reported explicitly. Effect sizes are reported as unstandardized regression coefficients (B) with 95% confidence intervals for all main effects and interactions. For significant interactions involving continuous moderators, conditional (simple‐slope) estimates were computed by evaluating the focal predictor at clinically interpretable values, based on commonly used interpretive anchors for 0–10 pain scales (e.g., Serlin et al. 1995). Conditional estimates and simple slopes were computed in R 4.5.2 (R Core Team 2025) and estimated using the emmeans package (Lenth et al. 2026). For the significant interactions, bootstrap confidence intervals were obtained using the lmeresampler package (Loy et al. 2023) to provide more robust confidence interval estimation. Additional statistical analyses were carried out using JASP (JASP Team 2022; version 0.16.2). For comparisons across control and CP groups, we used t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U‐tests when normality assumptions were not met. ggplot2 (Wickham 2016), lme4 (Bates et al. 2015), and ggeffects (Lüdecke 2018) packages were used for data visualization. For clarity, model‐based interaction plots are presented in the main figures with participant‐level aggregated summaries, and pain measures distributions are provided in the Supporting Informations. Hypotheses and analysis plan were not pre‐registered. Data and analyses files may be accessed here: https://osf.io/5uns3/.

Results

Self‐Reported Pain Measures

As expected, we observed significant group differences across all pain‐related measures (BPI‐PS: Mann–Whitney two‐tailed U‐test, U = 18, p < 0.001, rB = −0.97, 95% CI [−0.98, −0.94]; BPI‐IS: Mann–Whitney two‐tailed U‐test, U = 52, p < 0.001, rB = −0.91, 95% CI [−0.95, −0.84]; VAS: Mann–Whitney two‐tailed U‐test, U = 26.5, p < 0.001, rB = −0.95, 95% CI [−0.97, −0.92]), with the chronic pain group reporting greater perceived pain and greater pain interference than the control group (Table 1).

The Impact of Pain on the Predictability of Facial Expressions

We conducted an HLM analysis with trial‐level predictability ratings as the outcome variable, match condition (matched and non‐matched trials) as a dummy‐coded trial‐level predictor variable, and group (chronic pain vs. control) as a dummy‐coded subject‐level predictor variable, with the two‐way interaction term included. As expected based on previous studies (Chanes et al. 2018; Draganov et al. 2023; Ramos‐Grille et al. 2022; Robles et al. 2024), matched facial expressions (i.e., stereotypical facial expressions for the evoked emotion) were rated as significantly more predictable than non‐matched facial expressions (mean ± SE: controls: matched = 2.96 ± 0.08, non‐matched = 1.57 ± 0.05; chronic pain: matched = 2.93 ± 0.06, non‐matched = 1.61 ± 0.04) in the control group (B = 1.39, SE = 0.05, t(65) = 25.63, p < 0.001, 95% CI [1.28, 1.50]) and among patients with chronic pain (B = 1.32, SE = 0.05, t(65) = 27.44, p < 0.001, 95% CI [1.23, 1.42]), consistent with a match effect. Patients with chronic pain did not differ significantly from the control group (B = −0.14, SE = 0.10, t(65) = −1.40, p = 0.165, 95% CI [−0.33, 0.06]), indicating a comparable match effect.

To examine whether continuous measures of pain experience were associated with task performance across participants, we next conducted secondary analyses using continuous measures of pain intensity and pain interference across all participants (n = 67) rather than group as a discrete category. Because pain interference and intensity showed, as expected, low values among controls and greater variability within the chronic pain group, analyses using continuous pain measures may partially reflect the underlying group structure and were interpreted as complementary to group‐based contrasts (see Figure S1 for distributions).

We performed three HLM analyses with trial‐level predictability ratings as the outcome variable, match condition (matched and non‐matched trials) as a dummy‐coded trial‐level predictor variable and pain intensity (VAS or BPI‐PS) or interference (BPI‐IS) scores as a subject‐level predictor variable. Consistent with the group‐based analyses, match effect was not significantly modulated by pain intensity (VAS: B = −0.02, SE = 0.02, t(65) = 1.05, p = 0.297, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.02]; BPI‐PS: B = −0.02, SE = 0.02, t(65) = 1.26, p = 0.211, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.02]). In contrast, pain interference showed an interaction with match condition (B = −0.03, SE = 0.02, t(65) = 2.03, p = 0.046, 95% CI [−0.069, −0.001]), suggesting that the relationship between match condition and predictability was modulated by pain interference. To assess robustness of the pain‐interference finding, a case‐resampling bootstrap analysis (5000 samples, cluster‐resampled at the participant level) was conducted for the pain interference × match condition interaction. The bootstrap confidence interval excluded zero (B = −0.03, 95% bootstrap CI [−0.06, −0.0003]), consistent with the original model, suggesting that the observed associations were not solely attributable to a small subset of participants. However, given the proximity of the upper confidence bound to zero, replication in larger samples will be important.

Conditional estimates were additionally probed at representative clinically interpretable values corresponding approximately to mild (2), moderate (5), and high (8) pain levels. Across all levels, matched trials were rated as more predictable than non‐matched trials, although the magnitude of this difference decreased with increasing pain interference, consistent with a reduced match effect at higher interference levels (see Table S1).

Emotion‐specific analyses were conducted to probe the specificity of the observed effects and are reported in Supporting Information S1.

The Impact of Pain on the Reliance on Predictability for Social Evaluation

The impact of predictions on social evaluation was assessed as the relationship between predictability and likability ratings using an HLM analysis with trial‐level likability ratings as the outcome variable, predictability ratings as a trial‐level predictor variable, and group (patients with chronic pain and control individuals) as a dummy‐coded subject‐level predictor variable, with the two‐way interaction term included in the model. Consistent with prior work, this analysis revealed a positive relationship between predictability and likability ratings (Chanes et al. 2018; Ramos‐Grille et al. 2022; Robles et al. 2024). Facial expressions that were rated as more predictable were also rated as more likable for the control group (B = 0.25, SE = 0.03, t(65) = 8.12, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.19, 0.31]). This association was significantly stronger among patients with chronic pain (B = 0.37, SE = 0.04, t(65) = 8.45, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.29, 0.46]), as indicated by a significant Predictability × Group interaction (B = 0.13, SE = 0.05, t(65) = 2.4, p = 0.021, 95% CI [0.02, 0.24]). Thus, while predictability played a significant role in social evaluation for all participants, patients with chronic pain exhibited stronger reliance on their predictions when making these evaluations (Figure 2b).

Next, we examined whether similar patterns were observed when replacing diagnostic group with continuous measures of pain experience across all participants (n = 67). These analyses were treated as secondary, hypothesis driven. We performed three HLM analyses with trial‐level likability ratings as the outcome variable, predictability ratings as a trial‐level predictor variable, and pain (VAS, BPI‐PS or BPI‐IS) scores as a subject‐level predictor variable. Results revealed that higher pain intensity levels, both current (VAS; B = 0.03, SE = 0.01, t(65) = 3.51, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.012, 0.044]) and within the last 24 h (BPI‐PS; B = 0.03, SE = 0.01, t(65) = 3.28, p = 0.002, 95% CI [0.012, 0.047]), were significantly associated with a stronger relationship between likability and predictability ratings. The positive association between predictability and likability remained significant across all clinically interpretable pain‐intensity levels and increased with higher pain intensity (see Table S1). In contrast, pain interference showed only a marginal association with a stronger relationship between likability and predictability ratings (B = 0.02, SE = 0.01, t(65) = 1.87, p = 0.066, 95% CI [−0.001, 0.036]).

To assess robustness of the pain‐intensity finding, a case‐resampling bootstrap analysis (5000 samples, cluster‐resampled at the participant level) was conducted for the pain intensity × predictability interaction for both VAS and BPI‐PS. The bootstrap confidence interval excluded zero (VAS: B = 0.03, 95% bootstrap CI [0.02, 0.04]; BPI‐PS: B = 0.03, 95% bootstrap CI [0.02, 0.04]), suggesting that the observed associations were not solely attributable to a small subset of participants. To further probe the specificity of the group effect, we conducted exploratory emotion‐specific analyses separately for trials evoking happiness, sadness, and fear (see Supporting Information S2).

Discussion

The present study investigated whether patients with chronic pain show differences in social–emotional expectations, relative to controls, that extend beyond pain perception. Using a social perception task, we compared social predictions in patients with chronic pain and healthy controls.

Our findings replicated previous research (Chanes et al. 2018; Draganov et al. 2023; Ramos‐Grille et al. 2022; Robles et al. 2024), demonstrating that facial expressions were perceived as more predictable when they matched the emotion evoked in a preceding scenario. This match effect (i.e., the difference between matched and non‐matched trials) was evident in both groups, with no significant differences between groups. However, analyses using continuous measures related to pain experience revealed a more nuanced pattern. Higher levels of pain interference (as measured by the BPI‐IS) were associated with a reduced match effect, reflected in a smaller difference in predictability ratings between matched and non‐matched trials, consistent with less stereotypical expectations. Importantly, this association was specific to pain interference and was not observed for pain intensity (as measured by BPI‐PS and VAS). In accordance with the literature linking chronic pain to negative‐valence emotional states such as sadness and fear (Baliki and Apkarian 2015; Poublan‐Couzardot and Talmi 2024; Karoly 2021), exploratory emotion‐specific analyses suggested that this pattern may be more pronounced for negative‐valence scenarios than positive‐valence ones. One possible interpretation is that the association between pain interference and reduced stereotypicality of predictions may reflect differences in processing efficiency, although this account remains speculative. Prior work suggests that reliance on stereotypical predictions may be cognitively efficient, simplifying social perception and reducing processing demands (Hinton 2017; Macrae et al. 1994; Yzerbyt et al. 1999). From this perspective, maintaining more nuanced (i.e., less stereotypical) predictions, especially in emotionally negative contexts that share affective features with pain, may require cognitive resources that could otherwise support pain regulation and may therefore be associated with greater interference.

As for social evaluation (Figure 3), across both groups, predictability of facial expressions was positively associated with likability ratings, consistent with prior findings (Chanes et al. 2018; Ramos‐Grille et al. 2022; Robles et al. 2024), showing that individuals rely on their predictions to evaluate others. Notably, patients with chronic pain demonstrated a stronger relationship between predictability and likability ratings than controls, suggesting that expectations may play a greater role during social evaluation. Exploratory emotion‐specific analyses suggested that these group differences may be more pronounced for negative‐valence scenarios (see Supporting Information) and a similar pattern was observed in secondary analyses using continuous measures of pain intensity, but not pain interference. Taken together, these findings suggest that pain interference and pain intensity are differentially associated with (i) how stereotypical expectations are and (ii) how strongly those expectations are used during social evaluation.

Regarding the latter, one possible account is that pain intensity, as an indicator of momentary or recent somatic burden, may reflect a sustained allostatic load (Sibille et al. 2017; Paulus et al. 2019; Rabey and Moloney 2022), i.e., a chronic stress state that redirects physiological and cognitive resources toward perceived threats (Lunde and Sieberg 2020) and potentially leaves fewer resources for other tasks such as social evaluation. Additionally, pain intensity may impose cognitive demands (Eccleston and Crombez 1999) that could limit resources for flexible information processing (Berryman et al. 2014). Within predictive processing frameworks, a possible interpretation is that ongoing nociceptive input may be associated with greater perceptual uncertainty, which in turn may shift the balance toward greater reliance on prior expectations (Paulus et al. 2019; Wiech 2016). This interpretation aligns with accounts suggesting that chronic pain may be related to differences in updating internal models, resulting in persistent reliance on outdated, negatively skewed predictions (Chen et al. 2022; Ingvar 2015). Neuroimaging evidence further suggests that chronic back pain is associated with a shift from nociceptive to affective networks (Hashmi et al. 2013), consistent with an increased influence of emotional priors on perception and evaluation. However, the present results do not allow strong conclusions regarding underlying mechanisms. Rather, these findings highlight candidate processes and generate testable hypotheses.

In summary, while pain interference was associated with reduced stereotypicality of expectations, pain intensity was consistently associated with a stronger relationship between predictability and likability ratings, suggesting that these two aspects of pain experience may relate differently to expectation formation and use. Although this pattern was not initially anticipated, it aligns with prior work distinguishing pain interference, linked to functional disruption and affective burden, from pain intensity, which may reflect immediate somatic load (Jensen et al. 2006; Cleeland and Ryan 1994). In line with this distinction, pain interference has been associated with emotional distress (Jensen et al. 2006), executive dysfunction (Solberg Nes et al. 2009), reduced cognitive efficiency (Berryman et al. 2014), and maladaptive self‐regulatory strategies (Karoly 2021), all of which may influence how expectations are formed in social contexts. In contrast, higher pain intensity may impose a real‐time cognitive load (Hamilton et al. 2018; Telesca et al. 2024), potentially contributing to greater reliance on prior expectations during evaluation. Importantly, no diagnostic group differences were observed in the match effect, indicating that the basic construction of context‐based expectations appeared broadly preserved at the group level. In contrast, a significant group difference emerged in the relationship between predictability and likability, with individuals with chronic pain relying more strongly on their expectations during social evaluation. Taken together, this pattern is consistent with the possibility that chronic pain may not substantially alter the generation of social predictions but rather be associated with differences in the degree to which those predictions guide social evaluation. In other words, differences may emerge more strongly at the stage of applying expectations than at the stage of generating them.

While these findings provide novel insights into affective predictions in chronic pain, several limitations should be noted. Our sample primarily consisted of patients with chronic back pain, which may limit generalizability to other pain conditions. Similarly, the control group was defined as those with self‐reported absence of major physical or mental health conditions, ensuring a well‐characterized comparison group but potentially limiting generalizability to populations with non‐clinical pain or greater variability in health status. Replication in more diverse samples will therefore be important. Furthermore, pain mechanisms were not formally phenotyped; thus, the sample likely included a mixture of nociceptive, neuropathic, and potentially nociplastic components, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Moreover, while the theoretical framework draws on concepts such as allostatic load and reduced cognitive–emotional resources, the present study did not directly assess executive function, attention, affective symptoms, catastrophizing, or sleep quality. Importantly, within predictive‐processing frameworks, several of these factors, including attention and imagery, may be conceptualized as integral components involved in the formation and weighting of expectations (Feldman and Friston 2010; Kok et al. 2012). Within such frameworks, attention has been proposed to influence the weighting or precision of expectations (Feldman and Friston 2010), while imagery may shape the content or vividness of anticipated representations (Pearson et al. 2015). However, the present design does not allow these mechanisms to be empirically isolated. Accordingly, multiple cognitive and affective processes likely contribute to the formation and use of expectations in the current task. Future studies directly assessing these variables will be important for clarifying their potential contribution to the effects observed here. In addition, although participants contrasted their expectations to observed facial expressions, the content and qualitative properties of these expectations (e.g., clarity, level of detail, confidence), along with related dimensions such as uncertainty or context sensitivity, were not directly assessed, nor disentangled from related processes such as imagery. Future work could characterize these properties and examine whether they differ across groups and emotional contexts, as well as how they relate to the observed effects. The present design prevents determining whether the observed associations involving predictability and social evaluation cause, result from, or share common factors with pain, and bidirectional explanations remain plausible, including the possibility that differences in how expectations are formed and used may contribute to greater pain experience over time. Incorporating facial expressions evoking pain and scenarios involving pain‐specific content could further clarify the interplay between emotional and pain‐related priors. Future research comparing chronic pain with experimentally induced acute pain could further clarify how predictive mechanisms evolve across pain states. Finally, despite the large number of trials per participant, the relatively modest sample size at the participant level limited statistical power for interaction effects, particularly in emotion‐specific analyses; replication in larger samples will be important.

Conclusions

This study investigated how chronic pain (specifically chronic back pain) may relate to emotion predictions and social evaluation, suggesting differences in how expectations are used rather than how they are formed, alongside a dissociation between different aspects of pain experience in their associations with predictive processes. By integrating predictive processing concepts with chronic pain research, these findings contribute to a growing understanding of chronic pain as a multidimensional condition extending beyond nociception to emotion and social cognition. While the observed associations warrant cautious interpretation, they suggest distinct ways in which pain intensity and pain interference may relate to predictability and its association with social evaluation. This perspective may inform future interventions aimed at promoting cognitive‐emotional flexibility in patients with chronic pain.
