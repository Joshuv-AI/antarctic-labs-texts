The Chow Ring of the 2-Sylow Subgroup of GL ⁡ ( 4 , 2 ) \mathrm{GL}(4,2)
Alexander Ziegler

Abstract
. This paper provides a computation of the mod 2 Chow ring of the motivic étale classifying space of the finite group Syl 2 ​ ( GL ​ ( 4 , 2 ) ) \mathrm{Syl}_{2}(\mathrm{GL}(4,2)) . It outlines a general computation strategy, adapted from work by Burt Totaro, that has been largely automated by the author. This strategy can be used to compute more examples of Chow rings of motivic étale classifying spaces quickly.

In

) Chow groups of quasi-projective

2.
Group cohomology and Chow rings of classifying spaces

This section will very briefly introduce group cohomology
and Chow rings of classifying spaces. For a more detailed account, we strongly recommend
[
Tot14
]
, as it is the main source for this section.
Suppose
G
is a topological group. To it we can associate a topological space
BG
, called its classical classifying space,
by taking the quotient of a free action of
G
on a contractible space
EG
. There is a clash of notations
with the motivic étale classifying space, but it will be always clear from context which classifying space we are referring to. For a precise definition, check
[
Hat02
, Example 1B.7]
. This object is rife with interesting data about
G
. Most notably:

Theorem 2.1

(Steenrod)
.

There exists a universal principal
G
-bundle
\gamma_{G}

on the classical classifying space
BG
such that for every paracompact Hausdorff
space
X
the map

\displaystyle[X,BG]

\displaystyle\to\{\text{isomorphism classes of principal $G$-bundles on $X$}\}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto f^{*}\gamma_{G}

is a bijection. By
[X,BG]
we denote the set of homotopy classes of maps
X\to BG
.

Of particular interest is also:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
R
be a ring.
We call the singular cohomology ring
H^{*}(BG,R)
the group cohomology of
G
with
R
-coefficients.

We can understand some of the classes in the group cohomology ring via complex representation theory.
Singular cohomology rings are the natural habitat of Chern classes of topological finite dimensional complex vector bundles. Now take a complex
G
-representation
\phi
of finite degree. Clearly
\phi
is a
G
-equivariant finite dimensional vector bundle over a point, which can be uniquely (up to isomorphism) extended to the contractible space
EG
. By taking the quotient of the action of
G
on

\phi
we obtain a vector bundle over
BG
. We can conclude, group cohomology is the natural habitat of Chern classes of finite degree complex representations.

Motivic homotopy theory gives us an analogous motivic étale classifying space
that is much closer to the algebro-geometric theory. It is a nice
feature of this theory that the motivic étale classifying
space of a group can be expressed as a formal filtered colimit of schemes. Other topologies than the étale topology can be considered, but then we can’t approximate the corresponding motivic classifying space by schemes. Using this approximation, one can define a notion of an associated Chow ring that can be expressed
in purely algebro-geometric terms. This Chow ring can be seen as the motivic analogue of group cohomology.
The Chow ring of an motivic étale classifying space was independently defined in
[
Tot99
]
and
[
MV99
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let G be an affine finite type group scheme over a base field
k
and
i
a natural number. There exists
V
a
k
-vector space of finite dimension with an action
G\to\mathrm{GL}(V)
i.e. a
k
-linear representation of
G
and
S\leq V
a closed
G
-invariant subscheme such that
G
acts freely on
V-S
,
(V-S)/G
exists as a scheme
and
S
is of codimension greater than
i
. Furthermore the graded ring

CH^{*}((V-S)/G)
is independent of the choice of
V,S
up to degree
i
.

Proof.

See
[
Tot14
, Theorem 2.5]
for a proof.
∎

Definition 2.4
.

We define the Chow ring of the motivic étale classifying space
BG
to be given as

CH^{j}(BG):=CH^{j}((V-S)/G)

for
j<i
by varying

and according to ?THM?
choosing
V,S
. We obtain a graded ring
CH^{*}(BG)
.

We will use

as both the notation for the classical and
the motivic étale classifying space. From now on they will only appear in the context of group cohomology or the Chow ring. Furthermore, we
will interpret finite groups as discrete topological groups or complex affine group schemes, depending on the context.

Suppose for now

is a compact Lie group and
R(G)
its ring of virtual complex representations.
The extended degree map
R(G)\to\mathbb{Z}
has a kernel

called the augmentation ideal.
The Atiyah-Segal completion theorem states that
K^{0}(BG)
is naturally
isomorphic to
R(G)^{\wedge}
which is

completed at

.
Suppose now that

is an affine finite type group scheme over a base field

.
In
[
Tot99
, Theorem 3.1]
a similar isomorphism

is constructed
where
K_{0}(BG)
is just the colimit of the
K_{0}((V-S)/G)
for the approximation steps

(V-S)/G
approaching
BG
as in ?THM?
and
R(G)^{\wedge}
is the ring of
k
-linear virtual representation of

completed at the analogous augmentation ideal

. The
n
-th Chern class is a natural map

so finite degree

-linear representations have Chern classes in

.

Throughout our computations we make heavy use of the powerful theory for these characteristic classes.

Theorem 2.5
.

For cyclic groups
C_{n}
with
\#C_{n}=n

CH^{*}(C_{n})\cong\mathbb{Z}[c_{1}(\sigma)]/(nc_{1}(\sigma))

where
\sigma
is a faithful (i.e. non-trivial) degree 1 representation.

Proof.

This+ is proven in
[
Tot14
, Theorem 2.10]
.
∎

Another important tool is the fact that assigning the Chow ring of a classifying space to a
group (scheme) is a contravariant functor. For subgroups this specializes to a notion of restriction, which will be heavily exploited in our computations. There is also a notion
of transfer in the other direction, that should be mentioned in this context.

Definition 2.6
.

Take
H\to G
to be a map of
k
-group schemes and
U_{G}
resp.
U_{H}

to be the
V-S
chosen in the approximation of
BG
resp.

as in ?THM?
.
The pullback along

(U_{H}\times U_{G})/H\to U_{G}/G

gives rise to a graded ring homomorphism preserving the degree

CH^{*}(BG)\to CH^{*}(BH).

If
H\leq G
is a subgroup scheme we call this restriction.
Suppose
H\leq G
is a closed subgroup scheme of finite index.
The usual pushforward map for Chow rings yields a homomorphism of graded abelian groups preserving the degree

\mathrm{tr}_{H}^{G}\colon CH^{*}(BH)\to CH^{*}(BG).

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Then
CH^{i}(BG)
with
i\geq 1
is
\#G
-torsion.

Proof.

In
[
Tot14
, Lemma 2.15]
it is shown for any subgroup
H\leq G

that restriction is a ring homomorphism and transfer is a homomorphism of

CH^{*}(BG)
-modules. Furthermore it is shown in loc. cit. that the transfer of
1\in CH^{*}(BH)
along

H\to G
is
[G:H]
. In particular restricting and then transferring along

H\to G
is multiplication by
[G:H]
.
Picking
H
to be the trivial group we get

CH^{*}(BH)=CH^{*}(\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}))\cong\mathbb{Z}
meaning
multiplication of elements of
CH^{*}(BG)
by
\#G=[G:1]
factors through

CH^{*}(\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}))
which is concentrated in degree 0.
∎

The third tool in our arsenal will be a comparison functor to group cohomology called
the cycle class map. For

a smooth scheme of dimension
n
over
\mathbb{C}
there is a realization

as a complex manifold given by the complex points of
X
.
There is also a natural ring homomorphism

CH^{*}(X)\to H^{2*}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z})
called the cycle class map.
An analogue of the cycle class map for the motivic étale classifying spaces is described in
[
Gui04
, Foreword]
.

Definition 2.8
.

Suppose
G
is an affine finite type group scheme over
\mathbb{C}
and denote by
G(\mathbb{C})
the set of complex points of
G
.
There exists a map of graded rings

CH^{*}(BG)\to H^{2*}(B(G(\mathbb{C})),\mathbb{Z})

which doubles the degree meaning its image is concentrated in even degrees. This map is natural with respect to pullbacks as defined in ?THM?
. It is called the cycle class map of
BG
.

This induces a map
CH^{*}(BG)/p\to H^{*}(BG,\mathbb{Z})/p\to H^{*}(BG,\mathbb{F}_{p})
,
which we will call the cycle class map modulo
p
. The integral cycle class map is always an
isomorphism in degree 1.

Proposition 2.9
.

CH^{1}(BG)\cong H^{2}(BG,\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathrm{Hom}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times}).

Proof.

This has been proven in
[
Tot14
, Lemma 2.26]
.
∎

Let’s finish this section by describing how finite abelian groups behave very well in this theory.

Suppose

to be an affine group scheme over

In particular the regular and modulo

CH^{*}(BC^{n}_{p})\cong\frac{\mathbb{Z}[c_{1}(\sigma_{1}),\dots,c_{1}(\sigma_{n})]}{(pc_{1}(\sigma_{1}),\dots,pc_{1}(\sigma_{n}))}

where the

are the degree 1 representations
that project
C_{p}^{n}
to its
i
-th coordinate which is then identified with
\mu_{p}

the group of

-th roots of unity.

Proof.

This has been proven in
[
Tot14
, Lemma 2.12]
.
∎

Let

3.
Controlling the Chow ring in high degree

The purpose of this section is to gather some powerful computational tools in our pursuit for explicit descriptions of
CH^{*}(BG)/p
for a prime number
p
and
G

a finite group. More concretely, we introduce two theorems that will reduce the computation of
CH^{*}(BG)/p
to computations in degrees below some upper bound. This bound scales with the degrees of direct summands of the faithful representation of
G
with lowest degree. These tools have been introduced in
[
Tot14
]
, where they were used to compute all the mod
p
Chow rings of finite groups of order
p^{4}
with
p=2,3
and all but two groups of order
p^{4}
for
p\geq 5
. We will use the same tools for our computations. They fall under two umbrellas, detection of cycles on elementary abelian subgroups and bounds for the degrees of generating elements and relations in a presentation. Representations will always assumed to be complex of finite degree.

3.1.
Detecting cycles on elementary abelian groups

We will denote by
M^{\leq d}
the quotient of a graded module
M
by its elements of degree greater than
d
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
G
has a faithful representation of degree
n
and its center has
p
-rank
c
. For any subgroup
H\leq G
denote by
C_{G}(H)
the centralizer of
H
in
G
. The
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra homomorphism

CH^{*}(BG)/p\to\prod_{V}CH^{*}(BV)/p\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}CH^{\leq(n-c)}(BC_{G}(V))/p

is injective. Here the component maps are given by the pullback along the multiplication map

V\times C_{G}(V)\to G
and
V
runs over the set of elementary abelian subgroups of
G
.

Proof.

This is proven in
[
Tot14
, Theorem 12.7]
.
∎

?THM?
means in theory that we need to check for relations in a presentation of
CH^{*}(BG)/p
in degrees larger than

only on the collection of maximal abelian subgroups.
Putting this into practice is a bit subtle as the image of the map in
CH^{*}(BV)/p\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}CH^{\leq(n-c)}(BC_{G}(V))/p
can contain mixed terms that are non-zero but restrict to zero in

CH^{*}(BV)/p
and
CH^{\leq(n-c)}(BC_{G}(V))/p
.

The following observation will be very useful in dealing with this.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
G
has a faithful representation of degree
n
and its center is of
p
-rank
c
.
Assume furthermore that
CH^{*}(BC_{G}(V))/p
for non-central elementary abelian subgroups
V\leq G
contains no nilpotents. The modulo
p
cycle class map for
BG
is injective in degrees
>n-c
.

Proof.

Suppose there is a non-zero element
\sigma\in CH^{k}(BG)/p
with
k>n-c

in the kernel of the modulo
p
cycle class map, which means by ?THM?
it restricts to zero in
all Chow rings of elementary abelian subgroups of
G
. By ?THM?
there exists an elementary abelian
V\leq G
such that
\sigma
restricts to a non-zero nilpotent
\sum_{i}(a_{i}\otimes b_{i})
in
CH^{*}(BV)/p\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}CH^{*}(BC_{G}(V))/p
. We assume
\sum_{i}(a_{i}\otimes b_{i})
to be a reduced term i.e. no pair
a_{i},a_{j}
is
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-linearly dependent and no pair
b_{i},b_{j}
is
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-linearly dependent. Furthermore we can assume that the
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are all homogenous elements.
Using ?THM?
the

must be all of positive degree and at least one of degree
\leq n-c
while at least one

must be of positive degree. This means in particular that
the
b_{i}
with

of positive degree has to be nilpotent, as
CH^{*}(BV)/p\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}CH^{*}(BC_{G}(V))/p
is just a polynomial ring over
CH^{*}(BC_{G}(V))/p
by ?THM?
.
By our assumptions

has to be a central elementary abelian group
which means
\sigma
is not mapped to zero in
CH^{*}(BV)/p\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}CH^{\leq n-c}(BG)/p

and thus is mapped to non-zero in

via pullback along the identity
G\cong\{1_{V}\}\times G\to V\times G\to G
. This violates our original assumptions for
\sigma
.
∎

Regularity of the modulo

In even higher degrees we have much more control thanks to the following result.

Proof.

Let

\max\{|y_{i}|,\sigma(R)\}

Take

if

By induction such a sequence exists for any finite sequence of prime numbers. We pick now a finite sequence

The strategy for our computations is going to be to compute low-degree parts of the Chow ring explicitly,
then check that the modulo

.
If we are lucky, sufficiently high means ?THM?
kicks into effect and tells us that all

tells us that at least powers of these relations still hold.

4.
Filtrations on the representation ring

This section will be used to introduce several filtrations on the representation ring of an affine group scheme
G
over
k
.
The
\gamma
-filtration is very well computable and it is our main lever to achieve our computations.
The geometric filtration as introduced in
[
KM19
]
is a much closer approximation of the Chow ring that stems from the motivic Atiyah-Hirzebruch spectral sequence. It is a priori very difficult to compute. Luckily the two filtrations coincide in the zeroth and first
steps. In all further steps, the
\gamma
-filtration is a subset of the geometric filtration.
The crux of this work is to compute as much as one can using the
\gamma
-filtration
such that the tools from the previous section take care of the rest and we never need to
directly touch the geometric filtration. For a more detailed account on
\gamma
-filtrations on representation rings, we recommend
[
Che20
]
. Representations will be assumed to always be of finite degree and complex. Furthermore, we will denote
n
-fold multiples of the trivial representation of
G
as just the natural number

, because the unique ring homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}\to R(G)
identifies both.

Definition 4.1
.

We define a
\lambda
-structure on
R(G)
via exterior powers

\lambda^{i}(x):=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{i}x\text{ for representations }x.

This can be uniquely extended to a map
R(G)\to R(G)

such that

\lambda^{n}(x+y)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda^{i}(x)\lambda^{n-i}(y)\text{ for }x,y\in R(G).

This gives rise to so-called
\gamma
-operations

\gamma^{i}(x)=\lambda^{i}(x+i-1).

There is a purely axiomatical notion of (augmented)
\lambda
-rings that we don’t explore,
see for example
[
Zib17
, Generalities]
.
The concept of
\gamma
-filtrations can be generalized to this more abstract setting.

Definition 4.2
.

The augmentation ideal
\mathrm{I}
of

is defined to be the kernel of the extended degree map

\mathrm{deg}\colon R(G)\to\mathbb{Z}.

Definition 4.3
.

The

-ideal

is defined as the abelian group generated by the finite products
\prod_{i}\gamma^{j_{i}}(x_{i})
with

and

.
The descending chain of
\gamma
-ideals defines the
\gamma
-filtration on

.

Definition 4.4

The associated graded to the
\gamma
-filtration is defined as the abelian group

which comes equipped with a ring structure because
\Gamma^{m}\Gamma^{n}\leq\Gamma^{mn}
.
We call the residue class

the

of a representation

These Chern classes behave very similarly to those we already know as can be seen from the following proposition.

Chern classes in

Proof.

This has been proven in

Denote

Proposition 4.6

Let

Take

Firstly we see that the generating set already generates

5.
Universal polynomials

We need one more tool in our arsenal to make our computations more accessible. In practice computing
gr^{*}_{\gamma}R(G)
is so involved that it has to be done by a computer, thus obfuscating concrete steps. In an ideal world all steps
should be human readable. The advantage of formulating our calculation steps using universal polynomials is thus twofold:
we make our computations more accessible to humans and we give meaning to the computed relations.

Theorem 5.1

(Splitting principle)
.

Let
G
be a group and
R(G)
its representation ring. There exists an extension of
\lambda
-rings
R^{\prime}
over
R(G)
such that
gr_{\gamma}^{*}R(G)\to gr_{\gamma}^{*}R^{\prime}

is an injection and every element of
R(G)
decomposes into a direct sum of line elements in
R^{\prime}
where
x\in R^{\prime}
is called a line element if
\lambda^{n}(x)
vanishes for
n>1
.

This splitting principle and ?THM?
are also available in the setting of Chow rings and singular cohomology which means the following statement is applicable to Chern classes in
CH^{*}(BG)
as well as
H^{*}(BG,\mathbb{Z})
and
gr^{*}_{\gamma}R(G)
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose
x,y
are representations of
G
. There exist integer polynomials
p_{m}^{\mathrm{mul}},p_{m,l}^{\mathrm{ext}}
such that

c_{T}(x\otimes y)=\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\mathrm{mul}}(c_{1}(x),\dots,c_{i}(x),c_{1}(y),\dots,c_{i}(y))T^{i}

and

c_{T}(\lambda^{m}(x))=\sum_{i}p_{i,m}^{\mathrm{ext}}(c_{1}(x),\dots,c_{i}(x))T^{i}.

Proof.

This simply follows from ?THM?
together with the splitting principle and the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials. For more details we refer the reader to
[
FL85
, Section I.§1]

∎

In the following computations we will sometimes justify relations in a presentation of
CH^{*}(BG)
as coming from universal polynomials given by some tensor product

or exterior power

of representations
x,y
. In theses cases it is feasible to compute one of the above polynomials by hand. One can then find a second description
of

resp.

, usually by decomposing
the representations into irreducibles and applying the Whitney sum formula. Both descriptions of

resp.

have to be equal

(also in
H^{*}(BG,\mathbb{Z}),gr^{*}_{\gamma}R(G)
).

Corollary 5.3
.

The subrings of

,

and

generated by Chern classes of representations are generated by Chern classes of generators of

.

Proof.

This follows form ?THM?
together with the splitting principle.

C.1.

As before, we compute the number of conjugacy classes as

\frac{\#\{(x,y)\in L\times L\mid xy=yx\}}{\#L}

To do so we observe for arbitrary

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&a&e\\

0&0&1&c\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&0&v&z\\

where we require both sides of both equations to be non-zero given by an arbitrary choice of

It has

.

The case where we require the second equation to be non-zero and the first to be zero likewise has

solutions.

The last case is that both sides of both equations are zero, which requires a distinction
of the cases for

being zero or non-zero. If

there are

solutions determined by an arbitrary choice of

and there are

solutions given by arbitrary

and arbitrary

. Likewise there are

Lastly there are
