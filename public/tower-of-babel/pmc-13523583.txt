Limited benefit of half-normal vs normal saline irrigation for augmenting current delivery with open-irrigated temperature-controlled ablation
Authors: Henry D Huang, Jonathan Gordon, Jeffrey Winterfield, Pasquale Santangeli, Jackson Liang, Hagai Yavin, Zaid Aziz, Jeremy Treger, Srinath Yeshwant, Andrew Beaser, Cevher Ozcan, Gaurav A Upadhyay, Rishi Arora, William H Sauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHalf-normal saline (HNS) irrigation increases radiofrequency (RF) lesion size during power-controlled ablation by augmenting current delivery to tissue. Whether this benefit persists with temperature-controlled ablation, which modulates power delivery on the basis of catheter tip temperature, remains unknown.ObjectiveThis study sought to compare lesion characteristics using HNS vs normal saline (NS) irrigation with power-controlled and temperature-controlled open-irrigated catheters in an ex vivo model.MethodsRF applications were delivered to left ventricular porcine tissue using a power- and temperature-controlled irrigated catheter with NS and HNS irrigation. Lesions were performed at 10 g contact force perpendicular to tissue. Temperature-controlled ablation used manufacturer-preset maximum temperature cutoffs of 50°C at 30W and 55°C at 50W.ResultsAmong 104 lesions analyzed, HNS irrigation with the power-controlled catheter produced larger lesions at 30W × 30 seconds than did NS (depth: 7.5 ± 1.0 vs 6.5 ± 0.6 mm, P = .0005; width: 10.5 ± 1.1 vs 9.4 ± 1.0 mm, P = .005). However, no difference in lesion dimensions was observed with the temperature-controlled catheter using HNS vs NS at 30W (depth: 6.1 ± 0.4 vs 6.2 ± 0.7 mm, P = .44; width: 9.4 ± 0.7 vs 9.6 ± 1.2 mm, P = .23) or 50W (depth: 7.2 ± 0.7 vs 7.5 ± 0.8 mm, P = .35; width: 10.9 ±1.3 vs 11.8 ± 1.6 mm, P = .19). With HNS, the temperature-controlled catheter showed reduced average power delivery (30W: 23.3 ± 5.4 vs 27.1± 4.6 W, P < .05) and increased irrigation flow rates compared with NS, likely owing to temperature cut-off enforcement. Fewer steam pops were observed with the temperature-controlled than with the power-controlled catheter using HNS (5% vs 23%, P = .09).ConclusionUnlike power-controlled ablation, HNS irrigation does not increase lesion size with temperature-controlled ablation. Enforcement of maximum temperature cutoffs reduces actual power delivery, blunting the current augmentation benefit of HNS irrigation while potentially improving safety.Keywords: Radiofrequency ablation, Half-normal saline, Temperature-controlled ablation, Power-controlled ablation, Ventricular arrhythmia

Introduction

Achieving adequate lesion depth during radiofrequency (RF) ablation of ventricular arrhythmias remains challenging, particularly in patients with structural heart disease when arrhythmogenic substrate may be deep within the myocardium.1 Half-normal saline (HNS, 0.45% sodium chloride [NaCl]) irrigation has emerged as a strategy to potentially increase RF lesion size compared with normal saline (NS, 0.9% NaCl). The lower ionic concentration of HNS reduces electrical shunting in the fluid surrounding the catheter tip, thereby augmenting current delivery to tissue rather than the blood pool.2,3

Previous studies have revealed that HNS irrigation with power-controlled open-irrigated catheters produces larger and deeper lesions than does NS, attributed to increased tissue current density and higher tissue temperatures.3,4 However, these benefits come with potential risks, including increased incidence of steam pops due to excessive tissue heating.5

Recently, a novel temperature-controlled open-irrigated ablation catheter (QDOT MICRO, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) has been approved for clinical use. This catheter incorporates 6 embedded thermocouples directly at the electrode tip-tissue interface, representing a fundamental advance on previous-generation catheters with remotely positioned temperature sensors.6 These embedded sensors provide a more accurate tissue interface temperature measurement, enabling real-time automated adjustment of power output and irrigation flow rate based on actual contact temperatures rather than cooled irrigant temperatures. The system enforces manufacturer-preset maximum temperature cutoffs designed to optimize lesion formation while mitigating risks of tissue overheating. In theory, this accurate temperature-regulated approach could fundamentally alter the biophysical effects of HNS irrigation compared with traditional power-controlled ablation or earlier "temperature-controlled" systems with remote sensors.

The impact of HNS irrigation on lesion characteristics during temperature-controlled ablation has not been well characterized. Recent clinical studies have focused primarily on safety outcomes and steam pop prevention with HNS in temperature-controlled systems,7,8 but mechanistic understanding of lesion formation dynamics remains limited. We hypothesized that the advantage of HNS over NS on lesion size would be attenuated with temperature-controlled compared with power-controlled irrigated ablation, given automated power modulation in response to increasing tissue temperatures might limit the current augmentation typically achieved with HNS.

Methods

Experimental preparation

A total of 9 fresh porcine hearts were obtained within 1 hour of killing from a United States Department of Agriculture-approved abattoir. Hearts were immediately sectioned into transmural slabs approximately 15–20 mm thick and submerged in a circulating bath of NS (0.9% NaCl) maintained at 37°C using a temperature-controlled water bath. The experimental setup included an nGen RF generator (Biosense Webster), a 56-hole porous-tip contact force-sensing Thermocool SmartTouch Surround Flow catheter (STSF, Biosense Webster), and a 66-hole porous-tip contact force-sensing QDOT MICRO catheter (Biosense Webster), using a dispersive electrode (grounding patch) submerged in the fluid bath.

Both the power-controlled STSF catheter and temperature-controlled QDOT MICRO catheter achieve contact force sensing through a precision spring mechanism attached to 3 electromagnetic receiving coils that generate proportional electrical current to the deflection force, thereby providing vector and magnitude contact force information. Contact force data were displayed and recorded by the CARTO 3 mapping system (Biosense Webster) during each RF application.

The indifferent electrode (grounding patch) was submerged in the fluid bath. The ablation catheter tip was pressed into the myocardium with 10 g of contact force, creating direct tissue-electrode contact. The measured impedance values reflected the combined resistance of the tissue-electrode interface and the conductive path through the bath. Although some current flow occurred through the surrounding saline, the tissue itself contributed substantially to the measured impedance, particularly given the direct contact force application.

Ablation protocol

Ablation lesions were created with the catheter tip held perpendicular to the tissue surface using a fixed contact force of 10 g, monitored continuously through the CARTO system. This perpendicular orientation and consistent contact force were maintained to minimize variability in energy delivery. The nGen generator was operated using nominal factory settings for both STSF and QDOT MICRO catheters.

For temperature-controlled ablation using QMODE (temperature regulation mode) with the QDOT MICRO catheter, the system used nonprogrammable absolute temperature cutoffs of 50°C at 30W and 55°C at 50W power settings as preset by the manufacturer. During RF applications, actual power delivery was dynamically modulated by an algorithm.

Overall, 3 comparison groups were evaluated: (1) power-controlled catheter at 30W for 30 seconds; (2) temperature-controlled catheter at 30W for 30 seconds; and (3) temperature-controlled catheter at 50W for 30 seconds. Each group was tested with both NS and HNS (0.45% NaCl) irrigation. All lesions were created in left ventricular tissue to reflect the clinical target for most ventricular arrhythmia ablations.

This was an exploratory study without formal a priori power calculation. Sample sizes were determined on the basis of the feasibility constraints related to tissue availability and prior experience with similar ex vivo ablation protocols. On the basis of our previous work showing significant differences in lesion dimensions with power-controlled catheters using similar sample sizes, we anticipated adequate power to detect clinically meaningful differences if present with temperature-controlled catheters.

We sought to perform approximately 20 ablations per experimental condition. However, the final number of analyzable lesions varied owing to (1) occurrence of steam pops, which caused exclusion of those lesions from dimensional analysis; (2) technical factors such as loss of stable catheter contact or equipment malfunction requiring abortion of individual applications; and (3) availability of adequate myocardial tissue in suitable anatomic locations. Lesions were distributed across different ventricular regions to minimize potential confounding from anatomic location. All technically successful ablations without steam pops were included in the analysis, with no selective inclusion or exclusion based on observed lesion characteristics. Ablations were distributed across multiple left ventricular regions (apex, anterolateral wall, anterior wall, septum, inferior wall) rather than clustering all ablations in 1 area. Ablations were performed sequentially across different anatomic regions rather than completing all ablations in 1 region before moving to another, which allowed time for thermal dissipation between applications. The tissue bath was continuously circulated with temperature-monitored saline maintained at 37°C throughout the experiment.

Infrared thermography

In a subset of 4 porcine hearts, digital infrared thermal imaging videos were recorded during RF delivery using an infrared thermal camera (Shot PRO, Seek Thermal) with resolution of 320 × 240 pixels (76,800 pixels), frame rate of 9 Hz, thermal sensitivity <0.07°C, and spectral range of 7.5–14 μm. Emissivity was set at 0.90 to account for myocardial tissue properties. The camera was positioned to visualize the tissue surface at the ablation site, allowing real-time assessment of tissue heating patterns and thermal evolution during RF applications (Figure 1).

Lesion assessment

After completion of ablations, myocardial samples were cross-sectioned perpendicularly to the tissue surface through the center of each lesion and immersed in 2% triphenyltetrazolium chloride solution at 37°C for 15 minutes. Triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining delineates viable myocardium (which stains brick red) from necrotic tissue (which appears pale yellow-white), enabling precise visualization of lesion boundaries. Lesion depth (maximum perpendicular depth from tissue surface) and width (maximum diameter parallel to tissue surface) were measured using a digital micrometer accurate to 0.01 mm by an investigator blinded to ablation parameters.

Lesions with audible or visually apparent steam pops during RF application were excluded from dimensional measurement analysis but were tracked to assess incidence rates across groups. A steam pop was defined as an audible explosive sound or visible sudden expansion of tissue.

Raw ablation data for each RF application, including delivered power, tip electrode temperature, irrigation flow rate, impedance, and calculated current (amperes = watts/ohms), were extracted from the nGen generator for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons were planned a priori on the basis of the study hypothesis. Primary comparisons evaluated the effect of irrigation solution (NS vs HNS) within each catheter type and power setting using nonpaired Student t tests, given these paired comparisons directly address whether HNS augments lesion size under different ablation conditions. Secondary comparisons evaluated the effect of power setting (30W vs 50W) within the temperature-controlled catheter group using NS irrigation to establish that higher power settings produced larger lesions as expected. These focused comparisons avoid issues of multiple testing inherent in all possible pairwise comparisons among 6 groups. We did not apply correction for multiple comparisons given the exploratory nature of the study, but our primary conclusions are based on the NS vs HNS comparisons, which represent independent hypotheses for each ablation mode. Categorical variables, including steam pop occurrence, were compared using Fisher exact test. A 2-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 25 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Ethical considerations

This study used ex vivo animal tissue obtained from animals slaughtered for food purposes and did not involve animal experimentation. Therefore, institutional review board approval and animal care committee approval were not required. The research reported adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals guidelines for tissue handling.

Results

Overall cohort characteristics

A total of 114 RF applications were performed using power-controlled and temperature-controlled irrigated catheters. Of these, 93 lesions without steam pops during RF application were included in the dimensional analysis. All ablation lesions were performed in left ventricular tissue, distributed across the following regions: apex (n = 11), anterolateral wall (n = 28), anterior wall (n = 24), septum (n = 18), and inferior wall (n = 12). This distribution ensured sampling across varied tissue thickness and regional characteristics.

Lesion dimensions: Power-controlled catheter

With the power-controlled STSF catheter at 30W × 30 seconds, HNS irrigation produced significantly larger lesions than did NS irrigation in both depth (7.5 ± 1.0 mm vs 6.5 ± 0.6 mm, P = .0005) and width (10.5 ± 1.1 mm vs 9.4 ± 1.0 mm, P = .005) (Table 1). This represented a 15% increase in depth and 12% increase in width with HNS, consistent with enhanced current delivery to tissue. The impedance decrease during ablation was significantly greater with HNS than with NS (24.2 ± 6.1 Ω vs 14.1 ± 3.7 Ω, P = .0001), reflecting the altered electrical properties of the lower ionic strength irrigant heating tissue. Although starting impedance was similar in HNS and NS (127 ± 5.1 Ω vs 123 ± 7.3 Ω, P = .47) final impedance was lower after ablation with HNS (102 ± 9.1 Ω vs 109 ± 9.6 Ω, P = .05).

Lesion dimensions: Temperature-controlled catheter

In contrast to the power-controlled results, the temperature-controlled QDOT MICRO catheter showed no significant difference in lesion dimensions between HNS and NS irrigation at either power setting tested using the automated temperature-dependent modulation of power and irrigant flow rates.

At 30W × 30 seconds, lesion depth was similar in HNS and NS (6.1 ± 0.4 mm vs 6.2 ± 0.7 mm, P = .44), as was lesion width (9.4 ± 0.7 mm vs 9.6 ± 1.2 mm, P = .23) (Table 1). Similarly, at 50W × 30 seconds, no significant differences were observed in depth (7.2 ± 0.7 mm vs 7.5 ± 0.8 mm, P = .35) or width (10.9 ± 1.3 mm vs 11.8 ± 1.6 mm, P = .19). The impedance reduction during ablation was similar in HNS and NS (12.3 ± 2.3 Ω vs 12.7 ± 3 Ω, P = .72), as were starting impedance (122 ± 2 Ω vs 123 ± 4.7 Ω, P = .07) and final impedance after ablation (110 ± 2.7 Ω vs 113 ± 7.7 Ω, P = .37).

However, increasing the power setting from 30W to 50W with the temperature-controlled catheter did cause significantly larger lesions when using NS irrigation (depth: 7.5 ± 0.8 mm vs 6.2 ± 0.7 mm, P = .0002; width: 11.8 ± 1.6 mm vs 9.6 ± 1.2 mm, P < .01), showing that the temperature-controlled system can produce larger lesions when operating at higher power with correspondingly higher temperature cutoffs (Figure 2). Impedance reduction, however, was similar in ablation using the temperature-controlled catheter at 50W and 30W with NS (12.8 ± 1.6 Ω vs 12.7 ± 3 Ω, P = .93), as were starting impedances (124 ± 4.9 Ω vs 123 ± 4.7 Ω, P = .47) and final impedance after ablation (114 ± 5.3 Ω vs 113 ± 7.7 Ω, P = .68).

Ablation parameters and power delivery

Critical differences in actual power delivery emerged between irrigation types with the temperature-controlled catheter. When using HNS, mean delivered power was significantly lower than with NS at the 30W setting (23.3W ± 5.4 W vs 27.1 ± 4.6 W; P < .01; Table 2). This power reduction occurred despite identical user-programmed power settings, indicating that the temperature control algorithm frequently decreased output power to maintain the 50°C maximum temperature limit. Concurrently, mean irrigation flow rates were higher with HNS than with NS (8.9 ± 5.5 cc vs 7.1 ± 5.3 cc at 30 W) because the system attempted to manage increasing temperatures through increased cooling.

Infrared thermography findings

Infrared thermal imaging revealed distinct heating patterns between power-controlled and temperature-controlled ablation with HNS irrigation. During power-controlled ablation with HNS, tissue surface temperatures increased rapidly and reached higher peak values than with NS, consistent with increased tissue current density. The thermal profile showed progressive expansion of the heated zone throughout the 30-second application.

During temperature-controlled ablation with HNS, initial tissue heating was similarly rapid, but peak tissue surface temperatures plateaued at levels comparable to NS irrigation. This thermal behavior correlated with the observed reductions in delivered power because the temperature control algorithm limited output to maintain the preset temperature ceiling. The thermal images confirmed that the temperature regulation system effectively prevented excessive tissue heating when using HNS, despite its lower ionic strength (Figure 3).

Steam pop incidence

Steam pops occurred with varying frequency across groups. Using the power-controlled catheter at 30W × 30 seconds, there were more steam pops with HNS than with NS (5/22 lesions, 23% vs 2/20 lesions, 10%; P = .41), although this did not reach statistical significance.

Notably, when comparing HNS irrigation in catheter types at the same nominal 30W power setting, there were fewer steam pops with the temperature-controlled catheter than with the power-controlled catheter (1/19 lesions, 5% vs 5/22 lesions, 23%; P = .09) although again, this did not reach statistical significance.

Among temperature-controlled ablation groups, steam pop rates were similarly low across all comparisons: 30W NS vs 30W HNS (0/20 lesions, 0% vs 1/19 lesions, 5%; P = .31), 30W NS vs 50W NS (0/20 lesions, 0% vs 1/17 lesions, 6%; P = .28), and 50W NS vs 50W HNS (1/17 lesions, 6% vs 1/16 lesions, 7%; P = .97) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study indicates that HNS irrigation does not increase lesion dimensions compared with NS when using a temperature-controlled open-irrigated ablation, in contrast to the significant lesion size benefit observed with power-controlled ablation. This finding has important implications for clinical practice as temperature-controlled ablation systems become more widely adopted.

Mechanism of differential effect

The fundamental reason HNS fails to augment lesion size with temperature-controlled ablation lies in the biophysical interaction between reduced ionic strength irrigation and an automated temperature regulation algorithm. HNS irrigation reduces electrical shunting in the fluid layer surrounding the catheter tip, thereby increasing the proportion of current delivered to tissue rather than dissipating in the irrigant. With power-controlled ablation, this enhanced tissue current delivery directly translates to increased tissue heating and larger lesion dimensions.3,9 However, temperature-controlled systems dynamically modulate power output to maintain catheter tip temperature below preset maximum thresholds.

Our data reveal that when HNS is used with the temperature-controlled catheter, the enhanced current delivery to tissue causes more rapid heating, prompting the temperature control algorithm to reduce power output to maintain the 50°C (at 30 W) or 55°C (at 50 W) temperature limits. The resulting average delivered power with HNS (23.3 W at 30 W setting) was 14% lower than with NS (27.1 W), effectively negating any current augmentation benefit.

Infrared thermography provided direct visualization of this phenomenon, showing that although initial heating was rapid with HNS during temperature-controlled ablation, peak tissue temperatures plateaued at similar levels to NS, consistent with effective temperature regulation through power reduction to limit excessive tissue heating.

Safety implications

Steam pops during RF ablation occur when tissue temperatures exceed 100°C, causing rapid vaporization of intracellular and extracellular water with explosive tissue disruption.10 These events carry risks of cardiac perforation, thrombus formation, and char deposition on the catheter electrode. In this study, we saw a nonsignificant reduction in steam pop incidence with temperature-controlled ablation compared with power-controlled ablation when using HNS (1/19 lesions, 5% vs 5/22 lesions, 23%; P = .09).

Recent clinical series have shown that with careful technique, including power titration to impedance reduction and intracardiac echocardiography monitoring for tissue whitening and microbubble formation, HNS irrigation can be performed safely even with power-controlled ablation.7,8 However, the inherent temperature regulation of temperature-controlled catheters may provide an additional safety margin by preventing excessive tissue heating that can trigger steam pops. The manufacturer-preset temperature cutoffs (50°C at 30W, 55°C at 50W) appear to maintain tissue temperatures well below the steam formation threshold even when using HNS.

The overall steam pop rate of 9% (10/114 applications) likely represents an overestimate compared with clinical practice, given ex vivo tissue lacks normal perfusion-mediated convective cooling and has less heat dissipation. Furthermore, the fixed perpendicular catheter orientation and stable contact force may have promoted more efficient and consistent energy delivery than achievable in beating hearts, potentially increasing steam pop risk.

Clinical implications for lesion size augmentation

These findings suggest that operators seeking to maximize lesion depth for ablation of deep ventricular substrates should not expect increased lesion dimensions from HNS irrigation when using temperature-controlled catheters with preset temperature limits. Strategies to increase lesion size with temperature-controlled systems should instead focus on using higher power settings (which allow higher temperature cutoffs) or considering adjunctive techniques such as bipolar ablation, ethanol ablation, or epicardial approaches for refractory cases.11

Our data confirm that the temperature-controlled catheter can produce larger lesions at 50W than at 30W settings when using the same irrigation type (depth 7.5 mm vs 6.2 mm with NS, P = .0002). This size increase results from the higher 55°C temperature cutoff at 50W, which permits greater sustained power delivery and tissue heating. Therefore, operators using temperature-controlled systems who desire larger lesions should preferentially select higher power settings rather than relying on irrigation fluid composition.

The potential safety benefits of temperature-controlled ablation with HNS may justify its continued use even without lesion size augmentation, particularly in high-risk locations such as near conduction system tissue or in thin-walled structures. The reduced steam pop trend observed in this study supports this safety consideration.

Comparison with previous studies

Previous studies evaluating HNS irrigation with catheters described as temperature-controlled have reported conflicting results. A critical distinction exists between the QDOT MICRO catheter used in the present study and previous generation catheters including Thermocool, Thermocool SF, and TactiCath. The QDOT MICRO incorporates 6 thermocouples embedded directly in the electrode tip at the tissue interface, providing high-fidelity measurement of actual contact temperatures. This enables genuine temperature-controlled operation when the system accurately detects tissue temperature rises and triggers appropriate power reduction to maintain preset temperature limits.

Earlier catheters incorporated temperature sensors located proximally to the electrode tip, positioned several millimeters away from the actual catheter-tissue interface. This remote sensor placement measured temperature in the irrigant-cooled region rather than at the point of tissue contact, potentially underestimating actual tissue interface temperatures. This difference in temperature-sensing fidelity may explain some discrepancies in the literature regarding the effects of HNS irrigation, although direct experimental comparison would be needed to definitively establish the role of temperature sensor location. Our findings are specific to the QDOT MICRO catheter and may not be generalizable to other systems described as temperature controlled.

Study limitations

Several important limitations merit consideration. First, this ex vivo porcine study cannot fully replicate in vivo human conditions. Absence of blood flow eliminates convective cooling that would occur in beating hearts, potentially affecting both absolute lesion dimensions and relative differences between irrigation types. Ex vivo tissue also lacks inflammatory responses, edema formation, and chronic remodeling that influence clinical lesion characteristics. Nevertheless, the model provides a controlled environment to isolate the specific effects of irrigation composition and ablation mode while minimizing confounding variables such as catheter stability, contact force variation, and regional perfusion differences.

This study was exploratory in nature without formal power calculations. Although our sample sizes were sufficient to detect significant differences in the power-controlled catheter group and in comparisons of 30W and 50W temperature-controlled ablations, we cannot exclude the possibility that larger sample sizes might detect smaller but clinically relevant differences between NS and HNS irrigation with temperature-controlled catheters. However, the magnitude of effect observed with HNS in power-controlled ablation (depth increase from 6.5 to 7.5 mm, width increase from 9.4 to 10.5 mm) was not approached in temperature-controlled ablation (depth 6.2 vs 6.1 mm, width 9.6 vs 9.4 mm at 30 W), suggesting that any undetected difference would be clinically modest.

Our findings are specific to the catheter platforms tested (QDOT MICRO for temperature-controlled ablation and Thermocool STSF for power-controlled ablation). Temperature-controlled ablation systems differ in their temperature sensing mechanisms, algorithmic approaches to power modulation, and irrigation control strategies. QDOT MICRO uses 6 embedded tip thermocouples with manufacturer-preset temperature cutoffs that vary by power setting. Other temperature-controlled systems may use different sensor configurations, temperature targets, or control algorithms, which could produce different responses to changes in irrigation osmolarity. Therefore, our conclusions should not be extrapolated to all temperature-controlled or open-irrigated catheter systems without further investigation.

We did not systematically measure myocardial wall thickness at each ablation site. Although ablations were distributed across left ventricular regions in a balanced fashion, we cannot exclude the possibility that regional variations in wall thickness contributed to variance in lesion dimensions.

Second, the steam pop rate observed in this study (9% overall) is likely higher than would be encountered clinically owing to the stable catheter position, fixed perpendicular orientation, absence of tissue perfusion, and absence of cardiac motion. The trend toward reduced steam pops with temperature-controlled ablation (5% vs 23% with HNS at 30W) may therefore underestimate the magnitude of benefit in clinical practice.

Third, the nGen generator does not allow user reprogramming of the maximum temperature cutoffs associated with each power level. The impact of adjustable temperature limits on the efficacy of HNS irrigation with temperature-controlled ablation remains unknown and could be an area for future investigation if such capability becomes available.

Finally, this study evaluated only acute lesion dimensions and steam pop incidence. Chronic lesion characteristics, including scar formation, dimensions over time, and transmurality in different tissue types, were not assessed. In vivo studies with histologic follow-up would be valuable to characterize the long-term lesion characteristics and potential late complications.

Conclusion

Lesion size was not increased using HNS irrigation during ablation with an open-irrigated, temperature-controlled catheter tested in this study (QDOT MICRO), which features embedded tip thermocouples, in contrast to the significant augmentation observed with power-controlled ablation. When using HNS with authentic temperature-controlled systems that accurately measure tissue interface temperature, average power delivery is comparatively lower owing to enforcement of preset maximum temperature cutoffs, effectively blunting the increase in current delivery to tissue expected with HNS irrigation. This temperature regulation may provide safety benefits, as evidenced by a trend toward reduced steam pop incidence with temperature-controlled compared with power-controlled ablation using HNS.

Clinicians using modern temperature-controlled ablation systems with embedded tip temperature sensing should not expect larger lesions from HNS irrigation and should instead consider higher power settings (with correspondingly higher temperature limits) when seeking to maximize lesion depth for ablation of deep ventricular substrates.
