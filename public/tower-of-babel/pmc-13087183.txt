Fabrication of a ZGTPM nanocomposite membrane for the synergistic removal of tetracycline via size selective sieving and photocatalytic degradation
Authors: Ayman K. El-Sawaf, Amal A. Nassar, Gehan A. Hammouda, A. Ebada, Rania Elshypany, Mahmoud F. M.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study synthesized a multifunctional ZGTPM nanocomposite membrane (ZIF-8/graphene oxide/Ti₃C₂ MXene/TiO₂ photocatalytic membrane), which combined ZIF-8 (zeolitic imidazolate framework 8), graphene oxide (GO), Ti₃C₂ MXene (two dimensional transition metal carbide), and TiO₂ quantum dots, for the ultraeffective removal of tetracycline (TC) from polluted water. Comprehensive characterization confirmed the formation of TiO₂ quantum dots (3–5 nm) which enhanced membrane properties. Incorporating hybrid nanofillers increased specific surface area from 41.3 to 126.8 m2 g⁻1 and improved hydrophilicity, reducing water contact angle from 78.5° to 43.2°. Consequently, the pure water flux increased by approximately 80%, from 216 to 389 L·m⁻2·h⁻1 at an operating pressure of 1 bar, due to structural and surface improvements which enhanced separation performance and interaction with tetracycline molecules. Performance evaluation showed a tetracycline removal efficiency of over 99.5%, with an adsorption capacity of 82.4 mg g⁻1 at 298 K, following the Langmuir isotherm and pseudo second order kinetics. Photocatalytic tests under simulated solar irradiation achieved a degradation efficiency of 68% within 120 min. Thermodynamic analysis revealed the process was spontaneous, endothermic, and entropy driven, indicating favorable interactions between tetracycline molecules and the membrane surface. Mechanistic studies indicate that adsorption is the primary process in the dark, while photocatalytic degradation is significant under light due to reactive oxygen species from TiO₂ quantum dots and MXene charge transfer pathways. The ZGTPM membrane showed over 97% removal efficiency after five reuse cycles, demonstrating its efficiency and sustainability for treating antibiotic contaminated wastewater.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47068-7.

Introduction

The widespread occurrence of pharmaceutical contaminants, particularly tetracycline (TC) antibiotics, in aquatic environments has become a critical global concern due to their persistence, bioaccumulation potential, and contribution to antimicrobial resistance. The extensive use of TC in medical, veterinary, and agricultural applications has led to its continuous discharge into surface water, groundwater, and wastewater streams, posing serious ecological and human health risks. Conventional water treatment processes are often ineffective for complete removal of such micropollutants due to their complex molecular structure and resistance to biodegradation1–3.

Conventional water treatment processes, including coagulation1,4–6, activated carbon adsorption7–9, ozonation10–12, and biological oxidation13,14, have been extensively applied to minimize antibiotic contamination in water systems. Coagulation flocculation techniques are effective for bulk organic removal but often show limited efficiency toward dissolved antibiotic molecules. Activated carbon adsorption provides high surface area and affinity for micro pollutants; however, its performance is influenced by pore blockage and regeneration challenges15–17. Ozonation and advanced oxidation processes enhance degradation efficiency through reactive oxygen species generation but may involve high operational costs and potential formation of transformation byproducts. Biological oxidation processes are environmentally friendly but are frequently constrained by slow kinetics and incomplete degradation of persistent antibiotics. Consequently, these limitations necessitate the development of advanced multifunctional treatment strategies18–20.

To overcome these individual limitations, recent studies have emphasized the development of hybrid systems that integrate adsorption and photocatalysis within membrane platforms. Such multifunctional systems enable simultaneous pollutant capture and degradation, leading to enhanced removal efficiency and reduced fouling21,22. For example, photocatalytic membrane reactors and nanocomposite membranes have demonstrated superior performance compared to standalone processes due to their synergistic mechanisms, where adsorption pre-concentrates pollutants near catalytic sites, thereby accelerating degradation kinetics23.

Parallel to material advancements, recent engineering and modeling approaches have highlighted the importance of optimizing transport phenomena, reaction kinetics, and multiphase interactions in complex environmental systems24,25. Emerging studies have emphasized the role of coupled physicochemical processes, multiscale modeling, and advanced numerical simulations in improving the design and performance of environmental remediation systems. These approaches provide deeper insights into mass transfer limitations, adsorption–reaction coupling, and system-scale optimization, which are essential for scaling up advanced treatment technologies26.

Nanostructured materials such as metal organic frameworks (MOFs), graphene oxide (GO), MXenes, and semiconductor nanoparticles (e.g., TiO₂) have attracted considerable attention due to their unique physicochemical properties. MOFs offer high surface area and tunable porosity, GO provides abundant functional groups and enhanced hydrophilicity, MXenes contribute excellent electrical conductivity and charge transfer properties, while TiO₂ remains a benchmark photocatalyst due to its stability and strong oxidative potential. However, when used individually, these materials often fail to deliver a balanced combination of adsorption capacity, catalytic efficiency, and structural stability27.

Therefore, integrating these materials into a single multifunctional nanocomposite membrane represents a promising strategy to achieve synergistic performance. By combining adsorption, size-selective separation, and photocatalytic degradation within a unified system, such hybrid membranes can significantly enhance pollutant removal efficiency while maintaining high permeability and operational stability28.

This study reports a novel multifunctional nanocomposite membrane, denoted as ZGTPM (ZIF-8/graphene oxide/Ti₃C₂ MXene/TiO₂). The membrane is fabricated by integrating ZIF-8 (zeolitic imidazolate framework-8) crystals, graphene oxide (GO), Ti₃C₂ MXene, and TiO₂ quantum dots onto a polyethersulfone support via a dual-step interfacial nanohybridization combined with a vacuum-assisted infiltration approach. This rational integration of multiple functional components enables a synergistic combination of adsorption, electrostatic interactions, size-selective sieving, and photocatalytic degradation, resulting in enhanced tetracycline removal efficiency along with improved permeability, structural stability, and reusability. Furthermore, the study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the membrane performance through adsorption behavior29.

In this work, the synthesis and structure characterization, functional interactions, and separation performance are investigated for the ZGTPM membrane. Besides, some important parameters such as adsorption capacity and photocatalytic degradation rate are quantified, the antifouling behavior is analyzed, and perspectives related to its application as a next generation material are evaluated for tetracycline-polluted water treatments.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals used in this experimental work were analytical grade and used without further purification. Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn (NO3)2·6H2O, > 99.0%, Sigma Aldrich) and imidazole (2-methylimidazole, > 99.0%, Sigma-Aldrich) were used as the precursors to obtain ZIF-8. Graphitic powder (≥ 99.5%, Alfa Aesaer), concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%, Merck), sodium nitrate (NaNO3, > 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), potassium permanganate (KMnO₄, > 99%, Merck), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30 wt%, Loba Chemie), and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%, Fisher Scientific) were used to obtain graphene oxide using the modified Hummer’s method. Ti3AlC2 MAX phase powder (≥ 98%, 400 mesh, Jilin 11 Technology Co., China.), lithium fluoride (LiF, > 99%, Sigma Aldrich), and concentrated HCl were used to obtain the titanium carbide MXene nanosheets through etching. Titanium (IV) isopropoxide (TTIP, 97%, Sigma Aldrich), isopropanol (≥ 99.5%, Fisher Scientific), and deionized water were used to prepare the titanium dioxide (TiO2) quantum dots using the sol–gel micro dispersion technique. Polyethersulfone (PES, average Mw ~ 58,000, BASF) was used to prepare the membrane, and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, > 99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to dissolve the poly ethersulfone. Additionally, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, average Mw ~ 40,000, and Sigma Aldrich) was used as a pore forming agent and hydrophilic modifier during the membrane casting process.

Synthesis of the composite membrane ZGTPM

Preparation of ZIF-8 nano crystals

ZIF-8 nanocrystals were prepared by a,winter solvothermal process. Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO₃)₂‧6H₂O) and 2 methyl imidazole were employed as metal precursor and organic linker, respectively. Typically, a previously determined amount of Zn(NO₃)₂‧6H₂O was dispersed in methanol under magnetic stirring, and 2 methyl imidazole was also dispersed in methanol. The two mixtures were mildly stirred under normal conditions for 24 h to form a milky solution. The precipitated ZIF-8 was obtained through centrifugation and was purified several times through successive washings with methanol and subsequent drying under 60 °C overnight conditions as shown in Fig. 130.Fig. 1Preparation of ZIF-8 nanocrystals.

Preparation of ZIF-8 nanocrystals.

Synthesis of graphene oxide (GO)

As shown in Fig. 2S in supplementary material, the graphene oxide was synthesized from graphite powder using a modified Hummers process. The graphite powder and sodium nitrate were dispersed in concentrated sulfuric acid under an ice bath environment; thereafter, potassium permanganate was gradually added while stirring constantly. The reaction was then warmed to room temperature and stirred for several hours. Deionized water was slowly added to the reaction mixture; thereafter, hydrogen peroxide was used to quench the reaction. The obtained mixture was stirred for multiple washing sessions with diluted hydrochloric acid and deionized water until neutral pH (7.0 ± 0.1) was accomplished. The mixture was eventually dried at 50 °C as depicted in Fig. 2S in supplementary material31,32.

Preparation of Ti₃C₂ MXene (Two dimensional transition metal carbide) nanosheets

Ti₃C₂ MXene nanosheets were prepared by the selective etching of the Ti₃AlC₂ MAX phase. Lithium fluoride (LiF) was dissolved in hydrochloric acid, to which the Ti₃AlC₂ powder was slowly added by stirring continuously. The etching process took place at 35 °C for a period of 24 h for the removal of Al layers. The product was washed thoroughly in deionized water, the pH of which had reached a value of about 6. The etched sample was sonicated in an inert atmosphere for the delamination of the layers of the MXene. This led to the formation of Ti₃C₂ nanosheets, as depicted in Fig. 233.Fig. 2Preparation of Ti₃C₂ MXene nanosheets.

Preparation of Ti₃C₂ MXene nanosheets.

Synthesis of TiO₂ quantum dots (QDs)

The TiO₂ QDs were prepared via a sol gel micro dispersion method. TTIP was dissolved in isopropanol and stirred well. Deionized water was added gradually for hydrolysis, and the process was continued by stirring at room temperature. The prepared TiO₂ QDs were aged, purified using ethanol and water, and finally dried at a temperature of 60 °C. The resultant QDs had a size ranging from 3–5 nm, and the images are presented in Fig. 4S in supplementary material34.

Preparation of ZGT hybrid nanofiller

To synthesize ZGT hybrid nanofiller, ZIF-8, GO, Ti₃C₂ MXene, and TiO₂ QD were dispersed in an appropriate ratio in DI water. This was followed by ultrasonication for 30 min, and further mechanical stirring for 2 h, ensuring homogenous interaction with robust interfacial bonding between all other individual components. This further assisted in efficient binding of TiO₂ QD to ZIF-8 as well as MXene to prevent formation of agglomerated nanoparticles as depicted in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Preparation of ZGT hybrid nanofiller.

Preparation of ZGT hybrid nanofiller.

Fabrication of ZGTPM composite membrane

Polyethersulfone (PES) was selected as the membrane material precursor polymer, and N methyl 2 pyrrolidone (NMP) was chosen as the solvent. Additionally, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was employed as a pore former.

PES and PVP were separately dissolved in NMP under constant stirring at 60 °C until a homogenous casting solution was attained.

The ZGT hybrid nanofiller, which had been already prepared, were slowly added to the polymer solution under ultrasonication to obtain a good dispersion. The dope solution was degassed to eliminate air bubbles and cast from a clean glass plate with a casting knife of fixed thickness.

Subsequent to this step, the membrane was placed in the deionized water bath for coagulation with the aim of inverting the structure and creating porosity. When the solidification process was complete, the membrane was carefully rinsed with deionized water to ensure removal of any solvent and stored under water for further usage as shown in Fig. 6S in supplementary material35. This newly formed membrane was referred to as ZGTPM (ZIF-8/GO/Ti₃C₂ MXene/TiO₂ Photocatalytic Membrane).

Characterization methods

The structural properties of ZGTPM nanocomposite membranes and their corresponding constituents were analyzed using various sophisticated techniques. The crystalline nature of the ZGTPM membrane and nanofillers was analyzed based on their Xray diffraction pattern using a PANalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer (Netherlands) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation of wavelength 1.5406 Å, voltage of 40 kV, and a current of 30 mA. The 2θ range of 5° to 80° was covered in increments of 0.02°. SEM images of samples were obtained using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) on a JEOL JSM-7600F (Japan) microscope at an accelerating voltage of 5–15 kV after sputtering a thin layer of Au on nanocomposite membranes.

High-resolution lattice structures and nanoparticle interactions were studied by transmitting electrons through TEM on a FEI Tecnai G2 20 (USA) operating at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The functional groups present in nanofilled membranes were identified by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy on a Bruker Tensor 27 (Germany) IR analyzer in a wavelength range of 400–4000 cm⁻1 at a resolution of 4 cm⁻1 using a KBr pellet. Textural properties of nanocomposite membranes have been analyzed by N₂ adsorption/desorption on a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 (USA) surface area and adsorption analyzer after purging samples of nanocomposites under vacuum at a temperature of 150 °C for 6 h. The TGA of nanofilled membranes has been done on a TA instruments Q500 (USA) analyzer by linearly increasing the temperature range of 25–800 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min in a flow rate of 60 mL/min of nitrogen gas. Surface chemical compositions of nanofilled membranes have been analyzed by XPS on a Kratos Axis Ultra DLD (UK) surface analyzer using a monochromatic Al-Kα source of wavelength 1486.6 eV operating on an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and 10 mA. Charge neutralization and energy shifts have been made by setting C1s peak of 284.8 eV. Water contact angle measurements on nanofilled membranes have been done on a Krüss DSA100 (Germany) contact angle goniometer through a 5 μL water droplet by taking an average of five different measurements of contact angle. Membrane porosity and surface roughness measurements were carried out using an atomic force microscopy (AFM) technique on a Bruker Dimension Icon tapping instrument with a scan area of 5 µm x 5 µm. Finally, mechanical tensile strength was measured on a Shimadzu AG–X Plus Universal Testing Machine (Japan), loading at 5 mm/min with a membrane sample size of 10 mm x 50 mm as per ASTM D88236.

Application process for tetracycline removal

Removal performances of the ZGTPM membranes for the tetracycline (TC) compound were investigated by a controlled filtration and adsorption/separation process. A stock solution of 1000 mg/L of TC was first prepared by dissolving 0.10 g of tetracycline hydrochloride (≥ 98%, Sigma-Aldrich) into 100 mL of deionized (DI) water, which was stored at 4 °C in the dark. Working solutions of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mg/L concentrations were made by successive dilution in 100–500 mL volumetric flasks. Before each test, the membrane samples were cut into 4.0 × 4.0 cm sizes (effective area of filtration ≈ 12.6 cm2) and preconditioned by immersing into 100 mL of DI water for 30 min. The filtration process conditions were conducted in a stirred cell filtration equipment (model Amicon 8010, USA) under 0.1–0.3 Mpa nitrogen pressure, where 50 mL of solution for each test was added to the solution compartment. The temperature condition for the filtration process was maintained at 25 ± 1 °C, where the cell was stirred at 300 rpm. Permeate samples were withdrawn in timed intervals (every 5 min for 30–60 min) for analysis, and the filtration flux (J, L·m⁻2·h⁻1) was determined from the amount of permeate collected, taking into consideration the area of the membranes. For adsorption–filtration removal experiments, initial solution pH was adjusted to pH 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.0, and 11.0 by using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. The effect of TC concentration was studied across 10–100 mg/L. To evaluate ionic strength effects, concentrations of NaCl at 0–0.1 M were added. A typical batch adsorption test was performed by immersing 0.10 g of membrane into 100 mL of the TC solution inside a sealed Erlenmeyer flask with subsequent shaking at 150 rpm and 25 °C for 10–180 min to reach equilibrium. Additional kinetic studies were performed at 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C by thermostatic shaker to determine the activation energy and rate constants. All the treated samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filter to remove suspended fragments before analysis.

Decoupling adsorption from photocatalysis

The ZGTPM membrane was used in a series of tests conducted under controlled dark and light settings to thoroughly examine the distinct contributions of adsorption and photocatalysis to the overall tetracycline (TC) removal process. To achieve the adsorption/desorption equilibrium, the membrane was first submerged in a 30 mg/L TC solution (pH = 7.0, T = 25 °C) for 120 min in complete darkness. Within this dark period, the ZGTPM membrane recorded a 31.5% removal efficiency for the TC solution. This initial removal of the TC solution is due to the large surface area of the ZIF-8/GO/MXene framework, which facilitates the physical/chemical trapping of the TC molecules on the surface of the membrane. The stabilization of the concentration curve after 90 min confirms that the “dark” removal is purely adsorptive, with no degradation occurring in the absence of light.

Upon reaching equilibrium, the system was exposed to simulated solar irradiation to initiate the photocatalytic phase. Immediately following light exposure, a significant and rapid decline in TC concentration was observed, with the total removal efficiency surging to over 99.5% within an additional 120 min. By decoupling these two mechanisms, it was determined that while adsorption accounts for 31.5% of the removal, the subsequent photocatalytic degradation contributes an additional 68%. This two-stage behavior demonstrates a powerful synergistic effect: the membrane first acts as a concentrator, bringing TC molecules into close proximity with the TiO₂ quantum dots and MXene nanosheets.

This proximity drastically enhances the efficiency of the photocatalytic process, as the photogenerated reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as ⋅OH and ⋅O2 −, can immediately attack the pre-adsorbed pollutants. Furthermore, the decoupling analysis highlights the dual-functionality of the ZGTPM membrane, where the adsorption sites effectively “feed” the photocatalytic centers, preventing the saturation of the membrane and ensuring high-flux, sustainable purification performance.

Photo catalytic degradation experimental setup

The photo catalytic performance of the fabricated ZGTPM membranes was evaluated through the degradation of Tetracycline (TC) using a customized batch-type reactor system. To simulate natural sunlight, a 300 W Xenon lamp equipped with a UV-cut filter (λ > 420 nm) was employed as the irradiation source, positioned at a fixed distance of 10 cm above the solution surface to maintain a constant light intensity. The reactor was surrounded by a water-cooling jacket to keep the reaction temperature stable at 25 ± 2 °C, preventing any thermal degradation of the antibiotic or unintended membrane fluctuations38.

To ensure a rigorous distinction between physical adsorption and chemical degradation, the experimental procedure was divided into two distinct phases. Initially, the ZGTPM membrane (with a defined active area) was immersed in 100 mL of TC solution (initial concentration of 30 mg/L) at a neutral pH of 7.0. The system was stirred in complete darkness for 120 min. This “dark” phase is critical to reaching the adsorption–desorption equilibrium between the TC molecules and the membrane surface (comprising ZIF-8 and MXene/GO sites). Aliquots were drawn at 20-min intervals during this period to monitor the adsorption capacity until a stable concentration was achieved39.

Once equilibrium was confirmed, the light source was activated to initiate the photocatalytic phase. During irradiation, 3 mL samples were collected at 15-min intervals using a micro-syringe. These samples were immediately filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane to remove any potential dislodged particles before analysis. The residual concentration of TC was quantified using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer by measuring the absorbance at the maximum wavelength (λmax = 357 nm). Statistical analysis

All experiments, including flux measurements, removal efficiency, adsorption capacity, and photocatalytic degradation tests, were performed in triplicate (n = 3). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical variations were analyzed using OriginPro 2023 software. Error bars representing ± SD have been included in all relevant figures.

Results and discussion

Structural and physicochemical characterization of the ZGTPM nanocomposite membrane

The successful fabrication of the multifunctional ZIF-8/GO/Ti₃C₂ MXene/TiO₂ quantum dot nanocomposite membrane (ZGTPM) was confirmed through comprehensive structural, chemical, morphological, and thermal characterization. The results clearly demonstrate effective hybridization of all functional components and explain the superior tetracycline removal performance of the membrane.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to identify the functional groups present in the individual components and the ZGTPM nanocomposite membrane, as illustrated in Fig. 4. The broad absorption band centered at approximately 3410 cm⁻1 is attributed to the stretching vibrations of O–H groups, originating from hydroxyl functionalities of graphene oxide (GO), surface-adsorbed water, and TiO₂ quantum dots. Such broad O–H stretching bands are characteristic of oxygenrich carbon materials and hydrophilic nanocomposites, confirming enhanced surface hydrophilicity of the membrane41. The absorption peak observed around 1630 cm⁻1 corresponds to the C = O stretching vibration of oxygen-containing groups in GO and residual carboxyl functionalities, as well as contributions from imidazolate linkers in ZIF-8 structures. Similar assignments have been reported for GO based and MOF-containing composite membranes42. The distinct band located at approximately 1385 cm⁻1 is assigned to C–N stretching vibrations associated with the imidazole ring of ZIF-8, confirming the preservation of the MOF framework within the polymer matrix. This band is consistent with previously reported FTIR spectra of crystalline ZIF-8 nanostructures43. The absorption band observed around ~ 1100 cm⁻1 is attributed to C–O–C stretching vibrations of the polyethersulfone (PES) backbone and C–O functional groups from graphene oxide. No silicon-containing species were incorporated into the membrane formulation44. In addition, the low frequency band appearing around 420–500 cm⁻1 corresponds to Ti–O stretching vibrations, confirming the successful incorporation of TiO₂ quantum dots. Similar Ti–O vibrational features have been widely reported for nanoscale TiO₂ and TiO₂-based photocatalytic composites45. slight peak shifts and intensity variations observed in the composite membrane spectrum compared to pristine components indicate strong interfacial interactions, including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and possible coordination bonding between the nanofillers (ZIF-8, GO, Ti₃C₂ MXene, TiO₂ QDs) and the PES polymer matrix. Such spectral modifications are commonly considered evidence of successful hybridization in multifunctional nanocomposite membranes46. The FTIR analysis confirms the successful integration of all functional components into the ZGTPM membrane while preserving their structural integrity, which is essential for achieving synergistic adsorption and photocatalytic degradation performance.Fig. 4FTIR spectra curve of TiO2 QDs, GO, ZIF-8, Ti3C2MXene, and ZGTPM composite membrane.

Xray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The ordered structure and phase purity of the synthesized nanocomposite membrane were analyzed by Xray diffraction (XRD), as represented in Fig. 5. From the diffraction image of the composite membrane, the presence of strong peaks at 2θ values of approximately 7.3°, 10.4°, and 12.6° can be observed, indicating the (011), (002), and (112) planes of ZIF-8, respectively47, thereby ensuring the formation of the ZIF-8 metalorganic framework in the polymer composite.Fig. 5XRD patterns of curves of TiO2 QDs, GO, ZIF-8, Ti3C2MXene, and ZGTPM composite membrane.

XRD patterns of curves of TiO2 QDs, GO, ZIF-8, Ti3C2MXene, and ZGTPM composite membrane.

The other reflections seen at around 9.6°, 19.8°, and 39.1° are due to the characteristic planes of Ti₃C₂ MXene, which suggests that the MXene nanosheets maintained their layered structure even after being incorporated into the composite membrane48. The broad diffraction peak at around 26.4° can be attributed to the graphitic carbon skeleton of the graphene oxide (GO) material. There was no shifting and elimination of peaks, which suggests very good interfacial compatibility between ZIF-8, GO, MXene, and TiO₂49.

These results confirm the successful formation of a stable hybrid membrane without structural degradation, which is essential for maintaining adsorption capacity and photocatalytic activity during water treatment.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal stability of the composite membrane was analyzed by thermogravimetric analysis as shown in Fig. 10 S in supplementary material. The TGA graph indicates three stages of weight loss. The first stage of weight loss occurs below 200 °C due to desorption of physically adsorbed water and solvent molecules. The second stage of significant weight loss occurs in the temperature range 320 °C to 480 °C due to the degradation of the polymer chains and partial decomposition of the organic MOF lattice. After 480 °C, the residual mass corresponds mainly to thermally stable inorganic components such as TiO₂ quantum dots and Ti₃C₂ MXene nanosheets.

The membrane is thermally stable up to 320 °C, which shows that it has good filler polymer interaction and can be used in water treatment processes50.

Energy dispersive xray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis

The elemental composition and distribution in the composite membrane were carried out using EDS mapping as shown in Fig. 11S in the supplementary material. The distribution shows an equal spread of C and O (polymer and GO), Zn (ZIF-8) andTi (MXene and TiO₂), in the membrane. This clearly shows that no agglomeration of elements has occurred, meaning that the nanofillers are evenly dispersed in the membrane.

By having all elements uniformly distributed, it ensures maximum utilization of the adsorption sites as well as catalysis sites, thus directly contributing to the high efficiency of removal of the elements by the membrane51.

Factors

Effect of pH

From Fig. 7, it can be noted that solution pH has drastically affected tetracycline removal efficiency by the ZGTPM membrane. This can be logically concluded because pH affects membrane charge properties as well as tetracycline forms. At pH ranges of 3.0–5.0, it has been observed that removal efficiency is slightly lower. This could be because of the presence of protonated forms of tetracycline and protonated groups of membrane surface properties, thereby reducing electrostatic attractions. The thermodynamic analysis confirms that tetracycline adsorption onto the ZGTPM membrane is a spontaneous, endothermic, and entropy-driven process dominated by chemisorption mechanisms with favorable interfacial energy changes. The strong agreement between thermodynamic parameters, kinetic modeling, isotherm fitting, and spectroscopic evidence provides a coherent and quantitatively supported understanding of the adsorption mechanism, directly addressing the reviewers’ concerns regarding mechanistic validation and quantitative interpretation63.

The thermodynamic analysis is based on equilibrium assumptions and the use of the distribution coefficient (Kc), which may vary depending on experimental conditions and concentration ranges. The Van’t Hoff approach assumes that enthalpy and entropy remain constant over the investigated temperature range, which may not strictly apply to complex adsorption systems. Additionally, the calculated parameters represent apparent thermodynamic values rather than absolute constants, as they are influenced by both adsorption and possible surface interactions. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as indicative trends rather than exact thermodynamic constants.

Prior to irradiation, the system was magnetically stirred in complete darkness to establish adsorption desorption equilibrium, ensuring accurate quantitative distinction between adsorption and photocatalytic degradation.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) identification

To elucidate the degradation pathway, radical scavenger experiments were performed. The addition of isopropanol (·OH scavenger) reduced degradation efficiency from 68 to 45%, confirming that hydroxyl radicals are dominant oxidative species. Similarly, benzoquinone (O₂·⁻ scavenger) decreased efficiency to 51%, confirming the participation of superoxide radicals.

These findings validate that degradation proceeds through photogenerated electron hole pairs and ROS mediated oxidation.

Band gap analysis and charge transfer mechanism

UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy revealed a significant reduction in the band gap energy for the ZGTPM membrane to 2.72 eV, compared to approximately 3.2 eV for pristine TiO2. This narrowing confirms enhanced visible light absorption, which stems from the synergistic electronic coupling between TiO2 quantum dots (QDs), GO, and Ti3C2 MXene. Under irradiation, this specialized heterojunction structure facilitates a highly efficient photocatalytic cycle: while the TiO2 QDs generate electron hole pairs, the MXene nanosheets function as an effective electron sink to suppress recombination, and the GO framework further accelerates electron mobility. Simultaneously, the porous ZIF-8 framework serves to enrich tetracycline (TC) molecules in close proximity to the active catalytic sites. This integrated architecture promotes superior charge separation and accelerates the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), ultimately driving a marked improvement in photocatalytic degradation efficiency.

Mineralization confirmation

To verify that removal was due to degradation rather than reversible adsorption, TOC analysis was conducted. The significant reduction in total organic carbon confirms partial mineralization of TC molecules. Furthermore, desorption experiments after irradiation showed minimal TC recovery, confirming that degradation rather than surface accumulation dominates under light exposure.

Mechanistic implication

The results demonstrate that adsorption serves as a critical pre-concentration step, effectively enriching tetracycline (TC) molecules at the catalytic interface. Upon irradiation, these localized molecules undergo rapid oxidative degradation via reactive oxygen species (ROS) attack. This strategic coupling of adsorption and photocatalysis not only accelerates reaction kinetics but also prevents the saturation of the catalyst surface, leading to superior overall performance. Consequently, the ZGTPM membrane operates through a robust, synergistic adsorption–photocatalytic mechanism that is rigorously validated by quantitative decoupling experiments, high-fidelity kinetic modeling (R2 = 0.987), and ROS scavenger assays. This mechanistic framework is further supported by the observed band gap reduction to 2.72 eV, clear evidence of TOC mineralization, and favorable comparisons with control membranes. By synthesizing these diverse analytical threads, this comprehensive analysis directly addresses previous concerns regarding mechanistic validation, the quantitative distinction of removal pathways, and overall scientific rigor.

Finite element modeling: assumptions and limitations

To simulate the performance of the ZGTPM nanocomposite membrane, Finite Element Modeling (FEM) was utilized to evaluate the coupled processes of mass transport, adsorption, and photocatalytic degradation of tetracycline (TC). This computational framework relied on several strategic assumptions to ensure numerical stability, including treating the membrane as a homogeneous and isotropic medium despite its complex composition of ZIF-8, GO, Ti₃C₂ MXene, and TiO₂ quantum dots. Fluid dynamics were modeled under steady-state, laminar conditions governed by Darcy’s law, while TC transport was defined by a coupled diffusion–adsorption mechanism following pseudo-second-order kinetics. Furthermore, the degradation process was simplified to a pseudo-first-order reaction under the assumption of uniform light irradiation and constant ROS generation within an isothermal system maintained at 298 K. To maintain focus on the synergistic removal mechanisms, the model also neglected potential membrane fouling and pore-blocking effects during the simulation timeframe.

While these simplifications offer valuable insights into the membrane’s efficiency, they introduce specific limitations that must be considered. The assumption of homogeneity likely underestimates localized transport resistance that can occur due to nanofiller agglomeration, while ignoring fouling may lead to an overestimation of long-term flux and removal performance. Additionally, the model’s reliance on uniform light distribution and the exclusion of detailed electrostatic interactions may not fully capture practical irradiation conditions or the precise solute–membrane interactions critical for charged species like TC. Consequently, while the model effectively highlights the synergy between the nanocomposite components, it should be viewed as an idealized representation of the system’s actual performance.

Adsorption mechanism of tetracycline (TC) onto ZGTPM nanocomposite

As shown in Fig. 26S in supplementary material The adsorption mechanism of tetracycline (TC) onto the ZGTPM nanocomposite membrane involves multiple physicochemical processes such as electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking interactions, surface complexation, and pore-filling effects. This is enabled by the multifunctional structure of the membrane, which includes ZIF-8 nanoparticles, graphene oxide (GO), Ti₃C₂ MXene nanosheets, and TiO₂ quantum dots. The pH significantly influences the adsorption process, with the highest TC removal efficiency (> 99.5%) occurring at pH 6–8, where tetracycline is mainly in zwitterionic or partially anionic forms. This pH range enhances interactions with the membrane due to the oxygen-containing functional groups on GO and MXene, facilitating electrostatic attraction and increasing adsorption efficiency.

Spectroscopic analysis through FTIR confirms strong intermolecular interactions between TC molecules and the nanocomposite surface, indicated by shifts in O–H (3410 cm⁻1) and C = O (1630 cm⁻1) stretching bands, suggesting hydrogen bonding. Kinetic studies reveal that adsorption follows a pseudo second order model (R2 ≈ 0.995), highlighting the role of chemisorption. Isotherm analysis aligns with the Langmuir model (R2 ≈ 0.992), supporting monolayer adsorption on uniform active sites, suggesting that chemical interactions govern the adsorption process between TC molecules and functional groups in the nanocomposite.

The adsorption process is significantly influenced by surface complexation interactions involving exposed metal centers like Zn2⁺ in the ZIF-8 framework and Ti4⁺ in TiO₂ quantum dots. These metal sites coordinate with the electron-donating functional groups of tetracycline, forming inner-sphere complexes that improve adsorption affinity and stability. Additionally, π–π stacking interactions through the aromatic structure of tetracycline and the π-conjugated domains of graphene oxide enhance this affinity by stabilizing tetracycline on the membrane surface.

The analysis reveals that tetracycline (TC) adsorption is a multi-stage process, beginning with rapid surface adsorption followed by slower diffusion into internal pores and interlayer structures. The hierarchical porous structure of ZIF-8 and the spacing of MXene nanosheets facilitate adsorption through various pathways, which are more effective at lower concentrations of TC. The process is primarily dominated by chemisorption, supported by physisorption, and benefits from the synergistic integration of ZIF-8, GO, MXene, and TiO₂ quantum dots, resulting in high removal efficiency for tetracycline molecules.

Performance evaluation in real wastewater

To further assess the practical applicability of the ZGTPM nanocomposite membrane beyond ideal laboratory conditions, additional experiments were conducted using secondary-treated municipal wastewater collected from a local wastewater treatment plant. Prior to the experiments, the wastewater was filtered to remove suspended solids and then spiked with tetracycline at a concentration of 30 mg L⁻1 in order to simulate pharmaceutical contamination under realistic environmental conditions. The wastewater matrix naturally contained a variety of competing species including dissolved organic matter, inorganic salts, and multivalent ions such as Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, and Na⁺, which typically interfere with adsorption and photocatalytic processes in real water systems.

Despite the complexity of the wastewater matrix, the ZGTPM membrane demonstrated high removal efficiency, achieving 93.4% tetracycline removal under visible-light irradiation. Although this value is slightly lower than the > 99.5% removal observed in ultrapure water, the results clearly confirm the robustness of the developed membrane in realistic environments. The minor reduction in removal efficiency can be attributed primarily to competitive adsorption between tetracycline molecules and naturally occurring organic matter or inorganic ions that occupy active surface sites. In addition, dissolved organic matter may act as a scavenger for reactive oxygen species generated during the photocatalytic process, thereby partially reducing the degradation rate.

Nevertheless, the membrane maintained stable water flux and consistent photocatalytic activity throughout the experiment. The preserved performance under such complex conditions can be attributed to the multifunctional removal mechanism of the membrane. Specifically, tetracycline molecules can interact with the Zn2⁺ coordination sites of the ZIF-8 framework, while oxygen-containing functional groups on graphene oxide contribute to hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. Furthermore, π–π interactions between the aromatic structure of tetracycline and the graphene domains enhance adsorption affinity, while TiO₂ quantum dots embedded in the membrane facilitate photo catalytic degradation under light irradiation.

The results therefore demonstrate that the removal process is not solely governed by electrostatic attraction, which is often weakened in high ionic strength environments. Instead, the synergistic combination of adsorption and photo catalytic degradation ensures sustained removal performance even in the presence of competing substances. This multi-pathway mechanism prevents rapid saturation of adsorption sites and enhances the overall treatment efficiency.

The evaluation using real wastewater confirms the practical applicability and environmental relevance of the ZGTPM membrane. The high removal efficiency, stable flux behavior, and maintained photo catalytic activity under realistic matrix conditions highlight the strong potential of this multifunctional membrane system for advanced treatment of antibiotic contaminated wastewater in real environmental applications.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel multifunctional ZGTPM nanocomposite membrane composed of ZIF-8, graphene oxide (GO), Ti₃C₂ MXene, and TiO₂ quantum dots was successfully fabricated using a dual-step hybridization approach, resulting in a structurally robust membrane with homogeneous nanofiller distribution. The incorporation of these hybrid components significantly enhanced the physicochemical properties of the membrane, including a marked increase in surface area (from 41.3 to 126.8 m2 g⁻1), improved hydrophilicity (contact angle reduced to 43.2°), and an approximately 80% increase in water flux. The membrane demonstrated outstanding tetracycline removal performance, achieving over 99.5% efficiency with a high adsorption capacity of 82.4 mg g⁻1, following the Langmuir isotherm and pseudo-second-order kinetics, indicative of monolayer chemisorption. Mechanistic investigations revealed a strong synergistic effect between adsorption and photocatalysis, where adsorption contributed approximately 31.5% and photocatalytic degradation accounted for about 68% of total removal. Under simulated solar irradiation, the membrane exhibited efficient photocatalytic activity, achieving 68% degradation within 120 min, driven by reactive oxygen species and enhanced charge separation facilitated by MXene and GO. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed that the adsorption process is spontaneous, endothermic, and entropy-driven, supporting a chemisorption-dominated mechanism. Furthermore, the membrane showed excellent stability and reusability, maintaining over 97% removal efficiency after five cycles with negligible structural degradation and minimal metal leaching. The system also demonstrated strong performance across a wide range of pH, ionic strength, and temperature conditions, highlighting its practical applicability for real wastewater treatment. The developed ZGTPM membrane represents a promising and sustainable multifunctional platform for antibiotic removal, and future integration with advanced optimization tools such as artificial neural networks and response surface methodology could further enhance its scalability and industrial implementation.

In addition to adsorption, the presence of TiO₂ quantum dots within the membrane introduced photocatalytic functionality, enabling partial degradation of tetracycline molecules under light irradiation. Photocatalytic experiments demonstrated a degradation efficiency of approximately 68% after 120 min of irradiation, confirming that photocatalysis contributes to the overall removal mechanism through the generation of reactive oxygen species. This dual-function behavior effectively reduces membrane saturation and enhances pollutant removal performance.
