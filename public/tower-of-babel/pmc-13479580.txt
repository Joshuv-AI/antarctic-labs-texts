Epineuronal space: an emerging hidden hierarchy bridges neuron and mind space
Authors: Yi-Xi Zhang, Qiang-Long You, Xiang-Dong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergent nature of life hinders reductionist paradigms of mapping microscale fundamental components with macroscale phenotypes. Such dilemma of mapping problems can be resolved through seeking hidden hierarchies among them. In developmental biology, the introduction of epigenotype highlighting developmental tissue scale interactions bridges the gap between phenotype and genotype, providing an instance for coping with mapping problems. As an analogy, we summarize a space above neuron level, named as epineuronal space, which represents an organization form constructed by neural assemblies, oscillations, their collective dynamics and working states. This hidden hierarchy is hypothesized to bridge neuron and mind space in a paradigm combining systemic and reductionist approaches. That is, to elucidate organization forms among epineuronal space, and to dissect the latter into circuit and neuron level. Advent of techniques like Neuropixel enables us to construct such space, making this paradigm a testable frame.Keywords: Epineuronal space, Neuron-mind mapping, Emergence, Neural oscillations, Systems biology, Reductionism, Weinberg explanatory arrows

Introduction to mapping problems

A central purpose of modern biology is to establish mappings between fundamental components and complex phenotypes, as exemplified by genotype–phenotype and neuron–mind mapping. Through the powerful lens of reductionism, complex macroscale phenotypes can be deconstructed and understood in terms of their fundamental constituents. In neurobiology, foundational ideas about neural doctrine date back to the era of Santiago Ramón Cajal [1]. These were later followed by the proposal of conceptual neurons exemplified by Jerome Lettvin’s “grandmother cell” hypothesis and subsequently by the identification of category-responsive neurons in the limbic system as well as neurons encoding abstract concepts in the medial temporal lobe [2, 3]. Additionally, Giacomo Rizzolatti recognized the so-called “mirror neuron” in macaque premotor cortex, firing when observing grip, providing a theory of mirror neurons in empathy [4, 5]. Coincidentally, John O’Keefe, Jim Rank and their colleagues also identified neurons responsive to place, head direction and space boundary in a paradigm akin to natural neuroscience [6–9]. These achievements aim at establishing a mapping from a set of basic components to a set of holistic phenotypes. Nevertheless, mapping efforts now encounter a bottleneck as the relationships have grown increasingly sophisticated: a single fundamental element can diverge into multiple phenotypes, and conversely, one phenotype may arise from various fundamental elements. Such a mapping problem holds true across biology fields, as investigations in basic elements like neurons and genes, have revealed tremendous functional heterogeneity between them and corresponding phenotypes [10,11]. Generally, the complex nature of organisms often hinders the effectiveness of reductionist paradigm, which seeks to directly deconstruct macroscopic phenotypes into their purported microscale constituents. Hence, shifting current paradigm toward a new integrative approach to understand such mapping problems is rather requisite.

Emergence, reductionism and systemic organization

As Ludwig von Bertalanffy, who pioneered systems theory in biology, stated: “The aim is the statement of exact laws, which, according to the essential characteristics of living phenomena, must to a large extent, have the nature of system laws…It seems to be the task which is set to our age, to accomplish in biology that ‘Copernican revolution’” [12]. Hence, the very issue we shall cope with is what the essential characteristics of living organisms are? This question could be brought together with an old, classic platitude, which is favored by people opposed to the theory of evolution, that why can’t a sophisticated watch be naturally evolved while the much more complex structure of organisms can? The answer lies in the self-organizing, dissipative nature of organisms [13, 14]. Accordingly, processes of self-organization can be best exemplified by epigenesis transforming fertilized egg to an individual [15]. Moreover, self-organizing patterning and morphogenesis can even emerge in vitro through elegant physical and chemical interplay. Utilizing three-dimensional suspension stem cell culture, Yoshiki Sasai reported self-organizing formation of optic cup structure [16, 17]. Apart from epigenesis, the brain is also a highly self-organized system with neural assemblies firing under local and long-range coordination [18–21]. Additionally, self-organization prevails even in subcellular level (i.e. macromolecule assembly) [22–25]. In a review of the year preceding his passing, Sasai stressed the importance and universality of self-organization phenomena in organisms from virus particle assembly to morphogenesis of complex structure [24]. Hence it can be inferred that, Bertalanffy’s “essential characteristics of living phenomena” seems to be self-organization.

Self-organization will lead to the concept of emergence, which is defined as “a qualitative property that can only occur in the limit that the number of microscopic constituents tends to infinity” by condensed matter physicist Steven Kivelson [24, 26]. Through self-organization, numerous foundational components can be integrated to form a holistic structure exerting qualitative property inexplicable solely by components quantitatively. Now, we can logically assert that macroscale phenotypes of organisms abound with emergence phenomenon. Given that the brain is an organization of neuron networks, it follows that the mind is an emergent phenomenon produced by their coordination [11]. And this also holds true for relationships between genes and higher phenotypes. Thus, the major problem haunted mapping works in the biology fields is emergence.

The concept of emergence highlights how broken symmetry across hierarchical levels reveals the limitations of reductionism [27, 28]. As Bertalanffy argued, principles governing lower levels cannot be directly extrapolated to higher ones, which instead require a more generalized theoretical framework [12]. This idea was later famously encapsulated by condense matter physicist Philip Anderson in his motto “More is different”, proposed two decades after Bertalanffy [27]. Together, these perspectives underscore that emergent properties in nature are, at least partly, irreducible [29]. Nevertheless, in contrast to radical antireductionism thoughts, we suppose reductionism is still an essential paradigm for studying organism as the mapping problem is in essence a reductionism problem. As subjective reductionism stressed by Steven Weinberg, the explanatory arrows will ultimately converge to fundamental fields [30]. In biology, such “Weinberg explanatory arrow” projects from mind to neuron then to collective behaviors of ion channels, as well as from complex structure genesis to gene regulatory networks. Thus, as proposed by Weinberg, we cannot well accept a theory interpreting the mind independent of neurons [31]. Besides, biologists shall not abandon the paradigm of reductionism to devote themselves into mathematical games detached from real world. Instead of an either-or choice, a paradigm that integrates both reductionist and systemic approach should be considered. For biological systems, they not only possess bottom-up construction using basic components, but also preserve top-down organization forms [32, 33]. In such hierarchical systems, emergence of phenotype is determined by their basic units, organization forms and holistic structure [12, 33].

Aiming to establish such approaches, a hierarchical reduction and integration paradigm should be adopted. Specifically, a reductionist Weinberg explanatory arrow projects from phenotype space to subspace then to subspace of subspace, resembling reduction process. Conversely, the fundamental hierarchy is highly organized and subjected to mutual interaction and organization forms of higher space, resembling systemic process (Fig. 1). Thus, to decipher complex phenotypes, it is essential to deconstruct them into distinct hierarchical levels. For instance, uncovering the hidden hierarchy connecting genotype to phenotype will help mitigate the persistent challenges posed by reductionist-only approaches. Furthermore, the interrelationships and organizing forms among elements within and across hierarchies must be integrated into a comprehensive, systemic diagram [33].

An integrative paradigm combining systemic and reductionist approach. Higher hierarchy is formed by subspaces, while the latter are subjected to organization of the former. A hidden hierarchy locates between phenotype and subspace as a bridge. Through this hidden hierarchy, phenotype space can thus be understood to its subspace following Weinberg explanatory arrows

Hidden hierarchy during development

The study in developmental biology illustrates how to map genotype and complex structure epigenesis [34, 35]. Back to the 1960s, Eric Davidson conceptualized genotype space as a network structure composed of integrator, receptor, and producer genes [36]. From his perspective, sensors would trigger integrators, which in turn activate receptors acting as cis-regulatory elements, thereby leading to transcription of producer genes [36]. His later work uncovers the presence of such well-organized genetic regulatory networks during sea urchin development [37–39]. Genetic regulatory networks contain conservative kernels for body plan patterning, utilizing substream cis-acting elements and transcription factors as linkages to deploy gene boxes responsible for structure genesis (i.e. Shh, Pax6) [40]. Such systemic organization of genes among genotype space enables spatial-temporal coordination of development process [15, 41]. However, there lies a hidden hierarchy between genotype and phenotype, noted by Conrad Waddington in 1942, named as epigenotype. This concept derived from epigenesis, representing developmental scale (not to be confused with genetic process above classical genetics) interplay [42]. In evolutionary developmental biology, Gerd Müller also proposed the idea of epigenetics in 2000, alluding to physical and chemical interactions among cellular and tissue levels, thus representing an epigenotype space, bridging genotype and phenotype in their views [43–45]. Recent studies on cnidarian regeneration highlight the essentiality of understanding epigenotype, as body self-organization requires mechanical forces and extracellular matrix modulation at the tissue scale, thus elucidating organizing forms among epigenotype space [46].

Neuron space in neurobiology

These seminar works in developmental biology can currently be conceptually mapped onto the field of neurobiology, the neuron-mind mapping problem. This problem arises with advent of the neuron doctrine. Through tissue staining, Cajal suggested neuron as independent elements in nervous system. His idea was proposed as derivation of cell theory developed in 19th century [47]. Neurons are well interconnected with connection structure nominated as synapse [48]. To elucidate how synapses transmit information, Otto Lewi and Henry Dale found acetylcholine as a chemical transmitter released by vagus nerve [49, 50]. Transmitters are packaged in vesicles boarding at presynaptic zone, and their release is triggered by presynaptic calcium influx, with the help of SNAREs to secret into synaptic space [51]. Using electrophysiology, mechanisms for neuron firing are quite clear. Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley quantitatively described how does coordination of sodium, potassium and leaky currents generate an action potential [52]. Release of neurotransmitters elicits postsynaptic potentials, discovered by John Eccles, and whether these effects are excitatory or inhibitory depends on types of receptor involved [48]. Synapse connections are plastic rather than constant, as the phenomenon of frequency potentiation and long-term potentiation in synapse transmission upon various stimuli has been identified by Per Anderson, Tim Bliss, Terje Lømo [53–55]. Synapses transmit information in circuits. And back propagation of information in forms of action potential, long-term potentiation/depression has also been reported [56–58]. Hence, to understand neural functions, neurobiologists often focus on neurons along with their circuits, pioneered by Sherrington’s study in spinal reflexion [59]. Understanding circuits in anatomical and functional aspects soon became one of the most prevailing paradigms in neurobiology. For instance, LTP and novel synapse formation in hippocampus can be interpreted as mechanisms responsible for memories [60–62]. Standing on shoulders of these giants, our current understandings and paradigms are inherited in the same lineage.

In contemporary experimental neurobiology, a widely adopted paradigm involves presenting specific stimuli to subjects, followed by the identification of candidate responsive brain regions through c-fos staining and calcium imaging, ultimately narrowing the focus to neurons defined by specific molecular markers. After hypothesizing a coding process through statistical associations of neural firing with particular stimuli, investigators often proceed to manipulate upstream or downstream circuits by optogenetics or chemogenetics to acquire functional validation. We summarized such paradigm, composed of marker-defined neurons along with their upstream and downstream circuits, as focusing on neuron space. This well matured, streamlined approach indeed generate considerable amounts of datum, greatly strengthening our understanding in working principles of the brain. However, some issues might better be taken seriously. The coding capacity of a single neuron in neuron space depends on its mean firing rate and spike timing precision [63]. However, for instance, firing patterns of grid cells (neurons responsible for generating grid representation of space) are far more complicated than clear-cut environment responsive activities. To identify neurons firing like grid cells, implement of statistical methods has been criticized as generating considerable false-positive results [64]. Besides, John O’keefe had revealed that boundaries in polarized environments persistently shift grid orientation, breaking hexagonal symmetry of grid firing, challenging views of grid cells as authentic, context-independent measure for space65]. Similar issues also present in other functional cell types in entorhinal cortex, since traditional clear-cut definition of neurons specialized as boundary, place or speed cells will leave numerous neurons unidentified, while they also function to navigation66]. Additionally, classification of place cells strongly depends on detection methods, generating distinct identification results [67]. These problems can be improved by assuming cells in hippocampus and entorhinal cortex exert their role in a corporative manner. When considering neural activity at population scale, unclassified neurons under traditional dogma could be counted together, while the imperfect response to spatial cues should be viewed as a particular state of population, leading to better encoding performance [68]. While preserving circuits methods for elucidate neuron space, we must take caution in dealing with directly defining neuron with particular higher function.

Emerging epineuronal space

As discussed above, we shall seek hidden hierarchies between macroscale phenotype and microscale basic elements to integrate reductionist and systemic paradigm. The hidden space between neuron and mind space comprises neural assemblies, which are populations of neurons with collective, synchronizing activities, as conceptualized by György Buzsáki [69]. We termed this space as “epineuronal space”, an analogy to epigenotype space between genotype and phenotype in developmental biology (Table 1). Rather than an absolute umbrella term covering all sorts of research among neuron network level, epineuronal space summarizes an emerging neural organizing form across scales, comprising neural assemblies, neural oscillations, their rhythm synchronies and global brain state. And we advocate to establish such well-organized architect when investigating brain mechanisms behind cognition. Furthermore, through constructing epineuronal space by reductionist strategies, we could uncover circuits and neurons organized by it, finally understanding mind in neuron space.

Analogy and distinction between epigenotype space and epineuronal space

Given the highly interconnected and non-linear nature of the brain, neuron firing is proved as organized in neural assemblies, as firing probability of one neuron is more accurately predicted by combining sensory stimuli with the activity of its peers rather than by considering stimuli in isolation [70]. Clearly, it can be assumed that neurons are not functionally isolated [71]. Utilizing in vivo recordings, brain-wide neural cooperations during movements and cognition have been acknowledged. During uninstructed movements, behavior associated neural activities dominate brain-wide spontaneous activity [72–74]. For mice in decision making, spatial and temporal coordination of activities across the brain corresponds to decision as well as its priori information coding [75, 76]. In contrast to concepts suggesting neurons function to fire robustly in response to specific stimuli, which can be supported by reports that neuron spiking patterns are almost constant rather than shifting, recent works on engram cells have already reported neuron identity shifts [77, 78]. It has been acknowledged that, neurons spiking patterns in stratum upon stimuli are gradually changing [79]. However, these results are obtained under training condition, impacts from learning process may dominate neural response shifts. Nevertheless, Driscoll et al. reported that, in a virtual-navigation decision task, after completing task training, shifts in single neuron firing pattern can still be significant, and they argue that such neuron firing pattern shifts are systematically organized by network activities using population decoder. These findings lead to the assumption, as discussed by Yuste, an engram might happen on a network level [47]. From our perspectives, neurons are incorporated into assemblies, organized by the latter. The robust firing pattern of an individual neuron may represent a node with greater weights. While the collective effects of holistic structure are emergent, the weights of its basic elements can be dynamical, thus generating shifts in neuron identity. As the most basic building blocks in epineuronal space, how do we quantify the organization forms of neural assemblies? To decode such neural corporation, we can sort each single cell spiking information of neural assemblies, then construct them in a matrix to build a manifold structure. This empirical and phenomenological model enables the visualization of dynamic activity trajectories of neural populations in neural assemblies [80]. For instance, the coding of aggressive behavior of mice among VMHvL neurons can be modeled as a linear attractor, and neural coding for head turning can also be abstracted as a ring attractor [81–83]. With the Neural manifold, we can also differentiate distinct states in different behavior trails as well as comparing their topologies [84]. It represents a quantitative, abstract neural dynamics. However, the conformity of the neural manifold framework with differential geometric principles remains elusive, and such empirical models often lack strong generalizability. While we do not deny its considerable power in modeling neural states, the associated modeling and interpretation require utmost caution. Thus, neural manifolds are quantitative and theorized organization forms of neural assemblies, while the latter belong to the fundamental entity in the ontology of epineuronal space.

Beyond this, collective activities of neural assemblies among epineuronal space will physically generate distinct oscillations, initially recognized by Hans Berger and Edgar Adrian [85–87]. These oscillations can be designated by frequency as α, β, γ, etc., with the ratio of the median frequencies between adjacent bands approximating constant e [88]. Unlike artificial and abstract neural manifolds, neural oscillations are concrete and robust phenomena. Neuron spiking is often phase related to neural oscillation waves [11, 89, 90]. John O’keefe reported phase precession of hippocampal place cells spiking in θ bands during movement [91, 92]. For mice in social interactions, phase locking of parvocellular oxytocin neuron spikes to θ bands has also been recognized [93]. In humans, spiking of neurons among amygdala, entorhinal cortex and other regions are phase locked to hippocampal θ oscillation during spatial memory [94,95]. Therefore, it has been suggested that such oscillations are natural organizing forms tuning the temporal spiking of individual neurons in a systemic way [11, 21]. As to their function aspect, oscillations are proved to represent corresponding working state. Occurrence of sharp wave ripples at hippocampus CA1 region functions in memory consolidation and recall [96–100]. Increase of γ oscillations in the visual cortex and enhancement of visual procession occurs in mice of arousal state [101]. In contrast, waves at low frequency of 3–5 Hz waves in the visual cortex reduce neuron response to preferred visual cues [102]. Furthermore, it is well-established that the direct or indirect synchronization of oscillations distributed across brain regions represents a form of dynamic coordination that collectively shapes a global working state, which ultimately leads to emergence of mind. The global working state of brain is sometimes called “brain state” by several researchers [103]. In NREM sleep period, the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) together with thalamocortical circuits, will generate a set of oscillations including spindle, δ waves and slow 1 Hz waves [104–106]. As hypothesized by Mircea Steria, spindle waves originate in TRN, while thalamocortical circuits generate δ waves [105]. Burst firing triggered by optogenetics in TRN leads to cortical spindle-like waves, accompanied by transition to quiescence behavior state of mice [107]. It can be inferred that, thalamocortical synchronization of these rhythms sets the global brain into unconscious state (a brain state with low arousal level) [108–110]. For mice in a dissociation-like brain state induced by ketamine, slow oscillations of 1–3 Hz in retrosplenial cortex are detected [111]. In situ induction of such oscillations leads to dissociation-like behavior, and electrophysiology recording suggests coupling of retrosplenial cortex slow waves to thalamus [111]. More details about neural oscillations have been splendidly discussed by György Buzsáki in his landmark masterpiece Rhythm of the Brain [11]. One might be confused with the relationship between neural manifolds and neural oscillations in epineuronal space. However, though sharing common properties that both constraining neural activities, they differ in essence. Like neural assemblies, neural oscillations are entities in the ontology of epineuronal space, while neural manifolds are inferred abstract structure by design. At the level of neural assembly, neural manifolds could be viewed as the visualization of neuron population dynamics evolution, which can be predicted through manifold topologies by algorithms. And neural oscillations exert particular biological functions. Therefore, considering neural assembly level, they are complementary. Nevertheless, neural oscillations also possess higher functional role, as mentioned above, which is to mediate brain region synchrony, generating brain states, while mind activities are hypothesized to emerge from them. Thus, what’s exactly the purpose of these states?

A brain functions to cycles of learning, integrating experiences, predicting future and exerting actions. How actions impact the brain has been extensively discussed. In modern neurobiology, deducing mind space through behaviors is a mainstream paradigm [112]. Charles Darwin argued expressions as bona fide reflections of animal emotions. In his monograph, he proposed that expressions emerge as functional behaviors occurring simultaneously with corresponding emotions, such that even after the behaviors lose their original function, they continue to co-occur with the associated emotions [113]. Modern psychology establishes a framework of fundamental emotions which can be deduced from facial expressions [114, 115]. This paradigm has been criticized as expressions vary under various circumstances and defining emotions through them is erroneous [116–118]. As for lab animals, exemplified by fields of emotion, a set of behavior tests including forced swim test, tail suspension test, sucrose preference test, etc., are designed to examine depression-like behavior such as desperation and anhedonia [119, 120]. These tests are widely criticized as artificial, discouraging, and disastrously biasing our understanding of emotions in lab animals [121]. It’s reasonable to conclude that these artificially engineered, non-natural behaviors are unreliable, particularly in a context where the validity of natural facial expressions is itself widely contested. However, expressions of lab animal can represent particular emotion states [122, 123]. A recent study suggests patterns of uninstructed movements like pupil diameter and whisker motions can predict mice’s decision making [124]. From our perspectives, elucidating mind space by uninstructed movements or expressions should not be the induction of direct statistical correlation, like problems encountered by other mapping works, that hidden hierarchies exist between the two levels. The hidden hierarchy is also epineuronal space. It’s notable that mind space emerges from epineuronal space, and behaviors are also instructed by the latter. Uninstructed movements have been proved to be capable of eliciting spontaneous across the brain [72]. Relationships between pupil size and states in epineuronal space are also fascinating. Pupil changes correlate with arousal- quiescence state conversion and alterations of oscillations in visual cortex, spiking modes of neurons in dorsolateral geniculate nucleus, cortical activities in so-called quiet wakefulness and sensory response magnitude, and ripples in hippocampus CA1 region [101, 125–127]. David McCormick and Matthew McGinley hypothesized an internal arousal state with best sensory task performance at moderate alert level [126]. Changes in arousal state are accompanied by pupil size and oscillations pattern alterations in the sensory cortex [126]. Thus, we hypothesized that uninstructed movements are bond to various states in epineuronal space. Future research could focus on mapping these natural movements with epineuronal space. Consequently, we could better gain insight into mind space.

Apart from neurobiologists, some system biologists and theorists come up with more abstract models to some extent independent of biological ontology to induct brain’s function128. Can these models uncover the essence of the mind? We refrain from drawing hasty conclusions on this matter since we prefer models based upon particular biological entities, like gene regulatory networks by Eric Davidson, or neural manifolds reflecting neuron population activities. Yet they still provide a more fundamental and abstract systematic understanding of life. However, we take issue with the introduction of mathematical models that are absolutely devoid of experimental justification. Likewise, we also rebel the idea considering the brain as an emergent structure that cannot be understood through its fundamental components as indicated by Weinberg explanatory arrows. Such a view essentially introduces a certain “divinity” detached from physical substance, which is no different from Descartes’ dualism or Driesch’s vitalism. Science shall eliminate such divinity rather than creating it. Besides, contrary to a pure introduction of strategies belonging to systems biology, we still advocate to reduce mind space to neuron space, with epineuronal space as a bridge.

To map neuron and mind space

Here we present a paradigm to understand emergence in the neuron-mind mapping problem by introducing the hidden hierarchy and combining reductionist and systemic approaches (Fig. 2). Weinberg explanatory arrows can be drawn from mind space, through epineuronal and neuron space. The subspace, then, is extensively interactive, well self-organized and subject to the holistic organization of higher space. More specifically, we shall not only record and sort individual neuron spiking preference for particular stimuli, but also considering epineuronal space bridging neurons and mind. That is, to establish a space spanning neural assemblies, neural oscillations, oscillation synchrony, and their emerging global brain states. Such patterns are architected by neural assemblies, the fundamental building blocks, which emerge from population of neurons. The collective dynamics, in turn, impose constraints on the firing of their constituent neurons. Using techniques such as neuropixel recording, we can construct these collective dynamics in the form of neural manifolds based on spiking information of each neuron, as well as local field potential information indicating neural oscillations [129]. Through manifolds, the systemic dynamics of neural assemblies can be visualized and predicted. Having established this, the underlying molecular, genetic and cellular mechanisms must then be elucidated with methods like circuit tracing, patch clamp electrophysiology, etc., thereby converging our understanding into neuron space utilizing top-down systemic organization and bottom-up reductionist construction.

Epineuronal space bridges neuron and mind space. Epineuronal space constitutes a hidden hierarchy conveying Weinberg explanatory arrows. Mind activities emerge from brain state, which are functional global working state posed by oscillation synchrony. As indicated by arrows, brain functions to integrate various spaces systematically, and it could be understood eventually to neuron level

To better illustrate the testability of our hypothesis, we could design a research plan implementing this integrative approach. Once upon seizing a clear scientific question about the neural mechanism behind particular cognition, we shall firstly demonstrate the brain state responsible for it, like thalamocortical patterns during slow wave sleep reported by Steriade [104]. This could be achieved by electroencephalography or local field potential recording. Natural, uninstructed movements could be utilized to represent the actions driven by brain state. After confirming the brain state, driving efforts generating this state should be found. For brain states generated spontaneously, which would be common since brain is self-organizing, identifying pacemaker regions driving these rhythms are the next step. In Steriade’s monograph The Intact and Sliced Brain, he named these pacemaker regions as “command system” [130]. Thus, finding pacemakers capable of generating self-sustaining rhythm is rather essential. To identify them, we should separate the pacemaker candidate regions into slices, blocking synaptic transmission to observe if oscillations still remain. Then we could study the synchrony between pacemakers and effector regions, to clarify the phase relationships between these brain regions, paving the way to dissect circuits synchronizing them. With the help of neuropixel recoding, we could move to model the neural manifolds of these brain regions, characterizing neural assemblies, illustrating the organization forms and elucidating their dynamics. After uncovering epineuronal space, we could reduce this cognition activity to neuron space, with neuron labeling, circuit tracing, and neural manipulating.

This paradigm would encounter several technique problems. Neural oscillations detection results, like spindle, sharp wave ripples, differ drastically when using distinct methods [131, 132]. Oscillations may spread through volume transmission rather than cicuits [133]. Furthermore, optogenetic manipulation often activate entire populations of marker-defined neurons simultaneously, overlooking profound functional heterogeneity among them. Even within a single neuron, functional diversity exists across its segments, and critically, the expression of a common molecular marker does not guarantee functional homogeneity, evidenced by miniscope recordings, which consistently reveal heterogeneous response patterns among neurons sharing the same marker [134–137]. While acknowledging tremendous contributions of optogenetics to current neuroscience, we should consider methods that enable more precise control of neural spiking. In a manner of speaking, issue of heterogeneity haunting neuron labeling and optogenetics also hinders the integrative approach discussed above, as utilization of these circuit level techniques during understanding mind space to neuron space following Weinberg’s explanatory arrow is eventually needful.

Forward-looking: Implications for evolution

A severe problem haunting modern neurobiology is the lack of evolutionary consideration [138]. Specifically, once devoted to mapping neuron and mind, we shall ponder whether the neural mechanisms of lab animals can be applied to human. To properly address this, we must frame it as a question of evolution, since this question actually asks about the phylogenetic distribution of neural mechanisms behind mind or behavior. Evolution takes phylogeny as the form with homology as the kernel. Thus, defining homology of mind should be the priori focus of so-called evolutionary psychology. Descriptive studies akin to natural history are inadequate to answer this question. In other fields, homology recognition often requires comparable elements to identify ancestral and shared derived traits for phylogenetic analysis. Thus, inferring tree of species depends on genetic or morphological traits, while elucidating cell type evolution depends on genetic regulatory networks [139–142]. Hence, comparable entities among genotype space, neuron space and epineuronal space shall be theorized to recognize mind evolution. For instance, with epineuronal structure for particular cognitive function, we can identify the homology of these structures among taxa. Utilizing genetic regulatory network, cell type evolution theory and single-cell sequencing technique, developmental traits of brain region could infer homology among phylogeny. From this perspective, some might propose that the higher cognition function may not be homologous between reptiles and mammals since the avian dorsal ventricular ridge and mammalian neocortex are constructed independently [143–145]. However, this hypothesis is based on the idea that cognition arises from a highly complex brain structure like neocortex. As an emergent phenomenon, cognition actually emerges from corporation among epineuronal space. The clinical case has reported one patient with severe ventricle enlargement and hydrocephalus has normal cognition [146]. Such hydrocephalus may occur extremely early and progress gradually to force neural networks remapping. Reorganized neural networks can still coordinate well at epineuronal space for emergence of mind. As for evolutionary research, homoplasy of these distinct structures could be identified by comparison of oscillation patterns or homeomorphism between neural manifolds among phylogeny. An animal lineage with simple brain structure may utilize distinct organization forms of epineuronal structures to generate analogous emergent cognition. These will ultimately lead to the mapping questions about sufficient organizations in the epineuronal space for mind emergence.

Conclusion

We should not reduce a holistic phenotype directly to the simplest building blocks, nor should we utilize complexity as the excuse to refuse reductionist paradigm. Engineer Emerson Pugh once had a celebrated proverb that brain is too complex to self-understanding. However, hominins in ice age couldn’t imagine modern humans in 20th century can predict presence of black holes by general relativity. In physics, Albert Einstein devoted his efforts to seek a unified theory of physics in his late life, though the results are in vain [147]. Does there exist a unified law for neurobiology, or even biology? Although Ernst Mayr, who founded modern synthesis, besides being an antireductionist, put forth no law present in biology, evolution can be regarded as a law governing biology [148]. Self-organization and emergence processes are also universal in biology [24]. To understand this dominance rule, new paradigms shall seek to resolve the hidden hierarchy between macroscale and microscale. And epineuronal space, established by neural assemblies, oscillations, synchrony and brain states, happens to be this hidden hierarchy, locating between neuron and mind space. For investigating neurobiology, one might pessimistically tend to regard the work of pioneers as “low-hanging fruit”, claiming that “the low-hanging fruit has already been picked by predecessors, and our work is far more difficult in comparison with them.” However, this perspective arises only when we look back through the paradigm which has already been well established by pioneers, making their foundational contributions appear deceptively simple and self-evident—a classic case of hindsight bias. So-called “low-hanging fruit” only emerges once a paradigm is just in place and researchers begin to flock to it in droves. Thus, transformation of scientific thoughts, and shift of scientific paradigms, shall always bring about revolutionary changes to our understanding of nature. As for understanding our mind, we’d rather adopt mathematician David Hilbert’s motto: “We must know, we will know”.
